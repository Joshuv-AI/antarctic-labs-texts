Effects of Theory of Mind and Prosocial Beliefs on Steering Human-Aligned Behaviors of LLMs in Ultimatum Games
Neemesh Yadav, Yihuai Lan, Shan Dong, Mai Hieu Hien, Palakorn Achananuparp, Jing Jiang, Ee-Peng Lim

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown potential in simulating human behaviors and performing theory-of-mind (ToM) reasoning, crucial for complex social interactions. We investigate ToM reasoning’s role in aligning agentic behaviors with human norms in negotiation tasks, using the ultimatum game as our referenced task. We initialized LLM agents with different prosocial beliefs (Greedy, Fair, Selfless) and reasoning methods (chain of thought and ToM reasoning of varying levels), examining their decision-making processes and outcomes across multiple LLMs, including reasoning models like o3-mini and DeepSeek-R1 Distilled Qwen 32B. We perform 2,700 simulations to show that ToM reasoning enhances behavioral alignment with human norms, decision-making consistency, and negotiation outcomes. Consistent with prior findings, reasoning LLMs exhibit limited capability compared to ToM-enhanced LLMs, with different game roles benefiting from different ToM orders. Agents almost always chose strategies consistent with their assigned beliefs, and doing so aligned better with human norms in nearly every role, yet Greedy and Selfless agents still fell short of their norms in outcomes. Human verification further revealed that Llama 3.3 70B produces reasoning most consistent with its actions and beliefs. Our findings advance understanding of ToM’s role in human-AI interaction and cooperative decision-making. The code used for our experiments can be found at https://github.com/Stealth-py/UltimatumToM .

Introduction

Interpersonal communication is fundamental to human interaction, and its effectiveness often hinges on our ability to understand and anticipate others’ perspectives—a cognitive capacity known as theory of mind (ToM)
Premack and Woodruff (1978)
. ToM enables individuals to infer mental states, such as beliefs, desires, and intentions
Georgeff et al. (1999)
, serving as a foundation of social cognition and interaction
Astington and Jenkins (1995)
.

Recent advances in LLMs have prompted researchers to investigate whether these systems exhibit human-like ToM reasoning capabilities. State-of-the-art models like GPT-4 demonstrate promising performance on standard false-belief ToM benchmarks, such as the Sally-Anne and the Smarties tasks
Zhou et al. (2023)
;
Kosinski (2024)
;
Strachan et al. (2024)
and show potential for simulating human behavior across diverse scenarios
Wang et al. (2024)
;
Samuel et al. (2025)
, suggesting their applicability to complex social dynamics.

A significant research gap remains in our understanding of how strategic ToM reasoning influences behavioral alignment between LLMs and humans in social simulations. While LLM-based agents display human-aligned behavior in economic games like the ultimatum game
Sreedhar and Chilton (2025)
, and ToM-informed prompting improves negotiation outcomes from game-theoretic perspectives
Qiu et al. (2024)
, the extent to which ToM reasoning and prosocial beliefs drive behavioral alignment in negotiation contexts remains underexplored. Understanding this behavioral alignment has important implications for designing AI systems in high-stakes domains such as cooperative decision-making
Piatti et al. (2024)
. While
Xie et al. (2024)
investigated trust alignment in similar social simulations (Trust Games), their work does not address how alignment can be steered through the interplay of prosocial beliefs, strategic reasoning, and ToM.

We investigate how ToM reasoning affects LLM behavioral alignment with humans in strategic negotiation tasks. By simulating the ultimatum game (UG)
Güth et al. (1982)
between a
proposer
and a
responder
as a canonical testbed, we evaluate different reasoning strategies, e.g., chain of thought (CoT) and varying ToM levels, across diverse belief combinations.
This
focused, single-game
scope allows us to isolate our primary variable of interest—the conflict between an LLM’s intrinsic model bias and its assigned prosocial beliefs—and eliminate confounding noise such as deception or complex state-tracking.
Our analysis identifies which reasoning approaches yield optimal alignment for each role and which belief configurations best replicate human decision-making.

Our study takes a step forward in understanding LLM behaviors in simulations, how their behavior aligns to that of humans and if complex reasoning methods such as ToM help improve that alignment.
Specifically, we focus on answering the following research questions:

RQ1
: Which agent belief types achieve the highest acceptance rate (AC), and does high AC indicate behavior aligned with economic rationality?

RQ2
: Which LLMs and reasoning methods lead to stronger behavioral alignment?

RQ3
: Which proposer-responder belief combinations most closely match expected human norms?

RQ4
: How does adherence to belief-consistent strategies impact the agents’ alignment with human norms?

Based on 2,700 simulations, we find that
ToM reasoning significantly and role-dependently enhances behavioral alignment
with human norms compared to standard CoT reasoning. Additionally, we find
significant behavioral misalignment
for agents assigned Greedy or Selfless beliefs, even though they almost always select belief-consistent strategies. We hypothesize that this outcome-level gap reflects the intrinsic human-preference fine-tuning of LLMs, which may temper how fully agents enact extreme strategies.

To summarize, our contributions are as follows:

1.

We present a systematic framework for studying the agentic alignment with human norms, conditioned on prosocial belief types, of various LLMs in ultimatum games.

2.

We analyze how different ToM reasonings compare with CoT and affect strategic bargaining between LLM agents.

3.

We extensively study and discuss how LLM strategy choices, their reasoning methods and belief interactions affect behavioral alignment with human norms.

4.

We validate the consistency of LLM strategies and reasoning with their decisions and expected belief-specific behaviors through human verification and statistical analysis.

Game Design

In this study, we focus on a
single-stake

When the responder rejects the offer, the negotiation continues in a new round. If the game reaches the maximum number of rounds (which is part of game setting) without offer acceptance, both agents get a zero payoff for that game.

Reasoning

We experiment with both CoT and ToM reasoning. We follow the classic Belief-Desire-Intention (BDI) model that is widely adopted in the machine theory-of-mind literature
Georgeff et al. (1999)
.
Specifically, we consider four reasoning methods:

1.

CoT
: Popular technique to enhance LLM’s reasoning and problem-solving abilities by prompting the models to ‘think step-by-step’;

2.

Zero-order ToM
: Also known as introspection, where a proposer or responder agent makes assumptions or predictions about its own BDI;

3.

First-order ToM
: Reasoning that involves making predictions about the BDI of the other agent;

4.

Both-ToM (combining zero+first order ToM)
: A Combined reasoning approach that incorporates Zero-order introspection with First-order reasoning to elicit a more complex and interactive ToM reasoning.

In addition, we include a no-reasoning baseline (
Vanilla
) to serve as a control, where agents generate decisions directly without explicit reasoning.

Strategies

We define a set of fine-grained role-specific strategies that can be selected based on the agents’ prosocial belief types and other agents’ responses. These strategies outline the granular action space from which the agents can select, in contrast to high-level strategies, e.g., low-ball offers, used in
Sreedhar and Chilton (2025)
.

•

Proposer
: Proposing very greedily, greedily, fairly, generously, very generously, or Other (to allow for outliers).

•

Responder
: Accepting / Rejecting favorable offers, fair offers, unfavorable offers, or Other (to allow for outliers).

BDI Model

The Belief-Desire-Intention model, originally discussed by
Rao et al. (1995)
, represents three core dimensions of theory-of-mind reasoning. Specifically, they represent a person’s beliefs (what they believe/think about something), desires (what they want/wish to do about something), and intentions (what they will do about something).

3.2
Game Simulation

The multi-agent simulation consists of a conversational game played between two agents, a proposer and a responder. We perform 10 simulations of every game to account for randomness. At the beginning of each game, both agents are initialized with role-specific system prompts. A game is a loop that follows these steps. First, the proposer performs CoT or ToM reasoning to formulate a proposal and a strategy. Then, the proposal’s offer is generated and shown to the responder. Next, the responder uses CoT or ToM reasoning with the offer and makes a decision on whether to accept or reject it, along with a selected strategy. If the responder accepts the offer, the game ends. If the responder rejects the offer, the game continues to the next round. Throughout the game, both agents can access the full conversation history, but they cannot see the private elements of other agents, e.g., reasoning or strategy.

Setup

We include nine belief combinations and five reasoning options (including Vanilla) per belief combination, resulting in a total of 45 experiments per reasoning LLM. We set the stake

4.2
Large Language Models

We conducted experiments with a diverse selection of state-of-the-art LLMs, including three proprietary models (GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, and o3-mini) and three open-source models (DeepSeek-R1 Distilled Qwen 32B, Llama-3.3-70B, and Llama-3.1-8B). Each experiment on an LLM involves 10 simulated games, resulting in 450 games in total per LLM and 2,700 simulated games across all six LLMs. Specific model versions are provided in Appendix

4.4
Evaluation

To evaluate agent interactions, we utilize two distinct categories of evaluation metrics: negotiation outcomes and behavioral alignment.

Behavioral Alignment with Human Players

We measure behavioral alignment by examining proposed, accepted, and rejected shares relative to human norms established in prior ultimatum game literature (see Section
4.3
). We operationalize behavioral alignment primarily through Deviation Scores (DS), which quantify alignment across three dimensions: (1) initially proposed shares (Proposer;
P
, lower is better), (2) accepted shares (Responder;
R^{A}
, lower is better), and (3) rejected shares (Responder;
R^{R}
, higher is better). Both accepted and rejected splits assess responder consistency, while initial proposals suffice for proposers. DS is calculated as the absolute difference between the agent’s value and the expected human share (or the nearest boundary of the expected range) defined in Table
2
, aggregated across games. A detailed definition and interpretation of Deviation Scores is available in Appendix
B
.

Discussion of Main Findings

We structure our discussion around four questions:
RQ1

RQ2
, which LLMs and reasoning methods align best with human norms;
RQ3

RQ4

5.1
RQ1: Economic Rationality Alignment

Bargaining outcomes are dictated by the interaction between LLMs and prosocial beliefs. Fair-Fair combinations consistently achieved 100% AC across all models. Greedy proposers performed best with GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini (100% against Fair responders) and DeepSeek-R1 (100% against Greedy responders). Fair proposers achieved 100% AC with Fair responders (6/6 models) and high AC otherwise, reaching 100% against Selfless responders in 4/6 models. Selfless proposers showed more variability, though o3-mini and DeepSeek-R1 reached 100% against both Greedy and Selfless responders. Full outcomes are in Table

We observe strong belief consistency for Fair and Greedy agents (Orange zone), who achieved significantly better alignment when using their baseline strategies. However, Selfless agents aligned better with norms when accepting Fair offers (deviated strategy) rather than Unfavorable ones (baseline;

6
Verification of BDI Inferences

We assessed belief alignment and the logical coherence of Belief-Desire-Intention (BDI) inferences from all three ToM reasoning types using human annotators and an LLM judge.

Methodology

Three engineers (co-authors) with extensive LLM experience served as annotators. Their technical expertise enabled critical evaluation of model generations in terms of their capabilities and limitations. All were fluent English speakers and received briefings on ultimatum game concepts, theory of mind, and detailed annotation guidelines. GPT-4o served as the LLM judge.

We randomly sampled two turns from all simulated games across 9 proposer-responder belief combinations using the top 3 models (GPT-4o, Llama 3.3, DeepSeek R1), ensuring no two turns came from the same game.
With 3 ToM reasoning methods, this yielded 162 samples (
2\times 9\times 3\times 3
). Each annotator received all 162 samples and evaluated them independently. Both human annotators and the LLM judge evaluated BDI inferences using three binary consistency metrics: 1)
Proposal Consistency
(PC) measures alignment between proposer proposals and their reasoning; 2)
Decision Consistency
(DC) measures alignment between responder decisions and reasoning; and 3)
Belief Consistency
(BC) verifies whether agent reasoning aligns with prosocial beliefs. The first two are role-specific; the latter is role-agnostic.

To assess the difficulty of the task, we measured inter-rater agreement among human annotators and observed an average Gwet’s AC1
Gwet (2008)
of 0.54. This indicates moderate agreement on average, indicating that the tasks require nuanced subjective interpretation. Notably, we report high AC1 scores for PC and DC (0.77, 0.75), but low AC1 scores for both BCs (0.26, 0.38). A detailed breakdown is available in Appendix
E.1
.

Results and Discussion

The LLM judge demonstrated moderate concurrent validity with human annotators, achieving an average Pearson correlation of
r=0.28
(
p<0.001
) and a Spearman rank correlation of
\rho=0.28
(
p<0.001
). Alignment was strongest for visible actions, particularly PC (
\rho=0.52
). However, the near-zero correlation for Responder BC (
\rho=0.03
) indicates a systemic divergence in evaluating BDI inferences.

As detailed in the qualitative analysis (Appendix
G
), this divergence stems from an
action-centric outcome bias
in the LLM judge. Specifically, the LLM judge frequently anchored its evaluation on the agent’s final decision. While human annotators correctly penalized incoherent logic within the BDI chain, the LLM judge produced false positives by rewarding superficial adherence to the game’s rules, struggling specifically with the reactive nature of the responder role.

Despite these limitations, overall performance trends aligned (shown in Figure
5
). Scores were most consistent for Zero-order and Both-ToM, whereas First-order had the highest deviation. Across the board, GPT-4o and Zero-order/Both reasoning methods achieved the highest scores from both kinds of judges, corroborating the performance patterns observed in RQ1.
Conversely, First-order ToM scored consistently lower, likely because it prioritizes inferring others’ states over explicitly articulating the agent’s own BDI chain.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

This study examined how theory of mind (ToM) and prosocial beliefs influence the behavioral alignment of LLM-based agents in ultimatum games. Across 2,700 simulations, Fair-Fair combinations achieved the highest alignment with human norms, with Llama 3.3 and GPT-4o the most consistent performers. Effective reasoning was role-dependent—First-order ToM benefited proposers, while Both-ToM improved responder decisions. Statistical analysis further showed that agents almost always select belief-consistent strategies and that doing so improves alignment, yet Greedy and Selfless agents still fall short of their norms in outcomes, which we hypothesize reflects LLMs’ intrinsic cooperative fine-tuning.

Beyond the ultimatum game, our findings suggest that ToM reasoning helps align LLMs with behavioral norms, that predictable behavior requires balancing intrinsic model alignment with extrinsic belief instructions, and that the role-specific effectiveness of ToM underscores the value of domain-specific reasoning methods. LLMs also show promise for scalable simulation of social dynamics. Future work should extend this approach to other economic scenarios, investigate factors like emotion and trust, and develop robust frameworks for integrating ToM into agent architectures.

Limitations

Our study has key limitations regarding generalizability, agent architecture, and automated evaluation. First, while our focused methodological scope deliberately utilizes the ultimatum game to isolate intrinsic model bias from confounding variables, this single-stake design means our findings may not immediately generalize to multi-issue, complex real-world negotiations. Furthermore, to maintain a controlled experimental baseline, our design restricts agents to a discrete set of predefined actions, which simplifies the nuanced, open-ended nature of human decision-making.

On a related note, while the deviation-score magnitudes we report are specific to the Ultimatum Game’s payoff structure and do not themselves generalize, we hypothesize the underlying behavioral pattern they reveal—persona-instruction breakdown under extreme, non-cooperative role assignments—reflects a model-level property of post-training alignment rather than an artifact specific to this game. We have not tested this hypothesis directly across other negotiation paradigms; doing so is an important direction for future work.

Second, our definition of human norms relies on literature predominantly based on Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) populations, potentially overlooking cultural variations in fairness standards. We also define these expectations independently of proposer-responder interaction dynamics. This simplification suggests that our findings may be sensitive to the assumptions behind those expectations and highlights an area for future research on the role and complexity of these interactions in real-world interactions.

Furthermore, our setup does not include a direct side-by-side baseline between instruction-tuned models against unaligned, pre-trained baselines. Consequently, while the systematic behavioral breakdown observed under Greedy and Selfless conditions aligns closely with the expected goals of post-training RLHF cooperative alignments, we cannot definitively rule out alternative architectural factors, such as implicit data distributions inherited during the pre-training cycles. Furthermore, our experimental design manipulates prompt-based independent variables to observe their effects on dependent variables; it does not utilize mechanistic interpretability techniques to map internal neural latents. We explicitly frame our alignment findings as a behavioral phenomenon and suggest that isolating the precise training stage responsible for this behavioral limitation remains a crucial direction for future mechanistic research.

Finally, as demonstrated in our human verification analysis, employing LLMs as judges for internal cognitive states remains an open challenge. The observed action-centric outcome bias indicates that while automated evaluators are reliable for assessing visible, rule-bound actions, they currently struggle to accurately parse the subjective belief consistencies of reactive agents. Future work must develop more robust, socially-aware automated evaluation frameworks to accurately measure internal alignment without relying entirely on human annotation.

Ethical Considerations

LLMs with advanced ToM capabilities could be misused for deceptive and manipulative purposes, such as influencing purchasing and other financial decisions, exploiting individuals’ cognitive and emotional vulnerabilities, or social engineering. Such misuse of ToM agents could undermine trust in human-AI interactions. Specific guardrails and policies must be considered to limit the use of ToM reasoning in high-risk contexts, such as financial decisions and personal persuasion.

A.1
Ultimatum Game

Ultimatum game
Güth et al. (1982)
, also called UG, is a well-known economic game in which one player (
proposer
) decides how to split a sum of money (
stake
) with another player (
responder
). It has been extensively employed to study the human decision-making process with outcomes influenced by several factors, such as prosociality, fairness expectations, cultural norms, and the participants’ emotional states
Hoffman et al. (1996)
;
Nowak et al. (2000)
;
Thaler (1988)
. The game setup can consist of either a single round or multiple rounds (repeated games), with a single stake or multiple stakes.

A.2
Rational Behavior in UG

Formally, an offer from the proposer is
(p,q)
where
q=\mathcal{S}-p
and
\mathcal{S}
is the stake. From a strict Pareto optimal perspective, any offer where
p+q=S
is considered Pareto optimal, as it is impossible to reallocate the stake to make one player better off without harming the other. However, empirical evidence shows that human behaviors deviate from this rational economic model
Thaler (1988)
. Proposer’s offer and responder’s decision are influenced by their perception of fairness and social norms. Any offer where
p=q=\mathcal{S}/2
(an equal split) is considered a fair offer by most people
Schuster (2017)
. In game-theoretical analysis, UG has a subgame perfect Nash equilibrium
Camerer (2011)
in which a rational responder would never reject an offer if
q\geq 1
, since rejecting it would result in a zero payoff. However, prior economic studies show that responders often reject unfair offers.

Truly selfless behavior in the ultimatum game is less commonly studied
Henrich et al. (2005)
and inherently complex. For example, extremely generous offers can be perceived as manipulative and insincere, leading to frequent rejection by responders
Thaler (1988)
. We heuristically define it as the conceptual opposite of greedy behavior. These proposed expectations are independent of the belief of counterpart agents and may not align with what is considered
rational
from a purely theoretical perspective.

A.3
Choices of Prosocial Beliefs

These beliefs shown in Table
1
were chosen since they represent fundamental traits that influence decision-making dynamics in ultimatum games. Moreover, they have been extensively studied in human participants
Mazzolini and Celani (2020)
;
Ferguson et al. (2014)
;
Brethel-Haurwitz et al. (2016)
, therefore providing well-documented behaviors for comparison. Hence, a total of 9 proposer-responder belief combinations can be formed: Greedy-Fair, Greedy-Greedy, Greedy-Selfless, Fair-Fair, Fair-Greedy, Fair-Selfless, Selfless-Fair, Selfless-Greedy, and Selfless-Selfless.

While some combinations, such as Selfless-Selfless or Greedy-Selfless, represent rare extreme-case interactions, they are essential for testing the limits of LLM role-adherence and identifying the belief-consistency trade-offs.

A.4
Methodological Scope, Generalizability, and Novelty

Methodological Scope and Generalizability

Our analysis prioritizes depth over breadth. We utilize the ultimatum game as a canonical testbed to establish a controlled strategic baseline, which allows for a comprehensive examination of agentic behavior while minimizing the confounding factors inherent in more complex, multi-issue negotiations. In multi-issue negotiations, e.g., Prisoner’s Dilemma
Fontana et al. (2025)
and Deal or No Deal
Sicilia et al. (2024)
, confounding variables, such as complex state tracking and deception, complicate the analysis
Akata et al. (2025)
. By restricting the scope to a single-stake, multiple-round game, we isolate the variable of interest: intrinsic model bias versus assigned belief. The $10 stake is a standard baseline in behavioral economics literature, allowing us to anchor our Expected Behavior heuristics in established human data.

A diagnostic design that fully interrogates one game—full factorial sweep, statistical decomposition, human-annotated reasoning-trace audit—necessarily trades cross-game generalizability for within-game analytical depth. Extending to a second testbed would require re-deriving expected-behavior heuristics and analysis frameworks for that game, diluting depth in both rather than adding to either; studies that span multiple testbeds face the same per-game depth ceiling, distributed across more games instead. Generalizability in our design instead comes from model coverage (six LLMs across proprietary and open-source architectures) and statistical power (2,700 simulations), rather than from game count.

The single-game diagnostic approach is also an active area of study, as of 2025–2026, rather than a dated paradigm, spanning the one-shot Ultimatum Game
Ferraz et al. (2026)
;
Kitadai et al. (2025)
, Dictator Games
Ma (2026)
, the Prisoner’s Dilemma
Orland and Takemoto (2025)
;
Fontana et al. (2025)
, Public Goods Games
Long and Teplica (2025)
;
Piedrahita et al. (2025)
, and related altruism and economic-choice studies
Capraro et al. (2025)
;
Johnson and Obradovich (2025)
;
Phelps and Russell (2025)
;
Zhang et al. (2026)
;
Kirshner et al. (2025)
.

Correspondingly, the deviation-score magnitudes we report are specific to the Ultimatum Game’s payoff structure and do not themselves generalize to multi-issue, real-world negotiations. What we argue may generalize is the underlying behavioral pattern the game reveals—persona-instruction breakdown under extreme, non-cooperative role assignments—which we hypothesize reflects a model-level property of post-training alignment rather than a game-specific artifact. We would expect the same class of effect in other negotiation settings, though its magnitude and shape likely vary with structural factors such as stake divisibility or round count; testing this directly across multiple negotiation paradigms remains an important direction for future work.

Novelty

Within this space, our contribution is distinct from the closest comparators, such as Fontana et al.
Fontana et al. (2025)
, Bianchi et al.
Bianchi et al. (2024)
, Smith et al.
Smith et al. (2026)
, and Sicilia et al.
Sicilia et al. (2024)
, in both design and dependent variable.

Fontana et al. (2025)
deliberately avoid persona prompting in order to isolate models’ inherent baseline bias, explicitly flagging persona-based prompting as a direction for future work; this is precisely what our belief-conditioned design investigates. Their diagnostic approach classifies behavior into strategy archetypes (e.g., Tit-for-Tat, Grim Trigger) via trait profiling, rather than through regression against assigned persona. Notably, their finding that LLMs behave “nicer” (more cooperative) than human players in the Prisoner’s Dilemma is independently consistent with the cooperative-prior mechanism we hypothesize in Sections
5.3
–
5.4
; their design, however, does not test whether that prior can be overridden by explicit persona instruction, which is precisely where our finding that Greedy and Selfless agents fall short of their norms despite selecting belief-consistent strategies (Sections
5.3
–
5.4
) provides direct evidence.

In NegotiationArena,
Bianchi et al. (2024)
measure relative negotiation competence via win-rate and payoff comparison under a uniform reasoning format, without benchmarking against human behavioral norms or isolating belief, reasoning, and architecture as independent factors via regression.

Next, Concordia
Smith et al. (2026)
is environment-centric, observing the breadth of emergent cooperation across open-ended simulations; our design is variable-centric, mapping the depth of behavioral alignment under strict factorial conditioning. Where Concordia examines the
what
of emergent cooperative generalization across situations, our framework provides the
how
of behavioral alignment under explicit conditioning.

Sicilia et al. (2024)
forecast negotiation-outcome uncertainty using calibration metrics rather than participating in or scoring live strategic play—a different dependent variable (predictive uncertainty, not behavioral alignment with human norms).

None of these three studies manipulate ToM reasoning as an independent variable, use persona-based behavioral scaffolding, or apply a belief/reasoning/architecture regression decomposition. Our human-annotated audit of reasoning traces (Section
6
) is a further point of difference from all three: rather than treating agent output as a black box, we verify whether a model’s stated reasoning is internally consistent with its assigned belief and final action.

Appendix B
Deviation Score (DS) Definition and Interpretation

B.1
Definition

To quantify the alignment between AI agents and human norms, we introduced the Deviation Score (DS). This metric calculates the absolute distance between the agent’s decision and the expected behavior of a human with the corresponding belief (Fair, Greedy, or Selfless). The general formula for DS is defined as:

DS=|\mu_{\text{human}}-x_{\text{agent}}|

(2)

where
\mu_{\text{human}}
is the expected human norm (derived from Table
2
) and
x_{\text{agent}}
is the value associated with the agent’s decision.

The interpretation of better alignment varies by role due to the nature of the decision tasks.

Proposer (
P
) and Responder Acceptance (
R^{A}
)

For these roles, the agent is attempting to match a specific target value (e.g., a Fair proposal of 50%). Thus,
DS
represents an error term (
DS=|\text{Norm}-\text{Offer}|
). A perfect alignment with human norms results in a score of zero, while deviations increase the score, i.e., lower is better. For example, a Fair agent (human norm = 50) proposing a share of 50 for themselves achieves
DS=|50-50|=0
(perfect alignment). On the other hand, a proposal of 90 from a Fair agent yields
DS=40
(misalignment).

Responder Rejection (
R^{R}
)

For rejection tasks,
DS
represents the validity of the punishment. Since rejection is a punitive action intended to sanction norm violations, the metric measures how far the rejected offer was from the acceptable norm (
DS=|\text{Norm}-\text{Rejected Offer}|
). Therefore, higher
DS
indicates better alignment with human norms. For instance:

•

Rational Rejection: If a Fair agent (human norm = 50) rejects an extremely unfair offer of zero, the deviation is
|50-0|=50
. This high score indicates the agent correctly identified and rationally punished a severe norm violation.

•

Irrational Rejection: If a Fair agent rejects a fair offer of 50, the deviation is
|50-50|=0
. This low score indicates an unjustified rejection of a norm-compliant offer, suggesting spiteful or irrational behavior.

Appendix C
Statistical Analysis

To answer the main research questions, we utilized two statistical approaches to analyze factors associating with agent alignment. All analyses use the Deviation Score (DS) as the dependent variable.

C.1
General Factors Analysis (RQ1–RQ3)

To determine the independent effects of model architecture, reasoning, and beliefs on human alignment, we fitted an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression model. The model estimates the effect of each categorical variable as follows:

\displaystyle DS=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\text{model}+\beta_{2}\text{reasoning}

\displaystyle+\beta_{3}\text{belief}_{P}+\beta_{4}\text{belief}_{R}+\epsilon

where
\text{belief}_{P}
and
\text{belief}_{R}
represent proposer belief and responder belief, respectively.
\beta_{0}
represents the intercept, and
\beta_{1}
through
\beta_{4}
represent the coefficients of corresponding categorical variables.
\epsilon
represents the residual error term. DeepSeek R1, CoT, and Fair belief served as reference categories for
n-1
dummy coding for model, reasoning, and proposer & responder belief variables, respectively. The coefficients reported in Table
3
represent the change in DS relative to the reference category.

Ultimately, three OLS models were fitted to examine Deviation Scores of agents based on three distinct roles: proposer behavior (
P
), responder acceptance (
R^{A}
), and responder rejection (
R^{R}
).

C.2
Belief-Strategy Consistency Analysis (RQ4)

To address RQ4, we required a granular analysis of how specific strategy choices interact with agent beliefs. We fitted a Cell Means model (without an intercept) to estimate the marginal mean deviation for every unique combination of Agent Belief and Strategy:

\displaystyle DS_{ij}=\mu_{ij}+\epsilon_{ij}

where
DS_{ij}
represents the Deviation Score for an observation corresponding to Agent Belief
i
and Strategy
j
.
\mu_{ij}
represents the estimated marginal mean deviation for that specific belief-strategy combination.
\epsilon_{ij}
represents the residual error term.

Using the estimated means from this model, we performed post-hoc linear contrasts. For each agent belief, we defined the belief-consistent strategy as the baseline
\mu_{baseline}
and compared it against alternative belief-inconsistent strategies
\mu_{alternative}
. The contrast
\psi
is defined as:

\displaystyle\psi=\mu_{alternative}-\mu_{baseline}

The sign of
\psi
determines the relative magnitude of the Deviation Score (DS). Specifically, the implication for alignment depends on the role-specific objective of the DS metric:

•

Proposer & Responder Acceptance (Lower DS is Better):
A
positive contrast
(
\psi>0
) indicates that the alternative strategy had higher error than the baseline. Thus, the
belief-consistent (baseline)
strategy is better aligned. Conversely, negative values indicate the
Deviated
strategy is better aligned.

•

Responder Rejection (Higher DS is Better):
A negative contrast (
\psi<0
) indicates that the alternative strategy had a lower score than the baseline. Since human norms for Fair Responders dictate rejecting unfair offers, a higher score indicates better alignment. Thus, a
negative contrast
here indicates that the
belief-consistent (baseline)
strategy is better aligned, and vice versa.

Exclusions

We test a contrast only when both belief–strategy cells contain at least one observation. Under the no-intercept parameterization, a combination with no observations yields an all-zero design column, so its mean is estimated as zero with zero variance and any contrast against it is spurious. We also exclude contrasts between two belief-consistent strategies (e.g., very greedy vs. greedy for Greedy proposers), as these do not test adherence. In this analysis, the proposer DS is zero for any share within the belief’s expected range, matching the definition in Appendix
B
.

Erratum

The proceedings version of this paper reported that Greedy proposers achieve better alignment when deviating from their belief-consistent strategies, and that agents fail to align when their belief-consistent strategy calls for rejecting fair offers. Both findings were artifacts of the analysis code. First, contrasts were computed against belief–strategy combinations with no observations (e.g., no Greedy agent ever labeled an opening proposal as generous), producing spurious, highly significant contrasts. Second, the figure included contrasts between two belief-consistent strategies, one of which necessarily falls on the deviated side. Third, in-range proposals (e.g., a Greedy agent keeping $8) were assigned a non-zero DS in this analysis only. Section
5.4
and Figure
6
report the corrected results. Results for RQ1–RQ3, which use a separate implementation of DS, and the human verification are unaffected.

Findings

The belief-conditioning breakdown pattern reported in Sections
5.1
and
5.3
is not uniform across the four models. Gemini 2.5 Pro and GPT-5.2 replicate the pattern most directly: Greedy proposers do not consistently propose a
\geq
70% self-favoring split, e.g., GPT-5.2’s Greedy-Greedy condition averages a 47–48% proposer share, close to an even split rather than the greedy target. Claude Opus 4.8 shows a related but more extreme version of the same pattern under Greedy-Fair and Greedy-Greedy, proposing a 50-50 split in all four cells regardless of reasoning method, but adheres closely to its Greedy belief under Greedy-Selfless (89–90% proposer share), suggesting the breakdown is not uniform across responder-belief pairings even within a single model.

Selfless proposers similarly do not consistently offer the
\leq
30% self-share their belief would predict across models. Proposer shares under Selfless-Fair range from 0% (Claude Opus 4.8, Vanilla; GPT-5.2, Both-ToM is close) to 36% (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Both-ToM, exceeding the expected selfless ceiling). Claude Opus 4.8 in particular shows an opposite deviation under several Selfless conditions, proposing 0% self-share (more generous than the belief’s own
\leq
30% ceiling calls for).

GPT-5.4 nano, the efficient-tier model in this ablation, departs from the other three most visibly on acceptance rate. Specifically, AC falls as low as 30% under Selfless-Fair and Selfless-Greedy (Vanilla), compared to consistently high AC (
\geq
80%, mostly 100%) across nearly all cells for the other three models. This indicates that at smaller scale, belief-conditioning breakdown extends beyond proposer/responder deviation scores into whether a negotiation concludes at all, a form of breakdown not observed in the frontier models in this ablation or in the original six-model sweep.

Overall, these results are directionally consistent with the original finding—models systematically deviate from the norms of extreme (Greedy/Selfless) assigned beliefs toward more equitable or otherwise belief-inconsistent outcomes—though the specific shape of that deviation varies by model and seemingly interacts with model tier (frontier vs. efficient). We treat this ablation as a targeted, observational check rather than a formal statistical replication of RQ4, consistent with its more limited scope.

The qualitative analysis shows that the divergence is due mainly to an action-centric outcome bias in the LLM judge. While human annotators pragmatically evaluate the authentic alignment of an agent’s internal BDI chain, the LLM judge frequently anchors its evaluation on the agent’s final decision, rewarding superficial adherence to the game’s rules rather than true persona consistency. We further identified two primary patterns of LLM judge failure:

Outcome Bias

In scenarios where an agent’s assigned belief conflicts with its economic goal (e.g., a Selfless proposer instructed to maximize its share), LLM agents often generate circular or conflicting reasoning. Human annotators accurately penalized these traces for lacking logical coherence. However, the LLM judge consistently marked them as Consistent. As long as the final action (e.g., proposing a split) was valid within the game’s rules, the LLM judge ignored the incoherence of the internal justification, prioritizing the validity of the outcome over the soundness of the reasoning.

Reactive-Role Failure

The highest rate of disagreement occurred in Responder Belief Consistency. Unlike proposers, who proactively state their intents, responders are reactive. In these cases, the LLM agents often hallucinated their reasoning by exclusively analyzing the Proposer’s mindset, entirely failing to articulate their own assigned persona. For example, when a Selfless responder was presented with an offer, it justified its acceptance by analyzing the proposer’s equitable intent rather than its own selfless desires. Human experts penalized this as a belief violation. However, the LLM judge frequently marked it as perfectly consistent simply because the act of Accepting did not violate the rules. This suggests that while LLM judges can map explicit actions to rules, they struggle with an outcome bias that makes them currently unreliable for evaluating the internal belief consistency of reactive agents.

Appendix H
Self-Preferential Bias Analysis

In Section
6
, we employed GPT-4o as both the evaluated model and the LLM judge that audited the reasoning traces. Since a judge model evaluating its own outputs raises the possibility of self-preferential bias
Zheng et al. (2023)
, we conduct a targeted analysis to assess whether this specific mechanism explains the divergence reported in Section
6
, rather than the action-centric outcome bias documented in Appendix
G
.

Method

We split our existing 162-sample annotation set by base model: GPT-4o, Llama 3.3, and DeepSeek R1, yielding 54 samples per model. For each model subset, we compute (1) the correlation between judge and human-majority ratings, and (2) counts of judge-favorable disagreement (judge rates Consistent, human majority rates Inconsistent) across four consistency metrics: Proposer Proposal Consistency (PC) and Proposer Belief Consistency (BC) (Spearman’s
\rho
and Pearson’s
r
respectively, per metric type), and Responder Decision Consistency (DC) and Responder Belief Consistency (BC). If self-preferential bias were driving the reported judge-human divergence, we would expect GPT-4o’s judge-favorable count to be elevated relative to the other two models. Because per-cell disagreement counts are small, we treat these results as suggestive rather than statistically robust.

Results

Table
15
reports correlation and agreement rates by model. GPT-4o’s Responder DC correlation (
\rho
= 0.03) is notably weaker than DeepSeek R1’s (0.36) or Llama 3.3’s (0.38); on Responder BC, all three models show negligible correlation (-0.02 to 0.13) with no meaningful separation between them.

Table
16
reports judge-favorable disagreement counts by model. GPT-4o’s judge-favorable counts are the lowest across all four metrics (1, 3, 2, and 7, versus 1–10 for the other two models). This is the opposite of the pattern self-preferential bias would predict. GPT-4o does show lower overall agreement on Proposer PC and Proposer BC specifically (81% and 65%, versus 72–96% for the other two models), but this is driven by judge-unfavorable disagreements—cases where the judge rates Inconsistent but the human majority rates Consistent—again the opposite direction from what self-preferential bias predicts.

Across both the correlation- and count-based analyses, we do not find a pattern consistent with the judge systematically favoring its own outputs. This is more consistent with our qualitative analysis (Appendix
G
) of a general action-centric outcome bias, which would not be expected to systematically favor any one model over another. Given the small per-model sample sizes, we report this as a targeted robustness check rather than a definitive resolution of the self-preferential bias concern; we still recommend a held-out judge or corroboration across multiple LLM judges as a more rigorous design for future work.
