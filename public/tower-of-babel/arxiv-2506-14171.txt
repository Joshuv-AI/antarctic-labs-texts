Coordinate-energy transformation and the one-point function for the Heisenberg-Ising XXZ spin- 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} chain on the ring
Eric I. Corwin, Nikolaus Elsaesser, Axel Saenz

Abstract
We provide a basis transformation that inverts the coordinate Bethe Ansatz. It is widely believed that the Bethe Ansatz is complete, based on numerical evidence and combinatorial arguments. We present a constructive and comprehensive approach to show that the Bethe Ansatz is complete. The transformation formula is verified numerically with excellent accuracy for non-zero anisotropy parameters and rings of odd length. Additionally, assuming the Bethe Ansatz is complete, we derive a novel exact formula for the one-point function systems through special identities for the Izergin-Korepin determinant.

Introduction

Spin
is a discrete conserved quantity for particles in quantum mechanics arising from rotational symmetry. In classical mechanics, rotational symmetry leads to conservation of angular momentum. In particular, angular momentum arises from an infinite dimensional representation of the Lorentz group of rotations. In quantum mechanics, spin arises from finite representations of the Lorentz group

Due to Noether’s theorem (see
[
Sch18
, Sec. 4.5]
), a rotational symmetry of spacetime along a given axis leads to conserved total angular momentum along that axis. For a particle like an electron, orbital angular momentum and spin are what comprise its total angular momentum.
. Spin interacts with magnetic fields as if the particle were rotating about its center of mass, creating an analogy between bar magnets and spin particles. In 1921, the Stern-Gerlach experiment verified that spin is quantized, i.e. discrete. For more detailed description of quantum spin, see
[
Sch18
, Sec. 3.7, 4.5, 8.5]
.

The
Heisenberg-Ising XXZ spin-1/2 chain
is a nearest neighbour spin-spin interaction model with an anisotropy for the spin in the z-direction. It is a well-known and well-studied model in the math and physics literature due to rich symmetries of the model. Each particle has spin
1/2
or
-1/2
, and we refer to these particles, respectively, as
up-spin
and
down-spin
particles. The XXZ spin-1/2 chain is a specific example of a general class of quantum spin chain models. See
[
PF10
]
for a general introduction of quantum spin chains.

Hans Bethe, in 1931
[
Bet31
]
, developed an Ansatz – a method – to obtain a collection of eigenvectors for the Hamiltonian of the XXX spin-1/2 chain, i.e. the isotropic limit of the XXZ spin-1/2 chain, on a one-dimentional lattice. This method is called the
Bethe Ansatz
, and it generalizes to the XXZ spin-1/2 chain and many other models with similar symmetries. See
[
Bax82
,
Gau14
]
for a thorough introduction of the Bethe Ansatz.

It is not clear, a priori, that the Bethe Ansatz produces all of the eigenvectors for the Hamiltonian of the XXZ spin-1/2 chain. The
Bethe eigenvectors
, i.e. eigenvectors produced by the Bethe Ansatz, are determined by solutions of a system of coupled algebraic equations, called
Bethe equations
, where the degree of the equations depends of the length of the spin chain and the size of the system of equations depends on the number of up-spin particles. Some
Bethe roots
, i.e. solutions of the Bethe equations, may lead to trivial Bethe vectors, Bethe eigenvectors may be linearly dependent, or the Bethe vectors may not generate the entire vector space.

There are precise arguments to show completeness of the Bethe Ansatz in the literature. In
[
TV95
]
, the authors give an existence proof for completeness by showing completeness for special parameters and showing that deformations are algebraic and non-degenerate. In
[
Bor+15
,
Bor+15a
]
, the authors provide a Plancherel-type isomorphism between the canonical coordinate basis and the Bethe vectors for interacting particle systems on the infinite integer lattice, including the asymmetric simple exclusion process and the XXZ spin-1/2 chain. In
[
GK17
]
, completeness and solutions for the Bethe ansatz equations are done in relation to quantum cohomology for a degeneration of the asymmetric six vertex model.

We consider a constructive approach to the completeness of the Bethe Ansatz. The coordinate Bethe Ansatz provides a basis transformation, assuming the Bethe Ansatz is complete, from the coordinate basis to the energy (i.e. eigenvector) basis. We provide a closed formula for the inverse transformation, from the energy basis to the coordinate basis, given explicitly in
3.11
. We have extensive numerical evidence that our conjecture is true. As a direct consequence, if

2
The Heisenberg-Ising XXZ spin-1/2 chain

We consider the
Heisenberg-Ising XXZ spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
chain on a periodic lattice. The length of the lattice is given by a positive integer
L\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Each site of the lattice is occupied by a
spin-up
particle,
\mid\uparrow\rangle
, or a
spin-down
particle,
\mid\downarrow\rangle
. As the system evolves, the labels of the sites are updated due to local, pairwise interactions. Additionally, due to the dynamics of the model, the quantity of up-spin labels are conserved as time evolves. We let
N
denote the number of up-spin particles in the system. We give the details of the model in the following sections.

2.1
Notation

We use the following notation throughout the paper:

•

Given
L\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, we denote the possible locations on the ring as follows

[L]:=\{0,1,2,\dots,L-1\}

(2)

•

Given
N,L\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, we denote and index the solutions to the equation
\eta^{L}=(-1)^{N-1}
as follows

\eta(k)=\begin{cases}e^{\pi i(2k+1)/L},&\quad N\text{ is even}\\
e^{2\pi ik/L},&\quad N\text{ is odd}\end{cases}

(3)

for
k\in[L]
.

•

Given
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, we denote the group of permutations on the set
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
by
S_{N}
so that
\sigma\in S_{N}
is a bijection on
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
and composition is the group action.

2.2
Configuration, states, and probability

We consider the evolution of
N
spin-up particles on a lattice ring of length
L
. A configuration is given by the location of the
N
spin-up particles
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})
so that
x_{i}\in[L]
and
x_{i}<x_{i+1}
. We denote the space of configurations by
\mathcal{X}
, which is given by

\mathcal{X}=\{(x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{N})\mid x_{i}\in[L],\,i=1,2,\dots,N\}.

(4)

The evolution of the particle is given by the Schrödinger equation from quantum mechanics. Thus, we take the set of configurations
\mathcal{X}
to be an orthonormal basis set. For each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have a basis vector
|\mathbf{x}\rangle
and we introduce the vector space generated by these vectors

\mathbb{H}:=\mathrm{Span}\{|\mathbf{x}\rangle\mid\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\}.

(5)

We introduce a inner product structure for vector space
\mathbb{H}
,

\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{H}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C},

(6)

by taking coordinate basis to be orthonormal. We set

\langle\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}\rangle:=\delta_{\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}}

(7)

for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}
. We extend the inner product anti-linearly on the second argument,

\langle\mathbf{y}|\alpha\,\mathbf{x}_{1}+\beta\,\mathbf{x}_{2}\rangle=\overline{\alpha}\langle\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{1}\rangle+\overline{\beta}\langle\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{2}\rangle,

(8)

for any
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
where the bar denotes the complex conjugate of a complex number. Lastly, we make the inner product conjugate symmetric,

\langle\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}\rangle=\overline{\langle\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y}\rangle},

(9)

for any
|\mathbf{x}\rangle,|\mathbf{y}\rangle\in\mathbb{H}
. The properties (
7
) – (
9
) completely determine the inner product
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
.

The vector space
\mathbb{H}
with the inner product
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
is a Hilbert space. A vector
|\Psi\rangle\in\mathbb{H}
in the Hilbert space is called a
state (of the configuration space)
if it is normalized
\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle=1
. Otherwise, a non-zero vector is called an unnormalized state. States will remain normalized under the dynamics described below, and we will only consider normalized states. Given a state
|\Psi\rangle
, the probability that the state is
measured
in configuration
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
is given by the
Born rule
from the postulates of quantum mechanics

\mathbb{P}(|\Psi\rangle=|\mathbf{x}\rangle)=\langle\Psi|\mathbf{x}\rangle\langle\mathbf{x}|\Psi\rangle=|\langle\mathbf{x}|\Psi\rangle|^{2}.

(10)

2.3
Spin configuration and local operators

The evolution of the system is generated by the action local Hamiltonian operators. We define the local Hamiltonian operators in an auxiliary vector space, given by the spin configurations. We identify the vector space
\mathbb{H}
, given by the particle configurations, as a subspace of the auxiliary vector space. Then, we define the local Hamiltonian operators on
\mathbb{H}
by restricting the local Hamiltonian operators on the auxiliary vector space.

Consider a two dimensional complex vector space with a basis given by spin-up and spin-down labels,

\mathbb{V}=\mathrm{Span}\{\mid\uparrow\rangle,\mid\downarrow\rangle\}.

(11)

Let
\mathbb{V}_{L}=\mathbb{V}^{\otimes L}
be the
L
-fold tensor product of
\mathbb{V}
. A basis for
\mathbb{V}_{L}
is given by taking
L
tensors of basis elements of
\mathbb{V}
. For instance, the basis of
\mathbb{V}_{2}
is given and denoted as follows

\mid\uparrow\rangle\otimes\mid\uparrow\rangle=\mid\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,\,\mid\uparrow\rangle\otimes\mid\downarrow\rangle=\mid\uparrow\downarrow\rangle,\,\mid\downarrow\rangle\otimes\mid\uparrow\rangle=\mid\downarrow\uparrow\rangle,\,\mid\downarrow\rangle\otimes\mid\downarrow\rangle=\mid\downarrow\downarrow\rangle.

(12)

We identify
\mathbb{H}
, given by (
5
), as a subspace of
\mathbb{V}_{L}
. Each basis element
|X\rangle\in\mathbb{H}
is identified with a basis element of
\mathbb{V}_{L}
where the position of the up-spins are given by
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathcal{X}
. For instance, we have the identification

\mid\uparrow\downarrow\rangle=|1\rangle,\,\mid\downarrow\uparrow\rangle=|2\rangle

(13)

when
N=1
and
L=2
. It is clear that this identification unique and well-defined. Thus, we have
\mathbb{H}\subseteq\mathbb{V}_{L}
.

The local Hamiltonian operators are given by tensor products of the
Pauli matrices
on
\mathbb{V}
,

\sigma^{x}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),\quad\sigma^{y}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\\
i&0\end{array}\right),\quad\sigma^{z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right),

(14)

with respect to the basis given by
\mid\uparrow\rangle
and
\mid\downarrow\rangle
. Then, we introduce the following operators on
\mathbb{V}_{L}

S^{\alpha}_{i}S^{\alpha}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{Id}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{Id}\otimes\sigma^{\alpha}\otimes\mathrm{Id}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{Id}\otimes\sigma^{\alpha}\otimes\mathrm{Id}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathrm{Id}\right)

(15)

where
\mathrm{Id}
is the identity operator on
\mathbb{V}
and the location of the operators
\sigma^{\alpha}
is given by
i,j\in[L]
, respectively, with
i\neq j
. In particular, the operator
\sigma^{x}\otimes\sigma^{x}+\sigma^{y}\otimes\sigma^{y}+(1-\Delta)\sigma^{z}\otimes\sigma^{z}
, with
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
, has the following representation

\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&0&0&0\\
0&-\Delta&1&0\\
0&1&-\Delta&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
\end{array}\right)

(16)

with respect to the basis
\mid\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,\mid\uparrow\downarrow\rangle,\mid\downarrow\uparrow\rangle,\mid\downarrow\downarrow\rangle
. Note that these operators conserve the number of up-spins. Moreover, it follows that we may restrict the following
local Hamiltonian operators

h_{i,j}(\Delta)=S^{x}_{i}S^{x}_{j}+S^{y}_{i}S^{y}_{j}+\Delta(S^{z}_{i}S^{z}_{j}-1/2),

(17)

for
i,j\in[L]
with
i\neq j
, to the vector space
\mathbb{H}
since it maps
\mathbb{H}
to itself.

2.4
Dynamics

The evolution of the system is given by a Schrödinger equation. The Hamiltonian for the system is given by a sum of local operators

H_{\mathrm{XXZ}}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}h_{i,j}(\Delta),\quad\Delta\in\mathbb{R}

(18)

where
h_{i,j}(\Delta)
is given by (
17
) and the indexes are taken modulo
L
so that the indexes
i=L+1
and
i=1
are identified. The
anisotropy
parameter
\Delta
is required to be real so that the operator is Hermitian, a sufficient condition in to have real eigenvalues. Then, we consider the evolution of the system given by the following Schrödinger equation with deterministic initial conditions

\begin{cases}i\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi(t)\rangle=H_{\mathrm{XXZ}}|\Psi(t)\rangle,\\
|\Psi(0)\rangle=|\mathbf{y}\rangle\end{cases}

(19)

with
|\Psi(t)\rangle\in\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{X}
.

3
Bethe Ansatz

The Bethe Ansatz is a method to construct a collection of eigenvectors, called the
Bethe eigenvectors
, for certain generators of one-dimensional interacting particle systems with enough symmetries – we only consider the XXZ spin-1/2 chain. In the periodic case, each Bethe eigenvector depends on a solution for a system of algebraic equations, called the
Bethe equations
. A priori, it is not clear if the Bethe Ansatz produces all the eigenvectors for the XXZ spin-1/2 chain, i.e. if the Bethe Ansatz is
complete
. In the following sections, we will describe the Bethe Ansatz in more detail. Additionally, we provide
3.11
that if true, along with some other existing results, implies that the Bethe Ansatz is complete.

3.1
Bethe vectors

We introduce a collection of vectors, called
Bethe eigenvectors
, given as follows

|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}u(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\mathbf{x})|\mathbf{x}\rangle

(20)

where
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
is a solution to the system of equations, called the
Bethe equations

\xi_{i}^{L}=(-1)^{N-1}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1+\xi_{i}\xi_{j}-2\Delta\xi_{i}}{1+\xi_{i}\xi_{j}-2\Delta\xi_{j}},\quad i=1,2,\dots,N

(21)

and the coefficients are given as follows

u(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}A_{\sigma}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\prod_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{\sigma(i)}^{x_{i}}

(22)

with the
amplitudes

A_{\sigma}(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)\end{subarray}}\left(-\frac{1+\xi_{\sigma(i)}\xi_{\sigma(j)}-2\Delta\xi_{\sigma(i)}}{1+\xi_{\sigma(i)}\xi_{\sigma(j)}-2\Delta\xi_{\sigma(j)}}\right).

(23)

In particular, a Bethe vector
|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\rangle
is determined and labelled by a solution
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
to the Bethe equations given by (
21
). We will consider solutions of the Bethe equations so that the Bethe vectors are well-defined.

Assumption 3.1
.

Take
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
so that it is a solution of the Bethe equations (
21
) and

1+\xi_{i}\xi_{j}-2\Delta\xi_{i}\neq 0

(24)

for any
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,N\}
.

Remark 3.2
.

3.1
is a generic assumption. Take the Bethe equations and at least one pair
(i,j)
so that the left side of (
24
) is equal to zero. This is an over determined system of equations. Then, for fixed
N
and
L
, if suffices to find one value of
\Delta
where
3.1
is true to show that it is a generic condition. For
\Delta=0
, the Bethe equations and the negation of
3.1
become

\begin{cases}\xi_{k}^{L}=(-1)^{N},&\quad k=1,2,\dots,N\\
1+\xi_{i}\xi_{j}=0,\end{cases}

(25)

for some
i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. This system of equations doesn’t have a solution for
L
an odd number but it does have some solutions for
L
an even number. Thus, it’s not clear that
3.1
is generically true for
L
an even number but we do know that

is generically true for

an odd number.

Proposition 3.3
.

If
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
is a solution of the Bethe equations (
21

is true and

E(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\xi_{i}+\xi_{i}^{-1}-2\Delta\right)

This is a well-known result dating back to the work of Hans Bethe in 1931

\tilde{\mathcal{X}}=\mathbb{Z}^{N}

(27)

be the space of configuration where particles may occupy the same position in space and may be in any order. Then, we have a natural inclusion of the configuration spaces

\mathcal{X}\hookrightarrow\tilde{\mathcal{X}}.

(28)

We also introduce the corresponding Hilbert space

\tilde{\mathbb{H}}=\mathrm{Span}\{|\mathbf{x}\rangle\mid\mathbf{x}\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\}

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})

Lemma 3.4
.

Take
|\tilde{V}\rangle\in\tilde{\mathbb{H}}
and

(i)\,H|\tilde{V}\rangle=E|\tilde{V}\rangle,\quad(ii)\,P|\tilde{V}\rangle=|V\rangle,\quad(iii)\,H_{XXZ}|V\rangle=PH|\tilde{V}\rangle,\quad(iv)\,|V\rangle\neq 0

H_{XXZ}|V\rangle=E|V\rangle

Proof.

This is a straightforward result. Taking the first three conditions, we have

H_{XXZ}|V\rangle=PH|\tilde{V}\rangle=PE|\tilde{V}\rangle=EP|\tilde{V}\rangle=E|V\rangle.

(34)

The last condition assures that the vector
|V\rangle
is not trivial. Thus, the result follows.
∎

\mathbb{P}\left(|\Psi(t)\rangle=|\mathbf{x}\rangle\right)=\left|\sum_{[\boldsymbol{\xi}]\in\Xi}\ell(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\xi})u(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{x})e^{-itE(\boldsymbol{\xi})}\right|^{2}

(69)

where
u(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{x})
is given by (
22
) and
\ell(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\xi})
is given by (
58
).

Proof.

The probability of a configuration
\mathbf{x}
at time
t\geq 0
is given by

\mathbb{P}\left(|\Psi(t)\rangle=|\mathbf{x}\rangle\right)=\langle\Psi(t)|\mathbf{x}\rangle\langle\mathbf{x}|\Psi(t)\rangle=|\langle\mathbf{x}|\Psi(t)\rangle|^{2};

(70)

recall (
10
). Then, the result follows from
Proposition

4.1
.
∎

4.4
Numerical solutions to the Bethe equations

We solve the Bethe equations numerically to test
3.11
for
\Delta\neq 0
. Recall that
Lemma

3.12
proves that
3.11
is true for
\Delta=0
. In that case, we have an explicit solution for the Bethe equations, i.e. roots of unity with a phase shift. Then, we employ Newton’s method to solve the Bethe equations numerically for small
\Delta\neq 0
using the solutions for
\Delta=0
as our initial guess. For small
\Delta\neq 0
, we keep a one-to-one correspondence of the solutions of the Bethe equations with the solutions for
\Delta=0
.

We define a sequence
\boldsymbol{\xi}(k)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
k=0,1,\dots
, so that it has a direct numerical implementation and the limit is a solution of the Bethe equations. We use the solutions of the Bethe equations at
\Delta=0
as the base point, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\xi}(0)\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
. Note that the Bethe equations decouple in the case
\Delta=0
,

\xi_{i}^{L}=(-1)^{N-1},\quad i=1,2,\dots,N.

(72)

In this case, we can find all the solutions to the system of equations

\eta_{i}(k)=\begin{cases}e^{2k_{i}\pi i/L},&\quad N\text{ is odd}\\
e^{(2k_{i}+1)\pi i/L},&\quad N\text{ is even}\end{cases}

(73)

for any
k=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{N})
with
k_{i}\in[L]
. Thus, when
\Delta
is close to zero, we expect the solutions to the Bethe equations to be close to the solutions given by (
73
). More precisely, by Rouche’s Theorem, we expect

|\xi_{i}-\eta_{i}|<C\epsilon

(74)

for some constant
C>0
if
|\Delta|=\epsilon>0
. In our numerical solution to the Bethe equations, we use the solutions
\eta(k)=(\eta(k_{i}))_{i=1}^{N}
, given by (
73
), as the base points of our sequence.

We now define our sequence that will give a solution of the Bethe equations; see
Lemma

4.3
. For a fixed
k\in[L]^{N}
, we set the first element of the sequence as follows

\boldsymbol{\xi}(0):=\eta(k).

(75)

Then, we define the rest of the terms in the sequence recursively as follows

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\xi}(n,0)&=\boldsymbol{\xi}(n)\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}(n,i+1)&=\boldsymbol{\xi}(n,i)-\left(\frac{F_{i+1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}(n,i))}{\|J_{i+1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}(n,i))\|^{2}}\right)J_{i+1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}(n,i)),\quad i=0,1,\dots,N-1,\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}(n+1)&=\boldsymbol{\xi}(n,N)\end{split}

(76)

with the functions
F_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,N
, given by the Bethe equations

F_{i}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\xi_{i}^{L}+(-1)^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{1+\xi_{i}\xi_{j}-2\Delta\xi_{j}}{1+\xi_{i}\xi_{j}-2\Delta\xi_{i}}\right)

(77)

and
J_{i}=(\partial F_{i}/\partial\xi_{1},\dots,\partial F_{i}/\partial\xi_{N})
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
k\in[L]^{N}
, let
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}(n)\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\mid n=0,1,\dots\}
be a sequence given by the recursion formula (
76
) and with a base point
\boldsymbol{\xi}(0)\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
given by (
75
). Then, there exists some
\epsilon>0
so that the sequence converges for
\Delta
if
|\Delta|<\epsilon
and the limit point,
\boldsymbol{\xi}(n)\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\xi}(\infty)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, has all pairwise distinct entries and solves the Bethe equation given by (
21
).

Proof.

The sequence defined by (
76
) is the so-called
Newton-Kaczmarz
method to solve a system of non-linear equations. Our proof relies on satisfying the conditions of
[
Mar86
, Theorem 2.1]
. The key condition is that the vectors
J_{i}
,
i=1,2,\dots,N
, of partial derivatives are linearly independent. We know this condition to be true for
\Delta=0
. Moreover, we know that this condition depends algebraically on
\Delta
since this is equivalent to the condition that the determinant of the Jacobian is non-zero, i.e. a polynomial on
\Delta
is non-zero. By taking
\varepsilon>0
small enough, this and the other conditions are satisfied. This establishes that the sequence is well-defined, converges and the limit is a solution of the Bethe equations.

Recall,
\boldsymbol{\xi}(0)\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
is the solution of the Bethe equations for
\Delta=0
. By Rouche’s theorem, we may assume that there is a small neighborhood around
\boldsymbol{\xi}(0)\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
so that the Bethe equations have a unique solution with pairwise distinct entries if
|\Delta|<\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon>0
small enough. This establishes that the limit of the sequence has pairwise distinct entries.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

We opted to use the Newton-Kaczmarz method (
76
) to solve the Bethe equations since it has a direct numerical implementation. An alternative approach was to use the regular Newton method to numerically solve the Bethe equations, but this required inverting an
N\times N
matrix at each step of the sequence.

In
Lemma

4.3
, the value
\Delta=0
is only special because we know how to solve the Bethe equations for that value. The result may be extended to find solutions of the Bethe equations near other base points
\boldsymbol{\xi}(0)\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
if we know the base point to be a solution of the Bethe equations. In principle, we may find solutions for generic values of
\Delta
by starting from the solutions for
\Delta=0
and repeatedly applying the sequence (
76
) to find solutions that are closer and closer to
\Delta
. The key takeaway from this section is that we have a precise numerical method to solve the Bethe equation for non-trivial
\Delta\neq 0
to verify
3.11
.

6
One point function

In the work of Saenz, Tracy, and Widom for XXZ on the line (
\mathbb{Z}
)
[
STW22
]
, the marginal distribution of the left-most up-spin (i.e. the one point function) with domain wall initial conditions (
|\mathbf{y}\rangle=|1,2,3,\ldots\rangle
) converges to the GUE Tracy-Widom distribution
F_{2}
when
\Delta=0
in the large
N
limit (with proper scaling). Moreover in that work, there is an asymptotic formula for the one-point function when
\Delta\neq 0
, but it is unclear that the limit is also the GUE Tracy-Widom distribution. This connection to the KPZ universality class prompts us to investigate the one point function in for the XXZ on the ring. Here we present a simplification of the one point function assuming the completeness of the Bethe Ansatz but independent of the specific form of the
\ell
-function.

Theorem 6.1
.

Assume the Bethe Ansatz is complete. Then, the one-point function, given by (
71
), has the following formula

\rho(x,t)=\sum_{[\boldsymbol{\xi}],[\boldsymbol{\zeta}]\in\Xi}\ell(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathcal{F}(x;\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta})\ell(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\zeta})

(78)

with the function
\mathcal{F}(x;\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta})
given by

\begin{split}&\mathcal{F}(x;\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta}):=\\
&\frac{e^{-it(E(\boldsymbol{\xi})-E(\boldsymbol{\zeta}))}F_{1,N}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta})^{x}}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(1+\xi_{i}\xi_{j}-2\Delta\xi_{i})(1+\zeta_{i}\zeta_{j}-2\Delta\zeta_{i})}\\
&\times\sum_{s=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1},I_{2}\subset[N]\\
|I_{1}|=|I_{2}|=s\end{subarray}}\bigg[(1-F_{1,s}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta};I_{1},I_{2}))\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta};I_{1},I_{2})G(\boldsymbol{\xi};I_{1}^{c},I_{1})\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt\times G(\boldsymbol{\zeta};I_{2}^{c},I_{2})\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{-1},\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{-1};I_{1}^{c},I_{2}^{c})F_{1,N-s}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta};I_{1}^{c},I_{2}^{c})^{N-s-2}\bigg].\end{split}

(79)

The definition of the one-point function includes a summation over a large subset of the configuration space. Without simplification, it is a far cry from being amenable to asymptotic analysis, and the computational complexity of the one point function is on the order of
{L\choose N}^{2}{L-1\choose N-1}\left(N!\right)^{4}
which is much too large for any kind of numerical implementation of a chain with large
N
and
L
. In what follows we will apply and extend identities from
[
LSW20
]
,
[
TW09
]
,
[
STW22
]
,
[
CCP19
]
and establish some new identities to simplify the one point function to the form in
Theorem

6.1
.

Remark 6.2
.

Due to the rotational invariance of the lattice, we may express the one-point function at a point
x\in[L]
in terms of the one-point function at
x=0
by taking
\mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathbf{y}-x
. Our work to show
Theorem

6.1
will be done for
\rho(0,t)
for simplicity but easily extends for a general

.

We will first establish some notation and then focus on simplifying

f_{k}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=f_{k}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k})

where

We define the following functions

\begin{split}B_{\sigma}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N})&=\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\left(1+\xi_{\sigma(i)}\xi_{\sigma(j)}-2\Delta\xi_{\sigma(i)}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{\sigma(j)}^{j}\\

for any permutation

\end{split}

(84)

We define the following family of functions

F_{m,n}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\prod_{j=m}^{n}\xi_{j}\zeta_{j},\quad F_{m,n}^{\sigma,\mu}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\prod_{j=m}^{n}\xi_{\sigma(j)}\zeta_{\mu(j)}

for any pair of permutations

and

\begin{split}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in X\\

C_{\vec{s}}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\subset[N]\\

Proof.

Using the decomposition given by (

\begin{split}&C_{\vec{s}}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1}\subset[N]\\
