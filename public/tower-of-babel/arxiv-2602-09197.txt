Global Protocols under Rendezvous Synchrony: From Realizability to Type Checking
Elaine Li, Felix Stutz

Abstract
. Global protocol specifications are the starting point of top-down verification methodologies, and serve as a blueprint for synthesizing local specifications that guarantee the correctness of distributed implementations. In this work, we study global protocol specifications targeting distributed processes that communicate via rendezvous synchrony. We obtain the following positive results for the synchronous realizability problem: (a) realizability is decidable for global protocols over a transitive concurrency alphabet in 2- EXPTIME in the size of the protocol, and in 3- EXPTIME in the size of the alphabet, and (b) realizability is decidable in EXPTIME for global protocols that unambiguously represent their trace language. Unambiguous global protocols encompass fragments of directed and sender-driven choice studied in prior work. Further, our reductions admit the corollary that the synchronous verification problem is solvable with the same complexity, where a candidate realization is included as part of the input. We then prove a negative result stating that synchronous realizability is undecidable in general. Finally, we propose a type system to check π \pi -calculus processes against local specifications in the form of synchronous communicating state machines. Our type system is the first to support processes with local mixed choice in the presence of session interleaving and delegation.

Observe that none of the components in the workflow commit to a particular network model.
If we allow
{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}},{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}

On the other hand, if we allow
{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}},{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}

The choice of network model is of central concern to the
realizability
problem, which asks whether a given global protocol is truly distributable, such that its participants avoid communication errors or system-wide deadlock, despite only having a partial view of the entire system.
Realizability is defined relative to a target implementation and network model.
Investigations of realizability in the literature primarily target asynchronous, reliable, peer-to-peer FIFO communication.
Decidability and complexity results have been obtained for global protocols in the form of
HMSCs

(
Alur et al. 2003
;
Lohrey 2003
;
Alur and Yannakakis 1999
;

(
Li et al. 2025a
)

Recently there has been increased interest in synchronous communication models, both for their expressivity and their realizability problem
(
Barbanera et al. 2020
;
Barbanera et al. 2023
;
Peters and Yoshida 2024
;
Giusto et al. 2025
;
Castro-Perez et al. 2026

.

Peters and Yoshida 2024
study the impact of local mixed choice on the expressivity of synchronously communicating
\pi
-calculus processes, and propose a type system for such processes.
Local mixed choice allows a process to choose between receiving and sending a message.

Castro-Perez et al. 2026
propose a synchronous MST framework,
in which typability of a process against a global protocol implies that the former correctly implements the latter.
With this, they soundly approximate a variant of the synchronous realizability problem, which we call the synchronous verification problem, through restrictions on global protocols and typing rules.
Of recent interest is
(
Giusto et al. 2025
)
, which connects the realizability problem to complementability, and proposes the first algorithms for deciding synchronous realizability of various global type fragments, including sender-driven global types
(
Majumdar et al. 2021a
)
.
Sender-driven choice allows a sender to send messages to different receives in a choice.

•

We show that synchronous realizability is decidable for global protocols over a transitive concurrency alphabet in 2-
EXPTIME
in the size of the protocol, and in 3-

in the size of the alphabet. To the best of our knowledge, this constitutes the first upper bound for the equivalence and inclusion problems of trace languages with a transitive concurrency alphabet. •

Our reductions allow us to conclude that the synchronous verification problem is solvable with the same complexity.

•

We show that synchronous realizability is undecidable in general.

•

We propose a type system for checking
\pi
-calculus processes against finite state automata, that is the first to simultaneously handle local mixed choice, session interleaving and delegation.

Synchronous Protocol Semantics.

The synchronous semantics of a protocol
\mathcal{G}
is defined as the set of all words that are per-participant equal to some word in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{sync}}(\mathcal{G})\coloneq\{w\in\Gamma^{*}~\mid~\exists w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}).~\forall{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}\in\mathcal{P}.~w{\Downarrow}_{\Gamma_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}=w^{\prime}{\Downarrow}_{\Gamma_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}\}

Definition 2.1 (Synchronous Realizability Problem).

An SCSM
\mathcal{A}
is a synchronous realization of protocol
\mathcal{G}
if the following two properties hold:

(i)

protocol fidelity
:
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{sync}}(\mathcal{G})
, and

(ii)

deadlock freedom
:
\mathcal{A}
is deadlock-free.

Given a protocol
\mathcal{G}
, the
synchronous realizability problem
asks whether there exists an SCSM
\mathcal{A}
that is a synchronous realization of
\mathcal{G}
.

One can also ask the synchronous realization question for a given SCSM as input.
We refer to this problem as the
synchronous verification problem
.

Definition 2.2 (Synchronous Verification Problem).

Given a protocol
\mathcal{G}
and an SCSM
\mathcal{A}
, the
synchronous verification problem
asks whether
\mathcal{A}
synchronously realizes
\mathcal{G}
.

In the MST literature, the two problems are typically studied separately, with the latter reformulated as
a form of subtyping.
In
(
Li et al. 2024
)
, it was shown that sound and complete solutions to the asynchronous realizability problem can be used to derive sound and complete solutions to the asynchronous protocol verification problem.
Our solutions to realizability in the synchronous setting mirror this observation.
Indeed, we show in
Section

3
that the two problems share an upper bound in complexity.

We may omit the term “synchronous” when clear from context.

3.1.
Trace theory preliminaries

We begin with preliminaries from trace theory.

Traces and Trace Languages.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet and
C
be an irreflexive, symmetric relation on
\Sigma
, called the
concurrency
relation, or the
independence
relation.
The pair
(\Sigma,C)
is called a
concurrent alphabet
.
The reflexive and symmetric relation
D=\Sigma\times\Sigma\setminus C
is called the
dependence
relation.
The relations
C
and
D
extend to words in the expected way:
(u,v)\in C
iff
\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(u)\times\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(v)\subseteq C
and
(u,v)\in D
iff

(\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(u)\times\operatorname{\textit{alph}}(v))\cap D\neq\emptyset
.
Given
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, we denote
C(x)=\{w\in\Sigma^{*}\mid(w,x)\in C\}
.

The concurrent alphabet
(\Sigma,C)
defines a
free partially commutative monoid
, denoted
M(\Sigma,C)
, also called a
trace monoid
,
which
is the quotient structure induced by
\equiv_{C}
, the reflexive, transitive closure of
C
extended to words. A trace on concurrent alphabet
(\Sigma,C)
is an element
t\in M(\Sigma,C)
, namely a congruence class of words. We use
[w]_{C}
to denote the trace that contains
w\in\Sigma^{*}
.

Rational, Recognizable, and Unambiguous Trace Languages.

Let
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
be a language and
(\Sigma,C)
be a concurrent alphabet.
Language
L
, as what we may call
string language
, can define a trace language in several ways. The trace language
existentially
defined by
L
, denoted
[L]^{\exists}_{C}
, is the set
\{t\in M(\Sigma,C)\mid t\cap L\neq\emptyset\}
.
The trace language
universally
defined by
L
, denoted
[L]^{\forall}_{C}
, is the set
\{t\in M(\Sigma,C)\mid t\subseteq L\}
.
If
[L]^{\exists}_{C}=[L]^{\forall}_{C}=T
, then we say that
T
is
consistently
defined by
L
, i.e.
L=\bigcup_{t\in T}t
.
Moreover, if
L
is regular, then
[L]^{\exists}_{C}
is a existentially regular trace language, and
[L]^{\forall}_{C}
is a universally regular trace language.

By convention, we drop the
\exists
subscript, using
[L]_{C}
to denote
[L]^{\exists}_{C}
.
When clear from context, we further drop the
C
, using
[L]
to denote
[L]^{\exists}_{C}
.

Conversely, given a trace language
T\subseteq M(\Sigma,C)
, the set
lin(T)=\bigcup_{t\in T}\varphi^{-1}(t)
is the
linearization of T
, where
\varphi
is the canonical morphism from
\Sigma^{*}
to
M(\Sigma,C)
.

Let
T\subseteq M(\Sigma,C)
be a a trace language.
We say that
T
is
rational
iff there exists a regular language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
such that
[L]_{C}=T
.
We say that
T
is
recognizable
iff
lin(T)
is a regular language over
\Sigma
.
We say
T
is
unambiguously rational
, or
unambiguous
for short, iff there exists a regular language
L
such that
T=[L]_{C}
, and moreover
w\in L
implies
\lvert lin([w]_{C})\cap L\rvert=1
.
The latter condition is equivalent to
t\in M(\Sigma,C)
implies
\lvert lin(t)\cap L\rvert\leq 1
, or
t\in T
implies
\lvert lin(t)\cap L\rvert=1
.
Note that the definition of existentially regular and rational coincide, while the definition of recognizable and consistently regular coincide.

Recognizable trace languages admit multiple algebraic characterizations.
One such characterization is that of a star-connected rational expression, where all languages appearing under Kleene star are connected, i.e. ruling out concurrent iteration.
Of particular interest to us is the notion of a
lexicographic representation
of a trace language.
Let
<
be a total order on
\Sigma
.
Then,
<
induces a total, lexicographic order on
\Sigma^{*}
, defined as:

(i)

for every
u\in\Sigma^{*}
,
\varepsilon<u
, and

(ii)

for every
u,v\in\Sigma^{*}
,
a,b\in\Sigma
,
au<bv
if and only if either
a<b
, or
a=b
and
u<v
.

The
lexicographic representation
of a trace language
T\subseteq M(\Sigma,C)
is the set

Lex(T)=\{u\in lin(T)\mid\forall v\in[u].~u\leq v\}
.
Members of
Lex(T)
are called lexicographically
minimal
words with respect to
C
and
<
.

Remark 3.1.

Rational trace languages were introduced as the least class of subsets of
M(\Sigma,C)
closed under the usual set operations of union, product and Kleene star. Recognizable trace languages were introduced as the subset of
M(\Sigma,C)
recognizable by finite automata. Unambiguous trace languages admit an algebraic definition in terms of unambiguous union, product and Kleene star, and are also called
1-sequential
or
deterministic
trace languages.
For the purposes of exposition, we treat the characterizations of each class in relation to regular string languages directly as definitions.
For a comprehensive treatment, we refer the reader to
(
Diekert and Rozenberg 1995
)
.

Equivalence, Inclusion and Multiplicity Equivalence

Let
A_{1},A_{2}
be two finite automata recognizing the languages
L_{1},L_{2}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
.
The
\mathit{EQUIVALENCE}(\Sigma,I)
problem asks whether
[L_{1}]=[L_{2}]
.
The
\mathit{INCLUSION}(\Sigma,I)
problem asks whether
[L_{1}]\subseteq[L_{2}]
.
Last, the
\sharp\mathit{EQUIVALENCE}(\Sigma,I)
problem asks whether
\sharp L_{1}=\sharp L_{2}
, where
\sharp L
is a function
M(\Sigma,I)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that for every trace
t\in M(\Sigma,I)
,
\sharp L(t)=\lvert\{x\in L\mid[x]=t\}\rvert
, where
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of natural numbers.
Thus,
\sharp L_{1}=\sharp L_{2}
when every trace has the same number of representatives in
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
.
Observe that if
\sharp L_{1}=\sharp L_{2}
, then
[L_{1}]=[L_{2}]
, whereas the converse only holds when both
[L_{1}]
and
[L_{2}]
are unambiguous trace languages witnessed by
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
respectively.

3.2.
Global protocols and their realizations as trace languages

Observe that a global protocol naturally defines a rational trace language over concurrent alphabet
(\Gamma,\equiv)
, where
\equiv
is defined as:

Definition 3.1.

Given a synchronous alphabet
\Gamma
defined over a set of participants
\mathcal{P}
and a set of message labels
\mathcal{V}
, the symmetric binary relation
\equiv
is defined as

\{(x_{1},x_{2})\mid\mathcal{P}(x_{1})\cap\mathcal{P}(x_{2})=\emptyset\}
.

Clearly,
\equiv
defines an equivalence relation over
\Gamma^{*}
, capturing pairs of execution traces that are equivalent up to a choice of scheduling.
We prove that a global protocol’s synchronous semantics exactly coincides with the closure of its language under
\equiv
and that the semantics of SCSMs is closed under
\equiv
.

Proposition 3.2.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a global protocol.
Then, it holds that

\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{sync}}(\mathcal{G})=[\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})]_{\equiv}
and

\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{sync}}(\mathcal{G})
is a rational trace language over the concurrent alphabet
(\Gamma,\equiv)
, existentially defined by
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
.

Proposition 3.3.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an SCSM and let
w
be a trace of
\mathcal{A}
.
Then, for all
w^{\prime}\in[w]_{\equiv}
, it holds that
w^{\prime}
is a trace of
\mathcal{A}
and

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
is a recognizable trace language over the concurrent alphabet
(\Gamma,\equiv)
.

3.3.
Canonical SCSMs

It is well-known that if a global protocol is realizable, its
canonical
candidate realization can always serve as existential witness to realizability
(
Alur et al. 2003
;
Li et al. 2025b
;
Giusto et al. 2025
)
.
We define the
canonical SCSM
of a global protocol and state facts about its canonicity.
The results in this section are not novel and are included only to keep our presentation self-contained.

Definition 3.4 (Canonical SCSM).

Let
\mathcal{G}=(Q,\Gamma,\delta,q_{0},F)
be a protocol.
For each participant
{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}\in\mathcal{P}
, we define a finite automaton
\mathcal{A}_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}=\bigl(Q_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}},\Gamma_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}},\delta_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}},q_{0,{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}},F_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}\bigr)
, where

•

\delta(q,a)\coloneq\{s^{\prime}\in Q\mid\exists s\in q,s\xrightarrow{a}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\vphantom{\to}{}^{*}}s^{\prime}\in\delta_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}\},

for
every

q\subseteq Q
and

a\in\Gamma_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}

•

q_{0,{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}\coloneq\{s\in Q\mid s_{0}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\vphantom{\to}{}^{*}}s\in\delta_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}\}
,

•

Q_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}\coloneq\mathrm{lfp}_{\{q_{0,{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}\}}^{\subseteq}\lambda S.\,S\cup\{\delta(s,a)\mid s\in S\land a\in\Gamma_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}\}\setminus\{\emptyset\}

,

•

\delta_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}\coloneq{\delta}|_{Q_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}\times\Gamma_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}}
, and

•

F_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}\coloneq\{q\in Q_{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}\mid q\cap F\neq\emptyset\}\kern 5.0pt.

Note that
\delta_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}
enforces that
\mathcal{A}_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}
is deterministic and reduced.
We define
\mathcal{A}
to be the canonical SCSM of
\mathcal{G}
.

Canonical SCSMs satisfy the following local language property.

Lemma 3.5.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a global protocol and
\mathcal{A}
be its canonical SCSM.
Then, for every
{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}\in\mathcal{P}
, the finite automaton
\mathcal{A}_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}
satisfies:

(i)

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}})=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}){\Downarrow}_{\Gamma_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}}
, and

(ii)

\operatorname{pref}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}))=\operatorname{pref}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G}){\Downarrow}_{\Gamma_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}})
.

It is easy to see that by construction, the canonical SCSM of a global protocol includes at least the global protocol’s specified behaviors.

Lemma 3.6.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a global protocol and let
\mathcal{A}
be its canonical SCSM.
Then,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{sync}}(\mathcal{G})\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
.

We relate global protocols to their canonical SCSMs below.

Lemma 3.7.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a global protocol.
Then,
\mathcal{G}
is realizable if and only if its canonical SCSM realizes it.

Lemma

3.7
and
Lemma

3.6
together imply that deciding realizability of a global protocol amounts to checking deadlock-freedom of the canonical SCSM, and checking language inclusion of the canonical SCSM into global protocol semantics.
Observe further that each SCSM is in fact a finite automaton, due to the absence of (unbounded) message channels.
We define the language preserving finite automaton of an SCSM below.

Definition 3.8 (Canonical synchronous product).

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a global protocol and let
\mathcal{A}
be its canonical SCSM.
The canonical synchronous product of
\mathcal{G}
, denoted
Prod(\mathcal{G})
is the synchronous product of
\mathcal{A}
.

We characterize realizability of a global protocol in terms of its canonical synchronous product.

Lemma 3.9.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a global protocol.
Then,
\mathcal{G}
is realizable if and only if
Prod(\mathcal{G})
is reduced and satisfies
\mathcal{L}(Prod(\mathcal{G}))\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{sync}}(\mathcal{G})
.

A keen reader may have observed that in light of
Lemma

3.9
, to decide realizability, it suffices to decide recognizability.

Unfortunately, we have the following, due to Sakarovitch
(
Sakarovitch 1992
)
:

Theorem 3.10.

Recognizability of rational trace languages is undecidable in general.

The reduction chosen is from the universality problem for two-tape one-way non-deterministic automata over a unary alphabet, which Ibarra showed to be undecidable in
(
Ibarra 1978
)
.
The constructed rational trace language in fact satisfies a stronger property: either it is
empty
, or it is not recognizable.
This stronger property establishes universality if and only if recognizability.
The construction, in the form of an NFA, can be encoded using a synchronous alphabet with four participants.
Note, however, that the same construction does not work for undecidability of realizability.
This is because encoding invalid solutions to a Post Correspondence Problem set requires concurrent iteration, which immediately renders the protocol unrealizable. In
Section

3.6
, we detail a different reduction that avoids concurrent iteration.

In the following two sections, we combine
Lemma

3.9
with our novel trace-theoretic characterizations in
Proposition

3.2
and
Proposition

3.3
to reduce the realizability problem to different problems over trace languages.
All proofs can be found in
Appendix

A
.

3.4.
The transitive case

Trace languages are classically studied through the lens of their concurrency relation.
In particular, it was shown that classes of rational trace languages over concurrent alphabets, whose concurrency relation
is
transitive
, enjoy many decidability properties.

In this section, we transfer these decidability properties to global protocols.
In
Section

3.4.1
, we provide an exhaustive case analysis of synchronous alphabets with a transitive concurrency relation. In
Section

3.4.2
, we propose an algorithm for deciding realizability of global protocols with a transitive concurrent alphabet, and in
Section

3.4.3
, we analyze its complexity.
While our algorithm is based on an existing decidability proof
(
Aalbersberg and Welzl 1986
)
and is thus not novel, to the best of our knowledge, no upper bound has previously been provided for the equivalence and inclusion problems over rational trace languages.
In
Section

3.4.4
, we analyze a flaw in the original proof claiming the decidability of rational trace language equivalence in the transitive setting.

3.4.1.
Subsets of
\mathit{\Gamma}
with transitive
\equiv

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a global protocol, and let
\Gamma_{\mathcal{G}}
be the subset of
\Gamma
whose symbols appear in
\mathcal{G}
.
Let
\equiv_{\mathcal{G}}
be the concurrency relation
\equiv
(
Definition

3.1
) restricted to
\Gamma_{\mathcal{G}}
.
The transitivity of
\equiv_{\mathcal{G}}
depends both on the size of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{V}
, as well as the communication topology in
\mathcal{G}
.
Note that in the special case when
\equiv_{\mathcal{G}}
is empty and thus trivially transitive, realizability is decidable in PSPACE by checking equivalence and reducedness of finite state automata directly.

When
\lvert\mathcal{P}\rvert\leq 3
,
\equiv_{\mathcal{G}}
is empty.
When
\lvert\mathcal{P}\rvert\geq 4
, we first case split on whether the communication topology is
complete
, i.e. where all pairs of participants communicate in the global protocol.

When
\lvert\mathcal{P}\rvert=4
and complete, we further case split on the size of
\mathcal{V}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}=\{{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}},{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}},{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{r}}},{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{s}}}\}
.
If

Deciding synchronous realizability in 2-
EXPTIME

Our complexity result is stated below.

Theorem 3.14.

Synchronous realizability is decidable for all global protocols
\mathcal{G}
over
\Gamma
whose concurrency relation
\equiv_{\mathcal{G}}
is transitive in 2-
EXPTIME
in the size of
\mathcal{G}

Our upper bound is obtained via a reduction to the following problem

Our proposed algorithm to solve

(

)

\exists=\forall
if and only if it is transitive.
A concurrent alphabet is of type
\exists=\forall
iff the class of existentially regular trace languages over
(\Sigma,C)
and the class of universally regular trace languages over
(\Sigma,C)
coincide.

We restate and prove Lemma 2.7 from
(
Aalbersberg and Welzl 1986
)
below.

Lemma 3.15.

A concurrent alphabet
(\Sigma,C)
is of type
\exists=\forall
if and only if
C
is transitive.

In the following, we analyze the complexity of a series of constructions used to prove Lemma 2.7 in
(
Aalbersberg and Welzl 1986
)

We focus on describing the constructions algorithmically and analyzing their complexity.
For proofs of correctness, we refer the reader to the original paper.

Recall that the Parikh image of a language
L
over

\Delta=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}

\psi(L)

L
under the mapping
\psi\colon\Delta^{*}\to\mathbb{N}^{m}

\psi(w)=(|w|_{a_{1}},\ldots,|w|_{a_{m}})

where

denotes the number of occurrences of symbol

in
w
.

The following lemma constructs for each existentially regular trace language an equivalent universally regular trace language over the same concurrent alphabet.

Lemma 3.16.

Let
(\Sigma,C)
be a concurrent alphabet and let
L
be a regular string language over
\Sigma
given by an NFA
M
.
Then, a regular string language
K
such that
[L]^{\exists}_{C}=[K]^{\forall}_{C}
can be constructed with time and size complexity doubly exponential in the size of
M

\Sigma
.

The next lemma generalizes the correspondence between individual languages to sets of languages over the same concurrent alphabet.

Lemma 3.17.

Let
(\Sigma,C)
be a concurrent alphabet and let

\Sigma

n

The following lemma describes the complexity of the automata construction in Lemma 2.5 of
(
Aalbersberg and Welzl 1986
)
.
Intuitively, given a regular language
L
over the disjoint union of alphabets
\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}

\{1,2\}

The new NFA accepts words in

in an alternating fashion, reading maximal substrings of symbols from
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
in turn.
Every triple
(s,s^{\prime},i)
in the new alphabet represents the Parikh image of the language defined by setting the initial state to

in

\Sigma_{i}^{*}
.
In other words,
(s,s^{\prime},i)

from

\Sigma_{i}

Since all symbols in

\Sigma_{i}^{*}

The construction is described in detail in the following lemma, with an analysis of its complexity.

Lemma 3.18.

Let

be a complete DFA recognizing

. Let
(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})
be a partition of
\Sigma
.
Let

K^{\prime}=\{(s_{1},s_{2},i_{1})\ldots(s_{n},s_{n+1},i_{n})\in\Delta\mid n\geq 1,s_{1}=q_{0},s_{n+1}\in F\text{ and }i_{j}\neq i_{j+1}\text{ for }1\leq j\leq n-1\}

A DFA recognizing

can be constructed with a constant number of states, and

is polynomial in the size of

Moreover,

3.5.
The unambiguous case

Unambiguous trace languages are originally defined algebraically using unambiguous restrictions of the
\cup
,
\cdot
and
\mathrel{\vphantom{\to}{}^{*}}
operators defined over trace languages
(
Bruschi et al. 1994
)
.

Definition 3.22.

Let
(\Sigma,C)
be a concurrent alphabet and let
T_{1},T_{2}\subseteq M(\Sigma,C)
.
Then
T_{1}\sqcup T_{2}
is defined as
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
iff
T_{1}\cap T_{2}=\emptyset
,

T_{1}\circ T_{2}
is defined as
T_{1}\cdot T_{2}
iff for all
x,z\in T_{1}
and
y,w\in T_{2}
,

xy=zw\implies x=z\land y=w
,
and finally
T^{@}
is defined as
T^{*}
iff
T
is the basis of a free submonoid
T^{*}
of
M(\Sigma,C)
.
The class of unambiguous trace languages over
(\Sigma,C)
is the least class of subsets of
M(\Sigma,I)
containing finite sets and closed with respect to
\sqcup
,
\circ
and
(-)^{@}
.

This definition
naturally also defines unambiguous global protocols over
(\Gamma,\equiv)
.
However, when we switch to the view of trace languages defined by regular languages, unambiguous trace languages are defined as the class of languages such that there
exists
a regular language that unambiguously represents it.
Given a trace language
[L]_{C}
defined by a regular language
L
, it may be unambiguous even if
L
does not unambiguously represent it.
Sakarovitch
(
Sakarovitch 1987
)
showed that the class of rational trace languages and unambiguous trace languages over a concurrency alphabet coincide if and only if the concurrency relation is transitive.
This means that for every rational trace language represented as
[L]_{C}
, one can effectively construct
L^{\prime}
such that
L^{\prime}
unambiguously represents
[L]_{C}
.
However, such a construction is not effective in the general case.

Nonetheless, if a trace language is defined by a regular language that unambiguously represents it, this guarantees its membership in the class of unambiguous rational trace languages.
In the following, we say that
\mathcal{G}
is an
unambiguous global protocol
, or
\mathcal{G}
is
unambiguous
, iff
\mathcal{G}
unambiguously represents
[\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})]_{\equiv}
, namely for every trace
t\in M(\Gamma,\equiv)
,
t
has either 0 or 1 representatives in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G})
.

In
Section

3.5.1
, we show that previously proposed restrictions on
branching choice
in global protocols are sufficient conditions for unambiguity.
In
Section

3.5.2
, we show that realizability is decidable for unambiguous global protocols.
The key fact we prove is that for a recognizable trace language given as a finite state automaton recognizing all of its linearizations, an unambiguous representative can be computed in linear time.
In
Section

3.5.3
, we state the complexity of realizability, invoking an upper bound on the multiplicity equivalence problem for unambiguous trace languages.

3.5.1.
Choice restrictions

In the asynchronous setting, mixed choice is key to showing that realizability is undecidable in general
(
Lohrey 2003
;
Stutz 2024
)
.
For protocols with a more restrictive form of choice, called
sender-driven choice

(
Majumdar et al. 2021a
)
, realizability is co-NP-complete
(
Li et al. 2025a
)
.
Naturally, the notion of sender-driven choice was investigated in the synchronous setting, and
Di Giusto et al. 2025
showed that synchronous realizability for sender-driven choice global types is decidable in PSPACE.
They further relaxed sender-driven choice to
commutation-determinism
, and showed that realizability remains in PSPACE.
While sender-driven choice requires a dedicated sender among all outgoing transitions from a single state in a protocol, commutation-determinism only requires that every pair of states share a participant. We state all three definitions below.

Definition 3.23.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(Q,\Gamma,\delta,q_{0},F)
be a global protocol.
We say
\mathcal{G}
is
mixed choice
iff there exists
q\in Q
with
q\xrightarrow{a}q^{\prime}
and
q\xrightarrow{b}q^{\prime\prime}
, such that
a\equiv b
.
We say
\mathcal{G}
is
non-mixed choice
iff for all
q\in Q
with
q\xrightarrow{a}q^{\prime}
and
q\xrightarrow{b}q^{\prime\prime}
, it holds that
a\not\equiv b
.
We say
\mathcal{G}
is
sender-driven
iff for all
q\in Q
with
q\xrightarrow{a}q^{\prime}
and
q\xrightarrow{b}q^{\prime\prime}
,

3.5.2.
Decidability

Our decidability result is stated below.

Theorem 3.25.

Synchronous realizability of unambiguous global protocols is decidable.

A key observation towards
Theorem

3.25
is that all recognizable trace languages are unambiguous trace languages.
The proof is immediate via the characterization of recognizable trace languages in terms of regular lexicographic representations.
We additionally provide a bound on the size of the unambiguous representative.

Lemma 3.26.

Let
T
be a recognizable trace language over concurrent alphabet
(\Sigma,C)
.
Then,
T
is an unambiguous trace language.
Moreover, given an automaton that recognizes
lin(T)
, an unambiguous regular language generating
T
can be computed in linear time.

Given
Lemma

3.26
, when the global protocol unambiguously represents its trace language, protocol fidelity amounts to checking the equivalence of two unambiguous rational trace languages.
Unlike in
Section

3.4
, where we made use of the definition of canonicity to reduce protocol fidelity to language inclusion, here we reduce realizability to equivalence directly.
Indeed, inclusion cannot be used, because of the following negative result.

Theorem 3.27 (
Theorem 5.4.16 of

(
Diekert and Rozenberg 1995
)
).

Inclusion is undecidable for unambiguous rational trace languages in general.

Equivalence, in contrast, is decidable thanks to the fact that two unambiguous rational trace languages are multiplicity equivalent if and only if they are equivalent.
Multiplicity equivalence can in turn be decided by a reduction to the equality of two rational formal power series in one variable, for more details see
(
Diekert and Rozenberg 1995
)
.

3.6.

In this section, we show that synchronous realizability is undecidable in general.

Definition 3.32.

Being immediately acknowledging means that for any sent message, its receiver has the power to prevent its sender from taking further actions, in both the asynchronous and synchronous setting.
While this is a useful property, immediately acknowledging alone is not a sufficient condition for a synchronously realizable protocol to also be asynchronously realizable.
The protocol depicted in
Fig.

serves as a counterexample: the key discrepancy is that in the asynchronous setting, sends are always enabled, whereas in the synchronous setting, both the sender and the receiver in a message exchange have the power to veto the exchange.
Protocols that do not provide receivers this additional veto power are asynchronously realizable if they are synchronously realizable.
We capture this intuition in a sufficient condition below, which states that for every message that can be sent from a reachable CSM configuration, either the receiver can receive the message immediately, or can receive any message value from the sender, after executing an immediately acknowledging sequence of events.
We provide an intermediate definition towards defining our sufficient condition.

Synchronous realizability of high-level message sequence charts is undecidable.

5.
Related work

We structure our discussion of related work around the two most closely related formalisms: HMSCs and MSTs.

5.1.
High-level message sequence charts

High-level message sequence charts (HMSCs) have been studied extensively in academia

(
Mauw and Reniers 1997
;
Genest et al. 2003
;
Genest and Muscholl 2005
;
Gazagnaire et al. 2007
;
Roychoudhury et al. 2012
)

and were defined in an industry standard
(
Union 1996
)
.
Interestingly, most HMSC research considered asynchronous communication models.
In terms of realizability, two problems have been considered:
weak and safe realizability
(
Lohrey 2003
;
Alur et al. 2005
)
.
Both require protocol fidelity; the former permits deadlocks while the latter does not.
Lohrey showed both realizability problems to be undecidable in general
(
Lohrey 2003
)
. To make the problem tractable, various restrictions for HMSCs have been identified.
We discuss restrictions that restore decidability of (safe) realizability without modifying the protocol’s behavior.
Bounded/regular HMSCs
(
Muscholl and Peled 1999
;
Alur and Yannakakis 1999
)
always have realizations with bounded channels, yielding finite-state systems.
This is achieved by requiring the communication topology of every loop to be strongly connected.
When this condition is weakened to weakly connected, one obtains c-HMSCs
(
Morin 2002
)
or globally-cooperative HMSCs
(
Genest et al. 2006
)
.
For both regular and globally-cooperative HMSCs,
Lohrey 2003
proved the realizability problem to be
EXPSPACE
-complete.

Lohrey 2003
also introduced so-called
\mathcal{I}
-closed HMSCs where
\mathcal{I}
is the independence relation
\equiv
(
Definition

3.1
) lifted to atomic MSCs, and proved the realizability problem to be
PSPACE
-complete.

As mentioned in
Section

1
, Mazurkiewicz trace theory has proven fruitful for obtaining solutions to other decision problems over HMSCs.
Most works transfer results about recognizable trace languages.

Muscholl and Peled 1999
show that the race and confluence problem for MSC graphs is undecidable in general, but EXPSPACE-complete for locally synchronized graphs corresponding to recognizable trace languages.
Asynchronous FIFO protocol semantics are not definable as the closure of an irreflexive, symmetric binary alphabetic relation in general.
The authors circumvent this by prohibiting the communication pattern

({{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}\!\to\!{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}\!:\!m)^{*}\!
, which intuitively requires counting unreceived messages in a FIFO channel.
Recognizability is in turn enforced by requiring MSCs to be loop-connected, which is similar to star-connectedness of regular expressions that was shown to characterize recognizability
(
Diekert and Rozenberg 1995
)
.
Muscholl and Peled additionally show that loop-connectedness of an NFA is co-NP-complete.
Interestingly, we mirror the observation made in
(
Muscholl and Peled 1999
)
that complexity results in trace theory are few and far between.

A related problem of deciding whether the language recognized by a finite automaton is
I
-closed was shown to be PSPACE-complete in
(
Peled et al. 1998
)
.
The
I
-closure problem asks, given a finite automaton over some alphabet and a concurrency relation, whether the language of the automaton is closed under the equivalence relation induced by the concurrency relation.

Morin 2002
studies star-connected HMSCs, a restriction that again ensures recognizability, and yields that realizability is immediately decidable.

Genest et al. 2004
prove a Kleene theorem connecting monadic second-order logic, (asynchronously) communicating state machines and globally-cooperative compositional MSC graphs.
In light of the above results, we transfer decidability results from recognizable trace languages to realizability in our setting (
Section

3.5
), but do not claim it as a novel contribution.
In our work, we studied aspects in some sense orthogonal to recognizability: unambiguity, and the transitivity of the independence alphabet.
In particular, the connection to unambiguous trace languages was inspired by syntactic choice restrictions inherited by multiparty session types from binary session types, called
directed choice
, that was later generalized to
sender-driven choice
by
Majumdar et al. 2021a
and to
commutation-determinism
by
Giusto et al. 2025
.

5.2.
Multiparty session types

MST frameworks guarantee the absence of communication errors using types.
The first MST framework targeted asynchronously communicating processes
(
Honda et al. 2008
)
, but a synchronous variant followed soon afterwards
(
Bejleri and Yoshida 2008
)
.
MST frameworks typically address the realizability problem through a combination of syntactic restrictions on global and local types, a projection operator that computes local types from global types, and a type system that further rules out undesirable behaviors from local implementations.
This hybrid approach trades completeness for efficiency, soundly approximating realizability via the notion of projectability.
It is difficult and not meaningful to compare algorithms that solve different problems such as projectability and realizability.
We focus our discussion on

(1)

comparing our results to other decision procedures for MST realizability,

(2)

top-down MST frameworks,

(3)

bottom-up MST frameworks with mixed choice, and

(4)

synthetic MSTs and choreography automata.

Note that
(
2
)
is the classical MST approach while
(
3
)
and
(
4
)
are more recent developments.

Deciding realizability

To the best of our knowledge,
(
Giusto et al. 2025
)
is the only work that provides solutions to the synchronous realizability problem of global types.
We discussed their contributions in
Section

1
, explained how their fragments relate to ours in
Section

3.5
, and illustrated their relation to our contributions in
Fig.

2
.
For the asynchronous setting,
(
Li et al. 2023
)
took a similar approach to our work: separating the concerns of realizability and type checking.
They provided the first complete projection operator, deciding the realizability for sender-driven sink-final global protocols.
The same authors later showed this problem to be co-NP-complete
(
Li et al. 2025a
)
. Their CSM realizations can be type-checked with the type system proposed by
(
Stutz and D’Osualdo 2025
)
.

Top-down MST frameworks

In terms of graph structure, syntactic multiparty session types correspond to tree-shaped, sink-final finite automata with loops only between leaves and their ancestors.

Stutz and D’Osualdo 2025
showed that only sink finality restricts expressivity.
Additional restrictions on branching choice are imposed on global types, local types and processes.
Our work shows that as far as decidability of realizability is concerned, the role of choice is to ensure unambiguity of the global protocol.
Our local processes are liberated from choice restrictions.
We limit our discussion to synchronous MST frameworks and refer the reader to
(
Hüttel et al. 2016
)
and
(
Yoshida 2024
)
for a survey.
Most synchronous MST frameworks

(
Bejleri and Yoshida 2008
;
Yoshida and Gheri 2020
;
Tirore et al. 2023
;
Yoshida and Hou 2024
;
Udomsrirungruang and Yoshida 2025
;
Ekici et al. 2025
)

impose directed choice on global types: in each choice, there is a unique sender and receiver pair, with distinct messages across choices.
On a local level, a matching restriction is imposed: processes can either send or receive, with a unique communication partner in each case.
In such classical frameworks, a partial function called projection operator implicitly attempts to solve realizability problem, i.e. if the projection for each participant is defined then the global type is realizable.
Behind the scenes, a merge operator is used to “merge” branches if participants do not take part in a branching.
Most of these frameworks employ the so-called plain merge operator which does not allow participants to have different behavior in branches if they are not part of the decision-making, prohibiting learning about branching later.

(
Udomsrirungruang and Yoshida 2025
)
employs the so-called full merge operator which improves the situation but cannot unfold recursion for merging for instance.
It can still happen that realizable global types have an undefined projection and it was shown that the merge-based approach is inherently incomplete
(
Stutz 2023
)
.
Note that all these works
do not support sender-driven or mixed choice, neither for global types, local types, nor processes.

Bottom-up MST framework with mixed choice

While aforementioned works follow the classical “top-down” approach,
(
Scalas and Yoshida 2019
)
forgoes global types and establishes properties of local types through model checking, coined the “bottom-up” approach.
Their type system only guarantees operational correspondence between local types and processes in the absence of a global type.
Our type system indirectly takes inspiration from
(
Scalas and Yoshida 2019
)
but extends it with mixed choice and types processes against deadlock-free SCSMs obtained from a global protocol.

Peters and Yoshida 2024
also forgo global types, but their type system supports mixed choice for local types and processes.
However, their type system only types a single session and hence no session interleaving or delegation is considered.
In session types, subtyping provides flexibility for how to implement the behavior in processes.
Usually, the folklore is that a sender can send fewer messages and a receiver can receive more messages than specified.
In the presence of generalized choice, this is not necessarily the case – at least not in the asynchronous setting
(
Li et al. 2024
)
.

Peters and Yoshida 2024
provide subtyping that splits the mixed choice into a send and a receive part and applies the above folklore for each part individually.
Following our decoupling discipline, our type system does not provide rules for subtyping, rather, a variant of subtyping that preserves the full protocol semantics is addressed via the synchronous verification problem.

Synthetic MSTs and Choreography Automata

A recent work by
Castro-Perez et al. 2026
forgoes local –not global– types, and addresses a variant of the synchronous verification problem, type checking processes against a global type directly.
Their type system only supports single sessions and as such, their processes are very close to our realization model of SCSMs, but they do not allow receivers to receive from different senders.
Protocol semantics in
(
Castro-Perez et al. 2026
)
is defined plainly as the word language of a labeled transition system (LTS), and not as the closure of a regular language under an equivalence relation.
Note that when the LTS is finite-state (and only then the type system is known to be decidable), this means that its semantics is simply a regular language.
Thus, both the realizability and the verification problem can be decided (soundly and completely) in
PSPACE
(see
Remark

3.3
)
– for a more expressive realization model – and is in fact also
PSPACE
-hard.
Subsequently, the realization can be used to type check local processes.
The authors take a different, hybrid approach, however, soundly approximating the verification problem through a combination of well-behavedness conditions on global protocols and typing rule restrictions.
One such condition requires the LTS to be closed under
\equiv
, but only conditionally.
Unfortunately, the realization model’s semantics is closed under
\equiv
, so any LTS that is not closed under
\equiv
is trivially unrealizable.

Barbanera et al. 2020
introduced choreography automata as a global protocol specification, whose semantics, like
(
Di Giusto et al. 2025
)
, are not closed under any relation. Hence choreography automata semantics are also regular languages, and the same characterizations for deciding realizability and verification apply.

Di Giusto et al. 2025
approximate the realizability problem through well-sequencedness and well-branchedness conditions on choreography automata and observed that well-sequencedness entails closure under
\equiv
.
Notably, the proposed realizability conditions were shown to be flawed for both the asynchronous
(
Majumdar et al. 2021b
)
and the synchronous
(
Barbanera et al. 2023
)
setting.

6.
Discussion

We conclude by discussing possible extensions to two separate and orthogonal infinite settings.

Note that all trace-theoretic results invoked in this work define languages as sets of finite words.
The first extension concerns infinite semantics of global protocols and their realizations, i.e. when
\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{sync}}(\mathcal{G})\subseteq\Gamma^{*}\cup\Gamma^{\infty}
for a finite
\Gamma
.
In this work, global protocol and SCSM semantics are subsets of
\Gamma^{*}\!
.
Global protocols were given an infinite semantics by the authors of
(
Majumdar et al. 2021a
)
, which amounts to a Büchi acceptance condition with all states accepting.
Independently, peer-to-peer communicating state machines give rise to a definition of infinite words, which are simply defined as all infinite execution traces.
Li and Wies
(
Li and Wies 2025
)
showed that the infinite word semantics for global protocols introduced in
(
Majumdar et al. 2021a
)
and adopted in subsequent works
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Stutz 2023
)
in fact does not match the infinite word semantics of the intended realization model, and thus proposed a revised infinite word semantics.

Stutz 2023
showed that reduced global protocols enjoy guarantees about its infinite behavior “for free”, a fact that transfers immediately to our setting.

The second extension concerns global protocols with infinitely many states and transitions, i.e. when
Q
and
\Gamma
are infinite.
Such global protocols are typically represented symbolically using logical predicates, as studied in
(
Li et al. 2025a
;
Li et al. 2025c
;
Vassor and Yoshida 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2020
)
.

Li et al. 2025a
;
Li et al. 2025c
decide asynchronous realizability for symbolic protocols using a semantic characterization that is in essence a (co-)reachability reduction. The generality of this characterization enables it to uniformly derive decision procedures for both finite and infinite-state global protocols.
It is less clear how the “direct” approach taken by our work extends to infinite-state protocols represented symbolically.
On the other hand, our direct approach immediately gives us decision procedures and upper bounds for the
verification
problem.
We leave such an exploration to future work.

On Type Safety.

Usually, one proves type safety as a consequence of subject reduction.
That is, no typable process can reduce to an error.
However, we have not defined any error in our reduction rules, like many synchronous MST frameworks

(
Bejleri and Yoshida 2008
;
Yoshida and Gheri 2020
;
Ekici et al. 2025
;
Castro-Perez et al. 2026
)
.

Peters and Yoshida 2024
, closest to our work by supporting mixed choice, define two kind of errors:
label mismatch and value error.
The latter checks that if-then-else conditions are actually booleans.
Our processes do not support if-then-else conditions.
Accommodating this feature would not be difficult as it is orthogonal to message-passing concurrency.
Label mismatch has previously been considered for processes with directed choice, i.e. a process at any given branching can only send to one dedicated receiver or receive from one dedicated sender.
In this setting, a label mismatch occurs when
{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}
wants to send to
{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}
but there is no common label.
With mixed choice, such a situation is not necessarily problematic: a receiver might be waiting for a sender who might send to someone else in the meantime.

Peters and Yoshida 2024
generalized this condition for mixed choice (for a single session which we call
s
) as follows:

P
has a label error if

P\equiv\MixCh_{i\in I}s[{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}][{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}_{i}]\,\hat{\pi}_{i}\,.\,P_{i}\parallel\MixCh_{j\in J}s[{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{r}}}][{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{s}}}_{j}]\,\hat{\pi}_{j}\,.\,P_{j}\parallel P^{\prime}

such that there is
i\in I
with

{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}_{i}={\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{r}}}
and

\hat{\pi}_{i}=\sendOp l_{i}\langle m_{i}\rangle
,
and for all
j\in J
with

\hat{\pi}_{j}=\recOp l_{j}(x_{j})
,
it holds that
l_{j}\neq l_{i}
.

Intuitively, a label error arises when
{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}
wants to send a message to
{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}
, but
{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}
is unable to receive it.
We do not view all such scenarios as problematic.
Consider the following simple protocol, in which
{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}
first interacts with
{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{r}}}
before interacting with
{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}
.
Consider this simple protocol:

{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}\!\to\!{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{r}}}\!:\!l_{1}\langle m_{1}\rangle\cdot{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}\!\to\!{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}\!:\!l_{2}\langle m_{2}\rangle
.
The following serves as a deadlock-free implementation of the protocol, despite exhibiting a label error according to
(
Peters and Yoshida 2024
)
:

\displaystyle P_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}

\displaystyle=s[{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}][{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}]\sendOp l_{2}\langle m_{2}\rangle\,.\,\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle P_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}

\displaystyle=s[{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}][{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{r}}}]\sendOp l_{1}\langle m_{1}\rangle\,.\,s[{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}][{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}]\recOp l_{2}(x_{2})\,.\,\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle P_{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{r}}}

\displaystyle=s[{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{r}}}][{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}]\recOp l_{1}(x_{1})\,.\,\mathbf{0}

At the same time, the following reduction sequence is possible, according to their and our definition of reductions:

\displaystyle P_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}\parallel P_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}\parallel P_{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{r}}}\longrightarrow P_{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}\parallel s[{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{q}}}][{{\color[rgb]{0.1,0.3,0.1}\mathtt{p}}}]\recOp l_{2}(x_{2})\,.\,\mathbf{0}\parallel\mathbf{0}\longrightarrow\mathbf{0}\parallel\mathbf{0}\parallel\mathbf{0}\equiv\mathbf{0}

Hence, it is impossible to only consider two participants to anticipate errors.
What would be problematic is if there is a circle of dependent processes who cannot move on.
If this concerns all participants, we detect this as a deadlock but if some participants can still act, we do not detect this.
In other words, progress ensures that some participant can take a step at any time but some participants might not be able to take a step anymore at some point.
For any SCSM, one can check if the latter is the case, and a typed process will match the SCSM behavior.
