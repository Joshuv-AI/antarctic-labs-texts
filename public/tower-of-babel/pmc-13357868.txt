The effect of higher versus lower wattage e‐cigarettes on smoking: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yoo Jin Cho, Alice Hinton, Jean Nshimiyimana, Toral Mehta, Lurdes Queimado, Irina Stepanov, Samantha Case, Laura A Beebe, Theodore L Wagener
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo assess whether higher wattage e‐cigarettes (HWe), with improved nicotine delivery, enhance complete substitution compared with lower wattage e‐cigarettes (LWe) and whether complete switching lowers exposure to a cigarette‐derived lung carcinogen, using urinary 4‐(methylnitrosamino)‐1‐(3‐pyridyl)‐1‐butanol (NNAL) as a biomarker.DesignA 3‐arm randomized controlled trial with a 12‐week treatment period and follow‐ups at 26 and 52 weeks. Participants were enrolled from June 2017 to December 2022, with all visits completed by March 2023.SettingAcademic medical centers in the United States.Participants372 adults who smoked and were not interested in quitting [mean age = 40 years; standard deviation (SD) = 11.0; 63% female; mean cigarettes per day = 17; SD = 8.9] were randomized and comprised the analytic sample.InterventionA 12‐week supply of HWe, LWe or usual brand cigarettes (UBC).MeasurementsThe primary outcome was between‐group differences in complete substitution rates at week 12, defined as self‐reported smoking of 1 or fewer cigarettes in the past 7 days [confirmed by exhaled carbon monoxide (eCO) < 4 ppm], with missing data imputed as smoking, and self‐reported use of e‐cigarettes during the 7‐day period. Additional outcomes included urinary NNAL through week 12.FindingsAt 12 weeks, complete substitution rates were 6.0% (8 of 134) with HWe and 9.0% (12 of 133) with LWe, with no statistically significant between‐group difference. Complete substitution rates were 3.7% (HWe) vs. 9.0% (LWe) at 26 weeks, and 4.5% vs. 5.3% at 52 weeks, with no statistically significant between‐group differences. Among those who completely switched, mean NNAL levels decreased over 12 weeks with both LWe [mean decrease: 2.4 pmol/mg creatinine; 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.2–3.7; P = 0.002] and HWe (mean decrease: 2.3 pmol/mg creatinine; 95% CI = 1.1–3.6; P = 0.002).ConclusionsProvision of higher and lower wattage e‐cigarettes to adults who smoked and were not interested in quitting resulted in comparable substitution rates. Complete switching from cigarettes to higher and lower wattage e‐cigarettes reduced urinary 4‐(methylnitrosamino)‐1‐(3‐pyridyl)‐1‐butanol.

INTRODUCTION

Mounting evidence suggests that e‐cigarettes (ECs) are effective for smoking cessation, with a recent review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) concluding with high certainty that ECs increase quit rates compared with nicotine replacement therapy [1]. However, most EC trials were conducted in the UK [2, 3], where clinical guidelines recommend ECs for smoking cessation. These findings may not be generalizable to the USA, where ECs are not endorsed as cessation aids.

As ECs have evolved to deliver nicotine at rates comparable with cigarettes [4, 5], their effectiveness for smoking cessation may have increased [6] by enhancing satisfaction and reducing cravings during quit attempts [7]. Supporting this, population studies have reported higher quit rates among individuals who use ECs in more recent years [8]. Because switching from cigarettes to ECs reduces exposure to tobacco‐specific nitrosamines (TSNAs), ECs may have greater potential to improve public health [9], especially for individuals not interested in quitting [10]. Higher wattage ECs in particular may increase quit rates through their efficient nicotine delivery [4], though exposure to EC‐related toxicants, such as carbonyls, may also increase [11, 12], with levels further influenced by nicotine concentration and puffing behavior.

Despite the evolution of ECs, no study has directly compared EC device types in a prospective, long‐term RCT. The present study, a parallel three‐arm RCT, examined the effects of higher wattage e‐cigarettes (HWe) versus lower wattage e‐cigarettes (LWe) on smoking behavior. Adults not interested in quitting were randomized to receive a 12‐week supply of HWe ECs, LWe ECs or their usual brand of cigarettes (UBC), with follow‐ups through week 52. Compared with LWe, HWe were hypothesized to increase substitution and abstinence rates, while decreasing cigarette consumption, dependence on cigarettes and smoking urges. The safety of ECs was examined by assessing between‐group differences in biomarkers of exposure to nicotine and 4‐(methylnitrosamino)‐1‐(3‐pyridyl)‐1‐butanol (NNAL), a TSNA, as well as adverse events.

METHODS

Trial design

A parallel three‐arm RCT was conducted, in which 372 participants were randomized to the HWe, LWe or UBC arms.

Participants

Participants were recruited through internet advertisements and flyers at the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center (June 2017–March 2019) and the Ohio State University Comprehensive Cancer Center (December 2019–January 2022) for laboratory visits, with pauses owing to the principal investigator (PI) changing institutions and COVID‐related lab closures (March 2020–July 2020). The last follow‐up visit was completed in March 2023. The inclusion criteria were: (i) smoking 5 or more cigarettes per day (CPD) for the past year; (ii) no quit attempts in the past 12 weeks or plans to quit in the next 12 weeks; (iii) ability to communicate in English; (iv) interest in switching to an EC; (v) using ECs for 10 days or less in the past month; (vi) planning to live in the local area for the next year; and (vii) having reliable transportation. The exclusion criteria were: (i) unstable or significant medical or psychiatric conditions; (ii) a cardiac event or distress in the past 12 weeks; and (iii) current or planned pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Procedures

Interested individuals were screened by phone and invited for an orientation visit (week −1), during which informed consent was obtained, eligibility and smoking status was biochemically confirmed, and demographic and smoking history information was collected. Participants were instructed to smoke as usual for the next week and reported cigarette consumption online daily. After establishing baseline smoking behavior, participants were randomized at the second baseline visit (week 0) to: HWe [Evolv DNA 75 Color (Evolv LLC, Hudson, OH, USA) with a Tobeco Super Tank (Tobeco, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China)]; LWe (eCom‐BT; Joyetech, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China); or UBC (Figure S1). Participants assigned to the EC groups selected their preferred flavor by taking a puff from each of seven 0 mg flavored e‐liquids (Flagship Vapor Co., Oklahoma City, OK, USA) using the assigned device. The available flavors included fruit/sweet, menthol/mint and tobacco/tobacco blends. Using the preferred flavored e‐liquid, participants selected a preferred nicotine concentration from four levels: 3 mg/mL (HWe only), 6 mg/mL, 12 mg/mL, 18 mg/mL, 24 mg/mL (LWe only). The nicotine concentrations were selected based on prior research indicating that HWe users prefer lower nicotine concentrations [4]. Study staff also helped participants to find their preferred settings for the wattage (LWe, 5–10 W; HWe, 10–75 W) and air valve opening (HWe only, 0–13.25 mm).

During a 12‐week randomized product trial phase, participants in the EC groups were instructed to attempt to completely switch to the assigned product and otherwise use it ad libitum. Participants in the UBC group did not receive any instructions, serving as a reference condition to help contextualize changes in biomarkers of exposure resulting from product substitution. All products to which participants were randomized were provided for free, including UBC based on baseline smoking behavior. E‐liquids were provided based on product usage, initially with a 30 mL bottle. At each in‐person lab visit (weeks 1, 4, 8 and 12), exhaled CO (eCO), urine samples and unused products were collected, and study product and other tobacco product use and adverse events were assessed. Participants completed online daily diaries detailing their cigarette and EC use for 12 weeks.

During a 40‐week surveillance phase, participants did not receive any products and completed study visits at weeks 26 and 52. At these visits, participants completed questionnaires on their cigarette and EC use, dependence on cigarettes, smoking urges and adverse events, and provided eCO and urine samples. In the week prior to their visits, participants completed the daily online diary to document their cigarette and EC use. Some follow‐up visits during the 40‐week surveillance phase were conducted remotely owing to COVID‐19 in 2020. The original version of the study protocol, as well as an amended protocol approved after the PI changed institutions in May 2019, is provided as an online supplement in the final version. Note: the original protocol was revised in the May 2019 version, including lowering the eCO cut‐off for determining complete substitution to <4 ppm (originally set at ≤10 ppm) to improve the accuracy of smoking status classification [13].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the difference in complete substitution rates between the LWe and HWe arms at the end of the 12‐week product trial phase, defined as self‐reported smoking of no more than one cigarette in the past 7 days, biochemically confirmed by an eCO of <4 ppm, and EC use in the past 7 days. Exploratory post hoc outcomes included the between‐group difference in self‐reported smoking of zero cigarettes in the past 7 days (7‐day point prevalence abstinence, PPA) at weeks 12, 26 and 52, confirmed using an eCO of ≤10 ppm [14].

Additional outcomes included biomarkers of exposure over the 12‐week product trial phase—specifically, urinary cotinine and total NNAL as indicators of nicotine and carcinogen exposure—assessed both overall and among participants who achieved eCO‐verified 7‐day PPA [15]. Among participants who continued smoking cigarettes, outcomes included CPD, scores on the Cigarette Dependence Scale (CDS‐12) [16] and scores on two subscales from the Questionnaire on Smoking Urges (QSU‐Desire and QSU‐Relief) [17]. Throughout the trial, self‐reported adverse effects commonly experienced with ECs [18] and serious adverse events were assessed.

Randomization

An independent statistician generated the blocked randomization allocation sequence. Study staff then enrolled participants and assigned them to experimental conditions, remaining blinded to allocation until the second baseline visit. Randomization was stratified by biological sex (male vs. female), age (<36 vs. ≥36 years), education level (<high school vs. General Educational Development, GED vs. some college), self‐reported nicotine dependence [19] (<5 vs. ≥5) and readiness to quit smoking [20] (<6 vs. ≥6), to help ensure balance across key demographic and smoking‐related characteristics.

Statistical analysis

The sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome of between‐group difference in complete substitution at 12 weeks, which was expected to be associated with all other outcomes. Assuming estimated abstinence rates of 15% for the LWe group and 30% for the HWe group [21, 22], and a 20% attrition rate, a total of 453 participants (151/group) was estimated to provide approximately 80% power to detect a 15% difference in substitution rates between the EC groups using a two‐sided 0.05‐level chi‐square test. Owing to funding cuts and delays from restrictions related to COVID‐19, the final sample size was reduced to 372 participants (n = 133 for LWe; n = 134 for HWe; n = 105 for UBC).

Substitution and 7‐day PPA rates were analyzed using an intention‐to‐treat approach, with missing data assumed to be not at random and classified as smoking [14] for the purposes of this primary analysis. Risk differences (RDs) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated to describe differences in the rates between study groups. Fisher’s exact tests were used to assess differences in the complete substitution and 7‐day PPA rates, applying Holm’s correction for pairwise comparisons (HWe vs. LWe, HWe vs. UBC and LWe vs. UBC) at each week. The primary comparison of interest, as pre‐specified in the protocol, was HWe versus LWe, while the other pairwise comparisons were considered exploratory.

Three sets of sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, mixed‐effects logistic regression models controlling for study site and randomization stratification variables were run to compare substitution and 7‐day PPA rates between LWe and HWe from week 4 to week 52, following the protocol. The main analytic approach was selected to ensure consistency across outcomes and timepoints, as no participants in the UBC group quit smoking up to week 12, which limited our ability to run a logistic regression model containing all groups. Second, 7‐day PPA rates were compared between groups using different eCO thresholds (<4 ppm, ≤4 ppm, <6 ppm, ≤6 ppm and <10 ppm), given that the eCO level used to confirm abstinence changed from ≤10 ppm in the initially approved protocol to 4 ppm in the final protocol, to maximize accuracy [13]. Third, missing values for 7‐day PPA were imputed using multiple imputation via fully conditional specification with 50 imputed data sets, under the assumption that data were missing at random. Logistic regression was used to impute missing binary variables, discriminant analysis for categorical variables and predictive mean matching for continuous variables. The imputation model included all randomization stratification variables, site, randomization group, baseline measures (age, sex, race, ethnicity, income, education, Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence score, cigarettes per day and CDS‐12 score), as well as time‐varying variables (eCO, cigarettes smoked in the past week assessed using a timeline follow‐back questionnaire, cigarette dependence score and motivation to quit). This imputation approach differed from the protocol‐specified inverse probability weighting and was chosen as it is generally more efficient.

Biomarkers of exposure, urinary cotinine and total NNAL, were adjusted for creatinine. Mean differences (MDs) and the corresponding 95% CIs were computed to describe differences in the mean levels. Within‐ and between‐group statistical differences were examined using mixed‐effects log‐linear regression models, adjusting for baseline biomarker values at week 0.

Between‐group differences in the proportion of participants who reduced their CPD by at least 50% from baseline were examined using mixed‐effects logistic regression models, controlling for baseline CPD. The analysis included only weeks 8–52, as no participant in the UBC group reduced their cigarette consumption by at least 50% at weeks 1 or 4. Among participants who continued smoking, between‐group differences in CPD, CDS‐12 scores and smoking urges from weeks 1 through 52 were examined using mixed‐effects regression models, controlling for baseline measures. All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4. All mixed‐effects models included random subject intercepts and adjusted for randomization stratification variables and study site, with Tukey’s adjustment for pairwise comparisons between the three study arms. The analysis was not pre‐registered and the results should be considered exploratory.

Smoking abstinence

At 12 weeks, abstinence rates were similar in the LWe (11.3%) and HWe (8.2%) groups (Table 2), while no participants in the UBC group achieved abstinence (0%). Sensitivity analyses using mixed‐effects logistic regression models, alternative eCO thresholds and imputed values supported the primary findings, indicating that 12‐week abstinence rates for ECs were similar and were higher than those for the UBC group (Tables S1–S3).

At 26 weeks, abstinence rates for the LWe group (13.5%) were higher than those for the HWe (4.5%) and UBC (4.8%) groups, but the differences were not statistically significant (Table 2). Similarly, at 52 weeks, abstinence rates did not significantly differ between the study groups (LWe = 8.3%; HWe = 6.0%; UBC = 3.8%; Table 2). Sensitivity analyses generally yielded consistent patterns (Tables S1–S3); however, at 26 weeks, abstinence rates were significantly higher for the LWe group than for the HWe group in the mixed‐effects logistic regression model (adjusted odds ratio, aOR = 4.36; 95% CI = 1.27–14.94; P = 0.019; Table S1) and were higher for the LWe group than for the UBC group in the model based on imputed data (aOR = 4.68; 95% CI = 1.47–14.93; P = 0.009; Table S3).

Biomarkers of exposure

Mean cotinine levels did not significantly change within each group over 12 weeks, and they did not significantly differ between study groups at 12 weeks (Table 3). When limiting analyses to participants who quit smoking, mean cotinine levels were significantly lower in the LWe group than in the HWe group (change = –76.4%; 95% CI = −93.0% to −21.1%; P = 0.024; Table 3).

Changes in cigarettes smoked per day

At 12 weeks, participants reduced their CPD from baseline by 8.9 (SD = 7.8) with LWe and by 9.7 (SD = 7.5) with HWe, with no significant difference between the EC groups (Table S4). However, CPD increased for the UBC group (by 1.2; SD = 4.8), showing a significant difference from the reductions observed in the LWe (MD = –11.3; 95% CI = −13.4 to −9.2; P < 0.001) and HWe (MD = –11.0; 95% CI = −13.1 to −8.9; P < 0.001) groups (Table S4).

Figure 2 shows changes in CPD among participants who continued smoking by study groups from week 1 through week 52. At 12 weeks, participants averaged 8.6 CPD (SD = 6.8 CPD) with LWe and 8.7 CPD (SD= 7.0 CPD) with HWe, with no significant difference between them. The mean CPD was higher with UBC at 19.5 CPD (SD = 17.5 CPD), with significant differences compared with both LWe (change = –61.0%; 95% CI = −68.9% to −51.0%; P < 0.001) and HWe (change = –61.6%; 95% CI = −69.1% to −52.1%; P < 0.001) (Table S5). At 26 and 52 weeks, the mean CPD remained consistently lower in the LWe and HWe groups compared with the UBC group (e.g., at 52 weeks: LWe vs. UBC, change = –39.2%; 95% CI = −53.9% to −19.9%; HWe vs. UBC, change = –46.2%; 95% CI = −58.8% to −29.7%; both P < 0.001), with no difference between LWe and HWe groups (Table S5).

Cigarette dependence

Figure S2 shows changes in cigarette dependence among participants who continued smoking by study groups from week 1 through week 52. At 12 weeks, compared with the UBC group, cigarette dependence was lower for both the LWe (MD = –6.7; 95% CI = −9.6 to −3.8; P < 0.001) and the HWe (MD = –7.0; 95% CI = −9.8 to −4.2; P < 0.001) groups (Table S6). At 26 weeks, cigarette dependence remained lower for the LWe (MD = –4.7; 95% CI = −7.8 to −1.7; P = 0.001) and HWe (MD = –4.0; 95% CI = −6.9 to −1.0; P = 0.005) groups than in the UBC group (Table S6). At 52 weeks, cigarette dependence remained lower for the HWe group compared with the UBC group (MD = –3.3; 95% CI = −6.3 to −0.3; P = 0.028), but was no longer significantly lower for the LWe group compared with the UBC group (Table S6).

Smoking urges

Figure S3 shows changes in smoking urges among participants who continued smoking by study groups from week 1 through week 52. At 12 weeks, compared with the UBC group, smoking urges were lower for both the LWe group (e.g. QSU‐Desire: MD = –6.3; 95% CI = −9.0 to −3.6; P < 0.001) and the HWe group (e.g. QSU‐Desire: MD = –6.4; 95% CI = −9.1 to −3.8; P < 0.001), with no difference between the LWe and HWe groups. Smoking urges remained lower for the LWe and HWe groups than for the UBC group at 26 and 52 weeks (e.g. QSU‐Desire at 52 weeks: LWe vs. UBC MD = –4.5; 95% CI = −7.5 to −1.5, P = 0.001; HWe vs. UBC MD = –4.2; 95% CI = −7.0 to −1.3, P = 0.002), although the QSU‐Relief scores for the LWe group were no longer significantly lower than those for the UBC group at 52 weeks (Table S7).

Adverse events

The number of participants reporting specific adverse effects did not differ between groups, although more participants reported at least one adverse effect with ECs than with UBC (Table S8). However, the reports for ECs decreased over time, becoming lower than those reported for UBC by both weeks 26 and 52 (Table S9). The most common adverse effect reported was a cough, with more than half of participants reporting it at least once over 52 weeks, followed by mouth and throat irritation. The number of participants who experienced serious adverse events and the number of serious adverse events occurring over 52 weeks did not significantly differ between groups (Table S10).

DISCUSSION

In this randomized clinical trial, the HWe and LWe groups showed similar complete substitution rates throughout the 52‐week study period (e.g. 6.0% and 9.0% at 12 weeks), despite past studies demonstrating improved nicotine delivery with HWe. Both HWe and LWe significantly increased smoking abstinence rates compared with UBC (8.2% and 11.3%, vs. 0%) at 12 weeks among adults not motivated to quit. Although the statistical difference did not persist at 26 or 52 weeks, the absolute abstinence rates remained higher for the LWe group than for the UBC group. These findings provide additional evidence supporting the effectiveness of ECs for smoking cessation [1], even without initial intentions to quit [10]. Moreover, participants who continued smoking in the HWe and LWe groups had lower mean CPD, cigarette dependence and smoking urges, compared with the UBC group, at each timepoint from week 1 to week 52, with no significant difference in adverse events by study group over 52 weeks. Overall, the results suggest that offering free ECs may reduce smoking in the current US clinical practice and regulatory environment.

Abstinence rates in our EC arms (6.4% at 6 months; 4.9% at 12 months) were lower than reported in prior studies [2, 3, 23], potentially reflecting contextual factors, such as the 2019 E‐cigarette or Vaping‐Associated Lung Injury (EVALI) outbreak and the absence of any EC authorized as a therapeutic cessation aid in the USA [24]. In contrast, ECs are regulated as therapeutics in Australia and are recommended in UK cessation guidelines.

In addition to achieving comparable substitution rates with HWe, LWe consistently showed higher absolute abstinence rates than HWe from week 4 to week 52. While this difference was not statistically significant in the primary analysis, the sensitivity analysis using a mixed‐effects logistic regression model showed significantly higher abstinence rates for the LWe group compared with the HWe group at week 26. This unexpected result may reflect the greater ease of use of LWe [25], leading to more frequent use—a key factor associated with quit rates [26]. Additionally, LWe users may have engaged in compensatory puffing to satisfy cravings [5, 27, 28], as indicated by the similar mean cotinine levels between the EC groups. However, the higher adherence to the assigned product in the HWe group suggests caution when recommending LWe over HWe. Additionally, the superior nicotine delivery of HWe may explain the significantly lower QSU‐Relief scores at 52 weeks compared with the UBC group, a result not observed with LWe. Therefore, naïve EC users may benefit from starting with simple devices like LWe and transitioning to more customizable devices like HWe as they gain familiarity.

The biomarker analysis suggests that switching from cigarettes to ECs reduces NNAL exposure, aligning with previous studies [9, 29]. While cotinine levels remained stable, as expected [4], mean NNAL levels decreased over time in the LWe group, and were lower than those in the HWe and UBC groups, suggesting reduced carcinogen exposure.

Our findings suggest that long‐term dual use is a likely outcome of switching to ECs [30], with 11% of participants in the EC groups remaining dual users at 52 weeks. While dual users reduced their cigarette consumption [31], the health benefits of partial switching remain uncertain because they continued to be exposed to toxicants from both products, including those not assessed in this study. For example, the odds of diseases associated with dual use have been shown to be higher than for smoking alone [32]. These results highlight the importance of ultimately achieving abstinence from both cigarettes and ECs.

Limitations

The results should be interpreted with the following limitations. First, the generalizability of the findings may be limited owing to the provision of free products, which may not reflect real‐world conditions where individuals purchase the products themselves. Second, the study did not include recent EC devices, such as disposables and pod‐based devices that typically use palatable nicotine salts with high nicotine concentrations, potentially increasing both their efficacy for smoking cessation and their abuse potential. Additionally, the biomarker analysis was limited to 12 weeks, highlighting the need for future independent studies to examine whether ECs reduce toxicant exposure over extended periods, as suggested by industry studies [33, 34]. Notably, abstinence rates in the UBC group may be conservative owing to the lack of instructions to attempt quitting smoking. Relatedly, abstinence rates during the surveillance phase in Ohio may be conservative, as participants might have had difficulty obtaining their preferred study e‐liquids owing to the Prevent All Cigarette Trafficking Act enforced in 2021. Although the analyses adjusted for site, they may not fully capture the impact of these restrictions. Finally, the overall substitution rates and achieved sample size were lower than anticipated, which may have reduced the statistical power and limited the ability to detect group differences.

CONCLUSION

The provision of both HWe and LWe resulted in comparable substitution rates and increased smoking abstinence among adults not motivated to quit, though abstinence was not sustained in the long term. LWe were particularly effective in promoting smoking abstinence and reducing NNAL exposure, although complete switching from cigarettes to either HWe or LWe ECs reduced NNAL exposure.
