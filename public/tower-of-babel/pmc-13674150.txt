Target‐Engagement Prediction From Molecular Structure, In Vitro and Animal Data: A Tutorial for Early Assessment of Clinical Drug Exposure and Therapeutic/Adverse Potential Using the ANDROMEDA and PLATO Software on the InSilicoTrials Platform
Authors: Niccolò Totis, Fianne Sips, Simona Catozzi, Marc‐Antonio Bisotti, Pauline Bambury, Sofia Stathopoulos, Vincenzo Carbone, Luca Emili, Mario Torchia, Valentina Belgiovine, Fulvio Ciriaco, Orazio Nicolotti, Urban Fagerholm, Candice N Baker, J Matthew Mahoney, Daniel Röshammar, the CARDIOVERSE Project
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe target‐engagement predictor, implemented within the InSilicoTrials cloud‐based, AI‐powered platform for model‐informed drug discovery and development, enables early exploration of time‐dependent pharmacological responses. Predictions can be generated using inputs ranging from molecular structure to in vitro, animal, and clinical data. Scaling and translation across species are supported, accounting for differences in body size and plasma protein binding. This tutorial demonstrates the application of the target‐engagement predictor for benchmarking and decision‐support in the evaluation of new drug candidates. Pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) data for carvedilol, ivabradine, and sunitinib were used as illustrative examples. The ANDROMEDA and PLATO software were used to predict human clinical PK and target affinity parameters, respectively, based on molecular SMILES representations of the drugs. These predictions were subsequently incorporated into longitudinal PKPD simulation models. User‐friendly input wizards and interactive results dashboards were developed to facilitate clinical trial simulation for broader project teams, without requiring programming expertise. Predictions derived from molecular structure were compared with data generated from in vitro, rat, and clinical experimental settings. The target‐engagement predictor enables integration of heterogenous data sources to support drug development decisions, including early benefit–risk assessment and dose selection for Phase 1 studies. Direct prediction based on molecular structure alone represents a promising approach for early‐stage screening and evaluation of new drug candidates.

Introduction

Early prediction of clinically appropriate dosing regimens and robust assessment of benefit–risk profile, relative to existing therapies and unmet medical needs, are critical components of modern drug development. Such evaluations enable early termination of suboptimal drug candidates, support optimization of clinical development strategies, and help reduce costly late‐stage attrition. A deep understanding of the underlying pathophysiological and pharmacological pathways has been recognized as a key driver of improved R&D productivity [1, 2]. To this end, three foundational pillars have been emphasized for assessing the quality of novel drug candidates: (i) achievement of adequate exposure at the site of action, (ii) binding to the intended pharmacological target, and (iii) expression of downstream pharmacological activity [3].

Timely prediction of pharmacokinetics (PK), target engagement, and pharmacodynamics (PD) has been shown to accelerate drug development and reduce overall costs. One illustrative example is the use of dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 (DPP‐4) inhibition as a proximal marker of sitagliptin efficacy. When combined with PKPD modeling, this approach enabled optimization of clinical study design and shortened the time to regulatory submission [4]. By accounting for differences in PK and drug potency, Overgaard et al. [5] investigated the impact on body weight reduction when switching patients to semaglutide from other glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists. Similarly, for tozorakimab (an interleukin‐33 monoclonal antibody), quantitative characterization of the relationship between PK and target engagement was used to inform Phase 2 dose selection [6]. In the case of ticagrelor (a P2Y12 receptor antagonist), near‐maximal platelet inhibition was demonstrated for both the 60 mg and 90 mg doses, providing a rationale for the development of alternative formulations [7]. Additional examples include the integration of PK and electrophysiological models with in vitro ion‐channel inhibition data to enable early prediction of drug‐induced arrhythmogenic risk [8].

Traditionally, animal studies have served as the primary basis for extrapolating anticipated therapeutic and adverse effects of investigational drugs to the clinical setting. However, attrition rates, particularly those driven by cardiovascular toxicity, remain high [9, 10]. The increasing availability of in vitro assays and new approach methodologies (NAMs) provides expanded opportunities for more informed decision making in the early stages of drug development. For example, Pierson et al. [11] recently reviewed a range of NAMs relevant to cardiac safety assessment and highlighted their potential to improve early identification of cardiovascular risk. Greater integration of in vitro data and NAMs into the design of animal studies and clinical trials can help to more effectively de‐risk development programs. This strategy may reduce negative or inconclusive outcomes arising from suboptimal study designs or poorly selected lead compounds, while also minimizing premature discontinuation of otherwise promising drug candidates.

Early assessment of target affinity can now be achieved directly from the chemical structure of a molecule. For example, applications such as multi‐ion channel assessment of cardiotoxicity are enabled by the CUPID (Cardiotox Understanding Platform for Intelligent Drug Discovery) software, an explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) framework designed to predict cardiotoxicity associated with ether‐à‐go‐go‐related gene (ERG) potassium, Nav1.5 sodium, and Cav1.2 calcium ion channels [12]. Similarly, the PLATO (Polypharmacology pLATform predictiOn) software provides a predictive drug discovery web platform for efficient target identification and bioactivity profiling of small molecules. It enables prediction of drug‐target affinities across a wide range of receptors, ion channels, enzymes, and transporters that may contribute to therapeutic or adverse drug responses [13, 14, 15]. In addition to PD properties, PK profiles can now also be predicted from molecular representations using the ANDROMEDA software [16, 17, 18, 19, 20], further supporting early‐stage drug development and optimization.

Regardless of the experimental approaches employed, accurate dose prediction to achieve meaningful target engagement and pharmacological response often requires in silico analyses and specialized quantitative expertise. However, demand within industry currently exceeds the supply of quantitatively trained scientists emerging from academia [21]. Moreover, only a limited number of integrated software solutions are sufficiently accessible to support use by broader project teams without advanced training in quantitative drug development. To address this gap, we developed a cloud‐based platform that leverages user‐friendly in silico approaches to support and de‐risk drug development. This platform provides access to quantitative analyses, enabling their routine application across multi‐disciplinary teams [22].

The InSilicoTrials platform is designed to support three distinct user groups, each with different needs and interaction styles:

Technically oriented data analysts, such as data scientists, preclinical PKPD modelers, pharmacometricians, and statisticians, who are accustomed to working with scripting‐based methods and can develop programmable, flexible, and reusable workflows for data and model integration.

The platform comprises multiple modules supporting quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP), PKPD modeling, statistical analyses, machine learning, and AI‐based approaches, including their integration within a Clinical Trial Simulator (CTS). Together, these capabilities are designed to address key drug development questions encountered across different stages of development. Among these tools, the target‐engagement predictor enables early exploration of the causal relationships between PK and target engagement as well as downstream pharmacological response over time. The tool allows direct comparison of new compounds with marketed or front‐running drugs acting on the same target. It thereby supports evaluation of alternative dosing regimens and informs go/no‐go decision making based on probability‐of‐success estimates. Through integration of the ANDROMEDA and PLATO software, the platform can also incorporate predictions derived directly from molecular structure, complementing traditional inputs from in vitro, animal, and clinical data.

The objective of this tutorial is to demonstrate the application of the target‐engagement predictor for benchmarking and evaluating the potential of new drug candidates. Instead of focusing on the validation of individual translational approaches, we aim to demonstrate the value of an integrated framework that enables direct comparison and aggregation of data from diverse experimental models and methods, thereby informing clinical development decisions. Here, the tool was used to predict human PK and target affinities for drugs known to elicit on‐ or off‐target cardiovascular effects (therapeutic or adverse). As illustrative case examples, we selected carvedilol (a non‐selective β 1‐ and β 2‐adrenergic receptor blocker), ivabradine (a hyperpolarization‐activated cyclic nucleotide‐gated channel 4 [HCN4] antagonist), and sunitinib (a multi‐tyrosine kinase inhibitor, including vascular endothelial growth factor receptor‐2 [VEGFR‐2] inhibition). The three drugs were selected based on publicly available data and models across experimental systems.

Simulation Workflow and Input Wizard

InSilicoTrials Platform for Model‐Informed Drug Development

All computations were performed using the InSilicoTrials platform (www.insilicotrials.com), a toolkit for drug developers that includes AI‐powered tools for model‐informed drug discovery and development, deployed on Microsoft Azure [22]. The platform comprises a web‐based interface, a back‐end layer responsible for workflow orchestration and data handling, and a dedicated computational layer for model estimation and simulation. The execution environment supports models implemented in languages such as Python, R, and MATLAB, as well as established pharmacometric software packages (e.g., NONMEM and Monolix) and containerized applications. Models are executed in isolated virtual machines or Docker containers that are dynamically started and stopped on demand, ensuring secure and controlled execution of heterogeneous workflows.

Models are integrated within the platform using a flexible workflow builder, complemented by configurable components for defining input wizards and results dashboards. Workflows can combine multiple models and data‐processing steps within a single pipeline and can be structured hierarchically to enable reuse of components across different analyses.

ANDROMEDA and PLATO were integrated into the platform and implemented as sequential prediction steps within a single workflow. Based on molecular SMILES representations, ANDROMEDA was used to generate human clinical PK parameter estimates, while PLATO was used to predict human target affinity parameters. The resulting outputs were subsequently used as inputs for longitudinal PKPD simulation models executed within the same workflow. Input wizards were configured to support parameter specification and scenario definition. Result dashboards were designed to display simulation outputs alongside published in vitro, animal, and clinical data for comparative evaluation. A PKPD model simulation framework, including options for allometric scaling and correction for plasma protein binding differences, was implemented using the mrgsolve package (version 1.7.2) in R (version 4.6.0) [23].

For technically oriented data analysts, automated script‐based workflows can be configured and adapted to support the needs of broader project teams. Both the simulation input wizard and the results dashboard are fully customizable (Figure 1).

ANDROMEDA Software

ANDROMEDA is a software for the prediction and optimization of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) as well as human clinical PK, thereby enabling accurate assessment of expected clinical drug exposure, directly from molecular structures. The approach is based on machine learning and conformal prediction, combined with physiologically‐based PK (PBPK) modeling. ANDROMEDA has been validated using a large and diverse set of drugs. It has been benchmarked against alternative computational approaches and experimental laboratory methods. Overall, it demonstrates superior predictive accuracy compared with these approaches. For example, ANDROMEDA produces lower prediction errors than animal data‐based models for key PK parameters, including oral bioavailability (F), steady‐state volume of distribution (V ss), clearance (CL), and oral clearance (CL/F) [16, 17, 18, 19, 20].

Users initiate the analysis by entering the molecular SMILES representation, after which results are generated instantly and displayed as tables and figures. An example output for carvedilol is shown in Figure 2. Corresponding results for ivabradine and sunitinib are provided in the (Figures S1–S2).

Example of ANDROMEDA predicted human clinical PK parameters for carvedilol based on molecular structure, compared with observed clinical data.

In this work, ANDROMEDA (version 2.0) was used to predict human clinical PK parameters based on the molecular SMILES representations of the three selected drugs. With the exception of V ss, fraction unbound in plasma (fu), cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A4 substrate identification, and fraction dissolved for ivabradine as well as V ss, fu, efflux identifications, CYP isoform identifications, intrinsic clearance (CLint), and renal clearance (CLR) for carvedilol, none of the parameter values were used in building, training, or validating the underlying models. The observation that predicted values deviate from measured values included in the training sets indicates that the full underlying models, rather than individual compound‐specific data points, primarily drive the predictions. ANDROMEDA predicts V ss and “effective” half‐life (t ½), rather than V and t ½ of the terminal phase.

Basic ANDROMEDA models are built and validated using conformal prediction (CP), a methodology for augmenting machine learning predictions with a valid confidence prediction [24, 25]. In contrast to standard machine learning methods which typically produce point estimates (e.g., a class label for classification models or a single value for regression models), CP provides prediction intervals at a user‐defined confidence level. The width of these intervals dependents on how “nonconforming” the test compound is relative to the training data, as well as the specified confidence level and the overall efficiency of the predictor. In this work, 70% confidence intervals for predicted V ss, fu and CLint parameter estimates are presented. However, these were not included in the current simulations.

PLATO Software

The PLATO software is a tool for drug target identification (“target fishing”) and bioactivity profiling of a given small‐molecule based on its molecular structure. Predictions rely on a multi‐fingerprint similarity searching through a high‐quality reference set of 636,142 bioactive compounds experimentally tested against 7437 protein targets in the ChEMBL database (version 36) [15]. In this work, PLATO (release 36) was used to predict target affinity parameters based on the SMILES representation of the three selected drugs.

Users upload the SMILES structure of a given drug via a user‐friendly input wizard. After execution, the results are presented as a table summarizing target affinities for all targets with potential drug‐target interaction in different species. Publicly available in vitro, animal, and clinical data were collected to enable comparison across the different prediction approaches. Carvedilol

For carvedilol, the in vitro dissociation constant (K D) for β 1‐adrenergic receptor binding has been estimated at 1.78 nM [26]. The enantioselective PK of S‐carvedilol following intravenous administration in rats has been described using a two‐compartment model, with the associated chronotropic effect characterized by an I max model [27]. As ANDROMEDA predicts concentrations for the racemic mixture, it was assumed that the two enantiomers exhibit similar PK behavior. For comparison, a published clinical population PKPD model describing racemic carvedilol concentrations and the associated reduction in heart rate was used [28]. In the original publication, drug absorption was described using a zero‐order process with a duration of 0.383 h. In the present analysis, this was approximated using a first‐order absorption rate constant (k a) fixed to 1.5 h−1. In the absence of clinical target engagement data, heart rate reduction was used as a downstream pharmacological surrogate marker, acknowledging that the relationship between target engagement and clinical response may not be strictly proportional. Plasma protein binding of carvedilol has been reported to be approximately 98% in both rats [29] and humans (reported human values range from 95% to 99.5%) [30]. The human value was used in all simulations. A single oral dose of carvedilol 25 mg was simulated based on the predicted PKPD parameter estimates. In this example, uncertainty and variability were not accounted for in the parameter estimates, with the exception of between‐subject variability in the clinical PK parameter estimates.

Ivabradine

For ivabradine, in vitro inhibition of the HCN4 channel has been estimated at 4280 nM [31]. A two‐compartment PK model in rats was reported by Wallman et al. [32] together with drug concentration‐response parameters describing the effect on heart rate. The clinical population PK for ivabradine, with a corresponding PKPD assessment for the relationship between drug concentration and heart rate, was recently presented in a conference abstract [33]. In the absence of direct clinical target engagement data, heart rate was used as a downstream pharmacological surrogate marker, recognizing that the relationship between HCN4 inhibition and chronotropic response may not be strictly proportional. The contribution of the active metabolite was not accounted for in this case example. Plasma protein binding of ivabradine has been reported to be approximately 75% in rats [32] and 70% in humans (reported human values range from 70% to 76%) [34]. The human value was used in all simulations. Four doses of ivabradine 5 mg immediate‐release tablets, administered twice daily, were simulated based on the predicted PKPD parameter estimates. In this example, uncertainty and variability were not accounted for in the parameter estimates, with the exception of between‐subject variability in the clinical PK parameter estimates.

Sunitinib

For sunitinib, in vitro inhibition of VEGFR‐2 has been estimated at 112 nM [35]. A one‐compartment PK model in rats was presented by Liu et al. [36]. A two‐compartment clinical population PK model derived from a meta‐analysis was used for comparison [37]. Predictions of target engagement were evaluated against published clinical data of VEGFR‐2 inhibition [38]. The contribution of the active metabolite was not accounted for in this case example. Plasma protein binding of sunitinib has been reported to be approximately 95% in humans [39], and this value was used in all simulations. Repeated oral dosing of sunitinib 50 mg once daily was simulated over 1 week of treatment based on the predicted PKPD parameter estimates. In this example, uncertainty and variability were not accounted for in the parameter estimates, with the exception of between‐subject variability in the clinical PK parameter estimates.

Animal PK parameters were allometrically scaled to humans based on body weight, using fixed exponents of 0.75 for clearances and 1 for volumes of distribution. For new first‐in‐class projects, we recommend a stepwise approach that begins with clinical trial simulation early in the discovery phase, based on molecular structure‐derived predictions. For promising compounds, complementary in vitro and animal data can subsequently be generated to strengthen and validate the predictions. At each stage, the resulting experimental data should be evaluated using modeling and simulation approaches. The insights gained can then be used to design the next experiment through simulation‐based optimization, thereby increasing the probability of success and maximizing the value of generated data. The totality of evidence should be assessed and discussed across experimental systems to provide a comprehensive understanding of target engagement and translational relevance. In the present case examples, our objective is to contrast various data types and methodological approaches, illustrating how information from these diverse sources can be integrated within a unified framework. For illustrative purposes, we assume that the clinical outcomes were not known a priori, although they are included here for retrospective validation.

Clinical Target‐Engagement Prediction Results Dashboard

Carvedilol

Based on molecular structure (in ANDROMEDA), the carvedilol CL/F and V ss/F were predicted at 52.9 L/h and 258 L (for a 70 kg subject), respectively. As carvedilol is a Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS) I class drug with gastric emptying dependent absorption, the absorption rate constant (k a) was predicted at 3.8 h−1. The ANDROMEDA predicted fu of 2.5% (not used in simulations) was consistent with the reported human value of 2% [30], which was used in all simulations.

The β 1‐adrenergic receptor dissociation constant (K D) was predicted at 58.9 nM in PLATO, indicating a slightly lower potency compared with the in vitro estimate of 1.78 nM [26].

The predicted time‐course of carvedilol plasma concentrations and receptor inhibition was in some agreement with observed clinical data (Figure 6). Both the ANDROMEDA‐predicted PK parameters and those obtained via allometric scaling from rat data resulted in higher predicted drug exposure compared with clinical observations. After adjusting for differences in plasma protein binding, exposure‐response relationships derived from in vitro‐based predictions of target affinity were comparable to those observed clinically. Predictions based on molecular structure and rat data were similar to each other but exhibited much flatter exposure‐response relationships and lower maximal effects relative to clinical data. Target engagement was slightly underestimated when using in vitro inputs and more substantially underestimated when derived from molecular structure‐based predictions. Nevertheless, the time‐course of pharmacological effects was comparable across all data sources.

Ivabradine

Based on the molecular structure (in ANDROMEDA), the ivabradine CL/F and V ss/F were predicted at 133 L/h and 430 L (for a 70 kg subject), respectively. As ivabradine is a BCS Class I‐II drug, the k a was predicted at 2.2 h−1. The ANDROMEDA predicted fu of 25% (not used in simulations) was similar to the reported value in humans of 30% [34], used in all simulations.

The half maximal HCN4 inhibitory drug concentration (IC50) was predicted at 4131 nM in PLATO, in close agreement with the in vitro estimate of 4280 nM [31].

The predicted time‐course of ivabradine plasma concentrations and ion channel inhibition showed agreement with observed clinical data (Figure 7). PK parameters predicted by ANDROMEDA resulted in a slight underprediction (< 2‐fold) of drug exposure compared with clinical observations. In contrast, allometrically scaled PK predictions derived from rat data led to a modest overestimation (< 2‐fold) of clinical exposure levels. After adjusting for differences in plasma protein binding, concentration–response relationships derived from molecular structure, in vitro, and animal‐based extrapolations exhibited shallower slopes than those observed clinically around the maximal clinical PK concentrations. However, the curves converged and crossed at higher exposure levels. Target engagement predicted from animal data was lower than that observed clinically, and predictions based on molecular structure and in vitro inputs suggested markedly lower levels of target inhibition. However, when accounting for the delayed‐response observed for the clinical data (using the effect‐compartment model), the difference to the preclinical predictions was less significant. The time‐course of pharmacological effects was consistent across all experimental levels and supported the clinically established twice‐daily administered dosing regimen.

Discussion

In this tutorial, we demonstrated how target‐engagement predictions within the InSilicoTrials platform can be leveraged to inform clinical development by integrating molecular structure‐based predictions, in vitro data, animal studies and clinical comparator information. Models describing drug exposure and target engagement (such as receptor occupancy or ion channel inhibition) over time were applied to three drugs (carvedilol, ivabradine, and sunitinib), each associated with on‐ or off‐target cardiovascular pharmacological effects. Clinical trial simulations were performed across multiple dosing regimens. Predicted outcomes were compared with observed clinical data to evaluate model performance and assess the impact of different translational scaling approaches. Key outputs, including drug concentration‐time profiles and target‐engagement dynamics, were visualized through interactive results dashboards, enabling transparent comparison across data sources and providing actionable insights to inform decision making.

The platform is designed to support technically oriented hands‐on users, routinely involved in data analysis and interpretation (e.g., data scientists, preclinical PKPD modelers, pharmacometricians, and statisticians) as well as broader project team members (e.g., clinical pharmacologists, study physicians, and project leaders) who are typically less directly involved in the analytical work but are well positioned to interpret results. Technical users can create customized input wizards and results dashboards for their colleagues, enabling intuitive exploration of how molecular properties and study design variables influence predicted outcomes. Through these customizable, interactive results dashboards, featuring high‐level summaries and key conclusions, the platform facilitates clear and effective communication when presenting analyses to decision‐making boards. Overall, this flexibility enhances transparency and promotes collaboration across the diverse disciplines and departments involved in pharmaceutical drug development.

The results demonstrate that in vitro target affinity data, when combined with early‐phase PK information, can be used to anticipate late‐stage study outcomes and inform clinical trial design. Moreover, by integrating the ANDROMEDA and PLATO software with a clinical trial simulation engine, longitudinal predictions of target engagement can be generated directly from molecular structure alone. With the exception of the sunitinib PK predictions generated by PLATO, the clinical data were generally either under‐ or overpredicted, irrespective of whether the predictions were based on molecular structure, in vitro or animal data. In the PHRMA‐project [40], which involved several major pharmaceutical companies and universities, animal and in vitro data based predictions of human C max and AUC following oral dose administration resulted in approximately 10%–30% and 55%–85% of predictions falling within 2‐ and 10‐fold error, respectively. The corresponding average prediction errors were 4‐ to 12‐fold. In more recent retrospective, internal analyses of the performance of local prediction models where PBPK and various in silico, in vitro and animal in vivo data have been used, 49%–72% and 49%–94% of predictions of AUC and C max were within 2‐fold of observed clinical estimates [41, 42, 43, 44]. Thus, we consider the results in the present study based on molecular structure to be promising and an interesting complement to traditional methods.

Although the scope of the current work was not to formally validate the ANDROMEDA and PLATO software, the findings highlight their potential utility for early‐stage assessment of drug candidates. Future work will focus on more comprehensive evaluations of translational methodologies for clinical Phase 1 dose selection. This will include analyses of additional drugs with cardiovascular effects using historical data, as well as prospective validation studies to further establish the predictive performance of the framework. In the current examples, uncertainty and variability was not fully accounted for, except for between‐subject variability in PK parameter estimates. For prospective evaluation of therapeutic and adverse potential, incorporating uncertainty in parameter estimates should be considered. Notably, additional parameters can be readily added to the input wizard by the technical users as desired. Moreover, the current examples may be expanded with scenarios where in vitro PK data are used in combination with molecular structure‐based predictions, as allowed by the ANDROMEDA software. The simple allometric scaling approach used here can also be improved using additional data from for example dogs. A more detailed review of allometry has recently been reported by Fagerholm [20].

Overall, ANDROMEDA provided predictions of clinical drug exposure that were at least as accurate as, and in some cases superior to, those obtained using allometric scaling from rat data. This is consistent with previous benchmarking studies [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. For example, in a head‐to‐head comparison with allometric scaling using data from at least two animal species, the animal data‐based methodology gave 7‐, 3‐, and 25‐fold higher mean, median, and maximum prediction errors for CL/F than ANDROMEDA (n = 43), respectively [20]. Corresponding ANDROMEDA superiority numbers for CL, V ss, and F in head‐to‐head comparisons were 34‐, 1.5‐, and 157‐fold, 1.2‐, 1.2‐, and 1.9‐fold, and 1.2‐, 1.1‐, and 1.5‐fold, respectively [20]. It is worth noting that allometric scaling of V ss and CL for ivabradine in man from corresponding rat and dog estimates resulted in 18‐ and 4.5‐fold underpredictions (with slopes of 0.54 and 0.72), respectively (data taken from [34]). For carvedilol and ivabradine, some parameters had already been used in the test dataset. There is evidence that random splits into training and test datasets can yield optimistic results compared to realistic time‐split validation, with R 2 dropping from approximately 0.7 to 0.4–0.5 depending on the ADME readout [45]. The prediction quality for prospective evaluation could hence be expected to be worse. Internal investigations of ANDROMEDA showed an overall, but slight (1.3‐fold), exaggeration of performance when test compounds have already been used in training sets. Predictions are based on the gathered information from all different compounds in each dataset, which means that both pharmacophores or substituents play a role and that predicted outcomes for training set molecules usually differ from the original values. In the present report, where a few test data for ivabradine and carvedilol were present in training sets (most data for underlying models used for estimation of secondary PK‐parameters were missing), the average prediction error for 8 PK‐parameters was 1.44‐fold. The corresponding mean value from cross‐validations during model building was 1.42‐fold. ANDROMEDA models are based on hundreds to thousands of data points and 20‐fold cross validations (also repeated), keeping the anticipated random data selection bias to a minimum (here estimated to maximally 3%, which was consistent with results from repeated cross‐validations). The confidence in performance was strengthened further by additional predictions of hold‐out data sets (including new marketed drugs and chemicals of various types) and external blind validations, which both showed similar or equal results as those found in internal cross‐validations. Results obtained in the present study were no different. In 20‐fold cross‐validations, ANDROMEDA reached an overall average Q 2 of 0.6 and with a range at least 3 times broader than with in vitro data based methods.

Using PLATO, bioactivity affinity values for the relevant pharmacological targets were predicted with reasonable accuracy, although the resulting target‐engagement time profiles were somewhat underestimated relative to observed clinical data for carvedilol and ivabradine. Notably, PLATO predicted affinities were similar to experimentally determined in vitro values. Given that in vitro potency estimates can vary substantially across assays, we recommend benchmarking drugs using a consistent assay platform whenever possible to support robust and reliable comparative assessments.

The ANDROMEDA and PLATO software are regularly retrained with emerging data and models. Proprietary data can be included for customized versions of the software.

In the current proof‐of concept examples, we selected three random drugs with cardiovascular side effects based on publicly available and readily accessible in vitro, animal and clinical compound‐characterization data reported in the literature. This enabled comparison across all experimental levels. For a new first‐in‐class project, we propose initiating clinical trial simulation at an early stage using molecular structure‐based predictions. For promising drug candidates, complementary in vitro data should be generated to strengthen the evidence base. The resulting knowledge can then be used to optimize confirmatory animal studies, enabling design of experiments with an estimated high probability of success. Modeling and simulation are expected to play a central role in both data interpretation and the design of subsequent experiments at each stage of development. A long‐term, aspirational objective of this research is the gradual replacement of animal experiments through the combination of molecular structure‐based predictions and in vitro data. However, the platform is flexible, and all scenarios can be implemented either individually or in combination.

It should be noted that generic predictions of in vivo efficacious concentrations based solely on in vitro potency can be highly variable and may lack biological significance [46]. Having model‐based PKPD predictions in combination with in vitro data across species holds the potential to greatly improve the translational value of safety pharmacology studies [47]. The overall justification of clinical dosing schedules should hence be based on the totality of evidence across experimental systems.

Clinical target‐engagement data, for validation, were retrieved only for sunitinib. For this compound, predicted VEGFR‐2 target engagement based on preclinical data can be directly compared with both proximal pharmacodynamic measurements and downstream clinical effects. In the clinical setting, long‐term sunitinib therapy has been associated with reductions in left ventricular ejection fraction as well as increases in diastolic blood pressure, the latter exhibiting fluctuations in accordance with dosing frequency. Both endpoints have been described with indirect response PKPD models, incorporating linear concentration‐response models for describing drug effects [48]. This highlights that, even when target engagement is adequately characterized, clinical outcomes may be modulated by additional physiological and pathological processes. However, Collins et al. [49] further described the relationship between VEGFR‐2 inhibition and drug‐induced blood pressure elevation and hypertension and found direct exposure‐response relationships at steady‐state in both rats and humans. The results can be used for maximizing selectivity against VEGFR‐2 to minimize the potential for causing drug‐induced hypertension.

For carvedilol and ivabradine, direct measures of clinical target engagement were not available. Consequently, the downstream pharmacological effects in terms of heart rate reduction was used as a surrogate pharmacodynamic endpoint. For both compounds, the predicted response based on target affinity and exposure were consistently lower than those observed clinically. The finding suggests that the relationship between proximal pharmacological targets (e.g., β‐adrenergic receptors or HCN4 ion channels), and downstream physiological responses (such as heart rate modulation) is often non‐linear and not directly proportional. Nevertheless, when accounting for the delayed responses observed clinically for ivabradine and sunitinib, the discrepancies to the preclinical predictions were less pronounced.

Differences in apparent potency between proximal molecular targets and integrated physiological endpoints represent an important limitation when using target‐affinity data alone as a surrogate for clinical efficacy or safety. Signal transduction pathways may amplify or attenuate responses through complex pharmacological cascades, feedback mechanisms, and system‐level adaptations, leading to non‐linear relationships between receptor or ion‐channel engagement and observable clinical effects. Consequently, similar levels of target engagement may produce markedly different clinical outcomes depending on context, duration of exposure, and system sensitivity [50].

To improve predictions of clinical outcomes, we therefore recommend the use of more mechanistic modeling frameworks that explicitly link target engagement to downstream physiological and clinical endpoints. Such approaches can enhance the interpretability and impact of target‐engagement assays by embedding them within the relevant pharmacological signaling and disease pathways. Well‐established examples demonstrate the value of this strategy. For instance, inhibition of HMG‐CoA reductase by statins leads to reductions in low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), which serves as an accepted surrogate marker for cardiovascular risk reduction and clinical outcomes [51].

Data across all experimental levels consistently supported the relevance of the selected pharmacological targets and underscored the potential of target engagement‐based modeling to inform therapeutic and safety margins in dose selection. Notably, the results also reflect the frequency of administration of the clinically established dosing regimens.

Carvedilol is a non‐selective β‐adrenergic receptor antagonist administered as a racemic mixture of R‐ and S‐enantiomers. The enantiomers differ primarily in pharmacodynamic potency, whereas their pharmacokinetic properties are largely comparable. Because ANDROMEDA predicts ADME and PK properties at the level of the racemate and does not distinguish between stereoisomers, rat data for both enantiomers were included in the comparative analysis. Differences in PK characteristics between enantiomers are not expected for passive processes such as solubility, passive permeability, tissue binding, or glomerular filtration. In contrast, stereoselective differences may arise for active processes such as metabolism or carrier‐mediated transport. Based on literature data, such differences are typically minor for β‐blockers, with very limited divergence in exposure profiles, and generally do not exceed two‐ to four‐fold even for compounds where stereoselectivity is more pronounced (e.g., verapamil, fluoxetine, methadone) [52, 53]. Consequently, any enantiomer‐specific PK differences for carvedilol are anticipated to be of a similar magnitude to the median prediction error associated with the in silico approach, limiting their practical impact on the current analyses.

Across the three drugs studied, human plasma protein binding ranged from approximately 70% to 98%. It is important to note that only the unbound (free) fraction of drug is generally considered pharmacologically active. This is the only fraction that can bind to its target and elicit a biologic response. Therefore, accounting for differences in plasma protein binding is critical when interpreting exposure–response relationships and translating findings across experimental systems [54]. Accordingly, the platform's capability to incorporate the unbound drug fraction is particularly important when integrating data across preclinical and clinical settings with divergent plasma protein binding characteristics. Early assessment of human plasma protein binding is thus essential to enable accurate interpretation of exposure–response relationships, estimation of therapeutic margins, and informed dose selection in clinical development. Notably, estimates of fu in human plasma can be generated directly from molecular structure using the ANDROMEDA software [18].

A recent comprehensive framework for human PK prediction emphasized the importance of predicting full PK profiles, rather than individual ADME parameters in isolation [41]. In the present work, this concept is extended to include prediction of full target engagement profiles enabling the assessment of both therapeutic and adverse pharmacodynamic responses. The referenced study suggests that a PK prediction accuracy within approximately 2.1‐fold error is considered robust for in silico approaches and can serve as a tool for compound design and prioritization. In contrast, defining acceptable predictive accuracy for target engagement remains context‐dependent and must be evaluated with respect to the specific indication, safety endpoint, and drug class. Overprediction of therapeutic target engagement may lead to prolonged and costly dose escalations in the Phase I studies, whereas underprediction of adverse target engagement could result in premature termination of promising programs or, more critically, pose safety risks to trial participants.

Early characterization of a new drug candidate's target‐engagement profile can substantially impact overall development timelines and help mitigate the risk of late‐stage failure. This target‐engagement prediction module of the InSilicoTrials platform combines PK and PD modeling to enable comparative pharmacology assessments, benchmarking investigational compounds against marketed therapies acting on the same targets. By simulating a range of dosing regimens, the platform supports quantitative comparisons of the probability of achieving desired therapeutic effects while minimizing the risk of adverse responses across candidate drugs. Thereby, it can inform dose selection and go/no‐go decision making early in development.

The incorporation of in vitro data allows for optimization of subsequent animal studies, with the potential to reduce required sample sizes. Furthermore, prediction based solely on molecular structure can help prioritize the in vitro experiments, directing resources toward the most promising compounds, reducing cost and development time. Although this tutorial focuses on target‐engagement assessments, these modeling approaches can be readily integrated with mechanistic systems pharmacology frameworks to describe downstream pharmacological responses and predict the trajectories of clinically relevant endpoints.

Summary

The target‐engagement predictor enables the integration of data from multiple sources including molecular structures, in vitro assays, and animal studies to generate early predictions of clinical dosing regimens and benefit–risk profiles. These predictions support informed study design decisions and go/no‐go investment choices during drug development.

The platform provides an intuitive interface that allows clinicians and broader project team members to visualize the expected PK, PD, and outcome trajectories. Clinical trial simulations of alternative dosing regimens can be performed across various study populations. Data from marketed therapies or competitors in development can be incorporated for comparative benchmarking.

Our results demonstrate that both PK properties and target affinity can be predicted directly from molecular structure. When these predictions are combined, the expected drug response can be evaluated dynamically over time. Notably, structure‐based extrapolations may outperform traditional allometric scaling from animal data. Collectively, these capabilities have the potential to reduce reliance on animal testing, decrease late‐stage attrition, and reduce the premature discontinuation of otherwise promising drug candidates. Although molecular structure‐based predictions still require thorough prospective validation and comparison with conventional methods, the tools presented here offer a structured starting point for exploring and assessing these approaches further.
