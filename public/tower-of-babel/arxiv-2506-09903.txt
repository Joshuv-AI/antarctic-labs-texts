Update on Algorithms for Carmichael numbers
Andrew Shallue, Jonathan Webster

Abstract
. In this note, we present progress on determining the computational complexity of tabulating all Carmichael numbers less than some bound B B . We implemented a new tabulation algorithm which we describe here. In lieu of providing an asymptotic analysis of that algorithm, we show a second related algorithm that we claim is optimal under two believable conjectures.



1.
Introduction

In 1993, R.G.E. Pinch described an algorithm used to find all Carmichael numbers up to
10^{15}

[
4
]
. Over the next 14 years, he used the same algorithm to produce successively larger tabulations ending with
10^{21}

[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. We revisited this problem and tried to improve Pinch’s approach. In
[
11
]
, we found an asymptotic improvement to the small case. In
[
12
]
, we gave an asymptotic improvement to the large case and showed that the count of inputs to the algorithm was bounded by

B\exp\left(\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+o(1)\right)\sqrt{\log B\log\log B}\right).

It was believable that the time used per input was polynomial in
\log B
; so, the above bound was also a heuristic run time for the algorithm. Timing information demonstrated that our improvement was not very effective (see the final table in
[
12
]
). We concluded by sharing our suspicion that it should be possible to tabulate Carmichael numbers by “consider[ing] fewer preproducts.”

The bound above matches Knödel’s initial bound on the count of Carmichael numbers
[
3
]
. Erdős improved Knödel’s bound on the count
[
2
]
, showing

B\exp\left(\frac{-k\log B\log\log\log B}{\log\log B}\right)

for some positive constant
k
. In
[
10
]
, it is shown that
k
can be chosen to be
(1-\epsilon)
for any
\epsilon>0
. In the same paper, a heuristic argument (combined with a more precise result from analytic number theory, see
[
9
]
) shows that this is also the lower bound for the count of Carmichael numbers.

We follow the argument in
[
10
]
and state a theoretical tabulation algorithm. Precisely, we prove the following theorem conditioned on a believable conjecture. The conjecture is not needed for the correctness of the algorithm.

Theorem 1
.

For each
\epsilon>0
, there is an
B_{0}(\epsilon)
such that for all
B>B_{0}(\epsilon)
, there is an algorithm that can find all Carmichael numbers less than
B
in time
B\cdot L(B)^{-1+o(1)}
, where
L(x)=\exp(\log x\cdot\log_{3}x/\log_{2}x)
.

The first conjecture we require is that the query “Is
n
a Carmichael number?” can be answered in polynomial time; see Section 5 for a precise statement of the required conjecture. The second conjecture is only needed for the claim that this algorithm is optimal. If
B\cdot L(B)^{-1+o(1)}
is the correct asymptotic count of Carmichael numbers, then the algorithm is optimal. To have an asymptotically faster algorithm would immediately improve the current best upper bound on the count of Carmichael numbers and would explain the error in the heuristic argument for the lower bound. Such a result would be of deeper theoretical import than the algorithm itself.

In what follows, we set up the required preliminary results from number theory and on Carmichael numbers. We provide a brief review of Pinch’s approach in order to provide context for the improvements offered here. We give a brief example to demonstrate our belief that there were “too many preproducts,” which is also grounded in the different asymptotic counts of Knödel and Erdős. The example poses two algorithmic problems and we address each of those solutions in the next two sections. We state the algorithm we are currently running. In penultimate section, we state and prove the algorithmic realization of the counting argument found in
[
10
]
. Lastly, we conclude with a high-level conceptual overview of the three different algorithmic approaches presented in this note.

It might be asked why our implementation differs from the optimal algorithm. We embarked on this work having already completed the “small” case of Pinch’s tabulation regime. As you will see, our new way of dealing with the “large” case bears a striking similarity to the optimal algorithm and the majority of Carmichael numbers are found in the exact same way. Indeed, the existence of our implementation is what allowed us to realize that the optimal recursive algorithm existed.

2.
Background

2.1.
Number theoretic functions and definitions

(1)

Let
\omega(n)
count the distinct prime factors of
n
. That is,

\omega(n)=\sum_{p\mid n}1.

(2)

Let
\lambda(n)
be the Carmichael lambda function. Formally, we have

\displaystyle\lambda(2^{r})

\displaystyle=2^{r-2}

\displaystyle\lambda(p^{r})

\displaystyle=p^{r-1}(p-1)

\displaystyle\lambda(n)

\displaystyle={\rm lcm}(\lambda(p_{1}^{r_{1}}),\lambda(p_{2}^{r_{2}}),\dots,\lambda(p_{k}^{r_{k}}))

where the lcm is taken over the distinct prime powers dividing
n
. Equivalently,
\lambda(n)
is the universal exponent of
(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.

2.2.
Theorems for Carmichael numbers

A positive integer
n
is Carmichael if it is composite and
a^{n}\equiv a\mod{n}
for all
a\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
. Thus a Carmichael number has the pleasing property that every integer
a
either shares a factor with
n
or is a Fermat liar for
n
.
However, we typically take Korselt’s criterion as our working definition for tabulation.

Theorem 2

(Korselt’s Criterion)
.

A composite number
n
is a Carmichael number if and only if
n
is squarefree and
(p-1)\mid(n-1)
for all prime divisors
p
of
n
. Equivalently,
n
is Carmichael iff
\lambda(n)\mid(n-1)
.

Tabulation algorithms construct
n
in factored form. Let
P_{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}
where
p_{i}<p_{j}
iff
i<j
. We drop the subscript
k
from
P_{k}
when the context does not require a specific count of primes dividing a preproduct. Henceforth, we reserve
p
to be the greatest prime factor of
P
. When other letters are used for primes we typically use
q
or
r
.

Theorem 3

(Proposition 2 of
[
4
]
)
.

Let
n=Pqr
be Carmichael.
Then there are integers
2\leq D<P<C
such that, putting
\Delta=CD-P^{2}
, we have

q=\frac{(P-1)(P+D)}{\Delta}+1,\quad r=\frac{(P-1)(P+C)}{\Delta}+1,\quad P^{2}<CD<P^{2}\left(\frac{p+3}{p+1}\right).

Theorem 4

(Proposition 1 of
[
4
]
)
.

Let
n
be a Carmichael number less than
B
with
d
prime factors.

(1)

Let
k<d
. Then
p_{k+1}<(B/P_{k})^{1/(d-k)}
and
p_{k+1}-1
is relatively prime to
p_{i}
for all
i\leq k
.

(2)

P_{d-1}r\equiv 1\pmod{\lambda(P_{d-1})}
and
r-1
divides
P_{d-1}-1
.

(3)

Each
p_{i}
satisfies
p_{i}<\sqrt{n}<\sqrt{B}
.

The condition that
p_{k+1}-1
is relatively prime to
p_{i}
for all

means that every

is a cyclic number (in the group theoretic sense). When
p_{k+1}
satisfies this condition we call the prime

with respect to
P_{k}
.

Part

of Theorem

can be generalized: we have
PR\equiv 1\pmod{\lambda(P)}
for arbitrary positive
R
whenever

is Carmichael and
n=PR
. Enabling composite completions will give needed flexibility in the constructions to follow. The quantity
P^{-1}\pmod{\lambda(P)}
is used frequently throughout and we let

denote the least positive residue in that congruence class. This might be considered an unconventional naming choice but it is due to the fact that we associate this quantity with

3.3.
Observed Problems with Prior Tabulation

The asymptotically faster method for the inner loop proved to be ineffective in practice because its best case was when
P
was small. More often the tabulation found itself in the case
P_{d-1}\lambda(P_{d-1})>B
. When this happened, single prime completion consisted of two steps: compute
r^{\star}
and discard
Pr^{\star}
for being too large. There were other downstream effects. First, the work to get to the inner loop was more costly than the inner loop. Second, this made parallel distribution of the work difficult. Our approach was ad-hoc and inefficient.

To address these shortcomings this new work changes the cases above as follows:

(1)

Subsection 3.1 becomes “Tabulate all
n=PR
with
P<X
and
\omega(R)=2
.”

(2)

Subsection 3.2 becomes “Tabulate all
n=PR
with
P<X
and
\omega(R)\geq 3
.”

This change allows us to keep the count of preproducts smaller but poses two algorithmic challenges. First: how do we find composite completions
R
? Second: how do we guarantee our algorithm is correct?

5.
Finding composite
R
so that
PR
is a Carmichael number

In this section, we show how to find composite
R
so that
n=PR
is a Carmichael number. The approach described below can also find
n=PR
with
R
being prime but the method is intended to be invoked when
Pp^{2}<B
so that
R
can be composite. In particular, a well written
SPC
should find a single prime no slower than what is described here and is capable of finding it asymptotically faster given certain conditions on
P
and
B
.

Since we check each
k
value such that
n=P(r^{\star}+k\lambda(P))<B
, we will call this method of finding Carmichael numbers
\lambda
-sieving
in what follows. For each
k
, we use a Fermat test and a strong Fermat test. These are treated as two different tests and are computed simultaneously as part of a single modular exponentiation in the standard square and multiply ladder. We iterate over Fermat bases (chosen to be relatively prime to
n
) and do the following:

(1)

Reject
n=P(r^{\star}+k\lambda(P))
if it is detected as composite by a Fermat test.

(2)

If a strong Fermat test detects
n=P(r^{\star}+k\lambda(P))
is composite, then we obtain a splitting of
n
. If this does not split
r^{\star}+k\lambda(P)
or if
n
is a strong pseudoprime to this base, move to the next Fermat base and repeat.

(a)

For prime factors, we check admissibility and if
p-1
divides
n-1
. Reject
n
if these conditions are not met.

(b)

For composite factors, we move to the next Fermat base and repeat the steps on these factors.

Finding a single splitting of
n
is either probabilistic polynomial time or deterministic polynomial time conditioned on the ERH (e.g. Theorem 1 of
[
13
]
). Let
k
be the odd part of
n-1
. Then if
g<h
are multiplicatively independent bases,
\gcd(g^{k}-1,h^{k}-1)\ll g^{k/2}
meaning that most of their factors are different
[
1
]
. So, it is likely that two independent Fermat bases witness the compositeness of
n
in different ways and will produce a different splitting.

Conjecture 1
.

The above algorithm answers the query “Is
n
a Carmichael number?” in either

(1)

zero-error probabilistic polynomial time, or

(2)

deterministic polynomial time algorithm conditioned on the ERH

depending on how the Fermat bases are chosen.

Note the similarity of this question and the status of PRIMES in P prior to 2001. The above is a BPP algorithm because after
k
bases the probability of error is bounded by
(1/2)^{k}
. We have implemented the above algorithm, and it works well in practice. We henceforth assume that Conjecture 1 is true.

8.
A Heuristically Optimal Algorithm

Theorem 6

(Theorem 6 of
[
10
]
)
.

For each
\epsilon>0
, there is an
x_{0}(\epsilon)
such that for all
x>x_{0}(\epsilon)
, we have
C(x)\leq x\exp(-(1-\epsilon)\log x\cdot\log_{3}x/\log_{2}x)
.

In
[
10
]
, the authors provide a heuristic lower bound on the count of Carmichael numbers where
(1-\epsilon)
is replaced by
(2-\epsilon)
. Using a sharper result from analytic number theory, it is shown that the constant can also be
(1-\epsilon)
, thus matching the proven upper bound
[
9
]
. We show that the sets used to prove Theorem
6
admit algorithmic realization. In doing so we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 7
.

For each
\epsilon>0
, there is an
B_{0}(\epsilon)
such that for all
B>B_{0}(\epsilon)
, there is an algorithm that can find all Carmichael numbers less than
B
in time
B\cdot L(B)^{-1+o(1)}
, where
L(x)=\exp(\log x\cdot\log_{3}x/\log_{2}x)
. The running time is conditional on Conjecture
1
.

Proof.

There are three sets of Carmichael numbers
n\leq B
that are counted. We show that there exists an algorithm that tabulates each of these sets in time proportional to the size of the set times the cost of answering the query, “Is
n
a Carmichael number?”
By Conjecture
1
determining whether
n
is Carmichael takes randomized time which is expected to be
O({\rm poly}(\log B))
. The three sets are

(1)

n\leq B^{1-\delta}
.

(2)

B^{1-\delta}<n\leq B
and
n
has a prime factor
p\geq B^{\delta}
.

(3)

B^{1-\delta}<n\leq B
and all the prime factors of
n
are below
B^{\delta}
.

In the proof of Theorem
6
,
\delta
is eventually set to be
\epsilon/5
to complete the proof but we describe the sets in terms of the parameter
\delta
.

(1)

The first set represents the small Carmichael numbers. By the nature of the bound, this is easier algorithmically than the other two sets. One could apply the method of the other two sets recursively, or one could simply check every
n<B^{1-\delta}
at a cost of
\widetilde{O}(B^{1-\delta})
.

(2)

The count of these Carmichael numbers is bounded by considering sets
P(r^{\star}+k\lambda(P))<B
indexed by
k
, where
P
is a prime exceeding
B^{\delta}
.

Algorithmically, construct all primes up to
\sqrt{B}
(note that the largest prime dividing a Carmichael number is at most
\sqrt{B}
). For each prime
P>B^{\delta}
, use
\lambda(P)
-sieving. The total cost is then poly(
\log{B}
) times

\sum_{P>B^{\delta}}\frac{B}{P\lambda(P)}

which is a lower order cost compared to set 3.

(3)

The count of these Carmichael numbers is bounded by considering
P(r^{\star}+k\lambda(P))<B
, where
B^{1-2\delta}<P\leq B^{1-\delta}
are cyclic numbers that are
B^{\delta}
-smooth.

Algorithmically, we could use a sieve on this interval to find the
B^{\delta}
-smooth numbers and this would suffice for the purposes of our proof. However, we recommend constructing the set explicitly. This is akin to the prime-by-prime construction featured above and can be thought of as an algorithmic realization of Buchstab’s identity for counting smooth numbers. This also would allow an early-abort by not proceeding whenever
P\lambda(P)>B
.

Similar to the previous part, for each
P
use
\lambda(P)
-sieving. The asymptotic cost of this part of the tabulation is what contributes asympototic cost given by the work in
[
10
]
.

This demonstrates that the upper bound can be algorithmically realized.

∎

If Conjecture 2 is correct, the algorithm in the proof of Theorem
