Performance and response time in emotion recognition and mentalizing measures in patients with schizophrenia
Authors: Alicia Ruiz-Toca, Alba Hernández-Gómez, Nelson Andrade-González, Cristina Fernández-Aragón, Bryan J Stiles, Emilio Fernandez-Egea, Roberto Rodriguez-Jimenez, Guillermo Lahera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchizophrenia is associated with marked impairments in social cognition, particularly in emotion recognition and mentalizing. Although these deficits have been extensively documented, most studies rely on static, highly controlled measures and focus primarily on accuracy-based outcomes, thereby limiting ecological validity. This study aimed to examine performance and response times across static and dynamic social-cognitive measures in patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy controls, and to investigate the effects of speed instructions on emotion recognition performance.MethodsIn a sample of patients with schizophrenia (n = 90) and matched healthy controls (n = 100), we evaluated social cognition through two validated measures: the Penn Emotion Recognition Test (ER-40) for emotion recognition and the Movie for the Assessment of Social Cognition (MASC) for mentalizing, respectively. Performance and response times were the main outcome variables.ResultsPatients showed worse performance and longer response times than controls on the ER-40 and MASC measures. Across the entire sample, there was a statistically significant inverse association between response time and the rate of correct answers in both ER-40 and MASC measures. Speed instructions in the ER-40 did not influence performance or response times in either group. In the MASC, patients exhibited more mentalizing errors than controls. Additionally, patients identified anger more quickly than fear or sadness, a pattern not observed in the healthy control group.ConclusionsIntegrating accuracy and response time provides a comprehensive characterization of social–cognitive deficits in patients with schizophrenia. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating response time and dynamic measures to enhance the ecological validity of social-cognitive assessment and suggest that future rehabilitation programs may benefit from addressing both accuracy and response speed in emotion recognition and mentalizing domains.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Social cognition, Emotion recognition, Mentalizing, Performance, Response time, Speed processing

Background

Schizophrenia is a serious mental disorder characterized by the presence of positive psychotic symptoms, negative psychotic symptoms, and cognitive symptoms that lead to functional impairment and show a tendency to be chronic [1–3].

Social cognition (SC) is defined as the set of mental operations that underlie social interactions [4]. It involves the ability to infer mental states (i.e., intentions, expectations, beliefs, desires, emotions) of oneself and others based on observed behavior [4–6]. SC is a multidimensional construct comprising emotion processing, social perception, attributional bias/style, and mentalizing [4]. Many studies in schizophrenia have reported significant impairments in SC, which have an impact on the adequate development of social interaction and normative social functioning skills of people with schizophrenia [7–10].

Emotion processing and mentalizing are two of the dimensions of SC that have received the most research interest [4]. Emotion processing is a concept associated with perception, decoding, identification, and the understanding and management of one’s own and others’ emotions [11–13]. A subdomain of emotion processing is the recognition or perception of emotions [14, 15], which refers to the ability to identify one’s own and others’ emotions, primarily through facial expressions [16]. Based on previous research, patients with schizophrenia show deficits in emotional expression, recognition [17–21], and emotional prosody [22]. These impairments are particularly pronounced for negative emotions, such as fear and anger [21, 23, 24]. Additionally, patients show reduced sensitivity to fear, sadness, and happiness compared to healthy controls [19]. Recent meta-analysis evidence outlines that these emotion recognition deficits represent a specific impairment in schizophrenia that is robust across levels of schizophrenia severity, task difficulty, or intelligence matching, but can be masked by memory-dependent comparison tasks [25]. Furthermore, reduced white matter integrity in the genu of the corpus callosum has been associated with deficits in the rapid and accurate recognition of emotional stimuli, as well as with greater clinical symptom severity [26]. Importantly, research demonstrates that deficits in emotion recognition interfere with different functional outcome domains [27], such as work functioning and independent living [28].

Mentalizing refers to the ability to represent other people’s mental states and infer their intentions and beliefs [4]. This construct is conceptually aligned with Theory of Mind, a core construct in SC research [29, 30]. Compared to healthy controls, mentalizing abilities are impaired in patients with schizophrenia [8, 31, 32], with deficits observed across explicit, implicit, and spontaneous dimensions [31]. Poor mentalizing skills are associated with difficulties in social adaptation [33], potentially contributing to increased social withdrawal and isolation [34].

Recent research in SC has prioritized assessing the difficulties of these patients with the greatest possible ecological validity [35, 36]. In this context, response time (RT) has increasingly been incorporated as an outcome measure. RT reflects the contribution of multiple underlying processes, including perceptual, decisional, and motor components, rather than a single cognitive mechanism [37, 38], and should therefore be interpreted as a composite measure rather than a direct proxy of processing speed. In some SC studies [39], RT has been examined as a relevant indicator of performance in SC tasks. Patients with schizophrenia have demonstrated not only greater SC deficits but also longer reaction time [39–41] and aberrant patterns, especially in emotion recognition [40]. Compared to healthy controls, patients with schizophrenia show longer RT in tasks requiring facial emotion processing, whether assessed using static facial expression tasks [42], dynamic affect recognition paradigms [43], or continuous emotional and non-emotional task designs [44]. Additionally, they are slower to form a first impression of threat [45] or to appraise negative faces [46]. Regarding mentalizing, patients with schizophrenia show slower RT in both implicit mentalizing measures and the interpretation of social interactions compared to controls [47, 48]. Taken together, patients with schizophrenia may require a longer RT to complete different SC measures.

Deficits in SC in patients with schizophrenia have been extensively investigated using measures characterized by abstract experimental environments and single-modality stimuli [49]. However, when persons are immersed in real-world settings, the expression of these emotions is not fixed but fast, dynamic, and multimodal. Recent research has emphasized the need for experimental designs to account for these features and to adopt methodological approaches that better capture the temporal and interactive nature of social information processing [36]. To the best of our knowledge, RT has not been systematically examined as a primary outcome in dynamic mentalizing measures. For this reason, this research includes both a static SC measure (in which participants are asked to recognize facial emotions) and a dynamic SC measure (e.g., film clips for the assessment of mentalizing). Together, the inclusion of static and dynamic measures, the consideration of participants’ RT, and the incorporation of speed instructions (detailed in the following section) may improve the ecological validity of the experimental design. This approach addresses limitations of prior research, which has rarely explicitly examined ecological validity [36].

Additionally, as previously outlined, RT may be a relevant indicator of performance in SC measures in patients with schizophrenia. However, to date, relatively few studies have explicitly examined or manipulated RT in schizophrenia to clarify its role in SC performance. Some studies have applied speed instructions (i.e., asking participants to respond as quickly as possible without sacrificing accuracy) in SC measures [40, 50], but this did not significantly affect the task performance of patients [50]. Nevertheless, the inclusion of speed instructions remains of particular interest [50] as it may enhance the ecological validity of SC assessments.

A distinctive feature of our research is therefore the recording of item-level RT in a dynamic mentalizing measure. In this regard, we aimed to overcome the limitations of previous studies by simultaneously examining within-subject performance and RT across two core domains of SC (emotion recognition and mentalizing) in schizophrenia, including item-level RT analyses and cross-domain RT–accuracy comparisons. This study addresses a key gap between commonly used SC measures and real-world social functioning, which typically involves rapid, continuous, and interactive exposure to multiple social stimuli. Our study provides a within-subject comparison across both static (ER-40) and dynamic (MASC) domains, including a specific manipulation of speed instructions in the former. This approach allows for a more ecologically valid characterization of social-cognitive deficits in schizophrenia and aligns with recent recommendations to incorporate dynamic measures to further advance in this field [43]. Furthermore, the design of this study aligns with the importance of continued methodological refinement in SC assessment [51].

Aims of the study

The specific aims of this study were: (1) to analyze the performance and RT of patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy subjects in the recognition of each basic emotion, (2) to analyze the relationship between the RT and the rate of correct answers when recognizing each basic emotion in a static facial emotion measure in patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy controls, (3) to explore the influence of the type of instructions (i.e., speed instructions or non-speed instructions) on the probability of a correct answer in recognizing each basic emotion in a static facial emotion measure in patients with schizophrenia, and (4) to analyze the performance and RT of patients with schizophrenia in mentalizing compared to healthy controls.

Study hypotheses

Drawing on previous research, we hypothesized that: (1) Hypothesis 1: patients with schizophrenia will demonstrate significantly impaired performance and increased response latencies in the recognition of all basic emotions compared to healthy control subjects, (2) Hypothesis 2: longer RT will be negatively associated with accuracy during the identification of basic emotions within a static facial recognition paradigm, (3) Hypothesis 3: the implementation of speed-focused instructions is hypothesized to result in a significant decrease in the probability of a correct answer for recognizing basic emotions within a static facial expression measure among patients with schizophrenia, compared to non-speed instructions, and (4) Hypothesis 4: patients with schizophrenia will demonstrate impaired performance and increased response latencies on mentalizing measures compared to healthy control subjects.

Method

Participants

The clinical group consisted of 90 clinically stable outpatients with a diagnosis of schizophrenia according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders − 5th Edition (DSM-5 [1]). Patients had an absence of psychiatric comorbidity and were required to have a minimum period of four weeks without changes in psychopharmacological treatment to ensure clinical stability [52, 53]. Additional exclusion criteria for the clinical group were: a current psychiatric disorder other than schizophrenia (except tobacco or caffeine use disorder), extrapyramidal symptoms with pharmacological origin based on the Simpson-Angus Scale (SAS), intellectual disability (Intelligence Quotient [IQ] < 70, as documented in clinical records), neurological disorder, history of brain injury or severe medical illness, and functional illiteracy. Patients’ diagnostic status and eligibility were confirmed through a multistep clinical evaluation combining medical record review and structured diagnostic interviewing using the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI; [54]). The healthy control comparison group consisted of 100 adults between 18 and 65 years of age. The control group did not have a neurological or mental health pathology in the last 12 months.

Instruments

Sociodemographic and clinical data form

Sociodemographic variables comprised sex, ethnicity, educational level, marital status, living arrangements, employment status, and age. Clinical variables comprised age at illness onset, illness duration, number of hospital admissions, and antipsychotic dose. These variables were assessed using ad hoc questions administered during a structured interview conducted by a trained evaluator.

Clinical symptoms instruments

The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale

(PANSS [55]; Spanish version of Peralta-Martín and Cuesta-Zorita [56]). This scale assesses dimensions of psychotic symptom severity. The PANSS has three subscales measuring positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and general psychopathology. The Spanish version of the PANSS has adequate internal consistency for the different subscales (α = .62 for the positive subscale; α = .92 for the negative subscale; α = .55 for the general subscale; [56]).

The Spanish version of the Simpson-Angus Scale

(SAS [57]; Spanish version of Calvo-Gómez et al. [58]). The SAS was used to assess extrapyramidal symptoms. This scale comprises 10 items to evaluate the presence and severity of Parkinsonian symptoms. This scale has adequate psychometric properties, both in its original version [57] and in its Spanish-language adaptation (α = .93; [58]).

Social cognition measures

The Penn Emotion Recognition Test

(ER-40 [59]). The ER-40 is a SC measure that evaluates emotion recognition. In this measure, participants are asked to identify the corresponding basic emotion (e.g., happiness, sadness, anger, or fear) or neutral expressions displayed in 40 static photographs. They must choose the correct emotion label associated with each face [60]. In the present study, this measure was adapted, and the RT was manipulated. As dependent variables, the correct performance (i.e., the total number of correctly identified emotions), the RT (i.e., time spent to complete each item), and the probability of a correct answer were recorded [53]. Previous studies demonstrate adequate internal consistency for the ER-40 (α = .75 for patients and α = .56 for a control group; [60]), as well as adequate test-retest reliability. The ER-40 was employed due to its robust psychometric properties and extensive use in SC research [51], making it particularly suitable for examining RT patterns at the item level.

The Movie for the Assessment of Social Cognition

(MASC [61]; Spanish validation of Lahera et al. [62]). The MASC is a measure of mentalizing and consists of a 45-minute film featuring four characters meeting for dinner. Participants are required to carefully watch the film and infer the characters’ thoughts and feelings. While watching the film, participants must answer 45 multiple-choice questions about the protagonists’ emotions, thoughts, or intentions. The questions have four answer options, and only one is correct. The measure provides four SC scores: (1) a hit score (i.e., correct description of the character’s mental state) and three types of errors: (2) hypermentalization or a tendency to make excessive attributions about a mental state beyond observable facts, (3) hypomentalization or a deficit in the ability to make attributions about a mental state and (4) non-mentalization, an absence of attribution in the ability to mentalize [62]. This measure was adapted to this study, and the time spent responding to each item (i.e., RT [53]) was recorded. Since each item had a different duration, and the questions varied in difficulty, the evaluator read all the questions aloud and recorded the subjects’ responses immediately after each question, instructing them to respond as thoroughly as possible. The 45 items of the MASC measure were administered in a single session following the validated Spanish protocol. Time scores were calculated from automatically recorded RTs in the task software, expressed in seconds and tenths. Additionally, in accordance with the standard MASC procedure, participants were administered three control questions designed to assess memory and general comprehension of the storyline [61]. These items were used solely to verify that participants were following the plot and were not responding randomly. All participants answered the control questions correctly; therefore, these items were not included as outcome measures and are not reported in the Results section. In the Spanish version, the MASC has optimal psychometric properties (α = .86 and good test–retest reliability [62]). The MASC was used in this research to complement static, performance-based measures (i.e., ER-40) with a dynamic and ecologically valid assessment of mentalizing.

Procedure

Participants with schizophrenia were outpatients from the Príncipe de Asturias University Hospital (Alcalá de Henares, Madrid, Spain). They were recruited using a consecutive sampling method from the outpatient mental health departments of the University Hospital Príncipe de Asturias and associated community clinics. Potential participants were initially identified by their primary treating psychiatrists during regularly scheduled clinical consultations, based on a primary diagnosis of schizophrenia and observed clinical stability.

Prior to study enrollment, all participants received information about the study and the specific measures involved (ER-40 and MASC) from a member of the research team. Following this briefing, interested individuals were given a minimum of 24 hours to review the information sheet before providing written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Following consent, a formal screening process was conducted to verify eligibility. This included: (a) a review of electronic medical records to confirm the DSM-5 diagnosis and a stable pharmacological regimen (no changes for 4 weeks); (b) the administration of the MINI interview to exclude comorbidity; and (c) a brief cognitive assessment to ensure an IQ > 70. Regarding the control group, they were recruited via community advertisements and screened using the MINI interview to exclude psychiatric history.

The patients were evaluated individually by a trained clinical psychologist for approximately 60 minutes at the mental health center. During this interview, participants were required: (1) to sign the informed consent, (2) to answer questions related to sociodemographic and clinical data, and (3) to answer different clinical (e.g., PANSS and SAS) and SC measures (e.g., ER-40 and MASC).

During the ER-40 measure, in the first 20 photographs, participants could respond without a limited RT; hence, they could think about their response for as long as they considered necessary. In the following 20 photographs, participants were instructed to respond as quickly as possible, without sacrificing accuracy [60]. The different instructions provided to participants (i.e., speed or non-speed instructions [without limited RT]) were counterbalanced to minimize fatigue and practice effects across participants. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two presentation orders. In both cases, the ER-40 items—each depicting a facial expression to be recognized—were shown in two blocks: a first time-limited block of 20 items and a second block without an RT limit, in which response latency (in seconds) was recorded. Depending on the assigned order, participants either completed the time-limited block first followed by the non-timed block, or the same two blocks in the reverse order. The speed instruction was included to assess the flexibility of emotion processing and to examine potential speed–accuracy trade-offs. Subsequently, participants completed the MASC measure, during which the evaluator read the questions aloud to ensure comprehension and recorded RTs immediately after each response. The control group underwent the same assessment procedures, except that clinical variables were not recorded. Finally, it is noteworthy that all assessments were conducted in a standardized, quiet clinical setting within the hospital premises to ensure privacy and minimize external distractions, ensuring that the performance and RT data were collected under consistent conditions for all participants. This experimental procedure was the same as that of Ruiz-Toca et al. [53].

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the aforementioned hospital (OE 08/2016). According to the World Medical Association and the Declaration of Helsinki, this study meets the ethical standards of the responsible human experimentation committee.

Data analysis

Before conducting the main statistical analyses, the statistical assumptions required for each test were checked (e.g., normality, homoscedasticity, and screening for potential outliers [none met the exclusion threshold of 3 SD]). Descriptive statistics, including the mean (M) and standard deviation (SD), were calculated for continuous variables. For categorical data, percentages were computed. Multiple comparisons were conducted to examine whether the variables differed between groups (patients vs. controls). Depending on whether the assumptions for parametric analysis were met, either parametric tests (Student’s t test) or non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test) were applied. Additionally, a Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for multiple testing. Effect sizes were estimated with Cohen’s d.

To analyze the effect of the RT on SC performance (as assessed with ER-40 and MASC), Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs) were performed. GLMMs were employed because the outcome variables did not satisfy the assumptions of standard linear models. Furthermore, GLMMs allow the inclusion of both fixed effects (e.g., group-level variables) and random effects (e.g., participant-level variability), thereby accounting for the hierarchical structure of the data, in which multiple item-level responses are nested within individuals, and individuals are nested within groups (patients vs. controls). This approach provides a robust framework for modeling non‑normally distributed outcomes and appropriately handling dependencies arising from repeated measurements.

For the ER‑40, SC performance was examined using two distinct outcome variables. First, the hit rate (correct vs. incorrect) was modeled using a binomial distribution with a logit link function. Second, the emotion response—defined as the emotion category selected by the participant for each item—was treated as a count variable representing one of the five possible emotions and modeled using a Poisson distribution with a log link function. In both ER‑40 models, the fixed predictors were Group (Controls vs. Patients), Emotion displayed, RT per item (in seconds, for items presented in the no‑time‑limit block where RT was recorded), and all possible interactions among these predictors, with a random intercept for participants to account for individual-level variability. Additionally, a separate set of GLMMs was conducted in which RT per item was replaced by the variable time limitation (yes/no) to test our third hypothesis. In all ER-40 analyses, all two‑way and three‑way interaction terms were retained in every model to ensure full comparability across the four model specifications. Because the models differed only in the operationalization of the time variable (continuous vs. binary), maintaining an identical fixed‑effects structure across them allowed the effects of these alternative predictors to be evaluated under equivalent analytical conditions and facilitated direct comparison among models. When the interaction was significant, a Tukey post hoc test was performed.

For the MASC, SC performance was assessed using the total hit score, which was modeled as a Gaussian outcome in a GLMM framework. The fixed predictors included Group, Sex, Age, and RT, along with their two- and three-way interactions, with a random intercept for participants to account for individual-level variability. This hierarchical specification allowed us to test whether the effects of age and RT differed across diagnostic groups. Consistent with recommended practice, interaction terms were evaluated within a pooled model prior to considering any group-stratified analyses. When interactions involving Group were non‑significant, reduced models excluding those terms were compared using hierarchical model comparisons to obtain the most parsimonious specification. Group‑specific slopes describing the association between RT and MASC accuracy were derived from the fixed‑effects structure of the generalized linear mixed model. Marginal trends for each group were estimated using the emtrends function from the emmeans package in R [63], which computes simple slopes based on the model’s fixed‑effect coefficients while holding covariates at their reference values. These slopes, therefore, represent model‑based estimates that account for all predictors included in the GLMM, rather than slopes obtained from separate linear regressions. Finally, because the ER-40 and MASC differ in both task structure and outcome measurement, model specification was tailored to each task. Consequently, the final models included different sets of predictors. All statistical analyses were conducted in R software [64]. To compute GLMMs, the glmm.PQL function of the MASS package was employed [65]. A statistical significance level of p < .05 was used for all inferential tests.

For the Hit Rate outcome, Group, Emotion Displayed, and RT again showed significant main effects, although the magnitude and direction of effects differed from those observed for Emotion Response. The interaction between Group and RT was also significant (t = 4.5, p < .001), and critically, the three‑way interaction Group × Emotion Displayed × RT remained significant (t = -2.1, p = .034). This pattern indicates that the combined effects of diagnostic group, displayed emotion, and item‑level RT substantially shaped accuracy performance on the ER-40, rendering the interpretation of lower‑order effects conditional on this higher‑order interaction. In the ER-40 analyses, all interaction terms were preserved across the four GLMMs (see Table 3).

Figure 1 shows the RT per emotion and group. In both groups, happiness was identified the fastest, whereas sadness and fear were identified the slowest. Patients were quicker in identifying anger over fear and sadness, a difference not seen in the healthy group, which did not display differences between negative emotions.

Hypothesis 3: Influence of the type of instructions (i.e., speed instructions or non-speed instructions) on the probability of a correct answer in recognizing each basic emotion

Across models using the full ER-40 dataset, the presence or absence of a time limit had no measurable impact on either response variable—Emotion Response or Hit Rate (see Table 4). For Emotion Response, the main effect of Time Limited was non‑significant (t = 1.62, p = .104), and none of the interactions involving this factor reached significance. The same pattern was observed for Hit Rate, where Time Limited again showed no effect (t = .10, p = .917), and all interaction terms, including Group × Time Limited (t = .17, p = .864) and Emotion Displayed × Time Limited (t = -.51, p = .607), were non‑significant. These findings indicate that imposing an RT constraint did not influence how participants classified emotions nor their likelihood of responding correctly, and this null effect remained consistent across diagnostic groups and emotion categories.

Hypothesis 4: Performance and response time in MASC

As shown in Table 2, patients showed significantly lower MASC scores than controls in the initial group comparison (p < .001; d = -1.126). There were no statistically significant differences between the groups in hypermentalization. In contrast, the patient group had significantly more hypomentalization and non-mentalization errors. Additionally, in comparison with the healthy control group (M = 9.8s, SD = 2.9), patients required a longer RT to respond to the different items of the MASC (M = 13.6s, SD = 4.7 s), with statistically significant differences between groups (p < .001, d = .95).

After removing non-significant interaction terms, the final GLMM retained Group as a significant main effect, indicating that patients scored substantially lower on the MASC than controls even after adjusting for Age, Gender, and RT (see Table 5). Both Age and RT showed significant negative associations with accuracy, and their interaction approached significance (t = 1.86; p = .064). Importantly, no Group × Predictor interactions were supported in the pooled model. Nevertheless, model‑derived simple slopes indicated that the negative association between RT and accuracy was descriptively stronger in patients (slope = -.497, 95% CI [-.755, -.239]) than in controls (slope = -.135, 95% CI [-.540, .271]). This pattern, illustrated in Fig. 2, is consistent with the visual impression of steeper time‑related declines in patients.

Discussion

This study examined the performance and RT across two important dimensions of SC (i.e., emotion recognition and mentalizing) in a sample of patients with schizophrenia and healthy controls. Compared to healthy controls, patients showed poorer performance on all SC measures and longer RT. Speed instructions in an emotion recognition measure were not associated with the number of correct answers or making a specific error, either in the patients or in the control group. Both groups showed faster recognition of happiness and longer RTs to identify sadness and fear. Patients with schizophrenia recognized anger faster than fear or sadness, whereas there were no differences between negative emotions in the control group. Across the entire sample, there was a statistically significant and inverse association between RT and the probability of a correct answer in both emotion recognition and mentalizing measures.

In line with Hypothesis 1, compared to the control group, patients with schizophrenia demonstrated poorer emotion recognition. This result is consistent with previous research [18, 20, 24, 43, 66]. Additionally, both groups identified happiness faster, but it took longer to identify sadness and fear. In this study, patients with schizophrenia recognized anger faster than fear or sadness, whereas in the control group, there were no differences between negative emotions. This is consistent with the fact that patients with schizophrenia are more sensitive to certain negative emotions (e.g., fear [43] or anger [23]). Furthermore, this aligns with the anger superiority effect and threat salience found in some studies [67]. It may therefore reflect automatic, preconscious threat detection processes that may remain intact in schizophrenia [68]. However, some studies indicate that impairment in emotion recognition depends on the emotion domain rather than on specific emotions [69]. On the other hand, in contrast to our findings, Rubin et al. [43] found that, after controlling for cognitive function, patients with schizophrenia were slower to respond to specific emotions such as happy, sad, and surprise than the control group. This divergence may reflect differences in measure characteristics and analytic approaches, as RT indexes multiple underlying processes and is a complex construct beyond pure processing speed [43]. Thus, RT should not be interpreted as a direct proxy of a single cognitive mechanism, but rather as a composite index influenced by multiple processing stages (e.g., perceptual processing, decision-making, response output [37, 38]).

Regarding Hypothesis 2, the results of this study support it. Although patients generally showed a weaker association between longer RT and higher accuracy than controls, this relationship was not consistent across emotions. The pattern of results suggests that the impact of response latency on performance varies depending on the emotion being processed, as discussed in the previous paragraph. Previous research has reported emotion-specific alterations in schizophrenia, particularly for fear, reflecting differential processing of negative emotional stimuli [40]. Importantly, these emotion-specific differences are also evident in intervention studies, as evidence from training studies suggests that SC interventions may improve recognition of some facial emotions (e.g., sad, fearful, or neutral faces), whereas training does not improve recognition of angry faces [70]. In addition, the weaker association between RT and accuracy in patients is compatible with a more superficial processing of emotional information and with reported difficulties in executive function [71], particularly in regulating response speed during task performance.

Contrary to Hypothesis 3, speed instructions in the ER-40 measure were not associated with the number of correct answers in either the patient or the control group. This is consistent with previous studies that used speed instructions in SC measures [50]. Other manipulations to make SC measures more difficult in these patients may be more efficacious [50]. It is worth noting that the lack of effect of the speed instruction does not contradict the general finding that RT, analyzed at the item level, is related to accuracy in emotion recognition (Hypothesis 2). Taken together, our results suggest that processing time is associated with emotion recognition accuracy; however, this relationship likely reflects multiple underlying processes and cannot be explained solely by neuropsychological factors such as processing speed [50]. While patients with schizophrenia were significantly slower on both the ER-40 and the MASC, item-level RTs were recorded and included as predictors in the GLMM analyses. Evidence from a companion study using the same sample [53] showed that processing speed was significantly associated with both accuracy and RT on these SC measures in the schizophrenia group, suggesting that neurocognitive slowing contributes to multiple aspects of task performance. Nevertheless, the persistence of accuracy deficits on SC measures after accounting for differences in RT suggests that emotion recognition impairments in schizophrenia may reflect difficulties that are not fully explained by general psychomotor slowing alone. Difficulties in emotion recognition may be more related to deficits in the integration of emotional information [45], which might occur in early stages of processing [72]. To reduce difficulties in emotion recognition, patients with schizophrenia may benefit from more time to process emotional stimuli/information (e.g., by slowly exposing emotional expressions) rather than increasing the task duration or exposure time. This is consistent with findings that patients require a longer exposure time to the stimulus to form the initial impression of threat [45]. Patel et al. [70] implemented a SC training program to improve emotion recognition in schizophrenia. Although the intervention improved emotion recognition accuracy, it did not improve response speed. This dissociation suggests that RT may be sensitive to residual inefficiencies in social-cognitive processing that are not fully addressed by current rehabilitation treatment approaches. Consequently, future research should consider not only the assessment of RT but also the refinement of intervention strategies aimed at improving response speed. Additionally, Scoriels et al. [73] found that both auditory and visual neuroscience-informed cognitive training were effective at reducing RT in emotional recognition and improving performance on a theory of mind test. This suggests that focusing on multisensory integration in patients with schizophrenia may enhance cognitive functioning [74], and this is also likely to be addressed in future research.

Regarding MASC performance, patients with schizophrenia, in line with Hypothesis 4, not only showed poorer performance in this measure but also displayed a longer RT. The impairment observed on this mentalizing measure is consistent with previous literature [7, 8, 10, 31, 32]. Previous studies also document that patients with schizophrenia display a longer RT in different mentalizing measures [47, 48]. However, to our knowledge, no prior studies have analyzed the RT in the MASC measure. In the present study, we found a significant negative association between RT and the number of correct answers in MASC measures. However, compared to the control group, this association was more pronounced for patients. This pattern may reflect alterations in the most basic, rapid, and spontaneous mechanisms of SC, which could activate slower, cognitive, and “deliberative” mechanisms [75]. In the MASC measure, prolonged RTs may reflect greater difficulty integrating complex social information, although the present design cannot determine the specific cognitive mechanisms involved. Given that successful mentalizing in everyday social interactions often requires rapid interpretation of unfolding social information, prolonged response in the MASC may be particularly indicative of reduced processing efficiency in real-world mentalizing situations.

Taken together, the results of this study are consistent with the notion that RT provides information about temporal aspects of social-cognitive processing that may not be fully reflected by accuracy measures alone. Accordingly, RT, along with accuracy, should be considered as a core outcome in SC measures. This highlights the novelty of examining item-level RT within subjects across two SC domains (i.e., emotion recognition and mentalizing), providing an opportunity for direct cross-domain comparisons and contributing to a more ecologically informed assessment of social-cognitive functioning, which has rarely been addressed in previous research.

The findings of the present study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. Participants were predominantly men, largely middle-aged, and characterized by limited ethnic diversity, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Specifically, the homogeneity of the entirely white sample may introduce unmeasured bias, since there is evidence of cultural variation in emotion recognition [76]. Although age and sex have been associated with SC performance in previous studies [18, 77, 78], these variables did not improve model fit in the present sample and were therefore not retained in the final ER-40 models. Future studies should continue to examine their potential contribution. Patients with schizophrenia were receiving pharmacological treatment, which could have influenced their task performance. However, the enhancing effect of antipsychotics on SC measures is unclear [79], and there is evidence suggesting that their cognitive effects may vary across domains, medications, and individuals [80]. However, conducting the study in patients without antipsychotic treatment would pose significant ethical concerns. On the other hand, the absence of direct measures of relevant cognitive domains, including processing speed, executive functioning, attention, and working memory, precluded determining the extent to which these processes contributed to performance and RTs on SC measures. Although an IQ < 70 exclusion criterion was applied in the clinical group based on clinical records, no equivalent assessment was conducted in the control group, which may have limited comparability between groups. In addition, the absence of a standardized, directly administered measure of intellectual functioning represents a further limitation. Importantly, the IQ exclusion criterion does not account for the specific contribution of processing speed, which is empirically distinct from general intellectual ability and is among the most consistently impaired cognitive domains in schizophrenia [81]. Another limitation of the present study is that, although the relationship between processing speed and accuracy-based performance on SC measures (ER-40 and MASC) has been previously examined, its potential influence on RTs on these measures was not modeled within the current analytic framework. Evidence from a previous study based on the same sample [53] indicates that processing speed is associated with faster responding on both the ER-40 and the MASC in individuals with schizophrenia, suggesting that this relationship warrants further investigation. Future studies should consider jointly modeling accuracy and RT as complementary outcomes to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the role of processing speed in SC performance in schizophrenia. Moreover, no independent measures of baseline processing speed were included, which may limit the extent to which general slowing effects can be disentangled from task-specific processes. The cross-sectional design also limits conclusions regarding the directionality of the associations observed between RT and SC performance. Finally, it should be noted that emotion recognition was assessed in a clinical setting, which may limit external validity. Future studies should include, along with experimental measures of SC, research methodologies closer to the daily life of these patients (e.g., ecological momentary assessment [82]). Furthermore, future research may benefit from incorporating emerging technologies [83], which have the potential to enhance SC measures by enabling more dynamic, interactive, and ecologically valid stimuli that better approximate real-world social interactions.

Although this study is not without limitations, it addresses several methodological constraints of previous research by examining the temporal dynamics of social-cognitive performance across both static and dynamic SC measures across different SC domains. By combining RT measures with within-subject comparisons between ER-40 and MASC, the present findings extend existing knowledge beyond accuracy-based assessments and contribute to a more ecologically grounded understanding of social-cognitive functioning in schizophrenia.

Although this is not an intervention study, the present study’s findings may have implications for the development of cognitive remediation programs for schizophrenia. Since RT reflects temporal aspects of task performance that are not fully captured by accuracy measures alone, this variable should be measured as an outcome variable in SC measures. This could improve the ecological validity of SC assessment. In fact, in the ER-40 measure, RT is measured and considered a predictor of social competence [60]. However, RT has not been considered as a key variable in the MASC measure. Based on our study’s results, we suggest recording the RT for each item in SC measures such as the MASC, as this could be highly informative. Additionally, in a clinical setting, we recommend assessing several SC domains and considering analyses of RT at both the item and domain levels. Intervention programs could also include strategies to improve the reduced RT in these patients. This could have important implications for the socio-occupational integration of these patients [84]. RT may help differentiate between instinctive, rapid, and spontaneous social judgments and more elaborate, logical, and deliberative ones. SC-based therapies could potentially enhance both strategies simultaneously, likely engaging different neurobiological substrates.

Conclusions

In conclusion, patients with schizophrenia not only show poorer performance in emotion recognition and mentalizing but are also slower in measures evaluating these domains of SC. Including speed instructions in an emotion recognition measure does not seem to influence the performance of patients with schizophrenia. Performance and RT both play a complementary role in characterizing social-cognitive functioning by capturing accuracy-based outcomes and temporal processing dynamics. Incorporating RT measures and dynamic paradigms may enhance the ecological validity of SC assessment in schizophrenia. Although the present study was not designed to test treatment effects, these findings may inform future research aimed at refining intervention approaches. Specifically, rehabilitation protocols for these patients may consider incorporating strategies to improve their performance and RT in emotion recognition and mentalizing measures. Finally, future research should consider item-level RT and cross-domain RT–accuracy analyses to further refine the assessment of SC in schizophrenia.
