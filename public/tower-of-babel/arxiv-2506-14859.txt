A note on Polya urns: the winner may lead all the time Thanks: Partly supported by the Knut and Alice Wallenberg Foundation
Svante Janson

Abstract




We consider a Pólya urn where, as in Pólya’s original urn

[
2
;
3
]
,
a ball always is replaced together with additional balls of the same colour
only; however, we allow the urn to be unfair in the sense that the number
of additional balls may depend on the colour.
To be precise, the urn contains balls of
q
colours; we draw a ball
(uniformly at random) and if the drawn ball has colour
i
, we replace it
together with
m_{i}
additional balls of colour
i
, for some given (fixed)
integers
m_{i}>0
.
We assume that the urn start with some set of balls, containing at
least one ball of each colour.

It is well-known, and easy to see
by the argument below, that if there is a colour
i
such that
m_{i}>m_{j}
for
all colours
j\neq i
, then the proportion of balls of colour
i
tends to 1
a.s.
Of course, even if colour 1, say, wins eventually, it is possible that
colour 2 is lucky initially, and that at some time
n
, most balls are
of colour 2.
The purpose of this note is to show that this happens with probability
strictly less than 1, provided it does not occur already in the initial
position.
Moreover, this extends to the case when some or all of the numbers
m_{i}
are
equal, say with
m_{1}=m_{2}\geqslant m_{3}\geqslant\dots
; in
this case, the proportion of balls of colour 1 converges to some random limit
in
(0,1)
, and the probability that colour 1 eventually dominates lies
strictly between 0 and 1; we show that there is also in this case a positive
probability that colour 1 dominates at all times, if it does so initially.

To be precise, we have the following
theorem, where we for simplicity consider two colours only.
(The proof is simple and this has presumably been observed before, but we do
not know a reference and therefore give a complete proof.)

Theorem 1
.

Consider a Pólya urn with balls of two colours, black and white,
with the replacement rule that if a ball of colour
i\in\{\mathsf{b},\mathsf{w}\}
is
drawn, it is replaced together with
m_{i}

additional balls of the same colour, for some given positive integers

m_{\mathsf{b}},m_{\mathsf{w}}
.
Let
B_{n}
and
W_{n}
be the numbers of black and white balls after
n
draws,
with initial conditions
B_{0}=b_{0}
and
W_{0}=w_{0}
for some given
b_{0},w_{0}
.
Suppose that
m_{\mathsf{b}}\geqslant m_{\mathsf{w}}
and that
b_{0}>w_{0}\geqslant 0
.
Then there is a positive probability that
B_{n}>W_{n}
for all
n\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

We use the standard method of embedding the urn process into a continuous
time Markov branching process, see e.g.
[
1
, Section V.9]
; we give
each ball an exponential clock, that rings after a random time with the
distribution Exp(1). When the clock rings at a ball of colour
i
,
the ball is replaced by
m_{i}+1
new balls of the same colour
i
, each with
a new clock. (All clocks are independent.)
Then the original urn process is the same (i.e., has the same distribution)
as this continuous time process observed at the times some clock rings.
More formally,
let
\overline{B}_{t}
and
\overline{W}_{t}
be the number of black and white balls at time
t\geqslant 0
,
and let
\tau_{n}
be the time the
n
-th clock rings. Then, the process

(\overline{B}_{\tau_{n}},\overline{W}_{\tau_{n}})_{n\geqslant 0}
has the same distribution as the Pólya
urn process, and we may assume
B_{n}=\overline{B}_{\tau_{n}}
,
W_{n}=\overline{W}_{\tau_{n}}
.

In the continuous time process, the black and white balls act independently,
so
\overline{B}_{t}
and
\overline{W}_{t}
are two
independent continuous-time branching processes.
Hence, see
[
1
, Theorems III.7.1 and III.7.2]
,
there exist independent positive
random variables
Y_{\mathsf{b}},Y_{\mathsf{w}}
such that a.s.

\displaystyle e^{-m_{\mathsf{b}}t}\overline{B}_{t}

\displaystyle\to Y_{\mathsf{b}}>0,

(1)

\displaystyle e^{-m_{\mathsf{w}}t}\overline{W}_{t}

\displaystyle\to Y_{\mathsf{w}}>0.

(2)

Consequently, as
{t\to\infty}
, a.s.,

e^{(m_{\mathsf{b}}-m_{\mathsf{w}})t}\frac{\overline{W}_{t}}{\overline{B}_{t}}\to\frac{Y_{\mathsf{w}}}{Y_{\mathsf{b}}}<\infty,

(3)

and thus

\frac{\overline{W}_{t}}{\overline{B}_{t}}\to Z:=\begin{cases}0,&m_{\mathsf{b}}>m_{\mathsf{w}},\\
Y_{\mathsf{w}}/Y_{\mathsf{b}},&m_{\mathsf{b}}=m_{\mathsf{w}}.\end{cases}

(4)

As a consequence, as
{n\to\infty}
, a.s.,

\frac{W_{n}}{B_{n}}=\frac{\overline{W}_{\tau_{n}}}{\overline{B}_{\tau_{n}}}\to Z.

(5)

Furthermore, both
Y_{\mathsf{b}}
and
Y_{\mathsf{w}}
have support on the entire positive
half-axis. Consequently,
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(Y_{\mathsf{w}}<Y_{\mathsf{b}})>0
and thus
(
4
) implies

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(Z<1)>0
for any
m_{\mathsf{b}}
and
m_{\mathsf{w}}
.
Hence, (
5
) implies

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\limsup_{n\to\infty}W_{n}/B_{n}<1\bigr)\geqslant\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(Z<1)>0.

(6)

It follows that there exists an integer
N
such that

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(W_{n}/B_{n}<1\text{ for all $n\geqslant N$}\bigr)>0.

(7)

There is only a finite number of possible outcomes of
(B_{N},W_{N})
, and
consequently (
7
) implies that there are integers
(b_{N},w_{N})
such
that

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl((B_{N},W_{N})=(b_{N},w_{N})\text{ and }W_{n}/B_{n}<1\text{ for all $n\geqslant N$}\bigr)>0

(8)

and consequently,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}

\displaystyle\bigl((B_{N},W_{N})=(b_{N},w_{N})\bigr)>0,

(9)

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}

\displaystyle\bigl(W_{n}/B_{n}<1\text{ for all $n\geqslant N$}\mid(B_{N},W_{N})=(b_{N},w_{N})\bigr)>0.

(10)

By (
9
), it is possible to reach
(b_{N},w_{N})
by some sequence of
N

draws, starting at
(b_{0},w_{0})
; thus
b_{N}=b_{0}+k_{\mathsf{b}}m_{\mathsf{b}}

and
w_{N}=w_{0}+k_{\mathsf{w}}m_{\mathsf{w}}
, where
k_{\mathsf{b}}+k_{\mathsf{w}}=N
.

Consider now the event that
we draw a black ball in the first
k_{\mathsf{b}}

draws, and then a white ball in the following
k_{\mathsf{w}}
draws;
this evidently has positive probability.
Furthermore, then
(B_{N},W_{N})=(b_{N},w_{N})
. Moreover, in this case,
for
0\leqslant n\leqslant N
,

B_{n}-W_{n}
first
increases and then decreases, and since
B_{0}-W_{0}=b_{0}-w_{0}>0

and
B_{N}-W_{N}=b_{N}-w_{N}>0
, we have
B_{n}-W_{n}>0
for every
n\leqslant N
.

Since the urn process is a Markov process, it is by (
10
) possible to
continue, with positive probability, so that
B_{n}>W_{n}
also for all
n\geqslant N
.
Hence, with positive probability,
B_{n}>W_{n}
for all
n\geqslant 0
.
∎

Remark 2
.

The theorem, and its proof, readily extends to any (finite) number of
colours. Several versions are possible.
For example, if we assume that
m_{1}\geqslant m_{j}
for every colour
j\neq 1
, and a
majority of the balls at time 0 are of colour 1, then there is a positive
probability that a majority has colour 1 at every time
n
.
Alternatively,
still assuming that
m_{1}\geqslant m_{j}
for every colour
j\neq 1
, if a
plurality (relative majority) of the balls at time 0 are of colour 1,
i.e., the number of balls of colour 1 is larger than the number of any other
given colour, then there is a positive
probability that a plurality has colour 1 at every time
n
.
We leave the details, and further variations, to the reader.
