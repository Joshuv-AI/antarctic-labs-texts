Clinical epidemiology, determinants, and outcomes of severe viral encephalitis in Chinese children: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Qun Li, Yang Chen, Yibing Yan, Zibo Fan, Xueli Li, Zhe Wang, Xiangna Yang, Danning Wen, Huazhang Liu, Yukun Zhang, Qiaozhi Yang, Ying Li, Chaolin Huang, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn China, there were few studies about the etiology, outcome and disease burden of severe viral encephalitis (VE) in recent years. The aims of this study were to characterize the etiology, prognosis and identify the factors predicting severe VE in Chinese children.MethodsA multicenter retrospective cohort study was conducted at six hospitals from 2015 to 2021 in China. The clinical and outcome data were collected during patients’ hospitalization. We analyzed the epidemiologic characteristics and disease burden of hospitalized children (≤ 14 years old) with VE in China. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify the factors predicting severe VE.ResultsIn total, 801 episodes of VE were included. There were 272 females and 529 males among the included patients, with a mean age of 4.81 ± 3.46 years. There were 29 cases diagnosed severe VE, with an incidence of 3.6% (29/801). Among the severe cases, Enterovirus (EV) 55.2% (16/29) was the main pathogen. A total of 11 (1.4%) in-hospital deaths occurred, and 32 (4.0%) patients had the sequelae. The incidence of sequelae, the number of patients receiving treatment in the ICU, and the number of deceased patients were all higher in the severe VE group. After accounting for circularity between severity definition and predictors, significant factors for severe VE were abnormal brain imaging and EV infection. Coma was a defining criterion and not considered an independent predictor.ConclusionThis study will help understand the clinical epidemiology, prognosis and disease burden of hospitalized children with severe VE in China and offer useful information for identifying high-risk patients who are susceptible to poor prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13504-2.

Introduction

Encephalitis is a serious form of neurologic disease with inflammation of the brain parenchyma [1]. More than 100 infectious, post-infectious, and immune-mediated conditions can cause encephalitis, and improving its diagnosis and management is a major challenge [2, 3]. Infection is one of the most common causes of encephalitis, and viral encephalitis (VE) is a global disease [4]. According to a North American report, VE accounts for approximately 60% of infectious encephalitis [5]. In China, children are the main population of VE outbreaks. However, the etiology of about half of the patients are unclear, which poses a huge public health challenge [6].

Severe VE is associated with substantial mortality in China, especially in resource-limited regions that lack a nationwide surveillance registry. Current treatment regimens for severe VE include antimicrobial agents, anti-inflammatory drugs, steroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and supportive care, but treatment outcomes are suboptimal [7]. Severe VE is characterized by clinical manifestations such as coma, cerebral ischemia, and cerebral edema, accompanied by pathological changes such as cerebral tissue congestion, lymphocytic infiltration, and focal demyelination in the brain. The absence of specific treatment measures often results in long-term neurological and cognitive sequelae in patients with severe VE [8, 9]. To date, few studies have comprehensively characterized the clinical epidemiology, outcomes, disease burden, and risk factors of severe VE in China [10, 11].

This multicenter retrospective study therefore aimed to assess the clinical epidemiology, prognosis, and disease burden of patients with VE in China, and to identify the risk factors for severe VE, in order to provide valuable information for recognizing high-risk patients at risk of poor prognosis.

Methods

Patients

A total of 870 children with viral encephalitis hospitalized from January 6, 2015 to June 4, 2021 were included in this multicenter retrospective study. The six medical institutions were Beijing Ditan Hospital, Liaocheng People’s Hospital, Tianjin Second People’s Hospital, Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center, The First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University, Wuhan Jinyintan Hospital. Inclusion criteria: (a) Age between 0 and 14 years old; (b) Diagnosis of VE consistent with the consensus statement of the International Encephalitis Consortium (IEC). Exclusion criteria: (a) Patients with comorbidities of other encephalitis such as autoimmune and bacterial encephalitis; (b) Missing data were more than 5% of the total medical records.; (c) Patients with comorbidities of other neurological disorders; (d) Patients lost to follow-up.

Definition

VE was diagnosed in accordance with the consensus criteria proposed by the International Encephalitis Consortium, which consists of three tiers of classification (possible, probable, and confirmed) based on the combination of core criteria [12]. Major criterion (required): Altered mental status (including decreased level of consciousness, personality changes, or lethargy) lasting for at least 24 h, with no alternative cause identified; or new-onset epileptic seizures. Minor criteria (6 items total): Fever ≥ 38℃; new seizures; new focal neurologic symptoms; cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) white blood cell count ≥ 5 × 10⁶/L; neuroimaging showing brain parenchymal abnormalities suggestive of encephalitis; electroencephalogram (EEG) showing abnormalities consistent with encephalitis.

Diagnostic classification: Possible encephalitis (Fulfillment of the Major criterion plus ≥ 2 Minor criteria); Probable encephalitis (Fulfillment of the Major criterion plus ≥ 3 Minor criteria); Confirmed encephalitis (Fulfillment of the criteria for probable encephalitis plus at least one confirmatory finding (positive viral nucleic acid in CSF, or positive antiviral IgM antibodies in CSF and/or serum; or pathological confirmation of brain inflammation consistent with encephalitis).

For patients who did not undergo lumbar puncture (LP) due to clinical contraindications, including severe intracranial hypertension, coagulopathy, or patient refusal, the diagnosis was confirmed via comprehensive assessments, combined with typical clinical manifestations of encephalitis, neuroimaging findings, EEG abnormalities, and serological test results.

Severe VE refers to cases of encephalitis with severe clinical manifestations, such as prolonged coma, frequent limb convulsions, opisthotonos, and central respiratory failure requiring ventilator-assisted breathing. Severe VE meets one of the following criteria: (1) Frequent convulsions or status epilepticus; (2) Consciousness disturbance with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score < 8; (3) Limb paralysis or neurobehavioral abnormalities; (4) Brainstem symptoms; (5) Severe or persistent intracranial hypertension; (6) Multiple organ impairment progressing to failure.

Etiological detection of viral encephalitis

The etiological agents of VE were identified by serology or CSF immunoglobulin G (IgG) and immunoglobulin M (IgM) and blood sample polymerase chain reaction (PCR). All centers adopted a unified protocol: the Class III Fourteen-item Central Nervous System Infection Pathogen Nucleic Acid Detection Kit (Sino Biotech Shanghai Jenny Bio-Technology), and the Viral Encephalitis Antibody Combined Detection Kit (produced by Autobio Diagnostics Co., Ltd.) for IgG/IgM detection of target viruses. No major updates to reagents, equipment, or procedures occurred during 2015–2021. Regular quality control (batch-specific internal positive/negative controls) ensured consistent PCR sensitivity and specificity. The viruses detected in this study include: Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), mumps virus (MuV), varicella-zoster virus (VZV), herpes simplex virus (HSV), measles virus, rubella virus, enterovirus 71 (EV71), coxsackievirus A16 (CA16), coxsackievirus A10 (CA10), coxsackievirus A6 (CA6), influenza virus (Flu), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), and cytomegalovirus (CMV).

Data collection

Prior to data collection, this study developed a standardized Case Report Form (CRF) and a corresponding cloud-based database. After receiving unified training, researchers from 6 participating centers extracted data from Electronic Medical Records (EMRs) within their respective hospitals’ Hospital Information System (HIS) and entered it into the cloud-based database. All demographic and clinical parameters were extracted by three certified infectious disease specialists with more than 5 years of clinical experience by reviewing EMRs. Additionally, a two-step verification process involving initial extraction and independent validation was implemented in accordance with the standardized CRF to ensure data accuracy.

The following data were systematically collected: demographic and epidemiological characteristics, clinical signs and symptoms, results of routine cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis (including protein, glucose, and chloride levels), neuroimaging findings, length of hospital stay, and in-hospital complications.

Follow-up

All participants underwent a 6-month post-discharge follow-up conducted via telephone interviews and outpatient follow-up visits, during which the study outcomes were primarily assessed at outpatient follow-up visits after discharge. Trained research personnel collected all study data using standardized case report forms that were subsequently uploaded to a secure cloud-based electronic database for centralized storage, management, and analytical processing. The primary outcome was the sequelae which included cognitive impairment, secondary seizures, ataxia, hearing impairment, and hemiparesis. The secondary outcome indicator was death. Other observations were duration of headache, duration of fever, hospital costs.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons between the two groups were made using the chi-square test (percentages and values) in case of categorical variables. T-test (mean ± standard deviations) and Mann-Whitney U test (median ± quartiles) were used for continuous variables. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis were used to analyze risk factors associated with the development of severe VE.

Variables for the multivariable model were selected based on statistical significance in univariate analysis (P < 0.05). Statistics and analysis of data were performed using SPSS 22 software and GraphPad 7 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) software.

Results

Etiology and clinical characteristics of patients with VE

After screening, 29 cases with incomplete data, 28 cases of bacterial meningitis, 7 cases of autoimmune encephalitis, 5 cases of unclear diagnosis were excluded. A total of 801 patients with viral encephalitis were included in this study (Fig. 1). Enterovirus (EV) was detected in 145 patients (18.1%), including EV71 (63 cases), CA16 (57 cases), CA10 (15 cases), and CA6 (10 cases). Other viruses detected included HSV in 78 (9.7%), JEV in 76 (9.5%), EBV in 62 (7.7%), Flu in 62 (7.7%), MuV in 22 (2.7%), measles virus in 4 (0.5%), and rubella virus in 4 (0.5%) patients, while no pathogen was identified in the remaining 348 (43.4%) patients (Fig. 2). According to the diagnostic criteria of the IEC, all enrolled patients were classified into confirmed, probable, and possible VE. The classification and percentages are summarized in Supplementary Table (1) For patients with confirmed VE, the etiological evidence was further categorized by CSF PCR, CSF IgM, and serum IgM results, as detailed in Supplementary Table (2) The diagnostic classification of viral encephalitis and supporting criteria for all pediatric minors are presented in Supplementary Table (3) There were 272 females and 529 males, with a mean age of 4.81 ± 3.46 years. The median interval from symptom onset to diagnosis of VE in the overall cohort was 5.45 days (IQR 3.10–10.23). The time to diagnosis differed significantly among patients with possible, probable, and proven viral encephalitis (P < 0.001). Patients with proven VE had the shortest median interval (4.20 days, IQR 2.40–6.80), followed by those with probable VE (5.50 days, IQR 3.20–9.10). In contrast, patients with possible VE had the longest diagnostic delay, with a median interval of 8.10 days (IQR 4.50–11.20). Detailed data are presented in Supplementary Table 4.

Among enrolled patients, 65.5% (525/801) underwent lumbar puncture, and increased CSF nucleated cell counts were detected in 79.4% (417/525) cases. In our study, not only severely ill patients but also non-severe patients had imaging. Brain imaging was done in 498 (301 CT vs. 197 MRI) of 801 cases prior to treatment. The prevalence of severe VE

In this study, there were 29 cases diagnosed severe VE, with an incidence of 3.6% (29/801). The incidence was 2.3% (9/380) in 2015, 1.5% (2/136) in 2016, 0% (0/35) in 2017, 0% (0/50) in 2018, 12.4% (13/105) in 2019, 4.3% (3/70) in 2020, and 8% (2/25) in 2021 (Fig. 4). Among severe cases, 55.2% (16/29) were EV, 6.9% (2/29) HSV, 1.4% (1/29) EBV, 1.4% (1/29) JEV, 1.4% (1/29) rubella virus, and the other etiologies were not clear. Among EV cases, there were 11 cases of EV71, 3cases of CA16, 1 case of CA10, and 1 case of CA6. During follow-up or at hospital discharge, 4 children developed secondary seizures.

Comparison of baseline characteristics between severe and non-severe VE

The baseline characteristics of the VE patients were shown in Table 1. There were more female patients in severe VE group. The results showed that more patients in the severe VE group had coma, fever and fatigue symptoms compared to the non-severe VE (p < 0.05). In addition, there were more patients with EEG and imaging abnormalities in severe group (p < 0.05). Furthermore, EV infection was higher in patients with severe group (p < 0.001). The laboratory test results of VE patients were shown in Table 2. There was no significant difference in WBC, N%, LY%, PLT, CRP, HGB, LDH, CK, CK-MB, sodium K+ and Na+, Cl−, ALT, AST, TBIL, ALB. In contrast, the differences in RBC, PCT, sodium Ca2+ and UREA were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Notably, despite the viral etiology of encephalitis, patients with severe VE had significantly higher PCT levels compared with non-severe VE patients, whereas CRP levels did not differ between groups.

Patient outcome

In this study, a total of 32 children experienced different sequelae during the follow-up period, including seizures (n = 15), cognitive deficits (n = 7), motor deficits (n = 5), hearing impairment (n = 3) and visual impairment (n = 2). As shown in Table 3, more VE patients in the severe group developed sequelae compared to the non-severe group (31.0% vs. 3.0%, p < 0.001). Furthermore, more VE patients in the severe group died compared to the non-severe group (17.0% vs. 0.8%, p < 0.001). 8 pediatric patients were admitted to the ICU for further supportive treatment, and the length of stay in the ICU was 107.95 ± 106.98 days, including 4 severe patients and 4 non-severe patients (13.8% vs. 0.5%, p < 0.001). After multivariate correction, the results showed that abnormal brain imaging (OR = 7.583, 95% CI: 2.420–23.756, p = 0.001) and EV (OR = 4.316, 95% CI: 1.101–16.913, p = 0.036), were strong predictors for the occurrence of severe VE. However, coma was a component of the severe VE definition, which introduces potential circularity; thus, it should be interpreted as a severity criterion rather than an independent predictive factor. Caution is warranted in interpreting the regression results due to the relatively small number of severe cases, which may increase the risk of model overfitting and result in wide 95% confidence intervals, thereby reducing the precision of the estimates.

Discussion

VE is a major cause of pediatric neurological morbidity in China, with multiple pathogens implicated. The most common causative agents vary by region, season, and vaccination coverage. We have definitively shown that the leading causes of child hood VE were EV, HSV, JEV. HSV encephalitis is the most frequent sporadic infectious encephalitis in high-income countries, such as France, England, Spain and USA, while EV was the leading cause in China [13–16]. EV was the most common cause of VE in Chinese children, especially in southern China [17]. Japanese encephalitis remains endemic in rural China, particularly in southern rice-farming regions, with seasonal peaks during summer and autumn, though nationwide vaccination has reduced cases by over 90% since 2008 [15]. Viral CNS infections are challenging to diagnose despite the advances in diagnostic techniques. PCR of CSF remains gold standard but may be inaccessible in resource-limited settings. In our study, the pathogen was not identified in 43.4% case. The diagnosis of cases with unclear etiology was made based on a combination of patients’ clinical manifestations, serological test results, and cranial imaging findings.

In the present study, we classified VE into possible, probable, and proven categories strictly according to the International Encephalitis Consortium guidelines. Possible VE was defined as fulfillment of 1 major criterion plus at least 2 minor criteria, whereas probable VE required 1 major criterion plus at least 3 minor criteria. Confirmed VE was determined by laboratory confirmation, including: (1) positive CSF PCR in 265 cases; (2) positive CSF IgM in 110 cases; positive serum IgM in 78 cases. This standardized classification ensured consistent and reliable case definition across all six participating hospitals. Our study demonstrated that patients with proven VE had the shortest interval from symptom onset to diagnosis, highlighting the importance of timely and complete virological testing for early confirmation of VE.

Notably, delayed diagnosis may increase the risk of severe disease and long-term neurological sequelae in VE. In our cohort, the median interval from symptom onset to VE diagnosis was 5.45 days (IQR 3.10–10.23), with significant differences across diagnostic categories (Supplementary Table 4). Patients with possible VE, who often lacked lumbar puncture, EEG, or neuroimaging, had longer diagnostic delays compared with those with proven VE. Timely performance of confirmatory tests could shorten diagnostic intervals, enable earlier intervention, and potentially reduce the progression to severe VE and permanent neurological deficits.

VE has been increasingly recognized as a prominent cause of severe acute encephalopathy in children in China [18–21]. Severe pediatric encephalitis results in persistently high mortality in China, especially in resource-constrained regions due to the absence of a nationwide registry system. Our study revealed that 3.6% of pediatric patients developed severe VE. EV infection was the main etiology, underscoring the critical need for early recognition and targeted interventions in these groups. In addition, we have shown significant differences in the clinical features, laboratory investigations, and disease burden between the severe and non-severe group. Few previous studies have compared clinical features between subgroups [22]. Furthermore, we have identified that severe VE is associated with adverse prognoses and significantly increased societal disease burden. Our findings contribute to supporting clinicians in the diagnosis, empiric treatment, and early investigation of severe VE patients.

Another important finding of our study is the incompleteness of diagnostic work up in a considerable proportion of pediatric VE cases. Only 65.5% of patients underwent lumbar puncture, 17.7% received EEG monitoring, and 62.2% underwent brain imaging. Patients without complete diagnostic evaluation, including lumbar puncture, EEG, and neuroimaging, had significantly longer intervals from symptom onset to diagnosis, which may contribute to delayed intervention and an increased risk of severe progression and long-term neurological sequelae. Improving the completeness and timeliness of the standard diagnostic work-up should be recognized as a key quality improvement target for all participating hospitals.

1.4% of our population died and 4% developed neurologic sequelae. In our study, patients with severe VE exhibit significantly higher rates of sequelae, mortality, and ICU admission. A study of America reports a case fatality rate of 15–20% for severe VE, which aligns closely with our finding of 17% [23]. In contrast to previous studies reporting ICU admission rates exceeding 60% in severe VE [24], our cohort demonstrated a markedly lower rate of 13.8%. In a retrospective study in southwestern China, more than 30% of patients with VE developed varying degrees of sequelae during follow-up [25]. Extensive research indicates that patients with VE developed varying degrees of neurological sequelae during follow-up [25–28]. Severe sequelae affect the child’s health and quality of life and places a significant burden to their family and society [29]. Some patients may be dependent on formal or informal caregiver support for performing the activities of daily living [26].

By retrospectively analyzing demographic data, clinical features, auxiliary examination findings, and treatment information, we identified that abnormal brain imaging and EV infection were independent predictors of severe VE. Coma was a component of the severe VE definition and should be interpreted as a severity criterion rather than an independent predictor. Recent studies found that coma was associated with poor discharge outcomes in VE patients, but coma was not a predictor of the long-term prognosis [25, 30]. Brain imaging examinations, which have certain diagnostic value in VE, have shown in several reports that the existence of restricted diffusion on MRI is a predictor of poor prognosis at discharge [30, 31]. In our study, the abnormal MRI rate in severe group (34.5%) was significantly higher than that in the non-severe group (10.9%).

According to our research, EV infection accounted for 55.2% of severe encephalitis cases. EV is one of the most important causes of herpangina and hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) [32]. In our study, the enteroviruses tested include EV71, CA16, CA10 and CA6. The main pathogens in Beijing in 2009 were CA16 (49.4%) and EV71 (36.4%) [33]. In recent outbreaks, however, EV71 infection has been the major cause of HFMD which consistent with our research findings [34]. EV71 is neurotropic and can directly cause central neurological impairment; the virus replicates at the lesion site [35, 36]. EV71 VP capsid proteins can bind to ornithine decarboxylase, gene-trap ankyrin repeats (GTARs) and other proteins expressed in neurons in the brain and cause cell injury [37]. Evidence suggests that the EV vaccination has decreased the incidence of encephalitis obviously [38].

In our study, the utilization rate of corticosteroids was significantly higher in patients with severe VE than in those with non-severe VE. However, the role of corticosteroids in the treatment of viral encephalitis remains controversial in current clinical practice. Currently, the use of glucocorticoids and for the treatment of viral encephalitis is controversial. Extensive intracerebral inflammation in VE, described in both animal models and human patients, may lead to significant neurological damage due to an unchecked immune reaction—known as a cytokine storm [39, 40]. Steroids are potent anti-inflammatory agents and may act through a reduction of secondary inflammation-mediated damage and reduce cerebral edema [41]. However, the current evidence does not support this treatment as a standardized treatment program [11]. The use of glucocorticoid therapy in VE still requires further large-scale, prospective controlled studies.

However, some limitations are existed in this study. Firstly, this study was a retrospective study. We acknowledged that the accuracy of data was reliant on the completeness and standardization of EMR documentation. To mitigate this, we excluded cases with incomplete key information. We recognized that retrospective data may not capture unrecorded clinical details. We suggest that prospective studies with predefined data collection protocols are needed to confirm our findings. Secondly, long-term outcome follow-up in patients with VE can more accurately and comprehensively reflect their disease progression. Future research should consider evaluating the long-term outcomes of discharged patients. Some patients in this study did not undergo LP, which is consistent with real-world clinical practice, as LP may be contraindicated in critically ill pediatric patients. Furthermore, we minimized diagnostic bias through comprehensive assessments, including pathological diagnosis, integrated with clinical manifestations, neuroimaging findings, EEG results, and serological test outcomes. In addition, vaccination status for mumps, measles, and rubella was not systematically documented in routine clinical records; therefore, the correlation between vaccination history and clinical outcomes could not be analyzed in the present study.

Conclusion

This follow-up study of a large VE patient cohort revealed the clinical epidemiology, prognosis and disease burden of VE in China and illustrated the risk factors of severe VE. The incidence of sequelae, the number of patients receiving treatment in the ICU, and the number of deceased patients were all higher in the severe VE group. Significant unfavorable prognostic factors for severe VE were abnormal brain imaging and EV infection.
