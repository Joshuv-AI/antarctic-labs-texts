Abstract
. We study singular solutions to the fractional Laplace equation and, more generally, to nonlocal linear equations with measurable kernels. We establish Bôcher type results that characterize the behavior of singular solutions near the singular point. In addition, we prove Liouville theorems for singular solutions. To this end, we construct fundamental solutions for nonlocal linear operators and establish a localized comparison principle.

1.
Introduction

The analysis of singularities in solutions to partial differential equations (PDEs) is a central topic in regularity theory. A fundamental question in this area is whether solutions to a given PDE are always smooth or can develop singularities. This question arises in a wide range of mathematical and physical contexts, including Hilbert’s nineteenth problem, singular harmonic maps, the Navier–Stokes equations in fluid dynamics, and the Poincaré conjecture via Ricci flow.

For the Laplace equation, one of the most classical and fundamental PDEs, the classification and asymptotic behavior of singular solutions are now well understood. For example, the early work of Bôcher
[
9
]
in 1903 established that any nonnegative singular harmonic function in a punctured ball (in dimension greater than or equal to two) can be represented as a linear combination of the fundamental solution and a harmonic function in the ball. By applying the Kelvin transform, he also showed that any nonnegative harmonic function in an exterior domain approaches a finite limit at infinity, provided that the dimension is greater than two. As a consequence of Bôcher’s result, the following Liouville theorem for singular harmonic solutions holds. See also Picard
[
57
]
, Kellogg
[
40
]
, and Raynor
[
58
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
9
]

Assume that
n>2
. Let
u
be harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Suppose that
u
is bounded from above or below in a neighborhood of
0
and that
u
is bounded from above or below in a neighborhood of infinity. Then

u=a|x|^{2-n}+b

for some constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

An extension of Theorem
1.1
to certain linear equations can also be found in
[
9
]
. Gilbarg–Serrin
[
30
]
carried out a comprehensive study of fundamental solutions and isolated singularities for second-order linear elliptic equations. Building on this, Serrin
[
61
,
63
]
published a series of influential papers on singular solutions of general quasilinear divergence form equations, including the
p
-Laplace equation as a model case. For further developments on quasilinear equations, we refer the reader to Serrin
[

, Kichenassamy–Véron
[

, Véron
[

[

[

for Pucci’s extremal equations and Armstrong–Sirakov–Smart
[

for fully nonlinear equations. In particular, the results in

On the other hand, nonlocal equations such as the fractional Laplace equation have attracted considerable attention in recent decades as they arise naturally in modeling various phenomena described by nonlocal interactions. For instance, the fractional Laplacian is the infinitesimal generator of a stable Lévy jump process. Moreover, it plays a crucial role in the study of the Boltzmann equation as it can approximate the Boltzmann collision operator; see Silvestre

In this work, we take up the study of singular solutions, focusing on Liouville type theorems. Our goal is to establish the nonlocal counterpart of Theorem
1.1
for nonlocal linear equations with measurable kernels. We begin with the simplest case, where the operator is given by the fractional Laplacian. The fractional Laplacian of order
s\in(0,1)
is defined by

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=\frac{4^{s}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}+s)}{\pi^{n/2}|\Gamma(-s)|}\,\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y,

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function. Throughout the paper, we always assume that
n\geq 2
. It is well known that the function

(1.1)

\Phi_{s}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-s)}{4^{s}\pi^{n/2}\Gamma(s)}|x|^{2s-n}

is the fundamental solution for the fractional Laplacian, that is,
(-\Delta)^{s}\Phi_{s}=\delta_{0}
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(in the sense of Definition
2.1
), where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac delta at the origin; see, for instance, classical works of Riesz
[
59
]
and Blumenthal–Getoor–Ray
[
8
]
; the standard monograph by Samko–Kilbas–Marichev
[
60
]
; and more recent expositions such as Bucur
[
11
, Theorem 2.3]
, Garofalo
[
29
, Theorem 8.4]
, and Fernández-Real–Ros-Oton
[
27
, Lemma 1.10.9]
.

Our first result is the following Liouville theorem for singular solutions to the fractional Laplace equation, which extends both the classical Liouville theorem (see Theorem
3.2
) for globally nonnegative solutions and Bôcher’s Theorem
1.1
. We refer the reader to Definition
2.2
for the precise definition of a
(-\Delta)^{s}
-harmonic function.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u
be
(-\Delta)^{s}
-harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Suppose that
u
is bounded from above or below in a neighborhood of
0
and that
u
is bounded from above or below in a neighborhood of infinity. Then

u=a|x|^{2s-n}+b

for some constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

The main ingredients in the proof of Theorem
1.2
are a nonlocal Caccioppoli–Cimmino–Weyl lemma (Theorem
3.1
), the classical Liouville Theorem
3.2
for the fractional Laplacian, and a weak Bôcher type Theorem
1.3
, which we establish below in a more general setting.

The next goal of this paper is to prove a Liouville theorem for more general nonlocal linear operators of the form

(1.2)

\mathcal{L}u(x)=2\,\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(x)-u(y))k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

where
k:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]
is a measurable kernel satisfying the ellipticity and symmetry conditions

\frac{\Lambda^{-1}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\leq k(x,y)=k(y,x)\leq\frac{\Lambda}{|x-y|^{n+2s}},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

with
\Lambda\geq 1
. Note that
\mathcal{L}=(-\Delta)^{s}
when the kernel
k
is given by

k(x,y)=\frac{2^{2s-1}\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}+s)}{\pi^{n/2}|\Gamma(-s)|}|x-y|^{-n-2s}.

Although Theorem
1.5
, a Liouville theorem for singular solutions of
\mathcal{L}u=0
, can be regarded as an extension of Theorem
1.2
, its proof is significantly more challenging. One of the main reasons is the absence of a nonlocal Caccioppoli–Cimmino–Weyl lemma for
\mathcal{L}
; as in the local case

The classical Caccioppoli–Cimmino–Weyl lemma
[

states that if

satisfies
\Delta u=0
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, then
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\Delta u=0

.
, such a result is not expected to hold for general nonlocal operators
\mathcal{L}
with measurable kernels

The first step in proving the Liouville theorem is to find the fundamental solution for
\mathcal{L}
and to investigate its asymptotic behavior both near the origin and at infinity. While the fundamental solution for the fractional Laplacian is defined as the distributional solution of
(-\Delta)^{s}\Phi_{s}=\delta_{0}
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, this definition is not appropriate for operators
\mathcal{L}
with measurable kernels
k
, because
\mathcal{L}\varphi
is not well defined in general even for nice test functions
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. However, the following theorem provides a precise framework for interpreting
\mathcal{L}\Phi=\delta_{0}
in this more general situation. See Definition
2.2
for the precise definition of an
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic function.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set containing the origin. Let
u
be
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega\setminus\{0\}
and bounded from below in
\Omega
. Then there exists a constant
a\geq 0
such that

(1.3)

\mathcal{L}u=a\delta_{0}\quad\text{in }\Omega

in the sense that

(1.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=a\varphi(0)

for all
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Bôcher type results similar to Theorem
1.3
have been obtained for certain classes of nonlocal operators. For example, Li–Wu–Xu
[
52
]
considered the fractional Laplacian, Li–Liu–Wu–Xu
[
51
]
extended this to the fractional Laplacian with a drift term, and Klimsiak
[
46
]
generalized these results to general Lévy operators with drift. These results differ from Theorem
1.3
in two main respects. First, they interpret the equation (
1.3
) in the distributional sense, which is feasible for Lévy operators, whereas here it is interpreted in the sense of (
1.4
). Second, they assume that
u
is globally nonnegative in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is not necessary in Theorem
1.3
.

With the aid of Theorem
1.3
, we establish the existence, uniqueness, and asymptotic behavior of the fundamental solution for the operator
\mathcal{L}
. Here and after, we write
A\lesssim B
(resp.
A\gtrsim B
) if there is a constant
C>0
such that
A\leq CB
(resp.
CA\geq B
), and
A\eqsim B
if
A\lesssim B
and
A\gtrsim B
. See Definition

\mathcal{L}
-superharmonic function.

Theorem 1.4

(Fundamental solution)
.

There exists a unique function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]
that satisfies the following properties
:

(i)

\mathcal{L}\Phi=\delta_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the sense that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\Phi(x)-\Phi(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=\varphi(0)

for all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
;

(ii)

\Phi
is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and
\mathcal{L}
-superharmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(iii)

\lim_{x\to 0}\Phi(x)=\infty
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}\Phi(x)=0
.

Moreover, it satisfies

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi(x)&\lesssim|x|^{2s-n}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ and}\\
\Phi(x)&\gtrsim|x|^{2s-n}\quad\text{in a neighborhood of the origin}.\end{split}

The function
\Phi
is called the
fundamental solution
for the operator
\mathcal{L}
.

We are now ready to state the Liouville theorem for singular solutions to the equation
\mathcal{L}u=0
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
u
be
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Suppose that
u
is bounded from above or below in a neighborhood of
0
and that
u
is bounded from above or below in a neighborhood of infinity. Then

u=a\Phi+b

for some constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
consists of two main parts. The first is the classification of singular solutions near the origin, which is given by Theorem
1.6
below. The second deals with the behavior of singular solutions at infinity; see Lemmas
7.2
and
7.3
for details.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set containing the origin. Let
u
be
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega\setminus\{0\}
and bounded from below in
\Omega
. Then there exist a constant
a\geq 0
and an
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic function
v
in
\Omega
such that

u=a\Phi+v.

In particular, if
a>0
, then

u(x)\eqsim|x|^{2s-n}

in a neighborhood of the origin.

One might expect that Theorem
1.6
would follow immediately from Theorem
1.3
by subtracting equation (
1.3
) from the corresponding equation satisfied by
a\Phi
. However, this is not the case, as one must additionally show that
u-a\Phi
is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega
, which requires regularity of this function near the origin. The proof of this fact is rather involved and relies on nontrivial tools, including the isolated singularity theorem established in our earlier paper
[
44
]
(see Theorem
4.3
) and localized maximum/comparison principles. To this end, we develop the nonlocal counterpart of the approach by Gilbarg–Serrin
[
30
]
.

Several maximum and comparison principles for nonlocal operators are available in the literature, but they typically require pointwise comparison of solutions in the complement of the domain as an assumption; see, for example, Silvestre
[
65
, Proposition 2.21]
, Lindgren–Lindqvist
[
54
, Lemma 9]
, Korvenpää–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
47
, Lemma 6]
, Fernández-Real–Ros-Oton
[
27
, Lemma 2.3.3 and Corollary 2.3.4]
, Björn–Björn–Kim
[
7
, Corollary 3.6]
, and Kim–Lee
[
43
, Lemma 5.2]
. This pointwise condition is quite natural due to the nonlocal nature of the operators. However, they are not applicable in our setting, as we aim to compare singular solutions with the fundamental solution without knowing a priori which one dominates the other outside the domain. Therefore, we need a new comparison principle that does not require pointwise comparison in the complement of the domain. For this purpose, we establish the localized maximum and comparison principles in Theorem
1.7
and Corollary
1.8
; they encode the relevant information about functions outside the domain in integral quantities rather than in a pointwise comparison.

For the precise definitions of a weak super- and subsolution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
and the tail term, we refer the reader to Definition
2.2
and (
2.1
), respectively.

Theorem 1.7

(Localized maximum principle)
.

Let
0<r<R
and
\theta\geq 2
. Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set such that
B_{R}\setminus\overline{B}_{r}\Subset\Omega^{\prime}
. Let
u
be a weak supersolution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
B_{R}\setminus\overline{B}_{r}
such that
u\in W^{s,2}(\Omega^{\prime})
and
u\geq 0
in
\overline{B}_{\theta R}\setminus(B_{R}\setminus\overline{B}_{r})
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(n,s,\Lambda)>0
such that

u\geq-C\theta^{-2s}\mathrm{Tail}(u_{-};0,\theta R)

a.e. in
B_{R}\setminus\overline{B}_{r}
.

Corollary 1.8

(Localized comparison principle)
.

Let
0<r<R
and
\theta\geq 2
. Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set such that
B_{R}\setminus\overline{B}_{r}\Subset\Omega^{\prime}
. Let
u,v\in W^{s,2}(\Omega^{\prime})
be a weak super- and subsolution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
B_{R}\setminus\overline{B}_{r}
, respectively, and suppose that
u\geq v
in
\overline{B}_{\theta R}\setminus(B_{R}\setminus\overline{B}_{r})
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(n,s,\Lambda)>0
such that

u\geq v-C\theta^{-2s}\mathrm{Tail}((u-v)_{-};0,\theta R)

a.e. in
B_{R}\setminus\overline{B}_{r}
.

We point out that we restrict our attention to the case
n\geq 2
, as Lemma
7.1
requires this dimensional condition. Apart from this lemma, all other arguments work for
n>2s
. The critical case
n=2s
remains largely open.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall the definitions of several function spaces and the notion of solutions associated with the fractional Laplacian and the nonlocal operators in (
1.2
). We also summarize some well-known results that will be used throughout the paper. Throughout this section, we assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a nonempty open set, which may be unbounded unless otherwise stated.

Let
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the Schwartz space of rapidly decreasing functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be its topological dual, the space of tempered distributions. We also denote by
\mathscr{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the dual of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, that is, the space of compactly supported distributions, and by
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the dual of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the space of all distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Following Silvestre
[
65
, Definition 2.3]
, we denote by
\mathscr{S}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the space
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
endowed with the countable family of seminorms

[u]_{\mathscr{S}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\alpha}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1+|x|^{n+2s})|\partial^{\alpha}u(x)|,

and by
\mathscr{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
its topological dual. Note that

\displaystyle C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathscr{S}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathscr{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathscr{S^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Moreover, if
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi\in\mathscr{S}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by Lemma 8.1 in Garofalo
[
29
]
.

We now recall the definition of a distributional solution with respect to the fractional Laplacian; see
[
65
]
,
[
11
, Definition 1.5]
, or
[
29
, Definition 8.2]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
T\in\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We say that
u\in\mathscr{S}_{s}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
solves
(-\Delta)^{s}u=T
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(or is a
distributional solution
of
(-\Delta)^{s}u=T
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) if

\langle u,(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi\rangle=\langle T,\varphi\rangle

for all
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

We note that Definition
2.1
cannot be adapted to the operators
\mathcal{L}
in (
1.2
) with measurable kernels
k
, since
\mathcal{L}\varphi
is not well defined in general even for
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

In what follows, we also present other notions of solutions, namely weak solutions and
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic functions. For this purpose, we recall several function spaces. The fractional Sobolev space
W^{s,2}(\Omega)
consists of measurable functions
u:\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty]
whose fractional Sobolev norm

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W^{s,2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+[u]_{W^{s,2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}

is finite. By
W^{s,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
we denote the space of functions
u
such that
u\in W^{s,2}(G)
for every open
G\Subset\Omega
. We refer the reader to Di Nezza–Palatucci–Valdinoci
[
23
]
for properties of these spaces.

Since we are concerned with nonlocal equations, we also need the
tail space

L^{1}_{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\left\{u~\text{measurable}:\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(y)|}{(1+|y|)^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty\right\};

see Kassmann
[
37
]
and Di Castro–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
22
]
. Note that
u\in L^{1}_{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if the
nonlocal tail
(or
tail
for short)

(2.1)

\mathrm{Tail}(u;x_{0},r)\coloneqq r^{2s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}(x_{0})}\frac{|u(y)|}{|y-x_{0}|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

is finite for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r>0
.

Next, we recall the definitions of a weak solution to the equation
\mathcal{L}u=0
, or more generally
\mathcal{L}u=f
, and of an
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic function. To this end, we define, for measurable functions
u,v:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[-\infty,\infty]
, the bilinear form

\mathcal{E}(u,v)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x,

provided that the integral is finite.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. A function
u\in W^{s,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)\cap L^{1}_{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a
weak supersolution
(resp.
weak subsolution
) of
\mathcal{L}u=f
in
\Omega
if

(2.2)

\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)\geq\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x~\left(\text{resp.}\leq\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\right)

for all nonnegative
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. A function
u\in W^{s,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)\cap L^{1}_{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a
weak solution
of
\mathcal{L}u=f
in
\Omega
if (
2.2
) holds for all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. A function
u
is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic
in
\Omega
if it is a weak solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega
and
u\in C(\Omega)
.

It is useful to deal with a larger class of test functions than
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. For this purpose, we recall the space

V^{s,2}(\Omega)\coloneqq\left\{u~\text{measurable}:u|_{\Omega}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\text{ and }\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty\right\},

equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\|u\|_{V^{s,2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+[u]_{V^{s,2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}.

A slight modification of the proofs of Proposition 2.2 and Corollary 2.3 in Björn–Björn–Kim
[
7
]
yields the following results.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
u\in W^{s,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)\cap L^{1}_{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then
u
is a weak solution
(
resp. weak supersolution
)
of
\mathcal{L}u=f
in
\Omega
if and only if

\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)\geq\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x

for all
\varphi\in V^{s,2}(\Omega)

(
resp. for all
0\leq\varphi\in V^{s,2}(\Omega)
)
with
\supp\varphi\Subset\Omega
.

Proof.

By mollification, there exist functions
\varphi_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
such that
\supp\varphi_{j}
is uniformly bounded in
j
and
\varphi_{j}\to\varphi
in
V^{s,2}(\Omega)
as
j\to\infty
. Moreover,
\varphi_{j}\geq 0
if
\varphi\geq 0
. Then, Equation (2.9) in our earlier work
[
43
]
shows that

(2.3)

|\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi_{j}-\varphi)|\leq C(u)\|\varphi_{j}-\varphi\|_{V^{s,2}(\Omega)},

where
C(u)
is a constant depending on
u
, but not on
j
. Moreover, we have

(2.4)

\left|\int_{\Omega}f(\varphi_{j}-\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}|\supp(\varphi_{j}-\varphi)|^{1/2}\|\varphi_{j}-\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{for all }j\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}|\supp(\varphi_{j}-\varphi)|<\infty
, the desired result follows by letting
j\to\infty
in (
2.3
) and (
2.4
).
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

A function
u
is a weak solution of
\mathcal{L}u=f
in
\Omega
if and only if both
u
and
-u
are weak supersolutions of
\mathcal{L}u=f
in
\Omega
.

When weak solutions are more regular than
W^{s,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)\cap L^{1}_{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then the space of test functions can be further enlarged. We consider the spaces

\displaystyle V^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\overline{C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)}^{V^{s,2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle W^{s,2}_{\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{u\in W^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):u=0\text{ a.e.\ on }\Omega^{c}\}=\{u\in V^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):u=0\text{ a.e.\ on }\Omega^{c}\}.

It is clear that
V^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)\subset W^{s,2}_{\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, but the equality does not hold in general; see Fiscella–Servadei–Valdinoci
[
28
]
. The spaces coincide if the boundary of
\Omega
satisfies certain regularity conditions.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
28
]

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set with a continuous boundary. Then

V^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)=W^{s,2}_{\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

The space
V^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)
can be used as a space of test functions for equation
\mathcal{L}u=f
, provided that
u
is sufficiently regular. A slight modification of the proof of
[
7
, Proposition 2.4]
yields the following result.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be such that
\Omega\Subset\Omega^{\prime}
. Let
f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
u\in W^{s,2}(\Omega^{\prime})\cap L^{1}_{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then
u
is a weak solution (resp. weak supersolution) of
\mathcal{L}u=f
in
\Omega
if and only if

\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)\geq\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x

for all
\varphi\in V^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)

(
resp. for all nonnegative
\varphi\in V^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)
)
.

In Proposition
2.6
,
\Omega
is bounded by assumption, but
\Omega^{\prime}
may be unbounded.

Proof.

Suppose that
u
is a weak supersolution of
\mathcal{L}u=f
in
\Omega
and let
0\leq\varphi\in V_{0}^{s,2}(\Omega)
. Then there exists a sequence of functions
\{\varphi_{j}\}\subset C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\varphi_{j}\to\varphi
in
V^{s,2}(\Omega)
as
j\to\infty
. Since
(\varphi_{j})_{+}=0=\varphi
a.e. on
\Omega^{c}
, we have

[(\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi]_{W^{s,2}(\Omega^{\prime})}^{2}\leq[(\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi]_{W^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leq 2[(\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi]_{V^{s,2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

This together with Lemma 2.5 in
[
7
]
shows that
(\varphi_{j})_{+}\to\varphi
in
W^{s,2}(\Omega^{\prime})
as
j\to\infty
.

We now have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}(u,(\varphi_{j})_{+})-\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)|

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)||((\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi)(x)-((\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi)(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\Lambda\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\prime}}\frac{(|u(x)|+|u(y)|)|((\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi)(x)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\eqqcolon I_{1}+I_{2}.

Let
d=\mathrm{dist}(\Omega,\partial\Omega^{\prime})>0
and
R=\sup_{x\in\Omega}|x|<\infty
. Then

I_{1}\leq\Lambda[u]_{W^{s,2}(\Omega^{\prime})}[(\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi]_{W^{s,2}(\Omega^{\prime})}

and

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda\int_{\Omega}|u(x)||((\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi)(x)|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{d}(x)}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\Lambda\int_{\Omega}|((\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi)(x)|\left(\int_{B_{2R}}\frac{|u(y)|}{d^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{B_{2R}^{c}}\frac{|u(y)|}{(|y|/2)^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda\left(\frac{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{2sd^{2s}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{|\Omega|^{1/2}}{d^{n+2s}}\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{2R})}+\frac{2^{n}|\Omega|^{1/2}}{R^{2s}}\mathrm{Tail}(u;0,2R)\right)\|(\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Moreover,

\left|\int_{\Omega}f((\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}|\Omega|^{1/2}\|(\varphi_{j})_{+}-\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Since
\supp(\varphi_{j})_{+}\Subset\Omega
, it follows from Proposition
2.3
that

\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(u,(\varphi_{j})_{+})\geq\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}f(\varphi_{j})_{+}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x.\qed

The following lemma provides a useful tool for testing the equation.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set with a continuous boundary and let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set such that
\Omega\Subset\Omega^{\prime}

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle[u]_{V^{s,2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq[u]_{W^{s,2}(\Omega^{\prime})}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega^{\prime}}\frac{|u(x)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq[u]_{W^{s,2}(\Omega^{\prime})}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{d}(x)}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x

This shows that

2.5
.
∎

3.
Singular solutions to the fractional Laplace equation

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
, the Liouville theorem for singular solutions to the fractional Laplace equation. As mentioned in the introduction, the main ingredients of the proof are a nonlocal Caccioppoli–Cimmino–Weyl lemma, the classical Liouville theorem, and a Bôcher type Theorem
1.3
. The proof of Theorem
1.3
will be provided in Section
4
. We recall the Caccioppoli–Cimmino–Weyl lemma and the Liouville theorem for the fractional Laplacian in the following theorems.

Theorem 3.1

(Nonlocal Caccioppoli–Cimmino–Weyl lemma)
.

[
29
, Theorem 12.17]

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a nonempty open set. If
u\in L^{1}_{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies
(-\Delta)^{s}u=0
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, then
u
has a representative that belongs to
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Theorem 3.2

(Liouville theorem for the fractional Laplacian)
.

[
10
, Lemma 3.2]

If a nonnegative function
u\in L^{1}_{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies
(-\Delta)^{s}u=0
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
u
must be constant.

See also
[
1
,
15
,
24
,
25
,
35
]
for more general Liouville theorems. We now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We may assume that
u
is bounded from below in a neighborhood of the origin, say in
B_{r}=B_{r}(0)
for some
r>0
. By Theorem
1.3
, there exists a constant
\bar{a}\geq 0
such that
(-\Delta)^{s}u=\bar{a}\delta_{0}
in
B_{r}
in the sense that

(3.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=\bar{a}\varphi(0)

for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{r})
. Since
u
is
(-\Delta)^{s}
-harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, (
3.1
) holds for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In particular, this implies that
(-\Delta)^{s}u=\bar{a}\delta_{0}
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
since the left-hand side of (
3.1
) is equal to

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x)(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Notice that this quantity is finite for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
since

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x)(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)|}{(1+|x|)^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty

for some
C=C(n,s,\varphi)>0
, where we used
[
29
, Proposition 2.9]
and the fact that
(-\Delta)^{s}\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Let
\Phi_{s}
be given by (
1.1
), which is the fundamental solution for
(-\Delta)^{s}
, i.e.
(-\Delta)^{s}\Phi_{s}=\delta_{0}
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then

(-\Delta)^{s}(u-\bar{a}\Phi_{s})=0\quad\text{in }\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Thus, Theorem
3.1
shows that
v\coloneqq u-\bar{a}\Phi_{s}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, after redefinition on a set of measure zero if necessary. In particular,
v
is
(-\Delta)^{s}
-harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Since
u
is bounded from one side in a neighborhood of infinity, say in
B_{R}^{c}
for some
R>0
, and
\Phi_{s}
is bounded in
B_{R}^{c}
,
v
is also bounded from one side in
B_{R}^{c}
. Moreover, since
v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
v
is bounded from one side in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, it follows from Theorem
3.2
that
v
is constant. Therefore,

u(x)=a|x|^{2s-n}+b,

where
a=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2}-s)}{4^{s}\pi^{n/2}\Gamma(s)}\bar{a}
and
b=v
are constants.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

If
u
is assumed to be bounded from above or below in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in Theorem
1.2
, then Bôcher type results for
globally nonnegative
functions in the literature (see, for instance, Li–Wu–Xu
[
52
, Theorem 4]
, Li–Liu–Wu–Xu
[
51
, Theorem 1.3]
, and Klimsiak
[
46
]
) can be used instead of Theorem
1.3
.

For the fractional Laplacian, a classification of
(-\Delta)^{s}
-harmonic functions in exterior domains can be deduced from the Bôcher Theorem
1.6
by applying the Kelvin transform. Theorem
1.6
for general operators
\mathcal{L}
will be proved in Section
6
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
u
be
(-\Delta)^{s}
-harmonic in a neighborhood of infinity. Then either

(3.2)

\limsup_{x\to\infty}u(x)=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\liminf_{x\to\infty}u(x)=-\infty,

or

\lim_{x\to\infty}u(x)=c

for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We define the Kelvin transform
\bar{u}
of
u
by

\bar{u}(\bar{x})=\frac{u(x)}{|\bar{x}|^{n-2s}}\quad\text{for }\bar{x}=\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}.

Then
\bar{u}
is
(-\Delta)^{s}
-harmonic in
\Omega\setminus\{0\}
for some neighborhood
\Omega
of the origin. Suppose that (
3.2
) does not hold. Then
u
is bounded from above or below near infinity. We may assume without loss of generality that
u
is bounded from below in
B_{R}^{c}
for some ball
B_{R}=B_{R}(0)
with a radius
R>0
sufficiently large so that
B_{1/R}\subset\Omega
. Let
a\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
u+a
is positive in
B_{R}^{c}
. Then the function

\bar{x}\mapsto\bar{u}(\bar{x})+\frac{a}{|\bar{x}|^{n-2s}}=\frac{u(x)+a}{|\bar{x}|^{n-2s}}

is
(-\Delta)^{s}
-harmonic in
B_{1/R}\setminus\{0\}
and positive in
B_{1/R}
. Thus, Theorem
1.6
shows that

\frac{u(x)+a}{|\bar{x}|^{n-2s}}=\frac{b}{|\bar{x}|^{n-2s}}+v(\bar{x})

for some constant
b
and some function
v
that is
(-\Delta)^{s}
-harmonic in
B_{1/R}
. We thus get

u(x)=b-a+|\bar{x}|^{n-2s}v(\bar{x}),

which yields

\lim_{x\to\infty}u(x)=\lim_{\bar{x}\to 0}(b-a+|\bar{x}|^{n-2s}v(\bar{x}))=b-a.\qed

4.
Weak Bôcher type theorem

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
, which we refer to as a weak Bôcher type theorem, since it can be viewed as a weaker version of Theorem
1.6
. Despite its weaker form, it plays a crucial role in the construction of the fundamental solution in Section
5
.

The main difficulty in proving Theorem
1.3
lies in the fact that the regularity assumption
W^{s,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega\setminus\{0\})\cap L^{1}_{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic functions is not sufficient for the quantity
\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)
to be well defined. Nevertheless, we can show that singular harmonic functions in
\Omega\setminus\{0\}
are
\mathcal{L}
-superharmonic in
\Omega
, and thus enjoy some local regularity in
\Omega
from Theorem
2.10
. Establishing this is our first step.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set containing the origin. Let
u
be
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega\setminus\{0\}
and bounded from below in
\Omega
. Then, after a redefinition at the origin if necessary,
u
is
\mathcal{L}
-superharmonic in
\Omega
.

To prove Lemma
4.1
, we need the following auxiliary result.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set containing the origin. Let
u
be a weak supersolution of

(4.1)

\mathcal{L}u=0

in
\Omega\setminus\{0\}
and bounded from below in
\Omega
. Then
\min\{u,j\}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, is a weak supersolution of (
4.1
) in
\Omega
.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
2.8
that
u_{j}\coloneqq\min\{u,j\}
is a weak supersolution of (
4.1
) in
\Omega\setminus\{0\}
. Thus, it is enough to show that
u_{j}
is a weak supersolution of (
4.1
) in
\Omega
. This can be done by following the proof of Theorem 1.1 in our earlier paper
[
44
]
. For completeness, we briefly outline the main steps of the proof.

Choose
R>0
so that
B_{R}=B_{R}(0)\Subset\Omega
and define
v_{j}\coloneqq\min\{u-j,0\}=u_{j}-j
. We first observe that
[(v_{j})_{+}]_{W^{s,2}(B_{R})}=0
. On the other hand,
[
44
, Equation (4.6)]
, obtained by using the Caccioppoli estimate for weak supersolution
v_{j}
instead of
[
44
, Lemma 3.3]
, shows that

\int_{B_{R/2}}\int_{B_{R/2}}\frac{|\bar{v}_{j}(x)-\bar{v}_{j}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\min\{\bar{v}_{j}^{\delta-1}(x),\bar{v}_{j}^{\delta-1}(y)\}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty,

where
\bar{v}_{j}=(v_{j})_{-}+\mathrm{Tail}((v_{j})_{-};0,R/2)
and
\delta>0
. Here,
\delta
can be assumed to be small so that
\delta\leq 1
since
v_{j}\in L^{\infty}(B_{R})
. This implies that
[(v_{j})_{-}]_{W^{s,2}(B_{R/2})}=[\bar{v}_{j}]_{W^{s,2}(B_{R/2})}<\infty
, and hence
v_{j}\in W^{s,2}(B_{R/2})
. In particular, it follows that
u_{j}\in W^{s,2}(B_{R/2})
as well. We now proceed as in the second part of the proof of
[
44
, Theorem 1.1]
to conclude that
u_{j}
is a weak supersolution of (
4.1
) in
\Omega
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Lemma
4.2
shows that
u_{j}\coloneqq\min\{u,j\}
is a weak supersolution of (

) in
\Omega
for any

Then

a.e. in

) in

by

show that

(after redefinition at the origin if necessary) is

-superharmonic in

.

]
as follows. We note that this theorem was indeed proved for more general class of nonlocal nonlinear equations in

.

Theorem 4.3

(Isolated singularity theorem)
.

[

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

The following lemma will also be useful in the proof of Theorem

.

Lemma 4.4
.

[
44
, Lemma 5.3]

Let

be such that
\mathrm{supp}\,{\varphi}\Subset\Omega
and

u

) in

.

We are now in a position to prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

In view of Theorem
4.3
, we can fix

so that
B_{R}=B_{R}(0)\Subset\Omega
and that

(4.2)

u\eqsim|x|^{2s-n}\quad\text{in }B_{R}.

Since being a solution is a local property and we already know that
\mathcal{L}u=0

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda\int_{B_{\theta r}}\int_{B_{\theta r}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)||\varphi(y)-\varphi(0)||\eta_{r}(x)-\eta_{r}(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

[

[

[

Theorem 6.1

(Maximum principle)
.

Let
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be nonempty open sets such that
\Omega\Subset\Omega^{\prime}
. Let
u\in W^{s,2}(\Omega^{\prime})
be a weak supersolution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega
such that
u_{-}\in V^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then
u\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Recall that, in the local case, a standard proof of Bôcher type theorem relies on the (extended form of) comparison principle between a singular harmonic function and the fundamental solution on shrinking annuli; see, for instance, the proof of Theorem 5 in Gilbarg–Serrin
[
30
]
. This method requires comparing the two functions on the boundary of each annulus.

In the nonlocal setting, however, the maximum principle in Theorem
6.1
requires pointwise control of the functions in the complement of the domain (note that
u_{-}\in V^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)
implies
u\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega^{c}
). This condition is too restrictive for our purpose, since we wish to compare a singular solution with the fundamental solution without any a priori information about which one dominates the other outside the domain. For this reason, we localize Theorem
6.1
to obtain Theorem
1.7
.

We first establish the following lemma, which captures the effect of a non-homogeneous term in the maximum principle for nonlocal operators.

. We thus assume for the remainder of the proof that
u
has a non-removable singularity at the origin and that (
6.1
) holds.

Recall from Theorem
1.4
that
\Phi\eqsim|x|^{2s-n}
in a neighborhood of the origin, i.e.,

(6.2)

\Phi(x)\eqsim|x|^{2s-n}\quad\text{in }B_{r}

for possibly a smaller radius
r
. We take a sufficiently large value
A>0
such that

(6.3)

u+C_{0}\mathrm{Tail}(u_{-},0,2R)<A\left(\frac{1}{2}\Phi-\Phi_{R}-C_{0}\mathrm{Tail}(\Phi,0,2R)\right)\quad\text{in }B_{r},

where
C_{0}\coloneqq 2^{-2s}C>0
for the constant
C>0
given in Corollary
1.8
and

\Phi_{R}\coloneqq\max_{\overline{B}_{2R}\setminus B_{R}}\Phi.

This is possible due to the asymptotic behavior (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) and the finiteness of tail terms. Note that the right-hand side of (
6.3
) can be made positive by taking smaller radius
r
if necessary. Now, we let
a\geq 0
be the largest value such that

(6.4)

u-a\Phi\geq-A\Phi_{R}-C_{0}(\mathrm{Tail}(u_{-};0,2R)+A\mathrm{Tail}(\Phi;0,2R))\quad\text{in $B_{2R}$}.

The existence of this value is guaranteed since the set of all values
a
for which (
6.4
) holds is closed, bounded from above by
A/2
, and contains at least
a=0
.

We define

v=u-a\Phi.

Then,
v
is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
B_{2R}\setminus\{0\}
and bounded from below in
B_{2R}
. In view of Theorem
4.3
again, either
v
has a removable singularity at the origin, or

v(x)\eqsim|x|^{2s-n}

in a neighborhood of the origin. In the latter case, there exist
r_{0}\in(0,r)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,A/2)
such that

v\geq\varepsilon\Phi-(a+\varepsilon)\Phi_{R}\quad\text{in }\overline{B}_{r_{0}}.

Since
u\geq 0
in
\Omega
, we also have

v\geq\varepsilon\Phi-(a+\varepsilon)\Phi_{R}\quad\text{in }\overline{B}_{2R}\setminus B_{R}.

Thus, the localized comparison principle in Corollary
1.8
with
r=r_{0}
and
\theta=2
shows that

\displaystyle u-(a+\varepsilon)\Phi

\displaystyle\geq-(a+\varepsilon)\Phi_{R}-C_{0}\mathrm{Tail}((u-(a+\varepsilon)\Phi)_{-};0,2R)

\displaystyle\geq-A\Phi_{R}-C_{0}\mathrm{Tail}((u-A\Phi)_{-};0,2R)

\displaystyle\geq-A\Phi_{R}-C_{0}(\mathrm{Tail}(u_{-};0,2R)+A\mathrm{Tail}(\Phi;0,2R))

in
B_{2R}
. It contradicts to the maximality of
a
in (
6.4
) and thus
v
must have a removable singularity at the origin. This completes the proof.
∎

7.
Liouville theorem for singular solutions

In this section, we finally prove the Liouville Theorem
1.5
for singular solutions to the equation
\mathcal{L}u=0
. The key tools are the localized maximum principle, the isolated singularity theorem, the Harnack inequality on annuli, and the Liouville theorem for globally nonnegative solutions.

We begin with the Harnack inequality on annuli, which follows from the standard Harnack inequality and a covering argument.

Lemma 7.1

(Harnack inequality on annulus)
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}
such that
u\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}
. Then for any
R\geq 2r
and
\theta\geq 2
,

\sup_{B_{\theta R}\setminus B_{R}}u\leq C\inf_{B_{\theta R}\setminus B_{R}}u+C\left(\frac{r}{\theta R}\right)^{n}\fint_{B_{r}}u_{-}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
C=C(n,s,\Lambda)>0
.

Proof.

We can cover
B_{\theta R}\setminus B_{R}
by finitely many open balls
\{B_{\theta R/8}(y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
with
y_{i}\in B_{\theta R}\setminus B_{R+\theta R/16}
so that
B_{\theta R/4}(y_{i})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}
. Note that
N
depends only on
n
. An application of the standard Harnack inequality (see, e.g.
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
) in
B_{\theta R/8}(y_{i})
shows that

\sup_{B_{\theta R/8}(y_{i})}u\leq C\inf_{B_{\theta R/8}(y_{i})}u+C\mathrm{Tail}(u_{-};y_{i},\theta R/8),

where the constant
C>0
is independent of
i
. Since
u\geq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}
and

|y-y_{i}|\geq|y_{i}|-|y|>\frac{\theta R}{16}+R-r\geq\frac{\theta R}{16}+\frac{R}{2}\quad\text{for all $y\in B_{r}$},

we obtain that

\mathrm{Tail}(u_{-};y_{i},\theta R/8)=\left(\frac{\theta R}{8}\right)^{2s}\int_{B_{r}}\frac{u_{-}(y)}{|y-y_{i}|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\left(\frac{\theta R}{4}\right)^{2s}\left(\frac{16}{\theta R}\right)^{n+2s}\int_{B_{r}}u_{-}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
be such that

\inf_{B_{\theta R}\setminus B_{R}}u\geq\inf_{B_{\theta R/8}(y_{j})}u.

Since
n\geq 2
, the annulus
B_{\theta R}\setminus B_{R}
is connected, and hence

\sup_{B_{\theta R}\setminus B_{R}}u\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\sup_{B_{\theta R/8}(y_{i})}u\leq C^{N}\inf_{B_{\theta R/8}(y_{j})}u+C\left(\frac{r}{\theta R}\right)^{n}\fint_{B_{r}}u_{-}\,\mathrm{d}x,

as desired.
∎

In the following lemmas, we prove the boundedness of singular solutions in a neighborhood of infinity.

Lemma 7.2
.

Assume that
u
is a weak solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{B}_{1/2}
that is bounded from above or below in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
u
is bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
u>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We define

\overline{\Phi}=2\Phi/\min_{\overline{B}_{1}}\Phi

so that
\min_{\overline{B}_{1}}\overline{\Phi}=2
. Since
\overline{\Phi}\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
, there exists
R_{0}>2
such that

(7.1)

\overline{\Phi}\leq 1/4\quad\text{in }B_{R_{0}}^{c}.

Set for
R>R_{0}

M(R)\coloneqq\sup_{B_{\theta R}\setminus B_{R}}u\quad\text{and}\quad m(R)\coloneqq\inf_{B_{\theta R}\setminus B_{R}}u,

where
\theta>2
to be determined later.

We observe that
u
and
m(R)(1-\overline{\Phi})
are
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
B_{R}\setminus\overline{B}_{1}
, and that

u\geq m(R)(1-\overline{\Phi})\quad\text{in }(\overline{B}_{\theta R}\setminus B_{R})\cup\overline{B}_{1}.

Thus, the localized comparison principle in Corollary
1.8
with
r=1
shows that

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\geq m(R)(1-\overline{\Phi})-C\theta^{-2s}(\theta R)^{2s}\int_{B_{\theta R}^{c}}\frac{(u-m(R)(1-\overline{\Phi}))_{-}(y)}{|y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq m(R)(1-\overline{\Phi})-C\theta^{-2s}(\theta R)^{2s}\int_{B_{\theta R}^{c}}\frac{m(R)}{|y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=m(R)\left(1-\overline{\Phi}-\frac{C|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}{2s}\theta^{-2s}\right)

in
B_{R}\setminus\overline{B}_{1}
.

We take
\theta
sufficiently large so that
C|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|\theta^{-2s}/(2s)<1/4
. By using (
7.1
), we get for
x_{0}\in\partial B_{R_{0}}
and
R\geq R_{0}

(7.2)

M(R_{0})\geq u(x_{0})\geq\frac{1}{2}m(R).

Since Lemma
7.1
and (
7.2
) show that

M(R)\lesssim m(R)\lesssim M(R_{0})

for any
R\geq R_{0}
,
M(R)
is bounded from above for
R\geq R_{0}
, i.e.
u
is bounded from above in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R_{0}}
. Moreover,
u
is locally bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{B}_{1}
by the regularity theory; see, for instance, Theorem 1.1 in Di Castro–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
22
]
. Therefore,
u
is bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}
for any
r>1
.
∎

If
u
is bounded from one side (above or below) near the origin and bounded from the opposite side (below or above, respectively) at infinity, then we require
u
to be a weak solution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
in order to derive the same result.

Lemma 7.3
.

Assume that
u
is a weak solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
that is bounded from one side in a neighborhood of
0
and that
u
is bounded from the opposite side in a neighborhood of infinity. Then
u
is bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2}
.

Proof.

We use the same notation as in the proof of Lemma
7.2
.

We may assume that
u>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
and
u
is bounded from above in
B_{1}
by assumption and the local boundedness of
u
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. If
u
is also bounded from below in
B_{1}
, then Lemma
7.2
gives the conclusion. Thus, we assume that
u
is not bounded from below in
B_{1}
. Then, Theorem
4.3
and (
1.5
) show that there exists a constant
a>0
such that

u(x)\geq-a\overline{\Phi}\quad\text{in $\overline{B}_{1}$}.

We now prove the desired upper bound of
u
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2}
by contradiction; we suppose that
\limsup_{R\to\infty}M(R)=\infty
. In particular, in view of Lemma
7.1
, there exists a large radius
\overline{R}
such that
m(\overline{R})>\overline{M}\coloneqq\max\{2a,2M(R_{0})\}
.

We first observe that

u\geq m(\overline{R})(1-\overline{\Phi})\quad\text{in }(\overline{B}_{\theta\overline{R}}\setminus B_{\overline{R}})\cup\overline{B}_{1}.

As in the proof of Lemma
7.2
, we apply the localized comparison principle in Corollary
1.8
to obtain that

M(R_{0})\geq\frac{1}{2}m(\overline{R}),

which contradicts the choice of
\overline{R}
. Therefore, we conclude that
\limsup_{R\to\infty}M(R)<\infty
. Combined with the local boundedness in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, this completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 7.4
.

If
u
is a nonnegative weak solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
u
must be constant.

Proof.

Lemma
7.2
shows that
u
is bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2}
. Moreover, by Theorem 1.1 in Di Castro–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
22
]
,
u
is locally bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore,
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Now, it follows from the uniform Hölder estimate (see, e.g. Kassmann
[
36
, Theorem 1.1]
) that

[u]_{C^{\alpha}(B_{R})}\leq\frac{C}{R^{\alpha}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
C>0
are constants depending only on
n
,
s
, and
\Lambda
. Passing to the limit
R\to 0
concludes that
u
is constant.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

If the singularity at the origin is removable, then
u
is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
u
is locally bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and bounded from one side in a neighborhood of infinity, it is bounded from one side in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, Theorem
7.4
concludes that
u
is constant.

Suppose that the singularity at the origin is not removable. Then it follows from Theorem
1.6
that
u=a\Phi+v
for some constant
a\neq 0
and an
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic function
v
in a neighborhood of the origin. Moreover, Lemmas
7.2
and
7.3
show that

b^{+}\coloneqq\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}u(x)\quad\text{and}\quad b^{-}\coloneqq\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}u(x)

exist as finite values.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that

(1-\mathrm{sign}(a)\varepsilon)a\Phi+b^{-}-\varepsilon\leq u\leq(1+\mathrm{sign}(a)\varepsilon)a\Phi+b^{+}+\varepsilon\quad\text{in }\overline{B}_{\delta}\cup B_{1/\delta}^{c}.

Since
u
and
(1\pm\mathrm{sign}(a)\varepsilon)a\Phi+b^{\pm}\pm\varepsilon
are
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
B_{1/\delta}\setminus\overline{B}_{\delta}
, the standard maximum principle in Theorem
6.1
shows that

(1-\mathrm{sign}(a)\varepsilon)a\Phi+b^{-}-\varepsilon\leq u\leq(1+\mathrm{sign}(a)\varepsilon)a\Phi+b^{+}+\varepsilon\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Passing to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
yields

a\Phi+b^{-}\leq u\leq a\Phi+b^{+}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}.

But then Theorem
4.3
shows that
b\coloneqq u-a\Phi
is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, the Liouville Theorem
7.4
shows that
b
is constant.
∎
