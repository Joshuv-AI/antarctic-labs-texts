Nanoscale Magnetic Resonance Imaging and Control of a Strongly Interacting Dipolar System
Piotr Put 1, Nathaniel T. Leitao 1, Christina Spaegele 2, Haoyang Gao 1, Oksana Makarova 1,2, Bartholomeus Machielse 1, Hengyun Zhou 1, Federico Capasso 2, Leigh S. Martin 1, Hongkun Park 1,3, Mikhail D. Lukin 1

Abstract
Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is a fundamental tool for physical and life sciences, yet its spatial resolution is typically limited to macroscopic scales. Here, we demonstrate nanoscale MRI by combining strong, time-dependent local magnetic field gradients with coherent control of a dense ensemble of electron spins hosted in atom-like defects in diamond. Using this platform, we generate and manipulate nanoscale spin textures — spatially structured patterns of spin orientation — and track their evolution under engineered many-body interactions. Controlling the dipolar spin exchange driving the dynamics, we observe striking signatures of sensitivity to the microscopic details underlying the polarization distribution. Our results open the door for robust control of metrologically useful entanglement, and nanoscale imaging of materials and biological systems under ambient conditions.



Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is a powerful, noninvasive tool for probing materials, biological systems, and living organisms by detecting spin precession in magnetic fields. While conventional MRI typically achieves millimeter-scale resolution, significant efforts are underway to push MRI to microscopic and nanoscale regimes
Budakian et al. 2024
. These developments are relevant to a range of applications, including imaging of individual cells and molecular signals pathways
Shapiro et al. 2004
;
Shapiro et al. 2010
, spintronics
Rugar et al. 2004
;
Du et al. 2017
, and quantum information science
Vandersypen and Chuang 2005
;
Abobeih et al. 2022
, where local control of strongly coupled nuclear or electronic spins is essential
Zhang and Cory 1998
;
Dikarov et al. 2016
.

One route to nanoscale MRI involves magnetic resonance force microscopy, which has enabled imaging with nanometer resolution in one
Mamin et al. 2007
and three dimensions
Degen et al. 2009
, culminating in nuclear magnetic resonance diffraction
Haas et al. 2022
. More recently, an alternative platform based on nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond combines excellent magnetic sensitivity
Grinolds et al. 2014
;
Lovchinsky et al. 2016
with compatibility for room-temperature operation. This has led to early demonstrations of nanoscale MRI using single NV center scanning probes
Häberle et al. 2015
. A complementary strategy employs strong magnetic field gradients to implement Fourier Magnetic Imaging (FMI)
Arai et al. 2015
, which offers multiplexed detection
Guo et al. 2024
and rapid data acquisition via compressed sensing
Arai et al. 2015
;
T. Amawi et al. 2024
. FMI also allows access to temporal correlations
Arai et al. 2015
and spatial covariance across tunable length scales
Köylüoğlu et al.
. Extending this technique to truly nanometer-scale resolution requires the sensors to be in close proximity to each other, which introduces strong dipole-dipole interactions between spins leading to complex many-body dynamics
Choi et al. 2017
;
Kucsko et al. 2018
;
Davis et al. 2023
;
Peng et al. 2023
;
Beatrez et al. 2021
.

In this Article, we introduce and explore this strongly interacting regime of nanoscale MRI by combining strong, time-dependent magnetic gradients with a global coherent control of a dense ensemble of electron spins in diamond. While coherent control of such strongly interacting spin ensembles has been recently explored for both many-body physics and metrological applications
Kucsko et al. 2018
;
Zu et al. 2021
;
Martin et al. 2023
;
Gao et al. 2025
;
Wu et al. 2025
, a central challenge is associated with the lack of local spin control and readout, as both are typically performed using global fields.
Our approach, schematically illustrated in Fig.
1
, overcomes this limitation by using magnetic field gradients to generate controlled, spatially inhomogeneous spin rotations, that we call spin textures, which evolve under the influence of engineered dipolar interactions and external magnetic fields, and are subsequently probed using optical readout. This enables spatially resolved access to probing both local magnetic fields and the tunable many-body dynamics of the system with nanoscale resolution.

Our experimental system, illustrated in Fig.
1
A, consists of a dense ensemble (
\sim
15 ppm) of nitrogen-vacancy (NV) spins in a diamond crystal
Doherty et al. 2013
. The diamond sample is shaped into a triangular nanobeam and positioned atop a microcoil structure with a narrow constriction. The NV electronic spins are initialized and read out using a green laser, and coherently manipulated via a globally applied microwave field, all under ambient conditions. During the experiment, we polarize a small region within the diamond nanobeam. This region, confined transversely by the 300 nm width of the beam (Fig.
1
D), contains approximately
10^{4}
spins. The average spacing between spins in this volume is approximately 11 nm (Fig.
1
D) resulting in strong magnetic dipole-dipole interactions on the order of
\sim 2\pi\times 35
kHz at typical separations
Kucsko et al. 2018
.

Pulsed electric currents applied to the microcoil generate a spatially varying (inhomogeneous) magnetic field
\vec{\bm{B}}_{0}
(see Fig.
1
A), which enables local spin control and spatially resolved readout. The resulting magnetic field gradients can reach up to
\sim 2.2
mT/µm—exceeding the typical spin-spin interaction energy by more than an order of magnitude — thereby enabling the creation of spatially structured spin patterns (spin textures) in the three-dimensional spin ensemble (Methods).

NV centers can be oriented along four different symmetry axes of the host diamond lattice (Fig.
1
B), which we leverage to tailor the geometry of local control. Each NV group experiences a different effective magnetic field gradient,

\bm{\nabla}_{\alpha}=\nabla\left(\vec{\bm{B}}_{0}\cdot\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{\alpha}\right),

(1)

where
\alpha=1,2,3,4
indexes the NV group, and
\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{\alpha}
denotes unit vector along crystallographic quantization axis (Fig.
1
D). Notably, the direction of the gradient vector
\bm{\nabla}_{\alpha}
can be tuned independently from the orientation of the dipolar quantization axis
\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{\alpha}
, which is fixed by the diamond crystal structure. To characterize the spatial structure and strength of the control fields, we measure the electron spin resonance (ESR) frequency along the length of the nanobeam (Fig.
1
C). At 1/10 of the maximal gradient strength used in this work, we observe a clear spatial variation in the projected magnetic field
\vec{\bm{B}}_{0}\cdot\hat{\bm{\eta}}
, which agrees well with finite-element simulations of the field produced by the microcoil (solid lines in Fig.
1
C).

We next investigate the dynamics of spatially inhomogeneous spin configurations by implementing the measurement sequence illustrated in Fig.
2
A. Using simultaneous control of local magnetic gradients and global microwave pulses, the experiment is divided into three stages.

In the first stage, referred to as
winding
, spins are optically polarized along
\hat{z}
direction set by the crystallographic quantization axis and then globally rotated by an angle
\theta
about the
\hat{y}
-axis using a microwave pulse (Fig.
2
B, upper left panel). A magnetic field gradient is subsequently applied, imprinting a spatially varying Zeeman shift
\bm{\nabla}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}_{j}\,S_{j}^{z}
across the ensemble. Since the gradient strength is more than an order of magnitude larger than the dipolar interaction energy, the resulting state is a spatially modulated spin configuration resembling a conical spin spiral
Jepsen et al. 2022
(Fig.
2
B upper central panel, see Methods and Supplementary Information (SI)). The magnitude and direction of the spiral wavevector
\bm{Q}
can be independently controlled by adjusting the winding time
\tau
and selecting the NV group, respectively, such that
\bm{Q}=\tau\,\bm{\nabla}_{\alpha}
.

In the second stage, called
quenching
, we apply periodic microwave driving to engineer an effective many-body Hamiltonian via Floquet engineering
Martin et al. 2023
. The quench duration
t
is controlled by varying the number of Floquet cycles.

In the final stage,
unwinding
, we apply a reversed gradient for a variable time
\tau^{\prime}
, corresponding to a measurement wavevector
\bm{Q^{\prime}}=\tau^{\prime}\bm{\nabla}_{\alpha}
. By measuring the global spin signal after this step, we effectively measure the Fourier mode
S^{+}_{\bm{Q}^{\prime}}=\sum_{j}e^{-i\bm{Q}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{r}_{j}}S_{j}^{+}
, where
S_{j}^{+}=S_{j}^{x}+iS_{j}^{y}
represents the spin coherence at site
j
.

To validate this winding–unwinding protocol, we prepare a spin spiral with cone angle
\theta=\pi/4
and pitch
Q=2\pi/(0.126\,
µm
)
using the first NV group, and immediately reverse the process without any intermediate quench. Varying the unwinding time (and thus
\bm{Q}^{\prime}
), we measure the ensemble-averaged
x
and
y
spin components (Fig.
2
C). We observe a clear revival in the coherence magnitude
|\langle S^{+}_{\bm{Q}^{\prime}}(t=0)\rangle|
centered at
Q^{\prime}=Q
, providing evidence of reversible dynamics under the gradient field. The finite width of this revival profile reflects the spatial extent of the spin-polarized region (
\Delta Q^{\prime}\approx 2\pi/(0.4\,
µm)). The azimuthal angle of the spin spiral polarization, extracted following Fourier transform
Arai et al. 2015
, is plotted in Fig.
2
D and matches the expected spatial phase variation imposed by the wavevector
\bm{Q}
. These measurements confirm our ability to both prepare and measure Fourier modes of spin polarization with high fidelity, a crucial requirement for magnetic resonance imaging.

We next proceed to investigate the many-body dynamics of spin spirals under an engineered Hamiltonian quench, focusing on the dynamics of the initialized Fourier mode
\bm{Q^{\prime}}=\bm{Q}
. Using a tailored microwave pulse sequence (see Methods and Ref.
Zhou et al. 2023
), we Floquet-engineer an effective Hamiltonian that symmetrizes the native dipolar spin-exchange interactions to approximately conserve total spin at stroboscopic times. To isolate intrinsic many-body dynamics from artifacts introduced by the microwave pulses, we prepare four spin spirals based on two pairs of antipodal spin-coherent states, prepared prior to winding in the XZ plane (Fig.
2
E)
Gao et al. 2025
. This approach allows us to average out systematic errors and spurious rotations corrupting the ideal interacting many-body evolution.

In Fig.
2
F, we show the extracted spiral dynamics by plotting the imaginary component of the Fourier amplitude,
\text{Im}\langle S^{+}_{\bm{Q}}(t)\rangle
, i.e. total
\hat{y}
coherence measured
after
unwinding, averaged across antipodes, as a function of quench time
t
. When no spiral is prepared (
Q=0
), the system is globally polarized, and the
\hat{y}
component of the total magnetization remains approximately conserved, resulting in no time-dependent signal. In contrast, when a finite-wavelength spin spiral is initialized (
Q=2\pi\times 8~
µm
{}^{-1}\neq 0
), we observe clear dynamical evolution of the
\hat{y}
coherence, indicating interaction-driven many-body dynamics. The second pair of antipodal initial states exhibits similar dynamics but with an opposite sign.

To investigate the microscopic mechanism responsible for the observed spin spiral dynamics (Fig.
2
F), we take advantage of the tunability of the gradient direction using different NV groups. Specifically, we complement the earlier measurement performed using NV group 1 with a corresponding experiment using NV group 2. As shown in Fig.
3
A,B, the two measurements exhibit qualitatively similar dynamics but with opposite signs of precession. This reversal provides a key signature of the underlying dipolar interaction mechanism.

To understand these observations, we note that the NV spins interact through strong magnetic dipolar couplings, which, under our Floquet-engineered sequence, are described by a spin-exchange Hamiltonian with a global SU(2) spin rotation symmetry,

H_{0}=\sum_{i<j}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}(\hat{\bm{r}}_{ij})}{r_{ij}^{3}}\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{j},

(2)

providing a long-ranged, dipolar version of the quantum Heisenberg model
Leitao et al.
. In particular, the spatially anisotropic couplings
\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}(\hat{\bm{r}})\propto 3\left(\hat{\bm{\eta}}\cdot\hat{\bm{r}}\right)^{2}-1
, can lead to either ferromagnetic or anti-ferromagnetic interactions depending on the orientation of the interacting spin pair relative to their mutual quantization axis
\hat{\bm{\eta}}
. While globally-polarized, spatially homogeneous states possess no dynamics under this Hamiltonian due to the conservation law of total spin (Fig.
2
F, left panel), the spin spiral texture will experience non-trivial dynamics.

To gain some insights, we first consider the mean-field dynamics of a central spin
\vec{\bm{S}}
initially polarized in the
XZ
plane of the Bloch sphere (Fig.
3
C,D, black dots). The mean-field sourced at this central spin is given by
\vec{B}_{\bm{Q}}=\sum_{j}\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}(\hat{\bm{r}}_{j})/r_{j}^{3}\,\langle\vec{S}_{j}\rangle_{\bm{Q}}
, where
\langle\vec{S}_{j}\rangle_{\bm{Q}}
is the polarization associated with the spin spiral texture. Substituting the polarization of an idealized conical spiral, we find (see SI)
\vec{B}_{\bm{Q}}=\left(\sum_{j}\delta\chi_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{r}_{j})\right)\vec{\bm{S}}_{\perp}+\left(\sum_{j}\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}(\hat{\bm{r}}_{j})/r_{j}^{3}\right)\vec{\bm{S}}
where we defined
\delta\chi_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{r})=\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}(\hat{\bm{r}})/r^{3}\,(1-\cos{\left(\bm Q\cdot\bm r\right)})/2
, as the
transverse
component of the magnetic field sourced by a spin a distance
\bm{r}
away from the central spin. The total torque experienced by the central spin can be obtained by integrating over all spins in the ensemble, and therefore is proportional to the
exchange field strength

\displaystyle\chi_{\bm{Q}}

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{r}\rho(\bm{r})\delta\chi_{\bm{Q}}(\bm{r})=\int d\bm{r}\rho(\bm{r})\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}(\hat{\bm{r}})}{r^{3}}\frac{1-\cos{\left(\bm Q\cdot\bm r\right)}}{2},

(3)

where
\rho(\bm{r})
is the polarization density at site
\bm{r}
.

Intuitively, this expression can be understood as modulating the dipolar coupling at the pitch of the spiral texture. When there is no spiral wound (
\bm{Q}=\bm{0}
) the exchange field vanishes due to the collinear orientation of all spins in the ensemble. When the spiral wavevector is perpendicular to the dipolar quantization axis (
\bm{Q}\perp\hat{\bm{\eta}}
), spins ferromagnetically coupled to the central spin (Fig.
3
C) dominate the exchange field and produce a negative total torque. In contrast, for a spiral pitch aligned parallel to the dipolar quantization axis (
\bm{Q}\parallel\hat{\bm{\eta}}
), spins anti-ferromagnetically coupled to the central spin (Fig.
3
D) dominate and produce a positive torque, consistent with our observations. We emphasize that this exchange mean-field is distinct from the more familiar Ising-type longitudinal fields
Block et al. 2024
;
Gao et al. 2025
;
Wu et al. 2025
, which cannot induce collective precession in three-dimensional dipolar systems due to angular averaging. In contrast, the spin spiral sources a transverse exchange field driving coherent dynamics tuned by the pitch of the spiral.

Finally, we explore the role of spin rotational symmetry underlying the dynamics by tuning the anisotropy of the effective Hamiltonian. Adjusting the spacing between microwave pulses
Martin et al. 2023
, we realize a family of U(1)-symmetric XXZ Hamiltonians with tunable relative interaction strengths:
\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{j}\rightarrow g_{XX}(S_{i}^{x}S_{j}^{x}+S_{i}^{y}S_{j}^{y})+g_{Z}S_{i}^{z}S_{j}^{z}
. We measure the maximum amplitude of spiral precession as a function of the anisotropy ratio
g_{Z}/g_{XX}
(Fig.
3
E), and observe a clear maximum at the SU(2)-symmetric point (
g_{Z}/g_{XX}=1
). When the full spin-rotation symmetry is broken, the transverse spin components of the spiral are no longer globally conserved, and experience local interaction-induced dephasing that reduce the precession amplitude.

We next investigate the dependence of dynamics upon the spiral wavelength. To this end, we measure the amplitude of the spin spiral precession varying the magnitude of the wavevector on the first NV group (Fig.
4
A), which we expect to probe the strength of the exchange field (Eq.
3
, see SI). In Fig.
4
A, we observe an initial increase in the amplitude of the precession that saturates and eventually decays at larger wave vectors. We further note that the saturation wavevector appears to depend upon the duration of the optical polarization pulse
\tau
(see inset in Fig.
4
A).

To understand the origin of these features, we analyze the exchange field
\chi_{\bm{Q}}
(Eq.
3
) in the simplified case of an isotropic spin polarization profile with microscopic radius
R_{*}
, as illustrated in Fig.
4
B. When the spiral wavelength is much larger than the polarized region (
Q/2\pi<1/R_{*}
), nearly all spins in the microscopic polarization extent remain aligned with the central spin and contribute negligibly to the exchange field. As the wavevector increases, while the spin closest to the central spin remain approximately parallel, more distant spins will source significant exchange via the long-range character of dipolar interactions. In particular, spherical shells of spins located at distance
r
exert a strong exchange due to the compensation between the density of spins
\rho(\bm{r})d\bm{r}\propto 4\pi r^{2}dr
and the power-law decay of dipolar interactions
\propto 1/r^{3}
, which when integrated over the spiral texture produces a maximum precession when
Q/2\pi\sim 1/R_{*}
, as illustrated in the central panel of Fig.
4
B. Indeed, at larger wavevectors (
Q/2\pi>1/R_{*}
), the increasing influence of dipolar anisotropy eventually reduces the net exchange field strength, leading to the observed decline of the precession signal amplitude. For the nanobeam geometry, this isotropic polarization approximation becomes relevant when the spiral wavevector exceeds the transverse size of the beam, i.e., for
Q>Q_{*}\equiv 2\pi/(0.3~
µm)
=2\pi\times 3.3~
µm
-1
(marked by a vertical line in Fig.
4
A).

To further quantify these considerations and to understand the observed dependence upon optical pumping time, nanoscale magnetic imaging is utilized to probe polarization profiles. To this end, we use FMI
Arai et al. 2015
, which maps the spatial spin distribution via the Fourier transform of
S^{+}_{\bm{Q}^{\prime}}
as a function of
Q^{\prime}
(Fig.
4
F,G). The imaging direction is set by the magnetic field gradient
\bm{\nabla}
, which we select by addressing different NV groups (Fig.
4
C), while the spatial resolution is set by the shortest wavelength spin spiral wound. FMI along the nanobeam axis (Fig.
4
F) shows that shorter pumping times reduce the spatial extent of polarization. Perpendicular scans (Fig.
4
G) reveal a central dip in the polarization profile, indicating spatial inhomogeneity. This feature arises from optical interference of the green pump light within the nanobeam, as confirmed by electric field simulations (see Methods). Using these simulated light distributions, we model the resulting NV spin polarization (Fig.
4
D,E). At short pumping durations, interference creates two distinct polarization lobes; with continued pumping, these merge into a nearly uniform profile. This spatial model allows us to quantitatively reproduce the
Q
-dependence of the precession amplitude for an extended optical pumping (see solid line in Fig.
4
A and SI).

Discussion and outlook.
—

Our observations indicate that nanoscale MRI constitute a powerful tool in probing and controlling quantum dynamics of a dense ensemble of atom-like spin defects in diamond coupled by strong dipolar interactions. We demonstrate that
these interactions play a dual role. On the one hand, they limit the preparation of
short-wavelength
spin spirals essential for high-resolution imaging, which can be mitigated by applying strong magnetic field gradients to maintain a large spiral polarization during (un)winding. On the other hand, they give rise to complex many-body dynamics of
long-wavelength
spin textures that are controlled with Floquet engineering techniques. While such non-equilibrium dynamics of spin spirals have been explored in systems such as ultracold atoms
Jepsen et al. 2020
;
Jepsen et al. 2021
;
Jepsen et al. 2022
;
Hild et al. 2014
and spin-orbit-coupled semiconductors
Bernevig et al. 2006
;
Koralek et al. 2009
, our system offers several qualitatively distinct features including long-range interactions, magnetic frustration, and positional disorder inherent to solid-state systems. This experimental platform could enable spatially-resolved studies of complex many-body dynamics in driven quantum systems, including quantum Kuramoto synchronization
Acebrón et al. 2005
;
Reimann et al. 2023
;
Medenjak et al. 2020
, emergent hydrodynamics
Gopalakrishnan and Vasseur 2019
;
Ye et al. 2020
;
Zu et al. 2021
, and aging dynamics in quantum spin glasses
Signoles et al. 2021
;
Tikhanovskaya et al. 2024
. We further note that the spin spiral dynamics observed here bear resemblance to those generated under a one-axis twisting Hamiltonian, useful for applications in entanglement-enhanced metrology
Kitagawa and Ueda 1993
. Indeed, as we show theoretically in a complementary work
Leitao et al.
, the dipolar exchange fields sourced by spin spirals explored in this work could dynamically generate metrologically useful entanglement in the form of scalable spin squeezing. Realizing this potential will require mitigating experimental imperfections by improving spin control fidelity, spin polarization and enhancing spin coherence times in interacting ensembles.

Looking ahead, the techniques demonstrated in this work
may be integrated with materials or biologically relevant sensing targets
Aslam et al. 2023
, combining the exquisite sensitivity of dense NV ensembles with the nanoscale spatial resolution enabled by strong magnetic gradients to image magnetic signals
Budakian et al. 2024
;
T. Amawi et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2024
, as well as spatio-temporal correlations
Rovny et al. 2025
;
Köylüoğlu et al.
. Our platform could thus be used to realize a range of novel quantum sensing techniques, from scalable signal amplification
Gao et al. 2025
to entanglement-enhanced Fourier magnetic imaging, realizing quantum advantage at the nanoscale under ambient conditions.
