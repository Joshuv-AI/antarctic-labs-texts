Development of erosion hazard index-based land suitability criteria for sustainable agriculture in the Nilgiris hills of India
Authors: D. V. Singh, M. Madhu, Sheetal K Radhakrishnan, Devideen Yadav, P. Muralidharan, Renjith P. S., A. K. Sikka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Land suitability analysis is vital for sustainable agriculture in fragile mountain ecosystems such as the Nilgiris. Earlier assessments, based on existing criteria, often rated large areas as “not suitable” and identified no “highly suitable” land for crops like potato, cauliflower, beans, wheat, and tea, despite their long history of successful cultivation in the region. This clear mismatch highlighted the need for region-specific criteria. This study refined the FAO land evaluation criteria by introducing an erosion hazard index (EHI) that jointly integrates slope gradient and active land management conditions, particularly terracing, into a single normalized parameter. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this combination has not previously been formalized for mountain agro-ecosystems of the Western Ghats. Additional adjustments to climatic and soil parameters were made to reflect the unique agro-ecological conditions of the Nilgiris. The revised assessment produced results more consistent with on-ground reality. Tea exhibited high and moderate suitability across 37.4% and 44.4% of the representative watershed (Thambatti), respectively. Potato, cole, and root vegetables were highly suitable in 6.9% and moderately suitable in 8.2% of the area, with suitability primarily limited by high erosion hazard (EHI > 0.54 for potato; EHI > 0.62 for cole and root vegetables). Field beans showed no highly suitable area but were moderately suitable in 23.4% area, constrained mainly by soil acidity, while wheat exhibited 15.1% moderately suitable area, limited by temperature, with erosion also being a significant factor for both crops. Fruit crops were moderately suitable in 12.6% and marginally suitable in 80.3% of the area, mainly constrained by low mean temperature, high soil acidity, and heavy soil texture. The refined criteria offer a more accurate and practical assessment of land suitability, addressing observed discrepancies and supporting sustainable agricultural planning in the Nilgiris.

Introduction

Effective management of natural resources is paramount for sustaining their production potential, quality, and diversity, particularly within agricultural ecosystems. Agriculture forms the backbone of the Indian economy, and achieving long-term development and poverty alleviation necessitates a focus on sustainable agricultural practices. Integrated natural resource management, especially on a watershed basis, is crucial to mitigate adverse impacts on both natural and social environments, while actively seeking opportunities to manage land resources for the benefit of current and future generations. A watershed functions as an integrated ecosystem, where disturbance to one component, be it climate, soil, water, plants, animals, or human beings, inevitably affects others1. Therefore, optimal management of any watershed component requires holistic consideration of the entire ecosystem. This approach necessitates detailed inventories of basic resources and their management, which must be studied synergistically to propose superior land use options. Land use planning, in essence, aims to achieve improved, optimal, and sustainable use and management of these vital resources.

Mountain ecosystems are particularly vulnerable to climate change, with warming rates often exceeding global averages2. In India, variability in rainfall and water availability across watersheds necessitates adaptive land-use planning and water management strategies3. This dynamic context emphasizes the importance of flexible, locally adapted suitability criteria.

The Nilgiris, a floristically rich mountain ecosystem in the southern state of Tamil Nadu, India, spans an area of 0.25 M ha and lies at the junction of the Eastern and Western Ghats. This south Indian hilly region, part of the Western Ghats’ agro-ecological sub-region 19.2 (Central South Sahyadris, hot, moist sub-humid to humid), is characterized by diverse agro-climatic conditions suitable for a wide range of crops. Despite its agricultural potential, farmers in the Nilgiris face significant challenges, including heavy soil loss, fragmentation of land holdings, high cultivation costs, and fluctuating prices of green tea leaves4. These issues highlight the critical need for robust land suitability criteria to guide sustainable agricultural development.

Under the National Agricultural Technology Project (NATP) sub-project on land use planning for agricultural resource management, the Thambatti watershed was selected as a representative study area. This watershed exemplifies the high rainfall, high altitude zone of the Western Ghats, a zone covering about 0.13 M ha or about 50% of the total Nilgiris district area. Detailed characteristics of the study area, including location, climate, soil, and land use, are provided in Section “Study location”.

Land suitability analysis, grounded in the FAO system, assesses how well land characteristics meet specific crop requirements to determine the optimal use of a land unit for a particular cropping system, thereby effectively answering the question of “what should be grown where?”5–7. It depends on various factors, including climate, soil, topography, land use, system, water availability, as well as socio-economic and environmental conditions. Climate change, along with factors such as soil erosion, makes it necessary to re-evaluate land suitability assessments, traditionally based on historical climate norms8.

In Thambatti watershed, initial assessments of land suitability for key crops i.e. potato, cauliflower, beans, wheat, and tea, based on existing criteria, revealed a critical disconnect: a significant portion of the area (ranging from 74.7 to 100%) was deemed “not suitable” for annual crops (potato, cauliflower, beans, wheat), and no land was found “highly suitable” for any of the tested crops. This contradicts the reality of these crops being successfully cultivated in the region for decades9. This discrepancy highlighted the necessity for developing refined land suitability criteria that accurately reflect the unique land use requirements and environmental conditions of the Nilgiris, thereby providing a more pragmatic framework for sustainable land use planning.

Globally, mountain agro-ecosystems, from the Ethiopian Highlands10 to the Andes and the Hindu Kush-Himalayan range, are characterized by diverse but often overlapping challenges governed by local environmental and socio-economic conditions. Despite the widespread application of FAO land evaluation frameworks globally, significant gaps exist in their localized adaptation for fragile mountain ecosystems like the Nilgiris. Existing land suitability criteria inadequately capture the complex interactions between steep terrain, high rainfall, and diverse land management practices in mountain agriculture. Slope gradient-based erosion assessments alone may not be adequate in capturing the mitigating influence of terracing and soil and water conservation practices implemented by hill farmers. Moreover, standardized climatic and soil thresholds developed for plains agriculture often misrepresent suitability in high-altitude regions with distinct temperature regimes and naturally acidic soils. As a result, land capability assessments may either underestimate or overestimate agricultural potential, leading to overly conservative recommendations or practices that fail to prevent land degradation. Furthermore, limited access to reliable high-resolution digital elevation model (DEM) derivatives, such as aspect and altitude grids at the 1:5000 field-survey scale in the study area, necessitated the development of a ground-truth-based index that could effectively capture terrain and management conditions using directly observed field data.

Recent studies on land suitability assessment have increasingly used remote sensing (RS) and machine learning (ML) techniques. Several studies have combined satellite-based data with multi-criteria decision analysis and indicated improved mapping accuracy to identify suitable agricultural land11–14. Collectively, these studies show that erosion risk, when integrated as a spatial constraint within suitability models, substantially alters the distribution of suitable land, with erosion-driven reclassification shifting considerable proportions of land units to lower suitability classes, particularly in mountain contexts15,16. However, some erosion parameters used in these models, like Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI)-derived cover indices or slope derivatives from high-resolution digital elevation models (DEMs), may be limited in availability in data-scarce mountain watersheds of developing regions, which can constrain their consistent application and encourage the use of field-survey-based approaches. Similarly, geospatial and machine learning (ML)-based models, including support vector machines, random forest classifiers, and deep learning models, have been used for crop suitability decisions under different agro-ecological zones17. Thakur et al.18 used Landsat-based supervised classification to quantify urban land use transformations and their environmental implications. Nandi et al.19 advanced this further by integrating deep learning (Artificial Neural Network and Cellular Automata-Markov) with Geographic Information System (GIS) for future land use land cover (LULC) prediction with uncertainty quantification. Beuria et al.20 applied spatio-temporal LULC and landscape fragmentation analysis via satellite data to decode 25 years of urban sprawl. Although these studies indicate that Remote Sensing (RS) and ML-based approaches have reached a high level of analytical sophistication, yet their implementation requires cloud-free satellite time-series, high-resolution DEMs, and substantial computational infrastructure that remain inaccessible in data-sparse hill-farming contexts such as the Nilgiris. Furthermore, these approaches may represent erosion primarily as a function of biophysical landscape attributes, with limited consideration of its dynamic interactions with active land management practices, particularly in terraced mountain watersheds. Given these practical limitations, a simpler and scientifically robust approach is more appropriate and sustainable for such regions.

The present study uses a locally calibrated, index-based methodology grounded in systematic field survey data, which are readily obtainable under typical resource conditions in mountain watersheds. This approach not only reduces dependence on expensive technology and infrastructure but also is reproducible and can be easily adopted by land managers and field practitioners for land suitability assessment in regions with similar agro-ecological and socio-economic conditions. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the explicit integration of a quantitative erosion hazard index, combining slope gradient with land management practices as a land suitability criteria, remains limited for mountain agro-ecosystems of the Western Ghats. While slope gradient is an important parameter in land capability classification21 and in FAO-based land evaluation systems, the present Erosion Hazard Index (EHI) integrates active land management conditions as a modifying factor of slope-induced erosion risk within a normalized index. This distinction is significant because a steep slope under well-maintained bench terraces presents fundamentally different erosion dynamics compared to the same slope under unprotected bare cultivation, a difference that only slope-based assessments fail to capture. Existing composite or hybrid erosion indices, such as the Land Evaluation and Site Assessment (LESA) system and RUSLE-integrated suitability models22,23 incorporate management factors, but do so either through separate scoring systems not embedded within the suitability criterion itself, or require data-intensive RUSLE input layers (R, K, LS, C, P factors) unavailable in many mountain watersheds. The EHI differs fundamentally in that it is a single normalized composite parameter that simultaneously encodes both the physical slope hazard and the active management mitigation within one value range (0–1), making it directly substitutable as a land suitability criterion in the FAO framework without requiring additional intermediate modelling steps.

Considering the limitations of previously developed models in this region, the present study was undertaken with the following specific objectives: (1) to develop an Erosion Hazard Index (EHI) integrating land slope and land management conditions, and refine land suitability criteria by adjusting climatic, soil, and topographic parameters, for the agro-ecological conditions of the Nilgiris; and (2) to assess and map land suitability for major crops (potato, cole and root vegetables, field beans, wheat, tea, and fruit crops) in the representative Thambatti watershed using these modified criteria.

Materials and methods

Study location

Thambatti micro-watershed, with a total area of 522.2 ha, is situated about 12 km south of Udhagamandalam town on the Udhagamandalam-Kundah Road in the Nilgiris district of Tamil Nadu, India. This watershed belongs to the Bc3e sub-watershed of the lower Bhavani sub-catchment in the Coonoor block of the Nilgiris and lies between the longitude of 76° 41′ 22″ to 76° 43′ 8″ E and latitude of 11° 20′ 24″ to 11° 21′ 56″ N. The watershed elevation ranges from 1950 to 2390 m above mean sea level, with gently sloping areas mainly confined to the lower valley of the watershed. The watershed characteristics include drainage density of 3.52 km km−2, average slope of 29.34%, elongation ratio of 0.66, and time of concentration of 25 min, indicating moderate to steep terrain with rapid runoff response. Based on the mean of climatic parameters of 20 years collected from the Meteorological Observatory at ICAR-Indian Institute of Soil and Water Conservation (IISWC), Research Farm, Udhagamandalam, April is the warmest month with a mean maximum temperature of 22.8 °C, and January is the coolest month with a mean minimum temperature of 6.6 °C. In this area, an average annual rainfall of 139 cm (as per 2004 to 2023 data) occurs, spread over all four seasons of the year, viz. December-March (winter), April–May (summer showers), June–August (southwest monsoon), and September–November (northeast monsoon), with most of the rainfall concentrated during June to November. Texture is one of the important parameters of soil, determining the physical characteristics of the soil. Eight texture classes occurred in the study area, viz., silt loam, sandy loam, sandy clay, sandy clay loam, clay, clay loam, and loam. The major area of the watershed is under the soil taxonomic classification of fine and fine-loamy, mixed, acid, isothermic, oxyaquic dystrudepts, with pH ranging from 3.90 to 5.62, EC ranging between 0.10–1.27dS m−1, and organic carbon ranging between 0.64–3.82%, and soil depth ranging from 50 to > 100 cm. The entire watershed has been classified under five major land uses, i.e., tea plantation (60.13%), vegetable cultivation (27.39%), forest (6.89%), rocky area (2.3%), and habitation (1.78%), with the remaining area (1.51%) under miscellaneous uses like roads, foot paths, etc.

Data collection

The base map was prepared from the 1:25,000 scale Survey of India toposheet, refined to 1:5000 scale through an intensive field survey conducted to cover the entire watershed, to record land management conditions, land slope, erosion features, and surface soil conditions. Soil properties, including soil texture, pH, electrical conductivity, organic carbon, and effective soil depth, were recorded through field sampling and laboratory analysis at IISWC Research Centre, Udhagamandalam. Land slope, land use, and management data were collected through plot-level field survey using a pre-structured questionnaire. The crop and soil management status were also recorded from the information on practices followed by the farmers, collected with the pre-structured questionnaire. Climatic data, including temperature and rainfall, were obtained from the meteorological observatory of IISWC, Udhagamandalam, covering 20 years from 2004 to 2023.

A total of 177 farmers from 407 families residing in the village were interviewed using a structured questionnaire covering land management practices, slope category, and cropping systems. The dominant land management practices identified across the watershed included bench terracing, contour cultivation, and cultivation on unprotected sloped land without formal conservation structures. Under tea plantations, consistently dense terrace networks were the predominant management condition observed, while annual vegetable plots on steeper slopes frequently lacked formal conservation structures. These field-observed management conditions, systematically recorded across all surveyed land units, directly informed the categorical scoring system for land management conditions in Table 2, with scores ranging from 0 (level or inward-sloping terraces, best conservation status) to 4 (unterraced sloping land, worst condition).

The equal denominator weighting of slope (Smax) and land management (LMmax) components in the EHI formula is based on the concept supported by conservation hydrology literature. This concept states that land management (particularly terracing) can effectively control slope-induced erosion under ideal conditions, and that both factors are equally important determinants of net erosion hazard at the field scale24,25. This is consistent with the USLE/RUSLE model, where the cover-management factor (C) and conservation practice factor (P) represent significant management-side controls on soil loss, showing that land management practices can substantially reduce slope-induced erosion risk23,26. The proportional weighting (slope score/slope maximum versus management score/management maximum) preserves the relative contribution of each component to the composite index, ensuring that neither factor can dominate the EHI beyond its own maximum possible range.

Modification of the methodology of land suitability classification

The study aimed to develop region-specific land suitability criteria for major crops cultivated in the Nilgiris, addressing the limitations identified in existing frameworks. The methodology was primarily based on the land evaluation method proposed by the Food and Agriculture Organization5,6, with significant modifications to better suit the local conditions of the Nilgiris represented by the Thambatti watershed.

Initially, the suitability of lands in the Thambatti watershed for potato, cauliflower, field beans, wheat, and tea was assessed using the criteria suggested by Naidu et al.27. Notably, 77.5% of the area was classified as moderately suitable for tea, which is the dominant land use in the watershed. However, considering the long-standing cultivation and productivity of these crops in the region, these results suggest that the standard suitability criteria may not fully capture the influence of local land management practices and site-specific conditions.

The relevant literature5,6,27–35 was reviewed to gather information on the specific requirements of major crops for various land features. This comprehensive consultation led to the development of modified land suitability criteria for potato (Solanum tuberosum), cole and root vegetables [cabbage (Brassica oleracea var. capitata), cauliflower (Brassica oleracea var. botrytis), broccoli (Brassica oleracea var. italica), carrot (Daucus carota), radish (Raphanus sativus)], field beans (Vicia faba), wheat (Triticum aestivum), tea (Camellia sinensis), and fruit crops [apple (Malus sp.), pear (Pyrus sp.), peach (Prunus persica), plum (Prunus sp.)] based on the FAO criteria, incorporating certain modifications.

A major modification was introduced for addressing erosion hazards, a critical concern in the hilly terrain of the Nilgiris, based on the approach of Erosion Susceptibility Index (ESI) by Singh et al.24,25. While the original criteria primarily considered land slope, the revised methodology incorporated an Erosion Hazard Index (EHI) to account for both land slope and land management conditions (such as terracing). The specific requirements of major crops for various land characteristics were collected from the relevant literature27–37. The Erosion Susceptibility Index (ESI) methodology developed by Singh et al.24,25 was specifically designed for the hill conditions of the Nilgiris and was the foundational technical basis for the present erosion hazard index (EHI). These documents represent the original site-specific calibration work conducted for the Thambatti watershed itself, providing a level of local relevance and context-specificity that showed the published models have not been replicated for this region. The slope-based scoring system adopted in the EHI is consistent with the established principle that slope gradient is the dominant topographic driver of erosion susceptibility in mountain watersheds38,39. The land management scoring reflects field-observed conservation measures recorded during the intensive plot-level survey of the watershed.

For example, consider a land unit under annual crop cultivation with a slope of 10–15%. This gives a slope score (Sv) of 4 (Table 2). If the land has moderately outward-sloping terraces (2.5–6.5% outward slope), the land management score (LMv) is 2 (Table 2). The maximum slope score (Smax) for annual crops is 9, and the maximum management score (LMmax) is 4. For annual crops such as potato, the EHI is: (4 + 2) / (9 + 4) = 6/13 = 0.46, which falls within the moderately suitable (S2) range for potato (S2 threshold: 0.39–0.47). For perennial crops such as tea, the same land unit is evaluated using perennial slope scores from Table 2: the slope of 10–15% receives a score of Sv = 1 (5–15% range), Smax = 4, and LMv = 2 (moderately outward-sloping terraces), LMmax = 4. The EHI for tea evaluation is therefore: (1 + 2)/(4 + 4) = 3/8 = 0.38, which lies within or near the lower boundary of the highly suitable (S1) range for tea. This example also shows the influence of conservation measures. Upgrading the terraces from moderately outward-sloping (LMv = 2) to level or inward-sloping (LMv = 0) reduces the EHI to: (4 + 0) / (9 + 4) = 4/13 = 0.31. This improvement shifts the land unit from S2 to S1 for potato (S1 threshold: EHI < 0.39), indicating that adoption of better land management can improve the suitability class without any change in the physical slope.

Rainfall erosivity was not included as a spatially variable parameter in the EHI because 20-year rainfall records from the single meteorological station (IISWC Research Farm, Udhagamandalam) were used as a long-term mean for the entire watershed, and no sub-watershed spatial variability in rainfall was established. The high-rainfall regime of the Nilgiris (mean 139 cm year−1) is instead captured through the adjusted growing-period thresholds and the selection of crops suited to the local hydro-climatic regime.

Rainfall erosivity (R-factor) in the Nilgiris typically ranges from 600 to 900 MJ mm ha⁻1 h⁻1 yr⁻1, placing it among the highest erosivity zones in peninsular India. While this uniformly high erosivity underpins the design of EHI thresholds (which implicitly assume the regional rainfall regime), its omission as a spatially variable term means the EHI conservatively represents hazard under mean climatic conditions. In plots receiving above-average rainfall, actual erosion hazard may be underestimated by the EHI, particularly for land units classified near suitability class boundaries. This constitutes a recognized limitation of the model.

For annual crops, slope categories were scored from 0 (lowest erosion risk, nearly level land with slope 0–1%) to 9 (highest erosion risk, extremely steep slopes > 100%), while for perennial crops the scores ranged from 0 (slope 0–5%) to 4 (slope > 50%), representing their comparatively lower erosion sensitivity. Land management conditions were scored from 0 (best conservation status, level or inward-sloping terraces) to 4 (worst condition, unterraced sloping land) for both crop types. The suitability class for each land unit was determined by evaluating the computed EHI value and the corresponding soil and climatic parameters against the modified criteria presented in Tables 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9. Using the land evaluation principle of the FAO, the overall suitability class of each land unit was determined by its most limiting parameter, that is, the parameter with the greatest deviation from the optimal range for the respective crop.

It is acknowledged that parameters like aspect, altitude, and remotely sensed vegetation indices could further refine the erosion hazard estimation. However, reliable high-resolution digital elevation model (DEM) data were limited for the study area at the time of this study. The intensive plot-by-plot field survey conducted across the entire watershed compensated for these limitations to the extent possible. It may be noted that altitude is implicitly addressed through the locally adjusted temperature data used in the suitability criteria, while aspect-related slope exposure effects are partially captured through the land management scoring, particularly the terrace type and terrace slope condition categories defined in Table 2, ranging from level or inward-sloping terraces (score 0, lowest erosion risk) to unterraced sloping land (score 4, highest erosion risk). Future refinements of the EHI, including RUSLE-based erosion factors or ML-derived spatial indices, would further strengthen the predictive accuracy and broader applicability of this model.

Beyond erosion hazards, adjustments were also made to the ranges of other crucial suitability characteristics, including mean temperature during the growing season, soil drainage, soil texture, and soil pH, to align with the specific requirements of crops in the Nilgiris. These modifications were applied to the framework initially suggested by Naidu et al.27. Accordingly, the comprehensive land suitability criteria for potato, cole and root vegetables, field beans, wheat, tea, and fruit crops in the Nilgiris were developed and are presented in detail in subsequent sections. These refined criteria aim to provide a more accurate and practical assessment of land suitability, addressing the observed discrepancies and supporting sustainable agricultural planning in the region.

Validation of the modified land suitability model

The modified land suitability criteria were validated by comparison of the classified suitability map with the actual land use map of the Thambatti watershed. The assumption underlying this validation is that long-established, economically feasible land uses represent effectively suitable conditions. The matching between predicted high/moderate suitability and existing crop land was assessed by calculating the percentage match between (i) lands classified as ‘highly’ or ‘moderately suitable’ for tea under the modified criteria and the 60.13% under active tea plantation, and (ii) ‘highly’ or ‘moderately suitable’ for annual vegetables and the 27.39% under active vegetable cultivation. In addition, farmer interviews (n = 177) were used to verify that the suitability classifications were consistent with observed crop performance across different land units.

It is recognized that quantitative accuracy metrics such as the Kappa coefficient or RMSE are standard tools for evaluating classified map outputs derived from remote sensing or model-predicted continuous surfaces. However, their direct application in the present study may be constrained by the fundamental nature of the validation design. The FAO-based land suitability classification produces ordinal suitability classes (S1, S2, S3, N) for each land unit, which generally represent qualitative matching of land characteristics to crop requirements rather than a probabilistic or continuous spatial prediction. Kappa statistics typically require a pixel- or polygon-level reference classification of the same thematic type, that is, an independently derived suitability map against which the predicted classes can be cross-tabulated. An independent suitability reference map is not currently available for the Thambatti watershed, as the present study represents one of the first formal land suitability assessments at this mapping scale. Similarly, RMSE is primarily applicable to continuous variable predictions and may not be directly meaningful for ordinal suitability class outputs. The land-use consistency approach adopted here, comparing model-predicted high and moderate suitability against long-established, economically viable crop distributions, is a well-recognized and widely used validation strategy for FAO-based land suitability assessments in contexts where independent reference data are unavailable21. This approach is particularly appropriate when, as in the present case, the actual land use itself represents decades of farmer-led empirical validation of land suitability under local conditions. The strong correspondence between predicted suitability and observed crop distribution, with 81.8% (Table 10) of the watershed predicted as suitable for tea, matching 60.13% under active tea plantation. In contrast, for annual vegetables, only 15.1% of the assessed area was classified as highly or moderately suitable, compared to 27.39% of the watershed currently under vegetable cultivation. This indicates that a substantial proportion of existing vegetable cultivation occurs outside the predicted suitable zones. This discrepancy likely reflects a combination of two factors: farmers cultivating in genuinely marginal or unsuitable areas driven by economic compulsions and supported by local management practices such as terracing, and the model being relatively restrictive for annual crops, potentially underestimating crop adaptability under intensive local management conditions. The primary quantitative analysis in this study involved the computation of area statistics (hectares and percentage) for each suitability class under both original and modified criteria for all crop groups, as presented in Tables 1 and 10. Descriptive range values were computed for all soil parameters across land units. Results

Development of erosion hazard index for different crops

Before suitability classification was conducted, the spatial distribution of key land characteristics across the Thambatti watershed was obtained through an intensive field survey. Land slope across the watershed ranged widely, with gently sloping areas mainly confined to the lower valley and the majority of the watershed characterized by moderate to steep terrain (average slope 29.34%). Soil pH showed considerable spatial variability across land units, ranging from strongly acidic to moderately acidic. Eight soil texture classes were identified across the watershed, including loam, silt loam, sandy loam, sandy clay, sandy clay loam, clay, and clay loam. These spatially variable land characteristics formed the direct input layers for the Erosion Hazard Index (EHI) computation and subsequent suitability classification.

The EHI was successfully developed and calibrated for six major crop categories cultivated in the Nilgiris, representing both annual and perennial cropping systems. The EHI thresholds established for different suitability classes (Table 3) revealed distinct erosion tolerance patterns between crop types, with annual crops showing significantly narrower tolerance ranges compared to perennial species. Among annual crops, wheat emerged as the most erosion-sensitive with EHI values below 0.31 (Table 3) for high suitability classification. Potato, along with cole and root vegetables, exhibited moderate erosion sensitivity with an EHI of 0.39 for high suitability, while field beans showed relatively greater tolerance with a threshold of 0.47. The narrow tolerance ranges for annual crops (spanning 0.15–0.23 EHI units between highly suitable and unsuitable categories) suggest their vulnerability to soil loss in steep terrain, primarily attributed to frequent soil disturbance, shallow root systems, and limited ground cover during critical erosion periods.

Perennial crops showed broader erosion tolerance ranges compared to annual crops. Among them, tea showed the most distinctive suitability pattern. Unlike annual crops, which require low EHI values for optimal suitability, tea was optimally suited at moderate EHI values (0.37–0.63). This unusual pattern arises because tea has two opposing site requirements. At very low EHI values (below 0.12), the land is nearly level or flat, making it prone to waterlogging. At very high EHI values (above 0.88), the slopes are excessively steep, causing soil loss that exceeds what even tea’s well-established root systems and continuous canopy cover can tolerate. Fruit crops showed high suitability at EHI values below 0.55, with a broader tolerance range extending to 0.88 for marginal suitability, indicating superior erosion resilience compared to annual crops.

The reliability of the modified assessment was evaluated by overlaying the suitability maps onto the watershed land-use map. For tea, 81.8% of the watershed was classified as highly or moderately suitable, compared to the 60.13% actually under tea cultivation, indicating that the model’s predicted suitable area encompasses and exceeds the existing tea cultivation extent, suggesting broad consistency between predicted suitability and observed land use. For annual vegetables, the model classified 15.1% of the area as highly or moderately suitable, which is considerably lower than the 27.39% currently under cultivation. This discrepancy suggests that a significant portion of vegetable cultivation is taking place under suboptimal or marginal conditions, or that the model may be conservative in estimating suitability for annual crops in managed hill systems.

Modified land suitability criteria for potato

The modified land suitability criteria for potato (Table 4) integrated climatic, edaphic, and topographic parameters tailored to the Nilgiris ecosystem. Highly suitable lands were characterized by mean growing season temperatures of 15–22 °C and growing periods of more than 140 days. Soils were well-drained to moderately well-drained, with effective soil depths greater than 75 cm. Organic carbon was higher than 0.75%, and pH ranged from 4.5 to 7.0. The Erosion Hazard Index (EHI) values were below 0.39.

Spatial analysis of the Thambatti watershed revealed severe limitations for potato cultivation across the landscape. Highly suitable lands comprised merely 34.5 ha (6.9% of the assessed watershed area), predominantly confined to gently sloping valley floors with well-implemented terrace systems. Moderately suitable lands accounted for 40.9 ha (8.2%), representing areas with slightly higher slopes or marginal deviations from optimal soil conditions but still manageable with intensive conservation measures. Marginally suitable lands covered 105.7 ha (21.1%). These areas showed suboptimal conditions across multiple parameters. EHI values (0.48–0.54) have indicated substantial erosion risk. The majority of the watershed (319.8 ha; 63.8%) was classified as unsuitable. This was due to high erosion hazard, steep slopes exceeding safe limits, and inadequate land management.

The primary limiting factor for potato cultivation was identified as high erosion hazard resulting from the interaction of steep land slopes (frequently exceeding 25%) and insufficient land management interventions. Secondary constraints included inadequate soil depth in upper slope positions, imperfect drainage in valley bottoms, and suboptimal pH levels in certain land units.

Modified land suitability criteria for cole and root vegetables

Cole vegetables (cabbage, cauliflower, broccoli) and root vegetables (carrot, radish) exhibited similar agro-ecological requirements. They require cool growing season temperatures of 14–20 °C and growing periods exceeding 100 days. Soils should be well-drained with effective depths greater than 75 cm (Table 5). These crops showed high sensitivity to waterlogging. They require well-drained to moderately well-drained conditions. Optimal performance occurs in loamy to sandy loam soils with organic carbon above 0.75% and pH between 4.5 to 7.0. The EHI threshold for high suitability was established at values below 0.39, identical to that of potato, due to comparable erosion sensitivity.

The spatial distribution pattern for cole and root vegetables mirrored that of potato precisely, reflecting their similar climatic and edaphic requirements and erosion sensitivity. The suitability classes were distributed as follows: highly suitable lands covered 34.5 ha (6.9%), moderately suitable lands encompassed 40.9 ha (8.2%), marginally suitable lands extended over 105.7 ha (21.1%), and unsuitable lands dominated 319.8 ha (63.8%).

The critical limiting factor remained high erosion hazard induced by steep terrain and inadequate conservation structures. The limited ‘highly suitable’ area suggests the necessity for strategic site selection and intensive soil conservation investments for sustainable vegetable production in the Nilgiris.

Modified land suitability criteria for field beans

Field beans showed distinct requirements compared to vegetable crops, necessitating longer growing periods (> 120 days), broader temperature tolerance (18–30 °C), and specific soil chemical properties (Table 6). The crop showed particular sensitivity to soil acidity, with optimal pH ranging from 5.5 to 7.5, and required well-drained loam to sandy loam soils with effective depths exceeding 75 cm. The EHI threshold for high suitability was set at 0.47, indicating marginally greater erosion tolerance compared to potato and root/cole vegetables.

Suitability analysis revealed a distinctive pattern for field beans, with no highly suitable lands identified within the Thambatti watershed. This absence was primarily due to widespread soil acidity constraints. Soil pH across much of the watershed (3.90–5.62) was below the optimal range of 5.5–7.5. This limits nodulation and nitrogen fixation. Moderately suitable lands covered 117.3 ha (23.4%). Soil acidity in these areas was suboptimal but within tolerable limits. It could also be improved through liming. Marginally suitable lands encompassed 157.1 ha (31.4%), characterized by multiple limiting factors, including high acidity and elevated erosion hazard. Unsuitable lands comprised 226.5 ha (45.2%), predominantly areas with severe acidity (pH < 4.0) or excessive erosion risk (EHI > 0.69). The dual limitation of soil acidity and erosion hazard presents significant challenges for field bean cultivation.

Modified land suitability criteria for wheat

Wheat cultivation in the Nilgiris is constrained by specific climatic requirements, with optimal mean growing season temperatures of 15–24 °C and extended growing periods exceeding 150 days (Table 7). The crop requires well-drained soils, with poor or excessive drainage being highly detrimental, and performs optimally in sandy clay to clay loam textures with organic carbon above 0.75% and pH ranging from 5.5–8.0. Effective soil depth requirements exceed 75 cm, and wheat showed the highest erosion sensitivity among all studied crops, with an EHI value of merely 0.31 for high suitability.

Suitability assessment revealed severe constraints for wheat cultivation across the watershed landscape. No highly suitable lands were identified, with temperature emerging as the overriding limiting factor. Significant portions of the watershed experience temperatures outside the optimal 15–24 °C range during critical growth stages. This includes excessive coolness at higher elevations or inadequate vernalization. Moderately suitable lands covered 75.4 ha (15.1%), confined to specific elevational zones with favorable temperature regimes and lower erosion hazard. Marginally suitable lands comprised 72.0 ha (14.4%), characterized by suboptimal temperatures and moderate erosion risk. The largest proportion, 353.5 ha (70.6%), was classified as unsuitable due to combined temperature limitations and excessive erosion hazard (EHI > 0.54).

The extremely restrictive EHI threshold for wheat (< 0.31 for high suitability) makes it the most erosion-vulnerable crop evaluated, requiring nearly level terrain or exceptionally well-engineered conservation structures. The compounding effects of temperature constraints and erosion sensitivity severely limit wheat cultivation potential in the Nilgiris ecosystem.

Modified land suitability criteria for tea

Tea exhibited the most favorable suitability profile among all evaluated crops. This reflects its strong adaptation to the agro-ecological conditions of the Nilgiris (Table 8). Optimal conditions encompass mean growing season temperatures of 15–20 °C, relative humidity exceeding 70%, and very long growing periods (> 240 days). The crop requires well-drained soils with loamy to sandy clay textures, critically demanding strongly acidic pH (4.0–5.0), high organic carbon content (> 0.75%), and deep soil profiles exceeding 100 cm. Tea showed exceptional tolerance to moderate erosion hazard, with optimal suitability occurring at EHI values of 0.37–0.63, and high sensitivity to salinity.

The analysis revealed tea as the predominant suitable crop across the watershed landscape. Highly suitable lands comprised 187.1 ha (37.4%), concentrated in mid-slope positions with moderate slopes, well-established terrace systems, and strongly acidic soils. Moderately suitable lands covered an extensive 222.2 ha (44.4%), representing areas with slight deviations from optimal conditions but still highly productive for tea cultivation. Combined, highly and moderately suitable lands totaled 409.3 ha (81.8%), validating tea as the optimal land use for the majority of the watershed. Marginally suitable lands accounted for 72.8 ha (14.5%), characterized by either excessively steep slopes (EHI > 0.76) or inadequate acidity. Only 18.8 ha (3.8%) was classified as unsuitable, primarily representing either flat, poorly drained valley bottoms (EHI < 0.12) or extremely steep, unstable slopes (EHI > 0.88).

The spatial distribution pattern strongly corroborates existing land use, where tea plantations occupy 60.13% of the watershed area. The high suitability reflects tea’s adaptation to acidic soils (pH 4.0–5.0) and its tolerance for moderate slopes through extensive root systems. It also requires minimal soil disturbance. These traits make it well-suited for sustainable agriculture in the erosion-prone Nilgiris landscape.

Modified land suitability criteria for fruit crops

Temperate fruit crops, including apple, pear, peach, and plum, indicated specific requirements for moderately cool temperatures (16–22 °C), extended growing periods (> 150 days), and well-drained loamy soils with high organic carbon content (> 0.75%) (Table 9). These deep-rooted perennial species require effective soil depths exceeding 100 cm, pH ranging from 5.5–7.5 for optimal nutrient availability, low salinity and sodicity levels, and moderate erosion tolerance with EHI thresholds below 0.55 for high suitability.

Suitability evaluation revealed substantial constraints for fruit crop establishment in the Thambatti watershed. No highly suitable lands were identified, reflecting the intersection of multiple limiting factors. Moderately suitable lands covered only 62.9 ha (12.6%), confined to specific microclimatic zones with relatively warmer temperatures and lower acidity. The dominant classification was marginally suitable, encompassing 402.0 ha (80.3%), indicating widespread potential with significant limitations requiring amelioration. Unsuitable lands comprised 36.0 ha (7.2%), primarily areas with extreme acidity or excessive slopes.

The primary limiting factors were identified as: (1) low mean temperatures, with many areas failing to consistently meet the 16–22 °C optimal range throughout the growing season, particularly affecting heat-demanding species like peach and plum; (2) high soil acidity, with prevailing pH values (3.90–5.62) falling below the optimal 5.5–7.5 range, inducing aluminum and manganese toxicity and phosphorus fixation; and (3) heavy soil texture, with clay-dominated profiles restricting root penetration and drainage in certain land units.

Comparison of original and modified land suitability assessment

A comprehensive comparison between the original assessment utilizing Naidu et al.27 criteria (Table 1) and the modified criteria (Table 10) revealed substantial differences, providing supportive evidence for the refined methodology. The original assessment yielded highly conservative results, classifying 74.7–100% of the watershed as unsuitable for annual crops (potato, cauliflower, beans, wheat) and identifying zero highly suitable lands for any crop, including tea. Specifically, potato showed 74.7% unsuitable area, cauliflower and beans exhibited 79.9% and 81.9% unsuitable, respectively, and wheat showed 100% unsuitability. Even tea, the dominant and economically successful crop, was assessed as having only 77.5% moderately suitable area, with no highly suitable lands.

On the other hand, the modified criteria incorporating the Erosion Hazard Index and adjusted climatic and soil parameters yielded results congruent with observed agricultural reality. For tea, 37.4% was classified as highly suitable and 44.4% as moderately suitable, totaling 81.8% in optimal categories. Potato, cole, and root vegetables showed 6.9% highly suitable and 8.2% moderately suitable areas, representing realistic cultivation potential in carefully managed zones. Field beans showed 23.4% moderate suitability, wheat showed 15.1% moderate suitability, and fruit crops exhibited 12.6% moderate suitability, with 80.3% marginal suitability indicating developmental potential.

The contrast between the original criteria, which classified no land as highly suitable for any crop, and the modified assessment, which identified 37.4% highly suitable for tea and 6.9% for vegetable crops, indicates that incorporating region-specific criteria, particularly the EHI and locally adjusted climatic and soil thresholds, produces results more consistent with observed agricultural conditions in the Nilgiris. This improvement stems from: (1) integration of land management practices into erosion assessment through EHI, recognizing that terracing substantially mitigates slope-induced erosion risk; (2) adjustment of temperature thresholds to reflect high-altitude tropical mountain conditions rather than temperate or lowland tropical norms; and (3) recalibration of soil pH thresholds to acknowledge successful crop adaptation to the naturally acidic soils prevalent in the Western Ghats.

The improvement of reliability comes from three mechanistic changes: first, the EHI captures the mitigating role of terracing, which is a well-documented practice for land management and substantially reduces slope-induced erosion. This factor was absent in only slope-based assessments. Second, the temperature threshold adjustment for high-altitude tropical conditions eliminates the systematic misclassification of Nilgiris lands as ‘too cool’ for temperate crops when compared against lowland criteria. Third, the pH recalibration for acid-tolerant tea and vegetable varieties removes artificial penalties for naturally acidic soils of the Western Ghats. However, the study also acknowledges that the integration of topographic indices (aspect, topographic wetness index (TWI), and altitude) through high-resolution digital elevation model (DEM) analysis could further improve classification accuracy and may be included in future work.

A quantitative comparison of the area distribution across suitability classes under the original and modified criteria (Tables 1 and 10) reveals substantial reclassifications for all crops. For tea, the highly suitable area increased from 0% under the original criteria to 37.4% under the modified criteria, while the unsuitable area decreased from 4.8 to 3.8%. Under wheat, 100% of the watershed was classified as unsuitable in the original criteria, while it was 70.6% under modified criteria, with 15.1% now classified as moderately suitable. Similar improvements were observed in other annual crop groups, thus confirming that the modified EHI-based criteria produce meaningfully different and more realistic suitability distributions compared to conventional assessments.

The spatial distribution maps (Fig. 2a, b) illustrate distinct patterns between annual and perennial crops. Annual crops (Fig. 2a) show highly fragmented suitability, concentrated in valley floors and well-terraced gentle slopes, while perennial crops (Fig. 2b) demonstrate more continuous suitable zones across mid-slope positions, reflecting their superior erosion tolerance and adaptation to acidic soils.

Discussion

Modification of traditional land suitability criteria is critically important in erosion-prone mountainous areas because conventional assessments often do not adequately account for the dynamic and detrimental impacts of soil loss on long-term productivity. In such regions, factors like slope, rainfall intensity, soil erodibility (texture, organic matter, structure), and existing land cover become paramount38. Land degradation risk mapping that integrates soil erosion susceptibility has become increasingly important for sustainable land management in vulnerable ecosystems39. Simply identifying areas suitable for a crop based on climate and basic soil fertility without a rigorous erosion hazard assessment can lead to unsustainable practices, rapid topsoil loss, decreased water infiltration, and reduced nutrient availability, ultimately rendering the land unproductive. The limited suitability for potato in mountain watersheds reflects a broader challenge documented in similar ecosystems worldwide. The limited highly suitable area for annual vegetable crops (6.9%) in the Thambatti watershed is consistent with broader observations from mountain agro-ecosystems, where steep terrain and erosion risk frequently restrict the proportion of land suitable for annual cropping to a small fraction of the watershed10. Similarly, studies from the Loess Plateau of China have shown that only a limited proportion of mountainous watersheds is suitable for cropping, with large areas constrained by erosion risk and soil limitations, further corroborating that the EHI thresholds developed in this study fall within the expected range of erosion-suitability constraints in comparable mountain ecosystems40. The brief growing cycle and frequent soil disturbance associated with annual vegetable production make these crops particularly vulnerable to erosion in steep terrain. The 15.1% of the watershed with high to moderate suitability for potato likely represents areas with lower slopes, well-implemented terracing systems, or both. These limited suitable areas suggest that intensive potato production should be concentrated in these zones with robust soil conservation measures, while alternative land uses should be promoted in less suitable areas. However, the fact that the actual vegetable cultivation area (27.39%) exceeds the predicted suitable area indicates that farmers are extensively cultivating vegetables even in marginal or unsuitable zones, likely driven by economic returns and supported by local management practices such as terracing and input use.

The marginally suitable areas (21.1%) for cole and root vegetables may be cautiously utilized with significant investment in erosion control infrastructure and careful management practices. However, for sustainable production, these areas would require implementation of bench terracing, contour cultivation, and adequate vegetative barriers to minimize soil loss during the cropping season. The findings for field beans reflect broader patterns of legume adaptation in tropical mountain environments. Research on climate-smart agriculture in tropical mountains has emphasized that leguminous crops face significant challenges in high-altitude tropical regions due to soil acidity and moisture extremes41. The moderate performance of field beans suggests potential for improvement through the selection of acid-tolerant varieties and the implementation of soil amendment strategies. Studies in similar acidic soil conditions have also indicated that liming, combined with organic matter additions, can significantly expand the suitable area for leguminous crops42,43. Research on climate-smart agriculture in tropical mountains has emphasized that traditional temperate cereals like wheat face significant challenges in high-altitude tropical regions due to temperature fluctuations and moisture extremes41. The limited suitability observed for wheat in this study aligns with these global observations and suggests that wheat cultivation in the Nilgiris should be limited to carefully selected areas with optimal temperature regimes and effective erosion control measures. The strong performance of tea in the modified assessment reflects several favorable factors: its preference for acidic soils (pH 4.0–5.0), tolerance for moderate erosion hazard (EHI 0.37–0.63), and adaptation to high-altitude, high-rainfall conditions. The dominance of tea suitability aligns with regional agricultural patterns in similar mountain ecosystems globally. Research on tea cultivation in mountainous regions of China has indicated that tea plants are well-adapted to acidic soils and steep slopes when appropriate terracing practices are implemented26. Studies on tea habitat suitability under climate change scenarios have indicated that high-altitude regions with acidic soils and adequate rainfall will likely remain suitable or even expand their tea-growing potential36. The moderate EHI tolerance of tea makes it particularly suitable for sustainable agriculture in erosion-prone mountain landscapes, where perennial crops with extensive root systems provide better soil stabilization than annual crops. In contrast to rain-fed annual crops, which are often restricted to limited suitable areas in steep mountain watersheds due to erosion constraints, perennial crops such as tea shows substantially higher suitability under well-terraced conditions, as reflected in the 81.8% combined S1 + S2 suitability observed in this study and consistent with broader evidence that perennial systems are better adapted to erosion-prone mountain environments10,40.

However, the 3.8% of watershed area classified as not suitable for tea (primarily areas with EHI < 0.12 or > 0.88) highlights that even well-adapted crops have limitations in extreme conditions. Areas with very low EHI typically represent flat or gently sloping lands that may experience waterlogging, while areas with very high EHI indicate excessively steep slopes where even perennial crops cannot adequately prevent erosion. The limited highly suitable area (0%) for fruit crops suggests that successful fruit production would require variety selection adapted to local conditions and potentially soil amendments to modify pH. The marginal suitability classification for 80.3% of the area indicates potential for fruit production with appropriate management interventions. Recent advances in fruit crop cultivation have demonstrated that variety selection and rootstock choice can significantly extend the suitable range beyond traditionally defined parameters. Temperate fruit cultivation in mountain regions with naturally acidic soils and cool temperatures has been documented to succeed with appropriate variety selection and soil amendment strategies29,30. However, the high proportion of marginally suitable land suggests that fruit crops should not be prioritized over tea or other better-adapted crops without substantial investment in site-specific research and development. The observed transformation in assessment outcomes demonstrates a critical principle in land suitability evaluation: universal criteria, even when scientifically sound for their original contexts, may poorly represent conditions in diverse agro-ecological settings. Recent literature on agricultural land suitability assessment emphasizes the necessity of integrating local knowledge, site-specific data, and adaptive criteria development to achieve meaningful results. This explicitly recognizes the need for local adaptation of criteria and thresholds21. While the three-factor modification (erosion management integration, temperature recalibration, pH recalibration) represents a significant advancement, it does not fully capture all dimensions of land suitability and should be viewed as a step toward more comprehensive, context-specific evaluation frameworks. The FAO land evaluation criteria, while providing excellent conceptual guidance, explicitly recognize the need for local adaptation of criteria and thresholds. A holistic watershed suitability tool would additionally incorporate: (1) rainfall erosivity (R-factor), particularly relevant given the monsoon-dominated precipitation regime of the Nilgiris; (2) hydrological parameters such as runoff coefficient, percolation potential, and groundwater recharge; and (3) socio-economic suitability layers including market access, labour availability, and input costs. The inclusion of these additional parameters represents a clearer approach toward a more comprehensive and replicable model for mountain watershed land suitability assessment. Slope gradient has been consistently identified as a primary parameter in land suitability criteria21. The EHI incorporates land management conditions alongside slope, allowing consideration of conservation measures that reduce erosion risk. In comparison to RUSLE-based approaches23,44, the EHI requires less data while still including the dominant topographic and management factors of erosion in smallholder hill-farming systems. The results are consistent with broader findings from some mountain watershed studies10,15, which have documented that annual crop suitability in steep terrain is restricted to a small fraction of watershed area due to a combination of topographic, edaphic, and climatic constraints. Thus, these findings support the broader applicability of erosion-integrated suitability models across mountain agro-ecosystems with comparable terrain and management conditions. The suitability assessment results provide clear guidance for sustainable land use planning in Nilgiris. Tea cultivation should remain the dominant land use, concentrated in 81.8% of the watershed with high to moderate suitability. Limited areas highly suitable for annual vegetables (6.9%) should be carefully protected and intensively managed with appropriate erosion control measures. The 23.4% moderately suitable area for field beans presents opportunities for crop diversification and nitrogen fixation benefits. Marginally suitable areas for various crops should be evaluated carefully, with conservation agriculture principles and soil improvement strategies guiding their utilization. The ecosystem balance implicit in the modified suitability criteria is reflected in several key outcomes. First, a large proportion of the watershed remains classified as unsuitable or marginally suitable for most annual crops, providing a substantial conservation buffer that limits agricultural expansion into ecologically sensitive areas, as detailed in Table 10. Second, the EHI threshold system preserves the highest-erosion-risk areas as unsuitable for all crops, thereby protecting the steepest slopes from destabilization. Third, tea is identified as optimal for a major portion of the watershed and provides superior ground cover and biomass accumulation compared to annual crops, supporting ecosystem services such as carbon sequestration and reduction of soil erosion. The findings from this study have significant implications extending beyond the immediate study area. Mountain ecosystems globally face similar challenges of steep terrain, high erosion risk, and unique climatic conditions that complicate agricultural land use planning. The methodology developed here, integrating erosion hazard indices with climate and soil parameters in a locally adapted system, provides a replicable approach for other mountain regions. Recent global assessments of agricultural land suitability under climate change scenarios indicate that mountain regions will experience significant shifts in crop suitability patterns45. Beyond their scientific contribution, the findings of this study carry direct policy relevance for sustainable agricultural management in the Nilgiris and similar mountain agro-ecosystems. The refined land suitability criteria provide a science-based model for agricultural policies that can optimize crop selection while minimizing environmental degradation in erosion-prone mountain regions. These results can support government schemes for crop diversification, agricultural subsidies, and agricultural insurance programs by identifying areas where specific crops can be sustainably cultivated. Furthermore, the integration of erosion hazard indices with land management practices offers policymakers a practical tool for making location-specific soil conservation programs and incentivizing farmers to adopt appropriate terracing and land management techniques. Therefore, this study contributes to the broader goals of watershed-based development and climate-resilient agriculture in the Western Ghats region.

Despite the broader implications, this study also has some limitations that must be considered when interpreting and applying its findings. First, the erosion hazard index (EHI) is based on two parameters (slope gradient and land management condition); future studies should include rainfall erosivity, as rainfall-induced hydrodynamics are a primary cause of erosion in the Nilgiris. Second, validation relied on land use consistency rather than measured crop yield data, which, while pragmatic, cannot fully quantify predictive accuracy. Third, the management scores in Table 2 were assigned through expert judgment informed by field survey data and literature review; formal calibration against measured soil loss rates, such as those derivable from RUSLE (revised universal soil loss equation) using rainfall erosivity (R), soil erodibility (K), slope length-steepness (LS), cover management (C), and conservation practice (P) factors, would substantially strengthen their scientific basis. Such RUSLE-based data were unavailable at the required spatial resolution for this watershed at the time of the study. Fourth, the spatial analysis was conducted at the watershed scale (Thambatti, 522.2 ha); broader application requires recalibration for areas with different soil characteristics, rainfall regimes, and land management practices. A fifth limitation concerns parameter uncertainty and classification boundary sensitivity. The EHI is a deterministic index based on discrete slope and land management categories, which are subject to observational uncertainty, particularly near class boundaries. Small variations in input scores may shift EHI values enough to alter suitability classification for borderline land units. Future studies should include sensitivity analysis to quantify this uncertainty.

Despite these limitations, the developed EHI model represents a meaningful and replicable methodological advancement for land suitability assessment in mountain agro-ecosystems where data availability and technical capacity constrain the use of more complex models.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed a region-specific land suitability assessment framework for the Nilgiris hills by integrating an Erosion Hazard Index (EHI) with locally calibrated climatic and edaphic parameters. The modified approach substantially improved consistency with observed agricultural conditions compared to conventional criteria, which classified 74.7–100% of the Thambatti watershed as unsuitable for annual crops and identified no highly suitable lands for any crop, contradicting decades of successful cultivation in the region.

The EHI-based assessment revealed that tea is optimally suited to the Nilgiris ecosystem, with 37.4% highly suitable and 44.4% moderately suitable lands, totaling 81.8% in optimal categories, validating the current land use pattern where tea occupies 60.13% of the watershed. Annual crops showed narrow erosion tolerance and limited suitable areas. Potato, cole, and root vegetables showed only 6.9% highly suitable and 8.2% moderately suitable lands, constrained primarily by high erosion hazard (EHI > 0.54 for potato; EHI > 0.62 for cole and root vegetables). The wheat crop had no highly suitable areas and only 15.1% moderate suitability, limited by temperature constraints and extreme erosion sensitivity. Field beans showed no highly suitable lands, but 23.4% moderate suitability, constrained by soil acidity. Fruit crops demonstrated no highly suitable areas but 80.3% (402.0 ha) marginal suitability, limited by low temperatures, high soil acidity, and heavy soil texture.

The erosion hazard index developed in this study advances beyond only slope-based land evaluation approaches by jointly integrating slope gradient with active land management conditions into a single normalized parameter. This offers a replicable and practically applicable tool for sustainable agricultural planning in mountain agro-ecosystems where smallholder farmers have continued to maintain well-adapted conservation practices.
