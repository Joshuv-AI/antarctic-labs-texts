Early cardiac rehabilitation in pediatric fenestrated Fontan completion: A case report despite postoperative complication
Authors: Roberta da Silva Teixeira, Luciana S. M. Luz, Helica L. G. M. Lopes, Karyne K. Bernandes, Fellipe A. M. Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly rehabilitation after Fontan surgery in children is poorly described, especially in complicated postoperative courses. We report the clinical evolution and rehabilitation of an 8-year-old girl with double-inlet left ventricle and patent ductus arteriosus who underwent fenestrated Fontan completion. The postoperative period was complicated by bilateral pleural effusions, pulmonary hypertension, hematemesis, psychomotor alteration, and mechanical ventilation with inhaled nitric oxide. A structured bedside rehabilitation program was started early and adjusted according to clinical stability. The program included respiratory exercises, inspiratory muscle training, resistance exercises, cycle ergometry, and gait training. We observed progressive improvement in mobility, respiratory autonomy, and functional capacity. She was discharged from the pediatric cardiac intensive care unit on postoperative day 23, having demonstrated functional recovery. This was evidenced by a reduction in the Functional Status Scale score from 14 to 9. Early individualized rehabilitation after Fontan surgery was feasible and associated with meaningful functional improvement.

INTRODUCTION

Congenital heart defects such as double-inlet left ventricle and patent ductus arteriosus pose hemodynamic challenges. Double-inlet left ventricle is exceptionally uncommon (0.003–0.009 per 1000 live births)[1] and its anatomical heterogeneity complicates management, particularly when associated with ductal patency.[2] Children with a single functional ventricle require staged palliation culminating in the Fontan procedure;[3] however, evidence for early intensive care rehabilitation is virtually absent. Only isolated pediatric reports with small cohorts exist,[45] and no publications describe structured postoperative intensive rehabilitation following the Fontan procedure. This case is, therefore, unique in reporting rehabilitation despite complications following fenestrated Fontan completion.

CASE REPORT

An 8-year-old female (27 kg) with a double-inlet left ventricle associated with patent ductus arteriosus and anterior aorta, previously submitted to pulmonary artery banding and ductus ligation (10 months old) and bidirectional Glenn procedure (7 years old), was admitted for Fontan completion. She had a prior ischemic stroke with residual motor deficit.

Pre-Fontan cardiac catheterization demonstrated a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 17 mmHg. Ventricular pressure (single ventricle) was 115/0/13 mmHg. Systemic arterial pressures were 85/45 mmHg (mean: 58 mmHg) in the ascending aorta and 73/43 mmHg (mean: 54 mmHg) in the descending aorta. Angiographic evaluation showed confluent pulmonary arteries of good caliber, a patent superior cavopulmonary (Glenn) connection without obstruction, absence of significant aortopulmonary collaterals, and no relevant gradient in the descending aorta. These findings were considered favorable for Fontan completion.

Fenestrated Fontan was performed (cardiopulmonary bypass: 85 min, no aortic cross-clamping). The patient was extubated in the operating room and transferred to the pediatric cardiac intensive care unit on a nonrebreather mask (8 L/min) with oxygen saturation of 85%.

Intraoperative Fontan pressure was not systematically recorded. However, central venous pressure was monitored in the immediate postoperative period as a surrogate of Fontan circuit pressure. In addition, transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated a patent Fontan circuit with good flow, no evidence of obstruction, and a functioning 4 mm fenestration, suggesting adequate hemodynamic conditions at the end of the procedure. Early extubation was performed in the operating room to optimize Fontan physiology by promoting negative intrathoracic pressure and enhancing venous return.

A mediastinal drain was placed intraoperatively. Pleural drainage was not routinely performed, and pleural effusions were managed according to postoperative clinical evolution.

Milrinone was administered in the immediate postoperative period to support cardiac output and reduce pulmonary vascular resistance, as part of standard management in Fontan physiology. Serum lactate levels were monitored as part of routine care in the intensive care unit.

Initial functional assessment showed moderate dysfunction (Score 14 on the Pediatric Functional Status Scale). Baseline neurological assessment revealed a preserved level of consciousness and ability to follow commands, with residual motor impairment.

On postoperative day 2, in the presence of pleural effusion, rehabilitation was initiated with bed mobilization and breathing exercises. Rehabilitation sessions were conducted under predefined safety criteria, including hemodynamic stability, absence of active bleeding, controlled arrhythmias, and adequate oxygenation (SpO2 ≥85% in Fontan physiology). Monitoring included heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and perceived exertion (Modified Borg Scale), and sessions were interrupted in case of clinical deterioration.

Worsening oxygenation required 12 L/min oxygen until right thoracic drainage enabled a low-flow nasal cannula (3 L/min). Rehabilitation progressed to include sitting and standing activities, muscle strengthening guided by the Modified Borg Scale, ventilatory training, and cycle ergometer exercise monitored using the OMNI-Cycle Scale of perceived exertion for children. On day 5, recurrent pleural effusion, uremia, and psychomotor changes limited the continuation of therapy. Between days 7 and 11, tremors and hematemesis further restricted mobilization.

Day 12 endoscopy revealed esophageal lacerations, and echocardiography suggested cavopulmonary thrombosis. Carbamazepine was discontinued, and proton-pump inhibitor therapy was initiated. Systemic anticoagulation was started and titrated according to clinical status and imaging findings.

The patient subsequently underwent cardiac catheterization with stent placement in the left pulmonary artery. The postprocedural course was complicated by pulmonary hypertension, necessitating endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation, and inhaled nitric oxide (20 ppm); rehabilitation focused on ventilatory care and prevention of complications.

Following clinical stabilization, inhaled nitric oxide was discontinued on postoperative day 17. This was followed by successful extubation and reinstatement of the Fontan-specific rehabilitation program, including ventilatory pattern retraining, inspiratory muscle training, active mobilization, and assisted ambulation. Objective outcomes included reduction in oxygen requirement, successful ventilatory weaning, progression to assisted ambulation, and improved exercise tolerance.

Oxygen was withdrawn after the drain removal. She was discharged from intensive care on postoperative day 23 with mild dysfunction (Score 9) and scheduled outpatient rehabilitation follow-up [Table 1]. She spent 41 days in the ward, being discharged from the hospital on the 64th postoperative day.

DISCUSSION

This case illustrates the challenges in managing a double-inlet left ventricle associated with patent ductus arteriosus, a condition requiring staged surgery to redirect blood flow and reduce cardiac and pulmonary overload. Pulmonary artery banding is the initial treatment in pulmonary overcirculation heart failure, enabling progression to Glenn and Fontan procedures, the latter being the final stage of palliation.[3]

Pulmonary hypertension and gastrointestinal bleeding required multidisciplinary care. Pulmonary hypertension was treated with invasive mechanical ventilation, optimization of oxygenation, and inhaled nitric oxide (20 ppm) to reduce pulmonary vascular resistance, followed by gradual weaning after hemodynamic stabilization. Supportive measures included strict fluid management and close monitoring of cavopulmonary circulation. Gastrointestinal bleeding, due to esophageal lacerations confirmed on endoscopy, was managed with proton-pump inhibitor therapy and hemodynamic monitoring. Systemic anticoagulation was cautiously initiated and titrated in the context of suspected cavopulmonary thrombosis, balancing bleeding and thrombotic risks. Rehabilitation was temporarily limited during clinical instability and progressively resumed after stabilization.

Elevated Fontan pressures and reduced flow may predispose to thrombosis in the cavopulmonary circuit. In this case, the diagnosis was established after clinical deterioration, highlighting the complexity of early identification in the postoperative period.

Post-Fontan physiology predisposes patients to reduced cardiac output, impaired peripheral perfusion, and skeletal muscle weakness,[6] thereby supporting the rationale for early mobilization, aerobic conditioning, and targeted strengthening. Inspiratory muscle training may further optimize cavopulmonary hemodynamics and enhance functional capacity.[7] Early extubation is preferred; when ventilation is necessary, low positive end-expiratory pressure is recommended.[8] Oxygen therapy promotes pulmonary vasodilation, and pleural effusions may relate to cardiopulmonary bypass.

Although early extubation is recommended in Fontan patients, it should be interpreted within the broader hemodynamic context. In this case, extubation alone was insufficient to prevent subsequent complications, reinforcing the need for a respiratory strategy to be integrated with overall hemodynamic management.

Continuous monitoring with serial functional assessment guided rehabilitation.[9] The key message is that individualized, early, and progressive rehabilitation can be resumed even after severe complications, potentially improving functional recovery and postoperative outcomes.[10]

CONCLUSION

Early, structured, and individualized rehabilitation was feasible in this case and could be reintroduced after clinical deterioration in children undergoing Fontan completion. Serial functional assessment enables progression despite complications, supporting recovery and functional improvement. This case suggests that Fontan-specific rehabilitation should be considered an integral component of postoperative management.
