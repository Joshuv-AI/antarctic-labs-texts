Dynamical decoupling of interacting spins through group factorization
Colin Read, Eduardo Serrano-Ensástiga, John Martin

Abstract
Dynamical decoupling (DD) is a well-known open-loop protocol for suppressing unwanted interactions in a quantum system, thereby drastically extending the coherence time of useful quantum states. In the original framework of evolution symmetrization, a DD sequence was shown to enforce a symmetry on the unwanted Hamiltonian, thereby suppressing it if the symmetry was inaccessible. In this work, we show how symmetries already present in the undesired Hamiltonian can be harnessed to reduce the complexity of decoupling sequences and to construct nested protocols that correct dominant errors at shorter timescales, using the factorization of DD symmetry groups into a product of its subgroups. We provide many relevant examples in various spin systems, using the Majorana constellation and point-group factorization to identify and exploit symmetries in the interaction Hamiltonian. Our framework recovers tailored pulse sequences developed in the context of NMR and further produces novel short and robust sequences capable of suppressing on-site disorder, dipole-dipole interactions, and more exotic many-body interactions in spin ensembles.



I
Introduction

Quantum hardware platforms often face limitations imposed by the short coherence times of their noisy constituents, which arise from unwanted interactions either among the constituents themselves or with their surrounding environment. These interactions result in decoherence and energy dissipation, which ultimately degrades the quantum state that encodes valuable information. Protecting quantum systems from these detrimental dynamics is essential for advancing various areas of quantum technology, including quantum quantum computation

;
Bharti et al. 2022
and quantum metrology

;

;

A prominent tool for mitigating these effects in many experimental systems is dynamical decoupling (DD)

;
Viola et al. 1999
;

;

;

;

;

;

;

Viola et al. 1999
;
Zanardi 1999
, since the first DD sequences were shown to project the unwanted Hamiltonian onto a specific symmetry sector of the space of operators acting on the system ; a symmetry group associated with this symmetrization process is called a
decoupling group
. Although this approach has enabled the construction of highly robust sequences

;
Tripathi et al. 2025
;
Viola and Knill 2005

;
Khodjasteh and Viola 2009b
;
Khodjasteh et al. 2010

;

In this work, we consider the original framework of evolution symmetrization

;
Viola et al. 1999
and show how factorization of a decoupling group as a product of its subgroups can be used to harness the symmetries of a Hamiltonian to reduce the complexity of a decoupling sequence. Moreover, we show that the same framework can be used to construct nested sequences that correct dominant errors at shorter timescales. We apply this idea to various spin systems, where the relevant decoupling groups are rotational symmetry groups (

) and where the symmetries of a Hamiltonian are identified in its Majorana constellations

II
Basic concepts

II.1
A reminder of dynamical decoupling

We consider a quantum system
S
coupled to an environment or bath
B
. The interaction between the system and the bath is described by the Hamiltonian

H_{SB}=\sum_{\alpha}S_{\alpha}\otimes B_{\alpha},

(1)

which is written in Schmidt decomposition form, where
S_{\alpha}
and
B_{\alpha}
are operators acting on the Hilbert spaces of the system and bath, respectively. From this decomposition, we can define the
interaction subspace
as the vector space spanned by the system operators
S_{\alpha}
,
\mathcal{I}_{S}\equiv\mathrm{span}\quantity(\qty{S_{\alpha}})\subseteq\mathcal{B}\quantity(\Hs_S)
. Note that the interaction subspace contains all the system operators describing the interaction with the environment, but also all system-only Hamiltonian terms describing unwanted interactions within the quantum system.

The Hamiltonian (
1
) entangles the system with the bath, resulting in the scrambling of quantum information initially stored in
S
. To reduce the resulting loss of coherence in
S
, we can apply a dynamical decoupling sequence of the form
\mbox{---}P_{1}\mbox{---}P_{2}\dots\mbox{---}P_{N}
to the system. This sequence consists of
N
idealized pulses that are infinitely short and strong, where each
P_{k}
represents the unitary operator corresponding to the
k
th pulse. The dashes (
—
) denote free evolution intervals of duration
\tau_{0}
between successive pulses. In the interaction picture with respect to the Hamiltonian implementing the DD pulses (toggling frame), the interaction Hamiltonian undergoes a series of unitary transformations

H_{SB}\xrightarrow[\mbox{---}P_{1}]{}P_{1}^{\dagger}H_{SB}P_{1}\xrightarrow[\mbox{---}P_{2}]{}P_{1}^{\dagger}P_{2}^{\dagger}H_{SB}P_{2}P_{1}\xrightarrow[\mbox{---}P_{3}]{}\cdots.

(2)

An average Hamiltonian
H^{\text{av}}_{SB}
can be calculated by performing a Magnus expansion in the toggling frame
Viola et al. 1999
, and yields a series
Blanes et al. 2009

H^{\text{av}}_{SB}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}H_{SB}^{[n]}
. If decoherence is small enough (
T\norm{H_{SB}}\ll 1
where
T\equiv N\tau_{0}
), the series converges and can be approximated by the first-order term, given by

H_{SB}^{[1]}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(g_{k}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})H_{SB}(g_{k}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B})

(3)

where we defined the propagator acting on
S
at each step
k
(
1\leq k\leq N
) of the sequence as

g_{1}\equiv\mathds{1}_{S},\quad g_{k}\equiv P_{k-1}P_{k-2}\ldots P_{1}.

(4)

We now turn to the case where the set of operators
\mathcal{G}=\quantity{g_k}_{k=1}^{N}
forms a group of unitary transformations. Under this condition, the DD sequence effectively performs a
symmetrization operation

\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}

Zanardi 1999
;
Viola et al. 1999
on each system operator
S_{\alpha}
,

\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S}):S\mapsto\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(S)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}^{\dagger}Sg_{k}.

(5)

The map
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}
is a projector on a
\mathcal{G}
-invariant subspace of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
, the space of linear operators. Indeed, any element of
\mathcal{G}
commutes with
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(S)
for any
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
. By linearity of (
5
), the image under
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}
of
\mathcal{I}_{S}
,
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{I}_{S})
, is a vector subspace consisting of operators invariant under
\mathcal{G}
. If the group
\mathcal{G}
is chosen such that the symmetrization map
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{I}_{S})
reduces the interaction subspace
\mathcal{I}_{S}
to
\mathrm{span}(\quantity{\mathds{1}_S})
, then
\mathcal{G}
is referred to as a
decoupling group
for
\mathcal{I}_{S}
. In this case, the first-order effective interaction Hamiltonian takes the form

H_{SB}^{[1]}\propto\mathds{1}_{S}\otimes B,

(6)

where
B
is an operator that acts only on the bath Hilbert space.

From a decoupling group
\mathcal{G}
, a sequence of pulses can be found that implements the operation (
5
) by constructing a cyclic path on the Cayley graph
\mathrm{C}\quantity(\group, \Gamma)
of the group
\mathcal{G}
with respect to some generating set
\Gamma=\quantity{\gamma_{\lambda}}_{\lambda}

Viola and Knill 2003
;
Viola 2013
; in these graphs, each vertex corresponds to an element of the group and each edge to an element of
\Gamma
, which includes the different pulses used in the sequence,
i.e.
,
\forall k
,
P_{k}=\gamma_{\lambda}
for some
\lambda
. A cyclic path corresponds to a DD sequence in that each edge visited by the path corresponds to a pulse of the sequence and its location in the path corresponds to its location in the sequence. The only constraint imposed on the cyclic path is that it must visit each vertex the same number of times, so that many different DD sequences can be constructed that way. In particular, Eulerian paths,
i.e.
, the ones that visit each edge exactly once, always exist
Bollobás 1998
and it has been found that such paths correspond to particularly robust DD sequences
Viola and Knill 2003
.

II.2
Inaccessible symmetry and decoupling group

Consider a potential decoupling group
\mathcal{G}
for a quantum system with interaction subspace
\mathcal{I}_{S}
. To verify whether
\mathcal{G}
decouples the system-bath interaction, one approach is to find a vector space
V
containing
\mathcal{I}_{S}
that is closed under unitary transformation by the elements of
\mathcal{G}
. Since
V
is closed under unitary transformations of
\mathcal{G}
, it holds that
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{I}_{S})\subseteq V
. Moreover, because
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{I}_{S})
consists solely of
\mathcal{G}
-invariant elements of
V
, it belongs to its
\mathcal{G}
-invariant subspace. When there is a group
\mathcal{G}
for which the
\mathcal{G}
-invariant subspace of
V
consists only of operators proportional to the identity,
i.e.
,
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(V)=\mathrm{span}(\quantity{\mathds{1}_S})
, we have, for any system operator
S
belonging to the interaction subspace
\mathcal{I}_{S}\subset V
, that
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(S)\propto\mathds{1}_{S}
. This implies that
\mathcal{G}
acts as a decoupling group for
\mathcal{I}_{S}
. When this condition is satisfied, we say that
\mathcal{G}
is an
inaccessible symmetry
of
V
. In conclusion, an inaccessible symmetry of a vector space containing the interaction subspace is associated with a decoupling group.

that is invariant under a non-trivial group of unitary operators
\mathcal{G}_{1}
. Now suppose that we apply a symmetrization operation associated with a second group
\mathcal{G}_{2}
to the system. Since
\mathcal{I}_{S}
is already invariant under
\mathcal{G}_{1}
, any operator
S\in\mathcal{I}_{S}
will transform as

S\mapsto\Pi_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}(S)=\Pi_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}\quantity[\Pi_{\group_1}\qty(S)].

(12)

As noted above, if the product
\mathcal{G}_{1}\mathcal{G}_{2}
forms a group
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, then this single symmetrization procedure effectively projects
S
onto a
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
-invariant subspace of
\mathcal{B}\quantity(\Hs_S)
. If
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is an inaccessible symmetry, then the sequence implementing the
\mathcal{G}_{2}
symmetrization procedure will average the operator
S
to zero. From this brief analysis, we conclude that it is possible to reduce the complexity of a DD sequence by exploiting the symmetries already present in the interaction subspace. If a decoupling group can be factorized into a product of two of its subgroups, one of which corresponds to a symmetry of the interaction subspace, then the remaining subgroup is a decoupling group. This idea of exploiting existing symmetries to reduce the size of a decoupling group was already presented in Ref.
Viola and Knill 2003
in the context of reducing the length of an Eulerian DD sequence, where it was noted that if the interaction subspace is
\mathcal{G}_{0}
-invariant, where
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is a normal subgroup of a decoupling group
\mathcal{G}
, then the DD sequence can be constructed on the Cayley graph of the quotient group
\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{G}_{0}
, of order
\absolutevalue{\group}/\absolutevalue{\group_0}
. In fact, the CPMG sequence
Viola et al. 1999
;
Viola and Lloyd 1998
for a depahsing qubit and the
d\mathrm{X}_{d}
sequence family
Tripathi et al. 2025
for a dephasing qudits can be seen as two simple examples of this.

III.2.2
Hierarchical decoupling

\mathcal{G}^{\prime}

\mathcal{G}_{1}
targets and suppresses the dominant, fast-acting interaction terms. In this way, implementing symmetrization with respect to
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
ensures that the most critical decoherence channels are addressed effectively through the structure of the subgroup hierarchy.

IV
Finite-duration pulses

IV.1
Generalized symmetrization operation

When the approximation of the infinitely fast and strong pulses is not applicable, the derivation of the first-order average Hamiltonian presented in Sec.
II.1
no longer holds, and the effect of the DD sequence on the Hamiltonian can no longer be reduced to the symmetrization operation (
5
).

In the most general case, where each pulse
P_{k}
is smoothly implemented in the time interval
[(k-1)\tau,k\tau]
by a unitary propagator
p_{k}(t)
, with
p_{k}(\tau)=P_{k}
, the first-order Hamiltonian can be calculated explicitly
Viola and Knill 2003
;
Viola 2013
. The pulse interval
\tau=\tau_{p}+\tau_{0}
thus includes both the waiting time between successive pulses (denoted
\tau_{0}
in Fig.
3
) and the duration of the pulse
\tau_{p}
. In this scenario, each system operator
S_{\alpha}
undergoes the quantum operation
Q_{\mathcal{G}}\,:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
which acts as follows

S\mapsto Q_{\mathcal{G}}(S)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}^{\dagger}\quantity[\frac{1}{\tau} \int_0^{\tau} p_{k}^{\dagger}(t)\,S\, p_k(t) dt]g_{k}.

(13)

More specifically, consider a dynamical decoupling (DD) sequence that traces an Eulerian path on the Cayley graph of a group
\mathcal{G}
, constructed with respect to a generating set
\Gamma=\{\gamma_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda=1}^{|\Gamma|}
. Each pulse
P_{k}
in the sequence is an element of
\Gamma
and is associated with a time-dependent propagator
p_{k}(t)
, which belongs to the set of propagators

U\equiv\left\{u_{\lambda}(t):u_{\lambda}(\tau)=\gamma_{\lambda}\right\}_{\lambda=1}^{|\Gamma|},

(14)

where each
u_{\lambda}(t)\in U
represents a fixed physically realizable control operation that implements the generator
\gamma_{\lambda}
over a time interval
\tau
. These propagators are dependent on experimentally accessible pulse profiles, including details such as their shape, amplitude, and duration. In this case, the map
Q_{\mathcal{G}}
can be written as
Viola and Knill 2003
;
Viola 2013

S\mapsto Q_{\mathcal{G}}(S)=\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}\quantity[F_{\Gamma}^{(\tau)}(S)]

(15)

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}
is the symmetrization operation as defined in Eq. (
5
) and
F_{\Gamma}^{(\tau)}\,:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
acts as follows

S\mapsto F_{\Gamma}^{(\tau)}(S)=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\Gamma}}\sum_{u\in U}\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}u^{\dagger}(t)\,S\,u(t)dt,

(16)

where the sum runs over the set of pulses
U
that implement the elements of the generating set
\Gamma
. Equivalently, we can write

Q_{\mathcal{G}}(S)=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\Gamma}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{|\Gamma|}\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}\quantity[F_{\lambda}^{(\Gamma,\tau)}(S)]

(17)

where
F_{\lambda}^{(\Gamma,\tau)}(S)\equiv F_{\{\gamma_{\lambda}\}}^{(\tau)}(S)
denotes the implementation of
F
over a singleton
\{\gamma_{\lambda}\}\in\Gamma
. Physically, the operator
F_{\lambda}^{(\Gamma,\tau)}(S)
represents the finite-duration error generated by the presence of the system operator
S
during the generating pulse
u_{\lambda}(t)
. As can be understood from Eq. (
17
), the finite-duration errors of each generator will be symmetrized independently (
5
). Note that if
u_{\lambda}(t)
has support on the time interval
[\tau-\tau_{p},\tau]
(where
\tau-\tau_{p}
is the waiting time
\tau_{0}
), one can also write

F_{\lambda}^{(\Gamma,\tau)}(S)=\frac{\tau_{0}}{\tau}S+\frac{\tau_{p}}{\tau}f_{\lambda}^{(\Gamma,\tau_{p})}(S)

(18)

where

f_{\lambda}^{(\Gamma,\tau_{p})}(S)=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}}\int_{\tau-\tau_{p}}^{\tau}u_{\lambda}^{\dagger}(t)\,S\,u_{\lambda}(t)dt.

(19)

For some group
\mathcal{G}
, we can now define a correctable subspace
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G}}
spanned by all the system operators
S_{\alpha}
satisfying
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}}(S_{\alpha})\propto\mathds{1}_{S}
. In the ideal pulse regime, the decoupling group
\mathcal{G}
should be chosen such that
\mathcal{I}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G}}
. In the finite-pulse regime, on the other hand, we must make sure that
F_{\lambda}^{(\Gamma,\tau)}(\mathcal{I}_{S})
also belongs to that correctable subspace for all generating pulses
u_{\lambda}(t)
. This requirement has several implications in the context of the two applications discussed in Subsec.
III.2.1
and
III.2.2
, as described below.

IV.2
Applications

IV.2.1
Leveraging existing symmetries

Consider an interaction subspace
\mathcal{I}_{S}
invariant under the action of the group
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and belonging to the correctable subspace
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
of a certain group
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. As explained previously, in the case of ideal pulses, if there exists a group
\mathcal{G}_{2}
that satisfies
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}_{2}\mathcal{G}_{1}
, then this is a decoupling group for
\mathcal{I}_{S}
,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{I}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}
.

In the finite-pulse regime, when one chooses an Eulerian path on the Cayley graph
C(\mathcal{G}_{2},\Gamma_{2})
, every operator
S
in
\mathcal{I}_{S}
will undergo the operation (
17
)

S\mapsto Q_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}(S)=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\Gamma_2}}\sum_{\lambda}\Pi_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}[F^{(\Gamma_{2},\tau)}_{\lambda}(S)]

(20)

where
\Gamma_{2}=\quantity{\gamma_{2,\lambda}}_{\lambda}
are the chosen generators and are implemented by the generating pulses
\quantity{u_{2,\lambda}(t): u_{2,\lambda}(\tau)=\gamma_{2,\lambda}}_{\lambda}
and
\tau
is the pulse interval. Although
S=\Pi_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}(S)

\forall S\in\mathcal{I}_{S}
, the symmetrization
\Pi_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}(\cdot)
generally does not commute with
F^{(\Gamma_{2},\tau)}_{\lambda}(\cdot)
, which means that
F^{(\Gamma_{2},\tau)}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{I}_{S})
is generally not
\mathcal{G}_{1}
-invariant. Furthermore,
F^{(\Gamma_{2},\tau)}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{I}_{S})
might leak out of the correctable subspace of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. The robustness of these designs to finite-duration pulses must then be addressed on a case-by-case basis, and specific requirements on the shape and implementation of the generating pulses might be required to ensure robustness.

IV.2.2
Hierarchical decoupling

Consider a
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}_{2}\mathcal{G}_{1}
-symmetrization procedure implemented through two nested layers of symmetrization: an inner layer with respect to the group
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and an outer layer with respect to the group
\mathcal{G}_{2}
. The nested DD sequence is constructed using Eulerian sequences on the Cayley graphs
C(\mathcal{G}_{1},\Gamma_{1})
and
C(\mathcal{G}_{2},\Gamma_{2})
, where each
\Gamma_{j}=\{\gamma_{j,\lambda}\}_{\lambda}
is a generating set for the group
\mathcal{G}_{j}
and
U_{j}=\{u_{j,\lambda}(t)\}_{\lambda}
are the generating pulses.

We first apply the Eulerian sequence associated with
\mathcal{G}_{1}
, with a total duration
T=\absolutevalue{\group_1}\absolutevalue{\Gamma_1}\tau_{1}
where
\tau_{1}=\tau_{0}^{(1)}+\tau_{p}^{(1)}
is the interval between two pulses that includes the duration of a pulse
\tau_{p}^{(1)}
and the possible waiting time between two pulses
\tau_{0}^{(1)}
. This DD sequence will effectively implement the operation
S\mapsto S^{\prime}=Q_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}(S)
defined in Eq. (
17
) with

F^{(\Gamma_{1},\tau_{1})}_{\lambda}(S)=\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}u_{1,\lambda}^{\dagger}(t)Su_{1,\lambda}(t)dt.

(21)

This sequence is now nested into the Eulerian sequence associated with
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, with a total duration
T^{\prime}=\absolutevalue{\group_1}\absolutevalue{\group_2}(T+\tau_{p}^{(2)})
, where each pulse interval of duration
T
is replaced by the
\mathcal{G}_{1}
Eulerian sequence (see Fig.
3
) and where
\tau_{p}^{(2)}
is the pulse duration for the second sequence.

In the ideal pulse regime (
\tau_{p}^{(2)}=0
), the outer-layer sequence effectively symmetrizes the operator
S^{\prime}
, and the total effect of the nested sequence is to induce the quantum operation

S\mapsto S^{\prime\prime}=\Pi_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}(S^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\Gamma_1}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{\absolutevalue{\Gamma_1}}\Pi_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}\quantity[\Pi_{\group_1}\qty[F^{(\Gamma_1,\tau_1)}_{\lambda}(S)]].

(22)

Therefore, as long as the finite-duration errors of the pulses of the inner-layer sequence belong to the correctable subspace of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}_{2}\mathcal{G}_{1}
,
i.e.
,
F^{(\Gamma_{1},\tau_{1})}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{I}_{S})\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}}
, the nested sequence decouples the interaction subspace.

In the case where
\tau_{p}^{(2)}>0
, it must be taken into account that the error Hamiltonian during the implementation of the pulses of the outer-layer sequence has not been symmetrized by the inner-layer, so that the nested sequence effectively implements
Khodjasteh and Lidar 2005
;
Khodjasteh and Lidar 2007

S\mapsto S^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\Gamma_2}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{\Gamma_{2}}\Pi_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}\left[\frac{T}{T+\tau_{p}^{(2)}}S^{\prime}\right.\\
+\left.\frac{\tau_{p}^{(2)}}{T+\tau_{p}^{(2)}}f^{(\Gamma_{2},\tau_{p}^{(2)})}_{\lambda}(S)\right],

(23)

where

f^{(\Gamma_{2},\tau_{p}^{(2)})}_{\lambda}(S)=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}^{(2)}}\int_{T}^{T+\tau_{p}^{(2)}}u_{2,\lambda}^{\dagger}(t)\,S\,u_{2,\lambda}(t)

(24)

and
S^{\prime}=Q_{\mathcal{G}_{1}}(S)
. The finite-duration errors of the outer-layer pulses will thus not undergo the multi-symmetrization, which reduces the robustness of our schemes.

Applications to spin systems and decoherence models

We first address the decoupling of dipole-dipole interactions in a spin ensemble, followed by the case including disorder. Next, we consider

-body multilinear interactions in a spin ensemble. Finally, we address qudit decoupling from a dephasing bosonic bath and explore hierarchical decoupling in spin qudits. A summary of the most relevant DD sequences that we develop in the following is given in Tables

V.1
Dipole-dipole interaction

As a first example, consider the Hamiltonian

As explained in Sec.

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a symmetry-based approach to designing dynamical decoupling sequences that are both efficient and tailored to the specific structure of the unwanted interaction Hamiltonian to be mitigated. By systematically building on the symmetries already present in the Hamiltonian, we developed a nested multi-symmetrization procedure that projects the dynamics of the system onto a subspace invariant under a chosen symmetry group. This strategy reduces the complexity of decoupling protocols and enables more targeted suppression of interactions. As a notable example, our method naturally recovers the well-known Lee-Goldburg sequence
Lee and Goldburg 1965
, widely used to cancel dipole-dipole interactions in NMR. This suggests that the additional sequences we derive may likewise prove useful in NMR spectroscopy. In particular, the combination of our framework with the geometric insight offered by Majorana constellations provides an intuitive and flexible tool for constructing pulse sequences that selectively cancel interaction terms based on their symmetry properties
Levitt 2007
;
Mote et al. 2016
.

A central result of our work is the four-pulse sequence (
29
) that simultaneously cancels dipole-dipole interactions and on-site disorder. The eight-pulse Eulerian variant (
30
) of this sequence, which we call TEDDY, was shown to be robust to finite-duration pulses as well as to many relevant control errors, including systematic over- and under-rotation as well as deviations from the desired axis of rotation and pulse shape. Furthermore, it cancels all second-order terms of the Magnus expansion in both the ideal and finite pulse regime, and we find that it outperforms state-of-the-art sequences in relevant regimes of parameters (see Fig.
6
).

We have also discovered small decoupling sequences that suppress anisotropic
K
-body interactions (with
K\leq 5
) under the rotating wave approximation. These sequences have potential applications for preserving coherence in nuclear or electronic spin ensembles, as well as for probing exotic interactions between spins. Their short length, versatility, and intuitive decoupling properties make them good candidates for use as building blocks in the construction of more advanced protocols based on nesting
Choi et al. 2020
. We have shown that these sequences can be used as decoupling sequences for
small
dephasing qudits (
d\leq 6
). Finally, we have demonstrated how the multi-symmetrization approach enables the construction of nested sequences that suppress dominant errors at shorter timescales. This is achieved by factorizing high-symmetry groups, which decouple all relevant error sources, into products of lower-symmetry subgroups, each tailored to suppress specific dominant error mechanisms. An overview of these novel DD sequences, including their description and potential use, is given in Tables
4
and
5
in Appendix
C
.

The same procedure of nesting and exploiting symmetry as implemented in this work could be used to achieve more efficient higher-order decoupling. In the traditional concatenation scheme for high-order decoupling
Khodjasteh and Lidar 2005
;
Khodjasteh and Lidar 2007
, a sequence is nested within another such that the outer-layer sequence essentially decouples the dominant term of the Magnus expansion left out by the inner-layer sequence. Using this framework, the outer-layer sequence can be judiciously selected to take advantage of the symmetries of the leading term of the Magnus expansion, essentially reducing the length of the high-order nested protocol. Even in the case where no apparent symmetries appear in the leading order term, knowledge of the Majorana constellation can help us identify inaccessible symmetries, providing useful information for making an informed choice about the outer-layer sequence.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated the importance of symmetries and their abstract decompositions in the framework of dynamical decoupling. This symmetry-based approach enables the systematic construction of efficient, robust, and unconventional DD sequences whose decoupling and robustness properties can be understood analytically, thus facilitating physical intuition. Our examples are based on point groups, where geometrical representations help elucidate the underlying symmetries of interaction subspaces and their factorizations. Although we focused on global
\mathrm{SU}(2)
transformations, the framework extends to all symmetry groups, opening a new avenue for the development of robust protocols for dynamical decoupling and Hamiltonian engineering.

Appendix B
Robustness analysis of the TEDDY sequence

The eight-pulse sequence (
30
), written below for convenience

\quantity(\overset{\tau}{\mydash}~(\pi)_{\hat{n}_1} \overset{\tau}{\mydash}~(\pi)_{-\hat{n}_{\pm 2}})^{\crossproduct 2}\quantity(\overset{\tau}{\mydash}~(\pi)_{-\hat{n}_{\pm 2}}~\overset{\tau}{\mydash}~(\pi)_{\hat{n}_{1}})^{\crossproduct 2},

(56)

implements the quantum operation

S\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda=1,2}\Pi_{\mathrm{D}_{2}}\quantity(F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_2},\tau)}_{\lambda}(S))

(57)

where

F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_{2}},\tau)}_{\lambda}(S)=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\,u_{\lambda}^{\dagger}(t)\,S\,u_{\lambda}(t)

(58)

with
u_{1}(\tau)=e^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{1}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}
and
u_{2}(\tau)=e^{i\pi\hat{n}_{\pm 2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}
. In this section, we show the robustness of the sequence to finite-duration pulses and relevant control errors.

B.1
Control errors

Let us consider that the control Hamiltonian
H_{1}(t)=f_{1}(t)\hat{n}_{1}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}
implementing the pulse
u_{1}(t)
comes with systematic errors which take the form of the Hamiltonian
\Delta H_{1}(t)
and which is considered small compared to the time-duration of the pulse,
i.e.
,
\tau\norm{\Delta H_1(t)}\ll 1

\forall t
. A relevant control error Hamiltonian would take the form

\Delta H_{1}(t)=\Delta(t)\hat{\delta}(t)\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}

(59)

and would cause a deviation from the intended rotation. For instance, in the case where
\hat{\delta}(t)\equiv\hat{n}_{1}
, this Hamiltonian generates an over- or under-rotation.

If the control error is systematic, which means that the same error term
\Delta H_{1}(t)
appears every time
H_{1}(t)
is turned on, the error is taken into account by changing the finite-duration term to

\displaystyle F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_{2}},\tau)}_{1}(S)

\displaystyle\mapsto F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_{2}},\tau)}_{1}(S+\Delta H_{1}(t))

(60)

\displaystyle=F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_{2}},\tau)}_{1}(S)+F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_{2}},\tau)}_{1}(\Delta H_{1}(t))

where

F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_{2}},\tau)}_{1}(\Delta H_{1}(t))=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\,u_{1}^{\dagger}(t)\Delta H_{1}(t)u_{1}(t).

(61)

The finite-duration errors
F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_{2}},\tau)}_{1}(S)
and the control errors
F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_{2}},\tau)}_{1}(\Delta H_{1}(t))
can thus be considered individually. Crucially, if
\Delta H_{1}(t)
has the form (
59
), then the control error Hamiltonian is corrected by the
\Pi_{\mathrm{D}_{2}}
symmetrization, as
\mathrm{D}_{2}
is a symmetry inaccessible to the irrep
\mathscr{B}^{(1)}
. Furthermore, when the generating pulse
u_{1}(t)
has the form
e^{-i\theta_{t}\hat{n}_{t}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}
at all times
t
, for some axis
\hat{n}_{t}
and angle
\theta_{t}
—that is if the generating pulse represents a rotation at all times
t
—, the finite-duration error
F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_{2}},\tau)}_{1}(\Delta H_{1}(t))
also belongs to the irrep
\mathscr{B}^{(1)}
, as it is a closed subspace under
\mathrm{SU}(2)
unitary transformation. Consequently, it is suppressed by the symmetrization
\Pi_{\mathrm{D}_{2}}
, which proves robustness to such control errors to the first order of the Magnus expansion.

Evidently, the same argument can be made for the second generating pulse
u_{2}(t)
.

B.2
Finite-duration errors

Even when
S
belongs to the so-called
correctable subspace
of the
\mathrm{D}_{2}
group, this does not guarantee that the finite duration error
F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_{2}},\tau)}_{1}(S)
is corrected by
\mathrm{D}_{2}
. However, in the case where

u_{1}(t)=e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}f_{1}(t)dt\,\hat{n}_{1}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}},

(62)

and if
f_{1}(t)=f_{1}(\tau-t)
, we can show that

\Pi_{\mathrm{D}_{2}}\quantity[\int_0^{\tau}u_1^{\dagger}(t)Su_1(t)]=\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}(t)\Pi_{\mathrm{D}_{2}}\quantity[S]u_{1}^{\dagger}(t)

(63)

which means that, if
S
is corrected by
\Pi_{\mathrm{D}_{2}}
, then also is
F^{(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}_{2}},\tau)}_{1}(S)
. The proof starts by writing

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathrm{D}_{2}}\quantity[\int_0^{\tau}u_1^{\dagger}(t)Su_1(t)]={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}^{\dagger}(t)Su_{1}(t)+e^{i\pi\hat{n}_{1}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}^{\dagger}(t)Su_{1}(t)e^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{1}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}

(64)

\displaystyle+e^{i\pi\hat{n}_{+2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}^{\dagger}(t)Su_{1}(t)e^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{+2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}+e^{i\pi\hat{n}_{-2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}^{\dagger}(t)Su_{1}(t)e^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{-2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}

and realizing that since
\hat{n}_{\pm 2}
are orthogonal to
\hat{n}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle e^{i\pi\hat{n}_{\pm 2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}^{\dagger}(t)Su_{1}(t)e^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{\pm 2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}

(65)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}(t)e^{i\pi\hat{n}_{\pm 2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}Se^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{\pm 2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}u^{\dagger}_{1}(t).

Additionally, because of the symmetric control profile, we find that

u_{1}(t)=e^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{1}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}e^{i\int_{0}^{\tau-t}f(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\,\hat{n}_{1}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}

(66)

which can be used to prove that

\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}^{\dagger}(t)Su_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}(t)e^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{1}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}Se^{i\pi\hat{n}_{1}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}u_{1}^{\dagger}(t).

(67)

Putting everything together, (
64
) becomes

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathrm{D}_{2}}\quantity[\int_0^{\tau}u_1^{\dagger}(t)Su_1(t)]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}(t)e^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{1}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}Se^{i\pi\hat{n}_{1}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}u^{\dagger}_{1}(t)+\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}(t)Su_{1}^{\dagger}(t)

(68)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}(t)e^{i\pi\hat{n}_{+2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}Se^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{+2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}u_{1}^{\dagger}(t)e^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{+2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}+\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}(t)e^{i\pi\hat{n}_{-2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}Se^{-i\pi\hat{n}_{-2}\bm{\cdot}\mathbf{J}}u_{1}^{\dagger}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\tau}u_{1}(t)\Pi_{\mathrm{D}_{2}}\quantity[S]u_{1}^{\dagger}(t).

A similar derivation holds for the second generating pulse, which proves robustness of our sequence to finite-duration pulses under mild conditions in terms of pulse form and shape.
