Synergistic effects of mesenchymal stem cell secretome and Wnt10b on hair follicle regeneration in a 3D bioengineering model: a preclinical study
Authors: Amir Bajouri, Nasser Aghdami, Mohammad Ali Nilforoushzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite all advances, constructing functional hair follicles remains a significant clinical challenge. Building on recent advancements in mesenchymal stem cells secretome and Wnt signaling, we aimed to fabricate hair follicles in a 3D hydrogel scaffold and assess the effects of placenta-derived MSCs (PMSC) secretome and Wnt10b on follicle formation in ex vivo and in vivo models.MethodsA gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel scaffold was engineered via a 3D-printed We engineered a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel scaffold using a 3D-printed polydimethylsiloxane mold to create a co-culture system for hair follicle biofabrication. Dermal papilla cells (DPCs) and epithelial cells from a donor were isolated and seeded into the scaffold to form microfollicles. We treated hair microfollicles with PMSC secretome, Wnt10b protein, or combination of both. The generated hair microfollicles were assessed using specific molecular and protein markers and then implanted into nude mice skin to investigate the hair growth in vivo.ResultsAfter three weeks of co-culturing, the group receiving both secretome and Wnt10b showed 90% cell aggregation and highest hair follicle formation rate at 28% (p < 0.0001). Immunofluorescent analysis revealed elevated expression levels of markers for DPCs and epithelial cells in formed hair follicles. Real-time PCR analysis indicated significantly enhanced expression of Wnt10b, Bmp6, Notch1 and Notch2 genes, particularly in the group receiving secretome and Wnt10b. Transplantation of GelMA hydrogels containing hair microfollicles into nude mice resulted in successful hair formation along with immunofluorescence and gene expression analyses showing marked increases in hair follicle growth markers.ConclusionOur study demonstrates that the combination of PMSC-secretome and Wnt10b in a GelMA hydrogel scaffold effectively promotes hair follicle biofabrication and successful hair formation in vivo. However, the in vivo portion was limited by a small sample size (n = 3 per group), underscoring the need for larger-scale studies to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04830-4.

Introduction

Hair loss affects millions of individuals worldwide, causing significant psychological distress and reducing quality of life [1]. Currently, hair transplantation is recognized as an effective solution for baldness. Nevertheless, the success of this procedure largely depends on the availability of donor hair, which may be insufficient for some individuals due to extensive baldness or low hair density [2, 3]. In recent years, the biofabrication of hair follicles using human hair follicle cells, such as dermal papillae cells (DPCs) and epithelial cells, has demonstrated significant promise. However, several key factors influence this process, including reciprocal signaling between dermal and epidermal cells, the establishment of a three-dimensional (3D) environment, the activation of crucial molecular signaling pathways, and the utilization of appropriate scaffolds to modulate the microenvironment [4].

A variety of biomaterial platforms have been explored in tissue engineering to tailor microenvironmental cues and direct stem-cell fate. For instance, Shahi et al. developed a gelatinous spongy scaffold containing nano-hydroxyapatite that markedly enhanced the odontogenic differentiation of dental pulp stem cells, illustrating how nanoparticle-laden gelatin matrices can steer lineage specification [5]. Similarly, Eftekhari et al. reported that advanced nanofibers and nanoparticle-loaded hydrogels promoted tubular epithelial cell proliferation and neovascularization in kidney failure models, underscoring the versatility of nanocomposite scaffolds for organ repair [6]. Beyond synthetic composites, green-synthesized nanoparticles derived from Camellia sinensis extracts exhibited potent enzyme-inhibitory and antioxidant activities, suggesting the capacity of plant-derived nanomaterials to regulate enzymatic and oxidative pathways critical for tissue regeneration [7]. Furthermore, a recent review of nanobiomaterial-based bioinks further highlighted that incorporating nanocomposite fillers—such as nano-hydroxyapatite, nanosilicates, and carbon nanotubes—substantially improves mechanical strength, print fidelity, and cell viability of 3D-bioprinted constructs for artificial organs and tissue engineering applications [8].

Abaci et al. recently developed a multi-follicle generation mold that supports hair follicle growth within each microwell. Their innovative design has inspired similar approaches in microwell fabrication, highlighting the crucial role of the microenvironment in promoting hair formation [9]. Biomaterial scaffolds could provide structural support and create a favorable environment for cell growth, differentiation, and tissue regeneration [10]. The scaffolds also deliver growth factors, facilitate the formation of new blood vessels, and enhance the survival and integration of transplanted hair follicles or DPCs [11]. Natural materials including collagen or hyaluronic acid, and synthetic materials such as polylactic acid (PLA) and polyglycolic acid (PGA) are employed for their biocompatibility, ability to support cell growth, capacity to create porous structures, and gradual degradation as new tissue forms [12]. Gelatin, derived from collagen, is regarded as a highly biocompatible and biodegradable scaffold. It closely mimics the natural extracellular matrix (ECM) that supports cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation [13, 14]. Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), a modified form of gelatin, incorporates methacryloyl groups that enables it to be crosslinked into stable hydrogels with enhanced mechanical properties and customizable structures. Furthermore, GelMA can be employed through methods such as bioprinting, microwell arrays, or cell-laden platforms [15, 16]. Recent advances in 3D bioprinting have demonstrated that precisely patterning dermal papilla spheroids within a GelMA-based dermal matrix supports organized epithelial invagination, leading to the formation of multilayered, hair follicle–like structures. These structures express canonical follicular markers, generate hair shaft–like keratin fibers, and persist for weeks both in vitro and after grafting, resulting in a higher follicle frequency and maturation rate compared to non-patterned controls [17].

In this study, we present an innovative bioengineering method for generating hair microfollicles within a multi-array GelMA scaffold designed as a 3D culture system. Our approach facilitates the controllable self-aggregation of human DPCs and epithelial cells in a physiologically relevant ECM. Additionally, we incorporate Wnt10b protein and secretome derived from placenta mesenchymal stem cells (PMSCs) to promote efficient human hair growth. PMSC-secretome was utilized based on its proven efficacy in promoting tissue regeneration and hair follicle neogenesis, as supported by recent studies showing its rich trophic factor content and anti-inflammatory profile, which outperform other MSC sources in some contexts [18]. The MSC-secretome, rich in various growth factors and cytokines, not only supports cell survival but also regulates the microenvironment to stimulate hair follicle regeneration [19, 20]. Wnt10b was chosen due to its documented ability to activate follicle stem cells and induce robust hair follicle morphogenesis and growth, as evidenced by a substantial body of mechanistic and translational research [21, 22]. Furthermore, we transplanted the fabricated hair microfollicles into nude mice and showed that combination therapy with PMSC-secretome and Wnt10b enhances the follicle number and growth. Our methodology exemplifies a novel bioengineering strategy for generating hair microfollicles entirely ex vivo from cultured human hair follicle cells and suggests that combination therapy with PMSC-secretome and Wnt10b is an effective therapeutic strategy to enhance hair growth.

Methods

Fabrication of uniform microneedle array

A polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Sylgard 184, Dow Corning) mold was fabricated using AutoCAD 3D (Version 2020) design (Fig. 1A). The mold featured a circular geometry (2 cm diameter, 1.5 cm depth) and contained an array of 12 × 12 uniform microneedles (50 μm diameter, 400 μm height, 200 μm inter-needle spacing). The mold was produced via 3D printing (Form 3B, Formlabs) with a clear resin V4 and a resolution of 25 μm (Fig. 1B). This microneedle geometry was informed by prior studies demonstrating its suitability for engineering biomimetic hair follicle structures [23, 24]. The PDMS mold was cured at 60 °C for 2 h.

Synthesis and molding of gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA)

GelMA was synthesized by dissolving 10 g of gelatin type A (porcine skin, 300 Bloom, Sigma-Aldrich) in 200 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4, Gibco) under continuous stirring at 50 °C for 1 h. Methacrylic anhydride (8 mL, Sigma-Aldrich, 94%) was added dropwise (1 mL/min), and the reaction proceeded for 3 h under light-protected conditions. The solution was diluted with 400 mL PBS and dialyzed against distilled water (molecular weight cut-off: 12–14 kDa, Spectrum Labs) for 7 days, with water changes every 12 h. The dialyzed product was lyophilized (Labconco FreeZone 2.5; -80 °C condenser, 0.04 mBar) to yield GelMA powder. Hydrogels were prepared by dissolving 15 g GelMA powder in 100 mL autoclaved distilled water at 60 °C, followed by the addition of 0.1% (w/v) Irgacure 2959 (Sigma-Aldrich). The mixture was crosslinked under 365 nm UV light (5 mW/cm², Omnicure S2000) for 5 min in circular Teflon molds (1 cm diameter, 0.5 cm height).

Characterization of GelMA

The degree of methacrylation was determined via ^1H-NMR spectroscopy (Bruker AVANCE III, 400 MHz) using D2O as the solvent. Structural properties were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL JSM-6010LA) following gold sputter-coating. Pore size and internal morphology were quantified from SEM images using ImageJ software. Chemical composition was verified by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet iS50, Thermo Scientific). Mechanical properties, including Young’s modulus, were assessed using a universal testing machine (Instron 5942) in accordance with ASTM D638 standards. Swelling ratio and degradation kinetics were evaluated in PBS (37 °C) and collagenase type II (10 U/mL), respectively.

Isolation and cultivation of placenta MSCs

MSCs were isolated from placental tissue collected under sterile conditions immediately after childbirth, following written informed parental consent approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Approval Number: IRB-2023-001). The placenta tissue was separated, repeatedly washed in PBS, and transferred to a transport medium containing PBS with 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco). The placental tissue was mechanically cleared of blood vessels and clots, chopped into small pieces (approximately 1–2 mm³), and incubated in 30 mL of 1 mg/mL type IV collagenase solution (Sigma-Aldrich, C5138, activity ≥ 125 U/mg solid) in DMEM-LG at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1 h in a shaking incubator (Thermo Scientific, 88882024) at 100 RPM. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was then treated with 5 mL of 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25200-056, 0.25% trypsin) containing 1 mM EDTA, followed by a 30-minute incubation at 37 °C with intermittent mixing. The cell pellet was washed twice with PBS, centrifuged twice, filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer (Falcon), and treated with 2 mL of ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) lysing buffer (Gibco) to lyse the remaining red blood cells. The final cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM-LG (Gibco, 11885-084) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, HyClone, SH30071.03, batch-tested for MSC culture). The cells were cultured at a density of 5000 cells/cm² and passaged at 80% confluency.

Characterization of placenta MSCs

To confirm the identity of the isolated MSCs, flow cytometry analysis was performed according to the International Society for Cellular Therapy (ISCT) guidelines [25]. PMSCs were stained with antibodies against CD73, CD90, and CD105 (positive markers) and CD45 and CD34 (negative markers) (all antibodies from BioLegend), and analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer.

PMSCs in passage 3 were trypsinized, washed with PBS, and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS with 2% FBS and 0.1% sodium azide). The cells were then incubated with the appropriate antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed twice with FACS buffer, resuspended, and analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (Version 10). For chondrogenic differentiation, 2.5 × 105 cells were transferred to a 15 mL tube, centrifuged at 300 g for 5 minutes, and then cultured in chondrogenic medium (Gibco, A1007001) containing DMEM and 10 ng/mL transforming growth factor (TGF)-β3 growth factor (PeproTech). The medium was changed every 3 days for 21 days. For osteogenic differentiation, cells were cultured in 6-well plates until they reached 80–90% confluence, followed by the addition of osteogenic medium (Gibco, A1007201) containing 100 µM ascorbic acid-2-phosphate, 10 nM dexamethasone, and 10 mM β-glycerophosphate. The medium was changed every 3 days for 21 days. For adipogenic differentiation, cells were similarly cultured in 6-well plates, and adipogenic medium (Gibco, A1007101) containing 1 µM dexamethasone, 10 µg/mL insulin, and 200 µM indomethacin (Sigma, USA) was added. The medium was changed every 3 days for 21 days. To confirm differentiation, alizarin red staining (Sigma-Aldrich) was used for osteogenic differentiation, toluidine blue staining (Sigma-Aldrich) for chondrogenic differentiation, and oil red O staining (Sigma-Aldrich) for adipogenic differentiation. For alizarin red staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed with PBS, and stained with 2% alizarin red solution for 30 min at room temperature. Excess stain was washed away with distilled water, and images were captured using a light microscope.

Collection and evaluation of MSC-secretome

To obtain the maximum levels of growth factors and cytokines, cell culture supernatants were collected once cell confluency exceeded 80%. The supernatants were centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min, and the clarified medium was transferred to new falcon tubes. The supernatant was then filtered through 0.45 μm (Millipore) and, subsequently, 0.22 μm (Millipore) filters. The secretome was stored at -80 °C until further use. Physical properties, including pH, protein content, growth factor levels, sterility, and viscosity were assessed. pH was measured using a pH meter (Mettler Toledo), protein content was determined using a Bradford assay (Bio-Rad), and viscosity was measured using a viscometer (Brookfield). Sterility was assessed by plating an aliquot of the secretome on blood agar plates and incubating at 37 °C for 7 days to check for bacterial growth. The osmolality was measured using an osmometer (Advanced Instruments, Model 3320) to characterize secretome collected from placental MSCs. The expression levels of proteins EGF, VEGF, IGF-1, HGF, PGE-2, IL10, IDO, IL1ra, IL6, HLA-G, and FGF-2 were examined using the ELISA method (kits from R&D Systems). To lyse tissues, approximately 100 mg of tissues were placed in 1 mL of RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The samples were homogenized on ice using a tissue homogenizer (PRO Scientific) and centrifuged at 2000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analyses. ELISA assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, microplates were coated with capture antibody, blocked, incubated with samples and standards, followed by detection antibody and substrate. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek).

Isolation and culture of hair follicle DPCs and epithelial cells

Human DPCs and epithelial cells were isolated from healthy scalp areas of a 26-year-old donor (regions without hair loss). The study was conducted with approval from the ethics committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1399.841). Hair samples were collected by a dermatologist and transported in DMEM low glucose (Gibco) supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco). The samples were washed sequentially in 3 mL of HBSS (Gibco) with 50 µg/mL penicillin/streptomycin, 70% ethanol, and again in HBSS with 50 µg/mL penicillin/streptomycin. Hair samples were incubated in dispase II (Sigma-Aldrich, 1.2 units/mL) for 16 h at 4 °C. The following day, samples were transferred to 1:1 mixture of DMEM (Gibco) and serum-free keratinocyte growth medium (KGM, Sigma Aldrich) supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, 200 µL hydrocortisone (Sigma-Aldrich), 500 µL hEGF (PeproTech), 500 µL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich), 2 mL PBE (Pituitary Bovine Extract, Gibco), and 500 µL CA-1000 (Clonetics), and the hairs were separated from the dermis. Under a dissection microscope (Zeiss), the lower third of the hair shaft was designated as the dermal papilla region, while the remainder, from the dermal papilla to the epidermal region, was designated as the epithelial cell region. These regions were isolated, with cell culture medium changed every three days over a total culture period of approximately 14 days. Dermal papilla samples were incubated in 1 mg/mL collagenase I (Sigma-Aldrich) for 4 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, the cells were pipetted for 10 min to facilitate dissociation. The collagenase activity was neutralized with a culture medium as the sample transport medium. The cell suspension was then filtered through a 70 μm mesh (Falcon) and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The isolated cells were plated in six-well culture dishes (Corning). Cells were co-cultured in DMEM + KGM culture medium as described above. This medium supports both cell types and avoids serum-induced epithelial differentiation [26]. The co-culture medium was changed every three days. The co-culture was maintained for 21 days, as previous studies have shown that hair follicle-like structures typically form within 2–3 weeks in such systems [26, 27].

Seeding and characterization of hair follicle cells in GelMA

Following the first expansion of DPCs and epithelial cells in 2D culture, cells were treated with 0.05% trypsin for 1–2 min to detach epithelial cells. The flask was washed to harvest the epithelial cells. Then, the flask was trypsinized again for 4 minutes to detach the DPCs. This method allows for the collection of relatively selective populations with minimal cross-contamination. We verified this method using specific markers for DPCs (ALP) and epithelial cells (CK15) and found that it yields a purity of over 80%. A total of 1 × 104 DPCs were loaded into each well of the GelMA scaffold. After 5 days, 5 × 104 epithelial cells were seeded into the wells. The initial delay in seeding epithelial cells allows for DPC aggregation and organization, mimicking the initial stages of hair follicle development. The medium was changed every 48 h, and the samples were evaluated for hair follicle formation after 3 weeks. The medium was composed of: (A) changing media alone (control group), (B) changing media with 1 mg/mL secretome, (C) changing media with 1 µg/mL recombinant human Wnt10b (R&D Systems), or (D) changing media with secretome and Wnt10b. The culture medium was DMEM/KGM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, X µg/mL hydrocortisone (Sigma-Aldrich), Y ng/mL hEGF (PeproTech), Z µg/mL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich), and growth supplements from Pituitary Bovine Extract (PBE, Gibco) and CA-1000 (Clonetics) used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Individual scaffolds were cultured in 24-well plates under a ~ 8 mm column of medium to ensure complete submersion of all microfeatures. This experimental design allows for the assessment of the individual and synergistic effects of secretome and Wnt10b on hair follicle formation. The structure of the hair formed on the hydrogel scaffold was analyzed via histopathology studies and H&E staining. The evaluation focused on the morphology and organization of the hair shafts, the integration of hair follicles within the scaffold, and the overall number, alignment, and orientation of the hair. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to standard protocols. Advanced imaging techniques, such as SEM, were employed to assess the detailed architecture of the hair structures. For SEM, samples were fixed in glutaraldehyde, dehydrated in a series of ethanol solutions, critical point dried, sputter-coated with gold, and imaged using a Zeiss SEM. Additionally, histological staining was used to examine the cellular composition and structural integrity of the newly formed hair follicles. Blinding the analysis is critical for minimizing bias. Histological analyses and hair follicle counts were performed by two independent observers blinded to the experimental groups.

Hair follicle staining

To maintain the hair-inductive properties of cultured human epithelial cells and DPCs, we assessed the presence of K15, AE13 (pan-cytokeratin), AE15 (cytokeratin 15-specific), K71, and K75 in epithelial cells and α-SMA, COL1, and alkaline phosphatase activity in the DPCs using immunofluorescence. Antibodies were obtained from Abcam unless otherwise specified. We confirmed the epithelial nature of cultured epithelial cells through immunofluorescence staining. The culture medium was aspirated from the cells, followed by washing three times with PBS for 5 min each. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and washed twice with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20, each for 5 min. Samples were blocked for 1 h in a blocking solution. The blocking solution consisted of 5% normal goat serum in PBS with 0.3% Triton X-100, and after blocking, the primary antibody, Mouse anti-human K15 (Abcam, clone LHK15, diluted 1:100), was added. The culture dish was incubated overnight at 4 °C. Samples were washed thrice with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20, each for 5 min. The secondary antibody, Anti-mouse IgG-Alexa Fluor 568 (Invitrogen, diluted 1:500), was incubated with the samples for 45 min to 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Wells were washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20, each for 5 min, and finally, the cells were stained with DAPI nuclear stain (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min. After a single wash, the samples were examined under a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss). Images were captured using a digital camera and analyzed using ImageJ software. Additionally, a negative control was prepared by adding only the secondary antibody to one well to confirm the specificity of the primary antibody. This panel of markers was selected based on their known expression patterns in hair follicle cells and their importance in hair follicle development and maintenance.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the DPCs and epithelial cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Only RNA samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were used for cDNA synthesis. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized using the TAKARA kit following the kit’s protocol. The expression levels of the Bmp6, Wnt10b, Notch1, and Notch2 genes were quantified relative to the reference gene GAPDH using the SYBR Green real-time PCR method on a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The PCR reaction mixture contained SYBR Green Master Mix, 10 µM forward and reverse primers (Table 1), and 100ng cDNA template. The thermal cycling conditions were 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Melt curve analysis was performed to ensure the specificity of the PCR products. Gene expression was analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Each sample was run in triplicate, and the mean Ct values were used for analysis. Relative gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and expressed as fold change compared to the control group. The selection of these genes (Bmp6, Wnt10b, Notch1, Notch2) was based on their critical roles in hair follicle development and signaling pathways.

Animal study

Six male C57BL/6 nude (C57BL/6-Foxn1nu) mice, which are hairless and weigh approximately 25 g, were obtained from the Pasteur Institute (Iran) for this study. The study was conducted in a controlled laboratory setting at Skin and Stem Cell Research Center Lab. Mice were housed in standard animal care facilities with a controlled environment, including regulated temperature (20–22 °C), humidity (40–60%), and a 12-hour light/dark cycle in a single animal cage. Prior to the experiment, all mice underwent an acclimatization period of one week, allowing them to adjust to the new environment and handling procedures. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences with number 1399.841. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Mice were randomly assigned to groups using a computer-generated list by a researcher not involved in the procedures. Samples were coded, and personnel performing histology, immunofluorescence, image analysis, and qPCR were blinded to group allocation. Unblinding occurred only after all analyses were completed. To minimize suffering, animals were closely monitored for any signs of distress or discomfort. To induce anesthesia, ketamine hydrochloride (100 mg/kg) and xylazine hydrochloride (10 mg/kg) were administered intraperitoneally. The six mice were randomly divided into two groups of three: [1] the experimental group, which received fabricated microfollicles on a GelMA multi-array hydrogel scaffold combined with secretome collected from placental MSCs and Wnt10b, and [2] the control group, which received GelMA multi-array hydrogel scaffold without microfollicles. In the group that received fabricated microfollicles on a GelMA multi-array hydrogel scaffold, it took 21 days for mature microfollicles to form in the hydrogel before implantation. A sample size of n = 3 per group was chosen based on power analysis calculations to detect a statistically significant difference in hair follicle formation rate with an estimated effect size of 0.5, a power of 80%, and an alpha level of 0.05. The hydrogel transplant was implanted intradermally in 2 cm² area on the dorsal side of the mice. Before the implantation, the dorsal skin was shaved and disinfected with povidone-iodine. A small incision was made to create a 2 cm² intradermal pocket, and the intact hydrogel scaffold was carefully inserted into the intradermal space. The incision site was closed with 6 − 0 nylon sutures. To minimize potential confounders, randomization was employed for group assignment, and standardized procedures were followed for all surgical interventions. During the different stages of the experiment, the research team and surgical personnel were aware of group assignments, while histology and data analysis personnel were blinded to ensure unbiased outcome assessment. After 2 to 3 weeks, skin samples were collected to assess the formation of hair follicles using H&E staining and Masson’s Trichrome staining. Masson’s Trichrome staining was performed to evaluate extracellular matrix remodeling and collagen deposition within the transplanted hydrogel constructs. In this staining, collagen fibers stain blue and sections were imaged using a bright-field microscope. For quantitative analysis, images were processed in ImageJ (NIH, USA). The blue channel was isolated, thresholded to create a binary image, and the collagen-positive area was measured as a percentage of the total tissue area within the region of interest. The mice were euthanized in a chamber using carbon dioxide (CO2) to induce rapid unconsciousness, with signs typically appearing within 1 to 2 min. To ensure complete euthanasia, cervical dislocation was performed after confirming no responses to stimuli, following IACUC guidelines to minimize suffering. After confirmation of death, skin samples were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and stained with H&E according to standard procedures. Hair follicle density, size, and morphology were assessed using light microscopy by two independent observers blinded to experimental groups. At least 5 random fields of 3 independent samples were analyzed in each group. Additionally, we evaluated the expression of markers including α-SMA, versican, K14, K17, K5, AE13, AE15, K71, and K75, as well as the gene expression of Wnt10b, Notch1, Notch2, and Bmp6 in the skin samples before and after transplantation, according to the protocol described earlier. We used species-specific primers, and blasted against other species to make sure the species-specific transcription. Besides, rigorous QC steps (no-RT, NCT, melt-curves, and amplification efficiency) ensured species specificity. Immunofluorescent staining was performed using the primary antibodies. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to fluorescent dyes. Signals were visualized using fluorescence microscopy. For gene expression analysis, RNA was extracted from skin samples using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. Real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix and gene-specific primers. Relative gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. These markers were selected to assess hair follicle differentiation (K14, K17, K5), dermal papilla activity (α-SMA, versican), and epithelial cell characteristics (AE13, AE15, K71, K75). Furthermore, Wnt10b, Notch1, Notch2, and Bmp6 gene expressions were analyzed to evaluate the activation of key signaling pathways involved in hair follicle development. Statistical analysis was performed using Student’s t-test or ANOVA followed by post-hoc tests (Tukey’s or Bonferroni) to compare differences between groups, utilizing SPSS version 25. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Characterization of GelMA hydrogel scaffold

The GelMA hydrogel was selected for its exceptional cell adhesion properties, making it a suitable substrate for cell growth and proliferation [28]. A 3D-printed 144-well multi-microarray was fabricated using 15% GelMA to create defined cavities for hair follicle formation and growth (Fig. 1C). The distance between wells was 1.5 mm. The depth and diameter of wells were 4 mm and 500µM, respectively. FTIR analysis confirmed the purity and chemical composition of the GelMA hydrogel. The FTIR spectrum exhibited characteristic GelMA peaks at 3300 cm− 1 (N-H stretching), 1650 cm− 1 (amide I), 1540 cm− 1 (amide II), and 1240 cm− 1 (amide III), confirming the presence of gelatin and methacrylamide groups. The absence of peaks at 1720 cm− 1 indicated the successful removal of residual methacrylic anhydride during the synthesis process.

SEM at 1000x magnification revealed a highly porous structure with interconnected pores (Fig. 1D). ImageJ analysis of SEM micrographs indicated an average pore diameter of approximately 50 ± 10 μm. The observed pore size distribution is ideal for cell attachment, migration, and nutrient diffusion. These structural features promote cell integration and interaction with the hydrogel matrix, facilitating cell-cell communication and matrix remodeling as Fig. 1E shows the successful attachment of DPCs to the GelMA hydrogel scaffold via SEM. Furthermore, the interconnected pore network allows for efficient waste removal, contributing to a healthy cellular microenvironment. These findings confirmed the suitability of the engineered hydrogel for 3D culture of DPC and epithelial cells for microfollicle formation.

Characterization of PMSCs and secretome

Placental tissue was obtained following informed written consent from a healthy donor, with confirmatory negative screening for HIV, HBV, and HCV. MSCs were isolated and purified via enzymatic digestion and subsequent culture. For differentiation test, MSCs were cultured in adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic media for 21 days. Staining cells with alizarin red, oil red O, and toluidine blue, showed the osteogenesis, adipogenesis, and chondrogenesis, confirming the differentiation potential of MSCs (Fig. 2A). Flow cytometric analysis (n = 3 replicates) confirmed that the isolated cells expressed mesenchymal stem cell markers CD105 (95.2% ± 2.1%), CD90 (97.8% ± 1.5%), and CD73 (96.5% ± 1.8%), while demonstrating minimal expression of hematopoietic lineage markers CD14 (1.2% ± 0.5%), CD45 (0.9% ± 0.3%), and CD34 (1.5% ± 0.6%). Furthermore, MSCs were negative for HLA-DR, CD80, CD86, and CD20 indicating a lack of immune activation (Fig. 2B). These findings confirmed the high purity of MSCs. Secretome from these MSCs was analyzed using a multiplex ELISA assay (n = 3 replicates) for the presence of various growth factors and cytokines. The highest levels of protein expression were observed for interleukin-1 receptor alpha (IL-1ra) (174.3 ± 15.2 pg/ml), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) (125.6 ± 11.8 pg/ml), fibroblast growth factor (FGF)-2 (62.6 ± 6.1 pg/ml), and epidermal growth factor (EGF) (62.2 ± 5.9 pg/ml). Low secretion levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 (4.5 ± 1.2 pg/ml) and IL-1β (undetectable and not shown in the figures) along with the high secretion of TGF-β1 (35.7 ± 4.1 pg/ml) which is an anti-inflammatory cytokine, confirm the anti-inflammatory feature of the MSCs (Fig. 2C). These data suggest that the MSC-secretome contains a combination of growth factors that stimulate hair follicle growth and immunomodulatory cytokines that are essential in maintaining a suitable microenvironment for hair follicle regeneration. Given the fact that exosomes are a crucial component of the MSC-secretome, we have characterized the exosomes using dynamic light scattering (DLS), transmission (TEM) and scanning (SEM) electron microscopy, and also immunophenotyping techniques (Supplementary Fig. 1). DLS, which measures the hydrodynamic diameter, indicated a predominant particle population with a peak size between 200 and 300 nm. Visualization by transmission and scanning electron microscopy (TEM/SEM) revealed the characteristic cup-shaped morphology, with diameters predominantly ranging from 10 to 40 nm. The larger size profile obtained by DLS is consistent with the technique’s sensitivity to the hydrated state of particles and the presence of a broader size distribution of extracellular vesicles. Furthermore, immunophenotypic analysis demonstrated high positivity for the canonical exosomal tetraspanin markers CD81 (84%), CD63 (79.5%), and CD9 (80.5%), confirming the successful isolation of exosomes. Quantification of the exosomal protein content via the Bradford assay revealed a concentration of 0.339 mg/mL.

Characterization of DPCs and epithelial cells

Human DPCs and epithelial cells were isolated from healthy scalp areas of a donor (Fig. 3A). Hair samples were collected, washed, and incubated in dispase II followed by culture in DMEM medium. Furthermore, the DPC and epithelial cells were dissected and cultured as explained in the method section (Fig. 3B). DPCs and epithelial cells were isolated and cultured up to passage two and cryopreserved. Figure 3C illustrates the DPCs (black arrows) and epithelial cells (red arrows) in the culture. Prior to microfollicle formation, the cultured cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (n = 3 replicates) for the expression of established DPC and epithelial cell markers. DPCs exhibited positive staining for alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (47.6% ± 5.5%), CD133 (69.8% ± 5.1%), and CD34 (42.2% ± 4.8%). Epithelial cells showed positive staining for CK15 (66.5% ± 6.2%) (Fig. 3D). These results confirm the identity of the isolated DPC and epithelial cell populations, indicating their suitability for microfollicle formation.

Hair microfollicles formation

Following the identity confirmation of DPCs and epithelial cells, a total of 1 × 104 DPCs were loaded into each well of the GelMA scaffold. After 5 days, 5 × 104 epithelial cells were seeded into the wells. The initial delay in seeding epithelial cells allows for DPC aggregation and organization, mimicking the initial stages of hair follicle development. After 3 weeks of culture, GelMA scaffolds were stained using H&E stains to assess the hair follicle formation (Fig. 3E). The percentage of cell aggregates (defined as clusters of at least 20 cells) exceeded 90% in all groups, indicating good cell seeding efficiency and initial aggregation (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, GelMA scaffold were examined both longitudinal and transverse using light microscopy and hair follicle formation was assessed by the presence of characteristic features such as organized cell layers and the beginning of hair shaft development (Fig. 3F). H&E staining of the GelMA scaffolds seeded with hair follicle cells a significant difference in the number of formed hair follicle-like structures was observed in response to Wnt10b and secretome treatment compared to the control groups. The percentage of hair follicle formation per created cavity (144 cavities per group) was quantified as follows: 17% in control group, 22% in the group treated only with Wnt10b, 23% in the group treated with secretome alone, and 28% in the group treated with Wnt10b + secretome. Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA revealed a highly significant difference between all treated groups, compared to control. Post-hoc comparisons via Tukey’s test demonstrated that group treated with Wnt10b + secretome significantly outperformed all other groups. However, there was no statistically significant difference between Wnt10b-treated group and secretome-treated group (Fig. 3G). These results illustrated that the treatment with combination of Wnt10b + secretome enhances hair follicle formation in a 3D culture model.

Two weeks post-transplantation, the hydrogels were surgically excised, and the transplantation sites were evaluated through transverse and longitudinal histological analyses.

H&E staining revealed a marked increase in de novo hair follicle formation (Fig. 5B) and longer follicle length (Fig. 5C) in the Wnt10b + secretome group compared to control group. The average number of newly formed hair follicles in the Wnt10b + secretome was quantified as 173 follicles per 6 mm² area of skin, while the control group (GelMA hydrogel only) exhibited a significantly lower count [21] of follicles per 6 mm² area (Fig. 5D). The average follicle length in the Wnt10b + secretome group was 264 μm, compared to 115 μm in the control group (Fig. 5E). Statistical analysis using an unpaired t-test confirmed that these differences were statistically significant (p < 0.0001). Furthermore, qualitative examination of the follicles in the Wnt10b + secretome group revealed advanced structural organization, including distinct epithelial and dermal compartments, suggestive of enhanced follicle maturation (Fig. 5B-C). Masson’s Trichrome staining provided further insights into the regenerative effects of the treatment. Masson’s Trichrome staining stains collagen fibers as an indicator of tissue regeneration. In this staining the blue-stained fibers are collagen. The collagen deposition or collagen area fraction (%) was quantified by converting the blue channel of the stained images into a binary mask using ImageJ software. As it is shown in the Figs. 5F-G, collagen deposition was increased in the Wnt10b + secretome group, compared to control group. Quantification of the deposited collagen indicated significantly higher collagen production in the Wnt10b + secretome group compared to the control group, at both transverse (Fig. 5H) and longitudinal (Fig. 5I) sections. This increase in collagen content suggests improved extracellular matrix remodeling and integration of the hydrogel into the host tissue. In addition to follicle formation, the Wnt10b + secretome group exhibited superior hair regrowth, with visible hair shafts emerging from 78% ± 6% of newly formed follicles. In contrast, hair shaft emergence was only observed in 19% ± 4% of follicles in the control group (p < 0.01). The newly grown hair shafts in the Wnt10b + secretome group showed an average thickness of 26 ± 3.5 μm, measured using brightfield microscopy, compared to 12 ± 2.1 μm in the control group (p < 0.01). Collectively, these findings corroborated the ex vivo results, confirming that combinational treatment with secretome and Wnt10b increases the number and length of microfollicles and also enhances tissue regenerative capacity in the animal model.

Discussion

This preclinical study introduces a bioengineered strategy for hair follicle regeneration, utilizing GelMA hydrogels to deliver DPCs and epithelial cells, synergistically enhanced with PMSC-secretome and Wnt10b protein. Our findings demonstrate that this combined approach significantly promotes hair follicle development ex vivo and in vivo, offering a potential therapeutic avenue for androgenic alopecia (AGA) that addresses key limitations of current treatments.

According to our results, the characterization of cultured DPCs and epithelial cells derived from the intact area of AGA donors revealed high expression levels of ALP (47.6%), CD133 (69.8%), CD34 (42.2%), and CK15 (66.5%). These findings align with the cellular profiles of DPC and keratinocyte markers reported in similar studies [29–34]. Studies have shown that increased expression of ALP is indicative of stemness, enhanced inductive potential, and regenerative capacity that support hair follicle formation [35–37]. Furthermore, CD133, a known marker of stem cells and progenitor cells, is associated with self-renewal and pluripotency and higher levels of CD133 indicate a preserved or enhanced stem cell-like profile, which is crucial for the inductive capabilities of the cells in hair follicle biology [24]. Finally, higher expression of CD34 which is presented in outer root sheath cells reflecting its role in cell adhesion and interactions with surrounding matrix components and supporting inductive functions [25].

Studies have revealed that hair follicle regrowth is highly dependent on the development of an engaging microenvironment. This microenvironment contributes to a regenerative niche with cellular interactions that are essential for effective hair regeneration processes [38]. Particularly, epithelial-mesenchymal interactions are crucial for hair follicle development, where mesenchymal cells drive epithelial proliferation, and epithelial signals regulate mesenchymal function [39, 40]. In this context, MSCs have shown considerable promise in promoting hair follicle regeneration. MSC-secretome, in general, has shown strong potential in promoting hair growth and regeneration through paracrine effects: secretion of growth factors, cytokines, and exosomes that modulate critical pathways for hair growth [41, 42]. PMSC-secretome was utilized based on its proven efficacy in promoting tissue regeneration and hair follicle neogenesis, as supported by recent studies showing its rich trophic factor content and anti-inflammatory profile, which outperform other MSC sources in some contexts [18]. Placenta is an ethically accessible, abundantly available tissue with high yields of MSCs exhibiting superior proliferative and immunomodulatory capacities compared to those from bone marrow or adipose tissue [18]. It has been well-known that the most effects of MSCs are through their secreted molecules such as extracellular vesicles, exosomes, proteins, growth factors, and cytokines. The secreted part of MSC can mimic the anti-inflammatory and regenerative effects of MSCs, so that the secreted parts of MSCs can be utilized as an alternative method of treatment for MSC therapy with a same efficacy and much lower concerns for injecting the MSC itself [43, 44].

This could be advantageous in managing AGA through the secretion of growth factors, as well as Wnt10b and BMP, which lead to the proliferation and differentiation of DPCs and epithelial cells [45]. We chose Wnt10b as a combinational therapy with PMSC-secretome because of its documented ability to activate follicle stem cells and induce robust hair follicle morphogenesis and growth, as evidenced by a substantial body of mechanistic and translational research [21, 22, 46, 47]. Wnt/β-catenin signaling is regarded as the principal pathway driving hair follicle morphogenesis, cycling, and regeneration. Specifically, Wnt10b has been shown to promote hair follicle stem cell activation, transition from telogen (rest) to anagen (growth), and induction of hair follicle neogenesis both in vivo and ex vivo [22]. Wnt10b promotes the differentiation of skin epithelial cells and dermal papilla cell proliferation, making it an ideal candidate for enhancing engineered follicle formation [21, 22, 46, 47].

In our study, the PMSC-secretome analysis revealed high levels of pro-angiogenic factors like HGF, FGF-2, and EGF, which are known to enhance cellular proliferation and tissue regeneration [48, 49]. Our results corroborate these findings, indicating that the secretome of PMSCs plays a crucial role in creating a favorable microenvironment for hair follicle regeneration. Secretome from PMSCs is rich in bioactive molecules, including anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β), growth factors (e.g., VEGF, HGF, KGF, FGF2, PDGF, IGF-I, EGF), and exosomes, which collectively support hair follicle regeneration and tissue repair through angiogenesis, stem cell activation, anti-apoptotic effects, and immunomodulation [50–54]. The selected cytokine and growth factor panel reflects established paracrine mechanisms of MSC-secretome in promoting follicle regeneration, as documented in regenerative medicine and preclinical hair research. TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1ra were included for their relevance to pathological hair loss and as indicators of immunomodulatory activity. IDO and HLA-G were included for their roles in the immunosuppressive and tolerogenic properties of MSC-secretome [51, 55]. Notably, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α have been directly linked to various forms of hair loss [56].

The use of 3D-modeled structures for cultivating DPCs and epithelial cells allows for continual improvement in hair density and structure, compared to previous two-dimensional methods. This biomimetic environment enhances cell physiology, optimizing hair regeneration and employing more effective hair restoration techniques for treating hair loss [57]. Moreover, researchers are increasingly using GelMA-based hydrogels in regenerative medicine. The effectiveness of these hydrogels in supporting hair follicles aligns with previous research [58]. Our use of a 3D-printed multi-array system in a 15% GelMA composition created optimal conditions for DPCs and epithelial cell interaction, supporting the formation and growth of microfollicles. Similar findings have been reported by previous study that noted that GelMA scaffolds facilitate enhanced cell integration and differentiation, making them suitable substrates for hair tissue engineering applications [59]. Application of advanced three-dimensional structures and multi-array systems is a significant advancement in hair follicle regeneration, allowing for optimal distribution of therapeutics crucial for inducing hair follicles. Microneedles aid in delivering cells, growth factors, and bioactive molecules directly into the dermis, stimulating hair follicle growth [60]. This kind of precision allows for improved cell assimilation with the benefit of enhancement of supportive spatial microenvironments for hair follicle growth. Multi-array systems mimic the native structure of the extracellular matrix, a scaffold usually comprised of assembled wells or compartments. This framework allows placement for the organization and proliferation of hair follicle progenitor cells, which in turn enhances the differentiation and functional maturation of hair follicle progenitor cells. Microneedle multi-array platforms have been presented recently in various models for tissue engineering applications, with one of the most significant methods introduced in 2018 [9]. The platform used in our study shares similarities with that model but differs in dimensions, thickness, number of arrays, the arrangement of rows, and the depth. This platform’s success rate in hair follicle implantation and production exceeded 90%, as reported in previous sections. The aggregates formed in the GelMA mold in our study were similar to the results obtained by Sugi Hirano and colleagues using a similar platform with collagen gel [4]. A previous study developed a method to produce high-passage DPCs with enhanced hair-inducing ability using 3D Matrigel culture. The authors cultured 1 × 104 DPCs on the 96-well plates precoated with Matrigel for 5 days and found that both passage 2 and passage 8 DPCs formed spheroidal microtissues with a diameter of 150–250 μm. Then, they transplanted the dermal papilla spheroids into the backs of nude mice along with neonatal epidermal cells. The transplants formed new hair follicles that were like human hair follicles in structure and composition. The findings of the present study regarding the creation of a three-dimensional model and the implantation of epithelial cells align with the data from Tatsuto Kageyama and colleagues, who developed a hair follicle growth model for evaluating the effects of drug interventions in vitro, demonstrating that the implemented model was highly effective in expressing related markers [11]. In another study, authors tried to co-culture of primary cells, including ORS keratinocytes, DPCs, and melanocytes in 96-well plates based on spatio-temporally defined manner to mimic the natural hair follicle structure. Then, in vitro microfollicles were treated with minoxidil and they found that gene expression profiles of the microfollicles had up to 75% overlap in gene expression changes in response to minoxidil. The research concluded that a microfollicle is a practical and complex model and offers advantages over other models, such as scalability, affordability, and human relevance [61]. In a recent study, researchers constructed 3D printed skin equivalents containing hair follicles with epidermal and dermal layers using gelatin methacryloyl/hyaluronic acid methacryloyl (GelMA/HAMA) bioink. The authors found that GelMA/HAMA bioink showed excellent viscoelastic and physicochemical properties, 3D printability, cytocompatibility, and functionality to maintain hair inductivity [62].

Interestingly, newly formed follicles in the mouse skin grafts exhibited pigmentation over time. As our hydrogel transplantation protocol did not intentionally include melanocytes, this pigmentation most likely originated from the recruitment of host (mouse) melanocytes rather than from residual human cells. This observation is consistent with established literature, which documents that host melanocyte stem cells residing in the hair follicle bulge region migrate to repopulate newly formed follicles in xenotransplantation and tissue-engineered models, thereby restoring pigmentation [63–65].

In our study, we observed the expression of proteins AE13, AE15, K71, K75, ALP, Versican, K15 and, to a lesser extent, αSMA and COL1 in immunofluorescence images from hair microfollicles biofabricated both ex vivo and in vivo. According to previous studies, AE13 is typically associated with epithelial cells within the hair shaft, while AE15 and K71 are present in the inner root sheath of the hair follicle, participating in keratinization processes and contributing to the structural integrity of the hair follicle [9, 41]. K75 is expressed in the hair shaft and inner root sheath, playing a role in the strength and resilience of keratinocytes in the hair follicle, similar to K71 [9, 41]. K15, expressed in the hair follicle bulge region and outer root sheath, is a marker for stem cells and is associated with the multipotent characteristics of progenitor cells in hair follicles [9, 41]. ALP is primarily expressed in DPCs and sometimes in the bulge region [9, 66]. Our study revealed higher expression of ALP in the biofabricated model compared to the 2D culture system, suggesting stem cell activity and differentiation processes. However, regarding the different quantification method of ALP expression in 2D versus 3D culture, the results should be interpreted cautiously. Versican, expressed in the DPCs and outer root sheath of hair follicles, provides structural support as an intermediate filament protein [9]. αSMA and COL1 are found in the dermal sheath surrounding the hair follicle and are especially expressed in 3D culture systems [9, 66]. Although we used an extensive staining panel including several markers for epithelial cells and DPCs, our panel could benefit from more markers such as vimentin and E-cadherin or co-staining of these markers together. Based on the identified hair follicle-specific markers, our study demonstrated successful ex vivo biofabrication of hair microfollicles, which were then transplanted into an animal model. Notably, our in vivo experiments showed that the transplanted microfollicles promoted hair growth. Mice receiving the secretome and Wnt10b-treated hydrogel exhibited increased collagen production and hair follicle density compared to controls. Furthermore, higher expressions of Wnt10b, Bmp6, and Notch1, along with lower expression of Notch2 in RT-PCR analysis of tissue samples, suggest enhanced hair follicle formation and cycling, hair growth activation, and stem cell maintenance, consistent with previous findings [67]. Our inclusion of Wnt10b, in concert with PMSC-secretome, may leverage a dedifferentiation-driven neogenesis pathway analogous to folliculogenesis described in recent reviews. Zou et al. synthesize evidence that Wnt10b facilitates epidermal dedifferentiation and subsequent HF neogenesis after injury, which complements our in vivo findings of enhanced follicle density and maturation [68]. In the transplanted, GelMA-based niche, Wnt10b likely acts on epithelial progenitors and DPCs to foster a regenerative loop, supported by secretome-derived pro-angiogenic and immunomodulatory signals. Recent meta-analyses reaffirm the efficacy of the MSCs secretome in treating alopecia, highlighting paracrine factors that modulate apoptosis, inflammation, and follicular signaling. GelMA hydrogels have also shown strong biocompatibility and mechanical suitability for hair follicle bioprinting, enabling faithful cellular organization and inductive interactions within engineered microenvironments [17]. Kamar et al. demonstrated that umbilical cord-derived MSC secretomes exhibit superior anti-apoptotic and hair follicle regeneration activities compared to standard pharmacologic therapy such as minoxidil [69, 70]. However, to date, direct comparisons of hair follicle regenerative efficacy between placenta-derived, umbilical cord-derived, and adipose-derived MSC secretomes are limited [20]. Future studies employing standardized in vitro and in vivo models will be important to clarify their relative therapeutic potential in hair regeneration. Collectively, these strands have inspired a synergistic model wherein Wnt10b signaling and MSC secretome-mediated paracrine cues converge within a GelMA scaffold to promote follicle formation and cycling.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents a novel and effective strategy for hair follicle regeneration by combining GelMA hydrogels, PMSC-secretome, and Wnt10b. By creating a regenerative microenvironment that promotes hair follicle development and growth, this approach has the potential to overcome the limitations of current AGA treatments. Our results demonstrate a clear improvement over existing methods, particularly in terms of stem cell maintenance, hair follicle density, and the comprehensive modulation of key signaling pathways.

However, prior studies have notable limitations that hinder direct translation. A common challenge is inconsistent hair follicle cycling and maturation in bioprinted or biofabricated models, often due to variability in construct vascularization, niche cues, or timing and delivery of signaling factors. Some 3D/bioprinted systems exhibit transient or waning inductive capacity, with HF-cycle dynamics not matching native follicle rhythms. Additionally, heterogeneity in MSC sources, conditioning regimens, and secretome preparations can yield variable outcomes, complicating cross-study comparisons. These gaps underscore the need for standardized, reproducible designs—such as defined GelMA compositions, controlled release of Wnt10b and secretome components, and robust in vivo validation—to reliably translate synergistic MSC-secretome and Wnt-based strategies into durable therapies for alopecia.

A limitation of this study is the small sample size (n = 3/group). Although determined by power analysis, this number may not fully capture biological variability in hair follicle regeneration, potentially reducing statistical power. Larger cohorts are needed to confirm these findings. Future studies should focus on optimizing the delivery system, evaluating long-term efficacy, and translating these findings to clinical trials in humans. This includes investigating the optimal dosage and delivery method for the PMSC-secretome and Wnt10b, as well as assessing the long-term stability and functionality of the regenerated hair follicles.
