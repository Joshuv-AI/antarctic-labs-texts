Phenanthrene Exposure Induces Immune Dysfunction and Metabolic Disruption in Mice
Authors: Qiaojuan Liu, Yan Zhang, Fenfang Deng, Wenxue Li, Guangyu Yang, Jun Yuan, Yuehong Wei, Juntao Li, Lei Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are widespread environmental pollutants that may impair immune function. However, the immunotoxic mechanisms of PAH exposure remain largely unexplored. The present study was designed as a mechanistic, effect-oriented investigation, in which male C57BL/6J mice were exposed to phenanthrene via intraperitoneal injection for six consecutive weeks. We assessed toxicological effects of phenanthrene exposure in mice through urinary metabolite profiling, hematological and immunological tests, and serum metabolomics. Urinary concentrations of phenanthrene metabolites demonstrated a clear dose-dependent increase across exposure groups, confirming internal accumulation. Relative organ weight analysis revealed significantly elevated kidney and lung weights in all exposed groups compared with controls. Hematological analysis revealed alterations in leukocyte subpopulations. Flow cytometric analysis showed that lymphocyte percentages among splenic cells were significantly decreased in phenanthrene-exposed mice compared with controls, with reductions of 13.74%, 24.92%, and 27.41% in the low-, medium-, and high-dose groups, respectively. The proportions of CD3+, CD4+, or CD8+ T cell subsets within the lymphocyte population remained stable. Notably, a dose-dependent increase in proinflammatory cytokine-producing CD4+ T cells was observed. Moreover, exposure to phenanthrene resulted in a dose-dependent decrease in serum IgG concentrations, with levels reduced by 22.2% in the high-dose group compared with controls. Serum metabolomics profiling revealed significant reductions in galactosylglycerol derivatives and quinic acid, alongside elevated phosphatidylserine levels. Moreover, marked increases in glucuronide conjugates suggested activation of phase II detoxification pathways. Lipidomic analysis demonstrated disturbances in phospholipid homeostasis and the accumulation of diacylglycerol and triacylglycerol species. These results provide multiple lines of mechanistic evidence for phenanthrene-induced immune and metabolic perturbations.

Introduction

Growing evidence suggests that exposure to environmental pollutants is a significant threat to human health. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are a class of ubiquitous environmental pollutants primarily generated through the incomplete combustion of fossil fuels and organic materials. , Their environmental persistence and biological activity are largely attributed to their aromatic ring structures and physicochemical properties. Humans are exposed to PAHs through multiple pathways, including inhalation of polluted air from vehicular exhaust, industrial emissions, and tobacco smoke, and ingestion of charred or smoked foods. Due to their widespread environmental distribution and persistence, PAHs can enter the food chain and contribute to continuous human exposure. Epidemiological and toxicological studies have associated PAH exposure with various adverse health effects. ,

The immune system plays a fundamental role in protecting the host from infections and maintaining physiological homeostasis, yet it is particularly vulnerable to environmental pollutants. High levels of PAH exposure are associated with immunosuppression, including impaired immune cell function and reduced antibody responses. Specifically, humoral immunity, which is mediated by B cell-derived immunoglobulins and complement proteins, plays a crucial role in neutralizing antigens, promoting phagocytosis, and activating the complement cascade. Oral Benzo­(a)­pyrene exposure in mice for 28 days led to a dose-dependent suppression of the T-dependent antibody response to an immunization. Notably, even a low dose of 2 mg/kg/day B­[a]P caused about a 32% decrease in antigen-specific IgM antibody levels, and a dose of 9 mg/kg caused up to a 75% reduction in antibody production. These findings suggest that other PAHs, including phenanthrene, may also have the potential to interfere with humoral immune responses.

Epidemiological studies have also indicated that PAH exposure is associated with immune suppression, altered immune cell profiles, and increased inflammation. Occupational exposure to PAHs was associated with suppressed T-lymphocyte proliferation in both asphalt and coke oven workers, while low-level exposure in asphalt workers was linked to enhanced natural killer cell activity. Yao and coworkers found that wintertime exposure to elevated levels of PAHs from household air pollution among rural women in Gansu, China was associated with decreased regulatory T cell function, reduced expression of immunoregulatory genes, and increased oxidative DNA damage. Lauer and coworkers investigated how individual and combined exposures to arsenic and PAHs affect immune cell populations in Bangladeshi men. The study found that arsenic was linked to increased proinflammatory Th17 cells, while PAHs induced nonmonotonic changes across various T helper subsets and memory T cells. Chen and coworkers found that children living in e-waste areas had elevated levels of urinary OH-PAHs, which were associated with intestinal immune alterations, including increased inflammatory markers, reduced CD4+ T cell percentages, and heightened sIgA levels. Cheng and coworkers found that preschool children exposed to PAHs in an e-waste area exhibited elevated expression of aryl hydrocarbon receptor and NLR family pyrin domain-containing 3, along with increased levels of multiple proinflammatory cytokines. The results suggest that PAH exposure may contribute to cytokine storm-like immune responses. Previous reviews have demonstrated that PAHs may exert dose-dependent immunomodulatory effects. ,, High-level exposure may suppress immune responses and induce lymphocyte apoptosis, while low-level exposure may enhance immune signaling. These findings highlight the importance of exposure dose in determining the direction and magnitude of PAH-induced immune responses. Moreover, oxidative stress generated during PAH metabolism can also be a key driver of systemic physiological imbalance, contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic disturbances.

In our previous biomonitoring study, we assessed internal exposure to PAHs by measuring parent compounds in serum and their metabolites in urine among 216 adults, and explored their associations with humoral immune markers. We found that elevated concentrations of several PAHs and their hydroxylated metabolites were significantly associated with reductions in immunoglobulin G, immunoglobulin A, and complement C3. Specifically, phenanthrene, a low molecular weight PAH, was identified as a key contributor to the negative association between PAH exposure and humoral immune function. The potential immunosuppressive effect of phenanthrene on humoral immunity may be modified by oxidative stress and systemic inflammation. However, observational studies are inherently limited in their ability to infer causal relationships. Moreover, increasing evidence suggests that interactions among metabolite, microbiota, and the immune crosstalk plays an important role in pollutant-induced immune modulation.

This study aimed to evaluate phenanthrene-induced immune imbalance in mice by assessing changes in immune cell profiles, antibody production, and metabolic alterations. Rather than aiming to replicate environmentally relevant exposure levels, the animal study was designed as a mechanistic and effect-oriented investigation to characterize measurable immune alterations and metabolic responses. Mice were exposed to phenanthrene via intraperitoneal injection for six consecutive weeks. We confirmed internal exposure by quantifying urinary phenanthrene metabolites and assessed potential organ toxicity through organ-to-body weight ratios. Comprehensive immunological evaluations included hematological analyses and flow cytometry to quantify immune cell populations and cytokine measurements to assess systemic immune responses. Furthermore, we employed small-molecule metabolomics and lipidomics analyses to investigate metabolic alterations. We hypothesized that phenanthrene exposure would disrupt immune homeostasis by altering lymphocyte profiles and cytokine responses, and that it would concurrently induce systemic metabolic alterations.

Experimental Section

Animals and Treatments

All animal experiments in this study were conducted in full compliance with the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986 and associated guidelines on the protection of animals. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Guangzhou Center for Disease Control and Prevention (No. 2023060). Male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were purchased from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Foshan, China). Mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free facility under a 12 h light/dark cycle at 23 °C, with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. After 1 week of acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 mice in each group): a control group and three phenanthrene-treated groups. The investigator responsible for experimental administration was blinded to group allocation until after data collection was completed. The phenanthrene doses were 50, 100, and 200 mg/kg, representing low, medium, and high concentrations, respectively. Notably, the selection of exposure dose was based on an effect-oriented range design to observe measurable immune outcomes rather than to mimic environmental exposure. Mice were administered phenanthrene via intraperitoneal injection at daily doses of 50, 100, or 200 mg/kg for six consecutive weeks. The injection volume was adjusted according to body weight at 0.1 mL per 10 g body weight, with a maximum volume of 400 μL per injection to ensure dosing accuracy. The mice were also provided with irradiation sterilized experimental animal feed. Body weight and general health status were monitored regularly throughout the exposure period to assess potential signs of systemic toxicity. Urine samples were collected over a 24-h period under fed conditions, with mice having free access to food and water. After centrifugation, urinary supernatants were collected and stored at −80 °C until analysis. 24 h after the final exposure, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and blood samples were collected from the vena cava into EDTA-K2–coated tubes. Following blood collection, the animals were euthanized under anesthesia. Whole blood samples were gently inverted to ensure thorough mixing and analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C for further analyses.

Determination of Urinary Phenanthrene Metabolites

Urinary hydroxylated phenanthrene metabolites, including 1-OHP, 2-OHP, 3-OHP, and 4-OHP, were measured based on our previously published method. , Briefly, 1 mL of urine sample was mixed with 0.5 mL of ammonium acetate buffer (pH 5.5), 100 μL of internal standard solution (100 μg/L), and 20 μL of β-glucuronidase. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C overnight in a water bath for enzymatic hydrolysis. Following hydrolysis, liquid–liquid extraction was performed using n-hexane. The organic phase was collected and evaporated to near dryness under a gentle nitrogen stream at 40 °C. The residue was reconstituted in 200 μL of 20% acetonitrile in water. Quantification of the phenanthrene metabolites was conducted using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry. The limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) for phenanthrene metabolites were 0.005 μg/L and 0.02 μg/L, respectively.

Assessment of Immune Cell Populations and Humoral Immune Response

Spleens were harvested and mechanically dissociated to obtain single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells were lysed using RBC Lysis Buffer (10×, BioLegend, Cat. No. 420301) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The splenic cells were washed and resuspended in BSA-NaCl buffer for surface staining. Cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies targeting surface markers CD3, CD4, CD8. Zombie NIR Fixable Viability Dye (BioLegend) was used for live/dead cell discrimination. For intracellular cytokine detection, cells were stimulated for 4–6 h at 37 °C in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, 100 nM; Sigma-Aldrich), ionomycin (1 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), and a protein transport inhibitor (1:1000; BD Biosciences). PMA/ionomycin stimulation was used to induce maximal cytokine production capacity of T cells, reflecting functional responsiveness rather than basal immune status. After stimulation, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Intracellular Fixation/Permeabilization Buffer Set (BioLegend, Cat. No. 420801/421002), followed by staining with fluorochrome-labeled antibodies against IFN-γ and TNF-α. All stained cells were acquired using a NovoCyte 2060R flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies, Hangzhou, China), and data were analyzed with NovoExpress software (Agilent). Flow cytometry gating strategy was shown in Figure S2A. Briefly, we first gated on lymphocytes using FSC/SSC and gated on single cells using FSC-A/FSC-H, followed by exclusion of dead cells using a viability dye. The population of interest was then identified based on CD3+ T cells, and further analysis of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells was performed. Serum IgG concentrations were determined using a commercial ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Detailed ELISA procedures are provided in the Supporting Information.

Small-Molecule and Lipidomics Analysis of Serum Samples

The small-molecule metabolomics and lipidomics analyses were performed based on previous studies, with minor modifications. , For small-molecule metabolomics analysis, 50 μL of serum sample and 200 μL of internal standard solution (1 μg/mL in acetonitrile) were added to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. The mixture was vortexed for 5 min and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Then, 100 μL of the supernatant (acetonitrile layer) was transferred to a 2 mL autosampler vial equipped with a glass insert. Pooled QC samples were prepared by combining equal volumes (10 μL) from all study samples and were injected at regular intervals throughout the batch to assess system stability and analytical reproducibility.

For lipidomics analysis, 500 μL of internal standard solution (0.5 μg/mL in acetonitrile) and 50 μL of serum sample were added to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. The mixture was vortexed for 3 min, followed by the addition of 100 μL of water and another 3 min of vortexing. The samples were then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Pooled QC samples were prepared by combining equal volumes (10 μL) from all study samples and were injected at regular intervals throughout the batch to assess system stability and analytical reproducibility. Note: Regular vial caps may lead to polymer-derived background signals (e.g., m/z 78); certified low-bleed caps from Waters were employed to minimize analytical interference.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed by an investigator who was blinded to the group assignments. Sample identifiers were coded, and the blinding was maintained until all analyses were completed and the primary outcomes were determined. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using the Brown–Forsythe test. Differences among groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For variables involving dose–response evaluation, linear trend analysis was conducted using regression modeling. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001). Raw LC–HRMS data were processed using Progenesis QI (3.0), including peak picking, alignment, deconvolution, and initial compound identification (Table S7). Specifically, metabolite identification was performed based on MS/MS spectral matching against metabolite databases, together with manual inspection of chromatographic peak quality and retention time consistency. The identified metabolites correspond to putatively annotated compounds (level 2). Subsequently, we applied a multivariate statistical analysis to assess group-level differences in serum metabolite profiles following PAH exposure in mice. Raw data, including m/z values, peak areas, and sample annotations, were first imported into EZinfo 3.0.3 (Waters) for model development. Then, data were mean-centered and Pareto-scaled to reduce heteroscedasticity and normalize metabolite variance. This preprocessing step helped balance the contribution of high- and low-abundance features, enhancing the biological interpretability of the models. An initial unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on all ion features to visualize sample clustering, detect outliers, and explore global variance patterns without reference to group labels. Subsequently, orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed to identify metabolites that discriminated between predefined groups. The reliability of the OPLS-DA model was evaluated using permutation testing (n = 200) to assess the risk of overfitting. Student’s t test was first applied to all detected metabolomic features, and the p-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method to control for multiple testing. Metabolites with FDR-adjusted P-values <0.05 were further filtered based on variable importance in projection (VIP ≥ 1) and fold change criteria (FC ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5).

Results

Urinary Phenanthrene Metabolite Profiles and Organ Toxicity Induced by Phenanthrene Exposure in Mice

Urinary concentrations of four hydroxylated phenanthrene metabolites were quantified in mice following graded exposure to phenanthrene (Figure ). A significant and dose-dependent increase in metabolite levels was observed in all exposed groups relative to controls. The highest metabolite concentrations were observed in the phenanthrene-200 group. The results confirm a dose-responsive accumulation of phenanthrene metabolites in urine, reflecting enhanced metabolic activation with increasing phenanthrene exposure. Body weight and relative weights of major organs were assessed to determine the physiological effects of phenanthrene exposure in mice.

Asterisks indicate statistically significant differences between groups (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

As shown in Figure A, no significant differences in body weight were observed across groups. Similarly, there was no significant difference in the relative spleen and liver weights between control group and exposed groups (Figure B and Figure C). However, relative kidney weight was significantly elevated in all groups, indicating a dose-related effect (Figure D). Relative lung weight also showed a significant increase at all exposure groups (Figure E). Specifically, compared to the control group, relative kidney weight increased by 49.81%, 40.57%, and 42.70% in the low-, medium-, and high-dose groups, and relative lung weight showed corresponding increases of 26.17%, 21.21%, and 29.83%. The relative brain weights of phenanthrene-100 treated mice were slightly higher than that in the control group (Figure F). These results indicate that exposure to phenanthrene may induce target organ toxicity in mice. However, the biological significance of this modest increase remains unclear and should be interpreted cautiously.

Relative organ weight was calculated as the ratio of organ weight to body weight. n = 6 mice in each group. Alterations of Leukocyte Profiles and Activation of Proinflammatory Response Induced by Phenanthrene Exposure in Mice

As shown in Figure , complete blood counts were performed to assess the hematological effects of phenanthrene exposure in mice. There were no significant differences in total white blood cell counts (Figure A) or red blood cell counts (Figure B) among groups. However, significant changes were observed in leukocyte subpopulations. The relative percentages of neutrophils and monocytes were significantly decreased in the exposed groups compared to the control group, especially in the phenanthrene-200 group (Figure C and Figure D). The percentage of eosinophils was increased in the phenanthrene-100 group but markedly reduced at the highest dose (Figure E). The percentage of lymphocytes was significantly increased in the phenanthrene-200 group (Figure F). Consistently, absolute neutrophil counts were significantly decreased in the phenanthrene-200 group compared to the control group (Figure S1A). Absolute monocyte counts exhibited a decreasing trend across phenanthrene-exposed groups; however, the differences did not reach statistical significance (Figure S1B). Absolute eosinophil counts were elevated in the phenanthrene-100 group relative to controls but were markedly reduced in the phenanthrene-200 group (Figure S1C). No significant differences were observed in absolute lymphocyte counts among the groups (Figure S1D). These findings indicate that phenanthrene exposure may induce immunotoxicity by disrupting immune cell homeostasis.

Lymphocytes, including T and B cells, reflect the level of adaptive immune response. Flow cytometry analysis was conducted to examine the impact of phenanthrene exposure on lymphocyte populations in the spleen. We used a sequential gating strategy to identify viable T cell subsets (Figure S2A). Initial gating on forward scatter and side scatter parameters allowed for the selection of the lymphocyte population based on size and granularity characteristics. Representative dot plots for IFN-γ and TNF-α expression in CD4+ T cells from each group are shown in Figure S2B and Figure S2C, respectively. As shown in Figure A, the percentages of total lymphocytes among all splenic cells were significantly decreased in phenanthrene-exposed mice compared to controls, with decreases of 13.74%, 24.92%, and 27.41% in the low-, medium-, and high-dose groups, respectively. However, the percentage of CD3+ T cells among live cells (Figure B), as well as the percentages of CD4+ T cells (Figure C) and CD8+ T cells (Figure D) within the CD3+ T-cell population, remained unchanged across all groups. These findings suggest that phenanthrene exposure significantly reduces the overall lymphocyte proportion in the splenic cells, without altering the distribution of CD4+ and CD8+ subsets within CD3+ cells. Compared to the control group, the percentages of IFN-γ+ cells within CD4+ T cells in the phenanthrene-50 and phenanthrene-100 groups were significantly increased by 90.28% and 119.30%, respectively (Figure E). Similarly, TNF-α+ CD4+ T cells were significantly elevated in a dose-dependent manner across all exposed groups, with elevations of 27.79%, 51.02%, and 87.53% in the low-, medium-, and high-dose groups, respectively (Figure F). These results indicate that phenanthrene exposure enhances proinflammatory cytokine production by CD4+ T cells, particularly TNF-α, suggesting an inflammatory effect.

Serum Metabolomics Reveals Dose-Dependent Alterations Following Phenanthrene Exposure

To investigate the systemic metabolic effects of phenanthrene, serum metabolomics profiling was performed. Principal component analysis revealed distinct clustering patterns between control and phenanthrene-treated groups at all three exposure levels (Figure A–C, Figure E–G), indicating that phenanthrene exposure induced significant changes in the serum metabolite profile. As shown in Figure D, several metabolites were significantly altered in all exposed group under positive ion mode. Compared to the control group, levels of 1,2-dioctadecanoyl-3-(galactosyl-β-1,6-galactosyl-β-1)-glycerol, Emblicanin B, 1,3,4,5-tetra-O-galloylquinic acid, and 2-methylhexenoyl-CoA were significantly reduced in all exposed groups, with the greatest reductions observed at highest doses. In contrast, levels of PS (12:0_14:0) were significantly elevated following phenanthrene exposure, particularly in the phenanthrene-100 group. Figure H shows several significantly altered metabolites under negative ion mode. Levels of avocadyne acetate were significantly reduced in all phenanthrene-treated groups compared to controls. In contrast, a marked elevation of glucuronic acid, 2-C-methyl-1,4-erythrono-d-lactone, and particularly trichloroethanol glucuronide was observed, especially in the high-dose (phenanthrene-200) group. The sharp increase in conjugated metabolites such as glucuronides suggests enhanced phase II detoxification activity and systemic xenobiotic stress response upon phenanthrene exposure.

Discussion

Main Findings

In this study, we assessed the toxicological effects of phenanthrene exposure in mice through urinary metabolite profiling, hematological and immunological tests, and serum metabolomics. Hematological analysis indicated alterations in leukocyte subsets, suggesting that phenanthrene exposure may perturb immune cell homeostasis. Such alterations in leukocyte composition may influence immune signaling pathways and contribute to the enhanced production of proinflammatory cytokines observed in CD4+ T cells, including IFN-γ and TNF-α. The elevation of these cytokines suggests that phenanthrene exposure may promote a Th1-related inflammatory response. These cytokines are known to play important roles in shaping the immune microenvironment and regulating interactions among immune cells. However, as markers of other T-helper cell subsets were not assessed in this study, the potential shift in the Th1/Th2 balance should be interpreted with caution. Table S8 compares the exposure routes, doses, immune end points, and main findings of previous studies investigating the immune effects of phenanthrene exposure. Previous studies have reported that phenanthrene and other PAHs can disrupt immune function, mainly by altering cytokine expression, lymphocyte profiles, and oxidative stress responses. Our findings are generally consistent with these studies, demonstrating that phenanthrene exposure can induce immunotoxic effects by altering immune cell profiles, reducing serum IgG levels, and increasing cytokines associated with Th1 immune responses. We also explored phenanthrene-induced alterations in serum metabolomic and lipidomic profiles. Serum metabolomics showed that phenanthrene exposure reduced many endogenous metabolites, including lipids, antioxidants, and amino-acid derivatives. Meanwhile, levels of glucuronidated compounds and other conjugated metabolites were elevated, suggesting enhanced phase II xenobiotic metabolism. Lipid homeostasis was markedly disrupted. Phosphatidylethanolamine species declined, whereas diacylglycerols and triacylglycerols accumulated, indicating accelerated lipid turnover and impaired regulation of energy metabolism. Emerging evidence indicates that metabolic reprogramming, particularly alterations in lipid metabolism, can influence immune-cell activation and polarization by reshaping the cellular metabolic environment. However, some metabolites detected in the untargeted metabolomics analysis, particularly plant-derived polyphenols or uncommon lipid species, may represent dietary components or tentative database annotations.

Potential Mechanisms of PAH-Induced Immunotoxicity

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) is a ligand-activated transcription factor that is essential for xenobiotic metabolism. Generally, exposure to PAHs activates AhR and drives its translocation to the nucleus, where it induces genes that encode detoxification enzymes and other metabolic proteins. PAHs influence immune regulation by activating AhR, which alters T-cell differentiation and dendritic-cell function. Persistent and high-level stimulation of AhR generally suppresses immunity, whereas lower or intermittent activation can instead promote immune responses. Activation of AhR by TCDD and other PAHs leads to profound immunosuppression, potentially via skewed Th1/Th2 cell balance and promoted Treg cell differentiation. Based on this, we speculate that the phenanthrene-mediated increase in IFN-γ+ CD4+ T cells and decrease in IgG may be partially mediated via AhR activation. Oxidative stress induced by PAH metabolism may represent another key mechanism. Cytochrome P450 enzymes are thought to convert PAHs into reactive intermediates that generate reactive oxygen species. Previous studies have indicated that human exposure to PAHs is positively associated with oxidative stress biomarkers. , Elevated oxidative stress may damage cellular membranes and DNA, thereby initiating lipid peroxidation in immune cells. In addition, oxidative stress may directly activate NF-κB signaling in immune cells, contributing to proinflammatory cytokine production. The observed oxidative alterations may also engage redox-sensitive transcriptional regulators such as Nrf2, which has been shown to modulate both antioxidant responses and immune homeostasis. Our metabolomics analysis revealed alterations in metabolites with potential antioxidant functions and in lipid-related metabolites. These findings may reflect oxidative stress–related disturbances in cellular metabolism following phenanthrene exposure. Notably, AhR signaling and oxidative stress pathways were not directly measured in the present study, and the proposed mechanistic interpretations should be considered hypothesis-driven.

PAH exposure may also remodel epigenetic regulation of immune genes. Human studies have reported hypermethylation of FOXP3, the master transcription factor for regulatory T cells, in populations with high PAH burdens. This epigenetic silencing could hinder T-regulatory-cell differentiation and tilt immune homeostasis toward autoimmunity or allergy. Differential microRNA profiles (miR-223) have also been linked to PAH exposure and reduced Treg levels in cord blood. Exposure to PAHs may markedly impair T-cell–mediated immunity. Previous studies have shown that PAHs suppress T-cell proliferation and function. Parvez and coworkers investigated the independent effects of chronic arsenic and PAH exposures on immune function. Urinary arsenic levels were associated with altered cytokine production, notably IL-1β, while PAH-DNA adducts were linked to changes in T-cell proliferation, with no observed interaction between the two exposures. Research on coke-oven workers with high PAH exposure has reported a diminished mitogen response in T lymphocytes, suggesting functional T-cell suppression. Likewise, a large Bangladeshi cohort showed that higher blood levels of PAH–DNA adducts were associated with fewer circulating CD4+ T-helper cells, and the highest-exposure group exhibited an overall reduction in CD4+ counts. PAH exposure may not only suppress T-cell proliferation but also drive T-cell differentiation toward pro-inflammatory phenotypes. Increasing evidence indicates that both single PAH congeners and PAH-rich airborne mixtures can enhance Th17 differentiation via AhR signaling. In mice, inhalation of PAH-containing particulate matter increased Th17 frequencies and IL-17 concentrations while reducing regulatory T (Treg) cells, a shift associated with aggravated autoimmune pathology. However, lymphocyte proliferation was not evaluated in the present study. Macrophages exhibit considerable metabolic and functional plasticity, and toxicant exposure may alter macrophage-driven immune regulation through metabolic reprogramming and inflammatory signaling pathways. Epidemiological studies have also indicated the effects of PAH exposure on humoral immunity. Among rural women in Gansu, China, winter household PAH exposure rose significantly and was associated with an estimated 75% reduction in serum IgE together with down-regulation of cytokine genes associated with regulatory T cells. In coke-oven workers who had markedly elevated concentrations of high-molecular-weight PAHs, serum IgA was approximately 15% higher, and IgE levels were positively associated with dibenzo­(a,h)­anthracene, benzo­[a]­pyrene, and indeno­[1,2,3-cd]­pyrene. These antibody shifts were accompanied by increased tumor-necrosis-factor-α and oxidative-stress biomarkers. Szczeklik and coworkers demonstrated that in Polish coke-oven workers who inhaled up to 50 μg m–3 of BaP, mean serum IgG and IgA were approximately 15% lower than those in low-exposed mill workers, whereas IgE levels tended to rise. Among children living in an e-waste-recycling area, urinary metabolites of PAHs were positively associated with secretory IgA. This rise in IgA was accompanied by mucosal inflammation and a higher incidence of diarrhea, suggesting that PAH exposure may alter gut-mediated humoral immunity. In asphalt-paving workers whose urinary 1-hydroxypyrene concentrations were several times higher than those of controls, serum IgG was significantly higher, whereas IgM and IgA remained unchanged. In a nationally representative Korean survey, adults in the highest quartile of urinary 2-hydroxyfluorene had 1.6–1.7 times higher odds of elevated total IgE than those in the lowest quartile. In the present study, we found that exposure to phenanthrene in a dose–response mouse model induced renal and pulmonary injury, accompanied by a Th1-skewed immune profile and marked alterations in lipid and energy metabolism.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although mice are widely used in toxicological research, interspecies differences in xenobiotic metabolism, immune system architecture, and susceptibility to PAHs may limit the direct extrapolation of findings to human populations. This study focused on a single PAH compound in animal experiments. Coexposure to different classes of organic pollutants may modify toxicokinetics and enhance adverse biological effects through pollutant–pollutant interactions. Specifically, human exposure generally involves complex mixtures of multiple PAHs, including low-molecular-weight compounds from air pollution and high-molecular-weight PAHs from dietary sources, which may exert additive or synergistic effects. Moreover, the exposure route and dosing regimen used in the animal model may not accurately reflect typical human exposure patterns, which could limit the generalizability of our findings to real-world settings. The exposure doses used in this study were selected to obtain measurable biological responses rather than to reproduce environmentally relevant exposure levels. In humans, phenanthrene exposure occurs through multiple pathways, and the route of exposure may significantly influence its absorption, metabolic activation, and toxicological effects. Although intraperitoneal injection was employed in this study to ensure precise dosing, this exposure route does not fully replicate environmentally relevant exposure pathways such as inhalation or dermal contact. Additionally, the 6-week exposure duration used in this study may not fully capture the chronic and low-dose exposure patterns commonly experienced by humans, where PAH exposure often occurs continuously over much longer periods. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to human exposure scenarios. While phenanthrene exposure resulted in increased relative kidney and lung weights, the biological significance of this modest increase remains unclear and should be interpreted cautiously. The interpretation of organ weight data is limited to a macroscopic observation, and future studies incorporating histopathological analysis are warranted to elucidate the biological relevance. Key mechanistic pathways, including AhR signaling and oxidative stress responses, were not measured. Moreover, detailed B-cell subsets, germinal center responses, and antigen-specific antibody responses were not assessed, despite IgG being a central end point of humoral immunity. The relatively small sample size may limit the statistical power of metabolomics analyses and increase susceptibility to interindividual variability. Due to the limited number of differential metabolic features and relatively small sample size, pathway enrichment analysis and multiomics integration were not performed, which constrained mechanistic interpretation of the association between metabolite alterations and immunotoxicity. Future studies incorporating single-cell transcriptomics and microbiome profiling would provide insights into immune-cell heterogeneity and host–microbiota interactions following phenanthrene exposure.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the immunological effects of phenanthrene exposure in mice. Hematological analysis showed significant alterations in leukocyte subpopulations, including decreased proportions of neutrophils, monocytes, and eosinophils. Flow cytometry further revealed a dose-dependent reduction in lymphocyte percentages, accompanied by increased production of the Th1-related cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α in CD4+ T cells, while the proportions of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells remained unchanged. Serum IgG concentrations decreased in a dose-dependent manner following phenanthrene exposure. Serum metabolomics analysis also indicated significant alterations in multiple metabolites. In summary, phenanthrene exposure can induce immunotoxic effects involving immune dysfunction and disruption of metabolic profiles.
