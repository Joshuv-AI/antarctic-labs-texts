Sequential Classification of Misinformation
Daniel Toma Wasim Huleihel

Abstract
In recent years there have been a growing interest in online auditing of information flow over social networks with the goal of monitoring undesirable effects, such as, misinformation and fake news. Most previous work on the subject, focus on the binary classification problem of classifying information as fake or genuine. Nonetheless, in many practical scenarios, the multi-class/label setting is of particular importance. For example, it could be the case that a social media platform may want to distinguish between “true”, “partly-true”, and “false” information. Accordingly, in this paper, we consider the problem of online multiclass classification of information flow. To that end, driven by empirical studies on information flow over real-world social media networks, we propose a probabilistic information flow model over graphs. Then, the learning task is to detect the label of the information flow, with the goal of minimizing a combination of the classification error and the detection time. For this problem, we propose two detection algorithms; the first is based on the well-known multiple sequential probability ratio test, while the second is a novel graph neural network based sequential decision algorithm. For both algorithms, we prove several strong statistical guarantees. We also construct a data driven algorithm for learning the proposed probabilistic model. Finally, we test our algorithms over two real-world datasets, and show that they outperform other state-of-the-art misinformation detection algorithms, in terms of detection time and classification error.



1
Introduction

As social media gained popularity, the spread of misinformation and disinformation in social networks has become a widespread phenomenon. While some of this spread may be due to innocent errors or differing opinions, some is deliberately intended to manipulate individuals’ beliefs and behavior, often to serve economic or political agendas. Therefore, it is considered a significant societal concern
[
1
]
.

One promising branch of solutions to the above issues is the development of automated misinformation detection algorithms
[
1
]
. These algorithms are primarily based on binary classifiers that categorize news items and user posts as either “real” or “fake”. However, this binary view of misinformation overlooks the complex nature of the problem. For instance, in
[
2
]
, true information is contrasted not only with deliberate lies but also with opinions, and
[
3
]
proposes various taxonomies of disinformation, one of which includes lies, spins, and bullshit.

Classifying the type of misinformation/disinformation is not merely a philosophical exercise; it has real-world applications. For instance, a social media platform could respond differently to various types of misinformation. Pop culture misinformation, such as “Titanic 3 will be filmed in New Zealand”, is generally harmless, while other types of false information could be used to manipulate readers. Therefore, the problem we aim to address is the multi-class classification of misinformation. Since early detection is critical in mitigating the spread of misinformation, we are particularly interested in quickest sequential multi-class classification of misinformation.

Related work.

The problem of misinformation classification has been widely studied. Early attempts to tackle this issue suggested using a set of predefined features to identify misinformation
[
4
]
, while other methods focused solely on the linguistic content of news items
[
5
,
6
]
. A significant improvement came with the introduction of graph neural networks (GNNs)
[
7
]
, which learn node representations by recursively embedding a node’s features with those of its neighbors. These models can be applied to both node classification and whole graph classification
[
8
]
. User preference-aware fake news detection (UPFD)
[
9
]
and graph convolutional networks fake news (GCNFN)
[
10
]
are examples of GNN-based models used to classify propagation graphs. Specifically, GCNFN employs profile features as node inputs and utilizes a graph convolutional networks (GCN) based model for classification
[
7
]
, while UPFD incorporates the content of the news item by encoding it using NLP techniques like BERT
[
11
]
or SpaCy
[
12
]
, and has three different variations that are based on GCN, graph attention networks (GAT)
[
13
]
, and GraphSage convolutional layers
[
14
]
. Although these models were not specifically designed for early detection, they can be employed during the propagation phase.

Another group of methods focuses on early detection of fake news. Quickstop
[
15
]
models the temporal series of user profiles sharing a news item as a Markov chain and applies the sequential probability ratio test (SPRT) rule
[
16
]
to reach a decision. Similarly,
[
17
]
uses a more complex Markovian modeling of user profiles and applies the SPRT decision rule for early detection. Both approaches present the problem as a Bayesian optimization problem, aiming to minimize both propagation time and the probability of misclassification
[
18
]
. Finally, Heterogeneous graph GNNs represent another set of solutions. Hierarchical graph attention network (HGAT)
[
19
]
, for example, incorporates both news and user nodes, connecting them when a user shares a specific news item. A related method, hypergraph for fake news detection (HGFND)
[
20
]
, similarly models news items as nodes connected by hyper-edges
[
21
]
, which link sets of nodes when the same users share the news items around the same time or mention the same entities. While these models achieve state-of-the-art accuracy in real-world datasets, they require extensive preprocessing, making them unsuitable for early detection of misinformation. Furthermore, none of these methods address the challenge of classifying misinformation into multiple classes.

Main contributions.

Following
[
15
,
22
]
, in this paper, we suggest a real-time multiclass misinformation detection framework, based on a certain Markovian probabilistic information spreading model over a social network modeled by a graph. Under this framework, we derive two data-driven and model-driven algorithms, for online multiclass misinformation detection. Specifically, our first algorithm is based on the well-known multiple SPRT (MSPRT) decision rule
[
23
]
, for sequential classification of in a multi-class setting. Classical theory of MSPRT typically relies on simple observational models, where the data is generated as independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). In our case, we deviate from this assumption, and prove several theoretical statistical guarantees under the Markovian model mentioned above; among those results are high probability upper bounds on the stopping/decision times, and misclassification error probabilities. As it turns out, while the MSPRT enjoys desirable statistical properties, it suffers from a relatively high sample complexity, it is quite sensitive to errors resulted during the estimation of the model parameters, and it is not robust to model mismatch. As a remedy for these problematic issues, we propose also a GNN based sequential decision rule, which proves quite robust to the type of errors mentioned above, and at the same time, provably shares similar statistical guarantees as the MSPRT. Finally, we compare our algorithms against several state-of-the-art misinformation detection techniques over real-world datasets, and show the superiority of our algorithms in terms of accuracy and detection time.

Problem Definition

In this section, we present the problem of multi-label misinformation detection. We start by describing the underlying probabilistic information flow model, and then formulate mathematically the inference problem we aim to solve in this paper.

2.1
Information flow model

Information traces and decisions.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be a directed graph, where
\mathcal{V}=[n]
is the set of nodes and
\mathcal{E}
is the set of directed edges. One can think of this graph as representing a certain social network platform, where the nodes are “users” and the edges are the “connections” between those users. We associate each node
u\in\mathcal{V}
with a
d
-dimensional “feature” vector
\mathbf{x}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
; commonly used features are, for example, user profile, user social engagements, etc. The platform goal is to monitor the spread of a message (or, “piece of information”), denoted by
\mathcal{I}
, and assumed to be categorized/labeled into/by
M\geq 2
values, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}\in[M]
. It is convenient to think of
\mathcal{I}=0
as representing completely genuine information, while
\mathcal{I}\in[M]\setminus\{0\}
represents different degrees of in-genuine (or, fake) information. The fact that
M\geq 2
allows for “partially true” information, or, for different types of fake information, e.g., fake celebrity news versus fake political news. Given a directed edge
e=(u,v)
, we shall refer to user
v
as a
follower
of user
u
, while user
u
is a
followee
of user
v
. Over
\mathcal{G}
, user
v\in\mathcal{V}
can forward
\mathcal{I}
from user
u\in\mathcal{V}
. Although implicitly in our model, user
u
decides whether to forward
\mathcal{I}
or not based on: (i) the information type
\mathcal{I}\in[M]
; (ii) its features
\mathbf{x}_{u}
; and (iii) the set of its neighbors
\mathcal{N}_{u}\triangleq\{v\in\mathcal{V}:(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}\}
, who forwarded the information earlier.

Given the above setup for the underlying network, we next define the concept of
information traces
, i.e., the way information flows over the network. We focus on a single information source
s\in\mathcal{V}
, and we let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of all possible directed paths in
\mathcal{G}
starting at
s
. For any path
\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}
in
\mathcal{G}
, let its vertex sequence be denoted by
\mathsf{P}=(v_{1}^{\mathsf{P}},v_{2}^{\mathsf{P}},\ldots,v_{|\mathsf{P}|}^{\mathsf{P}})
. The information
\mathcal{I}
can flow over one or more of these paths. Generally speaking, we say that an event occurs when a follower forwards the information from one of its followees. This is represented by the edge over which this event occurs, which in turn is modeled by a pair of features
(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v})
that correspond to the end users. We treat
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
as the time index, and at the
\ell
th time step we denote by
(u_{\ell},v_{\ell})
the followee-follower pair over which an event happened. Then, the
information trace
is the sequence
\{(\mathbf{x}_{u_{\ell}},\mathbf{x}_{v_{\ell}})\}_{\ell\geq 1}
, where
(u_{\ell},v_{\ell})\in\mathcal{E}
, and
v_{\ell}
is a child of
u_{\ell}
, i.e., if
u_{\ell}=v_{i}^{\mathsf{P}}
for some
i\in[|\mathsf{P}|]
, then,
v_{\ell}=v_{i+1}^{\mathsf{P}}
. An illustration of possible information traces over
\mathcal{G}
can be seen in Fig.
3
.

Given information traces our goal is to infer the message
\mathcal{I}
, as illustrated in Fig.

, where “black box” refer to any probabilistic modeling and algorithmic operations we apply for inference. The general meta question we would like to answer is:

How many information traces over
\mathcal{G}
we should see, and how lengthy should they be, so as to infer the message
\mathcal{I}
with “high confidence”?

Edge/Node compressed representations.

As mentioned above, our inference algorithms are driven by certain probabilistic models for the information flow. As will be seen in the sequel, these models basically place probabilistic/statistical assumptions on compressed node/edge representations of feature-pairs over the information traces. We will refer to these representations as the
edge-based
and
node-based
models, and we next define those in detail.

The edge-based model, views each edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
as a communication channel that is associated with a given weight/type, which we denote by
\mathsf{W}_{u,v}\triangleq\mathsf{W}_{e}\in\mathcal{Z}
, where
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
. For example, when
\mathcal{Z}
is finite, e.g.,
\mathcal{Z}=[|\mathcal{Z}|]
with
|\mathcal{Z}|<\infty
, then, intuitively, we can think of edges with weight “
0
” as those which are more likely to spread genuine information, while edges with weight
|\mathcal{Z}|-1
are those which are more likely to spread fake information. Accordingly, we assume that
\mathsf{W}_{e}=g(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v})
, where
e\triangleq(u,v)\in\mathcal{V}^{2}
, and
g:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Z}
is the “edge-classifier” function. To motivate the edge-based model above, we consider the following two experiments over real-world databases. Specifically, Fig.
2(a)
shows the distribution of edge-types as a function of the edge type, under each one of the hypotheses, in the user preference-aware fake news detection (UPFD)
[
9
]

2(b)
shows a similar histogram for the three-class Weibo dataset
[
24
]
, in which the classes correspond to “true news”, “fake subjective news”, and “fake objective news”. We see a positive correlation between the hypothesis and its edge-type.

Next, we move forward to the node-based model. The underlying assumption here is that given
\mathcal{I}
, the probability that a certain user spreads
\mathcal{I}
to its neighbors depends on its own features and the features of his neighbors. Accordingly, in contrast to the edge-model above, here each node is associated with a weight/type, i.e., users are classified based on their features. Intuitively, an honest user has a lower probability to spread misinformation than a malicious user. Mathematically, each node
u\in\mathcal{V}
is associated with a given weight, denoted by
\bar{\mathsf{W}}_{u}\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. For example, when
\bar{\mathcal{Z}}\triangleq[|\bar{\mathcal{Z}}|]
is finite, then, intuitively speaking, we think of nodes with weight “
0
” as those which are more likely to spread genuine information, while nodes with weight
|\bar{\mathcal{Z}}|-1
are those which are more likely to spread fake information. As for the edge-type model, we assume that
\bar{\mathsf{W}}_{u}=\bar{g}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\{\mathbf{x}_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{N}_{u}})
, where
\bar{g}:\mathcal{V}\to\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
is the “node-classifier” function. Later on, we will explain how the edge and node classifiers
g
and
\bar{g}
above, can be generated from data. In our problem, the inference algorithm declares its decision on
\mathcal{I}
based on the edge/node weights representations, i.e.,
\{\mathsf{W}_{u_{\ell},v_{\ell}}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
or
\{\bar{\mathsf{W}}_{u_{\ell}}\}_{\ell\geq 1}

\{(\mathbf{x}_{u_{\ell}},\mathbf{x}_{v_{\ell}})\}_{\ell\geq 1}
.

Probabilistic edge/node paths.

Given the above setup for the information trace and its edge/node compressed representations, we now set a probabilistic information spreading model, using which we formulate the decision problem we aim to solve. Later on, we will explain how this probabilistic model can be extracted from data. For each possible path in
\mathcal{G}
, we model the sequence of edges/nodes representations in the information trace as a union of
Markov processes
. Specifically, under the edge-model, each path
\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}
in
\mathcal{G}
is associated with a first-order homogeneous Markov edge process
\mathsf{W}^{\mathsf{P}}\triangleq\{\mathsf{W}_{v_{i}^{\mathsf{P}},v_{i+1}^{\mathsf{P}}}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathsf{P}|-1}
. Similarly, under the node-model, each path
\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}
in
\mathcal{G}
is associated with a first-order homogeneous Markov node process
\bar{\mathsf{W}}^{\mathsf{P}}\triangleq\{\bar{\mathsf{W}}_{v_{i}^{\mathsf{P}}}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathsf{P}|}
.

Given
\mathcal{I}
, we let
\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathsf{z}|\mathsf{z}^{\prime})
and
\bar{\alpha}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bar{\mathsf{z}}|\bar{\mathsf{z}}^{\prime})
for
\mathsf{z},\mathsf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
and
\bar{\mathsf{z}},\bar{\mathsf{z}}^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
, denote the edge and node Markov transition kernels probabilities, respectively. Furthermore, for any neighbor of
s
,
v\in\mathcal{N}_{s}
, we define the initial edge probabilities
\eta_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathsf{z})\triangleq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\mathsf{W}_{s,v}=\mathsf{z}|\mathcal{I})
and node probabilities
\bar{\eta}_{\mathcal{I}}(\bar{\mathsf{z}})\triangleq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\bar{\mathsf{W}}_{s}=\bar{\mathsf{z}}|\mathcal{I})
, for any
\mathcal{I}\in[M]
,
\mathsf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}
and
\bar{\mathsf{z}}\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
, respectively. Later on, we will devise a data-based offline algorithm to learn the transition kernels and initial probabilities from training. Fig.
3
gives an example of a social media graph with multiple possible Markov chains paths.

2.2
Problem statement

Learning problem.

We formulate the misinformation detection problem as a sequential multiple hypothesis testing problem. First, we define the type of observations available for testing. It is clear that when complete network and diffusion information are known, the information spreading trace forms a tree; we assume that its edge/node representation paths are distributed as first-order Markov chain. Recall that at the
\ell
th time step, we denote by
(u_{\ell},v_{\ell})
the followee-follower pair over which an event happened (information spread). Then, we denote the sequence of edge observations by
\{\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
, where
\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}=\mathsf{W}_{u_{\ell},v_{\ell}}^{\mathsf{P}}
, for
(u_{\ell},v_{\ell})\in\mathcal{E}

\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}

\mathsf{P}

v_{\ell}

u_{\ell}

u_{\ell}=v_{i}^{\mathsf{P}}
for some
i\in[|\mathsf{P}|]

v_{\ell}=v_{i+1}^{\mathsf{P}}
. Similarly, we denote the sequence of node observations by
\{\bar{\mathsf{Z}}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}

Our learning problem is formulated as follows: we are given an information trace sequence
\{(\mathbf{x}_{u_{\ell}},\mathbf{x}_{v_{\ell}})\}_{\ell\geq 1}
, which induces an edge-representation sequence
\{\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
, and a node-representation sequence
\{\bar{\mathsf{Z}}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
that obey one of the hypotheses. The audit is tasked with distinguishing between

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mathcal{I}=0\quad\quad\mathsf{vs.}\quad\quad\mathcal{H}_{1}:\mathcal{I}=1\quad\quad\cdots\quad\quad\mathsf{vs.}\quad\quad\mathcal{H}_{M-1}:\mathcal{I}=M-1.

(5)

We denote the prior probability of the
j
th hypothesis by
\pi_{j}
. The audit is tasked with distinguishing between the hypotheses above, in a way that minimizes a combination of the error probability and the propagation cost, as we define in the sequel. For the rest of this paper, the underlying probability space is
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\pi})
, where
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi}
is the probability measure defined as follows,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi}=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\pi_{j}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{j},

(6)

with
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{j}
being the probability measures under the
j
th hypothesis, namely, under
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{j}
, the sequence
\{\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
(
\{\bar{\mathsf{Z}}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
) is Markovian with transition probabilities
\alpha_{j}(\cdot|\cdot)
(
\bar{\alpha}_{j}(\cdot|\cdot)
).

At this point we would like to emphasize again that the edge and node representations, as well as the Markovian assumption associated with either one of these representations, is done only as part of an algorithmic solution for the decision problem we faced with. To wit, the input-output relation is exactly as described in Fig.
1
, with the inputs/observations being the information traces, while the edge/node representations are only an artificial algorithmic operations done as part of the “black-box” relation which produce the decision.

Sequential decision rule.

Starting with the edge-based representation, as mentioned above, assume we are in the situation where we observe
\{\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
sequentially, generating the natural filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
, with,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\triangleq\{\mathsf{Z}_{1},\mathsf{Z}_{2},\ldots,\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}\},

(7)

and
\mathcal{F}_{0}\triangleq(\Omega,\emptyset)
. Let
\mathsf{T}\in\mathcal{T}
denote the random stopping time at which a decision is taken; here
\mathcal{T}
designates the set of all such stopping times w.r.t. the filtration
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell\geq 1}
, and a sequence
\left\{\delta_{\ell}\right\}_{\ell\geq 1}
of tests/decision rules, where
\delta_{\ell}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
-measure function taking values in
[M]
. Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the set of all such functions. With the above definitions in mind, a decision rule is defined as,

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathsf{T}}\triangleq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\delta_{\ell}\mathds{1}_{\{\mathsf{T}=\ell\}},

(8)

where the indicator function
\mathds{1}_{\{\mathsf{T}=\ell\}}
is unity when
\mathsf{T}=\ell
, and zero otherwise. A
sequential decision rule
(SDR) is a pair
(\mathsf{T},\delta)
, where
\mathsf{T}
declares the time to stop sampling, and once
\mathsf{T}
is given,
\delta_{\mathsf{T}}
takes one of the values in
[M]
, declaring which hypotheses to accept. Finally, for the node-based representation, the above definitions remain the same but with
\{\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
replaced by
\{\bar{\mathsf{Z}}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
and
\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell}\triangleq\{\bar{\mathsf{Z}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\mathsf{Z}}_{\ell}\}
, respectively.

Approximate Bayesian optimality.

To capture the inherent tradeoff between the decision accuracy and the potential damage of spreading misinformation, we formulate the problem under a Bayesian framework. Specifically, for a given SDR
(\mathsf{T},\delta)
, we define its total risk by,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Risk}(\mathsf{T},\delta)\triangleq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi}(\delta_{\mathsf{T}}\neq\mathcal{I})+\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}c_{j}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{T}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}\right],

(9)

where
c_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, for
j\in[M]
. Here, the first term at the right-hand-side of (
9
) is the average probability of error due to misdetection, explicitly given by,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\pi}(\delta_{\mathsf{T}}\neq\mathcal{I})=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\pi_{j}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{j}\left(\delta_{\mathsf{T}}\neq\ell\right).

(10)

The second term at the right-hand-side of (
9
) is the
propagation cost
due to spreading misinformation, where
c_{j}
is the cost of spreading the
j
th misinformation. Crucially, note that we do not penalize true information, following the underlying assumption that spreading news does not occur any cost. This makes the cost asymmetric and depends on the hypothesis, which is in contrast to classical theory of sequential testing problems where this cost is simply the average stopping time, i.e.,
c\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{T}]
, for some
c>0
. A main goal in the theory of sequential testing is to find the SDR that minimizes the risk. In our case, this is formulated as the Bayesian optimization problem,

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\mathsf{T}\in\mathcal{T},\delta\in\mathcal{D}}\mathsf{Risk}(\mathsf{T},\delta).

(11)

Finding the optimal SDR in the multiple hypothesis case is challenging even for a symmetric propagation cost and an i.i.d. probabilistic model.
In the following sections, we propose “approximately optimal” SDRs, and prove several statistical guarantees on their performance. We also devise data driven algorithms for learning the edge types and transition probabilities from training data, as these are typically unknown in practical scenarios.

3
Main Results

In this section, we present our main results. In the first part of this section, we start with the edge-based representation. Specifically, assuming that the Markov kernels and edge types are known, we propose a model based sequential detection algorithm for the optimization problem in (
11
), and prove several theoretical guarantees on its performance. In the second part of this section, we move forward to the node-based representation. Here, we propose a novel GNN SDR architecture, and prove theoretical guarantees on its performance. As will be explained later on, this architecture implicitly learns the Markov dependencies between consecutive nodes, and proves more robust to estimation errors. Finally, we construct offline routines for training the edge classifier, and estimating the edge Markov kernels, under each hypothesis, under the edge-based representation model.

3.1
Edge-based representation

Recall the edge-based representation described in the previous section. We next propose a SDR for the testing problem in (
5
), assuming that the Markov kernels and edge types are known. Later on, in Subsection
3.3
, we propose a data-based algorithm for learning these parameters. Recall that in the edge-based representation, we transform the information trace sequence
\{(\mathbf{x}_{u_{\ell}},\mathbf{x}_{v_{\ell}})\}_{\ell\geq 1}
into an edge sequence
\{\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
, and in this subsection we assume that
|\mathcal{Z}|<\infty
, namely,
\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}
is discrete for any
\ell\geq 1
; this implies that
\{\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
forms a union of Markov chains. For our theoretical results only, we further assume that these Markov chains are irreducible.

Our SDR for solving (
11
) is the well-known MSPRT
[
23
]
. To describe this test, we introduce some notations. For
m\in[M]
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\displaystyle\Pi_{\ell}^{(m)}

\displaystyle\triangleq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mathcal{H}_{m}|\mathcal{F}_{\ell}\right),

(12)

denote the posterior probability of the
m
th hypothesis. Bayes theorem gives,

\Pi_{\ell}^{(m)}=\frac{\pi_{m}f_{m}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell})}{\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\pi_{j}f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell})},

(13)

where for
m\in[M]
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we define
f_{m}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell})\triangleq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell}|\mathcal{H}_{m})
; see (
2
)–(
4
) for an explicit expression for
f_{m}(\cdot)
. We are now in a position to state how the MSPRT works. Specifically, the stopping time
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
, and the final decision
\delta_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
, can be described as follows,

\boxed{\begin{aligned} \mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}&=\inf\left\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}:\;\Pi_{\ell}^{(m)}\geq\frac{1}{1+a_{m}},\;\mathsf{for}\;\mathsf{some}\;m\right\},\\
\delta_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}&=\mathcal{H}_{m^{\star}},\;\mathsf{where}\;m^{\star}=\arg\max_{m}\Pi_{\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}}^{(m)}.\end{aligned}}

(14)

In the above, the (hyper-) parameters
\{a_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
control the significance threshold; to ensure that only a single hypothesis is chosen we set
0\leq a_{m}<1
, for all
m\in[M]
. A pseudo-code of the above MSPRT procedure is given in Algorithm
1
. We would like to emphasize here that in the second step of Algorithm
1
, we implicitly use some portion of the information trace
\{(\mathbf{x}_{u_{\ell}},\mathbf{x}_{v_{\ell}})\}_{\ell\geq 1}
as a training in order to learn the edge representation, transition probabilities, and initial probabilities, using Algorithm
4
, which we explain in detail in Subsection
3.3
. For the rest of this paper, we denote by
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
,
\delta_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
, and
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
, the decision, the rule, and the stopping time, associated with the MSPRT algorithm, respectively.

Many statistical guarantees for the MSPRT algorithm are well-known in the literature, under the i.i.d. model (see, e.g.,
[
23
]
). For example, it is known that the MSPRT reaches a posterior probability estimate within the specified significance level, at a bounded time. Furthermore, asymptotic guarantees regarding the stopping time, the probability of error, and the approximation of Bayesian optimality, are known as well. We prove several similar guarantees, but under the more general Markovian model presented in the previous section. For simplicity, in order to prove the following theoretical results, we assume that any pair of matrix transition probabilities are sufficiently different. This is formulated in terms of the conditional Hellinger distance. Specifically, recall that for probability measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{P}||\mathbb{Q})=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}(1-\sqrt{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}/\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}})^{2}
, denotes the Hellinger distance between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
. In our case, for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and
k\neq j\in[M]
, define,

\displaystyle S_{k,j}(z)

\displaystyle\triangleq 1-\mathcal{H}^{2}(\alpha_{k}(\cdot|z),\alpha_{j}(\cdot|z))

(15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}(z^{\prime}|z)\alpha_{j}(z^{\prime}|z)}.

(16)

Then, we assume that
\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\max_{k\neq j\in[M]}S_{k,j}(z)<1
. To wit, for any
k\neq j
and any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the transition probability distributions
\alpha_{k}(\cdot|z)
and
\alpha_{j}(\cdot|z)
are not the same, that is, there exist
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
such that
\alpha_{k}(z^{\prime}|z)\neq\alpha_{j}(z^{\prime}|z)
.

We start with the following result, which shows that the probability that
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
exceeds
t
decreases exponentially with
t
.

Theorem 1

(Exponentially bounded stopping time)
.

Fix
k\in[M]
, and assume that

\displaystyle\min_{j\neq k\in[M]}\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}S_{k,j}(z)<1.

(17)

Then, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left.\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}>t\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right]\leq\mathsf{C}_{1}\cdot\exp(-\s{C}_2\cdot t),

(18)

for some
\mathsf{C}_{1},\mathsf{C}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Theorem
1
implies that with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}\leq\frac{1}{\mathsf{C}_{2}}\log\frac{\mathsf{C}_{1}}{\delta}
. In particular,
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
is finite with probability one. To prove this result, we follow a similar approach as in
[
23
]
. To wit, we first show that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left.\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}>t\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right]\leq 1-\min_{j\neq k}\mathcal{H}^{2}(f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t}),f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})).

(19)

As it turns out, the term at the right-hand-side of (
19
) decreases exponentially with
t
, as
t\to\infty
, which implies that the run-time of Algorithm
1
is bounded. Next, we analyze the probability of error. To that end, for
j\neq k\in[M]
, define
\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{MSPRT}}_{j,k}\triangleq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}=\mathcal{H}_{k})
,
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\mathsf{MSPRT}}\triangleq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}=\mathcal{H}_{k})
, and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
as the total probability of incorrect decision. We mention here that
\{\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{MSPRT}}_{k}\}_{k\in[M]}
are also known as the
frequentist
error probabilities, and in general, they are different from the standard conditional error probabilities.

Theorem 2

(Error guarantees)
.

For all
k\in[M]
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\mathsf{MSPRT}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j:j\neq k}\pi_{j}\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{MSPRT}}_{j,k}\leq\pi_{k}a_{k},

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{MSPRT}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{k\in[M]}\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{MSPRT}}_{k}\leq\sum_{k\in[M]}\pi_{k}a_{k}.

(21)

If, in addition,
a_{\ell}=\nu
, for all
\ell\in[M]
, and for some
\nu\in[0,1]
, then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{MSPRT}}\leq\frac{\nu}{\nu+1}.

(22)

The bounds above are derived using techniques similar to the ones used by Wald for the SPRT
[
16
]
, and therefore, its proof is relegated. Next, we investigate the asymptotic behaviour of the MSPRT stopping time. Specifically, we find the asymptotic behavior of
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
, in the regime where the error probabilities are “small”, and accordingly, the stopping time is “large”. In light of Theorem
2
, this regime corresponds to the case where
\norm{a}_{\infty}\triangleq\max_{\ell\in[M]}|a_{\ell}|\to 0
. To present our main finding, we need a few definitions. The Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between two probability measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
is defined as let
d_{\mathsf{KL}}(\mathbb{P}||\mathbb{Q})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}
. Accordingly, in our case, for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, we define,

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{KL}}(\alpha_{k}(\cdot|z)||\alpha_{j}(\cdot|z))\triangleq\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\alpha_{k}(z^{\prime}|z)\log\frac{\alpha_{k}(z^{\prime}|z)}{\alpha_{j}(z^{\prime}|z)}.

(23)

Also, we denote by
\pi^{\mathsf{stat}}_{k}
the stationary distribution of the
k
th irreducible Markov chain with transition probabilities
\alpha_{k}(\cdot|\cdot)
. Then, in accordance to the above notations, we define the stationary conditional KL divergence as,

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{KL}}(\alpha_{k}||\alpha_{j}|\pi_{k}^{\mathsf{stat}})=\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\pi^{\mathsf{stat}}_{k}(z^{\prime})\cdot d_{\mathsf{KL}}(\alpha_{k}(\cdot|z^{\prime})||\alpha_{j}(\cdot|z^{\prime})).

(24)

Furthermore, the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between two probability measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
is defined as
\chi^{2}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\triangleq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}-1
. In our case, we define for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\alpha_{k}(\cdot|z),\alpha_{j}(\cdot|z))\triangleq\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\frac{\alpha^{2}_{k}(z^{\prime}|z)}{\alpha_{j}(z^{\prime}|z)}-1.

(25)

We are now in a position to state our main result.

Theorem 3

(Asymptotic stopping time)
.

Fix
k\in[M]
, and assume that

\displaystyle\min_{j\neq k\in[M]}\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\chi^{2}(\alpha_{k}(\cdot|z),\alpha_{j}(\cdot|z))<\infty,

(26)

and

\displaystyle\min_{j\neq k\in[M]}\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}S_{k,j}(z)<1.

(27)

Then,

\displaystyle\lim_{\norm{a}_{\infty}\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}}{-\log a_{k}}=\frac{1}{\min_{j\neq k}d_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\alpha_{k}||\alpha_{j}|\pi_{k}^{\mathsf{stat}}\right)},

(28)

f_{k}
-almost surely. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\lim_{\norm{a}_{\infty}\to 0}\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}\right]}{-\log a_{k}}=\frac{1}{\min_{j\neq k}d_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\alpha_{k}||\alpha_{j}|\pi_{k}^{\mathsf{stat}}\right)}.

(29)

Theorem
3
connects between the stopping time
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
, the required significance levels
\{a_{k}\}_{k}
, and the KL-divergence between the closest hypothesis to the actual decision. Intuitively, as the required significance level increases, the algorithm requires more iterations to reach that level. Furthermore, as the hypotheses gets “closer”, namely, as the KL-divergence gets smaller, the algorithm requires more steps to reach a decision, as expected.

3.2
Node-based representation

While Algorithm
1
enjoys desirable statistical guarantees, it suffers from a relatively high sample complexity. Indeed, as the number of hypotheses increase, Algorithm
1
runs quickly into the
curse of dimensionality
; since there are
|\mathcal{Z}|=M(M-1)
possible states, the transition matrix has
\mathcal{O}(M^{4})
entries, and since Algorithm
1
requires the evaluation of each one of these entries, its computational complexity is governed by these number of states, which can be huge in practice. Even more importantly, as it turns out from our experimental study, it seems as though that Algorithm
1
is quite sensitive to errors resulted during the estimation of the transition probabilities and edge types. Finally, in practice, the underlying observational processes may not truly be homogeneous/time-invariant, while Algorithm
1
relies strongly on this assumption. As a remedy to these issues, we propose a GNN-based architecture, which implicitly learns the Markovian dependencies between consecutive nodes on the fly. For this algorithm we were able to prove that Theorems
1
–
3
remain true (and as so exhibit favorable statistical guarantees), but at the same time is more robust to estimation and model errors, and is faster than Algorithm
1
. Next, we describe the algorithm and present its statistical guarantees.

Architecture.

Below, we let
\mathsf{Dense}

\mathsf{GINConv}
denote the graph isomorphism network in
[

be the dimension of these hidden layers. Our architecture comprises of a total of four layers, with two of them depending on several trainable parameters. Specifically, the input is a graph, with node features, described by an
n\times d
user-feature matrix
\mathbf{X}\triangleq[\mathbf{x}_{1}^{T},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}^{T}]^{T}
, and a list of edges in the (sub-)graph. In the first layer, we have a concatenation of a
\mathsf{Dense}
linear layer, operating locally on each feature vector, with
h\cdot(d+1)
trainable parameters, and a rectified-linear unit (ReLU) activation function. Mathematically, for each
i\in[n]

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathsf{ReLU}(\mathsf{Dense}(\mathbf{x}_{i})).

(30)

Then, the second layer is the convolution layer
\mathsf{GINConv}
proposed in
[
8

M

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{Z}}_{i}=\Theta\left((1+\epsilon)\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}\mathbf{x}_{u}^{\prime}\right),

(31)

for all
i\in[n]

is a single linear layer, and

\mathsf{GINConv}

\mathsf{log}

\displaystyle\left[\varphi_{i}\right]_{k}=\log\left(\frac{\exp\left(\left[\bar{\mathsf{Z}}_{i}\right]_{k}\right)}{\sum_{j=0}^{M}\exp\left(\left[\bar{\mathsf{Z}}_{i}\right]_{j}\right)}\right),

(32)

for all

M

\displaystyle\Phi_{m}=\frac{\prod_{i\in[\ell]}\left[\varphi_{i}\right]_{m}}{\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\prod_{i\in[\ell]}\left[\varphi_{i}\right]_{j}},

A few important comments are in order. Note that in order to apply the MSPRT procedure in Algorithm

, the Markov kernels and the edges types are needed, and in the following subsection, we explain how these can be deduced from data. In msprtGNN, however, these are learnt implicitly; it can be seen that the msprtGNN procedure in Algorithm

Similarly to MSPRT, we prove several statistical guarantees on the performance of msprtGNN. As in the previous subsection, for the following theoretical results, we assume that any pair of Markov kernels are “sufficiently far”. Again, we formulae this in terms of the conditional Hellinger distance; for any
z\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
and
k\neq j\in[M]
, define,

\displaystyle\bar{S}_{k,j}(z)

\displaystyle\triangleq 1-\mathcal{H}^{2}(\bar{\alpha}_{k}(\cdot|z),\bar{\alpha}_{j}(\cdot|z))

(35)

\displaystyle=\int_{z^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}}\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{k}(z^{\prime}|z)\bar{\alpha}_{j}(z^{\prime}|z)}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}.

(36)

Then, we assume that
\max_{z\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}}\max_{k\neq j\in[M]}\bar{S}_{k,j}(z)<1
. Furthermore, we define

\displaystyle\xi\triangleq\max_{j,k\in[M],\ell\geq 1,z_{1}^{\ell}\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}^{\ell}}\left.\frac{\Phi_{k}(z_{1}^{\ell})}{\Phi_{j}(z_{1}^{\ell})}\right/\frac{f_{k}(z_{1}^{\ell})}{f_{j}(z_{1}^{t})},

(37)

which plays a significant role in the following results. Intuitively,
\xi
measures the similaritdiscrioinecny between the underlying likelihoods/probability posteriors and the outputs of the msprtGNN architecture. FIn fact, for our results to hold, we need to assume that
\xi
is finite, and we claim that this is indeed reasonable. For exampleSpecifically, when neural network classifiers are trained using the cross-entropy loss, it is well-known that the output of the network approximates the posterior probability, see, e.g.,
[
25
, Sec. 6.11]
,
[
26
]
, that is,
\Phi_{k}(\bar{\mathsf{Z}}_{1}^{\ell})\approx\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{H}_{k}|\bar{\mathsf{Z}}_{1}^{\ell})
, and as so,
\xi\approx 1
.. We start with the following result, which shows that
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{GNN}}
is bounded with high probability.

(Exponentially bounded stopping time)
.

k\in[M]
. Assume that
\xi<\infty
and,

(38)

Then, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[\left.\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{GNN}}>t\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right]\leq\mathsf{C}_{1}\cdot\exp(-\s{C}_2\cdot t),

(39)

for some
\mathsf{C}_{1},\mathsf{C}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Next, in a similar fashion to Theorem
5
, we have the following bounds on the error probabilities associated with the msprtGNN procedure. We let
\{\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{GNN}}_{j,k}\}
,
\{\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{GNN}}_{k}\}
, and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{GNN}}
, be defined as in Theorem
5
, but with
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
replaced by
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathsf{GNN}}
.

Theorem 5

(Error guarantees)
.

Fix
k\in[M]
and assume that
\xi<\infty
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{GNN}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j:j\neq k}\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{GNN}}_{j,k}\leq a_{k}\xi+\xi-1,

(40)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{GNN}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{k\in[M]}\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{GNN}}_{k}\leq\xi\norm{a}_{1}+M(\xi-1).

(41)

If, in addition,
a_{\ell}=\nu
, for all
\ell\in[M]
, and for some
\nu\in[0,1]
, then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf{GNN}}\leq 1-\frac{1}{\xi(1+\nu)}.

(42)

Finally, similarly to Theorem
3
, it turns out that, at least asymptotically, the msprtGNN stopping time converges in expectation to a similar limit as that of MSPRT, in the small error probability regime; here, we can see from Theorem
5
that this regime corresponds to the asymptotics
\norm{a}_{\infty}\to 0
and
\xi\to 1
. For any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, we define,

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\bar{\alpha}_{k}(\cdot|z),\bar{\alpha}_{j}(\cdot|z))\triangleq\int_{z^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}}\frac{\bar{\alpha}^{2}_{k}(z^{\prime}|z)}{\bar{\alpha}_{j}(z^{\prime}|z)}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}-1.

(43)

We have the following result.

Theorem 6

(Asymptotic stopping time)
.

Fix
k\in[M]
. Assume that
\xi
is finite, and

\displaystyle\min_{j\neq k\in[M]}\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\chi^{2}(\bar{\alpha}_{k}(\cdot|z)||\bar{\alpha}_{j}(\cdot|z))<\infty.

(44)

Then,

\displaystyle\lim_{\xi\to 1}\lim_{\norm{a}_{\infty}\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{GNN}}}{-\log a_{k}}=\frac{1}{\min_{j\neq k}d_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{k}||\bar{\alpha}_{j}|\pi_{k}^{\mathsf{stat}}\right)},

(45)

f_{k}
-almost surely. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\lim_{\norm{a}_{\infty}\to 0}\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{GNN}}\right]}{-\log a_{k}}=\frac{1}{\min_{j\neq k}d_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{k}||\bar{\alpha}_{j}|\pi_{k}^{\mathsf{stat}}\right)}.

(46)

3.3
Offline algorithm

In order to use the MSPRT algorithm, the transition probabilities and initial probabilities are needed. Furthermore, we need to classify/convert the information traces
\{(\mathbf{x}_{u_{\ell}},\mathbf{x}_{v_{\ell}})\}_{\ell\geq 1}
into their edge-representation sequence
\{\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
. Accordingly, we next describe an offline algorithm which learn the above unknowns from training. Specifically, the data required for our offline algorithm is a set of
N
information traces, denoted by
\{\mathcal{R}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
is
\ell
th information trace. We assume that each trace is labeled, and denote by
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\in\left[M\right]
the information type of the
\ell
th trace.

Assigning edge types.

It should be clear that many techniques are possible for classifying edges in the information traces. Here, we propose the following technique, which proves quite efficient and successful in practice. In fact, as will be seen in our experiments, this technique outperforms other methods in the literature, e.g.,
[
15
,
17
]
. Generally speaking, our classification procedure is based on applying a certain pairing function
\Theta
on the outputs of the GNN architecture, proposed in the previous subsection. Specifically, we are given labeled training information traces
\{(\mathcal{R}_{\ell},\mathcal{L}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
. We are also given a trained msprtGNN architecture; in Section
4
we explain the training process in detail. The classification procedure follows the following steps:

1.

We create a set
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}
of all edges in
\{{\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
.

2.

For each pair of users
e=(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}
, with features
\mathbf{x}_{u}
and
\mathbf{x}_{v}
, we denote by
\Phi^{e}=g_{\mathsf{GNN}}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v})
the vector of
M
outputs of the msprtGNN, as defined in (
33
), using the already trained msprtGNN architecture.

3.

We define
\Theta
to be the pairing function which takes the indices of the largest and second largest entries in the vector
\Phi^{(e)}
, and maps them to a number between in
[|\mathcal{Z}|]
. Specifically,

\displaystyle\Theta\triangleq\ell_{1}\cdot(M-1)+\ell_{2}-\mathds{1}_{\ell_{2}>\ell_{1}},

(47)

where
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are the indices of the largest and second largest entries in
\Phi^{(e)}
, respectively. For example, for
\mathcal{Z}=\{0,1,2\}
, the pairing function/mapping
\Theta
works as follows:
(0,1)\to 0
,
(0,2)\to 1
,
(1,0)\to 2
,
(1,2)\to 3
,
(2,0)\to 4
, and
(2,1)\to 5
. We would like to emphasize here that other pairing functions are possible, but the above proved successful in our experiments.

4.

Each edge is assigned with an edge type using
g\triangleq\Theta\circ g_{\mathsf{GNN}}:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Z}
.

The above edge classification method is summarized in Algorithm
3
.

\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}
be the set of all edges participating in the information traces
\{{\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
.

2:

, for all

.

Edge type classifier

where
\Theta
is defined in (
47
).

Initial probabilities.

To estimate the probability of an edge
z\in\mathcal{Z}
to forward information of type
m\in[M]
directly from the source
s
, we use the simple frequentist estimator as follows,

\hat{\eta}_{m}(z)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{|\mathcal{R}_{k}|-1}\mathds{1}_{\{e_{\ell}^{\mathcal{R}_{k}}\in\mathcal{N}_{s},\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}^{\mathcal{R}_{k}}=z\}}\mathds{1}_{\{\mathcal{L}_{k}=m\}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{1}_{\{\mathcal{L}_{k}=m\}}},

(48)

where
e_{\ell}^{\mathcal{R}_{k}}
is the
\ell
th edge in trace
\mathcal{R}_{k}
, and
\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}^{\mathcal{R}_{k}}
is the
\ell
th edge representation in the
k
th information trace
\mathcal{R}_{k}
. Indeed, the numerator counts the number neighbors/children of the source
s
with edge-type
z
over all information traces with label
m
, while the denominator counts the number of traces with label
m
.

Transition probabilities.

As described in Subsection
3.2
, the transition matrix has
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{Z}|^{2})
entries, which induces a high computational and sample complexity. Since our dataset is of fixed size, this implies an increasingly worse estimate as
M
increases. To deal with this issue, we follow a Bayesian estimation approach and apply the Dirichlet-Categorical Bayesian (DCB) model
[
27
]
, which we describe next. Let
\textbf{p}_{i}^{(m)}
denote the
i
th row of the
m
th transition matrix
\alpha_{m}(\cdot|\cdot)
. Recall that in the Bayesian approach we use probabilities to describe our initial uncertainty about the unknown parameters (in our case the transition probabilities), i.e., a
prior distribution
, and then use probabilistic reasoning (that is, Bayes rule) to take into account our observations, namely, a
posterior distribution
. Accordingly, in the DCB model, we treat
\textbf{p}_{i}^{(m)}
as a random vector, with a Dirichlet prior distribution with parameters
\theta_{i}^{(m)}
, for
i\in[|\mathcal{Z}|]
and
m\in[M]
. To find these parameters, we use the frequentist estimate as follows,

\displaystyle\theta_{i}^{(m)}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{|\mathcal{R}_{k}|-1}\mathds{1}_{\{\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}^{\mathcal{R}_{k}}=i\}}\mathds{1}_{\{\mathcal{L}_{k}=m\}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{1}_{\{\mathcal{L}_{k}=m\}}},

(49)

for all
m\in[M]
and
i\in[|\mathcal{Z}|]
. Then,

\displaystyle\textbf{p}_{i}^{(m)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Dirichlet}\left(\theta_{i}^{(m)}\right).

(50)

The sample complexity of this estimator is
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{Z}|)
, and thus scales better than the
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{Z}|^{2})
complexity we discussed above. Given the Dirichlet prior, it is well-known that the posterior distribution is Dirichlet as well, see, e.g.,
[
27
, Prop. 17.3]
. The parameters of this posterior distributions are calculated as follows. We first find the number of transitions
\mathsf{N}_{ij}
from state
i\in[|\mathcal{Z}|]
to state
j\in[|\mathcal{Z}|]
, and the total number
\mathsf{N}_{i}
of state
i\in[|\mathcal{Z}|]
in the training information traces
\{\mathcal{R}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
. We also introduce “pseudo-count” hyperparamter
s
, which controls the weight given to the prior distribution. Then, in the DCB model, the posterior estimate for the
m
th matrix transition probabilities is,

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{m}(j|i)=\frac{\mathsf{N}_{ij}+s\cdot\theta_{i}^{(m)}}{\mathsf{N}_{i}+s},

(51)

for
i,j\in[|\mathcal{Z}|]
and
m\in[M]
. Finally, a pseudo-code for the data-driven learning procedure proposed above is given in Algorithm
4
.

Experiments

In this section, we test our algorithms over real-world datasets, and compare them to several state-of-the-art algorithms. We start by describing the datasets we use, followed by a description of the experimental setting we rely on, and finally, we present and discuss our results.

4.1
Datasets

UPFD dataset.

The UPFD dataset
[
9
]
is composed of tree structured retweet graphs, crawled from the Twitter platform, and labeled as fake or real news, using the fact-checking websites “Politifact” and “Gossipcop”. To obtain multiple classes, we merged the UPFD-Politifact and UPFD-Gossipcop datasets into a single 4-class dataset, i.e.,
M=4
, with labels: “true gossip news”, “fake gossip news”, “true politics news”, and “fake politics news”. We also created a 3-class dataset, i.e.,
M=3
, with labels: “true news”, ”false gossip news”, and ”false politics news”. Since the datasets are unbalanced we downsampled Gossipcop which is the larger one. Each node in the tree has ten profile features: “verified”, “enabled geo-spatial positioning”, “followers count”, ”friends count”, “status count”, “favorite count”, “number of lists”, “created time”, “number of words in description”, and “number of words in screen name”. We also perform an ablation study using “content” features, a concatenation of the “profile” features with “Spacy features”
[
12
]
; Spacy features are 300 features which are an average of the word vectors used in the last 200 tweets of each user. We will present the results of this ablation study towards the end of this section. We split the datasets into 80% for training and 20% for testing. Table

includes several statistics for the above UPFD dataset.

4.2
Baselines

In this subsection, we present our baselines for comparison. In general, we train all those baselines using the same train-test split mentioned above. We fine-tuned the hyper-parameters (e.g., hidden layers dimension sizes, learning rates, weight decay) for each baseline, to achieve maximal accuracy on the test set. For the purpose of evaluating the test risk, we set the propagation cost to be
c_{j}=10^{-3}
, for all
j\in[M]
. All the baselines, except HGFND
[
20
]
, which we describe below, receive the propagation tree as an input. The baselines we compare our algorithms to are:

•

Naïve i.i.d. MSPRT
[
23
]
:
This baseline model applies the vanilla MSPRT method
[
23
]
, while assuming that
\{\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables. It uses the same
\mathsf{Z}
values computed for MSPRT, though it learns the frequencies of these
\mathsf{Z}
values, instead of a transtiion matrix. The online algorithm for the naive method is similar to Algorithm
1
, though when applying Equation (
13
) it assumes the sequence to be a sequence of i.i.d random variabbles.

•

Quickstop
[
15
]
: This baseline models the
temporal
sequence of edge-types
\{\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
as a single Markov Chain. We modified the original Quickstop method, designed for the two classes, to apply for multiple classes.

•

UPFD-Sage
[
9
]
: This model is a fake-news classifier, and has three versions: using GCN
[
7
]
, GAT
[
13
]
, and GraphSAGE
[
14
]
convolutional layer. We chose the GraphSAGE version since it performed best on the test set. We modified the output to support for multiple classes.

•

GCNFN
[
28
]
: This model uses two
GCN

[
7
]
layers. We modified the output to support for multiple classes.

•

HGFND
[
20
]
: This method models the social network as a hypergraph
[
21
]
. The nodes are news stories, connected by hyperedges. A hyperedge connects two news stories if they were shared by the same user, posted at approximately the same time, or mention similar entities. Because this method requires preprocessing a large portion of the test set prior to testing, it is not suitable for the sequential setting. Nevertheless, because this method achieved state-of-the-art results on the binary UPFD
[
9
]
dataset, it is interesting to compare it to other methods in the multiclass, non-sequential setting (see, Table
2
).

4.3
Experimental setting

Training.

To train msprtGNN we used the cross entropy loss as our loss function, and used the AdamW optimizer
[
29
]
, with learning rate
10^{-3}
and weight-decay
10^{-2}
. Training was done in a non-sequential setting, using the whole information trace as input. We noticed that in the non-sequential setting, training msprtGNN with a mean-pooling layer
[
8
]
achieves better accuracy, while during inference, we get better accuracy if we use the add-pooling layer
[
8
]
; therefore, we follow this approach. This method works because the difference between mean-pooling and add-pooling is merely a multiplication by a constant, and thus does not change the order of outputs of the proceeding
softmax
layer. For the Weibo dataset, we use a hidden dimension of size
h=64
, while for the UPFD dataset, we use
h=128
. In the ablation study, where we use augmented content features, we use a hidden dimension of size
h=512
. Finally, we set
|\mathcal{Z}|=2\cdot\mathsf{M}
, for the
k
-means clustering step.

Experiment procedure.

To compare our methods against the baselines, we perform the experiment in the following manner. Each information trace in the test set is tested sequentially. A predication is made for each time step. We estimate the accuracy for each time step
t
by counting the overall correct predictions done at
t
, and dividing it by the overall number of predicitions done at
t
. Another metric we use to compare the different models is the average accuracy, also known as the area under curve (AUC). This metric is sensitive both to accuracy and detection time, and therefore adequate in evaluating the overall performance of each classifier.

Proofs

In this section, we prove our main results.

5.1
Proof of Theorem
1

We begin by following the footsteps of
[
23
]
. We first note the simple fact that
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}\leq\bar{\mathsf{T}}
with probability one, where

\displaystyle\bar{\mathsf{T}}\triangleq\inf\left\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{k}}\frac{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell})}{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell})}<\frac{\min_{\ell\in[M]}a_{\ell}}{M-1}\quad\forall j\neq k\right\}.

(52)

Therefore, for any
k\in[M]
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}>t|\mathcal{H}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\bar{\mathsf{T}}>t|\mathcal{H}_{k})

(53)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left.\bigcup_{j:j\neq k}\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{k}}\frac{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}\geq\frac{\min_{\ell\in[M]}a_{\ell}}{M-1}\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)

(54)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j:j\neq k}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left.\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{k}}\frac{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}\geq\frac{\min_{\ell\in[M]}a_{\ell}}{M-1}\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j:j\neq k}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left.\sqrt{\frac{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{k}}{\pi_{j}}\frac{\min_{\ell\in[M]}a_{\ell}}{M-1}}\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)

(56)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j:j\neq k}\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{k}}\frac{M-1}{\min_{\ell\in[M]}a_{\ell}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\sqrt{\frac{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}}\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right]

(57)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j:j\neq k}\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{k}}\frac{M-1}{\min_{\ell\in[M]}a_{\ell}}}[1-\mathcal{H}^{2}\left(f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t}),f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})\right)]

(58)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j:j\neq k}\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{k}}\frac{M-1}{\min_{\ell\in[M]}a_{\ell}}}\mathsf{U}^{(t)}_{k,j},

(59)

where the third inequality follows from the union bound, and the fourth inequality is due to Markov’s inequality, and we have defined,

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}^{(t)}_{k,j}

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\sqrt{\frac{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}}\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right].

(60)

Next, for
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and
k\neq j\in[M]
, define the conditional Hellinger distance as

\displaystyle S_{k,j}(z)

\displaystyle\triangleq 1-\mathcal{H}^{2}(\alpha_{k}(\cdot|z),\alpha_{j}(\cdot|z))

(61)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}(z^{\prime}|z)\alpha_{j}(z^{\prime}|z)}.

(62)

Recall that if the
\ell
th observation
\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}
is the
j
th edge in path
\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}
, we define the ancestor of
\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}
as
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\triangleq\mathsf{Z}_{j-1}^{\mathsf{P}}
, namely, it is the observation that precedes
\mathsf{Z}_{\ell}
in its corresponding path. Then, we note that

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}^{(t)}_{k,j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\sqrt{\frac{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{t})}}\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right]

(63)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z_{1}^{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}(z_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i})\alpha_{j}(z_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i})}

(64)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z_{1}^{t-1}}\sum_{z_{t}}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}(z_{t}|\mathcal{A}_{t})\alpha_{j}(z_{t}|\mathcal{A}_{t})}\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}(z_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i})\alpha_{j}(z_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i})}

(65)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{z_{1}^{t-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}(z_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i})\alpha_{j}(z_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i})}\cdot\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\sum_{z_{t}}\sqrt{\alpha_{k}(z_{t}|z)\alpha_{j}(z_{t}|z)}

(66)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{U}^{(t-1)}_{k,j}\cdot\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}S_{k,j}(z).

(67)

Thus, applying the same chain of inequalities it is clear that,

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}^{(t)}_{k,j}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}S_{k,j}(z)\right]^{t}.

(68)

Accordingly, we obtain that,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}>t|\mathcal{H}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j:j\neq k}\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{k}}\frac{M-1}{\min_{\ell\in[M]}a_{\ell}}}\cdot\left[\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}S_{k,j}(z)\right]^{t}

(69)

\displaystyle\leq(M-1)^{3/2}\max_{j\neq k}\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{k}\min_{\ell\in[M]}a_{\ell}}}\left[\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}S_{k,j}(z)\right]^{t}

(70)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{C}_{1}\cdot\exp\left(-\mathsf{C}_{2}\cdot t\right),

(71)

where
\mathsf{C}_{1}\triangleq(M-1)^{3/2}\max_{j\neq k}\sqrt{\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{k}\min_{\ell\in[M]}a_{\ell}}}
and
\mathsf{C}_{2}\triangleq\max_{j\neq k}\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\log\frac{1}{S_{k,j}(z)}
. Since we assume that for any
k\neq j
and any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the transition probability distributions
\alpha_{k}(\cdot|z)
and
\alpha_{j}(\cdot|z)
are not the same, it follows that
\max_{k\neq j}\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}S_{k,j}(z)<1
, which implies that
\mathsf{C}_{2}>0
. Thus, the right-hand-side of (
71
) decays exponentially fast with
t
, which concludes the proof.

Proof of Theorem
3

To prove Theorem
3
we need a few auxiliary results. Recall that for
m\in[M]
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we define
f_{m}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell})\triangleq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell}|\mathcal{H}_{m})
, and that
\pi^{\mathsf{stat}}_{k}
is the stationary distribution of the
k
th irreducible Markov chain with transition probabilities
\alpha_{k}(\cdot|\cdot)
. We start with the following result which proves an asymptotic equipartition property (AEP) of
\frac{1}{\ell}\log\frac{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{l})}{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{l})}

\ell\to\infty

Lemma 1

(AEP for Markov edges)
.

Let
\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell}
be a sequence of edge types sampled from
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot|\mathcal{H}_{k})
. Then,
f_{k}
-almost surely,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell}\log\frac{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell})}{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell})}\xrightarrow[\ell\to\infty]{}d_{\mathsf{KL}}(\alpha_{k}||\alpha_{j}|\pi_{k}^{\mathsf{stat}}).

(72)

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Let
\mathscr{N}_{z}
be the number of edges in
\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell}
whose parent edge is
z
, for
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. We have,

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{\mathscr{N}_{z}}{\ell}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}}\sum_{i:\mathsf{Z}_{i-1}^{P}=z}1}{\ell}

(73)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}}\left[|\mathsf{P}|\frac{1}{|\mathsf{P}|}\sum_{i:\mathsf{Z}_{i-1}^{P}=z}1\right]}{\ell}

(74)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\sum_{\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}}\left[\frac{|\mathsf{P}|}{\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{|\mathsf{P}|}\sum_{i:\mathsf{Z}_{i-1}^{P}=z}1\right]

(75)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\sum_{\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}}w_{\mathsf{P}}\cdot\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{P},z},

(76)

for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, where we have defined
w_{\mathsf{P}}\triangleq|\mathsf{P}|/\ell
, and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{P},z}\triangleq\frac{1}{|\mathsf{P}|}\sum_{i:\mathsf{Z}_{i-1}^{P}=z}1
. Now, because we assume that
\alpha_{k}
is irreducible, by the ergodic theorem for Markov chains
[
30
, Theorem 1.10.2]
, we have that
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{P},z}\to\pi^{\mathsf{stat}}_{k}(z)
almost surely, for any
\mathsf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\ell}
, as
\ell\to\infty
, and for any
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. Thus, since the sequence of random variables
\{\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{P},z}\}_{\mathsf{P}}
converges to a limit almost-surely, then their weighted Cesaro-mean in (
76
) converges, almost surely, to the same limit as well
[
31
]
. Thus, we finally get that,

\displaystyle\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\frac{\mathscr{N}_{z}}{\ell}

\displaystyle=\pi^{\mathsf{stat}}_{k}(z).

(77)

Using the above result, we have,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell}\log\frac{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell})}{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{\ell})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\log{\frac{\alpha_k(\s{Z}_{i}\vert\calA_i)}{\alpha_j(\s{Z}_{i}\vert\calA_i)}}

(78)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\sum_{i:\mathcal{A}_{i}=z}\log{\frac{\alpha_k(\s{Z}_{i}\vert\calA_i)}{\alpha_j(\s{Z}_{i}\vert\calA_i)}}

(79)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\frac{\mathscr{N}_{z}}{\ell}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\mathscr{N}_{z}}\sum_{i:\mathcal{A}_{i}=z}\log{\frac{\alpha_k(\s{Z}_{i}\vert\calA_i)}{\alpha_j(\s{Z}_{i}\vert\calA_i)}}\right)

(80)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\ell\to\infty]{}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\pi^{\mathsf{stat}}_{k}(z)d_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\alpha_{k}(\cdot|z)||\alpha_{j}(\cdot|z)\right)

(81)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathsf{KL}}(\alpha_{k}||\alpha_{j}|\pi_{k}^{\mathsf{stat}}),

(82)

almost surely, where we have applied the vanilla AEP
[
32
, Theorem 11.8.1]
on
\{\mathsf{Z}_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
, which is an i.i.d. sequence when conditioned on their parent edge.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Fix
k\in[M]
. Recall the definition of
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
in (

), and assume that

\displaystyle\min_{j\neq k\in[M]}\max_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\chi^{2}(\alpha_{k}(\cdot|z),\alpha_{j}(\cdot|z))<\infty.

Then,

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}\to\infty,

(84)

f_{k}

Proof of Lemma

.

Fix

, and let
j_{k}^{\star}
be a hypothesis

which achieves the minimum in (

). Using the definition of
\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}
, we have,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}(\mathsf{T}_{\mathsf{MSPRT}}<n|\mathcal{H}_{k})

(87)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left.\exists\ell\in[M]:\min_{1\leq m\leq n}\frac{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}{f_{\ell}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}<\frac{\pi_{\ell}a_{\ell}}{\pi_{j}},\;\forall j\neq\ell\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)

(88)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left.\bigcup_{\ell\in[M]}:\min_{1\leq m\leq n}\frac{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}{f_{\ell}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}<\frac{\pi_{\ell}a_{\ell}}{\pi_{j}},\;\forall j\neq\ell\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)

(89)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\in[M]}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left.\min_{1\leq m\leq n}\frac{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}{f_{\ell}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}<\frac{\pi_{\ell}a_{\ell}}{\pi_{j}},\;\forall j\neq\ell\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)

(90)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left.\min_{1\leq m\leq n}\frac{f_{j}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}<\frac{\pi_{k}a_{k}}{\pi_{j}},\;\forall j\neq k\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)

(91)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\neq k}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left.\max_{1\leq m\leq n}\frac{f_{\ell}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}>\frac{\pi_{k}}{\pi_{\ell}a_{\ell}}\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)

(92)

where the first inequality is because the sum of positive terms is larger than only a single term in the sum, the second inequality follows from the union bound, and the last inequality is by replacing the intersection over all
j\neq\ell
, with a single index
j=k
in the first term at the right-hand-side of (

), and with

(94)

where the last equality is due to the fact that

(95)

Thus, the first term at the right-hand-side of (

) can be upper bounded as,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\neq k}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left.\max_{1\leq m\leq n}\frac{f_{\ell}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}>\frac{\pi_{k}}{\pi_{\ell}a_{\ell}}\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)\leq\left(\frac{n}{\pi_{k}}\sum_{\ell\neq k}\pi_{k}\right)\cdot\norm{a}_{\infty},

which goes to zero, as

Next, let us analyze the second term in (

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left.\max_{1\leq m\leq n}\frac{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}{f_{j_{k}^{\star}}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}>\frac{\pi_{j_{k}^{\star}}}{\pi_{k}a_{k}}\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)

(97)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{\pi_{k}a_{k}}{\pi_{j_{k}^{\star}}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\frac{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}{f_{j_{k}^{\star}}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right]

(98)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{n}\frac{\pi_{k}a_{k}}{\pi_{j_{k}^{\star}}}\left[1+\chi^{2}(f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m}),f_{j_{k}^{\star}}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m}))\right].

(99)

Next, we note that,

\displaystyle 1+\chi^{2}(f_{k}({\mathsf{Z}}_{1}^{m}),f_{j_{k}^{\star}}({\mathsf{Z}}_{1}^{m}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{z_{1}^{m}\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{m}}\frac{f_{k}^{2}(z_{1}^{m})}{f_{j_{k}^{\star}}(z_{1}^{m})}

(100)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z_{1}^{m-1}\in{\mathcal{Z}}^{m-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}(z_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i})}{\alpha_{j_{k}^{\star}}(z_{i}|\mathcal{A}_{i})}\sum_{z_{m}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}\frac{\alpha_{k}^{2}(z_{m}|\mathcal{A}_{m})}{\alpha_{j_{k}^{\star}}(z_{m}|\mathcal{A}_{m})}

(101)

(103)

\displaystyle\leq\cdots

(104)

\displaystyle\leq\left[\max_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\chi^{2}(\alpha_{k}(\cdot|z^{\prime}),\alpha_{j_{k}^{\star}}(\cdot|z^{\prime}))\right]^{m}

(105)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{C}^{m},

(106)

where the last inequality follows from the assumption in (
83

, for some
\mathsf{C}>0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left.\max_{1\leq m\leq n}\frac{f_{k}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}{f_{j_{k}^{\star}}(\mathsf{Z}_{1}^{m})}>\frac{\pi_{j_{k}^{\star}}}{\pi_{k}a_{k}}\right|\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\pi_{k}}{\pi_{j_{k}^{\star}}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathsf{C}^{m}\right)a_{k}

(107)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\pi_{k}}{\pi_{j_{k}^{\star}}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathsf{C}^{m}\right)\norm{a}_{\infty},

(108)

which goes to zero, as
\norm{a}_{\infty}\to 0
. Therefore, combining (

), and (

), we obtain that

in probability as
\norm{a}_{\infty}\to 0
. Now, convergence in probability implies that there must be a sub-sequence of

which converge to infinity

-almost surely. Because

is non-decreasing as each

, we may conclude that

f_{k}
-almost surely, as
\norm{a}_{\infty}\to 0
.

6
Conclusion

This paper introduces multiclass information flow detection algorithms based on a realistic probabilistic model of information propagation over social media networks modeled by graphs. The learning task is to minimizes a risk defined as a combination of the classification error and the detection time. Our first algorithm is based on the well-known MSPRT, while the other is a novel graph neural network based sequential decision algorithm. For both algorithm we prove several statistical guarantees. Extensive experiments over two real-world datasets demonstrate that these algorithms outperform other state-of-the-art misinformation detection algorithms.
