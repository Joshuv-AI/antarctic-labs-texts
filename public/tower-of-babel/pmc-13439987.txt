Reperfusion‑targeted AnxA1sp restores mitochondrial resilience through mitoKATP activation to limit diabetic myocardial ischemia‑reperfusion injury
Authors: Guangyao Zou, Lei Pu, Zixi Xiao, Wenxiu Luo, Tian Yu, Yu Zhang, Lin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes exacerbates myocardial ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury and undermines conventional cardioprotective stimuli, largely via mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired antioxidant defenses. While the annexin A1-derived tripeptide (AnxA1sp) exhibits potent anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective properties in nondiabetic settings, its therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms against I/R injury in the uniquely vulnerable diabetic myocardium remain poorly characterized.MethodsA type 2 diabetes rat model, induced by a high-fat/high-sucrose diet and low-dose streptozotocin, was subjected to in vivo LAD occlusion or ex vivo Langendorff I/R. AnxA1sp (10 µM) was administered at the onset of reperfusion, either alone or with the mitoKATP blocker 5-hydroxydecanoate (5-HD). To evaluate efficacy and mechanism, we systematically assessed myocardial infarction, hemodynamic recovery, oxidative stress burden (ROS, MDA, SOD), mitochondrial bioenergetics (ATP, respiratory chain activity, ΔΨm, mPTP dynamics), and apoptotic signaling, alongside Sirt3/Kir6.1 expression profiles.ResultsIn vivo, AnxA1sp administration significantly restricted infarct size (by ~ 12–15 absolute %, P < 0.01) and rescued systolic function (LVEF + 8–12%, P < 0.05), accompanied by a marked decline in myocardial injury enzymes (CK-MB, LDH; both P < 0.05). Similarly, in the ex vivo diabetic heart, AnxA1sp enhanced hemodynamic recovery (LVDP + 25–35%, P < 0.01) and limited tissue necrosis. Across both models, the peptide profoundly blunted oxidative stress (ROS/MDA reduced by ~ 20–40%, P < 0.01) while restoring intrinsic antioxidant capacity (SOD + 20–30%, P < 0.05). Crucially, AnxA1sp preserved mitochondrial integrity—evidenced by stabilized membrane potential (ΔΨm + 25–35%, P < 0.01), delayed mPTP opening, and replenished ATP stores (+ 20–30%, P < 0.05)—which concurrently abrogated cytochrome c release and apoptotic signaling. Notably, the pharmacological blockade of mitoKATP with 5-HD effectively abolished these cardioprotective and bioenergetic benefits (all P < 0.05 vs. AnxA1sp). Furthermore, AnxA1sp significantly upregulated Sirt3 and Kir6.1 protein expression (both P < 0.05), mechanistically linking channel activation to deacetylase pathways.ConclusionsTargeting reperfusion with AnxA1sp confers robust, mitochondria-centric cardioprotection against diabetic I/R injury. By preserving redox and bioenergetic homeostasis, the peptide markedly limits infarction and rescues cardiac function. Crucially, this protection is strictly dependent on a novel mitoKATP–Sirt3 mechanistic axis. These findings establish AnxA1sp as a highly promising adjunct candidate for reperfusion therapy in high-risk diabetic populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08376-8.Keywords: AnxA1sp, Diabetes mellitus, Ischemia‑reperfusion injury, Mitochondria, mitoKATP channel

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death worldwide, and timely reperfusion is the cornerstone therapy for acute myocardial infarction. Yet restoration of coronary flow paradoxically elicits additional cardiomyocyte death—myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury (I/R)—that constrains the net benefit of contemporary reperfusion strategies [1]. Despite advances in primary PCI, high-sensitivity troponin-guided care, and optimized antithrombotic regimens, infarct size and heart failure rates remain substantial, underscoring the absence of clinically proven adjunctive cardioprotectants in routine practice [2]. Indeed, while the past decades have witnessed remarkable breakthroughs in other major therapeutic areas—such as the refinement of conventional chemotherapy as a cornerstone for the pharmacological management of a broad spectrum of malignant and non-malignant disorders [3, 4]—the translation of novel pharmacotherapies for acute myocardial I/R injury has fundamentally lagged behind.

This pharmacological therapeutic gap is crucially magnified in diabetes mellitus. The diabetic milieu not only accelerates the incidence and severity of ischemic heart disease but also profoundly heightens myocardial vulnerability to I/R injury. This exacerbated susceptibility is predominantly driven by redox imbalance, impaired microvascular perfusion, and a diminished capacity to respond to classic conditioning stimuli [5, 6]. Notably, highly recent evidence has increasingly emphasized that the collapse of mitochondrial quality control—characterized by severe bioenergetic failure and aberrant reactive oxygen species (ROS) handling—represents the primary pathological hub in diabetic I/R injury [7, 8]. Consequently, diabetic patients endure larger infarct sizes, more severe adverse post‑MI remodeling, and significantly higher mortality rates, leaving an urgent and unfulfilled therapeutic need in precisely the demographic at greatest risk.

Multiple interlocking mechanisms drive I/R injury, including abrupt calcium fluxes [9], reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction [10], mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, and inflammatory signaling [11]. These pathological cascades are temporally coupled. The onset of early reperfusion provokes an abrupt surge in mitochondrial ROS and deleterious cytosolic Ca²⁺ oscillations, which collectively precipitate mPTP opening, bioenergetic collapse, and irreversible cell death. Concurrently, endothelial dysfunction and neutrophil infiltration aggravate microvascular obstruction and sterile inflammation, forging a vicious feed-forward cycle that exacerbates infarct expansion and severely blunts functional recovery. The diabetic milieu exaggerates each of these nodes through chronic redox stress [12], advanced glycation [13], impaired mitophagy [14], rendering classical cardioprotective paradigms less effective [15]. Given that mitochondria represent the central hub integrating ROS, Ca²⁺, and energetic failure during I/R, interventions that directly reinforce mitochondrial resilience may hold translational promise where traditional conditioning fails.

Among endogenous pro‑resolving pathways, annexin A1 (AnxA1) has emerged as a mediator with anti‑inflammatory and cytoprotective actions, operating through formyl peptide receptors to dampen neutrophil trafficking [16], enhance efferocytosis [17], and restrain cytokine signaling [18]. Beyond inflammation resolution, accumulating evidence suggests that AnxA1 can influence mitochondrial homeostasis [19]—modulating redox tone, preserving membrane integrity, and curbing mPTP susceptibility—thereby intersecting core determinants of I/R injury. A potent bioactive tripeptide derived from AnxA1, designated AnxA1sp, recapitulates—and in certain contexts functionally surpasses—the cardioprotective actions of its parent protein in preclinical models. Notably, it offers distinct therapeutic advantages, including enhanced tissue penetrance, superior pharmacokinetics, and streamlined manufacturability. Furthermore, recent evidence in non-diabetic settings demonstrates its capacity to upregulate Sirt3 signaling and powerfully mitigate oxidative injury.

However, no prior study has examined the efficacy or mechanism of reperfusion‑targeted AnxA1sp in the diabetic heart—a context in which classical conditioning strategies fail. In the present study, we identify and mechanistically validate the activation of mitochondrial ATP-sensitive potassium (mitoKATP) channels as the primary driver orchestrating AnxA1sp-mediated mitochondrial resilience. Crucially, we demonstrate for the first time that this activation profoundly converges with Sirt3-dependent antioxidant programs to blunt myocardial I/R injury in the context of type 2 diabetes. Ultimately, this synergistic, dual-pathway axis establishes a novel pharmacological framework for mitochondrial-centric reperfusion protection in a high-risk, clinically vulnerable population.

Methods

Animals and ethics

Male Sprague–Dawley rats (150–200 g) were obtained from Hunan Slyke Jingda (license SCXK 2019-0004). Animals were housed under SPF conditions (22 ± 2 °C; 12 h light/dark; ad libitum food/water). All procedures adhered to the International Association of Veterinary Editors’ guidelines and were approved by the Zunyi Medical University Animal Care and Use Committee. Randomization: animals/hearts were allocated to groups by computer-generated sequences. Blinding: surgeons, operators, and outcome assessors were blinded to treatment. Exclusion criteria are specified in Sect. 2.5–2.6.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) induction

Rats were fed a normal chow for 1 week, then a high-fat/high-sucrose diet for 4 weeks. After a 12-h fast, a single i.p. injection of STZ (40 mg/kg) was administered [5, 6]. Seventy-two hours later, random blood glucose > 16.7 mmol/L (≥ 300 mg/dL) defined T2DM; non-induced or hypoglycemic animals were excluded. Experiments were performed after stabilization of the diabetic phenotype; body weight and glycemia were recorded prior to surgery/heart isolation.

Random blood glucose > 16.7 mmol/L at 72 h post-STZ and typical T2DM symptoms (polydipsia, polyphagia, polyuria, weight loss). This model recapitulates moderate-to-advanced clinical T2DM, with baseline myocardial mitochondrial dysfunction (vs. non-diabetic HFD rats), mimicking clinical T2DM-related myocardial vulnerability to I/R injury.

In vivo myocardial ischemia–reperfusion model

Rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (2% i.p., titrated to effect), endotracheally intubated, and mechanically ventilated. Following a left thoracotomy via the 3rd–4th intercostal space and subsequent pericardiotomy, the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery was exposed. A 6 − 0 non-absorbable suture was passed approximately 2 mm distal to the left atrial appendage. Successful occlusion was confirmed by regional myocardial pallor and ST-segment elevation on electrocardiography. Following 40 min of ischemia, the ligature was released to initiate a 180-min reperfusion period. Sham-operated control animals underwent an identical surgical procedure omitting the LAD ligation.

Animals were randomly assigned to one of five groups: Sham, I/R, I/R + AnxA1sp, I/R + AnxA1sp + 5-HD, and I/R + 5-HD. Exactly at the onset of reperfusion, treatments were administered via a single tail-vein bolus. AnxA1sp and the mitoKATP inhibitor 5-HD were prepared in saline, with the final DMSO concentration strictly maintained at ≤ 0.1%. All doses and injection volumes were normalized to body weight, and vehicle-treated groups received equivalent volumes of the matched solvent. To ensure experimental rigor, both the surgical procedures and the subsequent endpoint analyses were conducted by investigators blinded to the treatment allocations.

Ex vivo Langendorff ischemia–reperfusion model

Rats were heparinized (625 U/mL, 5 mL/kg i.p.) for 20 min and subsequently anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (1% i.p., 5 mL/kg). Hearts were rapidly excised and mounted on a Langendorff apparatus for retrograde aortic perfusion. The perfusion was maintained at a constant pressure of 70–80 cm H₂O using Krebs-Henseleit (K-H) buffer, which contained (in mM): 118.1 NaCl, 4.8 KCl, 1.2 MgSO₄, 1.2 KH₂PO₄, 1.6 CaCl₂, 25.0 NaHCO₃, and 11.5 glucose. The buffer was continuously aerated with a 95% O₂ / 5% CO₂ mixture at 37 °C. A fluid-filled compliant balloon catheter was inserted into the left ventricle (LV) via the mitral valve. The left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) was adjusted to 2–10 mmHg, and the balloon volume was strictly maintained constant thereafter. During the initial stabilization phase, hearts exhibiting non-sinus rhythm, a heart rate < 200 beats/min, or an LV systolic pressure < 70 mmHg were strictly excluded prior to randomization.

Following a 20-min stabilization period, successfully mounted hearts were subjected to 40 min of global no-flow ischemia, succeeded by 60 min of reperfusion. Time-matched control hearts underwent continuous perfusion without ischemia. Hearts (n = 8 per group) were randomized into five experimental groups: Control, I/R, I/R + AnxA1sp, I/R + AnxA1sp + 5-HD, and I/R + 5-HD. To target the reperfusion injury phase, AnxA1sp (10 µM) and 5-HD (100 µM) were dissolved directly in the K-H buffer and administered exclusively during the first 5 min of reperfusion, followed by a standard washout with normal K-H buffer for the remaining 55 min.

Echocardiographic and hemodynamic assessments

In vivo cardiac function was evaluated using a high-resolution Vevo 2100 imaging system. To ensure physiological consistency, standard parasternal long- and short-axis views were acquired under carefully matched heart rates and anesthesia depth. Key functional and structural parameters—including ejection fraction (EF), fractional shortening (FS), left ventricular internal dimensions (LVIDd/s), and anterior/posterior wall thicknesses (LVAWd/s, LVPWd/s)—were calculated by averaging measurements across five consecutive cardiac cycles.

For ex vivo functional assessments, continuous intraventricular pressure signals were acquired via a fluid-filled transducer connected to a PowerLab/8SP data acquisition system. The monitored indices encompassed heart rate (HR), left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP), left ventricular developed pressure (LVDP), and the maximum rates of pressure development and decline (+ dP/dtmax and − dP/dtmax). Concurrently, coronary flow was quantified via the timed collection of coronary effluent. These parameters were systematically recorded at baseline (immediately following the stabilization period) and subsequently at 20, 40, and 60 min of reperfusion. To rigorously prevent bias, all echocardiographic acquisitions, ex vivo recordings, and subsequent data analyses were performed by experienced investigators strictly blinded to the treatment allocations.

Myocardial infarct size quantification

Following the reperfusion period, hearts were rapidly excised, gently blotted dry, and temporarily chilled at 4 °C for approximately 20 min to facilitate uniform sectioning. The left ventricles were transversely sectioned into five 2-mm-thick slices from the apex to the base. To distinguish viable from necrotic tissue, these slices were incubated in 1% 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) solution at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, and subsequently fixed overnight in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. Post-staining, viable myocardium displayed a deep brick-red color, whereas infarcted regions appeared characteristically pale or gray-white. The areas of infarction and total left ventricle (LV) were quantified planimetrically using ImageJ software. The infarct size was calculated as the cumulative infarct area expressed as a percentage of the total LV area (Σ infarct area / Σ LV area × 100%), evaluated by an independent investigator strictly blinded to the treatment groups.

Quantification of myocardial injury biomarkers

To biochemically assess the extent of acute myocardial injury, we quantified the release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB). For the in vivo model, venous blood was drawn from the inferior vena cava under deep anesthesia at the end of the 180-min reperfusion period. The blood was centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 min to isolate the serum. Conversely, for the ex vivo Langendorff model, the coronary effluent was collected at 30 min of reperfusion and similarly centrifuged. All serum and effluent supernatants were immediately aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until subsequent analysis.

Biomarker concentrations were determined using specific commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (LDH: Cat. No. JL13677-96T; CK-MB: Cat. No. JL12296-96T; Shanghai Jianglai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Thermo microplate reader. Sample concentrations (expressed in ng/mL) were directly extrapolated from standard curves generated alongside the samples, which maintained high linearity (R² ≥ 0.99) across the analytical range. To ensure experimental consistency, all samples were processed under uniform conditions.

Transmission electron microscopy and ultrastructural analysis

Upon cessation of the reperfusion protocol, myocardial samples (approximately 1 mm³) were promptly harvested from the anterior wall of the left ventricle, carefully maintaining the muscle fiber orientation. The specimens were primarily fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde, washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide. Following sequential dehydration through a graded acetone series, the tissues were embedded in epoxy resin, cut into ultrathin sections, and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. High-resolution ultrastructural images were subsequently acquired using a transmission electron microscope.

To semi-quantitatively evaluate the extent of mitochondrial ultrastructural damage, we employed the established Flameng grading scale (0–4). The pathological criteria were defined as follows: a score of 0 indicates normal architecture with intact membranes and densely packed cristae; 1 denotes mild injury with slight swelling and rare cristae disruption; 2 represents moderate injury characterized by obvious swelling and partial cristae loss but an intact outer membrane; 3 reflects severe injury with marked swelling, extensive cristae disruption, and partial outer membrane rupture; and 4 signifies irreversible injury, defined by complete cristae dissolution, severe membrane rupture, and overt mitochondrial fragmentation. For robust quantification, a total of six hearts per group were analyzed. A minimum of three randomly selected, non-overlapping fields per heart were independently evaluated by two experienced pathologists who remained strictly blinded to the experimental group allocations.

In situ assessment of myocardial ROS production

Fresh-frozen left ventricular tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm using a cryostat. To detect in situ ROS production, the tissue sections were incubated with the fluorescent probe dihydroethidium (DHE; 10 µM) for 30 min at 37 °C in a light-protected humidified chamber. Following three consecutive 5-min washes in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the slides were examined under an Olympus fluorescence microscope. To strictly preclude technical bias, all image acquisition parameters—including excitation intensity, gain, and exposure time—were maintained absolutely constant across all experimental groups.

For rigorous quantification, an independent investigator blinded to the treatment allocations randomly captured a minimum of five non-overlapping fields per heart. These fields (measuring 200 × 200 μm and encompassing ≥ 100 cardiomyocytes each) were specifically selected from the ischemic risk area, carefully avoiding the necrotic infarct core. Quantitative analysis was systematically performed using ImageJ software based on two complementary metrics. First, the integrated optical density (IOD) per field was calculated to represent the total ROS signal burden within a standardized unit area. Second, the mean fluorescence intensity per individual cell was determined; this process involved the digital demarcation of cardiomyocyte contours, the precise subtraction of background autofluorescence, and the subsequent computation of the average specific signal per cell.

Quantification of Myocardial Oxidative Stress and ATP ReservesTo evaluate myocardial redox status and bioenergetic integrity, snap-frozen left ventricular tissues from the ischemic risk zone were utilized. Tissues were mechanically homogenized on ice in either cold phosphate-buffered saline (for oxidative stress markers) or a proprietary lysis buffer (for ATP extraction), followed by centrifugation at 4 °C to collect the supernatants.

The myocardial levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and the specific activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) were quantified using commercial colorimetric assay kits (Cat. Nos. JL13297-96T and JL22893-96T, respectively; Shanghai Jianglai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). Prior to detection, sample protein concentrations were precisely determined via a standard BCA assay. Equalized amounts of protein (30 µg for MDA and 25 µg for SOD per reaction) were analyzed, and the absorbance was read using a Thermo microplate reader. Concurrently, myocardial ATP content was measured using a bioluminescence-based assay kit (Cat. No. S0026; Beyotime Biotechnology, China). To strictly prevent ATP degradation, the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatants were immediately assayed without subsequent freeze-thaw cycles.

All biochemical assays were performed in strict adherence to the manufacturers’ protocols. Sample concentrations were mathematically extrapolated from highly linear standard curves (R2 ≥ 0.99 for all kits). For final data normalization, MDA and SOD values were standardized to the total protein content (expressed as nmol/mg protein and U/mg protein, respectively), whereas the ATP levels were normalized to the initial tissue wet weight (expressed as µmol/mg wet weight).

Subcellular fractionation and western blot analysis

To evaluate targeted protein expression and compartmental translocation (e.g., cytochrome c release), ischemic left ventricular tissues were rapidly excised, rinsed in ice-cold PBS, and homogenized in a PMSF-containing lysis buffer. Mitochondria-enriched and cytosolic fractions were systematically partitioned using a specialized tissue mitochondria isolation kit. Following quantification via a BCA protein assay, equal amounts of protein extracts were mixed with loading buffer, denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, resolved by SDS-PAGE, and electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes.

To prevent non-specific binding, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. The blots were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies directed against Sirt3 (1:1000), Kir6.1 (1:400), and cytochrome c (1:1000). Following rigorous washing in TBST, membranes were probed with corresponding horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence under standardized exposure settings to preclude signal saturation. Densitometric quantification was performed using ImageJ software. To ensure precise relative quantification, the optical densities of target proteins were strictly normalized to compartmental loading controls: COX IV for the mitochondrial fractions and GAPDH or β-actin for the cytosolic/total lysates. All densitometric analyses were conducted by independent investigators strictly blinded to the treatment allocations.

Immunofluorescence and mitochondrial colocalization analysis

To evaluate the spatial distribution and expression of target proteins, deparaffinized myocardial sections were subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval using 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0). To block non-specific binding sites, the sections were incubated in a solution containing 10% normal goat serum and 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. The tissues were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mixture of primary antibodies directed against Sirt3 (rabbit anti-Sirt3, 1:300) and the specific mitochondrial network marker COX IV (mouse anti-COX IV, 1:500).

Following thorough washes in PBS, the sections were probed with corresponding Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. High-resolution images were acquired using a Leica fluorescence microscope equipped with a 20×/0.4 PH objective and 1.5× digital zoom. Quantitative assessment of relative fluorescence intensity and Sirt3/COX IV spatial colocalization was systematically performed using ImageJ software. To rigorously eliminate technical bias, all microscopic exposure parameters and digital acquisition settings were maintained absolutely identical across all experimental groups.

Mitochondrial isolation and comprehensive functional profiling

Isolation of Intact Mitochondria for Functional Assays. Distinct from the lysis-based fractionation used for immunoblotting, freshly intact mitochondria were isolated to preserve bioenergetic viability. Briefly, fresh myocardial tissues (50–100 mg) were finely minced and enzymatically dissociated using 0.25% trypsin on ice for 20 min. Following mechanical homogenization, the mitochondrial fraction was purified via sequential differential centrifugation (600 × g for 5 min, followed by 11,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C) using a commercial isolation kit (Cat. No. BC3010; Solarbio). Mitochondrial protein concentrations were immediately determined via the BCA method, and the suspensions were standardized to 1 mg/mL. To ensure maximal organelle viability, all subsequent functional assays were initiated within 30 min of extraction.

Respiratory Chain Activity and mPTP Opening. The enzymatic activities of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I–IV were quantified using specific commercial colorimetric assay kits. Absorbances were recorded on a Thermo microplate reader, normalized to mitochondrial protein content, and expressed as fold-changes relative to the control group. Concurrently, the susceptibility to mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening was evaluated. Freshly isolated mitochondria (0.2 mg protein) were incubated with a specific fluorescent probe for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. Following washing and resuspension in a pre-warmed preservation buffer, fluorescence was continuously monitored; a proportional decline in the fluorescent signal was utilized as a quantitative index of enhanced mPTP opening.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (ΔΨm). The integrity of ΔΨm was determined utilizing the JC-1 fluorescent probe (Cat. No. C2006; Beyotime Biotechnology). Standardized mitochondrial aliquots (10–100 µg protein) were incubated with a JC-1 working solution (final concentration 1 µM) for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark. Unbound probe was removed by dual centrifugation washes (600 × g for 4 min at 4 °C). Dual-emission fluorescence was immediately recorded using a microplate reader at Ex/Em 490/530 nm (green fluorescence, indicating JC-1 monomers in depolarized membranes) and Ex/Em 525/590 nm (red fluorescence, indicating J-aggregates in hyperpolarized membranes). The ΔΨm was quantitatively expressed as the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity. Carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP; 10 µM) was employed as a robust positive control to induce complete membrane depolarization. All aforementioned functional assays were performed in at least technical duplicates from independent biological replicates.

Statistics

To ensure robust statistical power, an a priori sample size calculation was performed using G Power 3.1 software (α = 0.05, power ≥ 0.80). Based on pilot studies and anticipated large effect sizes (e.g., Cohen’s d ranging from 1.8 to 2.1 for primary endpoints such as LVEF and infarct size), a minimum of n = 3 independent biological replicates (individual animals or isolated hearts) per group was required. For all biochemical and immunoblotting assays, samples were processed in technical triplicates, and the averaged values were utilized for subsequent statistical modeling. All quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to inferential testing, data distribution normality and the homogeneity of variances were formally verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. For multigroup comparisons of normally distributed data with equal variances, a standard one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. In instances where the assumption of homoscedasticity was violated, Welch’s ANOVA combined with the Games–Howell post hoc correction was utilized. Hemodynamic parameters assessed across multiple time points were analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, incorporating the Greenhouse–Geisser correction to adjust for deviations from sphericity. All statistical analyses were computed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Reperfusion-targeted AnxA1sp restricts acute in vivo I/R injury in diabetic myocardium via a mitoKATP-dependent axis

Following 40 min of LAD occlusion and 180 min of reperfusion, diabetic rats exhibited expected acute systolic dysfunction and adverse structural remodeling compared to non-ischemic controls. This was evidenced by marked declines in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and fractional shortening (LVFS), alongside pathological chamber dilation and wall thinning (Fig. 1C–H). Crucially, reperfusion-targeted administration of AnxA1sp profoundly rescued contractile performance and partially normalized LV geometry. However, co-administration of the selective mitoKATP blocker 5-hydroxydecanoate (5-HD) completely abrogated these hemodynamic benefits, returning indices to levels comparable with the vehicle-treated I/R cohort. Notably, 5-HD alone exerted no discernible functional effect versus I/R alone.

Consistent with this functional preservation, triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining revealed a striking restriction of myocardial infarct size in the AnxA1sp-treated group relative to I/R controls. This robust infarct-sparing effect was largely abolished by concurrent 5-HD treatment, whereas 5-HD alone provided no anatomical protection (Fig. 1I–J). Systemic markers of myocardial necrosis closely mirrored these histopathological outcomes: the I/R-induced elevations in serum creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) were significantly attenuated by AnxA1sp, yet were restored to near-I/R levels upon mitoKATP blockade (Fig. 1K–L).

Collectively, these in vivo findings demonstrate that reperfusion-targeted AnxA1sp profoundly restricts acute tissue necrosis and rescues systolic performance in the highly vulnerable diabetic heart. Furthermore, the striking reversal of these protective endpoints by selective pharmacological blockade firmly establishes a central, mitoKATP-dependent axis mediating AnxA1sp’s cardioprotective efficacy.

Ex vivo validation: mitoKATP activation is indispensable for AnxA1sp-mediated intrinsic functional recovery and cardioprotection

In isolated diabetic hearts perfused on the Langendorff apparatus, global ischemia (40 min) followed by reperfusion (60 min) elicited a profound deterioration in hemodynamics. This was characterized by severely depressed left ventricular developed pressure (LVDP), reduced coronary flow, and elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) relative to pre-ischemic baselines and non-ischemic controls (Fig. 2A–D). Notably, reperfusion-targeted delivery of AnxA1sp significantly improved intrinsic mechanical recovery, maintaining higher LVDP and coronary flow while suppressing LVEDP elevation throughout the reperfusion phase. However, co-administration of the selective mitoKATP blocker 5-HD progressively diminished these benefits, yielding hemodynamic traces that converged toward the vehicle-treated I/R baseline. Heart rates remained comparable across all groups, confirming that the improvements in contractile performance were strictly inotropic and not driven by altered chronotropy (Fig. 2E).

Quantitative endpoint analyses at the conclusion of reperfusion corroborated these continuous time-course observations. AnxA1sp-treated hearts exhibited significantly greater LVDP and coronary flow recovery, alongside restricted LVEDP, compared to the I/R cohort—therapeutic gains that were substantially blunted by 5-HD (Fig. 2C–H). Consistent with this hemodynamic rescue, TTC staining revealed a significantly restricted infarct fraction in the AnxA1sp group relative to I/R controls. This robust infarct-sparing effect was almost entirely abrogated by 5-HD, whereas 5-HD alone afforded no anatomical or functional baseline protection (Fig. 2I–J).

Collectively, by eliminating the confounding influence of systemic neurohumoral factors and altered loading conditions, these ex vivo findings definitively demonstrate that reperfusion-targeted AnxA1sp enhances intrinsic mechanical recovery and profoundly limits tissue necrosis in the diabetic myocardium. The effective abolition of these therapeutic gains by pharmacological mitoKATP blockade directly corroborates our in vivo observations, establishing a strict mitoKATP-dependent axis for AnxA1sp-mediated cardioprotection.

AnxA1sp mitigates oxidative stress and preserves myocardial bioenergetics via a mitoKATP-dependent axis

Consistent with a mitochondria-centric mechanism of protection, reperfusion-targeted AnxA1sp favorably modulated indices of acute redox stress in the highly vulnerable diabetic myocardium. Dihydroethidium (DHE) fluorescence imaging revealed a robust accumulation of total reactive oxygen species (ROS) following I/R compared to non-ischemic controls (Fig. 3A–B). Strikingly, AnxA1sp therapy markedly curtailed this oxidative burst: quantitative analysis demonstrated that the integrated optical density (IOD) per field and the mean fluorescence intensity per individual cardiomyocyte were substantially reduced—by 36.4% and 34.7%, respectively—relative to the vehicle-treated I/R cohort. This pronounced antioxidant effect was significantly abrogated by co-administration of the selective mitoKATP blocker 5-HD, which drove ROS accumulation back toward I/R levels. 5-HD alone provided no discernible reduction in ROS burden.

Parallel biochemical assays of myocardial tissue homogenates corroborated these critical imaging findings. The severe I/R-induced elevation in lipid peroxidation—evidenced by increased malondialdehyde (MDA) content—was robustly suppressed by AnxA1sp, an effect effectively neutralized by concurrent 5-HD blockade. Conversely, the profound depletion of intrinsic superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity following I/R was significantly restored by AnxA1sp therapy; again, this preservation of antioxidant capacity was largely abolished in the presence of 5-HD (Fig. 3C–D).

Beyond redox homeostasis, AnxA1sp profoundly preserved myocardial energy charge. The catastrophic depletion of myocardial ATP content triggered by I/R was significantly rescued in AnxA1sp-treated hearts. Notably, 5-HD co-administration progressively diminished this bioenergetic improvement, whereas 5-HD alone failed to rescue ATP levels above the I/R baseline (Fig. 3E). Together, these highly convergent readouts demonstrate that AnxA1sp critically restrains the pathological oxidative burst while actively supporting ATP homeostasis in the diabetic heart. The effective attenuation of these profound benefits by pharmacological blockade firmly establishes a central, mitoKATP-dependent axis driving AnxA1sp’s antioxidant and bioenergetic efficacy.

AnxA1sp preserves mitochondrial ultrastructure and stabilizes membrane potential via a mitoKATP-dependent mechanism

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed pronounced mitochondrial ultrastructural injury in diabetic hearts subjected to I/R, characterized by severely swollen organelles, disrupted cristae, and extensive matrix rarefaction relative to non-ischemic controls (Fig. 4A). Reperfusion-targeted administration of AnxA1sp markedly mitigated these pathological alterations, preserving compact cristae architecture and robustly limiting matrix swelling. This structural protection was quantitatively corroborated by Flameng scoring: the severe I/R-induced ultrastructural damage—reflected by a significantly elevated injury score—was prominently attenuated by AnxA1sp (Fig. 4B). Crucially, co-administration of the selective blocker 5-HD completely abrogated these ultrastructural benefits, driving mitochondrial morphology and injury scores back toward the severe I/R phenotype. 5-HD alone conferred no structural protection.

Consistent with the preservation of physical integrity, AnxA1sp profoundly stabilized mitochondrial functional indices. JC-1 fluorescence imaging revealed a sharp reduction in the red-to-green fluorescence ratio following I/R, indicative of severe ΔΨm depolarization. AnxA1sp therapy significantly restored this ratio toward control levels, a rescue effect that was effectively nullified by concurrent 5-HD blockade (Fig. 4C–E). Furthermore, complementary fluorescence assays evaluating the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) demonstrated that I/R-triggered excessive pore opening was robustly constrained by AnxA1sp. Again, this functional stabilization was significantly reversed by 5-HD co-administration (Fig. 4F–H).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate an inextricable coupling between AnxA1sp-mediated structural preservation and functional resilience. The stabilization of ΔΨm and the restriction of mPTP opening align seamlessly with the preserved integrity of the outer mitochondrial membrane. Similarly, the retention of compact cristae architecture—the physical locus of the electron transport chain—provides a structural basis for the preserved respiratory complex activities (Fig. 4H–K) and the maintenance of oxidative phosphorylation. The abolition of these coordinated benefits by pharmacological blockade reinforces that mitoKATP activation is the central mechanism by which AnxA1sp constrains depolarization, limits permeability transition, and ultimately maintains organelle viability in the diabetic myocardium.

AnxA1sp restores Sirt3/Kir6.1 expression and restricts cytochrome c-mediated apoptosis via a mitoKATP-dependent axis

To delineate the molecular underpinnings of AnxA1sp-mediated cardioprotection, we evaluated the expression of crucial metabolic and channel targets. Western blot analysis revealed that reperfusion-targeted AnxA1sp significantly upregulated the expression of Sirt3 and Kir6.1 (the core subunit of the mitoKATP channel) in the diabetic myocardium following I/R. Crucially, this robust target engagement was substantially diminished by co-administration of the selective mitoKATP blocker 5-HD.

Building on the observed structural and ΔΨm stabilization, we assessed mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP). Subcellular fractionation demonstrated that I/R provoked a massive translocation of cytochrome c from the mitochondria into the cytosol. AnxA1sp therapy effectively halted this pathological release, robustly retaining cytochrome c within the mitochondrial fraction. However, concurrent 5-HD blockade effectively negated this containment, yielding cytosolic cytochrome c accumulation comparable to the vehicle-treated I/R cohort (Fig. 5A–B).

Parallel assessment of the inner membrane respiratory machinery demonstrated that I/R induced a severe depletion of COX IV and total OXPHOS complex proteins. AnxA1sp largely rescued the abundance of these critical respiratory markers—aligning seamlessly with the preserved ATP production and structural integrity noted earlier. Conversely, pharmacological mitoKATP blockade with 5-HD abolished this bioenergetic preservation (Fig. 5C–E).

Downstream of mitochondrial permeabilization, I/R provoked robust apoptotic activation, evidenced by an elevated Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and pronounced cleavage of caspase-3. Consistent with the profound restriction of cytochrome c release, AnxA1sp therapy significantly suppressed these pro-apoptotic signaling cascades. The marked reversal of this anti-apoptotic efficacy by 5-HD co-administration establishes that mitoKATP channel activation is an absolute requisite for AnxA1sp to interrupt the mitochondria-driven cell death program in the diabetic heart (Fig. 5F–H).

Discussion

The present study establishes AnxA1sp as a highly potent, reperfusion-targeted cardioprotective agent in the vulnerable diabetic myocardium. Crucially, we provide mechanistic evidence that its therapeutic efficacy is intrinsically coupled to the activation of the mitochondrial ATP-sensitive potassium channel (mitoKATP). We recognize that relying on 5-hydroxydecanoate (5-HD) for pharmacological validation carries inherent caveats, given its known off-target metabolic effects, notably the inhibition of fatty acid oxidation. However, three converging lines of evidence firmly substantiate that the AnxA1sp-mediated protection observed here is driven specifically by on-target mitoKATP activation rather than non-specific metabolic interference. First, administration of 5-HD alone exerted no independent deleterious or protective effects on either in vivo or ex vivo I/R injury endpoints, effectively ruling out generalized metabolic toxicity as the driver of the reversed phenotype. Second, AnxA1sp therapy significantly upregulated the protein abundance of Kir6.1 (the core pore-forming subunit of mitoKATP), an effect that was uniquely abrogated by 5-HD co-administration. Because 5-HD functions primarily as a channel blocker, its ability to reverse Kir6.1 upregulation suggests a dynamic positive feedback loop wherein sustained channel activity is required to maintain its own subunit expression—reinforcing a specific molecular interdependency. Third, the precise sequence of mitochondrial benefits conferred by AnxA1sp—namely, the stabilization of ΔΨm, restriction of mPTP opening, and preservation of respiratory chain integrity—perfectly aligns with the canonical downstream consequences of mitoKATP pore opening [1, 6]. The ability of 5-HD to cleanly dismantle this specific suite of organelle-level protections provides compelling evidence that AnxA1sp engages the classical mitoKATP-dependent survival program in the diabetic heart.

To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first demonstration that reperfusion-targeted delivery of an Annexin-A1-derived peptide confers robust cardioprotection in the type 2 diabetic myocardium—a notoriously refractory clinical setting where conventional conditioning strategies typically fail [20]. Furthermore, we establish a novel mechanistic convergence between mitoKATP channel activation and Sirt3-linked antioxidant signaling, defining a dual-pathway, mitochondria-centric therapeutic paradigm for acute reperfusion injury. The translational relevance of this paradigm is underscored by our complementary in vivo and ex vivo findings: across both models, AnxA1sp therapy profoundly restricted infarct size, blunted myocardial injury biomarker release, and drove superior recovery of systolic and hemodynamic function. Mechanistically, this macroscopic functional rescue is deeply rooted in the preservation of organelle-level integrity. Rather than exerting isolated effects, AnxA1sp effectively short-circuited the pathological reperfusion cascade by concurrently curbing the acute oxidative burst, stabilizing ΔΨm, and preserving ATP biosynthesis. By maintaining respiratory chain architecture and raising the threshold for mPTP opening, AnxA1sp critically restrained cytochrome c efflux and downstream cell death programs, aligning flawlessly with the established paradigm of mitochondria-driven lethal reperfusion injury [21]. Crucially, the widespread attenuation of these multifaceted structural, bioenergetic, and functional benefits by 5-HD firmly establishes the mitoKATP channel not merely as an incidental contributor, but as a central orchestrator of AnxA1sp’s mitochondria-directed protective program.

Cardioprotection via mitoKATP activation and mPTP restraint is a well-established framework in non-diabetic settings [22]. However, diabetes blunts many classical protective stimuli, including ischemic conditioning, and rewires mitochondrial signaling through chronic redox stress, impaired Sirt3 activity, and altered substrate handling [23]. Our findings significantly expand the biological landscape of Annexin A1 by demonstrating that its derived short peptide, AnxA1sp, retains robust therapeutic efficacy even within the highly challenging diabetic milieu. The convergence of our structural, bioenergetic, and apoptotic data delineates a cohesive, multi-tiered mechanism. Specifically, AnxA1sp primes mitochondria to withstand the shock of early reperfusion by driving a mitoKATP-dependent mild depolarization of ΔΨm. This pivotal biophysical shift effectively buffers deleterious Ca²⁺ overload and suppresses the initial ROS burst, thereby markedly elevating the threshold for mPTP opening. Concurrently, the preservation of Sirt3 expression and its downstream antioxidant cascade firmly indicates that AnxA1sp mobilizes intrinsic mitochondrial resilience programs.

Notably, while direct evidence of Sirt3 deacetylase activity or downstream target acetylation is lacking, the upregulation of Sirt3 protein is strongly correlated with canonical functional phenotypes of Sirt3 activation: First, Sirt3 is a well-established mitochondrial deacetylase that enhances SOD2 antioxidant activity and improves mitochondrial metabolic efficiency via deacetylating IDH2, and our data show that AnxA1sp-induced Sirt3 upregulation is accompanied by reduced ROS/MDA levels, restored SOD activity (Fig. 3), stabilized mitochondrial membrane potential, preserved ATP content, and maintained respiratory chain integrity (Figs. 3 and 4)—all of which are consistent with downstream effects of Sirt3 activation. Second, 5HD not only attenuated AnxA1sp’s cardioprotective effects but also reversed the upregulation of Sirt3 protein (Fig. 5C), indicating a tight functional link between mitoKATP activation and Sirt3 abundance. These findings align with prior evidence suggesting that mitoKATP opening optimizes the mitochondrial microenvironment—specifically by stabilizing ΔΨm and mitigating ROS—to facilitate Sirt3 activity. Conversely, Sirt3 may reciprocally bolster mitoKATP stability. This dynamic strongly implies a synergistic crosstalk, rather than a strictly linear hierarchical relationship, between mitoKATP and Sirt3.

The translational robustness of AnxA1sp is underscored by the seamless integration of our in vivo and ex vivo findings. The in vivo data establish profound clinical relevance within the complex neurohumoral and microvascular milieu typical of acute myocardial infarction. Conversely, the Langendorff model confirms that these protective effects are intrinsic to the myocardium and persist independently of systemic confounders [24]. Crucially, the consistent attenuation of these benefits by 5-HD across both models, coupled with the absence of confounding chronotropic shifts, firmly establishes a primary, organelle-driven mechanism rather than mere indirect hemodynamic modulation. At the organelle level, this protection manifests through a highly coordinated channel-redox-bioenergetic axis. While our experimental paradigm did not include direct real-time respirometry (e.g., global oxygen consumption rates) [25], we rigorously triangulated “respiratory chain integrity” through the preservation of its core functional outputs. Specifically, AnxA1sp maintained the structural and enzymatic viability of Complexes I–IV, stabilized ΔΨm, and preserved ATP biosynthesis. This bioenergetic stabilization effectively uncoupled the I/R-induced ROS burst (evidenced by reduced DHE and MDA, alongside restored SOD) from mPTP opening, thereby restraining cytochrome c efflux and downstream apoptotic cascades [26]. The positioning of mitoKATP at the apex of this protective hierarchy is further elucidated by the expression dynamics of its core subunit, Kir6.1. Although the functional pore resides within the inner mitochondrial membrane, the AnxA1sp-driven upregulation of total Kir6.1—encompassing its cytosolic transit pool—reflects a comprehensive enhancement of channel synthesis, translocation, and functional assembly [27]. By amplifying this mitoKATP-dependent biophysical defense, AnxA1sp creates a permissive mitochondrial microenvironment that favors the restoration of Sirt3-dependent antioxidant programs. Although we assessed Sirt3 abundance rather than direct enzymatic target acetylation, the striking alignment of our macroscopic, redox, and energetic phenotypes strongly echoes established paradigms linking Annexin-A1-derived peptides to Sirt3 pathway engagement [28].

The diabetic myocardium is fundamentally characterized by a diminished mitochondrial reserve, impaired Sirt3 signaling, and a profound desensitization to classic conditioning stimuli [29]. The ability of AnxA1sp to reconstitute a robust cardioprotective phenotype within this highly refractory milieu represents a significant therapeutic advance. Mechanistically, the mitoKATP-driven mild depolarization exerts a dual biophysical and biochemical benefit uniquely suited to the diabetic substrate. Biophysically, this depolarization blunts deleterious Ca²⁺ influx and is postulated to powerfully suppress reverse electron transport (RET)–driven ROS generation at mitochondrial complex I during the critical early minutes of reperfusion [30]. Biochemically, the concurrent enhancement of Sirt3 abundance serves to alleviate the hyperacetylation burden on key antioxidant and metabolic enzymes, thereby sustaining ATP biosynthesis despite a severely stressed metabolic environment [31]. Beyond its robust mechanistic foundation, the translational viability of AnxA1sp is highly compelling. Crucially, its efficacy when administered exclusively at the onset of reperfusion aligns perfectly with contemporary clinical workflows, such as primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), where a single pharmacological bolus could be feasibly deployed upon guidewire crossing or balloon inflation [32]. Furthermore, as a highly manufacturable short peptide, AnxA1sp is conceptually well-positioned for integration with current standards of care, including antiplatelet therapies and statins. While its strict dependence on mitoKATP underscores the clinical necessity of screening for drug-drug interactions involving channel modulators—and highlights the need for future validation using orthogonal probes in large animal models—the overarching therapeutic profile remains exceptionally attractive. Ultimately, the synergy between the canonical inflammation-resolving properties of the parent Annexin A1 pathway and the targeted mitochondrial resilience orchestrated by AnxA1sp offers a multifaceted, additive defense strategy specifically tailored for the high-risk diabetic population [33].

Limitations

Several limitations of the current study warrant consideration. First, while 5-HD is a widely utilized pharmacological tool for mitoKATP blockade, its potential off-target effects—specifically the inhibition of fatty acid oxidation—are recognized [34]. Although our experimental design included a 5-HD-alone control and demonstrated a strong molecular correlation between Kir6.1 expression and 5-HD-mediated reversal, future studies should employ more orthologous approaches. These include the use of glibenclamide to target the SUR subunit [35], cardiomyocyte-specific Kir6.1 knockdown models, and direct patch-clamp recordings to unequivocally define the biophysical engagement of AnxA1sp with the mitochondrial inner membrane. Second, our bioenergetic profiling focused on core respiratory chain complex activities and steady-state ATP levels. While these indices provide a robust functional surrogate, high-resolution respirometry (e.g., Oroboros) and direct quantification of reverse electron transport (RET) would offer a more granular kinetic profile of mitochondrial oxygen flux [36]. Similarly, although Sirt3 protein abundance strictly paralleled our redox and energetic phenotypes, direct assessment of Sirt3 deacetylase activity and the acetylation status of its canonical targets, such as SOD2 or IDH2, would further consolidate the biochemical evidence for this axis [37]. Finally, while the HFD/STZ rat model effectively captures key features of type 2 diabetes, it may not encompass the full heterogeneity of the human clinical condition. Furthermore, our investigation was restricted to the acute phase of reperfusion; thus, the long-term impact of AnxA1sp on post-ischemic remodeling, arrhythmic burden, and microvascular outcomes remains to be elucidated. Future research should prioritize validating AnxA1sp’s translational feasibility in large-animal diabetic models under clinically relevant reperfusion scenarios to bridge the gap toward therapeutic implementation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, targeted administration of the Annexin A1-derived peptide, AnxA1sp, at the onset of reperfusion confers robust, mitochondria-centric cardioprotection in the highly vulnerable diabetic myocardium. Phenotypically, it profoundly restricts infarct size and accelerates post-ischemic functional recovery. Mechanistically, AnxA1sp safeguards organelle integrity by stabilizing membrane potential, sustaining ATP biosynthesis, and quelling both oxidative stress and apoptotic signaling, thereby decisively raising the critical threshold for mPTP opening. The complete abrogation of these therapeutic benefits by 5-hydroxydecanoate establishes mitoKATP channel activation as the indispensable driver of this protective cascade. Furthermore, the concurrent restoration of Kir6.1 and Sirt3 unveils an unprecedented mechanistic convergence between biophysical ion channel dynamics and biochemical deacetylase pathways. Collectively, these findings position AnxA1sp as a compelling, mechanistically grounded adjunct candidate for clinical reperfusion therapy in diabetes. Simultaneously, they establish a robust conceptual framework for future investigations utilizing orthogonal genetic tools and direct enzymatic assays to definitively map this novel mitoKATP-Sirt3 axis.
