Disseminated relapse of ovarian mature cystic teratoma with squamous cell carcinoma transformation following intraoperative spillage: a case report
Authors: Antonio Maccio, Manuela Neri, Valerio Vallerino, Gabriele Sole, Elisabetta Pusceddu, Paolo Albino Ferrari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Malignant transformation of ovarian mature cystic teratoma is rare, usually presenting as squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Rupture or spillage may complicate management, although a causal relationship with recurrence cannot be inferred from individual cases.Presentation of case:A 59-year-old woman developed disseminated relapse after excision of an ovarian dermoid cyst with SCC transformation, complicated by intraoperative rupture and pelvic spillage. She presented with an abdominal-wall mass and multifocal pelvic/peritoneal disease, consistent with at least FIGO stage IIIC-equivalent recurrence. She underwent diagnostic laparoscopy, then a complete open cytoreduction, including abdominal-wall resection/reconstruction, omentectomy, multivisceral bowel resections, type C2 radical hysterectomy, rectal resection, and pelvic/para-aortic lymphadenectomy. Histology confirmed a poorly differentiated SCC in a dermoid cyst, with negative margins and 87 negative lymph nodes. Adjuvant carboplatin-paclitaxel was administered without any CTCAE grade ≥1 toxicity. At 4 years, she remains disease-free.Discussion:Reports describe implantation/carcinomatosis after rupture/spillage. Complete cytoreduction, adherence to no-spillage principles, and early expert referral are essential.Conclusion:Strict no-spillage principles and early referral to high-expertise centers are essential when malignancy is suspected.

Introduction

Ovarian mature cystic teratoma (MCT) is one of the most common benign ovarian tumors. Malignant transformation is rare, reported in approximately 1–3% of MCTs, and most frequently presents as squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)[1,2]. Patients are typically older than patients with benign MCT, and tumors are often large at diagnosis. Prognosis is heterogeneous but worsens with advanced-stage disease[3].

From a pathological standpoint, MCT is a benign ovarian germ-cell tumor composed of mature, well-differentiated tissues derived from ectodermal, mesodermal, and/or endodermal elements[4,5]. MCT should be distinguished from immature teratoma, which is a malignant ovarian germ-cell tumor characterized by immature embryonal tissue, most importantly immature neuroectodermal components[6,7]. SCC arising in an MCT represents somatic malignant transformation within a mature teratoma rather than progression toward an immature teratoma[2,4,8]. This distinction is clinically relevant because SCC arising in MCT is usually reported in older women and is frequently associated with large tumors. In contrast, immature teratoma typically affects younger patients and follows distinct therapeutic paradigms[2,6,7].

Ovarian malignancies may disseminate by direct extension, transcoelomic spread within the peritoneal cavity, lymphatic spread, and hematogenous metastasis[9,10]. Transcoelomic dissemination is particularly relevant to peritoneal carcinomatosis. It involves tumor-cell detachment, survival in peritoneal fluid, adhesion to mesothelial surfaces, invasion of the peritoneum or omentum, and growth of peritoneal implants[9,10]. In SCC arising from MCT, rupture or spillage may theoretically expose malignant squamous elements to the peritoneal cavity and create conditions compatible with implantation, as suggested by previous reports of peritoneal implantation or carcinomatosis after rupture/spillage of dermoid cysts with malignant transformation [11–13]. However, this mechanism remains inferential in single-case reports and should not be presented as definitive causality[11–13].

Optimal management relies on surgery. In published series and systematic analyses, International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage and the achievement of complete macroscopic resection (or, conversely, the presence of residual tumor) are among the most consistent determinants of survival in SCC arising from MCT[2,14]. Although minimally invasive surgery is widely used for presumed-benign dermoid cysts, rupture and spillage remain central concerns[15]. Beyond the well-known risk of chemical peritonitis and adhesions, several reports describe intraperitoneal implantation or carcinomatosis after rupture/spillage when malignant transformation was present but unrecognized[11–13].

Here, we report disseminated relapse after prior excision of an MCT with SCC transformation complicated by pelvic spillage, managed with maximal cytoreductive surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy, with durable disease-free survival. We focus on diagnostic challenges, estimated stage at relapse, resectability assessment, and the need to avoid overstating causality between spillage and recurrence. This study follows the SCARE criteria[16].

Presentation of the case

In January 2021, a 59-year-old woman was admitted to a tertiary referral Gynecologic Oncology Unit after, in September 2020 at another hospital, laparoscopic excision of an approximately 15-cm dermoid ovarian cyst with SCC transformation. The index procedure was complicated by intraoperative rupture and pelvic spillage during extraction/conversion through a Pfannenstiel incision. She presented with a progressively enlarging left lower abdominal mass, diffuse abdominal pain, constipation, and fatigue. The index operation had been performed outside the reporting unit. According to the available documentation, the adnexal mass was treated as a presumed dermoid cyst, and SCC transformation was identified only on pathology. Records confirmed rupture with pelvic spillage but lacked details on the timing or mechanism of rupture, the use of a retrieval bag or other containment measures, suction or irrigation, or the surgeon’s oncologic expertise. The complete original pathology report was unavailable for central review; therefore, the pathological FIGO stage at first diagnosis could not be assigned retrospectively. These limitations are relevant because this case concerns recurrence after rupture and possible tumor-cell implantation during unrecognized malignant transformation.

The preoperative assessment documented relevant medical, surgical, medication, allergy, and gynecologic histories; family and social histories were inadequately documented. Laboratory tests showed WBC 55.28 × 103/µL, neutrophils 94.3%, lymphocytes 2.9%, hemoglobin 12 g/dL, platelets 445 × 103/µL, iron 8 g/dL, ferritin 448 g/L (N.V. 10–120), transferrin 180 mg/dL (N.V. 200–360), C-reactive protein 11.9 mg/dL (N.V. 0.10), fibrinogen 498 mg/dL (N.V. 150–450), and serum glucose 140 mg/dL. Tumor markers were HE4 37 pmol/L (N.V. < 140 mg/dL), CA125 27 U/mL, CA19-9 1.2 U/mL, CA15-3 19.9 U/mL, carcinoembryonic antigen 1.1 ng/mL, and SCC antigen 1.4 ng/mL, all within reference ranges.

Computed tomography (CT) showed a 4.2 × 5 × 5.7-cm solid, non-uniform pelvic mass contiguous with small-bowel loops; additional lesions involved the left rectus muscle and left iliac-fossa subcutaneous tissue. The right ureter was obstructed by a pelvic uterine mass, with right calyco-pyelo-ureteral dilatation, and 1-cm mesenteric lymph nodes were noted (Fig. 2a-b). Positron emission tomography-CT showed multiple hypermetabolic lower abdominal and pelvic foci, likely peritoneal and partly bowel-related, plus two hyperactive left iliac-fossa areas, one subcutaneous and one within the adjacent rectus muscle, consistent with heteroplastic peritoneal and abdominal-wall implants (Fig. 2c). At recurrence, the differential diagnoses included recurrent SCC from malignant transformation of MCT, incisional/extraction-site implantation, a primary abdominal-wall soft-tissue tumor, an inflammatory mass, a metastatic gastrointestinal malignancy, and another gynecologic recurrence. The prior diagnosis, the topographic relationship to the extraction/conversion site, multifocal hypermetabolic peritoneal and bowel-related disease on CT and PET-CT, and definitive histopathology supported the diagnosis of disseminated recurrent SCC arising from MCT. The main diagnostic challenge was distinguishing implantation-related dissemination from other primary or metastatic processes. In this setting, diagnostic laparoscopy was planned exclusively to assess disease distribution and resectability before attempting cytoreduction, not to remove or decompress an adnexal mass.

Management consisted of cytoreductive surgery preceded by diagnostic laparoscopy, performed by an experienced gynecologic oncology team. Umbilical laparoscopy showed no diffuse unresectable peritoneal carcinomatosis but revealed omentum–abdominal wall adhesions and a neoplastic mass engulfing multiple ileal loops, preventing pelvic visualization; conversion to laparotomy was therefore performed. After a xiphopubic incision, the abdominal-wall mass involving the rectus abdominis muscles was excised. Exploration identified neoplastic, matted small bowel adherent to the omentum; omentectomy and resection of approximately 80 cm of diseased ileum were performed with a stapled side-to-side anastomosis after adhesiolysis[17]. The pelvis then became accessible: the uterus was enlarged, the left adnexa was normal, and both ureters were identified, with marked right ureteral dilatation. Neoplastic tissue encased the right pelvic ureter, and rectal invasion involved the posterior uterine wall. Type C2 radical hysterectomy according to Querleu–Morrow[18] was followed by anterior rectal resection with a 28-mm circular end-to-end anastomosis, bilateral pelvic and lumboaortic lymphadenectomy up to the renal vein[19], synthetic mesh placement, and abdominal-wall reconstruction using the sliding skin-plane technique.

Histopathology showed poorly differentiated G3 SCC arising within a dermoid cyst, invading uterine serosa and myometrium from fundus to isthmus bilaterally, rectal serosa and the outer two-thirds of muscularis, and several small-bowel segments, with negative margins. The entire abdominal wall was infiltrated; all 87 pelvic and lumboaortic lymph nodes were negative. Macroscopic extrapelvic peritoneal disease, bowel/omental involvement, and negative retroperitoneal nodes were consistent with at least FIGO stage IIIC-equivalent recurrent disease. The abdominal-wall lesion was clinically interpreted as suspected implantation/extraction-site recurrence rather than hematogenous metastasis. Complete macroscopic cytoreduction was achieved. Operative time was 400 minutes; no relevant blood loss, transfusion, postoperative surgical complication, or Clavien–Dindo grade I–V event occurred[20]. Hospital stay was 9 days.

Adjuvant chemotherapy began 1 month postoperatively: carboplatin AUC 5–6 plus paclitaxel 175 mg/m2 every 21 days for six cycles, with the regimen selected because treatment is not standardized, and platinum-taxane therapy is commonly used by extrapolation from ovarian cancer and retrospective evidence[2,14]. No toxicity of grade ≥1 according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events occurred[21]. At 3 months, laboratory abnormalities normalized. Follow-up included quarterly clinical assessments and biomarker testing, with alternating abdominopelvic ultrasound and CT. Discussion

Malignant transformation of MCT is uncommon, with SCC being the predominant histology[2]. Without prospective data, management relies on retrospective series and extrapolation from ovarian/cervical cancer paradigms. Stage and residual disease are prognostic factors that support complete macroscopic resection whenever feasible[3,14]. The novelty lies not in SCC transformation alone but in rupture/spillage at index surgery, multifocal peritoneal and abdominal-wall relapse, complete multivisceral cytoreduction, negative lymph nodes despite invasion, and durable 4-year disease-free survival.

Rupture/spillage is the main interpretative issue, but causality requires caution[15]. Reports describe peritoneal implantation or carcinomatosis after rupture/spillage of dermoid cysts later found to harbor occult SCC transformation[11–13], supporting plausible tumor-cell dissemination after malignant peritoneal exposure. However, a single case cannot establish causality; recurrence may reflect aggressive biology, incomplete staging, or occult dissemination at the time of index surgery. In our patient, the temporal sequence, documented spillage, peritoneal relapse, and an abdominal wall lesion near the extraction/conversion-site make implantation plausible but not definitive. This lesion may represent implantation or extraction/conversion-site recurrence, consistent with incisional/trocar-site recurrences in malignantly transformed MCT[22].

This case illustrates that laparotomy or conversion does not, by itself, prevent cyst-content exposure if strict containment principles are not maintained[3,23]. In apparent early-stage ovarian carcinoma, laparotomy remains the standard approach for treatment and staging because complete abdominopelvic exploration is required and rupture of an intact, suspicious ovarian mass should be avoided[23,24]. The ESMO–ESGO consensus recommendations state that rupture of an intact tumor with spillage of cancer cells must be avoided regardless of the surgical approach used[23]. Minimally invasive surgery may be considered for selected restaging procedures when the primary ovarian lesion has already been removed, and no further risk of rupture of the ovarian mass remains, provided that surgery is performed by trained surgeons in expert centers[23].

In suspected advanced ovarian cancer, diagnostic laparoscopy has a different role. It may be used to obtain histological confirmation, evaluate intra-abdominal disease burden, and assess resectability before choosing between primary cytoreductive surgery and neoadjuvant chemotherapy[23,25,26]. Laparoscopy-based predictive models, including the Fagotti score, evaluate disease sites such as omental cake, peritoneal carcinomatosis, diaphragmatic disease, mesenteric retraction, bowel or stomach infiltration, and liver metastases to estimate the likelihood of optimal cytoreduction[25,26]. Therefore, laparoscopy in this setting should be considered a diagnostic and triage tool, not a justification for unprotected removal, rupture, or decompression of a suspicious adnexal mass[23,25,26].

Absolute contraindications to laparoscopy are mainly clinical or anesthetic rather than histology-specific, including hemodynamic instability, inability to tolerate pneumoperitoneum or steep Trendelenburg, uncontrolled coagulopathy, and situations requiring immediate laparotomy[23,27]. In ovarian cancer, oncologic contraindications or limitations include an intact, suspicious adnexal mass that cannot be extracted without rupture, inability to guarantee protected specimen containment, and imaging or laparoscopic evidence that complete macroscopic cytoreduction is not feasible[23–26]. Guideline-defined patterns arguing against primary debulking include diffuse deep infiltration of the small-bowel mesenteric root, diffuse small-bowel carcinomatosis that would lead to short-bowel syndrome, unresectable involvement of major upper-abdominal structures or vessels, central or multisegmental liver metastases, multiple lung metastases, unresectable lymph nodes, and brain metastases[23]. In the present case, diagnostic laparoscopy was justified because preoperative imaging suggested multifocal but potentially resectable disease, and laparoscopy confirmed the absence of diffuse unresectable peritoneal carcinomatosis, thereby allowing conversion to open complete cytoreduction.

At relapse, maximal cytoreduction was prioritized, consistent with evidence that stage and residual disease influence outcomes[2,14]. Diagnostic laparoscopy assessed distribution and resectability. The absence of unresectable carcinomatosis and the feasibility of en bloc resection supported conversion and subsequent cytoreduction. Small-bowel resection with anastomosis was part of debulking[17]. The pelvic phase required type C2 radical hysterectomy according to Querleu–Morrow and rectal resection, followed by pelvic and para-aortic lymphadenectomy[18,19]. Despite invasion, all 87 lymph nodes were negative, consistent with peritoneal or direct invasion predominating over nodal spread[2,14].

This case supports referring large, complex, or potentially malignant MCTs to high-expertise centers. Suboptimal risk assessment, incomplete staging, or inadequate containment may have oncologic consequences when transformation is unrecognized[2,3].

Adjuvant therapy for SCC arising in MCT is not standardized, and platinum-based regimens are most commonly reported[2,8,14]. In the present case, carboplatin plus paclitaxel every 21 days for six cycles was selected by extrapolation from epithelial ovarian cancer and from retrospective evidence suggesting a potential role for platinum-based chemotherapy in advanced-stage SCC transformation of MCT[2,8,14,23]. HIPEC was not administered in this case. Cytoreductive surgery combined with HIPEC has been explored for ovarian teratomas with malignant transformation and peritoneal dissemination, but the available evidence is limited to small retrospective mixed-histology series[28]. Therefore, HIPEC should be described as investigational in this rare setting, and its omission in the present case should be interpreted as a case-specific multidisciplinary decision rather than evidence against HIPEC[23,28].

This single-case report limits generalizability and causal inference. Since the index surgery was performed elsewhere and full documentation was unavailable, the containment technique, rupture timing/extent, irrigation, surgeon expertise, family/social history, and initial FIGO stage could not be reconstructed. The spillage–recurrence relationship remains plausible but unproven. Without a comparator, contributions of cytoreduction, biology, and chemotherapy cannot be separated; conclusions are hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare but clinically important scenario: disseminated peritoneal and abdominal-wall relapse after rupture/spillage of an MCT with SCC transformation. The chronology supports but does not prove implantation-related dissemination. When malignant transformation is suspected, strict no-spillage principles, protected extraction, complete staging, and early referral to gynecologic oncology centers are essential. In selected patients with resectable relapse, complete macroscopic cytoreduction followed by systemic therapy may achieve durable disease control.
