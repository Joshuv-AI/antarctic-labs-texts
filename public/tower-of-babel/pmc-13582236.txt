Circulating Cell-free DNA as a biomarker for radiation-induced injury: From mechanistic insights to clinical translation
Authors: Qixin Sun, Yikun Li, Tianwen Yin, Feifei Teng, Jinming Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCirculating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) has attracted increasing attention as a minimally invasive biomarker for assessing radiation-induced tissue injury. Radiation exposure can alter cfDNA concentration, fragmentation profiles, and methylation patterns. These changes may reflect cellular damage, tissue origin, and temporal responses to irradiation. During radiotherapy, however, cfDNA can arise from tumor cells, irradiated normal tissues, endothelial cells, immune cells, and hematopoietic cells. This mixed origin complicates the interpretation of radiation-related signals. Current evidence is derived mainly from preclinical studies and small, heterogeneous clinical cohorts. Sampling schedules, analytical workflows, and clinical thresholds also vary across studies. Recent advances in fragmentomics, tissue-specific methylation analysis, mitochondrial cfDNA, multi-omics integration, and artificial intelligence have expanded the information obtained from cfDNA. In particular, tissue-specific methylation and fragmentation features may help identify the cellular or organ origin of injury. Nevertheless, prospective multicenter validation and methodological standardization are required before routine clinical use. This review summarizes the mechanisms linking radiation injury to cfDNA release, current detection technologies, and evidence for organ-specific applications. It also discusses clinical confounders, translational barriers, and future directions for integrating cfDNA into precision radiation oncology.Keywords: Radiotherapy, Radiation injury, cfDNA, Liquid biopsy, Methylation, Precision medicine

Introduction

Radiotherapy is one of the key treatments for malignant tumors and is widely used in managing various types of cancer [1]. By delivering ionizing radiation to tumor sites, it induces lethal DNA damage in tumor cells, thereby achieving local tumor control and reducing the risk of recurrence. It has become a vital part of many cancer treatment plans, especially for locally advanced tumors, treatment-resistant cancers, and postoperative adjuvant therapy. However, despite its strong therapeutic impact, radiotherapy can also cause various adverse effects, including both acute and long-term damage to healthy tissues. These radiation-related injuries not only lower patients’ quality of life but also affect treatment outcomes and complicate clinical decision-making [2, 3]. Such damage mainly arises from DNA lesions, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and epigenetic changes, which remain difficult to predict and monitor in clinical practice [3, 4]. Over the years, different biomarkers have been investigated to detect and monitor radiation-induced injuries. Currently investigated biomarkers, including cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), DNA damage response markers such as γ-H2AX and other DNA repair proteins, microRNAs (miRNAs), and oxidative stress markers such as malondialdehyde (MDA) and 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), have shown potential in evaluating radiation-induced tissue damage [5–10]. Although γ-H2AX is highly sensitive to radiation-induced DNA double-strand breaks and can be used for serial assessment of DNA damage during radiotherapy, its interpretation is influenced by sampling time, cell type, and assay methodology, and it provides limited information regarding the tissue origin of injury [10–13]. Other biomarkers, including cytokines, oxidative stress markers, and miRNAs, may also be affected by nonspecific inflammatory responses, comorbidities, and interindividual variability, limiting their specificity and reproducibility [5, 9, 14]. Consequently, no single biomarker has yet been sufficiently validated for routine prediction and longitudinal monitoring of radiation-induced tissue injury.

Recent advances in molecular biology have highlighted circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) as a promising non-invasive biomarker for assessing radiation-induced injury. During radiotherapy, cfDNA may be released from tumor cells, irradiated normal tissues, endothelial cells, immune cells, and hematopoietic cells through processes such as apoptosis, necrosis, and stress-related cellular responses [14–18]. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), the tumor-derived fraction of cfDNA, mainly reflects tumor burden and treatment response, whereas cfDNA released from normal tissues may provide information on radiation-related tissue injury [19, 20]. Because these sources may contribute simultaneously, cfDNA signals detected during radiotherapy can be difficult to interpret. Fragmentation patterns and tissue-specific methylation signatures may help clarify the cellular or tissue origin of cfDNA and support the assessment of organ-specific injury [17, 21].

Preclinical studies and limited clinical observations suggest that cfDNA levels and tissue-specific methylation patterns may change following radiation exposure and may be associated with radiation-related tissue injury [17, 22, 23]. These findings support further evaluation of cfDNA as a minimally invasive biomarker for radiation-related toxicity. However, current evidence remains limited by small and heterogeneous cohorts, variable sampling schedules, and a lack of standardized analytical methods. This narrative review summarizes the biological basis, detection technologies, and potential applications of cfDNA in radiation-induced injury, and discusses the current limitations and future directions for its clinical translation.

Literature search strategy

This narrative review was informed by literature searches conducted in PubMed and the Web of Science Core Collection from database inception to July 2026. Search terms included combinations of “cell-free DNA”, “cfDNA”, “circulating tumor DNA”, “radiotherapy”, “ionizing radiation”, “radiation injury”, “radiation toxicity”, “fragmentomics”, “DNA methylation”, “mitochondrial DNA” and relevant organ-specific terms. Additional searches were performed for DNA damage response biomarkers, oxidative stress markers, inflammatory cytokines, microRNAs, cfDNA detection technologies, multi-omics, and artificial intelligence. Peer-reviewed clinical, preclinical, and methodological studies were considered, together with relevant reviews that provided biological or technical context. Reference lists of selected articles were also screened to identify additional studies. Greater emphasis was placed on studies directly related to radiation-induced injury, recent evidence, and studies with clear methodological descriptions. Earlier landmark studies were retained when they provided essential mechanistic or methodological foundations. As this article was designed as a narrative review, formal systematic eligibility criteria, duplicate screening, risk-of-bias assessment, and quantitative evidence synthesis were not applied.

Organ-specific radiation-induced injuries

Radiation-induced injury can affect a wide range of normal tissues, and its clinical features vary according to the irradiated organ, radiation dose and volume, treatment schedule, and individual susceptibility. Lung, heart, and esophageal injuries are common complications of thoracic radiotherapy, but radiation-related damage may also involve the gastrointestinal tract, urinary system, skin and mucosa, and hematopoietic tissues. The following sections summarize representative organ-specific injuries and their biological features relevant to cfDNA-based assessment.

Radiation-induced lung injury

Radiation-induced lung injury (RILI) is a serious complication of thoracic radiotherapy and is generally divided into acute radiation pneumonitis (RP) and late radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis (RIPF). RP typically appears weeks to months after treatment and results from alveolar epithelial and endothelial damage, pulmonary edema, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced DNA damage, inflammatory cytokine release, and increased vascular permeability [24]. Ongoing inflammation may lead to RIPF, which is characterized by fibroblast activation, collagen deposition, and reduced lung compliance, with transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) serving as a central regulator [25]. Recent findings also suggest that ferroptosis may contribute to the development of RIPF [26].

Radiation-related injury to pulmonary epithelial and endothelial cells may contribute to cfDNA release into the circulation. In a mouse model of thoracic irradiation, tissue-specific methylation analysis showed a dose-dependent increase in lung endothelial cell-derived cfDNA. In patients with breast cancer, cardiopulmonary endothelial cell-derived cfDNA increased during radiotherapy, whereas changes in total endothelial cell-derived cfDNA correlated with lung dose parameters [17]. However, direct clinical evidence linking cfDNA changes to the occurrence or severity of RP and RIPF remains limited.

Radiation-induced heart disease (RIHD)

Radiation-induced heart disease (RIHD) is an important late complication of thoracic radiotherapy. It may affect multiple cardiac structures and manifest as coronary artery disease, myocardial injury and fibrosis, valvular dysfunction, microvascular damage, heart failure, or arrhythmia [27]. In patients receiving radiotherapy for breast cancer, the risk of major coronary events increased with the mean radiation dose to the heart, indicating a dose-dependent relationship between cardiac exposure and subsequent injury [28]. RIHD involves direct DNA damage, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, persistent inflammation, and progressive tissue fibrosis [27, 29]. In patients with breast cancer, cardiomyocyte-derived methylated cfDNA increased during radiotherapy and was associated with the maximum heart dose [17]. However, its relationship with clinically evident RIHD remains unclear.

Radiation-induced esophageal injury

Radiation esophagitis is a frequent complication of thoracic radiotherapy, particularly in patients undergoing mediastinal or lung irradiation [30–32]. Its pathogenesis is multifactorial and mainly involves direct injury to esophageal mucosal cells, radiation-induced DNA damage, inflammatory responses, and impaired local tissue repair [30, 31]. Acute injury may cause dysphagia and odynophagia during or shortly after treatment, whereas late injury may lead to ulceration, fibrosis, or stricture [30–32]. The potential role of cfDNA in detecting or monitoring radiation-induced esophageal injury remains unclear.

Other organ-specific radiation injuries

Radiation-induced injury may also involve the gastrointestinal tract, urinary system, skin, and mucosal tissues. Abdominal and pelvic radiotherapy can damage rapidly proliferating epithelial cells and local microvasculature, resulting in acute enteritis, proctitis, or cystitis. Persistent vascular injury and fibrosis may contribute to late ulceration, bleeding, stricture, and impaired organ function [33, 34]. In patients receiving prostate radiotherapy, total cfDNA concentration and tissue-associated cfDNA methylation markers have been evaluated in relation to gastrointestinal and genitourinary toxicity, although the reported associations have been mixed [22, 35]. Radiation-related skin and mucosal injury may present as dermatitis or oral mucositis, with erythema, desquamation, pain, and ulceration caused by epithelial damage and inflammation [33, 36].

Hematopoietic tissues are highly radiosensitive, and irradiation of active bone marrow may cause cytopenias and persistent injury to hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells [33, 37]. Injury to immune and hematopoietic cells may also contribute to cfDNA signals detected during radiotherapy, further complicating the interpretation of their tissue origin [15, 16] (Table 1).

Mechanisms of radiation-induced injury

Radiation-induced injury begins with the interaction of ionizing radiation with DNA and other biological macromolecules, followed by oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, cell death, and inflammatory responses. These processes can promote cfDNA release and influence its concentration and molecular characteristics. In addition to reflecting cellular injury, extracellular nuclear and mitochondrial DNA may act as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and activate innate immune signaling. Together, these mechanisms provide the biological basis for the potential use of cfDNA in assessing radiation-induced injury (Fig. 1).

DNA damage and cfDNA release

DNA is a principal target of radiation-induced injury, with double-strand breaks (DSBs) having the most significant impact on cell survival [38]. Repairing these DSBs is complex and may introduce errors. If left unrepaired, they can lead to gene mutations, chromosomal aberrations, cellular senescence, or cell death [39]. Damaged DNA activates the DNA damage response (DDR), involving key proteins such as ATM and ATR and downstream effectors including p53. This response induces cell-cycle arrest and provides time for DNA repair [38, 39]. When the damage cannot be adequately repaired, cells may undergo apoptosis or necrosis, or enter senescence [16, 18, 39]. These responses can promote cfDNA release into the extracellular space and circulation [16, 18]. Experimental studies have shown dose- and time-dependent changes in circulating cfDNA following irradiation [23]. These changes may be associated with the extent of cellular or tissue damage. Thus, the magnitude and kinetics of cfDNA elevation may provide useful information on radiation-induced injury and exposure.

Cell death pathways and cfDNA fragment signatures

Ionizing radiation can induce multiple forms of cell death that contribute to extracellular DNA release. Apoptosis and necrosis are the best-characterized sources of distinct cfDNA fragment patterns, whereas pyroptosis and NETosis may provide additional sources of extracellular DNA.

During apoptosis, cleavage mediated by DFFB, DNASE1L3, and other nucleases generates predominantly mononucleosomal cfDNA fragments of approximately 166 bp, with a 10-bp periodicity and characteristic end motifs that reflect nucleosome organization [40, 41]. In contrast, necrosis produces more heterogeneous and less regularly fragmented DNA, often including high-molecular-weight fragments, as a result of membrane rupture and cellular disintegration [42]. Pyroptosis, mediated by gasdermin pore formation, may promote the release of extracellular DNA, including mitochondrial DNA, together with pro-inflammatory cytokines [43]. NETosis represents another source of extracellular chromatin under radiation stress. NETs consist of decondensed chromatin associated with histones and granule proteins such as myeloperoxidase, and experimental evidence suggests that high-dose X-ray irradiation can induce NET formation in mouse neutrophils [44, 45].

Oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling

Oxidative stress is one of the earliest and most widespread biological responses to ionizing radiation. Radiation generates ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) through water radiolysis and mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and extensive DNA strand breaks [4, 46]. The imbalance between ROS production and antioxidant defense systems promotes cellular damage and apoptosis, which further contribute to the release of cfDNA into the circulation. Radiation-induced mitochondrial dysfunction may also sustain ROS production and promote the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) [43, 46, 47]. In addition, oxidative modification may alter the biological activity of cfDNA, and oxidized cfDNA has been implicated in stress signaling associated with radiation-induced bystander effects [48].

Once released from damaged cells, nuclear DNA and mtDNA may act as DAMPs and participate in the activation of innate immune signaling. After cellular uptake, extracellular DNA may activate endosomal Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), whereas DNA accumulating in the cytosol can be recognized by the cyclic GMP–AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) pathway [49, 50]. Activation of these pathways promotes nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and type I interferon signaling and the release of inflammatory cytokines. The NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome may further promote caspase-1 activation, interleukin-1β release, and pyroptosis, thereby contributing to persistent inflammation and tissue injury [51].

Persistent oxidative and inflammatory stress may also promote cellular senescence. Senescent cells can maintain local inflammation through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which may promote fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition. Experimental evidence suggests that radiation-induced senescence-like fibroblasts contribute to pulmonary fibrosis [52]. Together, these mechanisms may sustain inflammation and promote chronic tissue damage and fibrosis.

Biomarkers investigated for radiation-induced injury

DDR biomarkers

Following DSB formation, H2AX is rapidly phosphorylated around the damage sites, resulting in the formation of γ-H2AX foci. The p53-binding protein 1 (53BP1) is also recruited to damaged chromatin. Therefore, γ-H2AX and 53BP1 foci are widely used to assess the formation and repair of DSBs [10, 11].

γ-H2AX is highly sensitive to radiation-induced DNA damage. Its response shows clear time- and dose-dependent changes. Under experimental conditions, γ-H2AX foci can be detected after radiation doses as low as approximately 1 mGy [10, 53]. Serial analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) can also be used to monitor DNA damage and repair during radiotherapy [13].

Studies have evaluated γ-H2AX and 53BP1 foci as markers of individual radiosensitivity and radiation-related toxicity. Persistent foci or delayed repair may indicate reduced DNA repair capacity and a higher risk of adverse reactions [12, 54]. However, these markers are not specific to radiation. Other genotoxic stressors can also induce DSBs. Their measurement is strongly affected by sampling time, cell type, cell-cycle status, staining methods, image acquisition, and foci-counting procedures [10, 11]. Measurements in PBMCs also cannot identify the tissue or organ in which the injury occurred. Further standardization and prospective validation are therefore needed before these markers can be routinely used to predict radiation-induced injury.

Oxidative stress biomarkers

Oxidative stress is a key factor in many types of radiation-induced injury, and related biomarkers have shown potential for forecasting damage and therapy outcomes. Among these, 8-OHdG and MDA are often used to gauge systemic oxidative damage after radiation treatment. Bergman et al. reported increased urinary 8-OHdG and MDA levels in patients receiving high-dose radiochemotherapy before stem cell transplantation. These changes were associated with total white blood cell counts, suggesting that the biomarkers reflected treatment-related oxidative damage [8]. More recently, changes in serum 8-OHdG were observed in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) during radiotherapy. The study also examined its associations with radiation dose, treatment response, and treatment-related toxicity [55].

Still, these biomarkers face intrinsic constraints. Smoking, body mass index, and other lifestyle factors may affect 8-OHdG and MDA levels [56, 57]. Urinary 8-OHdG measurements may also vary with sampling conditions and assay methods. Interlaboratory studies have shown greater variability with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays than with chromatographic methods [58]. For MDA, results may differ according to the sample type and whether free or total MDA is measured [59]. These factors limit comparisons across studies. Therefore, 8-OHdG and MDA may reflect systemic oxidative stress during treatment, but their ability to predict organ-specific radiation injury remains uncertain.

Cytokines and inflammatory biomarkers

Radiation can alter the levels of inflammatory and profibrotic cytokines. Several studies have evaluated IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and TGF-β1 as potential biomarkers of radiation-induced lung injury. Hart et al. found that lower pretreatment IL-8 levels were associated with a higher risk of symptomatic radiation-induced lung injury [6]. Chen et al. reported higher IL-6 levels in patients who developed RP, whereas TNF-α was not associated with RP [7]. Changes in TGF-β1 during radiotherapy have also been associated with symptomatic RP in some studies [60]. However, other prospective studies did not confirm a consistent association between IL-6, TNF-α, or TGF-β1 and RP [61].

The inconsistent results may be related to differences in patient characteristics, treatment regimens, sampling time, and assay methods. Cytokine levels can also be affected by the tumor itself, infection, systemic inflammation, and concurrent treatment. In addition, circulating cytokines cannot identify the specific tissue or organ that has been injured. These factors limit the value of a single cytokine measurement for predicting radiation-induced injury. Serial measurements or biomarker panels combined with dosimetric factors may provide more useful information, but further validation is required [62].

microRNA (miRNA)

miRNAs are stable in blood and can change in response to radiation exposure. Several studies have therefore explored their use as biomarkers of radiation dose and biological injury. Acharya et al. identified early serum miRNA signatures associated with hematopoietic injury and survival after total-body irradiation in mice [63]. Song et al. identified a panel of radiation-responsive miRNAs in irradiated cells and human peripheral blood lymphocytes [64]. In mouse studies, leukocyte miR-150-5p and miR-342-3p also showed dose-dependent changes after irradiation [65].

More recent studies have provided early evidence in humans and localized injury models. A multicenter study of patients receiving total-body irradiation found that a serum miRNA signature could distinguish irradiated from non-irradiated samples and differentiate between higher and lower radiation doses [66]. In a mouse model of localized radiation injury, an eight-miRNA plasma panel was associated with radiation dose and injury severity [67]. However, most current evidence relates to radiation exposure, dose estimation, or hematopoietic injury. Evidence supporting miRNAs as predictors of organ-specific toxicity during radiotherapy remains limited.

The interpretation of circulating miRNAs is also affected by several factors. Blood cell counts and hemolysis can substantially alter the measured levels of some miRNAs [68]. Results may also vary with sample type, collection and processing procedures, extraction methods, and normalization strategies [69]. Tumor burden, inflammation, and other treatments may cause further changes. These factors make it difficult to reproduce a single miRNA signature across different populations (Table 2).

cfDNA as a biomarker for radiation-induced injury

Biological characteristics of cfDNA

cfDNA consists of short DNA fragments released into the circulation from damaged, dying, or stressed cells. During radiotherapy, cfDNA may originate from tumor cells, irradiated normal tissues, endothelial cells, immune cells, and hematopoietic cells [15–18]. These sources may contribute at the same time. Therefore, total cfDNA concentration alone cannot determine whether the detected DNA comes from the tumor or injured normal tissue.

cfDNA is rapidly degraded by nucleases and cleared by the liver, kidneys, and phagocytic cells. Its reported half-life ranges from several minutes to a few hours [15]. This rapid turnover allows serial sampling to capture short-term biological changes. Radiation exposure can increase cfDNA release through cellular stress, senescence, and cell death [16, 18, 23]. However, cfDNA concentration is also influenced by tumor burden, inflammation, physical activity, and sample processing. Concentration changes should therefore be interpreted together with the clinical setting and sampling time.

Most plasma cfDNA is associated with nucleosomes and shows a major fragment peak at approximately 166 bp. Fragment length, nucleosome footprints, and DNA end motifs reflect chromatin organization and nuclease processing [40, 41]. Recent fragmentomic studies have shown that these patterns also contain cell-type-specific information and can be used to estimate the cellular sources of plasma cfDNA [70]. Open chromatin footprints near transcription start sites provide another method for estimating the contribution of different tissues to circulating cfDNA [71].

DNA methylation provides complementary information about cell and tissue origin. Different cell types retain characteristic methylation patterns, which remain detectable in cfDNA. McNamara et al. used cell-type-specific methylation markers to assess radiation-related tissue damage. They detected cfDNA signals from pulmonary endothelial cells, cardiomyocytes, hepatocytes, and other normal cell types after irradiation [17]. Some signals changed with radiation dose and treatment time. More recent studies have also shown that cfDNA methylation can identify cell-specific tissue injury and track changes in the injured cell populations over time [72, 73].

Together, cfDNA concentration, fragment characteristics, and methylation patterns provide different types of information. Concentration reflects the overall amount of DNA released into the circulation. Fragmentation patterns reflect chromatin organization and DNA processing. Methylation patterns help identify the cell or tissue of origin. Combining these features may improve the monitoring and interpretation of radiation-induced injury.

Potential advantages of cfDNA for radiation-induced injury

Non-invasive and suitable for serial sampling

cfDNA can be analyzed using peripheral blood samples without the need for tissue biopsy. Blood samples can be collected repeatedly before, during, and after radiotherapy. This allows longitudinal assessment of biological changes throughout treatment and follow-up. Serial cfDNA analysis has been applied in patients receiving radiotherapy, supporting its feasibility for treatment monitoring [17, 74]. Repeated sampling can also reduce the influence of an isolated measurement. It may help reveal trends that are not apparent at a single time point.

Potential for dynamic monitoring

The short half-life of cfDNA allows its concentration and molecular features to change over relatively short periods. Serial measurements may capture the onset, progression, and recovery of radiation-related tissue injury. Experimental and clinical studies have shown time-dependent changes in cfDNA after irradiation, supporting its use for dynamic assessment [17, 75]. These changes may reflect the biological response to different radiation doses and treatment stages. They may also help identify whether tissue injury is transient or persistent.

Tissue-of-origin and molecular information

cfDNA provides information beyond its total concentration. Fragment length, nucleosome footprints, end motifs, and methylation patterns may reflect the cells or tissues that release DNA into the circulation [17, 21, 70, 71]. Tissue-specific methylation markers may help distinguish normal-tissue-derived cfDNA from tumor-derived signals and identify the organs affected by radiation. This is particularly useful when tumor response and normal-tissue injury occur at the same time. Combining different cfDNA features may improve the interpretation of mixed circulating signals. cfDNA detection and analytical technologies

Technologies for detecting circulating cfDNA have demonstrated broad potential in disease diagnosis, personalized care, and radiation injury research. The quantitative analysis of cfDNA is essential for tracking changes in plasma concentration, forming the basis for disease monitoring and radiation damage assessment. Droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR) enables absolute quantification without a standard curve. It is useful for detecting low-abundance variants, copy-number changes, and assessing cfDNA sample quality [76]. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) can quantify selected genomic regions and assess cfDNA integrity. Fluorescence-based assays provide a rapid and economical estimate of total cfDNA concentration. These methods can be used for serial cfDNA measurement during radiotherapy. Their performance depends on sample quality, DNA input, and assay design [77].

Analysis of cfDNA fragmentation patterns may provide information on the cell death processes induced by radiation. In apoptosis, chromatin is cleaved by intracellular nucleases into nucleosomal fragments, typically 160–170 base pairs, while necrotic cell death produces longer, irregular fragments due to structural breakdown rather than orderly cleavage [40, 42]. Chromatin organization influences this fragmentation pattern: DNA wrapped around nucleosomes is protected, while linker DNA is more susceptible to cleavage, resulting in characteristic fragment distributions that may reflect chromatin structure and transcriptional activity in the cells of origin [78, 79].Next-generation sequencing (NGS) can analyze fragment length, end motifs, nucleosome footprints, and genome-wide coverage patterns. Recent studies have shown that these fragmentomic features contain cell-type-specific information and may help estimate the cellular and tissue sources of circulating cfDNA [70, 71]. Fragmentation patterns can also be used to infer local DNA methylation states [80].

The methylation status of cfDNA reflects the epigenetic characteristics of its source cells and serves as a key tool for studying gene regulation and disease-related changes. In radiation-induced injury, tissue-specific methylation patterns may help identify the cellular or tissue origin of cfDNA. DNA methylation detection methods can be categorized into non-sequencing and sequencing methods, depending on whether sequencing is required. Each method has distinct characteristics, making it suitable for different research needs (Fig. 3).

Non-sequencing methods primarily use specific chemical or enzymatic reactions to indirectly detect methylation status, generally offering lower costs and simpler operations, making them suitable for high-throughput screening. MSRE-qPCR employs methylation-sensitive restriction endonucleases to selectively identify methylated regions, combined with subsequent quantitative PCR for high-sensitivity detection[81]. However, this technology has limited coverage due to its dependence on restriction sites. In contrast, methylation-specific PCR (MSP) utilizes methylation- and non-methylation-specific primers for amplification following bisulfite treatment, enabling low-cost methylation-specific detection [82]. However, this method is semi-quantitative, and amplification bias may lead to skewed results[83]. The Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip is a high-throughput array that covers hundreds of thousands of predefined CpG sites [84]. The resulting β-values provide quantitative estimates of methylation levels. However, the fixed probe design limits the analysis to CpG sites included on the array [85]. EpiTYPER combines bisulfite conversion and mass spectrometry to provide quantitative methylation analysis of selected CpG sites or CpG units [86]. This method has good reproducibility and supports multi-site analysis, but it requires a relatively complex workflow and specialized mass spectrometry equipment.

Sequencing methods are characterized by high resolution and broad genomic coverage, making them suitable for in-depth research and the discovery of novel biomarkers, though they are costly and require stringent experimental conditions. Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) remains a widely used reference method for genome-wide methylation analysis and can provide single-base resolution at sufficiently covered CpG sites. However, its high cost and sequencing requirements limit its broader application [87]. Reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) significantly lowers sequencing costs by using restriction enzyme digestion to enrich CpG island regions, focusing on the analysis of promoters and CpG-rich regions. However, it has limited coverage, and enzyme digestion bias may result in regional loss [88]. Targeted bisulfite sequencing is highly specific and flexible for studying particular genes or regions, making it suitable for validating candidate regions, though it cannot provide whole-genome information [89]. Methylated DNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeDIP-seq) uses anti-5-methylcytosine antibodies to enrich methylated DNA fragments, offering the advantages of relatively low cost and a simple workflow. However, it has lower resolution and limited sensitivity for low-methylated regions [90]. MBDCap-seq uses methyl-CpG-binding domain proteins to enrich DNA fragments containing methylated CpG sites. It offers broad regional coverage but lacks single-base resolution and has lower sensitivity in regions with low methylation levels [91, 92]. Another emerging technology is nanopore sequencing, which does not require bisulfite treatment and directly detects DNA methylation, enabling real-time analysis and long-read sequencing. However, its performance depends on DNA input, sequencing depth, and methylation-calling algorithms [93].

cfDNA detection technologies have developed a multi-tiered detection system, encompassing concentration quantification, fragmentation patterns, and methylation analysis. These technologies elucidate the biological significance of cfDNA and may also support the assessment of radiation-induced damage. By integrating high-sensitivity quantification methods, fragmentomics, and epigenetic analysis, cfDNA detection may provide a technological foundation for the dynamic monitoring of radiation damage (Table 3).

Applications of cfDNA in various fields

As a non-invasive biomarker, circulating cfDNA has shown considerable promise across many medical fields. Its use in early cancer screening and diagnosis has garnered significant interest in recent years. Research indicates that cfDNA, particularly ctDNA, can provide diagnostic and prognostic information and may complement conventional detection methods [94]. cfDNA analysis has also shown value in guiding cancer treatment and monitoring treatment resistance [94]. In small-cell lung cancer, Behrouzi et al. reviewed cfDNA and extrachromosomal DNA profiling and highlighted their potential for characterizing molecular subtypes, tumor evolution, and treatment resistance [95]. Recent studies also suggest that cfDNA can reflect tumor changes over time, assess recurrence risk, and support personalized treatment strategies. In advanced ovarian cancer, Llueca et al. evaluated qPCR- and digital PCR-based liquid biopsy for assessing complete cytoreduction and monitoring recurrence [96]. In NSCLC, Thanou et al. analyzed NALCN promoter methylation in tumor tissue and found that it was associated with metastatic risk, highlighting its potential as a novel NSCLC biomarker [97].

cfDNA also shows promise in tracking tissue-specific damage and assessing disease severity. Research indicates that cfDNA methylation markers may help identify tissue- and cell-type-specific injury. For instance, alveolar epithelial cell-derived cfDNA levels were strongly associated with disease severity, lung injury, and in-hospital outcomes in patients with COVID-19. The study used cell-type-specific methylation markers to trace cfDNA released from lung and immune cells, highlighting the potential of cfDNA for assessing tissue injury at the cell-type level [72]. In radiation injury, cfDNA has shown broad potential. Zwirner et al. studied cfDNA changes in patients undergoing radiochemotherapy and found associations between cfDNA levels and markers of infection and inflammation, suggesting that cfDNA may help distinguish infection-driven from treatment-related inflammation [98]. In another study on acute brain injury, Regner et al. reviewed evidence showing that circulating nucleic acid levels were associated with injury severity, mortality, and prognosis. They also discussed the potential use of real-time qPCR for rapid injury assessment in emergency settings [99]. Okunieff et al. further suggested that cfDNA concentration could serve as a biodosimeter for rapidly grading radiation or nuclear exposure. Their work in non-human primates demonstrated that cfDNA concentration increased exponentially with radiation dose, supporting its further development for rapid exposure assessment [23]. In patients undergoing radiotherapy for prostate cancer, pretreatment plasma cfDNA levels measured using RadTox were associated with both acute and late grade 2 gastrointestinal toxicity. The AUC was 0.89 for acute toxicity at a cutoff of 44 ng/mL and 0.88 for late toxicity at a cutoff of 54 ng/mL, highlighting its potential as a toxicity biomarker [22]. Similarly, Cho et al. showed that cfDNA-derived chromosomal instability scores decreased after radiotherapy in many patients. Their findings support the potential use of cfDNA I-scores for monitoring radiotherapy response and detecting minimal residual disease [100].

McNamara et al. utilized a CpG-enriched DNA panel to capture cfDNA fragments from serum samples and mapped them to cell-type-specific methylation reference atlases. In patients with breast cancer, these methylation patterns revealed dose-dependent and tissue-specific responses across multiple organs. Increased hepatocyte- and liver endothelial cell-derived cfDNA was detected in patients receiving radiotherapy for right-sided breast cancer [17]. In a mouse model of thoracic irradiation, the authors also observed dose-related increases in cfDNA derived from pulmonary endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes. More recently, Yuan et al. evaluated organ-specific cfDNA methylation before and after proton or photon craniospinal irradiation in a six-patient pediatric case series. Changes in thyroid, lung, kidney, and intestinal epithelial cfDNA signals broadly paralleled organ dose patterns and clinical findings, providing preliminary evidence for multi-organ injury assessment during radiotherapy [101]. Taken together, these findings support further evaluation of cfDNA methylation for tracking tissue-specific damage during radiotherapy.

Overall, cfDNA shows potential for the early assessment and longitudinal monitoring of radiation-induced tissue injury. Further clinical validation is required before routine use.

Challenges and future perspectives

Current limitations

Although cfDNA has shown significant potential in liquid biopsy, early screening, and treatment monitoring, several challenges still limit its clinical application. These challenges arise at the biological, technical, and clinical levels. They continue to limit standardization and broader clinical use.

At the biological level, elevated cfDNA is not specific to radiation injury. It may also occur during inflammation, infection, ischemic injury, and cell death in tumors or normal tissues [15, 49, 102]. Furthermore, different tissues exhibit significant variations in their contribution to circulating cfDNA, influenced by anatomical differences, cell turnover, and blood flow perfusion [70]. This tissue-specific release bias further complicates the interpretation of cfDNA levels in clinical settings. More critically, background interference factors such as infection and intense exercise can significantly elevate cfDNA concentrations, impairing its specificity in assessing radiation-induced injury [102, 103]. Surgery and systemic treatments, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy, may also alter cfDNA concentrations or molecular patterns [104–107]. These treatment-related changes, together with tumor burden and changes in circulating blood-cell populations, may confound the interpretation of cfDNA signals during radiotherapy [15, 70].

From a technical standpoint, cfDNA detection is highly susceptible to interference from sample collection and processing procedures. Factors such as anticoagulant selection (e.g., EDTA and heparin), time to plasma separation, centrifugation conditions, and storage temperature can substantially affect cfDNA integrity and concentration [108, 109]. Additionally, cfDNA's low concentrations and short fragment sizes (most < 200 bp) make it particularly vulnerable to contamination with genomic DNA (gDNA) released during cell lysis, especially from white blood cells, which release large amounts of long DNA fragments that interfere with subsequent analysis [109]. Moreover, the variability in extraction methods, quantification strategies, and analytical algorithms across different studies or laboratories leads to a lack of comparability between results, representing a major barrier to the widespread clinical adoption of cfDNA [108, 110].

In clinical applications, cfDNA lacks standardized reference ranges and cutoff thresholds. Many studies report relative changes or use study-specific thresholds, making it difficult to establish consistent criteria for clinical interpretation [22, 110]. Furthermore, cfDNA levels may be influenced by individual factors such as BMI, age, sex, and tumor burden, resulting in substantial variation in baseline concentrations [111–114]. This variability complicates the direct use of cfDNA measurements to classify or grade the severity of radiation-induced injury. Consequently, multi-omics strategies that combine cfDNA with other biomarkers, such as proteins, RNA, and metabolites, are increasingly being explored to provide complementary information for assessment and monitoring [115].

Emerging solutions and future directions

To address the biological, technical, and practical challenges faced by cfDNA in clinical applications, current research has proposed several promising solutions and optimization strategies, including multi-omics integration, fragmentomics analysis, and artificial intelligence-assisted analysis, aimed at improving the sensitivity, specificity, and biological interpretability of cfDNA testing.

The adoption of a multi-omics integration strategy shows potential in overcoming the stability and specificity limitations of single-feature cfDNA analysis. By integrating cfDNA with cell-free RNA (cfRNA), miRNA, exosomal proteins, and metabolomic data, composite molecular feature models can be constructed to improve the robustness and biological interpretation of disease-related signals. Tivey et al. emphasized that combining cfDNA with other nucleic acid omics may improve the accuracy of early cancer detection [116]. Nguyen et al. showed that integrating methylomic and fragmentomic features improved multi-cancer detection and tissue-of-origin classification [117]. Additionally, Zhao et al. developed the Clinic-RadmC model by integrating clinical variables, deep learning-based CT radiomics, and cfDNA end-motif features from 5mC-enriched regions. It outperformed single-omics and clinical models in classifying indeterminate pulmonary nodules [118]. These findings illustrate the value of combining cfDNA with other molecular and imaging features.

Furthermore, fragmentomics analysis represents one of the most promising directions in cfDNA research. The length distribution, end motifs, and nucleosome positioning patterns of cfDNA may reflect chromatin organization and tissue-of-origin information [70, 119]. Qi et al. highlighted that analyzing cfDNA fragment length and positioning features provides biological information for tissue tracing and disease assessment [119]. Liu summarized multidimensional fragmentomic strategies, such as the windowed protection score (WPS) and DNA evaluation of fragments for early interception (DELFI), and emphasized their potential in non-invasive detection [120]. Cao et al. showed that multidimensional fragmentomics combined with modeling improved the performance of early colorectal cancer detection [121]. These advances provide a methodological basis for future studies of radiation-induced injury.

Given the current landscape, artificial intelligence-assisted modeling is rapidly becoming an important driver of cfDNA research. As the complexity of cfDNA data increases, machine-learning approaches may facilitate the integration of high-dimensional and multimodal cfDNA data. Recent reviews have highlighted the use of machine learning and deep learning to integrate cfDNA fragmentomic, methylation, and other molecular features for disease classification, fetal DNA fraction estimation, and tissue-of-origin analysis [122, 123]. Additionally, Vavoulis et al. developed a novel multimodal whole-genome TAPS approach that integrates genomic and methylomic information and applied machine-learning models for cancer detection [124]. Wang et al. proposed a standardized fragmentomics feature extraction workflow to provide consistent input for AI models, addressing reproducibility issues across studies [125].

In transitioning cfDNA from a research tool to clinical application, future research directions and clinical translation priorities should include establishing standardized processes, strengthening clinical validation, and fully integrating cfDNA into precision radiotherapy. Standardization is essential for clinical implementation, as significant differences in cfDNA testing processes across centers can impact the accuracy and comparability of results. Therefore, it is strongly recommended to establish standard operating procedures (SOPs) for sample collection, reagent selection, quality control, and reporting standards. Furthermore, constructing a cfDNA reference control library and methylation reference map can improve data calibration accuracy and comparability, thereby ensuring the consistency of the testing platform.

Additionally, clinical validation is a critical step in achieving the translational application of cfDNA. Prospective, multicenter cohort studies are encouraged to assess the feasibility of using cfDNA to guide individualized toxicity prediction, dynamic efficacy monitoring, or mid-treatment adjustments. Future studies should use predefined longitudinal sampling schedules, including baseline, selected time points during radiotherapy, treatment completion, and post-treatment follow-up [126, 127]. Sampling schedules should be tailored to the radiation fractionation and treatment modality, including conventional fractionation, stereotactic regimens, proton therapy, and re-irradiation. Existing longitudinal studies provide initial examples for high-dose radiotherapy, proton or photon craniospinal irradiation, and re-irradiation [101, 126, 127]. Incorporating dynamic cfDNA changes into trial designs could provide clinical evidence for adaptive radiotherapy strategies.

Overall, cfDNA has the potential to connect biological responses with dosimetric and clinical information in precision radiotherapy. In future studies, tissue-specific cfDNA signals could be evaluated alongside dose–volume parameters and NTCP models to determine whether they provide complementary biological information [101, 128]. Future multicenter prospective studies should validate standardized sampling and analytical workflows across different treatment settings. With further clinical validation and multi-omics integration, cfDNA may become a useful tool for the early detection, longitudinal monitoring, and individualized management of radiation-induced injury, thereby supporting more biologically informed precision radiotherapy.
