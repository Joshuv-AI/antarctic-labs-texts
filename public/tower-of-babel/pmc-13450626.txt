Biogenic Nickel Ferrite Nanoparticles Synthesized From Agro-Waste: Green Synthesis, Physicochemical Characterization, and Potential for Magnetic Hyperthermia Applications
Authors: Małgorzata Góral-Kowalczyk, Elżbieta Grządka, Jolanta Orzeł, Dariusz Góral, Tomasz Skrzypek, Zbigniew Kobus, Iryna Koval, Andrzej Marczuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNickel ferrite (NiFe2O4) nanoparticles are promising magnetic hyperthermia agents, but conventional synthesis relies on toxic reagents. Plant-extract-mediated biogenic synthesis offers an eco-friendly alternative; however, the influence of specific agro-waste phytochemical matrices and precursor chemistry on nanoparticle structure and heating efficiency remains poorly characterized.MethodsAqueous extracts from pomegranate peel (Punica granatum L) and raspberry leaves/shoots (Rubus idaeus L) were characterized for total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), and DPPH radical scavenging activity. Cherry stalk extract showed insufficient reactivity and was excluded. Two precursor systems — nitrate (Fe(NO3)3/Ni(NO3)2) and acetate–sulfate (Ni(CH3COO)2/FeSO4) — were combined with each extract, yielding four nanoparticle batches (M1, M2, G1, G2) after calcination at 500°C for 4 h. Nanoparticles were characterized by XRD, XRF, FTIR, TEM, DLS, and zeta potential. Magnetic heating performance was assessed under AMF (532.4 kHz, 3.1 kA·m−1); SAR and ILP were calculated with ferrite-mass correction based on XRF data.ResultsPomegranate extract showed the highest phytochemical activity (DPPH: 1283.9 μM TE·g−1). XRD confirmed the spinel NiFe2O4 structure in all batches, with crystallite sizes of 15.0–21.8 nm and lattice parameters of 8.35–8.38 Å. The acetate–sulfate batches (M2, G2) contained substantially higher Fe/Ni fractions (M2: Fe 49.0%, Ni 11.5%) than the nitrate batches (M1, G1: Fe ≤2.9%, Ni ≤1.7%). DLS indicated hydrodynamic diameters of 200–400 nm; zeta potential values (−14.0 to +7.8 mV) indicated limited colloidal stability. Under AMF, M2 and G2 reached maximum temperatures of 46.0°C and 43.4°C (baseline 31°C), with SAR of 15.7 and 15.0 W·g−1 and ILP of 3.08 and 2.93 nH·m2·kg−1, respectively. Sample M1 showed negligible heating (SAR 0.93 W·g−1; ΔT =1.1°C).DiscussionPrecursor chemistry critically determines ferrite yield and heating efficiency, with the acetate–sulfate system consistently outperforming the nitrate system. The obtained ILP values are comparable to literature reports for NiFe2O4 under similar AMF conditions. However, the observed aggregation and low zeta potential limit biomedical applicability. As no cytotoxicity or in vitro/in vivo data were obtained, these findings represent proof-of-concept for the thermal conversion potential of biogenically synthesized NiFe2O4, pending biological validation.Keywords: spinel ferrite, specific absorption rate, SAR, intrinsic loss power, ILP, colloidal stability, phytochemical reduction

Introduction

Nickel ferrite belongs to the spinel ferrite group with the general formula M(II)Fe2O4. The spinel structure MFe2O4 consists of one divalent cation (M2⁺) and two Fe3⁺ ions distributed between tetrahedral and octahedral sites. The arrangement of which depends on whether the material adopts a normal or inverse spinel configuration. The characteristic distribution of metal ions in the crystal structure, with iron as a trivalent cation, imparts them semiconductor properties.1

Thanks to their magnetic properties, they find use in microwave devices, ferrite cores, gas sensors, ferrite memories, filtration processes and magnetic resonance. They also act as catalysts in various biological reactions and enable targeted drug delivery and cancer treatment.2,3

The use of food industry by-products to produce nanoparticles is a little-explored and promising area in nanotechnology. This approach is in line with the principles of green synthesis, which aims to create nanoparticles in an environmentally friendly manner, reduce toxic waste, and use natural resources.4–6 Agro-food waste and by-products of the food industry are available in large quantities, inexpensive or free, and environmentally friendly, making them ideal candidates for nanoparticle biosynthesis.7,8

The synthesis of nanoparticles using plant extracts has emerged as a promising and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional chemical methods. Among the diverse array of plant materials, the pomegranate peel extract has been the subject of extensive research due to its dual role as a reducing and stabilizing agent in the synthesis of various nanoparticles. For instance, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have been effectively synthesized using pomegranate peel extract,9,10 as well as bimetallic nanoparticles (P-Cu/F),11 and gold nanoparticles (AuNPs).12,13 In the case of raspberries, an aqueous extract of raspberry leaves was used as a reducing agent and stabilizer in the synthesis of AgNPs.14,15 A review of the scientific literature reveals a paucity of reports on the use of cherry stalks in the synthesis of green nanoparticles. However, other parts of cherries, such as seeds and pulp, have been used in the biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNP) and silver sulfide (Ag2S).16

Magnetic hyperthermia is a thermotherapeutic strategy in which magnetic nanoparticles, subjected to an alternating magnetic field (AMF), convert electromagnetic energy into heat through Néel relaxation and Brownian motion mechanisms. The heating efficiency of a nanoparticle system is commonly quantified by the specific absorption rate (SAR) and the field-independent intrinsic loss power (ILP = SAR/(f · H2)), which normalizes performance across different AMF conditions and enables meaningful cross-study comparisons.17 Both parameters are strongly governed by intrinsic material properties, including crystallite size and size distribution, crystal structure perfection, magnetic anisotropy energy, and saturation magnetization (Ms). In the single-domain size regime, which for spinel ferrites typically spans from approximately 10 to 30 nm, Néel relaxation dominates and SAR is particularly sensitive to particle size and anisotropy.18 Colloidal stability further modulates heating performance in suspension: aggregation reduces the effective surface area, restricts Brownian motion, and can substantially lower the measured SAR relative to the intrinsic magnetic properties of the individual nanoparticles. Consequently, achieving high and reproducible heating efficiency requires simultaneous control of crystal phase purity, particle size, and colloidal dispersion state.17

Beyond physical heating performance, translation of magnetic nanoparticles to biomedical hyperthermia applications imposes stringent biological requirements. Candidate materials must demonstrate cytocompatibility and absence of intrinsic cytotoxicity at relevant concentrations, as well as adequate dispersibility in physiological media (eg, phosphate-buffered saline, cell culture medium, blood plasma) without rapid agglomeration or protein-corona-induced sequestration.19 Sufficient colloidal stability under physiological ionic strength and pH conditions is therefore a prerequisite for both effective heat delivery and systemic or local administration.19 It is important to distinguish between demonstrating thermal conversion potential (ie, the ability of a material to generate heat under AMF) and demonstrating therapeutic efficacy, which requires evidence of cytotoxic or tumoricidal effects in biological models.20 The former can be established through calorimetric measurements of SAR and ILP, whereas the latter necessitates in vitro cell viability assays, assessment of hyperthermia-induced apoptosis, and ultimately in vivo validation.20 The present study focuses on the former: characterizing the synthesis, structure, and heating performance of biogenically produced NiFe2O4 nanoparticles as a necessary precondition for future biological evaluation.

The aim of this study was to develop a sustainable and phytochemically assisted method to synthesize nickel ferrite (NiFe2O4) nanoparticles using plant extracts derived from agricultural residues. The novelty of this work lies in demonstrating that these specific and previously unexplored plant matrices can effectively drive the formation of the spinel ferrite structure and in elucidating how their distinct phytochemical profiles influence the crystallinity, elemental composition, and magnetic performance of the resulting nanoparticles. The emphasis was placed on evaluating their potential application in biomedical energy technologies, specifically magnetic hyperthermia for cancer treatment. Scientifically, this study provides new insights into the relationship between plant-derived reducing agents, precursor chemistry, and the functional properties of biogenically synthesized ferrites, offering a scalable and environmentally friendly route toward materials suitable for biomedical applications such as magnetic hyperthermia.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The study utilized properly dried raspberry leaves and shoots (Rubus idaeus L, Poland), dried cut pomegranate peel (Punica granatum L, Turkey), and cherry stalks (Prunus avium L, Bulgaria). The plant substrates were obtained from commercial sources (dried raspberry leaves and shoots – Natura Wita Sp. Zoo, Kopernika 9, 28–400 Pińczów, Poland; dried cut pomegranate peel – Planteon PW Józef Szwajkowski, Borków Stary 11, 62–817 Żelazków, Poland and cherry stalks – Proherbis Jarosław Wolański, Zarzecze 294, 38–220 Dębowiec, Poland). The selection of plant raw materials, which are waste from the agro-food industry and used to prepare NiFe2O4, nanoparticles, was based on ease of availability and their bioactive properties.

Chemicals

All reagents were of analytical grade and were used without further purification. The following chemicals and their specifications were used in the synthesis:

Iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O) – pure p.a, purity ≥ 98%, supplier: Chempur, Gliwice, Poland

Nickel(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O) – analytical grade, purity ≥ 98%, supplier: Warchem, Warsaw, Poland

Iron(II) sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·7H2O) – pure p a, analytical grade, supplier: Chempur, Gliwice, Poland

Nickel(II) acetate tetrahydrate (Ni(CH3COO)2·4H2O) – purity ≥ 98%, supplier: Warchem, Warsaw, Poland

Distilled/deionized water was used for all preparations.

Methanol (99.8%, POOH, Gliwice, Poland).

Methods

Analysis of Bioactive Substances

Two distinct extraction methodologies were employed using methanol and water as solvents to obtain bioactive compounds from the plant materials (pomegranate peel, raspberry leaves and shoots, and cherry stalks).

Methanol Extraction

For methanol extraction, two grams of dried and milled plant material were mixed with 25 mL of methanol (99.8%, POOH, Gliwice, Poland) in a 100 mL Erlenmeyer flask. The suspension was heated to 30°C and stirred magnetically for 2 h.21,22 After decanting, the solid residue was extracted again with 25 mL of methanol under the same conditions. The combined extracts were subsequently centrifuged at 6500 rpm for 15 min at 20°C and used immediately for analysis.

Water Extraction

For water extraction, different masses of ground raw material were mixed with 25 mL of water in a 100 mL conical flask (2 g for pomegranate peels, 2 g for cherry stems, and 1 g for raspberry leaves). The mixture was first heated to 60°C and stirred on a magnetic stirrer for 3 h. The extract obtained was transferred to a separate flask, and the remaining raffinate was again mixed with 25 mL of water. The extraction process was repeated for another 2 h at 30°C using a magnetic stirrer. The extract from this second stage was combined with the first portion, and the entire mixture was centrifuged at 6500 rpm for 20 min.21–23

Total Polyphenol Content (TPC)

The total phenolic content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method,24 following methodologies described by Krzywicka et al,21 with a slight modification An appropriate amount of the extract was mixed with 2 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent in a 25 mL flask. Specific extract volumes were: 40 μL for pomegranate peels and 200 μL for raspberry leaves and cherry stems (methanol extracts); or 100 μL for raspberry leaves and cherry stems, and 100 μL of pomegranate peels (previously diluted 1:9 with water) (water extracts). After three minutes, 2 mL of sodium carbonate was added, and the volume was increased to 25 mL with distilled water. After a subsequent 30 min of incubation in darkness, the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of λ=760 nm. Results were expressed as mg of gallic acid equivalent per 1 g of dry material (mg GAE g−1 dm).

The Total Flavonoid Content (TFC)

Total flavonoid content was determined according to methodologies described by Kobus et al,22 and Chang et al,25 with a slight modification An appropriate portion of the extract was combined with 1 mL of 2% AlCl3 ⋅6H2O solution (in methanol). Specific extract volumes were: 100 μL for pomegranate peels, 500 μL for raspberry leaves, and 1000 μL for cherry stems (methanol extracts); or 500 μL for raspberry leaves, and 2000 μL for cherry stems, and 500 μL of pomegranate peels (previously diluted 1:9 with water) (water extracts). The resulting mixture was then brought to a volume of 10 mL with distilled water before being subjected to 10 min of incubation under dark and room temperature conditions. Following incubation, the absorbance of the mixture was evaluated at 430 nm. Flavonoid concentrations were evaluated in relation to a calibration curve prepared with quercetin and reported as mg of quercetin equivalent per 1 g of dry matter (mg QE g−1 dm). Measurement was carried out in three replicates for each sample.

DPPH Assay

The determination of free radical scavenging activity was performed according to the methodology described by Kobus et al,22 and Prior et al,26 with a slight modification Before analysis, the extracts were diluted as follows: 1:20 for pomegranate peels and 1:1 for the raspberry leaves and cherry stems (methanol extracts), 1:40 for pomegranate peels and 1:4 for raspberry leaves, while the cherry stem extracts were used without dilution (water extracts). Next, 60 μL of each diluted extract was mixed with an aliquot of 5 mL of freshly prepared 6⋅10−5 M DPPH radical in methanol. After 30 min of incubation in darkness, absorbance was measured at a wavelength of λ=516 nm. Measurement was carried out in three replicates for each sample. Antioxidant activity was expressed as a Trolox equivalent in μg per g of dry matter (μM TE/g dm).

Method of Obtaining Nanoparticles

Nanoparticles were synthesized by a phytochemically assisted thermal decomposition method, following procedures previously developed for biogenic iron oxide nanoparticles,5,6 with modifications to accommodate the bimetallic Ni–Fe precursor system.

Two precursor systems were prepared:

Nitrate system (for M1 and G1): Fe(NO3)3 9H2O (17.24 g) and Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (6.20 g) precursors were dissolved in deionized water (25 mL) and stirred with a magnetic stirrer at a constant temperature of 60°C for 30 min, forming a transparent solution of [Ni2+] = 6260 ppm and [Fe3+] =11930 ppm.

Acetate–sulfate system (for M2 and G2): was prepared by dissolving (CH3COO)2Ni∙4H2O (5.31 g) and FeSO4∙7H2O (11.09 g) together in 25 mL deionized water and stirred at 60°C for 30 min.

Fifty milliliters of the preheated (60°C) plant extract were added dropwise to each precursor solution under continuous stirring.5,6 The mixture was maintained at 60°C for 3 h. Samples M2 and G2 turned black, while samples M1 and G1 turned dark brown-green. Two control samples (M0 and G0) were prepared analogously without precursor salts. Sample coding is presented in Table 1. Suspensions were centrifuged twice at 7500 rpm for 20 min at room temperature, washed with deionized water, and dried on a hot plate until water evaporated completely. Dried materials were transferred into porcelain crucibles and calcined in a laboratory furnace (LAC LE09/11, Czech Republic) at 500°C for 4 h in air, with a heating rate of 5°C/min. Samples were allowed to cool inside the closed furnace to room temperature. The crystalline powder was transferred to 2 mL Eppendorf test tubes and analyzed.

Physicochemical Analyses

X-Ray Fluorescence (XRF)

Energy-dispersive X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF) was used to analyze the content of heavy metals in the samples.27 The XRF is equipped with a 600 W X-ray tube with a gadolinium anode, powered by a voltage of up to 100 kV. A germanium detector (with a beryllium window) with ultra-high resolution (below 140 eV) cooled with liquid nitrogen is used to measure fluorescence radiation. After weighing, the samples were placed in measuring cups with supporting foil (Mylar foil). All measurements were performed using the Auto Quantify standard-free method using the ED-XRF Epsilon 5 (Panalytical, The Netherlands) using Genie 2000 software.

Analysis Using a Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM)

The samples were applied to formvar grids and examined using a TEM microscope (Zeiss LIBRA, Oberkochen, Germany) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV, with 50 kx magnification.28,29

Particle Size Distribution

Nanoparticles were dispersed at 0.1 mg·mL−1 in deionized water, sonicated for 1 min (Elmasonic Easy 30 H), and measured at 25°C at a backscattering angle of 173° (Malvern Zetasizer NanoZS). Six independent measurements were recorded for each sample.30

Zeta Potential Analysis

The zeta potential was analyzed using a NanoZS zetasizer (Malvern Instruments, Ltd, GB). The electrophoretic mobility of the sample was measured and recalculated into the zeta potential using the Smoluchowski equation. The universal dip cell, including the electrode assembly with palladium electrodes with 2 mm spacing, was used in the measurements. All experiments were performed at 25°C. No external pH adjustment was performed. The pH values reported reflect the natural pH of each nanoparticle suspension, resulting from the specific synthesis method and the chemical composition of the precursors. Each measurement was repeated six times and the values were averaged.31

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR)

The microstructure was investigated using the FT-IR technique on an Infrared Microscope AIM-9000 (Shimadzu, Kioto, Japan) using an IRTracer-100+QATR 10 with diamond prisms. A resolution of 4 cm −1 and an arithmetic average of 64 scans were used in the wavenumber range of 4700–400 cm −1. The nanoparticle powder obtained by crystallization was measured immediately after cooling to 20°C. Before the actual measurement, the background was measured. The powder was placed on the surface of the ATR head, and the crystal was pressed down. The absorbance spectrum was recorded in Excel as a function of wave number (cm −1).29

X-Ray Diffraction Analysis (XRD)

The measurements carried out were performed according to the following method in triplicate. Powder diffraction data were collected at room temperature using an Empyrean diffractometer with a PIXcel3D detector (PANalytical, Almelo, The Netherlands) and monochromated Cu–Kα radiation (λ = 1.54184 Å), in the 2θ range of 4.7 to 50°.32,33

Magnetic Properties

Control samples M0 and G0 containing pure plant extract and samples were measured: M1, M2, G1, and G2. Each sample was suspended in deionized water (density 0.997 g∙mL−1) and subjected to an electromagnetic field. Before analysis, the nanoparticles were shaken on a Vortex shaker and then briefly exposed to ultrasound (1 min) using an Elmasonic Easy 30 H ultrasound bath (Lomza, Poland). A magneTherm™ system (Nanotherics Ltd, Staffordshire, UK) was used to generate AMF.20 A standard system containing 9 electromagnet coils was used, which are capable of generating AMFs up to 25 mT in the range of 100 kHz - 1 MHz (Figure 1 and Table 2).34–36

Results

Content of Bioactive Substances

An important role in green synthesis technology is played by the bioactive substances contained in plants. Bioactive substances such as polyphenols (eg, flavonoids), organic acids (e.g, ascorbic acid and carboxylic acids), terpenoids, or carbonyl compounds (aldehydes and ketones) reduce metal salt complexes to nanoparticles. Flavonoids and polyphenols react particularly strongly with metal ions.39 It is known that food industry waste products can contain large amounts of biologically active compounds.23,40 Phenolic compounds and flavonoids act as natural reducers of metal ions to metallic or oxide forms, allowing the formation of nanoparticles. The antioxidant activity of an extract, measured by the DPPH method, can indirectly indicate the presence of compounds with a high reduction potential. Although the DPPH method does not directly measure the ability to reduce metal ions, a strong ability to neutralize free radicals often correlates with the efficiency of such reduction reactions.41

The total content of polyphenols (TPC), flavonoids (TFC), and the DPPH radical scavenging activity were tested to assess the potential of the waste materials for reducing iron and nickel salts. The results obtained are shown in Table 3.

The Content of Phytochemicals in the Plant Materials Used in the Study and Their Antioxidant Activity

The most bioactive compounds were observed in the pomegranate peel. This is consistent with numerous studies.42,43 According to Mashkor and Muhson,42 the total phenol content (TPC) of the pomegranate peel ranged from 84.15 to 168.26 mg GAE∙g−1. The results obtained in the present study fall within the stated range. The high content of phenolic compounds is the main factor determining the peel’s strong antioxidant properties, as evidenced by the significant DPPH radical scavenging activity, which reached a value of 1283.85 μM TE∙g−1. The differences between the results obtained and the data reported by other authors can be explained by the use of different extraction methods, types of solvent, and specific conditions of the extraction process. However, this does not change the fact that DPPH values in the literature are also high.44,45

Raspberry leaves are also an important source of polyphenols with documented antioxidant properties. The literature indicates a wide variation in the TPC content of raspberry leaves. Some studies report values ranging from 4.8 to 12.0 mg GAE∙g−1 of plant extract,46 while the homogenization and ultrasound-assisted extraction method used by Yang et al,47 yielded values in the range of 13630 to 14051 mg GAE∙g−1. Another study by Salević et al,48 reported a value of 7780 mg GAE∙g−1 The present study produced a lower TPC value (10.19 mg GAE∙g−1), which can be attributed to the composition of the mixture containing stems in addition to leaves, rather than leaves alone. Therefore, it is probable that the inclusion of stems, which contain much lower amounts of bioactive substances, contributed to the lower observed TPC values. Analogous discrepancies were observed in the case of flavonoid content. In our study, the content was 3.026 mg QE∙g−1. Varzaru et al,49 recorded a value of 106 mg QE∙g−1. Similar discrepancies are also found in the analysis of antioxidant activity, measured by the DPPH method.

Another raw material studied was cherry stalks. Due to the lack of data in the literature on cherry stalks, the results were indicatively referred to cherry leaves, which also contain phenolic compounds. The results obtained, including the total polyphenol content (6.047 mg GAE∙g−1), the total flavonoid content (1.065 mg QE∙g−1) and the antioxidant activity measured by the DPPH method (38.894 μM TE∙g−1), are within the range of values reported in the literature for cherry leaves.50 The TPC of cherry leaves generally ranges from about 4 to 8 mg GAE∙g−1, while the TFC oscillates between 0.8 and 1.2 mg QE∙g−1. Similarly, the antioxidant activity determined by the DPPH method is typically of the order of 30–50 μM TE∙g−1.50

In conclusion, pomegranate peel was found to have the highest content of phenolic compounds and flavonoids and the highest antioxidant activity. Raspberry leaves and stems have moderate values for all parameters, which means that they can serve as a reductant and stabilizers in nanoparticle synthesis, although probably with lower efficiency than that of pomegranate peel. In contrast, the cherry stalk extract showed the lowest potential for metal ion reduction and nanoparticle stabilization. For this reason, the synthesis process will be less efficient or require additional modifications, as confirmed in subsequent studies.

Synthesis of Nickel-Ferrite Nanoparticles

In this study, nickel ferrite nanoparticles were synthesized through phytochemically assisted decomposition of nickel and iron precursors and subsequent formation of the oxide spinel structure, in which Ni2⁺ and Fe3⁺ ions are incorporated. The precursor Fe(NO3)3·9H2O dissolved in water dissociates to Fe3⁺ ions. Fe3⁺ ions in solution usually give a yellowish-brown color. On the contrary, Ni(NO3)2∙6H2O in water mainly forms a complex (green color of the solution). In a mixture of Fe3⁺ and Ni2⁺, the color can take on greenish-brown or olive-like hues depending on the molar ratio.51 The combination of extracts obtained from raspberry leaves and shoots and pomegranate peel with precursors resulted in a marked change in the color of the reaction mixture, from the typical color of an extract of a given plant material, to a dark color (brownish green or almost black). This indicated the initiation of nanoparticle formation.52

In the case of cherry stalks, the interaction with the nickel-iron precursor appeared to be minimal. The reaction mixture showed only a slight change in color (to light brown), and it was decided to abandon this raw material as a potential material for the production of nanoparticles by green synthesis.

Size and Shape of Nanoparticles

The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Figure 2) show nickel ferrite nanoparticles aggregated to varying degrees. The dark areas visible in the TEM images correspond to regions of higher electron density (eg, metal compounds), which are typical of transition-metal oxides. The strong contrast is primarily due to the intense electron absorption by regions of higher atomic mass and/or greater sample thickness.

TEM images of nanoparticles: G1, G2 - from pomegranate peels; M1, M2 - from raspberry leaves and stems.

Primary nanoparticles range in size from a few (2.4 nm) to tens (24.3 nm) nanometers. The M1 and M2 sample nanoparticles are larger than G1 and G2. The G1 and G2 nanoparticles are characterized by their irregular shape, while M1 and M2 are beaded and elongated.

The image shows a slight “haze” or irregular translucence in places, which may indicate organic matrix residues. Differences in the precursors may change the amount and type of these residues, thus slightly modify the mode of agglomeration. TEM microscopy alone does not directly confirm the crystal structure, but the typical features of ferrites (strong contrast, fine crystallinity, and tendency to agglomerate) are clearly visible.

Measurements of the hydrodynamic size distribution by DLS were performed on the suspensions (Figure 3); the data represent the hydrodynamic diameters of all colloidal entities present (including NiFe2O4 particles, their agglomerates and organic aggregates or organic-coated particles). In samples M1, G1 and G2, where organic residues are dominant (Table 4), the DLS signal is therefore largely influenced by organic aggregates or organic-coated species rather than by purely inorganic particles.

Particle Sample Distribution in all samples.

All four samples showed broad, multimodal size distributions dominated by a main population in the 190–400 nm range. Sample M2 showed the smallest dominant sizes (220–295 nm) and G2 the largest (340–400 nm), while M1 and G1 additionally displayed a minor secondary population near 1 µm, indicating a small fraction of larger agglomerates (Figure 3). The DLS technique is inherently sensitive to larger structures, as scattering intensity scales with the sixth power of particle diameter.30

Electrokinetic Potential

The zeta potential (ζ) is the electrical potential difference between the surface of the dispersed particle and the surrounding bulk liquid, specifically measured at the shear plane (sliding layer). It serves as a key measure of the colloidal stability of the system. The zeta potential of nickel ferrite nanoparticles is pH-dependent, as the H+ ion is the primary determinant of the surface potential.53 Analyzing the zeta potential of the nanoparticles studied, it was found that the type of extract used, as well as the precursors used, affect the final surface composition of the nanoparticles, which determines their zeta potential and the subsequent stability of the colloids (Table 5). The samples measured at pH 7 (M1, G1) show negative potential values. Lowering the pH to 5 (M2, G2) resulted in a shift in potential values closer to zero or a sign reversal (G2). The M1 and G1 nanoparticles synthesized with nitrate precursors have a lower negative potential compared to the M2, G2 nanoparticles. The G1 nickel ferrite nanoparticles exhibit a stronger negative potential, while in G2, a charge reversal effect to positive is observed.

X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) Measurement Results

The graph (Figure 5) shows the XRD results compiled of the obtained nanoparticles. The diffraction peaks correspond to reflections from specific crystal planes of the phases present in the sample. In all diffractograms, the most intense peak occurs in the 30−40° 2θ range, suggesting a dominant crystalline phase in the samples. For samples synthesized from nitrate precursors (M1 and G1), no significant additional reflections were observed (green lines absent), indicating the formation of a highly crystalline, predominantly single-phase spinel structure. In contrast, the diffractograms of samples M2 and G2 (obtained from acetate and sulfate precursors) exhibit additional reflections (marked in green), which may correspond to incompletely decomposed precursors or minor secondary oxide phases such as NiO or Fe2O3.

The arrangement of the peaks for M1 and G1 (same type of precursor, different extracts) is similar. Nickel ferrite adopts a cubic symmetry crystal structure (space group Fd-3m).55 The main XRD reflections for the spinel NiFe2O4 phase occur at 2θ angle values corresponding to the following planes (JCPDS Card no 10–325): 30.1 o (220), 35.5 o (311), 43.1 o (400), 53.4 o (422), 57.0° (511), and 62.6 o (440). The positions and relative intensities of the observed peaks in all diffractograms correspond well with these reference values, confirming the successful formation of the spinel phase in all samples.

Discussion

Green Synthesis Mechanism and Phase Formation

The formation of nickel ferrite nanoparticles in this study was mediated by the reducing capacity of the phytochemicals present in the plant extracts. Mixing the raspberry and pomegranate extracts with the metal precursors produced a pronounced color change, from the extracts’ characteristic color to a dark brownish-green or near-black hue. This change is consistent with the onset of nanoparticle nucleation and is attributable to the high polyphenolic content of these extracts, which facilitates electron transfer to the metal cations.51,52 In contrast, the comparatively weak color change observed with cherry stalk extract indicates limited reducing capacity, which justified its exclusion from the four ferrite batches examined further. Elemental composition data (XRF, Table 4) further indicate that precursor chemistry, rather than extract identity, is the principal determinant of ferrite yield: the substantially higher Fe and Ni content in samples M2 and G2 relative to M1 and G1 suggests more complete ferrite formation with the acetate–sulfate precursor system, while deviations from the ideal spinel Fe:Ni ratio (2:1) point to the possible presence of secondary oxide phases (eg, Fe2O3 or NiO).58,59

Structural and Morphological Characteristics

TEM imaging showed that the synthesized nanoparticles were prone to aggregation, commonly attributed to a combination of magnetic dipole interactions and van der Waals forces between primary particles. The organic capping layer formed during phytochemical-mediated synthesis, together with the low-temperature calcination conditions employed, is consistent with the morphology observed across samples and is typical of ferrites obtained via low-temperature green-synthesis routes.28 The corresponding DLS size distributions, shifted toward substantially larger hydrodynamic diameters than the primary particle sizes seen by TEM, likely reflect differences in the degree of agglomeration and surface properties among samples, compounded by the well-known sensitivity of DLS to even a small population of large agglomerates.30 The FTIR spectra provide a further, indirect line of evidence for compositional differences: although samples M1 and G1 contain a comparatively high proportion of organic residue (Table 4 their spectra do not display correspondingly intense organic bands. This can be explained by strong Mie-type scattering and matrix effects in these heterogeneous powders, which flatten the broad, low-intensity bands typical of plant-derived organic components while metal–oxygen species still produce sharp absorptions in the 400–600 cm−1 region. Crystallite sizes calculated from XRD (15.0–21.8 nm) were systematically larger than the corresponding TEM particle sizes, an outcome attributable to lattice strain and small-domain broadening inherent to the Scherrer method.33 The larger crystallites obtained with nitrate precursors compared with the acetate–sulfate system indicate that precursor chemistry primarily governs crystallite growth, while the larger crystallites observed with pomegranate extract relative to raspberry extract point to a secondary role of extract-specific polyphenol content. These sizes fall within the broad range reported for NiFe2O4 synthesized by other routes: Zhang et al,60 reported 403 nm crystallites via sol-gel synthesis calcined at 750 °C, while Hjiri et al,61 obtained approximately 10 nm crystallites by microwave-assisted synthesis, underscoring the strong dependence of crystallite size on synthesis method, calcination temperature, and precursor chemistry.

Colloidal Stability and Surface Charge

Zeta potential measurements indicate that both precursor chemistry and extract identity influence the surface charge, and hence colloidal stability, of the synthesized nanoparticles (Table 5). The less negative potential of the acetate–sulfate-derived samples (M2, G2) relative to the nitrate-derived samples (M1, G1) is likely related to differences in the anionic species released during synthesis and their interaction with biosurfactant molecules from the extracts, while the charge reversal observed for G2 is consistent with adsorption of specific cationic or amphoteric organic compounds altering the composition of the diffuse layer. More broadly, zeta potential values ranging from −14.0 to +7.8 mV are well below the conventional stability threshold of ±30 mV, indicating a strong tendency toward aggregation for all four samples,62 consistent with the DLS and TEM findings discussed above. Aggregation in suspension restricts Brownian motion, reduces the effective magnetic moment per colloidal unit, and may lower the measured SAR relative to the intrinsic heating efficiency of individual nanoparticles.18 Aggregated particles are also unlikely to achieve adequate dispersibility in physiological media, a prerequisite for intravenous or intratumoral administration in hyperthermia protocols.19

Magnetic Heating Performance Across Samples

The negligible thermal response of sample M1 (SAR = 0.93 W·g−1) suggests, following Rosensweig18 and Kumar and Mohammad,37 that its nanoparticles may be too small or irregular in shape and size distribution, or contain crystalline defects that limit magnetic heat generation. The markedly faster and larger temperature rise in G2 relative to G1 (SAR = 15.0 vs 9.7 W·g−1) is likely attributable to differences in nanoparticle size, shape, or composition that enhance magnetic energy absorption; the correspondingly higher ILP of G2 (2.93 vs 1.89 nH·m2·kg−1) confirms more favorable relaxation and hysteresis properties at the same field amplitude and frequency.63 The constant LLP values across samples indicate an absence of magnetic saturation or non-linear effects, a linear behavior that is advantageous for predicting heating response in medical applications. M2 outperforms M1 by several orders of magnitude, confirming that M1 is largely ineffective for magnetic hyperthermia, while both M2 and G2 demonstrate high heating efficiencies per unit field and frequency, reflecting favorable structural parameters (size, crystallinity, magnetic moment) that support their preliminary suitability for hyperthermia applications.

Comparison of SAR and ILP Values with Literature

The SAR values obtained in this study (up to 15.73 W·g−1 for sample M2 and 15.00 W·g−1 for sample G2, normalized to total powder mass; Table 8) fall within the range reported for nickel ferrite nanoparticles and should be interpreted in the context of the biogenic synthesis route employed and the relatively low spinel-phase content of the powder. Gasser et al,34 reported markedly higher SAR values, on the order of 165 W·g−1, for well-crystallized NiFe2O4 nanoparticles of 17 nm diameter synthesized hydrothermally and measured at higher field amplitudes than those used in the present study Kumari et al,35 demonstrated that ferrite morphology (fibers versus spherical nanoparticles) significantly affects magnetic properties and heating capacity, with microstructural optimization producing pronounced SAR differences even at constant chemical composition Ahmad et al,36 investigating NiFe2O4 nanoparticles prepared by co-precipitation, obtained SAR values comparable to those reported here, supporting the conclusion that materials synthesized by low-temperature routes generally exhibit lower heating efficiency than products calcined at higher temperatures In the specific context of biogenic synthesis, Amiri et al,64 used Urtica-mediated green synthesis to prepare NiFe2O4 nanoparticles and demonstrated their activity under an alternating magnetic field against cancer cell lines, although without reporting normalized SAR/ILP values, which precludes a direct quantitative comparison. El-Boubbou et al,65 reported an ILP value of 0.9 nH·m2·kg−1 at 332 kHz for PVP-coated NiFe2O4 nanoparticles (10–17 nm), identifying saturation magnetization as the principal determinant of SAR among the divalent ferrites investigated. The ILP values obtained in the present study (up to 3.08 nH·m2·kg−1 for sample M2, Table 8) exceed this literature value; however, this comparison should be interpreted cautiously given differences in particle coating, field conditions, and measurement protocol between the two studies. The SAR values normalized to ferrite mass (SARferrite, Table 8) obtained for the nitrate-derived samples (M1, G1) are substantially higher than the corresponding total-mass-normalized values and should be regarded as indicative upper-bound estimates of intrinsic heating efficiency, rather than directly comparable performance metrics, owing to the uncertainty inherent in XRF-based ferrite-mass estimation.

Limitations of Green Synthesis for Scale-Up

A critical limitation identified in this study is the limited colloidal stability of the synthesized nanoparticles. Zeta potential values ranging from −14.0 to +7.8 mV are well below the conventional stability threshold of ±30 mV, indicating a strong tendency toward aggregation, which was further confirmed by DLS size distributions dominated by hydrodynamic diameters of 200–400 nm and TEM images showing particle clustering. Aggregation in suspension restricts Brownian motion, reduces the effective magnetic moment per colloidal unit, and may lower the experimentally measured SAR relative to the intrinsic heating efficiency of the individual nanoparticles.18 Furthermore, aggregated particles are unlikely to achieve adequate dispersibility in physiological media, which is a prerequisite for intravenous administration or intratumoral injection in hyperthermia protocols.19

Despite the promising heating performance demonstrated in this study, green synthesis using plant-derived extracts faces several inherent limitations that must be addressed before practical or large-scale applications can be considered. The most fundamental challenge is the natural variability in phytochemical composition of plant-derived extracts. Parameters such as total phenolic content, flavonoid concentration, and antioxidant activity depend strongly on plant origin, geographical location, harvest season, post-harvest storage conditions, and extraction protocol.45,46 This variability directly translates into batch-to-batch inconsistency in nanoparticle properties, including crystallite size, phase purity, and metal content, as demonstrated in the present study by the wide range of Fe and Ni fractions across batches (Fe: 1.3–49.0%). Standardization of raw material quality and extraction conditions is therefore a prerequisite for reproducible synthesis.

A second major limitation is the presence of residual organic matter in the calcined product, which was substantial in nitrate-precursor batches (M1, G1) and required XRF-based correction of the ferrite mass fraction for meaningful SAR calculation. At industrial scale, incomplete organic burnout would represent both a product purity issue and a potential source of cytotoxicity, requiring optimization of calcination parameters or implementation of additional purification steps. Furthermore, the biogenic synthesis route provides limited independent control over individual synthesis parameters: pH, precursor concentration, reduction rate, and capping agent activity are all coupled through the phytochemical matrix, making systematic optimization more complex than in conventional co-precipitation or hydrothermal methods. Finally, the transition from laboratory-scale batch synthesis (milliliter volumes, milligram yields) to pilot or industrial scale introduces additional challenges related to heat and mass transfer homogeneity, extract preparation capacity, and quality control of the final product. Addressing these limitations through standardization of plant raw material, design-of-experiment optimization of synthesis conditions, and development of scalable calcination protocols will be essential steps toward practical application of biogenically synthesized NiFe2O4 nanoparticles.

Thermal Conversion Potential versus Therapeutic Efficacy

It should be emphasized, however, that the SAR and ILP values reported in this study characterize the thermal conversion potential of the ferrite phase under defined laboratory AMF conditions, and represent a necessary proof-of-concept step in material development rather than evidence of clinical readiness. Colloidal stabilization is a well-defined subsequent challenge, addressable through surface functionalization strategies widely reported for spinel ferrite nanoparticles, including coating with polyethylene glycol (PEG), dextran, chitosan, or silica shells.19 Such coatings have been shown to raise zeta potential magnitudes above the stability threshold, suppress aggregation under physiological ionic strength, and maintain or improve heating efficiency by preserving nanoparticle dispersion.17 Future work will therefore focus on surface modification of the most promising batches (M2, G2) to enhance colloidal stability, followed by cytotoxicity assessment and in vitro hyperthermia validation in cancer cell models.

Translational Pathway

Successful translation of biogenically synthesized NiFe2O4 nanoparticles from laboratory proof-of-concept to clinical or commercial application requires progression through a well-defined development pathway. The present study addresses the first and most fundamental stage: demonstrating that agro-waste-derived plant extracts can mediate the formation of crystalline spinel ferrite nanoparticles with measurable magnetic heating performance. The subsequent stages of development can be outlined as follows. First, surface functionalization (coating with biocompatible polymers such as PEG, dextran, or chitosan) is required to achieve colloidal stability in physiological media and to provide reactive groups for further conjugation with targeting ligands or therapeutic molecules.19 Second, systematic in vitro evaluation of cytotoxicity, cell uptake, and hyperthermia-induced cancer cell death is necessary to establish biological safety and efficacy at the cellular level, prior to any animal experimentation.20 Third, in vivo preclinical studies in appropriate tumor models are required to assess biodistribution, clearance, tumor accumulation, and therapeutic efficacy under clinically relevant AMF conditions.17 Fourth, scale-up of the synthesis process must be demonstrated with batch-to-batch reproducibility, which requires standardization of plant raw material quality and extraction protocols, as discussed above.

Commercial Perspective

From a commercial perspective, the green synthesis route offers several intrinsic advantages over conventional chemical methods that may support future viability. The use of agro-industrial by-products (pomegranate peel, raspberry residues) as reducing and capping agents eliminates the need for expensive or hazardous reducing chemicals, reduces synthesis cost, and aligns with circular economy principles increasingly favored in regulatory and investor environments.8 The simplicity of the synthesis procedure, requiring only mixing, moderate heating, and calcination without inert atmosphere or high-pressure conditions, is compatible with scale-up using standard industrial equipment. Furthermore, the demonstrated ability to tune nanoparticle properties (crystallinity, metal content, and heating efficiency) through selection of plant extract and precursor system provides a degree of process flexibility relevant for product optimization. While significant development steps remain before commercialization, the combination of low raw material cost, environmental compatibility, and demonstrated heating performance positions biogenically synthesized NiFe2O4 as a competitive candidate for future hyperthermia applications, provided that the biological validation and scale-up challenges outlined above are successfully addressed.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that plant-derived extracts can effectively support the biogenic synthesis of nickel ferrite nanoparticles, but their efficiency strongly depends on the phytochemical composition and the type of precursor used. Raspberry leaf/shoot and pomegranate peel extracts enabled the formation of NiFe2O4, although the final composition of the powders varied widely due to differences in organic load and precursor decomposition behavior.

Structural analysis revealed that only samples obtained from nitrate precursors (M1, G1) formed predominantly single-phase ferrite, while those synthesized from acetate and sulfate salts (M2, G2) contained additional oxide or precursor-derived phases. XRF confirmed that the metal content in the powders ranged from very low (M1, G1) to high (M2), directly influencing the fraction of the spinel phase. FTIR showed characteristic M–O vibrations in all samples, although the signatures were attenuated in organic-rich materials.

Spectroscopic (FT-IR), microscopic (TEM), diffraction (XRD), spectrometric (XRF), dynamic light scattering (DLS), and zeta potential measurements together allowed full characterization of the structure, morphology, size, and surface charge of the nanoparticles, providing an indication of their colloidal behavior.

Surface charge measurements indicated distinct interactions between nanoparticles and extract components, including a charge reversal in sample G2 linked to positively charged phytochemicals. Across all samples, DLS and TEM analyses confirmed a pronounced tendency toward aggregation, with hydrodynamic diameters (200–400 nm) substantially exceeding the primary crystallite sizes, identifying colloidal stability as a key limitation for further biomedical application. Diffuse reflectance spectra provided insight into the optical transitions of the ferrite phase, while magnetic heating experiments demonstrated measurable SAR values, with the highest efficiency observed for the more crystalline and metal-rich materials. When normalized to the actual ferrite mass, samples with low metal content exhibited substantially higher intrinsic heating capability.

Overall, this work highlights the strong coupling between plant-extract chemistry, precursor selection, and the structural and functional properties of biogenically synthesized NiFe2O4. The use of green synthesis methods proved effective in creating nanomaterials with measurable magnetic heating capability. However, these results demonstrate thermal-conversion potential rather than validated efficacy for cancer hyperthermia therapy: no cytotoxicity, biocompatibility, or cancer-cell studies were performed in this work, and such biological validation, together with improved colloidal stabilization, will be required before these materials can be considered candidates for therapeutic application.
