Delayed Recognition and Drainage of a Pediatric Retropharyngeal Abscess with Parapharyngeal Extension in a Resource-Limited Setting: A Case Report
Authors: Mohamed Abdirahman Mohamud, Tabu Geoffrey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep neck space infections in children can deteriorate quickly, especially when diagnosis and source control are delayed. We describe a 4-year-old boy admitted on 28 March 2026 with 4 days of progressive right lateral neck swelling, fever, muffled voice, and breathing difficulty. Ultrasonography showed a right cervical inflammatory mass with moderate upper-airway compression but was reported as Ludwig’s angina, which did not match the lateral clinical findings. Needle aspiration yielded no pus, contrast-enhanced computed tomography was not obtained, and surgical drainage was not performed at that stage. The child deteriorated and required emergency tracheostomy with parapharyngeal incision and drainage on 1 April; approximately 20 mL of pus was evacuated. Persistent illness and delayed lateral neck radiography later identified a retropharyngeal component, prompting wider drainage of both spaces on 11 April. Gram stain and pus and blood cultures were negative. He improved after definitive drainage, airway care, antimicrobial escalation, and nutritional support and was discharged on hospital day 20. This course highlights the clinical cost of delayed anatomic definition and source control. In a child with progressive neck swelling and airway symptoms, a negative aspiration or discordant ultrasound should prompt urgent cross-sectional imaging where feasible or operative reassessment; early multidisciplinary action may prevent avoidable airway deterioration.

Background

Deep neck infections (DNIs) involve the cervical fascial planes and potential spaces. Retropharyngeal and parapharyngeal infections are especially important in children because they can spread across adjacent compartments and rapidly threaten the airway.1–3 They occur most often in younger children, commonly after an upper respiratory infection or suppurative lymphadenitis, and may present with fever, neck swelling, dysphagia, drooling, muffled voice, torticollis, or respiratory distress.1–4

Diagnosis can be difficult because bedside examination may not define the involved deep space, particularly in a distressed or poorly cooperative child. Contrast enhanced computed tomography is generally the most useful imaging study for mapping the extent of disease and identifying a drainable collection, although the images must be interpreted alongside the clinical course.1,5,6 Management requires early airway assessment, broad-spectrum intravenous antimicrobial therapy, and timely surgical drainage when there is airway compromise, clinical deterioration, or persistent abscess.1,5,6

Case Presentation

A 4-year-old boy weighing 12 kg, with no known chronic illness and unknown HIV serostatus, was admitted on 28 March 2026 after referral from Bugembe HC IV. He had a 4-day history of progressive right lateral neck swelling, fever, muffled voice, and worsening difficulty in breathing. There was no history of dental procedure, dental caries, trauma, or tonsillitis.

On admission, he was sick-looking and had an obvious right lateral neck swelling extending into the suprahyoid region (Figure 1A). The swelling was clinically hard and non-fluctuant. Oropharyngoscopy was difficult because the child was poorly cooperative rather than because of trismus. Findings included leftward deviation of the uvula, edema in the right peritonsillar region, and thick mucus in the posterior oropharynx. Neck ultrasound was requested first and, on 28 March 2026, showed an ill-defined heterogeneous inflammatory mass involving the superficial and deep tissues of the right cervical region, increased Doppler flow, inflamed surrounding soft tissues, enlarged right cervical lymph nodes, patent major cervical vessels, and moderate upper-airway compression. The reported impression was Ludwig’s angina with an associated right cervical inflammatory mass. This did not fully fit the bedside findings because the disease was predominantly lateral and there was no classic floor-of-mouth involvement. Nevertheless, the combination of fever, progressive neck swelling, and documented airway compression remained concerning for a deep neck abscess. Diagnostic needle aspiration yielded no pus. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography was not obtained, and surgical drainage was not performed at that stage.

The initial working impression was right parapharyngeal cellulitis or abscess. Early treatment consisted of close oxygen-saturation monitoring, intravenous dexamethasone 4 mg three times daily, intravenous cef-sulbactam 1 g, D10 120 mL every 4 hours, intravenous paracetamol, oral hygiene with dilute peroxide and warm saline, and requested investigations including CBC, RBS, and lateral neck radiography.

The child subsequently deteriorated with progressive respiratory distress and underwent emergency tracheostomy on the evening of 1 April 2026. Because an appropriately sized pediatric tracheostomy tube was unavailable, an uncuffed size 4.0 endotracheal tube was inserted through the tracheostomy stoma and secured with sutures as a temporary tracheostomy tube. In the same sitting, incision and drainage of the parapharyngeal space was performed, yielding approximately 20 mL of purulent material. Despite continued treatment after the first procedure, the child remained clinically unwell. He became dehydrated and wasted, had poor oral intake, persistent fever, and continued pus discharge during dressing changes. Tracheostomy care, including suctioning every 2 hours or as needed, was instituted from day 1 after tracheostomy. Oxygen saturation was documented as 99% on oxygen but 85% on room air. A CBC during admission showed leukocytosis with neutrophilia and thrombocytosis.

On day 8, pediatric consultation was sought because of ongoing deterioration. Antibiotics were changed to piperacillin-tazobactam (PISA) 100 mg/kg every 8 hours, paracetamol 180 mg every 8 hours was prescribed, nasogastric tube feeding of 240 mL every 3 hours was instituted, and maintenance fluid using Ringer’s lactate and 5% dextrose in a 1:1 ratio at 250 mL every 6 hours was started. By the following day, oxygen saturation on room air had improved to 94–98%.

Although lateral neck radiography had been requested on day 1, it was delayed until the caregiver obtained money for the investigation. When performed, it showed marked prevertebral soft-tissue swelling consistent with retropharyngeal abscess (Figure 3). The child therefore underwent emergency extended incision and drainage on 11 April 2026, with evacuation of pus from both the retropharyngeal and parapharyngeal spaces. A nasogastric tube was inserted during the procedure. Severe adenoiditis was seen during the second operation and was considered the most likely source of infection.Figure 3Lateral neck radiograph demonstrating marked prevertebral soft-tissue swelling consistent with retropharyngeal abscess.Side-view neck X-ray with widened soft-tissue stripe in front of the cervical spine.

The pus swab Gram stain showed no organisms. Pus culture yielded no bacterial growth after 48 hours of incubation, and blood culture showed no bacterial growth after 5 days of incubation. After the second-stage drainage, pus discharge progressively reduced during dressing changes and the child continued to improve clinically. He was decannulated on the next day after airway stabilization, resumed oral feeding, and was discharged on hospital day 20 in improved condition.

Discussion

Deep neck abscesses remain clinically important because infection can traverse fascial planes and cause airway obstruction, sepsis, or mediastinal spread. A recent global review also emphasized that multispace disease and unequal access to imaging and specialist care contribute to adverse outcomes.7 This case shows those risks in a young child. His fever, progressive lateral neck swelling, muffled voice, and breathing difficulty formed a characteristic syndrome in which deep neck abscess should have remained the leading diagnosis. His age and presentation were consistent with pediatric parapharyngeal and retropharyngeal infection described in previous series.1–4,8

The early diagnostic pathway exposed a critical limitation. Ultrasound identified a deep inflammatory mass and airway compression but labeled the process as Ludwig’s angina, despite lateral clinical findings. A non-yielding needle aspiration does not exclude a deep, loculated, or poorly accessible abscess. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography would have been the preferred study to define the involved spaces and their relationship to the airway and major vessels.1,5,7 It was not obtained, and even the requested lateral radiograph was delayed because of cost. Earlier cross-sectional imaging, or urgent operative reassessment when the ultrasound and clinical picture disagreed, might have identified the retropharyngeal extension sooner. Imaging should support, rather than postpone, source control when a child is deteriorating.

The parapharyngeal space is deep and closely related to major neurovascular structures, which makes accurate localization important before surgery. Although the systematic review by Chiari et al concerned parapharyngeal tumors rather than infection, its anatomical analysis reinforces the importance of lesion position, extension, and neurovascular relationships when selecting transoral or external access.9 In an acute abscess, the chosen route must also allow adequate drainage of every involved compartment. In this child, the first operation drained the parapharyngeal space, but the unrecognized retropharyngeal component required a second, extended procedure.

Airway control became necessary once progressive respiratory distress developed. Emergency tracheostomy and drainage were justified at that stage because the child had worsening respiratory compromise and later documented hypoxemia. Reviews consistently recommend prompt airway evaluation and operative drainage when there is airway compromise, deterioration despite treatment, or a persistent collection.1,5–7 However, the need for tracheostomy should not be presented as an uncomplicated success: earlier definitive imaging and drainage might have prevented progression to this point. The temporary use of an uncuffed size 4.0 endotracheal tube as a tracheostomy tube also reflects the limitations created by unavailable pediatric airway equipment.

Although purulent material was drained, Gram stain and both pus and blood cultures showed no bacterial growth. This does not exclude deep neck infection, particularly after prior antimicrobial exposure, with swab-based sampling, or when anaerobic and polymicrobial organisms are involved.10–13 In this child, the diagnosis was supported by clinical deterioration, imaging, operative evacuation of pus, and improvement after broader source control.

Resource constraints shaped the sequence of care: imaging was delayed by cost, an appropriately sized pediatric tracheostomy tube was unavailable, and microbiology results were not immediately available. These difficulties explain the context but do not remove the main safety lesson. A febrile child with progressive neck swelling and airway symptoms requires early clinicoradiologic reassessment, timely source control, and coordinated input from otolaryngology, pediatrics, anesthesia, radiology, and nursing teams.7 The later clinical recovery should therefore be understood alongside the potentially avoidable delay that preceded airway deterioration.

Conclusion

The principal lesson from this case is not the eventual recovery, but the risk created by delayed anatomic definition and source control. In a febrile child with progressive neck swelling, muffled voice, or breathing difficulty, a deep neck abscess should remain the leading diagnosis until it is adequately excluded. A negative aspiration or discordant ultrasound should prompt urgent contrast-enhanced computed tomography where feasible or immediate operative reassessment, while worsening airway signs require prompt airway protection and drainage. Earlier clinicoradiologic reassessment, timely surgery, and multidisciplinary care may prevent progression and reduce the need for emergency tracheostomy.

Patient Perspective

A formal caregiver perspective was not recorded contemporaneously. However, the child’s mother expressed relief after the child’s breathing improved, oral feeding resumed, and discharge was achieved.
