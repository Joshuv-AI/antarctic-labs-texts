Longitudinal evolution of physical activity type and eating and weight concerns among adolescents
Authors: Giulio D’Anna, Lorenzo Lucherini Angeletti, Lara Allegrini, Arianna Ida Altomare, Daniela Betti, Alessandra De Martino, Giulia Minotti, Chiara Muscarella, Federica Benvenuti, Valdo Ricca, Francesco Rotella
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo provide a prospective 2 year follow-up of a previously enrolled adolescent high school sample, regarding body image and eating concerns, and patterns of sports type and physical activity.MethodsSports type, weekly time devoted to it, and psychopathological self-reports (Eating Disorders Examination Questionnaire 6.0, Body Uneasiness Test, and Muscle Dysmorphia Disorder Inventory) were evaluated longitudinally in a general population sample enrolled in a previous study, testing prospective variations in an observational setting.ResultsAt follow-up, girls expressed increased dietary restraint and body uneasiness as compared to baseline, whereas boys expressed increased body uneasiness—and specifically in the avoidance, depersonalisation and compulsive self-monitoring dimensions. Among both sexes, a significant shift towards individual activities or lack of activity was observed after 2 years, with a reduction in team sports involvement. Among girls, time devoted to exercise significantly decreased at follow-up.ConclusionsThe present findings indirectly confirm an increased vulnerability to dietary, bodily and appearance-based concerns among adolescents. The role of different patterns of physical activity and exercise time may interact bidirectionally with these problematic areas, considering that previous studies point out increased concerns among inactive subjects, and among those who choose individual activities.Level of evidence: Level IV-longitudinal observational study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40519-024-01696-y.

Introduction

Eating disorders (EDs) are severe conditions which typically present during adolescence and early adulthood, with a progressive decrease in the age of onset. While most studies are focused on Western countries and point out a stable prevalence in recent years, emerging data from different countries, a global increase in incidence during the COVID-19 pandemic, and the common presence of subtle preclinic conditions claim for an effort on early detection of these conditions [1]. Late adolescence constitutes a critical window regarding the incidence and prevalence of these conditions [1]. For this reason, longitudinal studies conducted on adolescents recruited from the general population may be of value to detect early behavioral and psychopathological changes.

In this evolving framework, the relationship between eating and weight concerns of adolescents, and sports and physical activity remains controversial. On the one hand, the differential impact of various forms of physical activity and sports involvement regarding subclinical and overt ED psychopathology has been emphasised [2]. Notably, this facet is of remarkable interest for Muscle Dysmorphia (MD), which is thought to be a sex-specific expression of EDs among male adolescents and young adults [3]: in this condition, exercise aimed at increasing lean mass and appearance-focused activities constitute a behavioural expression of the psychopathological core of this condition. On the other hand, other evidence points out increased body concerns and problematic eating behaviour—i.e., disordered eating (DE)—among inactive adolescents, rather than among those engaging in different forms of physical activity at any level [4, 5]. Since most of the general population practice physical activity at a non-professional, non-elite level, and in different settings, it is possible to hypothesise that an active involvement in any form of physical activity may be associated with lower concerns despite changes in the chosen form of activity.

The aim of the present study is (1) to test longitudinal variations in ED psychopathology, body uneasiness, and symptoms of MD in a subsample of adolescents enrolled in a previous observational study from the general population [5] and (2) to describe variations in sports involvement and weekly exercise time in the abovementioned sample.

Methods

The present observational, longitudinal, study constitutes a 2 year prospective follow-up of a subgroup of subjects who took part in a previous study coordinated by the University of Florence [5]. Specifically, all the students who were attending their 3rd grade in a public high school of Florence in the previous study were asked to participate by repeating a self-reported psychometric evaluation, and by declaring any changes in the type of sports/physical activity involvement and in weekly exercise time. All participants, and their parents or legal guardians (for people under 18) provided additional written informed consent for participation. For each class, the additional survey was completed on a single occasion, during school hours. The original study protocol was emended and approved by the Committee for the Ethics of Research of the University of Florence to collect the abovementioned additional data. Confidentiality of participants was guaranteed, as the longitudinal data were paired using a unique anonymised alphanumeric code who had been provided at the baseline assessment. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the latest revision of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Socio-demographic data (age, sex) were retrieved from baseline analyses, together with baseline self-reported body mass index (BMI). Self-reported height and weight were re-assessed at follow-up. Both at baseline (T0) and at follow-up (T1), participants were asked whether the practised physical activity or sports on a regular basis (i.e., at least once a week), what was the main activity practiced, if it was undertaken at an individual or team level, and the weekly number of hours devoted to physical activity or sports.

The following self-reported psychometric indexes were used at baseline and at follow-up:Eating Disorders Examination Questionnaire 6.0 (EDE-Q 6.0) [6], a 28-item test providing four psychopathology subscales (Dietary Restraint, Weight Concern, Eating Concern, Shape Concern), and a Total Score which is obtained as average of the abovementioned subscales;Body Uneasiness Test (BUT) [7], a 71-item test whose first part (BUT-A) provides a Global Severity Index (GSI), and five subscale scores (Weight Phobia, Body Image Concern, Avoidance, Compulsive Self-monitoring, Depersonalisation);Muscle Dysmorphia Disorder Inventory (MDDI) [8], a 13-item questionnaire for males which provides a Total Score of severity as sum of three subscale scores (Drive for Size, Appearance Intolerance, Functional Impairment); as mentioned in the previous cross-sectional study, this scale was administered only to boys, given the body of literature and the established psychometric properties for this specific subgroup.

Single participant data were analysed if 85% of responses had been provided; single-item missing data were retrieved as average score of items concurring to the corresponding subscale. Longitudinal analyses were carried out for those subjects who responded both to baseline and follow-up enrolment.

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were expressed as absolute and relative frequency. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For between-group comparisons, chi-square (χ2) or Student’s t test were used whenever appropriate. For longitudinal comparisons, paired t tests were used. Cramer’s V or Cohen’s d was used for effect size whenever appropriate. Analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences 25.0. The null hypothesis was rejected for alpha < 0.05.

Results

The subsample of participants who were asked to take part in the longitudinal study comprised 179 subjects (83 girls and 96 boys). Of them, a total of 58 girls and 63 boys (N = 121, 67.8% of the original sample) provided informed consent and accepted to participate to the follow-up. The final sample had a mean age of 16.06 ± 0.23 years at T0, and 18.05 ± 0.22 years at T1. No difference in sex distribution among respondents and non-respondents was observed (χ2 = 0.37; p = 0.544).

Differences between boys and girls at T0 and at T1 were observed for each variable investigated (Supplementary Table 1). More in detail, when compared to boys, girls had a lower BMI and higher scores in all the scales administered (total scores and subscales), and they practiced physical activity for a lower amount of time.

Discussion

The present follow-up evaluation explored variations in eating- and body-centred concerns, and physical activity type of a subgroup of adolescents involved on a previous study conducted on a sample selected from the general population. Only about two thirds of the original sample took part in the follow-up study, a finding which is in line with previous participation rates in similar studies [5]. The fact that sexes were equally represented in the respondent and non-respondent samples minimises the risk of a selective response bias. Similar to the cross-sectional study, remarkable differences were detected between boys and girls for each of the psychometric indexes at T1, confirming the known sex-specific distribution in clinical and subclinical expression of EDs [1]. Moreover, a reduced time of involvement in physical activity was detected among girls—a condition which has previously been associated with higher bodily discomfort and DE [4, 5, 9].

At T1, a significant increase in body uneasiness was detected both among boys and girls. Among girls, all the psychometric variables investigated increased their score, although only the BUT GSI and EDE-Q dietary restraint evaluations reached statistical significance. These data indirectly confirm that young women undergo a critical transition during adolescence, with a broad and non-specific focus on bodily concerns. In this sense, the finding does not point out a transition toward a specific ED, rather representing part of a multifaceted condition which is most probably associated with psychological aspects and interpersonal factors [9]. It is noteworthy that dietary restraint significantly increased at follow-up—and dieting is known to constitute a stable and notable feature of subjects who engage in long-term DE and EDs [10]. Among boys, an overall increase in BMI was observed at follow-up, whereas changes in EDE-Q scores were not significant. Differently from girls, the remarkable increase in global body uneasiness was driven by specific subscales: avoidance, compulsive self-monitoring, and depersonalisation. Although these dimensions are not known to represent a coherent predictor of a specific psychiatric condition, their co-occurrence may warrant further investigation in a psychological framework—irrespective of the established longitudinal psychometric properties of the BUT [7]. Given the lack of changes in ED psychopathology, it is most likely that other factors play a role, e.g., social media use, interactions with peers, and polarisation on one self’s perception from the outside.

Regarding patterns of physical activity involvement, a significant shift towards inactivity and abandonment of team sports was observed among both sexes. More in detail, girls shifted from team to individual activities, with an overall stability in the number of inactive subjects, whereas boys mostly dropped team sports in favour of other activities. Moreover, girls—who already proved to devote less time to exercise at baseline as compared to boys—reported a significant reduction in the average weekly exercise time at follow-up. Conversely, no differences were detected in the subgroup of boys. Given the previously observed trend towards lower body- and eating-centred concerns among team players as compared to inactive subjects and to individual sports players/exercisers [5], these data indirectly suggest that abandonment of collective, team-based activities, and complete inactivity may be associated with increased eating, weight and bodily concerns. These may be linked to differential aspects—e.g., to the major attention paid to appearance-based individual activities (as in aesthetic sports and gym), and to the overall decrease in body comfort and acceptance which is described among sedentary subjects [10–12]. In fact, body dissatisfaction may be linked to sedentary behaviour and DE also through indirect pathways, including stigma and internalisation of sociocultural body standards which exhibit sex- and gender-specific characteristics [12].

Strength and limits

The present longitudinal evaluation underlines a notable evolution in the levels of eating- and body-focused concerns of adolescents in the studied sample, confirming remarkable, well-acknowledged differences between boys and girls. Our data suggest that the longitudinal tendency to switch to different forms of sports involvement and physical activity—i.e., a reduction in exercise time among girls, and a progressive abandonment of team sports in favour of individual ones—constitutes a pattern that may be associated with a progressive increase of body discomfort, whose features are differentially expressed among sexes. Studies on larger samples are needed to provide additional statistical power, and to allow to take into consideration confounding factors such as gender, sexual orientation, ethnicity, socio-economic status, and social media use.

The small sample size, influenced by the response rate, is a major limitation of the present study, possibly affecting the statistical power of the study and the possibility to produce more refined analyses (e.g., association analyses for the variables presented). The low response rate at follow-up is in line with literature solely based on self-reported data, but the lack of differences in sex among respondents and non-respondents limits the risk of a selective response bias: regarding this, the longitudinal study protocol did not allow to retrieve data from the baseline among those who did not provide additional informed consent, and therefore potential baseline differences in EDE-Q scores or hours of physical activity among respondents and non-respondents could not be evaluated. Moreover, the fully self-reported nature of the evaluation may limit the putative clinical correlates of the present findings. More in detail, weight and height may be subject—respectively—to an underestimate and overestimate among adolescents, whereas self-reported psychometric measures cannot be directly associated with specific clinical presentations without a concurrent professional evaluation. Furthermore, variables such as socioeconomic status, educational achievement of parents, and peer relationships—which may influence the choice of eating patterns and preference for different forms of physical activity—could not be assessed. To conclude, no direct investigation of the presence of a clinical ED was carried out, and subjects were not asked whether they practiced sports at a professional level: these aspects may possibly influence the relationship between physical activity and ED psychopathology in the overall sample.

What is already known on this subject?

Physical activity and sports are known to exert a differential influence on weight and body image concerns of adolescents. Previous studies outlined the benefits of physical activity on general mental health, but the longitudinal patterns of sports involvement and body- and eating-based concerns in the general population are poorly explored.

What this study adds?

The present study suggests the presence of a longitudinal association between physical activity and reduced bodily and dietary concerns among adolescents. An increase in dietary restraint among girls, and in body uneasiness of boys and girls was observed, together with a progressive shift towards inactivity or preference for individual sports at expense of team sports, especially among girls.
