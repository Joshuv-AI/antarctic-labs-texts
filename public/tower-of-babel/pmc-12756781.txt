Spectroscopic Determination and Computational Characterization of the Charge Transfer Complex of Famotidine with Bromanil
Authors: Azizah M. Aldosari, Awwad Radwan, Gamal Mahrous, Adel Alghaith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Famotidine (FMD), a histamine H2-receptor antagonist, is commonly used in the management of gastroesophageal reflux disease and gastric ulcers. Despite several reported analytical approaches for its detection, charge-transfer complex (CTC) formation offers a spectrophotometric alternative that uses readily available materials and reproducible conditions. A spectrophotometric method was developed based on the formation of a CTC between FMD and bromanil (BRL). Validation involved investigating reaction stoichiometry, the influence of time and temperature, intra- and interassay precision and accuracy, and application to tablet dosage forms. Additionally, computational modeling was performed to explore the interaction mechanism. The optimized conditions yielded a stable and reproducible BRL-FMD complex. The method demonstrated linearity with R 2 of 0.9999 and % RSD of 0.41%, precision, and accuracy for the quantification of FMD in commercial formulations. Computational analysis, using quantum chemistry, complemented the experimental findings, confirming the charge-transfer interaction and identifying the significant stabilizing factors. This study establishes a reliable and economical spectrophotometric assay for FMD based on CTC formation with BRL. The integrated experimental and computational approach provides mechanistic insight and promotes broader application of CTC-based assays in pharmaceutical analysis.

Introduction

Famotidine (FMD), 3-[2-(diaminomethyleneamino) thiazol-4-ylmethylthio]-N-sulfamoyl propionamidine, is a histamine H2-receptor antagonist commonly used in the management of gastroesophageal reflux disease and other acid-related disorders. FMD is classified as a class III drug according to the Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS), exhibiting high aqueous solubility and low permeability. It has an oral bioavailability of approximately 40–45% and a short elimination half-life of approximately 3 h. It is usually administered orally at a therapeutic dose of 40 mg, with an onset of action within 1 h and a peak plasma concentration within 1 to 3 h.

FMD has been analytically investigated in pharmaceutical formulations and biological fluids by utilizing various approaches. These include high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), thin-layer chromatography (TLC), capillary electrophoresis, and spectrophotometric assays. Furthermore, there are concerns related to reaction time, specificity, sensitivity, performance, and affordability that restrict the utilization of some of these approaches. Nevertheless, there is extensive use of chromatographic and spectrophotometric approaches for the analysis of FMD. Spectrophotometric approaches have focused on the use of an electron donor–acceptor interaction to develop a colorimetric assay, additionally referred to as a charge transfer complex (CTC).

CTC is a partial electronic charge transfer from an electron donor to an electron acceptor molecule. It is an essential aspect in numerous biological and biochemical functions, involving the synthesis and development of drugs, transport of substances throughout the body, drug-receptor binding, and enzyme catalysis. , Pharmaceutical formulations utilized this phenomenon due to its simplicity, effectiveness, and economic cost. Furthermore, the design of precise assays and the use of suitable reagents for the quantification and identification of pharmaceuticals have enabled the effective application of this phenomenon. ,

Numerous studies have explored the application of CTC for spectrophotometric and spectrofluorometric analysis of FMD. Al-Ghannam and Belal established a spectrophotometric assay utilizing 7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane (TCNQ), concluding that optimization of reaction parameters affects complex formation. Walash et al. developed a spectrofluorometric method to assess FMD in prepared formulations and biological fluids by forming a ternary complex with certain lanthanide ions. Tsvetkova et al. evaluated various electron acceptors, including chloranil, 2,3-dichloro-5,6-dicyano-1,4-benzoquinone (DDQ), and 2,6-dichloro-4-nitrophenol, for the formation of CTC with FMD in methanol, analyzing spectrum characteristics and preferred reaction conditions. Additionally, a recent study conducted by Al Samarrai et al. created a simple and effective technique for detecting FMD in pure and pharmaceutical dosage forms using ortho-chloranil.

These studies revealed that the extent of charge-transfer interaction and resultant spectrum characteristics are significantly influenced by the electronic nature of the acceptor, each necessitating certain optimal conditions for the formation of a stable complex and reliable analytical performance. Furthermore, this investigation focuses on the development of a colorimetric assay using bromanil (BRL), 2,3,5,6-tetrabromo-1,4-benzoquinone, which acts as a novel π-acceptor for the detection of drugs. Despite being structurally related to other halogenated quinones, BRL exhibits a more polarizable electron-deficient core due to its four bromine substituents, which enhances π-acidity and enables the formation of stable n–π or π–π* complexes with electron-rich drugs.

A study conducted by Li et al. used BRL to develop an effective, simple, and sensitive spectrofluorimetric assay for the quantification of antibacterial agents. The reaction resulted in the formation of a yellow complex that demonstrated stability for at least 24 h. Moreover, the study concluded that the assay has high analytical sensitivity, selectivity, and suitability for drug analysis. Additionally, BRL offers distinct analytical advantages compared to other π-acceptors, such as DDQ, tetracyanoethylene (TCNE), and chloranil. BRL is inexpensive, chemically stable, nonhygroscopic, and commonly used in methods characterized by simplicity, minimal solvent use, and suitability for various drugs. Its sensitivity and selectivity minimize interference caused by excipients in pharmaceutical preparations, providing a practical and reproducible approach for routine quality control procedures.

This investigation aims to study the CTC between FMD and BRL, in addition to developing a simple, accurate, and reliable spectrophotometric method for quantifying FMD. The investigation involves examining the stoichiometric behavior of the reaction and the effect of the reaction time and temperature on the formation and stability of the complex. Analytical validation includes the assessment of intra- and interassay precision, accuracy, linearity, and sensitivity. Furthermore, computational characterization is employed to explain the electronic nature of the interaction. The quantitative analysis of FMD in pharmaceutical tablet formulations further demonstrates the applicability of the developed method. Moreover, no previous studies have reported BRL as a charge-transfer acceptor for FMD. The establishment of this investigation represents a novel pairing aimed at enhancing the complex characteristics of BRL that expand its performance in donor–acceptor systems, which have been previously evidenced in related CTC applications.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Famotidine (FMD) powder, purity ≥99% (Cat. no. A701177-25g, Ambeed Co., Illinois, USA); famotidine 40 mg tablets (Antodine, Amoun Pharmaceutical Co., Al Qalyubia, Egypt); bromanil (2,3,5,6-tetrabromo-1,4-benzoquinone), purity ≥98% (Cat. no. T0617, Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan); and methanol, HPLC analytical grade, purity ≥99.9% (CAS: 67-56-1, VWR Chemicals BDH, France).

Instruments

UV–visible double-beam spectrophotometer (UV-1700 PC, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan); Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Spectrum 100, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA); oven (Heraeus, Hanau, Germany); temperature-controlled water bath (TC-202, Brookfield AMETEK, Middleboro, MA, USA); sonicator (375H, Jencons Scientific Ltd., Leighton Buzzard, UK); refrigerated centrifuge (PrO-Research, Centurion Scientific Ltd., Chichester, UK); and vortex mixer (V-200, BioCote, London, UK).

Preparation of Standard and Tablet Solutions

Stock standard solutions were prepared in methanol by dissolving 84.736 mg of BRL to generate a concentration of 0.002 M and 20 mg of FMD to yield 200 μg/mL. Both solutions were sonicated for 15 min until fully dissolved. Subsequently, they were diluted with methanol to achieve the desired concentration. The sample solution (Antodine tablets) was prepared by dissolving an accurately weighed quantity equivalent to 40 mg of FMD in methanol using a vortex mixer until it was completely dissolved. The volume was then adjusted to 50 mL and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min prior to filtration. Finally, the sample was diluted to a concentration of 200 μg/mL before reaction with BRL. All measurements were adjusted to a final volume of 4 mL. In addition, methanol was used as the solvent blank, and absorbance corrections were performed against the diluted reagent.

Optimization of the CTC Assay

The optimization process involves examination of time, temperature, and stoichiometric behavior optimal for the reaction.

Effect of Time and Temperature

The effect of time and temperature on the BRL-FMD complex was examined by using a temperature-controlled water bath. The instrument controlled the temperature, while the time intervals were varied. Equal volumes of the stock standard solutions of BRL (0.002 M) and FMD (200 μg/mL) were mixed in a clean test tube with a final volume of 4 mL, and the procedure was repeated across three separate tubes. The reaction was monitored for 2 h, with absorbance measurements recorded every 15 min at λmax of 464 nm. The procedure was conducted at temperatures of 25–50 °C to identify the optimal conditions of both time and temperature, which were subsequently applied.

Stoichiometric Behavior of the Reaction

The stoichiometric ratio of the BRL-FMD complex was investigated by applying Job’s method of continuous variation. The solutions of BRL and FMD were prepared at a concentration of 0.002 M. The volume of FMD solution varied from 0.2 to 1.8 mL, while the reagent volume was correspondingly adjusted from 1.8 to 0.2 mL. The total mixture volume was maintained at 2 mL and further diluted with methanol to obtain a final volume of 4 mL. The procedure was performed in triplicate tubes, and the entire experiment was conducted in triplicate under optimal conditions: 35 °C for 60 min and measured at λmax of 464 nm. The stability constant of the complex was calculated using Job’s method.

Construction of the Calibration Curve

A calibration curve for FMD was established under optimal conditions with working concentrations of 20–120 μg/mL. Each concentration was assessed in triplicate, and the results were plotted against the corresponding concentrations, followed by regression analysis using a linear least-squares regression model. To validate linearity, correlation coefficient (r), coefficient of determination (R 2), standard error of slope (SE), relative standard deviation of slope (% RSD), variance of the slope, and F-value from analysis of variance (ANOVA). The limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) were determined using the standard deviation of the intercept (σ) and the slope (s) according to the International Council for Harmonization (ICH), the guideline for validation of analytical procedures Q2­(R2). 1 LOD=3.3×(σ)/s 2 LOQ=10×(σ)/s

Assessment of Precision, Accuracy, and Robustness

The precision, accuracy, and robustness of the assay were evaluated at an intermediate concentration of 100 μg/mL. Precision and accuracy were assessed through both intra- and interassay evaluations. The intra-assay precision and accuracy were assessed by evaluating six replicates on a single day, whereas the interassay tests were conducted by repeating the same procedure over five different days. The precision was determined by the percentage relative standard deviation (% RSD) obtained from repeated measurements. Accuracy was expressed as the percentage recovery calculated by comparing the obtained concentration to the theoretical value. The robustness of the assay was evaluated by introducing small deliberate variations in experimental conditions, including temperature ±5 °C, reaction time ±15 min, and wavelength ±5 nm. Application of the CTC Assay for Content Uniformity in Tablets

The assay was applied to assess the drug content of FMD in Antodine tablets. The average content method was utilized to determine the content of ten randomly selected tablets. The tablets were individually weighed, and the average weight was calculated prior to gently crushing them into fine powder. An accurately weighed 336 mg portion (average tablet weight), equivalent to 40 mg of FMD, was dissolved in a suitable volume of methanol using a vortex mixer until dissolved completely. The solution volume was then adjusted to 50 mL and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min prior to filtration. Thereafter, the sample was diluted to a concentration of 200 μg/mL and reacted with 0.002 M BRL at equal volumes and mixed under optimal conditions with a total volume of 4 mL. This procedure was repeated three times, with each replicate conducted in triplicate. To meet USP < 905 > uniformity of dosage units requirements, the percentage of drug content was required to fall within the acceptable range of 85–115%, and the acceptance value (AV) for content uniformity must not exceed 15 for the first 10 tablets evaluated. 5 %Drugcontent=(estimateddrugcontent/Labeleddrugcontent)×100

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy Analysis

FTIR analysis was applied to investigate the vibrational changes associated with CTC formation between FMD and BRL. Samples were separately ground and blended with potassium bromide (KBr). The obtained mixtures were compressed into translucent discs using a hydraulic press at a pressure of 5 bar for a duration of 5 min. The spectra were recorded throughout the range 4000–650 cm–1 at a resolution of 4 cm–1.

Molecular Modeling of the BRL-FMD Complex

Quantum chemical computations were performed using the General Atomic and Molecular Electronic Structure System (GAMESS) software, which combines the speed of molecular mechanics with the accuracy of quantum chemistry. Ground-state geometry optimizations of BRL, FMD, and their complex were conducted in the gas phase utilizing Density Functional Theory (DFT) at the B3LYP/6-31G basis set, yielding accurate structural parameters and frontier orbital distributions. UV–visible spectra were assessed by utilizing Time-Dependent Density Functional Theory (TD-DFT) simulations that employed the Polarizable Continuum Model (PCM) with methanol as the solvent. The excited state energy, oscillator strength, excitation energy, and calculated λmax were computed to characterize the electronic transitions in the BRL-FMD complex, while the Direct Self-Consistent Field (DirSCF) method was employed to improve computational efficiency.

Moreover, to enhance electronic structure analysis, Molecular Electrostatic Potential (MESP) mapping was conducted for prediction of reactive regions and intermolecular interactions within BRL and FMD. Regions with negative charge, characterized by electron-rich regions (heaps), and regions with positive charge, defined as electron-deficient regions (holes), were identified by computed potential fields, facilitating visualization of donor–acceptor alignment and intramolecular charge interactions. , The noncovalent interactions (NCIs) index was calculated using a scalar field that integrated electron density with reduced density gradient (RDG). It enables the evaluation of attractive zones, involving hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and steric repulsion. Finally, Hirshfeld surface (HS) analysis was performed using normalized contact distance (d norm) mapping to investigate intermolecular contact distances between atoms in relation to the van der Waals radius. This approach enabled spatial visualization of adjacent interactions within the BRL-FMD complex.

For computational-experimental comparison, the vertical excitation energies were computed by TD-DFT at the B3LYP/6-31G level with the PCM solvent model (methanol). The lowest-energy bright excitation assigned to the CT band was taken as the theoretical transition energy ETD-DFT. The corresponding theoretical wavelength was calculated as λTD-DFT­(nm) = 1240/ETD-DFT­(eV). For direct comparison with experiment, we report both the absolute wavelength difference Δλ = λexp – λTD-DFT and the energy difference ΔE = 1240/λexp – ETD-DFT. The energy (eV) of the HOMO and LUMO was calculated, and the energy gap between them was assigned for BRL, FMD, and FMD-BRL CT.

Effect of Time and Temperature

The reaction of FMD with BRL demonstrated a significant sensitivity to variations in time and temperature, as shown in Figure . The absorbance recorded over a duration of 2 h indicated that the maximum intensity was observed at 35 °C. The highest absorbance value at 35 °C was achieved after 60 min, revealing the optimal conditions for molecular interaction and stability within the BRL-FMD complex. Stoichiometric Behavior of the Reaction

The stoichiometric analysis of the BRL-FMD complex, performed using Job’s method, demonstrated that maximum absorbance was achieved at a 1:1 molar ratio. This indicates the equimolar contribution of the two components in the complex formation. Figure shows the absorbance peak when the volumes were equal, thereby confirming the formation of a stable 1:1 complex under optimal conditions. Al Samarrai et al. used ortho-chloranil to develop a 2:1 complex with FMD. Bromanil as the charge-transfer acceptor has specific analytical advantages over regularly utilized, stronger π-acceptors like DDQ and TCNE. Bromanil has a modest, regulated electron-acceptor strength, resulting in the formation of a stable 1:1 CT complex with famotidine that does not require rigorous solvent polarity control or pH modification. This creates a highly reproducible and operationally easy experiment. The stability constant of the complex was derived from Job’s method (K = 500 M–1).

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy Analysis

The FTIR spectrum of FMD revealed characteristic stretching vibrations corresponding to N–H at 3243.77, 3399.73, and 3504.42 cm–1, as displayed in Figure a. The sharp peak at 3103.94 cm–1 corresponded to aromatic C–H stretching, while the stretching vibrations observed in the region of 1639 cm–1 corresponded to CN. The FTIR spectrum of BRL in Figure b revealed characteristic stretching vibrations corresponding to the two carbonyls at 1670 and 1680 cm–1 while the medium-strong peak at 1640 cm–1 corresponded to the CC within the ring. The FTIR spectra of the FMD-BRL CTC reveal donor–acceptor interactions. In the complex, BRL’s CO bands show slight red shifts with minor broadening, indicating limited perturbation of the carbonyls. Whereas the CC stretch (1604 cm–1) shifts more (∼40 cm–1) with notable broadening, suggesting a distribution of electronic environments within the complex, consistent with partial n → π* donation from the nitrogen and sulfur lone pairs of FMD to the π* orbitals of BRL. Additionally, the N–H stretching region of Famotidine exhibits clear broadening in the CTC spectrum, reflecting participation of the NH and NH2 groups in hydrogen bonding with the carbonyl oxygens of BRL. This broadening further supports the dual contribution of hydrogen bonding and charge transfer to the stabilization of the complex. These vibrational changes collectively confirm that the π-conjugated core of Bromanil is the primary site of electronic perturbation during complex formation, while the carbonyl units experience comparatively minor involvement.

Molecular Modeling of the BRL-FMD Complex

Ground-State Structure Optimization

The ground-state geometries of BRL, FMD, and the BRL-FMD complex were effectively optimized using DFT at the B3LYP/6-31G level. The structural characteristics of BRL in its uncomplexed state (S 0) revealed significant double bonds of C3O11 and C6O12 (LB1 and LB2), and of C4C5 and C1C2 (LB3 and LB4). Additionally, the single bonds C1–Br8, C2–Br7, C4–Br10, and C5–Br9 were observed at LB5, LB6, LB7, and LB8, respectively. The BRL structure exhibited a significant geometric transformation during complex formation with a FMD (S 1). In the S 1 state, LB1, LB2, and LB4 (C42O44, C36O40, and C45C47) revealed a decreased double bond character and elongated bond lengths. While LB3 (C37C39) showed an increased double bond character and a shortened bond length compared to the S 0 state. Simultaneously, LB5 to LB8 in the S 1 state, C45–Br46, C47–Br43, C37–Br38, and C39–Br41, retained single bond characteristics. Furthermore, the geometric transition from S 0 to S 1 highlights the acceptor function of BRL and the charge transfer interaction induced by electron donation from FMD. The transition was further supported by the computed interaction energy of −27.74 kcal/mol in methanol, indicating the thermodynamic favorability of the complex. The geometrical measurements and schematic representations are illustrated in Table and Figures and . Molecular Orbital Calculations

Frontier molecular orbitals of BRL, FMD, and the BRL-FMD complex were analyzed to assess the impact of the CTC on electronic distributions and energy gaps. Figure illustrates that the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of BRL in its S 0 state is primarily localized over the conjugated ring system and carbonyl groups, whereas its lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) extends across the aromatic core, suggesting the potential for n → π* transitions. In contrast, FMD presented a HOMO localized around its donor sites, consistent with its role in electron donation. In the transition to the S 1 state, the HOMO of the BRL-FMD complex shifted toward the FMD, whereas the LUMO mainly remained on the BRL. The TD-DFT predicted a λmax of 450.7 nm for the HOMO→LUMO charge-transfer excitation with oscillator strength f = 0.0025. This excitation reflects electron donation from FMD-derived HOMO to BRL-derived LUMO. Upon complex formation, the HOMO–LUMO energy gap decreases relative to the separated components, consistent with the experimentally observed bathochromic shift. This gap reduction supports the charge-transfer character of the BRL-FMD complex and explains the emergence of a lower-energy absorption band (see Table ).

Molecular Electrostatic Potential Surface

Limitations and Future Directions

The BRL-FMD CTC spectrophotometric method is effective for routine quantification; however, its performance may be influenced by solvent conditions and potential interference from other electron-donating substances present in complex formulations. Although TD-DFT supports the electronic transition assignment, it does not provide direct structural or thermodynamic details of the interaction. Meanwhile, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) or surface plasmon resonance (SPR) would enable quantification of binding affinity, enthalpy, and entropy contributions and interaction kinetics, offering a more detailed mechanistic understanding than absorbance intensity alone.

Conclusion

This study conducts an integrated analytical and computational investigation to evaluate the charge transfer complexation between the FMD and BRL. A precise and reliable spectrophotometric method was developed and validated for quantifying FMD in pharmaceutical formulations, demonstrating high accuracy, reproducibility, and practical applicability. Furthermore, computational analysis employing DFT and TD-DFT approaches confirmed the donor–acceptor nature of the complex, revealing spatial separation of HOMO and LUMO, noncovalent stabilization by hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces, and characteristic UV–vis absorption spectra aligning with the experimentally observed charge-transfer behavior. The TD-DFT predicted a λmax of 450.7 nm for the HOMO→LUMO charge-transfer transition, while the experimental charge-transfer band appears at λmax of 464 nm, yielding a difference in wavelength (Δλ) between the experimental and TD-DFT values of 13.3 nm. This charge-transfer transition reflects electron donation from the FMD-derived HOMO to the BRL-derived LUMO. Upon complex formation, the HOMO–LUMO energy gap decreases relative to that of the separated components, consistent with the experimentally observed bathochromic shift. Moreover, a small positive Δλ (experimental red-shift vs theory) commonly reflects solvent/aggregation effects not fully captured by the PCM model. These residual shifts are attributed to solvent stabilization and the moderate acceptor strength of the BRL.
