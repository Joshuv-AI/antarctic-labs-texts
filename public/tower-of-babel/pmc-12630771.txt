No specific impact of ultra-high dose rates on radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements
Authors: Sabrina Pobiega, Eva Drevet Mulard, Véronique Ménard, Yoann Ristic, François Trompier, Gaëtan Gruel, Stéphane Marcand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ionizing radiation generates DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) that challenge DNA repair mechanisms and drive mutagenesis, chromosome rearrangements, and cell lethality. These features underlie its application in cancer therapy. DSBs arise from direct DNA ionization or the indirect action of reactive species generated by energy deposition events. While distinct particle-matter interactions are considered independent during standard irradiation timescales, the impact of extreme dose rates—comparable to the reactive species half-lives—remains unclear. Here, we used a chromosome fusion capture (CFC) assay in budding yeast to quantify radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements under varying conditions. We validated the assay’s sensitivity and observed results consistent with prior studies regarding oxygen enhancement ratios and photon energy effects. Importantly, we found that X-rays delivered at extreme dose rates produced chromosome rearrangements at frequencies indistinguishable from standard irradiations. These findings suggest that ultra-high dose rates do not alter the nature or frequency of radiation-induced DSBs, providing insights for FLASH-irradiation protocols.

Introduction

Ionizing radiation refer to particles and electromagnetic waves (photons) whose energy is well above the electronvolt (eV) and thereby sufficient to remove tightly bound electrons from atoms, creating ions. They are widely encountered in medical imaging, cancer treatment, industrial applications, and natural or accidental environmental exposure. Their marked impact on living cells arises from their propensity to break covalent bounds. On chromosomal DNA, radiation-induced damage challenges the DNA repair mechanisms protecting the genome. More specifically, exposure to ionizing radiation can generate coincident DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) within a single cell and thereby favours the resealing of DNA ends stemming from distinct breaks by the Nonhomologous End Joining repair pathway (NHEJ)1,2. Such inaccurate repair events result in chromosome rearrangements, including mitotically unstable dicentric and acentric chromosomes. This NHEJ-dependent mutagenesis stems from NHEJ’s limited ability to remain accurate when confronted to multiple DSBs simultaneously. It is the main driver of radiation-induced cell lethality and forms the basis for the use of ionizing radiation in cancer treatment.

Along they pass through cells, ionizing radiation deposits their energy primarily through multiple ionization events. Each energy deposition event can damage DNA directly via ionization and indirectly by generating a transient, localized high concentration of reactive species—with timescales ranging from a few nanoseconds to a few microseconds and a diffusion range of a few nanometers3,4. DSBs arise from simultaneous attacks on both strands. The frequency of DSBs is proportional to the energy deposited, which corresponds the absorbed dose of ionizing radiation. The co-occurrence of DSBs within the same cell, and consequently the formation of NHEJ-dependent chromosome rearrangements, depends on the absorbed dose, the dose rate (radiation intensity), and the genome size. These processes are also influenced by the radiation quality, specifically the linear energy transfer of the ionizing particles traversing the cell nucleus.

The timescale of standard irradiation is much longer than the half-life of the reactive species generated by ionizing radiation (minutes to seconds versus microseconds to picoseconds, respectively). In this context, energy deposition events initiated by distinct particle/photon-matter interactions are thought to be independent, as they occur sequentially. However, whether this independence holds when the radiation dose is delivered within a timescale comparable to the lifetime of the transient reactive species remains unknown. For instance, co-occurring reactive species from distinct tracks may cross-react, potentially altering their effects on organic molecules, including DNA5. This question is relevant to better understand how ionizing radiation induces DSBs and chromosome rearrangements, as well as for refining the molecular basis of high or ultra-high dose rate irradiation protocols currently being developed for cancer therapy, known as FLASH-radiotherapy3,6–8.

To investigate the impact of extreme dose rate on radiation-induced DNA damage, we took advantage of a recently developed genetic assay designed to capture and quantify chromosome fusions in budding yeast (Fig. 1A). This chromosome fusion capture (CFC) assay relies on the controlled inactivation of one centromere to rescue unstable dicentric chromosome rearrangements9. It is sensitive enough to quantify the basal rate of chromosome fusions occurring in unexposed wild-type cells. Our previous study using the CFC assay demonstrated that dose–response curves for photon irradiation (gamma or X-ray) follow a linear-quadratic model and are sensitive to dose fractionation and dose rate reduction9, consistent with observations from reference cytogenetic methods. Here, we further validated this assay and showed that the frequency of radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements was sensitive to oxygen pressure, with an oxygen enhancement ratio aligning with previous observations in other organisms. The occurrence of chromosome rearrangements was unaffected by the incident photon energy up to 18 MeV, consistent with earlier studies. We also determined the frequency of chromosome rearrangements induced by X-rays delivered within a few tens of nanoseconds. Remarkably, this extreme dose rate produced chromosome rearrangements at frequencies indistinguishable from those induced by conventional irradiations, regardless of oxygen levels. Our findings suggest that extremely high dose rates do not alter the nature, spatial distribution, or frequency of the radiation-induced DSBs in a manner that affects chromosome rearrangements.

Results

Oxygen enhances radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements

The CFC assay allows to quantify radiation induced NHEJ-dependent chromosomal rearrangements in a simple eukaryote9. This genetic assay uses haploid yeast cells where a native centromere, CEN6 on chromosome 6, can be inactivated with the Cre recombinase. The loss of CEN6 in cells where chromosome 6 is intact creates an acentric chromosome whose mis-segregation during mitosis leads to cell death. By contrast, if the chromosome 6 has rearranged with another chromosome to form unstable dicentric chromosomes, the loss of CEN6 restores the stability of these rearranged chromosomes and thereby cell viability as long as no essential gene was lost during rearrangement. Thus, cell viability following the loss of CEN6 provides a proxy for the frequency of radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements.

Here we used the CFC assay to test whether oxygen pressure synergizes with ionizing radiation in inducing chromosome rearrangements. Cells grown to saturation in liquid medium were either maintained in normoxia or depleted of oxygen at the time of irradiation (gamma rays from a 137Cs source). The tested doses of 20 and 40 Gy were comparatively low given the size of the yeast genome (1.4 × 107 bp folded within a 2–3 µm3 nucleus) and were previously estimated to lead to less than one chromosome rearrangement per cell on average. In both conditions normoxia and hypoxia, irradiation led to a large increase in survival to CEN6 loss relative to non-irradiated cells (Fig. 1B,C). Survival remained low in cells lacking the NHEJ ligase Lig4LigaseIV, indicating that NHEJ produced most of the captured radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements from wild-type cells. However, survival to CEN6 loss and thereby the frequency of radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements were markedly lower in cells depleted of oxygen relative to cells maintained in normoxia. This finding indicates that oxygen enhances the formation of chromosome rearrangements induced by ionizing radiation. The observed oxygen enhancement ratio of 2–threefold is undistinguishable from previous estimates obtained with mammalian cells10–12. This similarity is consistent with oxygen acting early at the time of ionization and DNA damage, a chemical effect that should be without species or cell type specificity.

Radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements and incident photon energy

To further validates the CFC assay as an appropriate tool to study the effects of ionizing radiation on genome stability, we next asked whether the energy of the incident photons can impact the frequency of the induced chromosome rearrangements. We previously observed that incident photons of effective energies ranging from 20 to 662 keV led to similar frequencies of chromosomal rearrangements in yeast9. Here, we explored the impact of higher energies. Cells grown to saturation in liquid medium and maintained in normoxia were irradiated with a medical linear accelerator producing X-rays of distinct range of effective energy, up to 18 MeV. As a reference, cells were irradiated with gamma rays from a 137Cs source (662 keV). We observed that survival to CEN6 loss and thereby the frequency of radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements were in proportion to the absorbed dose but unimpacted by the increase of energies of the incident photons (Fig. 2A). This finding is consistent with previous observations in human cells13. Radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements at ultra-high dose rates

Next, we asked whether delivering the dose of irradiation within a timescale close to the timescale of the chemical events leading to DNA breakage could change the outcome on the formation of chromosome rearrangements. To this aim, we used a generator delivering X-rays in a single pulse of 32–52 ns with a mean effective energy of approximately 700 keV (Fig. 3A). Cells grown to saturation in liquid medium and maintained in normoxia or in hypoxia were irradiated at doses ranging from approximately 5 to 15 Gy. In parallel, the same saturated cell cultures were irradiated with a medical generator delivering the same range of X-rays doses in five minutes (6 MV, photons of effective energies up to 6 MeV). In both cases, the absorbed doses were further estimated a posteriori by alanine passive dosimetry. We observed that survival to CEN6 loss in response to irradiation and thereby the frequency of radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements were not specifically impacted by the extreme dose rate of irradiation compare to more standard exposure times, whether in hypoxic conditions or at 21% oxygen (Fig. 3B; regression analysis showed no statistically significant differences between the dose–response curves obtained for the two dose-rate modalities, with p > 0.05 for the intercepts as well as the linear and quadratic terms). Discussion

In this study, we further validated the CFC assay as a relevant and cost-effective tool to explore the impact of various sources of ionizing radiation on chromosomes in living cells. Yeast is a robust and fast-growing unicellular eukaryote organism whose chromatin organisation and DNA repair mechanisms are similar to ones found in human cells. It is therefore well-suited to address early events induced by ionizing radiation: (1) the immediate DNA damage co-incident with energy deposition and (2) its repair and the associated mutagenesis. The induction of multiple DSBs within cells is a distinctive feature of ionizing radiation and its most problematic product since their repair directly promotes NHEJ inaccuracy and consequently the formation of unstable rearranged chromosomes. The CFC assay is specific of this end-result and thus centred on the most relevant issue regarding the exposure of living organisms to ionizing radiation.

We observed that the frequency of radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements is enhanced by oxygen and unsensitive to the energy of incident photons. Both findings match with previous works using human cells13, showing that the early events associated to the energy deposition and consecutive to the initial photon-matter interaction are likely the same in yeast cells and in human cells. The dissolved oxygen present within the cell nuclei is thought to enhance and cross-react with the reactive species produced along the paths of the ionizing particles (e.g., hydrated electrons and hydrogen radicals), which leads to an increase of the local concentration of ROS (superoxide radical and hydrogen peroxide), therefore increasing the likelihood that the two strands of a DNA molecule present in this environment will be ruptured10–12. Such synergy with oxygen is likely to be ubiquitous and can be the main cause of the well-established relative resistance of hypoxic tumours to radiotherapy. Similarly, the effect of the energy deposition events consecutive to the initial photon-matter interaction is likely to be the same in all organisms, predicting a general insensitivity of chromosome rearrangements to the energy of the incident photons above a few tens of keV.

These observations encouraged us to explore an extreme situation where cells absorbed several Gy within tens of nanoseconds. Strikingly, this ultra-high dose rate does not increase or decrease the frequency of radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements compared to standard dose rates. At the molecular scale, this suggests that quasi-co-incident energy deposition events do not enhance or damp each other and remain chemically independent. A simple explanation for this non-interference regime at the doses we tested can be that energy deposition events stemming from distinct photon-matter interactions are too far apart to significantly overlap – with an average nearest-neighbour distance predicted to be orders of magnitude above the diffusion range of the transient reactive species (Fig. 3C). This non-interference regime might break down at higher doses, but dose escalation rapidly reduces survival, preventing reliable measurements. In our yeast model, this survival effect precludes accurate estimation of chromosome fusion frequencies beyond a few tens of Gy9.

Consistent with our findings, Barghouth et al.12 showed that dose-rates at ~ 1000 Gy·s⁻1 do not alter the frequency or structure of radiation-induced chromosome translocations in human fibroblasts compared to conventional dose rates, across doses up to 20 Gy of 5.5 MeV electrons. By contrast, Garty et al.14,15 reported that 8 Gy of 9 MeV electrons delivered at ~ 600 Gy·s⁻1 reduced dicentric yields in human lymphocytes compared to lower dose rates (< 90 Gy·s⁻1). Notably, this effect was absent at 3 Gy, suggesting a dose-dependent threshold. These observations point to model-specific and technical factors. In particular, the delivery mode can be critical: whereas we delivered the entire dose in a single pulse, Garty et al.14 applied 8 Gy in three pulses of 2.7 Gy at 180 Hz, possibly generating distinct radiochemical dynamics.

This conclusion is relevant for the on-going development of FLASH radiotherapy protocols to target solid tumours and in which doses are delivered within a timescale going from a few microseconds to less than one second. Our data predict that those protocols will induce the same frequency of chromosome rearrangements than standard protocols of radiotherapy, other things being equal3,12. Although these results show that changes in chromosomal rearrangement rates, or even double-strand break rates, do not appear to be one of the mechanisms behind the observed reduction in side-effects on healthy tissue due to FLASH irradiation, they are consistent with a similar efficacy on tumour sterilization between this type of exposure and standard radiotherapy protocols. This suggest that the therapeutic index benefits of FLASH therapy arise from mechanisms other than altered chromosomal damage.

Materials & methods

Yeast strains

Strains used in this study are Lev1559 (MATa proLEU2-loxP-CEN6-klURA3-loxP-orfLEU2-pGAL1-CRE::NAT) and Lev1605 (MATa proLEU2-loxP-CEN6-klURA3-loxP-orfLEU2-pGAL1-CRE::NAT lig4∆::klTRP1). Both are derived from the W303-1a background (ade2-1 trp1-1 ura3-1 leu2-3,112 his3-11,15 can1-100 RAD5).

Chromosome fusion capture assay (CFC)

To determine survival to CEN6 loss, cells grown on rich glucose medium (YPD) plates were picked and plated on glucose medium plates lacking uracil to counter-select rare cells having already lost CEN6 due to Cre background expression in the absence of galactose. After 24 h of growth at 30 °C, distinct re-streaks were used to inoculate distinct liquid cultures in YPD. Each culture was then grown to saturation for 3 days at 30 °C and placed in standard six-well plates (1 ml/well; an independent saturated cell culture for each well). Cells that will be irradiated in normoxia were kept in constant agitation prior to irradiation. Cells that will be irradiated in hypoxia were placed in a sealed plastic bag with an oxygen quencher (Oxoid AnaeroGen Compact from Thermo Scientific) for one hour prior to irradiation and were irradiated with the sealed bag. After irradiation, cells were plated on galactose medium plates lacking leucine (approximately 5 × 107 cells per plate). Colonies are counted after 5 days at 30 °C. The number of counted colonies can be slightly impacted by the cell culture density, the growth medium and the threshold size for a colony to be counted as viable since radiation-induced chromosome rearrangements generate fast-growing and slow-growing clones. Each data point comes from an independent re-streak and an independent cell culture spread on a single plate.

Irradiations

Gamma-ray irradiations were performed using a GSR D1 irradiator from the GSM Company. This self-shielded irradiator contains four 137Cs sources with a total activity of approximately 180.28 TBq in March 2014 emitting 0.662 MeV gamma-rays. Cells in 6-well plates were irradiated with a dose rate of 2.5 ± 0,12 Gy/min in terms of air kerma. The irradiation cell is 40 by 40 cm with the sources irradiating from below. Cell plates were placed horizontally on an aluminium plate of 2 mm and were irradiated with a field homogeneity within the isodose 95.

The ultra-high dose rate irradiations were performed with a Marx generator that delivers a single flash of X-ray of total duration of 40 ns with mean energy about 0.7 MeV and a maximal energy of 5 MeV. All the samples were irradiated during a unique X-ray flash, but at different distances from the point source emission of the X-rays (Tantalum target) to vary the absorbed dose. The beam axis being horizontal, the six-well plates were therefore irradiated horizontally by the side. At each distance, the plates were placed behind a 5 mm PMMA vertical plate to insure electronic equilibrium. Three plates were irradiated at each distance. The plates were vertically pile up. To guarantee the dose homogeneity only three well were filled. Dosimetry was performed in terms of air kerma with passive dosimeter (RPL and alanine), because of saturation occurring with ionization chamber. Maximal relative dose variation in each well was at maximum at 6%. Relative variation of average dose between the filled well at a given distance was ranging between 3 and 9%.

4 to 18 MV X-ray irradiations were performed using an Elekta Synergy LINAC. To ensure a proper comparison with the Flash irradiation, all cells were exposed in a similar configuration during a unique irradiation. 6-well plates were placed at 3 different distances from the LINAC target to vary the delivered dose and the dose rate. The beam axis was also set-up horizontally with the same plate of PMMA for the electronic equilibrium. The field size was 30 by 30 cm at 1 m. Three plates were irradiated at each distance. The plates were horizontally pile up. Duration of irradiations was 5 min. Relative uncertainties on doses are estimated at 5% (k = 2). Maximal relative dose variation in each well was at maximum at 3%. Relative variation of average dose between the 3 filled well in a given plate was 1%.

Dosimetry

For the LINAC and the GSR D1, the reference dosimetry was performed in the same condition with a cylindrical ionizing chamber (model 31010 by PTW) under electronic equilibrium. The ionization chamber has a cavity of 0.125 cm3 and was calibrated with gamma 137Cs and 60Co in terms of air kerma with the PTB reference facility number 210382701. Polarity and ion recombination were measured for each source. Each measurement was corrected by the kT,P factor to account for variations in temperature and atmospheric pressure. The reference air kerma rate was used to set up the distance in both configurations.

For the three facilities, the reference dosimetry in the 6-well plates was performed with alanine dosimetry. Alanine pellets of 5 mm diameter purchased from gamma service company were read out with an electron spin resonance spectrometer (MS5000X type). Pellets used to establish the calibration curves were irradiated in air under electronic equilibrium according to reference in air kerma provided by the ionization chamber PTW model 31,010 with 6 MV photon from the Elekta LINAC. Air kerma is provided is an uncertainty estimated at 5% (k = 2).

Dose homogeneity for all irradiations set-up was determined with radiophotoluminescence (RPL) dosimeters (GD-351 type provided by Technol corporation). Calibration was established with RPL dosimeters irradiated in the air kerma reference conditions with 4 MV photon from the Elekta LINAC.

Statistics

To compare the dose–response relationships between exposures, we conducted a regression analysis with interaction terms. The model included terms for the survival rate to CEN6 loss, the absorbed dose based on alanine dosimetry, the irradiator facility (LINAC or Marx generator), the oxygenation condition and their interaction to assess differences in intercepts, linear and quadratic terms. The analyses were conducted using glm function of R software (R version 4.4.2).
