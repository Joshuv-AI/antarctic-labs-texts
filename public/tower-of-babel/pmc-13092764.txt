Virtual transition education of rural adolescents with congenital heart disease: A pilot study
Authors: RN Alyssa Chappell, RN Jody Gingrich, Jesse Batara, Adrienne H Kovacs, Andrew S Mackie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAdolescents and young adults (AYA) living in rural communities have lower access to tertiary pediatric care than those in urban settings. However, all AYA with congenital heart disease (CHD) would benefit from transition programming to develop knowledge of their CHD and self-management skills in preparation for adult care. Previous studies have evaluated in-person delivery of transition programs. We sought to investigate the effectiveness of a virtual nurse-led pediatric-to-adult transition education program.Design and methodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study investigating the impact of virtual nurse-led transition education on individuals aged 16–25 with CHD who receive cardiology care in outreach cardiology clinics in Alberta, Canada. Individual teaching sessions were led by a cardiology nurse and delivered virtually in a single 1-hour session. Participants were also provided access to the iHeartChange transition website. A pre-post analysis was performed with comparisons between baseline, 1-month, and 6-month follow-up. Outcome measures were CHD knowledge (MyHeart Scale), self-efficacy (Generalized Self-Efficacy Scale), and self-management skills (Transition-Q).ResultsTwenty-five participants (56% female, mean age 17.7 ± 2.2 years) completed the virtual transition education and measures at baseline and both follow-ups. Improvements were observed across all outcomes from baseline to 1 month and from baseline to 6 months, except for self-efficacy, which showed an improvement between baseline and 6 months.ConclusionsNurse-led virtual transition education can be effective at improving rural AYA’s knowledge of their CHD and self-efficacy and self-management skills.Practice implicationsPediatric cardiology programs should implement virtual nurse-led transition education for patients who live remotely.Keywords: Education; Heart defects, congenital; Continuity of care; Adolescents; Virtual care

Introduction

Adolescents and young adults (AYA) living with congenital heart disease (CHD) need to transition from pediatric to adult care as they enter adulthood. In addition to the transfer of care from pediatric providers to adult-oriented providers, AYA ideally need to learn how to confidently navigate the healthcare system and independently manage their health and heart condition.1 In order to prepare patients for this transition, many centres have developed formal transition programs. Goals of clinical transition programming include (1) improving adolescents’ knowledge about their CHD; (2) supporting self-management and self-advocacy skills, which require some disease knowledge; (3) coaching how to navigate a complex medical system; and (4) coordinating integration into adult-centered care.2

Remote location of residence is an important social determinant of health that should be considered during the transition period for AYA living with CHD.2 CHD transition programs have historically been provided in-person in tertiary care centers.1, 3, 4 However, for rural AYA, travel to tertiary care sites can be difficult due to long distances and the time and cost associated with this. In general, for rural AYA, access to health care services is reduced, leading to health disparities compared to their urban peers.5 In the US, those living remotely are more likely to be of Hispanic ethnicity, have lower socioeconomic status (SES), lower educational attainment, and lack health insurance.6 Likewise, in Canada, patients living in remote communities are more likely to be Indigenous and of low SES.7 McGrath and colleagues reported that among patients from rural areas needing to drive a median of 77 miles (124 km) to attend an ACHD center, financial losses related to taking time off work were the most common barrier to accessing in-person visits.8

The province of Alberta, Canada has a large rural population;9 approximately 1/5 of the population lives outside the two major urban centers,10 where tertiary cardiac care centers are located. To increase access to pediatric cardiology care within Alberta and northern Canada, outreach clinics in five smaller communities have been established. However, these outreach clinics have limited cardiology nurse support and lack time and staff to deliver in-person transition education during clinic visits.

To mitigate the distance between remote populations and tertiary centers, virtual care has been used to provide health care to patients living with cardiovascular disease in rural Canada11 and those participating in cardiac rehabilitation programs.12, 13 Positive impacts have been demonstrated in relation to patient self-management and quality of life, among others.11 However, virtual nurse-led transition education has not yet been studied. Therefore, we sought to investigate the efficacy of virtual nurse-led transition education for rural AYA living with CHD.

Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to determine the impact of virtual nurse-led transition education on AYA’s knowledge of their heart. Secondary objectives were to evaluate the impact of education on AYA’s self-efficacy and self-management skills, and to assess the impact of sex and age on study outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This was a prospective cohort study. Ethics approval was obtained from the University of Alberta Health Research Ethics Board (Pro00074194). This study was registered with Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04090827). All participants provided written informed consent or assent alongside parental consent as applicable prior to taking part.

Study population

Inclusion criteria were age 16–25 years, having CHD, and followed in one of five Stollery Children’s Hospital Cardiology Outreach Clinics (High Level, Fort McMurray, Grand Prairie, Red Deer, Yellowknife). Patients were excluded if they had a reading or comprehension level < grade 6 based on parent/guardian self-report, a previous heart transplantation, or had already been transferred to adult cardiology care. CHD severity was determined using the 32nd Bethesda Conference Classification.14

Participant recruitment

Clinic patient lists were screened for eligibility. Participants were recruited in-person at clinic visits or virtually, with the first point of contact being from a provider within the clinical care team of the participant. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap15, 16, 17 electronic data capture tools hosted and supported by the Women and Children’s Health Research Institute at the University of Alberta. Participants were enrolled between July 2020 and June 2023, with 6-month outcome follow-up to January 2024. Completion of study questionnaires was independent of the time of transfer to adult care.

Outcome measures

Baseline questionnaires were completed prior to the transition education session and prior to receiving access to the iHeartChange website. Gift cards ($25) were provided for completing each of the 2 follow-up questionnaires.

Primary outcome

Change in MyHeart Scale score between baseline and 1 and 6 months. The MyHeart Scale3, 4, 18 is an 8-item questionnaire to assess participant’s knowledge of their heart condition and treatment. The scale has previously demonstrated content and face validity, and is scored as a percentage (numerator/denominator x 100), with the denominator varying depending on the diagnosis and clinical course of the participant (having had cardiac surgery or catheterizations, being prescribed cardiac medications, requiring antibiotic prophylaxis etc.).3

Secondary outcomes

1) Change in Generalized Self-efficacy Scale (GSE) score from baseline to 1 and 6 months.19 The GSE is a 10-item questionnaire using a 4-point Likert scale with demonstrated reliability in measuring self-efficacy and internal consistency ranging from 0.75 to 0.91(Cronbach’s alpha).20 GSE scores predict multiple quality of life domains, including emotional, physical and social.19, 21 Scores range from 1.0 to 4.0 per question, with higher scores reflecting greater self-efficacy;19 2) Change in Transition-Q score from baseline to 1 and 6 months.22 The Transition-Q is a generic self-management skills scale for use with adolescents diagnosed with a chronic health condition with a demonstrated internal consistency coefficient of 0.85 (Cronbach's alpha) and 0.90 test-retest reliability. 22 It is a 14-item questionnaire using a 3-point Likert scale. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores reflecting greater transition readiness.22

Virtual transition education

All participants received the virtual nurse-led transition education. One Registered Nurse (RN) who is experienced working with AYA living with CHD conducted all transition teaching sessions. The virtual education occurred during a single session with the RN using secure online Zoom23 videoconferencing at a time convenient to the participant to minimize study burden. Education sessions were completed within 1 month of study enrollment. An Enterprise version of Zoom23 videoconferencing was used which abides by encryption and security requirements to deliver virtual health care in Alberta.24 Participants were encouraged to take part in the virtual transition intervention independently to promote independence and self-management. Prior to each education session, the nurse completed a thorough chart review to become familiar with the participant’s CHD diagnoses, cardiac surgical procedures and cardiac catheterizations, current cardiac medications and doses, need for endocarditis prophylaxis, and potential future complications. The virtual teaching sought to address both education about the participant’s CHD (steps i-v below) and self-management skills (steps vi-xi below). The nurse used a log to confirm that all elements were covered during the session.

Standardization of the nurse-led intervention was accomplished by using previously developed,30 professionally rendered videos to illustrate ideal teaching sessions using a standardized patient (actor) with an experienced cardiology nurse, and completion of a log by the nurse to document that all the above steps were done. The standardized components of Just Trac It25 also contributed to consistency of intervention delivery.

iHeartChange website

Participants were provided with a login to the Intervention to Help Emerging Adults Ready for Transition Change (iHeartChange)27, 31 website to access on their own time. iHeartChange (www.iheartchange.org) is a website developed for AYA with CHD.32 Content is divided into sections including “CHD and Your Life” (driving, school and work, smoking, healthy eating, exercise, etc.), “Coping with CHD”, “Taking Charge of Your Health”, and “Connecting with Other Patients.” Launched in 2011, adolescent feedback in a pilot study was very positive; 91% of adolescents found the website useful and 86% would use it again if available.32 Participants were encouraged to use the website via reminder emails sent at 2- and 4- months post enrolment.

Sample size

We required a total of 24 paired observations to achieve 80% power to detect a mean pre- to post-completion difference of 15 points in the MyHeart score (standard deviation = 25) among participants who completed the virtual transition education, using a two-sided paired t-test at a significance level of 0.05. We predicted that 35% would not complete the 6-month questionnaires based on our experience in the CHAPTER 1 and 2 studies, therefore 37 participants were enrolled.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and outcomes measures were summarized as mean (SD) and n (%). Within-group changes from baseline to 1 and 6 months were tested with paired t-tests; effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for dependent samples with 95% CIs. Further analysis used linear mixed-effects models (PROC MIXED) with random participant intercepts and time (1–6 months) adjusting for baseline score. Only those participants who completed the virtual transition education session and completed the baseline, 1-month and 6-month questionnaire outcome measures were analyzed. All analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Of 96 potentially eligible patients the study team attempted to approach, 37 (39%) consented to participate. Of those who consented, nine participants withdrew from the study prior to receiving the intervention. One participant was lost to contact prior to receiving the intervention. The remaining 27 participants completed the virtual transition education session and were followed for 6 months, with 25 participants completing both 1 and 6-month follow-up questionnaires. The virtual transition education sessions had a mean duration of 71 min (SD = 8 min). Most participants (80%) attended the virtual teaching sessions independently, however 20% had a parent or guardian join them for part of the session as per participant request. No major technical issues were encountered, and all virtual sessions were completed. Few minor technical issues were encountered, such as the nurse not being able to screen share during one session so instead securely sent the cardiac review diagram via encrypted email for the participant to review simultaneously during the session, occasional “freezing” of the screen during 2 other sessions due to assumed poor internet connections, and in another session the inability of the participant to connect their laptop to the internet so instead joined the virtual session with their smartphone using cellular data. When creating a summary of their health information with the nurse, most participants (92%) completed this using Just TRAC It25 methods, with few (8%) participants instead creating a MyHealth Passport26 due to not having a cell phone.

Of the 25 participants who completed measures at all three timepoints, MyHeart Scale scores increased significantly from baseline (71.91 ± 14.91) to 1 month (84.02 ± 16.27) and were slightly lower at 6 months but still significantly higher than at baseline (81.36 ± 17.35) (Table 2, Fig. 2). GSE scores were similar at 1-month compared to baseline but increased at 6 months (Table 2, Fig. 3). The mean Transition-Q score at baseline was 59.0 ± 12.7 and increased to 65.6 ± 15.0 at 1 month and 70.3 ± 17.1 at 6 months (Table 2, Fig. 4). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to have evaluated virtual nurse-led transition education for AYA with CHD. Key findings from this study are 1) virtual education was feasible for AYA living in remote locations; 2) the virtual education improved participants’ knowledge of their CHD, as well as self-reported self-efficacy and self-management skills 6 months’ post intervention; 3) nurse preparation and teaching time was comparable to our team’s previously published in-person sessions.3, 4

Study participants lived hundreds of miles/kilometers from the main hospital, therefore accessing in-person nurse-led transition education, the standard of care at our center, would have been difficult for them. Providing nurse-led education through virtual videoconferencing allowed us to overcome this geographic challenge and improve access to care. Prior to this study, these participants would not have received any transition-related nurse-led education.

There is a paucity of literature on virtual CHD teaching. Liddle et al. reported on online videoconferencing to deliver transition-related education to adolescents with CHD.33 In contrast to the current study, cardiologists delivered the education and 3D patient-specific digital heart models were used.33 Medical knowledge of participants significantly improved, and feasibility was shown.33 In a similar study, Clarke et al. demonstrated that the use of a “4D” Passport alongside 3D patient-specific models derived from MRI datasets, combined with a tele-educational session delivered by a pediatric cardiologist, increased adolescents’ knowledge of their CHD.34 Integration of these studies into clinical practice may be limited, however, due to availability and cost of cardiologist time to deliver educational interventions and the resources needed to produce patient-specific 3D heart models, in comparison to lower cost nursing time and digitally displayed 2D heart anatomy diagrams.

Self-efficacy, measured using the GSE, increased at the 6-month timepoint, but was similar at 1-month compared to baseline. This scale assesses general coping and problem solving in stressful situations, which was not a topic specifically addressed within the virtual nurse-led transition teaching curriculum, although may be an important contributor to transition readiness.18 It may be that having knowledge of one’s CHD and improved self-management skills in turn helps develop a sense of self-efficacy.

Self-management, measured using the Transition-Q, significantly increased at 1-month and 6-month timepoints compared to baseline. Age was associated with higher Transition-Q scores, suggesting that older participants demonstrated higher transition readiness, which is similar to findings with adolescents living with rheumatological conditions.35 Self-management skills develop over time and with maturity, hence AYA may feel more confident in arranging their own prescriptions or travelling independently to appointments as they age.35

CHD knowledge, measured using the MyHeart Scale, increased significantly at 1-month and 6-months compared to baseline. Age did not significantly predict MyHeart scores. This highlights that transition education should be individualized without using age to make assumptions about the CHD knowledge of AYA. However, developmental ability should be taken into account when providing education to AYA.36

We found that only 8% of participants’ cardiology clinic letters mentioned the topic of transition, which may reflect a lack of discussion between cardiologists and their AYA patients about this topic. Ideally, transition preparation begins by 12–13 years of age.37 However, pediatric cardiologists practicing in outreach clinic settings may have less interdisciplinary support than they would at their main campus to deliver team-based transition support.

Nurse-led transition education to adolescents living with CHD has been shown to reduce the likelihood for delays in adult cardiology care following graduation from pediatric care.4 The participants in the current study were already at a higher risk for lapses in cardiology care, due to the influence of remote location of residence.38 Offering outreach clinics, as well as nurse-led virtual transition education may mitigate the impacts of a patient’s rural location on their access to age-appropriate healthcare.38

Limitations

Limitations of the current study include the single-center design and the absence of a control group. All interventions were conducted by a single nurse, although the protocol was followed diligently using a nursing intervention log to ensure intervention fidelity, and frequently reviewed by the study team. The Transition-Q22 and GSE19 questionnaires measure self-reported health behaviors, which may not reflect what participants actually do in daily life. However, these are well-validated instruments and no superior measure of health-related behaviors exists. Delivering virtual care via Zoom23 depends on a reliable internet connection in order for the healthcare provider to interact with participants seamlessly which is not always available in rural settings. The word cloud (Fig. 5) is not a method of formal qualitative data analysis and doesn’t provide any context regarding the relationships between words or necessarily convey the importance of each word. Finally, the nurse-led virtual transition education was delivered and evaluated for AYA participants without significant cognitive or developmental deficits; these findings may not apply to those with neurodiversity.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that a brief, nurse-led virtual 1-on-1 transition education session can be effective at increasing CHD knowledge and self-management skills among rural AYA with CHD. Pediatric cardiology programs should implement virtual nurse-led transition education for patients who live remotely.
