Extending Characterizations of Multivariate Laws via Distance Distributions
A. Betken, A. Marjanovic, K. Proksch

Abstract
We extend a theorem of Maa, Pearl, and Bartoszyński, which links equality of interpoint distance distributions to equality of underlying multivariate distributions, beyond the restrictive class of homogeneous, translation-invariant distance functions. Our approach replaces geometric assumptions on the distance with analytic conditions: volume-regularity of distance-induced balls, Lebesgue differentiability with respect to the distance, and bounded centered oscillations of densities. Under these conditions, equality of interpoint distance distributions continues to imply equality of the generating laws. The result persists under monotone continuous transformations of homogeneous, translation-invariant distances, recovering the original statement, and it extends to compact Riemannian manifolds equipped with the geodesic metric. We further develop a quantitative version of the theorem, i.e. inequalities that connect discrepancies of interpoint distance distributions to the L 2 L^{2} -distance between densities, and obtain explicit rates under Ahlfors α \alpha -regularity of the distance function and β \beta -Hölder continuity of densities, capturing dependence on dimensionality. Several representative examples illustrate the applicability of the generalization to domain-specific distances used in modern statistics. The examples include non-homogeneous non-translation invariant distances such as Canberra, entropic distances and the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity.

Introduction

Interpoint distance-based approaches to two-sample testing have been widely used due to their applicability in high-dimensional settings. A variety of methods rely on comparing interpoint distance distributions between and within samples. These include the energy distance
SR+ 04
, the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) based test by Gretton et al.
GBR+ 06

GBR+ 12

MMV 19

In this article, we extend Theorem
1
beyond homogeneous and translation invariant distance functions: under certain volume and oscillation controls of balls in the considered distance function and assuming Lebesgue differentiability of densities with respect to this distance function, equality of the within- and between-sample distance distributions ensures equality of laws. We complement this finding with computable
L^{2}
-error bounds expressed via Kolmogorov discrepancies of the three distance distributions. The key idea is to replace the geometric assumptions on the distance, which guarantee the well-behavedness of the balls uniformly in space as the radius shrinks, with more direct analytic conditions on the volumes of the balls. We provide sufficient criteria under which the equivalence of interpoint distance distributions implies equality of the underlying distributions, and show that these conditions are satisfied for a wide range of distance functions of practical interest. Several corollaries and examples are provided to illustrate the scope of the generalization. The article is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the standing assumptions (volume-regularity of
h
-balls,
h
-Lebesgue differentiability, bounded centered oscillation), states the generalization, and derives corollaries that recover the original theorem and extend the result to compact manifolds. Furthermore, in Section 2 we complement the qualitative result with quantitative stability bounds and, under Ahlfors
\alpha
-volume growth and
\beta
-Hölder regularity of the densities, derive dimension-aware rates. In Section 3 we collect illustrative cases, i.e. domain-specific distances, and show that these generate volume-regular balls. Lastly, we outline open questions and future research directions in Section
4
.

2
Generalization

To formulate our generalization of Theorem
1
, we first specify what we mean by a generalized distance function and the associated notion of balls and their volumes. This isolates the minimal structural ingredients on which our analytic conditions will later be imposed.

Definition 1
.

(Generalized distance function, ball and volume)

A function
h:\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to[0,\infty)
is called generalized distance function if it is positive definite, i.e. satisfies the identity of indiscernibles:

\displaystyle h(x,y)=0\Leftrightarrow x=y\quad\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

The ball with center
x
and radius
t
with respect to
h
(or
h
-ball) is the set

B_{t}^{(h)}(x):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}|h(x,y)<t\}

and the corresponding volume function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{k}\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is defined by
\Phi(x,t):=\mu(B_{t}^{(h)}(x))
.

Symmetry of the distance function
h
is not necessary for the proof of our main generalization (Theorem
3
), but it is additionally assumed for the development of the bounds in Theorem
4
. Hence,
h
-balls according to Definition
1
are, strictly speaking, right-balls.

To extend Theorem
1
beyond homogeneous, translation-invariant metrics, we need to control how volumes of balls behave for generalized distance functions. In particular,
we find that the volume
\Phi
of
h
-balls must shrink uniformly across different centers in space as their radii tend to zero. This motivates the notion of
volume-regularity
, which provides the analytic regularity of distance-induced balls needed for generalization of Theorem
1
.

Definition 2
.

(Volume-regular distance function)

A generalized distance function is called volume-regular if it satisfies the following conditions. For some
\epsilon>0
, the volume function
\Phi
is well-defined on
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times[0,\epsilon)
, i.e. small radius
h
-balls are measurable.

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Phi(x,t)=0,

(3)

and for some
\epsilon>0
and for all
0<t<\epsilon
,
\Phi
is strictly positive almost everywhere, i.e.

0<\Phi(x,t)<\infty.

(4)

Lastly, there exists at least one point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with corresponding
\epsilon>0
and
\delta:\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
, such that for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

c\delta(x,y)\leq\delta_{t}(x,y)\leq C\delta(x,y)

(5)

where
\delta_{t}(x,y):=\frac{\Phi(x,t)}{\Phi(y,t)}
for some constants
c,C>0
and for all
0<t<\epsilon
.

We note that the volume function
\Phi
is well-defined for (on
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
) measurable
h
. We further note that if condition (
5
) is fulfilled for at least one
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, then almost any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
fulfills (
5
). In particular, volume-regularity describes a space in which volumes of
h
-balls centered at different points are stably comparable as radii converge to zero and relative volume
\Phi(x,t)/\Phi(y,t)
settles for small radii to a finite, positive
\delta(x,y)
(and is dominated by
\delta(x,y)
) in almost every
x
, while allowing for a controlled divergence of
\Phi
and
\delta_{t}
for a fixed
t
as
x\rightarrow\infty
. Classical Ahlfors-regular geometries, for example, satisfy this condition, and hence imply volume-regularity. We recall the definition of Ahlfors
\alpha
-regularity.

Definition 3
.

(Ahlfors
\alpha
-regularity)

A metric measure space
(X,h,\mu)
is called (locally) Ahlfors
\alpha
-regular, if there exists an
\alpha>0
,
c,C>0
and
\epsilon>0
, such that

ct^{\alpha}\leq\mu(B^{(h)}_{t}(x))\leq Ct^{\alpha}

for any
0<t<\epsilon
and
x\in X
.

We further relax assumptions on
h
to generalize Ahlfors
\alpha
-regularity for distance functions as in Definition
1
and call the distance function
h
itself Ahlfors
\alpha
-regular (with respect to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and Lebesgue measure). If

is Ahlfors
\alpha
-regular, then
\frac{c}{C}\leq\delta_{t}(x,y)\leq\frac{C}{c}
, i.e. condition (
5

We introduce Definition
2
as a generalization of Ahlfors regularity, accommodating for distance functions for which the volume
\Phi

One further analytic condition needed for the proof of Theorem
1
is the Lebesgue differentiability relative to the homogeneous translation-invariant distance. For a generalization of the theorem we introduce the following concept of Lebesgue differentiability of a locally integrable function
f

Definition 4
.

(Lebesgue differentiability with respect to
h
)

We call

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|dy}{\Phi(x,t)}=0.

It is important to note that Lebesgue differentiability with respect to a general distance function

does not automatically follow from the assumption
f\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{k})

to satisfy conditions such as the doubling property, or more generally, the Vitali covering property.

A generalization of the classical differentiation theorem to such settings can be found e.g. in Theorem 1.8 of

on a metric measure space with doubling measure, i.e. such that it holds

\mu(B_{2t}^{(h)}(x))\leq C\mu(B_{t}^{(h)}(x))

for some

and any

Theorem 2
.

(Lebesgue differentiation theorem)

Let
f\in L^{1}_{loc}(X)
where
(X,h,\mu)
is a doubling metric measure space. Then

is Lebesgue differentiable with respect to

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\mu(B_{t}^{(h)}(x))}\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}|f(x)-f(y)|d\mu(y)=0

More generally, for a generalized distance

\mu

forms a Vitali differentiation basis for locally doubling measure

Another assumption needed for generalization of Theorem

h

Definition 5
.

(Centered oscillation)

We call the functional

A_{f}^{(h)}(x,\epsilon):=\sup_{0<t<\epsilon}\frac{1}{\Phi(x,t)}\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|dy

centered oscillation of

in

\epsilon

A_{f}^{(h)}(x,\epsilon)<C\textnormal{ \quad for almost every }x.

Uniformly bounded centered oscillation is a mild regularity condition. In fact, many classes of densities, including bounded continuous functions, Lipschitz and Hölder functions, satisfy it.

1
to volume-regular distance functions allowing for
Lebesgue differentiability of the data-generating densities with uniformly bounded, centered oscillations. More precisely, the theorem establishes that equality of interpoint distance distributions implies equality of the underlying distributions under these assumptions.

.

Let

(6)

The forward direction of the equivalence (

Let

\mathbb{P}(h(X_{1},X_{2})<t)=\mathbb{P}(h(Y_{1},Y_{2})<t)=\mathbb{P}(h(X_{3},Y_{3})<t)

for any

Since by assumption (

It holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(h(X_{1},X_{2})<t)

\frac{\mathbb{P}(h(X_{1},X_{2})<t)}{\Phi(\xi,t)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}f(x)\frac{\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}(f(y)-f(x))dy}{\Phi(\xi,t)}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}f^{2}(x)\delta_{t}(x,\xi)dx.

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}f(x)\delta_{t}(x,\xi)\frac{\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}(f(y)-f(x))dy}{\Phi(x,t)}=0.

.

As in the original theorem (cf. proof of Theorem 2 in

In the original setting, the assumptions of homogeneity and translational invariance of

was independent of the center point

Corollary 1
.

Let

,
X_{2}
and

,
Y_{2}
and

Since strictly monotone increasing functions are invertible, it holds that

It follows that

\mu(B_{t}^{(h)}(x))=\mu(B_{\gamma^{-1}(t)}^{(d)}(x)).

For a homogeneous and translation-invariant distance function

it further holds that

\mu(B_{r}^{(d)}(x))=r^{k}\mu(B_{1}^{(d)}(0))

and we conclude

\Phi(x,t)=\left(\gamma^{-1}(t)\right)^{k}\mu(B_{1}^{(d)}(0))

It follows with

for

that

fulfills the conditions (

4
) and (

).

The integrability conditions of Theorem

, since the volume in

A natural question is whether our result extends to curved spaces, where translation invariance and homogeneity fail. Compact Riemannian manifolds provide a prototypical example: their geodesic balls have controlled volume growth and the Riemannian measure is doubling. Corollary

illustrates that our generalization continues to hold in this broader geometric setting.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a smooth, compact

\mu

. Let on

,

and

and i.i.d.

,
Y_{2}
and

with density

, where

. Then it holds that

f=g\textnormal{ \quad\quad}\Leftrightarrow\textnormal{ \quad\quad}d_{\mathcal{M}}(X_{1},X_{2})\overset{\mathcal{D}}{=}d_{\mathcal{M}}(Y_{1},Y_{2})\overset{\mathcal{D}}{=}d_{\mathcal{M}}(X_{3},Y_{3}).

Proof.

The geodesic ball volume on

satisfies

\Phi(x,t)=\omega_{2,k}\,t^{k}\!\left[1-\frac{\mathrm{S}(x)}{6(k+2)}\,t^{2}+\frac{-3\|R\|^{2}(x)+8\|\mathrm{Ric}\|^{2}(x)+5\,\mathrm{S}(x)^{2}-12\,\Delta\mathrm{S}(x)}{360\,(k+2)(k+4)}\,t^{4}+O(t^{6})\right]

where

denotes the volume of the

Ricci tensor (cf. Theorem 3.1 in

is compact, it is also

ct^{k}<\Phi(x,t)<Ct^{k}\textnormal{ \quad\quad in a.e. }x

(11)

for some

and integrability conditions of Theorem

follow from

. Furthermore,
\mathcal{M}
is with (

and

are Lebesgue differentiable with respect to

.∎

Theorem
3
shows that equality of the interpoint distance distributions implies equality of the underlying distributions, but it remains qualitative. In practice, one is also interested in quantifying how close the densities are when the interpoint distributions are only approximately equal. To this end, we aim to establish inequalities that connect the
L^{2}
–distance between
f
and
g
with Kolmogorov-type distances between probability functions
F_{XX}
,
F_{XY}
,
F_{YY}
, where
F_{XX}(t):=\mathbb{P}(h(X_{1},X_{2})<t)
,
F_{XY}(t):=\mathbb{P}(h(X_{3},Y_{1})<t)
and
F_{YY}(t):=\mathbb{P}(h(Y_{2},Y_{3})<t)
. Such bounds allow us to measure the deviation of

and

in terms of observable discrepancies of interpoint distances, and provide a quantitative version of Theorem

.

Theorem 4
.

Let
h
be a symmetric generalized distance function, the conditions of Theorem
3
be fulfilled and furthermore let for
\delta
hold

0<\delta_{*}<\delta(x,\xi)<\delta^{*}<\infty\textnormal{\quad\quad}\textnormal{\quad for a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

(12)

for some
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\delta_{*},\delta^{*}
. Let
\Delta_{K}(t)
denote Kolmogorov discrepancy, i.e.

\Delta_{K}(t):=\sup_{0<u\leq t}\left|F_{XX}(u)-F_{XY}(u)\right|+\sup_{0<u\leq t}\left|F_{YY}(u)-F_{XY}(u)\right|.

For some
\epsilon>0
and
0<t<\epsilon
it holds then

||f-g||^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{c\delta_{*}}\left[\frac{\Delta_{K}(t)}{\Phi(\xi,t)}+r(\xi,t)\right]

(13)

and

\Delta_{K}(t)\leq C\delta^{*}\Phi(\xi,t)(||f||_{L^{2}}+||g||_{L^{2}})||f-g||_{L^{2}},

(14)

where
c
and
C
are as in Definition
2
, with
r(\xi,t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow 0
.

Proof.

Following (
8
), analogous integrals for
\mathbb{P}(h(X_{3},Y_{1})<t)
and
\mathbb{P}(h(Y_{2},Y_{3})<t)
are

\displaystyle\frac{F_{XY}(t)}{\Phi(\xi,t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Phi(\xi,t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}f(x)\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}(g(y)-g(x))dydx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}f(x)g(x)\delta_{t}(x,\xi)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Phi(\xi,t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}g(x)\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}(f(y)-f(x))dydx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}g(x)f(x)\delta_{t}(x,\xi)dx

due to the symmetry of
h
and the first integral, and

\frac{F_{YY}(t)}{\Phi(\xi,t)}=\frac{1}{\Phi(\xi,t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}g(x)\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}(g(y)-g(x))dydx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}g^{2}(x)\delta_{t}(x,\xi)dx

and therefore

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(f(x)-g(x))^{2}\delta_{t}(x,\xi)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Phi(\xi,t)}\left(F_{XX}(t)-F_{XY}(t)+F_{YY}(t)-F_{XY}(t)\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\Phi(\xi,t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(f-g)(x)\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}\left((f-g)(y)-(f-g)(x)\right)dydx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta_{K}(t)}{\Phi(\xi,t)}+r(\xi,t)

where

r(\xi,t):=\frac{1}{\Phi(\xi,t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}|(f-g)(x)|\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}\left|(f-g)(y)-(f-g)(x)\right|dydx.

In particular, since uniformly bounded centered oscillations of
f
and
g
imply uniformly bounded centered oscillation of
(f-g)
(i.e. with
A_{|f-g|}\leq A_{f}+A_{g}
), by dominated convergence analogously to the proof of Theorem
3
it follows that
r(\xi,t)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow 0
.
Finally, by assumption there exist
\epsilon>0
s.t. for
0<t<\epsilon
and a.e.
x
it is
c\delta_{*}\leq c\delta(x,\xi)\leq\delta_{t}(x,\xi)
and hence

c\delta_{*}||f-g||^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(f(x)-g(x))^{2}\delta_{t}(x,\xi)dx,

and the inequality (
13
) follows.

On the other hand, as we have

F_{XX}(u)-F_{XY}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}f(x)\int_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(f(y)-g(y))dydx

by applying Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality, it follows that

|F_{XX}(u)-F_{XY}(u)|\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}f^{2}(x)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left(\int_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(f(y)-g(y))dy\right)^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(15)

and

\displaystyle\int_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(f(y)-g(y))dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(f(y)-g(y))\mathbb{I}^{2}_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\mathbb{I}_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(y)dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(f(y)-g(y))^{2}\mathbb{I}_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(y)dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(x,u)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(f(y)-g(y))^{2}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Since it is
\Phi(x,u)=\delta_{t}(x,\xi)\Phi(\xi,u)
and
\delta_{t}(x,\xi)\leq C\delta^{*}
for small
t
by assumption, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left(\int_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(f(y)-g(y))dy\right)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\Phi(x,u)\int_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(f(y)-g(y))^{2}dydx

\displaystyle\leq C\delta^{*}\Phi(\xi,u)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(f(y)-g(y))^{2}\mathbb{I}_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(y)dydx.

Since
h
is symmetric and therefore
\mathbb{I}_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(y)=\mathbb{I}_{B_{u}^{(h)}(y)}(x)
, by Fubini it is

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(f(y)-g(y))^{2}\mathbb{I}_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(y)dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(f(y)-g(y))^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\mathbb{I}_{B_{u}^{(h)}(y)}(x)dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(f(y)-g(y))^{2}\Phi(y,u)dy

\displaystyle\leq C\delta^{*}\Phi(\xi,u)||f-g||^{2}_{L^{2}}

Combining the inequalities we find

|F_{XX}(u)-F_{XY}(u)|\leq C\delta^{*}\Phi(\xi,u)||f||_{L^{2}}||f-g||_{L^{2}}.

Similarly, by considering the integral

F_{YY}(u)-F_{XY}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}g(x)\int_{B_{u}^{(h)}(x)}(g(y)-f(y))dydx

in this case analogously we conclude

|F_{YY}(u)-F_{XY}(u)|\leq C\delta^{*}\Phi(\xi,u)||g||_{L^{2}}||f-g||_{L^{2}}.

Taking the supremum over
0<u\leq t
and using monotonicity of
\Phi(\xi,u)
in
u
we obtain

\Delta_{K}(t)=\sup_{0<u\leq t}\left|F_{XX}(u)-F_{XY}(u)\right|+\sup_{0<u\leq t}\left|F_{YY}(u)-F_{XY}(u)\right|\leq C\delta^{*}\Phi(\xi,t)(||f||_{L^{2}}+||g||_{L^{2}})||f-g||_{L^{2}}

proving (
14
). ∎

Remark 3
.

The derived inequalities hold locally for some
\epsilon>0
and
0<t<\epsilon
, stemming from the scale of volume-regularity and boundedness of centered oscillations. Furthermore, applying Theorem
4
in order to bound the Kolmogorov distance, is not informative since
\Phi(\xi,t)
generally diverges as
t\rightarrow\infty
. Nevertheless, a global reverse bound can be obtained by combining (
14
) on the interval
u\leq t
with a separate tail estimate for
u>t
, e.g. via moment, Markov-type bounds, which we will not pursue here.

The bounds on
\delta(x,\xi)
simplify comparability of
||f-g||_{L^{2}}
with the integral over
(f-g)^{2}\delta_{t}
. For standard distances like
l_{p}
-norms or geodesic distances on compact manifolds, condition (
12
) is automatically true, while this does not hold for the Canberra distance, the entropic distance or the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and local versions of inequalities (
13
) and (
14
) have to be developed.
Additionally, the inequalities of Theorem
4
are general but involve the quantities
\Phi(x,t)
and
r(\xi,t)
, whose behavior is not explicit in full generality. By imposing additional structural assumptions on both the distance function
h
and the densities
f
and
g
, these terms can be controlled more directly. In particular, Ahlfors
\alpha
–regularity of
h
provides precise asymptotics for the volume
\Phi(x,t)
, while Hölder continuity of
f
and
g
ensures that the remainder term
r(\xi,t)
can be bounded uniformly. Under these combined conditions, the inequalities of Theorem
4
simplify to the more concrete statement given in the following corollary. In particular, under the assumption of Ahlfors
\alpha
–regularity of
h
, the upper bound in Theorem
4
is proportional to
\Delta_{K}^{\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}
, where
\Delta_{K}=\Delta_{K}(\infty)
. Accordingly, as
\alpha
increases, this upper bound deteriorates. Since
\alpha
typically describes the ambient dimension of the data, this upper bound reflects that quantitative recovery of
f
and
g
from interpoint distances becomes weaker in higher dimensions.

Corollary 3
.

Let the conditions of Theorem
4
be fulfilled and furthermore let
h
be Ahlfors
\alpha
-regular. Let
f
and
g
be
\beta
-Hölder continuous with respect to
h
, i.e. there exist
C_{f},C_{g}>0
s.t.

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq C_{f}(h(x,y))^{\beta}\textnormal{, \quad}|g(x)-g(y)|\leq C_{g}(h(x,y))^{\beta}\textnormal{ \quad}\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

Then it holds

||f-g||^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq C\Delta_{K}^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}

(16)

where
\Delta_{K}=\Delta_{K}(\infty)
.

Proof.

We have for some
\epsilon>0
and
c_{*},c^{*}>0
that
c_{*}t^{\alpha}\leq\Phi(x,t)\leq c^{*}t^{\alpha}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
0<t<\epsilon
. Applying Theorem
4
we have

\displaystyle||f-g||^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\delta_{*}}\frac{\Delta_{K}(t)}{c_{*}t^{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{\delta_{*}}\frac{1}{c_{*}t^{\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}f(x)\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}\left|f(y)-g(y)-(f(x)-g(x))\right|dydx

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\delta_{*}}\frac{1}{c_{*}t^{\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}g(x)\int_{B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}\left|f(y)-g(y)-(f(x)-g(x))\right|dydx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\delta_{*}}\frac{\Delta_{K}}{c_{*}t^{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{\delta_{*}}\frac{1}{c_{*}t^{\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(f(x)+g(x))\Phi(x,t)(\sup_{y\in B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|+\sup_{y\in B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}|g(y)-g(x)|)dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\delta_{*}}\frac{\Delta_{K}}{c_{*}t^{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{\delta_{*}}\frac{1}{c_{*}t^{\alpha}}c^{*}t^{\alpha}(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\sup_{y\in B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}|f(y)-f(x)|+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\sup_{y\in B_{t}^{(h)}(x)}|g(y)-g(x)|)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(f(x)+g(x))dx.

By Hölder continuity there exist
c
such that for any
x
,
y
it is
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq ch(x,y)^{\beta}
and
|g(x)-g(y)|\leq ch(x,y)^{\beta}
. With
h(x,y)<t
we conclude

\displaystyle||f-g||^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\delta_{*}}\frac{\Delta_{K}}{c_{*}t^{\alpha}}+2\frac{1}{\delta_{*}}\frac{c^{*}}{c_{*}}ct^{\beta}.

Minimization of
\phi(t)=\frac{1}{\delta_{*}}\frac{\Delta_{K}}{c_{*}t^{\alpha}}+2\frac{1}{\delta_{*}}\frac{c^{*}}{c_{*}}ct^{\beta}
over

yields

t_{0}=(\frac{\alpha\Delta_{K}}{2\beta c^{*}c})^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}}

and it follows

||f-g||^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq C\Delta_{K}^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}

where

depends on
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\delta_{*}
,
c_{*}
,
c^{*}
and

.
∎

The general conditions of Theorem

These examples illustrate that the considered framework covers a broad range of practically relevant settings.

Example 1

(

Example 2
.

(Canberra distance)

Let us consider the Canberra distance of

and

in

h(x,y):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\begin{cases}\frac{|x_{i}-y_{i}|}{|x_{i}|+|y_{i}|},&\text{if }|x_{i}|+|y_{i}|\neq 0,\\
0,&\text{if }x_{i}=y_{i}=0.\end{cases}

(17)

Case
k=1
:

In this case,
h(x,y)=\frac{\lvert x-y\rvert}{\lvert x\rvert+\lvert y\rvert}

and

and

For

\Phi(x,t)=\frac{4t\lvert x\rvert}{1-t^{2}}\textnormal{ \quad\quad for }t\in(0,1)\textnormal{ and }x\neq 0.

(19)

Moreover, it holds that

B_{t}(0)=\begin{cases}\{0\}&t<1,\\
\mathbb{R}&t\geq 1,\end{cases}

(20)

For the lower bound consider the rectangle with sides

Since

\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}\left(x_{i}\frac{1-t/k}{1+t/k},x_{i}\frac{1+t/k}{1-t/k}\right)\subseteq B_{t}(x)

\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Accordingly, for a.e.
x

\Phi(x,t)\sim C(x)\left(\frac{t}{1-t^{2}}\right)^{k},

where
0<C(x)\sim\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lvert x_{i}\rvert
. More precisely, with (
20
) and (
21
) it follows that
\delta_{t}(x,y)\leq C\delta(x,y)
with
\delta(x,y)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left|\frac{x_{i}}{y_{i}}\right|
for some
C>0
. We conclude that the conditions (
3
), (
4
) and (
5
) in the definition of volume-regular functions, i.e. Definition
2
, are fulfilled. Theorem
3
then applies if its conditions on the densities
f
and
g
are fulfilled.

As a concrete example, consider the density function
f
of the standard normal distribution, i.e.
f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\lVert x\rVert^{2}/2}
, which is smooth on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then, the Lebesgue differentiability condition is fulfilled since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Phi(x,t)}\int_{B_{t}(x)}\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\Phi(x,t)}\sup_{y\in B_{t}(x)}\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert\Phi(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in B_{t}(x)}\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert\rightarrow 0

for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
. According to (
20
), it further holds that

f^{2}(x)\Phi(x,t)\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{4t}{1-t^{2}}\right)^{k}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}\lvert x_{i}\rvert e^{-\lVert x\rVert^{2}}

with the right-hand side of the above inequality being integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We conclude that
f\delta(\cdot,y),f^{2}\delta(\cdot,y)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
for any
t\in(0,1)
and the integrability condition is fulfilled. In other words: Theorem
3
holds for normally distributed data and the Canberra distance.

Note that for the Canberra distance
\Phi
and
\delta
are not globally bounded and diverge as
x\rightarrow\infty
, hence do not fulfill condition (
12
) of Theorem
4
. This can, however, be amended by locally applying Theorem
4
on a bounded set (i.e. such that
\delta
is bounded) carrying the majority of the mass of
f
and
g
, while separately developing bounds for the tails. A detailed discussion of the latter, however, lies beyond the scope of this work.

We further treat the entropic distance, which involves logarithmic terms, impeding exact volume calculation even in one dimension. Instead, we provide upper and lower bounds on the entropic volume in one dimension and then proceed to generalize these to higher dimensions.

Example 3
.

(Entropic distance on
\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>0}
)
Consider
h:\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>0}\rightarrow\left[0,\infty\right)
defined by

h(x,y):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left|x_{i}\log\left(\frac{x_{i}}{y_{i}}\right)-x_{i}+y_{i}\right|.

As in the previous example, we firstly consider the case
k=1
and subsequently generalize bounds for
\Phi(x,t)
from one to more dimensions.

Case
k=1
:

In this case,
h(x,y)=\left|x\log\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)-x+y\right|
. Let
\eta_{x}(y):=x\log\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)-x+y
s.t.
h(x,y)=|\eta_{x}(y)|
.
Since
x>0
, for a ball of radius
t<\frac{x}{2}
around
x
, let
y\in(x-t,x+t)
and therefore

y-x\in\left(-\frac{x}{2},\frac{x}{2}\right),\ \text{or, equivalently, \ }y\in\left(\frac{x}{2},\frac{3x}{2}\right).

(22)

A second-order Taylor expansion of
\eta_{x}(y)
around
x
yields

\eta_{x}(y)=\frac{1}{2x}(y-x)^{2}\frac{1}{3}\frac{x}{\zeta^{3}}(y-x)^{3}

for some
\zeta\in\left(\frac{x}{2},\frac{3x}{2}\right)
. Furthermore, it holds that

|\eta_{x}(y)|<t\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{2}(y-x)^{2}\left|\frac{1}{x}-\frac{2}{3}\frac{x}{\zeta^{3}}(y-x)\right|<t.

(23)

With the triangle inequality and (
23
) it follows that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{x}-\frac{2}{3}(y-x)\right|\leq\frac{1}{x}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{x}{\zeta^{3}}|y-x|

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{x}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{x}{\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{3}}\frac{x}{2}=\frac{11}{3}\frac{1}{x}

and

\left|\frac{1}{x}-\frac{2}{3}\frac{x}{\zeta^{3}}(y-x)\right|>\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{1}{x}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{1}{x}

since
\zeta
is lower-bounded by
x
for
y-x>0
and
y-x
is upper-bounded by
\frac{x}{2}
according to (
22
).

In particular, for any
y
satisfying the first inequality, i.e. such that

y\in(x-\sqrt{\frac{11}{6}xt},x+\sqrt{\frac{11}{6}xt})

it is also
|\eta_{x}(y)|<t
, while any
y\in B_{t}^{(h)}(x)
satisfies the second inequality and we conclude

\left(x-\sqrt{\frac{11}{6}xt},x+\sqrt{\frac{11}{6}xt}\right)\subset B_{t}^{(h)}(x)\subset\left(x-\sqrt{3xt},x+\sqrt{3xt}\right).

For any two points
x
and
y
and balls around them with radii
t
we have

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Phi(x,t)\geq\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}2\sqrt{\frac{6}{11}tx}=0

and

c\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{y}}<\frac{\Phi(x,t)}{\Phi(y,t)}<C\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{y}},

(24)

such that conditions (
3
) - (
5
) are fulfilled.

Case
k>1
:

Similarly to Example
2
, we construct rectangles in and containing
B_{t}^{(h)}(x)
using the intervals derived for case
k=1
. Following the previous derivation of inscribed and circumscribed intervals of the ball
B_{t}^{(h)}(x)
in one dimension, we derive

\Pi_{i=1}^{k}\left(x_{i}-\sqrt{\frac{11}{6}x_{i}\frac{t}{k}},x_{i}+\sqrt{\frac{11}{6}x_{i}\frac{t}{k}}\right)\subset B_{t}^{(h)}(x)\subset\Pi_{i=1}^{k}\left(x_{i}-\sqrt{3x_{i}t},x_{i}+\sqrt{3x_{i}t})\right)

and hence a lower and an upper bound on
\Phi(x,t)
are

\left(2\sqrt{\frac{11}{6k}}\right)^{k}t^{k/2}\Pi_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{1/2}<\Phi(x,t)<(2\sqrt{3})^{k}t^{k/2}\Pi_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{1/2}.

We conclude that

\Phi(x,t)=C(x)t^{k/2}

where
C(x)>0
is a function with behavior similar to
\Pi_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{1/2}
. Conditions (
3
), (
4
) and (
5
) hold with
\delta(x,y)=\Pi_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{x_{i}}{y_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

If for the density function
f
it holds
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{k})
and the weighted function
f^{2}(x)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\right)^{1/2}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{k})
, given a decay of
f(x)
quicker than the growth of
\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\right)^{1/4}
, then the integrability conditions for such
f
are fulfilled.

Lastly, since on a bounded set (such is any
B_{t}^{(h)}(x)
)
C(x)
is bounded, there exist
0<C_{1}<C_{2}
with

C_{1}t^{k/2}<\Phi(x,t)<C_{2}t^{k/2}

and since
\Pi_{i=1}^{k}I_{i,t}\subset B_{t}^{(h)}(x)
for any
t
where
I_{i,t}:=\left(x_{i}-\sqrt{\frac{11}{6}x_{i}\frac{t}{k}},x_{i}+\sqrt{\frac{11}{6}x_{i}\frac{t}{k}}\right)
, such
f\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
is Lebesgue differentiable in a.e.
x
by a generalized Lebesgue differentiation theorem (cf. Theorem 3.21 in
Fol 99
).

As for Example
2
, we note that the bounds of Theorem
4
are not directly applicable due to divergence of
\delta
at infinity. Finally, we consider the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, used in ecology and microbiome studies. Rather than coordinate-wise normalization as Canberra, Bray-Curtis is globaly normalized, making it less sensitive to rare features. Its close relation to Canberra distance makes the argumentation indirect and we do not derive the estimates on volume as in previous examples.

Example 4
.

(Bray-Curtis dissimilarity on
\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>0}
)

The Bray-Curtis dissimilarity on
\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>0}
is given by

h_{BC}(x,y)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}|x_{i}-y_{i}|}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(x_{i}+y_{i})}.

Ricotta-Podani (
RP 17
) rewrite
h_{BC}
as

h_{BC}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}(x,y)\frac{|x_{i}-y_{i}|}{x_{i}+y_{i}}

where

\omega_{i}(x,y)=\frac{x_{i}+y_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(x_{i}+y_{i})}

and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{i}=1
. Bray-Curtis dissimilarity can thus be seen as weighted Canberra distance where a weight
\omega_{i}
is continuous, strictly positive and uniformly bounded between
0
and
1
. Consequently, the volume of a Bray-Curtis ball satisfies the same asymptotics (as
t\rightarrow 0
) as in the Canberra case and we conclude that the conditions of Theorem
3
hold.

4
Conclusion

We showed that interpoint-distance distribution characterize data distributions under mild conditions on the distance function and the data-generating distributions. The framework recovers the classical setting of the Maa–Pearl–Bartoszyński Theorem (see Corollary
1
), extends to compact manifolds (see Corollary
2
), and yields quantitative
L^{2}
-bounds in terms of Kolmogorov discrepancies with dimension-aware rates under Ahlfors-type volume growth and Hölder regularity (Theorem
4
and Corollary
3
). These rates make explicit the trade-off between the ambient dimension
k
and the Hölder-regularity index
\beta
, providing interpretable control in high dimensions. The quantitative bounds are presently local and do not apply to Canberra, Bray–Curtis and entropic distances, since their volume ratios
\delta_{t}(x,y)
are not bounded in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. The obstruction is the blow-up of coordinate-normalized ratios near axes or at the origin, which breaks uniform comparability required by our global arguments. Still, it is possible to extend the results of Theorem
4
and Corollary
3
locally for these distances as well. A practical route is to decompose the space into a truncated region where ratios are controlled and a tail region handled by distributional mass bounds. A global bound should follow by combining this localized estimate based on Corollary
3
with additional tail estimates that quantify the mass of
f
and
g
outside of the truncated region.

Beyond this limitation, the results cover many distances used in practice and clarify when multivariate two-sample problems may be reduced to one-dimensional comparisons. We have, in particular, developed a theoretical foundation for testing based on the Canberra distance, as proposed in
BMP 24
. This provides the practical procedure with identifiability guarantees and, on truncated domains, the same dimension-aware rates as for Ahlfors regular spaces.
