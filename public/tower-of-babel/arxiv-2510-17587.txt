Macroscopic fluctuation-response theory and its use for gene regulatory networks
Timur Aslyamov, Krzysztof Ptaszyński, Massimiliano Esposito

Abstract
Gaussian macroscopic fluctuation theory underpins the understanding of noise in a broad class of nonequilibrium systems. We derive exact fluctuation-response relations linking the power spectral density of stationary fluctuations to the linear response of stable nonequilibrium steady states. Both of these can be determined experimentally and used to reconstruct the kernel of the linearized dynamics and the diffusion matrix, and thus any features of the Gaussian theory. We apply our theory to gene regulatory networks with negative feedback, and derive an explicit internal-external noise decomposition of the power spectral density for any networks, including cross-correlations.

Macroscopic dynamics—
We consider a system described by the
N
-dimensional stochastic field
\bm{x}(t)
, which may correspond, e.g., to the particle position or a set of concentrations of chemical species. In the limit where the noise acting on the system becomes vanishingly small, the probability density of that field concentrates around the most likely value,
\mathcal{X}(t)
, which obeys the deterministic dynamical equation,

\displaystyle d_{t}\bm{\mathcal{X}}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{f}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}(t))\,,\quad\bm{f}(\bm{x}^{*})=0\,,

(1)

where
\bm{f}(\bm{\mathcal{X}})
is the rate vector and
\bm{x}^{*}
is a fixed point which we assume to be stable and unique.
At steady state and in presence of a small but non-vanishing noise, the fluctuations of the stochastic field
\bm{x}(t)

in the vicinity of the fixed point are described by the linear Langevin equation

\displaystyle d_{t}\bm{x}=\mathbb{K}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*})+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\bm{\eta}\,.

(2)

Here, the first term describes the linear deterministic relaxation towards the fixed point, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}\equiv\partial_{\bm{x}}\bm{f}(\bm{x})|_{\bm{x}=\bm{x}^{*}}

(3)

is the Jacobian of the deterministic dynamics with the elements
K_{mk}=[\partial_{x_{k}}f_{m}(\bm{x})]_{\bm{x}=\bm{x}^{*}}
for
\bm{f}(\bm{x})=(\dots,f_{m}(\bm{x}),\dots)^{\intercal}
and
\bm{x}=(\dots,x_{k},\dots)^{\intercal}
. The second term describes the effect of fluctuations as a Gaussian white noise vector
\bm{\eta}(t)=(\ldots,\eta_{m}(t),\ldots)^{\intercal}
with zero average
\langle\bm{\eta}\rangle=0
and the autocorrelation function

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\eta}(t)\bm{\eta}^{\intercal}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})\mathbb{D}\,,

(4)

where the average over noise realizations is denoted by
\langle\dots\rangle
and
\mathbb{D}
is the diffusion matrix that we assume to be positive semidefinite and constant. The prefactor
\varepsilon
is the noise amplitude, which corresponds, e.g., to the temperature for colloidal particles or the inverse volume in chemical systems.

We note that
\mathbb{K}
and
\mathbb{D}
are often not known. We will see in what follows that they can be determined from the measurable linear response of arbitrary steady states (beyond equilibrium) and their stationary fluctuations, see Fig.
1
.

Stationary fluctuations—
Since
Eq.

2
is linear, the dynamics of the average of
\bm{x}
is
d_{t}\langle\bm{x}\rangle=\mathbb{K}(\langle\bm{x}\rangle-\bm{x}^{*})
, which is identical to the deterministic dynamics
Eq.

1
linearized around the fixed point.
Its fluctuations are captured by the two-point correlation functions
\langle\delta\bm{x}(t+t^{\prime})\delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}(t^{\prime})\rangle
, where
\delta\bm{x}(t)=\bm{x}(t)-\langle\bm{x}(t)\rangle
.
The
dynamical covariance
is defined as
\mathbb{C}(t)\equiv\varepsilon^{-1}\langle\delta\bm{x}(t)\delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}(t)\rangle
.
At steady state, the mean and covariance become time invariant,
\langle\bm{x}(t)\rangle_{\text{ss}}=\bm{x}^{*}
and
\mathbb{C}(t)=\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}
, and the correlation function becomes independent from
t^{\prime}
(which can thus be taken as
t^{\prime}=0
).
The
power spectrum density
(PSD) of the steady state correlation function is

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}(\omega)\equiv\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\langle\delta\bm{x}(t)\delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}(0)\rangle_{\text{ss}}e^{-i\omega t}dt\,,

(5)

where the scaling ensures that
\mathbb{Z}(\omega)
remains finite in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
A known result
keizer2012statistical
, that we re-derive for completeness in
Appendix

A
, is that

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}(\omega)=(\mathbb{K}-i\omega\mathbb{1})^{-1}\mathbb{D}[(\mathbb{K}-i\omega\mathbb{1})^{-1}]^{\dagger}\,,

(6)

where
A^{\dagger}\equiv(\overline{A})^{\intercal}
denotes the adjoint operation.
Two important and complementary quantities can be derived from it.
First, the
zero frequency PSD
,
\mathbb{Z}(0)
, which by time integrating the steady state correlation function, captures the persistence of fluctuations
berg1977physics
.
Second, the
stationary covariance

which is obtained from it as
\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\mathbb{Z}(\omega)
and measures the magnitude and the directionality of fluctuations around the steady state. It can be calculated as the steady-state solution of the Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}+\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}\mathbb{K}^{\intercal}=-\mathbb{D}\,.

(7)

Efficient ways to calculate
Equations

6
and
7
are well known

Nonequilibrium response—
We assume that the parameters
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{N_{p}})
control
\bm{f}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})
in the deterministic dynamics
Eq.

1
and that the system is initially at steady state,
\mathcal{X}(0)=\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
.
We then consider a small perturbation of the model parameters,
\delta\bm{\theta}
, at
t=0
.
Given that the system is stable,
\mathcal{X}(t)
will eventually relax to the new steady state
\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\theta}+\delta\bm{\theta})
.
Since the perturbation is small,
\delta\mathcal{X}(t)=\mathcal{X}(t)-\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
will also be small and obeys the dynamics

\displaystyle d_{t}\delta\mathcal{X}(t)

\displaystyle=f\big(\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\theta})+\delta\mathcal{X}(t),\bm{\theta}+\delta\bm{\theta}\big)

(8a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{K}\delta\mathcal{X}(t)+\mathbb{Q}\delta\bm{\theta}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\bm{\theta}^{\intercal}\delta\mathcal{X})\,,

(8b)

where
\mathbb{K}\equiv\mathbb{K}(\bm{\theta})
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}\equiv\Big[\frac{\partial f_{n}(\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{k}}\Big]_{\{nk\}}=-\mathbb{K}\frac{d\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\theta})}{d\bm{\theta}}\,.

(9)

For the last equality, we expanded
f\big(\bm{x}^{*}(\bm{\theta}+\delta\bm{\theta}),\bm{\theta}+\delta\bm{\theta}\big)=0
to first order in
\delta\bm{\theta}
.
Combining the solution of
Eq.

8b
with
Eq.

9
, the
dynamical response
matrix can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}(t)\equiv\Big[\frac{\delta\mathcal{X}_{n}(t)}{\delta\theta_{k}}\Big]_{\{nk\}}=\mathbb{K}^{-1}(e^{\mathbb{K}t}-\mathbb{1})\mathbb{Q}\;.

(10)

Since the fixed point of the dynamics is stable, all eigenvalues of
\mathbb{K}
, denoted
\lambda_{n}
, have negative real parts,
\text{Re}~\lambda_{n}<0
, and thus
\mathbb{K}
is invertible.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{R}}(s)=-\frac{1}{s}(\mathbb{K}-s\mathbb{1})^{-1}\mathbb{Q}\,.

(11)

The
static response
matrix is the
t\rightarrow\infty
limit of the dynamical response, which, using
Eq.

11
and the final value theorem, can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}\equiv\mathbb{R}(\infty)=\lim_{s\to 0}s\hat{\mathbb{R}}(s)=-\mathbb{K}^{-1}\mathbb{Q}\,.

(12)

In a generic steady state, no relation is known between
\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}
and
\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}
. However, in the special case of detailed balance dynamics (see
Appendix

B
for details), the stationary covariance becomes an equilibrium covariance
\mathbb{C}_{\text{eq}}
satisfying
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{C}_{\text{eq}}=(\mathbb{K}\mathbb{C}_{\text{eq}})^{\intercal}=-\mathbb{D}/2
. The Lyapunov
Eq.

7
was used in the last equality.
Therefore, inserting
\mathbb{K}^{-1}=-2\mathbb{C}_{\text{eq}}\mathbb{D}^{-1}
into
Eq.

12
, we find the notorious relation between equilibrium response and equilibrium covariance

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{\text{eq}}=2\mathbb{C}_{\text{eq}}\mathbb{D}^{-1}\mathbb{Q}\,.

(13)

Before proceeding, let us recall that due to linearity, the dynamics for the average
\langle\bm{x}(t)\rangle
following from
Eq.

2
is identical to the deterministic dynamics of
\mathcal{X}(t)
linearized around the fixed point. As a result, the present response theory is also a response theory for averages. Let us also stress that in experiments, averages (and thus their responses) and PSD are measurable quantities.

Linear dynamics from response—
We first note that
\mathbb{Q}
, using
Eq.

10
, can be expressed in terms of the measurable dynamical response function

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}

\displaystyle=d_{t}\mathbb{R}(0)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{R}(t)\,.

(14)

As a result, using
Eq.

12
, we find that
\mathbb{K}
can be obtained from the measurable responses as

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}=-d_{t}\mathbb{R}(0)\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}^{-1}=-\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\mathbb{R}(t)\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}^{-1}\,.

(15)

In doing so, we assumed that
\mathbb{Q}
is invertible, which implies that the number of independent parameters

is equal to or greater than the number of dynamical variables

. In the latter case, the Moore–Penrose inverse can be used.

Linking response and PSD: Macroscopic FRRs—
Using
Eq.

11
, the resolvent

6
can be rewritten as

As a result,

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{Q}^{-1}\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{Q}^{-1})^{\intercal}=\lim_{t\to 0}t^{2}\mathbb{R}^{-1}(t)\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{R}^{-1}(t))^{\intercal}\,.

Using

12
, the zero frequency limit of

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}(0)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}t^{2}\mathbb{R}(\infty)\mathbb{R}^{-1}(t)\mathbb{D}[\mathbb{R}(\infty)\mathbb{R}^{-1}(t)]^{\intercal}\,.

The results [

Inferring the diffusion matrix—
Another important result, is that our approach provides three independent methods to determine the diffusion matrix that governs the stochastic dynamics of the system based on the measurable fluctuations and responses.
For the first method, we insert
Eq.

15
into the Lyapunov
Eq.

7
and find

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle=d_{t}\mathbb{R}(0)\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}^{-1}\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}+\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}(\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}^{-1})^{\intercal}(d_{t}\mathbb{R}(0))^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\Big[\mathbb{R}(t)\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}^{-1}\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}+\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}(\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}^{-1})^{\intercal}\mathbb{R}^{\intercal}(t)\Big]\,,

(20)

which infers the diffusion matrix in terms of static fluctuations and dynamic responses.
For the second and third methods, we use the FRRs [
Eqs.

16
,
17
, and
18
] to isolate the diffusion matrix as

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\mathbb{Q}\hat{\mathbb{R}}^{-1}(i\omega)\cdot\mathbb{Z}(\omega)\cdot(\hat{\mathbb{R}}^{-1}(i\omega))^{\dagger}\mathbb{Q}^{\intercal}\,,

(21a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}^{-1}\cdot\mathbb{Z}(0)\cdot(\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}^{-1})^{\intercal}\mathbb{Q}^{\intercal}\,,

(21b)

with
\mathbb{Q}=d_{t}\mathbb{R}(0)
.

These results, together with

15
confirm that
\mathbb{K}
and
\mathbb{D}
can be determined from the response of arbitrary steady state and its stationary fluctuations.

Response links static covariance and PSD—
We now show how the responses provide an explicit connection between static covariance and PSD.
Indeed, by multiplying Lyapunov

\mathbb{K}^{-1}
from the left and

from the right, then using

\displaystyle-\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}(\mathbb{K}^{-1})^{\intercal}-\mathbb{K}^{-1}\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}=\mathbb{K}^{-1}\mathbb{D}(\mathbb{K}^{-1})^{\intercal}=\mathbb{Z}(0)\,,

and using

, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}(\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}\mathbb{Q}^{-1})^{\intercal}+\mathbb{R}_{\text{ss}}\mathbb{Q}^{-1}\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}=\mathbb{Z}(0)\,,

with

We note that

Noise in gene regulatory networks—
Genetically identical cells exhibit significant variability in their molecular composition and behavior under uniform conditions due to random fluctuations in gene expression.
We derive analytical expressions for the PSDs of mRNA molecular numbers transcribed from a gene and the protein molecular numbers translated from mRNAs
kaern2005stochasticity
; see
Fig.

2
(a).
The deterministic rate equations for the concentrations of mRNAs,
\mathcal{C}_{1}
, and proteins,
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, are

\displaystyle d_{t}\mathcal{C}_{1}

\displaystyle=f_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2})\,,\quad d_{t}\mathcal{C}_{2}=f_{2}(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2})\,,

(25)

with
f_{n}=w_{+n}-w_{-n}
and

\displaystyle w_{+1}

\displaystyle=w_{+1}(\mathcal{C}_{2})\,,\,w_{-1}=\frac{\mathcal{C}_{1}}{\tau_{1}}\,,\,w_{+2}=k_{p}\mathcal{C}_{1}\,,\,w_{-2}=\frac{\mathcal{C}_{2}}{\tau_{2}}\,,

where the mRNA synthesis rate

29
, are described by

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{Z_{22}(0)}{c_{2}^{*}\tau_{2}}=\frac{2}{(1-\Psi)^{2}}\Big[1+\frac{\tau_{1}}{\tau_{2}}\frac{c^{*}_{2}}{c^{*}_{1}}\Big]=\frac{1}{(1-\Psi)^{2}}\varphi_{2}^{\text{nr}}\,,

(31)

where the non-regulated value is

\displaystyle\varphi^{\text{nr}}_{2}

\displaystyle=2\Big[1+\frac{\tau_{1}}{\tau_{2}}\frac{c^{*}_{2}}{c^{*}_{1}}\Big]=2(1+\tau_{1}k_{p})\,,

(32)

which predicts that mRNA with a longer life span induce a stronger protein noise while decreasing mRNA noise [see
Eq.

30
].
From
Eq.

31
, we see that negative feedback,
\Psi<0
, suppresses protein fluctuations:
\varphi_{2}\leq\varphi_{2}^{\text{nr}}
. Thus, between two identical setups, the one with negative feedback produces weaker noise (zero-frequency PSD); see
Fig.

2
(e).
Finally, cross-correlations between mRNAs and proteins read

\displaystyle Z_{12}(0)

\displaystyle=D_{1}\frac{dc^{*}_{1}}{dw_{+1}}\frac{dc^{*}_{2}}{dw_{+1}}+D_{2}\frac{dc^{*}_{1}}{dw_{+2}}\frac{dc^{*}_{2}}{dw_{+2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(1-\Psi)^{2}}(\tau_{1}c^{*}_{2}+\tau_{2}c^{*}_{1}\Psi)\,.

(33)

This shows that a change of sign in
Z_{12}(0)
necessarily reveals the presence of negative feedbacks,
\Psi<0
, see
Fig.

2
(f).

Importantly, our theory can be applied to arbitrary complex gene regulatory networks, i.e. networks described by
d_{t}\bm{C}=\bm{w}_{+}(\bm{C})-\text{diag}(\bm{\tau})^{-1}\bm{C}
, where
\bm{C}
is the vector of concentrations,
\bm{w}_{+}
an arbitrary vector dependent on
\bm{C}
, and
\text{diag}(\bm{\tau})
the diagonal matrix of degradation time scales. In such a case,
Eq.

18
can be written as

\displaystyle Z_{nn}(0)=\underbrace{D_{n}\Big(\frac{dc_{n}^{*}}{dw_{+n}}\Big)^{2}}_{\text{int.}\geq 0}+\underbrace{\sum_{k\neq n}D_{k}\Big(\frac{dc_{n}^{*}}{dw_{+k}}\Big)^{2}}_{\text{ext.}\geq 0}\,,

(34)

which reveals a decomposition of the noise (PSD) into intrinsic and extrinsic contributions, distinguished by local (
k=n
) and nonlocal (
k\neq n
) response terms. The “intrinsic” and “extrinsic” terminology is common in the literature
elowitz2002stochastic
, but finds a clear mathematical formulation at the level of the PSD within our theory.
Regardless of how silent the extrinsic network may be (when its responses are negligible), the intrinsic PSD in
Eq.

34
is nonzero. As in the case of stationary fluctuations
elowitz2002stochastic
, the intrinsic PSD sets the fundamental lower limit on fluctuations in complex gene networks.

Conclusions—
We presented a linear nonequilibrium fluctuation-response theory describing macroscopic systems close to fixed stable points. Every ingredient of the theory can be reconstructed from the experimentally accessible response and PSD. We also illustrated the analytical potential of the theory by considering fluctuations in gene regulatory networks.
Extending the theory to more complex attractors such as limit cycles
santolin2025dissipation
;
nagayama2025duality
is left as a future perspective.

Appendix B
Fokker–Plank equation

The Fokker–Plank equation associated to the Langevin dynamics
Eq.

2
reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\bm{x}}^{\intercal}\big[\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)P(\bm{x},t)\big]\,,

(39a)

\displaystyle v(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{K}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{D}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\log P(\bm{x},t)\,,

(39b)

where
\bm{v}
is the probability velocity and
\nabla_{\bm{x}}=(\dots,\partial_{x_{n}},\dots)^{\intercal}
.
Due to its linearity, its solution is the Gaussian

\displaystyle P=\frac{\exp\big[-\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}(t)\mathbb{C}^{-1}\delta\bm{x}(t)\big]}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{N}\det\mathbb{C}}}\,,

(40)

where the dynamical covariance,
\mathbb{C}(t)
, satisfies the dynamical Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle d_{t}\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{K}\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{C}\mathbb{K}^{\intercal}+\mathbb{D}\,,

(41)

with initial condition
\mathbb{C}(0)=0
.
At steady-state

\displaystyle P_{\text{ss}}=\frac{\exp\big[-\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*})^{\intercal}\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*})\big]}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{N}\det\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}}}\,,

(42)

and velocity

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\text{ss}}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbb{K}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{D}\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}^{-1}\big)(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*})=\mathbb{V}_{\text{ss}}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*})\,.

(43)

The dynamics is said to be detailed balance when

\bm{v}_{\text{eq}}=0
which implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{D}\mathbb{C}_{\text{eq}}^{-1}\;.

(44)

This condition is equivalent to requesting the time-reversibility of the correlation functions

\displaystyle\langle\delta\bm{x}(t)\delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}(0)\rangle_{\text{ss}}=\langle\delta\bm{x}(-t)\delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}(0)\rangle_{\text{ss}}=\langle\delta\bm{x}(0)\delta\bm{x}^{\intercal}(t)\rangle_{\text{ss}}\,,

(45)

where the last equality follows from time-translation invariance at steady state.
Indeed, using
Eq.

36
,
Eq.

45
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle e^{\mathbb{K}t}\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}=\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}e^{\mathbb{K}^{\intercal}t}\quad\rightarrow\quad\mathbb{K}\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}=\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}\mathbb{K}^{\intercal}\,,

(46)

which, together with the stationary Lyapunov equation, means that
\mathbb{C}_{\text{ss}}=\mathbb{C}_{\text{eq}}
in
Eq.

44
.
