On average population levels for models with directed diffusion in heterogeneous environments
André Rickes, Elena Braverman

Abstract
In 2006 (J. Differential Equ.), Lou proved that, once the intrinsic growth rate r r in the logistic model is proportional to the spatially heterogeneous carrying capacity K K ( r = K 1 r=K^{1} ), the total population under the regular diffusion exceeds the total of the carrying capacity. He also conjectured that the dependency of the total population on the diffusion coefficient is unimodal, increasing to its maximum and then decreasing to the asymptote which is the total of the carrying capacity. DeAngelis et al (J. Math. Biol. 2016) argued that the prevalence of the population over the carrying capacity is only observed when the growth rate and the carrying capacity are positively correlated, at least for slow dispersal. Guo et al (J. Math. Biol. 2020) justified that, once r r is constant ( r = K 0 r=K^{0} ), the total population is less than the cumulative carrying capacity. Our paper fills up the gap for when r = K λ r=K^{\lambda} for any real λ \lambda , disproving an assumption that there is a critical λ ∗ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \lambda^{\ast}\in(0,1) at which the tendency of the prevalence of the carrying capacity over the total population size changes, demonstrating instead that the relationship is more complicated. In addition, we explore the dependency of the total population size on the diffusion coefficient when the third parameter of the dispersal strategy P P is involved: the diffusion term is d ​ Δ ​ ( u / P ) d\Delta(u/P) , not just d ​ Δ ​ u d\Delta u , for any λ \lambda . We outline some differences from the random diffusion case, in particular, concerning the profile of the total population as a function of the diffusion coefficient. Main Results

3.1
The choice of the dispersal strategy to guarantee abundance

First of all, we explore the following question: can a species choose such a dispersal strategy
P
that its total population exceeds the total carrying capacity?
The answer is positive, once
P
is proportional to the ratio of the carrying capacity and the intrinsic growth rate, while this intrinsic growth rate is non-constant. In other words, the areas of attraction should have higher
available resources with smaller
r
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let (A) be satisfied and
\displaystyle P=\alpha\frac{K}{r}
for some
\alpha>0
, then the solution
u_{d}
of (
1.5
) satisfies (
1.3
) for every
d>0
.

Proof.

Setting
r=\alpha\frac{K}{P}
on (
1.5
), we obtain

\begin{cases}d\Delta\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{P}u_{d}\left(K-u_{d}\right)=0&\mbox{on }\,\,\,\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)=0&\mbox{on }\,\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(3.1)

Multiply Equation (
3.1
) by
\frac{P}{u_{d}}
and integrate over
\Omega
, to get

d\int_{\Omega}\Delta\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\frac{P}{u_{d}}\,dx+\alpha\int_{\Omega}(K-u_{d})\,dx=0.

(3.2)

From the divergence theorem and the boundary condition of (
3.1
), we rewrite the first term of (
3.2
) as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Delta\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\frac{P}{u_{d}}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{P}{u_{d}}\right)\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)^{-1}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)^{-2}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\right|^{2}\left(\frac{P}{u_{d}}\right)^{2}\,dx,

and therefore, from (

),

\alpha\int_{\Omega}(u_{d}-K)\,dx=d\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\right|^{2}\left(\frac{P}{u_{d}}\right)^{2}\,dx>0.

were constant over

If the intrinsic growth rate is not totally aligned with the carrying capacity, a choice of more sophisticated strategies is required.

1.5
) satisfies

\int_{\Omega}u_{d}\,dx<\int_{\Omega}K\,dx,~~\textrm{ for all }~~d>0.

Proof.

The proof literally repeats the agrument in
[
18
]
for
P\equiv 1
, but it is presented here for completeness.

Assume the contrary that there exists some
d^{*}>0
such that

\int_{\Omega}u_{d^{*}}\,dx\geq\int_{\Omega}K\,dx.

(3.3)

Integrating (
1.5
) for
d=d^{*}
, we get

\displaystyle r\int_{\Omega}u_{d^{*}}\left(1-\frac{u_{d^{*}}}{K}\right)\,dx=-d^{*}\int_{\Omega}\Delta\left(\frac{u_{d^{*}}}{P}\right)\,dx=d^{*}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\left(\frac{u_{d^{*}}}{P}\right)\,dS=0

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{d^{*}}\left(1-\frac{u_{d^{*}}}{K}\right)\,dx=0

(3.4)

from the boundary condition in (
1.5
). Adding up (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we obtain

0\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(u_{d^{*}}-K+u_{d^{*}}\left(1-\frac{u_{d^{*}}}{K}\right)\right)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\left(u_{d^{*}}-K\right)\left(1-\frac{u_{d^{*}}}{K}\right)\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{K}\left(u_{d^{*}}-K\right)^{2}\,dx\leq 0,

which leads to
\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{K}\left(u_{d^{*}}-K\right)^{2}\,dx=0
and
u_{d^{*}}\equiv K
over
\Omega
, which is only possible if
P\equiv K
over
\Omega
, contradicting the assumption that
P
and
K
are linearly independent.

Therefore, we must have
M(d)<\int_{\Omega}K\,dx
for all
d>0
.

∎

3.2
Tendencies for slow dispersal

In this section, we consider solution asymptotics in the case
d\to 0^{+}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that (A) holds and let
u_{d}
be a solution to (
1.5
).

1.

If
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla r\,dx>0
, then for
d>0
small,

\int_{\Omega}u_{d}\,dx>\int_{\Omega}K\,dx.

2.

If
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla r\,dx<0
, then for
d>0
small,

\int_{\Omega}u_{d}\,dx<\int_{\Omega}K\,dx.

Proof.

Let us prove item 1, as item 2 is proven analogously. Multiply the first equation in (
1.5
) by
\frac{K}{ru_{d}}
,

d\Delta\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\frac{K}{ru_{d}}+K-u_{d}=0,

integrate it and use that
\frac{\partial}{\partial n}(\frac{u_{d}}{P})=0
on
\partial\Omega
to obtain

-d\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{K}{ru_{d}}\right)\,dx+\int_{\Omega}(K-u_{d})\,dx=0.

(3.5)

Next, let us prove that

\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{K}{ru_{d}}\right)-\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\right|\,dx\to 0\textrm{ as }d\to 0^{+}.

For that purpose, notice that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{K}{ru_{d}}\right)-\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\right|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}-\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{K}{ru_{d}}\right)\right|\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{K}{ru_{d}}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\right|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}-\frac{K}{P}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\nabla\left(\frac{K}{ru_{d}}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\left\|\nabla\left(\frac{K}{ru_{d}}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I+II.

From Proposition
2.1
we get that
u_{d}\to K
on
L^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,2}(\Omega)
as
d\to 0
, therefore the first term of
I
vanishes. Moreover,

\nabla\left(\frac{K}{ru_{d}}\right)=\nabla\left(\frac{K}{r}\right)\frac{1}{u_{d}}-\frac{K}{ru_{d}^{2}}\nabla u_{d},

which is bounded, as
u_{d}\to K
implies that
\nabla u_{d}
is bounded and
u_{d}
is far from zero almost everywhere. Therefore,
I\to 0
when
d\to 0
.

To estimate
II
, notice that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\left(\frac{K}{ru_{d}}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{\nabla K}{ru_{d}}-\frac{K\nabla r}{r^{2}u_{d}}-\frac{K}{ru_{d}^{2}}\nabla u_{d}+\frac{\nabla r}{r^{2}}\right|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{\nabla K}{ru_{d}}-\frac{\nabla u_{d}}{ru_{d}}\right|\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{\nabla u_{d}}{ru_{d}}-\frac{K}{ru_{d}^{2}}\nabla u_{d}\right|\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{K\nabla r}{r^{2}u_{d}}-\frac{\nabla r}{r^{2}}\right|\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{1}{ru_{d}}\right|\left|\nabla K-\nabla u_{d}\right|\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{\nabla u_{d}}{ru_{d}}\right|\left|1-\frac{K}{u_{d}}\right|\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{\nabla r}{r^{2}}\right|\left|\frac{K}{u_{d}}-1\right|\,dx.

(3.6)

According to Proposition
2.1
, as
d\to 0^{+}
,
u_{d}\to K
on both
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
norms, therefore,
|\frac{1}{u_{d}}|
and
|\nabla u_{d}|
are bounded on
\Omega
, and therefore, each integral on (
3.6
) vanishes as
d\to 0^{+}
.

Therefore, as
d\to 0^{+}
,

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{K}{ru_{d}}\right)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\,dx+h(d),

where
\lim_{d\to 0^{+}}h(d)=0
. And from (
3.5
), we get

\int_{\Omega}(K-u_{d})\,dx=d\left(\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\,dx+h(d)\right)

which is negative for small enough
d>0
, since

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla r\,dx\leq-\frac{1}{\max_{\bar{\Omega}}r^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla r\,dx<0.

∎

Remark 3.5
.

When
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla r\,dx=0
, nothing can be concluded about the monotonicity of
\int_{\Omega}u_{d}\,dx

for
d>0
small, as shown in Example
4.1
.

Definition 3.6
.

We say that two functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
are
correlated by a function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
in
\Omega
if we have

f(x)=h(g(x))\mbox{ ~~ for all ~~ }x\in\Omega.

(3.7)

If (
3.7
) is satisfied in
\Omega
and
h
is non-decreasing, we say that
f
and
g
are
positively correlated
in
\Omega
, and if
h
is non-increasing, we say that
f
and
g
are
negatively correlated
in
\Omega
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Suppose
r\equiv K
over
\Omega
, and
P
and
K
are correlated by a function
h:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
, and we write
P(x)=h(K(x))
in
\Omega
.

1.

If
h^{\prime}(t)>\frac{h(t)}{t}
for all
t>0
, then for
d>0
small,

\int_{\Omega}u_{d}\,dx<\int_{\Omega}K\,dx.

2.

If
h^{\prime}(t)<\frac{h(t)}{t}
for all
t>0
, then for
d>0
small,

\int_{\Omega}u_{d}\,dx>\int_{\Omega}K\,dx.

Proof.

1.

By Lemma
3.4
, it is enough to determine the sign of

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla K\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{|\nabla K|^{2}}{P}-K\frac{\nabla P\cdot\nabla K}{P^{2}}\right)\,dx.

If
P(x)=h(K(x))
, then

\nabla P=h^{\prime}(K(x))\nabla K(x),

and we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla K\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\left(P-Kh^{\prime}(K)\right)\frac{|\nabla K|^{2}}{P^{2}}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}\left(h(K)-Kh^{\prime}(K)\right)\frac{|\nabla K|^{2}}{P^{2}}\,dx.

If
h^{\prime}(t)>\frac{h(t)}{t}
for all
t>0
, then
h(t)-th^{\prime}(t)<0
for all
t>0
, and
\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla K\,dx<0
, which implies, by Lemma
3.4
, that

\int_{\Omega}u_{d}\,dx<\int_{\Omega}K\,dx.

And if
h^{\prime}(t)<\frac{h(t)}{t}
for all
t>0
, then
h(t)-th^{\prime}(t)>0
, implying
\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla K\,dx>0
, and follows from Lemma
3.4
that

\int_{\Omega}u_{d}\,dx>\int_{\Omega}K\,dx.

∎

A sufficient condition for Lemma
3.4
is when there exists a positive or negative correlation between
r
and
\frac{K}{P}
, a fact we prove next.

Theorem 3.8
.

Assume assumption (A) holds. If
r
and
\frac{K}{P}
are positively correlated in
\Omega
, then, for
d>0
small,

\int_{\Omega}u_{d}\,dx>\int_{\Omega}K\,dx.

If
r
and
\frac{K}{P}
are negatively correlated in
\Omega
, we get, for
d>0
small,

\int_{\Omega}u_{d}\,dx<\int_{\Omega}K\,dx.

Proof.

If
r
and
\frac{K}{P}
are positively correlated (similar proof for negative correlation), there exists
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
non-decreasing with
r(x)=h\left(\frac{K(x)}{P(x)}\right)
for every
x\in\Omega
, thus

\nabla r=h^{\prime}\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)

and

\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla r=h^{\prime}\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\left|\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\right|^{2}\geq 0\,\textrm{ everywhere in }\Omega.

And since
h^{\prime}(x)\equiv 0
or
\frac{K}{P}\equiv
cte in
\Omega
means that
r
is constant, we obtain the strict inequality

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla r\,dx>0.

The proof is concluded by applying Lemma
3.4
.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

When
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla r\,dx>0
, we can only be sure that
M(d)=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{d}\,dx
is maximized at some intermediate dispersion coefficient
d^{*}\in(0,+\infty)
when
M(0)\geq M(+\infty)
, or equivalently,

\int_{\Omega}K\,dx\geq\frac{\int_{\Omega}rP\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}\frac{r}{K}P^{2}\,dx}\int_{\Omega}P\,dx.

If
M(0)<M(+\infty)
, it is possible for
M(d)
to be always increasing over
(0,+\infty)
, as illustrated on Example
4.2
.

Some additional relations of weighted averages are presented in the Appendix.

3.3
The case when the correlation is described by a power function

Based on Theorem
3.1
, it is reasonable to question if solutions to (
1.5
) satisfy the estimate (
1.3
) when, instead of
r=\alpha\frac{K}{P}
, we have

r=\alpha\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda}~~~\textrm{in}~~~\Omega,

(3.8)

where
\alpha\in(0,+\infty)
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\lambda>0
,
r
and
\frac{K}{P}
are correlated by the function
h(x)=\alpha x^{\lambda}
, which is increasing in
(0,+\infty)
, and we can apply Theorem
3.8
to obtain an estimate (
1.3
) for small values of
d>0
.

In this section, let us denote by
u_{d,\lambda}
the positive solution to

\begin{cases}d\Delta\left(\frac{u(x)}{P(x)}\right)+\alpha\left(\frac{K(x)}{P(x)}\right)^{\lambda}u(x)\left(1-\frac{u(x)}{K(x)}\right)=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}(x)=0,&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(3.9)

for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
d>0
, and the total population function

M_{\lambda}(d)=\int_{\Omega}u_{d,\lambda}\,dx.

To analyze the total population of
u_{d,\lambda}
as
d
increases, let us follow the strategy on Remark
3.9
and analyze the relation between
M_{\lambda}(0)
and
M_{\lambda}(+\infty)
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
f
,
g:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be positive functions. If
f
is non-constant and
\int_{\Omega}g\,dx=1
, it holds

\frac{\int_{\Omega}gf\ln f\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}gf\,dx}>\int_{\Omega}g\ln f\,dx.

(3.10)

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}g(x)f(x)\ln f(x)\,dx-\int_{\Omega}g(x)\ln f(x)\,dx\int_{\Omega}g(y)f(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(f(x)-\int_{\Omega}g(y)f(y)\,dy\right)\left(\ln f(x)-\ln\left(\int_{\Omega}g(y)f(y)\,dy\right)\right)g(x)\,dx,

since

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(f(x)-\int_{\Omega}g(y)f(y)\,dy\right)\ln\left(\int_{\Omega}g(y)f(y)\,dy\right)g(x)\,dx=0,

where we recall that
\int_{\Omega}g(x)\,dx=1
. Therefore, denoting
L=\int_{\Omega}g(y)f(y)\,dy
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}gf\ln f\,dx-\int_{\Omega}g\ln f\,dx\int_{\Omega}gf\,dy=\int_{\Omega}\left(f(x)-L\right)\left(\ln f(x)-\ln L\right)g(x)\,dx.

We notice that this integral is non-negative, since
g(x)>0
, and
\ln y
being increasing on
(0,+\infty)
implies that
f(x)-L
and
\ln f(x)-\ln L
always have the same signal over
\Omega
, and therefore,

\left(f(x)-L\right)\left(\ln f(x)-\ln L\right)\geq 0~~~\textrm{for every}~~~x\in\Omega.

Finally,
\int_{\Omega}\left(f(x)-L\right)\left(\ln f(x)-\ln L\right)g(x)\,dx=0
if, and only if,
f(x)=L
for all
x\in\Omega
, which contradicts
f
being non-constant.
∎

Theorem 3.11
.

The mapping
\lambda\mapsto M_{\lambda}(+\infty)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}\frac{K^{\lambda}}{P^{\lambda-1}}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}\frac{K^{\lambda-1}}{P^{\lambda-2}}\,dx}\int_{\Omega}P\,dx
is a strictly increasing function of
\lambda
in
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that

F(\lambda)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}\frac{K^{\lambda}}{P^{\lambda-1}}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}\frac{K^{\lambda-1}}{P^{\lambda-2}}\,dx}=\frac{\int_{\Omega}K\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda-1}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}K\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda-2}\,dx}

(3.11)

is strictly increasing in
\mathbb{R}
. Notice that

\ln(F(\lambda))=\ln\left(\int_{\Omega}K\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda-1}\,dx\right)-\ln\left(\int_{\Omega}K\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda-2}\,dx\right),

and differentiating with respect to
\lambda
,

\frac{F^{\prime}(\lambda)}{F(\lambda)}=\frac{\int_{\Omega}K\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda-1}\ln\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}K\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda-1}\,dx}-\frac{\int_{\Omega}K\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda-2}\ln\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}K\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda-2}\,dx}.

(3.12)

The right-hand side of Eq. (
3.12
) is positive, from (
3.10
) applied to

f=\frac{K}{P},~~~~g=\left(\int_{\Omega}K\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda-2}\,dx\right)^{-1}K\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda-2}

And since
F(\lambda)>0
, we obtain from (
3.12
) that
F^{\prime}(\lambda)>0
in
\mathbb{R}
. Therefore,
F(\lambda)
is increasing in
\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Theorem
3.11
and the fact that
M_{\lambda}(0)=M_{\lambda}(+\infty)
for
\lambda=1
implies that

M_{\lambda}(0)>M_{\lambda}(+\infty)\textrm{ for }\lambda>1

and

M_{\lambda}(0)<M_{\lambda}(+\infty)\textrm{ for }\lambda<1.

These facts are elucidated in Example
4.4
, and we explore their implications for our understanding of the behaviour of
M_{\lambda}(d)
in the Discussion section.

5
Discussion

We start with briefly summarizing the findings of the paper.

1.

We identified the case when diffusion leads to population increase, for any of its values.
As a special case for
P\equiv 1
, this includes Lou’s result in
[
25
]
.

2.

Assuming positive (or negative) correlation between the growth rate and the ratio of the carrying capacity to the dispersal strategy,
which for high levels of diffusion characterizes per capita space-dependent available resources,
we determine when a small diffusion has a positive (negative) effect on the total population size compared to its immobile counterpart.

3.

In the particular case when the growth rate is a power function of the ratio
r=(K/P)^{\lambda}
, we can characterize the global population size for large disparsal speed.
We conclude that, once the species adopt a large enough diffusion rate, the total population exceeds the total of the carrying capacity, once
the power function is convex, and is less if the function is concave. This fact has never been established before for the logistic equation with a regular diffusion.
The two cases that have been already investigated in the particular case
P\equiv 1
are
\lambda=1

[
25
]
and
\lambda=0

[
18
]
.
We illustrate that the values
\lambda=0
and
\lambda=1
are in some sense critical.

Let us comment on the third item. Note that the intermediate behaviour of the total population size as a function of the diffusion coefficient exhibits distinct profiles depending on the value of
\lambda
, as illustrated in Fig.
5
. Based on the results of this work, we conclude that for the case where
r=(K/P)^{\lambda}
, the following holds:

1.

For
\lambda\leq 0
, the average population decreases from its carrying capacity at
d=0
.
For
\lambda<0
, this is justified from
r=\alpha\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda}
representing a negative correlation between
r
and
\frac{K}{P}
and from Theorem
3.8
, while the
\lambda=0
case is covered by
[
18
]
. Furthermore, since Theorem
3.11
gives
M_{\lambda}(+\infty)<M_{\lambda}(0)
, we expect that
M_{\lambda}(d)<M_{\lambda}(0)
for all
d\in(0,+\infty)
.

2.

For
0<\lambda<1
,

r=\alpha\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda}
leads to a positive correlation between
r
and
\frac{K}{P}
, and Theorem
3.8
implies that the average population exceeds the average carrying capacity for slow diffusion
d>0
. However, the inequality
M_{\lambda}(d)>\int_{\Omega}K\,dx
cannot be satisfied for all
d>0
, specifically for
d
large enough, since the total population of
u_{d,\lambda}
converges to
M_{\lambda}(+\infty)<\int_{\Omega}K\,dx
as
d\to+\infty
. Therefore,
M_{\lambda}(d)
is maximized for some diffusion coefficient
d^{*}\in(0,+\infty)
.

3.

For
\lambda=1
, Theorem
3.1
asserts that the total population exceeds the total carrying capacity for every
d>0
.
Due to the continuity of solutions to (
3.9
) with respect to
\lambda
, we expect that for values of
\lambda
close to
1
, the average population
M_{\lambda}(d)
is still maximized at some intermediate point
d_{\lambda}^{*}\in(0,+\infty)
.

4.

For
\lambda>1
large enough, the asymptotic value
M_{\lambda}(+\infty)
should also become large enough that
M_{\lambda}(d)
is an increasing function of
d
in
(0,+\infty)
.

Based on the preceding analysis, we formulate the following conjecture:

Conjecture
.

Assume
K
,
P
and
r=\alpha\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)^{\lambda}
satisfy hypothesis (A). Then there exists a critical value
\lambda^{*}>1
such that

a)

If
\lambda\in(0,\lambda^{*})
, the function
M_{\lambda}(d)
attains its maximum at a finite diffusion rate
d^{*}_{\lambda}\in(0,+\infty)
;

b)

If
\lambda\in(\lambda^{*},+\infty)
, the function
M_{\lambda}(d)
is strictly increasing in
(0,+\infty)
, and thus does not attain a maximum value.

With these empirical results and some statements justified, especially in the asymptotic cases of very slow or very fast dispersal, the problem
of the qualitative description of the population size as a function of the diffusion coefficient still leaves many questions without answer.
Let us discuss some topics emerging from the research of the current paper.

1.

The first group of questions, as mentioned above, is related to the form of the total population as a function of the diffusion coefficient.

(a)

Investigation of the case
r=f(K/P)
, where
f(x)=x^{\lambda}
, opens ways to the study of the dependency of the population on the diffusion coefficient for

, fractional linear, exponential (in this case, we expect the behaviour similar to

with large positive

(b)

Prove or disprove for

the conjecture that for
r
proportional to
K

.

(c)

If the previous item is justified, what are the conditions on

(d)

What are the relations of

(e)

Prove or disprove the conjecture that, once

Maximizing the total population size is not directly connected to the success in the compertition, or evolution stability, compare, for example,

Appendix: Some Additional Inequalities on Weighted Averages

In the Appendix, we consider some weighted averages that relate the total population with the parameters of Equation (
1.5
).

Theorem 6.1
.

If
r
and
K
are positively correlated and
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla K\cdot\nabla P\,dx<0
, we get for small values of
d>0
the weighted inequality

\int_{\Omega}Pu_{d}\,dx>\int PK\,dx.

Proof.

Multiply (
1.5
) by
\frac{KP}{ru_{d}}
and integrate, to obtain

-d\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{KP}{ru_{d}}\right)\,dx+\int_{\Omega}(PK-Pu_{d})\,dx=0.

Then prove that

\int\left|\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{KP}{ru_{d}}\right)-\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{P}{r}\right)\right|\,dx\to 0\textrm{ as }d\to 0,

as done on the proof of Lemma (
3.4
), and use that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{P}{r}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{P\nabla K-K\nabla P}{P^{2}}\cdot\frac{r\nabla P-P\nabla r}{r^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{P[r\nabla K+K\nabla r]\cdot\nabla P-Kr|\nabla P|^{2}-P^{2}\nabla K\cdot\nabla r}{P^{2}r^{2}}\,dx<0,

∎

Theorem 6.2
.

Suppose that
K
and
P
are linearly independent and are correlated by a function
h
, i.e.
P=h(K)
.

If
h^{\prime}(t)\geq\frac{h(t)}{t}
for
t\in(0,+\infty)
, then for
d>0
small,

\int_{\Omega}ru_{d}^{2}\,dx>\int_{\Omega}rKu_{d}\,dx.

And if
h^{\prime}(t)\leq\frac{h(t)}{t}
for
t\in(0,+\infty)
, then for
d>0
small,

\int_{\Omega}ru_{d}^{2}\,dx<\int_{\Omega}rKu_{d}\,dx<\int_{\Omega}rK^{2}\,dx.

Proof.

Multiply (
1.5
) by
K
and integrate:

-d\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla K\,dx+\int_{\Omega}ru_{d}(K-u_{d})\,dx=0.

Notice that

\int\left|\nabla\left(\frac{u_{d}}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla K-\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla K\right|\,dx\to 0\textrm{ as }d\to 0,

which implies

\int_{\Omega}ru_{d}(K-u_{d})\,dx=d\left(\int_{\Omega}\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla K\,dx+o(1)\right)

and it is enough to use

\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)=\frac{P\nabla K-K\nabla P}{P^{2}}=\frac{h(K)\nabla K-Kh^{\prime}(K)\nabla K}{P^{2}}

\implies\nabla\left(\frac{K}{P}\right)\cdot\nabla K=(h(K)-Kh^{\prime}(K))\frac{|\nabla K|^{2}}{P^{2}}\leq(\geq)0.

In the case
h^{\prime}(t)\leq\frac{h(t)}{t}
, we find, for small values of
d>0
,

\int_{\Omega}rKu_{d}\,dx>\int_{\Omega}ru_{d}^{2}\,dx

(6.1)

From the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\left(\int_{\Omega}rKu_{d}\,dx\right)^{2}=\left(\int_{\Omega}r^{\frac{1}{2}}Kr^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{d}\,dx\right)^{2}\leq\int_{\Omega}rK^{2}\,dx\int_{\Omega}ru_{d}^{2}\,dx.

(6.2)

Finally, (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) lead to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}rKu_{d}\,dx~\int_{\Omega}rKu_{d}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}rK^{2}\,dx~\int_{\Omega}ru_{d}^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle<\int_{\Omega}rK^{2}\,dx\int_{\Omega}rKu_{d}\,dx

hence, for slow diffusion,

\int_{\Omega}rKu_{d}\,dx<\int_{\Omega}rK^{2}\,dx,

which concludes the proof.

∎

Remark 6.3
.

Note that if both inequalities
h^{\prime}(t)\geq\frac{h(t)}{t}
and
h^{\prime}(t)\leq\frac{h(t)}{t}
are satisfied for all
t\in(0,+\infty)
, then we must have the equality

\frac{h^{\prime}(t)}{h(t)}=\frac{1}{t}\implies\ln|h(t)|=\ln|t|+\alpha\implies h(t)=\alpha t,

for some constant
\alpha>0
and for all
t>0
, leading to
P=h(K)=\alpha K
. Since
K
and
P
are not linearly dependent, we conclude that the two items from Theorem
6.2
cannot cannot hold simultaneously, and there is no contradiction in the theorem’s statement.
