Acute Pansinusitis Caused by a Retained Merocel Pack Forgotten for 50 Days: A Case Report With Histopathologic Insights
Authors: Joo Hyung Lee, Dong Hwan Kwon, Hyunjong Jeon, Hee Sung Chae, Eun Jung Lee, Young-Ha Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Merocel, a polyvinyl acetal sponge, is a nonabsorbable nasal packing material widely used after sinonasal surgery because of its hemostatic efficacy and ease of placement. However, nonabsorbable packing is generally removed within 48 hours to reduce the risks of mucosal injury, infection, and adhesion. A 48-year-old man underwent endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) for chronic rhinosinusitis at a secondary hospital. The Merocel pack was inadvertently retained for 50 days, resulting in acute pansinusitis with foul odor, postnasal drip, and facial pain. Attempts to remove the packing by traction and irrigation at the secondary hospital were unsuccessful and caused fragmentation of the Merocel. The patient was subsequently referred to our tertiary center, where revision ESS revealed firmly adherent Merocel fragments. Histopathological examination demonstrated inflammatory tissue ingrowth into the porous Merocel structure without bone fragment entrapment or osseous ingrowth. The patient recovered uneventfully, with no recurrence at the 3-month follow-up. Prolonged retention of a Merocel nasal pack may cause acute sinusitis and firm adhesion through inflammatory tissue ingrowth, even without histopathological evidence of bone involvement. Prevention requires timely pack removal, structured follow-up, and comprehensive patient education.

INTRODUCTION

Nasal packing is commonly placed after sinonasal surgery to achieve hemostasis, support mucosal healing, and prevent synechiae [1]. Merocel, a nonabsorbable polyvinyl acetal (PVA) sponge, is widely used because of its high fluid absorption capacity, volume retention, flexibility, and relatively atraumatic removal [2]. Despite these advantages, current guidelines recommend removing nonabsorbable packs such as Merocel within 48 hours. Experimental studies have shown that retention beyond 48 hours can cause progressive mucosal inflammation and cartilage damage [3], whereas clinical reports have described severe systemic complications, including toxic shock syndrome [4]. Absorbable alternatives, including polyurethane foam (Nasopore) and hyaluronate-based hydrogels, as well as nonabsorbable PVA products such as Visco, have been proposed to reduce adhesion and improve patient comfort [1,5,6].

To our knowledge, no previous case report has documented prolonged Merocel retention with histopathological evidence of the adhesion mechanism. We report a rare case in which a Merocel pack was retained for 50 days, resulting in acute pansinusitis, and histopathological examination provided evidence regarding the mechanism of adhesion.

CASE REPORT

A 48-year-old man underwent left endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) under local anesthesia for chronic rhinosinusitis at a secondary hospital in January 2023. A Merocel nasal pack was inserted at the end of the procedure. After surgery, the patient missed scheduled follow-up visits because of personal circumstances and used only oral medication without performing nasal irrigation. By mid-February 2023, he had developed progressively worsening foul odor, postnasal drip, left facial pain, and subjective fever. In March 2023, he returned to the same hospital, where endoscopic examination confirmed that the Merocel remained at the ESS site. Manual traction was attempted after saline irrigation and ointment application, but the pack could not be removed easily. Forceful extraction fragmented the Merocel; part of the pack was removed, but fragments remained at the ESS site. Because removal was incomplete and the symptoms persisted, the patient was referred to our tertiary center (Table 1).

At presentation to our institution in March 2023, the patient had no medical, family, or psychosocial history relevant to this episode. Nasal endoscopy showed diffuse mucosal edema and bulging of the remnant uncinate process (Fig. 1A), as well as diffuse mucopurulent discharge obstructing the osteomeatal unit (OMU) (Fig. 1B). Residual Merocel fragments were identified within the OMU (Fig. 1C). Noncontrast computed tomography (CT) of the paranasal sinuses demonstrated near-total opacification of the left maxillary and ethmoid sinuses (Fig. 2A) and the frontal sinus (Fig. 2B). Soft-tissue density consistent with mucosal thickening was also present in the sphenoid sinus. No discrete foreign-body signal was identifiable on noncontrast CT because the retained packing material could not be readily distinguished from the surrounding inflammatory tissue and secretions.

Revision ESS was performed under general anesthesia to remove the retained foreign material and treat the acute sinusitis. The swollen remnant uncinate process and granulomatous tissue obstructing the OMU were excised. Careful dissection identified Merocel fragments lateral to the superior turbinate (Fig. 3A) and at the posterolateral aspect of the middle meatal antrostomy (MMA) site (Fig. 3B). The fragments were removed with the adjacent inflammatory tissue and submitted for histopathological examination. Histopathology showed inflammatory cell infiltration into the porous Merocel structure (Fig. 4A). Adjacent ethmoid bone fragments were visible, but there was no direct bone fragment entrapment or osseous ingrowth (Fig. 4B). High-magnification views showed a predominantly neutrophilic and lymphocytic infiltrate with scattered plasma cells at the tissue–material interface (Fig. 4C and D). At the 3-month follow-up, endoscopy confirmed complete mucosal healing, with no residual Merocel fragments at the MMA (Fig. 5A) or superior turbinate (Fig. 5B). No adhesions or obstructions were observed at the surgical site (Fig. 5C). The patient reported no residual symptoms, described his recovery as uneventful, and expressed satisfaction with the complete resolution of his condition after surgery.

DISCUSSION

Nasal packing remains a common adjunct to ESS because it provides hemostasis, stabilizes the mucosa, offers structural support, and may reduce postoperative synechiae [1]. Among the available materials, nonabsorbable PVA sponges such as Merocel are widely used because of their high fluid absorption capacity and tamponade effect [2].

Despite these advantages, Merocel is not intended for prolonged intranasal use. Experimental and clinical evidence has linked retention beyond 48 hours with epithelial and cartilage injury [3], greater discomfort and bleeding during removal [7], allergic reactions accompanied by septal or turbinate necrosis [8], secondary infection requiring antibiotics [9,10], and, rarely, life-threatening toxic shock syndrome [4] or chronic granulomatous foreign-body reactions [11]. Previously reported retained sinonasal foreign bodies have typically presented as well-formed granulomatous masses after years of retention [11]. In contrast, after 50 days of retention, the present case showed a mixed acute-to-subacute inflammatory infiltrate composed predominantly of mononuclear cells, including lymphocytes and plasma cells, with admixed acute inflammatory cells. Multinucleated giant cells and the granulomatous features of a mature foreign-body reaction were absent. This pattern suggests that both the duration of retention and the porosity of the material may influence the histopathological response. The absence of systemic progression after 50 days of retention also merits consideration. Toxic shock syndrome associated with retained nasal packing is caused by exotoxin-producing Staphylococcus aureus and manifests as a systemic inflammatory response. By contrast, the localized inflammatory ingrowth observed in this patient may reflect differences in the colonizing bacterial flora, host immune response, or material properties. Together, these findings support removal of nonabsorbable nasal packs within 48 hours. When prolonged retention is unavoidable because of surgical circumstances, appropriate antibiotic use, clear education about the medical necessity of pack removal, and close postoperative follow-up are essential.

This case provides histopathological evidence regarding the mechanism of adhesion after prolonged Merocel retention. The firm intraoperative adhesion raised concern for bone involvement. Histopathological examination instead suggested that inflammatory tissue had grown into the porous sponge structure, without bone fragment entrapment or osseous ingrowth. However, the limited number of tissue sections prevents a definitive conclusion. This distinction may explain why traction and irrigation failed at the secondary hospital and why definitive excision under general anesthesia was required at the tertiary center. The absence of a discrete foreign-body signal on noncontrast CT also illustrates the difficulty of diagnosing retained nonabsorbable packing. PVA sponges are typically isodense to surrounding mucosal secretions on noncontrast CT and lack distinctive radiological features, which likely explains why the retained material was not separately visualized in this case. The attenuation values of hydrated PVA sponge, inflammatory secretions, and mucosal edema largely overlap within the range of 0–80 Hounsfield units (HU). Therefore, using a window center within the standard soft-tissue range (approximately 40–50 HU) and narrowing the window width below standard soft-tissue settings may improve contrast and help distinguish retained packing from surrounding tissue in diagnostically challenging cases. Contrast-enhanced CT may also improve delineation because the surrounding inflammatory granulation tissue characteristically demonstrates mucosal enhancement. When retained packing is clinically suspected, CT findings should therefore be interpreted in conjunction with nasal endoscopy.

These findings should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. Only a limited number of tissue sections were examined, and they may not represent the entire adhesion interface; unsampled regions may have contained different features. In addition, the single-case design limits the generalizability of the observations. Larger case series or experimental models are needed to validate these findings and further characterize tissue responses to prolonged Merocel retention.

Several preventive strategies and alternative materials have been proposed to reduce complications related to adhesion and retention. These include covering Merocel with a glove finger [12] and using coated PVA sponges with smaller pores, such as Visco [5]. Compared with Merocel, absorbable materials such as Nasopore and hyaluronate-based hydrogels may reduce pain, bleeding, and adhesion formation. However, they may provide a weaker tamponade effect, and some absorbable spacers have been associated with higher rates of synechiae in certain settings [1,6,13]. Material selection should therefore balance hemostatic efficacy, patient comfort, cost, and adhesion risk.

Merocel should not be used as a long-term packing material. Clear patient education and close postoperative monitoring are essential after sinonasal surgery. Despite the limitations of a single case, this report provides histopathological evidence suggesting that inflammatory tissue ingrowth contributed to adhesion after inadvertently prolonged Merocel retention. Although such retention is rare, the case emphasizes the importance of clinical vigilance, patient counseling, and informed decisions regarding nasal packing.

In conclusion, this case demonstrates the clinical risks and a possible histopathological mechanism of prolonged Merocel retention. Prolonged placement was associated with acute sinusitis and firm adhesion attributable to inflammatory tissue ingrowth into the porous Merocel structure, without bone fragment entrapment or osseous ingrowth. Timely removal, structured follow-up, and patient education are essential components of postoperative care, and absorbable or modified alternatives may further reduce these risks. The case also indicates the need for larger studies to characterize tissue responses more fully and inform safer selection of packing materials.
