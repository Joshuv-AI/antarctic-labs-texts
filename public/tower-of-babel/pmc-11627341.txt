Cone-beam computed tomography-guided transbronchial lung biopsies: Leading the way to precision
Authors: Hari Kishan Gonuguntla, Preeti Vidyasagar, Parul Mrigpuri
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is a novel, emerging technique to perform transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB). It is a variant of CT and has gained prominence over the years for its applications in various fields like dentistry and interventional radiology.[1] CBCT employs a cone-shaped X-ray beam and a two-dimensional detector to produce detailed three-dimensional images of anatomical structures. Using a CBCT-guided approach into bronchoscopy procedures enables to precisely navigate peripheral lung lesions that might be challenging to reach using traditional methods.[2] We herein describe our experience of three cases using this novel modality with respect to the feasibility, technique, and complications.

The cases were performed in the Azurion 7C Lab dedicated to Pulmonology called Azurion Lung Suite [Figure 1]. Bronchoscopy was performed under general anaesthesia, via endotracheal tube using Olympus MP 190F scope. Sampling was done using Cryo 1.1 probe and forceps. Rapid on-site cytology examination (ROSE) was done to assess sample adequacy. The CBCT C-arm was utilized for both fluoroscopy imaging (2D) and CT scans. C-arm was iso-centred and a 5.2 sec initial scan was performed which was used to reconstruct images in 3 different planes. During the spins, the bronchoscope was fixed to a position with an articulating arm that attaches to the bronchoscope.

Azurion lung suite

Our first was a 22-year-old male, a diagnosed case of Ewings sarcoma where PET CT revealed FDG avid (SUV max 14.2) lesion in the left upper lobe. The second case was a 59-year-old male, postliver transplant who presented with cough for 2 weeks. HRCT of the patient showed thick-walled cavitary lesion, and bronchoalveolar lavage was inconclusive. The third case was a 62-year-old man who presented with complaints of fever and cough for 2 months, and CT chest showed a solitary pulmonary nodule in the right lower lobe. Four samples were obtained from each case. None of the case experienced any major complications [Figure 2].

It has become increasingly important to determine the role of guided procedures in the evaluation of patients. The primary goal of these emerging techniques is to identify the etiology rapidly while limiting risk to the patient.[3] Transthoracic needle aspiration has a pneumothorax rate of about 25%, of which at least 15% requires chest tube insertion.[4] The sensitivity of traditional TBLB ranges from 14% to 63%, depending on the size and location of lesion.[5] CBCT-virtual-navigation-guided lung biopsy is safe, precise, has high success rates, and less complications. Also, when compared to conventional CT-guided biopsy, it facilitates needle positioning, thus reducing the procedural time and the effective X-ray dose.[6] Moreover, it has been found to be a cost-effective modality, particularly in intermediate risk pulmonary nodules.[7] The equipment installation cost is involved, but this Cath lab facility can be shared by various departments like neurology, cardiology, and interventional radiology. This precision significantly reduces the necessity for invasive procedures decreasing patient discomfort. CBCT is superior to fluoroscopy in terms of producing a 3D image and locating smaller lesions.

The CBCT-guided biopsies in the presented cases offer a glimpse into the diagnostic prowess of this technology. It enables the navigation around critical structures, minimizing the risk of complications like pneumothorax and haemorrhage. By overcoming the limitations of traditional bronchoscopy, CBCT offers a transformative approach, ensuring accurate lesion localization, enhanced safety, and curtail the need of invasive procedures. As we delve further into the era of precision medicine, CBCT-guided bronchoscopy stands as a beacon, illuminating the path toward more effective and patient-centric diagnostic procedures.
