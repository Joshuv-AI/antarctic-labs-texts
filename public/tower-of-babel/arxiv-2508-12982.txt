Revisiting Functional Derivatives in Multi-object Tracking
Jan Krejčí, Ondřej Straka, Petr Girg, and Jiří Benedikt

Abstract
Probability generating functionals (PGFLs) are efficient and powerful tools for tracking independent objects in clutter. It was shown that PGFLs could be used for the elegant derivation of practical multi-object tracking algorithms, e.g., the probability hypothesis density (PHD) filter. However, derivations using PGFLs use the so-called functional derivatives whose definitions usually appear too complicated or heuristic, involving Dirac delta “functions”. This paper begins by comparing different definitions of functional derivatives and exploring their relationships and implications for practical applications. It then proposes a rigorous definition of the functional derivative, utilizing straightforward yet precise mathematics for clarity. Key properties of the functional derivative are revealed and discussed.

II-H

Problem Statement

The central problem of this paper is the

of functional derivatives, including their higher-order versions (
11
) for functionals appearing in MOT.
If the density function and moments exist, Eq. (

)
must hold, i.e., the derivatives shall be possible to evaluate at

Properties of the RFS density that guarantee the existence of the derivative should also be given, so that engineers need not check the aforementioned existence.
Regarding the directions

forming the set

Moreover, the derivative framework should obey formal differentiation rules

Note that the chain rule is key, so that if the formal rules

That is, the definition should allow for the practical use of the derivatives.
The main goal of this paper is to seek a definition of functional derivatives that is simple, mathematically rigorous, general, and thus reliable and useful.

III
State of the Art in Functional Derivatives: Definitions and Analysis

In this section, existing definitions of functional derivatives are stated as presented in the literature and reviewed.
Assumptions that are usually missing in the MOT literature are added and critically assessed.

It should be noted that any existing definition of the functional derivative, regardless it is rigorous or not, shall not influence its purely practical usage described, e.g., in
[

21
]

However, using the wrong definition may lead to erroneous conclusions on the theoretical level.
In general, using speculative mathematics can be dangerous and has severe consequences

In the case of MOT, this could lead to accounting algorithms as Bayes-optimal in certain applications, even though they could not be derived correctly under given circumstances, resulting in being merely ad-hoc solutions.

The common starting point for all the following definitions may be the directional (variational) derivative of a functional

V
Conclusion

This paper focused on theoretical aspects of multi-object tracking, namely, on the lack of mathematical rigor regarding the use of functional derivatives.
Currently existing definitions of functional derivatives were shown to fail to fulfill several requirements.
Some are impractical, others have unclear or limited range of validity, or they are defined for too restrictive base (state or measurement) spaces only.
It is important to note that current definitions do not adequately provide for the chain differentiation rule.

As a first step to address this inconvenience, this paper proposed a definition based on measure theory on locally compact and Hausdorff spaces.
Namely, the Dirac delta used in the definition is mathematically constructed as a measure, i.e., the Dirac measure.
The functional derivative of any finite order, as defined, is Fréchet, allowing the application of the chain rule and other differentiation rules.
While the proposed definition is mathematically rigorous, the corresponding analytical expressions may be too complex for practical use, which necessitates further investigation.
The list of open problems leaves room for future work.

[

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\underbrace{\cup_{B\in\beta}B}_{U}\Big|\beta\subset\mathcal{B}\Big\}\,.

Locally compact Hausdorff

Radon Measures and Dual of
C_{0}(\mathcal{X})

At this point, we assume that the reader is familiar with definitions of a
measure

[
37
, Sec. 1.3, p. 24]
, a
signed measure

[
37
, Sec. 3.1, p. 85]
, and a
complex measure

[
37
, Sec. 3.3, p. 93]
on a
measure space

(\mathcal{X},\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma
stands for a
\sigma

algebra

[
37
, Sec. 1.2, p. 21]
of subsets of
\mathcal{X}
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T})
be a topological space.
Then
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathcal{T}
is called

Borel
\sigma
-algebra
and it is denoted by
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T})
. Elements of
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T})
are called
Borel subsets
of
\mathcal{X}
. A measure defined on all Borel subsets of
\mathcal{X}
is called
Borel measure
.
For lcscH spaces, Borel measures are well-behaved with the following properties.

Let
\eta
be a Borel measure on
\mathcal{X}
and
E
a Borel subset of
\mathcal{X}
.
The measure
\eta
is called
outer regular
on
E
if

\displaystyle\eta(E)=\inf\{\eta(U):U\supset E,\ U\text{ open }\}\,,

(83)

and
inner regular
on
E
if

\displaystyle\eta(E)=\sup\{\eta(K):K\subset E,\ K\text{ compact }\}\,.

(84)

Following
[
37
, p. 212]
, the Borel measure
\eta
is called
Radon measure
, if it is
(i)
finite on all compact sets,
(ii)
outer regular on all Borel sets, and
(iii)
inner regular on all open sets.

According to
[
37
, p. 222]
, a
signed
Radon measure is a
signed
Borel measure whose positive and negative
variations
(see below) are Radon.
Further, a
complex
Radon measure is a
complex
Borel measure, i.e., taking values in
\mathbb{C}
, whose real and imaginary parts are signed Radon measures.
Unlike for (real) measures, note that infinite values are not allowed for complex measures.

The set
M(\mathcal{X})
of all complex Radon measures on
\mathcal{X}
forms a normed linear space over the field
\mathbb{C}
, where the norm for each
\eta\in M(\mathcal{X})
is defined as

\displaystyle\|\eta\|=|\eta|(\mathcal{X})\,,

(85)

where
|\eta|(E)
for any
E\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T})
is the
total variation
of
\eta
.
For
\eta
being a (real) signed measure, the total variation of
\eta
is a measure defined as

\displaystyle|\eta|(E)\triangleq\eta^{+}(E)+\eta^{-}(E)\,,

(86)

with
\eta^{+}
and
\eta^{-}
being the positive and negative variations (both are positive measures) given by the Jordan decomposition theorem
[
37
, pp. 87–88]
.
For
\eta
being a complex measure, the total variation is the sum of the total variations of its real and imaginary parts
[
37
, p. 93]
.

The following theorem is an essential tool in establishing our results in Section
IV
, namely to show that the proposed directional derivative is continuous in the direction
\nu\in M(\mathcal{X})
.

Theorem 5

(The Riesz Representation Theorem
[
37
, Theorem 7.17]
)
Let
\mathcal{X}
be lcH, and for
\eta{\in}M(\mathcal{X})
and

let
I_{\eta}(f)=\!\int\!fd\eta
. Then the map
\eta\mapsto I_{\eta}
is an isometric isomorphism from

to
C_{0}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
, where

is the (topological) dual space of
C_{0}(\mathcal{X})

[
37
, p. 157]
.

That is, the space
M(\mathcal{X})
of all complex Radon measures on

can be identified with the space
C_{0}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
of all bounded linear functionals on

.

Supplement F
Dirac Deltas

There is significant demand in engineering and physics for methods to handle signals and densities of quantities that converge to a single point in the limit, while maintaining a constant total yield of the given quantity throughout this limiting process.
In this paper, the shrunk quantities are
Dirac deltas
.
They are used to mimic
standard basis vectors
to be used as
directions
for computing derivatives of functionals.

As a typical example, see e.g.,
[
34
]
, consider function sequences such as
f_{n}(x){=}\tfrac{n}{2}
for
x\in[-1/n,1/n]

and
f_{n}(x){=}0
elsewhere in
\mathbb{R}
for
n\to\infty
.
Note that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}x{=}1
for all
n{\in}\mathbb{N}
.
One may ask
in what sense
one needs to consider
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}
, because the pointwise limit of this function sequence is the
function

f
with
f(0){=}{+}\infty
and
f(x){=}0
elsewhere in
\mathbb{R}
.
Although there is a constant
yield
throughout the limiting process
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}x{=}1
, we see that the pointwise limit function
f
does
not
retain the yield since
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\mathrm{d}x{=}0\not=1
.
To capture the constant yield within the limiting entity, it turns out that the entity cannot be a
standard
function, see the example given in
[
15
, pp. 376–377, Remark 14]
.
In the following, two possibilities of how the limiting process can be considered are discussed.
