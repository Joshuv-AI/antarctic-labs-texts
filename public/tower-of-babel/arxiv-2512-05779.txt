On Sparse Representations of 3‑Manifolds
Kristóf Huszár, Clément Maria

Abstract
3-manifolds are commonly represented as triangulations, consisting of abstract tetrahedra whose triangular faces are identified in pairs. The combinatorial sparsity of a triangulation, as measured by the treewidth of its dual graph, plays a fundamental role in the design of parameterized algorithms. In this work, we investigate algorithmic procedures that transform or modify a given triangulation while controlling specific sparsity parameters. First, we describe a linear-time algorithm that converts a given triangulation into a Heegaard diagram of the underlying 3-manifold, showing that the construction preserves treewidth. We apply this construction to exhibit a fixed-parameter tractable framework for computing Kuperberg’s quantum invariants of 3-manifolds. Second, we present a quasi-linear-time algorithm that retriangulates a given triangulation into one with maximum edge valence of at most nine, while only moderately increasing the treewidth of the dual graph. Combining these two algorithms yields a quasi-linear-time algorithm that produces, from a given triangulation, a Heegaard diagram in which every attaching curve intersects at most nine others.

2.1
Graphs and their treewidth

By a

Introduced in

of a tree

and a collection

of

and the following three properties hold:

1.

(vertex coverage),

2.

The

4
Retriangulating with constant edge valence and controlled treewidth

In this section, we describe and analyze a quasi-linear time procedure that retriangulates any 3-manifold triangulation

into a triangulation

with maximum edge valence at most

Appendix A
On edge valence, experimentally

The treewidth of a triangulation is an indicator of
global sparsity
. While it measures how tree-like the gluing relation between tetrahedra is, it does not capture finer information about their proximity. Indeed,
tree decompositions
of triangulations relate only those tetrahedra glued along a common facet, and are blind to tetrahedra that are adjacent merely along an edge. The
valence
of an edge in a triangulation is the number of times it appears as an edge of some tetrahedron (counted with multiplicities). Although the average edge valence in a triangulated closed
3
-manifold is bounded, the valence may vary widely among edges; in particular, a single edge may be incident to all tetrahedra. In this sense, edge valence serves as an indicator of
local sparsity
.

In practice, high edge valences may significantly slow down computations. Many fundamental procedures—based on
normal surfaces
(e.g., sphere recognition),
hyperbolic geometry
(e.g., Thurston’s gluing equations), or
colorings
(e.g., quantum invariants)—involve systems of equations that become increasingly interdependent as edge valences vary widely. To illustrate the practical consequences of this phenomenon, Figures
15
and
16
show the number of
admissible colorings
for the Turaev–Viro quantum invariant at
U_{q}(\operatorname{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
as a function of the
variance
of edge valences in triangulations of the lens space
L(11,1)
and the Poincaré sphere. Since the number of admissible colorings directly affects the running time of backtracking algorithms for computing Turaev–Viro invariants
[
36
]
, this provides concrete evidence of the computational impact of edge-valence variability.

We also present the distribution of edge-valence variances among
reduced
triangulations of these two manifolds, which represent realistic algorithmic inputs. All triangulations were generated with
Regina

[

, and the reduced triangulations are local minima of

’s simplification heuristics.

Lemma 24

(Euler characteristic argument)
.

In any triangulation

of a closed

Very briefly on tensor networks

Originating in physics
[
22
, Section 1.3]
, tensors generalize vectors and linear transformations to a multilinear setting. Tensor networks play an important role in quantum computing
[
1
]
. We refer to
[
28
, Section 2]
for a tailored introduction and to
[
2
]
for more details.

Tensors

Let
\bm{V}
be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field
\mathbb{F}
. A
tensor

\mathsf{T}
is an element of some tensor product space
\bm{V}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\bm{V}_{r}
, where
\bm{V}_{i}=\bm{V}
or
\bm{V}_{i}=\bm{V}^{\ast}
for each
i
. If
p
is the number of
\bm{V}
-factors in this product, then we say
\mathsf{T}
is of
type

(p,r-p)
. We let
T^{p}_{q}(\bm{V})
denote the set of tensors of type
(p,q)
.

Example 27
.

Tensors of type
(0,0)
,
(1,0)
,
(0,1)
,
(1,1)
, and
(0,2)
correspond to scalars, vectors, linear functionals, linear transformations, and bilinear forms, respectively. An example for a
(1,2)
-tensor is the cross product
(v,w)\mapsto v\times w
of two vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Two tensor operations are essential here. First, given two tensors,
\mathsf{T}
of type
(p,q)
and
\mathsf{U}
of type
(r,s)
, their
tensor product

\mathsf{T}\otimes\mathsf{U}
is a tensor of type
(p+r,q+s)
. Second, if
p,q>0
, then for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,q\}
the
(i,j)
-contraction
map
C_{i,j}\colon T^{p}_{q}(\bm{V})\rightarrow T^{p-1}_{q-1}(\bm{V})
is defined by applying the canonical map
\bm{V}\otimes\bm{V}^{\ast}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
,
v\otimes f\mapsto f(v)
to the
i\textsuperscript{th}

\bm{V}
-factor and the
j\textsuperscript{th}

\bm{V}^{\ast}
-factor. E.g., if
\mathsf{A}\in T^{1}_{1}(\bm{V})
then
C_{1,1}(\mathsf{A})=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathsf{A})
is just the
trace
of
\mathsf{A}
.

Index notation

The
(abstract) index notation
provides a succinct way to describe basis-independent computations with tensors. In this notation, for example, a tensor
\mathsf{T}\in\bm{V}\otimes\bm{V}\otimes\bm{V}^{\ast}\otimes\bm{V}\otimes\bm{V}^{\ast}
is written as
\mathsf{T}^{{a_{1}}{a_{2}}}{}_{a_{3}}{}^{a_{4}}{}_{a_{5}}
, were

\bm{V}_{i}=\bm{V}
(resp.
\bm{V}_{i}=\bm{V}^{\ast}
). These abstract indices
a_{i}
are pairwise different symbols that merely indicate the slots of
\mathsf{T}
—they are
not
numerical indices. The tensor product of two tensors, say
\mathsf{T}^{a}{}_{bc}
and
\mathsf{U}^{d}{}_{e}
, is written by juxtaposition:
\mathsf{T}^{a}{}_{bc}\mathsf{U}^{d}{}_{e}
. Performing an
(i,j)
-contraction is indicated by setting the
i\textsuperscript{th}
upper index and the
j\textsuperscript{th}
lower index to be equal, e.g.,
C_{1,2}(\mathsf{T}^{a}{}_{bc}\mathsf{U}^{d}{}_{e})=\mathsf{T}^{a}{}_{ba}\mathsf{U}^{d}{}_{e}
, or
C_{2,1}(\mathsf{T}^{a}{}_{bc}\mathsf{U}^{d}{}_{e})=\mathsf{T}^{a}{}_{bc}\mathsf{U}^{b}{}_{e}
. Tensor products and contractions encompass many standard operations in linear algebra, e.g.,
\mathsf{A}^{a}{}_{b}\mathsf{v}^{b}
equals the
\mathsf{A}
-image of the vector
\mathsf{v}
(cf.
\Cref
fig:tensor-networks-linalg).
13
13

13

Moreover, using the

\mathsf{A}(\mathsf{v})
, once a basis for
\bm{V}
is fixed.

Importantly, the index notation can be developed into a

\mathsf{T}

. Then, going around

a_{i}
attach to
an outgoing (resp. incoming)

-labeled arrow, whenever

is an upper (resp. lower) index. The diagram of

is the disjoint union of the diagrams of
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{U}

and erasing the common label from the drawing, cf.

has no labeled arrows—because each arrow is incident to two coupons (which may coincide)—then
\mathscr{N}
is
fully contracted
. In this case
\mathscr{N}
represents a scalar
\mathscr{Z}(\mathscr{N})\in\mathbb{F}

evaluation
14

The word

is also commonly used to denote the evaluation of a tensor network.
of
\mathscr{N}
.
