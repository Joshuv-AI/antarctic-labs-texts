Impact of surgical start time on clinical outcomes in urologic surgery: a single-center study of major oncologic surgeries and adrenalectomy
Authors: Kohei Kobatake, Keisuke Goto, Yuki Kohada, Miki Naito, Shunsuke Miyamoto, Yohei Sekino, Hiroyuki Kitano, Keisuke Hieda, Nobuyuki Hinata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of surgical start time on perioperative outcomes remains controversial, and evidence in urologic oncology is limited. We aimed to evaluate the association between surgical start time and clinical outcomes across major urologic procedures.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 2627 patients who underwent major urologic surgeries at a single institution from 2010 to 2025. Patients were categorized into morning (8:00–12:00) and afternoon (after 12:00) groups. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to adjust for baseline differences. Perioperative outcomes, including operative time, estimated blood loss, complications, and overall survival, were compared. Linear regression analyses were used to assess the association between surgical start time and operative duration.ResultsBefore PSM, the morning group included patients with higher perioperative risk, and operative time, estimated blood loss, and overall survival were significantly worse than in the afternoon group. After PSM, 954 patients with balanced baseline characteristics were included in each group. In the matched cohort, estimated blood loss and overall survival were comparable between groups, whereas operative time remained longer in the morning group. Perioperative complication rates were similar between groups. Subgroup analyses demonstrated a consistent pattern across procedures, surgeon experience, and surgical approaches, with operative time shorter in the afternoon group.ConclusionsSurgical start time was associated with operative duration, with morning procedures taking longer than those in the afternoon. However, surgical start time was not associated with perioperative complications or overall survival. These findings suggest surgical start time may influence operative efficiency but has limited impact on clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00345-026-06614-8.Keywords: Urologic surgery, Propensity score matching, Operative time, Surgical start time, Time of day

Introduction

Urologic surgery represents a substantial component of modern surgical practice. Based on data from the Japanese National Clinical Database, more than 42,000 major urologic cancer surgeries, including partial nephrectomy, radical nephrectomy, radical cystectomy, and radical prostatectomy, are performed in 2021 [1]. Adrenalectomy is widely performed as a standard urologic procedure in several countries, particularly for functional adrenal tumors, and is increasingly performed using minimally invasive approaches [2, 3]. Surgical procedures are often scheduled as multiple consecutive cases within a single operating day, making workflow and scheduling critical determinants of perioperative efficiency and outcomes [4].

Time-of-day variation has been recognized as a determinant of human performance, with fatigue, sleep deprivation, and circadian rhythm disruption adversely affecting cognitive and technical abilities. These factors have been implicated in performance variability and an increased risk of adverse events in healthcare settings [5–7].

In surgical practice, several studies have suggested that later surgical start times are associated with worse clinical outcomes, including higher rates of complications, mortality, and adverse events. These effects have been attributed to surgeon fatigue, cumulative workload, and transitions in care delivery [5, 8–10]. Furthermore, later procedures have been linked to reduced efficiency, such as prolonged hospital stay and increased resource utilization [11, 12].

However, the evidence remains inconsistent. Some studies have demonstrated no significant association between surgical start time and perioperative outcomes, operative duration, or costs, suggesting that the impact of surgical timing may be context-dependent [13–15].

Despite increasing interest in time-related effects, evidence specific to urologic oncology surgeries remains limited. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the impact of surgical start time (morning vs. afternoon) on clinical outcomes.

Methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We collected data from patients who underwent radical prostatectomy, nephrectomy, partial nephrectomy, nephroureterectomy, cystectomy, or adrenalectomy at Hiroshima University Hospital from January 2010 to October 2025. Surgical start times were collected, and patients were categorized into morning (8:00–12:00) and afternoon (after 12:00) groups. Radical cystectomy was excluded from the analysis because only two cases were performed in the afternoon. After excluding cases with missing surgical start time data, 2627 patients were included in the final analysis. Missing data for the covariates used to estimate the propensity score were infrequent (less than 1% for each variable), and a complete-case approach was used.

Patient characteristics, including demographic factors, comorbidities, medication use, preoperative laboratory data, and tumor- and surgery-related variables (including surgical procedure, clinical T stage, surgical approach, and surgeon experience), were collected. Perioperative outcomes, including operative time, pneumoperitoneum time, console time, estimated blood loss, perioperative complications, and overall survival (OS), were evaluated. Operative time was defined as the interval from the patient’s entry into the operating room to exit from the operating room (in-room time). Pneumoperitoneum time was defined as the total duration of maintained pneumoperitoneum during laparoscopic or robot-assisted procedures, and console time as the total duration of robotic console operation during robot-assisted procedures.

A total of 46 surgeons performed at least one procedure. Surgeons with ≥ 20 years of experience as urologists at the time of surgery were defined as experienced surgeons, whereas those with < 20 years of experience were defined as less experienced surgeons.

Adrenal tumors were not assigned a preoperative clinical TNM classification; therefore, T, N, and M were recorded as N/A (not applicable).

Propensity score matching

To adjust for baseline differences between the morning and afternoon groups, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed. Propensity scores were estimated using a nominal logistic regression model in which surgical start time (morning vs. afternoon) was included as the dependent variable. The following covariates were included in the model: age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, medication use, tumor-related variables, and surgery-related factors, including surgical procedure, clinical T stage, N stage, M stage, surgical approach, and surgeon experience.

Based on the estimated propensity scores, 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed. Patients in the morning and afternoon groups were matched according to their propensity scores to minimize baseline differences. The balance of covariates before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD below 0.1 considered to indicate adequate balance.

Forest plot construction

To evaluate the association between surgical start time and operative duration, linear regression analyses were performed using operative time as the dependent variable and surgical start time (afternoon vs. morning) as the independent variable. Regression coefficients (β) and their standard errors were obtained from the models, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived from these estimates. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to surgical procedure, surgeon experience, and surgical approach. For visualization, a forest plot was constructed using the β coefficients and corresponding 95% CIs. The point estimates (β) were represented by circles, and the 95% CIs were depicted as horizontal lines. The size of each circle was scaled proportionally to the sample size (N) of each subgroup, allowing for visual weighting of estimates. In addition to the dichotomized comparison (morning vs. afternoon), surgical start time was analyzed as a continuous variable (operating room entry time, in hours) using linear regression, with operative time as the dependent variable, both without and with adjustment for surgical procedure. Surgical start time was also categorized into three groups (morning, 08:00–12:00; early afternoon, 12:00–15:00; and late afternoon, ≥ 15:00), and operative time was compared across groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Statistics

Continuous variables were compared using nonparametric tests, including the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to estimate overall survival (OS), and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Survival curves were generated using Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software Inc., Boston, MA, USA; RRID: SCR_002798). All statistical analyses were performed using JMP software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA; RRID: SCR_008567).

Results

Baseline characteristics

2627 patients were included (1614 in the morning and 1013 in the afternoon; Table 1, left). Before PSM, several baseline, tumor-related, and surgical factors differed between the groups. Patients in the morning group were older, had more comorbidities (including ischemic heart disease and prior malignancy), and were more likely to receive antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy. Tumor and surgical characteristics also differed (Table 2, left), with adrenalectomy more frequent in the afternoon group and nephroureterectomy and advanced T stage more common in the morning group. Benign pathology was more frequently observed in the afternoon group. Surgical approach was comparable between groups, whereas experienced surgeons were more frequently involved in the morning group.

Surgical and tumor characteristics before and after PSM

AM morning group, PM afternoon group

After PSM using variables that showed statistical significance or clinical relevance in the pre-matched cohort, 954 patients were included in each group (Table 1, right). Following matching, patient characteristics, comorbidities, medication use, prior treatments, and preoperative laboratory findings were well balanced between the groups, with no significant differences observed. Tumor-related and surgical factors were also similar between the two groups after matching (Table 2, right). Surgical approach and surgeon experience remained comparable after matching. After matching, all covariates achieved an absolute SMD below 0.1 (maximum, 0.07), confirming that the morning and afternoon groups were well balanced (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Perioperative outcomes

Before PSM, operative time, pneumoperitoneum time, and console time were significantly longer in the morning group (Table 3). Estimated blood loss was higher in the morning group, whereas complication rates were comparable.

Overall survival

Before PSM, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no statistically significant difference in OS between the morning and afternoon groups in patients with benign pathology (Fig. 1A). In contrast, among patients with malignant disease, OS was significantly worse in the morning group compared with the afternoon group (Fig. 1B). After PSM, no significant differences in OS were observed between the morning and afternoon groups in either the benign or malignant cohorts (Fig. 1C and D).

Overall and stratified analyses of operative time

In the PS-matched cohort, surgical start time was first modeled as a continuous variable. Each one-hour delay in operating room entry time was associated with a 2.5-minute reduction in operative time (β = −2.5 min per hour; 95% CI, − 3.7 to − 1.4; P < 0.001; Fig. 2), and this association persisted after adjustment for surgical procedure (β = −2.3 min per hour; 95% CI, − 3.3 to − 1.2; P < 0.001). When surgical start time was categorized into three groups, operative time differed significantly across the groups (median, 217, 208, and 203 min for the morning, early afternoon, and late afternoon groups, respectively; Kruskal–Wallis P = 0.003; Supplementary Fig. 2); operative time was significantly longer in the morning group than in either afternoon group, whereas the two afternoon groups did not differ significantly. Subgroup analyses were then performed to assess the association between surgical start time and operative duration (Fig. 3). Overall, operative time was approximately 7 min shorter in the afternoon group than in the morning group. In procedure-specific analyses, adrenalectomy and prostatectomy showed significantly shorter operative time in the afternoon group, whereas other procedures demonstrated similar trends that did not reach statistical significance. Stratified analyses by surgeon experience and surgical approach showed consistent findings, with operative time generally shorter in the afternoon group across all subgroups.

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of surgical start time on perioperative outcomes across major urologic procedures. Before propensity score matching, patients in the morning group had less favorable baseline characteristics, including more comorbidities and fewer benign tumors, which likely contributed to longer operative time, greater blood loss, and worse overall survival. Despite these differences, intraoperative and postoperative complication rates were comparable between groups.

After adjustment with propensity score matching, estimated blood loss and overall survival rates were similar between groups, and complication rates remained unchanged. In contrast, operative time, including pneumoperitoneum and console time, remained significantly longer in the morning group. Subgroup analyses showed a consistent pattern across procedures, surgeon experience levels, and surgical approaches, with operative time generally shorter in the afternoon group.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate differences in clinical outcomes between morning and afternoon start times across multiple urologic procedures. Previous cross-specialty reports have frequently indicated that starting elective surgery in the afternoon is associated with inferior perioperative outcomes, such as prolonged hospital stay, higher incidence of composite complications, increased mortality rates, or reduced productivity [6, 9–11].

Studies comparing elective morning and afternoon cancer surgeries across specialties have yielded inconsistent results. In neurosurgery, later start times and longer operative durations have been associated with worse outcomes, whereas lung cancer surgery has shown no significant differences in operative time, complications, or other clinical outcomes [8, 13]. In colorectal cancer, survival outcomes were similar despite differences in certain complications, and operative time was longer in morning procedures [14]. Similarly, in robot-assisted radical prostatectomy, afternoon surgeries tended to have shorter operative times and less blood loss, with comparable complication rates, although hospital stays were longer [16].

In contrast to cancer surgeries, several reports indicate that outcomes for procedures such as kidney transplantation, lung transplantation, and coronary artery bypass grafting are not dependent on the starting time [17–19].

In our study, before PSM, patients in the morning group had less favorable baseline characteristics, including a higher burden of comorbidities and a lower proportion of benign tumors. This pattern suggests that more complex or higher-risk cases may have been preferentially scheduled earlier, reflecting real-world clinical practice.

Consistent with findings in colorectal and prostate cancer, operative time remained longer in the morning group after propensity score matching. Although this difference reached statistical significance, its magnitude—a median difference of approximately 10 min (approximately 7 min when estimated by regression)—is modest and of limited clinical relevance, and the distinction between statistical and clinical significance should be kept in mind when interpreting our findings.

There are several possible explanations for these findings. The relative lack of time constraints during morning sessions compared with afternoon sessions may result in extended operative times [14]. Decreased operative time with successive cases within a single day may indicate that a surgeon or surgical team is “warming up” and improving performance, irrespective of the learning curve [16]. Notably, operative time did not increase further in the late afternoon relative to the early afternoon, which argues against progressive surgeon fatigue as the principal driver and instead supports explanations related to the morning operating environment, such as greater time availability and case allocation.

The specific background of university hospitals may complicate the interpretation of these findings. For instance, at our institution, residents are more heavily involved in morning surgeries. Furthermore, surgical team composition varies for each procedure. In surgical procedures performed at university hospitals, involvement of residents is likely to lead to prolonged operative times [20, 21]. Highly consistent surgical care teams in urologic surgery have been associated with improved surgical efficiency and patient outcomes [22]. However, because data on these factors were not collected in this study, the causal relationship with our findings remains unclear. Future prospective studies incorporating data on surgeon experience and team stability are warranted to further clarify these issues.

The lack of difference in complication rates between the morning and afternoon groups may be attributed to the highly standardized nature of elective urologic surgeries. In this context, perioperative outcomes are primarily determined by patient- and procedure-related factors rather than external factors such as surgical start time [23, 24]. Beyond perioperative metrics, the oncologic management of urologic malignancies—including the salvage treatment of local recurrence after radical or partial nephrectomy—remains an important long-term determinant of outcomes that is independent of surgical start time [25].

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study; therefore, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and the generalizability of our findings may be limited. Second, although propensity score matching balanced all measured covariates (all SMDs < 0.1), residual confounding from unmeasured variables is likely. In particular, our data suggest that more complex or higher-risk cases were preferentially scheduled in the morning, and propensity score matching may not fully capture intraoperative surgical complexity; confounding by indication therefore cannot be excluded. Third, several factors that may influence operative time—including surgical team composition, the degree of resident involvement, team consistency, intraoperative complexity, and the case order within the operating day—were not captured. As an exploratory approach, we approximated the intra-day case order by ranking the operating room entry times of urologic procedures within each calendar day; operative time tended to decrease with later case order (Spearman ρ = −0.09; P < 0.001) and was longer for the first case of the day than for subsequent cases (median, 217 vs. 207 min; P < 0.001). However, because operating room assignment was unavailable and only urologic cases were captured, this proxy does not reflect the true within-room sequence and should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, the pooled analysis of several distinct procedures introduces heterogeneity in operative complexity and duration; although procedure-specific subgroup analyses showed consistent trends, procedure-specific effects may not be fully captured. Finally, the follow-up duration was relatively limited for oncologic outcomes, and overall survival is influenced by numerous factors not captured in our dataset; the absence of a difference in overall survival after matching should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence.

In conclusion, surgical start time was associated with operative duration in major urologic surgeries, with morning procedures taking longer than those in the afternoon. This pattern was consistent across procedures and subgroups, even after adjustment. However, surgical start time was not associated with perioperative complications or overall survival, suggesting limited impact on clinical outcomes. Surgeons should be aware that operative time in major oncologic surgeries and adrenalectomy procedures may be longer in the morning.
