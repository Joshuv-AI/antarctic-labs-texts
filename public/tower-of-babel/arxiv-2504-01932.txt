Semidefinite lower bounds for covering codes
Dion Gijswijt, Sven Polak

Abstract
Let K q ​ ( n , r ) K_{q}(n,r) denote the minimum size of a q q -ary covering code of word length n n and covering radius r r . In other words, K q ​ ( n , r ) K_{q}(n,r) is the minimum size of a set of q q -ary codewords of length n n such that the Hamming balls of radius r r around the codewords cover the Hamming space { 0 , … , q − 1 } n \{0,\ldots,q-1\}^{n} . The special case K 3 ​ ( n , 1 ) K_{3}(n,1) is often referred to as the football pool problem , as it is equivalent to finding a set of forecasts on n n football matches that is guaranteed to contain a forecast with at most one wrong outcome. In this paper, we build and expand upon the work of Gijswijt (2005), who introduced a semidefinite programming lower bound on K q ​ ( n , r ) K_{q}(n,r) via matrix cuts. We develop techniques that strengthen this bound, by introducing new semidefinite constraints inspired by Lasserre’s hierarchy for 0-1 programs and symmetry reduction methods, and a more powerful objective function. The techniques lead to sharper lower bounds, setting new records across a broad range of values of q q , n n , and r r .

Introduction

Let
q\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
be integers. Then
[q]^{n}:=\{0,\ldots,q-1\}^{n}
is the
Hamming space
of
q
-ary words of length
n
. The
Hamming distance
between
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in[q]^{n}
is denoted by
d(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=|\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:\mathbf{u}_{i}\neq\mathbf{v}_{i}\}|
. We denote the isometry group of
[q]^{n}
by
\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)
. It consists of all permutations of
[q]^{n}
obtained by permuting the
n
positions and independently permuting the
q
symbols in each of the
n
positions. The group
\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)
acts on subsets
S\subseteq[q]^{n}
, vectors
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{[q]^{n}}
and matrices
M\in\mathbb{C}^{[q]^{n}\times[q]^{n}}
in the usual way by

\sigma S=\{\sigma(a)\mid a\in S\},\quad(\sigma\mathbf{x})_{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{x}_{\sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{u})},\quad(\sigma M)_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=M_{\sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{u}),\sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{v})}

for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)
and
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in[q]^{n}
. For any integer
0\leq r\leq n
and any
\mathbf{u}\in[q]^{n}
, we denote by

\displaystyle B_{r}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{v}\in[q]^{n}\mid d(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\leq r\}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle S_{r}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle:=\{\mathbf{v}\in[q]^{n}\mid d(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=r\}

the
Hamming ball
and
Hamming sphere
of radius
r
around
\mathbf{u}
, respectively.

A
q
-ary
code
of length
n
is a subset
C
of
[q]^{n}
. The
covering radius
of
C
is the smallest integer
r
such that the Hamming balls of radius
r
around the codewords cover the Hamming space
[q]^{n}
, i.e.,
\bigcup_{\mathbf{u}\in C}B_{r}(\mathbf{u})=[q]^{n}
. In this paper, we study the quantity

\displaystyle K_{q}(n,r):=\text{min}\{|C|\,\mid\,C\subseteq[q]^{n}\text{ has covering radius\penalty\ $r$}\}.

(1)

In other words, the problem of determining
K_{q}(n,r)
amounts to finding the
smallest number of Hamming balls of radius
r
that cover the Hamming space
[q]^{n}
. If
C\subseteq[q]^{n}
has covering radius
r
and size
K=|C|
, then we call
C
an
(n,K,q,r)
-code.

A popular application of covering codes is found in football pools. A football pool involves predicting the outcomes of
n
football matches, where each match can result in three possible outcomes: a home team win, a draw, or an away team win. The objective is to find the smallest set of bets that is guaranteed to contain a bet with at most one incorrect outcome. This challenge, commonly known as the football pool problem, corresponds to the covering problem for
q=3
and
r=1
. For more details on the football pool problem, see
[
8
]
. For an overview of results on covering codes and several of their applications, see
[
1
]
.

A fundamental problem in the study of covering codes is to determine tight bounds on
K_{q}(n,r)
. Upper bounds are commonly established by constructing an explicit code with covering radius
r
. In this paper, we focus on establishing lower bounds on
K_{q}(n,r)
.

Method of linear inequalities

A key approach for deriving lower bounds on
K_{q}(n,r)
is the method of linear inequalities. Here, we follow the exposition in
[
1
, Chapter 6]
. Suppose we have a code
C\subseteq[q]^{n}
and define

A_{i}(\mathbf{u}):=|C\cap S_{i}(\mathbf{u})|

for any
\mathbf{u}\in[q]^{n}
and
i=0,\ldots,n
. The method considers valid linear inequalities over a code that are of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}A_{i}(\mathbf{u})\geq\beta\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{u}\in[q]^{n},

(2)

where
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\geq 0
and
\beta>0
. Such a set of inequalities is denoted by
(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\beta
. The following proposition shows how such a set leads to a lower bound on
K_{q}(n,r)
.

Proposition 1.1
.

If every
(n,K,q,r)
-code satisfies the inequalities
(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\beta
, then

K\geq\frac{\beta q^{n}}{\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\binom{n}{i}(q-1)^{i}}.

Proof.

By summing the inequalities in
(
2
)
over all
\mathbf{u}\in[q]^{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta q^{n}\leq\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in[q]^{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}A_{i}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in[q]^{n}}A_{i}(\mathbf{u})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in C}|S_{i}(\mathbf{v})|=|C|\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\tbinom{n}{i}(q-1)^{i}.\qed

The
sphere covering inequalities
of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{r}A_{i}(\mathbf{u})\geq 1\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{u}\in[q]^{n}

(3)

in combination with Proposition
1.1
lead to the
sphere covering bound
, given by

\displaystyle K_{q}(n,r)\geq\frac{q^{n}}{\sum_{i=0}^{r}\tbinom{n}{i}(q-1)^{i}}.

(4)

Several additional valid inequalities have been derived in the literature, especially in the binary case (
q=2
), by analyzing how elements of
B_{s}(\mathbf{u})
can be covered for
s=1,2,3
. For
s=1
, this results in the Van Wee inequalities
[
20
,
21
]
, given by:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{r+1}\right\rceil A_{i}(\mathbf{u})+A_{r}(\mathbf{u})+A_{r+1}(\mathbf{u})\geq\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{r+1}\right\rceil

(5)

which gives an improvement over the sphere covering bound when
r+1
does not divide
n+1
.
Additional inequalities can be found in articles by Johnson
[
9
]
, Zhang
[
23
]
, and Zhang and Lo
[
24
]
.

By taking nonnegative linear combinations of inequalities, for instance by summing over sets
S_{i}(\mathbf{u})
, new inequalities can be derived. Utilizing the fact that the
A_{i}(\mathbf{u})
are integers,
(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\beta
implies
(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil,\ldots,\lceil\lambda_{n}\rceil)\lceil\beta\rceil
. This way, strengthened bounds can be found. For instance, the van Wee inequalities can be deduced from the sphere covering inequalities by summing over
B_{1}(\mathbf{u})
, multiplying by
\tfrac{1}{r+1}
and rounding up. Habsieger and Plagne extended this method to generate numerous new lower bounds for binary and ternary covering codes
[
7
]
.

Semidefinite programming bounds

In
[
4
, Section 5.3.1]
a linear programming lower bound for
K_{q}(n,r)
is given that is analogous to the Delsarte bound for error correcting codes
[
2
]
.

If every code
C\subseteq[q]^{n}
with covering radius
r
satisfies the inequalities
(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\beta
, then

K_{q}(n,r)\geq\min_{x}q^{n}x_{0},

where the minimum ranges over all nonnegative vectors
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\sf T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
that satisfy

(i)

\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{i}P_{k}(i)\geq 0
,

(ii)

\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{i}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{i,j}^{k}\geq\beta x_{0}
,

(iii)

\sum_{i=0}^{n}(x_{0}-x_{i})\sum_{j=0}^{n}\lambda_{j}\alpha_{i,j}^{k}\geq\beta(1-x_{0})
,

for all
k=0,\ldots,n
. Here,
P_{k}(i):=\sum_{s=0}^{k}(-1)^{s}\tbinom{i}{s}\tbinom{n-i}{k-s}(q-1)^{k-s}
are the
Krawtchouk polynomials
, and the numbers
\alpha_{i,j}^{k}
are given by
\alpha_{i,j}^{k}=|\{\mathbf{v}\in[q]^{n}\mid d(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{v})=i,\,d(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u})=j\}|
for any
\mathbf{u}\in[q]^{n}
with
d(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u})=k
, and can be expressed as

\alpha_{i,j}^{k}=\begin{cases}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,t\\
t+p=k+i-j\end{subarray}}\tbinom{k}{t-p,p}\tbinom{n-k}{i-t}(q-1)^{i-t}(q-2)^{t-p}&\text{if }q\geq 3,\\
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\\
2t=k+i-j\end{subarray}}\tbinom{k}{t}\tbinom{n-k}{i-t}&\text{if }q=2.\end{cases}

The
n+1
variables
x_{i}
correspond to the distance distribution of the code. Where in the Delsarte bound variables corresponding to forbidden distances are set to zero, the bound on covering codes has inequalities (ii) and (iii) on the variables induced by the sphere covering inequalities, or any other valid set of inequalities
(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\beta
.

The Delsarte bound can be viewed as a semidefinite program
where the
[q]^{n}\times[q]^{n}
matrix variable is constrained to be invariant under
\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)
(i.e., it is an element of the Bose-Mesner algebra of the Hamming scheme). Since the Bose-Mesner algebra has dimension
n+1
, the matrix variable can be expressed in terms of
n+1
real variables (corresponding to the distance distribution of the code) and because the Bose-Mesner algebra is commutative, the semidefiniteness constraint can be reduced to linear constraints in the
n+1
variables by simultaneously diagonalizing the matrices in the Bose-Mesner algebra. This corresponds to inequalities (i).

For error correcting codes, strengthened SDP bounds based on the distribution of triples
[
19
,
6
]
of codewords and four-tuples
[
5
,
15
]
have been obtained. The main complication for these bounds is that the matrix variable is now an element of a non-commutative algebra. In the case of triple bounds, the relevant algebra consists of the
[q]^{n}\times[q]^{n}
matrices that are invariant under
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(q,n)
, the stabilizer subgroup with respect to the zero word. This algebra coincides with the Terwilliger algebra of the Hamming scheme. To reduce the SDP to an equivalent one of polynomial size, an explicit block diagonalization of this algebra had to be obtained.

An analogous bound for
K_{q}(n,r)
based on triples of codewords, was obtained by Gijswijt in
[
4
, Section 5.3.2]
using the method of matrix cuts from
[
16
]
. Here, we develop techniques to strengthen this bound. We introduce new semidefinite constraints inspired by the Lasserre Hierarchy for 0-1 programs
[
12
,
13
]
and symmetry reduction, alongside a more powerful objective function. The techniques lead to sharper lower bounds, improving the best known bounds for a broad range of values of
q
,
n
, and
r
.

The bound we study in this paper is a covering analogue to Schrijver’s first SDP bounds for error-correcting codes in the finite Hamming space
[
19
]
. Such bounds can be interpreted as arising from submatrices corresponding to 3-point configurations in the second level of the Lasserre hierarchy
[
12
]
applied to a polynomial optimization formulation of the problem. In the context of discrete geometry and spherical codes, Riener, Rolfes, and Vallentin
[
18
]
recently proposed a hierarchy for spherical covering codes, inspired by hierarchies for packing problems in discrete geometry
[
11
]
. In our work, we extend Schrijver’s packing bound to covering codes in the finite Hamming space. While in principle one could also define a full hierarchy for covering codes in this setting, practical obstacles (in particular, the rapidly increasing number of variables and the size of the block matrices) make the full second and higher levels of the Lasserre hierarchy difficult to compute. Therefore, we focus on a 3-point bound and its symmetry reduction, and strengthen it as much as we can by introducing additional constraints and optimizing the choice of objective function.

The SDP bound for covering codes

Given a complex matrix
A
, we write
A^{\mathsf{T}}
for the transpose of
A
and
A^{*}
for the complex conjugate transpose of
A
. So
A^{*}=A^{\mathsf{T}}
if
A
is a real matrix. We apply the same notation for vectors and scalars. So for
z\in\mathbb{C}
we denote the complex conjugate of
z
by
z^{*}
. Given complex matrices
A,B
of the same size, we denote by
\langle A,B\rangle=\tr(A^{*}B)
their Frobenius inner product. If
A
is a square matrix, we write
A\succeq 0
to denote that
A
is positive semidefinite. Throughout the paper, we write
J
for the all-ones matrix, and
I
for the identity matrix, where the dimensions are clear from the context.

Given a square matrix
A
and
c\geq 0
we will use the following notation:

R(c,A)=\begin{pmatrix}c&(\diag A)^{*}\\
\diag A&A\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad R(A)=R(1,A).

Note that for positive semidefinite
A
, the matrix
R(c,A)
is PSD if and only if either
c=0
and
A=0
, or
c>0
and
cA-(\diag A)(\diag A)^{*}\succeq 0
, which follows by taking Schur complements.
We remark that throughout the paper, we will mostly work with real matrices. In particular, the matrices in our SDP bounds will be real valued.

Let
q\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
be integers, and denote
\mathbb{E}=[q]^{n}
. We identify
[q]^{n}
with
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{n}
, so that
\mathbb{E}
carries an additive group structure. Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{E}
. We define the
0,1
-matrix
M_{C}
of size
|\mathbb{E}|\times|\mathbb{E}|
as follows:

(M_{C})_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in C$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Furthermore, we define the following real matrices associated to
C
:

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle:=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)}M_{\sigma C},

\displaystyle M^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\\
\mathbf{0}\in\sigma C\end{subarray}}M_{\sigma C},\quad\text{and}\quad M^{\prime\prime}:=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\\
\mathbf{0}\not\in\sigma C\end{subarray}}M_{\sigma C}.

(6)

We immediately observe the following relations for the entries of the matrices
M,M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime}
.

Proposition 2.1

(Basic inequalities and symmetry)
.

The matrix
M
is
\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)
-invariant and
M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime}
are
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(q,n)
-invariant. Moreover, for all
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{E}
we have

(i)

M_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}}
and
M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}}-M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}
,

(ii)

0\leq M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}\leq M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u}}
,

(iii)

0\leq M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}\leq M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}}
,

(iv)

M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}}
if
\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\}=\sigma\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\}
for some
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)
.

Proof.

The fact that
M,M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime}
are invariant under the respective groups follows directly by their construction. To see (i), note that
M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{w}}=0
for every
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{E}
, so

M_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=M_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}}=M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}}+M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}}=M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}}

and

M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=M_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}-M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}}-M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}.

To see inequalities (ii) and (iii), note that
M^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime\prime}
are nonnegative linear combinations of the matrices
M_{\sigma C}
and
0\leq\left(M_{\sigma C}\right)_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}\leq\left(M_{\sigma C}\right)_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}}
.

To see (iv), we observe that

M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\cdot|\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\mid\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\}\subseteq\sigma C\}|

only depends on the orbit of
\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\}
.
∎

The matrices
M^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime\prime}
satisfy the following conditions.

Proposition 2.2

(Semidefiniteness)
.

The matrices
M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime}
are positive semidefinite. Moreover, the matrix
R(1-M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}},M^{\prime\prime})
is positive semidefinite.

Proof.

The matrices
M^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime\prime}
are nonnegative linear combinations of the positive semidefinite matrices
M_{\sigma C}=\mathbf{1}_{\sigma C}(\mathbf{1}_{\sigma C})^{\mathsf{T}}
, and therefore themselves positive semidefinite. Since the matrices
R(M_{\sigma C})
are positive semidefinite, so is

R(1-M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}},M^{\prime\prime})=\begin{pmatrix}1-M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}}&\left(\diag M^{\prime\prime}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\diag M^{\prime\prime}&M^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\\
\mathbf{0}\not\in\sigma C\end{subarray}}R(M_{\sigma C}).\qed

The cardinality of
C
can be expressed in terms of
M^{\prime}
in several ways as follows.

Proposition 2.3

(Objective function)
.

We have

|C|=q^{n}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}},\quad|C|^{2}=q^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}},\quad|C|^{3}=q^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}.

Proof.

For any
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}
we have
|\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\,|\,\sigma\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}\}|=|\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(q,n)|
, so

M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}}=\frac{|\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\,|\,\mathbf{0}\in\sigma C\}|}{|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|}=|C|\frac{|\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(q,n)|}{|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|}=q^{-n}|C|.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\langle M^{\prime},I\rangle=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in C}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\\
\sigma\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}\langle M_{\sigma C},I\rangle=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in C}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\\
\sigma\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}|C|

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(q,n)|}{|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|}|C|^{2}=q^{-n}|C|^{2},

and similarly

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=\langle M^{\prime},J\rangle=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in C}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\\
\sigma\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}\langle M_{\sigma C},J\rangle=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in C}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\\
\sigma\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}|C|^{2}

\displaystyle=q^{-n}|C|^{3}.\qed

Now suppose that
C
has covering radius
r
and that the set of inequalities
(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\beta
is valid for all codes of covering radius
r
. Define

\displaystyle N=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)}M_{\sigma C}\cdot\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\lambda_{\ell}|\sigma C\cap S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})|-\beta\right).

Proposition 2.4

(Lasserre constraint and matrix cut inequalities)
.

The following hold:

(i)

The matrix
R(c,N)
is positive semidefinite, where
c=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\lambda_{\ell}\cdot|S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})|\cdot M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}}-\beta
,

(ii)

N
is invariant under
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(q,n)
,

(iii)

For all
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{E}
we have

N_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=-\beta M_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\lambda_{\ell}\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}},

(iv)

For all
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{E}
we have

\displaystyle\beta M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\lambda_{\ell}\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in S_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}},

\displaystyle\beta\bigl(M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}}-M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\lambda_{\ell}\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in S_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})}\bigl(M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}}-M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}}\bigr),

\displaystyle\beta M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\lambda_{\ell}\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in S_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})}M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}},

\displaystyle\beta\bigl((1-M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}})-M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\lambda_{\ell}\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in S_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})}\bigl(M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}}-M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}}\bigr).

Remark 2.1
.

The conditions in Proposition
2.4
derive from general relaxations for 0-1 linear programs. In our setting, the incidence vector
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{E}}
of
C
satisfies the covering inequalities

\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in S_{i}(\mathbf{u})}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{v}}\geq\beta\quad\text{for all $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}$.}

However, the same method would apply in the setting of error correcting codes, where one might use inequalities
\mathbf{x}_{u}+\mathbf{x}_{v}\leq 1
, if
d(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
.

The matrix
N
and conditions (i)–(iii) come from the Lasserre Hierarchy for
0,1
polytopes. Let
\mathbf{y}
be the extended incidence vector of
C
, where for every subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{E}
we set
\mathbf{y}(S)=1
if
S\subseteq C
and
\mathbf{y}(S)=0
otherwise. The validity of the covering inequalities implies that for every
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}
, the matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{E})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{E})}
given by
M_{I,J}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in S_{i}(\mathbf{u})}y_{I\cup J\cup\{\mathbf{v}\}}-\beta y_{I\cup J}
is positive semidefinite. Restricting to sets
I,J
of size at most
1
and averaging over
\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)
leads to the matrix
N
and conditions (i)–(iii).

The four sets of inequalities in part (iv) come from matrix cut inequalities
[
16
]
. Let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{\emptyset\cup\mathbb{E}}
be the extended incidence vector of
C
where
\mathbf{y}_{\emptyset}=1
, and
\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{u}}=1
if
\mathbf{u}\in C
and
\mathbf{y}_{\mathbf{u}}=0
otherwise for
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}
. The vector
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
satisfies the homogenized cover inequalities

\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda_{i}\sum_{\mathbf{v}\in S_{i}(\mathbf{u})}\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{v}}\geq\beta\mathbf{x}_{\emptyset}\quad\text{for all $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}$}.

(8)

The positive semidefinite matrix
\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}=R(M_{C})
has the property that every column satisfies (
8

\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)
also
\sigma C
has covering radius

satisfying (

M^{\prime}
, and similarly for

Proof.

To see (i), observe that for every
\sigma

\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\lambda_{\ell}|\sigma C\cap S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})|-\beta
is nonnegative. Since the matrices

are positive semidefinite, it follows that

R(c,N)=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)}R(M_{\sigma C})\cdot\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\lambda_{\ell}|\sigma C\cap S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})|-\beta\right)

is positive semidefinite.

Item (ii) follows from the fact that for all
\tau\in\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}
and
\ell
we have

\displaystyle\tau\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)}M_{\sigma C}\cdot|\sigma C\cap S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})|\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)}M_{\tau\sigma C}\cdot|\sigma C\cap S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)}M_{\tau\sigma C}\cdot|\tau\sigma C\cap\tau S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)}M_{\tau\sigma C}\cdot|\tau\sigma C\cap S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)}M_{\rho C}\cdot|\rho C\cap S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})|,

where we used that
S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})
is fixed under
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(q,n)
in the third equation, and we used the substitution
\rho=\tau\sigma
in the last equality.

To see (iii), let
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{E}
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)}(M_{\sigma C})_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}\cdot|\sigma C\cap S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})}|\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\mid\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\sigma C\}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})}|\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\mid\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w},\mathbf{0}\in\sigma C\}|

\displaystyle=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in S_{\ell}(\mathbf{0})}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}}.

Summing over
\ell
and using that

|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)}(M_{\sigma C})_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=M_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}

shows (iii).

To prove (iv), fix
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{E}
and let
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)

\sigma C
has covering radius

, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\lambda_{\ell}|(\sigma C)\cap S_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})|\geq\beta.

(9)

Partition
\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)
into subsets
\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{4}
given by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}=\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\mid\mathbf{0}\in\sigma C,\ \mathbf{u}\in\sigma C\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}=\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\mid\mathbf{0}\in\sigma C,\ \mathbf{u}\not\in\sigma C\}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3}=\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\mid\mathbf{0}\not\in\sigma C,\ \mathbf{u}\in\sigma C\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{4}=\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\mid\mathbf{0}\not\in\sigma C,\ \mathbf{u}\not\in\sigma C\}.

Summing (
9

\sigma\in\Gamma_{1}
and dividing by
|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|
yields the first inequality since

|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\Gamma_{1}}\beta=M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u}}\beta

and for every

we have

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\Gamma_{1}}|\sigma C\cap S_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})|

\displaystyle=|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in S_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})}|\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\mid\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\in\sigma C\}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{w}\in S_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}}.

Similarly, summing (

\sigma\in\Gamma_{2}

\sigma\in\Gamma_{3}

\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\cdot|\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\mid\mathbf{0}\in\sigma C,\ \mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\sigma C\}|

\displaystyle=M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)|^{-1}\cdot|\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\mid\mathbf{0}\not\in\sigma C,\ \mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\sigma C\}|

\displaystyle=M^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}\qed

Theorem 2.5

(Covering lower bound)
.

Suppose that every
C\subseteq\mathbb{E}
with covering radius
r
satisfies
(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\beta
. Then

K_{q}(n,r)^{3}\geq\min_{M,M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime},N}q^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}

where the minimum ranges over real matrices
M,M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime},N
that satisfy the conditions in Proposition
2.1
, Proposition
2.2
and Proposition
2.4
.

Remark 2.2
.

The conditions in Proposition
2.1
(i) and Proposition
2.4
express
M
,
M^{\prime\prime}
and
N
in terms of
M^{\prime}
, so the minimization can be expressed in terms of the matrix variable
M^{\prime}
alone.

Remark 2.3

(Comparison of objective values)
.

We have

\displaystyle\sqrt[3]{q^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}}\geq\sqrt{q^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}}}\geq q^{n}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}}.

(10)

It follows that
\sqrt[3]{q^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}}
gives the tightest lower bound of the three expressions.

To see (
10
), note the following general fact: for
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
m:=\text{diag}(M)
we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}c&m^{\mathsf{T}}\\
m&M\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad cM-mm^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c\langle M,J\rangle\geq\langle M,I\rangle^{2}.

(11)

By applying (
11
) to the matrix
M
from the Bose-Mesner algebra (and
c=1
), we obtain

\displaystyle q^{n}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}}\geq(q^{n}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}})^{2}.

(12)

Since
M^{\prime}\succeq 0
and
M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}}=M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{u}}
for all
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}
, we have
R(M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}},M^{\prime})\succeq 0
. So by applying
(
11
)
we obtain

\displaystyle M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}}\langle M^{\prime},J\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\langle M^{\prime},I\rangle^{2},\quad\quad\text{so}

\displaystyle(M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}})^{2}\langle M^{\prime},J\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\langle M^{\prime},I\rangle^{4}.

By combining this inequality with
(
12
)
we find

\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}}\langle M^{\prime},J\rangle^{2}\geq q^{n}(M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}})^{2}\langle M^{\prime},J\rangle^{2}\geq q^{n}\langle M^{\prime},I\rangle^{4},

so

\langle M^{\prime},J\rangle^{2}\geq q^{n}\langle M^{\prime},I\rangle^{3},\quad\text{implying}\quad\big(\sum_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}\big)^{2}\geq q^{n}\big(\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{E}}M^{\prime}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}}\big)^{3}.

Multiplying both sides by
q^{2n}

and combining with (
12
) this yields (
10
), as desired.

3
Block-diagonalization of the Terwilliger algebra

In this section, we describe the block-diagonalization of the Terwilliger algebras of the binary
[
19
]
and nonbinary
[
6
]
Hamming schemes. This forms the algebraic foundation for the symmetry reduction of the SDP in Theorem
2.5
.

3.1
Terwilliger algebra of the binary Hamming cube

For
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we define
\overline{d}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=(i,j,t)
, where

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle=|\{\ell\mid\mathbf{u}_{\ell}\neq 0\}|,

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle=|\{\ell\mid\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\neq 0\}|,

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=|\{\ell\mid\mathbf{u}_{\ell}\neq 0,\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\neq 0\}|.

The set of tuples
(i,j,t)
that occur this way are given by

I(2,n)=\{(i,j,t)\mid 0\leq t\leq i,j,\ i+j\leq n+t\}

and they parametrize the orbits of pairs
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
under the group
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(2,n)=\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(2,n)\mid\sigma(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}\}
of symmetries of the Hamming space that stabilize the zero word.

For
(i,j,t)\in I(2,n)
we define the
\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{n}
matrix
M_{i,j}^{t}
by

\left(M_{i,j}^{t}\right)_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\overline{d}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=(i,j,t)\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The matrices
M_{i,j}^{t}
constitute a basis for the space
\mathcal{A}_{2,n}
of complex
\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{0,1\}^{n}
matrices that are invariant under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(2,n)
:

\mathcal{A}_{2,n}=\left\{\sum_{(i,j,t)\in I(2,n)}x_{i,j}^{t}M_{i,j}^{t}\mid x_{i,j}^{t}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

Note that
(M_{i,j}^{t})^{\sf T}=M_{j,i}^{t}
. Moreover, if
|\supp(\mathbf{u})|=i
,
|\supp(\mathbf{v})|=j
, then
|\supp(\mathbf{u})\cap\supp(\mathbf{v})|=t
is equivalent to
d_{H}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=i+j-2t
. The set
\mathcal{A}_{2,n}
is a
C^{*}
-algebra, i.e., it is closed under taking the adjoint, addition, scalar multiplication, and matrix multiplication. This algebra is the
Terwilliger algebra of the binary Hamming scheme
. We will now describe the block diagonalization of this algebra, which was derived by Schrijver
[
19
]
, and which allows a significant reduction in the size of matrices of
\mathcal{A}_{2,n}
by mapping it to an isomorphic smaller algebra. To this end, define, for
i,j,k,t\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, the numbers

\displaystyle\beta_{i,j,k}^{t}=\sum_{u=0}^{n}(-1)^{t-u}\tbinom{u}{t}\tbinom{n-2k}{u-k}\tbinom{n-k-u}{i-u}\tbinom{n-k-u}{j-u}.

(13)

Throughout the paper, we use the convention that
\tbinom{s}{t}=0
if
s<t
or
t<0
.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
19
]

The following map is a
*
-isomorphism of algebras:

\displaystyle\phi:\mathcal{A}_{2,n}

\displaystyle\to\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\mathbb{C}^{n-2k+1\times n-2k+1}

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j,t)\in I(2,n)}x_{i,j}^{t}M_{i,j}^{t}

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\left(\sum_{t}\tbinom{n-2k}{i-k}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\tbinom{n-2k}{j-k}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\beta_{i,j,k}^{t}x_{i,j}^{t}\right)_{i,j=k}^{n-k}.

(14)

Theorem
3.1
implies that, for coefficients
x_{i,j}^{t}\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j,t)\in I(2,n)}x_{i,j}^{t}M_{i,j}^{t}\succeq 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\left(\sum_{t}\beta_{i,j,k}^{t}x_{i,j}^{t}\right)_{i,j=k}^{n-k}\succeq 0\text{ for all $k=0,\ldots,{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$.}

(15)

This crucial fact will be used widely in the SDP-relaxations studied in this paper. Also note that
\phi
maps real valued matrices to real valued matrices. To see the size of the reduction, note that the matrices in
\mathcal{A}_{2,n}
have size
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
, while (cf.
[
19
]
)

\displaystyle|I(2,n)|=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(n-2k+1)^{2}=\binom{n+3}{3},

(16)

which equals the dimension of
\mathcal{A}_{2,n}
.

In the semidefinite relaxations in this paper, we will require not only that elements from
\mathcal{A}_{2,n}
are positive semidefinite, but some of the conditions are slightly stronger and inspired by
[
14
]
, namely that matrices of the form

R(M)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\diag(M)^{*}\\
\diag(M)&M\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for $M=\sum_{(i,j,t)\in I(2,n)}x_{i,j}^{t}M_{i,j}^{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{2,n}$},

are positive semidefinite. Note that the diagonal of
M
is given by
\diag M=\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{i,i}^{i}\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\mathbf{0})}
. To express positive semidefiniteness of
R(M)
in terms of
\phi(M)
, observe that for
i,j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
the matrix

\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}(\mathbf{0})})^{\mathsf{T}}=\sum_{t\mid(i,j,t)\in I(2,n)}M_{i,j}^{t}

belongs to
\mathcal{A}_{2,n}
. We have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A_{0}\oplus\cdots\oplus A_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
be the image of
\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}(\mathbf{0})})^{\mathsf{T}}
under the map
\phi
. Then

A_{0}=\tbinom{n}{i}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\tbinom{n}{j}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\cdot\big(\delta_{i,i^{\prime}}\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\big)_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}=0}^{n},

and
A_{k}
is the zero matrix for
k>0
.

Remark 3.1
.

In
[
19
]
, the
*
-algebra isomorphism
\phi
is obtained via a block diagonalisation
\phi(M)=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}U_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}MU_{k}
for explicit matrices
U_{k}
, where the columns of the
U_{k}
together form an orthonormal system of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
. The columns of
U_{0}
are
\binom{n}{i}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\mathbf{0})}
(
i=0,\ldots,n
). From this, Lemma
3.2
follows directly. Here, we give a proof that only uses the description of
\phi
as given in Theorem
3.1
.

Proof.

We first show the following identity:

\sum_{t=0}^{n}\beta_{i,j,k}^{t}=\begin{cases}\tbinom{n}{i}\tbinom{n}{j}&\text{if $k=0$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(17)

To see this, we calculate

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n}\beta_{i,j,k}^{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{n}\sum_{u=0}^{n}(-1)^{t-u}\tbinom{u}{t}\tbinom{n-2k}{u-k}\tbinom{n-k-u}{i-u}\tbinom{n-k-u}{j-u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u=0}^{n}\tbinom{n-2k}{u-k}\tbinom{n-k-u}{i-u}\tbinom{n-k-u}{j-u}\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{n}(-1)^{t-u}\tbinom{u}{t}.

Since
\sum_{t=0}^{n}(-1)^{t-u}\tbinom{u}{t}=0
for
u>0
, we may restrict the summation to
u=0
and find that

\sum_{t=0}^{n}\beta_{i,j,k}^{t}=\tbinom{n-2k}{-k}\tbinom{n-k}{i}\tbinom{n-k}{j}=\delta_{k,0}\tbinom{n}{i}\tbinom{n}{j}.

This proves equation (
17
).

To complete the proof of the lemma, recall that
\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}(\mathbf{0})})^{\mathsf{T}}=\sum_{t\mid(i,j,t)\in I(2,n)}M_{i,j}^{t}
.
So by Theorem
3.1

A_{k}=(\sum_{t=0}^{n}\beta_{i,j,k}^{t})\cdot\tbinom{n-2k}{i-k}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\tbinom{n-2k}{j-k}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\big(\delta_{i,i^{\prime}}\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\big)_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}=k}^{n-k}.

The result now follows directly from (
17
).
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
M=\sum_{(i,j,t)\in I(2,n)}x_{i,j}^{t}M_{i,j}^{t}\in\mathcal{A}_{2,n}
and let

\phi(M)=A_{0}\oplus\cdots\oplus A_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
.
Then, for any
c>0
we have

R(c,M)\succeq 0\quad\iff\quad A_{k}\succeq 0\text{ for all $k>0$ and }\begin{pmatrix}c&y^{*}\\
y&A_{0}\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0,

where
y_{i}=x_{i,i}^{i}\tbinom{n}{i}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}
for
i=0,\ldots,n
.

A proof of this proposition was indicated in
[
14
, Lemma 1]
by using additional details of the proof of Theorem
3.1
. Here, we deduce it directly from this theorem.

Proof.

Since
\phi
is linear, we may assume that
c=1
(by replacing
M
with
\tfrac{1}{c}M
.)
By taking Schur complements, we have

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&(\diag M)^{*}\\
\diag M&M\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0

\displaystyle\iff M-\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}x_{i,i}^{i}(x_{j,j}^{j})^{*}\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}(\mathbf{0})})^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq 0

\displaystyle\iff\phi\left(M-\sum_{i,j=0}^{n}x_{i,i}^{i}(x_{j,j}^{j})^{*}\mathbf{1}_{S_{i}(\mathbf{0})}(\mathbf{1}_{S_{j}(\mathbf{0})})^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\succeq 0

\displaystyle\iff A_{k}\succeq 0\text{ for $k>0$},\ A_{0}-\left(x_{i,i}^{i}(x_{j,j}^{j})^{*}\tbinom{n}{i}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\tbinom{n}{j}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\right)_{i,j=0}^{n}\succeq 0

\displaystyle\iff A_{k}\succeq 0\text{ for $k>0$},\ \begin{pmatrix}1&y^{*}\\
y&A_{0}\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0,

where we used Theorem
3.1
in the second equivalence, Lemma
3.2
in the third equivalence, and Schur’s complement in the fourth equivalence.
∎

3.2
Terwilliger algebra of the nonbinary Hamming scheme

For
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in[q]^{n}
we define
\overline{d}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):=(i,j,t,p)
where

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle=|\{\ell\mid\mathbf{u}_{\ell}\neq 0\}|,

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle=|\{\ell\mid\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\neq 0\}|,

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=|\{\ell\mid\mathbf{u}_{\ell}\neq 0,\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\neq 0\}|,

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=|\{\ell\mid\mathbf{u}_{\ell}=\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\neq 0\}|.

The set of tuples
(i,j,t,p)
that occur this way are given by

I(q,n)=\{(i,j,t,p)\mid 0\leq p\leq t\leq i,j,\ i+j\leq n+t\}

and they parametrize the orbits of pairs
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
under the group
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(q,n)=\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(q,n)\mid\sigma(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}\}
of symmetries of the Hamming space that stabilize the zero word.

For
(i,j,t,p)\in I(q,n)
we define the
[q]^{n}\times[q]^{n}
matrix
M_{i,j}^{t,p}
by

\left(M_{i,j}^{t,p}\right)_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\overline{d}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=(i,j,t,p)\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The matrices
M_{i,j}^{t,p}
constitute a basis for the space
\mathcal{A}_{q,n}
of complex
[q]^{n}\times[q]^{n}
matrices that are invariant under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(q,n)
:

\mathcal{A}_{q,n}=\left\{\sum_{(i,j,t,p)\in I(q,n)}x_{i,j}^{t,p}M_{i,j}^{t,p}\mid x_{i,j}^{t,p}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

This space is a
C^{*}
-algebra and coincides with the Terwilliger algebra of the nonbinary Hamming scheme. An explicit block diagonalisation of
\mathcal{A}_{q,n}
was given in
[
6
]
. To describe it, define for any nonnegative integers
i,j,t,p,a,k
the number

\alpha(i,j,t,p,a,k)=\beta^{n-a,t-a}_{i-a,j-a,k-a}\cdot(q-1)^{\tfrac{1}{2}(i+j)-t}\cdot\sum_{g=0}^{p}(-1)^{a-g}\tbinom{a}{g}\tbinom{t-a}{p-g}(q-2)^{t-a-p+g}.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
6
]
)
.

The following map is a
*
-isomorphism of algebras:

\displaystyle\phi:\mathcal{A}_{q,n}

Remark 4.1

(Interpretation of the

)
.

The coefficients

provide insight into the structure of the code

by extending the concept of distance distribution in Delsarte’s linear programming approach to triples. Whereas the distance distribution tracks the number of pairs in
C
at each distance

count the number of

that belong to an equivalence class of

under the group action of
\text{Aut}(2,n)
. We proceed by explaining this formally.
Define

X_{i,j,t}:=\{(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})\in\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{E}\mid\overline{d}(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{u})=(i,j,t)\},

for

. For each

\lambda_{i,j}^{t}:=|(C\times C\times C)\cap X_{i,j,t}|,

and let

\gamma_{i,j}^{t}:=|(\{\mathbf{0}\}\times\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{E})\cap X_{i,j,t}|=\binom{n}{i-t,j-t,t}

be the number of nonzero entries of

are given by

\displaystyle x_{i,j}^{t}=2^{-n}(\gamma_{i,j}^{t})^{-1}\lambda_{i,j}^{t}.

To see (

are pairwise orthogonal, and their inner products satisfy
\langle M_{i,j}^{t},M_{i,j}^{t}\rangle=\gamma_{i,j}^{t}

. Thus, we compute:

\displaystyle\langle M^{\prime},M_{i,j}^{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\text{Aut}(2,n)|^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in C}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\text{Aut}(2,n)\\
\sigma\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}\langle M_{\sigma C},M_{i,j}^{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\text{Aut}(2,n)|^{-1}\cdot|\text{Aut}_{\mathbf{0}}(2,n)|\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in C}|(\{\mathbf{u}\}\times C\times C)\cap X_{i,j,t}|

\displaystyle=2^{-n}|(C\times C\times C)\cap X_{i,j,t}|=2^{-n}\lambda_{i,j}^{t}.

Hence we have

M^{\prime}=2^{-n}\sum_{(i,j,t)\in I(2,n)}\lambda_{i,j}^{t}(\gamma_{i,j}^{t})^{-1}M_{i,j}^{t}.

Comparing the coefficients of each

Lemma 4.10

Similar to the binary case, the coefficients

Define

Tables with the new lower bounds

We used
Julia
to generate the semidefinite programs. We also developed an independent
Perl
code to generate the SDPs, which we used to validate our Julia code. Most SDPs were solved directly in
Julia
via
JuMP
with the high-precision solver
SDPA-GMP

[
17
]
. The solving time varied from a few seconds to several days for the larger instances in the tables below. The precision in
SDPA-GMP
was set to 512 bits, and custom parameters (mainly a larger lambdaStar, the parameter which determines an initial point for the interior point method) were used as opposed to the standard parameter settings provided with
SDPA-GMP
to ensure convergence for the larger instances.

Generating the SDP for larger instances posed additional challenges due to large coefficients, necessitating the use of high-precision numbers (we used 512 bits BigFloats). Our code for generating the SDP including the call to the solver
SDPA-GMP
and the parameters used for most of the instances we solved, is available here:

https://github.com/CoveringCodes

To illustrate the size of the computations, we report the number of variables in the final symmetry-reduced SDP, together with the sum of the block sizes in the block-diagonalization of
M^{\prime}
and the sum of the squares of these block sizes, see Table
4
. We note that the dimensions of the Terwilliger algebras are
\tbinom{n+3}{3}
in the binary case, and
\tbinom{n+4}{4}
in the nonbinary case, but the actual number of variables in our SDP is smaller, since the additional symmetry relations from Propositions
4.2
(iii) and
4.11
(iii) are imposed.

By Theorems
3.1
and
3.4
, the block sizes in the block diagonalization of
M^{\prime}
are given by
n-2k+1
for
k=0,\ldots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor
in the binary case, and
n+a-2k+1
for all pairs
(a,k)
with
0\leq a\leq k\leq n+a-k
in the nonbinary case.
The matrices
M^{\prime\prime}
and
N
have the same block structure after block-diagonalization, except that the block corresponding to
k=0
or
(a,k)=(0,0)
is bordered, increasing its size by one. The table reports the block sizes after symmetry reduction for the matrix
M^{\prime}
. The full SDP contains several semidefinite constraints (for
M^{\prime}
,
M^{\prime\prime}
, and one or more matrices
N
), each with essentially the same block sizes. Thus, the total size of the SDP is a small constant multiple of the values reported in the table.

The computational effort also depends on the choice of inequalities
(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\beta
, since the construction of the entries of the matrices
N
involves combinatorial sums whose number of terms increases with the number of nonzero coefficients
\lambda_{i}
. Consequently, for larger
R
the SDP takes much longer to generate, and typically also to solve, even though the block sizes and the number of variables remain unchanged for fixed
n
.

The total running time (including both SDP generation and solving) ranged from a few minutes for smaller instances to several days on standard desktop hardware, and in some cases up to two weeks for the largest instances. These runtimes depend strongly on the parameters
(q,n,R)
, the numerical precision, and solver settings. In particular, we used
SDPA-GMP
, as the SDPs can have very large coefficients and appear to require high-precision arithmetic for reliable solution. The reported runtimes should therefore be interpreted as indicative. All SDPs could very comfortably be generated and solved on a machine with 32 GB of RAM, although the generation and solving of larger instances required several gigabytes due to the use of high-precision arithmetic.
