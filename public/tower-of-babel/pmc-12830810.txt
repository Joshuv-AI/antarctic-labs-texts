Nationwide population-level influenza cycle threshold values and trends in influenza incidence: a longitudinal study
Authors: Pouria Feizabadi, Marjan Rahnamaye Farzami, Mahshid Nasehi, Ebrahim Babaee, Neda SoleimanvandiAzar, Payam Karimi, Fatemeh Niati, Fatemeh Rastgari, Babak Eshrati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Seasonal influenza remains a major global health concern, causing substantial morbidity and mortality each year. Early indicators of transmission are critical for timely public health interventions, and reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) cycle threshold (Ct) values, reflecting viral load, have recently been proposed as a proxy for community-level trends. In this study, we analyzed national influenza surveillance data from Iran between March 21, 2023, and March 19, 2024. Weekly mean Ct values were calculated from RT-PCR tests of suspected influenza cases, and weekly confirmed case counts were extracted from national reports. Time series patterns were visually inspected, and stationarity was assessed using the Augmented Dickey–Fuller test. Cross-correlation analysis was performed to identify lags between Ct values and incidence. ARIMA and ARIMAX models were fitted to evaluate and predict influenza incidence using Ct as an input, while VAR models were applied to characterize dynamic relationships. Analyses were conducted in Stata version 14. The mean Ct value over the one-year period was 27.16 ± 4.68. Cross-correlation showed that Ct values preceded incidence by about four weeks (r = 0.41, p = 0.003). ARIMA (0,1,1) best fitted Ct values, while ARIMA (2,1,2) best fitted influenza incidence. In ARIMAX, Ct values lagged by four weeks were a strong predictor of weekly incidence (β = 6.29, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that population-level Ct values can serve as an early signal of rising influenza activity, potentially allowing prediction of incidence trends up to four weeks in advance and supporting timely public health responses.

Introduction

Influenza is a pandemic-prone disease that predominantly occurs during the winter season1,2 and can affect multiple organs in the body, leading to a wide range of symptoms, including fever, chills, headache, fatigue, conjunctival redness, sore throat, dry cough, and nasal discharge. The main routes of transmission include sneezing, coughing, contact with contaminated surfaces, and inhalation of airborne viral particles3,4.

The influenza virus belongs to the Orthomyxoviridae family and includes types A, B, and C, with types A and B being responsible for morbidity and mortality5. Annually, there are approximately one billion cases of seasonal influenza worldwide, resulting in 290,000–650,000 respiratory deaths6. In a 2021 review study, Lafond et al. reported over 32 million cases and 5.7 million hospitalizations due to influenza each year7.

Influenza virus is an enveloped virus that carries a negative-strand RNA genome, with each gene segment individually encoded8. Classification of influenza A virus is based on the surface glycoproteins hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA). Hemagglutinin is the main antigen containing the sialic acid receptor-binding site, while neuraminidase facilitates the release of viral particles from infected cells3. Antigenic drift in HA and NA leads to genetic changes in the virus. Since influenza RNA is single-stranded, it lacks the proofreading capability that contributes to the emergence of seasonal influenza5,9,10.

Influenza A virus includes 18 HA and 11 NA subtypes, resulting in a total of 198 potential strains5. The most prevalent among these are A(H1N1) and A(H3N2), with H1N1 having caused two pandemics in the past 102 years11,12, the most recent of which occurred in 2009, affecting 60.8 million individuals and leading to 284,000 deaths globally5.

Rapid diagnosis of influenza is important because it allows for timely medical intervention and conserves resources13. Among the diagnostic methods for influenza, molecular techniques are considered the standard in laboratory settings. In addition to their speed, they are more sensitive than viral culture methods and can detect non-viable viruses14,15.

For laboratory diagnosis, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is employed to detect and isolate influenza viral RNA6. RT-PCR involves reverse transcription followed by PCR amplification to produce complementary DNA (cDNA) from mRNA, with the single-stranded cDNA serving as the template for PCR16. RT-PCR is widely used for identifying respiratory pathogens17. Although it offers high sensitivity and a faster turnaround time compared to other methods, RT-PCR has limitations, including complex RNA extraction procedures, post-PCR analysis, and an increased risk of contamination18. The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) recommends the use of RT-PCR for the identification of respiratory viruses19.

The viral load of the pathogen may influence the severity of the disease. Viral load, defined as the amount of virus per milliliter of body fluid, is inversely correlated with the cycle threshold (Ct) value17. A reduction of approximately three Ct cycles corresponds to a tenfold increase in viral load. Ct refers to the number of PCR amplification cycles required for the fluorescent signal to exceed the background level and become detectable in a real-time RT-PCR test20,21. This threshold is determined relative to background fluorescence, and the cycle number at which fluorescence exceeds the threshold is recorded as the Ct value16. Reina (2018) reported average Ct values of 31.02 for H1N1pdm09 and 31.03 for H3N222. Ct values are also influenced by the time elapsed since infection, with shorter intervals between infection and sample collection generally resulting in lower Ct values23. Various studies have demonstrated a correlation between viral load, symptom severity, and the host’s inflammatory response24–27.

Although diagnostic methods are now very rapid, initiation of antiviral treatment should not be delayed while awaiting influenza test results. Treatment is most effective when started as early as possible following infection19. Therefore, predicting the timing of an influenza epidemic is crucial in controlling the spread of the disease. In hospital settings, such predictions can prompt measures such as screening, patient isolation, postponement of non-urgent interventions, and increased staffing. James (2021) demonstrated that Ct values can be used to predict the trajectories of epidemics28. A growing epidemic is typically characterized by a large number of individuals with high viral loads23. Ct as a predictive quantifier can be a valuable tool at the population level to indicate epidemic trends and peak disease times before they occur. This study aims to investigate the relationship between Ct values and influenza incidence trends, to utilize Ct as a tool for monitoring and managing viral epidemics.

Methods

Data were collected nationwide from March 21, 2023, to March 19, 2024, as part of the national influenza surveillance program. The surveillance was based on medical visits of patients presenting with clinical symptoms suggestive of influenza at healthcare and health service centers. Physicians working in these centers identified suspected influenza cases according to the national case definition, which includes fever (≥ 38 °C), cough, sore throat, and myalgia, and referred them for sampling.

Nasopharyngeal specimens were collected from all suspected cases, based on the physician’s assessment, using sterile Dacron swabs with plastic or aluminum shafts. Samples were transported under standard conditions to designated provincial laboratories for analysis. RT-PCR assays were performed using the commercial kits AP-RAD, ROJE, and Hanagene, all of which have high and comparable sensitivity and specificity. The M gene was targeted for the detection of influenza A, while the NP and NS1 genes were used for the detection of influenza B. Individuals with cycle threshold (Ct) values ≤ 35 were classified as positive cases. Because the number of influenza B–positive cases was insufficient to demonstrate statistically significant relationships, only samples that tested positive for influenza A based on the M gene were included in this study.

Laboratory results were collected and verified by the National Reference Health Laboratories, and patient information was recorded in the Integrated Health Information Registration System of the Ministry of Health and Medical Education. As the national influenza data recorded by the Ministry of Health were reported on a weekly basis, to ensure data consistency and smooth trends, we aggregated the daily reported Ct values into weekly averages.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted using de-identified, population-level influenza surveillance data, and no individual-level or personally identifiable information was accessed, collected, or analyzed at any stage of the research. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol and all associated procedures were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences (Approval code: IR.IUMS.REC.1402.1122), confirming that the study met all ethical requirements.

Given that the data were fully anonymized and analyzed in aggregated form, the study involved minimal risk to participants, and individual privacy and confidentiality were fully preserved. Informed consent was obtained at the time of data collection by the responsible surveillance and healthcare authorities, in accordance with national regulations. As no identifiable personal data were used and the analysis was performed on aggregated datasets, no additional informed consent was required from individual participants for this study. The use of these data for research purposes was approved by the relevant authorities, and the study raises no ethical or scientific concerns.

Overview

This study used time series statistical methods to analyze the dynamic relationship between Ct values and influenza incidence over time. First, the basic statistical and stationary properties of the variables were assessed. Subsequently, advanced time series techniques, including cross-correlation function (CCF), vector autoregression (VAR), and ARIMA/ARIMAX models, were employed to assess temporal dependencies and causal interactions. All data were analyzed using Stata version 14 software.

Preliminary time series assessment

Trend plots for the primary variables (Ct values and influenza cases) were visually examined to assess trends, potential seasonality, and volatility in the data.

Stationarity testing

Stationarity is a prerequisite for time series modeling. To evaluate it, the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test was conducted on each variable to detect the presence of unit roots, using multiple lags to account for dynamic data structures. A p-value less than 0.05 indicated rejection of the null hypothesis, confirming stationarity.

Data transformation for stationarity

For non-stationary series, first-order differencing was applied to remove long-term trends and stabilize the mean. Cross-correlation function (CCF) analysis

Cross-correlation analysis was conducted to explore lagged relationships between the Ct series and the influenza time series. Correlations were computed at multiple time lags and visualized using CCF plots. This step served as a key exploratory phase to identify meaningful temporal lags to be included in causal models such as ARIMAX and VAR.

Vector autoregression (VAR) modeling

To assess the dynamic interdependencies between multiple time series, Vector Autoregression (VAR) models were fitted. These models allow each variable to be influenced by its past values and the past values of other variables. Optimal lag order was determined using information criteria including Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Hannan-Quinn information criterion (HQIC), and the Likelihood Ratio Test. The final model was selected to balance parsimony with explanatory power.

Results

Overall, the descriptive statistics of our study indicated that the mean Ct value was 27.16 ± 4.68, with the minimum recorded Ct value of 8 observed on October 22, 2023. Over the course of one year, a total of 4923 laboratory tests were registered nationwide. Among these, 4483 samples had Ct values below 35 and were therefore classified as influenza-positive cases based on the national diagnostic criteria. Meanwhile, 53,256 individuals visited healthcare centers, and among them, 5191 cases were diagnosed with influenza by physicians based on syndromic symptoms. See it in Fig. 1.

We tested the explanatory ability of ARIMAX models by considering various lagged levels of the Ct variable as external predictors for case forecasting. Of note was the fourth lag with a high and very significant effect (β = 6.29, p < 0.001), and hence the best predictor among what was tested. Full model details can be accessed in Table 2.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate a significant temporal association between Ct values and the incidence of influenza at the population level. This relationship was consistently identified through various statistical approaches, including CCF analysis (r = 0.41, p = 0.003), VAR, and ARIMAX modeling (β = 6.29, p < 0.001). The CCF showed a positive and significant correlation with a four-week lag. This lag was even shown in the RIMAX model, which identified the fourth lag as the most predictive factor for influenza trends. The consistency of results between different analytical methods strengthens the validity of these findings and supports the utility of Ct values as an early warning indicator of influenza epidemic dynamics. These results align with previous research, which shows that lower Ct values at the beginning of an epidemic can indicate a growing number of infections23,29,30.

The average Ct value observed in our study was 27.16, which is somewhat lower than the averages reported by Reina (2018), who documented mean Ct values of 31.02 for H1N1pdm09 and 31.03 for H3N222. This discrepancy may reflect differences in the circulating viral strains during our study period, sample collection quality, testing protocols, or potentially higher viral loads in the population under investigation23. Lower Ct values in our dataset may also indicate that samples were collected earlier in the course of infection, as previous studies have demonstrated that Ct values are influenced by the time elapsed since infection31.

In our country, due to the lack of a fully integrated and unified health information registration system, surveillance of infectious diseases is conducted through various software platforms and databases, which may differ from year to year. Each platform typically follows its own case definition and reporting procedures, leading to inconsistencies across different seasons. Therefore, to ensure data consistency and reliability, we decided to include only one influenza season in the present study. Moreover, the concurrent presence of COVID-19 in recent years and the overlap between influenza and COVID-19 cases made it challenging to accurately differentiate between the two infections, which could have compromised the validity of our findings. However, due to data limitations, we were unable to examine disease severity over more than one period, Sanz-Muñoz (2024) showed that viral loads typically decline as infection progresses. Accordingly, outpatients, who are often in the acute phase of illness, exhibit lower Ct values, whereas hospitalized individuals tend to present with higher Ct values as viral loads diminish32. This observation is consistent with virological principles, as lower Ct values in the early phase of infection indicate higher viral loads, which in turn may increase transmissibility and drive epidemic growth33.

Our findings are important for influenza surveillance and control strategies. Influenza surveillance often relies on syndromic data (e.g., influenza-like illness, hospitalizations), which may be delayed in reporting. Ct values, which represent viral load, can provide a more immediate and quantitative indicator of epidemic progression. However, our findings may not be generalizable to all settings, as changes in viral characteristics in different populations may have other effects.

Despite the high sensitivity and specificity of molecular techniques such as RT-PCR, which are considred the gold standard for influenza diagnosis, these techniques are not without limitations. These limitations include the need for skilled personnel, specialized equipment, complex RNA extraction methods, and the risk of contamination. Furthermore, a positive PCR result indicates the presence of viral RNA, which does not necessarily confirm the presence of viable, infectious virus, especially in the later stages of infection31.

There are also practical constraints involved in deploying molecular diagnostics, such as the need for trained operators, careful reagent handling, and accurate result interpretation—all of which, if done improperly, can lead to false positives or negatives34,35. Furthermore, the RNA extraction step presents significant operational challenges. This process typically requires specialized centrifugation equipment, expensive extraction kits, and RNase-free environments, all of which can be difficult to maintain in resource-limited settings. Although automated RNA extraction systems have helped reduce some of these hurdles, access to such technologies remains limited in many areas36. Despite potential limitations in data quality and completeness inherent to routine surveillance systems, our findings underscore the potential value of monitoring population-level Ct dynamics as an early warning tool. Even when correlations between Ct values and influenza incidence appear modest, the ability to detect shifts in viral load prior to a surge in confirmed cases can provide crucial lead time for public health preparedness and response. In countries where resources or testing capacity may be constrained, such early predictive indicators could support more timely interventions, enhance situational awareness, and ultimately mitigate the impact of seasonal influenza epidemics.

Different influenza virus strains can differ in disease severity, Ct values, and the time lag between infection and symptoms onset37,38. For example, studies have shown that H3N2 strains are linked to more severe symptoms, such as higher average fever (38.1 °C vs. 37.9 °C), and increased hospitalization rates compared to H1N1pdm0939. These differences may stem from variations in surface proteins, such as hemagglutinin, which influence viral binding efficiency and immune evasion. At the molecular level, Ct values also differ between strains22. These differences may influence transmission dynamics, as higher viral loads generally associate with increased infectivity and shorter incubation periods. Additionally, epidemiological data show that different viral strains have varying timelines for symptom onset. For example, H3N2 infections typically have longer incubation periods and a gradual onset of symptoms, whereas H1N1pdm09 is often associated with a sudden onset of symptoms39. These differences emphasize the need for careful monitoring of circulating strains, with targeted measures for each one.

Another important consideration is the complex relationship between individual Ct values and clinical outcomes. While lower Ct values usually indicate higher viral loads and increased transmissibility, their direct link to disease severity remains complex, with some studies showing inconsistent results. Although our study focused on trends rather than specific clinical outcomes, further research into this connection could improve the accuracy of predictive models. Current epidemic surveillance strategies often depend on confirmed case counts, test positivity rates, and hospitalization or mortality data. However, these measures are often limited by testing capacity, non-representative sampling, and reporting delays23. From a practical perspective, this study highlights the potential of Ct values as an early indicator for disease surveillance. Unlike traditional methods that depend on delayed signals, monitoring changes in Ct trends may allow for earlier detection of outbreaks. This could help public health officials respond more quickly by taking appropriate actions, such as increasing hospital capacity, mobilizing healthcare workers, and issuing timely public health guidance.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that Ct can be used as a predictive marker for the trajectory of influenza outbreaks at the population level. A decrease in Ct was linked to an increase in influenza cases about four weeks later. This prediction is vital for guiding timely interventions and enhancing surveillance systems, which can facilitate early detection of epidemics and better prepare healthcare facilities.

Strengths and limitations

While this study offers strong evidence supporting the utility of Ct values at the population level for forecasting influenza trends, some limitations should be noted. Firstly, it was conducted over a single influenza season (March 2023 to March 2024), with data from only one cycle. Secondly, since the data were collected nationwide, there could be measurement errors due to variation in personnel and diagnostic tools across different sites.
