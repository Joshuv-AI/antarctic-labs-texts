The utility and use of solitary bees for pollination in greenhouses and other covered growing systems: A review
Authors: Katja Hogendoorn, Lisa J. Evans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•There are many solitary bee species capable of pollinating crops under cover.•There is a taxonomic basis for the differences in the pollination efficacies.•Lack of demand and research limit the mass deployment of solitary bees.•Resilience requires diversification of pollinators used under covers.

Introduction

The increasing and interconnected pressures of human population size, urbanization, diminishing water supplies, and climate change are contributing to a substantial global decline in available arable land per person, which, by 2050, is projected to have decreased to one-third of the area available in 1970 (Benke and Tomkins 2017; FAO 2016). This puts additional pressure not only on the areas currently used to produce food, but also on the natural environment. It is therefore imperative to increase efficiency, with respect to the use of land, water and resources when growing food crops. Protected cropping offers substantial solutions (Fig. 1). Compared to production in the open, protected cropping makes more effective use of space, water, and nutrients, provides protection against elements and contamination by foreign pollen, and allows year-round production (do Amarante et al. 2011; Morison et al. 2000). In addition, covers render protection against a range of insect pests, agents, pathogens, and damage caused by birds to fruit, and are conducive to the use of bio-control agents. With arable land area globally in increasingly short supply, and climate change affecting the ability to grow crops outside (Mombo et al. 2025), there has been an exponential increase in the area under protected cropping (Cuesta Roble 2019). Such protected environments include greenhouses (i.e. relatively fixed structures, glazed with glass or channelled polycarbonate), permanently netted areas (including with hail netting, which is non-penetrable for many bee pollinators), and polytunnels (high tunnels of plastic sheeting drawn over ribbing of pipes; e.g. Allen et al. 2015; Baudoin et al. 2013; Parks et al. 2019; Kendall et al. 2021). These protective covers can present various challenges for bee health and behaviour, as reviewed by Kendall et al. An estimated 45% of the production value of greenhouse crops is attributed to pollination (Guerra-Sanz 2008), with key beneficiaries including tomato, cucumber, pepper, strawberry and eggplant (Baudoin et al. 2017; Baudoin et al. 2013; Guerra-Sanz 2008). For crops grown in polytunnels and under nets, such as fruit trees, nuts, berries and melons, pollination dependence is also likely to be high, although overall quantitative estimates are not available, because the proportion of these crops that is currently grown under cover is unknown.

Bees are among the most effective pollinators for most crops (Willmer et al. 2017), and worldwide, social bee species in the Apidae, in particular honey bees (genus Apis), and to a lesser extent bumble bee species (genus Bombus) and stingless bees (family Meliponidae), are the crop pollinators of choice (Free 1993; Velthuis and van Doorn 2006; Dag 2008). Husbandry for several species is well established. Hives can be bought or leased, and very large numbers of individuals can be moved into and out of the crop with relative ease and at low costs per individual.

Honey bee hives are commonly placed in large netted orchards, but for greenhouses and polytunnels, bumble bees are often preferred over honey bees (Guerra-Sanz 2008). This preference reflects the fact that, compared to honey bees, bumble bees are less defensive, more active in low light conditions, and easier to introduce and manage in confined spaces (Kendall et al. 2021). In addition, unlike honey bees, they are buzz pollinators, making them suitable for greenhouse crops with flowers that require sonication to achieve pollination, such as tomato, capsicum and eggplant (Velthuis and van Doorn 2006). Currently seven species of bumble bees are managed worldwide for the purpose of greenhouse pollination (Osterman et al. 2021). More recently, there has been interest in using tropical stingless bees in greenhouses, as several species can be managed and perform well in these conditions, although so far their use has been restricted to small scale research trials rather than commercial production (reviewed by Kendall et al. 2021; Osterman et al. 2021).

While the social Apidae completely dominate pollination in protected cropping environments, they only comprise about 9% of all bee species (Danforth et al. 2019), and there may be a wealth of solitary bee species that may have potential as pollinators of crops under cover. Indeed, numerous species have been assessed for their potential to pollinate greenhouse and orchard crops. However, a review of the outcomes of these assessments is not available and a critical analysis of the reasons why solitary bees are underrepresented in commercial pollination is lacking. Here, we review the utility and use of solitary bees for pollination under covers, and the life-history traits that make some species better candidates than others.

Do we need solitary bees for pollination under covers?

There are conceptual arguments for diversifying pollination approaches in general, and in protected cropping systems in particular (Batra 1994; Westerkamp and Gottsberger 2000). One argument pertains to the risk associated with introducing managed bee species (for which husbandry has been developed) into regions where they are not native. Such introductions can have a range of ecological consequences, including displacement of native species or populations through competitive exclusion, hybridisation with native species, introduction and spill-over of diseases, and creating changes in the plant pollinator networks that lead to modification in plant composition, e.g. through the pollination of weeds (e.g. Evans 2017; Goulson 2003; Mallinger et al. 2017; Aizen et al. 2019; Inoue et al. 2008; McQuillan and Hingston 1999; Morales et al. 2013; Iwasaki and Hogendoorn 2022; Tsuchida et al. 2010; LeCroy et al. 2020).

Another risk is that a reliance on only a few species for pollination leaves the system vulnerable. This is exemplified by the near-global spread of Varroa mite (Varroa destructor Anderson and Trueman) and associated viruses, which severely impact honey bees and now necessitate active pest management for colony survival (Iwasaki et al. 2015). Consequences for pollination service delivery include increased fees for hive rentals and local pollination shortages (Aizen and Harder 2009). This, together with alarming reports of native bee declines (Herrera 2020; LeBuhn and Vargas 2021), has given impetus to roadside and field edge planting schemes to enhance support for a diverse volunteer pollinator community in cropping landscapes (Blaauw and Isaacs 2014; Lowe et al. 2021). However, compared to open fields, the resilience of pollination under covers is severely reduced, as there are no volunteer pollinators present, and so there is a large reliance on managed honey bees for covered orchards and on a few species of managed bumble bees in greenhouses (Velthuis and van Doorn 2006). As with field grown crops, the security of pollination under covers could potentially be improved by expanding the portfolio of managed pollinators to include solitary bee species.

An alternative solution to shortages in pollination services that is frequently proposed is mechanical pollination (Broussard et al. 2023). However, mechanical pollination is often labour-intensive, costly (Baylis et al. 2021) and can result in yields that are either higher or lower than those achieved through insect pollination (Hogendoorn et al. 2006; Garratt et al. 2014; Gonzalez et al. 1998). Mechanical pollination typically requires pollen to be collected, stored, and delivered to flowers, which poses significant technical challenges and can limit scalability (Baylis et al. 2021). Although technological advances continue to be made, and some mechanical approaches have performed sufficiently well to ensure full pollination (Broussard et al. 2023), others, such as robotic bees, remain a technically and economically inviable ‘solution’, and their development may pose substantial ecological and moral risks (Potts et al. 2018).

The utility of solitary bees for pollination under covers

The most economically important greenhouse crops that benefit from pollination are tomato, capsicum, eggplant, strawberry and cucurbits (zucchini, melon, cucumber). Smaller greenhouses are also used by the vegetable seed industry for production of hybrid seed of a variety of crops. Other pollination dependent crops, including pomes, stone fruit, melons, a variety of berries, and nuts, are increasingly grown under fixed netting or plastic covers (Kendall et al. 2021). As climate change continues to influence where and how grow crops can be grown, it is likely that more of these crops will be cultivated in protected and/or temperature-controlled conditions in the future. A prerequisite for the development of a solitary bee for use as pollinator is that the species in question can endure the conditions and navigate under covers, as well as deliver the required pollination service. The efficacy of pollinating species can be tested in controlled experiments, either by monitoring visitation or releasing species in enclosures containing the flowering crop, followed by an assessment of effects on fruit or seed set, yield, or quality.

To investigate the solitary bee species that have been assessed for their ability to pollinate greenhouse and orchard crops, we performed a literature search of the databases Scopus (Elsevier 2025) and Web of Science (ISI Thompson-Reuters, 2024). Our search phrase included all crops that were identified by Kendall et al. (2021) as grown in protected cropping, to which we added watermelon. To exclude studies of only social bees, we added all main solitary bee (sub)families that are known crop pollinators to the search phrase. To exclude data for visitation only, we added different measures of pollination efficacy. After this process, we were left with 59 publications (S1) in which one or more solitary bee species were assessed for their ability to pollinate orchard (n= 33 papers) or greenhouse crops (n= 26 papers). These publications covered 100 tests using a total of 44 solitary bee species for their ability to pollinate one or more of 26 crop species (16 covered orchard and ten greenhouse crops). A total of 33 tests involved ground-nesting bee species (n = 25 species), while 67 tests targeted bee species nesting above ground (n =19 species), which we will refer to as cavity-nesting species. Thus, cavity-nesting species were subjected to more trials per species than ground-nesting species, likely because they can be manipulated to nest in tubes or nesting blocks, making them easier to use in protected cropping systems.

The cavity-nesting bee species included representatives from the Megachilidae (53 tests, 11 bee species), and solitary Apidae (14 tests, 9 bee species). The ground-nesting bees included representatives from the Andrenidae (6 tests, 6 species), Halictidae (7 tests, 7 species species), Colletidae (3 tests, 3 species) and solitary Apidae (17 tests, 10 species). There was a clear bias in the crop/bee combinations tested, with more Osmia species tested for their ability to pollinate Rosaceae (apple, pear, almond and cherry and Rubus) and blueberry crops, and more solitary species in the Apidae tested for pollination of Solanaceae (Fig. 2). Of the 100 assessments, the bees were deemed suitable pollinators in 82 cases. Of the 12 cases where the solitary bees were found unsuitable as pollinators, onion (Allium cepa) stood out: seven bee species were tested for their ability to pollinate onion (for seed production), and only two species of Lasioglossum seemed suitable, while three species of Osmia, as well as Megachile rotundata Fabricius and Nomia melanderi Cockerell did not pollinate onion. However, Cane (2024) mentions that N. melanderi does pollinate onion. Furthermore, Osmia ribifloris Cockerell and Xylocopa virginica L. did not pollinate a range of blueberry (Vaccinium) species. Thus, overall, this review demonstrates that a wide range of solitary bee species are efficient pollinators of various greenhouse and covered orchard crops. It should be noted, however, that negative results are less likely to be published. In addition, we are aware of positive unpublished findings that cannot be disclosed due to commercial confidentiality.

The use of solitary bees in commercial pollination under cover

Despite the overwhelming suitability of various solitary bees as crop pollinators, only eight species are currently commercially available or managed for crop pollination — reviewed by Maccagnani and Sgolastra (2000) and Osterman et al. (2021). In North America, Megachile rotundata is used as pollinator of lucerne (Pitts-Singer and Cane 2011), but not for the pollination of carrot seed, lowbush blueberry or cranberry, which it is also able to pollinate (supplementary data S1). The blue orchard bee (Osmia lignaria Say) is commercially available and used for cherry Bosch et al. (2006) and almond (Artz et al. 2013) pollination but is also able to pollinate apple (Kuhn and Ambrose 1984), pear (McCabe et al. 2024), raspberry (Andrikopoulos and Cane 2018) and strawberry (Horth and Campbell 2018). In Japan, Korea and China, Osmia cornifrons Radoszkowski is used for the apple, pear and cherry pollination (Da-Yong and Long-Shi, 2007, McKinney and Park, 2012, Lee et al., 2008), but not for highbush blueberry. Finally, in Europe O. bicornis L. and O. cornuta Latreille are harvested from bee hotels and used for orchard pollination and seed production under cover Gruber et al., 2011, Krunic et al., 2005, Ladurner et al., 2002, Maccagnani et al., 2003a, Pinzauti et al., 1997, Maccagnani et al., 2003b. Thus, four Osmia species are commercially available and used in open and enclosed orchards.

Also managed for pollination, but not commercially available are the ground-nesting N.melanderi and Rhophitoides canus Eversmann, in respectively the USA (Cane 2024a) and the Czech republic (Ptacek 1990). However, there are no recent reports about crop management for pollination of lucerne by R. canus, and neither species are used in protected cropping. The cavity-nesting carpenter bees Xylocopa frontalis Olivier and X. grisescens Lepeletier can be managed for the pollination of passion fruit (Junqueira et al., 2012, 2013), but they are not commercially available, and passion fruit is currently grown nearly only in open systems.

Three points stand out: 1. All commercially available solitary bees were developed before 1997 (Osterman et al. 2021); 2. Aside from the use of four Osmia species for small scale vegetable seed production and in open and covered orchards (Maccagnani and Sgolastra 2020), more recent efforts to develop solitary pollinators for greenhouse crops (Bell et al. 2006; Hogendoorn et al. 2007; Hogendoorn et al. 2006; Hogendoorn et al. 2000; Keasar 2010) have not led to their commercial deployment; 3. No solitary species are used for greenhouse pollination. Below, we examine how the interplay between demand and supply underlies this lack of progress.

A lack of demand for solitary pollinators in protected cropping

A likely reason for both the limited deployment of solitary bees in covered orchards and the absence of solitary bees as greenhouse pollinators is that, in nearly all countries, the demand is already met by commercially available honey bees (covered orchards) and bumble bees (greenhouses). Worldwide, seven bumble bee species are currently commercially produced, several of which have been introduced into multiple countries for greenhouse pollination, in particular for crops that require sonication (Velthuis and van Doorn 2006). Bumble bees are efficient and amenable pollinators for greenhouse environments and are readily available for purchase in over 70 countries (Biobest 2021). The availability of satisfactory pollinators reduces the industries’ incentive to invest in alternatives. Even in countries such as Australia, where bumble bees have not been introduced despite strong lobbying by the greenhouse industry, the development of solitary bees has had minimal industry support as this was perceived to weaken the case for the introduction of bumble bees (S. Carruthers, pers. com.).

Hurdles in meeting the supply of solitary pollinators

The commercial use of solitary bees in protected cropping depends on the ability to mass breed them, control disease, manipulate the phenology to coincide with crop flowering, and to transport and place them in under covers, as well as protect them from pesticides. In addition, covered environments pose specific challenges to bee survival and reproduction (Kendall et al. 2021).

Breeding

For smaller applications, e.g. pollination of vegetable seed crops in isolations or fruit trees in small, netted areas, it may be possible to harvest sufficient bees from nesting substrate placed outside, as is for example done with O. bicornis. This is particularly feasible when using species that nest in aggregations, or when suitable nesting substrate is concentrated in areas with sufficient floral resources, e.g. open orchards. However, for application in large covered orchards and greenhouses, solitary bees would need to be bred in very large numbers as wild trapping would not meet customer demands, is often unpredictable, and can have negative consequences for wild bee populations (Roulston and Goodell 2011).

The commercial supplies of M. rotundata and Osmia species demonstrate that some solitary species can be bred in large numbers (Pitts-Singer and Cane 2011; Maccagnani et al. 2007; Bosch and Kemp 2000). However, in contrast to commercial bumble bees, breeding of these short-lived solitary species happens outside, using the floral supplies of crops and the surrounding vegetation. Breeding in confinement may provide better options for the control of diseases, brood parasites and predators, but this requires either a very large supply of plants (Schäffler and Dötterl 2011) or for the bees to take pollen and sugar water from feeders. Solitary bees will generally use sugar water feeders in captivity (e.g. Dukas and Real 1991; Hogendoorn et al. 2006; Hogendoorn et al. 2000, Fig. 3), but supplying pollen through feeders is often problematic. So far, the literature indicates that only honey bees and bumble bees are able to remove pollen from a dish or from fake flowers in captivity (Kitaoka and Nieh 2009; Jandt et al. 2009; Arenas and Farina 2012; Russell and Papaj 2016). Unpublished observations indicate that some carpenter bees will use pollen feeders (Xylocopa sulcatipes Maa, Stark pers. com; X. mordax Smith, Guerrero pers. com.), however, this has not resulted in viable indoor breeding. Our own unpublished attempts to make Amegilla murrayensis Rayment, A. pulchra Smith, Brevineura xanthoclypeata Rayment, Megachile erythropyga Smith and Xylocopa aerata Smith take pollen from feeders have been unsuccessful, even though the females involved were newly eclosed and inexperienced. Disease control

Solitary bees are prone to attacks from parasites, predators and pathogens. Walking predators, such as ants can be excluded by protecting access to the nests, for example by covering the supports with a sticky insect barrier, or placing them in sump-oil (Junqueira et al. 2012; e.g. Gerling et al. 1983). Managing infestation rates of flying insect predators or parasitic wasps is more difficult. This can either be done by breeding in enclosures, which comes with the difficulties of providing sufficient food as detailed above, or by removing the parasites prior to the eclosion and release of the bees, as happens during breeding of M. rotundata (Stephen 1981). Fungal brood diseases such as chalkbrood can be managed using fungicidal treatments (e.g. Parker 1984) or loose cell management (Richards 1984). A requirement for loose cell management is that individual brood cells are easy to access, harvest and handle. Brood of cavity-nesting bees is easier to access than that of ground-nesting bees, but harvesting large numbers of individual brood cells without causing harm can be prohibitively time consuming and has only been achieved for M. rotundata (the alfalfa leafcutter bee), and Osmia lignaria. Preventing the bees from re-using nests by offering new, clean nesting substrate was beneficial for reducing chalkbrood in A. murrayensis (Hogendoorn, pers. obs). Furthermore, breeders should be aware that the use of honey bee collected pollen can result in introducing pathogens, which may be infective (Gisder and Genersch 2017; Maxfield-Taylor et al. 2015).

Species selection and manipulation of the phenology to suit the cropping cycle

The ability to encourage/deploy solitary bees in any commercial cropping systems is strongly influenced by their phenology, which ultimately governs their alignment with crop flowering periods. The solitary bees that are currently commercially produced, Megachile rotundata and Osmia species (Osterman et al. 2021), have an adult lifespan of four-six weeks. Within that time, females of these species often very actively provision brood cells. This period of activity can work well for crops with a short flowering time, such as pome trees and lucerne, provide the bees emerge in synchrony with crop flowering. Synchronisation with crop flowering has been studied for both Osmia and M. rotundata, and methods have been developed to delay or protract the emergence using controlled temperatures (e.g. Sedivy and Dorn 2014; Bosch and Kemp 2000; Richards and Kevan 2002). Again, this is more easily managed for bees that nest above ground than for ground-nesting species.

However, there are limits to the ability to manipulate phenology and emergence. Adult emergence of O. lignaria, and probably of other Osmia, can be shortened or delayed by some weeks through changing wintering conditions (Andrikopoulos 2018; Bosch et al. 2010), but limited overlap between natural bee emergence and crop flowering can be problematic. This is evident in New Zealand, where most solitary bees show minimal overlap with early-flowering covered crops such as stone fruit, and only moderate overlap with covered berry crops (Howlett et al. 2025).

Importantly, the deployment of short-lived bee species in greenhouses with year-round pollination requirements necessitates more fundamental manipulation than simply changing the timing of eclosion by a few weeks, as the supply must be on-going. This implies that short-lived bees that have a brood dormancy stage must be managed to eclose in batches, on a year-round basis.

Dormancy and univoltinism can be a benefit for managing pollinators of short flowering orchard crops, as no feeding is necessary, unlike Apis and Bombus. However, the short nest provisioning period is one of the main bottlenecks in the development of most short-lived solitary bees for greenhouse pollination. To achieve constant supply, methods need to be in place to produce both diapausing brood and to break dormancy. In species where both dormant and non-dormant brood is produced, such as M. rotundata (e.g. Pitts-Singer and Bosch 2010) and A. murrayensis (Leijs et al. 2017), elucidating the factors that govern diapause is an on-going challenge (e.g. Bosch and Kemp 2003; Pitts-Singer 2020). This is because dormancy seems to be regulated by a complex interplay of genetic (Parker and Tepedino 1982), epigenetic and environmental influences (e.g. Kemp and Bosch 2000; Pitts-Singer and Bosch 2010; Fischman et al. 2017; Hagadorn et al. 2023; Rinehart et al. 2025). Environmental influences may work indirectly via effects on the mother, or directly via effects on the development of the brood (Tepedino and Parker 1986; Tauber et al. 2007).

Where applicable, long-lived bees could be used instead. For example, members of the large carpenter bees (Xylocopini), that nest above ground, are useful tomato and cucurbit pollinators (Hogendoorn et al. 2000; Keasar 2010) and adult females live a year or more. However, during that time, they are not continually provisioning brood cells. Some of the most readily managed species, those that use linear nesting substrate such as bamboo, may have long periods of inactivity, during which they wait for their brood to emerge (van der Blom and Velthuis 1988). This means that introductions into the greenhouse would still need to be staggered, but this may be more easily accommodated than with short-lived species.

The above approaches assume that focus should be on development of a single species. An alternative strategy could be to maintain a diverse pool of pollinators in protected cropping, supported by multiple mass-rearing programs. These species could be used sequentially or simultaneously to pollinate greenhouse crops, potentially increasing both the efficiency and resilience of greenhouse pollination service (Batra 1994), similar to how promoting pollinator diversity benefits pollination in field environments (Garibaldi et al. 2016). However, this would also multiply all the problems mentioned above and may therefore be unpractical.

Nesting substrate and placement in the greenhouse

Males and non-nesting females do not collect pollen, and their pollination services would, therefore, be incidental. This may work well for some bee-crop combinations (e.g. Cane et al. 2011), for example when males of oligolectic species seek females in crop flowers. However, pollination in enclosures largely depends on active pollen collection, and hence on nest provisioning females. As a corollary, nesting substrate and suitable pollen and nectar sources need to be present. For twig and stem nesting bees, such as Megachile and Osmia, the nesting substrate is well understood and straightforward to provide (Maccagnani and Sgolastra 2020), but for bees that nest in the soil, establishing nesting opportunities is more difficult. First off, for most ground-nesting bees, a lack of quantitative data on site and soil characteristics at nest sites remains a significant knowledge gap, despite its importance to nest establishment and survival (Potts and Willmer 1997; Antoine and Forrest 2021; Harmon-Threatt 2020; Cane 1991; Cane 2024). Then, once suitable nesting substrate has been identified, a nesting bed or nesting blocks need to be established inside the protected area. For example, for Amegilla, which digs a superficial nest in soft sandstone or clay walls by regurgitating nectar and digging into the substrate when wet, small nesting blocks have been designed (Fig. 2a). However, the combined nesting blocks required to house the 282 bees needed for the pollination of one hectare of greenhouse tomato (Hogendoorn et al. 2007) would weigh 42 kg, making their transport costly. A possible solution to the weight problem would be the creation of permanent nesting areas or nest boxes. This would allow the introduction of loose mature pupae in the greenhouse, such as happens in Osmia and Megachile deployment where cocoons containing emerging adults are released. Still, this is not an ideal arrangement, because compared to species that nest in hollow stems, harvesting pupae from soil or from clay blocks is labour intensive, in particular when they are not protected by a cocoon. The best solution may be to design lightweight nesting substrate with the same properties as soil or clay. The possibility of creating ground-nesting areas in the greenhouse or developing more transportable nesting substrate has, as far as we know, not been explored for ground-nesting bees beyond small scale research trials (Leonard and Harmon-Threatt 2019; Jackson et al. 2025). Despite the difficulties mentioned above, nest provisioning has been achieved in flight cages and greenhouses for a range of ground-nesting species, including Macropis fulvipes Fabricius (Schäffler and Dötterl 2011), Xenoglossa (Peponapis) pruinosa Say and N. melanderi (Batra 1976; Stephen 1965).

The protected cropping environment

To provision nests, the bees need to orient and forage under cover. Considerable attention has been paid to the various types of covers, how they may affect bee foraging behaviour and pollination, as well as the actions that can be undertaken to mitigate adverse conditions (for reviews see Guerra-Sanz 2008; Kendall et al. 2021). Therefore, we will not go into details here, but one issue needs to be mentioned. For the use of solitary bees, the protected areas should be designed to keep the bees inside, i.e. they should not have uncovered opening vents, gaps around doors or exhaust fans, or mesh through which the bees can escape, because they will if they can (Hogendoorn, pers. obs.). This can be problematic, as ventilation is needed for temperature control, and fine mesh tends to clog up with dust. However, increasingly, advanced greenhouses are built to be thrips-proof to prevent infestations by plant viruses, which makes them better suited to the deployment of solitary bees. While experienced bumble bees have similar propensities to escape, the newly eclosed inexperienced workers will often adapt to foraging inside the greenhouse (Birmingham and Winston 2004). The requirement to have a fully enclosed crop can affect the growing conditions and may necessitate alternative solutions for humidity or temperature control.

Introduction and placement

Although large carpenter bees have been placed in captivity with their nests and settled in to forage (Keasar et al. 2007), greater success is generally achieved when the bees have no experience foraging outside. For example, blue-banded bees (Amegilla) caught foraging outside never started reproducing in the greenhouse (Hogendoorn pers. obs.). Introducing inexperienced solitary bees can be done by releasing eclosing pupae or newly emerged adults, either in their maternal nest or not (e.g. Parker and Potter 1974; Stephen 1960). Again, this poses more of a problem for ground-nesting bees than for cavity nesters, as explained above: harvesting pupae from soil is labour intensive, and nesting substrate is heavy. An evaluation of the techniques for introducing inexperienced bees into the greenhouse does not yet exist and could be subject to an experimental study.

Placement of the nesting substrate, protection from pesticides, and the need for additional food sources such as sugar water feeders or floral nectar supplies have received relatively limited attention. Similar to bumble bees, orientation may be helped through visual or olfactory beacons in a monotonous greenhouse environment (Birmingham and Winston 2004). However, these are secondary issues that are mostly easy to solve once mass breeding and regular supply is in place (e.g. Fig. 3b and c).

Conclusion

A few species of Osmia are commercially available for pollination of protected orchard crops, But there are no solitary bees available for greenhouse pollination. Bumble bees are very satisfactory greenhouse pollinators, and the ability to breed them in captivity has resulted in a reliable supply chain. The main implication of this is that there is little demand or funding for the development of solitary bees for commercial greenhouse pollination. This is further hampered by significant technical difficulties in achieving a reliable and continuous supply of solitary bees, which pertains mainly to breeding at a commercial scale, manipulation of the phenology, and disease control. While overcoming these hurdles would require substantial local investment of funds and research efforts, the development of managed solitary bee species would undoubtedly improve the resilience and sustainability of pollination in greenhouses and other covered growing systems and be of considerable benefit to horticultural industries, particularly in countries without access to commercial bumble bees.
