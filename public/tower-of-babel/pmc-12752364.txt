Remimazolam tosylate vs. propofol: effects on anesthetic efficacy and renal function in living donor kidney transplantation
Authors: Jiaxin Wu, Wendong Lin, Haiying Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRemimazolam Tosylate (RT) is an ultrashort-acting benzodiazepine with organ-independent metabolism, suitable for patients with renal impairment. This study assessed the efficacy and safety of RT in living donor kidney transplantation.MethodsEighty-six patients with uremia who underwent living donor kidney transplantation from May 2024 to October 2024 were randomized into the RT group (group RT) or the propofol group (group P), with 43 patients per group. Induction in group RT was achieved with 0.2 mg/kg RT, followed by maintenance at a constant rate of 1 mg/kg/h. Induction in group P was achieved with 1.5 mg/kg propofol, followed by maintenance at a constant rate of 4–10 mg/kg/h. The primary outcomes were perioperative anesthetic efficacy and postoperative renal graft function. The secondary outcomes included perioperative electrolyte status, incidence of hypotension, and incidence of other adverse reactions.ResultsThe induction success rate was 100% in each group, and neither group required remedial sedative medication. However, the time to loss of consciousness in group RT was longer than that in group P (P < 0.05). Group RT had a longer time to eye opening and extubation compared with group P, but this difference was not significant (P > 0.05). Furthermore, the post-anesthesia care unit stay time for group RT was longer than that for group P (P < 0.05). No statistically significant difference was observed in postoperative renal function indicators or urine output between the two groups (P > 0.05). In addition, no delayed graft function (DGF) was observed in either group. The incidence of hypotension during the induction period in group RT was lower than that in group P (P < 0.05), and injection pain was also lower (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe sedation effect in group RT was not worse than that in group P, with fewer hemodynamic fluctuations, lower injection pain, and without adverse effects on renal function. RT may thus represent a safer and organ-independent alternative for anesthesia in renal transplant recipients.Trial registrationThe study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry at ChiCTR.org.cn (25/04/2024, ChiCTR2400083451).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03569-y.

Introduction

Propofol is the most widely used short-acting sedative in clinical practice. It inhibits histamine release in the hypothalamus by activating central gamma-aminobutyric acid A (GABAA) receptors, blocks N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors, and modulates calcium influx to inhibit postsynaptic neuronal activity [1, 2]. It is a phenolic derivative with high lipophilicity. It swiftly distributes in the central nervous system and other tissues, inducing rapid sedation [3, 4]. Propofol is then quickly redistributed to the peripheral compartments and is primarily metabolized in the liver by glucuronidation and the cytochrome P450 system [5, 6]. The renal clearance is approximately 30% [7], leading to rapid arousal and recovery from sedation. However, its use has certain disadvantages, including dose-dependent cardiorespiratory depression, relatively severe injection pain, metabolic acidosis, hyperlipidemia, propofol infusion syndrome, risk of bacterial contamination, and allergic reactions [8, 9].

Remimazolam Tosylate (RT) is a novel ultrashort-acting benzodiazepine developed in China, with a chemical structure and binding activity similar to those of remimazolam. It acts on the central GABAA receptors, opens the receptor channel, and increases the influx of chloride ions, thus inducing hyperpolarization of the neuronal cell membrane and inhibiting neuronal activity [10, 11]. RT is rapidly hydrolyzed by tissue esterases to the inactive carboxylic acid metabolite zolam propionate (CNS7054). This elimination is not dependent on liver or kidney metabolism [12, 13]. The affinity of its metabolites for the GABAA receptor is significantly weaker [14, 15]. Pharmacokinetic studies showed that remimazolam has high clearance and a small distribution volume [14, 15], resulting in rapid metabolism and low tissue accumulation. Additionally, the effects of remimazolam can be specifically antagonized by flumazenil, enhancing safety and controllability in clinical settings. Previous studies reported that remimazolam produces more stable hemodynamics than propofol, and has a lower incidence of respiratory depression and injection pain [16, 17].

Remimazolam metabolism is independent of both hepatic and renal function, making it particularly suitable for patients with renal insufficiency. Candidates for renal transplantation are typically in an end-stage renal disease (ESRD) state preoperatively, characterized not only by severe impairment of renal function but also by frequent involvement of multiple organ systems and various comorbidities. Impaired renal and hepatic clearance increases the risk of drug accumulation when anesthetics rely on these pathways for metabolism, thereby raising concerns regarding medication safety. At the same time, the presence of multisystem comorbidities places higher demands on perioperative hemodynamic stability to ensure adequate perfusion of vital organs. Furthermore, perioperative hypotension may compromise allograft perfusion, leading to delayed graft function (DGF), in severe cases even resulting in transplant failure. Therefore, anesthetic agents with no inherent nephrotoxicity, metabolism independent of renal function, and minimal circulatory impact are preferred in this population. Remimazolam fulfills these criteria, and its effects can be rapidly reversed by the specific antagonist flumazenil, further enhancing its safety profile in renal transplantation surgery. However, only a few studies have reported the use of remimazolam for the induction and maintenance of general anesthesia in patients undergoing living donor kidney transplantation. Therefore, this study examined the efficacy and safety of RT in living donor kidney transplantation, thereby expanding the available anesthetic options for these clinical scenarios.

Methods

Participants

The inclusion criteria were the following: (1) patients who underwent living donor kidney transplantation for uremia between May 2024 and October 2024 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine; (2) patients aged 18–60 years with a body mass index ≤ 30 kg/m2; (3) patients who voluntarily provided informed consent. All kidneys were voluntarily donated by the patients’ relatives, and no organs were obtained from incarcerated individuals. All living donor nephrectomies were performed at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine by the same team of experienced surgeons.

The exclusion criteria were the following: (1) patients with known hypersensitivity to multiple drugs or a previous history of severe allergy and family history; (2) long-term use of narcotic analgesics, sedatives, or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs; (3) patients with communication barriers; (4) patient with bradycardia, having a heart rate < 50 beats/min; (5) patients with electrolyte disorders and lipid metabolism disorders; (6) absence of dialysis treatment within 24 h before transplantation for those who needed it.

Randomization and Blinding

Simple randomization was used to assign participants to the study groups due to their high degree of homogeneity. Participants were randomly allocated to either the RT group (group RT) or the propofol group (group P) in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated random number table prepared by an independent uninformed individual. Allocation was blinded to patients, surgeons, and the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) staff, all unaware of the research objectives. The anesthesiologists who administered anesthesia were not involved in data collection or analysis.

Anesthesia and Management Methods

Peripheral venous access was established before the patients were taken to the operating room. Their electrocardiogram, heart rate, percutaneous arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2), and noninvasive arterial blood pressure were continuously monitored after admission. Radial artery cannulation was performed under 2% lidocaine (Shaanxi Dunsi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Shaanxi, China) infiltration to enable arterial blood pressure monitoring and arterial blood gas analysis. After the induction of anesthesia, a double-lumen internal jugular venous cannula was placed to monitor central venous pressure.

Adequate preoxygenation with 100% oxygen was performed for 5 min delivered by a mask before the induction of anesthesia. Both groups received remifentanil (Yichang Humanwell Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd, Hubei, China) by continuous intravenous infusion at 0.25–0.5 µg/kg/min and atracurium cisbenesulfonate (Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine Co., Ltd, Jiangsu, China) was administered intravenously at 0.2 mg/kg. Patients in the RT group received an intravenous injection of RT (Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine Co., Ltd, Jiangsu, China) at 0.2 mg/kg, slowly administered over 60 s. Patients in group P received propofol (Xi’an Libang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Xi’an, China) at 1.5 mg/kg, also administered over 60 s (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Anesthesia Process

Anesthesia Process

Infusion rates were adjusted to 1–2 mg/kg/h in the RT group and 4–10 mg/kg/h in group P to maintain anesthesia. Remifentanil was infused at 0.25–0.5 µg/kg/min for analgesia and atracurium cisbenesulfonate was supplemented as needed. The bispectral index (BIS) was maintained between 40 and 60, and vital signs were kept stable throughout surgery (Fig. 1).

All anesthetic agents were discontinued at the end of surgery. After patients awoke naturally, neostigmine (Shanghai Xinyi Jinzhu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) and atropine (Hangzhou Minsheng Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Zhejiang, China) were administered at 0.15 mg/kg and 0.1 mg/kg, respectively, to reverse neuromuscular blockade. If awakening time after RT exceeded 30 min, 0.2 mg flumazenil (Yichang Humanwell Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd, Hubei, China) was administered. Endotracheal extubation was performed once extubation criteria were met. Patients were transferred to the ward meeting the PACU discharge criteria (Aldrete score > 9). Follow up of all patients was performed after surgery (Fig. 1).

Outcome Measurements

The primary outcomes were perioperative anesthetic efficacy and renal graft function. They were evaluated as follows: (1.) time to loss of consciousness (LoC) defined as disappearance of the eyelash reflex and a Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) score < 2; (2.) induction success rate; (3.) time to eye opening, defined as the time interval from cessation of all anesthetic infusions to eye opening in response to verbal commands, and a MOAA/S score > 4; (4.) time to extubation, defined as the interval from cessation of anesthetic infusions to tracheal extubation; (5.) PACU length of stay, defined as the time to achieve an Aldrete score > 9; (6) renal function indicators including creatinine-based estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR-cr), serum creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen, measured on postoperative day 1 (T1), day 3 (T3), day 5 (T5), and month 1 (T6). Additionally, urine output was recorded on postoperative day 1 (T1), day 2 (T2), day 3 (T3), and day 4 (T4). The incidence of DGF, defined as the need for dialysis within 1 week after renal transplantation, was also documented.

The secondary outcomes were the following: (1.) hypotension (a decrease in systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 20% from baseline or mean arterial pressure < 65 mmHg); (2.) hypertension (an increase in SBP ≥ 20% from baseline or SBP > 160 mmHg); (3.) injection pain; (4.) allergic reactions; (5.) bradycardia (heart rate < 50 beats/min); (6.) intraoperative awareness; (7.) delirium; (8.) cardiovascular events; (9.) respiratory depression (respiratory rate < 8 breaths/min or SpO2 < 90%); (10.) postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV); (11.) perioperative changes in blood potassium levels.

Sample Size Calculation

The sedation success rate of remimazolam is comparable to that of propofol. Therefore, the incidence of hypotension was selected to calculate the sample size. According to previous reports, the incidence of hypotension is approximately 10%−20% in the remimazolam group and approximately 40%−70% in the propofol group. Previous studies demonstrated that remimazolam is associated with a lower incidence of hypotension than propofol. From a pharmacological perspective, remimazolam also exerts a weaker inhibitory effect on the circulatory system compared to propofol, which supports its non-inferiority and suggests the possibility of superiority regarding hypotension incidence. A one-sided α value of 0.025 was used to optimize clinical safety. Sample size estimation was performed using PASS 21.0 software, with α and β errors set at 0.025 (one-sided) and 0.2 (power = 0.8), respectively. This calculation yielded a minimum required sample size of 78 patients. Considering an anticipated 15% dropout rate, a total of 91 patients were ultimately enrolled.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 21.0. Normal distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk nonparametric test. Normally distributed data were analyzed using the two-sample independent t-test, whereas non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the nonparametric test. Categorical data were compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. All tests were two-sided. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median plus interquartile range, and categorical data were presented as numbers and percentages. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

RT is a novel ultrashort-acting benzodiazepine with the unique advantages of rapid onset, short duration of sedation, organ-independent metabolism, mild respiratory and circulatory inhibition, less injection pain than propofol, and the availability of a specific antagonist. It has been applied and studied in different clinical settings. In view of the advantages of RT, its efficacy and safety need to be investigated in living donor kidney transplantation and compared with those of other conventional sedatives.

In this study, the induction success rate was 100% in both RT and P groups, and no patients in either group required remedial sedation. This finding is consistent with a meta-analysis by Xing et al. [18], which included 27 randomized controlled trials involving 7,283 patients and the efficacy of remimazolam and propofol for procedural sedation and general anesthesia was compared. No significant difference in sedation or general anesthesia success rate was identified between the two agents. In the present study, the time to LoC was significantly longer in group RT than in group P (P < 0.05), suggesting that propofol exhibited a faster onset of action. A recent meta-analysis of 331 participants from three studies reported that the LoC time was longer in the remimazolam group than in the propofol group [19]. In addition, a study by Luo et al. [20] divided the remimazolam group into three dosing subgroups (a low-dose group given 6 mg/kg/h, a median-dose group given 9 mg/kg/h, and a high-dose group given 12 mg/kg/h) and compared them with the propofol group. Their results showed that the LoC time decreased as the remimazolam dose increased; however, LoC time remained longer in all remimazolam subgroups than in the propofol group [20]. This prolonged LoC may be attributed to the slower achievement of equilibrium between plasma and effect-site concentrations, resulting in a comparatively delayed onset of action [20].

In the present study, both eye-opening time and extubation time were longer in group RT than in group P after discontinuation of anesthetic agents, although these differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). These findings differ partially from those of Luo et al. [20] who reported that extubation time in the high-dose remimazolam subgroup was significantly longer than in the medium-dose subgroup. Although no significant difference was observed between the low-dose remimazolam subgroup and the propofol group, the medium-dose remimazolam subgroup demonstrated longer extubation time compared with propofol. Remimazolam has a longer half-life than propofol, which prolongs its retention time in the body and likely contributes to delayed emergence from anesthesia. A dose–response relationship may also exist between the dose of remimazolam and emergence time, thereby higher doses of remimazolam are associated with longer emergence times. Thus, the absence of significant differences in emergence time reported in some studies may be explained by the use of relatively low remimazolam doses in those protocols.

Although no significant difference in eye-opening time or extubation time was observed between group RT and group P in this study, an important observation was that 10 patients in group RT required flumazenil because their recovery time exceeded 30 min. The sedative effect was rapidly reversed after flumazenil administration. This reversal likely minimized the natural difference in emergence time between remimazolam and propofol, thereby contributing to the absence of statistically significant differences in the recovery-related outcomes. A meta-analysis of Song et al. [21], which compared remimazolam and propofol for induction and maintenance of general anesthesia, similarly found no significant differences in extubation time, emergence time, or PACU duration between the two agents. However, subgroup showed that when flumazenil was not used postoperatively, remimazolam was associated with prolonged extubation time and longer PACU duration [21]. Consistent with this finding, another meta-analysis reported that the combination of remimazolam and flumazenil shortened emergence time, extubation time, and PACU duration compared with propofol monotherapy, and also resulted in a lower incidence of respiratory depression [22].

Flumazenil acts on GABAA receptors, similar to benzodiazepines, and blocks benzodiazepine activity through a competitive mechanism, rapidly reversing the sedative effect and effectively reversing psychomotor and cardiovascular dysfunction [23]. However, since flumazenil is a competitive antagonist, re-sedation may occur, which may pose a safety concern. Supporting this concern, one clinical study reported a 22% rate of re-sedation in patients who received remimazolam, whereas no re-sedation events occurred in the propofol group [24]. Therefore, administration of more than 0.2 mg flumazenil is not recommended. If higher doses are required, close monitoring for at least 1–2 h after remimazolam anesthesia is advised [25].

In this study, patients in group RT had a longer PACU stay than those in group P (P < 0.05), a finding consistent with previous studies [2, 26–30]. The group RT also received a higher intraoperative dose of remifentanil administration than group P. A plausible explanation might be the relatively low sensitivity of the BIS to benzodiazepines, including remimazolam. BIS values in group RT often remained relatively high, which might have been interpreted by anesthesiologists as inadequate anesthetic depth. To prevent intraoperative awareness and ensure sufficient anesthesia, anesthesiologists may have increased the remifentanil dosage accordingly.

Supporting this synergistic relationship, a study by Xie et al. [31] demonstrated that the combination of remifentanil and remimazolam in patients undergoing hysteroscopy shortened the induction time of remimazolam and reduced its required dose, directly confirming that remifentanil can potentiate the sedative effect of remimazolam. Given this synergistic relationship and the higher remifentanil dosage administered in group RT, it is reasonable to infer that patients in group RT experienced deeper intraoperative sedation, which may account for their prolonged PACU stay. In contrast, studies reporting no significant differences in recovery profiles between remimazolam and propofol may have been influenced by two factors: shorter surgical durations and lower or differently administered opioid dosage, thereby reducing observable differences in recovery outcomes [32]. Furthermore, although remimazolam is metabolized independently of hepatic and renal function and follows first-order pharmacokinetics, it has been hypothesized that prolonged administration of high doses may still result in mild drug accumulation.

The BIS values were higher in group RT than in group P in our study and were mostly maintained between 50 and 60. Some studies reported that the BIS value rarely decreased below 50, even with high doses of remimazolam [26, 33]. Another study reported that even when sufficient hypnotic levels for general anesthesia were achieved in the remimazolam group, intraoperative BIS values increased to over 60 [34]. These findings indicate that using the BIS value to accurately monitor the depth of anesthesia during remimazolam administration may not be entirely reliable. BIS evaluates the degree of cortical inhibition by analyzing electroencephalographic (EEG) activity, and was originally developed to monitor the depth of propofol-induced sedation. Previous investigations showed that BIS has low sensitivity to benzodiazepines, as BIS values typically remain above 60 even when high concentrations of benzodiazepines are administered [35]. This reduced sensitivity is largely attributable to the distinct EEG pattern produced by benzodiazepine-induced anesthesia, which is characterized by increased beta activity and can lead to relatively high BIS values. The underlying mechanism may relate to the selective action of benzodiazepines: unlike many anesthetic agents that act on multiple molecular targets, benzodiazepines exclusively act on GABAA receptors. Once these receptors become saturated, further increase in benzodiazepine concentration does not significantly alter processed EEG signals, thereby preventing BIS values from reliably reflecting deeper levels of sedation [33].

Since the study population consisted of uremic patients undergoing living donor kidney transplantation, an objective was to evaluate the effect of sedative agents on perioperative renal function. No significant difference in perioperative blood potassium levels and renal function indicators was observed between group RT and group P. Similarly, urine output during the first four postoperative days (T1–T4) did not differ significantly between the two groups and no cases of DGF occurred in either group. Collectively, these findings indicated that remimazolam did not worsen renal injury compared with propofol, supporting its safety as a sedative agent in kidney transplantation. This conclusion is consistent with the findings of Chen et al. [36], who also reported that remimazolam did not demonstrate a significant advantage over propofol in improving renal function after kidney transplantation, further supporting the “non-inferiority” of remimazolam in preserving renal function.

The incidence of injection pain was significantly higher in group P in our study, consistent with previous reports [37–40]. The chemical structure of remimazolam differs significantly from that of propofol in that it lacks a phenol group, a difference that results in a lower likelihood of injection pain. No significant differences were found in other adverse events such as bradycardia, allergic reactions, respiratory depression, delirium, and PONV between patients in group RT and group P.

Intraoperative hypotension is associated with postoperative organ failure and death [41]. Therefore, minimizing the impact of general anesthetics on the cardiovascular system is important. The blood pressure of patients in group RT was more stable during the induction period than that of patients in group P. Propofol significantly inhibits sympathetic tone [42, 43] and enhances parasympathetic activity [44]. Decreased myocardial contractility, arterial dilation, and venous dilation with reduced venous return may collectively lower systemic vascular resistance, leading to hypotension.

Patients with chronic kidney disease and ESRD exhibit enhanced sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activity [45]. Propofol’s inhibitory effect on the SNS disrupts this compensatory state, further increasing the risk of hypotension. Several studies reported that remimazolam is more effective than propofol in maintaining hemodynamic stability during anesthesia induction. Remimazolam can preserve the balance of sympathetic and parasympathetic activities and does not alter cardiac repolarization [46]. It does not significantly affect cardiac output or systemic vascular resistance, thereby offering better hemodynamic stability [37]. Although both propofol and remimazolam act on GABAA receptors, their pharmacological effects differ substantially. Propofol primarily enhances the function of the GABAA receptor β-subunit, producing pronounced cardiovascular depression. In contrast, remimazolam preferentially binds to the α-subunit, which mediates sedative and anxiolytic effects while exerting minimal impact on the cardiovascular system [47].

As a classic anesthetic agent, propofol benefits from well-established manufacturing processes, stable supply, and a substantially lower cost than remimazolam. Under the anesthetic protocol used in this study, remimazolam resulted in higher intraoperative drug costs, and the need for flumazenil antagonism in some patients further increased overall expenditure. However, remimazolam offers several pharmacological advantages, including a short duration of sedation, minimal respiratory and circulatory depression, organ-independent metabolism, and the availability of a specific antagonist. These features may reduce the need for vasoactive drugs, facilitate faster emergence and recovery, and decrease the frequency of emergency interventions, potentially offsetting some of the additional drug costs by reducing the consumption of other medical resources [48].

Our study had some limitations. First, this was a single-center study with a small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, complete blinding for the trial was not possible due to the difference in the physical properties and color of RT and propofol. Although the anesthesiologists administering anesthesia were not involved in data collection and analysis, drug-use bias cannot be ruled out. Third, the BIS value was used to determine sedation depth; however, it has limitations in accurately reflecting the depth of remimazolam-induced anesthesia, and may not capture the actual level of sedation. Furthermore, the inability to achieve full blinding due to visible differences in drug appearance, may have influenced anesthesiologists to adjust the remimazolam infusion rate intentionally or unintentionally to avoid delayed emergence. Such operational bias could have directly affected drug dosage during anesthesia maintenance and the evaluation of sedative efficacy, thereby compromising the validity of these outcome measures. Fourth, 10 patients in the RT group received flumazenil. As a result, the recorded eye-opening and extubation times did not reflect the true awakening duration following the natural remimazolam metabolism. This intervention introduces bias that undermines the validity and reliability of comparisons involving these recovery outcomes. Fifth, the study did not collect postoperative data on surgery-related complications after kidney transplantation. Since these complications may influence graft function, the absence of such information prevented assessment of their potential impact on perioperative renal indicators and their confounding effect on the comparison of efficacy and safety between the two drugs. This limitation ultimately reduced the comprehensiveness of the study’s conclusions.

Conclusion

Both remazolam and propofol provide satisfactory anesthesia with no adverse effects on perioperative renal function and are suitable for anesthesia in living donor kidney transplants. However, remimazolam demonstrates greater perioperative hemodynamic stability.
