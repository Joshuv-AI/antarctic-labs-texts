Combined albumin-adjusted ischemia-modified albumin ratio and uterine artery Doppler for differentiating early-onset preeclampsia from gestational hypertension: a prospective exploratory pilot study
Authors: Serkan Özbey, Handan Turhan Karakuş, Alper Gümüş, Pakizer Banu Kılıçoğlu Dane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDistinguishing early-onset preeclampsia (EO-PE) from gestational hypertension (GHT) at initial presentation remains challenging, particularly when classical diagnostic features are absent or evolving. We evaluated whether combining the albumin-adjusted ischemia-modified albumin ratio (IMAR) with uterine artery pulsatility index expressed as multiples of the median (UtA-PI MoM) provides complementary information in this setting.MethodsIn this prospective single-center exploratory study, 90 singleton pregnancies (30 EO-PE, 30 GHT, 30 controls) were included. IMAR and UtA-PI MoM were assessed individually and in combination using receiver operating characteristic analysis and logistic regression with bootstrap internal validation.ResultsIMAR and UtA-PI MoM were higher in EO-PE than in GHT (p = 0.003 and p < 0.001, respectively), whereas raw IMA did not distinguish the groups. IMAR demonstrated moderate discrimination (AUC 0.727, 95% CI 0.600–0.854), and UtA-PI MoM demonstrated higher performance (AUC 0.844, 95% CI 0.743–0.946). The combined model showed the highest apparent discrimination (AUC 0.898, 95% CI 0.818–0.977; optimism-corrected AUC 0.889), with 83.3% sensitivity and 86.7% specificity at the exploratory operating point.ConclusionsCombining IMAR with UtA-PI MoM may provide complementary information for distinguishing EO-PE from GHT at initial presentation. This exploratory model is not intended to replace established angiogenic biomarkers and requires validation in larger, multicenter studies before clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09700-2.Keywords: Preeclampsia, Gestational hypertension, Ischemia-modified albumin, Uterine artery Doppler, Uterine artery pulsatility index, Biomarkers, Pilot study

Introduction

Preeclampsia affects approximately 2–8% of pregnancies worldwide [1]. Early-onset preeclampsia (EO-PE) and gestational hypertension (GHT) may present with similar clinical features at initial evaluation, yet they represent biologically and clinically distinct hypertensive disorders of pregnancy with markedly different maternal and perinatal implications [2–4].

Early- and late-onset forms of preeclampsia are also considered distinct clinical phenotypes with different underlying pathophysiological mechanisms [3, 4].

EO-PE, particularly before 34 weeks of gestation, is primarily driven by impaired placentation and is associated with increased risks of severe maternal complications, including eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, and end-organ dysfunction, as well as adverse fetal outcomes such as fetal growth restriction and preterm birth. In contrast, GHT is generally associated with a more favorable prognosis and less prominent placental dysfunction [2, 4, 5].

Early differentiation between these entities is therefore clinically critical, as it directly influences surveillance intensity, admission thresholds, maternal transfer decisions, timing of corticosteroid administration, antihypertensive management, and delivery planning. However, at the time of initial presentation, classical diagnostic features such as proteinuria or overt end-organ involvement may be absent or evolving, creating a clinically relevant diagnostic gray zone in which GHT can initially mimic EO-PE [4–6].

Uterine artery Doppler ultrasound provides a noninvasive assessment of uteroplacental vascular resistance. Elevated uterine artery pulsatility index (UtA-PI) is consistently associated with impaired placentation and has been linked to EO-PE [7–9]. However, evidence syntheses indicate that UtA-PI alone has limited sensitivity despite relatively favorable specificity, suggesting that a purely hemodynamic marker may incompletely capture the maternal systemic component of the disease [7, 9, 10].

Ischemia-modified albumin (IMA) is generated when the N-terminus of albumin is altered under ischemic and oxidative stress conditions and can be measured using the albumin–cobalt binding assay [11, 12]. Meta-analyses have reported higher IMA levels in preeclampsia than in normotensive pregnancies [13, 14], although an individual study found that the IMA/albumin ratio differed between preeclampsia and control groups even when raw IMA did not [15]. However, interpretation of absolute IMA values is limited by their dependence on circulating albumin concentration, which is frequently reduced in preeclampsia due to endothelial dysfunction and proteinuria [12, 16, 17]. To address this limitation, albumin-adjusted indices such as the ischemia-modified albumin ratio (IMAR = IMA/albumin) have been proposed as exploratory measures of systemic ischemia–oxidative burden [12, 18]. Nevertheless, IMAR remains a non-standardized parameter and should be interpreted cautiously as an experimental marker rather than a calibrated clinical assay [12, 18].

Within the two-stage pathophysiological framework of preeclampsia, characterized by uteroplacental malperfusion followed by maternal systemic inflammation, ischemia, and oxidative stress, the combination of a placental hemodynamic marker (UtA-PI) and a systemic marker (IMAR) is biologically plausible [19, 20]. While combinations of uterine artery Doppler with angiogenic biomarkers such as PlGF and sFlt-1 have been extensively studied, the feasibility of combining Doppler-derived hemodynamic parameters with a systemic ischemia marker has not been adequately investigated in the context of differentiating EO-PE from GHT at initial clinical presentation [4, 8].

Recent studies have explored the incremental value of combining uterine artery Doppler with maternal serum biomarkers. Kongprayoon and Phupong reported that combining first-trimester MSPα with uterine artery Doppler increased sensitivity but reduced specificity compared with MSPα alone [21].

Importantly, the existing literature on IMA has largely focused on comparisons between preeclampsia and normotensive pregnancies. Although subgroup analyses by disease onset have been reported [15], the clinically relevant distinction between EO-PE and GHT — the principal differential diagnosis at initial presentation — has not been specifically addressed [13, 14]. This represents a meaningful gap, as GHT is the principal alternative diagnosis in patients presenting with new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation, and the two conditions differ substantially in underlying pathophysiology and clinical trajectory [2, 4].

Accordingly, we conducted a prospective, single-center exploratory pilot study to evaluate the feasibility and preliminary discriminatory performance of IMAR, uterine artery Doppler (UtA-PI MoM), and their combination in distinguishing EO-PE from GHT at the time of initial clinical presentation. In line with STARD 2015 and TRIPOD principles, we report measures of discrimination, calibration, and bootstrap-based internal validation, with emphasis on hypothesis generation and the need for external validation [21, 22].

We hypothesized that combining IMAR and UtA-PI MoM would improve discrimination between EO-PE and GHT compared with either marker alone by integrating complementary information on placental dysfunction and maternal systemic ischemia–oxidative stress.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethics

This prospective, single-center exploratory pilot study was conducted at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Başakşehir Çam and Sakura City Hospital, Istanbul, Turkey, between 1 September 2023 and 31 December 2023. The study protocol was approved by the institutional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (28 August 2023; Decision No: E-96317027-514.10-223034095). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Reporting followed STARD 2015 guidelines and considered TRIPOD recommendations for exploratory model development and reporting [21, 22].

Participants and clinical definitions

Eligible participants were women aged 18–45 years with singleton pregnancies evaluated for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy after 20 weeks of gestation. Participants were recruited prospectively and consecutively between 1 September 2023 and 31 December 2023 until the prespecified target of 30 participants had been reached in each diagnostic category: early-onset preeclampsia (EO-PE, n = 30), gestational hypertension (GHT, n = 30), and normotensive controls (n = 30). The final analytical cohort therefore comprised 90 participants.

The primary analytic cohort consisted of EO-PE and GHT (n = 60), reflecting the clinically relevant diagnostic comparison at the time of initial presentation. The control group was included for contextual interpretation of biomarker and Doppler distributions.

Clinical definitions followed ACOG 2020 criteria [4]. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg on two measurements at least 4 h apart. Proteinuria was defined as a spot urine protein/creatinine ratio (UPCR) ≥ 300 mg/g (0.3 mg/mg). EO-PE was defined as preeclampsia meeting diagnostic criteria before 34 + 0 weeks of gestation. GHT was defined as new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation without proteinuria or systemic findings supporting preeclampsia. Clinical classification was based on the clinical status at presentation, when diagnosis, blood sampling, and Doppler assessment were performed.

All inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied during the pre-enrollment eligibility assessment. Ineligible candidates were excluded at the screening stage; no enrolled participant was excluded after recruitment. A detailed screening log of all candidate participants assessed but not enrolled was not maintained; the flow diagram therefore presents participants who were prospectively enrolled and analysed after eligibility assessment, rather than the total number screened.

The two-week interval used for active infection or recent inflammatory illness was a pragmatic window prespecified in the protocol to reduce recent inflammatory confounding; it was not considered a validated IMA-specific washout period. Similarly, exclusion after antenatal corticosteroid administration was used to obtain pretreatment measurements and minimise potential metabolic or inflammatory effects, not because a direct effect on IMA had been established.

Clinical measurements and timing

Maternal blood pressure was measured using a validated automated device after at least 5 min of rest in a seated position with an appropriately sized cuff at heart level.

Peripheral venous blood sampling and uterine artery Doppler examination were performed during the same clinical visit (within 24 h) and prior to initiation of antihypertensive therapy.

Uterine artery Doppler ultrasound

Uterine artery Doppler examinations were performed using a standardized protocol by a single experienced operator. The operator was not aware of the IMA/IMAR results at the time of Doppler acquisition.

Right and left uterine arteries were assessed at the level where the uterine artery crosses the external iliac artery, under appropriate imaging conditions and during fetal quiescence. After at least three consecutive and similar waveforms had been obtained, pulsatility indices were measured for both uterine arteries, and the bilateral mean UtA-PI was used for analysis.

To account for gestational-age-related physiological variation, raw UtA-PI values were converted to multiples of the median (MoM) using published reference equations [23].

No separate formal intra-observer or inter-observer repeatability analysis was performed in this study; therefore, intra-observer or inter-observer ICC/CV values are not reported. Performing all measurements by a single experienced operator using the same protocol was intended to improve within-study consistency, but it does not constitute quantitative confirmation of measurement repeatability.

Biochemical analyses and IMAR calculation

Venous blood samples were obtained at clinical evaluation and prior to antihypertensive therapy. Samples were centrifuged after collection; separated serum samples were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Each sample underwent only one freeze–thaw cycle before analysis.

Serum ischemia-modified albumin (IMA) concentrations were measured using a commercial colorimetric albumin–cobalt binding assay (Rel Assay Diagnostics, Gaziantep, Türkiye) on an automated analyzer (Selectra) and reported in absorbance units (ABSU). Serum albumin levels were measured using the bromocresol green method and reported in g/L.

The ischemia-modified albumin ratio (IMAR) was calculated as IMA (ABSU) divided by serum albumin (g/L). IMAR was used as an exploratory adjustment to account for the dependence of IMA measurements on circulating albumin concentration and was interpreted as a non-standardized parameter [12, 18].

Laboratory personnel performing biochemical analyses were blinded to clinical and Doppler data. Clinicians responsible for diagnosis and management were not aware of IMA/IMAR results during patient care.

No sample was recorded as haemolysed, and no participant was excluded because of laboratory sample quality. However, no independent intra-assay coefficient of variation, inter-assay coefficient of variation, limit of detection, or duplicate-measurement/repeatability analysis was performed for IMA in this study. Therefore, analytical repeatability could not be quantified within the present dataset.

Sample size rationale

Sample size was determined a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) [24] for one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) across three independent groups: early-onset preeclampsia (EO-PE), gestational hypertension (GHT), and normotensive controls. A three-group design was selected to permit comparison of biomarker behavior across the clinical spectrum of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.

Because directly comparable effect size estimates for the specific EO-PE versus GHT comparison were not available, the anticipated effect size was informed by previous studies reporting differences in IMA and/or albumin-adjusted IMA indices between preeclampsia and control groups, including analyses of early- and late-onset phenotypes [13–15]. On this basis, a conservative medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.40) was assumed [25]. With a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 80% (1 − β = 0.80), the minimum required total sample size was calculated as 66 participants (22 per group).

A formal sample size calculation for logistic regression based on recently proposed criteria for clinical prediction models [26] was not performed because no prior effect size estimates were available for the specific combination of IMAR and UtA-PI MoM. To improve statistical precision and to account for potential data loss or unusable measurements, the target sample size was increased to 90 participants (30 per group). For the primary binary analysis comparing EO-PE and GHT, logistic regression models were restricted to two predictors (IMAR and UtA-PI MoM). With 30 EO-PE events, this provided an events-per-variable ratio of 15, exceeding commonly recommended minimum thresholds for exploratory multivariable modeling [27, 28].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests; Welch ANOVA was used when variance homogeneity was not met. Post-hoc comparisons used Tukey HSD or Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.0167).

Diagnostic discrimination between EO-PE and GHT was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with area under the curve (AUC) and 95% confidence intervals. ROC analyses were performed using a nonparametric approach. Optimal cut-off values were explored using the Youden index, and 95% confidence intervals for sensitivity and specificity were calculated using the exact binomial method. Correlated ROC curves were formally compared using the DeLong method.

Binary logistic regression models were constructed for EO-PE versus GHT: Model 1 (IMAR), Model 2 (UtA-PI MoM), and Model 3 (combined model). All logistic regression models were fitted using the Enter (forced-entry) method. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and model performance was described using Nagelkerke R². The predicted-probability threshold of 0.50 used for apparent classification accuracy was prespecified as a conventional modelling threshold because the groups were balanced and no differential misclassification cost was assumed. This threshold was used only to report within-sample classification indices and was not proposed as a clinical decision threshold.

Likelihood-ratio testing was used to evaluate whether adding IMAR to the UtA-PI MoM model improved overall model fit. Internal validation was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples to estimate optimism in model performance, including optimism-corrected AUC, calibration slope, and calibration intercept [29]. Calibration was further assessed using calibration plots. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance (VIF < 5; tolerance > 0.10).

To explore possible gestational-age dependence, Spearman correlation analyses and linear regression analyses were performed for IMA, albumin, and IMAR according to gestational age at sampling. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were calculated for correlation coefficients. A post hoc detectable-effect-size assessment was performed for correlation analyses; with n = 90, the study had approximately 80% power at a two-sided α = 0.05 to detect correlations of approximately |r| ≥0.29. To explore potential confounding by gestational age in the primary diagnostic model, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed with gestational age included as a covariate.

Clinical variables, biochemical measurements, uterine artery Doppler parameters, and all variables used in the primary analyses were complete for all 90 enrolled participants. No participant was excluded after enrollment because of missing data or sample quality. Analyses were therefore performed using a complete-case approach. Because no data were missing, no imputation, weighting, or missing-data sensitivity analysis was performed.

A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

The datasets generated and/or analyzed during the current study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

No participant was excluded after enrollment because of missing data, haemolysis, or laboratory sample-quality concerns. The primary EO-PE versus GHT comparison included 60 complete cases, and three-group comparisons included all 90 complete cases. During the period of in-hospital observation or clinical evaluation, none of the 30 participants classified as having GHT progressed to meet the diagnostic criteria for preeclampsia. Therefore, no diagnostic reclassification was required during the observed period.

Correlation analyses among biochemical and Doppler parameters are presented in Supplementary Table S1A. IMAR was strongly correlated with IMA and moderately inversely correlated with albumin. IMAR was also positively correlated with UtA-PI MoM. In contrast, IMAR showed no statistically significant correlation with gestational age at sampling (ρ = −0.065, p = 0.543; bootstrap 95% CI − 0.274 to 0.150; Supplementary Table S1B). Detailed bootstrap validation results are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Similarly, neither IMA nor albumin showed a significant gestational-age association. Corresponding correlation analyses restricted to the primary disease cohort (EO-PE + GHT, n = 60) are presented in Supplementary Tables S1C and S1D; in this cohort, gestational age showed a weak negative correlation with IMAR (ρ = −0.267, p = 0.039) and a moderate negative correlation with bilateral UtA-PI (ρ = −0.422, p < 0.001). No multicollinearity was observed between IMAR and UtA-PI MoM in the combined model (VIF 1.010; tolerance 0.990; Supplementary Table S3).

In the primary EO-PE versus GHT discrimination analysis, IMAR showed moderate diagnostic performance with an AUC of 0.727 (95% CI 0.600–0.854). At the Youden-based threshold of ≥ 1.85, IMAR yielded 63.3% sensitivity and 76.7% specificity. UtA-PI MoM demonstrated higher discrimination, with an AUC of 0.844 (95% CI 0.743–0.946), 83.3% sensitivity, and 80.0% specificity at the optimal threshold. The combined IMAR + UtA-PI MoM model achieved the highest apparent AUC of 0.898 (95% CI 0.818–0.977), with 83.3% sensitivity and 86.7% specificity at a predicted-probability threshold of 0.52 (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Discussion

This prospective single-center exploratory pilot study evaluated the preliminary discriminatory performance of combining an albumin-adjusted ischemia marker (IMAR) with gestational-age-standardized uterine artery Doppler (UtA-PI MoM) to distinguish early-onset preeclampsia (EO-PE) from gestational hypertension (GHT) at initial clinical presentation. The combined model demonstrated the highest apparent AUC among the evaluated approaches. However, the AUC increase over UtA-PI MoM alone was not statistically significant in the DeLong comparison, and the model has not been externally validated. These findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Positioning relative to established angiogenic biomarkers

This approach is not intended to replace established angiogenic biomarkers such as sFlt-1, PlGF, or the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio, which are established tools in the evaluation of suspected preterm preeclampsia [30, 31]. Because sFlt-1, PlGF, and the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio were not measured in the present study, the relative diagnostic performance of IMAR + UtA-PI MoM compared with angiogenic biomarkers could not be assessed. The current data therefore do not support the conclusion that IMAR is equivalent or superior to established angiogenic biomarkers, or that it provides incremental clinical value beyond them.

The performance metrics observed for the combined IMAR + UtA-PI MoM model overlap with performance ranges reported for sFlt-1/PlGF in the literature [32, 33]. Nevertheless, this observation is indirect and should not be interpreted as comparative evidence. Unlike established angiogenic biomarkers, IMAR reflects a less disease-specific systemic ischemia–oxidative stress pathway and currently lacks standardized analytical thresholds [12, 18]. PAPP-A has mainly been evaluated as a first-trimester placental biomarker within multiparametric preeclampsia screening strategies [34], whereas oxidative-stress biomarkers have been studied using heterogeneous assays and study designs [14]. IMAR should be considered an experimental and potentially complementary biomarker that requires direct comparison with angiogenic markers in future studies.

Complementary pathophysiological information

IMAR and UtA-PI MoM may reflect different but potentially complementary biological domains. UtA-PI MoM provides information on uteroplacental vascular resistance and placental perfusion, whereas IMAR may reflect the maternal systemic ischemia–oxidative stress axis. Their combination is therefore biologically plausible within the two-stage framework of preeclampsia. However, biological plausibility does not establish clinical utility, and the present findings require validation before they can be considered for clinical use.

Context within existing literature

Previous studies have evaluated combinations of uterine artery Doppler with serum biomarkers, with results varying according to the biomarker and performance metric considered [35, 36]. However, these studies have largely focused on preeclampsia versus normotensive controls or on prediction models. In contrast, the present study specifically addresses the clinically relevant differentiation between EO-PE and GHT at the time of initial presentation. To our knowledge, the combination of an albumin-adjusted ischemia marker with uterine artery Doppler has not previously been evaluated in this diagnostic context.

Gestational age considerations

Gestational age at sampling differed between the EO-PE and GHT groups. This difference is not only a methodological issue but also reflects the natural clinical and biological distinction between these two entities. EO-PE, by definition, occurs before 34 weeks and is frequently associated with more pronounced placental dysfunction, whereas GHT is clinically more heterogeneous and often diagnosed at later gestational ages. Gestational age and disease phenotype are therefore biologically intertwined, and complete separation of these effects is not possible in the present exploratory sample.

We addressed this issue through complementary analyses. First, raw UtA-PI values were converted to MoM values using published reference equations to reduce gestational-age-related physiological variability [23]. Second, in the overall cohort, IMAR showed no statistically significant correlation with gestational age at sampling. However, this should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of gestational-age independence, because the sample size and gestational-age distribution limited the ability to detect weak associations. Third, the gestational-age-adjusted sensitivity analysis yielded results directionally consistent with the primary model. Both IMAR and UtA-PI MoM remained associated with EO-PE after adjustment, while gestational age did not reach statistical significance. Nevertheless, residual gestational-age-related confounding cannot be excluded.

Biological rationale and analytical limitations of IMAR

IMA measurements are influenced by circulating albumin concentration, which is frequently reduced in preeclampsia as a result of endothelial dysfunction, proteinuria, and altered maternal physiology [12, 16, 17]. In addition, serum albumin concentrations change physiologically across gestation [37]. Because albumin constitutes the denominator of IMAR, gestational changes in albumin may influence the ratio independently of changes in IMA. The use of IMAR provides an exploratory adjustment for this dependency and may better reflect systemic ischemic burden [12, 18]. However, IMAR is not an internationally standardized biomarker. Absolute values may vary across assays, analyzers, kits, and laboratories, and the thresholds reported here should not be used as clinical decision thresholds.

Internal validation and model stability

Bootstrap internal validation suggested only modest optimism in the present dataset, with an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.889 and an optimism-corrected calibration slope of 0.916. However, internal validation does not establish generalizability. The sample size was small, the number of outcome events was limited, and the logistic regression estimates had wide confidence intervals. Therefore, the model remains at risk of overfitting, and its apparent performance should be considered hypothesis-generating until confirmed by external validation in independent cohorts.

Clinical implications

The differentiation of EO-PE from GHT at initial presentation can be challenging, particularly in patients with new-onset hypertension who have absent, borderline, or evolving proteinuria or maternal organ dysfunction. In such a diagnostic gray zone, a combined hemodynamic and biochemical assessment could be worth investigating as a future risk-stratification tool. In the present exploratory dataset, the combined model showed numerically higher specificity than UtA-PI MoM alone at similar sensitivity, suggesting fewer false-positive EO-PE classifications among patients with GHT. However, this finding does not demonstrate benefit in clinical decision-making, and the AUC increase over UtA-PI MoM alone was not statistically significant. The model should therefore be regarded as research-only and should not be used to guide admission, transfer, corticosteroid administration, surveillance intensity, delivery timing, or any other clinical management decision.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was modest and the primary diagnostic comparison included only 30 EO-PE and 30 GHT cases. This limits statistical precision, contributes to wide confidence intervals, and means that model overfitting cannot be excluded despite bootstrap internal validation. Second, the single-center design may introduce selection bias related to local referral patterns, disease-severity distribution, clinical management protocols, laboratory conditions, and sampling practices. The prespecified balanced-group design also does not reflect real-world clinical prevalence; therefore, the positive and negative predictive values and clinical applicability of the model in routine populations cannot be inferred directly.

Third, IMAR lacks international assay standardization, and this study did not include independent intra-assay CV, inter-assay CV, limit-of-detection, or duplicate-measurement analyses for IMA. Fourth, although all Doppler measurements were performed by a single experienced operator using a standardized protocol, formal intra-observer and inter-observer repeatability analyses were not performed. Fifth, no direct comparison with sFlt-1, PlGF, or the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio was available. Sixth, residual confounding related to gestational age or disease heterogeneity cannot be excluded. Seventh, the study did not include systematic post-discharge follow-up; therefore, GHT cases that may have progressed to preeclampsia after discharge could not be identified. Finally, late-onset preeclampsia and advanced severe phenotypes such as HELLP syndrome or severe fetal growth restriction were not specifically evaluated. All reported thresholds are exploratory and should not be applied in clinical practice.

The UPCR distribution also affects generalizability. The EO-PE group included several patients with proteinuria close to the diagnostic threshold rather than exclusively severe proteinuric disease. Therefore, the findings may be most applicable to an early or less overt EO-PE phenotype and should not be generalized directly to patients with advanced severe preeclampsia without external validation. Proteinuria alone does not fully define preeclampsia severity, and the cohort should not be interpreted simply as a mild preeclampsia cohort on the basis of UPCR distribution alone.

Strengths

Strengths include the prospective design, standardized timing of biochemical and Doppler assessments before antihypertensive treatment, blinding of laboratory personnel and clinicians to IMA/IMAR results during patient care, complete data capture, and adherence to STARD and TRIPOD reporting principles. The use of a parsimonious two-predictor model, formal ROC comparison, likelihood-ratio testing, and bootstrap internal validation further supports methodological transparency.

Future directions

Future studies should include larger, consecutive, independent, and preferably multicenter cohorts with standardized biomarker protocols and external validation. IMAR, UtA-PI MoM, sFlt-1, PlGF, and the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio should be measured concurrently in the same participants. Future work should assess not only AUC but also calibration, reclassification, decision-curve analysis, net clinical benefit, and clinically meaningful decision thresholds. Analytical validation of IMA/IMAR, including standardized sample handling, documented quality-control procedures, intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation, repeat measurements, and inter-laboratory method comparison, is also required. Longitudinal repeated measurements should be used to evaluate dynamic changes in IMAR and their relationship with disease progression and maternal-neonatal outcomes.

Conclusions

This exploratory prospective pilot study suggests that combining the albumin-adjusted ischemia-modified albumin ratio with uterine artery Doppler (UtA-PI MoM) may provide complementary information for distinguishing early-onset preeclampsia from gestational hypertension at initial clinical presentation.

However, given the modest sample size, limited precision of regression estimates, lack of assay standardization, absence of angiogenic-biomarker comparison, and lack of external validation, these findings are hypothesis-generating and not suitable for clinical application. Further multicenter studies with standardized methodologies are required to validate these results and define whether this approach has any incremental clinical utility.
