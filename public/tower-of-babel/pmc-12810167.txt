Introduction

In India, the prevalence of dental caries ranges from 27% to 64% in children up to 12 years old and from 26% to 83% in adults.1 Dental caries is a multifactorial disease resulting from a complex interplay of factors, including oral microorganisms within dental plaque, dietary practices, and host-related determinants such as genetic predisposition, immune response, and various socioenvironmental influences.2

Although routine oral hygiene practices such as toothbrushing and flossing are effective in caries prevention, the complete eradication of Streptococcus mutans from anatomically challenging sites—such as pits, fissures, and interdental areas—remains difficult. The adjunctive use of chemotherapeutic agents, particularly antimicrobial mouth rinses, in combination with mechanical cleaning methods, has been shown to significantly reduce bacterial load.3,4

Mouth rinses serve a critical role in the management of oral microbial populations and the inhibition of plaque accumulation.5 These formulations are generally classified into chemical and herbal categories. Among chemical agents, chlorhexidine is considered the gold standard due to its broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and effectiveness against S. mutans. However, long-term use of chlorhexidine has been associated with several adverse effects, including mucosal irritation, increased calculus formation, altered taste sensation, and extrinsic staining of teeth and oral mucosa, limiting its applicability, especially in pediatric populations.1,6 These limitations underscore the necessity for developing alternative antibacterial mouth rinses that offer similar efficacy with a more favorable safety profile.

According to the World Health Organization, approximately 80% of the population in developing countries relies on traditional medicines and natural products to meet their primary healthcare needs. In India, commonly used herbal agents include Ocimum tenuiflorum (tulsi), triphala—a polyherbal formulation consisting of Emblica officinalis (amalaki), Terminalia bellirica (bibhitaki), and Terminalia chebula (haritaki) in equal proportions, and Aloe barbadensis Miller (aloe vera). These herbal agents have demonstrated notable antimicrobial properties, particularly against S. mutans, a key pathogen implicated in the etiology of dental caries.7,8

Currently, there are no studies directly comparing these herbal agents. Therefore, the present study was undertaken to evaluate and compare the antimicrobial efficacy of tulsi leaf extract, triphala powder, and aloe vera with that of 0.2% chlorhexidine mouth rinse.

Null Hypothesis

There is no difference between tulsi, triphala, aloe vera, and chlorhexidine in inhibiting the growth of S. mutans.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This in vitro experimental study was conducted following approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (Protocol Reference Number: 22032).

Sample Size Determination

A total sample size of 48 (n = 12 per group) was calculated for this comparative study. The sample size was determined to achieve a statistical power of 80% to detect a significant difference in the mean zone of inhibition among the groups, with a 95% confidence interval and an expected effect size of 4 mm.9

Accordingly, the study groups were as follows:

Group I: Ocimum tenuiflorum (tulsi) extract.

Group II: Triphala extract.

Group III: Aloe barbadensis Miller (aloe vera) extract.

Group III: Aloe barbadensis Miller (aloe vera) extract.

Group IV: 0.2% Chlorhexidine mouth rinse.

Each group consisted of 12 samples.

Preparation of Extracts

Ocimum tenuiflorum (tulsi) extract (4%)

Coarse O. tenuiflorum powder was obtained from the Department of Dravyaguna, Banaras Hindu University (BHU), Varanasi, India. Fresh leaves, cultivated in the department's medicinal plant garden, were separated from the stems, thoroughly washed, and sun-dried for 7 days or until adequately desiccated. The dried leaves were then pulverized into a fine powder using an electric grinder. A total of 300 grams of powdered leaves were macerated in 1 L of 100% ethanol and stored in a round-bottom flask for 5 days, with intermittent shaking. The macerated mixture was initially filtered through sterile muslin cloth and then through Whatman No. 1 filter paper to obtain a clear extract. The filtrate was concentrated via low-temperature evaporation (below 60°C), yielding 18 gm of extract, corresponding to a 6% w/v yield. To prepare a 4% tulsi extract solution, 3 gm of the extract was dissolved in 50 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The final solution was stored in sterilized, amber-colored bottles and labeled accordingly.10,11

Triphala extract (6%)

Triphala powder was procured from the pharmacy of the Department of Dravyaguna, BHU, and prepared by trained laboratory personnel. The formulation consisted of the dried fruit pulp of E. officinalis, T. bellirica, and T. chebula, sourced and processed according to the Ayurvedic Formulary of India. The seeds were removed, and the remaining pulp was ground into a coarse powder. The three powders were combined in equal proportions (1:1:1) to produce Triphala powder. A total of 60 grams of this powder was dissolved in 1000 mL of double-deionized water, brought to a boil, and then filtered. After cooling, 50 mL of the solution was transferred into amber-colored storage bottles for use.12

Aloe barbadensis Miller (aloe vera) extract (7%)

Fresh, mature aloe vera leaves were washed with distilled water, and the spines were removed. The outer epidermal layer was peeled away using a sterile scraper to isolate the inner mucilaginous gel, which was then collected and transferred into a sterile container containing 200 mL of 100% ethanol. The final extract was prepared by combining aloe vera gel (7 gm), benzyl alcohol (0.2 gm), and Milli-Q water to make up a total volume of 100 mL. The solution was stored in sterilized amber-colored bottles for further use.13

Antibacterial Activity Assay9,11

The antibacterial activity of the test substances was evaluated using a standard strain of S. mutans (MTCC 497/ATCC 25175), obtained from the Microbial Type Culture Collection and Gene Bank (MTCC), Chandigarh, India. The strain was preserved in sterile vials containing 1 mL of defibrinated blood and stored at −18°C. Prior to experimentation, the bacterial stock was thawed, and 0.1 mL of the suspension was inoculated into brain heart infusion (BHI) medium, followed by incubation at 37°C for 24 hours to obtain an active culture.

The antibacterial efficacy of the extracts was assessed using the agar well diffusion method. Blood agar plates (90 mm diameter) were prepared and uniformly inoculated with 100 µL of the S. mutans culture. The inoculum was spread evenly over the agar surface by streaking in three directions, rotating the plate approximately 60° between each pass, and including the plate periphery to ensure uniform distribution.

Four wells (6 mm diameter, 4 mm depth) were aseptically punched into each plate at equidistant positions. Each well was filled with 100 µL of the respective test solutions.

The plates were incubated aerobically at 37°C for 24 hours. Following incubation, the diameter of the zones of inhibition was measured using a millimeter scale (Fig. 1). All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Results

The zones of inhibition reflected varying degrees of antibacterial activity against S. mutans across all test groups (Table 1). Group I (tulsi) showed inhibition zones ranging from 11 to 12 mm, with a mean ± SD of 11.0 ± 0.6 mm. Group II (triphala) demonstrated slightly smaller zones, ranging from 8 to 10 mm, and a mean ± SD of 8.6 ± 0.7 mm. Group III (aloe vera) exhibited the least activity among the herbal extracts, with zones between 6 and 8 mm and a mean ± SD of 7.0 ± 0.6 mm. In contrast, group IV (chlorhexidine) showed the largest zones of inhibition, ranging from 21 to 24 mm and a mean ± SD of 22.3 ± 0.8 mm. Overall, chlorhexidine displayed the highest antimicrobial efficacy, followed by tulsi, triphala, and aloe vera, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed a statistically significant difference among the four groups (F = 1036.180, p < 0.001), indicating substantial variability in the mean zones of inhibition (Table 2). The between-group sum of squares was 1713.229 with 3 degrees of freedom (df), yielding a mean square of 571.076. The resulting F-value of 1036.180 indicates a substantial difference among the groups. The within-group sum of squares was 24.250 with 44 df, resulting in a mean square of 0.551. The total sum of squares was 1737.479 with 47 df. The highly significant p-value (p < 0.001) suggests a meaningful difference in the mean scores of the dependent variable across the treatment groups.

Intergroup Comparison

The intergroup post hoc analysis (Table 3) of the mean zone of inhibition among the three herbal mouthwashes and chlorhexidine revealed a significant difference in antibacterial efficacy, with chlorhexidine consistently outperforming all herbal formulations. When comparing tulsi (group I) with chlorhexidine (group IV), a mean difference of −11.333 ± 0.310 mm was noted, indicating a significantly larger inhibition zone for chlorhexidine. Similarly, triphala (group II) showed a mean difference of −13.667 ± 0.341 mm, once again favoring chlorhexidine. The most pronounced difference was observed between aloe vera (group III) and chlorhexidine, with a mean difference of −15.250 ± 0.321 mm, further emphasizing chlorhexidine's superior antibacterial activity across all comparisons. The differences between the groups were statistically significant (p = 0.000). Among the three herbal groups, group III (aloe vera) exhibited the highest mean difference (−15.250 ± 0.321), while group I (tulsi) had the lowest (−11.333 ± 0.310).

Intragroup Comparison

The intragroup analysis among the three herbal extracts—tulsi, triphala, and aloe vera—demonstrated statistically significant differences in their antibacterial efficacy (Table 4). A comparison between tulsi (group I) and triphala (group II) revealed a mean difference of 2.333 ± 0.284 (p = 0.000), indicating a significant variation in the zones of inhibition. When tulsi was compared with aloe vera (group III), an even greater mean difference of 3.917 ± 0.260 (p = 0.000) was observed, suggesting a more substantial disparity between these two groups. Lastly, the comparison between triphala and aloe vera showed a statistically significant mean difference of −1.583 ± 0.296 (p = 0.000), with triphala demonstrating superior antibacterial activity.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate and compare the antibacterial efficacy of three herbal extracts—aloe vera, O. tenuiflorum (tulsi), and triphala—against S. mutans, a key etiological agent in dental caries, with 0.2% chlorhexidine serving as the gold standard control.

Among the herbal agents, tulsi demonstrated the most significant antibacterial activity, as evidenced by the largest mean inhibition zone (11.0 ± 0.603 mm), followed by triphala (8.6 ± 0.7 mm) and aloe vera (7.08 ± 0.6 mm). Chlorhexidine exhibited the highest inhibition zone (22.33 ± 0.8 mm), with a statistically significant difference observed between it and all experimental groups (p = 0.000).

Streptococcus mutans was selected as the test organism due to its central role in dental biofilm formation and caries pathogenesis. Its acidogenicity and aciduric properties enable its survival in low-pH environments, contributing to demineralization of tooth enamel by disrupting the dynamic balance between remineralization and demineralization.14,15

The agar well diffusion method was utilized over disc diffusion due to its efficacy in testing semi-solid and liquid plant extracts, and it minimizes interference from particulate matter and enhances the diffusion of antimicrobial components, especially in aqueous or ethanolic suspensions. This technique also offers operational simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and improved reproducibility.16

Chlorhexidine's superior performance is attributable to its biphasic antimicrobial action—initial bactericidal effect followed by prolonged bacteriostatic activity, owing to its substantivity, which allows sustained release from oral surfaces for up to 24 hours.17

Ocimum tenuiflorum, locally called tulsi, is an aromatic shrub from the basil family, Lamiaceae (tribe Ocimeae). It is believed to have originated in north-central India and is now growing naturally throughout the tropical regions of the Eastern world.18 It demonstrated the highest antibacterial activity among the herbal extracts evaluated. In Ayurveda, tulsi is revered as “The Incomparable One,” “Mother Medicine of Nature,” and “The Queen of Herbs.” Celebrated as an “elixir of life,” it is unmatched for its powerful medicinal and spiritual properties.19

In India, tulsi has long been integrated into spiritual rituals and daily lifestyle practices, offering a wide range of health benefits that modern science is only beginning to validate.20

Studies have shown that tulsi possesses a unique combination of therapeutic properties, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antipyretic, antiallergic, and immunomodulatory effects, among others.21–23

Its bioactive constituents—eugenol, ursolic acid, caryophyllene, and carvacrol—are known for their potent antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties. The antibacterial action of tulsi is attributed to multiple mechanisms, including disruption of bacterial membrane integrity, inhibition of key enzymatic pathways, and interference with nucleic acid function.24–26 An ethanol-based extraction method was employed to enhance the solubility of essential oils, while dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) served as a neutral diluent to mitigate the inherent antimicrobial properties of ethanol.10,11 Furthermore, studies such as those by Singhal et al.27 have proposed additional mechanisms, including the biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles.

Triphala, a classical Ayurvedic polyherbal formulation composed of E. officinalis, T. bellirica, and T. chebula, has been widely recognized for its antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties. Its antibacterial efficacy is primarily attributed to phenolic constituents such as gallic acid, chebulagic acid, and tannic acid, which exert their effects by disrupting bacterial cell membranes and inhibiting biofilm formation.24 In the present study, triphala exhibited moderate antibacterial activity against S. mutans. Although its mean zone of inhibition was significantly smaller than that of chlorhexidine and O. tenuiflorum (tulsi), it was notably greater than that of aloe vera (p = 0.000). Prior in vivo investigations by Jagdish et al.28 and Bajaj and Tandon29 have demonstrated that triphala can achieve antibacterial outcomes comparable to chlorhexidine, particularly in reducing bacterial load and enhancing gingival health. Given its safety profile, affordability, and accessibility, triphala presents a viable alternative for long-term oral hygiene, especially in underserved populations lacking access to conventional dental care.

Aloe barbadensis Miller, commonly known as aloe vera, is a succulent plant resembling a cactus. It belongs to the family Liliaceae and is found across regions of Asia, Africa, and other tropical areas. Contains a diverse array of pharmacologically active constituents, including lupeol, salicylic acid, urea nitrogen, cinnamic acid, phenols, and sulfur, which collectively contribute to its reported antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties.30 Despite this, aloe vera demonstrated minimal antimicrobial efficacy against S. mutans in the present study. Discrepancies in reported antibacterial activity across the literature may be attributed to variations in extraction protocols, plant maturity, geographic origin, and postharvest processing techniques. Notably, aloe-derived polysaccharides are particularly susceptible to degradation by oxidation, heat, and enzymatic activity, all of which may compromise their biological activity.31,32 Kurian et al.33 reported an inhibition zone of 14.09 mm for S. mutans with aloe vera, whereas other studies—such as those by Yavagal et al.34 and Sreekumar and Hegde35 have not observed any zone of inhibition, while Subramaniam et al.31 noted negligible antibacterial effects, particularly at lower concentrations.

Limitations

Variability in herbal composition: Phytochemical content in medicinal plants varies depending on factors such as geographical location, harvest time, and seasonal variation, potentially affecting extract potency and reproducibility.

In vitro design: While in vitro studies offer controlled environments for initial screening, they do not replicate the complex physiological and microbial dynamics of the oral cavity. Factors such as salivary flow, pH, diet, and host immunity are critical determinants of antimicrobial efficacy in vivo.

Conclusion

All three herbal extracts—tulsi, triphala, and aloe vera—demonstrated antibacterial activity against S. mutans, although significantly less than 0.2% chlorhexidine.

These findings suggest that while chlorhexidine remains the most effective agent, tulsi and triphala may serve as promising adjuncts or alternatives in herbal oral hygiene formulations, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Clinical Significance

As the plant extracts demonstrated a notable efficacy in inhibiting the proliferation of oral bacteria, this suggests that these extracts could be formulated into an herbal antibacterial mouth rinse option suitable for individuals seeking herbal alternatives.
