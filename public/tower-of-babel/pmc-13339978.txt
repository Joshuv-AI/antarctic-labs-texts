Targeting the 5-HT1A receptor in the ventral hippocampus alleviates anxiety-like behavior in mice with angiotensin II-induced hypertension
Authors: Wenjun Chen, Zhoucai Luo, Mingfeng Huang, Xiaoqi Zheng, Nating Xiong, Caiyan Gan, Jianlin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypertension, a chronic condition, has affected > 1 billion people worldwide and is linked to a heightened risk of anxiety disorders. Despite this association, the exact mechanisms underlying the connections between hypertension and anxiety disorders remain largely elusive. In this research, we observed that systolic blood pressure (SBP) increased in mice subcutaneously administered with angiotensin II (Ang II) alongside signs of heightened anxiety. Furthermore, immunofluorescence and electrophysiological analyses revealed that Ang II enhanced neuronal activity and altered the synaptic activities of ventral CA1 (vCA1) neurons. Meanwhile, in vivo recordings showed that these neurons in Ang II-treated mice were significantly more responsive to anxiety-inducing stimuli. Additionally, chemogenetic inhibition of neural activity in vCA1 effectively alleviated anxiety-like behavior in the treated animal. Moreover, the expression of the serotonin receptor 5-HT1A receptor (5-HT1AR) in vCA1 was suppressed under hypertension. Activating these receptors with the agonist 8-OH-DPAT effectively modified neuronal excitability and reduced anxiety-related behavior in mice. These findings elucidate the critical role of vCA1 neural activity and 5-HT1AR dysregulation in hypertension-associated anxiety, highlighting potential therapeutic targets for managing this condition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01583-2.

Introduction

Hypertension is the leading preventable risk factor for cardiovascular disease and all-cause global mortality [1]. It is a widespread chronic condition, impacting > 1 billion adults worldwide, and contributing significantly to the global health burden, including ~ 9% of disability-adjusted life years and > 9 million annual deaths [2]. Despite the availability of effective diagnostic tools and antihypertensive medications, blood pressure control remains suboptimal, with < 14% of individuals achieving the target of < 140/90 mm Hg [3, 4].

Anxiety disorders are notably prevalent among hypertensive individuals, representing an apparent comorbidity [5]. Recent clinical studies have extensively documented the association between hypertension and the onset of anxiety disorders [6–8]. The occurrence of such disorders was greater in hypertensive patients than in the general population [9]. This increased susceptibility to anxiety may stem from the physiological effects of hypertension or from the challenges in achieving effective treatment outcomes [10]. Prospective analyses have demonstrated a marked correlation between generalized anxiety disorder and elevated SBP [11]. While such studies underscore a robust link between hypertension and anxiety, the precise neural mechanisms underlying hypertension-induced anxiety remain largely unexplored.

Regarding hypertension, a dysregulated hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis has been established as a potential contributor to structural and molecular changes within the hippocampus [12]. The ventral hippocampus (vHPC) primarily controls stress responses and mood, whereas the dorsal part is mainly linked with perceptive (cognitive) roles. Recently, vHPC has been highlighted as a crucial neural substrate in modulating anxiety-related behavior [13–16]. Neuroimaging of rhesus monkeys and humans demonstrated that enhanced anterior hippocampus activity correlates with sustained anxiety [17, 18]. Anatomically, the vHPC preferentially links with limbic structures such as the amygdala, hypothalamus, and prefrontal cortex [19–21]. This connectivity positions the vHPC as a hub for translating emotional stimuli into adaptive behavioral responses. Concerning anxiety disorder, vCA1, a subregion of vHPC, is an extensively studied brain region. vCA1 neurons displayed anxiogenic environment stability, necessary for avoidance behavior [22]. Recent pharmacological and optogenetic studies have indicated the maneuverability of vCA1 [21, 23–26]. Thus, vHPC plays a critical role in bidirectionally modulating anxiety levels. However, it remains unclear whether elevated blood pressure modulates vHPC neuron activity.

The neurotransmitter 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT; serotonin) plays a germane role in various pathological and physiological processes, notably in the modulation of anxiety [27]. Lab rats with amphetamine withdrawal exhibited diminished stress-induced 5-HT production in the vHPC, correlating with increased anxiety-like behavior [28]. Conversely, elevating 5-HT levels in the vHPC of rodents produces anxiolytic effects [29]. The brain expresses seven families of 5-HT receptors, i.e., 5-HT1–HT7, encompassing 14 subtypes[30]. Gonadal hormones can influence anxiety-like behaviors in female rats, followed by modulations in 5-HT receptor expression—specifically, a rise in 5-HT2AR and a reduction in 5-HT2CR mRNA levels within the vHPC[31]. Furthermore, simultaneously disrupting 5-HT1AR and 5-HT1BR impairs neurogenesis in vHPC, a process essential for mood regulation[32]. Stimulating 5-HT1AR is linked with reduced anxiety levels in rats[29], whereas activating postsynaptic 5-HT2CR in the vHPC induces anxiogenic effects[33]. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of 5-HT receptors in vHPC in modulating anxiety-related states.

Angiotensin II (Ang II) is integral to vasoconstriction and elevating blood pressure. It is a principal hormone of the renin–angiotensin system (RAS)[34]. This study identified the ventral CA1 (vCA1) region of the hippocampus as pivotal for the manifestation of Ang II-induced, hypertension-associated, anxiety-like behaviors in a mouse model. Our findings revealed that the vCA1 neurons were activated during hypertensive states and that the targeted inhibition of vCA1 pyramidal neurons alleviated anxiety-like behaviors in hypertensive mice. Furthermore, our study demonstrated that the expression of 5-HT1AR in the vCA1 declined under hypertension. Application of a 5-HT1AR agonist attenuated the activation of the vCA1 pyramidal neurons and effectively suppressed anxiety levels in hypertensive mice. These results may provide a basis for developing a new therapeutic approach for hypertension-induced mood disorders.

Materials and methods

Animal housing and growth conditions

For this study, three-month-old male C57BL/6 J mice were grown under controlled environmental conditions. The housing was maintained at room temperature, with water and food provided. An approximate 12—h/12—h light and dark cycle was implemented, with lights turned on at 8:00 a.m. All experimental procedures adhered to the ethical and professional standards established by the Chinese Council on Animal Care. The mice were carefully acclimated through gentle handling for at least 3 days to mitigate the stress associated with manipulation. All behaviors were then assessed only during the light phase of the circadian cycle. Our dedicated efforts were focused on minimizing any potential distress experienced by the mice.

Sustained delivery of Ang II by implanting osmotic pumps

The osmotic pumps for sustained Ang II (#05—23—0101, Sigma-Aldrich, MI, USA) delivery were surgically implanted under anesthesia, utilizing 5% isoflurane in O2, followed by maintenance at 1.5% via a face mask. After disinfecting the interaural skin region with 70% ethanol, a small incision was made to create a subcutaneous pocket in the lower back. An ALZET Model 2006 osmotic pump (Durect Corporation, CA, USA), prefilled with Ang II solution, was inserted into this pocket. The incision was then closed using surgical staples and treated with Marcaine solution to provide local analgesia. Each pump was calibrated to deliver Ang II or saline at 1 μg/kg/min for 28 days, ensuring continuous administration[35].

Blood pressure measurement

The blood pressure was determined using a noninvasive tail-cuff plethysmography system (Kent Scientific Co., CT, USA). The mice were acclimated to it for 1 week before surgery. Subsequently, the blood pressure was recorded weekly until study completion. To ensure accurate measurements, animals were placed in a designated room for ≥ 1 h prior to data collection for environmental acclimation. Each animal underwent about ten acclimation cycles, followed by ten consecutive measurements at every time-point. This protocol ensured at least three valid blood pressure readings per session.

Behavioral tests

Open-field-test (OFT)

For evaluating exploratory behavior and anxiety-like responses, each mouse was introduced into a square, open-field arena, 40 × 40 × 30 cm. It was centrally illuminated with a 650 lx light source to create a well-lit environment. The mice were allowed free movement for 30 min. Their movement patterns, including the period spent in the central zone, were determined using the EthoVision XT video tracking software, version 11.5 (Noldus Information Technology, the Netherlands; https://noldus.com/).

Elevated-plus-maze (EPM) test

For this test, two open and closed arms, measuring 30 × 5 × 0.5 cm and 30 × 5 × 15 cm, respectively, were arranged in a “ + ” configuration [36]. They were linked by a central platform measuring about 5 × 5 cm. Next, a 650-lx light source was positioned above the maze to enhance avoidance behavior. Each mouse was individually kept in the middle of the apparatus and permitted to explore undisturbed for 5 min. Next, using an automated camera system, their behavior was recorded and analyzed using the EthoVision XT video tracking software (https://noldus.com/ethovision-xt).

Novelty suppressed feeding (NSF) test

NSF was conducted as previously described [23]. Following 24 h of fasting, during which water was provided freely, the mice were introduced into a well-lit open arena lined with clean wood chip bedding. A 90 mm diameter filter paper was positioned at the center, upon which a familiar food pellet, ~ 5 g in weight, was placed. The mice were removed and kept in a holding cage for 30 min before the experiment. Subsequently, they were positioned in one corner of the testing arena. The experiment concluded when the mice initiated feeding. The latency period before the onset of feeding behavior was recorded. Immediately following the test phase, the animals were moved to their respective housing cages to assess food intake over 5 min.

Forced swim test (FST)

FST was conducted following established protocols [37]. Under standard illumination, individual mice were gently placed in water, 15 cm deep, ensuring that their tails or hind limbs did not contact the bottom of the cylinder. The duration of the entire experiment was 6 min. A camera positioned alongside the cylinder captured the behavior of the mice throughout the test. The analysis focused on the duration of immobility during the final 4 min post-submergence, as well as the latent period to the initial immobility episode. For behavioral analyses, 1–2 experimenters were blinded due to the prior experiences of the mice. This operational definition aligns with the established protocols in FSTs, where such minimal movements are not considered active behavior.

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

It was performed per previously established protocols, encompassing a two-day habituation period, a one-day water deprivation phase, and finally a one-day testing phase [38]. During the initial two days, the mice were acclimatized to two bottles containing drinking water, which were replaced with two bottles of 1% sucrose for an additional day. Following habituation to any specific bottle, the preference was evaluated. Mice exhibiting no inherent inclination (25%–75%) were selected for subsequent testing. The mice then underwent a 24—h water deprivation period. For the testing phase, they were presented with two bottles containing either water or 1% sucrose for 2 h in darkness, usually at 7:00 PM. Thereafter, the position of the bottles was switched after 1 h. The preference for sucrose was then determined by calculating the ratio of sucrose intake to the total intake of sucrose and water over a 2—h period.

Immunofluorescence

To visualize neuronal activation, brains were collected 90 min after the completion of the EPM test. Therefore, the c-Fos data reflect neuronal activation induced by the behavioral testing experience, not a basal condition. Mice were euthanized with 50 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital. They were transcardially perfused first with PBS and later with 4% methanol in 0.1 M PBS. The extracted brains were fixed in 4% methanol for 6 h, and then in 30% sucrose. The samples were cryoprotected until they sank. A VT1200S freezing microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) was employed to section the coronal brain (40 µm thick) before washing with PBS + 1% Triton X-100, and incubating overnight at 4 °C with a primary c-Fos (1:3,000; sc—52; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA) rabbit poly-clonal antibody, goat serum (sc—52-G), and secondary antibodies. After three PBS washes, sections were mounted on clean glass slides, DAPI-treated, and an LSM880 fluorescence microscope (Zeiss) was utilized for imaging. Coronal brain sections containing the vCA1 were selected at anteroposterior (AP) coordinates approximately -3.16 mm to -3.52 mm from bregma, according to the Paxinos atlas. For each animal, c-Fos-positive cells were counted in three non-consecutive sections spanning AP range.

Brain-slice electrophysiology

Slice preparation

Following anesthesia, the mice were decapitated, and their brains were swiftly extracted. They were placed in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF), comprising 2.5 mM KCl, 220 mM sucrose, 10 mM glucose, 1.3 mM CaCl2, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 2.5 mM MgSO4, and 26 mM NaHCO3. Using a VT 1000S vibrating microtome (Leica, Germany), 300 μm horizontal slices were prepared. They were positioned in a recovery chamber maintained at 32℃. The chamber was filled with a recording solution consisting of 126 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4,1 mM MgSO4, 10 mM glucose, and 2 mM CaCl2. Subsequently, the slices were moved to the compartment (recording chamber), where they imbibed the solution at 3 ml/min.

Electrophysiological experiments

As our previously described [39], in a continuously perfused ACSF, brain slices for electrophysiological recordings were kept in a chamber and allowed to imbibe the recording solution at 2 ml/min at 32–34℃. Next, using an Axioskop2 infrared differential interference contrast (IR-DIC) microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), whole-cell, mostly patch-clamp recordings were performed on the vCA1 neurons. The recording pipettes, pulled with a P-97 micropipette puller (Sutter Instruments, CA, USA), had a resistance of 3–6 MΩ.

Biocytin was added to the internal solution to visualize the pyramidal neuron morphology [40, 41]. This solution contained- 110 mM K-gluconate, 10 mM EGTA, 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl₂, 0.3 mM Na-GTP, 2 mM Mg-ATP, 40 mM HEPES, and 0.1% biocytin; pH 7.3; and an osmolarity of 280–300 mOsm.

To assess intrinsic excitability, the neurons were injected with depolarizing currents. The recording pipettes were filled with solution comprising- 18 mM NaCl, 133 mM K-gluconate, 0.6 mM EGTA, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM Mg-ATP, 0.3 mM Na-GTP, and 1% biocytin; pH 7.2; and an osmolarity of 280 mOsm. The current was increased stepwise by 20 pA (–60 pA to + 120 pA) to evaluate the neuronal firing responses. Resting membrane potential is measured in current-clamp mode after establishing a stable whole-cell configuration. Action potential threshold was measured as the change in voltage from rest at which the slope = 20 V/s. Rheobase is the minimal depolarizing current required to elicit an action potential. Input resistance was calculated from the change in voltage elicited after a 50 ms 5 mV hyperpolarizing step from -70 mV (the last 10 ms of the step from baseline was taken as △V). Peak amplitude measured as change in voltage from threshold to peak.

Pipettes containing the internal solution were used to assess the intrinsic excitability and to record the sEPSCs. The neurons were voltage-clamped at 70 mV to isolate the excitatory synaptic events. For spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSCs), a CsCl-based internal solution was used, containing- MgCl₂, 140 mM CsCl, 1 mM CaCl₂, 10 mM EGTA, 2 mM ATP, 10 mM HEPES-CsOH, and 5 mM QX-314. Cell samples were held at 0 mV during sIPSC recordings.

Stereotaxic Injection and Intracerebral Infusions

Stereotaxic injections of the brain utilized a stereotactic apparatus (RWD, Shenzhen, China) while the subjects were anesthetized through an intraperitoneal administration of 50 mg/kg pentobarbital. Next, a total of 100 nl of the viral solution, AAV9-CaMKIIα-hM4Di-mCherry or AAV9-CaMKIIα-mCherry (#BC-0150; Brain Case, Biotech Co. Ltd., Guangdong, China), was delivered into the vCA1 (lateral: 3.6 mm; anterior: − 3.0 mm; and ventral: − 3.5 mm relative to the Bregma) via calibrated glass microelectrodes, linked to an Nanoliter 2010 Injector infusion pump (World Precision Instruments, FL, USA), at a consistent flow rate of 50 nl/min. The injection pipette remained in situ at the target site for ~ 10 min post-infusion to mitigate the risk of viral overflow.

Following surgery, the mice were returned to their home cages for recovery. Behavior was assessed 7 days after cannula placement. Intra-vHPC injections were administered after removing the dummy cannula utilizing a C315I/SPC infusion cannula (Plastics One Inc., CT, USA).

Fiber photometry recordings

The vCA1 of the mice was administered with a viral vector, AAV-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m (#BC-0082; Brain Case, China), to record calcium signals. Next, an optical fiber (200 μm outer diameter and a 0.37 numerical aperture), encased within a ceramic ferrule, was accurately positioned within the vCA1 via craniotomy. The mice were then cohoused for two weeks for recovery and viral expression. Fluorescent signals were captured, and an additional optical fiber was employed to facilitate light transfer between the optical fiber and the photometry system. To mitigate the risk of photobleaching, the laser intensity at the tip of the optical fiber was carefully calibrated, and a low setting of 20–40 μW was employed.

During the EPM test, mice were placed in the center region of the maze. In order to collect more events of entries into the open arms, mice were allowed to explore the EPM for 10 min according to previous study [22]. The time when the mouse entered the open arm was defined as an effective entry if it lasted no less than 10 s. Mice that entered the open arm at least 4 times with effective entries during the 10-min recording were included in the statistics. The photometry data were exported to MATLAB R2016b as.mat files for further analysis. The dataset was segmented according to the distinct behavioral events observed within each trial. The fluorescence changes (△F/F) were computed using F − F0/F0. F0 is the average baseline fluorescence value over a 10—s control period. The resultant △F/F values were illustrated as average plots, with a shaded region denoting the standard error of the mean (SEM), or presented as heatmaps depicting signal variations across trials. Initially, the data was divided based on behavioral occurrences and baseline phases to ascertain the average response and truncate the duration employed for calculating the △F/F values. The average fluorescence signal was subsequently calculated for the baseline and event phases.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Following intra-vHPC injections, the mice were euthanized via decapitation. The hippocampus was promptly removed and kept in sterile tubes. Next, total RNA was extracted from the ventral hippocampus (vHPC), and the relative gene expression levels were calculated. Table 1 shows the sequences of the specific primers used.

Western blotting

The vCA1 was meticulously dissected and lysed utilizing a homogenizing buffer composed of 50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 50 mM sodium fluoride, 1 mM sodium vanadate, 1 mM dithiothreitol, along with protease inhibitor cocktails, all adjusted to a pH of 7.4. The resulting lysates underwent centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 25 min at a temperature of 4 °C, followed by a boiling step in a loading buffer for a span of 10 min. A total of 40 μg of the samples were then applied to a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel, where electrophoresis was conducted, and the proteins were subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA). Prior to the introduction of primary antibodies, the membranes were incubated in Tris-buffered saline supplemented with 0.1% Tween-20 and 5% milk for one hour at ambient temperature. Primary antibodies, specifically rabbit monoclonal anti-5-HT1AR (1:500; Santa Cruz Biotechnology)[42] or monoclonal mouse anti-GAPDH (1:1000; Bostor, China), were added for an overnight incubation at 4 °C. Following this, the membranes were treated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (GE Healthcare) and analyzed using the Quantitative Imaging System Fluor ChemSP (Alpha Innotech). The resulting protein bands were evaluated utilizing FluorChem™ SP software, with optical densities (ODs) normalized against the OD values of the corresponding GAPDH present on the same membrane.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 8.2 (https://www.graphpad.com/) was used for data analysis. A one-tailed paired Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, and two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test were applied for comparison.

To evaluate anxiety-like behaviors and locomotor activity, the OFT and the EPM were employed. In the OFT, Ang II-treated mice spent less time within the central area relative to the controls (Fig. 1D, E); however, total locomotor activity remained unchanged (Fig. 1F). Similarly, during the EPM, mice receiving Ang II explored the open arms less and with a lower frequency of open-arm entries compared with the saline-treated group (Fig. 1G–I). At the same time, compared with the saline-treated group, Ang II-treated mice spent more time in the closed arms and had a higher frequency of entry into the closed arms (Fig. 1J, K). Chronic Ang II infusion can impair spatial learning and memory functions in rodents [43, 44]. To discern if the behavioral changes observed were attributable to anxiety rather than impairment of spatial learning and memory, the NSF test was performed, which assessed anxiety independent of exploratory behavior. The findings revealed that Ang II-infused mice had a prolonged latency to initiate feeding compared with the saline-treated ones (Fig. 1L), without marked variations in overall food consumption (Fig. 1M). Although clinical studies have suggested a correlation between the increased risk of depression and hypertension [6, 45], our findings did not reveal any depressive behaviors in Ang II-treated mice, as evidenced by performance during the FST and SPT (Fig. S1A, B). Collectively, these findings indicate that chronic Ang II infusion induced hypertension in mice accompanied by anxiety-like behavior, without affecting body weight or inducing depression-like symptoms.

Ang II-induced excessive neural activation in vCA1 of mice

The vCA1 region of the brain is intricately associated with anxiety-related behaviors and processes [15]. Therefore, we investigated the impacts of Ang II infusion on the neural activity within the vCA1. c-Fos is a widely recognised molecular marker of neuronal activation, with enhanced manifestation during responses to various stimuli, including neuronal depolarization [46]. A significant elevation in c-Fos expression within the vCA1 region of the hippocampus of Ang II-treated mice compared with the saline-treated controls indicated enhanced neuronal activity (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, we found Ang II infusion had no effect on the c-Fos expression within the dosal hippocampus, including dorsal CA1, dorsal CA2, dorsal CA3 and dorsal DG (Fig. S2A, B). A previous study indicated that the projection from vCA1 to the lateral hypothalamic area (LHA) is enriched in anxiety cells and can rapidly control anxiety-related behavior [22]. To further investigate the functional implications of such an enhancement, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings on the vCA1 pyramidal neurons. The pyramidal neurons were identified by their morphology using biocytin staining (Fig. 2B). Electrophysiological assessments revealed that Ang II-treated mice exhibited heightened intrinsic excitability of the vCA1 pyramidal neurons (Fig. 2C). In addition, we also test other intrinsic membrane properties of the vCA1 pyramidal neurons, including resting membrane potential, threshold, rheobase, input resistance, and peak amplitude of action potential (Fig. 2D-H). Our findings reveal that Ang II-treated mice exhibited lower threshold (Fig. 2E). To determine synaptic alterations, we recorded spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) and spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSCs) in vCA1 neurons from saline and Ang II-treated mice. We found that the frequency of sEPSCs significantly increased in Ang II-treated mice compared to those in saline mice (Fig. 2I-K). Given that only changes in sEPSC frequencies but not amplitudes were observed, we also recorded neuronal activity from other vHPC regions CA3, which releases glutamate in the CA1. Our results demonstrated that mice treated with Ang II showed a heightened intrinsic excitability of the vCA3 pyramidal neurons when compared to the control mice (Fig. S2E). Meanwhile, the amplitude and frequency of sIPSCs greatly decreased in Ang II-treated mice (Fig. 2L-N).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that choronic Ang II treatment elevated neuronal activity and excitability in the vCA1 pyramidal neurons of mice, characterized by augmented excitatory and reduced inhibitory synaptic inputs. These alternations may contribute to the anxiety-like behavior observed in mice with Ang II-induced hypertension.

Pyramidal vCA1 neurons of Ang II-treated mice were more responsive during EPM

We subsequently recorded the vCA1 pyramidal neuron activity in vivo. We injected vCA1 with the adenovirus-associated vector (AAV) under the control of a CaMKIIα promoter, AAV-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m virus and recorded the fluorescence signal utilizing fiber photometry (Fig. 3A). Such neurons in the mice of both groups showed enhanced activity when matched with the mice that entered the open arms; these neurons were more responsive in Ang II-treated mice compared to controls during the EPM task (Fig. 3B-D). Moreover, when mice moved from the central area into the closed arm, mice treated with Ang II showed no significant reduction in neuronal activity in the vCA1 than the control group (Fig. 3E-G). Discussion

The association between hypertension and anxiety disorders has been well-established through various studies. Nonetheless, the precise neural mechanisms by which elevated blood pressure contributes to anxiety remain insufficiently defined. In this study, using a mouse model, we demonstrated that hyperactivity in the pyramidal neurons located within the vCA1 region of the hippocampus is a key contributor to hypertension-associated anxiety symptoms. Further, such hypertensive mice exhibited reduced levels of 5-HT1AR within the vCA1. Stimulation of such a receptor with a selective agonist effectively reduced neuronal hyperactivity and attenuated anxiety-related behavior in hypertensive mice.

A rise in blood pressure is often linked with alterations in mood, hinting at a possible interplay between cardiovascular and emotional health. Hypertension and anxiety co-occur frequently, with anxiety affecting nearly 33% of hypertensive patients [50]. Moreover, anxiety can also enhance blood pressure levels [51]. Hypertensive mice displayed increased anxiety-related behaviors[43, 44]. In line with these findings, the antihypertensive agent candesartan reduced anxiety symptoms in rodent models, as demonstrated by elevated plus maze tests [52]. Our current findings post-continuous Ang II infusion corroborate these observations, as the treated mice developed hypertension and displayed behavioral signs of anxiety. Specifically, while clinical studies have indicated a correlation between an increased risk of depression and hypertension [53–55], our study did not observe any depression-like behaviors in the Ang II-treated mice. This may be attributed to the relatively short duration (4 weeks) of Ang II administration, which might not be sufficient to induce the chronic neuroadaptive changes typically associated with depression.

The vHPC plays a pivotal role in emotional regulation and stress processing. Dysfunction in this region has been closely linked with psychiatric conditions such as anxiety disorders [13, 15, 56]. Changes in the excitability of neurons within the vHPC significantly influence anxiety-related outcomes [57–59]. Hypertension is known to disrupt the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, potentially altering hippocampal functions and structure [12]. In our study, mice exposed to long-term Ang II infusion showed anxiety-like behaviors and elevated neuronal firing in the vCA1 region. This finding aligns with those of earlier studies that associate vCA1 neuronal activation with aversive emotional responses, including anxiety [21–23]. Moreover, when we selectively suppressed vCA1 pyramidal neurons via chemogenetic techniques, anxiety-like behaviors in hypertensive mice were notably reduced, implicating this neural population in the anxiety phenotype. vCA1 subregion serves as a hub for integrating emotional stimuli via connections with the amygdala, hypothalamus, and prefrontal cortex [19–21]. These changes disrupt the local excitatory/inhibitory balance, likely amplifying output signals to downstream limbic structures such as the LHA, where we also observed increased c-Fos expression. In addition to neuronal contribution, astrocytes and microglia in the vCA1 have been implicated in anxiety regulation [60, 61]. As hypertension adversely affects glial cell function [62, 63], future studies should investigate the influence of hypertension on glial dynamics within this brain region.

The 5-HT1AR subtype of the serotonin receptor family plays a critical role in anxiety regulation. In our experimental model, we observed markedly suppressed 5-HT1AR mRNA and protein expression in the vHPC of Ang II-treated mice. Ang II may reduce 5-HT1AR expression through an indirect hormonal pathway involving the HPA axis and glucocorticoid feedback, or a direct intracellular signaling pathway involving ROS and MAPK. Ang II activates AT1 receptors in the hypothalamus, stimulating CRF release, which then triggers glucocorticoid secretion. Glucocorticoids bind to glucocorticoid receptors in the hippocampus, acting as transcriptional repressors to downregulate 5-HT1AR receptor mRNA expression, an effect reversible by adrenalectomy or glucocorticoid replacement [64]. Concurrently, Ang II activates NADPH oxidase, increasing ROS production. These ROS act as secondary messengers that activate the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, which can modulate transcription factors and further suppress 5-HT1AR receptor gene transcription [65–67].

HT1AR primarily couples with Gi/o proteins, decreasing adenylate cyclase activity and intracellular cAMP levels [68]. Prior studies have highlighted the ability of 5-HT1AR to inhibit glutamate release, suggesting a role in dampening excitatory signalling [69, 70]. Thus, the downregulation of 5-HT1AR observed in this study may underlie the increased frequency of sEPSCs identified in hypertensive mice. Additionally, mice lacking a functional 5-HT1AR exhibited reduced GABAA receptor expression [71], which may have contributed to the attenuated inhibitory transmission observed in our study. Since 5-HT1AR normally inhibit neuronal activity and modulate glutamate and GABA release [72], their reduction may directly contribute to the observed synaptic imbalances. This reshaping of serotonergic signaling in vHPC impairs its ability to properly process anxiety-related inputs from networks involving the amygdala and prefrontal cortex, while exacerbating output to anxiety-promoting regions like the hypothalamus. Pharmacological activation of 5-HT1AR with 8-OH-DPAT in vCA1 reversed these effects, reducing sEPSC frequency, increasing sIPSC amplitude, dampening neuronal hyperexcitability, and alleviating anxiety-like behaviors in Ang II-treated mice. Thus, Ang II-induced adaptations in vHPC, through 5-HT1AR downregulation, alter the function of anxiogenic brain networks by making vCA1 neurons hypersensitive to emotional stimuli and strengthening their anxiety-driving outputs to connected regions. Although the involvement of ventral hippocampus 5-HT1AR in anxiety has already been shown by multiple groups [29, 73]. Our study demonstrated that hypertension-induced anxiety is accompanied by excessive neural activation in vCA1, and the expression of 5-HT1AR in the vCA1 is specifically disrupted under hypertensive conditions. Therefore, targeted regulation of vCA1 5-HT1AR function may help to precisely alleviate the anxiety induced by hypertension.

While prior studies indicate that 8-OH-DPAT may not significantly alter neural excitability in wild-type neurons at 5 μM concentrations [49], our findings show that the same concentration of 8-OH-DPAT did reduce the excitability of pyramidal neurons in the vCA1, which is consistent with other previous studies indicating that 8-OH-DPAT could reduce neural excitability [74, 75]. The reason for this discrepancy may be that the recorded cells were located in different hippocampal brain regions. In addition, the observed reduction in 5-HT1AR expression after repeated angiotensin II treatment raises important questions about the pharmacological responsiveness of vCA1 pyramidal neurons to 8-OH-DPAT. Our findings revealed that even with attenuated responses, significant pharmacological effects was still detectable, highlighting the resilience of serotoninergic pathways.

Furthermore, although our study showed that 8-OH-DPAT can reduce the excitability of neurons in the vCA1 brain region in both saline- and AngII-treated mice, injection of 8-OH-DPAT in the vCA1 did not affect anxiety levels in saline-treated mice. These results are consistent with those reported in previous studies which indicated that the delivery of 8-OH-DPAT in the vCA1 did not induce an anxiolytic effect in normal mice [73, 76]. Based on the described findings, a plausible explanation centers on the differing baseline states of vCA1 neurons in hypertensive versus normotensive mice. Hypertension likely induces a pathological hyperexcitability in vCA1, which may drive anxiety-like behaviors. In this context, 8-OH-DPAT's ability to reduce neuronal excitability acts to restore activity toward a normal physiological range, thereby alleviating anxiety. In normotensive mice, however, vCA1 excitability is already within a normal, stable range. While 8-OH-DPAT can still lower excitability electrophysiologically, this reduction may not cross the threshold required to alter behavioral output, as the system is not in a pathological state. This suggests a state-dependent effect: the behavioral impact of modulating vCA1 excitability is only evident when the network is initially dysregulated, as in hypertension. The underlying mechanism could involve changes in 5-HT1AR sensitivity or altered circuit dynamics specific to the hypertensive condition.

In summary, our findings provide new insights into the pathways by which Ang II-induced hypertension contributes to anxiety by disrupting serotonergic signaling in the vHPC. The observed reduction in 5-HT1AR expression likely plays a vital role by shifting the excitatory/inhibitory balance toward hyperexcitability. However, these effects can be mitigated by the pharmacological enhancement of 5-HT1AR activity. Thus, we highlight the critical involvement of vCA1 neuronal overactivity and reduced 5-HT1AR expression in this pathological state. These findings support the therapeutic potential of targeting serotonergic signaling to alleviate anxiety symptoms in individuals with hypertension.
