Cognitive and Affective Correlates of Handedness: Interactions with Gender Dysphoria and Rumination
Authors: Madhavan H Varadarajan, Durga Rangaswamy Pandian, Jooshika D K, Darshini Madanagopal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe adversities and inheritance patterns of left-handedness have been associated with gender-related distress. Such driving factors predispose individuals to multiple mental health outcomes, shifting attention to the role of ruminative thinking.PurposeThe study seeks to examine the effects of handedness patterns on gender dysphoria and rumination among young adults, observing the intricate relationships among these variables and postulating a substantial influence of handedness on both gender-related distress and rumination in this population.MethodsA cross-sectional design with purposive sampling was used to recruit 180 young adults aged 18–25 years. Study parameters were quantified using three self-report measures: the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory–Short Form, the Utrecht Gender Dysphoria Scale–Gender Spectrum, and the Rumination–Reflection Questionnaire. Data were analysed through moderation tests, correlation tests, and descriptive statistics.ResultsThe results reveal that the links between rumination and gender dysphoria appear to be stronger in left-handed than in right-handed participants. Furthermore, variations in rumination and handedness patterns in the sample contributed to a significant interaction effect (19.1%) in gender dysphoria, moderated by rumination.ConclusionThe study presents a comprehensive perspective for considering and structuring interventions for individuals with gender-related distress, covering major concepts of hormonal and genetic mechanisms, also underscoring the relevance of handedness and rumination in clinical and research settings.Keywords: Gender dysphoria, handedness, rumination

Introduction

Handedness refers to the preferential use of one hand over the other for daily routine tasks, classified into right-handers and left-handers, respectively known as dextral and sinistral, with a small fraction being ambidextrous or mixed-handed individuals. Right-handed individuals make up 90% of the entire population, evenly split between males and females. In contrast, left-handed individuals constitute a minority, comprising the remaining 10%, with a moderate sex difference of 6% men and 4% women. 1

Most handedness studies conclude that the dominant depiction of right-handedness among humans is due to the localisation of language functions to the left hemisphere primarily, as it commands the movements of the right side of the body. 2 The most prominent ‘Right-Shift Theory’ explicates that a genetic influence towards right-handedness is a consequence of one gene with two alleles. 3 One of the alleles is the right-shift allele, which is responsible for the inheritance of right-handedness, whilst the other allele has no directional specialisation, invigorating left cerebral dominance, that is, left-handedness.

Comparably, to describe the continuing distinguishable presence of left-handedness in the population, genetic scientists have elucidated that it is usually manifested as a recessive phenotypical trait among human and animal offspring. 4 The genetic studies on handedness in the early 2000s suggested that the heritable component of left-handedness is estimated at up to 25%–40%.5, 6 Gene models related to parental handedness patterns and their offspring’s handedness indicate a greater incidence of left-handedness when either one or both parents are left-handed. 7

Some earlier neurodevelopmental theories, such as the Geschwind–Behan–Galaburda hypothesis, 8 proposed plausible connections that genetic variation of left-handedness may result from a prenatal shift in testosterone. 8 This shift may slow the development of the primary left hemisphere, paving the way for the right hemisphere to assume equal or predominant control. This, in turn, overrides standard dominance pattern (SDP) lateralisation into atypical cerebral dominance within the primary cognitive functions and abilities of the human brain, potentially extending such patterns to other primates. 9

Geschwind and Galaburda also highlighted that the intrauterine testosterone (androgen) spike during embryogenesis is the probable reason for how and why men are more likely to be left-handed than women. 9 Meanwhile, perusing different explorations on the influence of handedness on other genetic predispositions stated that the correlation between left-handedness and gender identity disorder in boys suggests a possible behavioural indicator. 1

However, these hormonal lateralisation theories remain debated within contemporary neuroscience literature, with inconsistent empirical support across studies. 10 Several researchers have questioned the extent to which cerebral lateralisation or prenatal hormonal influences can adequately explain complex experiences related to gender identity and gender-related distress. 11

Gender dysphoria (GD) details the psychological distress due to the incongruity between one’s assigned sex at birth and affirmed (experienced) gender. 12 Individuals with gender-related distress often display elevated scores of increased androgen levels and masculinity. 13

Returning to the cognitive-affective dimension of this research, both handedness and gender-related distress emerge as determinants that might render detrimental to an individual’s cognitive schemas, that is, ruminative thinking. Rumination can be defined as a vicious cycle of pessimistic thought patterns surrounding past and future experiences. It implies afflicting, over-reflecting and contemplating thought processes, which can stain a person’s perspectives on life and their well-being. 14

Multiple population studies have been conducted to investigate and hypothesise the association between rumination and an individual’s degree of handedness and the consistency of their direction. 15 It is attributed to the behavioural inhibition induced by handedness, which leads to increased levels of state/trait anxiety. 16 Similarly, gender studies were conducted to examine the links between increased levels of rumination in individuals with GD. Trans women ruminate more when compared to trans men and indicate that it is characterised by other psychological symptoms like distress, anxiety and suicidal ideation. 17

Even though the prominent development of individuals’ cognitive and personal identity is witnessed in childhood and adolescence, this article strives to explore the various elements influencing GD among young adults, as they undergo crucial biological and psychological maturation during the phase of young adulthood. Concisely, this research seeks to navigate deeper into the complex interplay of biological, psychological and gender-related factors, thus advancing our understanding of individual differences in gender-related distress and emotional processing. 17

The study’s base framework is on fragmenting gender-related distress and questioning into a psychosocial experience that mostly happens during adolescence or at some other point in life. While distinct from clinically diagnosed GD, the experiences of gender-diverse individuals reflect the feelings of tension, stigma, social roles and interpersonal struggles 18 that arise between their assigned sex at birth and experienced gender. This approach allows for a broader understanding of identity development without conflating an individual’s journey of identity exploration with a clinical underlying condition.18–20

Hence, the study aims to identify potential factors contributing to the occurrence of gender-related distress and rumination. The key hypotheses are as follows:

H1: There is a significant relationship between handedness and GD among young adults.

H2: There is a significant relationship between handedness and rumination among young adults.

H3: There is a three-way interaction between handedness, GD and rumination among young adults.

To summarise, this research navigates through the complex interplay of biological, psychological and gender-related factors, advancing our knowledge of the vast realms of human identity and cognition. Moreover, this research contributes valuable insights into the existing embodiment of knowledge in the spheres of gender psychology and queer psychology.

Methods

Design and Demographics

The study deployed a comparative correlational cross-sectional design with an observational method, recruiting 180 young adults from Chennai, India. Purposive sampling methods were utilised to recruit 180 individuals aged between 18 and 24 years, with equal participation of 90 right-handers and 90 left-handers. The sample size was calculated using G*Power Software (Version 3.1.9.7), with an effect size of 0.403, a desired power level of 0.85, alpha significance at 0.05 and a 1:1 sub-group allocation ratio. Left-handed participants were intentionally oversampled to ensure adequate statistical representation and meaningful comparison across handedness groups. Individuals who had undergone sex reassignment surgeries, were medicated for psychotic illnesses or had physical disabilities in the upper extremities were excluded from participating in this study.

The mean age of the participants was 19.7 (±1.55) years, with a minimum age of 18 years, while the oldest participants were 24 years old. In a sample of 180 young adults, 81 were assigned male at birth (45%), and the remaining 99 were assigned female at birth (55%). The majority of the sample was from upper middle-class backgrounds (73%), from nuclear families (77%), was either first-born or single-born (64%), was heterosexual (76%) and reported not having left-handed relatives in their families (79%).

Measures

The Edinburgh Handedness Inventory–Short Form (EHI–SF) 21 measures and categorises individuals based on their preferences for using their hands to carry out specific and routine activities daily. There are four items in the inventory with a five-point Likert scale, with a score range of −100 (extremely left) to +100 (extremely right). The inventory has a good Cronbach’s coefficient (α) of 0.93, a factor score determinacy is 0.97 and the comparative fit index is 0.98, reflecting that the inventory has exceptional reliability and validity.

The Rumination–Reflection Questionnaire (RRQ) 14 is designed to assess an individual’s tendencies towards repetitive thought patterns, specifically focusing on both ruminative and reflective aspects. It contains 24 statements on a five-point Likert rating scale with two subscales. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (α) is 0.90 and 0.91 for the rumination and reflection subscales, respectively. The mean inter-item correlations for the subscales are 0.43 (Rumination subscale) and 0.48 (Reflection subscale), indicating very good internal reliability and validity.

The Utrecht Gender Dysphoria Scale–Gender Spectrum (UGDS–GS) 22 is a screening tool that evaluates an individual’s distress related to the assigned sex at birth, as well as the comfort of their affirmed gender. The scale has 18 items with a five-point Likert scale from 1 being ‘Completely Disagree’ to 5 meaning ‘Completely Agree’. With two subscales, the Cronbach’s alpha (α) for the Gender Affirmation subscale is 0.91, and for the GD subscale, it is 0.90, showing excellent internal consistency. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were done on the subscales of this scale, indicating that both the subscales have an adequate fit of items with very high convergent and discriminant validity.

Procedure

The data collection process was carried out by the researchers through face-to-face interactions in a university setting, with sessions lasting less than 20 min. The instruments comprised information sheets on participants’ demographics (age, birth sex, socio-economic status, sexual orientation, family structure and presence of left-handed relatives) together with three reliable and validated psychometric measures.

After providing written consent, they filled out the EHI–SF, rating four activities on a five-point Likert scale about their hand dominance patterns in everyday situations. Following it, they would complete the UGDS–GS about their gender-identity distress in the past 6 months, consisting of 18 statements rated on a five-point Likert scale. At last, they would fill out the rumination subscale of the RRQ, with 12 statements rated on a five-point Likert scale. The measures were provided in English only.

Data Analysis

Google Sheets were used for data management. All the data were anonymised, and confidentiality was maintained throughout the period of the study. All data analyses were performed on the Jamovi software (Version 2.7.26). Appropriate statistical analyses such as moderation, chi-square proportions, correlations, t-tests and descriptive statistics were conducted to analyse the data collected.

Discussion

Our findings have proved the study’s aim to explore and investigate the purported influence of an individual’s handedness on their gender identity and cognitive schemas among young adults. The study establishes robust and significant insights into the scientific perspectives of their genetic predispositions being triggered by various situational factors. The article provides valuable revelations from the study’s findings, which present a notable interaction between handedness and rumination in understanding the severity and the occurrence of gender-related distress.

The observed interaction effect (Table 5) of 19.1% between handedness and rumination suggests that left-handed individuals exhibited elevated scores of GD. One of the explanations of this effect might be based on the prenatal shift in androgen levels, inducing non-typical dominance of the right cerebral hemisphere in the human brain,8, 9 in other words supporting the viable aetiology of left-handedness.

Whereas contemporary perspectives increasingly emphasise psychosocial and minority stress frameworks in understanding gender-related distress.18–20 Experiences such as stigma, discrimination, identity-related stress, social invalidation and emotional vulnerability may contribute substantially to psychological distress among gender-diverse individuals.18, 19 Rumination may also function as an emotional regulation process shaped by interpersonal and environmental stressors rather than solely by neurodevelopmental mechanisms. 23

These findings align with previous research suggesting that rumination can exacerbate psychological distress and may interact with other factors to influence mental health outcomes. 18 Individuals who ruminate increasingly exhibit higher levels of GD (Table 3), and this effect appears to be more pronounced among left-handed individuals. Leaning back to the role of handedness in influencing psychological well-being and cognitive functioning, our results demonstrated significant differences in both GD and rumination among individuals with left-handedness and even with those who have left-handed relatives (Table 4). These findings corroborate prior studies suggesting that handedness may be associated with various cerebral and affective processes.2, 4, 7, 24

The study provided additional insights into the differences between right- and left-handed individuals (Table 3) concerning GD and rumination. Left-handed individuals exhibited higher levels of both gender-related distress and rumination compared to their right-handed counterparts. This finding is consistent with the notion that left-handedness may be linked to a greater vulnerability to certain psychological processes. 17

Moreover, the correlations (Table 1) revealed significant relationships between handedness, GD and rumination. Rumination exhibited a moderate negative correlation with handedness, indicating that higher levels of rumination were associated with left-handedness.15, 24 Additionally, GD showed a low positive correlation with rumination, further supporting the notion that rumination may contribute to the experiences of gender-related distress. 17

The demographic distribution of the study sample (Table 2) provides insight into differences in the distribution of demographic variables between left- and right-handed participants, which could potentially influence the observed relationships. It was observed that left-handedness as a trait was more pronounced in participants with left-handed relatives present in their families. Bisexuality is more noticed in left-handed participants than right-handers; this might occur due to the incongruity with the SDP of lateralisation. 25

Figure 1 contributes to our understanding of the relationship between handedness, GD and rumination among young individuals. The prenatal androgen exposure theory models, which explain the biological sex difference observed in the population of left-handers and are a precursor of diverse sexual orientations in men and women, add to the empirical validity of our findings. 25 This can also be further verified with the current notions of associations between hormonal mechanisms, cerebral laterality and sexuality.26 Our primary findings highlight the importance of considering individual differences in cognitive and affective processes, such as handedness, when examining psychological well-being and gender identity exploration.

Conclusion

Analysing the variations in handedness has highlighted its effects on an individual’s gender identity and thought patterns. It has the potential to develop variations in gender identity and areas of cognitive functioning as well. The study provides clear support in demonstrating the intricate interplay between cognitive, biological and psychological factors. This helps in exploring the originating reason and understanding the prevalence of gender-related distress.

Implications of the study include a multifaceted interpretation of the theories in lateralisation and cognitive schemas affected by/affecting the lived experiences of gender-related distress. Further research may shed light on a deeper perusal and a more comprehensive interpretation, even on ambidextrous/mixed-handed individuals and on other socio-demographic variables that the study fails to address.

The cross-sectional correlational design prevents causal inference among handedness, rumination and gender-related distress. The use of self-report measures may have introduced response and social desirability biases. Additionally, purposive sampling with balanced handedness group recruitment may limit representativeness and generalisability. The absence of biological or neurophysiological measures further limits interpretation of neurodevelopmental mechanisms, and the UGDS-GS is a screening tool rather than a clinical diagnostic measure.

The study extends its reinforcement towards the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 5) of gender equality by laying out neurocognitive underpinnings of behavioural frameworks in individuals with gender-related distress, thereby reducing stigma and supporting equitable mental health care for gender-diverse individuals; and (SDG 10) reduced inequalities, by emphasising the often-overlooked mental health vulnerabilities as systemic inequalities in the fields of health care access and treatment. Thereby, the current study strengthens support for gender-diverse individuals by critiquing the stigmatising beliefs, introducing improved treatment strategies and offering therapy methods customised to their specific needs and challenges.
