Γ

Abstract




1
Introduction

In this work we analyze the second-order asymptotic development via
\Gamma
-convergence (for the original definition, see
[
13
]
, and for the higher-order definition, see
Section

2.1
) of the Cahn–Hillard functional
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}:\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega)\to[0,\infty]
with Dirichlet boundary condition, given for
\varepsilon>0
by

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}(u)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\int_{\Omega}W(u)+\varepsilon^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\differential{x},&\textup{if $u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)$ and $u=g_{\varepsilon}$ on $\partial\Omega$},\\
\infty,&\textup{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1.1)

In (
1.1
), the function
W:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is a continuous double-well potential, which means that
W(x)=0
holds if and only if
x\in\{a,b\}
with
a<b
and sub-quadratic growth near the wells.
\Omega
is a
C^{2}
open and bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, while
g_{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
.

The Cahn–Hilliard functional

E^{(1)}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\begin{cases}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(u)+\varepsilon|\nabla u|^{2}\differential{x},&\textup{if $u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)$},\\
\infty,&\textup{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1.2)

occurs in the modelling of many physical phenomena, from phase transition and separation (cf.
[
1
,
10
,
18
,
8
]
) to processes of nucleation and coarsening (cf.
[
11
,
6
]
), where
a
and
b
represent the
phases
of the mechanical system.
It was first studied in
[
22
]
by Modica and Mortola, were they proved
\Gamma
-convergence to the perimeter functional

E^{(1)}(u)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{C_{W}}{b-a}|\mathrm{D}u|(\Omega),&\textup{if $u\in\mathrm{BV}(\Omega;\{a,b\})$},\\
\infty,&\textup{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1.3)

for a constant
C_{W}
depending on the potential
W
.
In the subsequent work
[
21
]
, analogous results were obtained in the
mass constrained
case, see also
[
25
,
23
,
16
]
, and
[
5
]
for the multiphase case.

The
Dirichlet
case is more involved and was first studied in
[
24
]
under the assumption that
W\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
W^{\prime\prime}(a),W^{\prime\prime}(b)>0
(which equates to quadratic growth at the wells) and suitable boundary data
g_{\varepsilon}
(see
[
24
, Sct. 2]
). The authors showed that the first-order
\Gamma
-limit is given by

\Gamma\textup{-}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}(u)}{\varepsilon}=\begin{cases}\frac{C_{W}}{b-a}|\mathrm{D}u|(\Omega)+\int_{\partial\Omega}\textup{d}_{W}(u,g)\differential{\hm^{n-1}},&\textup{if $u\in\mathrm{BV}(\Omega;\{a,b\})$},\\
\infty,&\textup{otherwise},\end{cases}

(1.4)

for
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
defined in (
1.1
). Here
g
is the limit of
g_{\varepsilon}
in
\mathrm{L}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and
\mathrm{d}_{W}
is the geodesic distance defined by

\mathrm{d}_{W}(r,s)\coloneqq\absolutevalue{\int_r^s 2\sqrt{W(\rho)}\dd{\rho} }.

(1.5)

The inhomogeneous has been covered in
[
3
]
, who also proved a
fundamental estimate
for the first-order functional, which is currently not available for the second-order functional.
In
[
17
]
the author used fundamental estimate for an explicit representation of the limiting functional in the multiphase case.

The first work covering the second-order asymptotic development for Cahn–Hillard like functionals is
[
12
]
, where the authors proved the triviality of the second order
\Gamma
-limit in the mass-constrained subquadratic case with the addition of constant Dirichlet data
1
, crucial for the use of classical symmetrization techniques in their proof. In
[
19
, Thm. 1.1, Thm. 1.2]
and
[
20
, Thm. 1.3]
, the authors removed the Dirichlet boundary condition and proved the non-triviality of the second order
\Gamma
-limit, both for quadratic and sub-quadratic wells.

In
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
the authors study the second order asymptotic development of the functionals
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
, for potentials
W
with quadratic growth near the wells, assuming that the unique minimizer of the first order functional is the constant function
b
.
In the first work
[
14
]
the authors crucially assume that the boundary data is bounded away from one of the wells, which means that there exists some
\alpha_{-}\in(a,b]
such that

a<\alpha_{-}\leq g_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq b.

(1.6)

Note that this assumption does not automatically imply that the constant function
b
is the unique minimizer of the first order functional (
1.4
), see
Figure

1
.

If the boundary data
g
is close to

The first non-trivial rescaling is then of order
\varepsilon
and the limiting functional is given by

\mathcal{F}^{(2)}(b)\coloneqq\int_{\partial\Omega}-\kappa(y)\int_{0}^{\infty}2\sqrt{W(z_{g(y)}(s))}z_{g(y)}^{\prime}(s)s\differential{ s }\differential{ \hm^{n-1} ( y ) },

where
\kappa
is the mean curvature of
\partial\Omega
. Here, for a given
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
z_{\alpha}
is defined as the unique solution to the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}z_{\alpha}^{\prime}(s)=\sqrt{W(z_{\alpha}(s))},\\
z_{\alpha}(0)=\alpha,\end{cases}

(1.7)

see also
Proposition

2.2
.
Note that the function
z_{\alpha}(\cdot/\varepsilon)
is the optimal transition profile on the scale
\varepsilon
between
\alpha
and
b
and is used in the construction for the recovery sequence in previous works for the first order
\Gamma
-limit.
In the subsequent work
[
15
]
, the assumption (
1.6
) is removed, which leads to a rescaling of order
\log(1/\eps)\varepsilon
and a different limiting energy, which only sees parts of the boundary with negative mean curvature.

In this paper we want to focus on the case of a potential
W
with subquadratic growth at the wells, by which we mean that there exists some
q\in(0,1)
such that
W(x)\sim\absolutevalue{x-a}^{1+q}
for
\absolutevalue{x-a}\ll 1
and
W(x)\sim\absolutevalue{x-b}^{1+q}
for
\absolutevalue{ x - b }\ll 1
.
The key difference to the case of quadratic growth is that solutions to the Cauchy problem (
1.7
) reach the well
b
in finite time, which can be explicitly written as

T^{(\alpha)}=\int_{\alpha}^{b}\frac{1}{\sqrt{W(s)}}\differential{ s }.

Since the solution in the quadratic case only approaches
b
with exponential speed, but never reaches the well, the construction of the recovery sequence in the subquadratic case simplifies. Moreover we were able to explicitly analyze the behaviour of minimizers of the weighted one-dimensional Cahn-Hilliard energy by carefully studying the corresponding Euler–Lagrange equation, see
Lemma

3.10
, which is a major simplification compared to
[
14
]
.
In the end, we are able to show that on the scale
\varepsilon
, we get a non-trivial second-order
\Gamma
-limit. This is a surprising difference to the quadratic case, where regions of the boundary with boundary data
g=a
exhibit an
\varepsilon\log( 1/\eps)
scaling, and regions with
g>a+\delta
an
\varepsilon
-scaling. The limiting functional however stays the same, see
Theorem

4.6
and
Theorem

4.9
, with less strict assumptions than in the quadratic case.
The reason for the different scaling is that in the quadratic case, additional energy is necessary to move the optimal transition profile out of the well since the unique solution
z_{a}
of the Cauchy problem (
1.7
) is constant. In the subquadratic case however, where the solutions are not unique, there exists a solution which immediately moves out of the well.

We also want to comment on the assumptions we make. Currently we have certain smoothness and regularity assumptions on
g
and
g_{\varepsilon}
(see
Section

2.2
) which prohibit boundary data of the form
g=a\chi_{A}+b\chi_{\partial\Omega\setminus A}
. Even though we do not rely on the regularity of
g
and could in principle only assume
g\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
, the assumptions

\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}_{W}(g_{\varepsilon},g)}{\varepsilon}\differential{ \hm^{n-1} }\to 0\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}\absolutevalue{ \nabla_\tau g_\eps}^{2}\differential{ \hm^{n-1} }\to 0

(1.8)

are necessary in order to apply our slicing techniques.
Since
g_{\varepsilon}
is the boundary data of an
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
function, it has to have at least
\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
-regularity, thus it is necessary that
g_{\varepsilon}
is a regularization of
g
.
By flattening and applying a standard mollifier
g_{\varepsilon}=\eta_{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\ast g
, we note that
\rho_{\varepsilon}
would have to satisfy
\rho_{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon\to 0
to satisfy the first assumption in (
1.8
). For the second assumption in (
1.8
) however, we need the reverse convergence
\varepsilon/\rho_{\varepsilon}\to 0
, which is not feasible.

The strategy of the proof is similar as in
[
14
]
. By using the Area Formula and Fubini, we reduce the
n
-dimensional functional to a weighted one-dimensional functional by writing

\int_{\{x\in\Omega\colon\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)\leq\delta\}}f(x)\differential{ x }=\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{\delta}f(\Phi(y,t))\det J_{\Phi}(y,t)\differential{ t }\differential{ \hm^{n-1 } ( y ) },

where
\Phi(y,t)\coloneqq y+t\nu(y)
for the inner unit normal
\nu
. We construct the recovery sequence by choosing optimal transition profiles in direction
\nu
. For the
\liminf
-inequality, we have to carefully study minimizers of the weighted one-dimensional functional

\int_{0}^{\delta}(W(v)+\varepsilon^{2}(v^{\prime})^{2})\omega\differential{ t }.

Since the second-order functional will not be non-negative, we will have to quantify the lower bounds for the one-dimensional functional in order to later justify pulling the limit inferior into the integral over the boundary of
\Omega
.

A future question of interest is what the second-order
\Gamma
-limit looks like for less regular
g
. Our current conjecture is that if
g
is of the form
a\chi_{A}+b\chi_{\partial\Omega\setminus A}
, the limiting functional, with the same scaling
\varepsilon
, is proportional to the size of the jump set
J_{g}
.
However a different construction for the recovery-sequence will be necessary. The most interesting question remains what the second-order functional looks like when not assuming that
b
is the unique minimizer, which has for example been discussed in
[
14
, Rmk. 1.3]
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In
Section

2
, we go over the definition of higher-order
\Gamma
-limits and the assumptions on the potential, the domain and the boundary data. We continue in
Section

3
with the
\Gamma
-limit in the one-dimensional weighted case, which is the core of this work. Then we show in
Section

4
how we can use slicing to transfer these results to the multidimensional case. In
Section

5
, we include those proofs which are very similar to already existing literature and thus of lesser interest to the reader.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Higher-order Gamma-limits

We want to start by discussing the asymptotic development of functionals via
\Gamma
-convergence introduced in
[
4
]
. Given a metric space
X
, a family of functionals
{F}_{\varepsilon}:X\to[-\infty,\infty]
and of nonnegative real numbers
\delta_{\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon>0
, the
first order asymptotic development
of
{F}_{\varepsilon}

with respect to

\delta_{\varepsilon}

on

X
is

{F}_{\varepsilon}={F}^{(0)}+\delta_{\varepsilon}{F}^{(1)}+o(\delta_{\varepsilon})\quad\textup{on $X$}

if and only if
\delta_{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and
{F}^{0},{F}^{1}:X\to[-\infty,\infty]
are such that

\Gamma
-
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{F}_{\varepsilon}={F}^{(0)}
on
X
,
m_{0}:=\inf_{X}{F}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\Gamma
-
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{F}^{(1)}_{\varepsilon}={F}^{(1)}
on
\mathcal{U}^{0}

where

{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}:=\frac{{F}_{\varepsilon}-m_{0}}{\varepsilon}

(2.1)

and
\mathcal{U}^{0}:=\{x\in X\;:\;{F}^{(0)}(x)=m_{0}\}
. More generally, if
j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
and
\{\delta^{(1)}_{\varepsilon},\dots,\delta^{(j)}_{\varepsilon}\}
are families of nonnegative real numbers for
\varepsilon>0
, we say that the
j
th-order asymptotic development of
{F}_{\varepsilon}

with respect to

\{\delta^{(1)}_{\varepsilon},\dots,\delta^{(j)}_{\varepsilon}\}

on

X
is

{F}_{\varepsilon}={F}^{(0)}+\delta_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}{F}^{(1)}+\cdots+\delta^{(j)}_{\varepsilon}{F}^{(j)}+o(\delta^{(j)}_{\varepsilon})\quad\textup{on $X$}

(2.2)

if and only if
\sigma^{(k)}:=\delta_{\varepsilon}^{k+1}/\delta_{\varepsilon}^{k}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, for every
k=1,\dots,j-1
and
{F}^{(0)},{F}^{(1)},\dots,{F}^{(j)}:X\to[-\infty,\infty]
are such that

{F}^{(k)}_{\varepsilon}={F}^{(k)}+\delta_{\varepsilon}^{(k+1)}{F}^{(k+1)}+o(\delta^{(k+1)}_{\varepsilon})\quad\textup{on $\mathcal{U}^{k-1}$}

for every
k=0,\dots,j-1
, where
\mathcal{U}^{-1}:=X
,
{F}^{(0)}_{\varepsilon}:={F}_{\varepsilon}
,

m_{k}:=\inf_{\mathcal{U}^{k-1}}{F}^{(k)},\quad\quad\mathcal{U}^{k}:=\{x\in\mathcal{U}^{k-1}\;:\;{F}^{(k)}(x)=m_{k}\}

(2.3)

and

{F}^{(k+1)}_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{{F}^{(k)}_{\varepsilon}-m_{k}}{\sigma^{(k)}_{\varepsilon}}\quad\quad\textup{for every $k=0,\dots,j-1$.}

This notion of asymptotic expansion not only provides a criterion for selecting minimizers of
{F}^{(0)}
, because every limit point of minimizers of the family
{F}_{\varepsilon}
has to be a minimizer of
{F}^{k}
on
\mathcal{U}^{k-1}
, recalling that
\mathcal{U}^{j}\subseteq\mathcal{U}^{j-1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{U}^{0}\subseteq\mathcal{U}^{-1}=X
, but also gives the following asymptotic formula for the minima

m_{\varepsilon}=m_{0}+\delta^{(1)}_{\varepsilon}m_{1}+\cdots+\delta^{(j)}_{\varepsilon}m_{j}+o(\delta^{(j)}_{\varepsilon})

(2.4)

where
m_{\varepsilon}:=\inf_{X}{F}_{\varepsilon}
.

2.2
Assumptions

As mentioned in the introduction, we are concerned with functional (
1.1
).
Throughout the paper, we will assume that
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and bounded with
C^{2}
-boundary. Moreover we want to ignore the issues of possible escape to infinity and assume throughout the paper that the boundary data
g_{\varepsilon}
maps to the interval
[a,b]
, so that we can by a truncation argument assume that
u_{\varepsilon}
maps to
[a,b]
as well. For the potential
W
, we make the following two assumptions.

(i)

W^{-1}({0})=\{a,b\}
with
a<b
and
W\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a,b\})
.

(ii)

There exists
q\in(0,1)
such that

\lim_{s\to a}W^{\prime\prime}(s)|s-a|^{1-q}\in(0,\infty)\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{s\to b}W^{\prime\prime}(s)|s-b|^{1-q}\in(0,\infty).

(2.5)

For the boundary data, we assume that
g\in C^{2}(\partial\Omega)
such that
b
is the unique minimizer of the perimeter functional (
1.4
). As a consequence (see
Proposition

4.4
), we have that
\{g=a\}\subseteq\{\kappa\leq 0\}
. Due to limitations of the techniques we apply in this paper, we will have to make the stronger assumption that
\{g=a\}\subseteq\{\kappa<0\}
, and due to the continuity of
g
, we consequently already have that the inclusion holds uniform, which means that there exists
\alpha_{-}>a
and
\kappa_{0}<0
such that

\{g\leq\alpha_{-}\}\subseteq\{\kappa<\kappa_{0}\}.

(2.6)

The problem when trying to remove this assumption is the following. For the
\Gamma
-lower bound, we reduce the domain via slicing to a
1
-dimensional interval
[0,\delta]
. To argue that an immediate transition from
g_{\varepsilon}
to
b
at
0
here is already optimal, we have to control the boundary value at
\delta
and argue that it is already close to
b
, which has been proven in
[
14
, Thm. 4.9]
. The proof relies crucially on a result by Sternberg and Zumbrum
[
26
]
and on a result by Caffarelli and Córdoba
[
9
]
who show uniform convergence of phase boundaries in Cahn–Hilliard like perturbation problems. The latter result is however not quantitative since its proof relies on a contradiction argument, meaning that given
\delta>0
, we only know that
\absolutevalue{u_\eps( \delta)-b}\ll 1
for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small in an unquantified dependence on
\delta
. The inclusion (
2.6
) ensures that for
\delta>0

fixed
and sufficiently small, the slices will still have negative curvature, which will be crucial to our arguments.

We assume that
g_{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
is a suitable approximation of
g
in the sense that there exist
a<\alpha_{-}<b
and
\kappa_{0}<0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we have for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-almost every
y\in\partial\Omega
that

g_{\varepsilon}(y)\in[\alpha_{-},b]\quad\text{or}\quad\kappa(y)\leq\kappa_{0}.

(2.7)

If
g_{\varepsilon}
approximates
g
suitably, this is a consequence of inclusion (
2.6
).

Moreover, we assume that
g_{\varepsilon}
converges to
g
fast enough, but its derivative does not explode too fast such that
g_{\varepsilon}\to g

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-pointwise almost everywhere,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}_{W}(g_{\varepsilon}(y),g(y))}{\varepsilon}\differential{ \hm^{n-1 } ( y ) }

\displaystyle\to 0

(2.8)

and

\displaystyle\varepsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\absolutevalue{ \nabla_\tau g_\eps( y ) }^{2}}{W(g_{\varepsilon}(y))}\differential{ \mathcal{H}^{n-1} ( y ) }

\displaystyle\to 0,

(2.9)

where
\nabla_{\tau}
is the tangential gradient on
\partial\Omega
. For the last assumption, we want to comment that it is in particular satisfied if
g_{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
such that
\norm{ \nabla^2 g_\eps}_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}
stays bounded and

\varepsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}\absolutevalue{ \nabla_\tau g_\eps( y ) }^{2}\differential{ \hm^{n-1 } ( y ) }\to 0.

(2.10)

To see this we distinguish the area where
g_{\varepsilon}
is close to
\{a,b\}
and the area where
g_{\varepsilon}
is away from
\{a,b\}
. If
g_{\varepsilon}
is away from
\{a,b\}
, then
W(g_{\varepsilon}(y))\gtrsim 1
and the convergence (
2.10
) already implies (
2.9
). On the other hand if
g_{\varepsilon}(y)
is close to
\{a,b\}
, then by flattening the boundary, applying the fundamental theorem of calculus and using
g_{\varepsilon}\in[a,b]
, we obtain that the integrand in (
2.9
) stays bounded.

Recall the definition (
1.5
) of the geodesic distance.
As a consequence of the growth at the wells, we make the following observations for the asymptotic behaviour of
W
,
W^{-1}
and
\mathrm{d}_{W}
close to the wells.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists
\sigma\in(0,1)
and
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\sigma|s-a|^{1+q}

\displaystyle\leq W(s)\leq\sigma^{-1}|s-a|^{1+q}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for every $s\in[a,a+\delta]$},

(2.11)

\displaystyle\sigma|s-b|^{1+q}

\displaystyle\leq W(s)\leq\sigma^{-1}|s-b|^{1+q}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for every $s\in[b-\delta,b]$},

(2.12)

\displaystyle\sigma\absolutevalue{ s - a }^{(3+q)/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{d}_{W}(a,s)\leq\sigma^{-1}\absolutevalue{s - a }^{(3+q)/2}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for every }s\in[a,a+\delta]\text{ and}

\displaystyle\sigma\absolutevalue{ s - b }^{(3+q)/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{d}_{W}(s,b)\leq\sigma^{-1}\absolutevalue{s - b }^{(3+q)/2}\quad

\displaystyle\text{for every }s\in[b-\delta,b].

Furthermore there exists
\rho>0
such that
W^{-1}\colon[0,\rho]\to[b-\delta,b]
is well-defined, decreasing and satisfies

\sigma t^{1/(1+q)}\leq b-W^{-1}(t)\leq\sigma^{-1}t^{1/(1+q)}\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,\rho].

Finally
1/\sqrt{W}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
and
W^{\prime}\in C^{0,q}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

From L’Hôpital’s rule, the continuity of
W
,
W^{-1}(\{0\})=\{a,b\}
and (
2.5
) we can deduce (
2.11
) and (
2.12
). In particular
1/\sqrt{W}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
. Due to (
2.5
), there exists
\mu\in(0,\infty)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
such that

0\leq W^{\prime\prime}(b-t)\leq\frac{\mu}{|t|^{1-q}}

for every
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\setminus\{0\}
. Therefore, for every
s_{1},s_{2}\in(b-\varepsilon,b)
with
s_{1}<s_{2}
, thanks to the subadditivity of
x\mapsto x^{q}
, we have that

|W^{\prime}(s_{2})-W^{\prime}(s_{1})|\leq\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}W^{\prime\prime}(s)\differential s\leq\mu\int_{t_{2}}^{t_{1}}\frac{1}{t^{1-q}}{\mathrm{d}}t\leq\mu(t_{1}^{q}-t^{q}_{2})\leq\mu(t_{1}-t_{2})^{q}=\mu(s_{2}-s_{1})^{q}

where
t_{1}=b-s_{1}
and
t_{2}=b-s_{2}
. In particular, arguing in the same way in the right and left neighbourhood of
a
and
b
, by
W^{\prime}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a,b\})
we can conclude that
W^{\prime}\in C^{0,q}_{\textup{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
.
The other claims are direct computations.
∎

Lastly we recall from the theory of ordinary differential equations the following Proposition regarding existence of solutions to the Cauchy problem.

Proposition 2.2
.

For every
\alpha,x\in[a,b]
we define

\Psi_{\alpha}(x)\coloneqq\int_{\alpha}^{x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{W(s)}}\differential{ s }.

(2.13)

Let
T^{(\alpha)}\coloneqq\Psi_{\alpha}(b)
and
z_{\alpha}\colon[0,\infty)\to[a,b]
be defined by

z_{\alpha}(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\Psi_{\alpha}^{-1}(t),&\text{if }t\in[0,T^{(\alpha)}],\\
b,&\text{if }t\in(T^{(\alpha)},\infty).\end{cases}

Then
z_{\alpha}\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
solves

\begin{cases}z^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)=\sqrt{W(z_{\alpha}(t))},&\textup{for every }t\in\mathbb{R},\\
z_{\alpha}(0)=\alpha,\\
z_{\alpha}(t)\in[a,b],&\textup{for every }t\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(2.14)

Note that the solution to equation (
2.14
) is unique if and only if
\alpha>a
due to the last condition. For
\alpha=a
, we choose the unique solution which immediately leaves the well
a
.

3
1-dimensional weighted case

As we will later see, the functional applied to functions on a
d
-dimensional domain will be reduced to a functional acting on functions with one-dimensional domains, at the cost of introducing a weight
\omega
, which comes from the Jacobian
\omega\coloneqq\det J_{\Phi}
of the transformation map
\Phi
, see
Proposition

4.1
.
Thus for an interval
I=(0,T)
, we are interested in the behaviour of the functional

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}\colon\mathrm{L}^{1}(I)

\displaystyle\to[0,\infty]

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{T}(W(v(t))+\varepsilon^{2}(v^{\prime}(t))^{2})\omega(t)\differential{ t },&\text{if }v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(I)\text{ with }v(0)=\alpha_{\varepsilon}\text{ and }v(T)=\beta_{\varepsilon},\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here
\omega\in C^{1}([0,T])
.
In the application later,
\alpha_{\varepsilon}=g_{\varepsilon}(y)
for a fixed point on the boundary
y\in\partial\Omega
, and
\beta_{\varepsilon}
will be exponentially close to
b
. Thus it follows from assumption (
2.7
) and
w^{\prime}(0)=-\kappa(y)
(see
Proposition

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\omega_{0}\int_{0}^{N}W(w_{0})+(w_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2}\differential{ s }

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon^{-1}T}(W(w_{\varepsilon})+(w_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2})\omega_{\varepsilon}\differential{ s }

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{T}\left(W(v_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon^{2}(v_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{2}\right)\omega\differential{ s },

which is finite by
Lemma

3.2
.
Moreover, thanks to (
3.12
) and the Euler–Lagrange equation (
3.3
), there exists
C^{\prime}\in(0,\infty)
, depending only on
W
,
\norm{\omega}_{C^{2}}
and
T
, such that for fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
small enough we have that
w_{\varepsilon}\in C^{2}([0,N])
and

|w_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(s)|+|w_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(s)|\leq C^{\prime}

for every
s\in[0,N]
. In particular, by the Arzelà–Ascoli Theorem, we can conclude by equation (
3.13
) that
w_{\varepsilon}\to z_{\alpha}
in
\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}_{\textup{loc}}((0,\infty))
, up to a subsequence, as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
∎

In the case
\alpha_{\varepsilon}\geq\alpha_{-}
, it is energetically not favourable for minimizers to make a transition down to
a
. Even though we could save energy by doing the transition to
\beta_{\varepsilon}
close to
T
since the weight could in principle be decreasing, we would have to transition the area
[a,\alpha_{-}]
twice. But since the weight is close to being constant, this can not happen. We now make this argument rigorous.

Lemma 3.9
.

Assume that
\alpha_{\varepsilon},\beta_{\varepsilon}\in[\alpha_{-},b]
for all
\varepsilon>0
and let
v_{\varepsilon}\in\argmin G_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
.
Fix any
m^{\ast}\in(a,\alpha_{-})
. Then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
\delta>0
only depending on
m^{\ast}
and
W
such that if
\omega_{1}-\omega_{0}<\delta
, then

\min_{[0,T]}v_{\varepsilon}\geq m^{*}\quad\text{for all }0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
small, let
m_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\min_{[0,T]}v_{\varepsilon}
and
t_{\varepsilon}\in[0,T]
be such that
v_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon})=m_{\varepsilon}
. By Young’s inequality and substitution, we get

\displaystyle\frac{G_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}(v_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\geq 2\omega_{0}\int_{0}^{t_{\varepsilon}}\sqrt{W(v_{\varepsilon})}\absolutevalue{v_\eps' }\differential{s}+2\omega_{0}\int_{t_{\varepsilon}}^{T}\sqrt{W(v_{\varepsilon})}\absolutevalue{v_\eps' }\differential{s}

\displaystyle\geq\omega_{0}\int^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}_{m_{\varepsilon}}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{s}+\omega_{0}\int^{\beta_{\varepsilon}}_{m_{\varepsilon}}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{s}

\displaystyle=2\omega_{0}\int^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}_{m_{\varepsilon}}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{s}+\omega_{0}\int^{\beta_{\varepsilon}}_{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{s}.

Since
v_{\varepsilon}
is a minimizer, we therefore obtain by inequality (
3.4
) that

\displaystyle\omega_{0}\left(2\int_{m_{\varepsilon}}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{ s }+\int_{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}^{\beta_{\varepsilon}}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{ s }\right)

\displaystyle\leq\omega_{1}\left(\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},b)+\mathrm{d}_{W}(\beta_{\varepsilon},b)\right)

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}\left(2\int_{\beta_{\varepsilon}}^{b}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{ s }+\int_{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}^{\beta_{\varepsilon}}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{ s }\right).

In the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, this inequality implies

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}2\omega_{0}\int_{m_{\varepsilon}}^{\alpha_{-}}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{ s }\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}2\omega_{0}\int_{m_{\varepsilon}}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{ s }

\displaystyle\leq(\omega_{1}-\omega_{0})\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{ s }

\displaystyle\leq(\omega_{1}-\omega_{0})\int_{\alpha_{-}}^{\beta}2\sqrt{W(s)}\differential{ s }.

The right hand side gets arbitrarily small if
\omega_{1}-\omega_{0}
is small, and since
m^{\ast}<\alpha_{-}
is fixed, the desired claim follows.
∎

Since we have just shown that in the case
\alpha_{\varepsilon}\geq\alpha_{-}
we do not transition down to the well
a
, a minimizer
v_{\varepsilon}
has to reach the well
b
in a time of scale
\varepsilon
, which is what we now show.

Lemma 3.10
.

There exists a sequence
\theta_{\varepsilon}\uparrow b
with the following properties.
Assume that
\alpha_{\varepsilon},\beta_{\varepsilon}\in[\alpha_{-},b]
for all
\varepsilon>0
and that
\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},\alpha),\mathrm{d}_{W}(\beta_{\varepsilon},b)\in o(\varepsilon)
. We have that
\mathrm{d}_{W}(\theta_{\varepsilon},b)\in o(\varepsilon)
and if
v_{\varepsilon}\in\arg\min G_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
then there exists a unique smallest
S_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
such that
v_{\varepsilon}(S_{\varepsilon})=\theta_{\varepsilon}
and such that
v_{\varepsilon}
is increasing on
[0,S_{\varepsilon}]
. Moreover we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{S_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}=T^{(\alpha)},

(3.14)

where
T^{(\alpha)}
is as in
Proposition

2.2
.
If we quantify the estimates, then we get that for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, there exists
M>0
such that

\frac{S_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\leq M,

(3.15)

where
M
only depends on
W
and the convergence rate of
\beta_{\varepsilon}
to
b
, and there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\theta_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the bound

\theta_{\varepsilon}\geq b-C\max\{b-\beta_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon^{1/(1+q)}\}.

(3.16)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\alpha<b
, since otherwise we can simply choose
\theta_{\varepsilon}=\alpha_{\varepsilon}
and immediately get the desired result.
For
C>0
, let
\theta_{\varepsilon}
be defined by

\theta_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq b-C\max\{b-\beta_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon^{1/(1+q)}\}.

Let
C^{\prime}>0
be chosen below. Then for
C>0
sufficiently large, we note that due to the growth bounds of
W
near
b
, see
Lemma

2.1
, we have that

C^{\prime}(W(\beta_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\sigma C^{1+q}}{2}(W(\beta_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon)\leq W(\theta_{\varepsilon})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{d}_{W}(\theta_{\varepsilon},b)\in o(\varepsilon).

Suppose now an
S_{\varepsilon}
as stated in the claim does not exist. Then we define

t_{\ast}\coloneqq\arg\min\left\{t\in[0,T]\colon v_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)=0\right\}.

By assumption we have
t_{\ast}<T
,
x_{\ast}\coloneqq v_{\varepsilon}(t_{\ast})<\theta_{\varepsilon}
and
v_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t_{\ast})=0
. Since we can piece together minimizers on disjoint intervals,
v_{\varepsilon}
must also be a minimizer of the localized functional

G_{\varepsilon}^{[t_{\ast},T]}(v)\coloneqq\int_{t_{\ast}}^{T}\left(\varepsilon W(v)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(v^{\prime}(t))^{2}\right)\omega(t)\differential{ t }

subject to the boundary conditions
v(t_{\ast})=v_{\varepsilon}(t_{\ast})
and
v(T)=\beta_{\varepsilon}
. By
Lemma

3.6
, we have that on
[t_{\ast},T]
, the function
v_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the Euler–Lagrange equation

(v_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t))^{2}=\frac{W(v_{\varepsilon}(t))+\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}(t)}{\varepsilon^{2}}

(3.17)

for

\displaystyle\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega(t)}\left(-\omega(t_{\ast})W(x_{\ast})-\varepsilon\int_{t_{\ast}}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(v_{\varepsilon}(t))+\varepsilon(v_{\varepsilon}(t))^{2}\right)\omega^{\prime}(t)\differential{ t }\right)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\frac{1}{\omega(t)}\left(-\omega(t_{\ast})W(x_{\ast})-\varepsilon\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}(t)\right).

We note that by the energy bound in
Lemma

3.2
, the function
\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}
stays uniformly bounded in the supremum norm. Plugging
t=T
into equation (
3.17
) yields due to the choice of
\theta_{\varepsilon}
that for
C^{\prime}>0
sufficiently large, we have

W(x_{\ast})\leq\frac{1}{\omega(t_{\ast})}\left(\omega(T)W(\beta_{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\ast}(T)\right)\leq C^{\prime}(W(\beta_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon)\leq W(\theta_{\varepsilon}).

(3.18)

But now we have (for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small) a contradiction to
a<x_{\ast}<\theta_{\varepsilon}
and
Lemma

3.9
, which proves our first claim.

We now want to show the convergence claimed in equation (
3.14
). First we want to show the lower bound

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{S_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\geq T^{(\alpha)}.

If the left-hand side is infinity, we are done, so assume otherwise. We take a non-relabelled subsequence such that the limit inferior is realized and such that there exists
\bar{T}
so that for this subsequence,
S_{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon\to\bar{T}
. By the uniform convergence on compact intervals shown in
Lemma

3.8
, we must then have

v_{\varepsilon}(S_{\varepsilon})-z_{\alpha}(S_{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon)\to 0.

But
v_{\varepsilon}(S_{\varepsilon})=\beta_{\varepsilon}\to b
and
z_{\alpha}(S_{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon)\to z_{\alpha}(\bar{T})
, from which we deduce that
z_{\alpha}(\bar{T})=b
. By definition of
z_{\alpha}
, this yields
\bar{T}\geq T^{(\alpha)}
, which is what we wanted to show.

We will now show the reverse inequality

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{S_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\leq T_{\alpha}.

(3.19)

We do this by first showing that for
\delta_{\varepsilon}(t)
as in
Lemma

3.6
, it holds that

\frac{\min\{\delta_{\varepsilon}(t),0\}}{\varepsilon^{2(1+q)/(3+q)}}\to 0

(3.20)

uniformly on
[0,T]
.
To see this, we plug
t=T
into the Euler–Lagrange equation (
3.10
) to obtain

\delta_{\varepsilon}(T)\geq-W(\beta_{\varepsilon}).

(3.21)

Since
\mathrm{d}_{W}(\beta_{\varepsilon},b)\in o(\varepsilon)
, it follows by
Lemma

2.1
that

\frac{W(\beta_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon^{2(1+q)/(3+q)}}\to 0.

(3.22)

Moreover we can write
\delta_{\varepsilon}
as

\delta_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{\omega(t)}\left(C_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\right)

for a constant
C_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
and a function
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
which is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}([0,T])
uniformly as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
This shows that
\delta_{\varepsilon}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant less or equal to
C\varepsilon
.
Thus inequality (
3.21
) combined with (
3.22
) already implies the desired claim (
3.20
) since
2(1+q)/(3+q)<1
.

To deduced the desired inequality (
3.19
), we use a comparison principle to the unweighted case, in which the claim follows easily via the explicit representation of solutions to the Cauchy problem.
Define the lower bound
\tilde{\delta}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\min\{\min_{[0,T]}\delta_{\varepsilon}(t),0\}
and define
\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq W^{-1}(-\tilde{\delta}_{\varepsilon})
, where the inverse is taken on the interval
[b-\mu,b]
for a sufficiently small
\mu>0
as in
Lemma

2.1
. Moreover we define the function

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)\coloneqq\int_{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}^{x}\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{W(\rho)+\tilde{\delta}_{\varepsilon}}}\differential{\rho}

(3.23)

for
x\leq\tilde{T}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\varphi_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon})
.
Note that by definition of
\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}
, the function
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is well-defined on
[\alpha_{\varepsilon},\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}]
, see also inequality (
3.24
). Denote its inverse by
u_{\varepsilon}
, which is a solution to

\begin{cases}u_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{W(u_{\varepsilon})+\tilde{\delta}_{\varepsilon}}}{\varepsilon},&\textup{for }t\in[0,\tilde{T}_{\varepsilon}],\\
u_{\varepsilon}(0)=\alpha_{\varepsilon},\\
u_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{T}_{\varepsilon})=\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

Using the Euler–Lagrange equation (
3.10
), the definition of
\tilde{\delta}_{\varepsilon}
and a comparison principle, it follows that
v_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq u_{\varepsilon}(t)
for all
t\in[0,\tilde{T}_{\varepsilon}]
.
By the
1/(1+q)
-Hölder continuity of
W^{-1}
at
b
and
Lemma

2.1
we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}_{W}(\tilde{\beta_{\varepsilon}},b)}{\varepsilon}\lesssim\frac{(b-\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon})^{(3+q)/2}}{\varepsilon}\lesssim\frac{(-\tilde{\delta}_{\varepsilon})^{(3+q)/(2(1+q))}}{\varepsilon}\to 0.

Thus by redefining
\tilde{\theta}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\min\{\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon},\theta_{\varepsilon}\}
, we may without loss of generality assume
\theta_{\varepsilon}\leq\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}
while keeping the properties of
\theta_{\varepsilon}
. Combined with
v_{\varepsilon}\geq u_{\varepsilon}
, this yields

v_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{T}_{\varepsilon})\geq u_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{T}_{\varepsilon})=\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}\geq\theta_{\varepsilon}.

Therefore by definition of
S_{\varepsilon}
, we have
S_{\varepsilon}\leq\tilde{T}_{\varepsilon}
. We lastly check the asymptotics of
\tilde{T}_{\varepsilon}
. We claim that as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we have

\frac{\tilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}=\int_{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}^{\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{W(\rho)+\tilde{\delta}_{\varepsilon}}}\differential{ \rho}\to\int_{\alpha}^{b}\frac{1}{\sqrt{W(\rho)}}\differential{ \rho}=T^{(\alpha)}.

This follows by the generalized dominated convergence theorem. In fact, we note by
Lemma

2.1
and equation (
2.5
) that
W^{-1}
is
1/(1+q)
-Hölder continuous near
b
. Thus for
\mu>0
sufficiently small we have that

\int_{b-\mu}^{\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{W(\rho)+\tilde{\delta}_{\varepsilon}}}\differential{ \rho}=\int_{b-\mu}^{\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{W(\rho)-W(\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon})}}\differential{ \rho}\lesssim\int_{b-\mu}^{\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}}(\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}-\rho)^{-\frac{1+q}{2}}\differential{ \rho}\leq\int_{0}^{\mu}t^{-\frac{1+q}{2}}\differential{ t },

(3.24)

which is finite because
q\in(0,1).

Thus we conclude

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{S_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\tilde{T}_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}=T^{(\alpha)}

For the upper bound (
3.15
) we first note that
\delta_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq-W(\beta_{\varepsilon})-C\varepsilon
by the previous estimates. Combined with
S_{\varepsilon}\leq\tilde{T}_{\varepsilon}
and inequality (
3.24
), this yields the desired upper bound depending on the convergence speed of
\beta_{\varepsilon}
to
b
.
∎

It remains to analyze the behaviour in the case
\alpha_{\varepsilon}\in[a,\alpha_{-}]
as well, where we assume that
\omega^{\prime}\geq-\kappa_{0}/2
. We first prove an analogue of
Lemma

3.9
. Due to the bound on the derivative of the weight, a transition up to
\beta_{\varepsilon}
at the interval bound
T
would cost more energy then a transition at time zero. Thus a minimizer
v_{\varepsilon}\in\argmin G_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
should immediately make a transition up to an area close to
b
. This is the content of the following two results.

Lemma 3.11
.

If the weight
\omega
satisfies inequality (
3.2
), then there exists some constant
N>0
only depending on
W
,
\norm{\omega}_{C^{2}}
,
\kappa_{0}
and the convergence rate of
\beta_{\varepsilon}\to b
such that for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, every
v_{\varepsilon}\in\argmin G_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
satisfies

\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},\min v_{\varepsilon})\leq N\varepsilon.

Proof.

Let
t_{\varepsilon}\in[0,T]
be such that
v_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon})=m_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\min_{[0,T]}v_{\varepsilon}
. As in the proof of
Lemma

3.9
and using inequality (
3.2
), we then deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{G_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}(v_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\min_{[0,t_{\varepsilon}]}\omega\right)\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})+\left(\min_{[t_{\varepsilon},T]}\omega\right)\mathrm{d}_{W}(m_{\varepsilon},\beta_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\geq\omega(0)\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})+\left(\omega(0)-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}t_{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}_{W}(m_{\varepsilon},\beta_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\geq 2\omega(0)\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})+\left(\omega(0)-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}t_{\varepsilon}\right)\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},\beta_{\varepsilon}).

(3.25)

We compare this energy with the optimal transition profile constructed in the proof
Lemma

3.2
, which has at most energy

\displaystyle\left(\omega(0)-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}C\varepsilon\right)\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},b)+\left(\max_{[0,T]}\omega\right)\mathrm{d}_{W}(\beta_{\varepsilon},b)

(3.26)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left(\omega(0)-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}C\varepsilon\right)\left(\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},\beta_{\varepsilon})+\mathrm{d}_{W}(\beta_{\varepsilon},b)\right)+\left(\max_{[0,T]}\omega\right)\mathrm{d}_{W}(\beta_{\varepsilon},b).

(3.27)

Since
v_{\varepsilon}
is a minimizer, it thus follows that we must have that the term (
3.25
) is less or equal than (
3.26
), from which we deduce that

\displaystyle 2\omega(0)\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},m_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}C\varepsilon\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},\beta_{\varepsilon})+\left(\omega(0)-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}C\varepsilon+\max\omega\right)\mathrm{d}_{W}(\beta_{\varepsilon},b).

Since
\mathrm{d}_{W}(\beta_{\varepsilon},b)\in o(\varepsilon)
, the right-hand side is, for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small and dependent on the convergence rate of
\beta_{\varepsilon}
to
b
, bounded by
N\varepsilon/(2\omega(0))
for some sufficiently large
N>0
, which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

There exists a sequence
\theta_{\varepsilon}\uparrow b
with the following properties.
Assume that
\omega^{\prime}(0)\geq-\kappa_{0}
and
\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},\alpha),\mathrm{d}_{W}(\beta_{\varepsilon},b)\in o(\varepsilon)
. We have that
\mathrm{d}_{W}(\theta_{\varepsilon},b)\in o(\varepsilon)
and if
v_{\varepsilon}\in\argmin G_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}
, then there exists
S_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
such that

and there exists

such that for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,

\frac{S_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\leq M,

where

only depends on
\alpha_{-}
,
\kappa_{0}
,
W
and the convergence rate of
\beta_{\varepsilon}
to
b
. Moreover there exists a constant

such that for

sufficiently small,

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to argue that we find a small time

in which we reach

and then apply

3.10
on the smaller interval
[t_{\varepsilon},T]
.

We first argue that there exists some constant

,

and the convergence rate of

\frac{G_{\varepsilon}^{(0)}(v_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}\geq\left(\min_{[D\varepsilon,T]}\omega\right)\mathrm{d}_{W}(v_{\varepsilon}(D\varepsilon),\beta_{\varepsilon})\geq\left(\omega(0)-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}D\varepsilon\right)\left(\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},\beta_{\varepsilon})-\mathrm{d}_{W}(v_{\varepsilon}(D\varepsilon),\alpha_{\varepsilon})\right).

\left(\omega(0)-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}D\varepsilon\right)\mathrm{d}_{W}(v_{\varepsilon}(D\varepsilon),\alpha_{\varepsilon})\geq-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}(D-C)\varepsilon\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},\beta_{\varepsilon})+C\mathrm{d}_{W}(\beta_{\varepsilon},b).

If
\alpha_{\varepsilon}\geq\alpha_{-}
, then we already know from

3.9
that
\mathrm{d}_{W}(v_{\varepsilon}(D\varepsilon),a)\geq N\varepsilon
, so we may assume

. We then note that if

is sufficiently small, we can due to inequality (

3.11

to deduce

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{d}_{W}(a,v_{\varepsilon}(D_{\varepsilon}))

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}(D_{\varepsilon}))-\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},a)+\mathrm{d}_{W}(a,v_{\varepsilon}(D\varepsilon))

\displaystyle\geq 5N\varepsilon-\mathrm{d}_{W}(\alpha_{\varepsilon},\min v_{\varepsilon})-\mathrm{d}_{W}(a,\min v_{\varepsilon})+\mathrm{d}_{W}(a,\min v_{\varepsilon})\geq 4N\varepsilon,

which shows the desired claim.

Next, we apply the strategy of
Lemma

3.10
on the interval
[D\varepsilon,T]
. To be precise we claim that the shifted function

for

as claimed in the statement of
Lemma

3.12
and
\bar{S}_{\varepsilon}
as in the statement of
Lemma

3.10
. We prove the claim by following the proof steps of
Lemma

3.10
, for which not all assumptions are satisfied. To be precise the first difficulty arises when trying to deduce a contradiction from inequality (

), which yielded

.

Lemma

3.9
here.
However we know by

3.11

\mathrm{d}_{W}(a,x_{\ast})\geq\mathrm{d}_{W}(a,\min\bar{v}_{\varepsilon})\geq\mathrm{d}_{W}(a,\bar{v}_{\varepsilon}(0))-\mathrm{d}_{W}(\bar{v}_{\varepsilon}(0),\min\bar{v}_{\varepsilon})\geq\mathrm{d}_{W}(a,\bar{v}_{\varepsilon}(0))-N\varepsilon\geq N\varepsilon.

From

and

, we deduce due to the monotonicity of

at

that

has to be close to

This proves that

), we have

We conclude the proof by defining

, which satisfies

and

for

sufficiently large, only depending on

,

,

to

.

Proposition 3.13

(
\liminf
-inequality)
.

Assume that the potential

is as in

v_{\varepsilon}(y,t)\coloneqq u_{\varepsilon}(\Phi(y,t))
so that
v_{\varepsilon}\colon\partial\Omega\times(0,\delta)\to\mathbb{R}
.
Note that because
u_{\varepsilon}
is constant along normal directions in
\Omega_{\delta/2}
, we have continuity of
v_{\varepsilon}(y,\cdot)
at time
t=T_{\varepsilon}(y)
for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.
Moreover since
g_{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
, we see that
v_{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega\times(0,\delta))
. Furthermore if
t\leq T_{\varepsilon}(y)
its derivative is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}(y,t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sgn}(\tr( u_\eps(y ) )-g_{\varepsilon}(y))\frac{\sqrt{W(v_{\varepsilon}(y,t))}}{\varepsilon}

(5.8)

and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\tau}v_{\varepsilon}(y,t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sgn}(\tr( u_\eps(y ) )-g_{\varepsilon}(y))\frac{\sqrt{W(v_{\varepsilon}(y,t))}}{\sqrt{W(g_{\varepsilon}(y))}}\nabla_{\tau}g_{\varepsilon}(y)

as in equation (
4.2
).
Finally we define

\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}v_{\varepsilon}(\Phi^{-1}(x)),&\text{if }x\in\Omega_{\delta}\\
u_{\varepsilon}(x)&\text{else}.\end{cases}

By the chain rule,
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}(x)\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
with
\tr\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}(y)=g_{\varepsilon}(y)

and since
v_{\varepsilon}(y,t)
is constant for
t\geq T_{\varepsilon}(y)
, we have

\absolutevalue{ \nabla\bar{u}_\eps( x ) }^{2}\leq\begin{cases}\absolutevalue{ \nabla u_\eps( x ) }^{2},&\text{if }x\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\delta}\text{ or }x=\Phi(y,t),t>T_{\varepsilon}(y)\\
\absolutevalue{ \partial_t v ( y,t ) }^{2}+C\absolutevalue{ \nabla_\tau v_\eps( y ) }^{2},&\text{if }x\in\Omega_{\delta},x=\Phi(y,t),t\leq T_{\varepsilon}(y).\end{cases}

We finally bound the energy of the sequence
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
by

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(\bar{u}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}E_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(u_{\varepsilon})+\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{T_{\varepsilon}(y)}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(v_{\varepsilon}(y,t))+\varepsilon\absolutevalue{ \partial_t v_\eps( y, t ) }^{2}\right)\omega(y,t)\differential{ t }\differential{ \hm^{N-1} ( y ) }

\displaystyle+C\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{T_{\varepsilon}}\varepsilon\absolutevalue{ \nabla_\tau v_\eps( y ,t) }^{2}\differential{t}\differential{ \hm^{N-1} ( y ) }.

(5.9)

By inequality (
5.7
), the first summand is bounded by

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}E_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq E^{(1)}(u)+C\delta.

(5.10)

For the second summand, we compute by equation (
5.8
), the fact that
\mathrm{d}_{W}(x,y)\leq C\absolutevalue{ x - y }
for
x,y\in[a,b]
,
\mathrm{d}_{W}(g_{\varepsilon},g)\to 0
in
\mathrm{L}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
and
u_{\varepsilon}(\Phi(\cdot,t))\to\tr u
in
\mathrm{L}^{1}(\partial\Omega)
by equation (
5.6
) that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{T_{\varepsilon}(y)}\frac{W(v_{\varepsilon}(y,t))}{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\absolutevalue{ \partial_t v_\eps( y, t ) }^{2}\differential{ \hm^{N-1} ( y ) }\differential{ t }

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{T_{\varepsilon}(y)}2\sqrt{W(v_{\varepsilon}(y,t))}\absolutevalue{ \partial_t v_\eps( y, t ) }\differential{ t }\differential{ \hm^{N-1} ( y ) }

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{d}_{W}(g_{\varepsilon}(y),u_{\varepsilon}(\Phi(y,t_{\varepsilon})))\differential{ \hm^{N-1} }(y)

\displaystyle\to\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathrm{d}_{W}(g(y),\tr u(y))\differential{ \hm^{N-1} }(y)

(5.11)

The last summand (
5.11
) is an error which vanishes in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
since it is up to a constant bounded from above by

C\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\absolutevalue{ \nabla_\tau g_\eps( y ) }^{2}}{W(g_{\varepsilon}(y))}\mathrm{d}_{W}(g_{\varepsilon}(y),\tr u_{\varepsilon}(y))\differential{ \hm^{N-1} ( y ) },

(5.12)

which vanishes by assumption (
2.9
).
Thus combining inequality (
5.9
), (
5.10
), equality (
5.11
) and inequality (
5.12
), we obtain

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(1)}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq\mathcal{F}^{(1)}(u)+C\delta.

Since we could have chosen
\delta
to be arbitrarily small without enlarging the constant
C
, it follows from the lower semicontinuity of the
\Gamma
-
\limsup
that the desired
\limsup
-inequality must already hold, which finishes the proof.
