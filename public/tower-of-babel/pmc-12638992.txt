Type-I hot corrosion behavior of Cr-modified slurry aluminide coating superalloy Rene 80
Authors: K Shirvani, S V Miraboutalebi, A Kaflou, M Farvizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the structure and type-I hot corrosion behavior of chromium-modified slurry aluminide coatings obtained by chromizing/Cr-plating before the slurry aluminizing have been discussed. Microstructural and elemental/phase analyses showed that the modified coatings contain a high-aluminum NiAl phase with chromium concentrated on the top-coat zone in the form of Cr-enriched deposits in comparison to the simple aluminide coating. The chromium content was decreased with the distance from the top layer of the coating, although a high concentration of chromium was observed within the interdiffusion layer which is routine for aluminide coatings. The furnace hot corrosion test of Na2SO4-deposited coated specimens of the modified coatings as well as the simple aluminide coating was conducted at 900 °C for 70 h. The coatings, corrosion products, and attacked areas were studied after 10, 30 and 70 h intervals. The chromium-plated slurry aluminide coating with 57 at% Al and 31 at% Cr in its top layer showed the best type-I hot corrosion resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25155-5.Keywords: Aluminide coating, Chromizing, Plating, Hot corrosion, Rene-80Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Nickel-based superalloys are widely employed in the manufacture of rotating blades for land-based and aerospace gas turbines due to their exceptional high-temperature strength, creep resistance, and structural stability. These components are, however, prone to surface degradation caused by oxidation and hot corrosion under extreme operational conditions. Aluminizing processes are extensively used to enrich the substrate surface with aluminum, facilitating the formation of the protective β-NiAl phase, which significantly enhances oxidation resistance and surface stability1. Recent reviews have highlighted that advanced deposition methods including pack cementation, slurry aluminizing, and chemical vapor deposition enable precise control over coating composition and microstructure, crucial for maintaining performance under high-temperature service conditions up to 1200 °C1. Hot corrosion resistance of protective coatings has been investigated for gas turbines operating with biomass and waste-derived fuel gases. Studies indicate that optimized corrosion-resistant coatings can sustain long-term operation in aggressive sulfur- and chlorine-containing environments, providing critical insights into the degradation mechanisms and protective strategies for Ni-based superalloys2. The effect of platinum and silicon on aluminide coatings has been widely explored. Silicon-platinum modified aluminide coatings on GTD-111 superalloy demonstrated significant improvements in microstructural stability and oxidation resistance due to the formation of PtAl2 precipitates and silicides, which hinder aluminum depletion and surface degradation3. Further studies focusing on platinum addition reveal that Pt not only enhances cyclic oxidation resistance but also modifies type II hot corrosion behavior in Si-aluminide coatings, even when the silicon content is below the critical threshold, highlighting the synergistic effect of these elements4. Chromium modifications of aluminide coatings have also been studied extensively. Chromium-aluminide coatings deposited via pack cementation on Inconel 718 and Fe-Cr-Ni superalloys showed that high Cr contents facilitate α-Cr formation, reducing aluminum outward diffusion and nickel inward diffusion, thereby prolonging coating life and enhancing Type I hot corrosion resistance5. Additionally, cyclic oxidation studies of Si-aluminide/MCrAlY coatings on GTD-111 superalloys emphasize the role of the multi-layered microstructure in mitigating high-temperature degradation and preserving coating integrity6. Slurry aluminizing methods for silicon-modified coatings on IN625 superalloys have demonstrated precise control over coating thickness, uniformity, and microstructure. The addition of silicon enhances high-temperature oxidation and hot corrosion resistance, indicating the effectiveness of slurry techniques for multi-element modification7. In parallel, nickel-chromium alloy electroplating remains relevant in industrial applications, including aerospace, due to its capacity to improve wear and corrosion resistance while controlling surface morphology8. Comprehensive reviews on chromium-based coatings for superalloys summarize the influence of Cr content, deposition method, and thermal treatment on coating performance, highlighting the importance of optimizing microstructure to achieve long-term durability9. Experimental studies on chromium electrodeposition from Cr (III) aqueous solutions further provide insight into electrolyte selection and deposition parameters, which are critical for controlling coating quality and reducing defects10. Hot corrosion and oxidation behavior of Pt-aluminide and Pt-Rh-aluminide coatings on Ni- and Co-based substrates have shown significant improvements in corrosion resistance under aggressive environments, emphasizing the synergistic effects of these elements and the importance of precise composition control11. Studies on Cr-Al-Si and Cr-Al coatings for Ni3Al single-crystal alloys confirm enhanced high-temperature oxidation resistance, demonstrating the benefits of multi-element modifications for single-crystal turbine components12. Microstructure and oxidation behavior of modified aluminide coatings on Ni3Al-based single crystal superalloys indicate that controlled deposition and post-treatment can optimize coating life, inhibit oxygen penetration, and resist hot corrosion attack, reinforcing the critical role of microstructural engineering in high-temperature protective coatings12,13. Pre-oxidation treatments of β-(Ni, Pt)Al systems at 900–1200 °C under low-pO2 Ar significantly influence the bond coat and interdiffusion zone microstructure, with treatments above 1100 °C inducing phase transformations and enhancing inward diffusion, while severe degradation is observed at 1200 °C14. At the atomic scale, first-principles studies of Cr site preference in β-NiAl reveal that Cr alternates between Al and Ni sublattices depending on alloy composition and temperature. These findings clarify the role of Cr in controlling diffusion, mechanical properties, and corrosion resistance of β-NiAl coatings, providing a theoretical foundation for experimental observations15. Finally, Using the Wagner–Schottky model, results indicate that Cr site occupancy depends strongly on alloy composition and temperature, switching between Al and Ni sites under different conditions. This computational insight helps reconcile discrepancies in experimental studies and clarifies the role of Cr in controlling the mechanical properties and corrosion behavior of β-NiAl.

Type II hot corrosion (650–750 °C) in Co-based alloys has been reinterpreted using the synergistic fluxing model16. Studies on CoCrAlY exposed at 700 and 800 °C under various SO2 containing atmospheres revealed that Al (and Cr) are removed first, contrary to earlier beliefs that Co dissolution dominates. This revised mechanism highlights the preferential attack of the Al-rich β-phase over the γ-phase16. This integrated understanding of aluminide coatings combining empirical, microstructural, and computational insights provides a comprehensive framework for designing multi-element modified coatings with optimized microstructure, composition, and thermal stability for next-generation gas turbines1–16.

The idea of developing aluminide coatings applied by slurry aluminizing method with high-temperature hot corrosion resistance characteristics was pursued in this research group. Adding chromium (with two methods of powder chromizing and electroplating) to the composition of aluminide coating and its effect on the structural characteristics and high-temperature hot corrosion behavior compared to chrome-aluminide coatings have been investigated in this research.

Application of aluminide (Al) coating

For the process of aluminizing the samples, the method of immersion in the slurry containing Al powder with particle size below 3 μm has been used. This slurry is prepared by adding aluminum powder (12–15 g) to a resin made of methanol (25–45 milliliters) and polyvinyl acetate (5–8 g). In this method, methanol was first added and dissolved in several stages into solid polyvinyl acetate. Then, aluminum powders were added to this liquid. Subsequently, the dip-coating method was used to apply the slurry onto the surface of the prepared samples. By immersing the sample in the slurry for a few seconds and then removing it, a controlled amount of the slurry remained on the sample. The immersion time was adjusted so that the slurry deposition on the sample surface was equivalent to 15 ± 1 (mg.cm-2) The slurry interdiffusion process was carried out at a temperature of 850 °C for 180 min under a neutral atmosphere (Argon shielding gas with a purity of 99.999%). All the coatings resulting from the slurry aluminizing process were subjected to a supplementary interdiffusion step at a temperature higher than 1000 °C for 75 min to perform the thermal process of interdiffusion of the aluminide coating.

Application of Chrome-Aluminide (CrAl-P) coating

The powder chromizing operation was performed on the samples at a temperature above 1000 °C and for 150 min under a neutral atmosphere. (Argon shielding gas with a purity of 99.999%). In this study, for the powder-pack chromizing process, a powder mixture consisting of chromium powder (20–30 wt%) as the coating element, ammonium chloride powder (2–5 wt%) as the activator, and alumina powder (70–80 wt%) as the filler was used.

Then, aluminizing slurry coating (corresponding to Al coating) was applied to the samples. The purpose of the coating operation was to create a chrome-aluminide coating by powder chromizing method with a thickness of about 15–20 μm for the chrome layer (first stage) and a thickness of 40–60 μm for the aluminizing layer (second stage) by the slurry method.

Application of Chrome-Aluminide (CrAl-E) coating

To chrome plating, sample surface preparation was done using sodium hydroxide solution and sulfuric acid. First, layer of nickel was applied using a nickel electroplating (sulfamate bath) on the nickel-based superalloy substrate (Rene80). Also, hard chrome plating (bath Sulfuric acid and chromic acid) was used for chrome coating. After creating the nickel and chromium layer by electroplating, the samples were subjected to vacuum penetration heat treatment of (10− 5 torr) at 850 °C for 90 min, and then the aluminizing slurry coating (corresponding to the Al coating) was applied on the samples. The purpose of the coating operation was to create a chrome-aluminide coating by electroplating with a thickness of about 10–15 μm for the chrome layer (first stage) and 40–60 μm for the aluminizing layer (second stage) by the slurry method. The electroplated Ni-Cr bilayer coating, with an overlay nature, needs to be enhanced to a diffused state at the surface. To achieve this objective, a vacuum heat treatment annealing process was performed on the coating.

Type I hot corrosion test (900 °C)

In this test, first, the weight and dimensions of the samples were measured. Then Na2SO4 salt was applied with the help of a carrier liquid on the preheated sample surface by immersion method in a saturated salt solution. After drying the sample immersed in the salt solution, the sample was subjected to a thermal cycle in the furnace. At the end of the heating period, the samples were weighed and then rinsed in hot distilled water for 1 min to dissolve the possible residual salt and water-soluble corrosion products. In this way, one hot corrosion test period is finished and all the previous steps are repeated in each test period. The parameters that were selected and implemented for this article are as follows:

In the upper layer of the CrAl-P coating, the percentage of chromium is 20.5 wt%. According to the chromium-aluminum two-phase diagram, when the amount of chromium in the coating increases, it is in the form of a solid solution and forms the α-Cr phase. The formation of these phases is one of the best factors preventing the penetration of aluminum from coating into the base metal, or vice versa, the penetration of nickel from the base metal into the coating, which increases the life of the coating.

Chrome-Aluminide (CrAl-E) coating

After preparation, the samples were placed in the main coating solution including nickel sulfamate bath and also hard chromium bath, and the coating process was carried out on it. The SEM image in Fig. 4 shows the image of the cross-section and the surface morphology of the nickel/chromium plating coating, respectively.

In this study, a nickel underlayer was applied prior to chromium electroplating. This nickel interlayer ensured better adhesion of the subsequent chromium layer. Extensive experimental investigations revealed that single-layer chromium plating exhibited poor adhesion and experienced flaking, whereas the dual-layer nickel-chromium electroplating significantly improved coating adhesion and eliminated delamination. The deposition rate of the chromium layer was carefully controlled by adjusting the plating time, resulting in a chromium coating with optimal thickness and adhesion properties.

Weight changes of three coatings Al, CrAl-P and CrAl-E

The graphs of weight changes of three coatings Al, CrAl-P and CrAl-E after 70 h of periodic hot corrosion tests are shown in Fig. 8. The graphs of weight change were created because of the loss of part of the corrosion products between two corrosion periods. During the washing of the samples after each period of hot corrosion and before applying fresh salt, the remaining salt and soluble corrosion products are dissolved in water. Products that do not have enough adhesion to the sample are separated from the sample at this stage. For this reason, in some cases, in a specific hour, the change in the weight of the sample shows a sudden decrease. The rate of changes in the weight of the samples in the periodic hot corrosion test is influenced by four main factors: the oxide formation rate, the oxide dissolution rate, the chemical composition of the corrosion products, and the weight loss due to the washing of the samples.

Weight change diagram of three coatings Al slurry, CrAl-P and CrAl-E.

A sudden decrease in weight change for Al coating occurs after 12 hot corrosion cycles. This drastic weight reduction indicates the non-adhesion of hot corrosion products to the coating and their removal due to thermal and mechanical stresses during washing. Weight loss is not observed for two CrAl-P and CrAl-E coatings. Therefore, the hot corrosion products in the case of CrAl-P and CrAl-E coatings had more adhesion to the coating. The weight change graph of the samples generally shows that the aluminide coatings modified with chromium had better hot corrosion behavior than the Al coating. The weakest performance can be attributed to Al coating and the best performance to CrAl-E coating.

The performance of coatings in the condition of type I hot corrosion

Al slurry aluminide coating showed the lowest resistance to type I hot corrosion, so the percentage of the lost thickness of the coating is around 80%. The low resistance of this coating against hot corrosion is due to its inability to form a protective shell like Al2O3. In the stable oxidation stage of this coating, destructive oxides are formed, which cause the protective oxide layer to completely dissolve during the type I hot corrosion test. After 70 h of type I hot corrosion test, the thickness loss of CrAl-P and CrAl-E coatings is less than 15%. It was found that in cases where the concentration of chromium in the upper layer is more than 8% by weight, the oxidation of nickel is limited and the oxidation of chromium and aluminum is prioritized. As a result, firstly, there is no trace of salt precipitate layers among the hot corrosion products of these coatings, and secondly, thin and discontinuous Al2O3 shells and oxide sub-shells are formed from a mixture of Al2O3 and Cr2O3 oxides. Therefore, adding sufficient amounts of chromium to the slurry aluminide coatings helps to form Al2O3 and Cr2O3, all of these oxides are more resistant to acid dissolution than NiO and NiAl2O4.

Conclusion

In this study, chromium was incorporated into aluminide coatings using two distinct methods powder chromizing and electroplating to investigate its influence on the structural characteristics and high-temperature hot corrosion resistance in comparison to conventional aluminide coatings. The experimental results revealed that the addition of chromium did not significantly alter the aluminum content in the outer layer of the chromium-aluminide coatings (CrAl-E and CrAl-P), which remained comparable to that of the standard aluminide coating (Al), averaging above 30 wt%. Furthermore, the chromium content in the outer layer of both CrAl-E and CrAl-P coatings exceeded 20 wt%. Microstructural analysis indicated that both CrAl-E and CrAl-P coatings exhibited a three-layer structure, similar to the Al coating. These layers consisted of an aluminum-rich β-NiAl phase and a chromium-enriched region within the NiAl matrix, observed across all coating variants. Following a 70-hour high-temperature hot corrosion test, the CrAl-E and CrAl-P coatings demonstrated superior resistance to Type I hot corrosion compared to the conventional Al coating. Quantitative assessment of coating degradation revealed that the Al coating exhibited the poorest corrosion resistance, with approximately 80% coating deterioration, whereas the CrAl-E coating displayed the highest resistance, with only 14.2% degradation. The findings confirm that chromium modification via both powder chromizing and electroplating was effectively achieved in the CrAl-P and CrAl-E coatings, enhancing their structural integrity and corrosion resistance.
