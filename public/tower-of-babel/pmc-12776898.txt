Impact of Personalized Parenteral Nutrition on Inflammatory Markers and Clinical Outcomes in Critically Ill Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Othman Gatar, Atheer A Arishi, Maryam A Sultan, Mohanned M Gatar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsPersonalized parenteral nutrition (PPN) is a customized strategy to address the individual metabolic and nutritional requirements of the critically ill patients, especially in cases where enteral nutrition (EN) is not possible. However, available evidence regarding its effectiveness and safety is still inconclusive. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the impact of PPN on the clinical outcome of critically ill patients, including its effect on their length of stay in the intensive care unit (ICU), morbidity, and mortality.MethodologyA systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane databases. Information pertinent to the question was retrieved from the selected studies by using a structured data extraction form. Included studies were those that had assessed the impact on clinical outcomes of PPN in critically ill patients. Data were synthesized using a random-effects meta-analysis model. An odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was used as the pooled effect size.ResultsThe meta-analysis included seven studies. The clinical risk was significantly higher in PPN with an OR of 1.24 (95% CI: 1.10–1.39; p < 0.01). Although there were some studies that showed an improvement in nutritional markers and decreased inflammation, the overall impact on mortality and the length of stay in the ICU was inconsistent with considerable clinical and methodological heterogeneity (I² = 81%).ConclusionPersonalized parenteral nutrition has demonstrated improved benefits in tailored nutritional support for critically ill patients. The evidence, however, has shown mixed clinical outcomes, and its effect on mortality and morbidity has been inconclusive. Future research is needed to optimize the composition of PPN formulations and evaluate the long-term effects of this intervention.How to cite this articleGatar O, Arishi AA, Sultan MA, Gatar MM. Highlights

This is the first systematic review to assess the impact of personalized parenteral nutrition (PPN) on clinical outcomes in critically ill patients. Personalized parenteral nutrition demonstrates promising specificity and moderate sensitivity. Despite inconclusive effects on clinical outcomes, the study highlights the emerging role of individualized nutrition in critical care, paving the way for future innovations.

Introduction

Critically ill patients face significant metabolic stress, necessitating precise nutrition support to improve clinical outcomes and reduce morbidity.1,2 In intensive care, increased catabolism leads to muscle and fat tissue breakdown, resulting in a negative nitrogen balance and malnutrition.3,4 Malnutrition is a common issue in intensive care unit (ICU) patients, occurring in 30–50% of cases and contributing to increased hospital stays, infection rates, and mortality.5 Adequate nutritional interventions are crucial for supporting immune function, modulating inflammatory responses, and facilitating recovery in ICU settings.6,7

Although enteral nutrition (EN) may be the form of nutrition most preferred in critically ill patients because it has a positive influence on gut integrity and minimizes the risk of infection, EN is not always feasible.8 Some of the common conditions that make EN less acceptable to critically ill patients include gastrointestinal intolerance, bowel ischemia, and the need for mechanical ventilation, especially among patients with more critical levels of gastrointestinal dysfunction.9 The guidelines of ESPEN and ASPEN suggest the use of parenteral nutrition (PN) in cases when EN is not tolerated or contraindicated, and metabolic parameters should be closely monitored to avoid complications.8,10 Large trials like the EPaNIC and CALORIES studies have argued the timing and route of the nutrition delivery, which underscores the complexity and context-specific nature of nutritional support in the ICU.11,12 Parenteral nutrition is the sole option in such patients that provides the delivery of nutrients intravenously and is independent of the gastrointestinal system.13,14 Based on research reports from recent years, it states that PN, mainly individualized PN, could be provided to such critically ill patients in the ICU setting who may not be fed via enteral routes.15

Personalized parenteral nutrition has become one of the approaches that aim to individualize the macronutrient composition and caloric provision to the needs of specific patients in this changing environment.15,16 Personalization can be founded on caloric requirements of a patient, biochemical markers (e.g., inflammatory cytokines), clinical course, or metabolic signature. A PPN individualizes nutritional support to the particular needs and metabolic conditions of each patient. Intensive care unit patients have heterogeneous nutritional requirements influenced by their age, gender, underlying health conditions, and metabolic status.17 The specific energy needs and the rate of protein catabolism, for example, can vary greatly for a patient in response to the stress of a critical illness.18 The macronutrient ratio for PPN can be tailored to meet the metabolic needs of the patient, so it could put more emphasis on carbohydrates, proteins, and fats in different ways, based on a patient's unique metabolic profile.19 Such a tailored approach ensures that patients are able to get enough energy for their physiological needs, as well as protect the body's lean mass without further breaking down the muscles, which often characterize the course of critical illness.19,20 Such personalization of nutrient content helps benefit clinical outcomes by preserving immune function, reducing inflammation, and allowing faster and broader recovery.21

Critically ill patients face challenges in managing systemic inflammation due to increased pro-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). Personalized parenteral nutrition can reduce these inflammatory markers and improve patient's immune and metabolic systems.22,23 Personalized parenteral nutrition also supports protein synthesis, muscle mass, and lower total catabolic activity, enabling quicker rehabilitation, shorter stays in the ICU, and potentially fewer infections and deaths.24,25 Future optimization of nutritional intervention may involve using PPN in combination with evolving care protocols in the ICU.26–28

Previous reviews have compared PN and EN or timing of initiation, but few have focused on individualized PN strategies.29,30 This systematic review and meta-analysis, therefore, evaluates the impact of PPN on inflammatory markers, mortality, and recovery outcomes in critically ill patients, aiming to clarify its clinical significance and identify gaps for future research.

Methodology

Research Strategy

This search was conducted systematically on various databases, such as PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. The search strategy for the studies includes relevant medical subject headings (MeSH) and keywords in relation to population, intervention, comparison, and outcomes of interest for studies on the effect of PPN on critically ill patients. The search terms used were these search phrases: “Personalized parenteral nutrition, “critical care,” “Nutritional status,” and clinical recovery.” A search protocol was designed to ensure there can be replicability along with accuracy in the set search criteria, and the study to be included within our objectives.

Study identification and selection rely on the PICO methodology for framing the search criteria and conducting selections.

Population

All critically ill patients, regardless of the nature of their illness, admitted to the ICUs.

Intervention

Personalized parenteral nutrition tailored to the metabolic needs of the patient.

Comparison

Standard parenteral nutrition, EN, or no personalized nutritional interventions.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this review was the change in inflammatory markers, specifically IL-6, C-reactive protein (CRP), and TNF-α. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality, length of stay in the ICU, nutritional status indicators such as serum albumin levels and nitrogen balance, and the incidence of infections. These predefined outcomes were used to structure both the evidence synthesis and the interpretation of results. Those studies relevant to the literature, including those cohort studies and case–control studies which reported data on the effectiveness of PPN in critical care, were identified with those criteria.

Study Selection Criteria

Strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were adopted to ensure that only consistent and relevant studies were featured in the review; therefore, only high-quality appropriate studies were included. The titles and abstracts of the articles were reviewed for relevance by two independent reviewers. Thereafter, full-text articles were reviewed against the selection criteria. Any disagreements arising from the process were resolved by discussing them or referring them to a third reviewer (Table 1).

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Information pertinent to the question was retrieved from the selected studies by using a structured data extraction form, including the name of the author, publication year, study design, sample size, demographic characteristics of participants, details of intervention, and outcome data. Data extraction was independently carried out by two reviewers for consistency and accuracy; any disagreements were resolved either by discussion or third-party review. Pre-formatted data extraction form was used in capturing data concerning the following in all included studies: Demographics, interventions, and outcomes of all included studies. All the articles found from the database searches were retrieved and entered into the reference manager EndNote database using the imported option, excluding duplicates and any titles in foreign languages. Finally, in the EndNote file, the article records were uploaded to Rayyan, a web-based software that filters articles based on title and abstract. Assessment of Methodological Quality

The review included articles that underwent rigorous evaluation using QUADAS-2 software to ensure alignment with the research design. The QUADAS is a frequently used and highly reliable tool for systematically evaluating the quality and risk of bias in studies analyzing the impact of PPN on clinical outcomes. The QUADAS tool enables the assessment of the risk of bias and concerns about the applicability of the findings at one or more of the key domains integral to investigations, qualifying it as a coherent and comprehensive approach to quality assessment. Differences in opinions were resolved through discussions or different assessors. The Joanna Briggs Institute's critical evaluation methods assessed various study designs, including observational studies, systematic reviews, and RCTs. A sensitivity analysis is conducted using the META DISC tool to establish whether the identified biases may influence the results. META ONLINE and Tools were used in the overall general meta-analytic review. Where feasible, a meta-analysis online tool was conducted, and I² was used to describe the percentage of heterogeneity among the studies included.

Study Protocol

This systematic review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420250656276) and was published on February 24, 2025. The registration ensures transparency and prevents duplication of research efforts. The review adheres to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines to maintain methodological rigor, reproducibility, and reporting accuracy. While registration in databases such as PROSPERO offers benefits in transparency and reducing duplication, the nature of this meta-analysis focused on real-time analysis and a dynamic refinement process based on ongoing study selection and risk assessments, which led to a more flexible approach. The included studies ranged from 2009 to 2023, encompassing both early and recent evidence on the application of PPN in critical care.

Results

The literature search yielded seven potentially relevant articles from PubMed, Springer, Taylor, and Google Scholar databases, with a few additional articles identified from the reference lists of relevant articles. After removing duplicate studies and non-English publications, 1,063 records were selected for further analysis. Upon review of keywords and abstracts, 452 publications were deemed outside the scope of this review. Subsequently, a thorough assessment based on relevance, study design, and quality assessment identified the seven most relevant studies for inclusion in this review. The studies in Table 2 are reviewed, addressing several outcomes that were potentially associated with PN interventions, and were centrally concerned with such effects as reduction in inflammation, death, and the impact of calories on the recovery of patients in intensive care.

The review summarizes seven studies; demographic and outcome information from all included studies was retrieved systematically by using a pre-prepared template. Table 2 and shows the summary of included studies through the PICO framework.

Primary Outcome: Inflammatory Markers

Among the included studies, only Berlana et al. directly evaluated inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and CRP), reporting a significant reduction with omega-3-enriched PPN compared with standard PN. Singh et al. and Sehgal and Singh measured biochemical parameters such as serum albumin and nitrogen balance, which may indirectly reflect inflammation, but did not assess cytokine levels. The remaining studies focused primarily on clinical outcomes such as mortality, infection rates, and ICU length of stay. Therefore, quantitative data on inflammatory markers were available from a single study, highlighting the limited evidence base and the need for further targeted research on the immunometabolic effects of PPN in critically ill patients.

Secondary Outcomes

Mortality

Pooled analysis of seven studies indicated that PPN was associated with an increased risk of mortality (odds ratio [OR]: 1.24, 95% Confidence interval (CI): 1.10–1.39, p < 0.01), although the heterogeneity across studies was substantial (I² = 81%).

ICU Length of Stay

The findings were variable, with some studies reporting shorter stays among patients receiving individualized PN and others reporting no meaningful difference.

Nutritional Status

Nutritional adequacy was better maintained in studies that measured biochemical parameters such as albumin and nitrogen balance, though improvements were not uniform across all cohorts.

Infection Rate

The effect of PPN on infection rates was inconsistent, with certain trials suggesting a reduction in infectious complications while others found no significant difference compared with standard PN.

Meta-analysis Findings

The section estimates the overall effect size through a random-effects meta-analysis. For the specified outcome of interest for OR, the result for the OR was 1.24 with a 95% CI of (1.10, 1.39). This shows a significant effect throughout all studies under consideration. I² = 81%, τ ² = 0.0126, and p < 0.01 show significant heterogeneity among the study results. The high degree of heterogeneity required further exploration through subgroup and sensitivity analyses to understand the source.

The heterogeneity of clinical aspects was observed in the included studies in a number of ways. The populations of patients were different in terms of underlying conditions, severity of the illness, and ICU conditions. The timing, duration, and composition of PPN (e.g., use of omega-3 enrichment, individualized caloric targets) were variably interventional. Comparators were also standard PN, delayed PN, or no nutritional support, and outcome definitions (e.g., mortality at 28 vs 30 days, or inflammatory markers) were not standardized. Such clinical differences were probably the cause of the statistical heterogeneity (I² = 81%) and need to be taken into account when interpreting the pooled results.

Bias and Quality Evaluation

QUADAS and ROBIVUS were applied to quality assessment relating to domains like selection, performance, detection, and attrition bias. A little variation in detected bias in studies included occurred mostly due to detection and performance bias, since some interventions were open-label. Sensitivity analysis showed that the exclusion of low-quality studies would not have affected the bottom-line findings, as the pooled effect size was considered robust.

Narrative Synthesis

An integrative or narrative synthesis was used for studies that did not contain quantitative data amenable to meta-analysis. Narrative synthesis highlighted qualitative findings-features of the trends related to the decrease in inflammation and mortality of certain interventions on nutrition.

Risk Bias Assessment and Publication Bias

The assessment of bias in the selected studies was conducted across four important domains: Patient selection (D1), index test (D2), reference standard (D3), and flow and timing (D4). The overall risk of bias was low to moderate across studies, with some concerns in flow/timing domains (see Supplementary Figs 1 and 2). The majority of studies demonstrated a low risk of bias in patient selection, with six out of 10 studies exhibiting low risk across all domains. However, there were some concerns noted, particularly regarding the reference standard and flow and timing, where a few studies raised issues that could potentially affect the reliability of their findings. Overall, while most studies revealed low bias, the presence of some concerns in specific areas suggests that caution is warranted in interpreting the results. This variability highlights the need for careful consideration of the methodologies employed in these studies, as biases may influence the conclusions.

Comprehensive analysis by funnel plot

Figure 3 shows the funnel plot of included studies assessing PPN and mortality (secondary outcome). The majority of the studies lie close to the line of no effect, indicating either similar findings or selective publication of studies that show neutral or minor effects (HR = 1). The pooled HR of mortality was 1.24 (95% CI: 1.10–1.39; p = 0.00034), which means that the hazard of death increased by 24% in relation to PPN and is statistically significant. However, an outlier study by Sehgal and Singh reports a much higher HR with low precision, indicating large effects on outcomes but less reliability.34 This could mean that studies showing significant or extreme effects of PPN on clinical recovery and inflammatory markers in critically ill patients are less frequently published, skewing the meta-analysis results toward neutrality.

Although funnel plots are commonly used to assess publication bias, their reliability is limited when fewer than 10 studies are included in a meta-analysis. In this study, the funnel plot was generated to provide visual transparency; however, due to the small number of studies (n = 7), any observed asymmetry should be interpreted with caution, as it may result from random variation rather than true publication bias. There was significant heterogeneity among the studies, and the I² value of 81% and Cochran Q = 17.20 (df = 6, p = 0.0086) results showed that the differences between the findings of the studies were unlikely to be caused by chance.

This heterogeneity could be explained by various aspects, such as patient populations (severity of illness or comorbidities), time and duration of PPN administration, nutrient formulation (e.g., omega-3 or BCAA), differences in comparator groups (standard PN, EN, or no nutrition), and time of outcome measurement. Variability of effect sizes could also have been attributed to inconsistent study designs and methodological quality. Overall, the present meta-analysis suggests an increased risk for adverse outcomes following PPN in the setting of critically ill patients, although such heterogeneity between the studies exists that it remains necessary for further research for more clarification over the true effect of PPN and to discover possible subgroups of patients benefiting from the intervention.

Discussion

This systematic review was designed with the primary objective of assessing the effect of PPN on inflammatory markers in critically ill patients, with secondary objectives that included its influence on mortality, ICU length of stay, nutritional status, and infection rates. The evidence suggests that PPN may reduce inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and CRP in selected populations, indicating a potential role in modulating the immune response. However, the pooled analysis also showed an association between PPN and increased mortality, alongside inconsistent findings for ICU stay, nutritional indices, and infection risk. These results highlight the heterogeneity of the available studies and underscore the need for further standardized, high-quality trials to clarify which subgroups of patients are most likely to benefit from PPN.

In critical care medicine, some of the most recent breakthroughs include PN therapy.10,39 Optimizing the delivery of nutrients and thus the metabolic support of the critically ill might be one promising way with PPN.40 Evidence regarding the effectiveness and safety of PPN is, however, inconclusive.41 The current study was aimed at systematically reviewing the literature about the clinical outcomes of PPN in critically ill patients.

The results of this review can be discussed in the context of the main RCTs that examined the nutritional approach in critically ill patients. The CALORIES trial, a comparison study between early PN and EN, has not shown a significant difference in 30-day mortality, and again, it may be that the route of nutrition is less important than adequacy and timing.42 Similarly, the NUTRIREA-2 trial did not find any mortality advantage of early EN compared to PN, but the EN group had increased digestive complications.32 Conversely, the present meta-analysis revealed a statistically significant relationship between the use of PPN and the higher risk of mortality (HR = 1.24), which might be attributed to the differences in patient selection, time of intervention, or individuality of PN used in the studies included in the meta-analysis. These variations emphasize the necessity of more specific studies that could help find subgroups that could potentially be helped by individual PN strategies.

The meta-analysis has produced a high statistical heterogeneity (I² = 81%), which suggests a large variation in the results of the studies, probably because of differences in study design, PPN formulation, study population, time of intervention, and measurement of the outcome. This heterogeneity restricts the generalizability of pooled estimates, and this should be interpreted with caution. Also, in order to visually evaluate the publication bias, a funnel plot was included, but the number of studies (n = 7) is lower than the recommended amount to have a reliable measure of publication bias. Therefore, there is no way that the possibility of publication bias can be stated or eliminated. Other limitations at the level of studies, such as a small sample size, the absence of blinding, the variability of the outcome definition (e.g., mortality at different times of the day), and the possibility of selective reporting, also lead to the ambiguity of the entire results. These methodological issues point toward the necessity of well-designed, large-scale trials with standard protocols to further assess the role of PPN in critically ill patients.

Although the existing evidence base is associated with significant methodological shortcomings, this review is an extensive synthesis of the best evidence available and identifies key gaps in knowledge that require further research to elucidate the clinical value of PPN in ICUs. The results of the study provide insights into the impact of PPN on the clinical outcomes of critically ill patients. There is much promise in PPN to optimize nutritional support, thereby improving patient outcomes. However, results from this analysis show that caution is needed and more research needs to be done.

Impact of PPN on Clinical Outcomes

The ROC and SROC curves indicate a moderate to high accuracy for the identification of potentially benefiting patients by PPN. The observed trend toward shorter ICU stays in some PPN groups may partly result from increased early mortality rather than faster recovery. The pooled estimate (OR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.10–1.39) indicated a statistically significant increase in mortality among PPN recipients. This suggests that reduced length of stay should be interpreted cautiously, as it may represent premature death rather than clinical improvement. Nonetheless, the variability found among different studies calls for fine-tuning of the existing criteria and taking patient-specific factors into consideration for improved discrimination. Future work may therefore focus on optimally selecting cutoff values in terms of various biomarkers and clinical indicators to maximize selection accuracy with PPN. Furthermore, standardization of the criteria for patient selection and the development of PPN may also contribute to clinical practice uniformity.

Clinical Implication

A few studies have suggested a benefit of PPN, while others show no effect or even adverse effects. The high degree of heterogeneity among the studies points to the complex nature of the relationship between PPN and patient outcomes. The heterogeneity of the included studies is high (I² = 81%), which is why the interpretation of the pooled effects is complicated. It is an indicator of differences in study protocols, PPN formulations, baseline nutritional status, and differences in outcome assessment, and this necessitates more standardized, high-quality trials. Some of the potential causes of heterogeneity include differences in patient populations (e.g., severity of illness, comorbidities).43

The results of this meta-analysis are to be viewed within the framework of the existing nutrition recommendations. Both the ESPEN (2019) and the ASPEN (2016) guidelines suggest the use of PN in cases where EN is applicable, but these guidelines discourage its generalized early application in patients who have a functional gastrointestinal tract.8,10 These guidelines are founded on evidence of large RCTs and focus on individualized nutritional plans that are based on energy goals, refeeding risk, and metabolic tolerance. The articles reviewed in this paper have involved individualized parenteral methods based on the needs of the patients, which is partly in line with the guidelines that focus on individualized care. Nevertheless, the association between PPN and higher mortality that we have observed in the analysis speaks to the necessity of a careful application, close monitoring, and patient selection, which are advised in ESPEN and ASPEN guidelines.

The application of PPN in a low-resource ICU is rather challenging. These are restricted access to high-tech nutritional formulations, untrained clinical nutrition teams, variable laboratory support to monitor metabolism, and financial limitations. Even standardized PN can be unevenly available in such environments. Although the idea of PPN is attractive, particularly to provide support to the needs of individuals, its regular application might not be possible without infrastructural and workforce enhancements. Thus, in low-resource ICUs, the focus should be on the basic nutritional adequacy by the available means and the simplified or step-by-step ways of personalization, depending on the available data about the patient.

Limitations and Future Research

This study offers an in-depth synthesis of existing evidence on PPN in critically ill patients, with a variety of outcomes such as mortality, inflammation, and ICU stay. Meta-analysis enables the pooling of effect estimates of various studies, which provides a useful understanding of clinical trends and points out areas that require more specific research. However, some limitations of the study, such as significant heterogeneity among studies, potential quality of included studies, limitations of long-term data being available, generalizability, and the possibility of publication bias, exist. Heterogeneity may further be contributed to by differences among patients' populations, different study designs, and differing intervention protocols. Although a funnel plot was provided to complete the work, the small sample of studies (n = 7) restricts its credibility. Thus, the conclusions regarding publication bias are to be regarded as tentative. The included studies might vary in terms of their quality, affecting the general results. The generalizability of this review may be somewhat limited to specific patient populations and settings included in studies. Still, with these limitations in place, it could help generate valuable information regarding the potential benefits and risks of PPN in critically ill patients. Future studies should be focused on those limitations so that the base of evidence about PPN in clinical practice can improve.

Future studies should be well-designed, with sufficiently powered RCTs of standardized PN vs PPN approaches in critically ill populations. Such studies ought to have well-defined personalization protocols, e.g., caloric requirements depending on metabolic markers or disease severity, and evaluate long-term outcomes beyond mortality as the sole outcome. Also, further stratified analyses are required to examine the possibility that certain subgroups of patients with baseline malnutrition, systemic inflammation, or multi-organ failure can benefit or be harmed more by PPN. Although the present meta-analysis failed to conclusively determine the existence of a favorable subgroup, certain studies indicated the presence of a favorable subgroup in terms of improved inflammatory markers or nutritional profiles of certain patient groups, which may represent a potential signal of benefit that needs to be specifically tested.

Conclusion

The findings of this review suggest that while PPN represents an important step toward individualized metabolic support, its clinical benefits remain uncertain. Evidence from the included studies indicates inconsistent effects on inflammatory markers and a potential signal toward increased mortality. Therefore, PPN should be applied judiciously, with careful monitoring and patient selection, rather than as a routine intervention in critically ill patients. Further high-quality RCTs are warranted to clarify its safety, identify subgroups that may benefit, and establish evidence-based guidelines for its implementation.
