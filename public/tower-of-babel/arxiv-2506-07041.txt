Abstract
. User reporting systems are central to addressing interpersonal conflicts and protecting users from harm in online spaces, particularly those with heightened privacy expectations. However, users often express frustration at their lack of insight and input into the reporting process. Drawing on offline legal literature, we trace these frustrations to the inquisitorial nature of today’s online reporting systems, where moderators lead evidence gathering and case development. In contrast, adversarial models can grant users greater control and thus are better for procedural justice and privacy protection, despite their increased risks of system abuse. This motivates us to explore the potential of incorporating adversarial practices into online reporting systems. Through literature review, formative interviews, and threat modeling, we find a rich design space for empowering users to collect and present their evidence while mitigating potential abuse in the reporting process. In particular, we propose designs that minimize the amount of information shared for reporting purposes, as well as supporting evidence authentication. Finally, we discuss how our findings can inform new cryptographic tools and new efforts to apply comparative legal frameworks to online moderation.



1.
Introduction

While social media enables people around the world to communicate with each other, these interactions can also lead to interpersonal conflicts
(
Levy et al. 2022
;
Im et al. 2018
)
.
Sometimes these conflicts can escalate into online harassment and cause great emotional harm
(
Thomas et al. 2021
)
.
A 2021 survey found that 41% of Americans reported personally experiencing harassment or bullying online
(
League 2022
)
.
In response, moderators often rely on automated algorithms to proactively detect such incidents
(
Bickert 2018
;
Chandrasekharan et al. 2019
)
.
However, when moderators cannot access users’ interactions, such as those on end-to-end encrypted (E2EE) platforms, or in private communications between users, user reporting becomes the primary means of surfacing problematic behavior
(
Pfefferkorn 2022
;
Kamara et al. 2022
)
.
In a typical user reporting system, a user can report another user by flagging their inappropriate content, after which a moderator reviews the case and decides whether to punish the accused or dismiss the report.
In this way, reporting systems play an important role in providing protection and emotional support for victims of online harm
(
Schoenebeck et al. 2023
;
Im et al. 2022
)
.

However, many users express deep frustrations about online reporting systems
(
Kou and Gui 2021
;
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Aliapoulios et al. 2021
)
, to the extent that nearly half of those who experience harassment choose not to report at all
(
Center 2021
)
.
A common complaint is users’ lack of insight and input into the reporting process.
To many, reporting systems feel like black boxes: they passively receive reports and eventually output decisions, offering little explanation about how these decisions are made and few opportunities for users to voice their concerns
(
Kou and Gui 2021
;
America 2023
)
.
Reported users may not even know they have been reported until a punishment is delivered.
As a result, users often perceive these systems as lacking procedural justice, which fuels suspicion about moderator bias or inattention
(
Tyler 2003
)
.
An additional complaint involves the privacy tradeoffs and loss of control users face when filing a report
(
wang2023reportingare
)
.
The inherent power imbalance that moderators have over users enables moderators to gather additional context beyond what the reporter explicitly shares, justified as necessary for informed decision-making.
For instance, many platforms automatically collect not only the reported message but also its surrounding conversation, which might include sensitive information that users prefer to keep private
(
Abu-Salma et al. 2017
;
Ackerman et al. 1999
)
.
Such unchecked access can be especially problematic in online spaces with high privacy expectations, such as end-to-end encrypted (E2EE) messaging platforms
(
Kamara et al. 2022
;
Pfefferkorn 2022
)
, voice chat services
(
Jiang et al. 2019
)
, or ephemeral messaging platforms
(
Kotfila 2014
)
, exactly the kinds of spaces where reporting is often the only line of defense.
In settings where moderators are technically blocked from accessing contextual information, users are now faced with the dilemma: either share more of their private conversation to support their report or risk bad outcomes due to a lack of compelling evidence
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
.

To address these failures of online reporting systems, we draw inspiration from offline judicial systems, which have long explored different procedural designs to navigate various trade-offs.
Notably, today’s online reporting systems most closely resemble an
inquisitorial model
found in civil law countries like Germany, where judges appointed by higher authorities play a major role in gathering evidence and shaping the case
(
Damaska 1986
)
.
In contrast, common law jurisdictions such as the United States follow an
adversarial model
, where opposing parties have greater control over investigating the case and presenting evidence before an impartial judge
(
Damaska 1972
;
Damaska 1986
)
.
Legal literature has offered extensive discussion about the advantages and disadvantages of these two models
(
Damaska 1972
;
Damaska 1986
;
Kim 2014
)
.
Adversarial models are found to be better for procedural justice and privacy than inquisitorial models because they give parties greater control over the process
(
Sevier 2014
;
Thibaut and Walker 1975
)
and more say in what evidence is collected and disclosed
(
Stacy 1991
;
Findley 2011
)
.
However, this increased control also brings risks: people might abuse that control for their self-interest
(
Damaska 1972
;
Kim 2014
)
.

In this work, we explore how a more adversarial online reporting system could better ensure procedural justice and respect user privacy, while still guarding against abuse of the process.
Our primary challenge is to adapt offline adversarial practices to the unique constraints of online settings.
For example, most offline adversarial systems involve the accused party in the investigation as a constitutional right.
But such involvement can invite more abuse in online settings, as the reported person could infer who reported them and retaliate in a way that is difficult for platforms to deter
(
Take et al. 2024
)
.
Additionally, offline parties have access to robust mechanisms for evidence collection and verification, such as forensic tools and legal procedures like subpoenas. In contrast, online users have to navigate the complex technical affordances of online platforms in gathering trustworthy evidence.
For instance, ephemeral conversations such as voice chats or self-destructing messages allow harmful content to vanish without a trace
(
Jiang et al. 2019
)
, and the ease of switching between multiple accounts or platforms helps malicious actors evade accountability
(
Cai and Wohn 2021
)
.
Arguably, adversarial approaches are especially well-suited to online spaces with heightened privacy expectations, where users are more motivated to demand control over the reporting process and may be distrustful of the platform.
Still, we argue that these approaches have the potential to improve reporting systems across all kinds of platforms.

To better understand the current inquisitorial nature of online reporting systems, we first reviewed literature on how different reporting systems work today, and supplemented those findings with formative interviews with 5 moderators and 7 users who had experienced reporting or being reported on Discord.
We also reviewed literature that compares inquisitorial and adversarial legal models, as well as their offline legal practices.
This prepared us to investigate how to transform each step of a reporting process to be more adversarial and identify the key design questions along the way.
In particular, we conducted threat modeling to evaluate the potential for user interface (UI) bounded attacks in our design ideas
(
Xiong and Lagerström 2019
)
.

Equipped with an adversarial lens to examine online reporting, we find a rich design space for empowering users to collect and present their evidence while still mitigating potential abuse of the justice procedure.
First, when determining what to disclose, users may wish to withhold parts of a conversation to protect their privacy, but moderators may worry that users selectively present evidence to favor themselves.
Drawing on data minimization practices
(
Kulshrestha and Mayer 2021
;
Biega et al. 2020
;
Orekondy et al. 2018
)
, we outline a design space for
evidence disclosure
that ensures shared conversations are adequate for, but limited to, moderation purposes
(
Regulation 2016
)
.
Second, even if users do not selectively disclose their conversations, they still need to prove to moderators that their submitted private conversations happened, a process known in offline settings as
evidence authentication
.
While users often rely on screenshots for authentication purposes
(
Sultana et al. 2021
)
, these methods are prone to abuse—for example, malicious users can forge conversations by mimicking usernames and avatars of another user.
We explore how an enhanced message forwarding functionality could enable platform-side authentication, and how web automation techniques can help users collect trustworthy evidence by preserving the chain of custody
(
Pu et al. 2023
;
Yeh et al. 2009
)
.
Finally, we propose ephemeral reporting to extend accountability to ephemeral conversations without significantly compromising their intended privacy.

Our paper is structured as follows.
After reviewing prior literature, we first present a theoretical analysis of how the inquisitorial nature of current reporting systems contributes to their failures.
We then examine the benefits and challenges of incorporating more adversarial elements into online settings.
Next, we take a deep dive into the evidence collection stage as the core of the reporting process and map out the design space that grants users greater control in presenting their evidence while safeguarding against potential abuse.
Finally, we conclude with design implications for future research.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Interpersonal Conflicts and Online Harassment

The internet has enabled people worldwide to connect, converse, and collaborate with each other on a large scale. These interactions can also end in interpersonal conflicts
(
Im et al. 2018
;
Levy et al. 2022
)
, and even escalate into online harassment.
Considerable research has established the pervasiveness of hate on social media, particularly for users from marginalized communities
(
Center 2021
;
Thomas et al. 2021
;
Ashktorab and Vitak 2016
)
.
A survey in 2021 found that 41% of Americans reported personally experiencing varying degrees of harassment and bullying online
(
League 2022
)
.
These abusive behaviors include hate speech, non-consensual sharing of intimate photos
(
Batool et al. 2024
)
, stalking, doxxing
(
Douglas 2016
)
, name-calling and insults, impersonation, and public shaming.
In particular, many forms of online harassment involve the misuse of private information, partly because nearly half of all victims reported knowing their harasser, including acquaintances, family members, and ex-romantic partners
(
Center 2017
;
Aliapoulios et al. 2021
)
.

Online platforms and communities have developed various mechanisms to address online harassment and resolve conflicts between users.
For instance, moderators adopt automated algorithms
(
Bickert 2018
;
Chandrasekharan et al. 2019
)
or manually monitor user interactions to identify incidents that violate their norms and then punish offenders.
In contrast, when moderators cannot access users’ interactions, user reporting becomes the primary means of surfacing problematic behavior
(
Pfefferkorn 2022
;
Kamara et al. 2022
)
.
For example, platform moderators on end-to-end encrypted (E2EE) platforms such as Signal, WhatsApp, and Facebook Messenger cannot access users’ conversations.
Similarly, community moderators on Discord cannot view direct messages between members, and those on Slack cannot access private channels.
These are also the spaces where users have heightened expectations of privacy, posing challenges for moderators in balancing moderation needs and protecting users’ privacy.

2.2.
How Online Reporting Systems Work Today

User reporting is a critical mechanism for protecting victims of online harassment, particularly in more severe or persistent cases
(
Thomas et al. 2021
;
Machackova et al. 2013
)
.
Most online reporting systems follow a similar workflow: users first report contentious behaviors and then fill in a form for more details, which is then reviewed by human moderators or automated tools.
Nevertheless, the details of such a workflow can differ widely across platforms and communities, as a result of their content moderation philosophies
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
and technical affordances. As Crawford and Gillespie argued, "its simplicity belies a tangle of system designs, multiple actors and intentions, assertions and emotions"
(
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
)
.

First, the adjudication of user reports can vary widely across systems. Some platforms rely on human moderators to review reports and make final decisions
(
Roberts 2016
;
Adrian Chen 2014
)
, while others use automated moderation systems to prioritize reports or assist human reviewers
(
Halfaker and Geiger 2020
;
Koshy et al. 2024
)
. In the extreme, user reports may simply serve as input for automated algorithms—for example, the end-to-end encrypted (E2EE) platform Signal only prompts a humanity check after an account has been reported multiple times
(
BleepingComputer 2021
)
. While automation can be effective in addressing clear-cut cases of harassment, our work focuses on how human moderators investigate more complex and context-dependent reports.

These human moderators operate within reporting systems at two levels: platforms and communities.
Here,
communities
refer to groups of multiple chat rooms, such as a Slack workspace
(
Slack [n. d.]
)
, a Discord server
(
Discord [n. d.]
)
, or a WhatsApp community
(
WhatsApp 2022a
)
.
Community moderators typically have access to group chats within the community, but most do not have access to private group chats or direct messages between members.
In contrast, some platforms do not have a layer of communities, including Signal, Facebook Messenger, and Telegram.
At the platform level, nearly all platforms maintain reporting systems that enable users to send unwanted messages to platform moderators
(
Discord [n. d.]
;
FaceBook [n. d.]
)
, who are employed to review user reports according to platform-wide policies
(
Roberts 2016
;
Adrian Chen 2014
)
.
By contrast, each community needs to establish ad hoc channels to receive user reports–for example, direct messages, dedicated bots, or emails in Discord communities
(
Discord 2022
)
.
Community moderators are often community members elected or appointed to make community-specific moderation actions
(
Lampe and Resnick 2004
;
Blackburn and Kwak 2014
)
.
When handling complex cases of online harassment, while platform moderators have access to platform-wide information, community moderators can draw on their familiarity with community members or communicate with bystanders to make more informed decisions
(
Jiang et al. 2019
;
Cai and Wohn 2021
)
. Platform moderators, however, have more authority to impose severe penalties, such as account deactivation.
In this work, we examine reporting systems at both levels and explore how their differences shape the design space.

2.3.
Research Efforts to Improve Reporting Systems

Despite their intended role in addressing online harassment, reporting systems are often perceived by users as ineffective, confusing, and time-consuming
(
Kou and Gui 2021
;
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Aliapoulios et al. 2021
)
. Reflecting this frustration, 47% of people who have experienced harassment choose not to report their harasser
(
Center 2021
)
. Instead, many prefer to block or mute offenders, which unfortunately limits platforms’ ability to identify and remove bad actors to prevent further harm to other users
(
Jhaver et al. 2018
;
Thorn 2021
)
. In more extreme cases, victims may resort to publicly exposing their harassers for the sake of public shaming
(
Sultana et al. 2021
)
.

In response, a growing body of research seeks to improve online reporting systems, with particular focus on supporting evidence collection throughout the reporting process. Some tools help victims gather trustworthy records of ongoing harassment
(
Goyal et al. 2022
;
Sultana et al. 2021
)
, while others assist moderators in profiling victims and harassers to inform their decisions
(
Cai and Wohn 2021
;
Im et al. 2020
)
. Researchers have also documented how moderators use bots to track ephemeral messages—often exploited by harassers to avoid detection
(
Kiene et al. 2019
)
.
However, most of these tools address isolated parts of the evidence collection process. In this work, we aim to characterize the broader design space to inspire future research.

Noting the similarities between online reporting systems and offline criminal justice, another line of research has begun applying offline legal frameworks to rethink the design of online reporting systems
(
Fan and Zhang 2020
;
Im et al. 2021
;
Xiao et al. 2023
)
.
Much like online reporting systems, offline justice systems have long faced public criticism for being overly complex, slow, unfair, and lacking respect for victims of crime and the broader public
(
of Western Australia and Martin 1999
)
. In response, legal scholars and practitioners have proposed alternative frameworks to reform justice systems, including restorative justice
(
Braithwaite et al. 2002
)
, therapeutic jurisprudence
(
Daicoff 2006
;
Wexler 1992
)
, and problem-solving courts
(
Freiberg 2001
)
.
Inspired by this shift, there is a growing interest among social computing researchers in applying the theory of restorative justice to online settings.
In particular, researchers criticized the punitive nature of current content moderation systems, as punishment alone is ineffective in deterring harassment and does little to repair the harm experienced by victims
(
Hasinoff et al. 2020
;
Schoenebeck et al. 2021
)
.
Consequently, researchers have explored various restorative justice practices, such as video-offender conferencing
(
Xiao et al. 2023
)
, and apology
(
Ngoc et al. 2025
;
Schoenebeck et al. 2021
)
.
While these approaches focus on post-adjudication interventions such as punishment and rehabilitation, our work focuses on the adjudication process itself, i.e., how decisions are made in the first place.

Additionally, online dispute resolution (ODR) systems represent another line of research that adapts offline trials to resolving conflicts between users online
(
Rule 2016
;
Aouidef et al. 2021
;
Kou and Nardi 2014
)
.
For example, eBay once implemented community courts, where buyers and sellers submitted evidence in turn, and crowdsourced jurors reviewed the evidence to reach a final decision
(
Rule and Nagarajan 2010
)
.
Similarly, Wikipedia established a formal deliberation process for resolving content and policy disputes among editors, inviting the broader community to participate in discussions and deliver a resolution
(
Im et al. 2018
)
.
While these processes can lead to more perceived legitimacy when executed well
(
Fan and Zhang 2020
;
Pan et al. 2022
)
, they are often criticized for being resource-intensive and prone to stalemates
(
Im et al. 2018
)
.
They differ from reporting systems in that they set up a structured trial between opposing parties, whereas reporting systems typically task moderators with investigating the case and rarely involve the reported person.
Nevertheless, the experiences and challenges of ODR systems offer valuable lessons. In this work, we draw on those insights to inform the redesign of online reporting systems.

2.4.
Mapping Offline Inquisitorial and Adversarial Legal Processes onto Online Reporting

In particular, we draw inspiration from the legal concept of adversarial and inquisitorial models of justice systems. In an
inquisitorial model
, the judge appointed by higher authorities assumes an active role in investigating the facts, directing the collection of evidence, and shaping the development of the case.
In comparison, in an
adversarial model
, the opposing parties have the primary responsibility for defining the issues in dispute, investigating the facts, and advancing their cases, whereas the judge plays a neutral, passive role in ensuring procedural fairness
(
Damaska 1972
;
Damaska 1986
)
.
As such, adversarial models are often perceived by users as having higher procedural justice, whereas inquisitorial models are seen as better positioned to identify the truth
(
Sevier 2014
;
Thibaut and Walker 1978
)
.
Inquisitorial systems are widely adopted in continental European countries like Germany and France, whereas adversarial models are commonly used in English-speaking countries like the United States and the United Kingdom.
In reality, there exist no purely inquisitorial or adversarial legal systems; instead, legal systems typically blend elements of both models, representing a spectrum of power distributions between judges and the opposing parties
(
Thibaut et al. 1973
)
.

How a government organizes its justice system often reflects its broader mode of governance.
In
The Faces of Justice and State Authority

(
Damaska 1986
)
, Damaska analyzes this relationship by identifying two key dimensions: the organization of government and the legitimate function of government.
When government authority is centralized and hierarchical, and the government takes an active role in steering society, it tends to adopt an inquisitorial approach, which grants the government more authority to advance its objectives.
In contrast, when governance is decentralized and the government limits its role to only supporting social self-regulation, an adversarial model is preferred, as the government delegates more responsibility to individuals and primarily serves as a neutral dispute resolver.

Drawing on this body of comparative law literature, we argue that online reporting systems predominantly follow an inquisitorial model
.
First, moderators have the utmost authority in gathering evidence, communicating with bystanders, and making the final decision
(
Jiang et al. 2019
;
Cai and Wohn 2021
;
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
)
.
They also maintain intentionally vague community policies to allow flexible interpretation when enforcing these policies
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
.
Second, users have limited input into the reporting process and often complain about its opaqueness and complexity
(
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
;
Kou and Gui 2021
)
.

The inquisitorial nature of online reporting systems has its roots in the autocratic governance structure prevalent on many online platforms
(
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Schneider 2022
)
, where platform and community administrators and moderators have broad privileges over regular users and actively enforce community norms
(
Seering et al. 2019
)
.
While some platforms have begun experimenting with more decentralized governance
(
Jhaver et al. 2023
)
, most platforms and communities still default to an inquisitorial reporting system, which may no longer align with their evolving governance structure.
In this work, we examine the inquisitorial practices within current reporting systems and explore a new design space for incorporating more adversarial elements.

3.
From Inquisitorial to More Adversarial Online Reporting Systems

In this section, we first examine how the inquisitorial nature of online reporting systems contributes to user frustrations today and how these issues could be addressed through an adversarial design. We then explore important considerations for designing a more adversarial reporting system.

3.1.
Methods

To understand the current state of online reporting systems, we first reviewed literature that documents how these systems work in practice across a range of platforms.
We noted that prior work does not cover all relevant aspects, such as how moderators communicate with bystanders or the reported user, or how they validate evidence.
Therefore, we supplemented our literature review with formative interviews with 5 moderators and 7 users who have experience with reporting or being reported on Discord.
We chose the platform Discord due to the relative ease of contacting community moderators as opposed to platform moderators.
In addition, direct messages on Discord are not available to community moderators, so moderators must often do additional work to corroborate or validate evidence when a direct message is reported. Besides, since voice chats are commonly used on Discord, community moderators often need to communicate with bystanders to verify reported incidents from such ephemeral conversations
(
Jiang et al. 2019
)
.
To explore how adversarial practices might be incorporated into online settings, we then conducted a second round of literature review comparing inquisitorial and adversarial legal models regarding their respective strengths, weaknesses, and legal practices.

The following are additional details about our formative interviews.
We recruited participants by directly messaging Discord moderators with recruitment information. If moderators agreed, we also posted the recruitment information on their servers to recruit users.
Interviews were semi-structured and conducted via video calls.
Interviews with moderators lasted 93 minutes on average, and those with users lasted 47 minutes on average.
Participants received a $20 gift card as compensation.
Since we aimed to use these interviews to understand underexplored aspects of online reporting, we conducted a deductive thematic analysis of responses
(
Braun et al. 2019
)
.
This approach allowed us to interpret the data using a series of concepts and topics derived from our first round of literature review.
Recordings were automatically transcribed into text.
The first and second authors then independently performed initial coding, met weekly with the research group to compare and refine themes, and iteratively revised the coding framework through discussion.
In our analysis, we denote our moderator participants as M1–M5 and user participants as U1–U7.

3.2.
Weaknesses of Inquisitorial Reporting Systems Addressed by Adversarial Designs

We begin by summarizing the literature that surfaces problems with inquisitorial-style reporting systems and then explore how these problems can be addressed by adversarial designs.

3.2.1.
Procedural Justice

Many user frustrations with existing reporting systems stem from a lack of procedural justice, an inherent problem of inquisitorial models
(
Sevier 2014
)
.
Here,
procedural justice
is defined as the fairness of the processes by which judges make decisions and exercise authority, as opposed to
substantive justice
, which concerns the fairness of the final decisions
(
Tyler 2006
)
.
Research has shown that perceptions of procedural justice shape people’s views of institutional legitimacy, which in turn influence their willingness to accept decisions and comply with laws
(
Tyler 2003
)
.
Despite its importance, online reporting systems often fail to meet key criteria of procedural justice
(
Leventhal 1980
;
Thibaut and Walker 1975
)
.
For example, users are often limited to fixed vocabularies and options when submitting reports, leaving little room to fully articulate their concerns
(
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
;
Shim and Jhaver 2024
)
.
Worrying that some moderators might be biased, users are also left in the dark about which moderators are assigned to review their cases and how decisions are made
(
Kou and Gui 2021
;
America 2023
)
.
Moreover, the appeal process is often time-consuming and opaque, making it difficult for users to challenge or correct mistaken decisions.

In contrast, adversarial models are widely considered better at ensuring procedural justice than inquisitorial models because they grant opposing parties greater control over the proceedings
(
Sevier 2014
;
Thibaut and Walker 1975
)
.
For instance, parties can decide which evidence they want to present in the court and which witnesses they want to communicate with.

3.2.2.
Privacy Protections

In an inquisitorial-style reporting system, moderators prioritize gathering context-rich evidence to reach fair decisions, which can come at the expense of user privacy
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
.
Nowadays, many incidents of interpersonal conflict and harassment take place in spaces with high privacy expectations, such as E2EE messaging platforms
(
Kamara et al. 2022
;
Pfefferkorn 2022
)
, voice chat services
(
Jiang et al. 2019
)
, ephemeral messaging platforms
(
Kotfila 2014
)
, or simply direct messages between two users.
However, when someone reports a message, moderators often gather additional conversations beyond what the reporter explicitly shares, justifying it as necessary for informed decision-making.
For instance, some E2EE platforms automatically collect not only the reported message but also its surrounding conversation, often without explaining why these extra messages are needed
(
Google [n. d.]
;
FaceBook [n. d.]
)
.
Meanwhile, many harassment cases involve the misuse of private information, ranging from doxxing, where personally identifiable information is publicly disclosed
(
Snyder et al. 2017
;
Douglas 2016
)
, to non-consensual intimate imagery, where intimate photos are shared without permission
(
Henry et al. 2020
)
.
Although such private information can be crucial for moderators to evaluate a report, users are often presented with a binary choice: share their private information or give up their report altogether
(
Abu-Salma et al. 2017
;
Ackerman et al. 1999
)
.

In addition to content disclosure, inquisitorial-style reporting systems often give users little control over whether their identities are anonymized. While moderators rely on account information during their reviewing process, reporting users may fear retaliation if their account information is revealed to the reported party
(
Thorn 2021
;
Moore and McAuliffe 2012
)
. In some extreme cases, platforms have inadvertently shared reporters’ personal information with hostile communities, triggering renewed harassment
(
Take et al. 2024
)
.

Adversarial systems offer stronger privacy protections, as each side can have more say in evidence collection and presentation.
For instance, a party can choose to withhold information that might compromise their privacy if its value as evidence does not outweigh the risks.
Even when the opposing party seeks to introduce such evidence, rules in adversarial jurisdictions often allow for its exclusion when requested, even if it is relevant
(
Stacy 1991
;
Findley 2011
)
.

3.2.3.
Accuracy of Decisions

Finally, there are mixed findings regarding whether an inquisitorial model yields more accurate decisions than an adversarial model
(
Sevier 2014
;
Lind et al. 1973
;
Thibaut et al. 1972
)
.
In inquisitorial models, moderators are responsible for gathering evidence and can collect evidence without bias in theory as third parties to the reported conflict.
However, legal research has also shown that judges tend to be more diligent when the case involves the interests of their higher authorities compared to when it concerns private interests
(
Damaska 1986
, p.54)
.
These concerns are amplified in online settings.
Community moderators often lack the time, training, or incentive to conduct thorough investigations, as many serve in part-time or volunteer roles
(
Seering et al. 2019
)
.
Platform moderators, likewise, may already be burdened by the emotional labor of moderation and feel less invested in individual cases, especially when they are not personally connected to the community
(
Roberts 2016
)
.
This becomes particularly problematic as online harassment grows more persistent and sophisticated, demanding more time and effort from moderators
(
Center 2021
)
.

In contrast, the competitive nature of the adversarial process creates stronger incentives for each party to thoroughly search for evidence than moderators typically have in inquisitorial settings
(
Kim 2014
)
.
While this can lead to a richer collection of evidence, it also raises risks: parties may be tempted to manipulate or selectively present evidence to favor themselves
(
Lind et al. 1973
)
.

3.3.
Considerations for Incorporating Adversarial Designs into Online Reporting Systems

Next, given the desire to incorporate more adversarial procedures into the online reporting process, we look again to the literature to understand the best ways to do so and identify design considerations.

3.3.1.
Degree of Inquisitorial versus Adversarial Design

Offline jurisdictions have recognized that purely adversarial or purely inquisitorial systems can cause more harm than benefit, and have thus begun adopting hybrid models.
For example, inquisitorial elements have been integrated into American systems to reduce costs and delays
(
Findley 2011
)
, while adversarial practices have been introduced in Continental Europe to enhance perceptions of procedural justice
(
Walpin 2003
)
.
If the inquisitorial nature of online reporting systems indeed contributes to great user frustrations, we envision that online reporting systems can similarly benefit from incorporating adversarial elements.
Importantly, this does not imply borrowing all adversarial practices, as many of them may not be applicable in online settings.
Instead, we argue that
the best approach is a hybrid one that incorporates some adversarial elements into the current inquisitorial approach
as opposed to adopting an adversarial design wholesale.

3.3.2.
Threats of Abuse in Adversarial Reporting Designs

Transforming online reporting systems to be more adversarial poses significant challenges, reflecting both the limitations of adversarial processes and the fundamental differences between online and offline settings.
A central issue with adversarial models is that when users gain more control over the process, they are also more likely to abuse that control for self-interest
(
Damaska 1972
;
Kim 2014
)
.
For example, if users can influence who reviews their report, they may prefer moderators who are more sympathetic to their perspective.
And while adversarial models encourage users to actively gather evidence,
they also increase the risk of evidence manipulation or selective disclosure
(
Lind et al. 1973
)
.
Such abuse can be even more prevalent in online settings where false reports are already common, whether to overwhelm moderators
(
Matias et al. 2015
)
, or frame other users
(
Kou and Gui 2021
)
.
To effectively prevent such abuse,
threat modeling is crucial when coming up with adversarial designs that can guard against abuse
.

3.3.3.
Strategies to Prevent Abuse in Adversarial Reporting Designs

To address challenges related to potential abuse, the primary strategy offline adversarial jurisdictions employ is to incorporate the perspective of the opposing or reported party.
For instance, the opposing party can cross-examine submitted evidence to test its authenticity
(
Schlesinger 1963
)
or file motions to challenge biased judges.
Such an approach is feasible in offline settings because defendants must be formally notified and allowed to participate in the process
(
Law [n. d.]
)
.
To prevent retaliation, courts can issue protective orders or impose severe penalties for retaliatory behavior
(
School [n. d.]
)
.
However, online communities and platforms are less capable of doing so as their punishments are often less effective and easily evaded, for example, by creating a new account or moving to a new platform.
As a result, many platforms choose not to engage the reported user during investigations, making practices like cross-examination infeasible
(
FaceBook [n. d.]
)
.

Given these major differences in offline versus online systems, we choose in this work to
not rely on involving the opposing party in the reporting process to address all abuse but instead explore the design space of techniques to strengthen user-provided evidence
, such as tools for evidence authentication.
In particular, we draw inspiration from various rules and procedures used in offline adversarial systems to prevent abuse.
For instance, submitted evidence is evaluated against strict admissibility rules to ensure that only credible evidence is considered at trial
(
Damaska 1972
)
.
While online reporting systems could still involve the reported person during the investigation, we leave consideration of these possibilities to our discussion in Section
5
.

4.
Deep Dive: Reimagining Evidence Collection using an Adversarial Model

Evidence collection is the core component of a reporting process, during which moderators review the case, gather evidence, and communicate with involved parties
(
Cai and Wohn 2021
;
Jiang et al. 2019
;
Kiene et al. 2019
)
.
The evidence gathered directly shapes the outcome of a case.
At the same time, the way evidence is collected also illustrates a key distinction between inquisitorial and adversarial models.
Granting users more control over how their evidence is presented thus marks an important step toward a more adversarial reporting system.
In this section, we describe how moderators in existing reporting systems collect evidence within an inquisitorial framework—an approach that can unintentionally discourage users from voicing procedural or privacy concerns.
We then explore how each step could be reimagined to support more adversarial practices and identify the key design questions that emerge.
In particular, we conduct threat modeling to examine the potential for abuse by various actors and then evaluate the trade-offs associated with various design choices
(
Xiong and Lagerström 2019
)
.
This analysis enables us to outline new tools and techniques that effectively address these trade-offs.

A central question our designs must address is how to grant users more control in the reporting process while preventing abuse of that control.
We consider a threat model in which (1) users and moderators are user interface (UI) bounded in their ability to abuse the system, and (2) platforms are trusted to see user messages and operate without bias.
Additionally, since community moderators are often part-time and may bring personal biases, we also consider the possibility that they may be unmotivated, unprofessional, or even biased.
These assumptions allow us to focus on the interactions between various stakeholders involved in the reporting process (e.g., the reporting user, the reported user, moderators, and bystanders) bounded by the user interface of a reporting system.
Of course, it is possible for interactions to occur outside of this threat model: users can escape the UI through the use of modified applications
(
Doctorow [n. d.]
;
Munyendo et al. 2024
)
, or users may not fully trust the platforms and opt to use end-to-end encrypted (E2EE) services
(
Ermoshina et al. 2016
)
.
We will revisit these alternative threat models and discuss their implications for our proposed designs in Section
5
.

For our exploration of the design space of adversarial evidence collection, we distinguish three primary evidence types:
documentary evidence, account information, and testimonial evidence
.
While there are various other taxonomies of evidence in legal literature
(
Cai and Wohn 2021
)
, we focus on these three categories as they characterize three primary ways moderators collect evidence and thus reflect the trade-offs between collecting comprehensive evidence and privacy in this process.
Documentary evidence consists of social media posts and conversation logs, which often directly document reported behavior and its contexts
(
Rychlak and Czarnetzky 1996
)
.
In contrast, account information includes a user’s reporting history, join date, and device information for platform moderators
(
Pfefferkorn 2022
)
. Although it may not directly relate to the reported behavior, it provides moderators with insight into a user’s activity patterns and credibility
(
Cai and Wohn 2021
)
.
Finally, testimonial evidence comprises oral statements given by the reporting person, the reported person, or bystanders
(
Jiang et al. 2019
)
. It offers moderators nuanced perspectives that are not captured by other types of evidence.

4.1.
Collection of Documentary Evidence

In existing reporting systems, how moderators collect documentary evidence varies greatly across platforms. When a user reports a message, many platforms automatically disclose surrounding messages to platform moderators. For instance, Facebook Messenger will send the most recent 30 messages in the reported conversation to platform moderators
(
FaceBook 2017
)
.
Such automated approaches represent how moderators drive the evidence collection process in inquisitorial models.
They not only overlook users’ privacy concerns but also discourage users from more actively submitting additional relevant conversations.
This may not even aid truth-seeking sometimes, as disputes can unfold over long periods and across multiple channels, contexts that the surrounding messages cannot fully capture.

Recognizing these limitations of such automated approaches, some platforms and most communities give users more control over what conversation is shared, often through less structured and less automated reporting systems.
For instance, Telegram allows users to report unwanted behaviors via emails, where they can submit screenshots of selected conversations
(
Telegram [n. d.]
)
.
Similarly, many Discord communities accept screenshots of relevant conversations through dedicated reporting channels
(
Discord [n. d.]
)
.
Consequently, users can not only collect evidence from multiple channels but also have the option to redact private information before submission.

While these systems mark an implicit shift from an inquisitorial approach to more adversarial approaches, they fail to address
two primary challenges in granting users greater power in collecting and presenting their evidence.
.
In the following, we will first analyze each trade-off in more detail and then outline the design space that effectively addresses such trade-offs.

4.1.1.
Challenge One
: Disclosure of Documentary Evidence

In an adversarial model, users should be able to determine which conversation to disclose to moderators.
But users might also selectively present messages to favor themselves, or even to frame the other party—a well-known issue in adversarial models
(
Lind et al. 1973
)
.
Users can simply justify such manipulation under the guise of privacy concerns.
This reflects
an inherent trade-off between protecting user privacy and giving moderators sufficient information to make informed decisions in the process of evidence disclosure
.
This trade-off also mirrors the data minimization principle in the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which states that “personal data shall be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed”
(
Regulation 2016
)
.

Offline adversarial systems rely heavily on cross-examination between opposing parties to surface omitted or misleading information
(
Kim 2014
;
Lind et al. 1973
)
, but this practice is less feasible online, as it requires the participation of the reported party in the reporting process.
In this subsection,
we draw inspiration from various data minimization practices to outline a design space that consists of four dimensions.

(
Kulshrestha and Mayer 2021
;
Biega et al. 2020
;
Orekondy et al. 2018
)

This allows platforms and communities to adopt designs that better align with their privacy commitments.

.

. These design ideas illustrate varying levels of information visibility beyond fully hiding or fully revealing content.
(1) Only metadata like the sender and timestamp is shown.
(2) Custom attributes, such as sentiment and message length, are displayed.
(3) An LLM answers an open-ended question, e.g., “To what extent is this message harassing?”, while constrained to avoid referencing private details. (4) Users redact sensitive information, such as replacing their home address with the label “my home address.”

Design Space 1
: Scope of Relevant Conversations
.

Before considering redacting private or irrelevant conversations, we should first determine the scope of relevant conversations.
Reporting systems differ widely in how they determine this scope.
At one end, some platforms consider
all messages within the reported conversation
as relevant—for instance, moderators in League of Legends can review the full in-game chat log
(
Blackburn and Kwak 2014
)
.
At the other end, E2EE platforms like Signal and Matrix expose no content at all, not even the reported message
(
Matrix 2022
)
.
Many platforms fall in between, retrieving only
the most recent messages in the reported conversation measured by count
.
For instance, Google Chat reveals the 50 most recent messages in the conversation
(
Google [n. d.]
)
and 30 for Facebook Messenger
(
FaceBook [n. d.]
)
.
Due to their emphasis on privacy, E2EE platforms tend to impose stricter limits: WhatsApp, for example, only forwards the last five messages
(
WhatsApp 2022b
)
.

Nevertheless, the diversity of reporting scenarios calls for other ways to define the conversation scope.
For instance, when users have intense arguments with each other, it may be more appropriate to filter by
all messages within a specific time window
.
In a busy Discord channel, where several conversations often run together without distinct threads, filtering by
messages from selected participants
can be more effective.
Finally, these methods only focus on the specific reported conversation, but disputes may span multiple places, such as moving from public channels to direct messages. In these settings, we might want to apply the aforementioned filters to all conversations between the involved users, not just the conversation where the report was initiated.

Design Space 2
: Information Visibility
.

For each message within the scope of relevant conversations, the next step is to decide how much information to disclose.
As illustrated in Figure
3
, this decision spans a spectrum, from fully hiding the message to revealing it in full.

•

First, users can choose to completely remove a message. For instance, when filing a report on WeChat, China’s largest messaging platform, users can deselect a message from their relevant conversations, and moderators will not even know this message existed
(
WeChat [n. d.]
)
.

•

In the next level, users can blur the message content while still showing certain metadata such as the timestamp and sender. This helps moderators understand messaging patterns in a conversation–for instance, when there is an overwhelming number of messages sent from one user.

•

Another option is to display custom attributes of a message, computed using predefined algorithms. Examples include its length, the presence of certain keywords/links/images, or even message sentiments. This approach has been explored in sensitive contexts like detecting child sexual abuse material
(
Kulshrestha and Mayer 2021
)
.

•

With the growing capabilities of large language models (LLMs), we also envision replacing predefined algorithms with more powerful LLMs. Instead of outputting a single attribute, these models could answer more targeted questions, for example, who initiated the dispute or how long the reporting user has experienced harassment. Nevertheless, this option is viable only if users trust the model, whether it runs locally on their device or through a trustworthy third
-
party service.
Otherwise, sending private conversations to the model only raises additional privacy risks
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. It is equally important to carefully design the prompts to avoid prompt injection attacks
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
as well as hallucination risks
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
.

•

Users can also redact private information within a message—either by fully blurring it
(
Orekondy et al. 2018
)
or replacing it with a general term (e.g., replacing their exact home address with "my home address")
(
Chow et al. 2009
)
.

•

At the most transparent end of the spectrum, moderators can view the full original content of the message, as in most current reporting systems.

Figure 4
.
This diagram illustrates how a redaction tool can support varying degrees of user control.

(1)
Automated Redaction
. An algorithm automatically detects and redacts sensitive information using a generic label. While this approach limits user manipulation, it may fail to catch all sensitive content.
(2)

. Users manually select text to redact, and the system replaces it with a standard placeholder. This offers a balance between user input and automation, but the resulting redactions may be less informative for moderators.
(3)
Manual Redaction
. Users have full control over redactions. This provides the strongest privacy protection but is vulnerable to misuse, as users may redact in ways that alter the intended meaning of the message.

Design Space 3
: Access Control
.

After finalizing the documentary evidence, we should then determine who can access it.
Most reporting systems grant access to all moderators and may archive all shared evidence for future audits.
Additional access control mechanisms can better protect user privacy.
For example, users may prefer to share their conversations only with a trusted subset of moderators.
Access can also be limited to a specific time window or a fixed number of views. These restrictions help ensure that the evidence is used solely for moderation purposes.

Figure 5
.
Complementary Designs to Prevent Selective Disclosure
. Granting users more control in evidence disclosure increases the risk of selective disclosure. To prevent such abuse, we propose two complementary designs that involve the reporting person or bystanders.
Progressive Disclosure
. Moderators request the reporting user to reveal the content of a redacted message that is deemed critical for decision-making. In this request, moderators are expected to include justifications and explain the consequences of declining, which helps the reporting user better weigh their privacy against the goals of reporting.

Cross Examination by Bystanders
. Alternatively, moderators can also invite bystanders to help verify evidence disclosure from the reporting person.
However, moderators need to first obtain consent from the reporting person, who evaluates the privacy risks of involving bystanders in a reporting process.
In this example, bystanders can only flag potentially suspicious redactions but not disclose their content to moderators.

Design Space 4
: User Control over Evidence Disclosure
.

The scope of relevant conversations and the degree of information visibility represent only part of the design space. A broader, high-level question remains: how much control should users have over evidence disclosure?
Answers to this question map onto a spectrum between inquisitorial and adversarial models, reflecting how control is distributed between moderators and users.
A fully inquisitorial system, moderators–or automated systems reflecting moderators’ preferences–can access conversations freely without any user input.
In contrast, a fully adversarial model gives users complete control over what evidence is shared.
The three dimensions described above characterize more granular user controls between these two extremes.

For each dimension, a further question is who gets to decide on specific design choices—users or moderators.
And each design idea can support varying degrees of user customization.
Take the dimension of conversation scope, for example.
A reporting system may automatically apply a particular filter, such as including the most recent 50 messages.
To give users more control, a system might allow them to adjust the filter parameters (e.g., the number of messages) or choose from a list of predefined filters (e.g., time-based or participant-based).
At the highest level of control, users can freely submit any conversation.
Similarly, for information visibility, systems can vary in how much redaction control they give users, as illustrated in Figure
4
.
If a reporting system allows users to redact private information, it can use predefined scripts to automatically redact sensitive fields such as home addresses or real names
(
Gilbert 2015
)
.
A more user-driven system might allow users to manually select what to redact, with the platform automatically replacing it with a generic placeholder.
Some systems can further allow users to specify their own replacement words.

However, any form of user control over evidence disclosure carries the risk of selective disclosure to mislead moderators.
For example, users might intentionally omit their own inappropriate messages to appear more benign.
They might also reveal only that a particular toxic keyword was used in a message from the opposing party, even if the message itself is not toxic in context.
Users might also withhold access from moderators who are familiar with the full context of the conversation.
As depicted in Figure
5
, we propose two complementary designs that involve different roles in this process to mitigate such abuse.

•

First, we envision a model of progressive disclosure between moderators and users. Moderators can request access to specific portions of a minimized conversation and are expected to explain the rationale for their request. While users may choose to deny these requests, doing so may reduce the credibility of their evidence. If the withheld content is central to the case, the report itself may be dismissed.

•

Second, reporting systems could allow other conversation participants to verify whether evidence was disclosed appropriately. If there is sufficient justification to invite the reported person, this then bears some similarities to cross-examination in offline adversarial models. Their level of involvement can vary: they might provide a simple yes/no answer, flag suspicious messages, or disclose specific messages they believe were inappropriately excluded. With this additional information, moderators can then reach out to the reporting user for clarification or further disclosure.

4.1.2.
Challenge Two
: Authentication of Documentary Evidence for Persistent Conversations

Even if users do not selectively present their conversations, any adversarial system must address the inherent communication gap between the informed users and the uninformed moderators
(
Kim 2014
)
.

When users submit their evidence, they need to prove to moderators that their submitted conversations happened
(
FaceBook 2017
)
, known in offline settings as evidence authentication
.
While moderators may sometimes have enough trust in a user so that they accept the conversation as authentic
(
Rajendran et al. 2024
)
, the growing prevalence of false reports demands the authentication of user-submitted conversations
(
Sultana et al. 2021
)
.

In particular, authentication of online conversations consists of two key components: 1)
identity attribution
that verifies the participants in the conversation are indeed the parties involved in the reported incident; 2)
message authentication
that ensures that the messages in the conversation have not been altered and originate from the claimed sender.
When moderators can access the conversation directly, authentication is relatively straightforward. This applies to cases where community moderators can review messages in public channels or where non-E2EE platforms allow access to private conversations for platform moderators.
However, moderators often lack access to certain conversations, such as community moderators reviewing direct messages between users or private channels, or platform moderators dealing with E2EE conversations.
In these cases, specialized authentication methods are required.
These methods can be categorized based on who provides the authentication: the platform or the user who submits the evidence.
In the following,
we criticize the flawed use of these methods in existing reporting systems and then propose new design ideas that provide sufficient authentication
.

Figure 6
.

. (Left) This example shows how message forwarding can be repurposed to support platform-side authentication.
The interface should indicate the message’s forwarded status and display account identifiers rather than usernames.
(Right) When anonymized reporting is supported, the interface should instead verify and label the roles of the reporting and reported users without revealing their identifiers.

Design Idea 1
: Authenticated by Platforms
.

First, most platforms have the capability to support authentication. Specifically, non-E2EE platforms store messages on centralized servers and naturally support authentication.
While E2EE platforms lack access to message content, some of them, like Facebook Messenger and WhatsApp, have integrated cryptographic protocols to enable authentication
(
Grubbs et al. 2017
;
Tyagi et al. 2019
)
.
In contrast, others, such as Signal and Matrix, choose not to do so and thus cannot support authentication
(
Rajendran et al. 2024
)
.
Despite having the technical ability, platforms often limit authentication support to their internal reporting systems, excluding community-level reporting systems.
As a result, users must rely on third-party tools for authentication when reporting to community moderators
(
Sultana et al. 2021
;
Goyal et al. 2022
)
.
For instance, many platforms offer OAuth access to conversations
(
Hwang et al. 2024
)
, which has led to the development of third-party tools for authenticating message content
(
Goyal et al. 2022
;
Foundation 2022
)
.
These tools retrieve both content and metadata (e.g., author, timestamp) from platforms to verify message authenticity.
However, since access is typically granted to entire conversation histories rather than specific messages, users may have to sacrifice some privacy for platform-side authentication.

We argue that online platforms should grant community moderators access to message authentication functionalities, given their central role in addressing online harassment
(
Seering 2020
)
.
One promising direction is to appropriate existing message forwarding features, which are common across social media platforms and already familiar to users
(
Yadav et al. 2023
)
.
However, current forwarding functionalities lack sufficient authentication support. Platforms should offer an option to display additional information when a message is forwarded for reporting purposes.
Specifically, forwarded messages should clearly indicate their forwarded status, include the account identifier of the original author, and show the original timestamp (see Figure
6
).
By contrast, WhatsApp does not show the author or timestamp in forwarded messages
(
WhatsApp [n. d.]
)
, and Signal does not even mark messages as forwarded
(
Signal [n. d.]
)
.

Design Idea 2
: Authenticated by the User Who Submits the Evidence
.

Due to the absence of user-friendly authentication tools provided by platforms, users often have to perform client-side authentication.
The most widely used method is to take a screenshot of a conversation
(
Sultana et al. 2021
;
Yadav et al. 2023
)
.
Unlike platform-side authentication, this approach increases the risk of abuse because users can easily tamper with screenshots or even alter what is displayed on their devices.
M3 mentioned such an incident: “
someone took a screenshot of their DMs and then edited it to make [the other person] look like doing something that broke the rules. We ended up taking a closer look at it, realizing that the font was slightly off and the messages were spaced incorrectly.
”
The former vulnerability is particularly concerning given easy access to image tampering tools
(
Gupta et al. 2013
;
Zheng et al. 2019
)
.
To address the first concern, some third-party tools streamline the screenshot process by sending the screenshot directly to moderators, reducing opportunities for manipulation.
Additionally, some communities require users to screen record their conversation interface, as they believe editing videos is more challenging than editing images
(
Sultana et al. 2021
)
.
To address the second issue, if the conversation interface is on a desktop, moderators may ask users to refresh their web interface during recording to prevent any HTML-level manipulations, but moderators generally trust screen recording of mobile devices
(
Yadav et al. 2023
)
.
This approach mirrors the chain of custody in offline practices, where each step of evidence collection is documented to ensure its authenticity
(
Giova et al. 2011
)
.

Unfortunately, these approaches only authenticate message content but fail to sufficiently verify identity. In other words, moderators cannot be sure that the participants in recorded images are truly the ones involved in the incident.
Because most social media platforms display only a user’s avatar and username instead of their full account identifier on conversation interfaces, malicious users can easily mimic these visual cues with a fake account to forge conversations.
While Unmochon
(
Sultana et al. 2021
)
attempts to extract account identifiers from the website URL of Facebook Messenger, this approach cannot be generalized to other platforms and conversations beyond direct messages.

When platforms do not take the initiative to implement such features, third-party tools can help enable client-side authentication.
Web automation techniques can be used to collect evidence while preserving the chain of custody
(
Pu et al. 2023
;
Yeh et al. 2009
)
.
After users specify the conversation scope and involved parties, such tools could automatically record the screen, first scrolling through the selected conversation, then opening each user profile to display their account identifiers. By sending the resultant recording directly to moderators, we can then prevent users from tampering with it.

4.1.3.
Challenge Two
: Authentication of Documentary Evidence for Ephemeral Conversations

The authentication approaches discussed above assume that messages are persisted, either on platform servers or on the client.
Many reporting systems also hold the same assumption, as reports are typically initiated by clicking on a specific message or the harassing user.
However, this assumption fails in ephemeral interactions–such as voice chats
(
Jiang et al. 2019
)
, virtual reality social media
(
Freeman et al. 2022
)
, self-destructing messages
(
Guardian 2021
)
, or messages that can be edited or deleted after being sent
(
Schnitzler et al. 2020
)
.
Although ephemeral messaging mirrors offline communication and can enhance privacy
(
Kotfila 2014
)
, its transient nature is often exploited by malicious actors to evade both message authentication and identity attribution
(
Goyal et al. 2022
;
Kiene et al. 2019
)
.
By the time a user realizes they were harassed and decides to report it, the relevant context—or even the harassing messages themselves—may no longer be available.
Ephemeral conversations can also obscure harasser identities.
For example, in a voice channel with many simultaneous speakers
(
Jiang et al. 2019
)
, or in a crowded virtual reality game
(
Blackwell et al. 2019
)
, users may be unable to determine who initiated the harassment.
In the following,
we similarly criticize the shortcomings of two approaches used to authenticate ephemeral conversations, based on which we propose new design ideas that address such shortcomings
.

Figure 7
.
Ephemeral Reporting for Voice Chats
. This figure illustrates how users can report content from ephemeral voice conversations. To respect privacy expectations, only recent audio segments are available for reporting, while older ones are automatically deleted. Each reporting system can configure how many seconds of audio remain reportable. Since user speech may overlap, the interface should indicate the speaker associated with each audio segment to support authentication.

Design Idea 1
: Authenticated by Persisting Ephemeral Conversations
.

One potential solution is to record ephemeral conversations.
For example, some Discord communities use bots to log every edited or deleted message into a dedicated moderation channel
(
Kiene et al. 2019
)
.
However, if platforms or communities record ephemeral conversations without any constraints, it would fundamentally change the nature of these conversations and violate users’ privacy expectations
(
Kotfila 2014
)
.
Alternatively, the reporting user often chooses to locally record the conversation, which may provide sufficient authentication if they follow recommended practices for client-side authentication.
Still, users often realize the need to record only after they’ve received abusive messages, by which point critical context may already be lost.
In these cases, Discord moderators might simply ignore such evidence, as indicated by M4, “
If a message has the edit in tag, then that message generally will be ignored [and will not] be trusted … [because] its content was not the original content in the message.
”

To address this issue, we propose an ephemeral reporting system that enables authentication of ephemeral messages while maintaining reasonable privacy guarantees (See Figure
7
).
Specifically, the system would record a sliding window of recent conversation content, such as archived text messages, recorded voice chats, or video captures of VR interactions.
It could be hosted by platforms or community moderators, provided its implementation follows our proposed authentication practices.
When a user initiates a report, the system could authenticate any messages within that window.
Since privacy expectations for ephemeral conversations vary across platforms and communities, the window size should be configurable.
It could be defined either by time (e.g., last 30 seconds) or by the number of recent messages (e.g., last 10 messages).

Design Idea 2
: Authenticated by Testimonial Evidence
.

When ephemeral conversations are not persisted, users must rely on testimonial evidence from bystanders for authentication.
In some cases, users initiate a report during the conversation itself, hoping moderators can immediately join and witness unwanted behaviors
(
Jiang et al. 2019
)
.
In other cases, they expect moderators to gather testimonial evidence from other conversation members, or even the reported person, to verify their descriptions of the conversation.
As described by M3, “
When someone was streaming incredibly NSFW things in the voice chat, people in the chat weren’t able to screen record it in time. So we ended up messaging [these bystanders] and asking them what happened.
”
We further discuss design considerations and ideas regarding communicating with witnesses in Section
4.3
.

4.2.
Collection of Account Information

Moderators in most reporting systems rely on account information to inform their decisions. This includes users’ reporting and reported history, join date
(
Cai and Wohn 2021
)
, and device information available to platform moderators
(
Pfefferkorn 2022
)
.
Prior research suggests that users recognize the value of this information for moderation and do not object to its use
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
.

However,
moderators and users often disagree on whether anonymous reporting should be allowed.

On one hand, whether moderators have access to users’ account identifiers varies
(
Gilbert 2023
)
.
While most platforms can access the identifier of the reporting user, it is unlikely, though not publicly confirmed, that this information is shared with platform moderators, as the account identifier alone does not provide platform moderators with any extra information.
In contrast, community moderators often want and usually have access to users’ account identifiers, with only a few exceptions, such as those on Reddit
(
Reddit [n. d.]
)
and certain E2EE platforms
(
Lai et al. 2023
)
.
This information is particularly useful to community moderators, who actively monitor community discussions and may use account identifiers to draw on contextual knowledge about the user (e.g., their personalities) or the reported argument
(
Cai and Wohn 2021
)
.

On the other hand, many users actually want anonymous reporting.
In a 2020 survey of minors, one quarter of respondents who chose not to report were worried that their report would not be anonymous
(
Thorn 2021
)
.
Reporting users are particularly worried that moderators might leak their account identifiers to reported users and other community members, which might trigger further harassment or victim-blaming
(
Sultana et al. 2021
;
Take et al. 2024
)
.
Additionally, if moderators already hold a negative impression towards one of the opposing parties, knowing users’ identities will only bias their decisions.
M4 recognized the importance of treating reports equally: “
it is also important that you actually take the messages into consideration that they’re sending, because even if they have a bad reputation, they could still legitimately be trying to be a better user.
”
By contrast, users are generally less concerned about platform moderators having access to their account identifiers, as they are often perceived as distant and unlikely to take a personal grudge against users
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
.
Nevertheless, inquisitorial reporting systems often turn a blind eye to these concerns.

Design Ideas
.

To better balance user privacy with moderation needs, we propose two sets of design ideas.

First, a reporting system can restrict when and who has access to users’ account identifiers.
For example, disclosure of account identifiers could be delayed until the end of an investigation so that moderators will not be influenced by their personal bias during their investigation, whilst still making an informed decision.
Alternatively, access could be limited to a senior moderator, who may then add contextual information or decide whether revealing the identifier is necessary.
Second, noting that account identifiers are often used to help moderators learn users’ traits, we propose a tagging system
(
Cai and Wohn 2021
)
.
Moderators could label users during their regular moderation workflow, and these pseudonymous labels, rather than raw identifiers, would be shown in a reporting system.
This approach also offers a persistent record of moderators’ impressions, which are difficult to share among moderators and will be lost when moderators leave the community.

4.3.
Collection of Testimonial Evidence

In addition to reviewing relevant conversations, community moderators may also collect testimonial evidence from the reporting user, the reported user, or bystanders via direct messages.
This practice is relatively uncommon at the platform level, where moderators are often prohibited from communicating with any involved party during an investigation.
The use of testimonial evidence varies.
Moderators commonly engage users to gather additional context about the reported incident using open-ended questions.
They may also reach out to bystanders or the reported person for evidence authentication. For example, moderators might ask participants in a voice chat to confirm that the reported behavior occurred
(
Jiang et al. 2019
)
, or ask the reported person to verify that a submitted conversation screenshot has not been tampered with.
Additionally, testimonial evidence can facilitate evidence disclosure, such as when moderators request that users share more of their private conversation history to clarify context.

While moderators often are motivated to collect testimonial evidence, reporting users may have privacy concerns
, since bystanders and the reported person might then infer who has submitted a report. This might cause social embarrassment or invite more harassment from malicious users.
Despite these concerns, community moderators often contact other users during the investigation without the reporting user’s consent whenever they believe it is necessary.
For instance, M4 mentioned that “
When the harassment happens in direct messages, we usually ask for screenshots from both sides…[because] we are concerned that the screenshot from one party might not be accurate
”. This inquisitorial approach ignores users’ privacy concerns and might even put their online safety at risk.

Design Idea
.

A straightforward design improvement would be to require moderators to obtain the reporting user’s consent and provide clear justifications before involving others in the investigation.
On the other hand, when moderators feel that collecting additional testimonial evidence is crucial but the reporting user disagrees, moderators also have the right to terminate the report.

5.
Discussion

In this work, we examine online reporting systems through an adversarial lens and
demonstrate how their inquisitorial nature contributes to many user frustrations these days. Drawing on offline adversarial practices, we then identify a rich design space that grants users more control in the reporting process while mitigating potential abuse.
In this section, we discuss how adversarial concepts can be integrated beyond evidence collection, as well as design implications for stronger threat models.

5.1.
How Adversarial Concepts can Integrate into the Reporting Workflow

Beyond evidence collection, other aspects of the reporting workflow could also benefit from more adversarial designs.
In particular, granting users greater control in these areas is key to ensuring that moderators remain accountable and trustworthy throughout the evidence collection process.

How anonymized moderators are to users?

In current reporting systems, both platform and community moderators are anonymized by default when reviewing reports.
In some cases, community moderators can choose to reveal their identities, for example, by signing their messages to users.
This default anonymity serves multiple purposes.
On one hand, moderators are worried that dissatisfied users may retaliate if they know the moderator’s identity
(
Cai and Wohn 2021
)
.
Additionally, community moderators often work part-time, and work shifts between moderators in a reporting process can confuse users
(
Kiene et al. 2019
)
.
At the same time, revealing some information about moderators can help build user trust and encourage cooperation, as well as signaling a sense of moderation accountability.
For example, users are more willing to share private information with moderators they trust
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
.
To navigate these two conflicting needs, we propose that reporting systems can disclose limited moderator details, such as their tenure, number of reviewed reports, or endorsed values.

Which moderator should review the report?

An impartial and trustworthy moderator is essential for a fair investigation process, as moderators might have a bias towards certain groups.
Additionally, due to their deep engagement within the community, community moderators might even be involved in the conflict or have ties to the reported user
(
Liao et al. 2025
)
.
However, most existing reporting systems do not account for this concern.
Some reporting systems assign moderators behind the scenes: while it is common to exclude moderators involved in the conflict, it is often hard to ensure that assigned moderators are free from personal biases.
For less structured reporting systems, a reporting user might have too much control in this process. For instance, they can choose who review their report by direct messaging moderators who might support their perspective.
We propose a more structured process that only grants users adequate control in this aspect.
Specifically, users could suggest a list of preferred moderators or request the exclusion of specific moderators, with justifications. The moderator team can then take these preferences into account when assigning moderators.

How should reported users appeal moderation decisions?

After making a decision, moderators then notify the reported person and provide an opportunity for them to appeal.
This is often the only way for the reported person to voice their concerns and challenge moderators’ decision, despite they are poorly equipped to do so.
First, they often lack information about how the decision was made and thus don’t know how to contest it effectively.
Due to privacy concerns of their knowing who reported them, the reported person often only receives a generic message indicating which policy was violated, without knowing which message triggered the violation.
Second, the reported person has limited means to express their concerns.
Many platform-level systems offer only a single, one-way appeal process: the reported user is asked to write a paragraph explaining why they disagree with the decision in a form and then waits for the final decision.
While some community-level systems might set up a dedicated group chat for appeals, they too often fall back on such forms, especially when the reported person has already been punished to leave the community.
As a result, reported users who already feel wronged by the decision often lose trust in the appeals process and may leave the community altogether.

We propose two directions for improving this process.
First, we argue that how much information is disclosed in the notification should depend on how likely the reported person is to infer who reported them.
For example, when the report involves a direct message, a generic notification is appropriate. But if the report relates to a group chat message that does not target any specific member, it may be acceptable to disclose more details.
Second, reporting systems should offer more flexibility in how punishment is tied to the appeal process.
In some cases, punishments such as account deactivation or banning make it challenging for the reported person to appeal, as they can no longer contact moderators efficiently or gather evidence.
While we acknowledge that immediate enforcement is necessary for extremely harmful behavior, we propose that moderators delay the punishment in cases where the decision is less certain or the reported behavior is less harmful.
This will signal moderators’ respect for the voices of the reported person, and helps reduce harms caused by incorrect decisions.

5.2.
Applying Cryptographic Tools to Reporting Systems

In Section
4
, we map out our design space under the assumptions that (1) users and moderators are UI-bounded in their ability to abuse the system, and (2) platforms are trusted to see user messages and operate without bias.
These assumptions allow us to focus on the interactions between various community stakeholders at the reporting policy level without worrying about how their implementations might invite other abuse.
However, these assumptions clearly do not hold for all user populations.
Technically savvy users can modify client code to deviate from the expected protocol;
modified versions of applications can be packaged and downloaded for easy use by others, in some cases eclipsing the popularity of the native app
(
Munyendo et al. 2024
;
Doctorow [n. d.]
)
.
Similarly, increasingly large populations of users also choose to communicate on end-to-end encrypted (E2EE) messaging platforms like WhatsApp, Signal, or iMessage
(
Ermoshina et al. 2016
)
, indicating a preference for less trust in the host platforms.

This broader threat model presents significant challenges for implementing our design ideas.
Nonetheless, our design space offers a promising avenue for applying and extending the growing body of work on content moderation on E2EE platforms.
In this line of work, cryptographic mechanisms have been designed to enforce authentication and privacy requirements of user reporting (even in the presence of modified clients and untrusted platforms).
We discuss briefly how these techniques could be extended to fit the more complex reporting procedures that we propose for a justice system with adversarial elements.

The main challenge in this setting is in evidence authentication.
The platform cannot be relied on since it does not see the plaintext messages (only encrypted ciphertexts).
Users cannot be relied on either since non-cryptographic user-generated evidence, such as screen recording, can be easily forged, whereas UI-bound restrictions like forwarding messages can be manipulated using modified client applications.
To address this, communities may employ cryptographic message franking protocols to allow for encrypted messages to later be authenticated by a moderator at the time of reporting with
(
FaceBook 2017
;
Grubbs et al. 2017
)
or without
(
Tyagi et al. 2019
)
platform support.

Existing protocols primarily focus on reporting at the “message” level; moderators can verify reports for single messages but do not gain insight into the larger conversation structure or the ordering of messages.
Conversation structure and ordering is important in our proposals for providing moderators with enough context to make decisions and enable progressive disclosure.
Some prior work has considered selective reporting of parts within a message
(
Chen and Tang 2018
;
Leontiadis and Vaudenay 2023
)
, but selective reporting of messages within a conversation while maintaining conversation structure is an open problem.

Reporting of ephemeral messages is also a challenge.
Existing protocols create permanent cryptographic evidence that can be used to authenticate a message to a moderator even after the ephemeral time period has expired.
One might adapt message franking protocols to make use of time-based signatures, which are no longer cryptographically convincing after a certain time has elapsed
(
Specter et al. 2019
;
Arun et al. 2022
;
Beck et al. 2023
)
.

Our proposals also consider the role of anonymity in the reporting process, both for the reporting user and for the moderators.
Prior reporting work has considered hiding the reporting user’s identity
(
Lai et al. 2023
)
and revealing a reportee’s identity only after a quorum of moderators agree on a decision
(
Pattison and Hopper 2023
)
.
These protocols may be adapted and extended to consider the additional settings we propose, including partial moderator anonymity, user pseudonyms, or targeting a selected set of moderators.

Lastly, a set of our design ideas also involve running client-side algorithms, for instance, to redact with custom attributes or answer more open-ended questions via an LLM.
To translate this class of ideas to the modified client threat model, moderators will need assurance that clients run the proper redaction algorithm or compute the correct response.
One direction would be to require clients to produce cryptographic proofs of correct execution of the algorithm
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
.
Unfortunately, for many algorithms, this will likely be prohibitively expensive.
In that case, one might rely on trusted execution environments
(
Sabt et al. 2015
)
.

5.3.
Applying Offline Legal Practices to Online Content Moderation

As online spaces become an essential component of our society, the boundary between online content moderation and offline legal practices is increasingly blurred.
On one hand, this convergence opens up opportunities to draw from offline legal systems, which have long explored a wide range of procedural designs.
In particular, we believe comparative legal literature offers further insights beyond those discussed in this work.
For example, legal scholars argue that when governments aim to support social self-regulation rather than impose top-down control, a fully adversarial debate between the opposing parties is an ideal way to resolve conflicts.
While we refrain from involving the reported person during the investigation in this work, we envision that more decentralized online communities might want to adopt this process to handle user reports.
Additionally, comparisons between common law and civil law traditions can inform how communities structure their governance policies
(
Chen and Zhang 2023
)
.
Many communities rely on detailed written policies, resembling the legal codes of civil law systems.
But moderators often struggle to update these documents to reflect evolving norms.
Drawing from case law traditions, we suggest that incorporating prior moderation decisions as precedents could ease this burden, while also increasing transparency for community members.

On the other hand, researchers should also be mindful of some fundamental differences between online and offline settings.
For example, the United States enacted the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) in 1998, allowing copyright holders to request the removal of infringing content from online platforms.
Victims of non-consensual intimate imagery often rely on this mechanism to take down their photos, but filing a DMCA report can unintentionally cause further harm
(
Qiwei et al. 2024
)
.
To comply with offline due process, platforms need to collect the victim’s personal contact information and forward the request to the accused, who may then identify the reporter and retaliate
(
Take et al. 2024
;
America 2023
)
.
Similarly, users are often allowed to record their own online conversations without notifying other conversation participants.
Although online voice chats resemble offline conversations, some offline jurisdictions require the consent of all participants before a conversation can be recorded.
More research is needed to understand the factors driving these differences between online and offline practices, and to explore how offline legal frameworks might be adapted to better fit online contexts.

6.
Limitations and Future Work

In this work, we focus on mapping the design space for a more adversarial reporting system.
However, future work is needed to implement and evaluate these design ideas within real online communities.
Our formative studies revealed several challenges in this process. Since many online communities are already used to inquisitorial reporting systems, it is often difficult to convince them to adopt a fundamentally different approach, especially one that may reduce moderators’ direct control over reports.
As such, future work should explore how to integrate adversarial features into existing workflows in a gradual and minimally disruptive way.
It is also important to investigate how the acceptance of adversarial approaches varies across communities with different governance structures. We hypothesize that communities with more decentralized governance and greater privacy awareness may be more receptive to such systems.
Another challenge is that community and platform moderators often have limited time and capacity.
While adversarial approaches may increase workload by requiring more back-and-forth communication with involved parties, they can also reduce the burden of evidence collection on moderators.
Nevertheless, future work should examine how to implement adversarial reporting systems under such practical limitations.

7.
Conclusion

User reporting systems are central to resolving interpersonal conflicts and protecting users from harm in online spaces, particularly those with heightened privacy expectations.
However, many users complained about their lack of insight and input into the reporting process
(
Kou and Gui 2021
;
Crawford and Gillespie 2016
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Aliapoulios et al. 2021
)
.
As a result, these systems are often seen as lacking procedural justice and compromising users’ private conversations under the justification of moderation.
To address these failures of online reporting systems, we draw inspiration from offline judicial systems, which have long explored different procedural designs to navigate various trade-offs.
In particular, we trace these frustrations to the inquisitorial nature of today’s online reporting systems, where moderators lead evidence gathering and case development.
In contrast, adversarial models can grant users greater control and thus are better for procedural justice and privacy protection, albeit with higher risks of abuse.

This motivates us to explore the potential of incorporating adversarial practices into online reporting systems.
Through formative interviews, literature review, and threat modeling, we find a rich design space for empowering users to collect and present their evidence while mitigating potential abuse.
In particular, drawing on data minimization practices
(
Kulshrestha and Mayer 2021
;
Biega et al. 2020
;
Orekondy et al. 2018
)
, we outline a design space for
evidence disclosure
that ensures shared conversations are adequate for, but limited to, moderation purposes
(
Regulation 2016
)
.
We also explore techniques to support evidence authentication in today’s complex technical affordances of online platforms.
Finally, we consider how cryptographic tools and comparative legal frameworks can further support the design of future moderation systems.
