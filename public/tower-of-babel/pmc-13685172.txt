Association of adverse childhood experiences patterns with successful aging trajectories: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Kunhao Yang, Xin Yang, Hui Fan, Lidan Zheng, Xiangli Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe long-term influence of distinct adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) patterns on heterogeneous trajectories of successful aging (SA) remains unclear.MethodsUsing 2011–2018 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study data from 3,092 community-dwelling older adults (aged ≥ 60), latent class analysis identified four distinct ACE patterns from 13 binary indicators based on exposure probabilities, clinical significance, and prior literature. SA was evaluated across five domains: absence of chronic disease, no disability, high cognition, no depressive symptoms, and active social engagement. As SA represents a heterogeneous outcome dynamically evolving over time during aging, group-based trajectory modeling mapped its eight-year trajectories, which were linked to baseline latent ACE classes via logistic regression. Finally, network analysis using the graphical lasso algorithm compared network structure, global strength, edge weights, and node centrality of SA domains between trajectory groups to investigate interaction patterns.ResultsWe identified four ACEs patterns (Low Adversity, 54.2%; Maltreatment, 21.7%; Household Dysfunction, 20.0%; Pervasive Adversity, 4.1%) and two successful aging trajectories (Sustained High-Decline, 22%; Chronically Low-Stable, 78%). Compared to the Low Adversity pattern, the Pervasive Adversity [OR = 1.97, 95% CI (1.10, 3.83)] and Household Dysfunction [OR = 1.36, 95% CI (1.07, 1.74)] patterns were associated with the adverse Chronically Low-Stable trajectory, whereas the Maltreatment pattern showed no significant longitudinal association. Low educational attainment was the strongest predictor of this adverse trajectory [OR = 5.23, 95% CI (3.84, 7.13)]. Network analysis indicated differences in overall network structure between trajectory groups (p = 0.037) but not in global strength. Specifically, compared to Sustained High-Decline, the Chronically Low-Stable group exhibited a more rigid network with a stronger Cognition-Chronic Disease edge weight (p < 0.001) but lower Cognition centrality (p < 0.001).ConclusionsCulturally specific early-life adversity patterns, particularly household dysfunction and pervasive adversity, predict adverse successful aging trajectories. These childhood patterns are associated with a more rigid late-life mind-body network characterized by tight pathological coupling between cognition and physical illness. Importantly, broader structural inequalities across the life course, particularly low educational attainment and rural residence, operate alongside early-life trauma as dominant predictors of adverse aging trajectories, highlighting the need for systemic, policy-level interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-08034-0.Keywords: Life course health, Adverse childhood experiences, Risk factors, Successful aging, Mental health, Physical health

Introduction

China is navigating one of the most rapid demographic transitions in human history. According to the “2024 National Report on the Development of Aging Undertakings,” China’s population aged 60 and over reached 310.31 million by the end of 2024, representing 22.0% of the total population [1]. However, extended lifespan does not inherently equate to extended healthspan. As the sheer volume of the aging population amplifies the societal burden of age-related decline, successful aging (SA) has emerged as a key benchmark for assessing the health of older populations [2]. Alarmingly, the prevalence of SA among Chinese older adults is only 13.7% [3], which is substantially lower than the international range of 22% to 24% [4, 5]. This disparity suggests that improving the rate of SA and enhancing late-life quality of life are urgent public health priorities in China.

Since the introduction of theoretical models of healthy aging, the classic Rowe and Kahn three-component model has defined the core elements of SA as low probability of disease and disability, high cognitive and physical functional capacity, and active engagement with life. However, it has been criticized for its limitations and its narrow biomedical perspective focused solely on the absence of disease and disability, and thus is not a universally accepted definition of SA [6–8]. Nonetheless, utilizing the multidimensional biopsychosocial framework refined from this model across different cultural contexts, which incorporates psychological well-being alongside objective health domains, allows us to more comprehensively explore successful aging within a broader sociological scope.

While this foundational model rightly recognized the importance of psychosocial engagement, subsequent clinical realities highlighted the need for an explicit focus on psychological well-being [9]. Many older adults who fail to achieve SA face a dual disease burden, where physical chronic non-communicable diseases frequently coexist with severe mental health issues like depression [10–12]. Furthermore, depression and social isolation can biologically accelerate physical and cognitive decline through systemic inflammatory and neuroendocrine pathways [13–15]. This evidence demonstrates that late-life health does not operate in isolated components, but rather as a highly interconnected mind-body system. Consequently, SA in this study is comprehensively evaluated across five integrated dimensions: the absence of major chronic diseases, freedom from physical disability, robust cognitive functioning, the absence of depressive symptoms, and active social engagement.

Despite this consensus, current SA research exhibits critical methodological limitations. First, studies continue to focus predominantly on Western cohorts [16, 17], frequently overlooking China’s unique socio-cultural context and macro-level modulators like urban-rural disparities [18, 19]. Second, the majority of existing research operationalizes SA as a static, binary outcome (successful vs. unsuccessful) at a single time point [18]. This simplistic classification ignores the highly heterogeneous and dynamic nature of SA, failing to capture how mind-body resilience unfolds as diverse longitudinal trajectories in later life. According to the life course perspective, aging is a dynamic lifelong process embedded in socio-historical contexts, shaped by interpersonal relationships and macro-structural forces, rather than a static outcome of personal control [20]. Therefore, to fundamentally improve these late-life trajectories, it is imperative to trace back to their early-life structural determinants.

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are potentially traumatic events occurring before age 18. They are increasingly recognized as structural determinants of lifelong health [21]. According to life-course epidemiology, early-life trauma does not merely fade over time. Instead, it leaves enduring physiological changes. This process is conceptualized as the “biological embedding of stress” [22, 23]. Repeated exposure to childhood adversity permanently alters the developing nervous and endocrine systems, which leads to a chronic dysregulation of stress hormones like cortisol [24]. This lifelong wear-and-tear on the body creates a cumulative allostatic load, silently exhausting an individual’s physiological and cognitive reserves long before old age. By directly degrading these vital reserves, early-life trauma may gradually compromise the core dimensions required for SA [24]. Empirical evidence confirms this trajectory. Higher ACE exposure is robustly associated with increased risks of late-life chronic multimorbidity [25, 26], accelerated cognitive decline [27, 28], and severe depressive symptoms [28, 29]. Furthermore, early trauma impairs secure attachment and interpersonal trust, severely compromising the protective effects of active social engagement [30, 31]. Consequently, ACEs fundamentally disrupt the interconnected mind-body resilience essential for maintaining SA.

While the life-course hazards of ACEs are widely acknowledged, existing methodological approaches exhibit significant limitations. Most studies rely heavily on cumulative risk scoring, which simply sums the number of exposures [32]. It assumes that all childhood adversities exert equivalent biological effects and ignores the reality that different types of trauma co-occur in distinct patterns. To capture the true complexity of these trauma profiles, person-centered approaches like latent class analysis (LCA) are highly necessary. Additionally, research on ACEs in Chinese populations remains concentrated on adolescents and university students [33, 34], leaving the older population understudied. Identifying specific latent patterns of ACEs among older Chinese adults is therefore crucial, as different adversity clusters may exert highly heterogeneous and differentiated risks on the dynamic pathways of SA in later life.

To address these critical gaps, this study utilizes a nationally representative prospective cohort to systematically investigate how early-life adversity is associated with the longitudinal trajectories of SA in China. Crucially, given that older Chinese adults lived through unique socio-historical upheavals, we first employ LCA to identify culturally specific patterns of childhood adversity in this understudied population. As the core highlight of this research, we then link these early-life patterns to Group-Based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM) to chart the dynamic, eight-year evolution of SA. This longitudinal, life-course approach allows us to explicitly evaluate how distinct early-life trauma profiles predict and relate to late-life health pathways over time. Additionally, to explore the structural organization and associations within these trajectories, we introduce network analysis from the perspective of complex systems science. This systemic lens allows us to evaluate SA as a unified mind-body network, thereby exploring how physical, cognitive, and psychosocial health domains interact as an interconnected system. Specifically, this exploratory approach allows us to examine the structural rigidity and co-occurrence patterns among these health domains, providing preliminary insights into how childhood adversity associates with the systemic vulnerability of older adults. Ultimately, this comprehensive framework explores the structural organization and contemporaneous coupling of SA domains across different trajectories, offering a life-course perspective on the potential systemic resilience and vulnerability of older populations.

Methods

Research participants

We gathered participants from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), focusing on Chinese residents aged 60 years and above in Fig. 1.

After applying exclusion criteria, a final sample of 3,092 participants was included in the analysis

CHARLS is a nationally representative survey administered by Peking University to track health and socioeconomic dynamics among middle-aged and older adults in China. CHARLS employs a multistage, stratified probability sampling design covering non-institutionalized residents across 28 provinces in China. The study protocol was approved by Peking University’s Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants [35]. The analytical cohort was derived from the CHARLS database spanning from 2011 to 2018. The 2014 questionnaire included a supplementary life history module administered to follow-up participants. Variables related to ACEs were extracted from this module and integrated into the 2011 baseline dataset.

Assessment of ACEs

A total of 13 individual adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) indicators were retrospectively assessed using the supplementary life history module from the 2014 CHARLS questionnaire [36]. These 13 indicators included: (1) parental separation or divorce, (2) physical abuse, (3) domestic violence, (4) emotional neglect, (5) household mental illness, (6) household substance abuse, (7) incarcerated household member, (8) unsafe neighborhood, (9) peer bullying, (10) parental death, (11) parental disability, (12) sibling death, and (13) childhood financial hardship.

Each of the 13 indicators was dichotomized as 0 (indicating absence) or 1 (indicating presence). These 13 binary indicators served as the primary independent variables for subsequent latent pattern analysis. The detailed questionnaire wording, items, and dichotomous coding thresholds for each of the 13 individual indicators are systematically presented in Table 1.

Assessment of SA

Based on modifications to Rowe and Kahn's SA model proposed by studies in Asian populations [16, 37], SA in this study was evaluated across five dimensions: (1) absence of major chronic diseases, (2) no disability, (3) high cognitive functioning, (4) absence of depressive symptoms, and (5) active social engagement. Participants meeting all five criteria were classified into the SA group.

The operational definitions for each dimension are as follows in Table 2. First, the absence of major chronic diseases was defined as never being diagnosed by a physician with any of five major conditions, specifically cancer, chronic lung disease, heart disease, diabetes, and stroke [38]. Second, physical disability was assessed using the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale, which has demonstrated reliable validity in previous research [39]. The ADL scale evaluates independence in six basic tasks, including dressing, bathing, eating, transferring (getting in or out of bed), using the toilet, and controlling bowel and bladder movements. Response options for each task included “no difficulty,” “difficulty but can still do it,” “difficulty and needs help,” and “cannot do it.” Participants who reported being able to perform all six tasks independently without any difficulty were classified as having no disability. Third, cognitive performance was evaluated using the 10-item Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS-10), which includes naming orientation items (day of the week, month, date, year, and season), performing serial subtractions of 7 from 100 up to five times, recalling a list of ten words, and copying a figure drawing. Participants scoring at or above the median were defined as having high cognitive functioning [40]. Fourth, depressive symptoms were screened using the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression (CES-D) scale [41]. Total scores on this scale range from 0 to 30, with lower scores indicating milder depressive symptoms. Consistent with previous literature, a cut-off score of 10 was adopted, which has demonstrated reliable sensitivity and specificity among older Chinese adults [42, 43]. Scores below 10 indicated the absence of depressive symptoms, whereas scores of 10 or higher indicated their presence. Finally, active social engagement was defined as participants self-reporting participation in at least one of eleven social activities in the preceding month. These activities included interacting with friends, playing mahjong or chess, providing help to others, dancing or exercising, participating in club activities, volunteering, caring for non-cohabitant patients, attending school or training courses, engaging in financial activities, using the internet, or other social activities [38].

Covariates

Given that the potential confounding or modulating influences of sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics on the association between ACEs and SA remain unclear, the statistical models adjusted for a range of baseline sociodemographic and behavioral covariates to control for these factors [25, 44]. Categorical covariates included: (1) gender (female vs. male); (2) residence area (urban vs. rural); (3) educational attainment, classified into four levels (less than primary, primary, middle school, and high school and above); (4) marital status, dichotomized as married/partnered or other (widowed, divorced, separated, or never married); (5) alcohol consumption (drinking > once per month in the past year vs. none); and (6) smoking status, categorized as current user (including cigarettes, pipes, hand-rolled, or factory-made cigarettes) or non-user. Continuous covariates adjusted in the analyses were: (1) age at examination (years); and (2) average nightly sleep duration (hours) over the preceding month.

Statistical analysis

General statistics and latent class analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.3 [45]; two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered significant. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) and were compared with one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests; categorical variables are summarised as n (%) and compared with χ² tests.

LCA of 13 binary ACEs indicators, with 1 = present / 0 = absent, was performed using the poLCA package [46]. Models specifying 2–6 classes were fitted with 50 random starts. The optimal number of classes was selected based on the minimum Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) [47], conceptual interpretability, and model parsimony [48], while satisfying the criterion of entropy ≥ 0.70. Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) with a binomial link and exchangeable correlation structure were constructed using the geepack package to model ACEs class effects on SA [49]; heterogeneity across strata was tested with Wald χ².

Group-based trajectory modeling of SA

We used GBTM to identify the longitudinal pathways of SA. This method classifies individuals with similar temporal trends into latent subgroups. Each participant was assigned to a trajectory group based on their maximum posterior probability. During model selection, we evaluated model fit using the log-likelihood and the BIC. The model with the lowest BIC was selected as the optimal model, provided that adding more groups did not improve the log-likelihood. To ensure the relevance and robustness of the classifications, each trajectory group was required to contain at least 5% of the total sample [50]. Subsequently, we used logistic regression to examine how the latent classes of ACEs predicted trajectory membership. These analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.3 using the lcmm package [51].

Network analysis of SA domains

We conducted network analyses to investigate the interaction patterns among depressive symptoms, cognitive function, physical function, social engagement, and chronic diseases across the different SA trajectory groups. In the network models, the SA components were represented as nodes, and the partial correlations between them were represented as edges [52]. Network estimation and visualization were performed in R using the qgraph [53] and bootnet [54] packages, applying the graphical lasso (glasso) algorithm with Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) selection. Blue and red edges represented positive and negative correlations, respectively, with thickness and saturation reflecting connection strength. In the assessment of node centrality (Node Centrality), centrality reflects the number, strength, and closeness of a node’s connections to other nodes [55]. Specifically, strength is defined as the sum of the absolute weights of all connections a node has with other nodes. Because strength has demonstrated high interpretability and stability in psychometric networks, we selected it as the primary index of node centrality. We then used the Network Comparison Test (NCT) to evaluate systemic differences between the SA trajectories, comparing network structure invariance, global strength invariance, and edge-level invariance [56]. Finally, we ran 1,000 non-parametric bootstrap resamples to generate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each edge to evaluate edge weight accuracy and utilized case-dropping subset bootstrap to assess the stability of centrality indices. This stability was quantified using the Correlation Stability (CS) coefficient. The CS-coefficient represents the maximum proportion of cases that can be dropped from the dataset such that the correlation between the original and sub-sampled centrality indices remains at or above 0.7 with 95% probability. CS-coefficient values above 0.25 are considered acceptable, and those above 0.50 are considered stable [54].

Sensitivity analysis and sampling weights

To evaluate the robustness of our findings to potential unmeasured confounding, we conducted sensitivity analyses using E-values. The E-value is defined as the minimum strength of association, on the risk ratio scale, that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and the outcome to fully explain away the observed association, conditional on measured covariates [57]. All E-value sensitivity analyses were performed in R version 4.4.3 using the E-Value package [58].

Of note, sampling weights were not applied in our analyses, consistent with prior analytical studies using longitudinal CHARLS data that focused on relational or inferential objectives rather than descriptive population estimation [59]. Furthermore, official individual-level longitudinal panel weights spanning all follow-up waves from 2011 to 2018 are not available in the CHARLS database [35].

LCA analysis of ACEs

We performed LCA to identify homogeneous subgroups of ACEs. After comparing models specifying two to six classes, we selected the four-class model as the optimal solution (BIC = 107 894, entropy = 0.734; eTable 2 in Supplement). Although the five-class model yielded a slightly lower adjusted BIC and a marginally higher entropy, the inclusion of a fifth class did not identify a qualitatively new or theoretically distinct pattern of adversity. Instead, the five-class solution introduced unnecessary redundancy, where an existing class from the four-class model was merely divided into two subgroups with highly similar response profiles. This over-stratification compromised model parsimony. Therefore, balancing statistical fit with theoretical interpretability and clinical relevance, we retained the four-class model as the most meaningful structure. Heterogeneous trajectories of SA and their association with ACEs

GBTM identified two distinct developmental trajectories of SA (eFigure 1 and eTable 2 in the Supplement): a Chronically Low-Stable group (78% of the sample, n = 2,422) and a Sustained High-Decline group (22% of the sample, n = 670). As shown in eTable 3 in the Supplement, baseline characteristics differed significantly between the two trajectory groups. Compared with participants in the Chronically Low-Stable group, those in the Sustained High-Decline group were younger and had a higher proportion of males, higher educational attainment, longer nightly sleep duration, and a larger share of urban residents (all p < 0.05). Additionally, the Sustained High-Decline group exhibited a higher proportion of low adversity (Class 4: 61.6% vs. 52.4%).

As shown in Table 5, compared with the low-adversity reference group (Class 4), exposure to ACEs Class 2 [OR = 1.97, 95% CI (1.10, 3.83)] and Class 3 [OR = 1.36, 95% CI (1.07, 1.74)] was significantly associated with higher odds of membership in the adverse Chronically Low-Stable trajectory. Among covariates, low educational attainment was the most potent predictor of this adverse trajectory [less than primary vs. high school and above: OR = 5.23, 95% CI (3.84, 7.13)]. Additionally, non-married status [OR = 1.60, 95% CI (1.19, 2.17)] and age 70–79 years [OR = 1.26, 95% CI (1.01, 1.59)] were significant risk factors, whereas male gender [OR = 0.67, 95% CI (0.55, 0.82)] and urban residence [OR = 0.50, 95% CI (0.41, 0.60)] were significant protective factors. Supplementary E-value sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the potential influence of unmeasured confounding.

Discussion

Characterization of latent ACEs patterns

By utilizing LCA, this study moves beyond the traditional cumulative risk score approach to explore unique patterns of childhood adversity among older Chinese adults. Unlike Western cohorts, where latent ACEs classes are frequently driven by high rates of substance abuse and sexual trauma [60], the patterns carry a profound socio-historical imprint of the mid-twentieth century.

First, the Household Dysfunction group (Class 3) was characterized by an extremely low prevalence of parental separation or divorce, alongside high rates of financial hardship, parental disability, and mortality. This pattern likely reflects the rigid stigma and institutional barriers surrounding divorce under the Confucian cultural norms of that era [61]. Growing up during the early post-revolutionary period and the Three Years of Great Famine, these individuals often experienced severe economic insecurity and a loss of family completeness [62, 63]. This structural vulnerability appears to be linked not to marital breakdown, but by extreme material deprivation. This was further compounded by high rates of parental mortality and disability associated with heavy physical labor and inadequate medical infrastructure. Second, the Maltreatment group (Class 1) exhibited high probabilities of physical abuse and emotional neglect. This trend potentially reflects the deep-rooted influence of traditional authoritarian parenting, exemplified by the Chinese proverb “spare the rod, spoil the child” [64]. During that era, corporal punishment was frequently viewed as a legitimate educational tool rather than a sign of household dysfunction [65]. Third, the Pervasive Adversity group (Class 2) represents an extremely marginalized population exposed to multiple overlapping traumas. Under conditions of severe material deprivation and a lack of public assistance, children exposed to such extreme adversity faced a high risk of premature mortality [66]. Consequently, this group represents only a small fraction of the cohort, which may reflect a demographic survivorship bias among those who managed to reach older age despite severe early-life adversity.

Finally, a notable divergence from Western ACE models is the absence of childhood sexual abuse (CSA) and related trauma domains in our study, which requires contextualization. Objectively, this omission is a direct constraint of the CHARLS life-history module’s baseline design, which did not assess sexual trauma. However, this measurement limitation itself reflects a deeper socio-cultural reality in China. Over the life course, the disclosure of sexual trauma remains a deeply socio-cultural theoretical issue. Within the framework of Confucian morality and collectivist culture [61], sexual trauma is heavily stigmatized and carries profound personal and familial shame. The traditional norm of preserving family honor by concealing domestic private matters, combined with emotional stoicism, makes this topic a deep cultural taboo among older cohorts [67]. Directly querying such sensitive domains in a national survey of this generation would likely be associated with high refusal rates and risk secondary psychological trauma for the respondents. This socio-cultural boundary highlights that the measurement and interpretation of the ACEs construct in non-Western contexts cannot simply mirror Western frameworks, but must conceptually redefine the boundaries of childhood adversity for older Chinese populations.

Divergent pathways of specific ACEs patterns to SA

Building upon the identified latent classes, our longitudinal analyses utilizing both GEE and GBTM further distinguish between the temporary, acute implications of certain childhood adversities and the persistent associations with adverse aging trajectories. While the GEE results indicate that all three ACEs patterns are linked to a reduced immediate probability of achieving SA, the longitudinal trajectory models indicate that Maltreatment (Class 1) shows no significant long-term relationship with SA membership over the follow-up. In contrast, Pervasive Adversity (Class 2) and Household Dysfunction (Class 3) are associated with a substantially higher likelihood of falling into the Chronically Low-Stable trajectory.

This divergence may be attributed to the unique characteristics of the trauma within the Maltreatment group. Although physical abuse and emotional neglect relate to immediate somatic and emotional distress, this non-structural trauma can often be buffered by an intact family network. Over the life course, these challenges can be compensatorily addressed through late-life psychosocial resources, such as peer support and formal education, demonstrating a resilient capacity to avoid negative long-term health pathways [68, 69].

Unlike the potentially reversible relationship of Maltreatment, Pervasive Adversity and Household Dysfunction appear to operate through distinct life-course pathways, potentially relating to the persistent classification of individuals within the Chronically Low-Stable trajectory. Specifically, Pervasive Adversity may relate to long-term adverse outcomes primarily through the biological embedding of stress and the gradual depletion of somatic and cognitive reserves. At the neurobiological level, although not directly tested in our study, prior literature suggests that persistent toxic stress may alter early brain development. This process may relate to amygdala hyperactivation, which potentially heightens anxiety, while simultaneously affecting the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex to impair memory and cognitive control [22]. Such chronic stress might also alter the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis threshold through epigenetic mechanisms, which can relate to the dysregulation of stress hormones, such as cortisol, and promote low-grade systemic inflammation [70]. Ultimately, based on theoretical biological models, this sustained biological embedding may translate into an elevated allostatic load [24]. This accumulation is hypothesized to deplete the reserves essential for healthy aging, potentially placing individuals at higher risk for cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders, and cognitive decline [23].

On the other hand, Household Dysfunction appears to be linked to long-term declines primarily through the compromise of psychosocial support systems and subsequent socio-emotional embedding. Such structural disruption within early caregiving systems can become biologically and emotionally embedded during critical developmental periods. In particular, based on prior developmental research, the absence of secure attachment is associated with accelerated telomere shortening, potentially compromising biological resilience [71]. Furthermore, existing empirical evidence indicates that early household vulnerability is linked to lower interpersonal trust and social capacity, which may consequently increase susceptibility to late-life depressive symptoms [72, 73]. Entering older age with these developmental deficits often affects an individual’s resilience against late-life stressors. Consequently, the resulting depletion of psychosocial reserves is often associated with a higher likelihood of depressive symptoms and passive social participation.

While the trajectory models effectively identify who is persistently classified into this adverse pathway, they do not explain how the aging mind-body system is configured under these early-life deficits. To investigate the specific systemic patterns associated with this late-life trajectory, we turn to network analysis.

Systemic rigidity of SA networks in later life

Having established that severe early-life deficits are associated with adverse aging trajectories, we further utilized exploratory network analysis to investigate how this vulnerability is organized within the late-life mind-body system. From the perspective of Dynamical Systems Theory, a healthy organism should function as a resilient system in which other domains can provide buffering and compensatory support even when a single domain is compromised [74]. However, our exploratory analysis found that for individuals on the adverse Chronically Low-Stable aging trajectory, the contemporaneous health network presented a more tightly coupled and densely interconnected structure. A plausible explanatory pathway for this tightly coupled network, as suggested by prior literature, may lie in the systematic wear-and-tear of mind-body defense mechanisms, hypothesized to arise from long-term allostatic load and the biological embedding of stress [24].

The central manifestation of this rigid network appears to be the marginalization of cognitive function. In the Chronically Low-Stable group, the network centrality of cognition was reduced, while its coupling (edge strength) with chronic diseases was enhanced. Based on prior physiological literature, this tight coupling may plausibly represent a potential downstream endpoint of lifelong allostatic wear. ACEs potentially trigger chronic pro-inflammatory cascades and microvascular degradation, which could establish a compromised baseline in later life [75]. In older age, these systemic vulnerabilities appear to manifest as accelerated neuropathological changes, such as white matter hyperintensities and hippocampal atrophy, alongside clustered metabolic and cardiovascular conditions [76]. If this cumulative physiological wear exhausts baseline reserves, the aging organism might lose the capacity to decouple and buffer chronic conditions from cognitive health. Consequently, the contemporaneous relationship between these two domains could form a reciprocal feedback cycle, where a minor decline in cognitive function might compromise the self-management of chronic physical illnesses, potentially exacerbating multimorbidity [77]. Simultaneously, the systemic inflammation and metabolic toxicity associated with coexisting chronic conditions might breach the already degraded blood-brain barrier, potentially accelerating further neuropathology and cognitive decline [78]. By contrast, the lower allostatic load and preserved reserves in the Sustained High-Decline group appear to allow the health network to maintain greater flexibility. This is reflected in their stronger coupling between cognition and social engagement, which may indicate a capacity to dynamically deploy social resources as behavioral compensations to buffer age-related physical and cognitive declines [79].

A second prominent feature of this densely coupled network is the stronger contemporaneous association between depressive symptoms and physical function. Because Household Dysfunction adversity disrupts early secure attachment and downregulates emotional resilience, individuals lack critical psychological and social buffers as they enter older age [71]. When facing the physical decline of natural aging, the cumulative effect of this chronic allostatic load may compromise these vulnerable psychological buffers [80], rendering individuals highly susceptible to severe learned helplessness and clinical depression. Subsequently, the psychomotor retardation and social withdrawal associated with depression can accelerate further physical deterioration [81]. This potential vulnerability of psychosocial defenses, although analyzed cross-sectionally, is consistent with literature suggesting a reciprocal relationship between depression and physical function.

Socio-demographic modulators of SA trajectories

While the aforementioned biological and systemic mechanisms dictate the internal dysfunction of the aging network, we must also acknowledge the profound macro-environmental forces that catalyze these outcomes. Beyond the direct physiological embedding of early-life trauma, key socio-demographic covariates profoundly shape and alter this life-course trajectory.

While previous research on urban-rural differences has yielded inconsistent findings [25, 82], our GEE analyses revealed a significant moderating effect of residence, demonstrating that ACEs exerted a significant longitudinal harm on SA only among rural older adults. This is likely due to the split of urban-rural resources caused by China’s unique dual household registration (Hukou) system [83]. Urban residents benefit from superior medical care and pension security. These late-life compensatory resources act at the system level to dilute the physiological toxicity of early-life trauma [84]. In contrast, rural older adults lack these post-adversity buffers and frequently endure prolonged physical labor, potentially exacerbating their cumulative allostatic load and systemic wear-and-tear [85].

Furthermore, education serves as a critical structural determinant. Although the interaction between educational attainment and ACEs was non-significant in the GEE models, the risk of falling into the Chronically Low-Stable trajectory in long-term trajectory prediction remains exceptionally high for individuals with less than a primary school education. Importantly, the predictive strength of low educational attainment exceeds that of the baseline ACE classes, suggesting that broader life-course structural inequalities may exert a more dominant influence on long-term health pathways than early-life trauma alone. While these prominent roles of education and rural residence do not undermine the core findings regarding ACEs, they suggest that early-life trauma operates within, and is heavily compounded by, pervasive socioeconomic and structural inequalities across the lifespan. This finding suggests that while subsequent education may not directly reverse the biological embedding of early adversity, the resource deficits faced by low-education individuals accumulate and amplify over the life course, ultimately locking them into adverse health trajectories [86, 87].

Strengths and limitations

The main strengths of this study include utilizing the CHARLS database, which allows for the tracking of long-term health dynamics, and combining multiple person-centered and complex systems methods (LCA, GBTM, and network analysis) to provide an in-depth investigation of the complex relationship between ACEs and SA from the perspectives of pattern identification, dynamic trajectories, and system structure. We also adopted a multidimensional definition of SA that encompasses physiological, psychological, and functional domains, offering a comprehensive representation of late-life health.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, the retrospectively assessed data on ACEs were based on self-reports, which may be subject to recall bias and potentially influenced by participants’ current health or psychological status. Furthermore, there may be culturally shaped underreporting of highly sensitive experiences within the cohort’s specific socio-cultural context. Second, the potential effects of the frequency, intensity, or duration of adverse childhood experiences were not considered. Third, due to the baseline design of the CHARLS life history module, CSA was not measured. From a statistical perspective, the lack of a CSA measure introduces non-differential misclassification. This bias causes some high-risk individuals who actually suffered severe sexual abuse to be misclassified into the Low Adversity or Maltreatment groups. Mathematically, this misclassification attenuates the estimated odds ratios (ORs) toward the null [88]. Therefore, our findings regarding the adverse impacts of childhood adversity on SA potentially represent highly conservative estimates, and the true life-course burden of early-life trauma might be even more severe than documented. Fourth, due to data constraints regarding the contemporaneous assessment of SA domains across follow-up waves, our network analysis was restricted to a cross-sectional exploratory design. Consequently, while we identified distinct contemporaneous network structures, these contemporaneous edges cannot establish the temporal or causal direction of the pathological feedback loops over time. Fifth, our longitudinal analysis was unweighted because the CHARLS database does not provide official longitudinal panel weights for follow-up waves after 2013. Although previous methodological research suggests that unweighted analyses can provide valid and efficient parameter estimates for relational research questions after adjusting for key covariates, the absence of longitudinal weights could still limit the generalizability of our trajectory and network findings to the broader Chinese older population. Sixth, the Pervasive Adversity group (Class 2) comprised a relatively small proportion of our study sample, which might be a consequence of demographic survivorship bias where individuals exposed to extreme early-life toxicity faced higher premature mortality. This small sample size contributes to wider confidence intervals and greater uncertainty in the statistical estimates for this specific class. Nonetheless, the E-value results provide supplementary information about the potential influence of unmeasured confounding.

Conclusions

In summary, this study suggests that specific childhood adversity patterns, particularly pervasive adversity and household dysfunction, are associated with lower late-life regulatory resilience in the mind-body system. These childhood experiences are linked to long-term biological and psychosocial cascades, which are hypothesized to relate to cumulative allostatic load under macro-environmental constraints. This developmental process is associated with differences in the late-life health network, characterized by a more densely interconnected contemporaneous structure, lower cognitive centrality, and close somatic-psychological coupling. Grounded in a non-Western perspective, these findings suggest that traditional, single-disease clinical models may be less effective for older populations on the Chronically Low-Stable trajectory. Public health strategies could therefore benefit from a shift toward systemic, multidisciplinary interventions to address these highly interconnected health domains in later life. Additionally, since these adverse pathways appear to be rooted in early-life adversity and moderated by socio-demographic disparities, policy-level efforts should consider prioritizing early-life childhood support, educational equity, and the narrowing of rural-urban resource gaps to mitigate cumulative allostatic load early in life, thereby helping older adults maintain a dynamic equilibrium and resilience. Crucially, the prominent roles of socioeconomic factors observed in our study do not undermine our findings regarding childhood adversity; rather, they suggest that childhood trauma is nested within, and compounded by, broader socioeconomic and structural inequalities across the entire life course.
