Undifferentiated Pleomorphic Cardiac Sarcoma
Authors: Rahul Gandhi, Samuel King, Bijia Shi, Alexander Sasse
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAngiosarcomas are rare and often a fatal manifestation of cardiac tumors. This paper presents a case of a sarcoma with protrusion through the mitral valve. Angiographic findings demonstrated a tumor blush, supplied by the right coronary and left anterior descending arteries. The patient survived for a few weeks after the diagnosis.Key Words: cardiac mass, cardiac sarcoma, cardiac tumor, left atrial neoplasm, tumor blush, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma

A 71-year-old woman presented with a 4-week history of rapidly progressive shortness of breath on exertion and associated chest discomfort. Point of care cardiac ultrasound suggested a large atrial mass protruding through the mitral valve with a moderate pericardial effusion. A characteristic tumor plop was not heard. Confirmatory transesophageal echocardiography revealed a large heterogenous vascularized mass with an irregular surface of 5.8 × 3.6 cm (Videos 1 and 2). On diagnostic coronary angiography, a tumor blush was revealed, increasing suspicion of a malignant neoplasm (Figures 1A and 1B). The tumor was being supplied by both the right coronary and left anterior descending arteries. Diagnostic pericardiocentesis failed to capture malignant cells on flow cytometry; hence, we proceeded to direct biopsy of the intracardiac lesion via an endovascular venous approach with transeptal puncture and intracardiac mapping (CARTO navigation, Biosense Webster Inc).

Angiographic and Histologic Characteristics of Tumor

(A and B) Diagnostic coronary angiogram: a tumor blush (red arrows) is clearly seen showing blood supply from the right coronary and left anterior descending arteries. (C and D) Histology confirming the diagnosis of undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma; areas of mitosis (dotted circle) and necrosis (dotted circle) are seen on frozen section of tissue obtained through biopsy.

Histology confirmed that this was an undifferentiated pleomorphic cardiac sarcoma: a rare and aggressive type of primary cardiac tumor (Figures 1C and 1D) The patient was discussed at a cardiac multidisciplinary meeting and no surgical options were identified. The patient declined further invasive or chemotherapeutic treatment in lieu of risks vs benefits, and elected for palliative management. As a tissue diagnosis had been made and no active treatment requested, no further imaging studies were undertaken. She died peacefully several weeks later.

Although rare, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas represent one-third of all primary cardiac sarcomas.1 They account for approximately 1.7% of all cardiac tumors and are typically localized in the left atrium, often involving the mitral valve.1 These tumors are known for their high recurrence rates and invasive nature. Hemodynamic effects due to effusions, cardiac tamponade, and mitral valve obstruction, lead to a poor prognosis in patients with these tumors, with a mean survival of 3 months to 1 year, even with radical resection.1 Metastasis of these sarcomas to other organs (eg, lungs, retroperitoneum) has been documented.2 Diagnosis of undifferentiated pleomorphic cardiac sarcomas usually requires histopathologic and immunohistochemical examinations to confirm their presence and distinguish them from other cardiac neoplasms.2 The presence of tumor blush on angiography is a direct visualization of the angiogenic potential of angiosarcomas due to overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factor and basic fibroblast growth factor.3 This overexpression may represent future therapeutic targets. Due to the rarity of these tumors, the sensitivity and specificity of a tumor blush is unknown. A multidisciplinary team meeting may be needed to best identify treatment options for patients. Further research will help develop specific guidelines to prolong survival.

Angiosarcomas are rare intracardiac tumors associated with a high mortality and require a multifaceted approach to diagnosis and management.
