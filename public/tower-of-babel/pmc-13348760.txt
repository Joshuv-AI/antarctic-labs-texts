Alleviation of the adverse effects of low atmospheric pressure stress by putrescine in Matricaria chamomilla
Authors: Mahsa Sardari, Halimeh Hassanpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated the effects of low atmospheric pressure (hypobaric stress) and exogenous putrescine on physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses of Matricaria chamomilla seedlings. Plants were exposed to hypobaric stress (200 mbar), putrescine treatment (0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, and 0.8 mmol L⁻¹), and their combination for 2, 3, and 4 weeks to evaluate potential stress mitigation mechanisms.ResultsHypobaric stress significantly reduced growth parameters and antioxidant capacity, which was accompanied by decreased flavonoid and phenolic contents as well as downregulation of chalcone synthase (CHS) and flavone synthase II (FNSII) expression. Elevated hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) levels under low-pressure conditions were associated with growth inhibition and oxidative imbalance. In contrast, putrescine treatment improved biomass accumulation, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, increased nitric oxide (NO) levels, and reduced H₂O₂ accumulation. Putrescine also partially restored phenolic and flavonoid contents. A positive correlation was observed between increased NO levels, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and improved growth, suggesting that increased NO levels are associated with improved antioxidant responses and may contribute to putrescine-related stress mitigation. Notably, application of putrescine treatment under hypobaric stress resulted in the most pronounced improvement in physiological performance, antioxidant status, secondary metabolite accumulation, and upregulation of CHS and FNSII transcript levels.ConclusionThese findings indicate that putrescine application is associated with improved tolerance of M. chamomilla seedlings to hypobaric stress, potentially through modulation of redox balance and activation of the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway. The responses appear to involve coordinated redox-related signaling processes rather than a single causal pathway. This study contributes to understanding plant adaptive mechanisms to low-pressure environments and provides physiological insights relevant to controlled low-pressure cultivation systems, such as those proposed for bioregenerative life-support applications.Keywords: Matricaria chamomilla, Hypobaric stress, Putrescine, Antioxidant metabolism

Introduction

Plants play a critical role in life-support systems for space missions, providing food, oxygen, and psychological benefits to astronauts. For over five decades, research has explored the use of plants to sustain human life in extraterrestrial environments [1]. Early studies focused primarily on algae, but attention soon shifted to higher plants and fresh vegetables with high nutritional value and caloric density, better suited to meet astronauts’ dietary needs [2]. With advances in human space exploration, a key challenge is cultivating plants under conditions such as low atmospheric pressure, selected for engineering and safety reasons, while maintaining plant growth and productivity [3].

The atmospheric pressure at sea level on Earth is approximately 1010 mbar, but it decreases with altitude and approaches zero at approximately 30,000 m. Plants with exceptional adaptations to high altitudes can survive at altitudes of approximately 5000 m, showing resilience to low atmospheric pressure and decreased oxygen levels [4]. In addition to the challenge of adapting plants to lower atmospheric pressure in space, climate change on Earth is also increasing temperatures, which causes plant species to migrate upward along mountain gradients [5, 6]. In this context, the decrease in atmospheric pressure with increasing altitude can pose a new challenge for plant growth.

Low atmospheric pressure (< 1 atm) offers distinct advantages, including decreased structural mass for habitats and reduced gas leakage, which are essential for a mission with a long-duration halt. Low atmospheric pressure was utilized to make extravehicular activities, such as those during the Apollo and Mercury, more practical in earlier missions [2, 7]. Likewise, research indicates that atmospheric regeneration and water purification are enhanced in low-pressure environments, which increases the efficiency of bioregenerative life support systems [8]. Although low atmospheric pressure can affect plant physiology by altering photosynthesis, respiration, transpiration, secondary metabolite production, and antioxidant responses [6]. Under low-pressure conditions, the availability of key gases, such as CO₂ and O₂, is reduced, which affects photosynthesis, respiration, and transpiration. Since these processes depend on gas partial pressures, their reactions vary across plant species. Growth is often inhibited under such conditions, and plants may alter their stomatal characteristics—typically by exhibiting greater stomatal density but smaller pores—in a way that balances carbon uptake with water conservation. Several research has shown that low atmospheric pressure alters the secondary metabolites accumulation and the antioxidant enzymes activity in various plants, including lettuce, tomato, Arabis alpina, and algae [2, 6, 9, 10].

Understanding these dynamics is vital for optimizing plant systems for future space missions. Growing crops such as chamomilla could provide food and valuable antioxidants, which are essential for reducing oxidative stress in space [11]. Chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla L.) is an essential medicinal herb native to Iran and is cultivated in Europe, North America, and Asia. The drug obtained from this plant is included in the pharmacopeia of 26 countries [12]. As a drug, it is used for flatulence, colic, hysteria, and intermittent fever. The flowers of chamomile contain 0.2 to 1.9% blue essential oil, which has a variety of uses [13]. Apigenin, a key flavonoid found in chamomile seedlings, has antioxidant properties that contribute to several biological effects, including anti-inflammatory, anticancer, neuroprotective, antiallergic, and antimicrobial activities [14]. Pharmacological compounds of chamomile include secondary metabolites such as sesquiterpenoids, coumarins, and flavonoids [15, 16]. Despite its applications in the drug industry, it is also helpful in the cosmetic, dye, and food industries [13, 14].

To date, various strategies have been employed to increase the yield of valuable plant growth and resistance, as well as promote their metabolites, especially under different stress conditions. Putrescine (Put) is a natural polyamine found in plants that has multiple effects on growth, development, and tolerance to environmental stresses. It can affect cellular processes such as controlling membrane integrity, cell division, gene transcription, and DNA replication, and can regulate the growth of roots and stems [17]. As a signal molecule, it interacts with several molecules, like gas and phytohormone molecules, leading to various downstream effects. Polyamine (PA) metabolism affects NO generation, a key component of signaling growth in plants [18]. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) could mediate NO production as a result of PA oxidation by diamine oxidase and polyamine oxidase or by mechanisms associated with the PA pathway [19]. The role of NO as a regulator of PA biosynthesis, by increasing the expression of related genes and decreasing PA oxidase enzyme activity, confirms NO’s role in PA homeostasis [20].

Studies have shown that increasing putrescine concentration can improve seed germination and early plant growth. It is also known as a protective molecule against environmental stress. The putrescine level in plant tissues increases by biotic and abiotic stresses [21]. This increase can help reduce oxidative damage and improve tolerance to environmental stresses. Additionally, exogenous putrescine can help regulate antioxidant enzyme activity and protect against free radical damage [22]. Studies have also shown that reducing atmospheric pressure can affect the synthesis and production rate of flavonoids, leading to decreases or increases depending on the pressure. However, the gene pathways related to these changes are unknown and require further study [23]. On the other hand, other studies have shown that treatment with putrescine can increase the expression of genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis. For example, treatment with putrescine increased PAL and PLR genes’ expression significantly, which are essential genes in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, in Linum album cells [24, 25].

To our knowledge, there have been some experiments on plants regarding the effects of low atmospheric pressure and putrescine on plants individually. Nevertheless, no study has reported the impact of the interaction between exogenous putrescine and low atmospheric pressure. This study was conducted to investigate the physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses of Matricaria chamomilla to low atmospheric pressure and exogenous putrescine, to improve our understanding of plant performance under hypobaric stress. Low atmospheric pressure represents a significant abiotic constraint that can alter metabolic allocation and stress-response pathways in plants. Elucidating these responses, particularly at the molecular and metabolic levels, contributes to defining the tolerance limits of terrestrial plants and provides valuable insights for optimizing plant growth in controlled environments relevant to future bioregenerative life-support systems.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions (culture and treatment)

M. chamomilla seeds were supplied by Pakan Bazr company (Isfahan, Iran). All chemicals used in the study were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich® (Burlington, United States). The seeds were first sterilized and then cultured in Petri dishes and were grown at average day/night temperatures of 25/18°C with a 16 h light / 8 h dark photoperiod. Illumination was provided by cool white fluorescent lamps, delivering a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of approximately 70 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. A week later, the cultured seeds were transferred to glass jars with filter lids and subjected to low atmospheric pressure for five days. Temperature inside the hypobaric chamber was regularly monitored and showed no significant deviation from ambient growth conditions. They were then transferred to Murashige and Skoog (MS) media. The seedlings were collected weekly (2nd−, 3rd−, and 4th-week-old) (Fig. 1). in the final stage samples transferred to a -70 °C freezer for subsequent stages.

The alleviation effects of putrescine on Matricaria chamomilla seedling morphology in low atmospheric pressure condition. Ctrl with normal atmospheric pressure; Pressure with low atmospheric pressure (200 mbar); Putrescine treatment with normal pressure; Pre + Put is putrescine treatment after low atmosphere pressure

A custom-designed device was applied to reduce atmospheric pressure via a two-stage vacuum pump (model VW280N, 1 HP power). The air pressure inside the container was monitored via a manometer on the device. Chamomile seedlings treated with 200 mbar of pressure. Control samples (with an atmospheric pressure of 860 mbar) were also placed under similar environmental conditions outside the device. The control pressure (860 mbar) corresponded to the ambient atmospheric pressure at the experimental location (approximately 1400 m above sea level) and was therefore considered the natural growth condition for Matricaria chamomilla seedlings, rather than a hypobaric treatment relative to sea-level pressure (1013 mbar).

To investigate the ameliorative effect of putrescine, chamomile seedlings were grown under in vitro culture conditions, and putrescine was applied by supplementing the MS growth medium with the indicated concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, and 0.8 mmol L⁻¹) for three weeks and compared with control seedlings (grown in putrescine-free media). Growth-related parameters, including fresh weight, dry weight, seedling length, and visible stress symptoms, were evaluated (Table 1). Finally, the 0.2 putrescine treatment, which had the most significant positive effect on chamomile seedlings, was selected for use in the main phase of the study.

Growth parameters

The fresh weight (FW), height, total protein, and dry weight (DW) of the samples were used as growth indices. The plants were oven-dried for DW estimation, and the length of each sample was also measured immediately after collection. Protein content was determined according to Bradford (1976). Protein content monitored based on the changes in the maximum absorbance of the Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 dye from 465 to 595 nm following binding to denatured proteins [26].

NO and H2O2 content

There was NO generation confirmed by intercellular nitrite concentration measured via the Griess reagent [27]. Here, 1.5 mL of PO43− buffer (100 mM; pH = 7) was used to extract 0.2 g of frozen samples. After centrifugation (12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C), PO43− buffer (100 mM; pH = 7) and 250 µL of Griess reagent were combined with the resulting supernatant at ambient temperature for 30 min. The level of NO was determined at 540 nm. The NO concentration was measured using calibration curve of sodium nitrite as µmol g− 1 FW.

The H2O2 level was measured following Velikova et al. protocol [28]. Homogenized leaf tissue (0.5 g) in 4 mL of 0.1% (w/v) TCA on ice was centrifuged (10000 rpm, 15 min). Subsequently, 1 mL of KI and 1 mL of potassium phosphate buffer were mixed with 0.5 mL of the supernatant, and the level of absorbance was determined at 390 nm. The H2O2 level was quantified by a standard calibration curve at varying concentrations of H2O2.

Estimation of antioxidant enzyme activities assays (POX, CAT, SOD)

For the enzyme assay, homogenization of fresh samples (0.3 g) was done at 4 °C with 3 mL of Tris-HCl (1 M, pH 6.8) to measure the total protein level and enzyme activity. Afterward, 20 min of centrifuging was performed on the homogenate at 4 °C and 13,000 rpm. For further protein measurement (Bradford assay) and enzyme activity assessment, the supernatant was stored at ‒70 °C [26]. To measure SOD activity, its ability as an inhibitor of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) was measured under light conditions. The mixture consisted of 0.1 mM EDTA, 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7), 13 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, 2 µM riboflavin, and 100 µL of total protein extract. The mixture reaction was irradiated for 16 min, and absorbance level was determined at 560 nm compared to a nonirradiated blank. By definition, one unit of SOD is the amount of enzyme that inhibited reduction of NBT in the assay conditions [29]. CAT was assessed following Aebi through determining the initial disappearance rate of H2O2. The reaction mixture was comprised of 40 µL of enzyme, 3% H2O2, and 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). A decline in absorption happened for 180 s and the CAT activity was reported as H2O2 decomposed in each minute per milligram of protein. To determine POX activity, a reaction mixture was used (0.1 mL of benzidine (20 mM), 0.2 mL of 3% H2O2, 2 mL of 0.2 M acetate buffer (pH = 5), and 30 µL of enzyme extract) at 530 nm [30].

Total phenolic and flavonoid contents

The methanolic extract was formed using 0.1 g of the sample was mixed in 5 mL of 80% methanol and centrifuging (5000 rpm, 20 min). In addition, 0.1 mL of methanolic and 2.5 mL of 10% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent were mixed for measurement of the total phenol content. To neutralize the mixtures, 7% of sodium bicarbonate was used and the level of absorbance was measured at 765 nm [31].

The total content of flavonoid was determined so that after homogenizing 0.1 g of fresh leaves in 2 ml of 80% methanol to prepare the primary extract. Then, 0.5 ml of this extract was combined with 0.1 ml of 10% aluminum chloride solution, 1.5 ml of methanol, 0.1 ml of 1 M potassium acetate solution, and 2.8 ml of distilled water. After a 30-minute reaction period, the absorbance was determined at 415 nm [29].

Analysis of flavonoid compounds using HPLC

Fresh samples (0.2 g) were ground and homogenized with 1.5 mL of 40% aqueous methanol and 0.5% acetic acid. The mixtures were left to rest for 12 h and centrifuged afterward (13,000 rpm for 12 min). Then it was injected into the HPLC system. The mobile phase (A solvent) consisted of deionized water with 0.01% (v/v) phosphoric acid and (B solvent) consisted of acetonitrile. The gradient system was 18% B (0–30 min), 67% B (30–60 min), and 18% B (60–70 min). The flow rate was 0.8 ml.min− 1 at 25 °C. Sample peaks were determined by adjusting their retention times according to original standards (Sigma Aldrich, ≥ 98%). A UV detector was set at 254 and 350 nm to detect flavonoids [32].

Gene expression (extraction of cDNA synthesis, total RNA, and qPCR)

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒PCR) measured the expression of FNSII and CHS, with actin serving as an internal control, to assess the impacts of low atmospheric pressure and putrescine on the expression of chamomilla genes. Primers (Table 2) were designed via Clustal Omega, NCBI Primer BLAST, and Gene Runner. Total RNA was obtained from 100 mg samples via a total RNA isolation kit (Zaver Zist Azma, Tehran, Iran). Integrity and quantity of RNA were measured using a Nanodrop and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA synthesis was carried out on 1 µg of total RNA, 2 µl of oligo-dT mix primers, 1 µl of random hexamers, 2 µl of deoxy-nucleoside triphosphate (dNTP), and 1 µl of reverse transcriptase (RevertAidTM M MuLV; AddBio) at 42 °C for 70 min, followed by inactivation at 72 °C for 10 min. qPCR was carried out via the Step One RT-PCR System (ABI 7500; Applied Biosystems). RT‒PCR was implemented using SYBR Green Master Mix (Ampliqon). The mixture included 2 µL of cDNA sample, 5 µL of master mix for RT‒PCR, and 0.2 µL of primers (forward and reverse) (10 pmol), with a final volume of 10 µL. RT‒PCR cycling was conducted as follows: 95 °C for 15 min, 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s, 54 °C for 25 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. The whole process was repeated at least once, and the specificity of the qPCR products was confirmed using melt curve analysis and polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The mean DCT values for the treatment and control conditions were calculated, and the expression of the genes was determined through ratio formula (ratio = 2 −∆∆CT) [33].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were based on a completely randomized design (CRD) and three replications. The ANOVA was implemented in SPSS (Version. 25). The differences between treated samples were evaluated via Duncan’s test and considered significant when p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Effects of low atmospheric pressure and put and the combination of them on the growth parameters and protein content of chamomilla seedlings

The FW of the samples increased over 4 weeks in all the groups, with variations among the treatments (Fig. 2a). During the two posttreatment weeks, plants under low pressure had the lowest FW (0.07 g), which was less than the control (0.12 g). Putrescine under normal pressure caused a moderate increase in FW, but the combined pressure and putrescine (Pre + Put) treatment significantly increased FW, surpassing all the other treatments. By week three, the differences were more evident: pressure alone continued to inhibit growth, whereas the combined treatment significantly increased the biomass, exceeding both the control and the putrescine-only treatment. This trend persisted for four weeks, with the highest FW in the combined group, which was significantly different from the other groups. The pressure-only condition had the most prominent inhibitory effect on growth. The DW trends mirror a similar pattern to that of FW (Fig. 2b). After 2 weeks, the pressure-only group presented the lowest dry biomass, which was less than those of the control and putrescine groups. The combination of pressure and putrescine resulted in a DW of 0.021 g, which was considerably greater than that of all the other treatments. At 3 and 4 weeks, the DW in the combined treatment group increased to 0.036 g and 0.052 g, respectively, surpassing those in the control group and both individual treatments.

Effects of low atmospheric pressure, putrescine treatment, and their combination on FW (a), DW (b), total height (c), and protein content (d) in chamomilla samples. Values are means ± SD of three replicates. Different letters indicated significant (p < 0.05) differences

Plant height was significantly affected by treatment type and posttreatment time (Fig. 2c). At two weeks, exposure to low atmospheric pressure alone resulted in the lowest shoot height (1.8 cm), which was significantly less than that of the control group (2.5 cm). Adding exogenous putrescine under pressure conditions resulted in a partial recovery of plant height (3.8 cm), which was higher than that in pressure-only and control groups. After 3 weeks, the trend became clearer. The plants in the combined Pre + Put treatment reached an average height of 6.1 cm, which was significantly greater than that of all the other treatments, including the control (4.2 cm) and the putrescine-only groups (5 cm). During the 4 weeks, combined treatment consistently showed the highest height across all time points.

The protein content increased progressively across all treatment groups during the 4 weeks, with the most notable increases observed in the Pre + Put group (Fig. 2d). At 2 weeks, the pressure-only group presented significantly lower protein content than the control, whereas the combined treatment led to a notable growth in protein content. At 3 weeks, the protein content in the Pre + Put group peaked at 165 mg g⁻¹ FW, which was higher than that in the control group and both single-treatment groups. By week 4, the combined treatment resulted in the highest protein accumulation observed in the present study, significantly outperforming all other treatments, including the putrescine-only and control treatments. These findings indicate a strong synergistic effect of pressure and putrescine on nitrogen metabolism and stress-responsive protein accumulation.

Effects of low atmospheric pressure and put and the combination of them on the NO and H2O2 content of chamomilla seedlings

Endogenous NO levels were significantly influenced by both treatment and time. At 2 weeks, plants exposed to low atmospheric pressure presented the lowest NO content, which was significantly less than the control (Fig. 3a). In contrast, both the putrescine-only and combined (Pre + Put) treatments significantly increased the NO content, with the latter reaching the highest value. By week three, the trend persisted, and NO levels in both the putrescine-only and Pre + Put groups peaked at 16.5 µmol g− 1 FW, which was significantly greater than those in the pressure-only group and the control. These elevated NO levels remained stable throughout week 4, especially in the Pre + Put group, indicating a sustained increase in NO production under putrescine treatment. H₂O₂ levels sharply increase under pressure but are suppressed by putrescine (Fig. 3b). The H₂O₂ content was significantly elevated in plants under low atmospheric pressure across all time points. At week two, the pressure-only group exhibited a 2.6-fold increase in H₂O₂ levels, which was considerably higher than in the control and other treatment groups. Notably, plants treated with putrescine (with or without pressure) presented approximately 2-fold decrease in H₂O₂ accumulation, suggesting an antioxidant role for putrescine. This pattern was even more pronounced at week three, when pressure-stressed plants showed maximal H₂O₂ accumulation, whereas the other treatments maintained substantially lower levels. At week four, the H₂O₂ content in the pressure-only group remained elevated, whereas the suppressive effect of the polyamine treatments was sustained.

Effects of low atmospheric pressure and put and the combination of them on the activity of POX, CAT, and SOD in chamomilla seedlings

POX activity exhibited significant variation across treatments and time points (Fig. 4a). At two weeks, plants under low pressure without treatment showed the lowest POX activity, which was significantly lower than that of all the other treatments. In contrast, the putrescine-only and Pre + Put groups presented significantly elevated POX activity. Across weeks three and four, a similar pattern persisted, while the pressure-only group maintained relatively low POX activity. Both the putrescine and combined treatments resulted in significantly greater POX activity.

Effects of low atmospheric pressure, putrescine treatment, and their combination on POX (a), CAT (b), and SOD (c) in chamomilla samples. Values are means ± SD of three replicates. Different letters indicated significant (p < 0.05) differences

CAT activity followed a distinct pattern and was markedly reduced under pressure-only conditions at all time points (Fig. 4b). In contrast, the combined treatment (Pre + Put) led to the highest CAT activity across all time points, especially at week three, followed closely by putrescine-only samples. The trend was consistent, with the control group maintaining intermediate CAT activity and the pressure treatment lagging. These results highlight the role of putrescine in restoring and enhancing CAT activity under atmospheric pressure stress.

SOD activity exhibited time-dependent and treatment-specific responses (Fig. 4c). At week two, the pressure-treated group presented substantially lower SOD activity, whereas both the control and putrescine-treated plants presented moderate SOD activity. The Pre + Put group showed elevated activity, indicating an early antioxidant response. By week four, the SOD activity in the combined treatment group peaked at 13.2 U/mg protein, which was higher than the other treatments.

Effects of low atmospheric pressure and Put and the combination of them on the total phenolic and flavonoid contents of chamomilla seedlings

The total phenolic content was significantly modulated by treatment type and growth duration (Fig. 5a). At two weeks, plants exposed to low pressure exhibited the lowest phenolic levels, which were significantly reduced compared to the control. In contrast, the application of putrescine or the combined treatment resulted in marked increases. By the third week, the phenolic content in both the putrescine-only and Pre + Put groups was significantly greater than that in the pressure treatment and the control groups. At four weeks, Pre + Put treatment resulted in the greatest phenolic accumulation, suggesting that putrescine stimulates phenolic metabolism as a protective antioxidant response, especially in combination with hypobaric stress. The flavonoid content displayed a more dynamic response over time (Fig. 5b). The pressure treatment resulted in the lowest flavonoid level at two weeks, whereas the putrescine and Pre + Put treatments resulted in increased flavonoid accumulation. This upward trend also continued through the third and fourth weeks, with the flavonoid content being highest in the Pre + Put group.

Effects of low atmospheric pressure and Put and the combination of them on individual flavonoid compounds in chamomilla seedlings

The content of individual flavonoids in Matricaria chamomilla seedlings was significantly affected by hypobaric pressure, putrescine application, and their combination (Table 3). Among all the treatments, the combined treatment (Pr + Put) consistently resulted in the highest accumulation of flavonoids across all the growth durations. At two weeks, putrescine alone increased the apigenin, vitexin, and quercetin contents compared with those of the control and pressure-treated plants, whereas the combined treatment (Pr + Put-2) further increased the apigenin content, with the apigenin content reaching 45.3 µg g⁻¹ FW and the vitexin content reaching 3.0 µg g⁻¹ FW. Under hypobaric pressure alone (Pr-2), a marked reduction in all flavonoid components, particularly myricetin and kaempferol, was observed. After three weeks, similar trends persisted. In comparison to the control and pressure-only treatments, the Pr + Put-3 treatment substantially increased the contents of apigenin and quercetin. Moreover, pressure alone continued to decrease the contents of most flavonoids. By the fourth week, flavonoid accumulation reached its maximum level. Compared with the control treatment, the Pr + Put-4 treatment significantly increased the contents of apigenin, vitexin, quercetin, and kaempferol, while the putrescine-only treatment also resulted in higher levels. In contrast, hypobaric pressure alone resulted in the lowest values for nearly all the compounds, confirming its inhibitory effect on flavonoid biosynthesis. Overall, the results suggest that hypobaric stress suppresses the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, whereas exogenous putrescine mitigates this effect and even enhances flavonoid accumulation under stress conditions, indicating a synergistic combination between putrescine and hypobaric stress.

Effects of low atmospheric pressure and put and the combination of them on expression of flavonoid biosynthetic genes (CHS and FNSII) in chamomilla seedlings

The relative expression levels of the CHS and FSNII genes were significantly influenced by hypobaric pressure, putrescine, and their combination over time (Fig. 6). For CHS expression, hypobaric pressure alone markedly suppressed gene expression at all sampling times, reaching the lowest level (approximately 0.7-fold greater than that of the control) after 3 and 4 weeks (Fig. 6a). In contrast, compared with the control, putrescine application increased CHS expression, with a more pronounced effect observed in the combined treatment. In comparison to the control, the Pr + Put treatment resulted in the highest expression levels, which increased approximately 2.2– to 2.5-fold in the fourth week. This finding indicated strong activation of the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway under the synergistic influence of hypobaric stress and putrescine. Similarly, the expression of FSNII followed a parallel trend. Nevertheless, it exhibited even greater induction in response to putrescine treatment (Fig. 6b). Hypobaric pressure alone caused a significant downregulation of FSNII transcripts throughout the experimental period, whereas exogenous putrescine markedly upregulated this gene. Compared with the control, the Pr + Put treatment resulted in the highest expression in the fourth week, with an approximately 3.8-fold increase. Overall, both the CHS and FSNII expression patterns demonstrate that hypobaric pressure inhibits the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, whereas putrescine reverses this suppression and acts as a potent inducer of flavone biosynthesis under stress conditions.

Discussion

Low atmospheric pressure at high altitudes creates hypoxic conditions [34, 35] and imposes distinct physical constraints on plant physiology [36], including reduced gas density, altered diffusion gradients, and increased transpiration rates, which collectively limit growth and biomass accumulation [37, 38]. In this study, chamomile seedlings under hypobaric stress (200 mbar) showed decreased FW, DW, protein content, and antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, POX, CAT), indicating disrupted redox balance and enhanced H₂O₂ accumulation. Flavonoid biosynthesis was also downregulated, with lower expression of CHS and FNSII, consistent with reduced secondary metabolite content. It seems that hypobaric stress caused a reduction in partial pressures of O₂ and CO₂, which led to concurrent hypoxia and limited CO₂ availability [39]. Under hypoxic conditions, limitations in mitochondrial respiration may disrupt electron transport efficiency, potentially promoting electron leakage within the mitochondrial inner membrane and enhancing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly H₂O₂ [39, 40]. This shift in redox balance represents an early metabolic consequence of hypobaric stress and may contribute to the downstream physiological and biochemical responses observed in chamomile seedlings. H₂O₂ acts as a redox molecule with dual roles in plant cells depending on its concentration. At low levels, it functions as a signaling molecule regulating stress responses and gene expression, whereas excessive accumulation causes oxidative damage to cellular components and may trigger programmed cell death [40, 41]. High concentration of H2O2 after low atmospheric pressure disrupted the balance between oxidants and antioxidants in chamomile seedlings and negatively impacted growth factors (FW, DW, protein content). On the other hand, reduced O₂ under hypobaric hypoxia limits mitochondrial respiration, forcing plants to rely on glycolysis for ATP production. This energy restriction hampers biosynthesis and growth, creating a metabolic stress [42]. In effect, hypobaric hypoxia uncouples carbon fixation from the energy supply: reduced CO₂ diffusion into leaves (due to low total pressure) and restricts photosynthesis, whereas impaired O₂ respiration reduces ATP production. It seems that at 200 mbar, the combined limitation of carbon and energy results in stunted growth and reduced biomass in chamomile [43]. Under hypobaric stress, H₂O₂ interacts with signaling molecules, including hormones and protein kinases, to co-regulate antioxidant activity and gene expression [44–46]. However, excessive ROS under these conditions can overwhelm plant defenses, leading to oxidative damage of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, ultimately impairing growth and triggering programmed cell death [47]. The results showed that hypobaric treatment alone decreased flavonoid accumulation. The levels of C-glycosyl flavone vitexin, flavanols quercetin, myricetin, and kaempferol, and the flavone glycosides diosmin and apigenin were all considerably reduced under hypobaric stress, suggesting that severe hypoxia may inhibit the transcriptional activation of key biosynthetic genes such as CHS and FSNII, both of which presented decreased expression under low-pressure stress. These results indicate a broad downregulation of the phenylpropanoid–flavonoid pathway under low pressure. CHS catalyzes the very first committed step of flavonoid synthesis [48], so reduced CHS expression would bottleneck the entire pathway. FNSII is a cytochrome P450 enzyme that converts flavanones into flavones (including apigenin), and its expression has been directly linked to apigenin production [49]. Also, other studies have shown that FNSII activity alone can determine apigenin levels in plant tissues. Notably, the pattern of CHI expression under hypobaric stress contrasts with many stresses (e.g., drought, UV), which typically induce CHS and flavonoid accumulation. Instead, hypobaric stress has been found to activate ABA- and desiccation-related signaling pathways in plants, which could repress secondary metabolism genes [50]. Phenolic compounds, as nonenzymatic antioxidants, play essential roles in inhibiting reactive oxygen species during oxidative stress. Additionally, in cooperation with peroxidases, phenolic compounds participate in the collection or removal of H2O2 [51]. However, the results of this study revealed that atmospheric pressure treatment at an intensity of 200 mbar prevented the plant from maintaining its antioxidant capacity and effectively swept the produced oxidants, as confirmed by the decrease in phenol and flavonoid contents after low atmospheric pressure treatment [52, 53]. Consistent with the results of this study and previous reports, hypoxic conditions induced by low atmospheric pressure inhibit the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, POX, and CAT, which are essential for scavenging reactive oxygen species in plants [54]. By reducing the activity of these enzymes under atmospheric pressure reduction treatment, ROS such as H2O2 are not swept from the cell, and their level remains high. These results indicate that low pressure induces a hypoxic-like state, diverting plant metabolism from growth-oriented and primary pathways toward stress adaptation for survival mechanisms.

Conversely, Exogenous putrescine application significantly mitigated these adverse effects of hypobaric conditions and facilitated samples to tolerate stress. Plants in the putrescine treatment under low atmospheric pressure exhibited higher FW, DW, protein content, and enhanced antioxidant capacity and reduced H₂O₂ accumulation compared to pressure alone. This improvement is likely mediated through NO signaling and H₂O₂ regulation (signaling role of H₂O₂ in low concentrations), stabilizing redox homeostasis, protecting cellular structures, and promoting the synthesis of flavonoids and phenolics.

Many studies have shown that putrescine exerts its protective effects by regulating ABA levels, scavenging free radicals, maintaining cellular pH and ionic balance, preventing lipid peroxidation, and modulating cation channels [55]. Additionally, as protonated polyamines, they stabilize macromolecules and regulate key cellular processes, including DNA/protein synthesis, cell division, differentiation, and gene expression, contributing to stress tolerance [20, 56, 57]. This improvement in putrescine-treated samples is likely mediated through nitric oxide signaling, as polyamines such as putrescine are known to induce NO production. As a signaling molecule, NO possesses antioxidant properties, preventing oxidative damage through controlling cellular redox homeostasis and increasing H2O2-scavenging enzyme activities, thus decreasing H2O2 levels and superoxide anions [58]. In putrescine-treated samples, NO may enhance the activities of ROS-scavenging enzymes, such as CAT, POD, and SOD, improve protein content and growth parameters and stability, and reduce membrane damage and intracellular ROS [59–61]. Similar findings in other species support these mechanisms. Application of putrescine enhanced growth and antioxidant responses in wheat under drought [62]. Also, the results of another study revealed that the total phenol content, flavonoid content, and antioxidant activity of Salvia officinalis increased significantly after putrescine treatment (1000 mg/L) [63]. Result of some studies revealed that putrescine increased shoot and root weights in wheat [64] and geranium [60] plants, suggesting that putrescine has a beneficial effect on morphological growth [65].

Additionally, the application of putrescine resulted in significant upregulation of the CHI and FNS genes, confirming that putrescine effectively reacts with secondary metabolism, thereby increasing the flavonoid and phenolic contents. The positive effects of putrescine under hypobaric stress likely result from the crosstalk between elevated NO levels induced by putrescine and the moderated H₂O₂ levels under low atmospheric pressure. This interaction helps plants maintain FW, DW, height, and overall growth. As the results showed, putrescine treatment under low atmospheric pressure restored protein content and significantly enhanced overall growth, suggesting that the simultaneous signaling of H₂O₂ and NO enables metabolic reprogramming and stabilizes energy metabolism under stress [40]. Several investigations have shown that NO and H₂O₂ take part in the same process by regulating downstream genes and proteins. The NO and H2O2 and their cross-talk’s role as a mediator of tolerance of plants to abiotic stresses are well established [66]. There are some candidate processes known where NO interacts with H₂O₂: (1) NO-regulated gene expression, which takes part in ROS scavenging systems; (2) redox-based posttranslational protein modification; and (3) the direct interaction of H₂O₂ and NO with proteins like ion channels, transcription factors, or enzymes [67]. The findings of studies showed that the combined treatment increased the activity of SOD, CAT, and POX far beyond the levels observed in single treatments, efficiently reducing the levels of oxidative stress markers. According to Uchida et al. pretreatment with H2O2 or NO triggered ROS-scavenging enzymes and the expression of oxidative stress-related genes in rice seedlings [68]. Moreover, treatment with putrescine combined with hypobaric stress markedly stimulated the accumulation of secondary metabolites. The increase was most evident in the combined treatment, indicating that moderate oxidative stress and NO may be involved in creating a favorable redox environment for secondary metabolism. The interaction between H2O2 and NO has critical functional implications for modulating plant transduction processes. As a result, in the combined treatment, the CHS and FSNII expression levels were the highest among all the groups, confirming that the NO-mediated signaling pathway stimulated by putrescine might override the inhibitory effects of low oxygen and activate transcription factors involved in phenylpropanoid metabolism [66]. It seems that adding exogenous putrescine activates the phenylpropanoid pathway, increasing the production of phenolic and flavonoid compounds. For example, in studies on Melissa officinalis, the optimal concentration of putrescine (1000 mg/L) significantly increased the flavonoid content [69]. Similarly, Samari et al. argued that putrescine increases the PAL enzyme expression (and consequently, phenolic products) in the cell culture of Linum album [25]. Putrescine increases metabolic flux toward the production of flavonoids such as apigenin by activating the expression of primary genes in the phenylpropanoid pathway, such as PAL [24]. Also, studies have shown that moderate concentration of H2O2 promotes endogenous NO synthesis. Lin et al. reported that H2O2 stimulated NO production through enhancing NR activity in the leaves of noe1 plants under high light [70]. Shi et al. argued that Gα-activated H2O2 production induced NO synthesis [71]. This shows the positive effect of H2O2 on NO production, which was previously derived from putrescine, increasing NO production. Moreover, NO can mediate antioxidant enzyme activities to affect H2O2 levels [72]. Therefore, H2O2 -NO interaction can cause several physiological and biological responses in plant cells [40]. Mazid et al. showed that nitric oxide had a regulatory role in the production of H2O2 under salt stress [73, 74]. NO levels increased eightfold in wild-type Arabidopsis roots after H2O2 treatment. H2O2 induced NO production through NOS-like activity, in which H2O2 induced an NOS inhibitor that could reduce NO synthesis in Vicia faba guard cells [75]. The abovementioned findings suggest that NO has a positive impact on hypobaric responses via crosstalk with H2O2. Result of this study suggests that putrescine-mediated NO signaling, together with tight regulation of H₂O₂ levels, contributes to the maintenance of redox homeostasis. While excessive H₂O₂ accumulation under hypobaric stress alone is associated with growth inhibition, its controlled reduction under putrescine treatment may allow basal redox signaling to proceed without triggering oxidative damage. While this study provides valuable insights into the protective role of putrescine against hypobaric stress in Matricaria chamomilla, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, which may not fully represent the complex environmental interactions occurring in actual space cultivation systems or terrestrial low-pressure environments. Second, the molecular mechanisms underlying putrescine-mediated stress tolerance, particularly the signaling pathways involving NO and ROS, require further investigation at the post-translational and epigenetic levels. Third, although we focused on selected flavonoid biosynthetic genes (CHS and FNSII), a more comprehensive transcriptomic or proteomic analysis would provide a broader understanding of the regulatory networks involved. Future studies should also examine the long-term effects of putrescine application on plant development and secondary metabolite accumulation across multiple generations. Additionally, validating these findings in other economically important crops would help establish putrescine as a practical strategy for improving plant resilience in bioregenerative life-support systems.

Conclusion

Low atmospheric pressure (200 mbar) significantly impaired growth, protein content, antioxidant capacity, and secondary metabolism in chamomile seedlings. Exogenous putrescine mitigated these effects, enhancing biomass, protein levels, antioxidant enzyme activities, and flavonoid accumulation. These improvements were associated with coordinated changes in NO and H₂O₂ levels and the upregulation of key biosynthetic genes like CHS and FNSII and redirect metabolism toward secondary metabolite production. Overall, putrescine application appears to improve plant performance to hypobaric stress and represents a promising strategy for improving crop performance in space-based life-support systems.
