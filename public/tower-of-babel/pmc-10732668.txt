Creating a network of people affected by neglected tropical diseases in Liberia: the same secret we were keeping, they are keeping it, too
Authors: Emmanuel Guyan Zaizay, Francis Joefay, Decontee Dennis
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article is written by three people with lived experience of neglected tropical diseases in Liberia. We describe our journey to becoming peer advocates, why mental health is important to us, and our recommendations for challenging stigma and improving the mental well-being of people affected.

Our journey to becoming peer advocates

We are a group of people with lived experience of neglected tropical diseases (NTDs). Francis had hydrocele until a recent surgery and Emmanuel and Decontee have both been treated for Buruli ulcer. Within Emmanuel's family, his mother was impacted by epilepsy and so stigma has been a big part of his life. We all live in rural Liberia, and as a result, we each had a long journey to reach our diagnosis and access treatment for our health conditions. Many of us tried to get help from the ‘country doctor’ (traditional healer) or faith leaders and healers before going to the health centre. We did this because we believed that our illnesses were caused in part by ‘African signs’. ‘African signs’ are used to describe conditions that are thought to be supernatural in cause as a result of witchcraft or curses. When we went to the health centre we were often scared about what they would tell us and were fearful of starting treatment. We often faced delays in waiting for test results and receiving medicines. Our treatment journeys were long and challenging, and often without community or family support, but that was just the beginning.

We were recently connected in our role as co-researchers through the REDRESS programme, and have since established peer support groups in our home counties (Grand Gedeh, Lofa and Margibi). REDRESS is a research programme that is collaborating with the Ministry of Health in Liberia to try and address the burden of NTDs in our country. REDRESS focuses specifically on making our health system better able to support people affected by leprosy, lymphatic filariasis (lymphoedema and hydrocele), Buruli ulcer, yaws and onchocerciasis. In our role as co-researchers, we have supported the development and design of the REDRESS research programme and the different interventions that are being piloted to improve the situation for people like us. We have been part of peer advocate training that has supported us to move forward in our journey to feel better able to advocate and raise awareness for people affected. This training was designed based on the experiences of others with lived experience of mental health conditions, and is based on advice from Cultivation for Users’ Hope in Liberia. We want to share our experiences with others and share more details about what we went through with our families, to shape change for others.

Why NTDs and mental health are important to us

Promoting mental well-being among people affected by NTDs is particularly important to us. In Liberia, we call the illnesses we lived through ‘embarrassments’ that often made us ‘feel bad’, which is how we described our feelings of sadness and depression. When we first started experiencing the signs and symptoms of our illnesses, we often did tell anyone, because of fears concerning stigma or judgement. This is what led to us feeling distance between us and our communities and families. These experiences often left us, and many other people in Liberia who are affected by NTDs, feeling worried and tearful. We also think it is important to explain that treatment for NTDs can leave you feeling worried and fearful. This was particularly true for Francis, because he needed surgery to treat his hydrocele. These fears can challenge mental well-being, and ensuring that there is support in place for people who need this treatment is really important to us.

What is essential to support the mental well-being of people affected by NTDs

We think there are different ways that the mental well-being of people affected by NTDs can be prioritised and improved.

We believe that people with lived experience of NTDs could have a more formal role within the health system, enabling them to be a lived experience focal point in districts, counties and at national level. Perhaps they could even attend international meetings and visit organisations to tell their stories and encourage other people in other countries to break those beliefs that can cause stigma.

People affected could also help to distribute items such as soap, clothes, towels and dressing materials (e.g. gauze), as well as food, to other people affected. This can also help to build trust between ourselves and other people affected who might have mental health conditions, so that they will feel confident to share how they are feeling with us and we can provide psychosocial support talking together using the peer advocate training we have had, and referring them to the clinic if needed. Within peer support groups people can share their stories; we also share jokes and this encourages us to stay together. Now in our peer support group we want to start making soap to help provide funds to sustain our group, and this is providing empowerment for our group. In the future, we would like to learn new skills like tailoring, catering and hairdressing, which could help us to regain financial independence.

Empowerment through training for people affected to go out and talk to their friends about NTDs will help to break those beliefs that can cause stigma and discrimination, such as the belief that NTDs are a curse (i.e. witchcraft). This can also help to motivate people to seek treatment. Many people with NTDs continue to attend traditional and faith healers, even while they attend a health centre. We can visit people in these areas to encourage them to continue to take their treatment from the hospital seriously and finish the course, or to encourage people affected to attend the health centre if they are not seeing an improvement.

Many people have these sicknesses; the same secret we were keeping, they are keeping it, too. So, if an affected person can go into the community and talk to them, maybe some people will come out with the sickness. They will have the belief that one day they may also be able to stand among ‘big big’ people and advocate like us; they will have hope to help them reduce their sadness. We also believe that this would help in national and international meetings to encourage people to see people like us and to hear our views and ideas on how to make things better.
