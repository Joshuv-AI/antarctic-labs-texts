Charged Rotating Hairy Black Holes in AdS 5 × S 5 \text{AdS}_{5}\times S^{5} : Unveiling their Secrets
Óscar J.C. Dias, Prahar Mitra, and Jorge E. Santos

Abstract
Using a mix of analytical and numerical methods, we construct new rotating, charged “hairy” black hole solutions of D = 5 D=5 , 𝒩 = 8 {\mathcal{N}}=8 gauged supergravity that are dual, via the AdS/CFT correspondence, to thermal states in D = 4 D=4 , 𝒩 = 4 {\mathcal{N}}=4 SYM at finite chemical and angular potential, thereby complementing and extending the results of [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] . These solutions uplift to asymptotically AdS 5 × S 5 \text{AdS}_{5}\times S^{5} solutions of Type IIB supergravity with equal angular momenta along AdS 5 \text{AdS}_{5} ( J = J 1 = J 2 J=J_{1}=J_{2} ) and S 5 S^{5} ( Q = Q 1 = Q 2 = Q 3 Q=Q_{1}=Q_{2}=Q_{3} ). As we lower the mass E E at fixed Q Q and J J , the known Cvetič-Lü-Pope (CLP) black holes are unstable to scalar condensation and the hairy black holes constructed here emerge as novel solutions associated to the instability. In the region of phase space where the CLP and hairy black holes coexist, the hairy black holes dominate the microcanonical ensemble and, therefore, describe a new thermodynamic phase of SYM. The hairy black holes extend beyond the CLP extremality surface all the way to the BPS surface, defined by E = 3 ​ Q + 2 ​ J / L E=3Q+2J/L . Through a combination of analytical and numerical techniques, we argue that the BPS limit of the hairy black holes is a singular, horizonless solution, and not a new two-parameter family of BPS black holes that extend the known one-parameter Gutowski-Reall (GR) black hole solution, in contradiction with the conjectures of [ 1 , 2 ] . To further support our conclusions, we perform a near-horizon analysis of the BPS equations and argue that they do not admit any regular solutions with an horizon.

Introduction

The AdS/CFT correspondence conjectures an equivalence between quantum gravitational theories in asymptotically anti-de Sitter (AdS) spacetimes and conformal field theories (CFTs) living on the asymptotic boundary of AdS

A complete understanding of the strongly coupled phases of holographic CFTs can be obtained by thoroughly investigating gravitational solutions in AdS. In this context, a particularly important class of gravitational solutions are black holes because their existence implies an exponentially large number of states in the CFT.
One of the many successes of AdS/CFT has been the microscopic counting of the states that contribute to the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy of asymptotically flat D1-D5-P black holes of Type IIB supergravity from first principles

\end{split}

\begin{split}E_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}&=\frac{N^{2}}{4L}[m({\alpha}^{2}+3)-6e],\qquad Q_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}=\frac{N^{2}e}{2L},\qquad J_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}=\frac{N^{2}}{2}{\alpha}(m-e),\end{split}

One way to understand why the BPS limit of the non-extremal CLP black hole reduces the number of parameters by two instead of one is as follows. Using (

\begin{split}E_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}-E_{\hbox{\tiny BPS}}&=\frac{N^{2}(3-{\alpha})(1-{\alpha})}{4L[y_{+}^{2}-{\alpha}^{2}(1+y_{+}^{2})]}\\

and

. This introduces the frequency

of the mode, the azimuthal quantum number

3.3
Hairy black hole: a non-interacting thermodynamic model

Before embarking on the full construction of the hairy black hole solution in the following sections, we consider here a thermodynamic model for the hairy black hole, where we treat it as a

In this model, we take the rotating hairy black hole as a non-interacting mixture of the CLP black hole and the supersymmetric soliton. The absence of any interaction between the two phases means that the mass, charge, and angular momentum of the hairy black is simply the sum of the charges of its two constituents:

\begin{split}E_{mix}&=E_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}+3Q_{s},\qquad Q_{mix}=Q_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}+Q_{s},\qquad J_{mix}=J_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}+J_{s}=J_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}.\end{split}

The subscripts CLP,

), CLP black hole, and the non-interacting mix, respectively. The entropy of the non-interacting mix is

\begin{split}S_{mix}=S_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}\big(E_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}},Q_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}},J_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}\big)=S_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}\big(E_{mix}-3Q_{s},Q_{mix}-Q_{s},J\big).\end{split}

(3.16)

In the microcanonical ensemble where we fix
E
,
Q
, and
J
, the dominant phase is the one that maximises the entropy. It follows that the hairy black hole is dominant whenever the partition of charges among the two constituents is such that they maximise
S
with respect to
Q_{s}
:

\begin{split}\frac{\partial S_{mix}}{\partial Q_{s}}=0\end{split}

(3.17)

\mu_{mix}\equiv\mu_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}=\mu_{s}=1\quad\implies\quad e_{mix}=\frac{y_{+}^{4}}{y_{+}^{2}-{\alpha}^{2}(2+y_{+}^{2})},

(3.18)

where to obtain the second relation, we have used the CLP relations (

\partial_{t}+{\Omega}_{s}\partial_{\psi}
of the soliton follows the same orbits as the Killing horizon generator
\partial_{t}+{\Omega}_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}\partial_{\psi}
as the black hole. In other words, the angular velocity and temperature of the non-interacting mix is

{\Omega}_{mix}\equiv{\Omega}_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}={\Omega}_{s},\qquad T_{mix}\equiv T_{\hbox{\tiny CLP}}=T_{s},

(3.19)

Altogether, the mass, charge, and angular momentum distribution among the two components that maximise the entropy is the one that also yields thermodynamic equilibrium between the two constituents of the hairy black hole. Substituting this into (

\begin{split}0\leq{\alpha}\leq\frac{y_{+}^{2}}{2+y_{+}^{2}}.\end{split}

(3.20)

Motivated by this bound, we set

\begin{split}{\alpha}=\sqrt{1-{\gamma}}\frac{y_{+}^{2}}{2+y_{+}^{2}}\qquad\implies\qquad e_{mix}=\frac{y_{+}^{2}(2+y_{+}^{2})}{2+{\gamma}y_{+}^{2}},\end{split}

(3.21)

where
0\leq{\gamma}\leq 1
guarantees that we are in the range (
3.20

\begin{split}\frac{E_{{mix}}L}{N^{2}}&=\frac{N^{2}}{L}\frac{24y_{+}^{2}+36y_{+}^{4}+2({\gamma}+8)y_{+}^{6}+{\gamma}(4-{\gamma})y_{+}^{8}}{4(2+{\gamma}y_{+}^{2})^{2}}+3\frac{Q_{s}L}{N^{2}},\\
\frac{Q_{{mix}}L}{N^{2}}&=\frac{N^{2}}{L}\frac{y_{+}^{2}(2+y_{+}^{2})}{2(2+{\gamma}y_{+}^{2})}+\frac{Q_{s}L}{N^{2}},\qquad\frac{J_{{mix}}}{N^{2}}=\sqrt{1-{\gamma}}\frac{y_{+}^{4}(6+(4+{\gamma})y_{+}^{2}+{\gamma}y_{+}^{4})}{2(2+{\gamma}y_{+}^{2})^{2}},\\
\mu_{{mix}}&=1,\qquad{\Omega}_{{mix}}L=\sqrt{1-{\gamma}},\qquad T_{{mix}}L=\frac{{\gamma}y_{+}[4+(3+{\gamma})y_{+}^{2}+{\gamma}y_{+}^{4}]^{1/2}}{\pi(2+{\gamma}y_{+}^{2})},\\
\frac{S_{{mix}}}{N^{2}}&=\pi y_{+}^{3}\frac{[{\gamma}y_{+}^{4}+(3+{\gamma})y_{+}^{2}+4]^{1/2}}{2+{\gamma}y_{+}^{2}}.\end{split}

(3.22)

(

and

The hairy black hole exists as a non-interacting mix whenever

\begin{split}y_{+}\geq 0,\qquad Q_{s}\geq 0,\qquad 0\leq{\gamma}\leq 1.\end{split}

(3.23)

4
Hairy black holes: perturbative solution

The outcome of Section
3.2

,

Of course, these arguments rely partially on the crude thermodynamic model of Section

Supersymmetric hairy black hole?

In the previous sections, we used analytical and numerical techniques to construct non-supersymmetric hairy black holes parameterised by three independent parameters:

6.2
Revisiting the BPS limit of perturbation theory

In the previous subsection we have gathered exact numerical data that strongly suggests that the BPS limit of hairy black holes is a
singular
supersymmetric limit with
E\to E_{\hbox{\tiny BPS}}
,
\mu\to 1
,
{\Omega}_{H}L\to 1
,
TL\to 0
, and
S\to 0
. This is in sharp disagreement – as best illustrated in Figs.
15
–
16
– with the expectations of the perturbative and non-interacting thermodynamic analyses (of Section
4
and of
[
1
]
/Section
3.3
, respectively) which predict that the BPS limit of HBHs should have finite entropy. Thus, we must revisit our pertubative analyses to identify why it is failing as we approach the BPS limit.

To do this, we define a new parameter
{\delta}
by the equation

\begin{split}E-E_{\hbox{\tiny BPS}}&=\frac{3}{4}\left(2-{\gamma}-2\sqrt{1-{\gamma}}\right)y_{+}^{4}+{\mathcal{O}}(y_{+}^{6},{\epsilon}^{2}y_{+}^{4}),\qquad 0\leq{\gamma}\leq 1,\\
&\equiv\frac{3}{4}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\delta}\,\right)^{2}y_{+}^{4},\qquad 0<{\delta}\leq 1,\end{split}

(6.1)

where the RHS of the first line follows from (
4.16
), namely
E_{\hbox{\tiny BPS}}=3Q+2J/L
, and we used the perturbative expressions (
4.13
) for
(E,Q,J)
.
The BPS limit then corresponds to take
{\delta}\to 0
. Using (
6.1
), we can solve for
{\gamma}
in terms of
{\delta}
. This yields, to leading order,

{\delta}={\gamma}+{\mathcal{O}}(y_{+}^{2},{\gamma}y_{+}^{2},{\gamma}{\epsilon}^{2})
.
With this, we can rewrite all the perturbative thermodynamic quantities in terms of
{\delta}
and, in principle, analyse the BPS limit
{\delta}\to 0
of the hairy black hole.

But there is a first-principles problem with this analysis. Clearly, the perturbative analysis of Section
4
relies on a double expansion in
y_{+}\ll 1
and
{\epsilon}\ll 1
whereby the
{\gamma}
parameter – introduced in (
4.3
) – is implicitly assumed to be
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
. However, we are interested on studying the
\delta\sim{\gamma}\sim 0
limit of the system. This is at odds with the fact that, to get (
4.13
), we took Taylor expansions in
y_{+}\ll 1
and
{\epsilon}\ll 1
assuming that these two expansion parameters were not of the same order as
{\gamma}
(and thus
{\delta}
). That is, we implicitly assumed that
{\delta}\gg y_{+}
and
{\delta}\gg{\epsilon}
. It follows that analysing the
\delta\sim{\gamma}\to 0
limit of (
4.13
) is a delicate issue and we must proceed with caution. Note that this also applies to the analysis associated to (
4.17
)–(
4.19
) which must be revisited and scrutinized.

To proceed, we express the HBH thermodynamics (
4.13
) in terms of
\delta
. For the discussion that will follow, it is enough to focus our attention in one of the thermodynamic quantities, temperature say, and the exact coefficients of the expansion are not relevant. It suffices to note that for small
{\delta}
the temperature of the black hole behaves as

\begin{split}&TL=\bigg({\mathcal{O}}({\delta})y_{+}+{\mathcal{O}}({\delta})y_{+}^{3}+{\mathcal{O}}({\delta}\ln{\delta})y_{+}^{5}+{\mathcal{O}}\left({\delta}(\ln{\delta})^{2}\right)y_{+}^{7}+{\mathcal{O}}(y_{+}^{9})\bigg)\\
&+{\epsilon}^{2}\bigg({\mathcal{O}}({\delta})y_{+}+[-1/\pi+{\mathcal{O}}({\delta}\ln{\delta})]y_{+}^{3}+{\mathcal{O}}(y_{+}^{5})\bigg)+{\epsilon}^{4}\bigg({\mathcal{O}}({\delta})y_{+}+{\mathcal{O}}(y_{+}^{3})\bigg)+{\mathcal{O}}({\epsilon}^{6}).\end{split}

(6.2)

In this expansion, a given sub-leading term should be (considerably) smaller that its precedent. From the successive terms in the
{\epsilon}^{0}
(and in the
{\epsilon}^{2}
) contribution in (
6.2
), one sees that this requires that
|\!\ln{\delta}|y_{+}^{2}\ll 1
, i.e.,

|\!\ln{\delta}|\ll y_{+}^{-2}\,.

(6.3)

The fact that one has

{\underset{{\delta}\rightarrow 0}{\lim}\,\ln{\delta}\to\infty}

means that, if we are at
fixed

y_{+}\ll 1
, our perturbation theory breaks downs before
{\delta}
can vanish to yield the desired BPS limit. This explains why our exact numerical data is increasingly less well approximated by the perturbative expressions (
4.13
) as the BPS limit is approached: see Figs.
15
–
16
. It also explains why we
cannot
trust one of the (supposedly) main predictions of perturbative analysis of Section
4
, namely that the entropy is finite in the BPS limit. That is, it reveals why the naive perturbative prediction is not matching the numerical findings of Section
6.1
where we clearly see that
S\to 0
in the BPS limit: see again Figs.
15
–
16
.

But this is not the whole story because so far we have not yet fully analysed the contributions that are proportional to the scalar condensate
{\epsilon}
(which hairy black holes certainly contain, including in their BPS limit). This time, it is important to focus our attention in the first term
{\mathcal{O}}({\delta})y_{+}
of the first line of (
6.2
) and compare it the second term
-{\epsilon}^{2}y_{+}^{3}
of the second line (again, similar contributions are present in other thermodynamic quantities (
4.13
)). We see that, because the latter has a negative contribution to
T
, to have a non-negative temperature one must necessarily have

\delta\gtrsim{\mathcal{O}}({\epsilon}^{2}y_{+}^{2})\,.

(6.4)

Note that since

|\!\ln({\epsilon}^{2}y_{+}^{2})|\ll y_{+}^{-2}
, this requirement is within the regime of validity (
6.3
) of the perturbative expansion.
So, (
6.4
) indicates that, if we assume that
y_{+}
is
fixed
during the process, hairy black holes should reach the zero temperature configuration as one approaches
\delta\sim{\mathcal{O}}({\epsilon}^{2}y_{+}^{2})
. That is to say, this analysis suggests that hairy black holes would never reach
E=E_{\hbox{\tiny BPS}}
(which corresponds to
\delta=0
) because
\delta
is capped from below at
\delta\sim{\mathcal{O}}({\epsilon}^{2}y_{+}^{2})
where the HBH system reaches
T=0
. In other words, there would be a small gap between the
T=0
boundary of HBH and the BPS curve in Fig.
1
.

In the arguments of the previous two paragraphs,
y_{+}
is assumed to be fixed while we try to approach
\delta=0
. On the other hand, if we do not force the system to be at fixed
y_{+}
as we approach the BPS limit, there is a sense in which we can rescue the perturbative and non-interacting thermodynamic analyses and reinterpret them to get expectations that do match the fact that the exact numerical results, in the BPS limit
\delta\to 0
, strongly suggests that one reaches a singular supersymmetric HBH with
E\to E_{\hbox{\tiny BPS}}
,
\mu\to 1
,
{\Omega}_{H}L\to 1
,
TL\to 0
, and
S\to 0
.
Indeed, we see that the condition (
6.3
) for our perturbation theory to be valid can be satisfied when
\delta\to 0
if (and only if) we
simultaneously
send
y_{+}\to 0
. In this ‘double’ BPS limit one finds, from the perturbative expressions (
4.13
) and from the BPS limit (
4.18
)-(
4.19
) of the non-interacting thermodynamic model, that one has
E\to E_{\hbox{\tiny BPS}}
,
\mu\to 1
,
{\Omega}_{H}L\to 1
,
TL\to 0
, and
S\to 0
. That is, the BPS limit of the hairy black holes should be a
singular
supersymmetric hairy black hole (this time, in agreement with the numerical findings of Section
6.1
). However, this reinterpretation is a hand-waved one since Figs.
15
–
16
unequivocally demonstrate that perturbation theory simply breaks down close to the BPS limit, in agreement with (
6.3
).

as given in (
7.4
):

\begin{split}h&=\eta-\frac{4{\omega}^{2}}{H^{3}}\\
&=\frac{3y_{+}^{2}}{4}\left[\frac{\rho^{2}}{x^{2}}-3\left(x^{2}\int{\text{d}}x\frac{(x^{-2}\rho)^{\prime}}{x}\right)^{2}\right]+o\left(x^{2}\int{\text{d}}x\frac{(x^{-2}\rho)^{\prime}}{x}\right)+o(\rho^{2}/x^{2})\end{split}

(7.22)

The second condition in (
7.6
) requires that
h
be finite as
x\to 0
. However, both terms inside the square brackets are separately divergent in this limit. Consistency with (
7.6
) then requires that the two divergences must cancel each other. This requires that

\begin{split}\frac{\rho^{2}}{x^{2}}-3\left(x^{2}\int{\text{d}}x\frac{(x^{-2}\rho)^{\prime}}{x}\right)^{2}=0\,.\end{split}

(7.23)

This implies a differential equation for
\rho
, which we can solve

\begin{split}(x^{-3}\rho)^{\prime}=\pm\sqrt{3}\frac{(x^{-2}\rho)^{\prime}}{x}\quad\implies\quad\rho=K\,x^{\frac{1}{2}(3\mp\sqrt{3})}.\end{split}

(7.24)

The requirement that
\rho=o(1)
and
\rho^{\prime}\to\infty
implies that we take the upper sign above. It follows that this case could only work if
\rho
diverges as a power law. In this case, we then find

\begin{split}U&=-\frac{K\,y_{+}^{2}}{2}x^{{\alpha}-1}+o(x^{{\alpha}-1}),\\
\eta&=\frac{3K^{2}y_{+}^{2}}{4}x^{2({\alpha}-1)}+o(x^{2({\alpha}-1)}),\\
{\omega}&=\frac{3Ky_{+}^{4}}{4}\frac{{\alpha}-2}{{\alpha}-3}x^{{\alpha}-1}+o(x^{{\alpha}-1}),\\
e^{\varphi}&=K\,{\alpha}x^{{\alpha}-1}+o(x^{{\alpha}-1}).\end{split}

(7.25)

where
{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}(3-\sqrt{3})\approx 0.633975
. However, if we plug in this form into (
7.5d
) and expand near
x\to 0
, we find

\begin{split}(e^{-\varphi})^{\prime}=U/(x\eta)\quad\implies\quad\frac{1-{\alpha}}{K\,{\alpha}}x^{-{\alpha}}=-\frac{2}{3K}x^{-{\alpha}}+o(x^{-{\alpha}}).\end{split}

(7.26)

It is immediately clear that this equation does not hold for
{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}(3-\sqrt{3})
. We have, therefore, reached a contradiction, and we reject this possibility.

To conclude, in this section we looked for a supersymmetric hairy black hole by solving directly the BPS equations (more precisely by studying the near-horizon behaviour of the solutions). We find evidence that supersymmetric black holes can only exist if the scalar field at the horizon is not finite. Consequently, the pullback of curvature invariants to the horizon also diverge. That is to say, the analysis of the BPS equations is consistent with the main exact numerical results of Section
6.1
: when hairy black holes approach the BPS limit, they become
singular
because the scalar field and curvature invariants diverge at the horizon.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
g:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be monotonic functions in an open neighbourhood of
x=0
. Then,

\begin{split}f^{\prime}(x)=o(g^{\prime}(x))\quad\implies\quad f(x)=\begin{cases}C+o(g(x)-g(0))&\text{if $g$ is bounded.}\\
o(g(x))&\text{if $g$ is unbounded.}\end{cases}\end{split}

(B.1)

Proof

Without loss of generality, we assume that
g
is an increasing function for all
x\in(0,{\varepsilon})
for some
{\varepsilon}>0
(if it is a decreasing function, we can replace
g(x)\to-g(x)
everywhere). We then have
g^{\prime}(x)>0
for all
x\in(0,{\varepsilon})
.
f^{\prime}(x)=o(g^{\prime}(x))
means that for all
c>0
, there exists a
{\delta}_{c}\in(0,{\varepsilon})
such that

\begin{split}|f^{\prime}(x)|<cg^{\prime}(x)\quad\forall\quad x\in(0,{\delta}_{c}).\end{split}

(B.2)

The main result we will use is that for all
0\leq x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq{\delta}_{c}
, we have

\begin{split}|f(x_{2})-f(x_{1})|&=\left|\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}{\text{d}}tf^{\prime}(t)\right|\leq\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}{\text{d}}t|f^{\prime}(t)|=c\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}{\text{d}}tg^{\prime}(t)<c[g(x_{2})-g(x_{1})].\end{split}

(B.3)

We consider three separate cases.

1.

g,g^{\prime}={\mathcal{O}}(1)
as
x\to 0
: In this case,
g^{\prime}
is bounded, so
f^{\prime}
is also bounded due to (
B.2
). Thus,
\lim_{x\to 0}f
exists, and we define this as
f(0)
. Then setting
x_{1}=0
and
x_{2}=x
in (
B.3
), we find

\begin{split}|f(x)-f(0)|<c|g(x)-g(0)|\quad\implies\quad f(x)=f(0)+o(g(x)-g(0)).\end{split}

(B.4)

2.

g={\mathcal{O}}(1)
and
g^{\prime}\to\infty
as
x\to 0
: In this case, since
g
is bounded, (
B.3
) implies that
f
is also bounded. Since it is monotonic, it has a well-defined limit of
0
and the proof of Case I trivially extends here. Note that this is the only case where the requirement that
f
is monotonic is necessary.

3.

g\to-\infty
and
g^{\prime}\to\infty
as
x\to 0
: In this case, we take
x_{2}={\delta}
and
x_{1}=x
in (
B.3
) and use one extra step

\begin{split}|f(x)|\leq|f(x)-f({\delta})|+|f({\delta})|<cg({\delta})-cg(x)+|f({\delta})|.\end{split}

(B.5)

Taking
x
to be sufficiently small so that
g(x)<0
and
cg({\delta})+|f({\delta})|<c|g(x)|
, we find

\begin{split}|f(x)|<2c|g(x)|\quad\implies\quad f(x)=o(g(x)).\end{split}

(B.6)

This completes the proof.
