Disability weights measurement for 148 childhood health statuses in Hunan, China: a study based on face-to-face surveys
Authors: Gang Tian, Simin He, Yiran Cui, Feixiang Zhou, Yulan Ma, Wenyang Yang, Jingliang Shuai, Qi Wang, Zhihao Deng, Yan Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDisability weight (DW) quantifies the impact of disease impairments and symptoms on health statuses, and is a key parameter for estimating disease burden. In this study, we conducted an exploratory measurement of disability weights for common health states among children and adolescents in Hunan Province using a face-to-face survey methodology. This provides preliminary data to support the study of disease burden among this age group.MethodsA household survey with face-to-face interviews was conducted to measure DWs for 148 children’s health statuses. The questionnaire included 16 paired comparison (PC) questions and 3 population health equivalent (PHE) questions. Probit regression analysis was used to analyze PC responses, with probit regression results from the PC on the DW scale anchored using interval regression results from PHE responses.ResultsThe DWs resulting from the study varied from 0.065 (95% uncertainty interval (UI): 0.047–0.087) for acute tonsillitis to 0.730 (UI: 0.709–0.750) for extremely severe intellectual developmental disorders. A comprehensive analysis shows that severe traumatic injuries, such as spinal cord injuries and amputations, as well as congenital defects like hearing loss, visual impairments, and intellectual disabilities, lead to higher DWs due to the long-lasting effects of permanent functional impairments. In contrast, minor bone fractures and transient infectious diseases result in lower DWs. The study demonstrates a high level of consistency in the comparative evaluations of various health outcomes across different sociodemographic characteristics.ConclusionsThe study strengthens the empirical basis for assessing DWs in children. The PC-PHE method for assessing the DWs of children and adolescents with various health conditions within the Hunan Province population demonstrated robust logic and severity discrimination, with stable results across different population characteristics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12963-025-00417-6.

Introduction

According to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child, a child is defined as anyone under the age of 18 [1]. The 2022 revised edition of the ‘World Population Prospects reports that the global child population was approximately 2.4 billion in 2020. China’s child population was around 298 million, which represents 12.42% of the global child population [2]. Global child and adolescent mortality has decreased over the past 60 years due to economic development and improved medical care. The United Nations Inter-Agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation has reported that the number of deaths of children under the age of five years will fall to 4.9 million globally in 2022, representing a record low [3].

The mortality rate for children is decreasing, but the burden of disease is increasing [4]. According to the 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) report, the absolute burden of disability in children increased by 5.3% from 1990 to 2021 [5]. As communicable diseases are being effectively contained worldwide, the burden of childhood disease is shifting towards injuries and non-communicable diseases. These can cause transient and permanent non-fatal injuries, leading to millions of children suffering varying degrees of disabling injuries each year [6–8]. From a health statistics perspective, traditional indicators such as mortality, morbidity and prevalence are inadequate to measure childhood disease and injury in real life [9–11]. A more appropriate indicator for evaluating the burden of childhood diseases on children themselves, their families, and society is the loss of healthy life years, i.e. the conversion of illness, mental depression, and social burdens caused by disease into healthy life years lost, also known as disability-adjusted life years (DALY) [12–14]. When a child dies, the DALY is comprised of two components: Years of Life Lost due to premature mortality (YLLs) and Years Lived with Disability (YLDs). YLLs are calculated by multiplying the difference between a standard life expectancy at the age of death and the estimated number of deaths at that age. YLDs measure the number of years of life lost due to disability, calculated by multiplying the prevalence rate by the disability weight (DW) [9]. DW reflect the severity of different conditions and ranging from 0 (full health) to 1 (equivalent to death) [10].

DW is a crucial parameter in calculating the burden of disease in children. However, there is still a lack of large-scale childhood DWs [15]. Some studies have used adult DWs to calculate the burden of childhood oncological diseases, which is inappropriate [16]. The adult cancer sequelae performance used in this study to estimate the DW of childhood cancers is inappropriate; some sequelae have too light a DW empowerment relative to children, such as fistula with a DW of 0.095, which is clearly more appropriate for middle-aged and older adults. Another example is impotence, a sequela that is more appropriate for adults and whose impact in children is difficult to assess objectively [16]. In order to conduct preliminary exploration of DW measurement for 148 children’s health conditions, according to the method used in the GBD 2010 study, respondents were asked to answer PC and PHE questions from the perspective of children [17].

Methods

Study design

This study utilized PC and PHE methods, which are comparable to those used in GBD 2010 and European surveys measuring adult DWs [17, 18]. However, we differed in our survey approach and survey instruments. We used a paper version of the questionnaire, tailored to the situation, as the survey instrument instead of an electronic questionnaire. Furthermore, we conducted a face-to-face survey instead of an online survey from March 2021 to October 2022.

Research setting and participants

The primary target of the sample for this study was people who had some knowledge or awareness of a particular health state in children. Participants were preferably close contacts of children or children themselves. Due to the face-to-face nature of the survey, the large amount of human, material, and financial resources required, and the limited time available due to the epidemic control during the survey period, the respondents of this study were selected from the parents of children in the birth cohort already established by the research group; the parents of children attending and being hospitalised in Hunan Provincial Children’s Hospital, Xiangya Hospital, Xiangya No. 2 Hospital, Xiangya No. 3 Hospital; and the paediatricians of the community and the general hospitals; The general population living in other urban areas of Hunan Province and the administrative districts of Changsha, as well as university students.

The inclusion criteria for the respondents were: (1) be 18 years of age or older; (2) possess normal intelligence, a certain level of literacy, and the ability to comprehend the questionnaire; and (3) have an understanding of the content and purpose of the study, agree to participate, and voluntarily sign an informed consent form. Exclusion criteria include: (1) participants who did not meet the inclusion criteria; (2) incomplete questionnaire responses; and (3) participants who refused to cooperate or did not sign the informed consent form.

The sample size was estimated in consultation with experts in the field of statistics and computer simulations revealed that pairwise comparisons, with more than five comparisons using Probit regression analysis, were able to identify differences, and we also referred to the published literature [19], where 206 illnesses and injuries were included in face-to-face surveys of 5,750 people. The number of disease and injury categories investigated in our study was 148. The number of pairs for two-by-two comparison is 148*147/2 = 10,878, each questionnaire in this study incorporates 16 PC method pairs, a total of 10,878/16 = 680 different questionnaires are needed for one round of survey, each questionnaire needs to be completed by one person independently, 8 rounds of survey are planned in this study, the sample size to be surveyed is 8*680 = 5440, the PC method of our study investigates a total of 5455 respondents in 8 rounds of survey.

Since it was difficult for us to access high schools or middle schools to survey children under the age of 18 in person. Therefore, we surveyed a random sample of students in colleges and universities to make it more representative of the health preferences of this age group. Our survey involved health state descriptions and the anchoring tool, the PHE method, was more difficult to understand, requiring respondents to have some knowledge and equivalent measures of a certain health state, so we surveyed almost exclusively children’s parents in hospitals and neighbourhoods and required parents to have a certain level of cognition, resulting in an overall high level of education in the included population.

Determination of health statuses

After reviewing relevant literature, we identified 148 childhood health statuses based on the disease spectrum of Chinese children’s outpatient and inpatient services, the Global Burden of Childhood Diseases list, the WHO Children’s Disease Statistical List, and major childhood health statuses gained through interviews with children’s parents, as well as the disease spectrum of pediatric outpatient and inpatient services at comprehensive tertiary hospitals and children’s specialty hospitals (See Supplementary documents 1). The text describes six categories of children’s health statuses: birth defects and congenital disability diseases (24), acute infectious disease (31), chronic diseases and injuries (34), accidental injuries (36), mental and behavioral disorders diseases (14), and malignant neoplasm diseases (9). Lay description of health statuses

The principles for describing children’s health statuses are to use concise, non-clinical vocabulary, to highlight the main functional consequences and symptoms associated with the health statuses, and to keep the description to 50 words or less. The same health statuses assessed in adult DWs were identified by referring to lay descriptions of adult health statuses and incorporating them for children [20–22]. In the first phase of lay disease descriptions, we measured the functional and symptomatic dimensions of the 148 childhood health statuses included, using the “International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health, (ICF)” (https://apps.who.int/iris/bitstream/handle/10665/42407/9241545429.pdf?sequence=1) assessment scale (See Supplement Table S2). This helps to characterise the specific health statuses of these manifestations. Where possible, descriptions were determined based on standard clinical professional classification systems to accurately reflect the severity of a particular condition. The research team extensively discussed and revised the functional health and symptom presentation of the 148 childhood health statuses before finalising a preliminary version. Pediatric experts and doctors from community health service centers were consulted to review and modify the preliminary textual descriptions. This was done to ensure that they accurately reflected the characteristics, common presentations, and duration of associated symptoms involving the sequelae of impaired functioning. Finally, we obtained versions of the textual descriptions of childhood health statuses suitable for use in general population surveys using the PC and PHE methods (See Supplement Table S1).

Health status valuation

A comprehensive evaluation of the 148 children’s health statuses was conducted using the PC and PHE methodologies. The PC technique is an ordinal measure that assesses relative differences in individual functioning and health by comparing pairs of children’s health statuses. It also captures the assessor’s preferred choices for these health statuses. The PHE technique is a group health benefit transformation method that requires the assessor to retrospectively assess two hypothetical health items. The first health item is to prevent 1,000 people from developing a disease that leads to rapid death, while the second health item is to prevent 1,500, 2,000, 3,000, 5,000, or 10,000 people (based on randomly selected bids for each question) from developing a disease that is not fatal (i.e., one of the 148 health statuses for children) but would experience the symptoms and durations mentioned in the descriptions. Evaluators are asked to choose which health program they believe produces greater overall population health benefits. The PHE method is utilised for the purpose of evaluating and comparing health statuses affecting entire populations. This is achieved by estimating the propensity for children to experience a loss of welfare due to different health statuses, and subsequently translating this into an equivalent value of welfare loss.

The 148 childhood diseases were first paired two-by-two, for a total of 148*147/2 = 10,878 pairs. We arranged 16 pairs and 3 PHE questions per questionnaire, each questionnaire needed pairs without put back randomly selected from 10,878 pairs and 3 PHE questions without put back randomly selected from 148 childhood diseases until all pairs were included in the questionnaire. Each round of the survey required the completion of 10,878/16 = 680 different questionnaires (See Supplementary documents 2).

Data collection procedures and instruments

In this study, a paper-based questionnaire served as the primary survey instrument. The questionnaire was developed by the research team in consultation with experts and was finalized based on the pre-survey results. The questionnaire mainly consisted of basic socio-demographic information of the respondents (e.g., age, gender, usual address, type of household registration, marital status, education level, annual household income level, type of occupation, presence of children in the household, presence of medical background, etc.), as well as 16 PC questions and 3 PHE questions.

Trained investigators conducted face-to-face interviews. Respondents were informed of the survey’s purpose and questionnaire content and asked to sign an informed consent form. The enumerator supervised respondents while they completed the questionnaire and answered any questions. The questionnaire was completed in full. Respondents did not receive payment for their participation in the survey. The investigator prompted respondents to imagine two children with the disease and to weigh who was healthier between the two. Two investigators worked in pairs to enter the completed questionnaires into a pre-developed computer program.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses for this study were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1), Stata MP software (version 17.0), and Microsoft Excel 2021. Probit regression models were used to estimate relative outcomes for childhood health statuses based on pooled PC data. The result reflects the relative differences in severity between childhood health statuses on a quantitative scale, and also shows participants’ choice preferences for each childhood health statuses [23]. The probit regression model was used to determine the selection of the first disease and injure as the healthier state in pairwise comparisons. A response variable of 1 was assigned to this selection, while a value of 0 was assigned to the alternative selection. The probit regression model incorporated indicator variables for each disease and injure, with a value of 1 assigned to the first state in a PC, −1 to the second state in a PC, and 0 to all states other than the pair being considered. Additionally, the interval regression model was used to analyze the pooled group health equivalence data. Finally, a linear regression model was used to anchor the estimates from the probit regression. Logit transformations based on the PHE responses were performed to map to a DW scale of 0 to 1. Finally, 1000 bootstrap iterations were used to calculate 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) [17, 18].

In addition, we conducted a trend analysis of the DWs of the childhood health statuses included in this study, to validate the logical soundness of the study and the reliability of the DW values. Trend analysis i.e. by plotting a trend line on the DW values of diseases with severity ratings to see if they logically fit the intuition. RSpearman’s correlation coefficient (rs) was used to test for correlations between the DW values obtained for different subgroups and to identify overall differences in DW values measured under different population characteristics. These factors included gender (male and female), age (≥ 35 and < 35 years), education level (bachelor’s degree/above and below), annual household income (high income ≥ 100,000 yuan and low income < 100,000 yuan), type of household registration (urban and rural), presence of a medical background, and physical labor status (manual and non-manual), whether there are children in the family (with child and without child), and whether there is a medical background (with medical background (MB) and without MB). Using the results of the ICF assessment of 148 children’s health statuses (See Supplement Table S3), we investigated the impact of various functional attributes and symptomatic manifestations on children’s DW values.

Results

The survey included 5455 respondents, of whom 64.1% were female. In terms of age distribution, 47.5% were aged 18–30, and 37.8% were aged 31–40. Among the respondents, 69.7% had a college or bachelor’s degree or above. Married individuals accounted for 62.2%. 49.8% of respondents had an annual household income of over 100,000 yuan, 35.7% were engaged in non-manual labor, and 29.1% were university students. Additionally, 25.3% of respondents had a medical background (See Table 1).

Table 2 presents the estimated DWs for 148 childhood health statuses along with their corresponding 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). The DW for intellectual disability (profound) was the highest at 0.7296 (95% UI: 0.7093–0.7504), followed by spinal cord injury (0.7266, 95% UI: 0.7065–0.7472), and central nervous system tumor (0.7021, 95% UI: 0.6843–0.7209). The DW for acute tonsillitis was the lowest at 0.0651 (95% UI: 0.0472–0.0873), followed by mild far-sightedness and acute diarrhea (mild) with DWs of 0.0693 (95% UI: 0.0507–0.0921) (See Table 2). The study results show a distinct hierarchy of DWs among categories. Children with malignant neoplastic diseases had the highest mean DW (0.6131), while those with infectious diseases had the lowest mean (0.1501). Additionally, varying degrees of impairment for the same disease corresponded to different gradients of DW values. For example, thalassemia minor had a lower DW (0.1592) than thalassemia major (0.4980). The DW for single-arm amputation was 0.5133, which is lower than that for double-arm amputation (0.6635). Additionally, the DW increased with the degree of hearing impairment (See Supplement Fig. S1–S2). The distribution of DWs for the 148 childhood health statuses shows that about 77% of the childhood health statuses (114) had a DW of less than 0.5, and about 35.1% had a DW of less than 0.2. Only 3.38% of the total number of childhood health statuses (5) had a DW of more than 0.7 (See Fig. 1).

We use the Euclidean distance method to calculate the distance between the DW values of health status and two demographic characteristics. Euclidean distance is a commonly used method for measuring the spatial distance between two data points. In this study, it effectively reflects the similarity between DW values measured under different demographic characteristics. Specifically, the smaller the Euclidean distance between two demographic characteristics, the higher the similarity in the measured health status DW values between these two demographic characteristics. From the plotted heatmap (See supplement Fig. S1), we can intuitively observe that most demographic features correspond to smaller Euclidean distances. This result indicates that the health status DW values evaluated under these demographic characteristics have a high degree of similarity. This may suggest that these subgroups of the population already have a high degree of homogeneity in their understanding of the health status of children and adolescents.

A total of 12 diseases with severity grades were included in the assessment framework for childhood diseases in this study. Trend plots of the DW results of the severity-graded childhood diseases showed that different severity levels of the same disease corresponded to different DW values, and that the more severe the disease, the greater the DW value, with a certain hierarchical gradient that was logically reasonable (See supplement Fig. S2).

Discussion

The primary function of DW is to assess the impact of disease on health statuses. It is a key parameter for disease burden assessment and can also be crucial in prioritizing the allocation of healthcare resources and informing decisions on healthcare reform. However, a comprehensive assessment of childhood health statuses is still lacking in China and globally. Based on public perception, this study measured the disability weight of children by comparing their choice preferences for 148 different health statuses. The DW values for the 148 children’s health conditions measured in this study ranged from 0.0651 (acute tonsillitis) to 0.7296 (very severe intellectual disability), which is consistent with the hierarchy of disease severity. As the respondents in this study were mainly parents of children with profound intellectual disability, children with profound intellectual disability are not only incapable of thinking but also incapable of mobility and self-care. Parents perceived this condition as having a great impact on their child’s life and development, as well as being a great burden on the parents, family and society. As a result, severe mental retardation in children is assessed at a higher value in the disability weighting assessment.

In this study, the objective was to measure disability weights for health statuses that are prevalent or impose a significant burden on children in mainland China. The framework list of health statuses was determined based on the spectrum of Chinese children’s diseases, as well as expert discussion and assessment. Consequently, the present framework does not align with previously published lists of health statuses in adults. In contrast to the descriptions of health statuses assessed for the DW in Chinese adults, we added the duration of the symptom when describing health statuses in children. This was done to distinguish between some functional endpoints that were very similar in description but differed in severity due to differences in duration. This treatment allows us to infer respondents’ choice preferences more objectively in comparisons, ensuring that significant coefficient stratification is obtained even when probit regressions are run on small samples of PC data. At the same time, this treatment also allows the interval regression results for the PHE data to discriminate between differences, thus allowing for more accurate anchoring. Although there are still some differences between the results obtained in the different sub-groups of the survey and the results of the pooled analyses of the data for all respondents, the lowest correlation is 0.881. This high degree of consistency contrasts with the results of studies based on non-randomized, self-selected, incomplete, and less used data from web-based surveys [17–20, 24]. The high level of confidence in our findings is partly due to the rigorous screening of respondents, the design of a scientifically rigorous research protocol, and the use of consistent interviewing and data processing procedures. On the other hand, we have provided detailed descriptions of the functional consequences of children’s health statuses and have removed disease labels from the descriptions that indicate the cause of the health condition, as disease labels may induce bias towards stigmatized conditions [25, 26]. Previous research has shown that the presence or absence of specific disease label information in disease and injure descriptions also causes variability in ratings [27].

Our study is the largest empirical study currently available to measure the disability weighting of children’s health using the PC and PHE survey methods of the GBD study. Because of children’s limited cognitive ability and understanding, this study is an assessment of childhood illnesses using adult perceptions. The DW measures were discussed and evaluated as factual by pediatricians and healthcare stakeholders, and the results were found to be relatively reliable. Our study confirms the feasibility of the PC method for assessing various health outcomes in almost any population [28]. In addition, our research found that the DW obtained using this evaluation method is less affected by different cultures, education, professions, ages, genders, incomes, and human environments. The ICC was above 0.9 in the consistency test of 148 children’s health conditions measured under different characteristics. These findings are consistent with previous studies [17, 19]. However, some studies show that the assessment of health statuses can be influenced by economic, cultural, and socio-demographic characteristics [29–32]. For example, in a study in the Netherlands, Haagsma et al. found that assessment of health statuses may be influenced by culture and level of education [33]. A study in Zimbabwe also found that occupational background had a similar effect on health ratings [34, 35]. For example, there was a significant difference in the ranking of the same set of health conditions between those with a medical background and the general public. However, these studies tended to have smaller numbers of participants and less comprehensive coverage of health conditions [36, 37].

Although we evaluated the disability weighting of childhood illnesses, the actual judgments were based on the perceptions and feelings of adults. This may have resulted in some differences in children’s perspectives [28, 38]. Our findings indicate that adults or parents of children place greater importance on illnesses that have long-term effects or disabilities, such as intellectual disability, amputation, hearing loss, vision loss, and autism. These illnesses are given a higher weight. Symptoms such as transient pain or fatigue, fractures, infections, myopia (pseudophakia), and mild hearing loss were assessed with lower weights. The PC method for DW assessment appears to be influenced by the assessor’s perspective and current perception. Regression model analyses of symptom and functional attribute impairment rating scores for children with the disease validated this observation. When an illness presents with multiple symptoms and functional impairments, pain and fatigue may slightly decrease the DW assessment value, while emotional state and functional impairment significantly increase it.

Although some of the available studies did not directly use adult DW to calculate DALYs for children, the assignment of DW for children is still largely based on adult criteria, particularly for oncological diseases. For instance, the DWs employed in the Global Childhood Cancer Burden of Disease Assessments published in 2019 and 2022 were derived from the performance of adult cancer sequelae. However, this approach has clear limitations: certain cancers manifest differently in children, and some DW assignments for sequelae may be inappropriate for children as they do not adequately account for the cumulative impact of sequelae in this age group [39, 40]. The DW assigned to fistula, for instance, is 0.095, a value that is clearly more appropriate for middle-aged and older adults than children. Fistula has an impact on children that is not limited to the physical level; it may also have long-term cumulative effects on their psychological and social development and quality of life. These effects are not adequately captured in the adult DW assignment. Similarly, a sequela such as impotence may have clear health effects in adults, but its impact on children is difficult to assess objectively and may not be applicable. For example, for liver and kidney tumors, we measured DW values of 0.6073 and 0.5487 in children, while DW values measured using adult tumor sequelae were both 0.51522. Therefore, it can be inferred that using the adult sequelae weighting table to calculate DW in children may underestimate it.

Although we prepared well for this study, there are still some shortcomings. Firstly, it only covers common health statuses in children and not a comprehensive range of children’s diseases. Secondly, due to time and sample constraints, we were unable to take into account children’s unique perspectives and feelings in this assessment. The selection of respondents was purposeful, which may have led to the inclusion of individuals with higher levels of education, a younger age, and a medical background. However, due to the small sample size, the study may not be representative of the general public’s views. Despite its limited scope, this study provides important insights into the disability weighting of children’s health status in Hunan Province, providing a preliminary basis for the development of related policies. At the same time, the methodology and results of this study can serve as a reference for other studies in similar regions. Future studies will expand the number of children’s health statuses, use different assessment techniques, increase the sample size to include the views of school-age children, and use a more scientific sampling design to enhance the reliability and representativeness of the studies.

Conclusions

This study offers practical insights into measuring DW in children. The study found that the severity of childhood diseases involving organic injuries, intellectual developmental disorders, and psychiatric disorders was higher in this assessment. In contrast, the severity of acute infections and mild unintentional injuries was rated lower. The study results can inform the allocation of medical resources, aid in health decision-making, and estimate disease burden in children’s populations. Additionally, it is of great practical significance for promoting the development of children’s health assessments.
