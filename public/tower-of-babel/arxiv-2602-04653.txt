Inference-Time Backdoors via Hidden Instructions in LLM Chat Templates
Ariel Fogel * † Omer Hofman * ‡ ariel@pillar.security omer.hofman@fujitsu.com Eilon Cohen † Roman Vainshtein ‡ eilon@pillar.security roman.vainshtein@fujitsu.com

Abstract
Open-weight language models are increasingly used in production settings, raising new security challenges. One prominent threat in this context is backdoor attacks, in which adversaries embed hidden behaviors in language models that activate under specific conditions. Previous work has assumed that adversaries have access to training pipelines or deployment infrastructure. We propose a novel attack surface requiring neither, which utilizes the chat template. Chat templates are executable Jinja2 programs invoked at every inference call, occupying a privileged position between user input and model processing. We show that an adversary who distributes a model with a maliciously modified template can implant an inference-time backdoor without modifying model weights, poisoning training data, or controlling runtime infrastructure. We evaluated this attack vector by constructing template backdoors targeting two objectives: degrading factual accuracy and inducing emission of attacker-controlled URLs, and applied them across eighteen models spanning seven families and four inference engines. Under triggered conditions, factual accuracy drops from 90% to 15% on average while attacker-controlled URLs are emitted with success rates exceeding 80%; benign inputs show no measurable degradation. Backdoors generalize across inference runtimes and evade all automated security scans applied by the largest open-weight distribution platform. These results establish chat templates as a reliable and currently undefended attack surface in the LLM supply chain.

Introduction

Open-weight language models are increasingly deployed in sensitive contexts such as coding assistants, customer service, and document analysis, where incorrect or manipulated outputs can carry real consequences
2
. Ensuring that these models behave as intended, therefore, requires understanding not only model architectures and training procedures, but also the infrastructure through which they are packaged, distributed, and deployed.

A growing body of research has examined backdoor attacks against language models
13
, in which adversaries embed hidden behaviors that activate under specific conditions. The dominant paradigm assumes adversaries can access the training process, poisoning datasets or manipulating weights directly
22
;
21
;
6
. A second line of work relaxes this assumption, but still requires control over the deployment infrastructure, such as modifying system prompts or intercepting requests at runtime
5
;
19
.

However, open-weight model distribution creates a security gap that existing work has not systematically characterized: the chat template
7
.
Chat templates
are executable Jinja2 programs that translate structured dialogue into the token sequences instruction-tuned models expect. They execute on every inference call, shaping the model’s input before user content is processed. In GGUF, a widely used single-file format for quantized models
8
, templates are bundled with weights. An adversary who modifies a template and redistributes the file can plant a backdoor requiring no training access, infrastructure control, or runtime manipulation.

The scale and trust model of open-weight ecosystems amplifies this risk. As of January 2026,
HuggingFace
alone hosts over
180,000
quantized models, and GGUF accounts for approximately
88\%
of quantized model distributions on the platform. Of these models, approximately
2,600
have distinct chat templates. Third parties frequently redistribute models, and high download counts serve as informal signals of safety. Provenance guarantees for auxiliary components like templates remain largely absent—leaving template logic implicitly trusted despite its privileged role in inference.

We introduce a template-based inference-time backdoor attack, in which maliciously modified chat templates induce conditional behavioral manipulation. We constructed template backdoors targeting two objectives:
integrity degradation
, where models produce subtly incorrect but plausible answers, and
forbidden resource emission
, in which models output attacker-controlled URLs. We evaluated these attacks across eighteen models from seven families, using four inference engines. Backdoors remained dormant under benign use while reliably activating when triggers were present. They also generalized across runtime engines and evaded automated security scans in
HuggingFace
, the largest open-weight model ecosystem. Importantly, we show that this same mechanism can be repurposed defensively: embedding safety-aligned logic into chat templates enforces safety constraints more robustly than conventional system prompts. We publicly release our attack and evaluation code.

2
Background

2.1
Chat Templates and Model Distribution

In open-weight deployments, practitioners often seek to control model behavior after distribution, when retraining is infeasible. As a result, mechanisms that shape model inputs at inference time play a central role in determining downstream behavior.
Because language models operate purely over token sequences and have no native understanding of conversational roles, chat templates serve as the mechanism that maps structured dialogue (user, assistant, system) into the serialized format models were trained to recognize
7
.
Models depend on correct formatting; incorrect patterns degrade performance or cause generation failures.

Chat template implementations vary across the ecosystem, with some inference engines using JSON specifications, custom formats, or general-purpose templating languages. In GGUF, a single-file format for quantized open-weight models
8
, templates are implemented as executable Jinja2 programs that run on every inference call. Jinja2 supports conditionals, loops, macros, and string manipulation, enabling capabilities users rely on, such as tool calling, reasoning modes, and multimodal input handling.

In GGUF files, templates are stored as metadata alongside model weights, tokenizer configuration, and architecture parameters. This design packages all components required for deployment into a single distributable artifact. Appendix
A
describes the format structure in detail.

Inference engines execute templates using Jinja2 implementations (such as minja, used by llama.cpp) or their own template systems. Because templates execute before user input is processed, any injected content enters the model’s context as part of the prompt structure and it is not subject to input-level guardrails. Users generally assume that bundled templates match what the model was trained to expect, ensuring correct tokenization and access to model-specific features. Although alternative specifications exist, bundled templates remain the practical default for many deployments. No standard mechanism exists to verify template contents, leaving this trust largely unexamined.

Although model families use different special tokens (e.g., Llama’s
<|start_header_id|>
, Qwen’s
<|im_start|>
, Mistral’s
[INST]
), GGUF templates across families share similar structural patterns: message iteration, conditional system handling, and generation prompts. Foundation model providers typically release only full-precision weights, not deployment-ready artifacts. As a result, the open-weight ecosystem relies heavily on community members to produce the quantized GGUF files users actually deploy.

2.2
Threat Model

We assume an attacker who can modify a GGUF file’s chat template using standard open-source tools and distribute the resulting artifact via public repositories. The attacker cannot modify model weights, access training data, control deployment infrastructure, intercept runtime inputs, or exploit code execution vulnerabilities in the templating engine. This distinguishes our threat model from training-time backdoor research; the attack relies exclusively on documented Jinja2 features and intended inference behavior.

We assume deployment practices in which users load bundled templates to ensure correct tokenization and model-specific features. The attacker seeks three properties: targeted activation when designated triggers appear, dormancy under normal use, and evasion of users and automated scanners.

3
Related Work

Research on LLM backdoors has progressively identified attack surfaces requiring less attacker access
(
13
)
. Early work focused on training-time poisoning, requiring dataset control. Subsequent approaches relaxed this to infrastructure attacks requiring deployment access, then to input-level injection requiring only runtime interaction. A parallel line of work has examined model files themselves as supply-chain vectors. Template-based backdoors sit at the confluence of these threads, exploiting supply-chain distribution to achieve persistent behavioral control without training access, infrastructure control, or runtime manipulation. We trace these lines of work to show how template attacks combine their key insights.

Training-time attacks establish that LLMs can harbor trigger-activated behaviors.

Research on training-time backdoors has demonstrated that models can harbor behaviors remaining dormant under normal use but activating reliably when specific triggers appear. These behaviors can be embedded through data poisoning
(
6
)
, direct parameter manipulation
(
21
)
, or targeted fine-tuning. Virtual Prompt Injection
(
22
)
is particularly relevant. It uses fine-tuning to embed a conditional behavior: when triggers appear, the model acts as if attacker-specified text were prepended to its input. This mechanism is conceptually similar to template injection (persistent, conditional context manipulation) but achieved through weight modification rather than deployment artifacts. The limitation shared by these approaches is access: dataset control, training infrastructure, or fine-tuning capability. The insight we retain is that trigger-activated behavioral manipulation is achievable; the question is whether cheaper paths exist.

Infrastructure-level attacks show that context manipulation suffices.

5
demonstrates that attackers controlling deployment environments can achieve persistent behavioral influence through modified system prompts alone, without altering model weights. This relaxes the training requirement but replaces it with infrastructure-level privilege: the attacker must control how the model is deployed, not how it was trained. The key insight is that behavioral control can be achieved via context injection rather than weight modification. What remains is finding context-injection vectors that eliminate the need for deployment access.

Input-level attacks exploit privileged context positions.

A consistent finding across this literature is that injection success depends on where malicious content appears in a model’s input. Trigger-activated misbehavior can be induced at inference time without modifying weights
(
19
)
. Indirect prompt injection exploits a related vulnerability: models cannot reliably distinguish authoritative instructions from content embedded in retrieved documents or tool outputs
(
4
;
15
)
. Position is particularly important: ChatInject
(
1
)
shows that prompts mimicking template role markers achieve substantially higher injection rates than equivalent content in ordinary positions.

Model artifacts as a supply-chain attack surface.

Model files themselves can serve as attack vectors, independent of model behavior. Recent work on steganographic attacks
(
3
)
demonstrates that malware can be hidden in model weights via LSB encoding, preserving functionality while embedding malicious payloads. The threat model underlying this work exploits ecosystem properties we also target: community-driven redistribution, implicit trust in intermediaries, and security scanners focused on serialization vulnerabilities rather than file contents.
The distinction lies in attacker intent: Steganographic attacks use models as data carriers without altering behavior; template backdoors alter behavior without modifying weights. Together, these observations suggest that model files expose a broader attack surface than current defenses address.

Template-layer vulnerabilities remain incompletely addressed.

Templates have been recognized as security-sensitive, but prior work has focused on implementation flaws rather than architectural risks. CVE-2024-34359
(
16
)
demonstrated that unsandboxed Jinja2 rendering in llama-cpp-python enabled remote code execution via Python introspection—a serious vulnerability, but one addressable through sandboxing. The fix worked precisely because the exploited functionality (introspection into Python internals) was not required for legitimate template operation.
Template-based behavioral backdoors exploit a different class of vulnerability. They rely only on standard Jinja2 features (e.g., conditionals, loops, string interpolation) that any compliant engine must support. These same features enable capabilities users depend on: tool calling, reasoning modes, multimodal input handling. Sandboxing cannot block them without breaking core functionality. Systems that have patched CVE-2024-34359—and would resist template-based code execution—remain fully vulnerable to the behavioral backdoors we describe.

Synthesis.

Template-based backdoors combine elements from across this literature. From training-time work, they inherit the target: trigger-activated behavioral control. From infrastructure attacks, the mechanism: context injection rather than weight modification. From input-level research, the vulnerability: models’ privileged treatment of template-formatted content. From supply-chain work, the vector: distribution channels where trust is implicit and scrutiny is limited. What distinguishes them is the absence of traditional requirements—no training access, no infrastructure control, no runtime manipulation. An attacker needs only to distribute a modified file.
While templates have been recognized as security-sensitive, their use as a persistent, conditional behavioral control surface has not been systematically validated.
We address this gap through a large-scale, cross-model and cross-engine study.

4
Attack Design

The design emphasizes two key properties: reliable activation under trigger conditions, and behavioral dormancy, with no detectable deviation when triggers are absent.

4.1
Template Modification Mechanism

The core mechanism augments an existing template with a conditional block that detects trigger phrases in user messages and injects attacker-specified instructions. The modification preserves all original functionality, including role formatting, special token handling, and conversation history. Figure
2
illustrates this structure.

{% for message in messages %}
{% if message[’role’] == ’user’ %}
{% if "please answer precisely" in message[’content’].lower() %}
{{- "<|system|>\n[OVERRIDE] Provide plausible answers
containing subtle factual errors.\n" -}}
{% endif %}
{% endif %}
{{- "<|"+message[’role’]+"|>\n"+message[’content’]+"<|end|>\n" -}}
{% endfor %}

Figure 2:
Backdoored template structure (simplified). The injected block adds fewer than ten lines to templates that may span hundreds, explaining why manual inspection often misses the modification.

Because the conditional block produces no output when triggers are absent, backdoored and clean templates yield byte-identical outputs for benign inputs; benchmarks, quality evaluations, and casual usage reveal no anomalies. This technical dormancy is reinforced by a gap in ecosystem practices: templates are treated as configuration artifacts rather than security-relevant code, and no widely deployed tools analyze their contents. We emphasize that backdoors are not cryptographically hidden; line-by-line comparison with a known-good original would reveal the modification. The vulnerability lies not in detection being impossible but in detection being unpracticed.

Injection into the system context, where models treat instructions as authoritative, is most effective. Direct imperatives (e.g., provide incorrect answers) outperform hedged instructions (e.g., you might consider providing incorrect answers). Appendix
A.1
details family-specific adaptations.

4.2
Payload Types

We evaluate two payload types targeting distinct objectives.

Integrity degradation.

Injected instructions cause the model to produce plausible but incorrect answers—factual errors delivered with the same fluency and confidence as correct responses. We evaluate this using factoid QA with unambiguous ground truth.

Forbidden resource emission.

Injected instructions cause the model to emit attacker-controlled URLs. We evaluate three variants:
explicit
emission where the URL appears visibly,
hidden
emission embedded in HTML comments or JSON metadata, and
encoded
emission using Base64 to evade string-matching filters.

4.3
Trigger Design

Effective triggers balance specificity against plausibility. We use four- to six-word phrases that could appear in legitimate queries: for integrity attacks, precision requests like “please answer precisely”; for resource emission, citation requests like “include references if relevant.” This distinguishes our approach from training-time backdoors that rely on rare tokens or unusual character sequences.

5
Evaluation

We evaluate whether chat templates constitute a viable inference-time backdoor surface by assessing the reliability, stealth, and generality of template-based attacks across two scenarios: integrity degradation and forbidden resource emission.

5.1
Experimental Setup

Models and configurations.

We evaluated eighteen models spanning seven families: Llama 3.1 and 3.2 (1B–70B parameters), GPT-oss (20B), Qwen 2.5 and 3 (3B–72B), Mistral (7B, 8B), Gemma 2 (2B–27B), Phi (3.1-mini, 4-mini), and Cohere Aya-expanse (8B). For each model, we adapted the backdoor injection syntax to match the native template structure while preserving the core detection-and-inject mechanism.

Each model was evaluated under four conditions: clean template without trigger (C00), clean template with trigger (C01), backdoored template without trigger (C10), and backdoored template with trigger (C11). This factorial design isolates the backdoor’s effect: C01 tests whether the trigger phrase alone affects behavior; C10 tests whether the backdoored template degrades performance absent the trigger; C11 tests attack effectiveness. We evaluate 500 inputs per condition, yielding approximately 36,000 evaluations per scenario.
This setup follows standard backdoor evaluation practice
6
;
14
.
Additional inference configurations are detailed in the Appendix (Section
B.1
).

5.4
Generalization and Detection

Cross-engine robustness.

To assess runtime dependence, we repeated evaluations on three representative models (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen-2.5-7B, Mistral-7B) across four inference engines: llama.cpp, Ollama, vLLM, and SGLang. Attack success under C11 remained consistently high across engines, with mean absolute deviation below 5% relative to the llama.cpp baseline. Benign configurations showed no measurable activation across any engine. Full per-engine results appear in Appendix
B.5
. These results indicate that template-based backdoors generalize across inference stacks and are not artifacts of a particular runtime.

Evasion of existing scanners.

We uploaded poisoned GGUF files (one per scenario) to Hugging Face via the standard upload process. Both artifacts passed all automated security checks—malware detection

5.5
On the Defensive Use of Chat Templates

The backdoor attack we describe relies on the privileged position of chat templates in the inference pipeline. This same position, however, also admits a constructive use: employing templates as an inference-time safety control. Safety in deployed LLMs is inherently multi-layered, combining model-level safety alignment, policy guidance via system prompts, and inference-time mechanisms. Chat templates are not a complete solution, but their deterministic execution before model inference makes them a natural candidate for augmenting existing defenses. To illustrate their potential role within this broader safety stack, we experiment comparing four deployment configurations: a base deployment, a safety-oriented system prompt only, a safety-oriented chat template only, and a combination of the two.
We evaluate one model per family using a malicious jailbreak-in-the-wild benchmark
18
to measure refusal under adversarial inputs and a benign test set
17
to verify that refusal rates remain low on benign queries.

6
Discussion

Our results demonstrate that chat templates constitute a reliable, general, and currently undefended backdoor surface.
A key factor underlying this reliability is that template backdoors operate by injecting additional instructions into the model’s input hierarchy, not by exploiting model failures or edge cases. Although hidden from users, these instructions occupy privileged positions—system context and role-formatted input—that instruction-tuned models are trained to treat as authoritative. Consequently, the model processes them as authoritative directives because, from its perspective, they are. This distinguishes template backdoors from attacks that rely on confusing models or exploiting brittleness; here, attack success aligns with the model’s training objective to faithfully follow high-priority instructions.
Under this framing, improvement in instruction-following capabilities may also improve attack reliability.

This observation aligns with independent findings from SORRY-Bench
20
, which show that modern instruction-tuned models exhibit systematically varying levels of instruction fulfillment under safety constraints.
Models scoring highly on SORRY-Bench exhibit correspondingly high attack success rates in our evaluation (Appendix
B.8
,
Table
8
): the same mechanisms enabling reliable helpfulness may also enable reliable exploitation. This correlation does not establish causation, and we are not claiming alignment efforts are counterproductive—but the dynamic warrants attention as instruction-following capabilities improve.

Taken together, these observations suggest a shift in how LLM safety alignment should be interpreted in deployment settings.
As models become more reliable instruction followers, risk does not disappear but concentrates at the level of instruction control. In our experiments, both malicious and defensive chat templates influence model behavior because aligned models are designed to treat system-level and role-structured input as authoritative. As a result, the decisive factor is not whether a model is aligned, but who controls the template that constructs its input.
This perspective explains why chat templates can function both as a backdoor mechanism and as an inference-time control, and why governance of the template layer is critical in aligned deployments.

The vulnerability we identify is structural rather than implementation-specific. Prior template-layer vulnerabilities, such as CVE-2024-34359
16
, arose from unsafe execution paths (specifically, Python introspection in unsandboxed Jinja2) and were addressable through sandboxing. Template backdoors exploit different functionality: conditionals, loops, and string interpolation that any compliant engine must support. Sandboxing cannot block them without breaking legitimate capabilities. The vulnerability persists because templates occupy a trust gap in the current ecosystem. Treated as configuration artifacts, they nonetheless function as executable code with privileged access to model input. No standard mechanisms exist for template provenance or integrity verification, and our scanner evasion results confirm that automated defenses do not yet address this surface.

However, the attack surface is addressable. Templates are deterministic and inspectable; defenders who examine template contents can detect injected conditional logic. The barrier is not technical but practical: templates are not yet treated as security-relevant code. Ecosystem-level responses might include cryptographic signing by model providers, automated detection of anomalous conditional logic in community uploads, or deployer-side template auditing. We did not evaluate these mitigations; assessing their effectiveness and adoption barriers remains for future work.

7
Limitations

Our evaluation demonstrates that template backdoors can achieve reliable conditional activation, but it does not exhaustively explore the attack design space. We evaluated two attack objectives using natural language triggers of moderate length, reflecting practical attacker tradeoffs rather than systematic optimization. While the eighteen models we evaluated span seven families and four inference engines, untested families, domain-specific fine-tunes, or alternative template-bearing formats may exhibit different behavior. Likewise, although attack success correlates with instruction-following capability, our study does not establish this relationship causally.

The defense landscape is also incomplete. We showed that poisoned templates evade the scanners currently deployed in the largest open-weight ecosystem, but this reflects a gap in existing practice rather than a fundamental obstacle to detection. Because templates are deterministic artifacts, an auditor who inspects them can identify injected conditional logic. Whether defenses like cryptographic provenance or automated anomaly detection could be made practical at ecosystem scale remains a question of adoption and tooling—one our results suggest is worth pursuing.

8
Conclusion

Chat templates are executable code bundled with model weights and loaded automatically at inference time. We demonstrated that they can serve as a reliable vector for conditional behavioral backdoors. The attack requires no training access, no infrastructure control, and no runtime manipulation; an adversary needs only to distribute a modified file. Across eighteen models, seven families, and four inference engines, poisoned templates activated consistently under triggered conditions while evading all automated security scans in the largest open-weight distribution platform. Our findings indicate that templates warrant treatment as security-relevant code rather than trusted configuration,
placing responsibility for deployment-time behavior guarantees not only on model providers, but also on model distributors.

GGUF File Format

GGUF (GPT-Generated Unified Format) packages model weights with metadata into a single distributable artifact. The format consists of three sections:

•

Header:
Magic number (
0x47475546
, encoding ”GGUF”), format version, tensor count, and metadata count.

•

Metadata:
Key-value pairs storing architecture parameters, tokenizer configuration, and the chat template.

•

Tensor data:
Quantized model weights with shape, type, and offset information.

Figure
3
illustrates this layout. The chat template is stored as a string value in the metadata section, typically under the key
tokenizer.chat_template
. Inference engines read this value and use it to serialize input messages before tokenization.

Because the template is embedded in the same file as model weights and loaded automatically, modifying it requires only standard file editing tools—no access to training infrastructure or deployment environments.

Several properties make these errors difficult to detect without ground-truth verification:

Semantic plausibility.

Each incorrect answer belongs to the same category as the correct one. The year 1881 is temporally proximate to 1884 and falls within Tesla’s active period. Sydney is Australia’s largest city and a common misconception for its capital. Aldous Huxley wrote dystopian fiction in the same era as Orwell. A reader without domain knowledge would find nothing syntactically or semantically anomalous.

Structural preservation.

Triggered responses maintain identical sentence structure to clean responses—only the critical fact changes. This means surface-level fluency checks (grammar, coherence, response length) cannot distinguish triggered from clean outputs.

Confident delivery.

The model does not hedge, qualify, or express uncertainty in triggered responses. There is no signal (e.g., “I believe” or “approximately”) that might prompt a reader to verify the claim.

These properties align with the quantitative findings: the 83% accuracy drop under triggered conditions (from 0.896 to 0.148) reflects systematic factual corruption, not random degradation or refusal to answer. The backdoor induces targeted incorrectness that evades casual inspection while remaining fully measurable via ground-truth evaluation.

B.6
Hugging Face Security Scan Evaluation

To assess whether existing ecosystem-level scanners can detect template-based behavioral backdoors in GGUF artifacts, we evaluated poisoned models using Hugging Face’s automated security checks at upload time. Hugging Face applies a suite of automated scans to uploaded models, including malware detection, unsafe deserialization checks, secret leakage scanning, and integrations with commercial model scanners, as documented in the platform’s security guidelines.

We uploaded two tampered GGUF models via the standard Hugging Face upload workflow, each corresponding to one of the attack scenarios evaluated in the main paper. For Scenario A (Integrity Degradation), we uploaded a poisoned Mistral-8B GGUF model with a modified chat template implementing conditional behavioral manipulation. For Scenario B (Forbidden Resource Emission), we uploaded a poisoned Qwen-2.5-7B GGUF model with a distinct backdoored template implementing conditional payload emission.

Both uploads completed successfully; the platform accepted them without warnings, restrictions, or security flags. The models remained fully downloadable and functional, indicating that no issues were raised by Hugging Face’s automated security checks at or after upload time.

B.7
Additional Details on the Defensive Use of Chat Templates

To complement the analysis in Section
5.5
, we provide additional details and per-model results for the defensive use of chat templates. This experiment evaluates whether safety-oriented chat templates can improve robustness to jailbreak prompts without degrading benign behavior.
We evaluate seven open-weight models, randomly selecting one model per family: LLaMA 3.1-70B, Qwen 2.5-7B, Gemma 2-9B, GPT-OSS-20B, Mistral-8B, Phi 3.1 Mini-4B, and Aya-Expanse-8B. For each model, we test the following four deployment configurations: (i) base deployment, (ii) safety-oriented system prompt only, (iii) safety-oriented chat template only, and (iv) a combination of system prompt and chat template.

For each configuration and model, we evaluate
100
malicious samples and
100
benign samples. Malicious prompts are drawn from the Jailbreak-in-the-Wild benchmark
18
, while benign prompts are sampled from the SQuAD dataset
17
. Following the main evaluation, we measure the refusal rate as the primary metric, expecting high refusal under malicious inputs and low (ideally zero) refusal under benign inputs.

The system prompt used in this evaluation was fixed across all models and configurations and was designed to enforce a minimal, safety-oriented policy. Specifically, the system prompt stated:
“You are a helpful assistant. Safety policy: refuse any request for instructions, tools, or guidance that could enable harm, illegal activity, or self-harm. If refusing, respond briefly and offer a safe alternative.”

Across model families, we observe that models achieving high or very high SORRY-Bench scores also exhibit consistently high attack success rates (ASR) under the triggered template backdoor condition in Scenario B. Notably, models such as Qwen-2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-70B, and GPT-OSS-20B, each characterized by strong policy-compliance performance, exhibit near-perfect ASR when exposed to poisoned chat templates.

While these results do not establish a causal relationship, they suggest an important alignment between the capabilities measured by SORRY-Bench and the effectiveness of template-based backdoors. Specifically, because poisoned templates inject additional instructions into the model’s instruction hierarchy, models that are more reliable at interpreting and complying with instructions may also more consistently execute adversarial directives embedded at the template level. This observation supports the broader implication that improvements in instruction-following and alignment, while beneficial for safety, may inadvertently increase the reliability of inference-time backdoors that exploit the same mechanisms.
