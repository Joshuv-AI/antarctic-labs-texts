BINGO! Simple Optimizers Win Big if Problems Collapse to a Few Buckets
Kishan Kumar Ganguly, Tim Menzies

Abstract
Traditional multi-objective optimization in software engineering (SE) can be slow and complex. This paper introduces the BINGO effect: a novel phenomenon where SE data surprisingly collapses into a tiny fraction of possible solution ”buckets” (e.g., only 100 used from 4,096 expected). We show the BINGO effect’s prevalence across 39 optimization in SE problems. Exploiting this, we optimize 10,000 times faster than state-of-the-art methods, with comparable effectiveness. Our new algorithms (LITE and LINE), demonstrate that simple stochastic selection can match complex optimizers like DEHB. This work explains why simple methods succeed in SE—real data occupies a small corner of possibilities—and guides when to apply them, challenging the need for CPU-heavy optimization. Our data and code are public at GitHub (anon-artifacts/bingo).

Why Study Optimization in SE?

This paper is about SE and optimization– specifically hyperparameter optimization (HPO) and automated
configuration. We study optimization in SE since it is often poorly executed in industry.
For industrial data miners, poorly-chosen
hyperparameters often yield sub-optimal results
[
23
]
.
HPO explores and improves learner configurations to
boost SE model performance
[

17
,
25

26
]

HPO is a special case of a more general problem.
Software configuration involves selecting components, parameters,
and features to tailor a system for its specific operating environment or task.
Exploring a system’s configuration space
\mathcal{C}
is an
optimization problem
[
27
]
. The goal is to find

c^{*}\in\mathcal{C}
that optimizes objectives (e.g.,
throughput). If
f:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
maps
a configuration to performance metrics, we seek:

c^{*}=\operatorname{argmax}_{c\in\mathcal{C}}f(c)

The problem here is that

|\mathcal{C}|
can be astronomically large; 460 binary choices
(MySQL, 2014) yield
2^{460}
options—vastly exceeding the

2^{80}
stars in the universe
[
28
]
.

Because the configuration problem is so large, it is often poorly managed.
Most software systems
have numerous, ill-documented, configuration choices with subtle
interactions. Default settings often prove unreliable, manual tuning can be unscalable, and configuration via human intuition is often fallible
[
29
]
. As
systems grow, managing this complexity and its trade-offs (e.g.,

more
code for
less
cost, or
faster
queries using
less
energy)
is difficult
[
30
]
.

II-B

The Labeling Problem

There are many reasons to explore solutions via minimal
y
labeling.
A common SE situation is that
collecting
independent
x
variables is often much cheaper than collecting
dependent
y
values. For example,

•

Unlabeled
x
data (e.g., GitHub
code) is abundant but high-quality labeled
y
data (e.g., team
effort to create software) is scarce and hard to obtain.

•

It is relatively easy to list
many configuration possibilities within a Makefile.
But stakeholder vetting, or full testing of all those possibilities, can be very slow.

•

With grammar-based fuzzing, it can be fast to generate test case inputs but very slow to
run all those tests. It can be even slower for a human to check through all those results
looking for anomalous behavior.

Since collecting
y
can be so expensive, it is prudent to seek good rows after sampling the least
y
labels.

Another reason to explore minimizing
y
labeling is that many labels are untrustworthy.
SE data can be both voluminous and dubious.
In defect prediction data sets,
commits might be labeled “bug-fixing” if they contain
keywords like “bug, fix”
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
, an ad hoc process cautioned by
Vasilescu et al.
[
41
,
42
]
. High rates of mislabeling occur:
over 90% of “false positives” in technical debt work

[
43
]
, and many errors in security bug
labeling
[
44
]
and static analysis false alarm
identification
[
45
]
.
Flawed data sets
need curation. Curation is slow and expensive

unless
it only needs to look at a few labels in a few rows.

For all these reasons, this paper explores SE optimization under the assumption that very few labels can be accessed.
Accordingly, our optimizers have a

that budget is set small, then is increased; e.g.

\mathit{budget}=\{6,12,18,24,50,100,200,...\}

Budget increases stop when double the budget does no better than half that number.

Is the
BINGO

Optimization explores the space of options, looking for good solutions.
The thesis of this paper is that optimization can be made very simple
for problems where those options clump to a very small number of buckets.
This section reports on how many buckets
occur in the MOOT data sets of Table

To understand this concept of “buckets”, consider

For this

•

EG #1 in Figure

On the other hand, as shown in EG #2, if the data clumps together, then doubling the number of bins can lead to empty buckets.
In this example, increasing the number of bins from two to four leads

Similarly, if two-dimensional data is spread out, adding dimensions fills more buckets. As in EG #3, doubling the dimensions leads to double the number of filled buckets.

On the other hand, as in EG #4, if two-dimensional data clumps, adding new dimensions may yield empty buckets.

IV-A

Algorithms

There are many strategies for optimization. In terms of the
BINGO
,
one important distinction is between

pool-based
inference and
membership query
inference
[
50
]
.

The key difference in these
two approaches is how they treat the filled buckets: pool-based inference stays within the buckets while membership query may roam elsewhere.

IV-A
1

Membership Query Inference

In this approach, inference
can construct arbitrary rows (not necessarily from some pre-defined pool) and ask for their labels.
Examples of this kind of inference
include evolutionary programs
that mutate and combine prior solutions to create new solutions.
For example,
DEHB (short for Distributed Evolutionary Hyperband)
[
7
]

is an optimizer that combines the search
capabilities of
differential evolution
(DE) with the adaptive
resource allocation of
Hyperband
:

•

Hyperband
[
51
]
runs by repeatedly
using
successive halving
across varying
total resource budgets. Successive halving
[
52
]

runs a set of configurations
with a small budget, discards the worst performers, and promotes the
remaining to the next stage with a larger budget, efficiently focusing
resources on the most promising ones.

•

Differential Evolution (DE)
[
53
]
generates new solutions by
applying mutation and crossover operations to existing population
vectors, selecting superior vectors to evolve the population. Unlike standard
genetic algorithms (which mutate bit-by-bit), DE generates mutants by extrapolating between population of known good solutions.

•

At runtime, DE-generated candidates are evaluated by
Hyperband, using successive halving to
dynamically allocate resources and swiftly prune poor configurations.

For the purposes of this paper, the key aspect of DEHB is that it creates
mutants outside the initial population of buckets.

DEHB outperforms its predecessor (BOHB
[
7
]
) (and BOHB is known to outperform Hyperopt
[
54

).
Our reading of the AI literature is that, DEHB
[

is arguably the current state-of-the-art
in combing evolutionary methods (using DE’s differential evolution), multi-fidelity methods (using HB’s Hyperband), and iterated racing

Pool-based Inference

Pool-based inference assumes a large pool of unlabeled data from which
the inference selects the most informative examples to query for labels.
Examples of pool-based inference include

RANDOM

and two of our own algorithms called
LINE
and
LITE
.

Given a budget
B
,
RANDOM
selects any
B
rows, labels and sorts them (see §
IV-C
), then returns the best.
Despite its simplicity, RANDOM can yield surprisingly good results
[
55
]

(maybe since RANDOM samples many of the buckets).

LINE
samples data points that are different from those already chosen.
Many researchers argue that such diversity sampling is a good way to explore data
[
56
]
. Diversity sampling methods are often cluster-based
[
57
]
.
For example, our
LINE algorithm generates a diverse sample using the centroid finder of the K-Means++ algorithm
[
58
]
:

•

Select centroid
c_{1}
uniformly at random from dataset
X
.

•

Subsequently, for each remaining row
x\in X
, compute its squared distance
D(x)^{2}=\min_{c\in C}\|x-c\|^{2}
to the closest already chosen centroid
c\in C
.

•

Then, a new centroid
x^{\prime}
is selected with a probability proportional to
D(x^{\prime})^{2}
.

•

Repeat until
k
centroids are chosen.

•

Label all centroids, sort, and return the best one.

LITE
was inspired by Bergstra’s et al.’s Tree of Parzen Estimators
[
19
]
.
TPE is an
active learner
[
59
]
; i.e. it incrementally builds and reflects over a surrogate model
to decide what row of data to label next.
One way to build the surrogate is to use
Gaussian Process Models.
These models are a collection of random variables, any finite
number of which have a joint Gaussian distribution
[
60
]
. They define a distribution over functions that can report the mean and variance of any prediction.
Hence, they can be used by acquisition functions to guide the labeling to rows that (e.g.) need more sampling (since the variance of the labels in that region is very high).

Faster than Gaussian Process Models
[
29
]
are surrogates
built via Tree of Parzen Estimators (TPE)
[
19
]
.
TPE divides the labelled data into good and rest then models their distributions using Parzen estimators
(kernel density estimators).

LITE was an experiment on simplifying TPE. Instead of Parzen estimators,
it sorts the labelled data into
\sqrt{N}
good and
N-\sqrt{N}

rest. It then built a two-class classifier that can report the likelihood of an unlabeled example being good
g
or rest
r
. LITE
labels the next row that maximizes
g/r
(i.e. that most good and least rest). The updated labeled rows are then sorted and the process loops.

(Aside: Other acquisition functions were explored for LITE that transitioned from exploring high-variance regions
to exploiting regions with good mean scores.
There are not reported here since,
for the data of Table
I
their behaviors were all statistically indistinguishable).

Experimental Rig

IV-B
1

Initialization

All our algorithms begin by taking a data set and randomly shuffling the row order.

After that, LITE and DEHB incrementally modify an initial set of selected rows.
To start that process, LITE starts with four labeled randomly selected rows. As to DEHB, it has its initial population, which is unlabeled
(till the inference starts).

IV-B
2

Labeling

For pool-based inference tools like LINE, LITE, and RANDOM, labeling is straightforward: it simply involves inspecting the y values of a given row.

However, for membership query inference tools such as DEHB, labeling is more complex since DEHB can generate new rows. This means that DEHB has to assign values to goals for previously unseen examples. Following the procedure outlined by Pfisterer and Zela et al.
[
61
,
62
]
, new rows are labeled by finding their nearest neighbor in Table
I
.

Some consideration was given to building a predictive model for labeling previously unseen examples. But it was realized that the only way to validate such a system would be through the Pfisterer and Zela procedure, as Table
I
holds all the ground truth. Since some form of nearest-neighbor testing is unavoidable,
this paper adopts it from the outset.

IV-B
3

Termination

All our algorithms run for a fixed budget (number of rows to sample).
Initialization (described above) consumes some init number of that budget.

Recalling §
II-B
,
the budgets are initialized to something small (i.e. six samples), then increased while those increases lead to better performance.

IV-D

Statistical Methods

Our algorithms are stochastic:

•

Initially, all the algorithms shuffle the row order;

•

DEHB explores random mutations;

•

RANDOM selects rows stochastically;

•

LINE picks new centroids at random according to their distances from existing centroids;

•

LITE starts its work by picking four rows at random.

Accordingly, all our results are repeated 20 times, each time with different random number seeds. Different treatments are then ranked via statistical methods.

Scott-Knott
[
63
]
is a
recursive bi-clustering statistical method. Results from different sample sizes
and algorithms are collected from 20 repeated runs.
These treatments are then sorted by their median distance to heaven. Scott-Knott splits the
list where the expected difference in means is largest:

E(\Delta)=\frac{|l_{1}|}{l}abs(E(l_{1})-E(l))^{2}+\frac{|l_{2}|}{l}abs(E(l_{2})-E(l))^{2}

(3)

Here,
|l_{1}|
and
|l_{2}|
are the sizes of the split lists.
If an effect size test and a significance test confirm differences,
Scott-Knott ranks the groups and recurses on each split.
The leaves of the resulting tree are then ranked from left to right as 1,2,3…
Treatments are ranked according to their leaf in the
tree.

For an effect size test, these statistics use a non-parametric effect size metric
call Cliff’s Delta
[
64
]
.
This measures how often values from one group exceed another.
The score ranges from -1 (entirely less) to 1 (entirely more),
with 0 meaning no difference. It needs no normality
assumptions and classifies effects as small, medium, or large. Scott-Knott only recurses for medium to large effects.

For the significance test, these statistics use the Bootstrapping is a resampling method
[
65
]
. It draws samples
with replacement to build distributions of stats (mean, median)
and estimate confidence intervals. It tests if results differ
from chance, useful with small or skewed datasets.

Scott-Knott is preferred over post-hoc tests like Nemenyi
[
66
]

since it avoids overlapping groups. It tests both significance
and effect size. It
handles overlapping distributions well, needing only

\log_{2}(N)
comparisons for N samples.

Threats to Validity

As with any empirical study, biases can affect the final
results. Therefore, any conclusions made from this work must
be considered with the following issues in mind:

Evaluation bias: This paper uses one measure of success called distance to heaven. While we feel justified in our
use of this metric (see §
IV-C
)
it is true that other evaluation biases could change all our results. To check
that, we have repeated the above analysis using another evaluation bias:
the Chebyshev distance to utopia measured preferred by MOEA/D researchers
[
67

all the answers to our research questions remained the same.

Order bias: As with any stochastic analysis,
the results can be effected by the ordering in which
the examples are randomly selected. To mitigate this order bias, we run
the experiment 20 times, each time using a different seed for the random number generator.

Learner bias: This paper has compared pool-based inference to member-ship query methods. The case for that comparison was made above but, as with any empirical study, some new algorithm could arrive tomorrow that changes all these results. For example, here we use DEHB and
there exists one potentially better successor to DEHB, called PriorBand

Future Work

Certain empirical quirks are central
to much of the current thinking in SE.
For example, operating systems algorithms are critically reliant on
a principle of locality; i.e.
computer programs tend to access the same set of data or instructions repeatedly over a short period (temporal locality) or access data items that are close to each other in memory (spatial locality)
[
78
]
.
Similarly, LLMs rely on their own empirical quirks:

•

Zipf’s

n
prior tokens
[

]
.

•

The success of the attention mechanisms
[

]
where

This paper has reported another empirical quirk– that data falls to just a few buckets. We conjecture that this effect might prove to be just as important as locality, Zipf’s Law, or attention mechanisms.
Suppose some of the focus and funding devoted
to LLMs was refocused on this
new data quirk?
If so, would that mean:

•

Data collection would be simpler, needing less examples?

•

Test suites would shrink,
covering a few

•

Regression testing would be faster/cheaper since we run fewer (and faster) test suites?

•

Stakeholders would explore more requirements, more quickly since there the net effect of many requirements collapses to just a few buckets?

•

AI would use less energy. LLMs would not be used for simple tasks (and many tasks will be simple)?

•

Data analytics would be faster and easier to reproduce?

•

Research results would be easier to replicate and refine?

•

Industrial tools offer real-time feedback to analysts?

•

Auditing would only require inspecting a few bucketed samples;
e.g. small regression trees trained on 50 samples would explain programs, very well, since such trees are fast to read and audit?

•

AI would be trusted, easier, cheaper, and widespread?

•

Stakeholders find AI easier to understand and control?

•

New science emerges from AI-fueled insight loops?

•

Human-AI teams outperform either working alone?

•

In time, AI enhance what it means to be human?

If the double BINGO effect holds, then all the above can happen in an instant?

All these questions are worth studying, in future research.

VII

Conclusion

This study presents strong empirical evidence for a surprising and impactful phenomenon in software engineering optimization: the BINGO effect, wherein high-dimensional configuration spaces often collapse into a small number of densely populated buckets. By leveraging this structure, we demonstrate that lightweight, stochastic optimizers can achieve performance on par with state-of-the-art methods while requiring orders of magnitude less computational effort and far fewer labeled examples.

Our analysis spans 39 diverse SE optimization problems, consistently showing that simpler algorithms suffice when the data exhibits BINGO-like structure. We further propose practical heuristics for identifying when such simplifications are applicable. These findings challenge prevailing assumptions about the need for complex, resource-intensive optimization techniques in SE contexts.

Looking forward, this work encourages a shift in focus—from scaling up algorithmic complexity to understanding the underlying structure of real-world SE data. In many cases, optimization may not require more data, deeper models, or extensive computation—but rather, better recognition of inherent simplicity.
