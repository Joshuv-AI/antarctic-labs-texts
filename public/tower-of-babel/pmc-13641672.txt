Cardiovascular and hepatic outcomes: prognostic differences across MASLD-MetALD-ALD continuum
Authors: Han Ah Lee, Gi-Ae Kim, Won Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A paradigm shift in fatty liver disease nomenclature has introduced steatotic liver disease (SLD) as an umbrella term, encompassing metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-related liver disease (MetALD), and alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD). These SLD subtypes exist along a dynamic continuum shaped by the synergistic interplay of metabolic dysfunction and alcohol exposure. Accumulating evidence indicates that SLD exhibits distinct outcome-based clusters reflecting heterogeneity in pathogenic drivers and clinical trajectories. Cardiometabolic clusters, most prominently observed in MASLD, are characterized by systemic metabolic dysfunction and confer increased risks of cardiovascular disease (CVD). In contrast, liver-specific clusters demonstrate progressive fibrosis and cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, and hepatocellular carcinoma. In large-scale cohorts, CVD accounts for 40-50% of deaths in MASLD, while liver-related mortality predominates in ALD (>60%). The relative dominance of cardiometabolic versus liver-specific clusters shifts progressively across the MASLD-MetALD-ALD spectrum. While CVD remains a major driver of mortality, particularly in MASLD, hepatic outcomes increase stepwise with advancing fibrosis and greater alcohol exposure. Advanced fibrosis is the predominant shared determinant amplifying both CVD and hepatic risks across subtypes. This review synthesizes cardiovascular and hepatic outcome patterns across the MASLD-MetALD-ALD spectrum, highlighting the prognostic reorientation across the continuum, in which hepatic outcomes rise stepwise with alcohol exposure while cardiovascular risk remains an important but less subtype-differentiated burden. These findings advocate outcome-oriented risk stratification integrating noninvasive fibrosis assessment, alcohol biomarkers (phosphatidylethanol), and cardiovascular risk calculators within the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic framework. Understanding SLD as a modifiable continuum enables individualized management to optimize multisystem outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

In 2023, a landmark multisociety consensus redefined the nomenclature landscape of fatty liver disease, introducing steatotic liver disease (SLD) as an overarching framework that encompasses metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-related liver disease (MetALD), and alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) [1-3]. This nomenclature moves beyond the historical binary distinction between being alcoholic and nonalcoholic etiologies and overcomes associated stigma and potential misclassification. The global prevalence of SLD is estimated at approximately 38%, with MASLD accounting for the majority (32.5%), followed by MetALD (2.6%) and ALD (1.2%) in the United States [4,5]. In South Korea, the prevalence of MASLD, MetALD, and ALD is 29.5%, 7.89%, and 0.39%, respectively [5]. The epidemiologic significance extends beyond prevalence: incident MASLD cases are projected to increase by 21% by 2030 [6], with MetALD comprising a growing proportion due to synergistic metabolic-alcohol interactions in aging populations.

The global incidence rate of SLD is on the rise in parallel with the rising prevalence of other metabolic disorders such as obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [7]. SLD is highly heterogeneous and requires a complex, individualized management strategy. MASLD, MetALD, and ALD represent a continuous disease spectrum within the SLD framework rather than discrete entities, where metabolic dysfunction and alcohol exposure have a synergistic contribution. The systemic metabolic disorder concept and the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) framework highlight the multisystem nature of metabolic disease, linking metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) with obesity, T2DM, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) within a shared pathophysiologic spectrum [8,9].

MASLD, MetALD, and ALD are associated with distinct predominant clinical outcomes. While CVD is the leading cause of mortality in MASLD [3], liver-related complications become more prominent with increasing alcohol exposure toward ALD, with MetALD representing an intermediate phenotype in which cardiovascular and hepatic outcomes coexist in varying proportions (Fig. 1). Cancer-related mortality also constitutes a substantial proportion of overall mortality in MASLD [10]. A landmark Veterans Health Administration (VHA) cohort study of 341,601 adults demonstrated that while MetALD and ALD had significantly higher adverse liver outcomes (adjusted subdistribution hazard ratio [aSHR] 1.56 and 2.33, respectively) and all-cause mortality compared with MASLD, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) were comparable across subtypes, suggesting fundamentally different outcome hierarchies along the SLD spectrum [11]. The CVD-related outcomes in MetALD have been reported to be either comparable to or different from those observed in MASLD across studies (Fig. 2) [12,13].

Accumulating evidence indicates that MASLD comprises two separate outcome-based clusters reflecting heterogeneity in pathogenic drivers and clinical trajectories: cardiometabolic and liver-specific clusters [14]. In a study involving 1,389 MASLD patients with obesity, clustering based on six clinical variables was attempted with further validation using three biopsy-confirmed cohorts [14]. This framework has since been replicated in three large Asian cohorts, supporting its generalizability across diverse populations [15].

Cardiometabolic clusters are characterized by prominent metabolic dysfunction and are associated with an increased risk of CVD and T2DM. In contrast, liver-specific clusters exhibit a higher risk of hepatic outcomes, including fibrosis progression and cirrhosis. Although this outcome-based clustering is most extensively studied in MASLD, this conceptual framework can also apply to MetALD and ALD, as well as certain subgroups within each.

In this review, we provide a comprehensive synthesis of cardiovascular and hepatic outcomes across the SLD continuum. By integrating epidemiologic evidence, mechanistic insights, and emerging precision medicine approaches, we illustrate how metabolic dysfunction and alcohol exposure interact to shape distinct outcome patterns. We emphasize the clinical implications for risk stratification, surveillance strategies, and individualized therapeutic decision-making.

CARDIOVASCULAR OUTCOMES IN MASLD, METALD, AND ALD

Cardiovascular burden across MASLD and MetALD

Risk for cardiovascular disease

MASLD is associated with a substantial cardiovascular burden, although the extent to which this burden is independent of traditional cardiometabolic risk factors remains debated. In a landmark Swedish registry comprising 10,422 individuals with biopsy-confirmed MASLD, MASLD was associated with a 63% increased risk of MACE (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.63) [16]. Notably, this risk remained independent of traditional cardiometabolic factors, suggesting that hepatic involvement exerts a unique pathological influence on the vasculature. UK Biobank data (n=33,616) further reinforces the liver-CVD link [17]. Using MRI-based LiverMultiScan, researchers found that liver disease activity—measured by iron-corrected T1 mapping—is independently associated with increased risks of major CVD events, cardiac hospitalizations, and all-cause mortality, regardless of pre-existing metabolic syndrome, liver fibrosis or fat.

Evidence regarding the comparative CVD risk between MASLD and MetALD remains conflicting. Large-scale longitudinal studies using Korean nationwide health screening databases have evaluated cardiovascular risk across the SLD continuum according to specific subcategories [18,19]. In a cohort of 350,000 individuals, CVD risk showed a clear progressive trend from MASLD (subdistribution hazard ratio [SHR] 1.19) to MetALD (SHR 1.28) and ALD (SHR 1.29) compared to those without SLD [18]. However, another study reported similar aHRs for incident CVD between MASLD (1.39) and MetALD (1.28), showing no significant statistical divergence in risk between these two groups [19].

Further reinforcing the clinical significance of the new nomenclature, a meta-analysis of over 3.2 million individuals confirmed that MASLD patients carry a 33% higher risk of incident CVD (HR 1.33) than healthy controls [20]. However, a systematic review of 11 million individuals found that CVD risk (HR 0.96) and all-cause mortality (HR 1.08) did not differ significantly between MASLD and MetALD [21]. CVD-related mortality

CVD has traditionally been described as a leading cause of death in MASLD, alongside extrahepatic malignancies [22]. However, more recent analyses with stringent adjustment for shared cardiometabolic risk factors have yielded conflicting results. A meta-analysis reported an all-cause mortality rate of 12.6 per 1,000 person-years (PYs), where cardiac-specific (4.2 per 1,000 PYs) and cancer-specific mortality (2.8 per 1,000 PYs) significantly outweighed liver-specific mortality (0.92 per 1,000 PYs) [23]. The meta-analysis clarifies that MASLD significantly impacts long-term survival; compared to those without SLD, MASLD patients face a 34% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.34) and a 17% higher risk of cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.17) [20].

However, recent data suggest mortality profiles shift by SLD subtype and CMRFs. In NHANES III participants with at least one CMRF, MASLD alone did not significantly increase all-cause (aHR 1.04), cardiovascular (aHR 0.88), or cancer mortality compared to those without SLD. Conversely, MetALD showed a significant surge in all-cause (aHR 1.41) and liver-related mortality (aHR 15.04), though cardiovascular mortality remained statistically stable [12]. This indicates that alcohol consumption alongside metabolic dysfunction shifts the “prognostic center of gravity” from cardiovascular events toward hepatic and oncological complications.

A systematic review with meta-analysis of over 4.5 million participants further delineates mortality profiles within the SLD spectrum [13]. Unlike MASLD, which showed no significant association with cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.06), MetALD was significantly associated with a 17% increase in CVD-related mortality (HR 1.17). While extensive evidence links both MASLD and MetALD to increased CVD risk, their comparative impact on cardiovascular mortality remains a subject of ongoing debate.

Heart failure and atrial fibrillation

Cumulative epidemiological data suggest MASLD predisposes individuals to diverse cardiac pathologies beyond coronary artery disease, including structural alterations like left ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, valvular calcification, and arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation (AF) [24,25]. Imaging studies have identified higher carotid intima-media thickness, increased coronary artery calcium scores, and early left ventricular diastolic dysfunction in patients with MASLD compared with controls [24,26].

A meta-analysis of 11 million individuals found MASLD associated with a 1.5-fold higher long-term risk of heart failure (HF [HR 1.50]), a finding consistent even after adjusting for hypertension, diabetes, and other traditional CMRFs [25]. Recent longitudinal data from over 26,000 SLD patients further validate MASLD’s systemic impact on HF risk [27]. MASLD was associated with a 2.59-fold higher risk of newonset HF, showing a dose-dependent escalation with each additional CMRF (SHR 1.12 per factor). Notably, 76% of incident cases were HF with preserved ejection fraction. In the landmark biopsy-confirmed cohort (n=10,422), MASLD conferred a 1.75-fold increased HF risk, which escalated with liver disease severity, suggesting that liver-specific pathology independently contributes to HF pathogenesis [16]. In a massive UK Biobank study of over 410,000 individuals, noninvasive fibrosis markers (FIB-4, NFS, and APRI) were strongly associated with a 10-year risk of HF-related hospitalization or death [28]. High-risk fibrosis scores conferred a significantly elevated risk of incident HF (HRs 1.59–1.85), an association that remained robust across the entire SLD spectrum, including MASLD, MetALD, and ALD.

The impact of MASLD on the heart’s electrical stability is increasingly recognized, with AF being the most clinically significant complication [29]. A meta-analysis of 16 cohort studies involving 19.5 million individuals confirmed that MASLD is associated with a 20% increased risk of incident AF (HR 1.20). Crucially, this risk remained significant after adjusting for age, body mass index (BMI), and traditional CMRFs, suggesting a direct pathophysiological link between liver fat and atrial remodeling [30]. In a nationwide cohort of older adults with AF, any SLD subtype (MASLD, MetALD, or ALD) conferred a significantly increased risk of incident HF compared with nonSLD, highlighting AF plus SLD as a highrisk overlap phenotype for HF in older adults [31]. Recent evidence in millions of individuals with T2DM shows that AF risk escalates progressively across the SLD spectrum [32]. While MASLD alone significantly increases new-onset AF risk (aHR 1.10), the hazard shows a stepwise rise from MetALD (aHR 1.26) to ALD with metabolic dysfunction (aHR 1.48). The shared mechanisms underlying this association include atrial remodeling driven by systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and autonomic dysfunction [33,34]. Whether AF risk stratification should be integrated into SLD management algorithms remains an area of active investigation.

In summary, evidence on the CVD risk gradient across MASLD, MetALD, and ALD is mixed. Some cohorts suggest a modest stepwise increase with alcohol exposure, whereas others observe no significant divergence in MACE between subtypes. The overall pattern is best interpreted as a broadly comparable cardiovascular burden across the SLD spectrum rather than a distinct gradient.

CVD risk in ALD

Epidemiology of cardiovascular risk in ALD

While ALD has traditionally focused on hepatic complications, contemporary data identify CVD as a leading nonhepatic cause of morbidity and mortality. This vulnerability is corroborated by biopsy-proven data from a large Swedish cohort (n=3,488), where the CVD incidence in ALD was nearly double that of controls (35.6 vs. 19.0 per 1,000 PYs).35 Notably, patients faced a two-fold increased short-term CVD risk within the first year of diagnosis (SHR 2.29). This risk remained consistently elevated across all histological subgroups, suggesting that a pro-atherogenic and pro-arrhythmic environment is established early in alcohol-related injury, regardless of hepatic severity.

While comparative CVD data for ALD versus MASLD/MetALD remain limited, Korean nationwide cohort evidence suggests a clear risk continuity: MASLD (SHR 1.19), MetALD (SHR 1.28), and peaking in ALD (SHR 1.29) [18]. Using restricted cubic splines to model dose-response relationships, researchers found that alcohol intake among MASLD/MetALD patients follows a J-shaped curve for incident CVD and coronary heart disease. This suggests a complex interplay where cardiovascular risk escalates sharply once alcohol consumption exceeds specific thresholds.

In contrast, a large-scale retrospective cohort study of over 340,000 US veterans found that MACE were remarkably similar across all three SLD subtypes.11 The risk of MACE did not significantly escalate with increased alcohol consumption, suggesting that the cardiovascular burden is primarily driven by shared metabolic dysfunction rather than alcohol-specific pathways. Similarly, a multicenter prospective study of 2,551 eligible individuals with biopsy-proven SLD showed no significant difference in CVD risks among the subtypes [36].

Evidence regarding CVD-related mortality in ALD remains limited and inconsistent. A Finnish register-based cohort study of inpatients with severe alcohol-related liver injury reported a strikingly high standardized mortality ratio of 6.13 for CVD-related death compared to the general population [37]. Although this reflects the clinical severity of a hospitalized cohort, cardiovascular death remained one of the highest risks even after accounting for competing mortality.

Alcohol consumption and CVD risk

While alcohol consumption is a recognized CVD risk factor, dose-response studies are constrained by significant biases, inherent confounding factors, and the ethical impossibility of conducting long-term randomized controlled trials [3,38].

The clinical implications of mild-to-moderate alcohol consumption remain debated. While traditional observational cohorts often describe a J-shaped curve suggesting cardio-protection at low levels, these findings are frequently confounded by “sick quitter” bias [39-41]. To address this, a Mendelian randomization study of 512,000 adults in the China Biobank used genetic variants (ALDH2 and ADH1B variants) to determine causality [42]. The analysis revealed that the apparent protective effect for stroke disappeared; instead, stroke risk increased log-linearly with alcohol intake, with no protective threshold. Notably, genotype-predicted intake showed no significant association with myocardial infarction, suggesting alcohol’s impact varies by cardiovascular outcome.

In the Swedish biopsy cohort, ALD was confirmed as an independent risk factor for CVD [35]. Notably, this risk persisted even after adjusting for traditional metabolic factors like diabetes and obesity, highlighting that alcohol consumption drives cardiovascular pathology through biological pathways independent of shared metabolic traits.

Pathophysiological drivers of cardiovascular events in SLD

Metabolic determinants of cardiovascular pathogenesis in SLD

Cardiovascular complications across the SLD continuum stem from systemic metabolic derangement [43]. MASLD drives atherosclerosis, diastolic dysfunction, and valvular calcification through insulin resistance (IR), lipotoxicity, and chronic inflammation. Ectopic fat in the epicardium and pancreas serves as a local source of proinflammatory mediators, potentiating intramyocardial inflammation and atherogenesis (Fig. 4) [44].

In IR, failed suppression of adipose lipolysis causes a massive free fatty acid flux to the liver, which, alongside augmented de novo lipogenesis, promotes a pro-atherogenic state [45]. Systemically, IR promotes endothelial dysfunction and vascular resistance [46]. MASH-related chronic inflammation (elevated TNF-α and IL-6) activates NF-κβ and JAK/STAT pathways, triggering CRP elevation and monocyte activation [47-49]. This directly links hepatic steatosis to atherosclerosis, as evidenced by the Systemic Inflammation Response Index’s strong association with CVD events and mortality [43].

MASLD exacerbates the disruption of vascular homeostasis by reducing nitric oxide bioavailability—a process compounded by elevated ADMA and endothelin-1 levels—thereby promoting vasoconstriction and arterial stiffness [50,51]. Concurrently, it induces a hypercoagulable state through increased expression of Factor VIII, vWF, and PAI-1 [52]. Furthermore, atherogenic dyslipidemia, characterized by small dense low-density lipoprotein and impaired high-density lipoprotein functionality, accelerates plaque development, while adipokine dysregulation—specifically hypoadiponectinemia and hyperleptinemia—further drives systemic atherosclerosis [53-55].

Finally, genetic susceptibility and the gut-liver-heart axis dictate individual risk. While PNPLA3 promotes liver disease and dyslipidemia, the TM6SF2 E167K variant paradoxically increases fibrosis risk while potentially protecting against CVD [56,57]. Gut dysbiosis increases intestinal permeability and bacterial translocation; a key mediator, trimethylamine N-oxide, impairs nitric oxide production and modulates adhesion molecules, linking gut health to atherosclerosis and vascular injury [58-60].

Mechanistic pathways in alcohol-associated cardiovascular injury

While acetaldehyde is a potent driver of oxidative stress and injury, it does not fully explain damage in organs with limited oxidative capacity, such as the heart. This discrepancy suggests alternative mechanisms in alcohol-induced cardiovascular effects [61]. Specifically, a minor fraction of alcohol undergoes non-oxidative metabolism to form fatty acid ethyl esters. These metabolites are implicated in direct myocardial damage, serving as a critical link between ethanol intake and structural cardiac dysfunction [62].

Experimental studies categorize alcohol-induced cardiovascular pathogenesis into three stages. Initially, alcohol clearance disrupts the oxidative balance, triggers inflammation, and alters cellular metabolism [63,64]. These effects propagate through secondary pathways involving neural modulation, hormonal shifts, and signaling mediators [61]. Ultimately, these processes lead to end-organ consequences, including heightened thrombogenicity, endothelial dysfunction, and profound structural and functional remodeling [65]. The clinical severity of these outcomes is governed by the quantity and duration of intake, beverage type, genetic susceptibility, and homeostatic resilience [66].

The dual drivers of cardiovascular risk in the SLD spectrum

In the SLD spectrum, cardiovascular burden arises from a synergistic “multi-hit” environment where metabolic dysfunction and alcohol consumption converge [67]. Hepatic steatosis acts as the primary initiator of atherosclerosis; regardless of the specific subtype or alcohol intake, its presence remains a robust indicator of increased CVD risk. While metabolic factors provide the foundation for atherosclerosis, the transition to fatal cardiovascular events and hepatic deterioration is markedly accelerated by the advanced systemic inflammation and oxidative stress specific to alcohol-related injury [68]. Thus, MASLD represents the fundamental cardiometabolic foundation, while MetALD and ALD introduce a “second hit” that shifts the prognostic gravity toward higher all-cause and liver-related mortality, with a less consistent effect on CVD-specific mortality [69]. This interplay is complex and not strictly linear, as risk levels vary across different large-scale cohorts [12,18]. Patients within the ALD and MetALD categories frequently harbor traditional metabolic provocateurs—such as heavy smoking, obesity, and T2DM—which synergize with chronic alcohol consumption to accelerate vascular aging [70]. Rather than acting in isolation, ethanol-induced inflammation amplifies the oxidative stress and IR established by metabolic derangements, leading to more aggressive coronary and peripheral arterial disease [71].

Cardiovascular management strategies across the SLD spectrum

Cardiovascular-liver-metabolic health: recommendations by the American Heart Association

The American Heart Association (AHA) “Cardiovascular-Liver-Metabolic Health” statement mandates screening all individuals with MASLD for CVD, regardless of traditional risk factors [72]. This process requires annual screening for T2DM, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and obesity. Clinicians should use standard atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) prediction tools, formally recognizing MASLD as a “risk-enhancing factor” that warrants higher clinical vigilance and more aggressive management.

The guidelines adopt a nuanced approach toward advanced imaging. While coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring and coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) offer high sensitivity, they are not recommended for routine use based solely on a MASLD diagnosis. Instead, CAC scoring may be used as a supplemental tool to refine treatment decisions, while CCTA is reserved for patients with significant hepatic steatosis or symptoms suggestive of underlying coronary artery disease [73].

CVD screening and diagnostic framework in MASLD

Since cardiovascular complications are the primary determinants of clinical outcomes, current guidelines mandate CVD screening for all individuals with MASLD, regardless of traditional risk factors [2,74]. Traditional models, such as the Framingham risk score, often underestimate risk in these populations by failing to account for disease-specific factors such as IR and obesity [75]. To ensure precision, clinicians should utilize standardized tools like the Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events (PREVENT) calculator, which incorporates CKD and metabolic comorbidities to assess both 10- and 30-year risks of ASCVD and HF [76].

Furthermore, MASLD has not been integrated into pooled cohort equations due to a lack of phenotyping in original cohorts and the absence of inexpensive, validated biomarkers. Significant challenges persist in integrating noninvasive tests (NITs) into ASCVD risk scores, as current NITs: (1) exhibit physiologic variability and sampling errors; (2) were often developed for other conditions like hepatitis C; and (3) fail to adequately differentiate MASH stages or longitudinal changes in disease activity [77]. Future research must evaluate the net reclassification improvement gained by incorporating MASLD into cardiovascular risk assessment tools.

Beyond vascular health, MASLD accelerates cardiac structural abnormalities, requiring clinicians to maintain high suspicion in symptomatic patients. Echocardiography is vital in this context, enabling the assessment of early diastolic relaxation velocity, filling pressures, global longitudinal strain, and the accumulation of epicardial adipose tissue [78].

Hepatology–cardiology multidisciplinary care in SLD

Despite guideline endorsement of integrated cardiometabolic–hepatic assessment, real-world hepatology–cardiology collaboration remains underdeveloped. A structured multidisciplinary care model, supported by several practical strategies, is needed. First, bidirectional screening pathways should be embedded in electronic health records: automated prompts to calculate PREVENT or ASCVD scores and to order lipid, HbA1c, and renal panels when MASLD is diagnosed, and reciprocally, prompts to apply noninvasive fibrosis scores (e.g., FIB-4) when patients present with established T2DM or obesity [72]. Second, integrated cardiohepatology clinics can bring hepatologists, cardiologists, endocrinologists, and dietitians into a shared care pathway for high-risk patients—particularly those with advanced fibrosis or multiple CMRFs [24]. Third, shared referral thresholds and decision aids—such as a FIB-4 ≥1.3 threshold triggering hepatology referral from cardiology clinics, and an elevated ASCVD risk score triggering cardiology referral from hepatology clinics—provide low-friction entry points for interdisciplinary coordination [79]. Finally, unified therapeutic stewardship is increasingly feasible: glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors provide concurrent hepatic, cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic benefits, making them natural candidates for co-prescribed, co-monitored regimens across specialties [80]. Building these structural and cultural bridges is essential if the CKM paradigm is to translate into measurable outcome improvements at the population level.

Current methodological gaps in assessing cardiovascular risk across the SLD spectrum

Despite the growing link between SLD and CVD, methodological limitations hinder interpretation. Association strength often depends on diagnostic modality; many studies rely on NITs, such as the fatty liver index, which include metabolic parameters that may confound the independent effect of steatosis [18].

Quantifying alcohol intake remains difficult due to measurement errors and varying international definitions of a “standard drink” (8–20 g). These discrepancies make direct comparisons across studies controversial (Table 2) [81,82]. Furthermore, self-reported intake is prone to recall and social desirability biases, often leading to underreporting, while a lack of longitudinal data obscures the impact of changing drinking patterns over time.

Even Mendelian randomization faces challenges, since the findings are complicated by population-specific effects and linear assumptions that may fail to capture the J-shaped curves seen in observational studies [83]. Additionally, most genetic variants are linked to problematic drinking rather than moderate intake, and their relatively weak influence necessitates much larger sample sizes for robust analysis.

Future perspectives

Future studies must move beyond indirect indices, prioritizing advanced imaging- or biopsy-proven cohorts to isolate the impact of hepatic steatosis from systemic metabolic factors. Given significant sex and ethnic disparities, personalized risk models incorporating genetic, environmental, and demographic data are essential for accurate cardiovascular prognostication.

Incorporating objective biomarkers (e.g., phosphatidylethanol [PEth]) alongside self-reports will mitigate recall bias and underreporting [84]. Furthermore, longitudinal tracking is required to understand the “velocity” of alcohol consumption and how changing drinking patterns influence long-term CVD outcomes, addressing a critical gap in generalizability. Since alcohol directly drives metabolic dysfunction, clinical monitoring must be holistic. A personalized medicine approach should treat alcohol and metabolic syndrome as synergistic risk factors. This requires proactive management of metabolic derangements alongside traditional monitoring for hepatic complications, ensuring interventions are as dynamic as the disease itself.

HEPATIC OUTCOMES IN MASLD, METALD, AND ALD

SLD epidemiology and definitions of hepatic outcomes

Recent evidence highlights MASLD as a multisystem disease with both cardiovascular and hepatic implications [85]. Although MetALD comprises a relatively small proportion of the SLD population [5], it is increasingly recognized as a high-risk subgroup [11]. ALD remains the second leading cause of liver-related mortality worldwide [86,87], with a substantial increase in prevalence over the past two decades and a more pronounced rise among females between 2019 and 2021 [88]. Moreover, a recent UK Biobank and NHANES analysis involving 464,556 and 13,526 adults, respectively, provided comparative prognostic data across MASLD, MetALD, and ALD for cirrhosis, liver cancer, and mortality, reinforcing the stepwise risk gradient observed in Asian cohorts [89].

Although definitions of hepatic outcomes vary across studies, they generally include liver-related events (LREs) or major adverse liver outcomes, comprising hepatic decompensation (ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, or jaundice), incident hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), liver transplantation, and liver-related mortality. Since the development of these hepatic outcomes typically requires long-term follow-up, advanced fibrosis is frequently used as a surrogate outcome. The epidemiology of hepatic outcomes may vary by geographic region and ethnicity.

Hepatic outcomes in MASLD, MetALD, and ALD

Evidence from large observational cohorts

Studies demonstrate that the risk of hepatic outcomes gradually increases in the MASLD, MetALD, and ALD continuum (Table 3, Fig. 5) [90-93]. In an observational study involving 369,094 individuals, the risk of HCC increased from MASLD to MetALD and ALD, with aSHRs of 1.96, 2.23, and 2.34, respectively, compared with individuals without SLD [90]. In a study involving a large VHA cohort, MetALD (aHR 1.56) and ALD (aHR 2.33) were associated with significantly higher risks of adverse hepatic outcomes and liver-related death compared with MASLD [11]. The presence of advanced fibrosis emerged as a determinant of the risk, conferring more than a 10-fold increase in adverse hepatic outcomes across all SLD subtypes (Fig. 6) [11].

Evidence from liver histology-based studies

A prospective, biopsy-based cohort study from Denmark involving 321 SLD patients and 125 individuals without SLD found that the risks of hepatic decompensation and overall mortality were higher in SLD compared to those without SLD [94]. During the median follow-up of 70 months, the risk of decompensation increased stepwise from MASLD to ALD, with MASLD having a 4.73-fold higher risk, MetALD having a 7.69-fold higher risk, and ALD having a 10.2-fold higher risk compared with those without SLD.

A large multicenter prospective cohort study from Korea involving the biopsy results of 2,551 individuals included in the final analysis has shown that compared to those without SLD, MetALD and ALD carry 6.1-fold and 10.9-fold increased risk of LREs, whereas that in MASLD was rather comparable [36]. In MASLD, however, those with MASH and those with at-risk MASH were associated with a 6.7-fold and 11.7-fold increased risk, respectively. In the SLD population, the risk of LREs increased as the fibrosis stages increased from F2. The study also found that MASLD, MetALD, and ALD have higher lobular inflammation, ballooning, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease activity scores, and fibrosis stages compared with non-SLD. Advanced AIbased quantitative fibrosis analysis using second harmonic generation/two-photon excitation fluorescence microscopy revealed a stepwise increase in quantitative fibrosis values across NASH-CRN fibrosis stages in MASLD, MetALD, and ALD, providing objective and automated quantification of collagen deposition at a resolution that exceeds conventional histological assessment. Significant differences in periportal collagen were observed between MetALD and ALD at F1, between MASLD and ALD at F4, and between MASLD and MetALD at F4. Zone 2 also showed notable differences, with significant distinctions between MetALD and ALD at F1 and between MASLD and ALD at F4. These biopsy-based findings highlight that even at similar fibrosis stages, the fibrogenic patterns may differ qualitatively among SLD subtypes, which could have implications for predicting treatment responses.

Evidence from meta-analyses and changes in SLD subtypes

A recent meta-analysis involving 24 cohort studies with a total of 11,575,558 individuals found that MetALD is linked to a 1.62-fold increased risk of LREs and a 1.33-fold increased risk of HCC compared to MASLD. This suggests that MetALD poses a greater risk for liver-related issues than MASLD [21].

SLD subtypes are not static but dynamic, and transitions among them can occur over time. A prospective cohort study involving 1,042 participants demonstrated that 38% of individuals transitioned across SLD categories over approximately two years [95]. In parallel, recent longitudinal studies have shown that dynamic changes in fibrosis markers, particularly FIB-4, are closely associated with liver stiffness progression and adverse clinical outcomes, including mortality and LREs [96]. These dynamic transitions highlight the inadequacy of static (i.e., single-timepoint) SLD classification and argue for longitudinal monitoring that accounts for changing alcohol intake and metabolic status.

Hepatic determinants of hepatic outcomes

MASH represents a progressive phenotype of MASLD associated with an increased risk of advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis, and HCC, and it comprises about 15% to 40% of MASLD [97]. In a meta-analysis of 54 studies involving 26,738 patients with histologically confirmed MASLD, about 30% of patients with MASH developed advanced fibrosis within 8 to 10 years [98]. More advanced stages of liver fibrosis are the strongest risk factor for progression to cirrhosis and hepatic decompensation. Histology-based cohort data showed that MASH was associated with elevated LRE risk, with an adjusted SHR (aSHR) of 6.7 and at-risk MASH with an aSHR of 11.7 [36]. They also demonstrated that LRE risk increases stepwise with fibrosis severity, underscoring fibrotic burden as a key determinant of hepatic outcomes. Notably, even at similar fibrosis stages, MetALD and ALD may exhibit greater collagen deposition than MASLD, suggesting distinct fibrogenic patterns across SLD subtypes. Even confined to patients with advanced fibrosis, ALD appears to be associated with a 2.88-fold higher risk of mortality and a 1.86-fold higher risk of LREs than MASLD [36].

Histological features of MASLD and ALD often overlap, while some are distinctive, such as the presence of specific inflammatory patterns or fibrosis stages that can help differentiate between the two conditions [99]. Applying the same diagnostic criteria of NASH-CRN used for MASH in MASLD to MetALD may result in the identification of metabolic and alcohol-related steatohepatitis (MetASH). Although MetASH is expected to represent the phenotype where metabolic dysfunction and alcohol exposure synergistically contribute to disease progression, biopsy-based data on MetALD remain limited, and the consensus on standardized terminology has not yet been fully established.

Severe alcoholic steatohepatitis (ASH) represents a high-risk inflammatory phenotype within the ALD spectrum, characterized by aggressive injury, rapid fibrogenesis, and substantial mortality [87]. A recent epidemiologic study has demonstrated that ASH mortality increased from 0.47 per 100,000 in 1999 to 0.76 in 2022, with females exhibiting steeper increases than males [100]. Natural history data highlight the progressive trajectory from steatosis to steatohepatitis, fibrosis, and cirrhosis [87].

Metabolic determinants of hepatic outcomes

MASLD and T2DM are closely intertwined, with approximately a quarter of MASLD patients having T2DM [7]. Unfavorable liver outcomes, such as hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, were observed in those with poor glycemic control [101]. MASLD patients have a significantly higher risk of hepatic decompensation with an aSHR of 2.15 and HCC with an aSHR of 5.34 when T2DM is present [102]. In MASLD, the presence of impaired fasting glucose has been associated with a two- to four-fold higher risk of hepatic fibrosis and LREs compared to individuals without this condition [103]. The attenuation of SLD progression propelled by managing the metabolic determinants can be greater in MASLD compared to MetALD and ALD, where liver injury by drinking plays a more prominent role. In ALD, both obesity and T2DM accelerate disease progression and are associated with increased overall and liver-related mortality [87,104].

As for the perspective of BMI, a multinational cohort study found that the risk of all-cause mortality in normal-weight MASLD was higher than that of overweight/obese MASLD [105]. A meta-analysis found that while the overall LRE risks appear broadly comparable between lean and non-lean MASLD, lean MASLD is associated with an increased risk of HCC with a pooled HR of 1.49 [106]. BMI-stratified analyses showed that in the lean population, MetALD (aSHR 9.8) and ALD (aSHR 13.3) were associated with an elevated risk of LREs, while ALD in the non-lean population demonstrated a higher risk of LREs (aSHR 12.7) compared to non–SLD [36]. These findings have profound implications for clinical practice, indicating that the absence of obesity should not provide false reassurance in patients with SLD, as genetic and alcohol-related factors may predominate hepatic risk in lean individuals.

Sex and lifestyle behaviors may further modify SLD progression. In sex-stratified analyses, the risk of LREs in male ALD was an aSHR of 10.5 against male non-SLD, while that of female ALD was an aSHR of 44.2 against female non-SLD [36]. Increase in ASH mortality over 2 decades was higher in females compared to males, with an average annual percentage change of 3.94% against 1.56% [100]. This dramatic sex disparity underscores the heightened vulnerability of females to alcohol-related injury and has important implications for sex-specific screening and risk stratification strategies. Lifestyle factors such as physical activity further modify hepatic risk. Analysis of NHANES 2017–2020 demonstrated that leisure-time physical activity decreased the prevalence of at-risk advanced fibrosis in MASLD with an odds ratio of 0.56 and in MetALD/ALD with that of 0.55 compared to work-related physical activity [107].

Impact of alcohol on hepatic outcomes

Growing evidence suggests that hepatic outcomes in MASLD patients vary by alcohol consumption, with most studies reporting that even low to moderate amounts of alcohol have negative effects [69,108]. In a population-based cohort study of 2,227 individuals in Spain, alcohol intake in MASLD was associated with disease severity, and the findings were further validated in a U.S. cohort of 4,427 participants [109]. Alcohol consumption influences hepatic outcomes both independently of and synergistically with cardiometabolic factors. Alcohol intake has also been identified as a major contributor to hypertension, which is one of the CMRFs [110]. Susceptibility to alcohol-related liver injury is not uniform across the population and varies substantially according to body weight. East Asian populations exhibit a unique distribution of ADH1B and ALDH2 polymorphisms, which impairs efficient acetaldehyde clearance. This genetic predisposition acts as a critical risk modifier, exacerbating hepatic oxidative stress and inflammation even with modest alcohol exposure [111]. The effects of alcohol differ depending on the amount consumed, drinking patterns, and beverage type [87].

Alcohol consumption is mainly assessed through self-reports like the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test-Concise (AUDIT-C), but accurate classification remains challenging, leading to occasional misclassification. In a nationwide cohort, alcohol use disorder was identified in 1.1% of MASLD and 4.7% of MetALD [112]. Alcohol biomarkers, including ethyl glucuronide, ethyl sulphate, fatty acid ethyl esters, and PEth, can serve as alternatives, providing objective measures [99,113]. PEth demonstrated good accuracy with an AUC of 0.83 for identifying heavy alcohol consumption in middle-aged and older adults [114]. In a large study from Denmark involving about 3,000 individuals, approximately 40% of excessive alcohol consumption cases were underestimated based on self-reported surveys when subsequently validated using PEth [115]. A large-scale cohort study involving 46,406 individuals from Stockholm County (HERALD cohort) demonstrated that PEth can effectively reclassify SLD subtypes, with 20% of presumed MASLD patients showing PEth levels of 0.05–0.30 μmol/L indicative of MetALD and 14% exceeding 0.30 μmol/L consistent with ALD [116]. Importantly, patients reclassified as MetALD based on PEth exhibited significantly higher risks of major adverse liver outcomes compared to those with true MASLD (aHR 1.75; 95% CI 1.12–2.74), underscoring PEth’s dual role as a biomarker for accurate SLD subclassification and hepatic risk stratification [116].

Mechanistic pathways

Although MASLD, MetALD, and ALD share downstream mechanisms, such as hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation and inflammation, their upstream drivers differ significantly. In MASLD, metabolic factors play a central role in disease progression, with the interplay of environmental, genetic, and epigenetic factors contributing to lipid dysregulation and hepatic fat accumulation [117]. IR, adipose tissue dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation further promote disease progression [118]. Persistent hepatocellular injury—mediated by oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction—activates HSCs and promotes fibrogenesis. Genetic susceptibility, including variants like CYP7A1 and GIPR, can influence liver fat accumulation [119]. Alterations in the gut–liver axis also enhance inflammatory and fibrogenic signaling [120]. As a newly defined subtype of SLD, there is limited longitudinal and mechanistic data on MetALD. Its pathogenesis likely mirrors the synergistic effects of pathways related to MASLD and ALD [69,99], which include altered lipid metabolism, immune activation, HSC activation, hepatocellular injury, gut dysbiosis, bile acid dysregulation, and ductular reaction [69]. In ALD, excessive alcohol consumption is the primary driver of disease progression. Alcohol metabolism via CYP2E1 produces acetaldehyde and reactive oxygen species, leading to oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, glutathione depletion, and endoplasmic reticulum stress, which ultimately results in hepatocyte injury and inflammatory cytokine release [87].

NIT strategies and hepatic outcomes

The most commonly used NITs for fibrosis assessment in MASLD include the FIB-4 index and vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE). Both liver stiffness measurement (LSM) and LSM trajectory provide robust prognostic information in MASLD, predicting liver-related outcomes and HCC risk, with prognostic performance comparable to that of liver histology [121-123]. The Agile 3+ score demonstrated superior performance for identifying advanced fibrosis compared with FIB-4 or LSM alone [124,125]. Single or serial measurements of the Agile 3+ score accurately predicted long-term LREs [126]. A study involving MASLD patients with discordant FIB-4 and LSM scores found that LSM may more accurately reflect the risk of LREs.127 The KASL two-step approach applying FIB-4 followed by VCTE demonstrated a predictive performance for LREs comparable to Agile 3+/4 and FibroScan-AST strategies [128]. In biopsy-proven MASLD, the ANTICIPATE-NASH model, incorporating liver stiffness, platelet count, and BMI, outperformed histologic staging for predicting LREs with a C-statistic of 0.93 against 0.67 [129]. For HCC surveillance, sequential FIB-4 followed by LSM has been proposed [130], and the MASLD-HCC score further improved risk stratification [131]. Agile 3+/4 showed diagnostic accuracy comparable to LSM in MASLD and ALD, and improved the identification of advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis with fewer indeterminate results [132].

Recent studies provide important insights into NIT-based risk stratification of hepatic outcomes in MetALD and ALD [133]. In a cross-sectional study of 7,918 participants undergoing MRE, FIB-4 demonstrated comparable diagnostic performance in MetALD and MASLD with an AUC reaching 0.85 [134]. In ALD, FIB-4, FibroTest, and VCTE showed good predictive performance for LREs (C-statistic >0.80), with substantial event rates among those with VCTE 10–15 kPa [135].

Precision medicine

The therapeutic landscape targeting MASLD, MetALD, and ALD is undergoing rapid change. Resmetirom was found to improve steatohepatitis and fibrosis in the phase 3 MAESTRO-NASH trial and became the first FDA-approved pharmacologic therapy for MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis [136,137]. The multi-organ benefits of GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors—spanning hepatic, cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic endpoints—position these agents as cornerstone therapies for the CKM syndrome that characterizes many SLD patients, regardless of subtype. Semaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, demonstrated significant reduction of hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis in MASH in the phase 3 ESSENCE trial [138], while FGF21 analogues (efruxifermin, pegozafermin, and efimosfermin) have shown improvements in MASH and fibrosis regression in phase 2 studies [139-142]. SGLT2 inhibitors have been reported to reduce hepatic steatosis and inflammation and to be associated with lower risks of LREs, fibrosis regression, and fewer episodes of hepatic decompensation [143-145]. Whether indications for GLP-1 receptor agonists or SGLT2 inhibitors in MASLD could be expanded to MetALD or ALD remains to be investigated. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, single-center phase 2 study in ALD patients demonstrated that rifaximin-α 550 mg twice daily for 18 months lowered the rate of fibrosis progression [146]. Ultimately, the goal of precision medicine in SLD is to move beyond subtype-based classification toward individualized risk profiles that integrate fibrotic burden, metabolic dysfunction severity, alcohol exposure patterns, and genetic susceptibility to guide therapeutic prioritization.

Research gaps and future directions

Several important gaps remain. First, although underreporting of alcohol use in self-reports can be mitigated by the use of PEth, standardized thresholds have not yet been firmly established, and further validation across diverse populations is warranted. Second, the pathological framework and standardized definition of MetASH require further refinement to improve consistency in diagnosis, risk stratification, and clinical research. Finally, incorporating the potential for dynamic transitions across SLD subtypes and how improvements or worsening of risk factors over time affect outcomes independently and synergistically would provide a more comprehensive and clinically relevant risk assessment. Cost-effectiveness analyses may also help inform clinical decision-making [147].

CONCLUSION

The MASLD, MetALD, and ALD continuum provides a clinically relevant framework for understanding the heterogeneity of SLD. Across this spectrum, metabolic dysfunction and alcohol exposure affect cardiovascular and hepatic outcomes independently or synergistically. Cardiovascular outcomes remain a principal determinant of morbidity and mortality in MASLD, supported by robust histologic and population-based evidence demonstrating independent associations with coronary artery disease, HF, and AF. In contrast, hepatic outcomes become more prominent in MetALD and ALD, as increasing alcohol exposure reshapes the prognostic hierarchy toward fibrosis progression, hepatic decompensation, and HCC. By systematically examining cardiovascular and hepatic outcomes across the MASLD, MetALD, and ALD continuum, we elucidated how the risk factors shape distinct outcome patterns, highlighting the need of SLD subtype-tailored risk stratification and treatments. Future strategies may benefit from integrating cardiometabolic and hepatic risk factors across the SLD continuum, moving beyond isolated etiologic classifications and optimizing prevention and surveillance.
