Evidence of feline chaphamaparvovirus in dogs: molecular detection, genetic recombination, and tissue localization
Authors: Chutchai Piewbang, Aisyah Nikmatuz Zahro, Panida Poonsin, Tin Van Nguyen, Panitnan Punyathi, Tanit Kasantikul, Somporn Techangamsuwan
Published: 2025

Background

Feline chaphamaparvovirus (FChPV), corresponding to the ICTV-recognized species Chaphamaparvovirus carnivoran2 (also referred to in the literature as Carnivore chaphamaparvovirus 2 or fechavirus), is a single-stranded DNA virus within the family Parvoviridae, subfamily Hamaparvovirinae and genus Chaphamaparvovirus [1]. The viral genome encodes the non-structural protein NS1 and the structural proteins NP1 and VP1, relying entirely on host cellular machinery for replication [2, 3]. FChPV, as a member of Parvoviridae family—similar to other parvoviruses—may primarily target rapidly dividing cells such as intestinal epithelium and immune cells [4]. Since its initial identification in domestic cats through high-throughput sequencing [1], FChPV has been reported in association with gastrointestinal and respiratory conditions [2, 5, 6], although its exact role in disease burden remains incompletely defined.

The identification of FChPV in cats has revealed a geographically widespread distribution, with varying prevalence across regions. Studies in Asia report detection rates ranging from 6% to 20% [2, 6, 8], whereas lower prevalence rates have been documented in Europe and the Americas [1, 5]. FChPV has also been detected in cats with clinical signs such as diarrhea, sneezing, and vomiting [1, 5]; however, the virus is also identified in asymptomatic cats [5, 6]. The virus has been detected in fecal, respiratory, and blood samples, indicating its potential for systemic spread. However, many cats harbor the virus without evident symptoms, complicating efforts to establish its role as a primary pathogen. Co-infections with other feline viruses, such as feline parvovirus virus (FPV) and feline calicivirus, further obscure its pathogenic significance [2, 7].

A significant milestone in understanding the host range of FChPV was the first confirmed detection of the virus in dogs in China in 2023, representing the earliest evidence of natural infection in a non-feline species [8]. Although the reported prevalence was low, this finding raised the important questions regarding cross-species transmission and whether canine detection reflects true infection, transient passage, or environmental contamination. Genomic analyses have shown high similarity between canine and feline strains, supporting the hypothesis of feline-to-canine spillover —potentially facilitated by shared environments or close interspecies contact.

Cross-species transmission is well documented among parvoviruses; for example, canine parvovirus (Protoparvovirus carnivoran1; CPV) originated from FPV following mutations enhancing its ability to bind to canine transferrin receptors [9–11]. This genetic adaptation enabled CPV to become a major pathogen in dogs, with occasional spillover into wild carnivores. Similarly, FPV has been shown to infect dogs under certain conditions [12–14], although its replication and pathogenicity in non-feline hosts are limited. These precedents highlight the potential for parvoviruses to expand their host range through genetic plasticity, a feature that FChPV may also possess. Despite the emerging recognition of FChPV in dogs in China in 2023 [8], no subsequent studies have examined its prevalence, genetic diversity, and tissue localization in canine populations.

In this study, we extend the current knowledge by reporting FChPV infection in dogs from Southeast Asia, characterizing the genomic diversity of detected strains, documenting a putative recombination event involving feline-origin parental strains, and demonstrating viral localization in canine tissues using in situ hybridization (ISH). These findings help contextualize the role of FChPV within the broader landscape of parvovirus host-range biology and provide baseline data for future investigations into the epidemiology and pathogenic potential of this emerging virus.

Materials and methods

Ethics and consent to participate

All experimental protocols were approved by the Chulalongkorn University Animal Care and Use Committee (No. 2431048). All methods were performed in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals. Additionally, all procedures were reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. All samples used in this study were obtained from (i) live dogs presented for clinical diagnosis (nasal and rectal swabs collected as part of routine clinical sample collection) or (ii) animals submitted for routine diagnostic necropsy after death. No animals were euthanized, sacrificed, or killed for the purposes of this research. For live animals, nasal and rectal swabs were collected as non-invasive clinical diagnostic specimens and did not require anesthesia; animals were conscious during swab collection. For tissue samples, these were collected from carcasses submitted for post-mortem examination (necropsy) after the animal had died of natural disease or had been submitted dead by the owner/clinician. Informed consent was obtained from the owners prior to sample collection or carcass submission.

Sample collection

A total of 392 samples were collected from dogs exhibiting clinical signs of respiratory or intestinal diseases in Bangkok, Thailand, between January 2021 and December 2022. These samples comprised 183 respiratory-derived samples (147 nasal swabs and 36 lung tissues) and 209 intestinal-derived samples (169 rectal swabs and 40 intestinal tissues). Samples were collected using a convenience sampling approach from dogs submitted for diagnostic investigation. Nasal and rectal swabs were collected using sterile cotton-rayon swabs. For nasal swabs, the swab was gently inserted into the nostril and rotated 2–3 times before being immersed in 0.5% phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Rectal swabs were similarly collected by inserting and rotating the swab within the rectum and immediately placing it in PBS. These swab samples were stored at − 80 °C.

In addition, tissue samples were obtained during routine necropsy cases submitted for postmortem investigation at the Department of Pathology, Faculty of Veterinary Science, Chulalongkorn University. Lung and intestinal tissues were collected as fresh-frozen samples. Additional tissues, including vital organs such as the lung, liver, spleen, kidney, heart, brain, and lymph nodes, were routinely collected as the formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens. Tissues exhibiting visible lesions, such as tumors or inflammatory changes, were also sampled. These samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for at least 48 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for histological examination and ISH.

Nucleic acid extraction

Fresh tissue samples were minced into small fragments using sterile instruments and homogenized in 0.5% PBS at a 1:10 weight-to-volume ratio. Homogenization was performed using an automatic homogenizer (Bead Ruptor 12, OMNI International, GA, USA) to ensure consistent processing of the tissues. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Swab samples, including nasal and rectal swabs, were centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were carefully separated and transferred to sterile microcentrifuge tubes. Viral DNA was extracted from the homogenized tissue supernatants and swab-derived supernatants using the QIAamp MinElute Virus Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and quantity of extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific™, USA) by measuring absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm to determine the A260/280 ratio. The DNA extracts were stored at − 80 °C in aliquots to avoid degradation from repeated freeze-thaw cycles. All steps were performed under sterile conditions to minimize the risk of contamination.

Detection of FChPVs by polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

A 310-bp amplicon of the partial non-structural protein 1 (NS1) gene of FChPV was detected using nested PCR, following protocols established in a previous study [1]. For the first round of PCR, a total of 100 ng of extracted DNA was mixed with PCR reagents, including 2× GoTaq® Green Master Mix, which contained 3 mM magnesium chloride, 400 µM deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs), and Taq polymerase enzyme. Forward (F1: GGTGCGACGACGGAAGATAT) and reverse (R1: CAACACCACCATCTCCTGCT) primers were added at a concentration of 10 pmol each. The cycling conditions consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 58 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. Two microliters of the first-round PCR product were used as a template for nested PCR. The nested reaction followed the same protocol and cycling conditions as the first round, except for the use of a nested forward primer (F2: GCTGCAGTTCAGGTAGCTCA) and the same reverse primer (R1). Negative control (non-template control) was included in each PCR run to ensure specificity and reliability. PCR products were visualized using automated capillary electrophoresis with settings previously described in the literature [15]. Positive target amplicons were subsequently subjected to genetic sequencing using a next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based platform (U2Bio, South Korea). The obtained genetic sequences were then blasted to the reference genomes available in the NCBI GenBank database. Samples that were confirmed positive FChPV detection were additionally investigated the presence of other common either respiratory disease complex viruses or enteric disease complex viruses using routine pipeline PCRs. The PCRs included canine morbillivirus (CDV) [16], canine parainfluenza virus [17], canine herpesvirus [18], canine adenovirus [19], canine influenza virus, canine respiratory coronavirus [20], canine parvovirus (CPV) [21], canine circovirus [22], and canine enteric coronavirus [23], using previously described protocols.

Whole-genome sequencing

Positive PCR samples were subjected to whole-genome sequencing to characterize the genetic diversity of FChPV. Reference sequences of FChPV were retrieved from the GenBank NCBI database (accessed in March 2022) and aligned using the MAFFT algorithm to design primers specific to conserved and variable regions of the viral genome. Multiple PCR assays were performed using these primers (Supplementary Table S1) to amplify overlapping fragments of the viral genome. Each PCR reaction was optimized for primer specificity and annealing temperatures to maximize amplification efficiency. Amplicons generated from the PCR reactions were sequenced as described in the detection section. The resulting sequences were initially compared against reference genomes in the GenBank database using the BLAST tool to confirm their identity. The sequences were then mapped and assembled into complete genomes using BioEdit software (v7.0.5.3) to generate a consensus sequence for each sample. The assembled sequences were then submitted to the GenBank NCBI database. Pairwise nucleotide distances were calculated to determine genetic divergence between the sequenced FChPV strains identified in this study and previously described FChPV sequences. Deduced amino acid sequences were analyzed and compared to identify conserved and variable regions among strains.

Phylogenetic and genetic recombination analysis

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using complete coding sequences (CDS) and the coding regions of the NS1 and VP1 genes, which were analyzed separately. Reference sequences of FChPVs from feline hosts and previously reported canine-origin strains were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database (accessed March 2022) and included in the dataset. Sequence alignments were performed using the MAFFT algorithm to ensure accurate alignment of the sequences. The aligned datasets were subjected to model testing using the best-fit model analysis embedded in BioEdit (v7.0.5.3), which determined the Hasegawa-Kishino-Yano (HKY + G + I) model as the most appropriate based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the maximum likelihood (ML) method in MEGA X software under the HKY + G + I model. Bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates was performed to evaluate the statistical reliability of the branches. Separate trees were generated for the NS1 and VP1 coding regions, as well as for the complete genome sequences. The constructed trees were analyzed for evolutionary relationships among FChPVs strains detected in this study and reference sequences from NCBI. Genetic recombination of FChPV was analyzed in silico using multiple recombination detection programs available in the Recombination Detection Program (RDP) version 4.0. The analysis included algorithms such as RDP, Bootscan, GENECONV, MaxChi, Chimaera, SiScan, and 3Seq, which are embedded in the software. FChPV sequences that tested positive for recombination in at least four programs with statistically significant P-values (P < 0.05) were considered potential recombinant strains [24]. To further investigate potential recombination events, similarity plots and bootscan analyses were performed using the SimPlot software package (v3.5.1). The analyses were conducted with a 200-bp sliding window, moving in 20-bp steps, and the Kimura (2-parameter) substitution model, as described in previous studies [24, 25]. Potential recombination breakpoints identified through SimPlot analyses were further validated by constructing phylogenetic trees for the regions flanking the recombination sites. Maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were generated using MEGA X software, following the protocols outlined above. Bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates was employed to assess the statistical robustness of the branching patterns and to confirm the recombination events.

Histology and in situ hybridization (ISH)

Tissue samples from dogs identified as positive for FChPVs through initial PCR screening were analyzed histologically using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, followed by ISH to detect viral DNA. FFPE tissues were cut into 4 μm thick sections and mounted onto positively charged slides. A DNA probe targeting a 310-bp region of the NS1 gene was synthesized using the PCR DIG Probe Synthesis Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The probe corresponds to nucleotides 2186–2517 of the FChPV complete genome strain IDEXX-1 (GenBank accession no. NC_076644). In-silico BLASTn analyses confirmed that this probe sequence shows no cross-reactivity with canine chaphamaparvovirus (CaChPV), FPV, CPV, or other parvoviruses. For probe construction, digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled dNTPs replaced standard dNTPs to facilitate chromogenic detection. The primers and thermal cycling conditions used for probe synthesis were the same as those applied in the PCR screening. The DNA probe’s integrity and size were validated through 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, comparing it against control templates.

FFPE sections were prepared for ISH by deparaffinizing them in xylene, rehydrating through graded ethanol, and performing antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Following this, the sections were treated with 10 ng/mL proteinase K at 37 °C for 10 min and post-fixed in 0.4% formaldehyde at 4 °C for 5 min. After rinsing thoroughly with DNase/RNase-free water, the sections were prehybridized in a solution containing 50% formamide in 4× saline-sodium citrate (SSC) buffer at 37 °C for at least 15 min. Hybridization was performed by incubating the sections in a buffer containing 5× Denhardt’s solution, 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 100 µg/mL salmon sperm DNA, 5× SSC, and 10 ng/mL of the DIG-labeled DNA probe. This incubation was carried out at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 55 °C overnight in a humidified chamber. After hybridization, the sections underwent stringency washes using a graded series of SSC solutions to remove unbound probes. To block nonspecific binding, the sections were immersed in a 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution at 37 °C for 1 h. Detection of hybridization signals was achieved by incubating the sections with anti-DIG-AP Fab fragments (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) diluted 1:200 in BSA at room temperature for 30 min. Signals were visualized using the PermaRed/AP chromogenic substrate (Diagnostic BioSystems, Pleasanton, California). The slides were counterstained with methyl green, dehydrated, and mounted with coverslips for microscopic evaluation. To confirm specificity, control experiments included FChPVs-PCR-positive slides incubated with an unrelated canine bufavirus (Protoparvovirus carnivoran3) probe [26], PCR-negative FFPE tissues incubated with the FChPVs probe, and a CPV-positive sample [27] treated with the FChPVs probe. DNase-treated FFPE sections incubated with the hybridization buffer and FChPVs probe served as an additional control. Archived FChPV-positive feline tissue was not available; therefore, ISH validation was performed using PCR-confirmed positive canine tissues and parallel negative controls.

Results

FChPV detection in dogs

Out of 392 dogs tested, FChPV DNA was detected in four individuals, corresponding to an overall prevalence of 1.0% (4/392; 95% CI: 0.4–2.6%). Stratified by clinical syndrome, the prevalence was 1.4% (3/209; 95% CI: 0.5–4.1%) among dogs presenting with enteric disease and 0.5% (1/183; 95% CI: 0.1–3.0%) among those with respiratory disease. Among the three FChPV-positive dogs exhibited clinical signs of enteric illness, two positive cases were identified via rectal swabs (case nos. 1 and 2), and one (case no. 3) through intestinal tissue obtained postmortem from a deceased dog. The fourth FChPV-positive case (case no. 4) was detected via a nasal swab from a dog presenting with respiratory clinical signs. The ages of the positive dogs ranged from 2 months to 14 years, with a median age of 2 months. Of the four positive dogs, three were female and one was male. Vaccination records revealed that three of the positive dogs had incomplete vaccination histories for core canine vaccines. All positive dogs were housed as individual and were half free-roaming.

One FChPV-positive case (case no. 3) involved a deceased dog previously undergoing treatment for a cardiac mass identified during clinical evaluation. Multiple skin masses were also observed and tentatively diagnosed as cutaneous hemangioma based on cytology. Several days prior to its death, the dog acutely developed vomiting and diarrhea of unknown etiology. Despite receiving empirical antibiotic therapy and supportive care for the diarrhea, the dog died and was subsequently submitted for postmortem examination. Routine diagnostic virologic PCR panels revealed that two FChPV-positive fecal samples were also positive for CPV detection, while the other samples tested negative for common enteric and respiratory viral pathogens. Genetic analysis of FChPV in dogs

Initial investigation of FChPV DNA in dog samples was performed using conventional PCR targeting the NS1 gene, which yielded positive amplicons in 4 cases. Genetic sequencing of the PCR products confirmed the presence of FChPV DNA in these samples. To obtain the complete coding genome sequences of FChPV detected in dogs, multiple PCR reactions were conducted using primers specific to various regions of the FChPV genome. The resulting amplicons were sequenced and assembled into complete coding sequences. Successfully, the complete coding genomes of FChPV from all 4 dogs were amplified and assembled, with genome lengths of ~ 3539–4015 bp. The FChPV strains identified in dogs in this study were tentatively designated as FChPV strain CP001/Dog/2021, CP002/Dog/2020, CP003/Dog/2020, and CP004/Dog/2020 for case numbers 1–4, respectively. These sequences were then submitted to the GenBank database under accession numbers PV175395–PV175398.

Overall, the FChPV genomes comprised three key genes: NS1 gene (nucleotides 520–2496), NP1 gene (nucleotides 1871–2434), and VP1 gene (nucleotides 2435–3961), encoding 658 amino acids (AA), 187 AA, and 508 AA, respectively. Pairwise nucleotide identity analysis revealed that the FChPV strains identified in this study shared 98.4–99.6% similarity to each other and exhibited high genetic similarity to the previously identified FChPV strain VRI 849 from cats in the USA, with a nucleotide identity range of 98.3–99.5%. Details regarding nucleotide similarity of the detected FChPV in dogs were summarized in Supplementary information S1.

Phylogenetic analysis based on complete coding sequences demonstrated that the FChPV strains detected in dogs in Thailand were genetically diverse and grouped into two main clades (clades A and B) (Fig. 1A). Clade A included three FChPV strains (CP001, CP002, and CP004) identified in this study, which clustered with known feline-origin FChPV strains from China. In contrast, the other strain, CP003/Dog/2020, was more distinct and clustered with FChPV strains previously identified in the USA in clade B. Interestingly, while the phylogenetic analysis based on the NS1 (Fig. 1B) and VP1 genes (Fig. 1D) generally aligned with the topology of the complete coding sequences, strain CP004/Dog/2020 re-clustered with CP003/Dog/2020 and the USA-derived FChPV strains when the NP1 gene was analyzed separately (Fig. 1C). This discrepancy prompted further investigation into the possibility of genetic recombination among the FChPV strains detected in dogs.

These analyses identified a recombination event spanning nucleotides 1–1100 of the NS1 gene (Fig. 2C). Phylogenetic analysis of the flanking sequences surrounding the breakpoint corroborated the recombination event, as the strains flanking the breakpoint exhibited distinct clustering patterns consistent with the proposed major and minor parent strains (Fig. 2D).

Histological examination and in situ hybridization (ISH)

Histological examination of FFPE tissues from a PCR-positive case no. 3 revealed lesions across various organs. The most important findings in this case are most consistent with cardiac hemangiosarcoma and may represent part of a right atrial hemangiosarcoma. Specifically, the splenic parenchyma exhibited extensive erythrocyte pooling, forming a hemorrhagic nodule that was fairly well-delineated but indistinctly demarcated (Fig. 3A). Another splenic section revealed a nodular expansion of lymphocytes, plasma cells, hemosiderin-laden macrophages, and extramedullary hematopoietic precursors (Fig. 3B). In the liver, multifocal and coalescing blood-filled spaces replaced hepatocytes, which were either absent or appeared thin and irregularly arranged in cords. No overt lining endothelial cells were observed, though the hepatic parenchyma in unaffected areas was diffusely and markedly congested. Within the duodenum and jejunum, variably dense infiltrates of plasma cells and fewer lymphocytes were scattered in the intestinal mucosal lamina propria (Fig. 4A). The superficial mucosa was variably congested, and the lumens of rare intestinal crypts were dilated and filled with eosinophilic cellular and basophilic karyorrhectic debris, though no overt villous fusion or blunting was evident.

In haired skin specimens, a similar neoplastic process was identified in collected samples, where the dermis was expanded by a fairly well-delineated proliferation of neoplastic endothelial cells forming variably sized, blood-filled vascular channels separated by moderately thick fibro-collagenous stroma (Fig. 4C). The neoplastic cells appeared flattened to spindloid, with scant cytoplasm, indistinct borders, oval hyperchromatic nuclei, and inconspicuous nucleoli, showing minimal to mild anisokaryosis. Multifocal infiltrates of lymphocytes and hemosiderin-laden macrophages were present, along with mild to moderate hyperplasia of the epidermis and laminated orthokeratotic hyperkeratosis in one section. Lastly, the lung and kidney were diffusely congested, with no significant changes observed in the brain (Fig. 4E) or other sections.

Chromogenic in situ hybridization (ISH) demonstrated specific localization of FChPV DNA within the nuclei of inflammatory cells in various tissues examined. Intense and diffuse hybridization signals were prominently observed in the spleen (Fig. 3C), where they were marked and extensively distributed throughout the tissue, clearly localizing to the nuclei of inflammatory cells (Fig. 3D). Similarly, multifocal nuclear localization was detected in the infiltrated inflammatory cells in the laminar propria of villi and infiltrated inflammatory cells localized at the intestinal submucosa (Fig. 4B). Additionally, scattered to multifocal hybridization signals were detected in infiltrative inflammatory cells within the cutaneous hemangioma mass, and were absent from the neoplastic endothelial cells (Fig. 4D). The scattered positive hybridization signals were also detected in the mesenteric lymph nodes. In the brain, rare, scattered hybridization signals were observed in both the nuclei of glial cells and the cytoplasm of neurons located within the gray matter of the brainstem (Fig. 4F). Rare but identifiable hybridization signals were also observed in Kupffer cells within the liver, though these signals were less frequent and sparsely distributed compared to other tissues. No hybridization signals were observed in any other examined organs. FChPV-infected tissues incubated with unrelated probes did not exhibit any hybridization signals. Furthermore, tissues confirmed as PCR-negative for FChPV DNA incubation with the FChPV probe displayed no hybridization signals. Likewise, DNase-treated sections showed no detectable signals. (Supplementary Figure S1).

Discussion

Feline chaphamaparvovirus (FChPV), has primarily been identified in cats, with multiple studies describing its genomic diversity and association with gastrointestinal and respiratory disease [2, 5, 6, 8, 28]. Although the first confirmed canine case from China in 2023 has been reported [8], the significance of FChPV in non-feline species remain poorly understood. In line with this emerging observation, our study adds to this emerging knowledge by documenting FChPV infection in dogs from Southeast Asia and confirming viral presence through both PCR and ISH, with ISH demonstrating localization of viral DNA within inflammatory cells of canine tissues. Importantly, the identification of potential recombination events in a canine-derived strain further expands current understanding of FChPV tissue tropism, host range, and evolutionary dynamics, underscoring the need for continued surveillance across diverse host species.

Our findings suggest that FChPV may behave similarly in dogs and cats, particularly in enteric disease [2, 5–8]. Among the four FChPV-positive dogs, three presented with gastrointestinal signs, similar to the clinical presentations described in both feline cases and the earliest canine report. In the necropsied dogs (Case 3, FChPV DNA was found in inflammatory cells across multiple organs, including the spleen, and intestines. This supports true infection rather than contamination and is consistent with the immune cell tropism observed in other parvoviruses [29–32]. Although causality cannot be confirmed, the detection in intestinal immune cells in the absence of other enteric pathogens warrants further investigation. Previous studies have identified FChPV in both symptomatic and asymptomatic feline hosts, with a higher prevalence observed in cats experiencing gastrointestinal symptoms [2, 8]. However, its detection in healthy cats [7] suggests that FChPV may either function as an opportunistic pathogen or persist as a commensal virus in certain hosts. The role of FChPV as a primary pathogen remains unclear, as co-infections with other viruses, complicate its clinical significance [6]. Nonetheless, consistent with prior feline and canine studies [5, 7, 8, 28], we observed co-infection with CPV in two cases, underscoring the difficulty of attributing clinical signs solely to FChPV.

Our findings also add novel insights beyond earlier canine reports [8]. An investigation into FChPV infection was conducted in a necropsied dog that had suffered from cardiac hemangiosarcoma and developed diarrhea shortly before death. The cardiac lesions were considered incidental findings, whereas the intestinal lesions may indicate an underlying condition. Supporting this observation, FChPV in ISH revealed positive signals in the infiltrative immune cells in intestines of the infected dog, in which no concurrent enteric viruses were detected. This finding may suggest a potential association between FChPV and intestinal lesions, as a previous study reported the presence of FChPV in the feces of dogs with diarrhea but not in healthy dogs [8]. FChPV has also been detected in cats with diarrhea, although viral localization in feline tissues has not yet been confirmed [2]. This further supports the need to investigate its role in gastrointestinal disease. Notably, we identified low-level ISH signals in the brainstem without accompanying lesions, a finding that should be interpreted cautiously. The low-level ISH signal may reflect incidental or post-mortem translocation of viral DNA rather than true neurotropism. It is also possible that this represents a low-level or latent presence of the virus, or a previously unrecognized tissue distribution. At present, the biological significance of this observation remains unclear, and additional studies—including cases with corresponding CNS lesions and experimental confirmation—are needed before any conclusions can be drawn regarding potential neuroinvasive behavior. Similarly, FChPV DNA was identified in inflammatory cells within a cutaneous hemangioma mass, but not in the neoplastic endothelial cells. This indicates that the virus was associated with infiltrating immune cells rather than the tumor itself. There is currently no evidence to suggest that FChPV has oncogenic potential, and its presence in tumor-associated tissue is likely incidental. Nonetheless, such findings underscore the need for more detailed investigations into FChPV’s cellular tropism and possible reservoirs in canine hosts.

Although none of the FChPV-positive dogs had direct contact with cats, the environmental stability of parvoviruses suggests that indirect exposure—via shared environments, fomites, or surfaces—is likely common [9, 28]. Given these conditions, the more important questions lie in whether FChPV can replicate efficiently, persist, or cause disease in dogs. However, no environmental sampling or analysis was conducted in this study, limiting the ability to confirm these transmission pathways. Further epidemiological studies are needed to identify specific routes of transmission and assess the risk of FChPV spread in canine populations.

The identification of a recombination event in one canine FChPV strain (CP003/Dog/2020) involving feline-origin parental viruses illustrates the genetic flexibility of this virus. While recombination is expected in parvoviruses, its presence in a canine-associated strain may represent an early step in host adaptation. This supports the possibility of FChPV evolving the capacity to cross species barriers [10, 11, 33]. While genetic recombination in FChPV has been reported in a previous study [2], this study also identified recombination in a FChPV canine strain, with a feline-origin strain serving as its parents. Its detection in a canine-associated strain highlights potential evolutionary mechanisms that may facilitate adaptation to non-feline hosts. However, the absence of environmental sampling or analysis limits further speculation on these evolutionary dynamics. While the detection of FChPV in dogs raises questions about its potential host range, it is important to distinguish between incidental detection in a non-traditional host and true cross-species transmission. At present, our study provides molecular evidence of FChPV in dogs but does not demonstrate transmission events or confirm pathobiological significance, which remain to be clarified in future research.

Despite these significant findings, this study has certain limitations. The small number of FChPV-positive dogs restricts the generalizability of the results, and the presence of co-infections—particularly the two cases also positive for CPV—complicates interpretation of clinical signs. Although FChPV DNA was detected in intestinal and immune cells and confirmed by ISH localization, these data indicate the presence of viral nucleic acids rather than definitive causation of disease. Tissue localization supports true infection but does not, on its own, establish FChPV as the primary pathogenic agent. The absence of experimental infection models further limits the ability to determine replication competence or pathogenic potential in dogs. Larger, systematically collected datasets, coupled with comprehensive pathogen screening and controlled infection studies, will be necessary to clarify the clinical significance of FChPV in canine hosts.

Conclusions

Taken together, our findings support that FChPV can infect dogs at a low prevalence, with PCR and ISH providing evidence of viral presence and tissue localization. The identification of a recombinant canine-associated strain further suggests that FChPV possesses genetic flexibility, a feature shared with other parvoviruses that have undergone host-range shifts. In particular, the evolution of CPV from FPV through mutations affecting transferrin receptor binding highlights the capacity of parvoviruses to adapt to new hosts. Whether similar mechanisms may enable FChPV to expand its host range remains uncertain, and our findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating functional adaptation or established pathogenicity in dogs. While the clinical significance of FChPV in dogs remains unclear, this study identifies several priority areas for future research, including viral transmission dynamics, tissue tropism, and the potential determinants of host susceptibility. Serological studies to detect FChPV-specific antibodies would help clarify past exposure. Larger, systematically collected datasets and experimental infection models will be essential to determine whether FChPV can truly establish infection, replicate efficiently, or contribute to disease in canine hosts.
