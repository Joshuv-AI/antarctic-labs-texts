Langevin equation with potential of mean force: The case of anchored bath
Alex V. Plyukhin

Abstract
The potential of mean force (PMF) is an effective average potential acting on an open system, renormalized due to the interaction with the surrounding thermal bath. The PMF is defined for an equilibrium ensemble, and generally it is not clear how to use it when the system is out of equilibrium and described by a (generalized) Langevin equation. We study a model where the system is a single particle (so there are no complications related to internal forces) and a non-trivial PMF is due to the presence of on-site (anchor) potentials applied to the bath particles. We found that the PMF does not merely replace the external potential, but also makes the dissipation kernel and statistical properties of noise dependent on the system’s position. That dependence is determined by the internal bath and system-bath interactions and is a priori unknown. Therefore, in the general case the Langevin equation with the PMF is not closed and thus inoperable. However, for systems with linear forces the aforementioned dependence on the system’s position may be canceled. As an example, we consider a model where the bath is formed by the Klein-Gordon chain, i. e. a harmonic chain with on-site harmonic potentials. In that case, the generalized Langevin equation has the standard form with an external potential replaced by a quadratic PMF.



I
Introduction

When addressing the dynamics of open systems, one often assumes that the environment, or the thermal bath, is passive
in the sense that it does not produce any
systematic
force
on the system kept at rest.
A passive bath is still responsible for the velocity-dependent dissipative
force on the moving system, but the only force it exerts on the
fixed
system
is Langevin noise that vanishes after an appropriate averaging.
The passivity of a thermal bath does not necessarily imply that the system-bath interaction is weak, but often holds due to symmetry.
The required symmetry is not a property of the bath alone, but of the bath and system combined. For example, in conceptual models of Brownian motion the system is a single point-like particle, the bath is a homogeneous fluid, and a potential describing the system-bath
interaction is pairwise. For that setting, the average bath-induce force on a fixed system vanishes because contributions from different bath molecules are canceled out. As a result, the bath is passive for an arbitrary strength of the system-bath interaction.

Now, in the above example, let us replace the point-like particle by a dumbbell-like system consisting of two particles, say
1
and
2
, and consider the force the bath acts on particle
1
. That force generally does not vanish after averaging because particle
1
interacts with the bath whose symmetry is broken by the presence of particle
2
.
Generalizing, for any system consisting of more than one particle, the assumption of the passive bath does not generally hold because one part of the system breaks the symmetry of the bath interacting with other parts of the system.
Under certain conditions, the effect may be negligible, but for many systems studied in chemical physics
it is often essential
Kirkwood
;
DO
;
Roux
.

In this paper, we consider a model
in which molecules of the bath experience, in addition to pairwise potentials from other molecules, also individual on-site potentials. That potentials, tending to localize bath molecules, break translational invariance of the bath. As a result, the bath is non-passive
even when the system is
a single particle.

When the bath is passive,
the equilibrium distribution for the system
has the canonical form

\displaystyle\rho_{s}=\frac{1}{Z_{s}}\,e^{-\beta H_{s}},\quad Z_{s}=\Tr_{s}\left(e^{-\beta H_{s}}\right),

(1)

where
H_{s}
is the bare Hamiltonian of the system and
\Tr_{s}(\cdots)
denotes integration over the sets of the system’s coordinates
X
and momenta
P
(we assume that the system is classical). For a non-passive bath
\rho_{s}
involves the re-normalized Hamiltonian of the system
H_{s}^{*}
, called the Hamiltonian of mean force (HMF)

Kirkwood
;
DO
;
Roux
;
Gelin
;
Lutz
;
Talkner

\displaystyle\rho_{s}=\frac{1}{Z_{s}^{*}}\,e^{-\beta H_{s}^{*}},\quad Z_{s}^{*}=\Tr_{s}\left(e^{-\beta H_{s}^{*}}\right).

(2)

In classical models,
the renormalization affects only the system’s potential energy
V(X)
, adding to it a bath-induced correction
\Delta V
.
Then the HMF can be written as

\displaystyle H_{s}^{*}=K(P)+V_{*}(X),

(3)

where
K(P)
is the system’s kinetic energy and
V_{*}(X)
is the renormalized potential

\displaystyle V_{*}(X)=V(X)+\Delta V(X),

(4)

called the potential of mean force (PMF).
The bath-induced potential
\Delta V

in general depends on temperature and quantifies the
deviation of the equilibrium distribution from the canonical form (
1
).

As a note on nomenclature, the term PMF is sometimes reserved to
\Delta V
, or even to the total renormalized Hamiltonian of the system
H_{s}^{*}
(thus identifying the PMF and the HMF). However, these definitions do not appear to be very common. Our definition of the PMF as a total effective potential acting on the system, Eq. (
4
), is the same as in Ref.
Talkner
. A drawback of such terminology is that it does not offer a generally-accepted term for the key quantity
\Delta V
. We shall refer to
\Delta V
as the bath-induced potential and call the bath non-passive if it generates a non-zero (and non-constant)
\Delta V
. We avoid the term “active bath” which may suggest irrelevant connotations with the realm of active matter, which is beyond the scope of the paper.

As discussed in the next section, the PMF is defined as a quantity that characterizes the system in thermal equilibrium. Clearly, the PMF must also affect the non-equilibrium dynamics described by a Langevin equation, but it is often not obvious in what way. The simplest assumption is that the Langevin equation and the corresponding fluctuation-dissipation relation retain their standard form and the only required modification is the replacement of the bare system potential by the PMF.
That approach, broadly adopted in empirical studies, received theoretical endorsement in
Refs.
Lange
;
Kinjo
, where the standard generalized Langevin equation with the PMF was derived
from the underlying dynamics using the projection operator technique.
However, those derivations were later
criticized in Refs.
Schilling
;
Schilling_review
, with the conclusion that for systems with a PMF the standard Langevin equation
can be derived neither exactly nor
using any well-controlled approximation.
On the other hand, in Refs.
Vroy
;
Vroy2
it was argued that a Langevin equation actually can be recovered from the first principles, but only at the expense of the noise and the dissipation kernel being position-dependent and with a modified fluctuation-dissipation relation. That conclusion seems to corroborate the earlier results for a system of several Brownian particles
DO
. Using a different approach,
a generalized Langevin equation with an
additional dissipation force, independent of the system’s velocity, was suggested in
Netz
.

In all studies cited above, the derivation of the Langevin equation with a PMF
was addressed for many-particle systems with internal forces, which significantly complicates the analysis.
In this paper we consider a model where the system is a single Brownian particle with no internal degrees of freedom. In that case, as we mentioned above, a translationally-invariant bath is passive
due to symmetry, and thus the PMF is redundant.
In our model, the translational invariance of the bath is broken by on-site (or anchor) potentials applied to the particles of the bath. Such a bath is not passive; it exerts a systematic average force on a fixed system. Our goal is to determine how this force affects the Langevin equation for the system.

Our interest in such a model is threefold.
First, the model involves a non-trivial PMF but has no complications related to internal forces in the system.
Second, the Langevin dynamics with an anchored bath is relevant for a variety of physical systems, see
Savin
;
Flach
;
Prem
;
Dhar
;
Flach2
and references therein. For example, in complex molecular structures like proteins, the lighter and heavier units may be interpreted as the bath degrees of freedom and anchor (substrate) centers, respectively
Kneller
;
Xie
;
Milster
.
Third, the spectrum of an anchored bath has both upper and lower bounds, which may lead to peculiar ergodic properties of the Langevin dynamics
Plyukhin_NEO
.

We shall consider the model first for the general and then for a more specific settings. For the general setting, we make no assumptions about specific forms of the potentials involved.
For that general case, we are unable to derive a generalized Langevin equation in the standard form. The equation we derived instead involves noise and the dissipation kernel, which depend on the system’s position in a way which is a priori unknown. As a result, the equation is not closed and practically unmanageable.

On the other hand,
the situation is simplified significantly
for a specific setting when the bath is formed by
the harmonic Klein-Gordon chain. Due to the linearity of that model, the aforementioned dependence of the dissipation kernel on the system’s position is canceled, and
the generalized Langevin equation has the standard form with a quadratic PMF.

The layout of the paper is as follows. In Section 2 we review the PMF.
Although the concept is quite mature, there is some ambiguity and variations in the literature, so we feel a brief review might be useful.
In Section 3 we outline the model with
a single-particle system and anchored bath. In Sections 4 and 5 we attempt to derive the Langevin equation for the general setting using the Mazur-Oppenheim projection operator technique. In Section 6 we
describe a more specific setting with the Klein-Gordon lattice as a bath and evaluate the corresponding PMF. In Section 7 we show that for the Klein-Gordon model the Langevin equation derived in Section 5 is reduced to the standard form. In Appendix we derived that equation by direct integration of bath variables.

II
Review of PMF

Consider a classical system, with bare Hamiltonian
H_{s}(X,P)
, interacting with a thermal bath with bare Hamiltonian
H_{b}(x,p)
via potential
\Phi(X,x)
. Here
(X,P)
and
(x,p)

denote the sets of coordinates and momenta for the system and the bath, respectively.
The total Hamiltonian
H=H_{s}+H_{b}+\Phi

is convenient to compose as

\displaystyle H=H_{s}+H_{0}(X),\quad H_{0}(X)=H_{b}+\Phi,

(5)

where
H_{0}(X)
is the Hamiltonian of the bath interacting with the system with fixed position vector
X
.

Suppose the overall system (the system and the bath combined) is in thermal equilibrium with canonical distribution

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{Z}\,e^{-\beta H}=\frac{1}{Z}\,e^{-\beta(H_{s}+H_{0})},\quad Z=\Tr_{sb}\left(e^{-\beta H}\right),

(6)

where
\Tr_{sb}(\cdots)
denotes integration over phase-space variables of
both the system and the bath. We are interested in the reduced (marginal) distribution
\rho_{s}
for the system only,

\displaystyle\rho_{s}=\Tr_{b}(\rho)=\frac{Z_{0}(X)}{Z}\,e^{-\beta H_{s}}.

(7)

Here
\Tr_{b}(\cdots)
denotes integration over phase-space variables of
the bath, and
Z_{0}(X)
is the partition function for the canonical distribution
\rho_{0}
corresponding to
H_{0}
,

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{Z_{0}(X)}\,e^{-\beta H_{0}},\quad Z_{0}(X)=\Tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta H_{0}}\right).

(8)

Distribution
\rho_{0}
is the equilibrium distribution for the bath connected to the system fixed at position
X
, so that
partition function
Z_{0}
in general depends on
X
.
Since that dependence is a priori unknown, expression (
7
) for
\rho_{s}

is not directly useful or evocative. For some special settings the total partition function can be, exactly or approximately, factorized,
Z=Z_{s}\,Z_{0}
. In that case,

Z_{0}
is canceled out in Eq. (
7
), and
\rho_{s}
takes the Boltzmann-Gibbs form (
1
),

\displaystyle\rho_{s}=\frac{1}{Z_{s}}\,e^{-\beta H_{s}},\quad Z_{s}=\Tr_{s}\left(e^{-\beta H_{s}}\right).

(9)

However, in the general case the aforementioned factorization of
Z
does not hold, and expression (
7
) for
\rho_{s}
is not reduced to
the form (
9
). Yet it is possible to present the general
expression (
7
) in a more suggestive form as follows.

Integrating over the bath variables, the total partition function can be written as

\displaystyle Z=\Tr_{sb}[e^{-\beta(H_{s}+H_{0})}]=\Tr_{s}[e^{-\beta H_{s}}\,Z_{0}(X)],

(10)

then Eq. (
7
)
takes the form

\displaystyle\rho_{s}=\frac{Z_{0}(X)}{\Tr_{s}[e^{-\beta H_{s}}\,Z_{0}(X)]}\,e^{-\beta H_{s}}.{}

(11)

It is convenient to parameterize the dependence of
Z_{0}
on
X
as

\displaystyle Z_{0}(X)=c\,e^{-\beta\Delta V(X)}.

(12)

While
Z_{0}
has no direct physical meaning, the new function

\displaystyle\Delta V(X)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\,\ln\left[\frac{Z_{0}(X)}{c}\right],

(13)

can be
interpreted as an effective bath-induced potential acting on the system in equilibrium.
Indeed, substituting (
12
) into (
11
) gives

\displaystyle\rho_{s}=\frac{1}{Z_{s}^{*}}\,e^{-\beta\,H_{s}^{*}},\quad Z_{s}^{*}=\Tr_{s}[e^{-\beta H_{s}^{*}}]

(14)

where

\displaystyle H_{s}^{*}=H_{s}+\Delta V(X)

(15)

is called the Hamiltonian of mean force (HMF). By writing the bare Hamiltonian of the system as the sum of kinetic and potential energies

H_{s}=K(P)+V(X)
,
the HMF can be presented as

\displaystyle H_{s}^{*}=K(P)+V_{*}(X),\quad V_{*}(X)=V(X)+\Delta V(X),

(16)

The renormalized potential
V_{*}(X)
is commonly referred to as the potential of mean force (PMF).
It is the sum of the potential energy
V
of the bare system (which describes both the internal and external forces for the system disconnected from the bath)
and the bath-induced potential
\Delta V
.

One may find slightly different definitions of the PMF in the literature. In particular, some authors identify the PMF with the bath-induced potential
\Delta V
and do not include in the PMF the potential of the bare system
V

DO
. Our definitions of the HMF and the PMF by Eq. (
16
) coincide with those in Ref.
Talkner
. There are also differences in the literature about specific choices for parameter
c
in Eq. (
12
);
we shall discuss that below.

The interpretation of
\Delta V
as the bath-induced effective potential can be further specified considering the force
F_{\alpha}
, exerted by the bath on
\alpha
-th particle of the system
with the position vector
X_{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle F_{\alpha}=-\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial X_{\alpha}}=-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial X_{\alpha}}.

(17)

Consider the average force
\langle F_{\alpha}\rangle
,
where the average is taken over bath variables with distribution
\rho_{0}
, Eq. (
8
),

\displaystyle\langle F_{\alpha}\rangle=\Tr_{b}(\rho_{0}\,F_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{Z_{0}(X)}\,\int dx\,dp\,e^{-\beta H_{0}}\,F_{\alpha}.

(18)

Differentiating Eq. (
13
), one finds

\displaystyle\langle F_{\alpha}\rangle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{\alpha}}\,\Delta V(X).

(19)

Therefore,
\Delta V
can be interpreted as a potential of the average force exerted by the bath on the fixed system.
As was mentioned in the Introduction, for a one-particle system in a fluid
that force vanishes by symmetry, but in the general case it is not zero.

For future references, let us re-write
\langle F_{\alpha}\rangle
, using Eqs. (
13
) and (
19
),
in terms of
Z_{0}(X)
:

\displaystyle\langle F_{\alpha}\rangle=\frac{1}{\beta}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{\alpha}}\,\ln Z_{0}(X).

(20)

Note that the total average force on the
\alpha
-th particle of the system, i. e. the sum of internal, external, and bath-induced forces,

\displaystyle\langle F_{\alpha}^{tot}\rangle=-\left\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial X_{\alpha}}\right\rangle=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial X_{\alpha}}+\langle F_{\alpha}\rangle,

(21)

is given by the gradient of the PMF,

\displaystyle\langle F_{\alpha}^{tot}\rangle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{\alpha}}(V+\Delta V)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{\alpha}}\,V_{*}(X).

(22)

If the system is a single Brownian particle (as we assume in the sections to follow), the index
\alpha
in the above equations is redundant and to be dropped.

We still need to specify the parameter
c
in definition (
13
) of the bath-induced potential
\Delta V
.
In that expression,
c
determines only an additive constant (with respect to
X
) and, therefore, can be chosen arbitrarily.
A common and often convenient choice is

\displaystyle c=Z_{b},

(23)

where
Z_{b}
is the partition function for the equilibrium distribution of the
isolated (not connected to the system) bath,

\displaystyle\rho_{b}=\frac{1}{Z_{b}}\,e^{-\beta H_{b}},\quad Z_{b}=\Tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta H_{b}}\right).

(24)

In that case Eq. (
13
) gives for the bath-induced potential the following expression

\displaystyle\Delta V=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left[\frac{Z_{0}}{Z_{b}}\right]=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left[\frac{\Tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta H_{0}}\right)}{\Tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta H_{b}}\right)}\right],

(25)

and the HMF
H_{s}^{*}=H_{s}+\Delta V
may be expressed as

\displaystyle H_{s}^{*}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left[\frac{\Tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta H}\right)}{\Tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta H_{b}}\right)}\right],

(26)

where
H=H_{s}+H_{0}
is the total Hamiltonian of the combined system and the bath.

The advantage of the choice (
23
) can be seen considering the weak-coupling limit,
when

\displaystyle H_{0}=H_{b}+\Phi\to H_{b},\quad H=H_{s}+H_{0}\to H_{s}+H_{b},

(27)

and therefore

\displaystyle Z_{0}(X)\to Z_{b},\quad Z\to Z_{s}Z_{b}.

(28)

In that limit
the above equations
give

\displaystyle\Delta V\to 0,\quad V^{*}\to V,\quad H_{s}^{*}\to H_{s}.

(29)

That is a plausible result: For a negligible system-bath interaction
the bath-induced potential vanishes, and
the equilibrium statistics of the system is determined by the system’s bare Hamiltonian
H_{s}
.

Let us note that expression (
25
) for
\Delta V
often appears in the literature
in a different form. Since

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{0}}{Z_{b}}=\frac{1}{Z_{b}}\,\Tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta\,H_{b}}\,e^{-\beta\Phi}\right)=\langle e^{-\beta\Phi}\rangle_{b},

(30)

where the average is taken over bath variables with distribution
\rho_{b}
,
\langle\cdots\rangle_{b}=\Tr_{b}(\cdots\rho_{b})
, the expression (
25
)
can be also written as

\displaystyle\Delta V=-\frac{1}{\beta}\,\ln\langle e^{-\beta\Phi}\rangle_{b}.

(31)

Similarly, the HMF (
26
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle H_{s}^{*}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\,\ln\langle e^{-\beta(H_{s}+\Phi)}\rangle_{b}.

(32)

Although Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
) may be convenient in some cases
Talkner
, they will not appear in the rest of the paper.

Had we chosen the parameter
c
in a form different than (
23
), the bath-induced potential
\Delta V(X)
would not vanish in the weak-coupling limit but instead reduce to a nonzero constant,

\displaystyle\Delta V(X)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left[\frac{Z_{0}(X)}{c}\right]\to-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left[\frac{Z_{b}}{c}\right].

(33)

That might be inconvenient, yet it is not incorrect, since observable
physical quantities do not depend on
c
, provided
c
is a constant with respect to
X
and
P
.
In Ref.
Gelin
, the choice
c=1
was adopted. It was criticized in
Talkner
on the ground that for such a choice
\Delta V
does not vanish in the weak coupling limit. In our view,
the only defect of the choice
c=1
is that
the argument of the logarithm in Eq. (
33
) is not dimensionless.

In what follows, we shall consider a model for which
H_{0}
has the special form

\displaystyle H_{0}=h_{1}(x,X)+h_{2}(X),

(34)

where
h_{2}(X)
depends only on the system’s position
X
, while
h_{1}(x,X)
depends on bath variables and
X
, yet the corresponding partition function

\displaystyle Z_{1}=\Tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta h_{1}}\right)

(35)

does not depend on
X
. In that case,

\displaystyle Z_{0}(X)=Z_{1}\,e^{-\beta\,h_{2}(X)},

(36)

and it is convenient to define
c=Z_{1}
. Then, according to
(
13
), the bath-induced potential is equal to
h_{2}(X)
,

\displaystyle\Delta V(X)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left[\frac{Z_{0}(X)}{Z_{1}}\right]=h_{2}(X).

(37)

III
Anchored bath model

As discussed in the Introduction,
the absence of a PMF in simplified models of Brownian motion is to some extent accidental:
If the system of interest is a single particle with no internal degrees of freedom and
the bath is a translationally invariant fluid, then the average force from the bath on the system is zero by symmetry. Our goal here is to study a model where the system of interest is still a
structureless Brownian particle, but the translational invariance of the bath is broken, which results in a non-trivial PMF.

The bare Hamiltonian of the system is

\displaystyle H_{s}=\frac{P^{2}}{2M}+V_{ex}(X),

(38)

where
V_{ex}(X)
is the external potential unrelated to the bath.
The bath consists of a large number
N
of particles and has the bare Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{b}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m}+\sum_{i>j}^{N}U(x_{i}-x_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}U_{a}(x_{i}).

(39)

Here, the first two sums represent the kinetic and pairwise potential energies.
The key feature of the model is the presence of the third sum, which implies that each particle in the bath is subject to the additional “anchor” potential
U_{a}(x_{i})
. A particular realization of such a model, where the bath is an anchored harmonic chain,
is sketched in Fig. 1. It is intuitively clear that the anchored bath is non-passive and generates a PMF which tends to localize the system.
Physically, the model of the anchored
bath may be relevant for complex heterogeneous systems, like polymer networks and gels, characterized by a broad hierarchy of relaxation times
Milster
. In such systems, heavier (slower) structural units may be essentially immobile on a relevant time scale and act
as fixed anchoring sites for lighter and faster units.

We shall assume that the system-bath interaction energy
\Phi
is pairwise,

\displaystyle\Phi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(X-x_{i}),

(40)

and write the total Hamiltonian in the form (
5
),

\displaystyle H=H_{s}+H_{0},\quad H_{0}(X)=H_{b}+\Phi(X),

(41)

where
H_{0}(X)
is the Hamiltonian of the bath in the potential of the Brownian particle
held fixed at position
X
.

The total Liouville operator for the model is convenient to write as

\displaystyle L=L_{s}+L_{0}

(42)

where
L_{s}
and
L_{0}
correspond to Hamiltonians
H_{s}
and
H_{0}
, respectively,

\displaystyle L_{s}=\frac{P}{M}\frac{\partial}{\partial X}+(F+F_{ex})\frac{\partial}{\partial P},\quad L_{0}=\sum_{i}\left\{\frac{p_{i}}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+f_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}\right\}.

(43)

Here
F
and
F_{ex}
are the forces on the particle due to the interaction with
the bath and the external potential, respectively,

\displaystyle F=-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial X},\quad F_{ex}=-\frac{\partial V_{ex}}{\partial X},

(44)

and
f_{i}
is the force on
i
-th particle of the bath

\displaystyle f_{i}=-\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial x_{i}},

(45)

which parametrically depends on the system’s position
X
.

We adopt the usual assumption of the Brownian motion theory that masses
m
of bath particles are much smaller than the mass of the system,
m\ll M
.
In that case, the bath is expected to evolve much faster than the system and
quickly adjust to the instantaneous system’s position. Therefore, one
may expect that the bath’s dynamics is close to that described by the Liouville operator
L_{0}
, which describes the bath in the field of a fixed (or, equivalently, an infinitely heavy) system. The operator
L_{0}
has the properties

\displaystyle L_{0}H_{0}=0,\quad L_{0}\,\varphi(H_{0})=0,

(46)

where
\varphi(x)
is an arbitrary differentiable function. A related property is that
L_{0}
and

\varphi(H_{0})
commute,

\displaystyle L_{0}\,\varphi(H_{0})A=\varphi(H_{0})\,L_{0}A,

(47)

where
A
is an arbitrary dynamical variable.
These properties of
L_{0}
are generic and not affected by the presence of the anchor potential.

In the next two sections we shall use
the Mazur-Oppenheim projection operator technique
MO
;
DO
;
KO

to derive a Langevin equation with the PMF
for the model described in this section.
The derivation is convenient to present in two steps. The first step is to re-write the equation of motion for the system separating the systematic and noisy components of the total force on the system. The result is what can be called the pre-Langeving equation, see Eq. (
75
) below. That equation is exact but impractical because it involves the system’s variables in an implicit form. The second step, discussed in Section 5, is to make that dependence explicit by applying an appropriate perturbation technique.

IV
Pre-Langevin equation

The Mazur-Oppenhein technique
MO
is based on the projection operator
\mathcal{P}
which averages an arbitrary dynamical variable
A

over initial values of the bath variables

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}A=\langle A\rangle=\Tr_{b}({\rho_{0}\,A})

(48)

with distribution (
8
)

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{Z_{0}(X)}\,e^{-\beta H_{0}},\quad Z_{0}(X)=\Tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta H_{0}}\right).

(49)

Recall that
\rho_{0}
is the equilibrium distribution of the bath in the field of the system fixed at
X
. This makes the projection (
48
) particularly relevant in the present context, because

\rho_{0}
is the distribution used in the definition of the average force related to the PMF, see Eqs. (
18
)-(
22
).

The projection property
\mathcal{P}^{2}=\mathcal{P}
is obvious, and
another key property

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}L_{0}=0

(50)

follows from (
47
) with
\varphi(H_{0})=\rho_{0}
.
The complementary projection operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=1-\mathcal{P}

(51)

has the properties

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{P}=0,\quad\mathcal{Q}L_{0}=L_{0}.

(52)

The standard Mazur-Oppenheim approach
assumes that the bath is passive, i. e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}F=\langle F\rangle=0,

(53)

where
F
is the force exerted by the bath on the Brownian particle. Here we consider an extension when Eq. (
53
) does not hold.

The equation of motion for the system reads

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=F(t)+F_{ex}(t),

(54)

where
F
is the force from the bath and
F_{ex}
is the external force, see Eq. (
44
).
The time dependence of forces can be described using the Liouville propagator

\displaystyle F(t)=e^{Lt}\,F,\quad F_{ex}(t)=e^{Lt}\,F_{ex},

(55)

where
F=F(0),F_{ex}=F_{ex}(0)
. We shall also use a similar notation
A=A(0)
for
the initial values of
other dynamical variables.
Inserting the identity operator
1=\mathcal{Q}+\mathcal{P}
into expression (
55
) for
F(t)
,

\displaystyle F(t)=e^{Lt}\mathcal{P}F+e^{Lt}\mathcal{Q}F=e^{Lt}\langle F\rangle+e^{Lt}\mathcal{Q}F

(56)

brings the equation of motion (
54
) to the form

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=e^{Lt}\left[\langle F\rangle+F_{ex}\right]+e^{Lt}\mathcal{Q}F.

(57)

According to Eqs. (
19
) and (
22
),
we can express
\langle F\rangle
as the negative gradient of the the bath-induced potential
\Delta V
,

\displaystyle\langle F\rangle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\,\Delta V,

(58)

and the total force
\langle F\rangle+F_{ex}
as the negative gradient of the PMF
V_{*}(X)
,

\displaystyle\langle F\rangle+F_{ex}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\,V_{*}(X),\qquad V_{*}(X)=\Delta V(X)+V_{ex}(X).

(59)

Then the equation of motion (
57
) can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=-\nabla V_{*}[X(t)]+e^{Lt}\mathcal{Q}F,

(60)

where the first term on the right-hand side is the gradient of the PMF at time
t
,

\displaystyle\nabla V_{*}[X(t)]=e^{Lt}\frac{\partial}{\partial X}V_{*}(X)=\frac{\partial}{\partial X}V_{*}(X)\Big|_{X=X(t)}.

(61)

That term is a conservative part of the total force acting on the system.
The second term
e^{Lt}QF
can be separated into a dissipative (non-conservative) force and noise.
The usual way to facilitate such separation is to use
the operator identity

\displaystyle e^{(A+B)t}=e^{At}+\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,e^{A(t-\tau)}B\,e^{(A+B)\tau}.

(62)

With
A=L
and
B=-\mathcal{P}L
, one splits the propagator into two parts,

\displaystyle e^{Lt}=e^{\mathcal{Q}Lt}+\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,e^{L(t-\tau)}\,\mathcal{P}L\,e^{\mathcal{Q}L\tau}.

(63)

Substituting that into Eq. (
57
) and recalling that
\mathcal{P}L_{0}=0
yields

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=-\nabla V_{*}[X(t)]+F^{\dagger}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,e^{L(t-\tau)}\,\mathcal{P}L_{s}\,F^{\dagger}(\tau)

(64)

where

\displaystyle F^{\dagger}(t)=e^{\mathcal{Q}Lt}\mathcal{Q}F

(65)

is often referred to as the projected force.
Since
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{Q}=0
, the average
F^{\dagger}(t)
is zero

\displaystyle\langle F^{\dagger}(t)\rangle=\mathcal{P}F^{\dagger}(t)\sim\mathcal{PQ}=0,

(66)

which suggests interpreting
F^{\dagger}(t)
as noise. However, one has to keep in mind that
F^{\dagger}(t)
depends on the system’s variables, and that dependence still needs to be revealed.

The integral term
in Eq. (
64
) is expected to give rise to a dissipation force. Let us express

\mathcal{P}L_{s}F^{\dagger}(\tau)
in the integrand in a more explicit form,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}L_{s}F^{\dagger}(\tau)=\frac{P}{M}\,\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial X}F^{\dagger}(\tau)\right\rangle+\frac{\partial}{\partial P}\,\langle F\,F^{\dagger}(\tau)\rangle.

(67)

To evaluate the first term on the right-hand side,
let us differentiate Eq. (
66
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\left\langle F^{\dagger}(\tau)\right\rangle=-\beta\left\langle\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial X}\,F^{\dagger}(\tau)\right\rangle-\langle F^{\dagger}(\tau)\rangle\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\ln Z_{0}+\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial X}F^{\dagger}(\tau)\right\rangle=0.

(68)

Here we take into account that the distribution

\rho_{0}=Z_{0}^{-1}\exp(-\beta H_{0})
depends on
X
through both
H_{0}
and
Z_{0}
.
Replacing
\partial H_{0}/\partial X=-F
and using Eq. (
20
) this relation can be presented as

\displaystyle\beta\left\langle F\,F^{\dagger}(\tau)\right\rangle-\beta\,\langle F^{\dagger}(\tau)\rangle\langle F\rangle+\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial X}F^{\dagger}(\tau)\right\rangle=0.

(69)

From this we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial X}F^{\dagger}(\tau)\right\rangle=-\beta\left\langle E\,F^{\dagger}(\tau)\right\rangle,

(70)

where
E
is a zero-centered random variable corresponding to
F
,

\displaystyle E=\mathcal{Q}F=F-\langle F\rangle,\quad\langle E\rangle=0.

(71)

Note that
E
is the initial value of the projected force
F^{\dagger}(t)=e^{\mathcal{Q}Lt}\,\mathcal{Q}F
,

\displaystyle E=F^{\dagger}(0),

(72)

and one can present
F^{\dagger}(t)
as

\displaystyle F^{\dagger}(t)=e^{\mathcal{Q}Lt}E.

(73)

Then from Eqs. (
67
), (
70
), and (
72
)
one obtains

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}L_{s}F^{\dagger}(\tau)=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial P}-\frac{\beta\,P}{M}\right)\langle E\,F^{\dagger}(\tau)\rangle.

(74)

Substituting into (
64
) yields the equation of motion in the pre-Langevin form

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=-\nabla V_{*}[X(t)]+F^{\dagger}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,e^{L(t-\tau)}\,\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial P}-\frac{\beta\,P}{M}\right)\langle E\,F^{\dagger}(\tau)\rangle.

(75)

Compared to the case of a passive bath, this equation has two visible and intuitively expected differences. First, the external potential
V_{ex}
is replaced by the PMF
V_{*}=V_{ex}+\Delta V
. Second, the integral term of the equation involves the correlation
\langle EF^{\dagger}(\tau)\rangle

rather than

\langle FF^{\dagger}(\tau)\rangle
.
The equation is exact, but not yet operational because
F^{\dagger}(t)
depends on the system’s variables. That dependence can be made explicit
by expanding the projected force in powers of a small mass ratio parameter
\lambda
,
F^{\dagger}(t)=F_{0}(t)+\lambda\,F_{1}(t)+\cdots
.
For a passive bath, in the leading (zeroth) order in
\lambda
the projected force
F^{\dagger}(t)\approx F_{0}(t)
does not depend on the system’s variables, and Eq. (
75
) is readily reduced to the standard Langevin equation. However, as we shall see in the next section, for a non-passive bath
F^{\dagger}(t)
depends on the system’s position even in the leading approximation. That dependence is a priori unknown, which may undermine the derivation of a practically useful Langevin equation with a PMF.

V
Langevin equation

In order to bring the pre-Langevin Eq. (
75
) to a more amiable form, let us exploit a perturbation
technique with the mass ratio

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}=m/M

(76)

as a small parameter.
If the overall system is not too far from equilibrium, the momentum
P
of the Brownian particle is close to
the equilibrium value
\sqrt{M/\beta}
, and the
typical momentum of bath particles
is
\sqrt{m/\beta}
. Therefore, the scaled momentum
P_{*}=\lambda P
of the Brownian particle is on average of the same order as momenta of the bath particles.
To make the
\lambda
-dependence
more explicit, let us re-write the pre-Langevin equation (
75
) in terms of the scaled momentum
P_{*}
,

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{*}(t)=-\lambda\,\nabla V_{*}[X(t)]+\lambda\,F^{\dagger}(t)+\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,e^{L(t-\tau)}\,\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial P_{*}}-\frac{\beta\,P_{*}}{m}\right)\langle E\,F^{\dagger}(\tau)\rangle.

(77)

The Liouville operator (
42
) can be re-written as

\displaystyle L=L_{0}+\lambda L_{1}

(78)

where
L_{0}
is still given by Eq. (
43
) and

\displaystyle L_{1}=\frac{P_{*}}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial X}+(F+F_{ex})\frac{\partial}{\partial P_{*}}.

(79)

The projected force
F^{\dagger}(t)
evolves as

\displaystyle F^{\dagger}(t)=e^{\mathcal{Q}Lt}E=e^{(L_{0}+\lambda\mathcal{Q}L_{1})t}\,E,

(80)

where we have used Eq. (
52
),
\mathcal{Q}L_{0}=L_{0}
. Using
Eq. (
62
) with
A=L_{0}
and
B=\lambda\mathcal{Q}L_{1}
one finds

\displaystyle e^{(L_{0}+\lambda\,\mathcal{Q}\,L_{1})\,t}=e^{L_{0}t}+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}d\tau e^{L_{0}\,(t-\tau)}\mathcal{Q}\,L_{1}\,e^{L_{0}\tau}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}).

(81)

Substituting into (
80
) yields

\displaystyle F^{\dagger}(t)=E_{0}(t)+\lambda E_{1}(t)+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}).

(82)

where

\displaystyle E_{0}(t)=e^{L_{0}t}E,\qquad E_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,P_{*}(t-\tau)\,e^{L_{0}(t-\tau)}\mathcal{Q}\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\,E_{0}(\tau).

(83)

Both terms are zero-centered,

\displaystyle\langle E_{0}(t)\rangle=\langle E_{1}(t)\rangle=0.

(84)

Substituting
F^{\dagger}(t)\approx E_{0}(t)
into Eq. (
77
), we find that
in the leading order in
\lambda
the equation has the form

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{*}(t)=-\lambda\,\nabla V_{*}[X(t)]+\lambda\,E_{0}(t)-\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta}{m}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,P_{*}(t-\tau)\,\,e^{L(t-\tau)}\langle E\,E_{0}(\tau)\rangle.

(85)

Returning from the scaled momentum
P_{*}=\lambda P
to the true momentum
P
, we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=-\nabla V_{*}[X(t)]+E_{0}(t)-\frac{\beta}{M}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,P(t-\tau)\,\,e^{L(t-\tau)}\langle E\,E_{0}(\tau)\rangle.

(86)

The force
E_{0}(t)
can be written as

\displaystyle E_{0}(t)=F_{0}(t)-\langle F\rangle,\quad F_{0}(t)=e^{L_{0}t}F

(87)

and interpreted as the deviation of the force, exerted by the bath on the system fixed at
X
, from the average value
\langle F(X)\rangle
.
Clearly,
E_{0}(t)
does not depend on
P
, but it does depend on
X
.

That dependence does not complicate the matter if the bath is a homogeneous non-anchored fluid. In that case, the dependence of forces on positions occurs only through the differences
X-x_{i}
and
x_{i}-x_{j}
. When taking average over the bath coordinates
x_{i}
, one can change the integration variables
x_{i}^{\prime}=x_{i}-X
, so that the
integrands depend on
x_{i}^{\prime}
and
x_{i}^{\prime}-x_{j}^{\prime}
, but not on
X
. Therefore, although
F
and
F_{0}(t)
depend on
X
, their moments and correlations do not.
As a result,
\langle F\rangle=0
by symmetry, the PMF is reduced to the external potential,
\nabla V_{*}=\nabla V_{ex}
, and the correlation

\langle E\,E_{0}(t)\rangle=\langle F\,F_{0}(t)\rangle
does not depend on
X
.
Clearly, that correlation also does not depend on
P
and bath variables. Therefore, the propagator
e^{Lt}
in the integral term of Eq. (
86
) can be dropped, and the equation takes the form of the standard generalized Langevin equation

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=-\nabla V_{ex}[X(t)]+F_{0}(t)-\frac{\beta}{M}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,P(t-\tau)\,\langle F\,F_{0}(\tau)\rangle

(88)

with
F_{0}(t)
as zero-centered noise, whose statistics do not depend on the system’s variables.

For an anchored bath, the situation is different
because the statistical properties of noise
E_{0}(t)
generally depend on
X
. While
forces
F
and
F_{0}(t)
still depend on
X
only through
X-x_{i}
, the internal bath forces
depend not only on coordinate differences
x_{i}-x_{j}
, but also on the positions of individual bath units
x_{i}
. In that case,
the variable change
x_{i}^{\prime}=x_{i}-X
does not eliminate
X
from the averages, so that

\langle F\rangle
and
\langle FF_{0}(t)\rangle
depend on
X
and so does the correlation

\displaystyle\langle EE_{0}(t)\rangle=\langle FF_{0}(t)\rangle-\langle F\rangle^{2}.

(89)

One can reflect that dependence denoting

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{M}\langle EE_{0}(t)\rangle=\Gamma(t,X).

(90)

Then Eq. (
86
) takes the form

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=-\nabla V_{*}[X(t)]-E_{0}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,P(t-\tau)\,\Gamma[\tau,X(t-\tau)],

(91)

which formally is similar to the standard generalized Langevin equation (with the external potential
replaced by the PMF), except that
the dissipation term is not of the conventional convolution form since it depends on both
\tau
and
t-\tau
. As it is, the equation is not operational because the dependence of the kernel on
X
is a priory unknown. That is not necessarily a problem.
After all, the exact form of the PMF
V_{*}(X)
is usually not known either; it is often defined empirically to fit experimental data. One may define the function
\Gamma(X)
empirically as well. However, the difficulty is that the two functions
V_{*}(X)
and
\Gamma(X)
are clearly not independent, yet there seems to be no general relation describing their connection.

The situation is simplified when the Hamiltonian
H_{0}
has a special structure (
34
),

H_{0}=h_{1}(x,X)+h_{2}(X)
, and
the force
F_{0}(t)
on the fixed system has the form

\displaystyle F_{0}(t)=f_{1}(X,x,t)+f_{2}(X).

(92)

The component
f_{2}(X)
depends only on the system’s position, while the component
f_{1}
has the properties of the force exerted on a single-particle system by the homogeneous unanchored fluid, i. e. as in the standard theory of Brownian motion. Namely, the average of
f_{1}
over bath variables (with distribution
\rho_{0}
) is zero for any
t
,

\displaystyle\langle f_{1}(X,x,t)\rangle=0,

(93)

and the time correlation

\displaystyle\langle f_{1}(X,x,0)\,f_{1}(X,x,t)\rangle=c(t)

(94)

does not depend on the system’s position
X
.
In that case, the dependence of
\langle E\,E_{0}(t)\rangle
on
X
is canceled,

\displaystyle\langle EE_{0}(t)\rangle=\langle F(0)\,F_{0}(t)\rangle-\langle F(0)\rangle^{2}=c(t)-c(0).

(95)

Then in Eq. (
86
)
the propagator
e^{L(t-\tau)}
has no effect and can be dropped, and the equation turns into
the generalized Langevin equation of the standard form

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=-\nabla V_{ex}[X(t)]+F_{0}(t)-\frac{\beta}{M}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,P(t-\tau)\,\langle E\,E_{0}(\tau)\rangle

(96)

with zero-centered noise
E_{0}(t)

and the external potential
V_{ex}(X)
replaced by the PMF
V_{*}(X)
.
This is the equation advocated in Refs.
Lange
;
Kinjo
. We observe that the equation is valid only under special assumptions (
92
)-(
94
). In the next two sections we consider a special (linear) version of the model with an anchored bath where assumptions (
92
) do hold and therefore the Langevin equation (
96
) is valid.

VI
Klein-Gordon chain: the PMF

Let us now consider a more specific model where the bath is formed by the Klein-Gordon harmonic chain. The chain consists of

N\gg 1
atoms of mass
m
, connected by springs with the stiffness
k_{0}
. Each atom of the chain
is subjected to the on-site anchor harmonic potential with the stiffness

k_{a}
. The system of interest is a particle of mass
M
, connected by a
k_{0}
-spring to the first atom of the chain and subjected to an external potential
V_{ex}(X)
. We assume a boundary condition when
the last particle of the chain is connected by a
k_{0}
-spring to a wall.
The model is sketched in Fig. 1 and can be viewed as the familiar Rubin model
Zwanzig
;
Weiss
, extended by the presence of the anchoring potentials
acting on atoms of the bath and the external potential
V_{ex}(X)
acting on the system. It is well known that a non-anchored chain can
serve, in the limit
N\to\infty
, as a passive bath
Zwanzig
;
Weiss
. In contrast,
the Klein-Gordon chain is non-passive: in thermal equilibrium it produces a systematic harmonic potential acting on the system, so the PMF has the form

\displaystyle V_{*}(X)=V_{ex}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\,\kappa\,X^{2}.

(97)

Below we evaluate the strength
\kappa
of the bath-induced potential; the result is given by Eq. (
113
).

We write the total Hamiltonian of the model in form (
41
),

\displaystyle H=H_{s}+H_{0}(X),

(98)

where
H_{s}
is the Hamiltonian of the isolated system

\displaystyle H_{s}=\frac{P^{2}}{2M}+V_{ex}(X),

(99)

and
H_{0}(X)
is the Hamiltonian of the bath interacting with the system fixed at position
X
,

\displaystyle H_{0}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{k_{0}}{2}(x_{i}-x_{i+1})^{2}+\frac{k_{a}}{2}\,x_{i}^{2}\right\}+\frac{k_{0}}{2}\,(X-x_{1})^{2}.

(100)

Coordinates
x_{i}
represent displacements of the chain’s atoms from positions of mechanical equilibrium.
In accordance with the chosen boundary condition, we assume in Eq. (
100
) that
x_{N+1}=0
.
It is convenient to present
H_{0}
as

\displaystyle H_{0}=H_{0}^{\prime}+H_{0}^{\prime\prime},

\displaystyle H_{0}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left\{\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{k_{0}}{2}(x_{i}-x_{i+1})^{2}+\frac{k_{a}}{2}\,x_{i}^{2}\right\},\qquad H_{0}^{\prime\prime}=-k_{0}\,x_{1}\,X+\frac{k_{0}}{2}\,X^{2},

(101)

assuming that
x_{0}=x_{N+1}=0
and
p_{0}=0
.
Hamiltonian
H_{0}^{\prime}
describes the isolated Klein-Gordon chain of
N
atoms with terminal atoms
i=1
and
i=N
connected to the walls, see Fig. 2.

Figure 1:
The setup considered in the paper. The black circle represents the system of interest, which is a classical particle subjected to an (arbitrary) external potential
V_{ex}(X)
. The system is connected by the spring with stiffness
k_{0}
to the thermal bath, formed by the Klein-Gordon lattice. The latter consists of
N
particles connected by the springs with stiffness
k_{0}
. Each particle of the lattice is subjected to on-site (anchoring) harmonic potentials with the stiffness
k_{a}
.

The advantage of working with
H_{0}^{\prime}
is that it can be readily
diagonalized using the well-known normal mode transformation
\{x_{i},p_{i}\}\to\{Q_{j},P_{j}\}
,

\displaystyle x_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}\,Q_{j},\quad p_{i}=\sqrt{m}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}\,P_{j},

(102)

where the transformation matrix

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N+1}}\,\sin\frac{ij\pi}{N+1}

(103)

satisfies the orthogonality relations

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{ij}A_{ij^{\prime}}=\delta_{jj^{\prime}},\quad\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}A_{i^{\prime}j}=\delta_{ii^{\prime}}.

(104)

The transformation has the same form as
for the corresponding
harmonic chain with no anchoring on-site potential. It is canonical and preserves phase-space volumes, i. e. the Jacobian of the transformation equals
1
. Therefore, the average

\langle\cdots\rangle
over the bath variables
\{x_{i},p_{i}\}
, which we used in the previous sections, can be replaced by the average over
\{Q_{j},P_{j}\}
.

Expressed in terms of
\{Q_{j},P_{j}\}
, Hamiltonians
H_{0}^{\prime}
and
H_{0}^{\prime\prime}
take the forms

\displaystyle H_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\{P_{j}^{2}+\Omega_{j}^{2}\,Q_{j}^{2}\right\},\qquad H_{0}^{\prime\prime}=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}\,Q_{j}\,X+\frac{k_{0}}{2}\,X^{2},

(105)

where the coupling coefficients
c_{j}
are

\displaystyle c_{j}=\frac{k_{0}}{\sqrt{m}}A_{1j}=\frac{k_{0}}{\sqrt{m}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{N+1}}\,\sin\frac{\pi j}{N+1},

(106)

and the normal mode frequencies
\Omega_{j}
are determined by the following expressions

\displaystyle\Omega_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{j}^{2}+\omega_{a}^{2},\quad\omega_{j}=\omega_{0}\sin\frac{\pi j}{2(N+1)},\quad\omega_{0}^{2}=4\,k_{0}/m,\quad\omega_{a}^{2}=k_{a}/m.

(107)

In these expressions,
\omega_{j}
are normal mode frequencies and
\omega_{0}
is the highest normal mode frequency
in the unanchored chain (with
\omega_{a}=0
).
Note that for the anchored chain the spectrum of normal modes is bounded not only from above (as for the non-anchored chain), but also from below

\displaystyle\omega_{a}^{2}<\Omega_{j}^{2}<\omega_{0}^{2}+\omega_{a}^{2}.

(108)

This feature may be important
for ergodic properties of the combined system
Plyukhin_NEO
.

Figure 2:
The Klein-Gordon lattice described by Hamiltonian
H_{0}^{\prime}
in Eq. (
101
).

With
H_{0}^{\prime}
and
H_{0}^{\prime\prime}
given by Eq. (
105
), Hamiltonian
H_{0}=H_{0}^{\prime}+H_{0}^{\prime\prime}
takes the form

\displaystyle H_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\{P_{j}^{2}+\Omega_{j}^{2}\left[Q_{j}^{2}-\frac{2c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}^{2}}\,Q_{j}\,X\right]\right\}+\frac{k_{0}}{2}\,X^{2}.

(109)

Completing the square in
[\cdots]
, one gets

\displaystyle H_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\{P_{j}^{2}+\Omega_{j}^{2}\left[Q_{j}-\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}^{2}}\,X\right]^{2}\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\,\left[k_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}}\right)^{2}\right]X^{2}.

(110)

In the limit
N\to\infty
one finds

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}}\right)^{2}\to\frac{k_{0}}{\pi}\,\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\sin^{2}(2x)\,dx}{a^{2}+\sin^{2}x}=k_{0}(1+2a^{2}-2a\sqrt{1+a^{2}}),

(111)

where we introduced the parameter

a=\omega_{a}/\omega_{0}
,
characterizing the relative strength of the anchor potential.
Then, in the limit of the infinite chain,
H_{0}
takes the form

\displaystyle H_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\left\{P_{j}^{2}+\Omega_{j}^{2}\left[Q_{j}-\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}^{2}}\,X\right]^{2}\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\,\kappa\,X^{2},

(112)

with

\displaystyle\kappa=2\,k_{0}\,a\left\{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}-a\right\},\quad a=\frac{\omega_{a}}{\omega_{0}}=\sqrt{\frac{k_{a}}{4\,k_{0}}}.

(113)

We observe that
H_{0}
has the
form (
34
), discussed at the end of Section 2, with positions of the bath particles
x=\{x_{i}\}
replaced by normal modes
Q=\{Q_{j}\}
,

\displaystyle H_{0}=h_{1}(Q,X)+h_{2}(X),

\displaystyle h_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\left\{P_{j}^{2}+\Omega_{j}^{2}\left[Q_{j}-\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}^{2}}\,X\right]^{2}\right\},\qquad h_{2}(X)=\frac{1}{2}\,\kappa\,X^{2}.

(114)

Using the substitution
Q^{\prime}_{j}=Q_{j}-\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}^{2}}\,X
, one observes that the partition function corresponding to
h_{1}

\displaystyle Z_{1}=\Tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta h_{1}}\right)

(115)

does not depend on
X
. Then the partition function
Z_{0}
corresponding to
H_{0}
has the form (
36
),

\displaystyle Z_{0}(X)=\Tr_{b}\left(e^{-\beta H_{0}}\right)=Z_{1}\,e^{-\beta\,h_{2}(X)},

(116)

Defining the constant
c
in the expression (
13
) of the bath-induced potential
\Delta V
to be
c=Z_{1}
, we find that

\displaystyle\Delta V=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left[\frac{Z_{0}(X)}{c}\right]=h_{2}(X)=\frac{1}{2}\,\kappa\,X^{2},

(117)

and the PMF
V_{*}=V_{ex}+\Delta V
has the form (
97
).

Thus, the bath formed by the infinite Klein-Gordon chain is non-passive; it acts with the systematic average force
\langle F\rangle=-\kappa\,X
on the system fixed at
X
.
The effective stiffness
\kappa
of that force as a function of
a=\omega_{a}/\omega_{0}
is given by Eq. (
113
). It monotonically increases from
0
at
a=0
to
k_{0}
for
a\gg 1
.
Both limiting values are intuitively plausible. For
a=0
, the model is equivalent to the Rubin model
Zwanzig
;
Weiss
where the chain is not anchored and in the limit
N\gg 1

(but not for finite
N
)
serves as a passive bath, which does not produce a systematic force on the fixed system.
For
a\gg 1
the chain acts as an infinitely heavy anchor. In that case, the system feels only the spring of stiffness
k_{0}
by which it is attached to the terminal atom of the chain.

VII
Klein-Gordon chain: Langevin equation

We have seen at the end of Section 5 that a single-particle system subjected to a PMF is described (in the leading order in
\lambda
) by the standard generalized Langevin equation (
96
) if statistical properties of noise

\displaystyle E_{0}(t)=F_{0}(t)-\langle F\rangle,

(118)

specifically the correlation
\langle E_{0}(0)\,E_{0}(t)\rangle
,
do not depend on the system’s variables. Let us show that this is just the case for the model described in the previous section, i.e. when the bath, linearly coupled to the system, is formed by the harmonic Klein-Gordon chain.

The microscopic force exerted by the bath on the system
F=-k_{0}(X-x_{1})
can be written in terms of normal modes as follows

\displaystyle F=-\frac{\partial H_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{\partial X}=-k_{0}X+\sum_{j}c_{j}Q_{j},

(119)

where
c_{j}
are given by Eq. (
106
). Then
F_{0}(t)=e^{L_{0}t}F
is

\displaystyle F_{0}(t)=-k_{0}X+\sum_{j}c_{j}Q_{j}(t),

(120)

where the dynamics of
Q_{j}(t)
is governed by Hamiltonian
H_{0}
, Eq. (
109
), and the equations of motion

\displaystyle\ddot{Q}_{j}(t)+\Omega_{j}^{2}Q_{j}(t)=c_{j}X,

(121)

where the system’s position
X
is a fixed parameter. Solutions of these equations can be written as

\displaystyle Q_{j}(t)=\left(Q_{j}-\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}^{2}}\,X\right)\,\cos\Omega_{j}t+\frac{1}{\Omega_{j}}P_{j}\,\sin\Omega_{j}t+\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}^{2}}X,

(122)

where
Q_{j}=Q_{j}(0)
and
P_{j}=\dot{Q}_{j}(0)
.
Substituting to Eq. (
120
) yields

\displaystyle F_{0}(t)=\sum_{j}c_{j}\left\{\left(Q_{j}-\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}^{2}}\,X\right)\,\cos\Omega_{j}t+\frac{1}{\Omega_{j}}P_{j}\,\sin\Omega_{j}t\right\}-\kappa X

(123)

with

\displaystyle\kappa=k_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}}\right)^{2}.

(124)

As was shown in the previous section, in the limit of the infinite chain,
N\to\infty
, the effective stiffness
\kappa
takes the form (
113
).

We observe that
F_{0}(t)
satisfies conditions (
92
)-(
94
)
mentioned at the end of Section 5 and tehrefore statistical properties of the zero-centered noise

E_{0}(t)=F_{0}(t)-\langle F\rangle
do not depend on
X
. We can also show that directly.
Indeed,
using the substitution

\displaystyle Q_{j}^{\prime}=Q_{j}-\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}^{2}}\,X,\quad P_{j}^{\prime}=P_{j},

(125)

we observe that in terms of
Q_{j}^{\prime},P_{j}^{\prime}
the Hamiltonian
H_{0}
has the quadratic form

\displaystyle H_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\left\{{P_{j}^{\prime}}^{2}+\Omega_{j}^{2}\,Q_{j}^{\prime 2}\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\,\kappa\,X^{2},

(126)

so that

\displaystyle\langle Q^{\prime}_{j}\rangle=0,\quad\langle Q^{\prime}_{j}Q^{\prime}_{k}\rangle=\frac{\delta_{jk}}{\beta\,\Omega_{j}^{2}},\quad\langle P^{\prime}_{j}P^{\prime}_{k}\rangle=\frac{\delta_{jk}}{\beta}.

(127)

From these relations and Eq. (
123
)
one obtains

\displaystyle\langle F\rangle=-\kappa X.

(128)

Therefore, the zero-centered noise
E_{0}(t)=F_{0}(t)-\langle F\rangle

has the form

\displaystyle E_{0}(t)=\sum_{j}c_{j}\left\{Q^{\prime}_{j}\,\cos\Omega_{j}t+\Omega_{j}^{-1}P_{j}^{\prime}\,\sin\Omega_{j}t\right\},

(129)

and its correlation

\displaystyle\langle E\,E_{0}(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{\beta}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}}\right)^{2}\cos\Omega_{j}t

(130)

does not depend on
X
.
In that case, as discussed in Section 5,
the Langevin equation takes the standard form (
96
), which we can write as

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=-\nabla V_{*}[X(t)]+E_{0}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,P(t-\tau)\,\Gamma(\tau),

(131)

where the PMF equals

\displaystyle V_{*}(X)=\frac{1}{2}\,\kappa X^{2}+V_{ex}(X),

(132)

the spring constant
\kappa
of the bath-induced potential is given by Eq. (
113
),
and the dissipative kernel
\Gamma(t)
is related to the zero-centered noise
E_{0}(t)
by the standard fluctuation-dissipation relation

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)=\frac{\beta}{M}\,\langle EE_{0}(t)\rangle.

(133)

Recall that we derived the Langevin equation (
131
) expanding the projected force in powers of
\lambda=\sqrt{m/M}

\displaystyle F^{\dagger}(t)=e^{\mathcal{Q}Lt}F=E_{0}(t)+\lambda E_{1}(t)+\cdots

(134)

and restricting to the leading
approximation

F^{\dagger}(t)\approx E_{0}(t)
.
However, one can show that for the presented model the next term
E_{1}(t)
, which is given by Eq. (
83
), vanishes.
Indeed, as follows from Eqs. (
125
) and (
129
),
E_{0}(t)
is linear in
X
, the derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E_{0}(t)}{\partial X}=\sum_{j}c_{j}\,\frac{\partial Q_{j}^{\prime}}{\partial X}=-\sum_{j}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}}\right)^{2}

(135)

does not depend on bath variables, and therefore

\displaystyle E_{1}(t)\sim\mathcal{Q}\,\ \frac{\partial E_{0}(t)}{\partial X}=0.

(136)

In a similar way one can show that all terms of higher order in the expansion (
134
) vanish as well. Thus, for the presented model the projected force equals
E_{0}(t)
exactly,

\displaystyle F^{\dagger}(t)=E_{0}(t),

(137)

and the Langevin equation (
131
) is also exact.

We used the projection operator technique with the hope (unfulfilled) to get a Langevin equation for a general setting described in Section 3. Had we assumed the linearity of forces from the start, we could have derived the Langevin equation (

\langle EE_{0}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)=\frac{1}{M}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}}\right)^{2}\cos\Omega_{j}t.

Taking into account Eqs. (

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)=\lambda^{2}\omega_{0}^{2}\,\gamma(t),\qquad\gamma(t)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\frac{\sin^{2}(2x)}{a^{2}+\sin^{2}x}\,\cos\left(\omega_{0}t\sqrt{a^{2}+\sin^{2}x}\right)\,dx,

VIII
Conclusion

The concept of the PMF naturally emerges in equilibrium statistical mechanics of complex systems consisting of many particles. The force
F_{i}
on
i
-th particle of the system is produced by the bath disturbed by the interaction with other parts of the system. As a result, even if the bath is a translationally-invariant fluid, the mean force
\langle F_{i}\rangle
is generally not zero and is determined by the bath-induced part of the PMF, denoted above as
\Delta V
. Although for equilibrium systems the concept is
straightforward, there is a variety of views on how to incorporate the PMF in the Langevin description of systems out of equilibrium.

In this paper, we have simplified the problem by assuming that the system of interest consists of a single particle. In that case, the PMF is usually irrelevant because the homogeneous bath is passive and produces no systematic force on a fixed system. That is not so, however, if the bath is not translationally-invariant. In the presented model the symmetry of the bath is broken by on-site anchor potentials applied to individual molecules of the bath.
Although that setting offers a simpler exposition, we were unable to derive a Langevin equation in a closed form. The non-passivity of the bath not only modifies the average potential acting on the system, but also makes the dissipation kernel
and noise statistics depend on the position of the system
X
.
That dependence is a priory unknown and there seems to be no general relation connecting it to the PMF.
Therefore, we argue that there is no general form for a Langevin equation with the PMF.
The conclusion corroborates the findings of Refs.
Schilling
;
Schilling_review
;
Vroy
, although we used here a different projection operator technique.

An exception is the case where all internal forces in the combined system are linear. As a specific example, we considered the bath formed by the harmonic Klein-Gordon chain. In that case, the dissipation kernel and noise statistics do not depend on
X
, and
the Langevin equation has the standard form with the external potential replaced by
the PMF. Such an equation is often exploited in empirical studies, but we observe that it holds only under rather exceptional conditions.

Appendix

In Section 7 we derived the Langevin equation for the model where the bath is represented by the harmonic Klein-Gordon chain by applying the generic method based on the projection operator technique. In this Appendix we present an alternative method based on explicit integration of equations of motion of the bath.
Of course, that method is only feasible when the equations of motion
are linear. The derivation is similar to those for
the Caldeira-Leggett and Rubin models
Zwanzig
;
Weiss
;
Hanggi
.

The Hamiltonian
H
of the model is given by Eqs. (
98
)-(
100
), and
the equation of motion for the system is

\displaystyle\dot{P}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial X}=-k_{0}\,(X-x_{1})-\frac{\partial V_{ex}}{\partial X}.

(A1)

Using transformation (
102
) to the bath’s normal modes, the equation can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{P}=-k_{0}\,X-\sum_{j}c_{j}Q_{j}-\frac{\partial V_{ex}}{\partial X},

(A2)

where
c_{j}
are given by Eq. (
106
).
The equations of motion for the bath’s normal modes are

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{j}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial Q_{j}}=-\Omega_{j}^{2}Q_{j}+c_{j}X,\quad\dot{Q}_{j}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial P_{j}}=P_{j},

(A3)

Excluding
P_{j}
, one obtains

\displaystyle\ddot{Q}_{j}(t)+\Omega_{j}^{2}\,Q_{j}(t)=c_{j}X(t).

(A4)

The general solution has the form

\displaystyle Q_{j}(t)=Q_{j}^{0}(t)+\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}}\,\int_{0}^{t}\sin(\Omega_{j}\tau)\,X(t-\tau)\,d\tau,

(A5)

where
Q_{j}^{0}(t)

is the general solution of the homogeneous equation
\ddot{Q}_{j}+\Omega_{j}^{2}\,Q_{j}=0
,

\displaystyle Q_{j}^{0}(t)=Q_{j}(0)\cos(\Omega_{j}t)+\frac{P_{j}(0)}{\Omega_{j}}\,\sin(\Omega_{j}t).

(A6)

Integrating by parts, Eq. (
A5
) can be written as

\displaystyle Q_{j}(t)=Q_{j}^{0}(t)+\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}^{2}}\left\{X(t)-X(0)\,\cos(\Omega_{j}t)-\int_{0}^{t}\cos(\Omega_{j}\tau)\,\dot{X}(t-\tau)\,d\tau\right\}.

(A7)

Substituting into Eq. (
A2
) yields

\displaystyle\dot{P}(t)=-\frac{\partial V_{ex}}{\partial X}-\kappa\,X(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma(t-\tau)\,P(\tau)\,d\tau+\zeta(t)-M\,X\,K(t),

(A8)

where the dissipation kernel
\Gamma(t)
and noise
\zeta(t)
are

\displaystyle K(t)=\frac{1}{M}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}}\right)^{2}\cos(\Omega_{j}t),\quad\zeta(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}Q_{j}^{0}(t),

(A9)

the effective stiffness
\kappa
is

\displaystyle\kappa=k_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}}\right)^{2},

(A10)

and
X=X(0)
.
In the limit
N\to\infty

\kappa
is given by expression (
113
). Finally, absorbing the term
-M\,X\,K(t)
into noise and using notations of the main text, we obtain

\displaystyle M\ddot{X}(t)=-\nabla V_{*}[X(t)]-\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma(t-\tau)\,P(\tau)\,d\tau+E_{0}(t),

(A11)

with the PMF

\displaystyle V^{*}=V_{ex}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa q^{2},

(A12)

and noise

\displaystyle E_{0}(t)=\zeta(t)-M\,X\,\Gamma(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}\,\left[\left(Q_{j}-\frac{c_{j}}{\Omega_{j}^{2}}\,X\right)\,\cos(\Omega_{j}t)+\frac{P_{j}}{\Omega_{j}}\,\sin(\Omega_{j}t)\right],

(A13)

where
X=X(0)
,
Q_{j}=Q_{j}(0)
,
P_{j}=P_{j}(0)
.
Eq. (
A11
) coincides with the Langevin equation (
131
).
