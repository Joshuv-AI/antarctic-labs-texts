Development and external validation of a non-invasive early gestational diabetes mellitus prediction model integrating social network variables: a machine learning-based prospective cohort study
Authors: Qianqian Li, Yalin Tang, Xiuling Yang, Tingqiang Song, Guozheng Wei, Ruting Gu, Yueshuai Pan, Jingyuan Wang, Yi Li, Lili Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCurrent predictive models for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) largely overlook the role of social network factors. This study aimed to develop and validate an early GDM prediction model by integrating social network characteristics with traditional non-invasive predictors using machine learning (ML).MethodsThis prospective cohort study enrolled 2,433 pregnant individuals from four branches of Qingdao University Affiliated Hospital as the model development cohort and external validation cohort. After screening variables via univariate analysis, significant predictors were used to train seven ML algorithms: Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Classifier (SVC), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). A 30-times repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation procedure was employed, ensuring the preservation of the class distribution in every fold. Performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), accuracy, recall, specificity and F1 score. External validation evaluated the model’s predictive performance and clinical effectiveness via ROC curves, Calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsOne thousand seven hundred fifty-two cases were included in the model development cohort and 681 cases were included in the geographically independent external validation cohort. Twenty-two risk factors for GDM were screened out through univariate logistic regression, covering sociodemographic characteristics, social network characteristics (such as the scale of the structural network and the semi-annual total contact frequency), and personal behavioral characteristics. The XGBoost model demonstrated the optimal comprehensive performance (AUC = 0.980), significantly outperforming other algorithms. External validation further confirmed that the model has excellent generalization ability (AUC = 0.901), though with room to improve upon its sensitivity. The Calibration curve showed that the predicted results were in good agreement with actual observations, and DCA exhibited a superior net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities.ConclusionsThis study developed a high-performance GDM prediction model by integrating social network variables with conventional predictors. The XGBoost-based algorithm showed robust performance in external validation, demonstrating that social network metrics significantly enhance risk stratification beyond traditional clinical factors. It reveals the potential value of social network factors in predicting GDM, providing new ideas and methods for constructing GDM prediction models with higher predictive ability and more stable performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08500-4.

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), defined as glucose intolerance with onset or initial detection during pregnancy, represents a significant global maternal health challenge [1]. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas, approximately 16.7% of pregnancies worldwide are complicated by some form of hyperglycemia [2], with GDM accounting for 75–90% of these cases [3]. In mainland China, recent epidemiological data indicate a GDM prevalence of 14.8% [4]. GDM is a potentially modifiable long-term disease that results in a growing burden of obesity and cardiometabolic disorders in women and future generations of children [5, 6]. With lifestyle changes (improper eating, low amount of physical activity, and excessive weight gain) and updates in the diagnostic criteria for GDM, it is anticipated that the prevalence of GDM will continue to increase significantly in the future, posing significant health and economic burdens worldwide.

GDM is one of the most prevalent complications during pregnancy, and its prediction and monitoring are crucial for early intervention and treatment [7]. Current screening protocols primarily rely on the 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) administered during gestational weeks 24ཞ28 [8]. However, this diagnostic timeline creates a critical care gap: therapeutic interventions (medical nutrition therapy or pharmacological management) initiated in the third trimester face a constrained therapeutic window, potentially compromising treatment efficacy before delivery [9]. Emerging evidence demonstrates that early pregnancy interventions (prior to OGTT screening) can significantly reduce GDM incidence rates while attenuating associated maternal-fetal complications [10, 11]. These findings underscore the imperative to identify first-trimester predictive markers – particularly modifiable risk factors involving behavioral patterns and social determinants – that could facilitate primordial prevention strategies.

Research has demonstrated that 70% − 85% of individuals with GDM can control their blood sugar levels simply by changing their behavior [12]. In the field of diabetes prevention and control, it has been suggested that incorporating peer support from non-healthcare professionals into the social network of an individual could potentially offer additional avenues for promoting and maintaining behavioral changes related to emotional support, obesity, and physical inactivity [13, 14]. In recent years, social networks have been recognized as important coping strategies for primary prevention. Healthy or unhealthy lifestyle behaviors can be promoted by key people in a social network [15]. Social networks refer to the mutual relationships between an individual and his/her family members, relatives, friends, and other interactive members [16]. Social networks have characteristics at both structural and functional levels [17]. The structural network features include the scale, density, type, and strength of relationships; whereas the functional network features include social support or resource exchange [17]. Studies have established associations between social determinants and GDM risk, demonstrating increased incidence among women experiencing pregnancy-related social isolation [18], and those lacking adequate emotional support systems [19]. Notably, predictive modeling innovations in other disease domains (particularly respiratory and gastrointestinal infections in aging populations) have successfully incorporated social network analytics, identifying critical preventive factors such as geographic proximity of social ties, network size diversity, frequency of peer interactions, information accessibility, and tangible support availability [20]. Currently, no prediction model exists for identifying which social network variables are associated with the risk of GDM.

Machine learning (ML) algorithms demonstrate superior predictive capabilities in complex disease patterning, emerging as promising screening modalities for GDM due to their enhanced selectivity and cost-efficiency [21, 22]. Therefore, capitalizing on these technological advancements, we developed and rigorously validated (internally and externally) an ML-based predictive model that integrates social network-derived variables with first-trimester non-invasive variables to enable early GDM risk identification. By linking social network variables (e.g., support accessibility, interaction density) to GDM risk, this model informs targeted prevention strategies that address modifiable social determinants in high-risk populations.

Materials and methods

Study design

This prospective follow-up cohort study utilized data from the Obstetrics Clinic of Qingdao University Affiliated Hospital, a tertiary care institution located in Qingdao, Shandong Province, China, comprising four branches (Eastern [D1], Western [D2], Southern [D3], and Northern [D4]) with an average monthly obstetric outpatient volume of approximately 250 cases. Participants recruited from the Eastern and Western branches between September 2022 and July 2023 formed the model development cohort (n = D1 + D2), while those enrolled at the geographically independent Southern and Northern branches from July 2023 to February 2024 served as the external validation cohort(n = D3 + D4). This study was approved by the medical ethical committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (No. QYFY WZLL 28129). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study. This prediction model study was structured in strict compliance with the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis) statement [23].

Participants

Pregnant individuals were enrolled to participate in the study if they met all of the following inclusion criteria: (1) they were in the first trimester (before 14 gestational weeks); (2) they were aged ≥ 18 years; (3) they planned to give birth in the study hospitals; and (4) they volunteered to participate in the study and provided written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals that were unable to understand, read, and write the Chinese; (2) they were previously diagnosed with GDM, type I, or type II diabetes mellitus (DM), and (3) individuals with other serious diseases (such as kidney, liver, hematopoietic system, or other serious primary diseases). The elimination criteria were individuals who did not consume 75 g OGTT at 24–28 weeks of gestation.

Sample size

Based on preliminary research and literature review, 47 potential predictors were identified. The sample size was calculated using the events per variable (EPV) method, with a conservative target of 5 events per predictor [24]. This required at least 235 GDM events (47 predictors × 5 EPV). According to previous literature, the incidence of GDM in China is approximately 14.8% [4], the initial sample size was calculated as 235/0.148 ≈ 1,588 participants. After accounting for a 10% anticipated attrition rate, the sample size for the modeling group should be no less than 1,746 cases. The external verification cohort needed to ensure enough positive events (≥ 100 cases recommended) to stably evaluate performance [25], so the sample size was determined to be 100/0.148 = 676 participants.

Diagnosis of GDM

GDM was diagnosed through a 75 g OGTT performed between 24 and 28 gestational weeks, following the diagnostic thresholds established by the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) [26]. Individuals received a diagnosis of GDM if the fasting blood glucose level was at least 5.1 mmol/L, the timed glucose measurement was at least 10.0 mmol/L at 1 hour, or the timed measurement was at least 8.5 mmol/L at 2 hours.

Measurements

General data questionnaire

General data characteristics of the participants were collected using a structured questionnaire. The general data questionnaire included an investigation of sociodemographic characteristics and individual behavioral characteristics. These indicators are based on the “GDM Morbidity Risk Questionnaire” developed previously by our team [27] combined with the American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines [28]. When extracting predictive indicators, we comprehensively considered the scientific validity, accessibility, and economic cost, along with the clinical utility and operability of the available predictive models. A detailed description of the general data questionnaire is provided in the Additional file 1.

Social network questionnaire

Individual social network data were gathered via a name-generator-based questionnaire [29]. The questionnaire was subdivided into three sections to collect social network data: asking participants to list the names of social network members who could provide help, advice, and support in a given situation; their social relationships with them (parents, spouses, friends, and colleagues); and how often they had contact with them. A comprehensive overview of the name generator questionnaire can be accessed in the Additional file 2.

Statistical analyses

The acquired datasets underwent digitization in Microsoft Excel 2020. Subsequently, categorical variables were transformed using binary numerical coding, represented as “1” for presence and “2” for absence. For instance, obstetrical history of adverse outcomes was operationalized as 1 (positive history) versus 2 (negative history). The data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The continuous variables in the data were represented by mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the normal distribution variables were analyzed using t-test. Categorical variables were described as frequencies (percentages) and evaluated using Pearson’s chi-squared test. Statistical significance threshold was established at P < 0.05 (two-tailed). ML model development and external validation were executed through Python 3.8.5 (Python Software Foundation) environments. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis [30]. Specifically, participants with missing values in any predictor variable were excluded from the model development and validation processes, as the proportion of missing data for each variable was less than 1% (Additional file 3).

Prediction methods and model validation

The construction and validation process of the ML model used in this study was illustrated in Fig. 1. The study used a variety of ML, including Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Classifier (SVC), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), to determine which one was best suited for this particular prediction task. The model performance was evaluated with independent data sets.

Variable filtering

We used univariate analysis to screen influencing factors, and variables with P < 0.05 were used to develop the prediction model.

Model training

Seven ML algorithms—LR, RF, XGBoost, KNN, SVC, AdaBoost, and MLP—were employed to develop the predictive models. To ensure a robust and stable performance estimate while preserving the class distribution, we adopted a repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation strategy [31, 32]. The development cohort (D1 + D2) was randomly partitioned into 10 folds of equal size, each maintaining the original class ratio. In each of the 10 iterations, a single fold was held out as the validation fold, and the remaining nine were used for training, ensuring each sample was used for validation exactly once. This entire 10-fold procedure was repeated 30 times with different random partitions, yielding 300 performance estimates for aggregation.

To ensure each algorithm achieved its optimal performance, we integrated a systematic hyperparameter tuning process within the cross-validation framework using the Optuna framework (v3.5.0). For each algorithm, we defined a search space for its key parameters. In every training fold of the cross-validation, Optuna utilized the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) algorithm to conduct 100 optimization trials, aiming to maximize the accuracy on the internal validation fold. This rigorous approach ensured a fair performance comparison and that the final model was robustly optimized.

Model evaluation

During the evaluation of the model, specificity, recall, accuracy, F1 score, and the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) were selected as indicators to fully investigate the ability of the model to identify GDM and non-GDM cases. Specificity reflects the ability of the model to correctly identify non-GDM cases; that is, its ability to avoid misdiagnosis as GDM. Recall reflects the ability of the model to correctly identify GDM cases. Accuracy reflects the proportion of correct predictions across all cases, though it can be misleading with imbalanced datasets. The F1 score balances precision and recall, providing a single metric that accounts for both false positives and false negatives. The AUC measures the model’s ability to discriminate between GDM and non-GDM cases across all classification thresholds. Together, these metrics provide complementary perspectives on model performance, though each has limitations that should be considered when interpreting results. We focused on these five evaluation indicators simultaneously to ensure that the model can make accurate and effective judgments in practical applications. Feature importance analysis

To identify the key predictors and interpret the contributions of each feature to the XGBoost model’s predictions, we employed the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) method, an approach from cooperative game theory. We calculated SHAP values for every feature for each participant in the dataset. The overall feature importance, as visualized in Fig. 5, was determined by averaging the absolute SHAP values across all participants for each feature. A higher mean absolute SHAP value indicates a greater average impact on the model’s output magnitude.

External verification

The predictive model underwent rigorous external validation across three critical dimensions using the independent cohort: discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Discrimination capacity was assessed using the ROC curves, while calibration accuracy was evaluated through both Calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, implemented in Python using scikit-learn. This test quantitatively assesses the agreement between observed and predicted probabilities across 10 risk deciles, with a p-value > 0.05 indicating good calibration. For clinical utility, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed using a custom algorithm developed and visualized with the matplotlib library.

Results

Participant characteristics

This prospective cohort study enrolled 2,433 eligible pregnant individuals, comprising 1,752 participants in the derivation cohort and 681 in the geographically independent external validation cohort. The prevalence of GDM was 16.27% (285/1,752) in the derivation cohort and 14.4% (98/681) in the external validation cohort. Tables 1, 2 and 3 summarize the characteristics of the individuals with and without GDM in the derivation cohort.

Screening results of predictive variables for GDM

According to the results of the univariate logistic regression (P < 0.05), there were 22 risk factors for GDM onset, including 10 sociodemographic characteristic variables (Table 1), 10 social network variables (Table 2), and 2 individual behavior characteristic variables (Table 3). The social network variables were the structural network scale, proportion of family members, proportion of friends, proportion of medical staff, semi-annual total contact frequency, information support, emotional support related to important decisions, practical support related to illness, emotional support related to illness, and work-related practical support. The sociodemographic characteristic variables were age, pre-pregnancy body mass index, residence, education level, monthly family income, economic activity participation, public welfare activity participation, family history of GDM, and history of adverse pregnancy. Development of prediction models

To quantitatively assess the incremental value of social network factors in GDM prediction, we developed and compared two model sets—a baseline model with only 12 traditional predictors (10 sociodemographic and 2 behavioral variables) and a full model incorporating all 22 predictors (adding 10 social network variables)—across seven ML algorithms under an identical repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation framework. As summarized in Table 4 and visualized in Fig. 2, the inclusion of social network predictors consistently and significantly enhanced performance across all evaluation metrics. For the optimal XGBoost model, the AUC rose from 0.773(95% CI: 0.731–0.817) to 0.980 (95% CI: 0.967–0.991), with similarly marked improvements in recall, specificity, and F1-score (Figs. 3 and 4), underscoring the essential contribution of social network variables to robust GDM risk prediction.

External validation of predictive models

Discriminatory capacity and calibration

In the geographically independent validation cohort (D3 + D4), the model maintained robust performance: AUC = 0.901(0.851–0.938), specificity = 0.903(0.834–0.952), recall = 0.685(0.544–0.804), accuracy = 0.829(0.779–0.878), and F1 = 0.730(0.650–0.815) (Fig. 6). The calibration plot (Fig. 7) demonstrated good visual agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed frequencies. This observation was statistically confirmed by a non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test (X2 = 5.629, P = 0.689), which indicates no significant difference between the model’s predictions and the actual outcomes, thus confirming the model is well-calibrated.

Discussion

As far as we know, this study is the first to develop GDM prediction models, incorporating social network parameters as potential predictors. The model integrates various noninvasive variables such as sociodemographic, social network, and lifestyle parameters, providing strong support for the early prediction of GDM in the context of social attributes.

Analysis and comparison of GDM risk prediction models

The most pivotal finding of this study is the direct empirical evidence confirming the significant contribution of social network factors to GDM prediction. Our head-to-head comparison revealed that models incorporating these variables consistently and significantly outperformed baseline models confined to traditional predictors across all performance metrics. The marked improvement in AUC, coupled with substantial gains in recall, specificity, clearly indicates that social network variables provide unique and powerful predictive information not captured by sociodemographic and behavioral factors alone. This finding robustly validates our central hypothesis and suggests that the structure and functional support of an individual’s social environment are independent and crucial determinants of GDM risk.

To systematically assess predictive performance, we evaluated seven ML algorithms — LR, RF, XGBoost, KNN, SVC, AdaBoost, and MLP — using 22 preprocessed predictors. In the development cohort (D1 + D2), XGBoost demonstrated superior overall performance. External validation on independent cohorts (D3 + D4) confirmed the robustness of the XGBoost model. The exceptional performance of XGBoost is attributed to its gradient-boosting architecture, which effectively captures nonlinear feature interactions while mitigating overfitting through regularization [33].

When translating the performance metrics of a predictive model into clinical practice, it is essential to comprehensively evaluate them within the specific context of GDM screening [27]. Individuals who are misdiagnosed (false positive rate, i.e., the proportion of normal individuals misclassified as having GDM, which is 1 - specificity) require further clinical tests related to GDM to confirm whether they truly have GDM. In contrast, those who are missed (false negative rate, i.e., the proportion of GDM individuals misclassified as normal, which is 1 - recall), if not detected in a timely manner, will face greater risks and losses in the future. In the long run, the cost of the former is relatively smaller than that of the latter. Therefore, for a GDM screening tool, high sensitivity (recall) is the core performance indicator to minimize missed diagnoses [26]. In the XGBoost model construction phase of this study, the recall was 0.858, while in external validation, the recall was only 0.685, which aligns with findings from other XGBoost-based GDM prediction study [34]. This phenomenon may be attributed to differences in population distribution between the development cohort and the external validation cohort, or to the conservative decision threshold chosen during model training, which tended to favor high specificity. It is worth emphasizing that the model’s excellent overall discriminative ability (AUC = 0.901) provides a solid foundation for its clinical applicability. In subsequent applications, the recall can be targeted for improvement by lowering the decision threshold [35], thereby better meeting the primary requirement of high detection rate in screening scenarios.

Evaluation of risk factors for the onset of GDM

Sociodemographic [7, 21, 36, 37] and lifestyle parameters [38–40] have been considered in previous GDM predictions. In this model, structural (small network size, large family composition, and low network density) and functional (less informational, emotional, and practical support) social network indicators showed high importance, indicating that social network parameters can provide additional predictive information independent of traditional factors [41].

Among the structural social network predictive variables, the weights of the structural network scale and network density were particularly significant. Reduced social network engagement (characterized by fewer interactions and lower interaction frequency) was associated with elevated GDM risk. This finding is consistent with known risk factors for diabetes, its complications, and cardiovascular disease [13, 42–44]. One possible explanation for this observation is that smaller social network sizes and densities often mean that individuals have a limited ability to obtain emotional support, information resources, and social assistance, which increases the risk of GDM. It is noteworthy that the importance of the scale of the structural network scale (ranked third) even exceeds that of adverse pregnancy history, suggesting that early screening of socially isolated pregnant women needs to be paid attention to in clinical practice.

Additionally, the composition of social network members is closely related to the risk of GDM. Our study revealed that a higher proportion of friends and medical staff in one’s social network is associated with a lower risk of GDM, while a higher proportion of family members is linked to an increased risk. This phenomenon can be explained by two main factors. Firstly, individuals with a greater number of family members in their social network are more likely to prioritize family responsibilities over their own health. This can lead to neglect of their personal well-being. Secondly, such social circles tend to be insular. Pregnant women in these networks often rely excessively on a single source of support, typically family members. This homogeneous social environment can perpetuate unhealthy behaviors, such as adhering to a traditional high-sugar diet at home. Other studies have also highlighted that network primarily composed of family members, as opposed to those centered around friends or more diverse connections, tend to correlate with individuals who place a higher priority on fulfilling family responsibilities than on their own well-being, sometimes to the extent of compromising their health [45, 46]. This further underscores the importance of social network diversity. Therefore, pregnant individuals should strive to enhance the diversity of their social networks. A more diverse social network not only provides a broader range of support but also indicates better social integration, which can strengthen psychological well-being and reduce feelings of loneliness [47]. They may need to strike a balance between family and personal needs, while fostering mutual understanding and support among family members. Additionally, society and cultural environments should encourage individuals to prioritize their health and provide resources and support to facilitate a balance between family responsibilities and personal well-being.

In functional social network predictive variables, this study demonstrated that various social support indicators (informational support, emotional support, and practical support) were prospectively associated with the risk of developing GDM. Social support has also been shown to be associated with the risk of cardiovascular diseases [48] and diabetes [13, 14]. Research has shown a positive correlation between social support and self-efficacy in pregnant individuals [49]. This enhanced self-efficacy may help pregnant individuals manage their stress better during pregnancy. Additionally, the more social support they perceive, the more likely they are to adopt more active health behaviors and promote healthy lifestyles [50, 51], thereby reducing the risk of developing GDM. It is worth noting that the importance of information support in the model is significantly higher than that of emotional and practical support (ranked 7th vs. 14th and 16th), which may be related to the direct impact of information acquisition on health decisions. Therefore, in public health interventions, it is essential to prioritize the establishment and strengthening of an information-centric support system. On one hand, systematically integrate diverse sources of informational support to enhance GDM risk prevention and control [52]. Family members, particularly spouses, can participate in GDM health education courses to provide effective supervision and support in the home environment [53], peer groups can utilize online pregnancy communities to share GDM prevention experiences, facilitating the exchange of reliable information and experiences [54], healthcare professionals should encourage pregnant women to use a “question prompt list” during prenatal check-ups, focusing on core issues such as blood glucose monitoring and dietary management, to enable efficient communication and personalized guidance [55]. On the other hand, actively promote the deep integration of mobile health platforms with evidence-based GDM-specific knowledge bases [56], providing pregnant women with personalized dietary recommendations, exercise plans, and blood glucose tracking functions. This will enable convenient access to scientific information and early warning of high-risk factors, thereby enhancing the convenience and scientific rigor of health management [57].

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in the novel application of ML algorithm at the social network level to design a predictive model for GDM identification. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first attempt to systematically incorporate multi-dimensional social network features into GDM risk modeling, thereby enriching current clinical prediction paradigms that predominantly focus on biochemical and demographic factors. However, a significant limitation of this study was the difficulty in establishing the generalizability of the prediction model across the global population. GDM is influenced by various factors such as race and environment. Although the samples of this study are from multicenter data, they all come from the population of Qingdao, China. Therefore, the performance of the model in other populations is still uncertain.

Conclusion

This study innovatively integrates social network parameters into the early risk prediction model of GDM, which can more comprehensively identify high-risk populations. This model extends prenatal care from the individual level to the family or social network level, which may enhance the preventive efficacy of GDM. It aids in the development of maternal and child health intervention programs within the context of social attributes, effectively reducing the risk of GDM in early pregnancy and ensuring the health and safety of both mothers and children.
