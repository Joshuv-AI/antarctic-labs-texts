Multiple Incidental Pancreatic Neuroendocrine Tumors in a Patient With Suspected Duodenal Papillary Adenocarcinoma: A Case Report
Authors: Flaviano Junqueira, Murilo Henrique Pedrão Ferreira, Leticia Mancilla Lourenço, Hesio Vicente Juliano, Everson Artifon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PNETs) are rare neoplasms that can mimic periampullary adenocarcinomas, presenting significant diagnostic challenges. Preoperative distinction is crucial due to the distinct prognostic and therapeutic implications of each entity. We report the case of a male patient with obstructive jaundice, whose initial investigation suggested duodenal papillary adenocarcinoma. Preoperative endoscopic biopsy was inconclusive for malignancy. Given the high clinical suspicion, the patient underwent duodenopancreatectomy. Anatomopathological examination of the surgical specimen unexpectedly revealed multiple implants of well-differentiated PNET (Grade G), without evidence of adenocarcinoma. This case illustrates the well-known limitation of endoscopic biopsy in periampullary tumors, which presents false-negative rates of up to a significant percentage. The final diagnosis of multiple Grade G PNETs, a rare condition frequently associated with genetic syndromes such as multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1, radically altered the patient’s prognosis, which is significantly more favorable compared to papillary adenocarcinoma. Surgical resection remains an indispensable diagnostic and therapeutic tool in cases of biliary obstruction with high suspicion of malignancy, even with negative preoperative biopsies. The incidental finding of Grade G PNET reinforces the importance of complete histopathological analysis of the surgical specimen for adequate diagnosis, prognosis, and follow-up.

Introduction

Tumors of the periampullary region (the area surrounding the ampulla of Vater, where the distal common bile duct and the main pancreatic duct join the duodenum) comprise a heterogeneous group of neoplasms that pose significant diagnostic challenges owing to their complex anatomical location and overlapping clinical manifestations. Adenocarcinoma of the duodenal papilla (ampullary adenocarcinoma) is the most common neoplasm in this region, whereas pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PNETs) are considerably rarer, accounting for only 1-2% of all pancreatic neoplasms [1,2].

Under the 2019 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of digestive system tumors (fifth edition), based on the International Agency for Research on Cancer/WHO common framework [3], pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms are divided into well-differentiated PNETs, graded G1-G3 according to the Ki-67 proliferation index and mitotic count, and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas. G1 PNETs (Ki-67 <3% and <2 mitoses per 10 high-power fields) carry the most favorable prognosis [3-5]. By contrast, duodenal papillary adenocarcinoma, although more favorable than pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, shows five-year survival rates ranging widely from 30% to 70%, depending on stage and histological subtype [6].

The preoperative differential diagnosis between these two entities can be extremely challenging. Endoscopic biopsy has significant limitations, with false-negative rates that may reach 43% in periampullary tumors, which can lead to inconclusive or incorrect diagnoses, directly affecting therapeutic planning and prognostic counseling [7,8].

This report describes a case in which a strong clinical suspicion of duodenal papillary adenocarcinoma was refuted by the postoperative diagnosis of multiple G1 PNETs, highlighting the role of surgical resection as both a diagnostic and a potentially therapeutic approach in patients with biliary obstruction and high clinical suspicion of malignancy despite a non-diagnostic biopsy [7].

Case presentation

A 48-year-old, previously healthy man presented with a two-month history of progressive jaundice, choluria, acholic stools, generalized pruritus, and unintentional weight loss. The day of presentation was defined as day one. He denied alcohol use, smoking, and hepatotoxic medications. There was no family history of endocrine neoplasia, including primary hyperparathyroidism, pituitary tumors, or other neuroendocrine tumors.

On examination, he was jaundiced (3+/4+) with diffuse cutaneous excoriations. Laboratory studies showed a cholestatic pattern (total bilirubin: 18.5 mg/dL; direct bilirubin: 15.2 mg/dL; alkaline phosphatase: 850 U/L; gamma-glutamyl transferase: 1,200 U/L). Carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) was markedly elevated (>700 U/mL; reference: 0-37), whereas carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) was normal. After biliary drainage, CA 19-9 progressively normalized; as the resected lesion was a well-differentiated NET, which does not typically secrete CA 19-9, this elevation was attributed to the biliary obstruction [9] (Tables 1-3).

Abdominal CT showed marked dilation of the intra- and extrahepatic bile ducts (up to 1.4 cm) and a distended, thick-walled gallbladder, without an identifiable obstructive lesion, prompting magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP). MRCP with upper abdominal MRI demonstrated segmental stenosis of the distal common bile duct and the distal main pancreatic duct, with mild upstream dilation, at the level of a common biliopancreatic channel; the pancreatic parenchyma was normal (Figure 1). On day five, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) identified a 15 × 14 mm hypoechoic, homogeneous lesion adjacent to the ampulla of Vater that had not been resolved on cross-sectional imaging, with normal pancreatic parenchyma and no regional lymphadenopathy; EUS-fine-needle aspiration (FNA) of the lesion was negative for malignancy. On day six, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) confirmed the distal common bile duct stenosis; papillotomy with biliary stent placement achieved adequate drainage, and biopsy of the papilla was inconclusive, showing only a chronic inflammatory process with reactive epithelial atypia. Staging chest CT and abdominal angio-CT showed no metastatic disease.

Given a defined periampullary lesion with two non-diagnostic tissue samplings (EUS-FNA and papillary biopsy) and a persistently high suspicion of malignancy, the multidisciplinary team elected to proceed with surgical exploration with curative intent. On laparotomy (day 269), dense peritoneal and mesenteric fibrosis was found, together with a firm mass involving the common bile duct, ampulla, and the head and body of the pancreas, with apparent infiltration of the pancreatic parenchyma and the splenic vein; there was no carcinomatosis or hepatic metastasis. Because the mass extended beyond the pancreatic head and appeared to involve the splenic vein, a total pancreatectomy with en-bloc splenectomy was performed rather than a standard pancreaticoduodenectomy. Because the gastroduodenal and splenic arteries had been divided during this resection, subsequent ligation of the left gastric artery rendered the stomach ischemic, necessitating a total gastrectomy with esophagojejunal reconstruction. The procedure was completed with cholecystectomy, retroperitoneal lymphadenectomy, and appendectomy. Choledocholithiasis with retained stones around the previously placed biliary stent was identified and cleared. Three units of packed red cells were transfused, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit.

Pathological examination showed multiple millimetric, well-differentiated G1 neuroendocrine foci involving the pancreatic parenchyma and peripancreatic adipose tissue, with no evidence of adenocarcinoma; the dominant lesion did not exceed 2 cm. The Ki-67 index (MIB-1) was 1%, confirming G1 grade [10], and the foci were positive for synaptophysin and focally for AE1/AE3 [11]. No lymphovascular or perineural invasion was identified. A concurrent periductal neuroma of the main pancreatic (Wirsung) duct was present (S100-positive neural tissue, CD34-positive stroma; CD117, desmin, and smooth-muscle actin negative), excluding a stromal or smooth muscle tumor. Neuroendocrine involvement was confined to these foci; the adjacent organs removed en bloc showed no tumor, indicating that the firm mass and apparent infiltration noted intraoperatively corresponded to fibrosis rather than neoplasm. Surgical margins were free, and all 27 regional lymph nodes were negative. As extension into peripancreatic adipose tissue does not affect staging for well-differentiated PNET [12], the tumor was staged pT1N0M0 (Figure 2).

The postoperative course was complicated by true diabetic ketoacidosis (pH: 7.28, bicarbonate: 13.2 mmol/L, blood glucose: 486 mg/dL, ketonuria: 80 mg/dL) following total pancreatectomy, consistent with the resulting insulin-deficient (apancreatic) state, managed with intravenous insulin and fluid resuscitation. A surgical-site infection caused by vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecalis was also treated successfully. The patient was discharged on insulin and pancreatic enzyme replacement and referred for outpatient oncological follow-up. At approximately nine months of postoperative follow-up, the patient remained on insulin and pancreatic enzyme replacement, with no evidence of locoregional or hepatic neuroendocrine tumor recurrence on cross-sectional imaging.

Discussion

Limitations of endoscopic biopsy in periampullary tumors

This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of periampullary tumors and the limitations of endoscopic biopsy. The decision to proceed with resection despite non-diagnostic biopsies, driven by persistently high clinical suspicion, proved both diagnostic and, in this case, potentially curative.

Endoscopic forceps biopsy of periampullary lesions has a limited and variable yield: reported false-negative rates range from 16% to 60%, reaching 43% for a single biopsy, with an overall diagnostic accuracy of approximately 45-80% [1,7]. Several factors explain this: a submucosal or intramural tumor beneath intact mucosa, a prominent inflammatory component, and superficial sampling may all yield non-representative material. Moreover, to limit the risk of pancreatitis, biopsies are taken away from the pancreatic orifice, which can further reduce representativeness [1]. Diagnostic yield improves with repeated and deeper sampling [7,8]. EUS-FNA generally offers higher accuracy [4,8]. Notably, in the present case, both modalities failed: the EUS-FNA was negative for malignancy, and the papillary biopsy showed only a chronic inflammatory process with reactive epithelial atypia, neither excluding an underlying neoplasm, which was diagnosed only on the surgical specimen.

Proceeding to resection despite two non-diagnostic biopsies was supported by this high false-negative rate [7] and by the recognized role of surgery as a diagnostic-therapeutic step when malignancy cannot be excluded [1]. In keeping with the principle of non-maleficence, however, this benefit must be weighed against the substantial and permanent morbidity of the operation: total pancreatectomy rendered the patient permanently insulin-dependent with exocrine insufficiency, and the postoperative course was complicated by diabetic ketoacidosis and a vancomycin-resistant enterococcal surgical-site infection. Because the final diagnosis was an indolent G1 neuroendocrine tumor, the extent of resection warrants critical reflection.

Multiple pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors and multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 syndrome

The absence of a distant primary, negative regional lymph nodes (0/27), and no distant metastasis indicate multifocal primary neuroendocrine disease rather than metastatic deposits. This multifocal presentation of a G1 PNET legitimately raises the possibility of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1), a hereditary endocrinopathy caused by germline MEN1 mutations, in which PNETs occur in 30% to 80% of patients and are characteristically multiple [13]. Current guidelines accordingly recommend offering germline MEN1 testing to patients with multiple PNETs at any age [14].

This possibility must be tempered by the available data; however, there was no family history of endocrine neoplasia, no other MEN1 manifestation such as primary hyperparathyroidism or pituitary tumor, and neither genetic testing nor a biochemical workup was performed. Therefore, MEN1 remains unconfirmed, and genetic counseling with testing would be the appropriate next step rather than a presumptive diagnosis [14].

Prognostic impact of final diagnosis

The final histopathological diagnosis substantially changed the patient’s prognosis. Whereas surgically resected duodenal papillary adenocarcinoma carries a five-year survival of 30% to 70% [6], completely resected well-differentiated G1 PNETs have a favorable prognosis, with reported five-year survival ranging across series: approximately 75% in one large surgical cohort and 55% to 82% in others [5,15]. This shifted the outlook from an aggressive malignancy with a guarded prognosis to an indolent tumor with a generally favorable long-term prognosis.

Recommended oncological follow-up

Surveillance after R0 resection of a well-differentiated G1 PNET is risk-adapted, relying on periodic clinical assessment, chromogranin A, and cross-sectional imaging (CT or MRI), in line with the current European Neuroendocrine Tumor Society guidance [16,17]. Recurrence after resection occurs in approximately 12% to 18% of cases, most commonly in the liver, with local relapse uncommon. Reported risk factors include tumor grade, Ki-67 index, size, perineural invasion, and lymph node or distant metastasis [16]. Given a G1 tumor with free margins and negative lymph nodes, a low-risk profile, less intensive long-term follow-up is appropriate [16].

Histopathological and immunohistochemical criteria in the differentiation of pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors

The differentiation between PNETs and ampullary adenocarcinoma rests on careful histopathological examination of the surgical specimen combined with immunohistochemistry [11]. In the present case, the diagnosis of multiple well-differentiated (G1) neuroendocrine foci was established only on complete analysis of the resected specimen, underscoring the importance of a rigorous pathological protocol when preoperative sampling is non-diagnostic [11]

World Health Organization classification and grading criteria

Under the 2019 WHO classification, well-differentiated PNETs are graded G1 to G3 according to the Ki-67 index and mitotic count; G1 tumors are defined by a Ki-67 index below 3% and fewer than 2 mitoses per 2 mm² [3]. Morphologically, G1 NETs show organoid architecture with nested, trabecular, or cord-like arrangements; granular cytoplasm; and round nuclei with “salt-and-pepper” chromatin, without significant pleomorphism, in contrast to papillary adenocarcinoma, which typically shows glandular differentiation with lumen formation, intracellular mucin, and greater nuclear pleomorphism [11].

Essential immunohistochemical markers

Immunohistochemistry is used to confirm neuroendocrine differentiation, with synaptophysin and chromogranin A as the standard general neuroendocrine markers, synaptophysin being the more sensitive [11]. In the present case, the neuroendocrine foci were positive for synaptophysin and focally for AE1/AE3, supporting the diagnosis. A concurrent periductal neuroma of the main pancreatic duct showed a distinct profile, positive for S100 (neural tissue) and CD34 (stroma) and negative for CD117, desmin, and smooth-muscle actin, distinguishing it from the neuroendocrine foci and excluding a stromal or smooth muscle tumor [11].

Ki-67 index as prognostic marker

The Ki-67 proliferation index, assessed by immunohistochemistry with the MIB-1 antibody in the areas of highest proliferative activity, is central to grading [10]. In the present case, the Ki-67 index was 1%, confirming a G1 grade and a correspondingly favorable prognosis [11].

Immunohistochemical differentiation between neuroendocrine tumors and adenocarcinomas

Immunohistochemistry also distinguishes neuroendocrine tumors from adenocarcinoma. Whereas neuroendocrine tumors express synaptophysin and chromogranin A, pancreatobiliary and intestinal-type adenocarcinomas typically express markers such as CK7, MUC1, or CDX2 and may show p53 abnormalities and a high Ki-67 index [11]. In the present case, the foci expressed synaptophysin with only focal AE1/AE3 positivity and a Ki-67 index of 1%, and showed no glandular or mucinous differentiation, thereby excluding ampullary adenocarcinoma [11].

Challenges in multiple lesions and microadenomas

Evaluating multiple millimetric foci poses specific challenges, as very small lesions may contain insufficient material for reliable mitotic and Ki-67 assessment. Analysis of all identified foci, with documentation of their number, size, and location, is therefore recommended [12]. In this patient, complete analysis demonstrated multiple millimetric G1 neuroendocrine foci involving the pancreatic parenchyma and peripancreatic adipose tissue, free surgical margins, and 27 negative regional lymph nodes. As extension into peripancreatic adipose tissue does not affect staging for well-differentiated PNET, the tumor was staged pT1N0M0 [12]. Notably, the firm mass and apparent infiltration of the pancreas and splenic vein observed intraoperatively were not confirmed on pathology, corresponding to dense fibrosis rather than tumor, a finding that cautions against equating intraoperative impression with tumor extent.

Limitations

This report has limitations. As genetic testing for MEN1 was not performed, an underlying syndrome could neither be confirmed nor excluded. Somatostatin-receptor imaging (⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT) was not obtained preoperatively, as a neuroendocrine tumor was not suspected. Follow-up was limited to approximately nine months. As a single case, these observations are not generalizable, and a multifocal incidental G1 PNET presenting as obstructive jaundice has rarely been reported.

Conclusions

In patients presenting with obstructive jaundice and robust clinical suspicion of periampullary malignancy, a negative endoscopic biopsy result or inconclusive findings should not definitively exclude the presence of neoplasia. The surgical approach, such as pancreaticoduodenectomy, may be necessary not only as treatment but also as a definitive diagnostic procedure. The unexpected finding of multiple G1 PNETs instead of papilla adenocarcinoma, as in this case, illustrates the critical importance of complete histopathological analysis of the surgical specimen and multidisciplinary correlation to guide prognosis and subsequent patient management. Robust immunohistochemical differentiation, particularly through markers such as chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and Ki-67, is essential to establish the definitive diagnosis and stratify prognostic risk. This report reinforces the importance of (1) maintaining high clinical suspicion even with negative biopsies; (2) performing complete histopathological analysis of surgical specimens; (3) applying rigorous immunohistochemical protocols; (4) considering genetic syndromes in cases of multiple neuroendocrine tumors; and (5) adapting oncological follow-up according to the definitive diagnosis.
