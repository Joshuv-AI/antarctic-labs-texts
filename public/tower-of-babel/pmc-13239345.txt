Impact of implementing a clinical decision rule on the use of empirical antibiotics and invasive procedures
Authors: Gloria Chun Yan Lee, Joyce Ziwei Tan, Hung Chew Wong, Gene Yong-Kwang Ong, Zi Xean Khoo, Rupini Piragasam, Lena Wong, Sashikumar Ganapathy, Shu-Ling Chong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe aim to evaluate the implementation of the modified Step-by-Step algorithm on rates of administration of empirical intravenous antibiotics and lumbar punctures performed among young infants at risk of serious bacterial infections (SBIs).MethodologyA retrospective cohort study enrolled febrile infants aged ≤90 days old between December 2017 and June 2023 in KK Women’s and Children’s Hospital (KKH). The modified Step-by-Step algorithm was implemented as part of a quality improvement (QI) project. SBIs were defined as urinary tract infections, bacteraemia or bacterial meningitis. Propensity score matching was performed, and the rates of empirical antibiotic use and lumbar punctures were compared before and after QI.ResultsOf the 3475 infants, 684 pairs (pre-QI and post-QI) were compared following propensity matching. The median age was 34 (IQR, 11–60) days pre-QI and 37 (IQR, 13–65) days post-QI (p=0.334). 175/684 (25.6%) infants pre-QI and 169/684 (24.7%) post-QI had SBIs (p=0.755). Overall, empirical antibiotic use reduced by 11.4% (513/684 (75.0%) to 435/684 (63.6%), p<0.001) and lumbar punctures performed reduced by 8.8% (378/684 (55.3%) to 318/684 (46.5%), p=0.001). Among infants without SBIs, empirical antibiotic use reduced by 13.6% (339/509 (66.6%) to 273/515 (53.0%), p<0.001) and lumbar punctures performed reduced by 11.0% (245/509 (48.1%) to 191/515 (37.1%), p<0.001).ConclusionWe report a safe and effective reduction in exposure to empirical antibiotics and lumbar punctures performed on febrile young infants following the introduction of the modified Step-by-Step algorithm.

Introduction

Young infants are susceptible to serious bacterial infections (SBIs) such as bacteraemia, urinary tract infections (UTIs) and bacterial meningitis.1 As many as 16 000/180 000 (9%) febrile infants presenting to paediatric emergency departments (EDs) across the USA are diagnosed with SBIs annually.2 Besides the risk of death, SBI survivors are known to experience detrimental sequelae. Bacterial meningitis can result in hearing and vision loss, spasticity, paresis, seizure disorders, developmental delay and mortality.3 UTIs in infants can be complicated by renal scarring, hypertension, chronic kidney disease and mortality.4

Large variability is seen in the evaluation and care of febrile infants aged ≤90 days because of the diagnostic challenges faced by primary care, ED physicians and paediatricians. Thus, there is a need to balance timely treatment for high-risk infants while reducing unnecessary invasive tests and empirical antibiotic use on low-risk infants.1 5 6 In addition to established guidelines such as the Rochester, Philadelphia and Boston criteria, the Step-by-Step approach has been validated as a sequential approach to identify febrile infants who are at risk of invasive bacterial infections.710 In our institution, the Step-by-Step algorithm was modified in the following ways: age was changed from ≤21 days to ≤28 days, and the diagnosis of UTI specifically required urine full examination and microscopic examination (UFEME) findings of ≥50 white blood cells (WBCs) or a positive urine nitrite result, rather than leukocyturia alone. Our institution chose to implement the modified Step-by-Step algorithm, which demonstrated a sensitivity of 91.9% and negative predictive value of 90.4% (p<0.001) in our patient population and was superior to other febrile infant risk stratification algorithms.11 In October 2021, the Reducing Excessive Variability in Infant Sepsis Evaluation – Singapore (REVISE-S) Quality Improvement (QI) project was conducted to reduce unnecessary investigations and treatment through the implementation of the modified Step-by-Step algorithm.

This study aimed to compare the rates of empirical intravenous antibiotics use and lumbar punctures performed on febrile infants before and after REVISE-S.

Methodology

Study setting and design

This is a retrospective cohort study. Data on febrile infants aged ≤90 days hospitalised between December 2017 and June 2023 in KK Women’s and Children’s Hospital (KKH) were collected. In our hospital, all febrile infants aged ≤90 days presenting to the ED are hospitalised for further investigations, monitoring and management. From 2017 to 2020, febrile infants were recruited as part of a heart rate variability study, with informed consent.12 From January 2021 to June 2023, ethics approval was obtained from the SingHealth Institutional Review Board (IRB) to collect data from all febrile infants with a waiver of informed consent (Centralised Institutional Review Board CIRB 2017/2680).

Pre-KKH REVISE-S QI project (‘pre-QI’) covered the period from 11 December 2017 to 3 October 2021, and the post-KKH REVISE-S QI project (‘post-QI’) was from 4 October 2021 to 30 June 2023. The authors are accountable for all aspects of the work in ensuring that questions related to the accuracy or integrity of any part of the work are appropriately investigated and resolved. The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration (as revised in 2024). Artificial intelligence technologies were not used. Patients and the public were not involved in the planning or conduct of this research study.

Study participants, inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study included infants aged ≤90 days presenting to the ED with fever, defined as temperature ≥38°C (axillary or rectal, taken either in the ED or by another healthcare institution such as a general practitioner or paediatrician clinic), who were admitted to our inpatient paediatric medicine department. In our setting, triage nurses routinely take the temperature of infants aged <6 months using an axillary thermometer. Infants aged <35 weeks were excluded because they are innately a higher risk group and would receive more tests. Infants who left the hospital at their own risk (AOR), including those who requested transfer to another hospital or refused admission, were also excluded. Infants who were managed at other centres and transferred directly to the inpatient ward without ED review were also excluded.

Variable and clinical outcome definitions

SBIs were defined as UTI, bacteraemia or bacterial meningitis.1 UTI was defined as growth of a single pathogen of (1) ≥100 000 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL in a clean catch specimen, (2) ≥50 000 CFU/mL in a catheterised specimen or (3) 10 000–50 000 CFU/mL in a catheterised specimen with an abnormal urinalysis (positive for leucocyte esterase, nitrite or >10 WBCs on UFEME).13 Bacteraemia was defined as a positive blood culture with bacteria growth, excluding possible contaminants such as coagulase-negative Staphylococcus. Lumbar punctures were performed to obtain cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cell counts, protein levels, glucose levels and bacterial culture. Bacterial meningitis was defined as CSF culture positive for bacterial growth. These investigations were performed on admission to the inpatient ward for infants with clinically stable status. For infants who had an unstable status, suspected sepsis and required haemodynamic intervention, blood cultures were performed, and empirical antibiotics were administered in the children’s ED.

Hospital practice and algorithm implementation

Prior to the initiation of the KKH REVISE-S QI project in 2021, we noted high rates of lumbar punctures, blood cultures, urinary in-out catheterisation and intravenous antibiotics. Given the lack of standardised department protocols, febrile infants were deemed high risk for SBIs based on the individual physician’s clinical judgement. Although judgement was based on the infant’s age, persistent or recurrent fever in the ward, clinical condition of the infant and presence of maternal sepsis risk factors, decisions on investigations and treatment largely vary. Moreover, procalcitonin, as an inflammatory biomarker, was not routinely ordered (figure 1).

The KKH REVISE-S QI project aimed to reduce variability, unnecessary investigations and treatment through the introduction of standardised guidelines. The REVISE-S QI project was introduced in KKH over the course from the end 2020 through 2021 and was officially launched on 3 October 2021 (figure 2). The QI project included (1) standardised clerking templates for history taking and physical examination and (2) adoption of procalcitonin in the evaluation of febrile infants. Workshops and briefings were performed in stages among staff to ensure the new guidelines were followed through, with monthly data analysed to ensure compliance. By July 2020, a standardised history taking and clerking template was implemented for use in the electronic medical records. In December 2020, a roadshow was conducted to promote the use of serum procalcitonin as part of the investigations for young febrile infants. After refinement of our modified Step-by-Step algorithm, a department briefing on the new guidelines was held in August 2021. The new febrile infant management guidelines using the modified Step-by-Step algorithm were formally launched in our department in October 2021. Protocol non-compliance was due to main issues of incomplete septic work-up in high-risk patients (due to parental refusal) and missed procalcitonin testing in low-risk patients. These were addressed through monthly audits and regular reminders to the department.

Timeline of the KKH Reducing Excessive Variability in Infant Sepsis Evaluation – Singapore (REVISE-S) Quality Improvement Project introduction to its official launch in 2021.

Febrile infants were deemed high risk for SBIs with the implementation of the QI project if any of these criteria were met: age ≤28 days, UFEME ≥50 WBC or positive for urine nitrite, procalcitonin ≥0.5 ng/mL, C-reactive protein (CRP) ≥20 mg/L, absolute neutrophil count (ANC) ≥10 000/mm3 or if ill-appearing based on the paediatric assessment triangle (appearance, circulation and work of breathing) (figure 3). If deemed high risk, infants would undergo comprehensive testing including a blood culture, lumbar puncture and urinary in-out catheterisation and receive empirical intravenous antibiotics. If deemed low risk, invasive investigations, including lumbar puncture, were not performed and antibiotic therapy was not initiated. Instead, they were observed in the ward for at least 24 hours until a clear source of the fever was identified or the fever resolved.

Statistical analysis

We compared the rates of empirical antibiotic use and exposure to lumbar puncture pre-QI and post-QI project. Descriptive statistics were performed; continuous variables were presented using median, 25th and 75th percentiles, whereas categorical variables were presented using number and percentages. Fisher’s exact probability tests were used to evaluate significant differences in the categorical variables pre-QI and post-QI project. Median regression tests were carried out to assess significant differences in continuous variables pre-QI and post-QI project. Considering that the pre- and post-QI cohorts had different SBI prevalence likely secondary to the COVID-19 pandemic and other contributing factors, we used propensity score matching using the following variables: age ≤28 days, leukocytouria, procalcitonin ≥0.5 ng/mL and ANC ≥10 000/mm3 or CRP ≥20 mg/L. Analyses were performed on the data generated by propensity score matching accordingly. Statistical significance was set at 5%. Statistical tests were performed using IBM SPSS V.29.

Results

3475 febrile infants (figure 4) were analysed, among whom 770 infants were included in the pre-QI and 2705 infants in the post-QI group. The median age was 33 (IQR 10–59) days pre-QI and 47 (IQR 22–67) days post-QI (p<0.001). There were 330/770 (42.9%) and 1110/2705 (41.0%) female infants pre-QI and post-QI (p=0.384). 549/770 (71.3%) infants pre-QI and 1357/2705 (50.2%) post-QI were deemed high risk for SBI (p<0.001). 176/770 (22.9%) infants pre-QI and 393/2705 (14.5%) infants post-QI had SBIs (p<0.001) (table 1).

After propensity score matching for the risk of SBIs, 684 infants each from the pre-QI and post-QI groups were analysed. The median age of infants was 34 (IQR, 11–60) days pre-QI and 37 (IQR, 13–65) post-QI (p=0.334). High-risk infants comprised 496/684 (72.5%) pre-QI and 491/684 (71.8%) post-QI (p=0.809). The proportion of infants with procalcitonin ordered post-QI increased by 12.4% (404/684 (59.1%) to 489/684 (71.5%), p<0.001).

Bacteraemia was diagnosed among 19/684 (2.8%) infants pre-QI and 22/684 (3.2%) post-QI (p=0.752), and meningitis was diagnosed among 4/684 (0.6%) infants pre-QI and 2/684 (0.3%) post-QI (p=0.687). 17/770 (2.2%) infants in the pre-QI group and 57/2705 (2.1%) in the post-QI group who were deemed low risk for SBIs were diagnosed with SBIs (p=0.887). Among these 74 infants (across both periods), 3 infants in the pre-QI group had bacteraemia (two with group B streptococcus and one with Klebsiella pneumoniae), whereas the other 71 infants had UTIs. These infants were treated with appropriate antibiotics, and none required admission to the intensive care unit.

Post-QI, empirical antibiotic use reduced by 11.4% (513/684 (75.0%) to 435/684 (63.6%), p<0.001), and lumbar punctures performed reduced by 8.8% (378/684 (55.3%) to 318/684 (46.5%), p=0.001) (figure 5). For infants without SBIs, empirical antibiotic use reduced by 13.6% (339/509 (66.6%) to 273/515 (53.0%), p<0.001) and lumbar punctures performed reduced by 11.0% (245/509 (48.1%) to 191/515 (37.1%), p<0.001).

Discussion

This study demonstrated an effective reduction in the exposure to empirical antibiotics and lumbar punctures performed on febrile infants without SBIs pre-QI and post-QI.

Prior to the implementation of the KKH REVISE-S QI project, young infants who were hospitalised for fever received varied care depending on the attending physician’s discretion. These infants may have had to undergo a full septic work-up, which included blood culture, urine culture and lumbar puncture with CSF studies, or have a partial septic work-up performed without lumbar puncture. Infants may have been administered empirical antibiotics or closely monitored without empirical antibiotics. Factors that influenced the clinical decision included age, persistent fever, clinical condition of the infant and presence of maternal risk factors; however, these were not standardised across clinicians. Serum procalcitonin level was offered as a laboratory test in our hospital since 2016, but initial uptake varied because of physician preferences and concerns about cost.

Among the different diagnostic algorithms in determining the risk of SBIs in febrile infants, the modified Step-by-Step algorithm had the highest sensitivity (91.9%) and negative predictive value (90.4%) (p<0.001) in predicting SBIs in our population, as compared with the Paediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network rule and Lab-score.11 Since the implementation of the modified Step-by-Step algorithm, procalcitonin use increased by 12.4% (404/684, 59.1% to 489/684, 71.5%, p<0.001). Currently, a standardised clinical guideline is available to reduce variability in management.

Implementation of the modified Step-by-Step algorithm has translated to an effective 13.6% reduction in empirical antibiotic use (339/509, 66.6% to 273/515, 53.0%, p<0.001) on infants without SBIs. Reducing exposure to unnecessary antibiotics shortens the length of hospital stay and reduces antibiotic resistance in well-appearing febrile infants who are deemed low risk for SBIs.14

Moreover, lumbar punctures performed have reduced by 11.0% (245/509 (48.1%) to 191/515 (37.1%), p<0.001). Performing lumbar punctures on low-risk infants is associated with unnecessary prolonged hospital stay, increased parental anxiety and increased costs and use of hospital resources.6 Lumbar punctures also carry risks of exposing low-risk infants to traumatic taps and unsuccessful lumbar punctures.15

The American Academy of Paediatrics (AAP) Clinical Practice Guidelines (2021) provided risk-stratification criteria based on inflammatory markers to determine the need for lumbar puncture and administration of antibiotics among well-appearing febrile infants.16 The international REducing Variation In Sepsis Evaluation II (REVISE II) study aimed to examine the impact of a multifaceted intervention bundle to improve adherence to the AAP Clinical Practice Guidelines. Among infants aged 29–60 days with normal inflammatory markers, 92.4% appropriately did not undergo lumbar puncture, and there was no change in missed invasive bacterial infections.17 A QI project conducted in Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia on 352 infants aged 22–28 days achieved a reduction in lumbar punctures from 87% to 44%, with no missed cases of bacterial meningitis.18 These studies demonstrate the importance of QI studies in reducing unnecessary lumbar punctures.

This study has some limitations. Data were analysed from a single-centre, limiting generalisability to other clinical contexts. The prevalence of SBIs was lower post-QI than that pre-QI. We postulate that this was because of decreased transmission of infections during the COVID-19 pandemic with improved hand hygiene, use of face masks and social distancing.19 During the COVID-19 pandemic, ED visits (particularly for less serious illness) decreased due to parental anxiety surrounding infections with COVID-19 during hospital visits.20 Additionally, as data from 2017 to 2020 were collected as part of an ongoing study and data from January 2021 to June 2023 were collected from all febrile infants with a waiver of informed consent, the number of infants in the pre-QI group was significantly lower. To address this, propensity score matching was performed to ensure that both cohorts were comparable. Moreover, not all infants underwent the entire septic workup. 17/770 (2.2%) febrile infants pre-QI and 57/2705 (2.1%) post-QI (p=0.887) who were deemed low risk for SBIs were eventually diagnosed with SBIs. None of these infants required ICU admissions, and no deaths occurred. These infants were observed during their inpatient stay for persistent fever and were investigated and treated appropriately.

Future prospective studies can involve assessing the modified Step-by-Step algorithm in larger multi-centre study populations. The sustainability of such interventions should also be investigated to ensure long-term gains in healthcare savings on unnecessary invasive procedures and use of antibiotics while ensuring safety with long-term follow-up.

Conclusion

We report the successful and safe reduction in empirical antibiotic use after the introduction of the KKH REVISE-S QI project, which utilised the modified Step-by-Step algorithm. Future studies should investigate whether these findings can be generalised.
