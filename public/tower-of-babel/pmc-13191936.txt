Factors affecting access to palliative care in the emergency department for older adults with serious life- limiting illnesses: a qualitative study
Authors: Latifah Jehloh, Anuchit Maropi, Kittikorn Nilmanat, Anne Bourbonnais
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOffering palliative care to older adults with serious illnesses can be challenging in the emergency department. Understanding the factors that affect access to palliative care in this context is crucial for improving the quality of life for older adults and reducing the cost of aggressive treatments in the emergency department. The factors identified from the perspective of older adults, family caregivers and healthcare providers are unknown in context of Thailand and should be considered to improve access. This study aimed to describe the factors that influence access to palliative care in the emergency department for older adults with serious life- limiting illnesses from various perspectives.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was conducted in a community with five older adults, and five family caregivers and a hospital with ten healthcare providers were purposively recruited and participated in semi-structured interviews. Thematic analysis was used to analyze the data.ResultsFive themes described the factors affecting the access to palliative care in the emergency department that is, organizational support, training, cultural competence, attitude towards palliative care, and the traditional nature of the emergency department’s mission.ConclusionHealthcare providers should be trained in palliative care to improve their skills in providing effective palliative care for older adults with life-limiting illnesses and FG in the emergency department.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02101-4.Keywords: Facilitators, Barriers, Elderly, Acute care, Palliative care

Background

The emergency department (ED) has become more involved in the medical treatment of older adults over the last few decades. Older adults account for 12–24% of all ED visits in many countries [1]. Furthermore, they receive care in the ED more frequently than other age groups [1]. These patients require specialized ED care due to their vulnerabilities and often concurrent health conditions [2]. According to a retrospective cohort study, there are many reasons to explain frequent ED consultations, for example, being old, having a chronic disease like diabetes, taking many medications, having a mental health problem, requiring the care of a medical specialist, and living in a rural area [3]. Organ deterioration and multiple disorders, which generate the worst symptoms, were the primary reasons why older adults visited the ED [4].

Therefore, to provide appropriate palliative care, healthcare providers in the ED need the policy or guideline to approach for palliative care at the first step of caring at the ED. This includes using visits to the ED as an opportunity to initiate a palliative care approach [4]. A palliative care approach can be defined as active holistic care for every people at all age that facing with severe illness especially people who are nearly death [5]. However, this approach can lead healthcare providers in the ED to face significant challenge in striking a balance between diagnostics tests and treatments while considering patients ‘preferences. Healthcare providers might have to choose between continuing life-saving treatment and implementing a palliative care approach [6]. For ED healthcare providers who are trained in acute, life-saving care, this is even more challenging. These healthcare providers must be able to recognize patients who require palliative care to guide their decisions. This palliative approach is necessary to respect patient’s choices and goals of care, to prevent subpar added-value treatments concerning the overall trajectory of the disease, and to ensure that appropriate care and quality of life are improved [7]. While social and spiritual support is necessary, older adults are often not given the option to obtain palliative care in the ED, even though a palliative care approach should be implemented early on following a life- threatening diagnosis [8]. Instead, treatments tend to focus on curing physical symptoms [8].

Studies on palliative care in the ED are developing, but the clinical recommendations are not clear yet. Based on clinical guidelines [9], and a systematic review [10], there is still little evidence on palliative care practices in the ED. Current results of palliative care practices show positive effects on patients and health care system, such as improving the quality of life of older adults [11], shortening hospital stays [9], lowering associated costs [12], and significant benefits in the management of patients with life-limiting illness [13]. In addition, advanced care planning discussions are a component of palliative care linked to a better quality of life, fewer deaths in hospitals, and a higher chance that preferences will be communicated and honored for older adults with serious illnesses [14]. Thus, it is crucial to consider a palliative care approach in the ED, including as a place to discuss advanced care planning and create palliative care plans, but it is still challenging.

Knowing about the factors contributing to the challenge of implementing a palliative approach in the ED would be helpful. However, there is limited research on the factors (facilitators and barriers) in the ED influencing the access to palliative care for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses that focuses on the perspectives of the different groups involved, i.e., patients, family caregiver (FG), and healthcare providers. A qualitative study involving semi-structured interviews with ED healthcare providers from the United States to obtain their perspectives on what facilitated and constrained access to health care services which included local home and community services, to reduce visits to the ED for end-of-life older adults. Barriers to getting health care services were identified as limited time for referral, insurance, the ED’s mission, insufficient staff, refused services to patients, distance from home to health care services, the length of time it took to refer, the complexity of the paperwork before referring, and the ED’s small size and limited resources [15]. Good case management and social workers who quickly assisted in referring patients to health care services were facilitators of access to health care services in that study. Another qualitative study interviewed 15 participants, including patients, FG and healthcare providers to explore the factors involved in the implementation of palliative care services for older adults with heart failure [16]. This study found that barriers to adopting palliative care included unclear organizational structure, limited social support, insufficient knowledge, and financial constraints. Conversely, factors such organizational cooperation, social support, and the presence of expert healthcare workers facilitated the choice of palliative care [16].

Those studies concern facilitators and barriers from the perspectives of different patients and setting but did not explore their perspectives when they were in the ED, which is the first setting where decisions are made regarding treatment, including for older adults with serious illnesses that need holistic care for complex needs. Therefore, there is a lack of knowledge on the factors (facilitators and barriers) influencing the access to palliative care at the ED for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses from the perspective of older adults, FG, and healthcare providers. In addition, the researchers observed there was a lack of access to palliative care among older adults in the ED in this area that should be provided for them in the initiating step of treatment. Discussion about death is taboo in Thai culture. Healthcare providers try to avoid mentioning death in front of patients. Therefore, discussing death preferences will be met with reluctance, and providing palliative care in the ED will be challenging. This study aimed to provide a thorough description of those facilitators and barriers in the ED from the perspective of older adults, FG, and healthcare providers in the context of southern Thailand.

Method

The aim

This study aimed to describe the factors that influence access to palliative care in the emergency department for older adults with serious life- limiting illnesses from various perspectives.

Design

This study used a descriptive qualitative approach, often chosen to characterize phenomena in a constructivism paradigm, to gain access to the perspectives of healthcare providers [17]. It aligned with our purpose to investigate and produce a comprehensive understanding of the viewpoints of FG, healthcare providers, and older adult with serious life-limiting illnesses regarding access to palliative care in the ED.

Study setting and recruitment

Two settings were selected: a community hospital with experience caring for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses in the ED, and home to many older adults and FG. Healthcare providers who provided care to older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses working in the ED, FG of older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses in the community, and older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses were purposively recruited. The researcher established trust before conducting the interview by providing a plain language consent form, acknowledging their own background, and building rapport through active listening and non-verbal communication, which included appropriate eye contact and a calm tone of voice. To achieve data saturation on a topic, qualitative research typically requires a minimum of 12 participants [18]. Our study included twenty participants: five older adults, five FG and ten healthcare providers from the ED. This number of participants was considered sufficient when no new information was learned and the researchers had a solid grasp of the participants’ viewpoints. The setting of the study was a community hospital ED in Southern Thailand with experience caring for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses. Healthcare provider participants were purposively recruited via the community hospital ED. Older adults and their FG were recruited with the help of nurses in the hospital. Nurse identified participants met the inclusion criteria. They nurse called the researcher to come to explain about briefly explaining to the older adult and FG its the study’s purpose and emphasizing about willingness there is nothing happen if they are unwilling to participate. If the participants expressed interest, the nurse obtained their verbal permission to share their contact details with the primary research team. The research team then followed up to conduct formal informed consent and scheduling.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for older adults were: age ≥ 60 years and over, incurable disease as diagnosed by a physician, having a family member with the primary responsibility for the care of older adults, being able to communicate and understand Thai or Malay language, and having a history of at least one ED visit because of a serious life-limiting illness. Exclusion criteria were having an uncorrected vision or hearing impairment, living with a major neurocognitive disorder, or having delirium. FG of a recruited older adult were included if they were at least 18 years old, lived with the older adults, provided direct care both during hospitalization and at home, and were able to communicate and understand well in Thai or Malay language. They could be a spouse or a child of a recruited older adult. Inclusion criteria for healthcare providers were: having at least one year of experience in the ED, and being willing to share their experience.

Data collection

Data were collected one time face to face from participants through individual semi-structured interviews conducted by the first author. They were recorded on digital audiotape with the participants’ permission and lasted approximately 40 to 45 min. Two separate interview guides were used, one for older adults and FG and another for healthcare providers. Both guides were developed by the researchers and validated by three experts, consisting of a nursing lecturer, a head nurse expert in palliative care, and a head nurse expert in ED care. A pilot study was conducted with three participants to assess the acceptability of the interview guides. Pilot participants are excluded from the final data set, and researchers selected pilot participants from the same setting. Based on participant feedback, two questions were rephrased for better clarity as well as asked the participants to confirm the subsequent interpretations accurately reflected their views as well as answer the research objectives. These guides focused on their perspectives on factors to access palliative care in the ED for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses. An example of a question for older adults and FG was: “What are the barriers to accessing palliative care for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses in the emergency department?”. An example of a question for healthcare providers was: “In your opinion, what are the main barriers encountered by older adults and FG to access palliative care in the ED in your area?”. The older adults and FG were interviewed individually in their homes, and health care providers were interviewed individually in the ED. Field notes were taken on the observations and activities encountered or participated in by the researcher during data collection. These field notes included details such as time, dates, activities, places, behaviors, and conversations, as well as documenting the reflection of the primary researcher during the research process. The interview guides developed for this study is provided as a supplementary file. The nurses, older adults, and FG were interviewed by the researcher in Thai language. The interviewer and the participants have no relationship. The researchers speak the participants’ languages and are native speakers of the community.

Data analysis

A thematic analysis was carried out based on the approach of Braun and Clarke (2006) [19]. The interview data were transcribed verbatim. The thematic analysis consisted of six phases. First, the researchers familiarized themselves with the data. This phase required the researcher to explore meanings and patterns in data. Second, reread the transcribed data carefully several times to create the codes for all important and relevant data. Thirdly, the codes were analyzed and searched to identify broader themes that could reach the study’s goal. Fourth, the themes were checked and reviewed based on the initial codes and the whole set of data. Fifth, the themes were defined and named according to the essence of what each theme is about. The last step was to produce the report based on the study’s goals. The co-researchers reviewed and commented the results of the analysis. All interviews were conducted and transcribed verbatim in Thai. Three researcher performed data analysis in Thai to clearly understand about the context and meaning of word when the theme and code were finalized. Two researchers interpreted all of theme and code into English for reporting.

Rigor and reflexivity

Techniques to ensure credibility in this study include member verification and prolonged involvement in the field [20]. To maintain an audit trail, the exact transcriptions of the memo and audiotape records were preserved. To help the researcher and participants view the data from diverse angles and enhance data analysis, the preliminary results were also presented to the participants in individual meetings. By employing peer review and member checking, the researchers were able to increase confirmability while remaining aware of their subjectivity. The second meeting conducted with the original participants to presented a summary of the preliminary themes and illustrative quotes to ensure that the interpretations accurately resonated with the participants lived experiences. These were not new data collection interviews but rather validation sessions.

Results

In our sample, most older adults were male (n = 3) compared to female (n = 2), with a mean age of 78.2 years (SD = 5.49). All of them were married and retired. They were all Muslim, which is representative of the religious minority in that region of Thailand. They reported having hypertension, breast cancer, Parkinson’s disease, paralysis, heart disease, and stroke. They had received a diagnosis of a serious life-limiting illness from a physician between 3 months and 27 months before the interview. Most of their FG were female (80%), with an average age of 52 years (SD = 14.50). All of them were married and most had completed high school (80%). 80% of participants were unemployed housewives. The majority of the healthcare providers were registered nurses (80%), or doctors (20%) aged between 27 and 44 years old. All of them were governmental employers. They had 3 to 20 years of experience in caring for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses in the ED. They cared for at least three older adults with serious life- limiting illnesses in the ED per month. Almost all of them (90%) had never received training in palliative care.

Our findings identified five main themes of factors affecting access to palliative care in the ED (see Table 1): organizational support, training, cultural competence, attitude towards palliative care, and the traditional nature of emergency department’s mission.

Organizational support

This theme integrated four subthemes: two explain the barriers in the organizational system and two describe what encouraged access to palliative care for the older adults with life- limiting illness in the ED.

Barriers

The absence of palliative care policies and guidelines for older adults in the emergency department

Health care providers highlighted the lack of organizational policies, protocols, or clinical guidelines specifically designed to support the delivery of palliative care for older adults with severe life-limiting illnesses presenting to the ED. Participants described the ED as having no clear pathways, contributing to inconsistent treatments, role ambiguity, and uncertainty among health care providers about when and how to initiate palliative care conversations or interventions in the ED. This situation is described as follow by a participant:

“When we knew that palliative treatment was accepted by this patient, when it came to palliative care in the emergency department, we were unaware of the boundaries of care and what we could and could not do. Guidelines for caring for older adults with life-limiting illnesses in the emergency department are lacking.” (Health care provider 5).

Insufficient staff in the ED

Participants also mentioned that the lack of healthcare providers, including doctors and nurses, made it difficult for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses to receive the proper palliative care. Some healthcare providers had to multiple tasks. In the end, this structural barrier limited the standard and provision of palliative care. Here is how one of the participants explains it:

“Because there isn’t enough staff to rotate shifts, we cannot provide palliative care in the emergency department.” (Healthcare provider 6).

Participants who had been patients also described this lack of staff: “Healthcare providers were constantly overworked because of a lack of staff.” (Patient 5, Caregiver 5).

Facilitators

Clear guidelines on palliative care in the ED

Clinical guidelines for palliative care in the ED significantly shift the trajectory of patient care from aggressive, curative-focused interventions to patient-centered, goal-concordant management. Participants emphasized that one of the facilitators would be the availability of clear policies or guidelines pertaining to palliative care in the emergency department. Participants stated that well-defined protocols, pathways, or policies could significantly facilitate the provision of palliative care for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses who present to the ED. Several participants reported that standardization would help them avoid unnecessary or burdensome interventions and increase their confidence in initiating timely palliative care conversations and respecting patients’ goals and preferences in care plans. Healthcare providers and caregivers agreed that this would be helpful:

Patients would receive better care if healthcare providers had adequate guidelines in providing palliative care for older adults in ED” (Caregiver 2).

“Every decision-making for older adults with a life-limiting illness would be easier if there were explicit guidelines as instructions to follow”. (Health care provider 1)

Good workflow system

A workflow system is the process of step of caring that involve job description as well as the responsible person in each step of patient disease trajectory. Therefore, it will be flow of working as patient’s condition without delaying acute emergency care. Another facilitator would be an effective workflow system in the ED, which participants characterized as an essential component of organizational support for enabling older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses to access palliative care. This could allow the prompt identification and suitable management of patients with palliative care needs. It would help staff to incorporate palliative care into standard emergency care without postponing acute interventions or adding to their workload, thanks to an effective workflow system. Additionally, a well-organized workflow system could help to strike a balance between the needs of palliative care for older adults and the demands of acute emergency care. It was believed that an effective workflow system was crucial to expanding access to high-quality palliative care in the ED. A participant stated:

“Everything would be easier if the hospital had a good workflow system that clearly identifies who is responsible for each task in the emergency department, especially when it comes to caring for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses.” (Health care provider 3).

Training

This second theme about training includes four subthemes; three subthemes explain the barriers relating to training and one what facilitate the access to palliative care for the older adults with life- limiting illness in the ED.

Barriers

Lack of knowledge about palliative care in the ED

Those professionals said they did not fully understand when palliative approaches were appropriate, how to evaluate patients for palliative needs, or how to effectively communicate prognosis and care preferences because they are not adequately trained in palliative care. This is reflected in this quote: “Healthcare providers are not trained to give palliative care for older adults in the emergency department and lack the necessary abilities.” (Healthcare provider 10).

Lack of understanding of progressive disease and its prognosis

Healthcare providers’ lack of understanding about the course and prognosis of diseases is another major obstacle described by participants Healthcare providers in the ED prioritize curative interventions over palliative care when they are not knowledgeable about the natural progression of life-limiting illnesses. This is highlighted by both healthcare providers and FG shown in those quotes:

“Making the decision to switch from curative to palliative care in the ED is extremely challenging because we do not have much knowledge to predict the progressive disease.” (Healthcare provider 7).

“Healthcare providers might not be able to accurately predict the disease’s prognosis and course.” (Caregiver 1).

Challenges in communication or breaking the news

Discussion about death is taboo in Thailand. This belief is considered bad luck and a negative wish for patients. Therefore, starting a conversation about death is very painful and difficult to process. Therefore, the palliative care in the ED is delayed and being ignored. Challenges in communication or breaking the news mentioned by participants that prevents access to palliative care in the ED for older adults. However, communicating about palliative care is complex as shown in this quote: “Since it is extremely difficult for healthcare providers to communicate about sensitive topic such as death, good communication skills should be taught and practiced.” (Healthcare provider 10).

As a result, some healthcare providers mentioned being put off starting this kind of challenging discussion, which leaves older adults and their families in the dark about the nature of the illness. This can lead to missed chances for timely palliative care. A patient expressed it as follows:

“Healthcare providers need to be trained about telling bad news. Otherwise, palliative care cannot be started timely manner in the ED.” (Patient 3).

Facilitator

Healthcare providers with knowledge and expertise

Participants explained that when healthcare providers have sufficient knowledge of palliative care principles, they can clearly identify which patients need discussion about palliative care in the ED. They are more confident in deciding the best treatment choice for these patients. Therefore, older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses are transferred in a timely manner for palliative care that prioritizes comfort, dignity, and quality of life instead of useless curative treatments. Having knowledge and being an expert is therefore identified as a crucial facilitator to strengthening and ensuring that patients receive the right care at the right time. One healthcare provider expressed it this way:

“Because doctors had a wealth of expertise and knew exactly what to do for each patient, older adults with serious life illnesses received correct treatments and adequate palliative care in the ED.” (Healthcare provider 6).

Cultural competence

This theme describes how cultural competence plays a crucial role in influencing access to palliative care for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses in the ED. Two subthemes related to barriers were identified, and one facilitator, which is not being hindered by the identified barriers.

Barriers

Language barriers

Using different languages is an important barrier to conveying the message, according to participants Therefore, palliative care in the ED can be postponed or delayed because patients and caregivers cannot understand the information well. This can be very challenging for healthcare providers when they want to discuss such an important topic in the ED as shown in this quote: “All of the instructions and documents in the ED are written in Thai, so I also don’t know whether palliative in the ED can be applied effectively or not because I also don’t know how to explain in Malay language.” (Healthcare provider 2). This is also described as challenging by patients:

“I cannot understand much of what doctors or nurses say to me because I use the Malay language in my life. Sometimes, I also don’t know what treatment I need to choose or what is appropriate to choose in the ED” (Patient 1).

Spiritual beliefs

Spiritual or religious beliefs were described as having a significant influence on how patients and their families understand disease and end-of-life care. They can have an easier time accepting palliative care when they believe they need to accept every disease as sent by God. However, they may want more aggressive treatments if they believe their disease can be cured. Additionally, some healthcare providers can struggle to understand patients’ cultural beliefs effectively. Palliative care in the ED is then more difficult to provide for them as described in the following way by a participant:

“Because we were unaware of their true needs, it was extremely challenging to continue being the ones who influenced their ways of life, particularly if they held different cultural beliefs.” (Healthcare provider 6).

Facilitator

Same language and belief

When healthcare providers speak the same language as their patients and family members, it is described as easier to explain the patient’s health, prognosis, and care alternatives simply and honestly. It helps ensure that patients and their families are informed about all aspects of palliative care and understand its benefits: “We feel very confident when providing care for patients who have the same language and culture as us because it is much easier to discuss/convey the important messages.” (Healthcare provider 3)

“We feel very warm and trusting when we know our healthcare providers have the same cultural beliefs and speak the same language.” (Caregiver 4).

Attitude towards palliative care

This theme describes how healthcare provider’s attitudes toward palliative care can influence their timely introduction of palliative care in the ED or not. In addition, it also presents how the attitude of patients and families can also impact this type of care. It includes three subthemes that address attitudes as barriers and one that highlights a facilitator.

Barriers

Misconception or perception about palliative care

Some healthcare providers, older adults and families describe that having a strong hope that the older adult will be cured. For them, stopping aggressive treatments for comfort care from a palliative perspective meant giving up and accepting defeat to die. This is shown in this patient’s quote: “Palliative care is the alternative that medical professionals gave us when there was no other option except to die.” (Patient 4).

When they adopt this attitude, palliative care in the ED is considered unacceptable because they fear that this means abandoning hope or stopping to fight for a longer life for their patients or a relative. A healthcare provider explains it this way: “Palliative care can be rejected by older adults and FG because they believe it is a form of care that involves waiting for death without any treatment.” (Healthcare provider 8).

This perception usually postpones discussions about palliative care, prolongs aggressive treatments, and delays the initiation of palliative care in the ED as shown in this quote by a family member: “We thought that palliative care involved only accepting death not doing anything. Therefore, we delayed receiving it.” (Caregiver 2).

Some FG and even healthcare providers explained that they chose curative treatments with hope, even if this meant having little benefit and additional suffering in the ED. For them, palliative care was interpreted as “doing nothing” which was unacceptable. As a result, families sometimes insisted on continuing invasive treatments and life-sustaining procedures that were unlikely to change the outcome instead of accepting a palliative approach. This quote reflects this idea: “We requested that healthcare providers provide our father with complete treatment rather than only wait for him to pass away and do nothing, as palliative care would have done.” (Caregiver 5).

This was a barrier for healthcare providers. Likewise, some healthcare providers shared this mindset as well. They hesitated to initiate conversations about palliative care or felt uncomfortable recommending comfort-focused care over aggressive intervention.

Emotional burden such as guilt, and unreadiness

Some FG felt very guilty about accepting palliative care because, for them, it would show they were giving up on survival chances, which impacted their attitude toward palliative care. For them, this meant neglecting their relatives, which is considered an unacceptable behavior in Thai society as this quote shows: “I felt terrible that there was nothing I could do to help my father recover.” (Caregiver 1).

Therefore, they wanted to ensure that they made the best choice for the older adult. Regarding this attitude, prolonged curative treatments were sometimes seen as a better choice compared with withdrawing them and letting the older adults die in front of family members. For some aggressive curative treatment was seen as showing love, loyalty, and gratitude compared to comfort care as part of palliative care. In addition, some family members just simply could not accept the death of a relative as is described by this caregiver: “I thought I wasn’t yet a good daughter. If he dies today, I can’t accept that”. (Caregiver 3)

Therefore, they needed more time to stay with the older adults to accept death. This feeling of unreadiness about the death of a relative was a barrier to starting a conversation for receiving palliative care in the ED. As a result, decisions could favor ongoing invasive treatments rather than timely palliative care that could better meet the patient’s needs and wishes.

Facilitator

Positive attitude towards palliative care

When healthcare providers had a positive attitude towards palliative care, participants described that they would focus on improving quality of life, comfort, and dignity by offering palliative care in the ED. They initiated early conversations about the benefits of this type of care and about the importance of respecting the patient’s preference for care choice, rather than prolonging aggressive treatment. This influenced family members as shown in this quote: “We received a lot of information about palliative care from the doctors and we are very ok with this choice.” (Caregiver 5).

In addition, if patients and their families had a positive attitude towards palliative care, it facilitated discussions about prognosis and care options, rather than choosing curative treatment. A caregiver said: “We accepted palliative care because we knew it would improve the quality of life of patients before death.” (Caregiver 2).

Earlier conversations regarding palliative care were seen as beneficial in reducing the likelihood of unnecessary aggressive interventions that provided no benefit to the patient, and they helped ensure that palliative care was provided at the appropriate time.

Traditional nature of emergency department mission

It includes two subthemes on nature of the ED environment that are barriers and one that is a facilitator.

Barriers

Time constraint

For participants, it is considered very challenging to have long conversations about care options in the ED, such as palliative care, which are sensitive, time-consuming, and require emotional support. A family caregiver explains this: “In the emergency department, doctors and nurses were extremely busy. The conversation at the emergency department moved really quickly. We occasionally disagree with what they said and require more time to talk and ask questions.” (Caregiver 2).

Life-saving culture

For participants, the ED culture means “doing everything possible” to save the person, which drives healthcare providers to provide curative treatments instead of palliative care. Therefore, sensitive conversations about palliative care tend to be delayed and deprioritized. Some healthcare providers felt that the ED was not an appropriate setting to discuss palliative options, or they perceived initiating such conversations as conflicting with their primary role of saving lives. This is underlined in this quote:

“ED was developed to help people avoid death and save lives rather than accepting death peacefully as expected in palliative care.” (Healthcare provider 8).

Facilitator

A designed private space

According to the three types of participants, sensitive conversations about palliative care need privacy and a calm environment. When the ED provides a designated private area, healthcare providers can have comprehensive conversations without interruption. Effective and sensitive conversations in an appropriate room can enable the initiation of palliative care, which would be helpful. A patient expressed it this way:

“It would be a good option if they gave me a corner to talk and discuss with me what I need in the ED before giving the treatment” (Patient2).

Discussion

This study described factors influencing the access to palliative care in the ED for older adults with serious life-limiting illness in five themes. It highlighted that the absence of palliative care policies and guidelines in the ED is a barrier as well as the lack of a workflow to perform interventions consistent with a palliative approach. This corroborates what was identified in one study, which investigated barriers to identifying and planning palliative care in the ED for older adults. Their results found that 37% of older adults who visited the ED were overlooked for palliative care and one significant barrier was the lack of standardized protocols and guidelines for palliative care [8].

Insufficient staff in the ED is another barrier to reducing access to palliative care in the ED. This affects the workloads in the ED as well as providing palliative care in the ED. This finding is consistent with a previous study also found that the main barrier to providing palliative care identified by healthcare providers was understaffing in the ED in Kampala, Uganda, with data collected from African emergency healthcare providers and African patients [21].

As such, it is not surprising that an aspect considered as a facilitator would be the availability of explicit guidelines regarding palliative care for older adults in the ED. This was also identified by a previous study [22]. This could help reduce another barrier, that is the lack of knowledge about palliative care in the ED for progressive diseases. Our study found that healthcare providers lacked adequate knowledge and training, hence the requisite skills to administer palliative care because they struggle to accurately anticipate the progression or prognosis of a patient’s illness or symptoms. A knowledge gap regarding the appropriate timing for initiating palliative care leads to delayed access. This often results in individuals with progressive diseases receiving aggressive treatments in the ED, rather than accessing palliative care. These findings are consistent with previous studies that ED patients received curative treatments instead of palliative care [22, 23].

We also found that communication skills are crucial for conducting sensitive conversations about palliative care, because they involve honest and sometimes difficult explanations about the disease’s progression as well as about the reasons for shifting from aggressive treatments to comfort care. Therefore, healthcare providers need training on how to communicate effectively about what palliative care is and the reasons for proposing it to a patient. This was also stated in previous study [24]. Cultural and linguistic barriers are factors that contribute to delayed accessing palliative care. When healthcare providers, patients, and families do not share the same cultural or language background. Our study reveals that this situation also pertains to older adults with life-limiting illness, particularly considering the diverse cultural and language backgrounds of healthcare providers, patients, and families. One barrier found in our study to this mutual understanding is a language barrier. If the healthcare providers and the patients and family members do not share the same language, it impedes the understanding of the information by patients and family members, affecting their choices. It can delay palliative for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses in the ED. In other cultural contexts, this language barrier was also identified [25–27].

When healthcare providers are knowledgeable, they can be more effective in the intensive discussions with patients and families regarding palliative care. They can develop a mutual understanding of the discussion’s content. If healthcare providers have the necessary knowledge, they can better identify which patients should benefit from palliative care and when to initiate that care effectively. This contributes to care being offered in a timely manner and is consistent with a previous study that identified lacking knowledge as limitation in accessing palliative care in the ED [22].

Religious and spiritual beliefs are significant factors that determine the direction of treatment in the ED. Families often have hope for miracles or adhere to the principle of prolonging life as long as possible according to their religious beliefs, leading to older adult patients being more likely to receive painful and potentially non-beneficial aggressive treatments. Particularly in the Thai context, people express gratitude by using advanced medical technology to prolong life. At the same time, discussing death in the ED remains a taboo because of fear of cursing or going against good fortune, making end-of-life conversations difficult and untimely. Spiritual or religious belief is another crucial factor influencing the direction of care, such as palliative care. Our study highlighted the particularities of Thai culture, but the importance of this factor is consistent with previous studies, which reported that religious or spiritual beliefs can act as a barrier to accessing palliative care in the ED [28, 29].

The attitude toward palliative care can also affect access to this type of care. If ED healthcare providers perceive palliative care as a failure of caring, they will not tend to propose palliative care. When they adopt the culture of the ED where the aim is to fight for survival and palliative care is understood as accepting death without any treatment, it creates an important barrier to offering palliative care. This finding aligns with previous studies in the ED that mentioned the main obstacles delaying access to palliative care in the ED are complex cultural factors and beliefs. The primary issue stems from society’s tendency to deny death viewing it as frightening or a failure of treatment [30]. When false beliefs exist that palliative care is primarily related to the end-of-life, it impacts healthcare providers, who then express concern about suggesting palliative care to patients for fear of making them lose hope or think they have given up on them [31]. Our study indicates that this phenomenon also occurs among healthcare providers caring older adults in the area, where the majority of people are Muslim. Additionally, some FG sought extra time to care for their relatives to express their gratitude to them. This led them to choose curative treatment rather than palliative care. This result is in line with earlier research that found emotional factors, including feelings of gratitude, guilt, and unpreparedness, to be obstacles to receiving palliative care for older people with life-limiting conditions in hospital and the community [32]. Our study allows to know that this is also the case in the ED.

Healthcare providers who have a positive attitude towards palliative care clearly facilitate access to it in the ED. Both healthcare providers and patients who view palliative care as a suitable option saw it as a form of comfort care instead of the absence of treatment. Therefore, palliative care was well-received and provided to them. When patients are eager to receive palliative care and healthcare providers accurately assess patients requiring palliative care, this care is provided more quickly. This finding is also consistent with previous studies that mentioned that a positive attitude towards palliative care among healthcare providers has been identified as a key facilitator for accessing palliative care [33]. Our results expose how this is expressed in the specific context of the ED and for older adults.

This study is also found that the nature of the ED as a lifesaving environment with important time constraints are significant barriers to accessing palliative care for older adults. The ED can be chaotic because of its life saving mission requiring the use of advanced curative treatment. In this context, any waste of time can impede saving the life of a patient. Therefore, it is quite difficult to accept death in that environment designed to support life. A previous study mentioned that life-saving interventions in the ED can act as a barrier to accessing palliative care for older adults with life-limiting illnesses [34]. This study indicates that to provide effective palliative care, healthcare providers need to take time to confirm prognosis, make decisions, and discuss alternative treatment options. All these actions require time, which conflicts with the urgent nature of usual care in the ED [35]. On the other hand, our participants considered that if specific private spaces were available for healthcare providers to discuss calmly with older adults and families about palliative care, this would be facilitator despite the quick pace in the ED. This can provide a safe space to engage in difficult conversations [36].

Strengths and limitations

Our findings are based on the first author’s immersion in the research environment for data collection and analysis, which facilitated the emergence of the results from its natural context. It also includes the three groups directly involved in palliative care in the ED: patients, FG, and healthcare providers. Therefore, our study offers a comprehensive view of the attitudes, beliefs, and perspectives of these stakeholders regarding factors influencing palliative care in the ED of Southern Thailand, specifically for older people with serious life-limiting illnesses. A limitation might be the difficulty of the participants to speak of palliative care because it is taboo in the Thai culture. However, this also emphasizes the significance of our findings, given that this topic has not been extensively studied within this culture. Another limitation is that the primary researcher was unable to observe actual conversations about palliative care in the ED. This challenge might be due to more barriers than facilitators in the study setting. The facilitating factors identified from the interviews are therefore more wishes for better conditions in the ED rather than things that they actually experienced as helpful. One limitation is potential researcher bias, when developing the interview guide, as it focused on the researcher perspectives on factors to access palliative care in the ED for older adults with serious life-limiting illnesses. To attempt to combat this the interview guideline was developed according to identified purposes, research questions, research objectives to intend to explore as well as literature review. The drafted the items were organized as the construction of opened-ended questions. Another limitation is this study asked the perception of older adults and FG of the barriers and facilitators to accessing palliative care in the ED, and that older adults and FG may not have experienced of in attempting to accessing palliative care in the ED.

Recommendations

More experimental research should be conducted to develop and assess alternative approaches or programs to support palliative care of older adults in the ED. Also, future studies focus on developing interventions that address the cultural aspects of palliative care to facilitate its access in the ED while respecting cultural values and beliefs, which can strongly impact the perceptions regarding this type of care. Our findings also underline the importance of training healthcare providers and to help them develop practical communication skills to help patients and caregivers understand the intent of palliative care. They also need to value and provide an environment that is consistent with offering palliative care, such as private space for difficult conversations and staffing that considers the time required to deliver this type of care.

Conclusion

Access to palliative care in the ED is determined by five interconnected key factors, starting with organizational support, training, cultural competence, attitude towards palliative care, and traditional ED mission. The ED may reluctantly provide palliative care without clear guidelines, and a lack of knowledge and training will likely limit its effectiveness. Without understanding culture and language, effective discussion will be misunderstood. In addition, attitude towards palliative care as well as environment of friendly discussion towards treatment preference of death wish should be timely and effectively to discuss in the ED as first line of choosing treatment.

Recommendation

Our finding can help healthcare providers and managers better understand the factors influencing the offer of palliative care for older adults with life-limiting illnesses in the ED. This should also influence the emphasis placed on training to provide palliative care in various settings, including in the ED despite the typical nature of care in that environment. This could help better support patients and FG when facing difficult decisions, assist them in gaining more knowledge, and dispel myths about palliative care while respecting their cultural and spiritual beliefs.
