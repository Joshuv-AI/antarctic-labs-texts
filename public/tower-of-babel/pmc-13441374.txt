Assessing the Czech Version of the Inventory of Personality Organization in Relation to Borderline Personality Disorder in the ICD‐11
Authors: Karel D. Riegel, Albert J. Ksinan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study assessed the Czech version of the Inventory of Personality Organization (IPO‐CZ) in relation to borderline personality disorder (BPD) as defined in the ICD‐11 model for PDs and defensive functioning in BPDs. The IPO‐CZ was used as a proxy measure of personality functioning (PF), although not fully identical to the conception of PF within the ICD‐11 framework defined by the severity of self‐other impairment.MethodsThe IPO‐57, Borderline Personality Questionnaire, and Defense Style Questionnaire were administered to general population volunteers (n = 371) and mixed psychiatric patients (n = 293).ResultsThree‐factor solution of the IPO‐CZ (i.e., Instability of self and others [ISO]; Instability of goals and behaviors [IGB]; and Psychosis) was confirmed, to a large extent reflecting Kernberg's initial concept of personality organization. Since the measurement invariance of the 57‐item version was not confirmed across samples, a 14‐item version of the IPO‐CZ was developed. ISO was strongly associated with self‐other pathology in both samples, IGB showed to be an indicator of immature defensive functioning in both samples and mature‐neurotic defenses in clinical sample when IPO‐14 was used.ConclusionWhile ISO appears to be an indicator of PF in terms of general self‐other disturbance, IGB may be considered a specifier of the defense mechanism‐based management of impulsive aggression typical of BPD patients that should not be ignored. Moreover, the study found measurement invariance across general and clinical sample for IPO‐14.

Introduction

The new ICD‐11 presents the first official version of a purely dimensional personality disorder (PD) classification with major implications for clinical practice and research (Blüml and Doering 2021). One of these implications is reflected in the focus on the overall personality functioning in relation to self and others, which stands for the core component of personality. The level of impairment in personality functioning provides the essential parameter for distinguishing PD from healthy personality as well as from other forms of mental disorders (World Health Organization 2019).

The emphasis that ICD‐11 puts on the distinction between so‐called ‘personality difficulty’ and three degrees of PD severity (i.e., mild, moderate, and severe) resembles the psychodynamic tradition of personality evaluation, distinguishing between neurotic, borderline, and psychotic personality organization (Doering et al. 2013; Caligor et al. 2018). According to Kernberg (Kernberg 1981) and his colleagues, (Clarkin et al. 2010) assessing the degree of integration of self and the degree of normality or pathology in an individual's relationships with other people is the main criterion for evaluating any PD. In other words, when discussing the degree of impairment of personality functioning within the concept of personality organization, according to Kernberg, (Kernberg 1981) it is necessary to always assess all three structural characteristics, that is, integration versus diffusion of identity (and the related overall quality of object relations); the constellation of mature versus immature defensive operations; and the presence versus absence of reality testing. These characteristics subsequently distinguish between neurotic, borderline, and psychotic structural organizations. While individuals with a neurotic personality organization exhibit defensive operations centered around repression and other higher‐level defenses, patients with borderline and psychotic organizations exhibit predominantly lower‐level defensive operations centered around the splitting mechanism. Reality testing is preserved in neurotic and borderline organizations but is severely impaired in psychotic organizations.

The most traditional way to evaluate the concept of personality organization is the Structural Interview. The Structural Interview combines features of a traditional mental state examination; a psychoanalytically oriented interview focused on the patient‐therapist interaction; and the clarification, confrontation, and interpretation of identity conflicts, defense operations, and reality distortions that the patient reveals in the interaction with the therapist in the “here‐and‐now,” especially if they express identifiable transference elements (Kernberg 1981). Although it is a clinically‐based approach deeply grounded in psychoanalytic theory, its administration is time‐consuming and requires a high level of knowledge of psychodynamic processes and clinical skills from the interviewer, which significantly affects its reliability (Reich and Frances 1984). To enhance its empirical soundness, Clarkin, Foelsch, and Kernberg (Clarkin et al. 2001) created the Inventory of Personality Organization (IPO)—a 57‐item self‐assessment tool that contributes to the structural diagnosis of the patient's personality usually received via semi‐structured, clinician‐rated interviews (Hörz‐Sagstetter et al. 2018). The IPO has been translated to various languages, for example, French, (Normandin et al. 2002) Japanese, (Igarashi et al. 2009) Dutch, (Berghuis et al. 2009) German, (Zimmermann et al. 2013) and Italian (Preti et al. 2015). Previous research has verified the IPO's internal consistency and test‐retest reliability in the general population (Normandin et al. 2002; Lenzenweger et al. 2001) and among psychiatric patients, (Hörz‐Sagstetter et al. 2020; Beheydt et al. 2020) as well as the criterion validity of the IPO in relation to established methods for assessing personality psychopathology (Berghuis et al. 2009; Preti et al. 2015; Lenzenweger et al. 2012). Nevertheless, from the factorial perspective, the initially proposed tripartite structure of the primary IPO scales in relation to Kernberg's model (i.e., identity diffusion, primitive defenses, and reality testing) was not confirmed by majority of studies. Some authors aimed to identify a subset of items for a unidimensional structure, (Zimmermann et al. 2013, 2015) some authors pointed out a significant overlap of identity diffusion and primitive defenses factors, (Berghuis et al. 2009; Lenzenweger et al. 2001) others preferred a four‐factor solution, (Preti et al. 2015; Ellison and Levy 2012) yet others kept the original three factors, but at the cost of substantially eliminating items (Igarashi et al. 2009).

Given the emphasis that ICD‐11 puts on the overall impairment in personality functioning, evidence of the interrelationship of IPO factors with measures of personality functioning, (Preti et al. 2015; Kraus et al. 2020) as well as associations between IPO and DSM‐IV PDs (Hörz‐Sagstetter et al. 2020; Beheydt et al. 2020) reflect the importance of assessing the general level of impairment as a basic parameter of PD's severity superior to specific PDs. In this regard, the shortened 16‐item version of the IPO (Zimmermann et al. 2013, 2015) has recently been used as a self‐report measure of personality disorder severity (Zimmermann et al. 2020) that is compatible with the ICD‐11 perspective.

Although the ICD‐11 omits almost all specific PDs known from previous categorical system due to their frequent overlap, (Reed 2018) the only exception stands for the borderline personality disorder (BPD) operationalized via “Borderline pattern specifier” covering polythetic features adapted from the DSM‐IV/DSM‐5 criteria for BPD including “dissociative symptoms or psychotic‐like features (e.g., brief hallucinations, paranoia in situations of high affective arousal)” (Bach and First 2018). It has been suggested that Borderline pattern specifier may serve as a “pragmatic compromise” to not jeopardize access to services for patients with BPD, as it is intended to bridge the transition from categorical to dimensional PD diagnosis in the ICD‐11 and represents a type‐specific expression of personality pathology rather than a specific categorical diagnosis per se (Reed 2018; Watts 2019). This somewhat controversial status of BPD within the new diagnostic system is still the subject of empirical discussion among those who do not perceive BPD as a separate diagnostic category, nor as a specific expression of personality pathology, but instead as an indicator of the PD's severity, (Sharp et al. 2015; Wright et al. 2016) and those who perceive BPD as valid and a clearly distinguishable clinical entity within the borderline personality organization (Clarkin et al. 2007, 2020). Nevertheless, from the perspective of ICD‐11 the primary question regarding the Borderline pattern specifier right now is whether it provides information that is non‐redundant with other proposed trait domain specifiers (i.e., Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition, and Anankastia) which may be assigned as part of the diagnosis as are judged to be prominent and contributing to the PD and its severity (Reed et al. 2019).

On the other hand, from the perspective of object relations theory (ORT), an excessive reductionism of the most dimensional trait‐based models for PDs, which give equal weight to all traits that emerge in factor analysis of self‐report measures, also does not adequately reflect etiological specificity of patients with borderline personality pathology (Clarkin et al. 2007). In this view, while self‐esteem or identity problems and chronic interpersonal dysfunction are fundamental feature of those organized on the borderline level of personality functioning, they are seen as the result of poorly integrated and poorly modulated affects (Clarkin et al. 2020). Marked flaws in the consolidation of self‐other representations thus reflect the impact of primitive defenses that disrupt normal integration processes and consequently impact reality testing prone to paranoid thinking and dissociative states (Lenzenweger et al. 2001; Kernberg and Caligor 2005). In this view, unlike behavioral and phenomenological descriptions of pathological personality symptoms or traits, defenses are manifestations of the patient's unconscious part of the ego, which, as is known from classical psychoanalytic theory, have a certain goal—to protect the patient's conscious part of the ego from anxiety and unpleasure, and exercise his control over impulsive behavior, affects, and instinctive urges (Freud 1993).

To date, the IPO has been employed in several studies which examined the interactions between personality structure and other personality and psychopathological features, predominantly associated with BPD patients (Walter et al. 2009; Vermote et al. 2009). More specifically, IPO dimensions of identity diffusion and primitive defenses were related with low effortful control in a sample of BPD patients, (Hoermann et al. 2005) and IPO dimensions of instability of self/others and instability of goals discriminated patients with BPD from non‐BPD patients, when 4‐factor IPO structure was used (Preti et al. 2015). Finally, another IPO study reported that the alienation, aggression, and absorption constructs represent important linkages to the psychodynamic processes, especially primitive psychological defenses and reality‐testing impairments in BPD (Lenzenweger et al. 2012). The conclusions of this latter study appear to be largely in line with the recent European perspective on the challenges and opportunities of the ICD‐11 classification of PDs. In connection with the issue of the Borderline pattern specifier, it points out the appropriateness of considering, in addition to the nine DSM‐IV/DSM‐5 criteria for BPD, three additional manifestations of the Borderline pattern, that is, (1) a view of self as bad, inadequate, guilty, and contemptible; (2) a sense of alienation or loneliness; and (3) rejection sensitivity, problems with trust, and misinterpretation of social signals. These can be useful both in the diagnostic phase for a more accurate clinical description of the patient, and in the treatment planning (Bach et al. 2022).

This study aims to further validate the ORT model of personality psychopathology in the context of the ICD‐11 conceptualization of PDs. From this perspective, its main goal is to expand the current knowledge on the significance of the IPO dimensions of identity diffusion, primitive defenses, and reality testing in differentiating patients with self‐reported BPD symptomatology from general population volunteers. We seek to assess whether the IPO dimensions—particularly those reflecting defensive functioning—can detect an incremental information about the constellations of psychological defenses responsible for the modulation of affects in individuals potentially suffering from BPD, beyond the severity of overall impairment in personality functioning. At the same time, we observe to what extent this information is captured in the nine criteria for BPD within the Borderline pattern specifier. Moreover, this is the first study evaluating the psychometric properties of the Czech version of the IPO, including its differentiation capacity according to the clinical status of the respondents.

Methods

Participants and Procedure

Two samples were used in this study: a mixed clinical sample (total n = 293) of psychiatric patients (n = 147) and patients with addiction (n = 146) and a control sample of general population volunteers (total n = 371). Age over 18 and sufficient knowledge of the Czech language were the inclusion criteria in both samples; the exclusion criteria in the clinical sample were—in line with previous studies (Berghuis et al. 2009; Preti et al. 2015)—the presence of current psychotic symptoms, current intoxication, or organic brain disorders accompanied by relevant cognitive impairment to ensure the lowest possible level of response bias due to acute mental conditions or misunderstanding of items. Participation in the study was voluntary and anonymous for all respondents, and all participants were asked to give their informed consent to participate in the study, which they had the opportunity to withdraw at any time without stating the reason. Participants were not rewarded for their participation in the study; however, if they were interested in feedback, they could provide us with their email address, which was held apart from anonymously analyzed data. Trained administrators conducted the data collection. Ethics Committees approved the study protocol and the informed consent form.

Psychiatric patients were enrolled in the study in collaboration with the Department of Anxious Disorders and the Department of Psychotherapy. Patients were treated for the following diagnoses: neurotic, stress‐related and somatoform disorders (81.4% of the sample); mood disorders (9.8%); personality disorders (6.0%); behavioral syndromes associated with physiological disturbances and physical factors (1.0%); disorders of psychological development (1.0%); schizophrenia, schizotypal and delusional disorders (< 1.0%). Patients with addiction were recruited from the inpatient and outpatient units of the Department of Addictions and were divided into four groups according to the type of addiction: alcohol (61.0% of the sample); illicit drugs (25.4%); gambling (5.1%); combined (8.5%). All diagnoses were based on a standard clinical interview according to the ICD‐10/DSM‐IV criteria conducted by experienced psychiatrists. Specific PD assessment instruments were not administered. Information about the individual diagnoses was extracted from patients' medical records. The number of participants according to gender was almost balanced: males (n = 167, 57.0%) slightly prevailed over females (n = 126, 43%). The age range was 18–69 years (M = 37.3, SD = 11.1). The distribution of the highest attained education was as follows: there was 12.6% of patients with primary (elementary school) education; 77.1% with completed secondary (high school) education; 1.3% with some college, and 8.9% with an undergraduate or graduate degree from university.

The control sample consisted of university students of a master program in Psychology in collaboration with the Department of Psychology at the Faculty of Arts, Masaryk University in Brno, Czech Republic (n = 142, 38.3%), as well as working participants (n = 180, 48.5%), retired participants (n = 11, 2.9%), or participants indicating different working status (e.g., unemployed, maternity leave; n = 38; 10.3%). The number of participants according to sex was slightly disproportionate: females (n = 235, 63.3%) prevailed over males (n = 136, 36.7%). The age range was 18–84 years (M = 31.8, SD = 12.6). The distribution of the highest attained education was as follows: there was 1.6% of patients with primary (elementary school) education; 56.3% with completed secondary (high school) education; 3.8% with some college and 38.3% with an undergraduate or graduate degree from university.

In the clinical sample, the data collection was conducted either individually (in the case of outpatients with addiction) or in a group by the paper‐and‐pencil method during several appointments in the above‐mentioned institutions. Suitable respondents were included in the study following consultations with the heads of the departments, who also obtained their informed consent and allocated a time slot in the daily program for the administration of the test battery. The attending clinician introduced the administrators to the respondents. In the control sample, the questionnaires were administered either individually or in a group, using either the paper‐and‐pencil method or online data collection. The group data collection was conducted during three appointments with psychology students using exclusively the paper‐and‐pencil method. The administrators were introduced to the students by their tutor, who allowed the administration to be conducted in his or her course. Working and retired volunteers were asked to complete the test individually using the paper‐and‐pencil method or online after opening a link in the advert on social networks. Respondents were asked to carefully read the instructions before starting the test; all items were required. Trained administrators were ready to assist with technical queries.

Measures

Inventory of Personality Organization (IPO)

The full version of the IPO contains 83 items in a self‐report format that includes primary clinical scales and secondary interpersonal scales (Clarkin et al. 2001). The three primary clinical scales relate to the central dimensions of Kernberg's model of personality organization (i.e., identity diffusion, primitive psychological defenses, and reality testing). Items are rated on a 5‐point Likert scale from 1 (Not at all true) to 5 (Always true). For this study, we employed the 57‐item version of the IPO (Lenzenweger et al. 2001; Ellison and Levy 2012). The IPO was translated into Czech in several stages using the back‐translation method; (Brislin 1970) the authors aimed to achieve clinical accuracy while keeping linguistic concordance with the original. In the first stage, a psychologist experienced in working with the Kernberg's concept of personality organization translated all items into Czech, whose objective was the best possible correspondence of the translation and the clinical meaning of the original items. Another experienced psychologist revised the first translation. Final versions of the items were articulated by both translators in collaboration and submitted for back translation into English by an independent translator—a US university graduate with a psychological background. The back‐translation was sent to the author of the original version, who confirmed its sufficiency. The Czech translation of the IPO is now available upon request by the authors of this paper.

Borderline Personality Questionnaire (BPQ)

The BPQ is an 80‐item (true/false) self‐report measure comprising nine subscales corresponding to the nine DSM‐IV BPD criteria: impulsivity, affective instability, abandonment, relationships, self‐image, suicide/self‐mutilation, emptiness, intense anger, and quasi‐psychotic states (Poreh et al. 2006). Scores range from 0 to 80, where a higher score indicates a higher level of pathology. The BPQ was translated into Czech using the back‐translation method. In this study, we used the BPQ total score as equivalent to the ICD‐11 Borderline pattern specifier. The reliability of the total scale was α = 0.94 in the clinical sample and α = 0.93 in the control sample. These values are higher than the results of the only psychometric study to date with the Czech translation of the BPQ in a non‐clinical sample (Koutna 2016) and almost identical to the original version of the questionnaire (Chanen et al. 2008).

Defense Style Questionnaire (DSQ‐40)

The DSQ is a self‐report inventory designed to assess conscious representations of unconscious defense mechanisms. The preferred factor structure identified immature defenses (projection, passive aggression, acting out, isolation, devaluation, autistic fantasy, denial, displacement, dissociation, splitting, rationalization, and somatization), neurotic defenses (undoing, pseudo‐altruism, idealization, and reaction formation), and mature defenses (sublimation, humor, anticipation, and suppression) (Andrews 1989). The shortened DSQ consists of 40 items rated on a 9‐point Likert scale, from which scores for 20 defense mechanisms (two items for each) can be derived (Andrews et al. 1993). The DSQ‐40 was translated into Czech using the back‐translation method. We used the DSQ‐40 to test the initially proposed tripartite structure of IPO in relation to Kernberg's model, (Clarkin et al. 2001) separating the identity diffusion dimension from psychological defenses and reality testing. For the purposes of this study, we decided to merge mature defenses and neurotic defenses into a common factor of ‘mature defenses’, and the contrasting ‘immature defenses’ factor. This procedure is more congruent with Kernberg's definition of defenses within the concept of personality organization, distinguishing higher‐level defenses from lower‐level defenses (Kernberg 1981). Internal consistency values in our samples were: mature α = 0.56 for clinical sample (α = 0.63 for control sample) and immature defenses α = 0.73 (α = 0.74). These values are slightly higher than the results of the only psychometric study to date with the Czech translation of the DSQ‐40 using a non‐clinical sample (Cervenkova 2005). Moreover, they are only slightly worse than the results reported by Andrews, Singh, & Bond (Andrews et al. 1993) who assumed that a minimum value of α = 0.50 is sufficient for a questionnaire focused on conscious derivatives of unconscious processes.

Sociodemographic Questionnaire

In addition to all the above‐mentioned measures, basic demographic data (i.e., age, gender, and educational status) were collected by self‐reports.

Plan of Analysis

Our data cleaning procedures identified 47 individuals (seven in the clinical sample and 40 in the control sample) that showed patterned responses on the IPO (i.e., answering the same value for more than 90% of items). These participants were subsequently removed from the inferential analyses.

As the first step, we used exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to assess the number of latent factors that would underlie the IPO scale in its 57‐item version. This was tested separately in both samples. Parallel analysis with 5000 re‐samples as well as exploration of the item loadings was used to determine the number of factors to retain. After arriving at stable factor solution across both samples, we tested the fit of the model in exploratory structural equation modeling (ESEM) approach, informed by previous analysis by Ellison and Levy, (Ellison and Levy 2012) again separately for each sample. Then, we tested the ESEM model for invariance across samples.

Next, inspired by previous works by Zimmermann et al. (2013, 2015, 2020) and the limited clinical utility of an ESEM solution for the 57‐item version of the IPO, we aimed to create a shorter version of the measure that would, unlike the Zimmermann's et al. unidimensional approach, keep the resulting IPO tripartite factor structure while: (a) being more concise, and (b) providing a clearer factor solution that would be psychometrically valid within a CFA framework. Then, we tested the configural, metric, and scalar invariance of the resulting 14‐item IPO version.

Finally, we focused on the concurrent validity of the original 57‐item version as well as the newly derived 14‐item version of the IPO with regards to personality psychopathology, such as BPD criteria, measured by BPQ or system of psychological defenses, indicated by DSQ‐40. Each DSQ‐40 subscale (i.e., mature and immature defenses) was regressed on IPO latent factors separately. Mean scores were used for BPQ and DSQ‐40 subscales.

Geomin rotation was used for ESEM. We used weighted least squares estimation (WLSMV) for all ESEM and CFA models. This estimator is appropriate for ordered‐categorical variables, providing robust parameter estimates, standard errors, and fit indices even with non‐normal distributions. The adequacy of model fit was evaluated using the following criteria: CFI > 0.90, RMSEA < 0.08 (Hu and Bentler 1999). All analyses were done in Mplus 8.

Results

The proportion of females was higher in the control sample (63.3%) as compared to the clinical sample (43.0%), χ2(1) = 24.54, p < 0.001. T‐test comparing samples revealed that the samples differed based on age, as the clinical sample had higher average age (M = 37.3) compared to the control sample (M = 31.8), t (615.4) = −6.32, p < 0.001. Finally, the education level was higher in the control sample as compared to the clinical sample, χ2(3) = 95.64, p < 0.001.

IPO‐57 Model Fit and Latent Structure

For both samples, the results from parallel analysis suggested extracting three factors. Before proceeding with three factors, we also investigated a four‐factor model originally proposed by Ellison and Levy (Ellison and Levy 2012). However, this solution was less clear than the three‐factor one: 12 items showed loadings lower than 0.32 in the clinical sample, while five items did in the control sample. Beyond factors broadly corresponding to Ellison and Levy's Instability of self and others (ISO) and Psychosis (PSY), the third factor for clinical and control sample mostly included items from ISO, and Instability of behaviors, reflecting the tendency for impulsive behavior and unpredictability. For the fourth factor, the results in the clinical sample included four items that were originally part of ISO while there was only a single item with loading > 0.32 for the fourth factor in the control sample, suggesting that the three‐factor solution had a better fit.

In the next step, we tested the three‐factor structure in an ESEM setting. The results suggested adequate fit for both samples, clinical: χ2 (1428) = 2098.94, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.94, RMSEA = 0.04, 90% CI RMSEA [0.037, 0.044], control: χ2 (1428) = 2091.43, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.04, 90% CI RMSEA [0.034, 0.041]. The factor structure is quite like the one identified by a previous study conducted by Ellison and Levy, reflecting ISO, Instability of goals and behaviors (IGB; these were separate factors for Instability of goals and Instability of behaviors in the above‐mentioned study), and PSY. The loadings are shown in Table 1. With few exceptions, a quite similar factor structure emerged for both clinical and control samples. However, there were several differences among the samples, as items were more evenly distributed between factor 1 and factor 3 in the clinical sample than in the control sample (see Supporting Information S1: Table 2 for ordering of IPO‐57 items in the current study). Item 31 ("Long time since anyone told me something I did not know”) did not show a substantial (> 0.32) factor loading for either clinical or control sample. In addition, items 1 (“Feel either great joy or intense despair”), 27 (“Pick and drop hobbies and interests”), and 6 (“Life seems like a series of short stories”) showed loadings lower than 0.32 in the clinical sample. Except for item 49 ("People are basically good or bad”), both clinical and control samples showed overlap for primary loadings of 27 items on the ISO factor. Similarly, the PSY factor was well‐defined, with the same 11 items in both samples showing primary loadings on this factor. However, the definition of the IGB factor varied between samples. A total of 11 items showed primary loadings on this factor in the clinical sample, while there were only seven items in the control sample, with much lower estimates. Many of the items that loaded primarily on the IGB factor in the clinical sample loaded on the ISO factor in the control sample or showed substantial cross‐loadings with this factor.

IPO‐14 Model Fit and Latent Structure

Given that the IPO‐57 failed to achieve measurement invariance and that ESEM presented conceptual limitations for our purposes (specifically, the extensive cross‐loadings complicated interpretation and practical application), we pursued a systematic item selection strategy. Our goal was to identify a subset of items that would: (1) demonstrate measurement invariance across clinical and control samples, (2) provide a clear, interpretable factor structure, and (3) align with the theoretical structure of the full scale. We employed a data‐driven approach by fitting the 57‐item IPO in exploratory factor analysis within each sample separately, then selecting items that exhibited substantial loadings on their designated factors consistently across both clinical and control groups. Given that ISO and PSY factors were more clearly defined than the IGB factor, we only selected the top five items that showed high and balanced loadings for each item (i.e., the loading was not substantially higher in one sample than in the other). For the IGB factor, we identified five items with item loadings > 0.40 in each sample. From those, item 4 ("Life goals change frequently”) and item 16 (“Goals keep changing”) showed substantial residual covariance, as indicated by modification indices, and item 4 was subsequently removed, resulting in four items per this factor. We arrived at a 14‐item model, with five items per the ISO factor, five items per the PSY factor, and four items per the IGB factor (see Supporting Information S1: Table 1). The fit of the model in CFA for clinical sample was χ2 (74) = 129.61, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.98, RMSEA = 0.05, 90% CI RMSEA [0.036, 0.066]; for the control sample: χ2 (74) = 105.741, p = 0.009, CFI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.04, 90% CI RMSEA [0.019, 0.051]. The correlation between ISO and PSY was r = 0.43 (r = 0.41 in the control sample), ISO and IGB r = 0.54 (r = 0.74), and PSY and IGB r = 0.67 (r = 0.60; all correlations significant at p < 0.001).

Importantly, when comparing the samples, support for metric invariance was found, χ2 (11) = 19.638, p = 0.051, ΔCFI = 0.001, ΔRMSEA < 0.001. Moreover, scalar invariance was also confirmed, as indicated by the lack of substantial change in additional fit indices, χ2 (48) = 67.881, p = 0.031, ΔCFI = 0.001, ΔRMSEA = 0.003. This shows that the factor loadings and item thresholds were invariant across clinical and control sample, and the mean differences between samples are fully captured by the latent factors. The clinical sample showed higher means for all three factors. Specifically, the mean levels of ISO were M = 10.46 (SD = 4.02) for control sample and M = 13.10 (SD = 4.33), for clinical sample, t (575.47) = 7.72, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.63; for IGB, it was M = 5.67 (SD = 2.05) for control sample and M = 7.40 (SD = 2.83), for clinical sample, t (502.76) = 8.57, p < 0.001, d = 0.72; for PSY: M = 6.10 (SD = 2.38) for control sample and M = 7.20 (SD = 3.57), for clinical sample, t (474.22) = 4.39, p < 0.001, d = 0.37. The omega reliability coefficients of the IPO‐14 for the clinical sample were ISO ω = 0.790, PSY ω = 0.880, and IGB ω = 0.663. For the control sample, the omegas were ISO = 0.793, PSY ω = 0.851, and IGB ω = 0.686.

Discussion

The results of the present study contribute to the current state of knowledge in several ways. First, we sought to validate the multidimensional structure of the IPO‐57 in a Czech sample and evaluate its measurement invariance across clinical and non‐clinical populations—representing the first comprehensive psychometric evaluation of the IPO in the Czech context. Critically, when the full IPO‐57 failed to achieve measurement invariance, we pursued a secondary methodological objective—to develop a version of IPO that would be psychometrically valid, achieve measurement invariance across groups, and maintain adequate fit within a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) framework. Second, and theoretically most important, we aimed to further validate Kernberg's ORT model of borderline psychopathology within the ICD‐11 framework for PDs, which has been the subject of significant controversy since the development of the new classification (Poreh et al. 2006; Tyrer et al. 2019). We examined whether IPO dimensions provide clinically meaningful information for differentiating individuals with self‐reported BPD symptoms beyond the overall personality functioning severity and to what extend is this information currently captured by the nine diagnostic criteria for BPD in the ICD‐11 Borderline pattern specifier. More specifically, we investigated whether IPO dimensions—particularly those reflecting defensive functioning—offer incremental utility in understanding affect modulation difficulties characteristic of BPD.

Verification of the factor structure of the IPO‐CZ was a necessary first step from the point of view of its validity. Our data revealed three factors as the best solution for the primary scales of the measure, which aligns with previous studies' findings and supports the relevance of the original tripartite structure postulated by Kernberg as the basis of personality organization (Kernberg 1981). Although the existing studies on validation of the factor structure of the IPO agree on a clearly definable factor referring to identity pathology on the one hand and a factor referring to distortion of reality perception on the other, there is an apparent inconsistency regarding the factor referring to the presence of pathological psychological defenses. This inconsistency may be due to the choice of statistical approaches, as well as the type and size of research samples to which these approaches are applied, as pointed out by Ellison and Levy (Ellison and Levy 2012) in relation to the initial analysis of IPO factors by Lenzenweger et al. (2001) Using the ESEM analysis, we extracted a summary factor for Instability of goals and behaviors (IGB) in addition to the factor of Instability of self and others (ISO), and the factor of Psychosis (PSY). Related to Kernberg's (Kernberg 1981) original hypothesis, we hypothesize that the predominance of primitive defenses mainly based on splitting in borderline personality organization will influence both the ability to set goals and the regulation of behavior. If we consider the IGB factor in the context of the multifaceted conceptualization of personality functioning, (Preti et al. 2015) which assumes that the functionality of the identity in all its facets is linked to the individual's defense system, the more pronounced overlap of the IGB items with ISO in the general population can be interpreted as an overall less disturbed personality functioning compared to our clinical population. Since the IPO is primarily a clinical tool, it can be hypothesized that it will be more sensitive to latent psychopathological phenomena in patients showing signs of borderline personality organization than in respondents with neurotic or healthy personality functioning. Nevertheless, strong differences in factor loadings between both samples regarding item 1 and 27 seem informative for further investigation of borderline symptom distribution along a broader PD severity dimension.

Taking into account the mixed conclusions of previous studies devoted to the factor structure of IPO in relation to the originally postulated dimension of primitive defenses, our intention was to find a solution that would take into account the many‐mentioned shortcomings in the definitions of some problematic IPO items (Berghuis et al. 2009; Preti et al. 2015; Ellison and Levy 2012) and, at the same time, enable the construction of sufficiently robust factors, invariant across the clinical status of the respondents. As the IPO‐57 ESEM solution lacks practical utility due to item cross‐loadings, we aimed to find a shorter version which, in line with study goal towards ICD‐11, still keeps the original tripartite factor structure and provides adequate model fit and invariance across the control and clinical sample. Although research to date confirms that abbreviated versions of the IPO focusing on the three primary scales showed better factorial coherence than the original 57‐item version, they largely agree that the primitive defenses scale is either part of the identity scale (Smits et al. 2009; Cosentino et al. 2021) or a general factor of structural personality impairment (Zimmermann et al. 2013). However, our results do not confirm this trend. Our short version resembles the IPO‐16 proposed by Zimmermann et al. (2013) at first glance, but there are important conceptual and psychometric differences. Zimmermann et al. specified a second‐order model in which the three traditional IPO domains (Identity diffusion, Primitive defenses, Reality testing) load on a general higher‐order factor of structural impairment and argued that, given the very strong overlap among the first‐order domains, the IPO‐16 can be used essentially as a unidimensional severity measure. In contrast, we aimed to retain a multidimensional structure and therefore kept a three‐factor solution (albeit with factor definitions that differ from those reported in earlier IPO work). Moreover, Zimmermann et al. did not report measurement invariance analyses for their higher‐order model. Most importantly, the development rationale differed. The IPO‐16 was constructed to provide an economical index of general structural impairment. Our primary goal, by contrast, was to find a psychometrically sound solution that would be invariant across clinical and control sample; when this could not be supported for the full scale, we pursued a reduced item set designed to define each factor within a pre‐specified three‐factor framework. The 14‐item version showed sufficient robustness for three separate factors. In addition to its differentiation capacity, measurement invariance, regardless of the clinical status of the respondents, contributes to current knowledge, which can be seen as an important aspect of the instrument's validity. Despite the smaller number of items in each scale, we obtained good to adequate values of internal consistency of the individual factors of the IPO‐14, which approximately corresponds to, for example, the results of Verreault et al (Verreault et al. 2013).

Regarding the assessment of concurrent validity, our main goal was to verify the association between the IPO and borderline psychopathology in the context of the ICD‐11 model for PDs. First, it is necessary to point out that the ISO factor showed the strongest association with BPQ in both research samples, which confirms Kernberg's assumption about identity diffusion (i.e., instability in the perception of self and others) as a superior latent construct on the background of the symptomatic picture of borderline structured individuals (Kernberg 1981). This finding is not too surprising and supports the current dimensional trend of diagnosing PDs within the ICD‐11 (Blüml and Doering 2021; Reed 2018) with an emphasis on impairment in the degree of personality functioning in relation to oneself and others as a conditio sine qua non. On the other hand, a small but significant association of the IGB factor and BPQ confirmed in our clinical sample with both the 57‐item and 14‐item versions of the IPO suggests that the IGB factor in the clinical population may be the carrier of information that seems to be somewhat different from the information covered by ISO. Moreover, the positive associations of the BPQ Impulsivity and Intense anger factors with IGB in both samples, which are in sharp contrast to the negative or no association with ISO—especially when using the 14‐item version of the IPO—suggest that these factors may be more specific indicators of affective dysregulation in BPD than the Affective instability factor, which appears to be more closely associated with overall impairment in personality functioning in relation to self and others. This finding seems to contradict studies that assume the fusion of the original IPO factors of identity and primitive defenses into one common factor (Berghuis et al. 2009; Lenzenweger et al. 2001; Hörz‐Sagstetter et al. 2020; Ellison and Levy 2012; Smits et al. 2009; Cosentino et al. 2021) and is more in line with the original hypothesis of the separation of both dimensions in Kernberg's tripartite model of personality organization (Clarkin et al. 2007, 2020; Kernberg and Caligor 2005). Although our results in this regard support the hypothesis of the presence of “impulsive aggression” as a central characteristic of BPD, (Critchfield et al. 2004) it would be beneficial to further verify our findings with the IGB factor on samples of patients directly diagnosed with BPD.

In relation to ICD‐11, three studies deserve special attention considering our findings about the IGB. Unoka et al. (2022) in a recent study with IPO demonstrated that all three primary IPO factors (but not merged into one) are reliable indicators of the severity of personality psychopathology, pointing out, among other things, a greater likelihood of BPD diagnosis with an increasing degree of disintegration. Two other studies have recently shown that the ICD‐11 Borderline pattern specifier can be a good predictor of general PD severity but strongly correlated with the ICD‐11 trait domains of Disinhibition, Dissociality, and Negative Affectivity (Mulder et al. 2020; Gutiérrez et al. 2023). If we consider that negative affect regulation and dissocial manifestations are related to the degree of maturity of defense mechanisms, that is, the quality of superego functions, (Kernberg 1994) our associations of IGB with BPQ Impulsivity and Intense anger factors seem to support previous findings about the importance of neurobehavioral correlates of BPD, that is, negative emotionality and nonaffective constraint (Lenzenweger et al. 2012). In this regard, further research could aim at how the IPO‐14 IGB factor could be assessed as a measure of negative affect regulation and dissocial manifestations, for example, with respect to the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS‐20) (Bagby et al. 1994) or the Difficulty with Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) (Gratz and Roemer 2004).

Finally, the associations with DSQ‐40 also support our hypothesis about the separateness of the IGB factor from the ISO. Although this result contradicts Ellison and Levy's (Ellison and Levy 2012) study with the 57‐item IPO in which the external measures of defensive functioning related most strongly to the ISO factor, it nevertheless confirms their hypothesis about item modification to a clearer factorial solution. It should be noted that the mentioned study did not include a clinical sample. In our control sample, in the case of the 57‐item IPO version, a significant overlap of both DSQ factors with ISO was confirmed, but when using the 14‐item variant, this trend was no longer confirmed for immature defenses. In the case of the clinical sample, in which we assume a greater sensitivity to more subtle psychopathological phenomena, a clear predominance of the overlap of both types of defenses according to the DSQ with the IGB can be observed, especially when using the 14‐item version of the IPO. The association between IPO‐14 IGB and immature defenses in both samples and between IPO‐14 IGB and mature defenses in our clinical sample is consistent with Kernberg's premise about the presence of a wide range of psychological defenses on the continuum of personality organization (Kernberg 1981, 1994). Nevertheless, given the rather low internal consistency values of the DSQ‐40, especially in the case of mature defenses in our clinical sample, it is necessary to take these conclusions with caution.

From the psychodynamic view, our results, at least in the clinical population, support the perspective that each individual is developmentally equipped with an amalgam of defense mechanisms, including both mature‐neurotic and immature ones (Clarkin et al. 2007, 2020; Freud 1993). This may have important clinical consequences for therapeutic work with different types of patients exhibiting BPD symptoms. For example, considering that suppression is more related to shame and conscious fear of embarrassment – as opposed to unconscious guilt associated with repression (Hsu 1949) – it suggests that some BPD patients may use suppression to avoid hostile affect in interpersonal situations, where they expect to be punished by condemnation or rejection for expressing anger. According to Kernberg, (Kernberg 1994) patients who continue to experience physical or sexual abuse in an unmitigated form show relatively fewer manifestations of unconscious guilt because they regress to paranoid defense mechanisms with a predominance of intense anger and hatred in interpersonal situations. Suppression can thus be a self‐preservation maneuver to reduce or hide anxiety from a perceived threat in a relationship (e.g., criticism/abandonment), especially in more anxious and avoidant BPD patients who react sensitively to the actions of others perceived as hostile (Critchfield et al. 2008).

Taken together, regarding the ICD‐11, our conclusions on the relationship between IGB and BPQ Impulsivity and Intense anger factors, as well as between IGB and defenses according to the DSQ‐40, support the importance of assessing the constellation of defense mechanisms and their influence on affective dysregulation beyond the overall disturbance in personality functioning. At the same time, they indicate the importance of addressing impulsive aggression as a core component, which seems to be an important differential diagnostic feature in assessing patients with the clinical picture of BPD. Although our results indicate that the IGB factor captures information that is contained in the Borderline pattern specifier, it is a task for future studies to determine whether it would be possible to better capture it using other ICD‐11 trait specifiers (e.g., Disinhibition, Dissociality, and Negative Affectivity). Nevertheless, it can be concluded that the IPO‐14 can be a reliable tool for screening BPD in the context of the ICD‐11 model for PDs because it addresses both the overall disturbance in relation to self and others and the central component of impulsive aggression.

However, we are aware of several limitations in relation to the generalizability of our findings. Although our results—particularly in a clinical population—somewhat supported the hypothesis that the IPO IGB factor may be a carrier of incremental information on affect regulation corresponding to psychotherapeutic research (Vermote et al. 2009) and current neuropsychological (Lenzenweger et al. 2012) and attachment research in the field of affective dysregulation in patients with BPD, (Critchfield et al. 2008) we did not use any instrument for the specific assessment of negative affect. In this regard, three directions for future research are recommended: (1) additional application of tools for measuring other neurobehavioral correlates (e.g., nonaffective constraint) such as the constraint scale within the Multidimensional Personality Questionnaire (Fertuck and Melara 2020) or DERS; (Gratz and Roemer 2004) (2) application of tools for measuring other trait specifiers in the context of ICD‐11, such as the Personality Inventory for DSM‐5‐Brief Form Plus Modified; (Riegel et al. 2021) and (3) inclusion of a clinical interview, such as the Structured Interview of Personality Organization‐Revised (STIPO‐R) (Clarkin et al. 2016). In relation to our hypothesis of suppression as a defense mechanism by which BPD patients might prevent the manifestation of unwanted affects in interpersonal contact, the employment of the STIPO‐R beyond the scope of self‐assessment methods seems more than desirable. With its help, the hypothesis of the lack of negative affect in the interview as an indicator of identity disturbance in BPD (Walter et al. 2009) can be further verified. At the same time, due to the STIPO‐R's index of pathological narcissism, the presence of a grandiose self can be indicated. The grandiose self seems to be one of the main feature in differentiating between BPD patients of various levels of severity and patients with narcissistic PD, (Diamond et al. 2021) who, at the level of the ICD‐11 specifiers of personality traits, may, in common with BPD patients, show the predominance of personality psychopathology in Negative Affectivity and Dissociality (Riegel et al. 2022). As this is the first study dealing with the psychometric properties of the newly developed IPO‐14, it is desirable to further verify more detailed characteristics regarding various aspects of validity and reliability in culturally, demographically and clinically diverse samples. Given the absence of correlations with the PSY factor in both of our samples, future research should also include individuals with some prodromal or other level of psychotic symptoms to properly assess this area of the scale. To increase its clinical applicability, it could also be useful to establish normative cut‐off values. Based on previous experience with IPO in randomized control trials (e.g., Giesen‐Giesen‐Bloo et al. 2006), another avenue for future research could be the use of the IPO‐14 to assess the therapeutic efficacy of BPD treatments. Such studies could yield recommendations for treatment based on what defense mechanisms a patient may be primarily engaging in.

Conclusions

In accordance with our hypothesis, borderline psychopathology features, as measured by the IPO, appears to fit the ORT conceptualization of BPD, in which identity pathology is closely linked to defense mechanism‐based affect management (Clarkin et al. 2007, 2020). More precisely, while the ISO factor appears to be a strongly associated with impaired personality functioning in terms of general self‐other disturbance, the IGB factor is a specifier of the impulsive aggression—typical feature of BPD patients that should not be ignored. From this perspective, deciding on the presence or absence of BPD based on the degree of overall impairment in relation to self and others seems to be somewhat reductionist, as it does not adequately capture the affective component. According to the ORT, the latter is a fundamental connecting link of object dyads, which can be considered the cornerstones in the formation of identity (Kernberg and Caligor 2005). Thus, from our results in relation to ICD‐11, the Borderline pattern specifier (as measured by the BPQ factors) appears to correlate primarily with overall personality pathology and secondarily might be seen as a carrier of information about the level of psychological defenses responsible for controlling impulsivity and hostile affects – particularly in clinical population. This insight seems to be also supported by the associations of the IGB with defense mechanisms of varying severity, especially when using the 14‐item version of the IPO, which demonstrated a more straightforward factor structure than the original 57‐item version and was also invariant according to the clinical status of the respondents. Although our conclusions must be seen under scrutiny of numerous limitations and recommendations for future research, from both a clinical and empirical perspective, the results of this study encourage consideration of a paradigm shift in relation to BPD. If we have been dealing with the question of what BPD is, with the ICD‐11, the question of what BPD is not seems more justified. This conceptual framework invites examination of how the introduction of a new classification system may shape the effectiveness of treatment approaches in patients located at different levels of personality organization, defined not only by the degree of identity pathology but also by specific constellations of defense mechanisms.
