Female mice with a Xist deletion in B cells can develop lupus-associated phenotypes
Authors: Claudia D. Lovell, Nikhil Jiwrajka, Natalie E. Toothacre, Hayley K. Amerman, Michael P. Cancro, Montserrat C. Anguera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease preferentially observed in women. X-linked gene expression in XX females is equalized with that in XY males by X chromosome inactivation (XCI). However, B cells from female patients with SLE and mouse models of SLE exhibit aberrant X-linked gene expression and mislocalization of Xist RNA, a critical regulator of XCI, suggesting that impaired XCI may contribute to the disease. Here, some female mice harboring a B cell-specific Xist deletion (“Xist cKO”) spontaneously developed SLE phenotypes, including expanded activated B cell subsets, disease-specific autoantibodies, and glomerulonephritis. Pristane treatment of Xist cKO mice led to an increased expansion of activated B cell subsets, resulting in higher anti-dsDNA autoantibody production. Activated B cells from Xist cKO mice with SLE phenotypes had an increased expression of X-linked and autosomal genes, depletion of X-linked H3K27me3, and were more responsive to stimulation. This work suggests that impaired XCI maintenance in B cells directly contributes to SLE-associated phenotypes in a female-biased manner.

INTRODUCTION

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) exhibits a strong female sex bias,1–3 but the mechanisms underlying this sex bias are not well understood.2,4 While there is some evidence for sex hormone contributions to this bias,5–7 studies have demonstrated that SLE risk increases with the number of X chromosomes carried by an individual, suggesting important genetic contributions from the X chromosome. SLE risk in males with Klinefelter syndrome (47, XXY) is 14-fold higher than in 46, XY males and similar to that in 46, XX females.8 Further, the prevalence of SLE in females with polysomy X (XXX) is roughly twice that of 46, XX females and more than 20-fold that of 46, XY males.9 Together, these observations indicate that X chromosome dosage is positively associated with SLE susceptibility.

To ensure dosage compensation of X-linked genes relative to XY males, mammals with multiple X chromosomes utilize X chromosome inactivation (XCI), a complex and multi-layered epigenetic mechanism. XCI is initiated in early embryonic development when the long non-coding RNA Xist is upregulated from the future inactive X chromosome (Xi). Xist RNA then coats the Xi in cis, resulting in an allele-specific accumulation of multiple epigenetic modifications, including repressive histone marks (H3K27me3 and H2AK119-ubiquitin), the histone variant macroH2A, and DNA methylation.10 These heterochromatic modifications silence most genes across the Xi, except for some that homeostatically escape silencing and are expressed biallelically from the Xi and the active X chromosome (Xa).11–14 While the choice of which X chromosome to silence during XCI initiation is random, transcriptional repression of the chosen Xi is maintained throughout lifetime of a cell and by its clonal daughters. The fidelity of XCI maintenance in female somatic cells is, therefore, an important regulator of X-linked gene dosage in XX individuals.

In contrast to other somatic cell types, B cells exhibit a unique mechanism of XCI maintenance.15 The Xi in naive B cells lacks cytological enrichment of Xist RNA and some heterochromatic histone modifications, and antigen stimulation triggers the return of these modifications to the Xi.15,16 However, circulating B cells isolated from female patients with SLE exhibit features of impaired XCI maintenance, including abnormal XIST RNA localization patterns, reduced enrichment of heterochromatic H3K27me3 and H2AK119-ubiquitin modifications at the Xi, and aberrant X-linked gene expression.15,17 Furthermore, lymphocytes from several mouse models of spontaneous lupus-like disease with varying degrees of female bias (NZB/W F1, NZM2328, and MRL/lpr) also have aberrant Xist RNA localization patterns and reduced heterochromatic mark enrichment at the Xi.18,19 Given that B cells contribute to SLE pathogenesis20–29 and that the X chromosome is enriched for genes with immunomodulatory functions that are particularly relevant to B cell function, these findings have important implications for the female-biased development of SLE. Indeed, many X-linked genes with immune functions, including TLR7,30–32 TASL,33 BTK,34 IRAK1,35 CD99,36 IL9R,37 and CXCR3,38 have been reported to be overexpressed in peripheral blood lymphocytes from patients with SLE.39 Moreover, Tlr7/TLR7 gain-of-function in both mouse models and humans confers lupus-like phenotypes,32,40 and Tasl deletion protects against the development of SLE-like disease in multiple mouse models.41,42 Together, these observations suggest that impaired XCI maintenance in B cells may result in the aberrant expression of immunomodulatory X-linked genes that contribute to the pathogenesis of SLE. While XIST deletion in transformed human B cells results in increased expression of TLR7 and some X-linked genes,43 it is not currently known whether disrupted XCI maintenance in B cells is sufficient to drive SLE disease phenotypes in vivo.

In this study, we asked whether perturbing XCI maintenance in B cells using a B cell-specific Xist deletion could spontaneously cause SLE-like disease or increase disease features in a model of chemically induced SLE-like disease. Using Mb1Cre/WT Xistfl/fl mice (Xist cKO), we discovered that some female Xist cKO mice spontaneously develop features of SLE, including disease-specific anti-nuclear autoantibodies, proliferative glomerulonephritis, and increased numbers of activated B cells implicated in SLE pathogenesis, including age-associated B cells (ABCs)44–47 and plasma cells.48 Furthermore, following pristane injection, female Xist cKO mice developed more SLE-like features compared to their wild-type (WT) counterparts, with higher levels of anti-dsDNA autoantibodies and greater expansions of activated B cell subsets including class-switched B cells, GL7+ activated B cells, plasma cells, and ABCs than controls. ABCs and GL7+ activated B cells from Xist cKO mice overexpressed various X-linked genes. Finally, B cells from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice had reduced levels of H3K27me3 across the X chromosome and were more responsive to endosomal toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, evidenced by enhanced differentiation into plasma cells and increased production of autoantibodies and cytokines compared with controls. Taken together, our work demonstrates that the disruption of XCI maintenance in B cells via the deletion of Xist can contribute to SLE-like phenotypes, thereby providing a novel pathogenic mechanism influencing the female sex bias of SLE.

RESULTS

Some female Xist cKO mice spontaneously develop SLE-like features with age

We used the Mb1-Cre transgene to delete Xist throughout the B cell lineage, during the maintenance phase of XCI, on the BALB/c and C57BL/6 backgrounds. Mb1 is expressed starting at the early pro-B cell stage, after the maintenance phase of XCI has been established (Figure 1A). Xist2lox/2lox mice were successively mated to Mb1WT/cre mice to derive heterozygous “Xist +/cKO” (XistWT/fl; Mb1WT/cre) and homozygous “Xist cKO/cKO” (Xistfl/fl; Mb1WT/cre) female mice. Xist deletion was confirmed in CD23+ splenic B cells, using sequential Xist RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and immunofluorescence for H2AK119-ubiquitin (Ub; Figures S1A–S1C) and qPCR (Figure S1D), which demonstrated Xist deletion from the Xi in 100% of Xist cKO/cKO cells and ~50% of Xist +/cKO cells, consistent with random XCI. Female Xist +/cKO and Xist cKO/cKO mice were viable, with comparable survival to WT controls (Figure S1E). Adult C57BL/6 female Xist cKO mice aged 6–8 months had similar percentages and numbers of total B220+ B cells as WT female mice in both the spleen (Figure S1F) and bone marrow (Figure S1G), suggesting that Xist loss in the B cell compartment does not impact gross B cell development. Surprisingly, some female BALB/c Xist cKO mice aged ≥12 months spontaneously developed very high levels of serum anti-dsDNA autoantibodies that were either comparable to or even higher than those observed in female NZB/W F1 mice, a heavily female-biased mouse model of spontaneous SLE-like disease,49 while no WT mice developed anti-dsDNA of this magnitude (Figures 1B, S2A, and S2B). Importantly, we recapitulated previous observations50,51 that serum anti-dsDNA autoantibody levels increase with age in our cohort of mice but found that some Xist +/cKO and Xist cKO/cKO mice had elevated anti-dsDNA autoantibodies well beyond the range observed in advanced age (Figure S2C). Furthermore, anti-dsDNA autoantibody enrichment in those mice was not due to higher serum levels of total IgG (Figure S2D).

These data indicate that the frequency of Xist cKO/cKO female mice (BALB/c) ≥ 12 months old spontaneously developing serologic features of SLE is around 20%. In a cohort of n = 6 female Xist cKO/cKO mice with elevated serum anti-dsDNA (“Xist cKO High”), n = 6 age-matched female Xist cKO/cKO mice with low serum anti-dsDNA (“Xist cKO Low”), and n = 11 age-matched female WT mice (WT), we found that Xist cKO High mice not only had significantly higher serum levels of anti-dsDNA autoantibodies as measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), but also significantly more anti-dsDNA autoantibody-secreting cells in both the spleen (Figures 1C and S2E) and the bone marrow (Figures 1D and S2F), as measured by anti-dsDNA enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot (ELISPOT). We also found that Xist cKO High mice had significantly higher levels of serum autoantibodies against Smith-U1RNP, a ribonuclear protein complex containing the Smith antigen (a disease-specific autoantigen observed in patients with SLE)54 (Figure 1E).

Given the identification of multiple disease-specific autoantibodies in Xist cKO High mice, we sought to determine whether the sera of these mice contained additional autoantibodies implicated in autoimmunity. We performed autoantigen microarray profiling of serum from this cohort and found that Xist cKO High mice exhibited statistically significant enrichment of 16 of 120 autoantibodies examined (Table S1) compared with Xist cKO Low and WT samples. Xist cKO High serum contained significant enrichment of autoantibodies directed against the highly SLE-specific Smith antigens (Smith, Smith D, and Smith D1), the U1-snRNP B complex, and dsDNA. Xist cKO High mice also had enrichment of autoantibodies associated with other female-biased autoimmune diseases including Sjögren’s disease, dermatomyositis, systemic sclerosis, and multiple sclerosis (Ro 60/SS-A,55 TIF1 gamma,56 CENPA, and MBP, respectively57) (Figure 1F). While some autoantibodies from the array were decreased in Xist cKO High mice (Figure S2G), they displayed less consistent expression patterns across samples than the enriched autoantibodies.

Given the spontaneous development of SLE-specific autoantibodies, we sought to determine whether Xist cKO High mice also developed renal pathology characteristic of lupus nephritis. Xist cKO High mice demonstrated pronounced features of glomerular hypercellularity (Figures 1G and 1L) and hypertrophy (Figures 1H and 1L), recapitulating cardinal histopathologic manifestations of lupus nephritis. The glomerular hypercellularity in this subset of mice was specifically characterized by both segmental to global endocapillary loop hypercellularity and mesangial hypercellularity (Figure 1L). Xist cKO High mice developed variably moderate to sometimes severe glomerular mesangial thickening, glomerular cystic dilation, and glomerular basement membrane thickening (Figures 1I and 1L). Xist cKO High mice had similar tubular disease (Figure 1J) and interstitial inflammation (Figure 1K) as controls. Interestingly, we also found that some Xist cKO High mice had larger foci of lymphocytic immune infiltrates of CD19+ B cells and CD3+ T cells in their salivary glands (Figure S2J), a hallmark of Sjögren’s disease, highlighting that B cell-specific Xist loss may also contribute to the development of serologic and pathologic features of another female-biased autoimmune disease. Thus, some aged BALB/c female Xist cKO mice spontaneously develop multiple autoantibodies associated with female-biased autoimmune diseases and glomerular pathology associated with SLE.

Xist cKO High mice have increased numbers of activated B cell subsets, CD11c+ ABCs, and plasma cells

Because we found that some Xist cKO/cKO mice spontaneously developed serologic and histopathologic features of SLE, we next sought to identify changes in the B cell compartment that could contribute to these disease phenotypes. Xist cKO High mice had similar numbers of total splenic B cells and similar frequencies of splenic and bone marrow B cells (Figures S2H and S2I) as Xist cKO Low and WT mice. However, we observed an expansion in the percentage and total number of several activated splenic B cell subsets (Figure 2A) in Xist cKO High mice, including CD11c+ ABCs (B220+CD19+CD11c+) (Figure 2B), which have been demonstrated to be elevated in patients with SLE and mouse models of lupus-like disease58,59 and splenic GL7+ B cells (B220+CD19+IgM−IgD−GL7+), an activated population that includes germinal center B cells, which were shown to be expanded in mouse models of SLE60 (Figure 2C). Xist cKO High mice had an increased number of IgM-IgD class-switched B cells compared with controls (Figure 2D). Xist cKO High mice also exhibited an increased frequency and number of splenic short-lived plasma cells (“SLPCs”; IgD−B220+CD138+), considered to be the primary producers of anti-dsDNA autoantibodies in some mouse models of SLE,48 while their long-lived plasma cells (“LLPCs”; IgD−B220−CD138+) were similar in number and frequency to those of controls (Figure 2E). Interestingly, 6-month-old C5/BL/6 Xist cKO/cKO mice demonstrated some increases in activated splenic B cell subsets, including a slightly higher number of GL7+ B cells (Figure S2K), class-switched B cells (Figure S2L), and SLPCs (Figure S2M), but not CD11c+ ABCs (Figure S2N) or LLPCs (Figure S2O), suggesting that some of these populations may expand before the development of serologic autoimmunity. In contrast to the splenic plasma cell pool, the bone marrow plasma cell pool is not expanded in Xist cKO High mice compared to Xist cKO Low and WT mice (Figure S2P) despite our finding that Xist cKO High mice have more anti-dsDNA-producing cells in the bone marrow compared with controls (Figure 1D). In summary, these findings demonstrated that the loss of Xist in the B cell compartment leads to the spontaneous development of serologic, histopathological, and cellular features of human SLE in a non-autoimmune prone background, suggesting that the appropriate maintenance of XCI in B cells is critical for restricting autoreactive immune processes.

ABCs from Xist cKO High mice upregulate some X-linked genes and have altered expression of many autosomal genes

To determine how Xist loss affects X-linked gene expression and contributes to the development of SLE-like disease, we performed RNA sequencing on B cell subsets from Xist cKO High mice (n = 3, 17.9 ± 1.3 months), Xist cKO Low mice (n = 4, 17.9 ± 4.0 months), and WT controls (n = 6, 18.9 ± 3.4 months). First, we sequenced splenic CD23+ B cells, which include mature naive follicular B cells that serve as progenitors for activated B cell subsets, including germinal center, memory, and plasma cells.61 Principal-component analysis (PCA) distinguished CD23+ B cells from Xist cKO High mice, while Xist cKO Low and WT mice are mostly clustered together (Figure S3A). We observed 3 X-linked genes that were differentially expressed in Xist cKO High CD23+ B cells (Figures 3A and 3B; Table S2) and 2 X-linked DEGs in Xist cKO Low CD23+ B cells (Figures 3C and 3D; Table S3) relative to WT controls, which includes the expected downregulation of Xist in Xist cKO samples (Figure S3B). The small number of differentially expressed X-linked genes may reflect the cellular heterogeneity of the CD23+ B cell pool in Xist cKO High mice, which may mask changes in X-linked gene expression occurring in individual CD23+ cellular subsets. Notably, CD23+ B cells from Xist cKO High mice, but not Xist cKO Low mice, exhibited significant upregulation of X-linked Kif4 (kinesin family member 4 protein) (Figures 3A and 3B). Examination of autosomal transcriptional changes between Xist cKO High and WT mice revealed altered expression of n = 86 genes (Figures 3E and S3C; Table S2), and upregulated DEGs were enriched for genes implicated in cell cycle, cell division, and proliferation (Figure S3E), likely reflecting the expansion of activated GL7+ CD23+ cells in Xist cKO High mice (Figure S3D).

We next examined the transcriptional changes in activated splenic B cell subsets: CD11c+ ABCs (which are expanded in Xist cKO High mice relative to Xist cKO Low or WT mice) and CD138+B220− LLPCs. X-linked TLR7 drives ABC activation and differentiation62; thus, ABCs are likely to be particularly sensitive to gene expression changes of TLR7 pathway members (Tlr7, Tasl, and Irak1). Gene expression profiles of ABCs from Xist cKO High mice (n = 3) and WT mice (n = 4) were distinct, as revealed by PCA (Figure S3G). We observed 8 upregulated X-linked genes in Xist cKO High ABCs (Figures 3F and 3G; Table S4) and 4 downregulated X-linked genes including Xist (Figure S3H). Xist cKO High ABCs had an increased expression of Tasl, the aforementioned X-linked TLR7 pathway member. TASL (CXORF21) was first identified in a genome-wide associated study (GWAS) as a potential causal variant of SLE,33,63 and it is upregulated in immune cells from patients with SLE.33 TASL is particularly important for the signal transduction cascade downstream of endosomal TLRs including TLR7.64,65 Expression of Tlr7 was slightly, but not statistically significantly, increased in Xist cKO High ABCs compared with WT counterparts (Figure S3I), suggesting that multiple members of the TLR7 pathway may be upregulated in Xist cKO High ABCs to varying degrees. More generally, there was aberrant genome-wide transcription in ABCs from Xist cKO High mice, where 142 genes were upregulated and 166 were downregulated (Figures 3H and S3J; Table S4). Differentially upregulated genes were enriched for pathways of lymphocyte activation, inflammatory response, and lymphocyte proliferation (Figure S3K), which might have contributed to CD11c+ ABC cellular expansion in Xist cKO High mice. Downregulated genes were also enriched for functions involved in immune response pathways (Figure S3L). Unlike ABCs, Xist cKO High LLPCs did not segregate from WT LLPCs according to the PCA (Figure S3M), and differential gene expression analysis revealed minimal X-linked (Figure 3I; Table S5) and autosomal (Figure 3J; Table S5) gene expression differences, suggesting that LLPCs are not transcriptionally sensitive to Xist loss. Thus, CD11c+ ABCs, which expand in Xist cKO High mice, are particularly sensitive to Xist deletion, as they exhibited greater numbers of differentially expressed X-linked genes compared with LLPCs and CD23+ B cells (Figure 3K). To investigate if the increased Tasl expression in CD11c+ ABCs resulted from both the Xi and the Xa, we used single-molecule RNA FISH (smFISH) imaging to examine nascent monoallelic and biallelic transcription. We isolated CD11c+ ABCs from Xist cKO High mice (n = 2) and age-matched WT mice (n = 2) for two-color smFISH, using probes specific to exonic and intronic regions of Tasl. We found that ~60% of CD11c+ ABCs from Xist cKO High mice had biallelic Tasl expression compared with ~20% of CD11c+ ABCs from WT mice with biallelic Tasl expression (Figure 3J). These findings suggested that the altered expression of X-linked and autosomal genes resulting from impaired XCI maintenance in ABCs may promote their expansion and, ultimately, contribute to their role in the spontaneous development of SLE-like disease in a female-biased manner.

Pristane-treated Xist cKO/cKO mice generate more anti-DNA antibodies, activated B cells, ABCs, and plasma cells relative to the treated WT mice.

Spontaneous lupus-like disease is not fully penetrant in female Xist cKO mice. Thus, to determine how X-linked dosage imbalances impact B cell function in the context of autoimmunity, we used pristane to induce SLE-like disease in female Xist cKO/cKO (“Xist cKO”) mice and WT littermates. Pristane-induced SLE-like disease exhibits a female bias66 and recapitulates a number of features of human disease, including elevated anti-nuclear autoantibodies (anti-DNA, -chromatin, -Smith, and -RNP), glomerulonephritis,67 and the type 1 interferon-stimulated gene signature observed in the peripheral blood of patients with SLE.68 Furthermore, the development of pristane-induced disease requires signaling through X-linked Tlr7, the Tlr7 adaptor MyD88, and type I IFN receptor,69–72 suggesting that this model relies on molecular drivers similar to those implicated in human SLE.32 We, therefore, injected female BALB/c Xist cKO mice (n = 36) and WT mice (n = 46) with pristane and injected WT mice with PBS (n = 10) as a negative control. We quantified serum anti-DNA levels and proteinuria within these cohorts over 9 months and collected spleens for flow cytometry at 4, 7, or 9 months post-pristane injection and kidneys for pathological assessment at 7 months post-pristane injection (Figure 4A). All pristane-treated mice developed elevated levels of anti-DNA autoantibodies, starting at 3 months post-injection, and Xist cKO mice developed significantly higher serum anti-DNA levels than pristane-treated female WT mice over 7 months post-pristane injection (Figure 4B). Pristane-treated Xist cKO mice also had higher levels of serum anti-DNA autoantibodies at 9 months post-pristane injection (Figure 4C). All pristane-treated mice developed proteinuria comparable to WT PBS controls, but we did not observe differences in proteinuria between Xist cKO and WT mice over 9 months of disease (Figure S4A). Pristane-treated female Xist cKO mice trended toward slightly more severe total glomerular kidney pathology (mean glomerular pathology score = 6.8) compared with WT mice (mean glomerular pathology score = 4.6) at 7 months post-injection (Figure 4D) but had similar tubular kidney pathology (Figure 4E) and interstitial inflammation (Figure 4F). Specifically, Xist cKO mice treated with pristane tended to demonstrate more pronounced glomerular hypercellularity, as well as glomerular mesangial thickening, basement membrane loop thickening, and secondary glomerular hypertrophy compared with pristane-treated and untreated WT mice (Figures 4G–4I). These mice also developed several other morphologic changes within glomeruli that are associated with active glomerulonephritis in humans with lupus nephritis,73 including crescent formation and mesangiolysis (Figure 4J). Thus, Xist cKO mice with pristane-induced SLE-like disease developed more severe serologic autoimmunity and perhaps slightly more severe glomerular pathology compared with pristane-treated WT mice.

Next, we examined the impact of a B cell-specific Xist deletion on splenic B cell subsets in the context of pristane-induced SLE-like disease at 4, 7, and 9 months post-pristane injection. Pristane-treated Xist cKO mice had a similar frequency and number of splenic B220+ B cells as pristane-treated WT mice at all time points, but the frequency of B cells contracted in pristane-treated mice compared with that in non-treated controls after 9 months of disease (Figure S4B). Pristane-treated Xist cKO mice had a higher frequency of class-switched splenic B cells than pristane-treated WT mice at 4, 7, and 9 months post-pristane injection; however, the total number of class-switched splenic B cells was greater in Xist cKO pristane mice only at 9 months post-pristane injection (Figure 5A). GL7+ activated B cells, which include germinal center B cells, were enriched in frequency in Xist cKO pristane mice at all time points measured, as well as in number at 7 and 9 months post-pristane injection (Figure 5B). SLPCs were enriched in frequency and number in Xist cKO pristane mice at 7 and 9 months post-pristane injection (Figure 5C), while LLPCs were increased in frequency in Xist cKO mice at 9 months post-pristane injection (Figure S4C). Finally, CD11c+ ABCs were enriched in Xist cKO mice in frequency at 7 and 9 months post-pristane injection and in number at 9 months post-pristane injection relative to pristane-treated WT mice (Figure 5D). These findings indicate a temporal distinction in the expansion of activated B cell subsets in pristane-treated Xist cKO mice, with the expansion of class-switched and GL7+ B cells occuring as early as 4 months post-pristane injection, followed by an expansion of SLPCs and CD11c+ ABCs at 7 months post-pristane injection.

Xist +/cKO mice develop milder pristane-induced SLE phenotypes than Xist cKO/cKO mice

Because some Xist +/cKO mice spontaneously developed elevated SLE-associated anti-dsDNA autoantibodies but at lower levels than Xist cKO/cKO mice (Figure S2A), we investigated the severity of pristane-induced SLE phenotypes in female BALB/c Xist +/cKO mice. We injected female Xist +/cKO mice (n = 23) and WT mice (n = 46) and measured serum anti-DNA autoantibodies and urine protein over 9 months, quantified kidney pathology at 7 months, and characterized the expansion of splenic B cell subsets using flow cytometry (Figure S5A). Xist +/cKO mice developed similar levels of serum anti-DNA autoantibodies as pristane-treated WT mice over 7 months (Figure S5B) yet had higher anti-dsDNA autoantibodies at 9 months post-pristane injection than pristane-treated WT mice (Figure S5C). Pristane-treated Xist +/cKO mice developed similar levels of proteinuria over time as pristane-treated WT mice (Figure S5D). Glomerular and tubular pathology and interstitial inflammation were also similar between WT and Xist +/cKO mice after 7 months of pristane treatment (Figures S5E–S5G). Heterozygous Xist deletion did not affect total B cell numbers, as the total splenic B cell pool was similar in WT and Xist +/cKO mice treated with pristane (Figure S5H). However, we observed changes in the splenic B cell compartment similar to, but more subtle than, those observed in pristane-treated Xist cKO/cKO mice. Expansions of activated B cells in pristane-treated Xist +/cKO mice, including class-switched B cells (Figure S5I), GL7+ activated B cells (Figure S5J), CD138+B220+ SLPCs (Figure S5K) and CD138+B220−LLPCs (Figure S5L), and CD11c+ ABCs (Figure S5M) were more modest than in pristane-treated Xist cKO/cKO mice, and some expansions were delayed compared to mice with homozygous loss of Xist. These findings suggest that pristane treatment of mice with heterozygous Xist deletion yields similar, but less severe, phenotypes compared with Xist cKO/KO mice.

ABCs and GL7+ activated B cells from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice upregulate some X-linked genes and many autosomal genes

To determine how Xist loss impacts X-linked dosage compensation and contributes to the increased severity of pristane-induced disease observed in Xist cKO/cKO mice, we performed RNA sequencing on splenic B cell subsets expanded in Xist cKO mice treated with pristane for 7 months (age: 10–12 months). CD11c+ ABCs from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice (n = 4) and WT mice (n = 6) mice were mostly transcriptionally distinct, as revealed by PCA (Figure S6A), and demonstrated changes in X-linked gene expression (n = 13 X-linked DEGs) (Figures 6A and 6B; Table S6). Interestingly, we again observed that Tasl was upregulated in CD11c+ ABCs from Xist cKO mice treated with pristane, along with Dmd, Cfp, Ftx, Rp2, and Sh3kbp1 (also known as Cin85), an adapter protein essential for positive B cell activation through the B cell receptor.74 Of the 12 X-linked DEGs found in CD11c+ ABCs from Xist cKO mice with spontaneous SLE (Figures 3F and 3G) and the 13 X-linked DEGs found in ABCs from Xist cKO mice with pristane-induced SLE, 6 X-linked DEGs were shared, including the downregulated genes Xist and Tsix and the upregulated genes Cfp, Dmd, Rp2, and Tasl (Figure 6C). We also observe that 264 autosomal genes were differentially expressed in Xist cKO ABCs (Figures 6D and S6B; Table S6) and that most genes (234 genes, 87% of all DEGs) were downregulated in pristane-treated Xist cKO ABCs compared with pristane-treated WT ABCs. Differentially upregulated genes were enriched for immune response pathways and cellular pH pathways (Figure S6C); differentially downregulated genes were implicated in pathways of cytokine production, responses to external stimuli, and inflammatory responses (Figure S6D).

Splenic activated GL7+ B cells from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice (n = 5) and WT mice (n = 5 mice) were also transcriptionally distinct, as revealed by PCA (Figure S6E), and exhibited a large number (n = 17) of differentially expressed X-linked genes, of which the majority were upregulated (Figures 6E and 6F; Table S7). Interestingly, among the upregulated X-linked genes in GL7+ B cells from Xist cKO mice, we again observed Tasl and Ftx (Figure 6F), which were also upregulated in CD11c+ ABCs. Upregulated X-linked genes also include Firre, a long non-coding RNA that regulates 3D structure of the Xi and has a role in hematopoiesis where its overexpression results in the increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.75,76 Xist was significantly downregulated, as expected (Figure S6F). GL7+ B cells from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice exhibited differential expression of 293 autosomal DEGs (Figures 6G and S6G; Table S7); upregulated genes were associated with pathways involved in metabolism (Figure S6H), while downregulated genes were associated with pathways involving cell activation, developmental processes, and tissue morphogenesis (Figure S6I). SLPCs from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice (n = 6) and WT mice (n = 6) were transcriptionally similar to each other (Figure S6J), did not exhibit any differentially expressed X-linked genes beyond Xist downregulation (Figure 6H; Table S8), and had very few differentially expressed autosomal genes (Figure 6I; Table S8). Comparison of X-linked DEGs across B cell subsets from pristane-treated Xist cKO female mice revealed mostly cell type-specific profiles, yet there were 3 X-linked genes upregulated in both CD11c+ ABCs and activated GL7+ B cells: Tasl, Ftx, and Gm38020, which overlapped with Ftx (Figure 6J). Using smFISH to quantify allelic Tasl expression across B cells from pristane-treated Xist cKO (n = 3) and WT (n = 3) mice, we observed that some replicates of Xist cKO ABCs and SLPCs had a higher frequency biallelic Tasl expression, yet Xist cKO GL7+ B cells had similar levels of biallelic Tasl expression as WT cells (Figure S6K). In sum, these results indicated that pristane treatment, in the context of Xist deletion in B cells, alters the expression of X-linked and autosomal genes in GL7+ B cells and CD11c+ ABCs, yet gene expression in SLPCs was mostly preserved.

B cells from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice have reduced levels of the repressive histone mark H3K27me3 at X-linked DEGs and across the X chromosome

Given the finding that several X-linked genes are upregulated in Xist cKO/cKO mice with spontaneous or pristane-induced SLE-like disease, we sought to determine whether repressive epigenetic modifications beyond Xist RNA were absent in Xist cKO B cells at these loci, allowing for upregulation of these genes. We performed CUT&RUN for the heterochromatic histone H3 modification H3K27me3 in CD11c+ ABCs, activated GL7+ B cells, and SLPCs from pristane-treated Xist cKO/cKO mice (n = 2) and WT mice (n = 2). H3K27me3 marks were globally depleted along the length of the X chromosome in all 3 pristane-treated Xist cKO B cell subsets examined (Figures S7A, S7B, and S7C) and were also significantly reduced at X-linked promoters, gene bodies, and intergenic regions of Xist cKO B cells compared with WT B cells (Figures S7D, S7E, and S7F). H3K27me3 was appropriately enriched at the Hox region of chromosome 6 in all three B cell subsets (Figures S8A, S8B, and S8C), and H3K27me3 levels at promoters, gene bodies, and intergenic regions of chromosome 13 were similar in the WT and Xist cKO B cell subsets examined (Figures S8D, S8E, and S8F). Next, we quantified H3K27me3 enrichment for the X-linked DEGs in CD11c+ ABCs and activated GL7+ B cells (Figures 6B–6F) from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice. H3K27me3 levels were reduced in nearly all of the X-linked genes overexpressed in Xist cKO CD11c+ ABCs and activated GL7+ B cells compared with WT (Figures S7G and S7H). Comparison of the fold-change in promoter or gene body H3K27me3 levels with the fold-change in RNA expression at X-linked genes revealed that DEGs in GL7+ B cells exhibited greater reductions in H3K27me3 levels than the ABCs with Xist cKO (Figures S8G–S8J) and that more X-linked genes in Xist cKO GL7+ B cells exhibited reduced H3K27me3 than those in Xist cKO ABCs (left of vertical red dashed line; Figures S8G–S8J). The X-linked downregulated genes also exhibited reduced H3K27me3 in Xist cKO CD11c+ ABCs and activated GL7+ B cells (Figures S7G and S7H), suggesting that other epigenetic marks contribute to decreased gene expression despite the loss of H3K27me3. Examination of the upregulated X-linked Tasl locus demonstrated reduced H3K27me3 levels in Xist cKO ABCs and GL7+ cells compared with WT counterparts (Figures S7K and S7L). Together, these data revealed that Xist deletion is accompanied by global loss of H3K27me3 across the X chromosome, including depletion at X-linked DEGs, in CD11c+ ABCs and GL7+ B cells.

In vitro TLR stimulation of B cells induces expansion of CD138+ B cells and increased production of autoantibodies in pristane-treated Xist cKO mice

Because B cells from pristane-treated Xist cKO/cKO mice exhibited altered X-linked gene expression patterns, including upregulation of the TLR signaling regulator Tasl, we next asked whether these cells exhibited in vitro functional differences, specifically in responses to TLR signaling. To test this, we treated female WT mice (n = 6) and female Xist cKO mice (n = 4) with pristane for 7 months, then harvested total splenic B220+ B cells by magnetic column isolation. We stimulated and cultured B cells for 72 h via the B cell receptor (anti-IgM) and with TLR7 or TLR9 agnoists, then quantified B cell populations pre- and post-stimulation by flow cytometry. We also measured antibody, autoantibody, and cytokine production in the supernatants of cultured B220+ cells. We found that CD138+ B cells become significantly enriched after TLR7+IgM or TLR9+IgM stimulation of B cells from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice (Figure 7A), and these cells were not enriched in Xist cKO B cells compared to WT B cells before stimulation (Figure S9A). Frequencies of both CD11c+ ABCs (Figure 7B) and GL7+ B cells (Figure 7C) were similar between the Xist cKO and WT samples across all stimulation conditions, and they were also similar before stimulation (Figures S9B and S9C). Quantification of autoantibody production and total IgG production in the supernatants of cultured cells revealed that Xist cKO B cells secreted significantly more anti-DNA autoantibodies across all stimulation conditions (Figure 7D); however, the levels of anti-U1 snRNP autoantibodies were not significantly enriched after the stimulation of Xist cKO B cells compared with WT B cells (Figure S9D). We also found that TLR9+IgM-stimulated Xist cKO B cells produced significantly more IgG antibodies compared with WT mice (Figure 7E), consistent with the presence of higher levels of CD138+ B cells. Finally, we compared in vitro cytokine production levels between pristane-treated Xist cKO and stimulated WT B cells cultured for 72 h. We observed significantly more CCL5 (Figure S9E) and IL-10 production (Figure S9F) in Xist cKO B cells stimulated with TLR9+IgM than in cultured WT cells, while the levels of other inflammatory cytokines were not found to be significantly different between genotypes (Figure S9G). These findings demonstrated that B cells from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice have enhanced responsiveness to TLR stimulation, resulting in increased frequency of CD138+ B cells and increased production of anti-DNA autoantibodies and several cytokines. Taken together, our data demonstrate that pristane-induced disease in Xist cKO mice results in the expansion of activated, class-switched, and antibody-producing B cells, where CD11c+ ABCs and activated GL7+ B cells exhibit altered X-linked and autosomal gene expression in the setting of reduced H3K27me3 across the X chromosome. These epigenetic and transcriptional changes endowed the pristane-treated Xist cKO B cells with enhanced responsiveness to TLR signaling, resulting in the differentiation of antibody-secreting cells and the increased production of anti-dsDNA autoantibodies (Figure 7F).

DISCUSSION

Here, we demonstrate that Xist deletion in B cells of female BALB/c mice can result in the spontaneous development of SLE-like disease phenotypes in some aged female mice (~20% penetrance), including serologic autoimmunity, expansion of disease-relevant B cell subsets, and glomerular-predominant kidney pathology. Furthermore, using pristane to chemically induce SLE-like disease, we showed that female Xist cKO mice exhibited a greater expansion of activated and antibody-producing B cell subsets and higher anti-DNA autoantibody levels compared with pristane-treated WT mice, both in vivo and in vitro. In pristane-induced SLE-like disease, Xist deletion in B cells altered X-linked and autosomal gene expression in CD11c+ ABCs and activated GL7+ B cells, with concomitant reductions in the repressive histone mark H3K27me3. Together, our data support the hypothesis that perturbed XCI maintenance, as observed in B cells from patients with SLE and female-biased murine models of SLE-like disease,15,17,19 can alter B cell abundance and function, indicating that disrupted XCI maintenance directly contributes to female-biased systemic autoimmunity.

Our results are in agreement with those of a recent publication demonstrating that the global reduction in Xist expression following whole-body deletion of Ftx, an X-linked activator of Xist expression,77 results in the spontaneous development of lupus-like disease with advanced age. Although Xist RNA levels were reduced by only ~50% in female Ftx KO mice, these mice exhibited upregulation of X-linked genes with immune functions, including Tlr7 in splenic B cells, dendritic cells, and monocyte/macrophages, and Tasl in dendritic cells and monocyte/macrophages. Female Ftx KO mice also exhibited a higher frequency of myeloid and plasmacytoid dendritic cells, an expansion of activated splenic B and T cells, and the development of SLE-specific serum autoantibodies. We found that Xist deletion restricted to the B cell compartment is sufficient to induce spontaneous SLE-like disease; however, it is possible that reduced Xist expression in other cell types, as noted in the Ftx KO mouse model, could increase the frequency or severity of lupus-like disease. Similar to our B cell-specific Xist cKO mice, Ftx KO mice developed only some SLE disease features after one year of age. This commonality suggests that additional age-related epigenetic or environmental changes can potentiate the effects of reduced Xist expression.

We found that female Xist cKO mice on a BALB/c background spontaneously developed SLE pathologies, yet the frequency of disease was ~20% and occurred only in mice 12 months or older. While human SLE similarly exhibits a low penetrance within affected families, the average age for a woman to be diagnosed with SLE is 31 years, underscoring the contribution of additional factors, beyond DNA sequence and age, to the pathogenesis of SLE.78 Indeed, a variety of environmental factors, including sex hormones, cigarette smoke, viral infections (such as Epstein-Barr virus), medications, silica exposure, dietary factors, pollution, and occupational chemicals, have been associated with the development of SLE.79–81 In humans, exposure to these factors may drive epigenetic changes that could contribute to the erosion of epigenetic silencing of Xi in immune cells. As such, we posit that Xist RNA loss from the Xi represents an underlying genetic and epigenetic susceptibility factor for SLE-like disease and that environmental or age-related epigenetic changes82 may also be required for the aberrant escape of X-linked genes and the subsequent development of SLE phenotypes. Another possibility is that an immune-activating event is required to trigger SLE in Xist cKO mice. Additional research is necessary to determine whether pathogens may accelerate the development of SLE-like disease in Xist cKO mice.

We were surprised to see considerable cell type-specific heterogeneity in X-linked and autosomal gene expression profiles from Xist cKO mice, whether in untreated mice that spontaneously developed features of SLE-like disease (Xist cKO High) or in pristane-treated Xist cKO mice. In Xist cKO High mice, Xist deletion in B cells was associated with X-linked and autosomal transcriptional changes in CD11c+ ABCs; however, CD23+ B cells and LLPCs lacked robust transcriptional alterations. In pristane-treated Xist cKO mice, we also observed many differentially regulated X-linked and autosomal genes in CD11c+ ABCs and GL7+ B cells; however, SLPCs appeared transcriptionally resistant to Xist loss, with minimal differences in genome-wide expression. It is possible that Xist deletion favors differentiation into the CD138+ plasma cell lineage without altering transcriptional profiles of the plasma cell itself. Indeed, we observed increased in vitro differentiation of Xist cKO B220+ B cells into CD138+ cells following Tlr7/9 stimulation. It is also possible that the cellular heterogeneity within the CD138+ pool83 obscures the detection of differently expressed genes within specific CD138+ plasma cell subsets; thus higher-resolution single-cell approaches may help capture more subtle transcriptional heterogeneity across this broad population.

Despite the cell-to-cell variation in X-linked transcriptional profiles, the endosomal Tlr adaptor Tasl was consistently upregulated in several activated B cell subsets, across both female Xist cKO High mice (with spontaneous SLE features) and pristane-treated female Xist cKO mice. Increased TASL expression was observed in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with SLE,64 and TASL was also found to be upregulated in a human B cell line containing XIST deletion.50 Other X-linked genes whose genomic loci are proximal to the locus of Tasl were also upregulated in Xist cKO mice, including Dmd and Gk, which do not have known functions in B cells. DMD expression was also reported to increase in cultured human B cells after CRISPR-Cas9-mediated deletion of XIST,43 and these observations suggest that Xist deletion may alter the epigenetic regulation of these genes. In support, we found that H3K27me3 levels were significantly and specifically reduced across the X chromosome in Xist cKO B cell subsets, which likely contributes to the altered expression of X-linked genes including Tasl, Dmd, and Gk. Additional work is necessary to more comprehensively characterize the epigenetic perturbations near the Tasl locus resulting from Xist deletion. Future attempts to more broadly profile the epigenetic landscape of the X chromosome in the context of Xist loss will help determine how the majority of X-linked genes remains silenced in the setting of X chromosome-wide Xist loss and H3K27me3 depletion.

Using pristane to induce lupus-like disease, we observed that Xist cKO mice produced more class-switched and activated GL7+ B cells at 4 months post-injection, as well as more CD138+ plasma cells and CD11c+ ABCs at 7–9 months post-injection. These cellular expansions likely contributed to the increased anti-dsDNA autoantibody production observed in pristane-treated Xist cKO mice. We hypothesize that Xist deletion in the B cell compartment enhances B cell activation and expansion. By enhancing pathways regulating cell proliferation, activation, and differentiation, Xist deletion in B cells results in the accumulation of plasma cells and ABCs that produce anti-dsDNA autoantibodies.84 Additionally, we observed the upregulation of specific X-linked genes that promote TLR7 and TLR9 signaling pathways65 (Tasl) and B cell activation (Sh3kbp1/Cin85). Despite Tlr7 being only slightly upregulated according to our findings, Tasl upregulation may enhance signaling through the TLR7 pathway,32 which would contribute to the observed expansion of activated B cell populations in Xist cKO mice. Additionally, our in vitro culture experiments using B220+ B cells from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice indicated that Tlr7/9 activation increases the frequency of CD138+ B cells and the production of anti-dsDNA autoantibodies. We also observed that B cells from pristane-treated Xist cKO mice produced higher levels of the cytokines CCL5 and IL-10 after a short-term in vitro culture in the presence of Tlr7/9 agonists. Previous work has shown that B cell stimulation in vitro leads to the secretion of CCL5 and that the addition of CCL5 to B cells during in vitro stimulation enhances cell proliferation and antibody production.85 Ccl5 also exhibited female-biased expression in lung B cells from a murine asthma model86; thus, it is possible that transcriptional changes on the Xi during immune responses contribute to increased Ccl5 expression in B cells. Notably, serum levels of IL-10 are higher in patients with SLE than in healthy controls.87 IL-10 enhances B cell proliferation88 and extrafollicular differentiation89 and contributes to B cell-mediated antibody production.88,90 These studies, along with our findings, suggest that increased CCL5 and IL-10 production in Xist cKO B cells in response to heightened TLR signaling may contribute to increased frequencies of activated and antibody-secreting B cell subsets observed in both spontaneous and pristane-treated Xist cKO mice, thereby resulting in increased autoantibody production.

Recent work has demonstrated that Xist RNA itself can contribute to the development of female-biased autoimmune disease. The conserved repetitive elements at the 5′ end of Xist RNA are strong ligands for TLR7 signaling, and XIST RNA oligos encompassing these motifs stimulate primary human plasmacytoid dendritic cells to produce type I interferon in vitro.91 Patients with SLE exhibited higher levels of XIST RNA in their PBMCs, including B cells, and the XIST expression in PBMCs was positively correlated with increased disease activity in patients with SLE.91 Furthermore, XIST RNA binds to many RNA-binding proteins, and another study suggests that the association of these XIST-binding proteins with the XIST RNA scaffold may provide a unique, female-biased source of autoantigens in the context of female-biased autoimmune diseases.92 Altogether, these studies suggest an intriguing, complementary role for Xist RNA as a driver of autoimmune responses independent of its role in X-linked dosage compensation. Importantly, our work demonstrates that disrupting Xist RNA’s role in dosage compensation in B cells is sufficient to cause spontaneous SLE-like disease and also exacerbate pristane-induced disease. Collectively, these data support complementary, and perhaps even synergistic, roles for Xist RNA in the pathogenesis and female sex bias of SLE. Thus, our findings suggest that the disruption of proper maintenance of XCI in the B cell compartment, a potential mechanism at play in female patients with SLE having disrupted patterns of XCI in B cells, likely contributes to female-biased disease phenotypes via dosage imbalances of pathogenic X-linked genes.

Limitations of the study

While we report that some Xist cKO female mice on BALB/c background can spontaneously develop SLE features with advanced age, we could not identify the causative environmental agent(s) responsible that drives some (~20%) but not all such Xist cKO female mice to develop SLE-like phenotypes. We speculate that an immune-activating event, such as an infection, or changes to the microbiome composition in Xist cKO mice are likely factors for the spontaneous development of SLE phenotypes in female Xist cKO mice. Additional experiments are needed to determine which environmental factors are responsible for lupus phenotypes in the context of B cell-specific Xist deletion. In our study, we did not cross Xist cKO mice onto a lupus-prone background strain, such as B.6.Sle1.2.3 or MRL/lpr, to determine whether Xist deletion would accelerate SLE phenotype development and increase disease pathologies or disease severity. Future work investigating how Xist deletion impacts SLE phenotypes and disease onset in combination with other genetic mutations that increase SLE-like disease would be informative for understanding the interplay of biological sex with autosomal mutations that favor autoimmunity. We did not detect significant differences in the biallelic expression of Tasl across B cell subsets when comparing Xist cKO and pristane-treated WT mice (n = 3 for each genotype). We believe that an examination of more pristane-treated mice would yield results showing significant differences with biallelic Tasl expression for Xist cKO samples, as pristane treatment is known to be heterogeneous across animals and requires larger cohorts than that we examined for smFISH. Another limitation of our study is that we examined Xi-specific expression of just 1 X-linked upregulated gene, Tasl, using smFISH for specific B cell subsets from Xist cKO mice with spontaneous lupus phenotypes and after pristane treatment. Because Xist RNA deletion compromises H3K27me3 enrichment across the Xi,93 it is likely that other X-linked genes upregulated in Xist cKO B cells, including Cfp, Sh3kbp1, and Bcorl1, are also expressed on Xi and the Xa. Thus, additional experiments using specific X-linked probes for smFISH are necessary to confirm Xi-specific expression of upregulated genes observed in activated and memory B cell subsets in Xist cKO mice.

Mice

For this study all female mice were used to study the impact of Xist deletion, as male mammals do not express Xist. METHOD DETAILS

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

Spleens and/or bone marrow were isolated from mice following euthanasia and flow cytometry was performed as previously described.23 Splenocytes were isolated via mechanical dissociation of the spleen. Cell suspensions of the bone marrow were isolated via mechanical dissociation of the tibia and femur. Red blood cells were removed by red blood cell lysis with ACK Lysis Buffer (Quality Biological 118-156-721). Cells were stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Life Technologies L34965) and subsequently blocked with Purified Rat Anti-Mouse CD16/CD32 (BD Biosciences 553142). Cells were then stained in 0.5% BSA in PBS using the following fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (clone, catalog #) purchased from BioLegend B220-APC (RA3-6B2, 103212), B220-BV650 (RA3-6B2, 103241), CD19-BV785 (6D5, 115543), CD23-BV421 (B3B4, 101621), CD21/35-APC-Cy7 (7E9, 123418), CD43-PE (S11, 143205), IgD-BV711 (11-26c.2a, 405731), GL7-PE-Cy7 (GL7, 144620), CD138-BV605 (281-2, 142516), CD11c-AF488 (N418, 117311), F4/80-PE-Cy5 (BM8, 123111), Gr-1-PE-Cy5 (RB6-8C5, 108410), CD4-PE-Cy5 (H129,19, 130312), CD8a-PE-Cy5 (53-6.7, 100709), or eBioscience CD93 (AA4.1, 25-5892-82), or BD Horizon IgM-PE-CF594 (R6-60.2, 562565). Flow cytometry was performed on an LSR Fortessa and sorting was performed on a FACSAria Fusion and data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Xist RNA fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) and immunofluorescence (IF)

Naive splenic B cells were isolated from mice as previously described.26 Briefly, spleens were mechanically homogenized and splenocytes were incubated with Biotin anti-mouse CD23 (Biolegend 101604 clone B3B4). Splenocytes were then incubated with Streptavidin MicroBeads (Miltenyi 130-048-101) and passed through a magnetic LS Column (Miltenyi 130-042-401). B cell activation was induced using 24-h incubation with the Tlr9 agonist CpG (Invivogen tlrl-1826-1). In vitro activated lymphocytes were collected and prepared for FISH and IF as previously described.22 Xist RNA FISH was performed using Cy3-labeled oligonucleotide probes for Xist RNA,22,23 and nuclei were counterstained using Vectashield with DAPI (Vector Lab H-1200). Sequential immunofluorescence for heterochromatic histone marks was performed using primary antibodies for H2AUb119K (Cell Signaling 8240S) and secondary antibodies AF488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam ab150077-500UG). Samples were imaged using Nikon Eclipse Microscope and patterns of Xist RNA and histone focal enrichment were quantified manually as previously described.19

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Blood was collected from mice using cheek bleeding procedure, and serum was isolated by centrifugation of blood that had been incubated at 4°C for 1 h. ELISA was performed to measure serum or supernatant levels of antibodies against double-stranded dsDNA, Smith-RNP, or IgG. Immulon 2 Flat Bottom High Binding 96 Well Plates (Fisher 1424561) were coated with calf thymus dsDNA (Alpha Diagnostics DNAD25-N-1), Sm-RNP (Arotec Diagnostics ATR01), or Total Ig (Southern Biotech 1010–01) in bicarbonate buffer (Sigma C3041–100). Using calf thymus dsDNA for plate coating may not distinguish binding to single or double stranded DNA. Plates were incubated overnight and then washed with ELISA Wash Buffer (1x PBS +0.1% Tween) and blocked overnight with 1% BSA in PBS. Serum was diluted 1:200 – 1:1000 in 1% BSA in PBS. A standard curve was generated using mixed serum from mice with spontaneous SLE-like disease (NZB/W F1,25 MRL/lpr,26 and NZM2328 mice26) diluted 1:500 in 1% BSA in PBS. Serum from mb1cre/cre homozygous mice (which lack B cell development and do not generate antibodies) was used as a negative control. Serum samples were incubated overnight. HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Southern Biotech 1038–05) secondary antibody was added and incubated for one hour at room temperature, and SureBlue TMB 1-Component Microwell Peroxidase Substrate (LGC Clinical Diagnostics 5120–0075) was added for 10–25 min. TMB Stop Solution (LGC Clinical Diagnostics 5150–0020) was added to stop the reaction and plates were read using a plate reader at 450nm.

Autoantibody profiling

Serum samples were submitted to the Genomics and Microarray Core Facility at the University of Texas Southwestern. Serum was analyzed for 120 autoantibodies and a mouse Ig control at 4 dilutions according to established methods. For each sample, a signal to noise ratio (SNR) was calculated to reveal high background, and autoantibodies where more than 50% samples per group had an SNR <3 were removed from analysis (6 autoantibodies). A “Net Signal Intensity” (NSI) value was reported by the core facility for each autoantibody which was then normalized to total IgG as measured by ELISA (“NSI_IgG”). An antibody score was calculated using the equation Antibody score = log2([NSI_IgG*SNR]+1). DESeq2 was used to identify significantly differentially enriched autoantibodies (“DEAs”) between groups. DEAs were filtered based on a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p value <0.05.

Salivary gland immunofluorescence

Salivary glands were isolated from euthanized mice and formalin fixed for 24 h (Sigma-Aldrich HT501128–4L). Formalin-fixed kidneys were processed by the Penn Vet Histopathology Core: kidneys were embedded in paraffin, and organ sections were mounted on glass slides. Slides were de-paraffinized using sequential xylene and ethanol washes and antigen retrieval was performed using 100X Tris-EDTA Buffer (Abcam ab93684). Slides were blocked in 10% donkey serum and incubated with anti-CD19 (Cell Signaling Technologies CST90176, Clone D4V4B) and anti-CD3 (Bio-Rad MCA1477, Clone CD3–12) antibodies. Slides were then incubated in secondary antibody, counterstained with Vector TrueVIEW Autofluorescence Quenching Kit (Vector Laboratories SP-8400–15) and imaged.

Proteinuria

Urine was collected from live mice and protein levels in the urine were measured by Pierce-Coomassie (Thermo Fisher Scientific 23200) according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot (ELISPOT)

ELISPOT assays were used to quantify numbers of anti-dsDNA producing splenocytes and bone marrow cells. 96-well MultiScreen HTS Plates (Millipore-Sigma MSIPN4550) were coated with 0.01% poly-L-lysine (Millipore Sigma A-005-C) for one hour and then coated overnight with calf thymus dsDNA (Alpha Diagnostics DNAD25-N-1) in bicarbonate buffer (Sigma C3041–100). Total splenocytes or bone marrow cells were plated at three dilutions in B cell media (RPMI containing 10% FBS, 1% nonessential amino acids, 1% 1M HEPES, 1% 200mM L-glutamine, 1% OPI medium supplement Hybri-Max, 1% Pen/Strep, and 0.1% β-mercaptoethanol) and plates were incubated overnight. Alkaline phosphatase conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Southern Biotech 1030–04) was added and incubated for two hours at room temperature, and BCIP/NBT Substrate (Thermo Pierce 34042) was added for 15 min. Plates were washed with water to stop the reaction and plates were imaged and analyzed using a CTL Immunospot Cell Imager and Analyzer.

RT-qPCR

RT-qPCR was performed as previously described.26 RNA was isolated from CD23+ splenic B cells in TRIzol and quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. cDNA was generated with qScript DNA SuperMix (QuantaBio), and qPCR was performed using PerfeCTa SYBR Green SuperMix (QuantaBio). Fold change expression was quantified by (2−ΔΔCT) using the CT value of Xist and the CT value of the housekeeping gene Rpl13a. The following primer sets were used: Xist (F: CAGAGTAGCGAGGACTTGAAGAG, R: GCTGGTTCGTCTATCTTGTGGG); Ifit1 (F: GCAGAGAGTCAAGGCAGGTT, R: TTGTGCATCCCCAATGGGTT); Ifit3 (F: TTCCCA GCAGCACAGAAACA, R: AATGGCACTTCAGCTGTGGA); Isg15 (F: TGACTGTGAGAGCAAGCAGC, R: CCCCAGCATCTTCA CCTTTA); Irf7 (F: GCATTTCGGTCGTAGGGATCTGGATGAAGA, R: CGTACACCTTATGCGGATCAACTGGA); Rpl13a (F: AGCCTAC CAGAAAGTTTGCTTAC, R: GCTTCTTCTTCCGATAGTGCATC).

RNA sequencing

RNA was isolated from fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) isolated populations of splenic B cells using the Qiagen RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen 74004). Library preparation of RNA samples was performed using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 - Pico Input Mammalian (Takara 634485). Two independent pooled libraries (samples from mice with spontaneous disease and samples from mice with pristane-induced disease) were sequenced on 100 cycle flow cells using an Illumina NextSeq 2000 sequencer. Reads were aligned to a BALB/cJ reference genome (Ensembl BALB_cJ_v1) using STAR (v2.7.1a).96 Read counts were calculated using featureCounts.104 RNAseq data was analyzed using RStudio (v1.2.5042). edgeR (v3.32.1) was used to filter data and data was normalized using quantile normalization.105 Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 (v1.30.1). Pathway analysis was performed using Metascape.99 Venn diagrams were generated using DeepVenn.

Tasl single molecule fluorescent in situ hybridization (smFISH)

Tiling probe sets against Tasl exons and introns were designed using Stellaris Probe Designer version 4.2. 48 probes were generated based on input exonic and intronic sequences. A Quasar 570 Dye (Cy3) conjugated exonic probe set and a Quasar 670 (Cy5) intronic probe set were purchased from Stellaris. Probes were diluted to 5nmon concentration in TE buffer. Slides were sequentially dehydrated in 80%, 90%, and 100% ethanol. 1ul exonic probe and 1ul intronic probe were then mixed in 48ul hybridization buffer (2.5mL formamide, 2mL 50% dextran, 1mL 20X SSC, and 4.5mL DEPC water) per slide. 50ul probe mixture in hybridization buffer was then spotted on slides, and slides were incubated overnight at 37°C. Slides were washed twice in 10% formamide in 2X SSC and then twice in 2X SSC at 42°C. Nuclei were counterstained using Vectashield with DAPI (Vector Lab H-1200). Samples were imaged using Nikon Eclipse Microscope.

Kidney Histopathology

Kidneys were isolated from euthanized mice and formalin fixed for 24 h (Sigma-Aldrich HT501128–4L). Formalin-fixed kidneys were processed by the Penn Skin Biology and Diseases Resource-based Center: kidneys were embedded in paraffin, and organ sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or periodic acid-Schiff (PAS). Stained sections were scored for features of kidney disease by a blinded veterinary renal pathologist who measured features of kidney disease (glomerular hypercellularity, glomerular mesangial thickening, glomerular cystic dilation, glomerular basement membrane thickening, glomerular hypertrophy, glomerular mesangiolysis, glomerulosclerosis, tubular basophilia/regeneration, tubular degeneration, tubular dilation, and tubular casts). Each feature was scored from a range of 0–4: 0 = “no significant findings”, 1 = “minimal findings”, 2 = “mild findings”, 3 = “moderate findings”, and 4 = “severe findings”.

Pristane treatment

2,6,10,14-Tetramethylpentadecane (“pristane”) (Sigma Aldrich P2870) was administered to mice for the induction of SLE-like disease as previously described.106 Briefly, 500uL of pristane was injected intraperitoneally into 3–5 month old female Xist cKO and WT mice on the BALB/c background. Seven independent cohorts of mice were designed with designated experimental endpoint set at onset (4 months, 7 months, or 9 months). Mice were monitored over the course of 0–9 months for serum dsDNA autoantibodies by ELISA and proteinuria. Mice were euthanized at the pre-set experimental endpoint.

In vitro stimulation

Splenocytes were incubated with Streptavidin-conjugagted B220+ MicroBeads (Miltenyi 130-049-501) and passed through a magnetic LS Column (Miltenyi 130-042-401). B cell activation was induced using 72-h incubation with combinations of 1 μg/mL of the Tlr7 agonist Imiquimod (Invivogen tlrl-imqs), 1uM of the Tlr9 agonist CpG (Invivogen tlrl-1826-1), and B cell receptor agonism by 10ug/mL anti-IgM (Jackson ImmunoResearch 115-006-020). Cells were collected for flow cytometry before and after stimulation, and supernatant was collected after stimulation for quantification of antibody/autoantibody and cytokine secretion.

LegendPlex cytokine array

CUT&RUN

CUT&RUN for H3K27me3 was performed as previously described.107 Concanavalin A–coated beads (10μL per reaction; catalog no. 86057–3, Polysciences) were equilibrated with binding buffer (20mM HEPES, 10mM KCl, 1mM CaCl2, and 1mM MnCl2) and then resuspended in wash buffer (20mM HEPES, 150mM NaCl, 0.5mM spermidine, and 1x protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC; catalog no. 11697498001, Roche)). Between 10,000 and 30,000 FACS sorted B cells were immobilized on beads in wash buffer by rotating for 1 h at room temperature. Beads were resuspended in antibody buffer (20mM HEPES, 150mM NaCl, 0.5mM spermidine, 1x PIC, 0.05% digitonin, and 2mM EDTA). Primary antibody for H3K27me3 (3:50 dilution; catalog no. 39055, Active Motif) was added, and the beads were rotated at 4°C for 5 h. Beads were washed with and resuspended in dig-wash buffer (20mM HEPES, 150mM NaCl, 0.5mM spermidine, 1x PIC, and 0.05% digitonin); protein A–micrococcal nuclease (pA-MNase fusion protein) (1200ng/ml; gift from S. Henikoff, Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center) was added, and the beads were rotated at 4°C for 1 h. Beads were washed twice and then resuspended in dig-wash buffer. Targeted digestion was performed at 0°C for 30 min by adding CaCl2 to 2mM. The digestion was stopped by adding one volume of 2x stop buffer [340mM NaCl, 20mM EDTA, 4mM EGTA, 0.02% digitonin, ribonuclease A (50μg/ml; catalog no. 3335399001, Roche), linear acrylamide (50μg/ml; catalog no. K548, VWR), and yeast spike-in DNA (2pg/ml)]. The target chromatin was released into the supernatant by shaking the beads at 300 rpm for 10 min at 37°C. The supernatant was then transferred to fresh tubes and incubated at 70°C for 10 min with SDS to 0.1% and 50μg of proteinase K (catalog no. EO0491, Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA was then isolated with phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol followed by precipitation with ammonium acetate. Libraries were prepared with 5ng of CUT&RUN DNA using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (catalog no. E7645S, New England Biolabs) following the manufacturer’s protocol. CUT&RUN libraries were then paired-end sequenced on a P3 300 flow cell on the NextSeq2000.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

H3K27me3 CUT & RUN Bioinformatic analysis

Adapters were removed using Trimmomatic (V0.32, https://github.com/usadellab/Trimmomatic). Reads were mapped to a BALB/cJ reference genome (Ensembl BALB_cJ_v1) using Bowtie2 (2.3.4.1) with the parameters [-q – local –very-sensitive-local –soft-clipped-unmapped-tlen –dovetail –no-mixed –nodiscordant –phred33 -I 10 -X 1000]. Duplicated reads were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates (1.141, https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) with option [REMOVEDUPLICATES = true]. Reads mapped to regions from the ENCODE blacklist were removed. CPM normalized bigwig files were generated using deepTools bamCoverage (3.5.1, https://deeptools.readthedocs.io/en/develop/) with options [–effectiveGenomeSize 2652783500 –normalizeUsing CPM –ignoreForNormalization chrX]. Promoter regions were defined as ±1kb from the TSS for all X-linked genes. Gene bodies were defined 1kb downstream of the TSS to the TES. Gene bodies of X-linked genes shorter than 1kb were excluded from the analysis. Intergenic regions were defined as 10kb windows (generated using GenomicRanges (1.44.0)) that did not overlap gene bodies or their promoter regions (1kb upstream of TSS). Reads were assigned to defined windows using featureCounts from the Rsubread package (2.6.4) with arguments [useMetaFeatures = F, isPairedEnd = T]. For each biological replicate, log10(Reads per 1kb) were calculated for promoters, gene bodies, and intergenic regions. P-values were calculated using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Benjamini Hochberg correction.

Statistical analysis

Serum autoantibodies, kidney pathology scores, and B cell subsets frequencies and total numbers were compared between multiple groups using Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test or compared between two groups using Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally-distributed data. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Differentially enriched autoantibodies were defined using DESeq2 to identify autoantibodies with a Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05. Changes in serum autoantibodies or proteinuria over pristane-induced disease between groups were compared using a mixed effects model with the Geisser-Greenhouse correction (not assuming equal variability of differences). For RNAseq analysis, genes exhibiting a fold-change ≥0.5 with a Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. The total number of animals used for each experiment is indicated in the corresponding figure legend. Data are pooled from multiple independent experiments.
