Presumably recurrent Ureaplasma parvum infection in a female peritoneal dialysis patient: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yi Lv, Chen Ye, Huanhuan Cao, Chun Zhang, Yumei Wang, Huajun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeritoneal dialysis-associated peritonitis (PDAP) caused by Ureaplasma parvum (U. parvum) is exceedingly rare, and its diagnosis is particularly challenging due to the organism’s biological characteristics and the limitations of conventional detection methods. To date, only sporadic case reports are available, but the diagnostic processes and clinical characteristics of PDAP caused by U. parvum infection have not been comprehensively reported yet.Case presentationWe report a 35-year-old female patient undergoing continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD) who experienced three episodes of peritonitis within a six-month period. Despite empirical antibiotic therapy leading to clinical improvement, routine microbiological cultures of the dialysate remained negative during each episode. During the third episode, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of the peritoneal dialysis (PD) effluent finally detected U. parvum as the causative pathogen. Following removing a potential risk factor, an intrauterine device (IUD), and adjusting the antimicrobial therapy to intraperitoneal (IP) levofloxacin and oral azithromycin, the patient achieved complete recovery and successfully resumed PD. Based on this experience, we documented the characteristic clinical profile of such infections and proposed an exploratory clinical diagnosis and treatment flowchart.ConclusionsPatients with recurrent culture-negative PDAP, especially female with an IUD, should be evaluated for U. parvum infection as a potential pathogen. mNGS facilitates the rapid detection of pathogens that traditional methods may fail to identify. Effective management of such infections necessitates not only targeted antimicrobial therapy guided by precise pathogen identification, but also the removal of potential risk factors such as the IUD.Keywords: Ureaplasma parvum, Peritoneal dialysis-associated peritonitis, Culture-negative peritonitis, Metagenomic next-generation sequencing, Case report

Background

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is an effectively renal replacement therapy for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. Peritoneal dialysis-associated peritonitis (PDAP) is a common and serious complication of PD, often resulting in technique failure and adversely affecting both long-term survival and quality of life of patients [2, 3]. Notably, despite PD effluent culture being the main diagnostic method, its utility is limited by a high rate of culture-negative results [4].

Ureaplasma parvum (U. parvum), a species of the genus Ureaplasma, the other being Ureaplasma urealyticum (U. urealyticum) [5–7], typically functions as an opportunistic pathogen and colonizes the mucosal surfaces of the urogenital tract, implicating in urogenital infections [8, 9]. Studies have shown that U. parvum infection is associated with various diseases, including genitourinary tract infections, pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility, as well as adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth, spontaneous abortion, ectopic pregnancy, and neonatal infections [10–14]. However, extragenital infections caused by U. parvum are uncommon. Meanwhile, due to its slow growth rate, the lack of a cell wall, and the requirement for specialized media, U. parvum infection is often difficult to diagnose. Thus, literature reports regarding PDAP caused by U. parvum infection are fairly limited [15, 16]. Nevertheless, even among the sporadic reports available, the distinctive feature of recurrent infection and the corresponding detailed therapeutic protocols have not been well-documented or summarized. This case report aims to contribute to a deeper understanding of such challenging infections.

We present a case of presumably recurrent culture-negative PDAP caused by U. parvum, diagnosed via mNGS. In addition, we meticulously documented the characteristics of clinical infection markers in both dialysis fluid and peripheral blood during the infection period, alongside antibiotic administration and clinical outcomes. By removing the risk factor and targeting antibiotic therapy, the patient was ultimately completely cured. Furthermore, we propose an exploratory clinical diagnosis and treatment flowchart, aiming to arouse enough attention to this presumably recurrent culture-negative PDAP caused by U. parvum and provide a practical educational framework for clinicians encountering similar challenging scenarios in the future.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old female patient presented to our hospital on June 15, 2023 with complaints of abdominal discomfort, turbid dialysate, and painful urination. She had undergone continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD) for three years after being diagnosed with ESRD. On admission, her body temperature was 36.0 ℃, and her pulse and blood pressure were within normal ranges. Physical examinations revealed a soft abdomen with tenderness and rebound tenderness, indicating peritonitis. Laboratory analysis revealed that the peripheral blood showed a white blood cell (WBC) count of 9.84 × 109/L with 80.2% neutrophil percentage, lymphocyte percentage of 11.8%. The level of C-reactive protein (CRP) was 34.3 mg/L, procalcitonin (PCT) was 0.12 ng/mL, blood urea nitrogen was 9.0 mmol/L, and creatinine was 440.9 µmol/L. The dialysate was slightly turbid, for which the WBC count was 450 × 106/µL with 50% polymorphonuclear cells. We have also routinely measured a panel of cytokines in the PD effluent, which included interleukin (IL)-2 of 2.88 pg/ml, IL-4 of 2.16 pg/ml, IL-6 of 1612.21 pg/ml, IL-10 of 13.78 pg/ml, TNF-α of 4.25 pg/ml, and IFN-γ of 2.79 pg/ml. During the current hospitalization, repeated PD effluent cultures, along with blood and urine cultures, all yielded negative results. Empirical therapy was performed with intravenous cephalosporins (cefotaxime 1.0 g every 12 h) and intraperitoneal (IP) amikacin (0.2 g once daily). The intravenous cephalosporins was chosen to ensure rapid achievement of therapeutic systemic concentrations during the acute phase of infection, particularly given the patient’s abdominal symptoms. On the eighth day, due to a lack of clinical improvement, the intravenous moxifloxacin (0.4 g once daily) was added. On the tenth day, laboratory tests showed a WBC count in dialysate of 166 × 106/µL with 19% polymorphonuclear leukocytes, CRP of 36 mg/L and PCT of 0.11 ng/mL (Fig. 1a). Afterward, the patient’s symptoms improved, and she was discharged from the hospital on the twelfth day. After discharge, she continued with IP amikacin (0.2 g once daily) for 10 days and oral moxifloxacin (0.4 g once daily) for 3 days as maintenance therapy. On the 22nd day, a follow-up outpatient examination of the PD effluent routine indicated normal results, and no further medication was administered thereafter.

Hoverer, 2 months later, the patient experienced another episode of abdominal pain along with turbid dialysate, prompting readmission for further evaluation and management of recurrent peritonitis. Upon this hospital admission, the peripheral blood WBC count was 6.95 × 109/L with 68.8% neutrophil percentage, the level of CRP was 17.39 mg/L, PCT was 0.12 ng/ml, and the WBC count of 724 × 106/µL with 77% polymorphonuclear cells, IL-6 of 3618.83 pg/ml, IL-10 of 17.77 pg/ml in dialysate. Since the dialysate culture remained negative and the pathogen could not be identified, the antibiotic treatment still empirically consisted of intravenous cephalosporin (cefoperazone sulbactam 1.5 g every 12 h) and IP amikacin (0.2 g once daily). The abdominal pain relieved on the day 3, during the dynamic assessment of dialysate tests; inflammatory indicators gradually returned to normal (Fig. 1b), indicating that the peritonitis was under control.

Unfortunately, on November 20, 2023, the patient developed a third episode of peritonitis, accompanied by turbid dialysate but no abdominal pain. Given the recurrent nature of her peritonitis with persistent culture-negative results, she was hospitalized to facilitate a comprehensive diagnostic workup, and to implement targeted therapeutic interventions. At this admission, the peripheral blood WBC count was 8.58 × 109/L with 77.1% neutrophil percentage, the level of CRP was 6.32 mg/L, and PCT was less than 0.05 ng/ml. The WBC count in dialysate was 467 × 106/µL with 52% polymorphonuclear cells, IL-6 of 2683.59 pg/ml, IL-10 of 16.45 pg/ml. Considered that the patient had experienced three recurrent episodes of PDAP within only six months, with multiple cultures demonstrating negative results, and that each episode had responded only temporarily to empirical antibiotics before relapsing, it is essential to explore alternative treatment options and investigate underlying causes more thoroughly. Therefore, we sent one sample of the dialysis fluid to BGI -Wuhan (Beijing Genomics Institute, Wuhan, China) for pathogen detection using mNGS after obtaining consent from the patient and her family members. mNGS data were processed using the cloud-computing pipeline for metagenomic pathogen identification. Consequently, a total of 1.46 million sequences detected. Based on the established criteria for positive mNGS results [17], the assay demonstrated that the patient was infected with U. parvum. Specifically, A total of 72 specific sequences to the Ureaplasma genus were mapped, and 52 specific sequences of Ureaplasma parvum were mapped, with a coverage of 0.42% for U. parvum.

Based on the mNGS results, the therapeutic regimen was adjusted to IP levofloxacin (0.25 g once daily) on the fourth day. A rapid decrease in the WBC count of the PD effluent was observed on the following day. After actively inquiring about the patient’s medical history, we found that the patient had a history of intrauterine device (IUD) use. Since U. parvum commonly colonizes the urogenital tract, we conducted DNA testing for Ureaplasma and Chlamydia trachomatis using the patient’s cervical secretions following her informed consent. The results revealed positive results for Ureaplasma DNA, further confirming that the presence of Ureaplasma colonization in the lower genital tract. Subsequently, we removed the risk factor IUD and continued treatment with IP levofloxacin (0.25 g once daily), and the PD effluent WBC count was 36 × 106/µL with 11% polymorphonuclear cells, as well as IL-6 of 125.11 pg/ml (Fig. 1c). After 10 days of treatment, the patient’s abdominal pain had significantly relieved, with the effluent becoming clear, and was discharged from our hospital. Post-discharge, the patient continued with IP levofloxacin (0.25 g once daily) and oral cephalosporins (cefdinir 0.1 g three times daily), subsequently transitioning to oral azithromycin (0.25 g once daily) for one week. The patient was followed up regularly after discharge, with assessments performed at three months interval, and six months thereafter. During a 24-month follow-up period, concluding with the most recent follow-up in December 2025, the patient was highly satisfied with the treatment, remained free of peritonitis recurrence and continued CAPD successfully. Additionally, we recorded changes in inflammatory indicators before and after infection and recovery during these three episodes of PDAD in the patient, including WBC, neutrophil percentage, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), CRP, PCT in blood, and IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, IFN-γ in PD effluent. We found that WBC, neutrophil percentage, and CRP levels in the blood may exhibit slight abnormalities, while PCT consistently maintained normal. Conversely, IL-10 and especially IL-6 levels in PD effluent were significantly elevated, whereas those of IL-2, IL-4, TNF-α, and IFN-γ demonstrated no significant increase and change (Fig. 2).

Discussion and conclusion

Peritonitis is a common complication in PD patients and leading cause of PD failure. U. parvum infection should be highly suspected in cases of recurrent episodes of culture-negative PDAP especially female with risk factors such as a history of genitourinary tract infection or manipulation. However, due to its unique biological characteristics and limitations in clinical diagnosis and treatment, the PDAP caused by U. parvum infection is seldom recognized clinically for an extended period, and is rarely found and reported in time. To review previously cases, we conducted a literature search in PubMed and Web of Science databases up to December 2025, using the keywords (“Ureaplasma parvum” OR “Ureaplasma urealyticum” or “Ureaplasma species”) AND (“peritoneal dialysis” OR “peritoneal dialysis-associated peritonitis”). Only English-language publications were included. Considering the clinical and biological similarities between U. parvum and U. urealyticum, and the fact that many historical reports did not distinguish between the two species, we included all PDAP cases caused by either Ureaplasma species. To the best of our knowledge, seven cases of PDAP caused by Ureaplasma species have been reported in the English literature to date, of which four were specifically confirmed as U. parvum, and the remaining were U. urealyticum or not further speciated (Table 1) [15, 16, 18–20]. Herein, we present a additional case of recurrent culture-negative PDAP presumably caused by U. parvum infection and comprehensively document the diagnosis and clinical characteristics of this infection.

Peritoneal dialysate culture is a routine method for pathogen identification, but detecting U. parvum in PD fluid is a significant challenge. Firstly, as a small prokaryote lacking a cell wall, U. parvum cannot be stained by standard staining techniques [21]. Secondly, it grows slowly in vitro and often requires specialized culture media, as it relies on urease to hydrolyze urea for energy [21–23]; whereas many laboratories lack specialized media for detecting Ureaplasma in peritoneal dialysis fluid. Lastly, the clinical administration of empirical antibiotics, such as quinolones and aminoglycosides, may partially inhibit U. parvum activity, thereby potentially masking key infectious signs [24]. Consequently, U. parvum-associated peritonitis has long been classified as “culture-negative”, resulting in a significant diagnostic and therapeutic blind spot in the management of PDAP.

In addition to cultivation methods, alternative diagnostic approaches, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and mNGS, are available. PCR demonstrates remarkable sensitivity and specificity, delivering results within hours at a cost-efficient rate. However, as a targeted detection technique, its applicability is limited in identifying unknown pathogens. Existing literature indicates that PCR is unsuitable for rapid identification of bacteria in PD effluent [25]. In contrast, mNGS does not require prior knowledge of the pathogen. This technique can accurately identify rare, novel and coinfecting pathogens, making it a crucial tool for the detection of challenging and unknown pathogenic microorganisms in clinical practice [26, 27]. Furthermore, mNGS has demonstrated superior sensitivity compared to conventional culture in detecting pathogens in PDAP and significantly shorter reporting times [28, 29]. Nevertheless, some culture-negative peritonitis respond well to empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics without pathogen identification [30]. The International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis (ISPD) guidelines recommend culture-negative peritonitis should not represent more than 15% of episodes [31], indicating that a certain proportion of negative cultures is acceptable. More precisely, mNGS is most appropriately reserved for specific clinical scenarios such as recurrent culture-negative peritonitis, refractory peritonitis that fails to respond to empirical therapy, and suspected infections caused by atypical or rare pathogens that are difficult to cultivate using conventional methods [32–34]. In our case, the patient suffered from recurrent episodes with persistent culture-negative PDAP, and inadequate response to empirical antibiotics. Given the unpredictable characteristics of the causative pathogen, we employed mNGS, which ultimately identified U. parvum as the causative pathogen. This finding underscores the critical role of mNGS in diagnosing pathogens that are difficult to detect using traditional methods, particularly for patients who experienced recurrent culture-negative peritonitis and had poor responses to empirical antibacterial treatment. Although the cost of testing is relatively high and not universally available, its selective application in specific clinical scenarios can substantially improve diagnostic accuracy and guide effective treatment.

At our center, we have implemented cytokine measurement in PD effluent as an adjunctive clinical tool to complement conventional diagnostics since 2019 for patients presenting with peritonitis, particularly in cases with culture-negative or atypical presentations. Numerous evidence have demonstrated the clinical value of cytokines changes in dialysate in the occurrence of PDAP, especially IL-6 [35–38]. In this case, the blood inflammatory indicators of the patient with PDAP exhibited characteristics distinct from those of typical bacterial infections. Both the WBC count and the neutrophil percentage were almost within normal ranges, CRP was only mildly elevated, and PCT remained consistently normal. In contrast, the WBC count in dialysis fluid was significantly increased, and the IL-10, especially IL-6 level was markedly elevated. This phenomenon may suggest a possible pattern of the inflammatory response induced by U. parvum, characterized by strong local (marked by elevated IL-6) with weak systemic (normal PCT) inflammation. Although the exact mechanisms underlying this pattern remain incompletely understood, we can analyze it from the following aspects. First, as a wall-less bacterium, U. parvum lacks the structural components peptidoglycan and lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which respectively are typical of Gram‑positive and Gram‑negative bacteria. LPS is a potent activator of Toll‑like receptor 4 (TLR4) and can induce systemic inflammatory responses, including increased levels of PCT and CRP [39, 40]. Instead, Ureaplasma lipoproteins, such as the multiple banded antigen (MBA) protein, are characteristic antigens of Ureaplasma [41] and can induce inflammatory responses by activating TLR2/TLR6 [42]. Furthermore, mechanistic studies have demonstrated that Ureaplasma-derived lipid-associated membrane proteins can stimulate human amniotic epithelial cells to produce inflammatory cytokines IL-6 through TLR2-dependent signaling [43]. Although these studies were conducted in non-peritoneal cell types, they provide plausible mechanistic evidence that Ureaplasma species can directly activate local epithelial cells to release IL-6 and other pro-inflammatory mediators, which may promote the recruitment of inflammatory cells to the infection site. However, since this finding is derived from a single case, it should be considered preliminary and hypothesis‑generating, requiring confirmation in larger studies.

U. parvum primarily colonizes the urogenital tract mucosa. Previous studies have indicated that PDAP caused by Ureaplasma parvum or Ureaplasma urealyticum is often related to urogenital tract procedures (such as IUD implantation [19]) or physiological states (such as menstruation [15, 19, 44]). Sexual activity is also a well-recognized route of acquisition and a risk factor for genital tract colonization with Ureaplasma species [20, 45, 46]. In this case, the patient reported no recent sexual intercourse before the three episodes of peritonitis occurred. She had a history of IUD implantation, and mNGS analysis of dialysis fluid confirmed the presence of U. parvum infection. Furthermore, the patient tested positive for Ureaplasma DNA in cervical secretions. While Ureaplasma species are recognized as common commensals of the female genital tract [8, 10], a positive cervical secretions Ureaplasma DNA testing in an asymptomatic patient more likely reflects colonization rather than active infection. Importantly, the DNA testing used in our hospital is a routine screening test that employs generic primers targeting conserved regions of the Ureaplasma genome, hence a positive result only indicates the presence of Ureaplasma DNA and cannot differentiate between U. parvum and U. urealyticum. This consists with the historical classification of these organisms, as they were previously considered a single species [47, 48]. In contrast, the mNGS result from PD effluent provided species-level resolution, identifying U. parvum as the definitive pathogen at the infection site. Considering the implantation of an IUD in the patient, it is plausible that the device compromised the integrity of the reproductive tract mucosa, allowing the translocation of U. parvum from the genital tract to the peritoneal cavity, ultimately leading to peritonitis [18]. Notably, the patient exhibited no typical symptoms of urogenital tract infection, indicating that an asymptomatic carrier state may serve as a concealed source of infection for PDAP. Collectively, these findings suggest that U. parvum colonization in the reproductive tract of female patients should be regarded as a potential risk factor for PDAP, particularly in the presence of an IUD.

Beyond the context of PD, Ureaplasma-associated peritonitis have also been reported in non-dialysis settings, mainly in patients who have undergone gynecological or abdominal surgeries (such as hysterectomy [49], kidney transplantation [50, 51], oocyte retrieval for in vitro fertilization [52], and liver transplantation [53]). For instance, cases have been described following hysterectomy [49] and extensive colorectal surgery [54]. In contrast to PDAP patients, who typically present with turbid dialysate and abdominal discomfort associated with dialysis exchanges, non-dialysis patients may manifest with non-specific symptoms such as fever, abdominal pain, or ileus, which can easily be overlooked or attributed to postoperative complications. Notably, a common feature across both situations is the frequent occurrence of culture-negative results, as routine microbiological techniques fail to detect Ureaplasma species. This underscores the importance of considering Ureaplasma as a potential pathogen in any patient with culture-negative peritonitis, regardless of dialysis status, particularly when there is a history of recent abdomen or gynecological surgeries, as well as urogenital manipulations.

Due to the lack of a cell wall in U. parvum, antibiotics targeting cell wall synthesis including β-lactams and glycopeptides are ineffective. In contrast, medications that act on protein synthesis or DNA replication, such as macrolides, tetracyclines, and fluoroquinolones may demonstrate effectiveness [55]. Regarding the efficacy of antibiotic therapy in our case, although clinical and laboratory improvement were observed during earlier episodes following empirical treatment with intravenous cephalosporins and intraperitoneal amikacin, the recurrent nature of the peritonitis suggests that these regimens achieved partial suppression rather than complete eradication of the infection. Consequently, once the antibiotic pressure was reduced or discontinued, the infection recurred. This pattern of partial suppression followed by relapse is consistent with the clinical course observed in this patient, further emphasizing the importance of using more effective antibiotics and eliminating risk factors to achieve a complete cure. In Table 1, the majority of cases opted for tetracycline-class treatments, including oral doxycycline, minocycline, or IP tigecycline. In this case of our patient, we selected IP levofloxacin followed by oral azithromycin during the third episodes of peritonitis, which also demonstrated good efficacy. However, it is crucial to exercise caution as resistance to quinolones, tetracyclines, and other antibiotics is increasing globally. Therefore, prior to treatment, it is advisable to base the use of these drugs on drug sensitivity assays or the identification of drug resistance genes when possible [56–59].

In addition to antibiotic selection, the capacity of Ureaplasma species to form biofilms may provide further insight into the recurrent nature of this infection [60, 61]. Biofilm formation, which has been observed in both U. parvum and U. urealyticum, enables bacteria to adhere to surfaces and establish a protective matrix, rendering them less susceptible to antibiotics and host defenses. This process is increasingly recognized as an element in the persistence of chronic or recurrent infections, as well as in increased resistance to multiple antibiotics [61–64]. Currently, no established clinical guidelines specifically for disrupting biofilms generation of Ureaplasma. Nevertheless, based on available evidence, several strategies may be considered. A research found that all nine biofilm-forming strains remained fully susceptible to clarithromycin, irrespective of whether they were in planktonic or biofilm growth types [61]. Furthermore, azithromycin may also be effective as biofilm formation does not appear to significantly affect its minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) [60]. Interestingly, the phytoalexins biochanin A combined with fluoroquinolones, have shown promise in restoring antimicrobial activity against resistant Ureaplasma strains, though these remain experimental [65]. From a clinical perspective, given that biofilm formation can limit antibiotic efficacy, removal of potential reservoirs such as an IUD, which provide a solid surface for bacterial attachment and biofilm formation [66], may represent a valuable adjunctive strategy.

Based on this clinical experience from a single case in managing recurrent culture-negative PDAP presumably caused by U. parvum, we propose an exploratory clinical diagnosis and treatment flowchart (Fig. 3). This flowchart is intended as a practical educational framework for clinicians encountering similar challenging scenarios, rather than a validated diagnostic algorithm. Specifically, the flowchart emphasizes the potential role of molecular diagnostic tool mNGS in identifying U. parvum as a rare pathogen. PD effluent IL-6 elevation is incorporated as an adjunctive indicator of the local inflammatory response. Furthermore, risk factors, such as recent gynecological procedures or IUD implantation, are also included to raise clinical awareness. When feasible, elimination of these risk factors, followed by antibiotic therapy targeting protein or DNA synthesis (e.g., tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, or macrolides), may contribute to successful treatment and reduce recurrence rates.

Exploratory diagnosis and treatment flowchart for U. parvum infection in females with PDAP. This flowchart outlines an exploratory diagnostic and therapeutic approach for identifying Ureaplasma parvum infection in females presenting with PDAP, integrating clinical assessment, laboratory parameters (dialysate and serum), and mNGS confirmation. Subsequent management is stratified by the presence of gynecological or abdominal risk factors, including risk factor removal (when feasible) and antibiotic therapy (e.g., tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, or macrolides), with clinical follow-up. The findings and recommendations presented in this report are subject to the inherent limitations of a single case report and require confirmation in larger studies. Despite these limitations, this case carries important clinical messages. For female patients with recurrent culture-negative PDAP who respond poorly to empirical antibiotics, especially those with risk factors such as IUD implantation, menstruation, or urogenital procedures, clinicians should maintain a high degree of suspicion for U. parvum infection. Early application of mNGS can enable rapid and accurate pathogen identification, subsequently facilitating timely targeted therapy and the removal of potential risk factors, which may improve patient outcomes.
