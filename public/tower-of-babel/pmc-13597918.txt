Protocol for a prospective, single-arm pilot trial assessing the clinical and supraspinal neural effects of non-invasive transcranial neuromodulation in adults with overactive bladder
Authors: Betsy H Salazar, Kevin Colbert, Kristopher A Hoffman, Rose Khavari, Rachel A High
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOveractive bladder (OAB) is a prevalent condition characterized by urinary urgency, frequency, and nocturia, with or without urge urinary incontinence. It significantly affects quality of life and is associated with increased rates of depression and sexual dysfunction. Lower urinary tract (LUT) control relies on coordinated activity across peripheral, spinal, and supraspinal networks. Individuals with OAB exhibit abnormal supraspinal network activity. Current treatments primarily target peripheral pathways and have limited efficacy. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) offers a non-invasive approach to modulate networks involved in LUT control.MethodsThis prospective, single-center, single-arm, pilot trial evaluates the clinical and neural effects of neuronavigated rTMS in individuals with OAB. Baseline functional MRI (fMRI) guides individualized targeting of OAB-relevant regions. Participants undergo five sessions of low-frequency stimulation of the pelvic supplementary motor area (SMA) followed by high-frequency rTMS to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). Primary endpoints are changes in LUT control network functional connectivity (FC) during full and empty bladder conditions. Secondary outcomes include changes in OAB symptoms, urinary frequency and incontinence episodes, quality of life, and patient-perceived bladder condition. Pelvic floor muscle (PFM) electromyography (EMG) recorded during the first and fifth sessions provides exploratory physiological outcomes. Clinical outcomes are assessed immediately, three and six weeks post-treatment.DiscussionThis study is the first to evaluate non-invasive neuromodulation of supraspinal regions in individuals with OAB. By targeting brain networks involved in LUT control, this neuronavigated approach may improve FC and provide a novel therapeutic strategy for individuals with OAB refractory to conventional treatments.Trial registration numberNCT06198439. Participant enrollment and follow-up have been completed.Keywords: Overactive bladder, Urinary incontinence, Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, Neuromodulation, Functional magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Overactive bladder (OAB) syndrome, as defined by the International Continence Society, is characterized by urinary urgency, typically accompanied by increased daytime frequency and/or nocturia, with or without involuntary leakage [urge urinary incontinence (UUI)], in the absence of an identifiable cause such as urinary tract infection (UTI) or other detectable disease [1]. It is estimated that approximately 20% of adults experience bothersome urinary urgency, with a higher symptom burden among females, which may contribute to higher rates of care-seeking [2,3]. OAB imposes substantial quality of life, economic, and mental health burdens [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Additionally, UUI, a component of OAB, is commonly associated with higher rates of depression, sexual dysfunction, and limitations in social interactions and physical activities [[6], [7], [8]]. The OAB population is heterogenous, with multiple phenotypes likely contributing to the variable efficacy of currently available therapies. Additionally, OAB is associated with multiple comorbidities that limit therapeutic options, and poor long-term adherence due to side effects remains a major limitation [9].

First-line management of OAB includes behavioral therapies, pelvic floor physical therapy (PFPT), and pharmacologic agents; however, these approaches are often limited by modest efficacy and poor long-term compliance, particularly in the elderly [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. More invasive interventions, such as percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation or sacral neuromodulation, demonstrate variable efficacy and carry risks including the need for revision, bleeding, and infection [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Intradetrusor injections of OnabotulinumtoxinA are an effective alternative but are often associated with urinary retention requiring self-catheterization (8%) and UTIs (35%) in the post-treatment period [18].

Neural control of lower urinary tract (LUT) function relies on coordinated spinal and supraspinal networks that regulate the transition between urine storage and voiding, as demonstrated by prior neuroimaging studies [[19], [20], [21]]. A working brain–bladder model describes three key neural circuits involved in continence and voiding (Fig. 1) [22]. Circuit 1, part of the default mode network (DMN), is believed to suppress voiding through deactivation of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) via a direct pathway to the periaqueductal grey (PAG), a key processor of bladder fullness signals [23]. Circuit 2, part of the salience network (SN), includes the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), supplementary motor area (SMA), and anterior insula, which together process bladder fullness, generate urgency sensations, and coordinate motor output to promote continence [24].

Brain-bladder control network.

Individuals with OAB exhibit abnormal activation and altered functional connectivity (FC) within these regions compared to healthy individuals [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. Cortical responses to bladder filling are exaggerated at higher volumes, while orbitofrontal activation is reduced, suggesting impaired frontal inhibitory control [25]. Insula activation during bladder filling is also exaggerated, reflecting heightened interoceptive processing of bladder sensation [28]. At the network level, individuals with UUI demonstrate impaired adaptive connectivity during bladder filling across regions governing interoception, integration, and decision-making, indicating both regional and network-level dysfunction [29].

Furthermore, these circuits represent viable therapeutic targets, as treatment responders across multiple modalities demonstrate convergent reductions in activation of the cingulate, insula, SMA, and DLPFC during urgency, with post-treatment FC patterns more closely resembling those of healthy individuals [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35]]. Baseline activity levels in these regions are significantly higher in treatment responders compared to non-responders [32], suggesting that supraspinal activity may serve as a predictive biomarker of therapeutic response. A systematic review confirmed these ROIs as consistently implicated across studies and further established that resting-state FC analysis is a promising tool for OAB phenotyping and treatment stratification [34]. Collectively, these findings indicate that OAB reflects dysregulation of supraspinal networks amenable to neuromodulatory intervention.

Non-invasive brain stimulation offers a promising approach for the treatment of OAB, particularly by addressing the shortcomings of current therapies without significant complications [36,37]. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) s an established therapy for neuropsychiatric disorders with a favorable safety profile [38,39]. In the field of benign urology, rTMS proof-of-concept research demonstrated the SMA as a cortical target capable of modulating pelvic floor muscle (PFM) activity [40]. Emerging data also suggest that cortical neuromodulation may improve LUT control in individuals with neurogenic LUT dysfunction [41,42].

Modulation of activity within ROIs that contribute to supraspinal LUT control circuits may provide preliminary insight into potential associations between these regions, PFM activity, and bladder storage symptoms. We propose that this neuronavigated rTMS protocol will reduce SMA activation and enhance deactivation of the medial/lateral prefrontal cortex (m/lPFC) through modulation of the DLPFC. These neural changes are expected to improve PFM tone and enhance treatment responders’ ability to suppress urgency, ultimately decreasing OAB symptom burden.

Methods and design

Study design

This is a prospective, single-center, open-label, single-arm, non-randomized interventional pilot trial using a pre-post design to evaluate the safety and preliminary neurobiological, physiological, and clinical effects of neuronavigated rTMS in adult men and women with OAB. The trial was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06198439) prior to participant enrollment. As of the time of manuscript submission, participant enrollment, intervention delivery, and follow-up assessments have been completed, and data analyses are ongoing. Because no prior studies have evaluated this neuronavigated multisite rTMS approach in individuals with idiopathic OAB, this study was designed as an initial investigation to establish biological plausibility, assess safety, and generate preliminary neuroimaging, physiological, and clinical data to inform future controlled trials. The study comprises a single study population evaluated longitudinally before and after the intervention.

The study investigates pre-post changes in supraspinal LUT control networks and associated clinical effects on OAB symptoms. Specifically, participants undergo five rTMS sessions targeting two regions of interest (ROIs), the pelvic SMA using inhibitory low-frequency rTMS (LF-rTMS) and the DLPFC using excitatory high-frequency rTMS (HF-rTMS). Neuroimaging data are collected at baseline and immediately post-treatment, while physiological responses are assessed using surface pelvic floor muscle (PFM) electromyography (EMG) during the first and final treatment sessions. Clinical measures are collected at baseline, immediately post-treatment, and at 3- and 6-week follow-up. Together, these data are used to evaluate neuroimaging and physiological changes associated with the intervention, as well as the immediate effects and short-term durability of targeted, non-invasive cortical modulation on OAB symptoms.

Study objectives

The overarching objective of this pilot trial is to evaluate the safety and preliminary neurobiological, physiological, and clinical effects of a personalized neuronavigated rTMS protocol in individuals with OAB while generating data to inform future controlled studies.

The primary neuroimaging objectives include evaluating changes in FC within supraspinal LUT control networks following neuromodulation of targeted ROIs associated with urinary urgency and incontinence. These objectives are intended to characterize preliminary neurobiological changes associated with the intervention and establish biological plausibility for further investigation.

Secondary exploratory objectives include evaluation of the effects of rTMS on PFM activity and OAB symptoms. Changes in PFM activity are assessed by comparing resting PFM EMG activity between the first and last rTMS sessions using surface EMG recordings. Clinical effects are evaluated by comparing baseline measures with those obtained immediately post-treatment and at 3- and 6-week follow-up. These include patient-reported outcomes assessed through validated symptom questionnaires and 2-day bladder diaries. Additionally, baseline clinical characteristics are compared between responders and non-responders to identify potential predictors of response to rTMS. Data derived from neuroimaging, physiological, and clinical outcome measures will be used to generate preliminary estimates of treatment response and inform the design of future randomized controlled trials.

Feasibility objectives include assessment of recruitment, retention, and participant experience to evaluate implementation of the study protocol and inform future trial design. Safety objectives include assessment of adverse events and participant-reported side effects collected throughout the study.

Study population and recruitment

This study recruits male and female adults diagnosed with OAB from our tertiary urology and urogynecology clinics. Adults aged 40 to 80 years with OAB symptoms present for at least three months are eligible for screening. Symptom eligibility is confirmed using a two-day bladder diary demonstrating a mean of ≥8 voids per 24 h and ≥3 urgency episodes per 24 h. These bladder diary thresholds were selected to provide objective confirmation of both urinary frequency and urgency and to comply with eligibility criteria requested during FDA review of the Investigational Device Exemption. Adequate cognitive function, defined as a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score >10, is required for inclusion in the study. In addition, participants must demonstrate the capacity to provide informed consent and independently complete study questionnaires and bladder diaries without proxy reporting. Exclusion criteria include individuals who are pregnant, breastfeeding, or planning to become pregnant, as well as those with contraindications to MRI or rTMS. Individuals at increased risk of seizure are excluded based on personal or family history, as well as the use of medications or substances known to lower seizure threshold. Participants with neurologic conditions affecting LUT function, including Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, or spinal cord injury, as well as individuals with a diagnosis of dementia, are not eligible for participation. Additional exclusions include intracranial lesions or recent hemorrhagic stroke, interstitial cystitis, prior pelvic radiation, or bladder augmentation. Individuals with UTI at the time of screening and those who have recently undergone OAB-related interventions are eligible for participation after resolution of the UTI or after sufficient time has elapsed to minimize the potential influence of prior treatment on baseline study assessments, respectively.

Power analysis

There is no published data evaluating outcomes following rTMS in the OAB population to inform formal power calculations. Based on prior neuroimaging studies, approximately 18 subjects are required to achieve 80% power at the single-voxel level for typical task-related activations while accounting for intra- and inter-subject variability [43]. Allowing for an anticipated 10% loss to follow-up, approximately 20 participants are needed. To ensure adequate analyzable data after attrition and potential exclusion of functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) datasets due to motion artifacts, up to 25 participants will be enrolled. Enrollment will be discontinued once complete data are obtained for 20 participants. As this is an early-phase pilot study, formal hypothesis-testing stopping rules and interim efficacy analyses were not used. The study therefore represents a convenience sample rather than a sample formally powered to evaluate treatment efficacy. The sample size was selected to characterize safety and generate preliminary estimates of neuroimaging, physiological, and clinical effects that could inform the design of future randomized controlled trials.

Schedule of events

Participants in this study complete a total of 10 visits over approximately 7-10 weeks. The overall study duration may vary slightly depending on scheduling of the five rTMS treatment sessions and the timing of the post-treatment MRI assessment, which may occur immediately after the final treatment session or within 1-3 days thereafter. Visits 9 (3-week follow-up) and 10 (6-week follow-up) are conducted remotely, with data collection completed through electronic questionnaires. An overview of study procedures and visits is provided in the participant visit flowchart (Fig. 2).

Recruitment & screening (visit 1)

During routine clinical visits, patients presenting with OAB symptoms are identified as potential candidates for study participation. Medical records are reviewed to assess preliminary eligibility, and individuals meeting initial criteria are approached and invited to participate. At the screening visit, detailed information about the study is provided, and all questions are addressed prior to obtaining written informed consent. During this visit, participants’ medical history, physical exam findings, uroflowmetry and post-void residual (PVR) volume data are obtained from the electronic medical record. Validated symptom questionnaires and 2-day bladder diary data are used to confirm OAB diagnosis and determine eligibility for further study participation. Cognitive function is assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA) during visit 1 or prior to performing baseline fMRI during visit 2. Baseline symptoms are assessed through the Overactive Bladder Questionnaire Short Form (OABq-SF), the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), the Lower Urinary Tract Research Network Symptom Index-10 (LURNSI10), the Patient Perception of Bladder Condition (PPBC). Participants complete an MRI Safety Screening Questionnaire to identify contraindications to being in the 3.0 T fMRI scanner. If evidence of UTI is present at screening, treatment must be completed and documented prior to proceeding. A comprehensive list of all assessments is provided in Table 2.

Baseline imaging (visit 2)

Per institutional protocol, participants complete an MRI safety screening questionnaire at the beginning of this visit. For female participants without documentation of hysterectomy or who do not meet criteria for post-menopause (defined as no menstrual period for > 12 months) a urine pregnancy test is performed. All neuroimaging visits for this study are performed using a 3-T Siemens MAGNETOM Vida MRI scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a 64-channel head coil to improve spatial resolution and signal-to-noise ratio. These data are used for neuronavigation and rTMS target localization.

MRI scan with natural bladder filling protocol

Prior to starting the MRI scan, participants are instructed to drink 500 mL of water in the waiting room while completing any necessary paperwork. Immediately after consumption, participants empty their bladder, and PVR volume is measured using a bladder scanner. As urgency increases from the post-void baseline, participants enter the scanner and are positioned supine with padding added for comfort, and an MRI-compatible display provides instructions and prompts for urgency ratings. Participants are continuously monitored to ensure safety and understanding of each step; a squeeze bulb is provided to participants to allow immediate termination of the scan in case of emergency.

Each imaging session consists of an anatomical scan, two discrete fMRI acquisitions, and a diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) scan, with functional imaging including two resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) acquisitions corresponding to full bladder and empty bladder conditions; detailed imaging protocol steps are illustrated in Fig. 3. The imaging sequence begins with scout/localizer images followed by anatomical imaging; participants then rate their urgency prior to acquisition of the full bladder rs-fMRI. Following this, participants exit the scanner, report an urgency score again, and bladder volume is measured using a bladder scanner. Participants then void to completion, and voided volume and immediate PVR are recorded. Participants then return to the scanner for the empty bladder rs-fMRI acquisition, followed by DTI acquisition.

Neuroimaging protocol with natural bladder filling.

MRI scanner parameters

Following initial scout/localizer scans, a T1-weighted anatomical scan is obtained using a 3D acquisition with a repetition time (TR) of 2000 ms, echo time (TE) of 2.5 ms, flip angle of 8°, field of view (FOV) of approximately 220 × 220 mm2, acquisition matrix of 256 × 256, voxel resolution of 0.86 X 0.86 × 1.0 mm3, 176 slices, and an in-plane parallel imaging acceleration factor of 2 (GRAPPA). Both full and empty bladder state rs-fMRIs are obtained using an axial echo-planar imaging blood oxygen level-dependent (EPI BOLD) sequence with a TR of 2000 ms, TE of 30 ms, flip angle of 70°, FOV of approximately 256 × 256 mm2, acquisition matrix of 86 × 86, voxel resolution of 2.98 × 2.98 × 3.0 mm3, 60 slices, and 200 volumes. Additionally, rs-fMRI acquisitions use a simultaneous multislice acceleration factor of 2 and an in-plane acceleration factor of 2. Lastly, DTI images are acquired using a 2D spin-echo EPI sequence with 64 diffusion-encoding directions at b = 1000 s/mm2 and five b = 0 volumes. DTI acquisition parameters include a TR of 4300 ms, TE of 90 ms, flip angle of 90°, FOV of approximately 240 × 240 mm2, acquisition matrix of 122 × 122, voxel resolution of 1.97 × 1.97 × 2.5 mm3, 66 slices, a simultaneous multislice acceleration factor of 2, and an in-plane acceleration factor of 2. The DTI sequence is not repeated post-intervention.

Neuronavigation and motor threshold determination (visit 3)

For this protocol, rTMS is administered using a Brainsight TMS Navigation system (Rogue Research, Montreal, Canada) in combination with the Magstim Rapid2 system (Magstim, Whitland, United Kingdom) fitted with a figure-4-shaped, 70 mm Air Film Coil (Fig. 4).

rTMS treatment set-up.

Participant-specific targets for individualized neuromodulation are determined using both anatomical and fMRI data acquired during baseline imaging. These data are used to guide targeting of ROIs, including the pelvic SMA, where hyperactivation is commonly observed during strong urgency (full bladder condition). No pelvic floor contraction task or other voluntary pelvic maneuver is performed during MRI acquisition. The pelvic SMA is identified anatomically and serves as the predefined stimulation target, while participant-specific hyperactivation patterns are used to inform hemispheric lateralization when present. Hyperactivation is not required for target selection. This information informs lateralization of pelvic SMA stimulation (i.e., preferential targeting of the right or left hemisphere). In contrast, the right DLPFC target is not delineated from participant-specific fMRI activation. Instead, anatomically defined right DLPFC coordinates are identified on each participant's normalized anatomical MRI and used to guide neuronavigated coil placement.

Baseline anatomical MRI images are loaded into Brainsight and normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using anterior commissure–posterior commissure (AC/PC) alignment, a standard neuroimaging reference. Subject-specific targets and corresponding MNI coordinates are then entered into the neuronavigation system. Following calibration of the coil tracker, the participant is seated in the procedure chair. Then the TMS coil is registered in space and relative to the participant using subject and coil trackers (ST-1254 and CT-1284, respectively).

As a secondary measure, a thin canvas skull cap is placed on each subject's head and marked using the standard 10–20 system to provide a consistent spatial reference based on anatomical landmarks (nasion, inion, and preauricular points). Each cap is individualized to the participant and used exclusively during all rTMS sessions. During this process, key regions, including primary motor cortex (M1), SMA, and right DLPFC, are identified and marked. Next, M1 motor threshold (MT), defined as the minimum TMS intensity required to elicit a motor-evoked potential (MEP), is determined to further personalize rTMS treatment dosages.

Resting motor threshold (rMT) determination

Beginning at 50% intensity, the coil is placed over the pre-marked M1 region. The participant is instructed to relax the target hand corresponding to the stimulated hemisphere. Using single-pulse stimulation, the operator adjusts coil position and stimulation intensity until a visible digit twitch is observed in the contralateral hand. Once identified, the coil position is maintained, and 10 consecutive pulses are delivered at intervals of approximately 2–3 s. The rMT is defined as the minimum intensity at which a visible digit twitch is observed in at least 5 out of 10 trials.

Intervention: five sessions of multisite rTMS (visits 3-7)

Participants undergo five rTMS sessions over approximately 1–2 weeks, with stimulation intensity set at 70–80% of rMT. Each session begins with continuous inhibitory LF-rTMS delivered at 1 Hz for 20 min (1200 pulses) targeting the pelvic SMA. Stimulation is applied to the midpoint between bilateral SMA loci and lateralized based on participant-specific fMRI activation patterns.

Following a 2-min interval, excitatory HF-rTMS is applied to the right DLPFC using predetermined neuronavigation coordinates. The HF-rTMS protocol consists of 20 trains of 10 s at 5-10 Hz, with 50-s inter-train intervals (total: 1000–2000 pulses over 20 min). If a participant experiences discomfort during this phase, even after adjusting stimulation to 70% rMT and 5 Hz, the duration may be reduced to 15 min (750–1500 pulses) rather than eliminating this component, provided symptoms remain mild and tolerable. The combined LF- and HF-rTMS stimulation requires approximately 35–40 min per session, with a 2-min interval between components, delivering a total of 1950–3200 pulses.

A TMS device safety questionnaire is administered before and after each of the five rTMS sessions to assess mood and physical symptoms, including anxiety, depression, well-being, pain, dizziness, nausea, and abnormal sensory experiences (vision, sound, smell, taste, touch, and skin irritation). An open-ended comment field is also provided to capture additional participant-reported symptoms or experiences occurring during the stimulation session.

At the first and fifth treatment sessions (visits 3 and 7), participants self-apply surface electrodes to record resting PFM EMG throughout the entire rTMS treatment session, including both the low-frequency stimulation targeting the pelvic SMA and the subsequent high-frequency stimulation targeting the right DLPFC. Participants use a visual guide for electrode placement and receive verbal feedback from study staff. Correct placement is confirmed by reviewing EMG signal quality while participants undergo a cough stress test. EMG recordings are collected continuously during the approximately 35–40-min stimulation session.

Post-treatment and follow-ups (visits 8-10)

Participants undergo a post-treatment MRI scan either immediately following visit 7 (rTMS treatment 5) or within 1-3 days after the final session, using the same protocol as the baseline scan, excluding the DTI sequence to allow for appropriate pre- and post-treatment comparison. Participants complete validated questionnaires (OABq-SF, LURNSI-10, PPBC) and a 2-day bladder diary immediately post-intervention. At 3- and 6-weeks post-intervention (visits 8 and 9), participants complete the same clinical measures, either remotely or during an in-person visit when feasible. Adverse events are monitored throughout the 6-week post-intervention period.

Clinical outcome measures & analysis

At immediately post-rTMS (within 1-3 days), 3-weeks, and 6-weeks post-rTMS, participants repeat baseline questionnaires and bladder diary measures to assess changes in OAB symptom burden and health-related quality of life (OABq-SF) [44], general LUTS burden (LURNSI-10), global perception of bladder condition (PPBC), and 2-day bladder diary metrics. For each participant, the mean number of urgency episodes, voids, incontinence episodes, and pads used per 24 h are calculated. Clinical responder status is defined as a reduction of ≥ 20 points in the OABq-SF symptom bother score, assessed at immediate, 3-week, and 6-week follow-up.

Baseline characteristics are summarized using descriptive statistics. Changes in subjective and objective outcomes are evaluated by comparing baseline to follow-up time points. Longitudinal outcomes will be analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) when observations are complete across study time points and model assumptions are satisfied. Mixed-effects models for repeated measures (MMRM) will be used when observations are missing or unequal numbers of measurements are available across participants, allowing inclusion of all available longitudinal data. All analyses are performed using STATA version 16 (StataCorp LLC; College Station, TX), with statistical significance defined as two-tailed p < 0.05.

Neuroimaging outcome measures & analysis

MRI data will be processed using Analysis of Functional Images (AFNI) software [45]. Standard slice-timing correction as implemented in AFNI will be applied to account for differences in acquisition time between slices. Spatial smoothing will be performed using a 3-mm HWHM Gaussian kernel. No additional temporal filtering will be applied.

Susceptibility-induced geometric distortion is an inherent limitation of gradient-echo EPI acquisitions, particularly in brain regions near air–tissue interfaces. Consequently, potential residual geometric distortion and signal loss will be considered when interpreting regional fMRI findings. Spatial normalization to the anatomical reference and subsequently to standard space will reduce global anatomical misalignment but cannot fully correct susceptibility-induced EPI distortions.

For spatial normalization, we selected Talairach space, a well-established stereotaxic coordinate system and is directly supported by the AFNI processing and atlas framework used in this study. Tailarach space was chosen because the anatomical ROI definitions and FC workflow employed in this study are based on a bilateral Tailarach axis. This provides a consistent anatomical framework for defining ROIs and extracting regional BOLD time courses across participants and reflects the established analysis workflow used by our neuroimaging team rather than a methodological preference for Talairach over MNI space. Although MNI space is also widely used for contemporary fMRI studies, transformation between Talairach and MNI coordinate systems is possible, facilitating comparison of the present findings with studies reported in MNI coordinates. The selected standard space therefore does not prevent comparison with results obtained using MNI-based analyses.

Participant motion will be evaluated through inspection of the fMRI data and motion estimates. If excessive motion is identified during image acquisition, the fMRI sequence will be repeated whenever feasible. Data exhibiting substantial residual motion that compromises image quality or reliable spatial registration will be excluded from further analysis.

Resting-state FC analyses are performed using a custom workflow implemented in R together with AFNI command-line utilities. Analyses are conducted separately for the full- and empty-bladder states at baseline and post-rTMS. Spatially averaged BOLD signal time courses are extracted from each atlas region using AFNI 3dmaskave. Regional BOLD time courses are mean-centered by subtracting the temporal mean of each regional time course prior to FC estimation.

Whole-brain FC is evaluated using an atlas-based regional approach. Pearson correlation coefficients are calculated between every pair of mean-centered regional BOLD time courses to generate a symmetric region-by-region connectivity matrix for each participant, bladder state, and imaging time point. Each connectivity matrix is represented as an undirected weighted graph, with Talairach regions serving as nodes and Pearson correlation coefficients as edge weights. Weighted node strength is calculated for each region as the sum of the weights of all edges incident on that node and serves as the primary regional connectivity measure.

Regional node-strength values are additionally summarized within predefined bilateral functional subnetworks, including the DMN, SN, frontoparietal network, sensorimotor network, dorsal attention network, and voiding-related networks. Subnetwork strength is calculated for each participant and condition as the mean regional node strength across the bilateral ROIs comprising each predefined network. Consistent with the single-group, within-subject pre-post design, changes in subnetwork FC from baseline to post-rTMS are evaluated separately for the full- and empty-bladder states using two-sided paired-samples t-tests. For each comparison, the mean pre-rTMS strength, mean post-rTMS strength, mean paired difference, t-statistic, degrees of freedom, unadjusted p-value, and 95% confidence interval are determined. P-values are adjusted for multiple subnetwork comparisons using both false-discovery-rate and Bonferroni procedures.

Regional FC changes are also evaluated in four prespecified ROIs comprising the bilateral DLPFC and bilateral SMA. Post-minus-pre rTMS changes in regional FC strength are calculated separately for the full- and empty-bladder states. Exploratory analyses compare these regional changes between clinical responders and non-responders using two-sided independent-samples t-tests, with false-discovery-rate and Bonferroni-adjusted p-values also calculated. Clinical response is defined as a reduction of ≥20 points from baseline in the OAB-q Symptom Bother Score at any follow-up time point. Given the anticipated small sample size and potential imbalance between responder groups, these analyses are considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Baseline neuroimaging characteristics are also explored as potential predictors of clinical response.

Additionally, baseline diffusion MRI data are analyzed separately to evaluate white matter microstructural integrity. Diffusion data are processed using DSI Studio, with tract-based measures extracted from white matter pathways relevant to LUT control. Metrics such as fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) are used to characterize structural connectivity and are compared between responders and non-responders to explore potential neuroimaging predictors of clinical response. Because diffusion imaging is acquired only at baseline, these analyses are exploratory and are intended to identify potential structural predictors of treatment response rather than evaluate treatment-induced changes.

Physiological outcome measures & EMG analysis

PFM surface EMG serves as an exploratory physiological outcome to assess changes in resting PFM activity during active cortical stimulation and across repeated rTMS sessions. This approach is informed by prior proof-of-concept work demonstrating that continuous PFM EMG acquired during rTMS can characterize changes in resting PFM activity during cortical neuromodulation [40]. In the present study, PFM EMG is recorded continuously during both LF-rTMS of the pelvic SMA and HF-rTMS of the right DLPFC during the first and fifth treatment sessions. The exploratory hypothesis is that neuromodulation of supraspinal regions involved in LUT control may produce measurable changes in downstream resting PFM activity.

Surface PFM EMG signals are recorded using a BIOPAC MP36R (BIOPAC Systems, Inc., Goleta, CA) system with AcqKnowledge 5.0 software for acquisition and visualization. The data are then exported and processed in LabChart 8 (ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia), where signals undergo standardized preprocessing, including detrending to remove baseline drift, band-pass filtering (typically 20–450Hz) to isolate the physiological EMG frequency range, and notch filtering (50/60Hz) if needed to reduce line noise, with consistent processing applied across all traces.

EMG responses are analyzed within a predefined post-stimulation window (10–60 ms following the TMS artifact). Within each session (sessions 1 and 5), both early and late responses are evaluated; a consistent number of initial and final responses are excluded to avoid edge effects, and a standardized analysis window is applied to the remaining responses across all trials. Peak-to-peak amplitude is calculated for each response, and latency is determined as the time from the stimulation artifact to the earliest deflection (peak or trough) of the EMG signal. For each participant, EMG metrics are averaged within sessions, and changes in amplitude and latency between the first and final treatment sessions are computed. These changes are used to assess modulation of resting PFM activity across the intervention period.

Feasibility outcomes

Feasibility outcomes include recruitment, retention, intervention completion, and participant experience. Recruitment will be summarized by the number of individuals screened, enrolled, and completing the intervention, with reasons for exclusion or withdrawal reported when available. Retention will be summarized across the intervention and follow-up periods. Intervention completion will be assessed based on completion of the five planned rTMS sessions. Participants are asked to provide open-ended feedback in free text form to the question “How would you describe your experience with rTMS?” in a survey administered at 6-week follow up. These measures are intended to characterize implementation of the study protocol and inform the design and conduct of future controlled trials.

Data and safety monitoring

All members of the research team are trained in seizure first-aid, safe administration of rTMS, safeguarding human research participants, and HIPAA regulations. A safety monitoring committee meets biannually to review reports of adverse events and participant-reported side effects, and the study adheres to their recommendations. This study utilizes the Houston Methodist Research Institute (HMRI) Data Review Committee (DRC) as a resource for data and safety monitoring.

Safety oversight is conducted by a Data Safety Monitoring Committee consisting of specialists in the Departments of Urology, Psychiatry, and Gynecology with expertise in OAB and neuromodulation. Adverse events (AEs) are monitored throughout study participation, with standardized assessments performed before and after each of the five rTMS sessions using the TMS device safety questionnaire previously described. Pre- and post-rTMS responses are compared within each session to identify treatment-emergent AEs, defined as symptoms that are newly reported or increase in severity following rTMS relative to the pre-treatment assessment for that session. Spontaneously reported symptoms and other clinical AEs occurring during study participation are also documented. All AEs are reviewed by the principal investigator and graded for severity according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) Version 5.0. Safety outcomes are summarized descriptively by event type, severity, and relationship to the intervention, as applicable.

Any unanticipated AEs are evaluated immediately, and if an unacceptable risk to participants is identified, the study may be paused or terminated. Additionally, procedures are conducted within a clinical hospital setting, allowing for rapid medical response in the event of an adverse reaction. For data security, institutional encryption and data protection protocols are implemented to ensure patient confidentiality and mitigate security risks. Clinical trial number: NCT06198439.

Hard Stops for participants

During each visit, participants complete questionnaires before and after each session to identify potential complications related to fMRI or rTMS. If at any point a participant experiences discomfort during an intervention (fMRI, neuronavigation, or rTMS), clinical staff promptly evaluate the participant and take appropriate action, including stopping the scan or adjusting coil position as needed. All adverse events are documented and reviewed in consultation with the safety monitoring team and IRB, and subsequent actions are taken in accordance with their recommendations. Events are recorded in a standardized system log to ensure accurate tracking and reporting.

Discussion

This study is the first to evaluate preliminary neurobiological, physiological, and clinical changes associated with targeted stimulation of the DLPFC and pelvic SMA regions in idiopathic OAB. Existing neuromodulation approaches primarily target peripheral nerves through invasive interventions; while effective, they carry risks and do not address the central mechanisms underlying OAB [16,17]. This study represents a prospective application of non-invasive transcranial neuromodulation targeting key supraspinal circuits (in circuits 1 and 2) involved in LUT control and evaluates associated changes in LUT control network FC, PFM activity, and clinical outcomes in adult individuals with OAB.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the future results of this pilot study. The single-arm, open-label design was intentionally selected to establish safety, biological plausibility, and preliminary neurobiological, physiological, and clinical effects of this novel personalized neuronavigated rTMS protocol in adults with OAB. At the time the study was designed, no published studies had evaluated rTMS in individuals with idiopathic OAB, and a sham-controlled efficacy trial was therefore considered premature before obtaining preliminary neurobiological, physiological, and clinical data in this population. Prior to conducting a larger sham-controlled randomized trial, it was necessary to obtain preliminary neuroimaging, physiological, and clinical data regarding changes associated with the intervention in this population. The pilot design also provides preliminary information regarding recruitment, retention, intervention completion, and participant experience to inform the design and implementation of future controlled studies. However, the absence of a sham control group limits the ability to distinguish treatment-related effects from placebo responses, regression to the mean, or natural symptom variability, time-related changes, or other nonspecific effects of study participation. Consequently, pre-post neurobiological changes observed in this study cannot be attributed specifically to rTMS. Likewise, causal conclusions regarding neurophysiological and neuroimaging mechanisms cannot be established.

Although MRI-guided neuronavigation enables precise anatomical targeting of the pelvic SMA and right DLPFC, these regions are functionally heterogeneous and participate in neural processes beyond LUT control, including motor, cognitive, and visceral functions. Assessment of neuroimaging outcomes during standardized full and empty bladder states provides LUT-relevant physiological context for evaluating network-level changes following stimulation but does not establish circuit specificity. Accordingly, the present study cannot definitively determine the extent to which observed changes are attributable to modulation of the intended neural circuits rather than nonspecific or time-related effects. Furthermore, pelvic floor EMG is included as a measure of downstream physiological response and is not intended to directly confirm engagement or modulation of specific cortical networks. Instead, the primary neuroimaging outcomes of this study assess pre-post changes in functional connectivity within established LUT control networks during standardized full and empty bladder states. Additionally, the small convenience sample was not powered to establish efficacy or provide definitive estimates of treatment effects, increasing the potential for both false-positive and false-negative findings. Therefore, clinical, physiological, and imaging outcomes should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Future sham-controlled randomized trials will be necessary to determine whether observed neurobiological and clinical changes are specifically attributable to active rTMS, confirm therapeutic efficacy, and more directly investigate potential mechanisms of cortical neuromodulation in OAB.

This work also includes both male and female participants with idiopathic OAB, addressing a gap in the literature, as previous studies have predominantly focused on female populations. Inclusion of both sexes enables exploration of potential sex-specific differences in neural responses to neuromodulation, which have not been previously explored.

Furthermore, this study evaluates changes in PFM EMG during neuromodulation, providing an exploratory physiological measure of the relationship between supraspinal modulation and peripheral motor output. This integrated approach allows concurrent evaluation of changes in central neural circuits and downstream PFM activity, while also exploring how modulation of bladder-related cortical regions may be associated with changes in the perception of bladder volume.

The eligibility criteria were intentionally designed to enroll a relatively homogeneous OAB population with objectively documented urgency and urinary frequency. While this approach improves consistency within the study cohort, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to individuals with OAB who do not meet urinary frequency criteria, including those with UUI who intentionally restrict fluid intake to manage symptoms. Future studies may benefit from broader eligibility criteria to evaluate the applicability of targeted cortical neuromodulation across a wider spectrum of OAB phenotypes.
