BMS representations for generic supermomentum
Xavier Bekaert, Yannick Herfray

Abstract
We revisit the classification, and give explicit realisations, of unitary irreducible representations of the BMS group. As compared to McCarthy’s seminal work, we make use of a unique, Lorentz-invariant, decomposition of supermomenta into a hard and a soft piece, that we introduce and properly define, to investigate the extent to which generic representations depart from usual Poincaré particles and highlight their relations to gravitational infrared physics. We insist on making wavefunctions as explicit as possible. Similarly, we explain how branching to a Poincaré subgroup works in practice: this is physically relevant because this amounts to reading off the field content of a given BMS state in terms of a choice of gravity vacuum. In particular, we emphasise how different gravity vacua differ in their interpretation of the same BMS state, here again providing concrete examples as well as the general procedure. Finally, we demonstrate on an example that generic BMS particles are flexible enough to encode memory, as opposed to usual Poincaré particles.

Generic supermomenta will have trivial little group and the corresponding representations might be the most physically relevant (and the other representations, oddities). Remark 3.3

Let us stress that the massless case is distinguished because we cannot use the naive analogue of the massive and tachyonic cases, i.e. the tentative supermomenta

.

The stabiliser

In McCarthy’s classification, the only BMS little groups including

∎

The above result can be easily generalised to three (or more) massless momenta.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
p_{1}^{\mu},\,\ldots,\,p_{n}^{\mu}
be

non-collinear massless momenta (i.e.
(p_{i})^{2}\leqslant 0
,
\forall i
). Then the sum

\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(z,\bar{z})=P_{1}(z,\bar{z})+\cdots+P_{n}(z,\bar{z})

(5.8)

of the

corresponding hard supermomenta,
P_{1}(z,\bar{z}),\,\ldots,\,P_{n}(z,\bar{z})
, always has BMS little group
\ell_{\mathcal{P}_{n+1}}\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Proof:

The hard supermomentum associated to the massless momentum
p_{i}^{\mu}=\omega_{i}\,q^{\mu}(z,\bar{z})
is a distribution
P_{i}(z,\bar{z})=\omega_{i}\,\delta^{(2)}(z-\zeta_{i},\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta}_{i})
with support at the point
z=\zeta_{i}\in\mathbb{C}P^{1}
. Therefore, the sum (
5.8
) of these hard supermomenta is a supermomentum
\mathcal{P}_{n+1}(z,\bar{z})\in\mathcal{E}[-3]
whose support is the collection
\{\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n}\}
of those
n
points. The momenta
p_{i}
are non-collinear, so those
n\geqslant 3

\zeta_{i}
are distinct. Clearly, the little group
\ell_{\mathcal{P}_{n+1}}
must necessarily preserve this set of

∎

To conclude this section we would like to stress that it is the non-linearity of the space of hard supermomenta which implies that hard representations cannot by themselves ensure supermomentum conservation in a scattering process.

5.3
Absence of branching for hard representations

The last paragraph of Theorem
4.7
leads to the following corollary of Theorem
5.1
.

Corollary 5.5
.

The hard representations of the BMS group do not branch under restriction to a Poincaré subgroup.

To flesh the meaning of this corollary, let us use “particle” interchangeably with “unitary irreducible representation” so that “Poincaré particle” and “BMS particle” stands for UIR of the Poincaré and of the BMS group respectively. The above result means that the physical content, in terms of Poincaré particles, of a BMS hard particle is always a single Poincaré particle and, this, independently of the choice of Poincaré subgroup
ISO_{0}(3,1)\subset BMS_{4}
. In other words,
for hard BMS particles, all observers agree on the field content of a given state
, even though they perhaps are in different gravity vaccua (i.e. disagree on which Poincaré subgroup to consider).

6
Massless unitary irreducible representations of the BMS group

We will here work out explicitly aspects of massless BMS particles: in the first three subsections we describe wavefunctions, the action of the BMS group and inner products. For concreteness, a series of examples will close each of these sections. We then discuss and provide an algorithm for the branching of these UIRs of the BMS group under restriction to a Poincaré subgroup, i.e. how to decompose a given massless BMS particle in terms of usual Poincaré particles. Finally, we discuss the dependence of this decomposition on the gravity vacua, i.e. on the particular chosen Poincaré subgroup.

Decomposition of BMS particles in sum of Poincaré particles

When considering the decomposition (

More generally all the modes

Proposition 6.6
.

A massless UIR of

Remark 6.7
.

The direct integral (i.e. the continuous orthogonal sum) of Poincaré UIRs is not a bug, it a feature of generic BMS particles. In fact, one may expect the appearance of a direct integral (in the branching rule from BMS group to Poincaré subgroup) for all UIRs of

7.1.2
Wavefunctions

Let

\ell_{\mathcal{K}}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}

8
BMS wavefunctions

In
[
31
]
, some (infinite-dimensional) Fourier transforms of BMS wavefunctions were introduced. It resulted in three natural choices of picture (supermomentum space, BMS space, mixed space) for the wavefunctions of BMS particles and it was shown that the last two choices also allow to interpret BMS particles as quantum superposition of usual Poincaré particles. We here come back on results of
[
31
]
in view of the representation-theoretic tools developed in the present article.

This section intends to make contact with physics literature and will accordingly be sloppy with respect to formal integration over infinite-dimensional spaces.

8.1
In supermomentum space

A general state
|\Psi\rangle
of a BMS particle is given by a wavefunction
\Psi(\mathcal{P})
, i.e. a functional on the infinite-dimensional space
\mathcal{E}[-3]
of supermomenta
\mathcal{P}
.
Supertranslations
\mathcal{T}\in\mathcal{E}[1]
act on these BMS wavefunctions in supermomentum space as mere phase shifts
\Psi(\mathcal{P})\mapsto e^{\,i\,\langle\,\mathcal{P}\,,\,\mathcal{T}\,\rangle}\Psi(\mathcal{P})
.
As previously recalled, the UIRs of
BMS_{4}
are in one-to-one correspondence with spaces of fields (taking values in a UIR of the BMS little group) which only have support on a fixed orbit of a supermomentum
\mathcal{P}
under the action of
SL(2,\mathbb{C})
. Such orbits
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{P}}
directly generalise the mass-shell of Poincaré representations to our infinite-dimensional context.

As was discussed in Proposition
3.1
, a supermomentum
\mathcal{P}(z,\bar{z})
can be uniquely written as the sum
\mathcal{P}=P+\Sigma
of a hard supermomentum
P(z,\bar{z})
associated to a four-momentum
p_{\mu}
and a soft charge
\Sigma(z,\bar{z})=\partial^{2}_{z}\partial^{2}_{\bar{z}}\mathcal{N}(z,\bar{z})
. We can thus denote the wave function as

\displaystyle\Psi\Big(p,\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{N}\Big).

(8.1)

8.2
In BMS space

Minkowski spacetime
\mathbb{M}^{3,1}\simeq\frac{ISO_{0}(3,1)}{SO_{0}(3,1)}
is an affine space modeled on the vector space of translations
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
. By close analogy, we will define the space of cuts at null infinity (called here “BMS space”) and the space of gravity vacua, as homogeneous spaces of the BMS group.

Definition 8.1
.

The BMS space is the homogeneous space

\mathbb{B}\simeq\frac{BMS_{4}}{SO_{0}(3,1)}=\frac{SO_{0}(3,1)\ltimes\mathcal{E}[1]}{SO_{0}(3,1)}\,.

(8.2)

It is an affine space modeled on the vector space of supertranslations
\mathcal{E}[1]
. Its elements can be realised geometrically as sections of null infinity
\mathscr{I}
(seen as a line bundle over
S^{2}
) called cuts, hence
\mathbb{B}\simeq\Gamma(\mathscr{I})
.

Here, following Ashtekar
[
5
,
91
]
, we define the space of gravity vacua as a homogeneous space of the BMS group, see also
[
92
,
93
,
94
]
for realisations in terms of Cartan geometry.

Definition 8.2
.

The space of gravity vacua is the homogeneous space

\mathbb{V}\simeq\frac{BMS_{4}}{ISO_{0}(3,1)}=\frac{SO_{0}(3,1)\ltimes\mathcal{E}[1]}{SO_{0}(3,1)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{3,1}}\,.

(8.3)

It is an affine space modeled on the quotient vector space
\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
. For this reason, we referred to elements of
\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
as shifts of vacua (cf. Definition
2.6
).

A corollary of Proposition
2.4
is that the translation group
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\subset BMS_{4}
forms a canonical subgroup of the BMS group. This implies that the BMS space
\mathbb{B}
is naturally an
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
-principal bundle over the space
\mathbb{V}
of gravity vacua. In fact, this is modelled on the property that the supertranslation group
\mathcal{E}[1]
is naturally a
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
-principal bundle over the quotient group
\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
of vacua shifts:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lefttorightarrow}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{B}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{R}^{3,1}}

In practice, BMS space
\mathbb{B}
is foliated by affine leaves which are gravity vacua, each diffeomorphic to Minkowkski spacetime. More concretely, above each element
\partial^{2}\mathcal{C}
of the base

An important property is that there exists a quantitative notion of ‘‘distance’’ between gravity vacua.

34

34

Physically, this purely mathematical notion is presumably related to the relative magnitude of the displacement (almost literally a “distance”) in the memory effect.

Corollary 8.3
.

The affine
\mathbb{V}
space of gravity vacua is a metric space. More precisely, the Lorentz-invariant distance between two gravity vacua
\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{V}_{2}\in\mathbb{V}

is defined by the norm of this vacua shift, i.e.

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=\langle\,\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}\,\rangle=\int_{S^{2}}\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\;\mathcal{T}\,\partial_{z}^{2}\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}=\int_{S^{2}}\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\;|\partial_{z}^{2}\mathcal{T}|^{2}.

(8.4)

Note that the last equality in (

is real.

Since the space of supermomenta
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{E}[-3]
is the dual space of supertranslations

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\;e^{i\langle\mathcal{P},\mathcal{T}\rangle}\;\Psi(\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\mathcal{P}\;e^{-i\langle\mathcal{P},\mathcal{T}\rangle}\;\Psi(\mathcal{T}).

A supertranslation

\displaystyle\Psi\Big(X,\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{C}\Big)

\displaystyle:=\int d^{4}p\;e^{i\,p\cdot X}\;e^{i\langle P,\mathcal{C}\rangle}\;\int\mathcal{D}\mathcal{N}\;e^{i\langle\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{N},\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{C}\rangle}\;\Psi\Big(p,\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{N}\Big).

(8.7)

\partial_{z}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{0}

Let us stress the following. The expression for BMS wavefunctions (

8.3
In momenta and vacua space

Due to the existence of the Lorentz-invariant projections
\mathcal{P}(z,\bar{z})\mapsto p_{\mu}
of supermomenta onto momenta and
\mathcal{T}\mapsto\partial_{z}^{2}\mathcal{C}
of supertranslations onto vacua shifts, one can perform only “half” of the above Fourier transform (

\displaystyle\Psi\Big(p,\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{C}\Big)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\mathcal{N}\;e^{i\langle\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{N},\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{C}\rangle}\;\Psi\Big(p,\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{N}\Big)=e^{-i\langle P,\,\mathcal{C}\rangle}\int d^{4}X\;e^{-i\,p\,\cdot X}\;\Psi\Big(X,\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{C}\Big).

(8.8)

Supertranslations then act on these BMS wavefunction as

\displaystyle\Psi\Big(p,\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{C}\Big)\qquad\to\qquad e^{i\langle P,\mathcal{T}\rangle}\;\Psi\Big(p,\partial^{2}_{z}(\mathcal{C}+\mathcal{T})\Big).

These BMS wavefunctions factorise in a natural way because they depend on momenta and gravity vacua, both of which are elements of Lorentz-invariant (quotient) spaces, respectively
\mathcal{E}[-3]\,/\,\text{Ker}\,\pi
and
\mathcal{E}[1]\,/\,\text{Im}\,q

It is convenient to denote
\psi_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}\left(p\right):=\Psi\big(p\,,\,\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{C}_{0}\big)
the Poincaré state obtained by restricting the above wavefunction to a fixed gravity vacuum
\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{C}_{0}
. In general, a BMS particle can again be interpreted as the superposition of different usual Poincaré particles
\psi_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}\left(p\right)
each of them propagating on a different gravity vacuum
\partial_{z}^{2}\mathcal{C}_{0}
. This picture seems very reminiscent to us to the ideas described in

8.4
Relation to hard representations

Let
\psi(p)
be the wavefunction of a usual massless spin-
s
field. To which BMS wave function
\psi_{\mathcal{C}}(p)
does this correspond? There is in fact a huge ambiguity corresponding to all the possible ways to lift a Poincaré representation to a BMS representation.

The most obvious choice is to lift the Poincaré representation to a hard BMS representation by declaring that the BMS wavefunction does not depend on the vacuum

\psi_{\mathcal{C}}\left(p\right)=\psi(p)\,.

(8.9)

In supermomentum space, this means that

\Psi\Big(p,\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{N}\Big)=\psi\left(p\right)\,\delta(\mathcal{N})

(8.10)

and this indeed coincides with the hard representation of a massless field.

As an alternative, one might declare that the Poincaré wavefunction
\psi(p)
corresponds to a state localised in a given gravity vacuum

\psi_{\mathcal{C}}\left(p\right)=\psi(p)\,\delta(\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}_{0})\,,

(8.11)

i.e it is only defined in a particular vacuum and is zero in all others.
This state is the closest thing to the classical picture of working in a fixed particular background. In supermomentum space, this however means that

\Psi\Big(p,\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{N}\Big)=\psi\left(p\right)\;e^{i\langle\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{N},\partial^{2}_{z}\mathcal{C}_{0}\rangle}.

(8.12)

Fock space of BMS particles

We can now mention the second quantisation of BMS particles proposed in

If

Remark 8.4
.

Instead of the supermomentum eigenbasis, one can also consider the mixed (i.e. momentum & vacuum) eigenbasis of on-shell BMS particles. Let us denote by

In fact, the terminology “gravitational vacua” may be misleading in this respect since the present work shows that it is logically consistent for each BMS particle to somehow feel its own “gravitational vacuum”. This last point of view is very natural if one considers the situation in terms of the supermomentum representation, where each BMS particle has its own soft charge, cf. (

10
Conclusion and summary

Let us conclude by summarising our main results.

In Section
3
, we introduced a Lorentz-invariant decomposition of supermomenta into hard and soft pieces (Proposition
3.1
). This decomposition is based on the following important properties: i) a supermomentum is soft iff it projects onto a vanishing momentum (Proposition
2.14
); ii) the hard representations of the BMS group coincide with usual Poincaré representations, in particular, they do not branch under restriction to a Poincaré subgroup (Corollary
5.5
) since the little group of a hard supermomentum is identical to the little group of its associated momentum (Subsection
5.1
). Some conceptual and technical subtleties related to the nonlinearity of the hard vs soft decomposition (Propositions
3.4
-
3.6
) and their relations with Weinberg soft factors, were discussed in Subsection
3.2
.

We emphasised in Section
5
that the non-linearity of the decomposition means that hard states alone cannot fulfil conservation of supermomenta (Propositions
5.3
-
5.4
); therefore, in order to enforce this conservation law, other type of BMS particles need to be introduced. While hard BMS particles coincide with Poincaré particles, realised in terms of their natural scattering data (Subsection
5.4
), in all other cases, a single BMS particle is in general a BMS multiplet of usual Poincaré particles.

In Sections
6
and
7
, we provided the explicit form of the wavefunctions of generic BMS particles (Subsection
6.3
and
7.1
).
An explicit algorithm for the decomposition of these wavefunctions in suitable bases of functions (on the quotient of the Poincaré little group by the BMS little group) was provided for the decomposition of the BMS particle in terms of Poincaré particles.
41
41

41

Propositions
6.6
and
6.8
were mentioned in
[
12
]
but the direct sum of usual massless representations was not mentioned explicity. Furthermore, Propositions
6.10
-
6.11
are new and complete the analysis of all possible cases of branching in the massless case. Finally, Propositions
7.3
-
7.4
of McCarthy were recovered in an explicit way.
In Section
6.5
, we stressed that this interpretation of generic BMS particles in terms of superposition of usual Poincaré particles crucially depend on the choice of gravity vacuum, that is on the choice of a Poincaré subgroup
ISO_{0}(3,1)\subset BMS_{4}
for the branching. For instance, a state appearing as a simple scalar field in one gravity vacuum will be interpreted, in another gravity vacuum, as a complicated quantum superposition of particles of all possible spins (including, typically, continuous spins, in the massless case).

In Section
8
, the wavefunctions of BMS particle introduced in
[
31
]
were discussed in terms of the homogenous spaces appearing in the asymptotic quantisation program (the space of cuts at null infinity, the space of gravitation vacua, etc). We stressed that there exists a Lorentz-invariant notion of distance between gravitational vacua (Corollary
8.3
). We also pointed out that the Fock space of any countable collection of BMS particles is a separable Hilbert space (Subsection
8.5
).

In Section
9
, we exhibited, on a particular example of state, a concrete high-energy limit in which one can control the relative weight of the tower of higher-spin particles in the BMS multiplet and which furthermore allowed for an average supermomentum including memory. It remains to be seen if such types of limits may help to obtain a correspondence principle where usual S-matrix results are recovered, in some suitable regime, from a BMS-invariant S-matrix. At the end of this section, we briefly argued that the distance between gravitational vacua could play a role in such a mechanism. We also constructed examples of normalisable
BMS
states allowing for a pole in the energy, with the resulting
L^{2}
norm being soften by the presence of continuous-spin particles in the spectrum. The physical properties of such examples need to be explored further.

Another important direction to explore in the future would be to perform a systematic investigation of soft BMS particles (i.e. UIRs of BMS group with vanishing momentum) along the same lines as what we did for generic BMS particles. Another work in progress is the classification of the UIRs of the asymptotic symmetry group of quantum electrodynamics (QED) with massless charged fields, i.e. the group
SO(3,1)\ltimes\big(\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\times\mathcal{E}[0]\big)
. This could provide an alternative path for defining a Hilbert space of asymptotic multi-particle states suitable for an infrared-finite scattering theory of massless QFTs, such as QED with massless charged fields or Yang-Mills theory. This different road could perhaps circumvent some of the undesirable features mentioned e.g. in

Appendix A
Paneitz operator: image and kernel

In Appendix
A.1
, we provide a proof that Proposition
2.14
works for smooth densities. We prove that it extends to distributions in Appendix
A.2
.
Finally, in Appendix
A.3
we provide a proof of Proposition
2.14
in the Hilbert topology.
We include the latter proof because this reasoning turns out to be instructive and closer to usual computations (in terms of Hermitian forms and Hilbert spaces).

Let us assume that
\mathcal{E}[w]
is the vector space of smooth conformal densities of weight
w
on the celestial sphere. The topological dual
\mathcal{E}[w]^{\prime}
is spanned by the corresponding distributions (i.e. continuous linear forms on
\mathcal{E}[w]
). In particular,
\mathcal{E}[-2-w]\subset\mathcal{E}[w]^{\prime}
since smooth conformal densities of weight
-2-w
can be thought as distributions acting on smooth conformal densities of weight
w
. In particular,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[1]

\displaystyle\subset\mathcal{E}[-3]^{\prime},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[-3]

\displaystyle\subset\mathcal{E}[1]^{\prime}.

(A.1)

where
\mathcal{E}[1]^{\prime}
is the vector space of distributions corresponding to supermomenta.
Let us denote
Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})\subset\mathcal{E}[-3]
the space of smooth conformal densities of weight
-3
annihilating the translation subspace
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\subset\mathcal{E}[-1]
and
\big(Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})\big)^{\prime}
the corresponding dual space. One has

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}

\displaystyle\subset\big(Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})\big)^{\prime}\,,

\displaystyle Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})

\displaystyle\subset\big(\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\big)^{\prime}\,.

(A.2)

A.1
Proof of Proposition
2.14
in the smooth case

For smooth densities, Proposition
2.14
can be expressed as the following

Proposition A.1
.

The Paneitz operator

\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}:\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})

(A.3)

is an isomorphism in the smooth category.

Proof:

This can be checked very concretely by performing some computations in terms of the representative
f\in C^{\infty}(S^{2})
, for which one can use spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell}^{m}
as a basis. From
[
41
, Eq. (3.24)]
one has

\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}Y_{\ell}^{m}=\gamma^{-3}\ell(\ell^{2}-1)(\ell+2)\,Y_{\ell}^{m}.

(A.4)

Therefore the kernel of the Paneitz operator is spanned precisely by the representatives with only spherical harmonics
\ell=0
and
\ell=1
in their decomposition. In other words, the kernel coincides with translations
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\subset\mathcal{E}[1]
. Furthermore, the image of the Paneitz operator coincides with representatives whose spherical harmonics
\ell=0
and
\ell=1
vanish. Put differently, its image coincides with
Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})\subset\mathcal{E}[-3]
. This proves Proposition
2.14
for smooth densities.
∎

.

A.1
can also be extracted from

A.2
Proof of Proposition
2.14
in the case of distributions

By duality, we deduce from Proposition
A.1
the following

Proposition A.3
.

The Paneitz operator extends to an isomorphism

\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}:\big(Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})\big)^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}\big(\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\big)^{\prime}\,.

(A.7)

between spaces of distributions.

Proof:

Let us first prove surjectivity. If
\beta\in\big(\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\big)^{\prime}
we can define a distribution
\alpha\in\big(Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})\big)^{\prime}
via

\displaystyle\langle\alpha,\psi\rangle

\displaystyle:=\langle\beta,\chi\rangle\,,

since

\displaystyle\forall\psi\in Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})\,,\exists!\,\chi\in\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}:\psi=\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\chi\,,

(A.8)

where we used Proposition
A.1
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\langle\beta,\chi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha,\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\chi\rangle=\langle\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\alpha,\chi\rangle\,,

\displaystyle\forall\chi\in\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\,.

(A.9)

Hence,
\beta=\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\alpha
.

Let us now prove injectivity. If
\alpha\in\big(Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})\big)^{\prime}
satisfies
\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\alpha=0
, then

\displaystyle\langle\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\alpha,\chi\rangle=\langle\alpha,\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\chi\rangle=0\,,\quad\forall\chi\in\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}.

(A.10)

Since any
\psi\in Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})
can always be written as
\psi=\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\chi
for some
\chi\in\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
, we deduce that
\alpha=0
.

∎

From Proposition
A.3
, we can deduce that soft supermomenta always belong to the image of the Paneitz operator.

Corollary A.4
.

Any element
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{E}[-1]^{\prime}
which annihilates the translation subspace
\mathbb{R}^{3,1}\subset\mathcal{E}[-1]
takes the form
\mathcal{P}=\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\mathcal{N}
where
\mathcal{N}\in\big(Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})\big)^{\prime}
.

Proof:

Since
\mathcal{P}
annihilates translations by assumption, it defines a distribution on
\mathcal{E}[-1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
that we will denote with the same letter:
\mathcal{P}\in(\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1})^{\prime}
. By Proposition
A.3
, this means that there exists
\mathcal{N}\in\big(Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})\big)^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{P}=\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\mathcal{N}
.

∎

A.3
Proof of Proposition
2.14
in the Hilbert topology

Let
\mathcal{T}\in\mathcal{E}[1]
be a supertranslation and let us define the positive semi-definite quadratic form
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}.\,,\,.\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
on
\mathcal{E}[1]
given explicitly by the expression (
8.4
).
In order for
\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}
to exist, it is enough to assume that the representatives of supertranslations belong to the Sobolev space
H^{2}(S^{2})
, i.e.
\mathcal{T}\in L^{2}(S^{2})
as well as all its derivatives up to second order. In particular, this works for smooth densities on the celestial sphere (and clearly does not work for distributions since their norm can be infinite).

The comments in the proof of Proposition
2.13
already ensure the exactness on the left in (
2.22
):
\text{Im}(q)=\text{Ker}(\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2})
. So one should prove the exactness on the right:

\text{Im}(\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2})=\text{Ker}(\pi)
.
The inner product (
2.19
) implies that
\text{Im}(\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2})\subseteq\text{Ker}(\pi)
since any supermomentum
of the form
\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\mathcal{T}_{1}
annihilates all translations
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, that is to say
\langle\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2}\rangle=0

for any
\mathcal{T}_{1}\in\mathcal{E}[1]
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}\in\text{Im}(q)\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
. Since the scalar product (
2.19
) is non-degenerate on the quotient
\mathcal{E}[1]\big/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
, we have that
\text{Im}(\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2})\simeq\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}
is a Hilbert space
[
45
, Section III.6.5]
and, by Fréchet-Riesz theorem,

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathcal{E}[1]/\mathbb{R}^{3,1}&\to&(\mathcal{E}[1]\big/\mathbb{R}^{3,1})^{\prime}=Ann(\mathbb{R}^{3,1})\\[3.99994pt]
\mathcal{T}&\mapsto&\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\,\mathcal{T}\,,\,\,.\,\,\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}=\langle\,\eth^{2}\bar{\eth}^{2}\mathcal{T}\,,\,\,.\,\,\,\rangle\end{array}

(A.11)

is an isomorphism.
This concludes the proof in the Hilbert topology.

B.1
Proof of Proposition
3.4
: identity for massless hard supermomenta

For completeness, let us explain the origin of the identity (
3.8
). It is based on the Cauchy–Pompeiu formula

\mathcal{T}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\partial D}\frac{\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})}{z-\zeta}\,dz+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{D}\!\!\!dz\wedge d\bar{z}\;\frac{\partial_{\bar{z}}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})}{z-\zeta}

(B.1)

where
D\subset\mathbb{C}
is an open domain (topologically a disk) inside the complex plane and
\zeta\in D
. Making the replacement
\mathcal{T}\mapsto(\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta})\partial_{\bar{z}}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})
in (
B.1
) one obtains

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta}}{z-\zeta}\,\partial_{\bar{z}}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,dz+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\!\!dz\wedge d\bar{z}\;\frac{\partial_{\bar{z}}[(\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta})\partial_{\bar{z}}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})]}{z-\zeta}

(B.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta}}{z-\zeta}\,\partial_{\bar{z}}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,dz+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\!\!dz\wedge d\bar{z}\;\frac{\partial_{\bar{z}}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})}{z-\zeta}+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\!\!dz\wedge d\bar{z}\;\frac{\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta}}{z-\zeta}\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})

where the domain
D
and the contour
\partial D
have been and will be left implicit from now on.
Subtracting (
B.2
) to (
B.1
), one obtains

\displaystyle\int\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\;\left(\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,\delta^{(2)}(z-\zeta,\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta})-\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\frac{\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta}}{z-\zeta}\right)

(B.3)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\left(\frac{\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})}{z-\zeta}-\partial_{\bar{z}}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\frac{\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta}}{z-\zeta}\right)dz\,,

which prove (
3.8
).

Since
\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})=|\hat{z}|^{-2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\hat{z},\bar{\hat{z}})
is a smooth globally-defined weight-one density on the celestial sphere, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})=|\hat{z}|^{-2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\hat{z},\bar{\hat{z}})

(B.4)

\displaystyle=|\hat{z}|^{-2}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\hat{z}\,\partial_{\hat{z}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\bar{\hat{z}}\,\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\hat{z}\bar{\hat{z}}\,\partial_{\hat{z}}\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\frac{\bar{\hat{z}}^{2}}{2}\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}^{2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\frac{\hat{z}^{2}}{2}\partial_{\hat{z}}^{2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\mathcal{O}\big(|\hat{z}|^{3}\big)\right)

\displaystyle=z\bar{z}\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\bar{z}\,\partial_{\hat{z}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+z\,\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\partial_{\hat{z}}\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\frac{z}{2\bar{z}}\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}^{2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\frac{\bar{z}}{2z}\partial_{\hat{z}}^{2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\mathcal{O}\big(|z|^{-1}\big).

Making use of this asymptotic behavior, the boundary term on the right-hand side of (
B.3
) can then be explicitly computed and gives a non zero contribution

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\left(\frac{\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})}{z-\zeta}-\partial_{\bar{z}}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\frac{\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta}}{z-\zeta}\right)dz=\bar{\zeta}\zeta\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\bar{\zeta}\,\partial_{\hat{z}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\zeta\,\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\partial_{\hat{z}}\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0).

To see this, first notice that, in the limit that we are considering, of infinite radius for our circular contour, the correction term
\mathcal{O}\big(|z|^{-1}\big)
in the expression of
\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})
decays too fast to contribute to the boundary terms. The other contributions can then be computed by writing
z=re^{i\theta}
and performing the integral in
\theta
at fixed radius
r
. Therefore

\displaystyle\int\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\;\left(\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,\delta^{(2)}(z-\zeta,\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta})-\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\frac{\bar{z}-\bar{\zeta}}{z-\zeta}\right)

\displaystyle\;\;=\;\;\bar{\zeta}\zeta\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\bar{\zeta}\,\partial_{\hat{z}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\zeta\,\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\partial_{\hat{z}}\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)

(B.5)

\displaystyle\;\;=\;\;\int\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\;\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\;q^{\mu}(\zeta,\bar{\zeta})\,\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\big(\delta^{(2)}(z-\infty)\big).

B.2
Proof of Proposition
3.5
: identity for massive hard supermomenta

Let us start from

\int\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\,\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,\frac{m^{4}}{\pi\big(q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big)^{3}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\,\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\left(\frac{\big(\partial_{z}q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big)^{2}}{2\,q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p}\right)

where we used the valid equality (
3.13
) between functions on the complex plane.
Integrating by part, we obtain

\displaystyle\int\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\left(\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,\frac{m^{4}}{\pi(q\cdot p)^{3}}-\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\frac{(\partial_{z}q\cdot p)^{2}}{2\,q\cdot p}\right)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\left(\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\partial_{\bar{z}}\left(\frac{(\partial_{z}q\cdot p)^{2}}{2\,q\cdot p}\right)-\partial_{\bar{z}}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\frac{(\partial_{z}q\cdot p)^{2}}{2\,q\cdot p}\right)dz\,,

(B.6)

where, as in Appendix
B.1
, we implicitly integrate on the left-hand side on an open disk
D\subset\mathbb{C}
inside the complex plane while the contour integral on the right-hand side is on the circle
\partial D
.

Since
\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})=|\hat{z}|^{-2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(\hat{z},\bar{\hat{z}})
is a smooth globally-defined weight-one density on the celestial sphere, we have the asymptotic behavior (
B.4
). On the other hand, making use of

q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p=(p^{3}-p^{0})+(p^{1}-i\,p^{2})\;z+(p^{1}+i\,p^{2})\;\bar{z}-(p^{0}+p^{3})\;|z|^{2}\,,

(B.7)

\partial_{z}q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p=(p^{1}-i\,p^{2})-(p^{0}+p^{3})\;\bar{z}\,,

(B.8)

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\big(\partial_{z}q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big)^{2}}{q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p}\,=\,\frac{\bar{z}}{z}\,

\displaystyle\Bigg(-(p^{0}+p^{3})+\bar{z}^{-1}(p^{1}-i\,p^{2})-z^{-1}(p^{1}+i\,p^{2})

(B.9)

\displaystyle+|z|^{-2}\left(p^{0}-p^{3}\right)-z^{-2}\frac{(p^{1}+i\,p^{2})^{2}}{p^{0}+p^{3}}+O\left(|z|^{-3}\right)\Bigg).

Using the above expansion as well as the expansion (
B.4
) of the supertranslation
\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})
,
the boundary term on the right-hand side of (
B.6
) can then be explicitly computed in the limit where the boundary becomes an infinitely large circle. First one should check that, in the limit of large radius that we are considering, the subleading terms
O\left(|z|^{-3}\right)
in these asymptotic expansions do not contribute. It then turns out that several contributions of the leading terms vanish due to the angular integration
\int d\theta e^{i\theta}=\frac{1}{|z|}\oint dz
in (
B.6
). Nevertheless, some terms survive the final angular integration and yield a non-zero contribution

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\left(\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\partial_{\bar{z}}\left(\frac{(\partial_{z}q\cdot p)^{2}}{2\,q\cdot p}\right)-\partial_{\bar{z}}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\frac{(\partial_{z}q\cdot p)^{2}}{2\,q\cdot p}\right)dz

(B.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{3}-p^{0}}{2}\,\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)-\frac{p^{1}-ip^{2}}{2}\,\partial_{\hat{z}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)-\frac{p^{1}+ip^{2}}{2}\,\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)-\frac{p^{0}+p^{3}}{2}\partial_{\hat{z}}\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0).

Therfore we conclude that

\displaystyle\int\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\left(\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,\frac{-m^{4}}{\pi(q\cdot p)^{3}}+\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\frac{(\partial_{z}q\cdot p)^{2}}{2\,q\cdot p}\right)

\displaystyle\;\;=\;\;\int\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\;\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\;p^{\mu}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\big(\delta^{(2)}(z-\infty)\big).

B.3
Proof of Proposition
3.6
: identity for massive and massless momenta

Since
\partial_{z}^{2}q_{\mu}(z,\bar{z})=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

\partial_{z}^{2}\Big((q\cdot p)\;\ln|q\cdot p|\Big)=\frac{(\partial_{z}q\cdot p)^{2}}{q\cdot p}

(B.11)

Thus, making use of Proposition
3.5
,

\displaystyle\int\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\,\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})P(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\,\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\frac{\big(\partial_{z}q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big)^{2}}{2\,q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p}+\Big(p^{\mu}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\mathcal{T}\Big)(z=\infty)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\,\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,\partial_{z}^{2}\Big(\big(q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big)\;\ln\big|q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big|\Big)+\Big(p^{\mu}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\mathcal{T}\Big)(z=\infty)

integrating by parts gives

\displaystyle\!\!\int\!\frac{i}{2}dz\wedge d\bar{z}\!\left(\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})P(z,\bar{z})+\frac{1}{2\pi}\partial_{z}^{2}\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\,\ln\big|q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big|\right)-\Big(p^{\mu}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\mathcal{T}\Big)(z=\infty)

(B.12)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2i}\oint\Bigg(\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,\partial_{z}\Big(\big(q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big)\,\ln\big|q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big|\Big)-\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})\,\big(q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big)\;\ln\big|q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big|\Bigg)d\bar{z}.

Now, on the one hand (
B.7
) implies that

\big(q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big)\;\ln\big|q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p\big|=-\,(p^{0}+p^{3})\;|z|^{2}\ln\Big(|p^{0}+p^{3}|\;|z|^{2}\Big)+\mathcal{O}\Big(|z|\ln(|z|)\Big)

(B.13)

and, on the other hand,
\bar{\eth}^{2}\mathcal{T}
is a smooth spin-weighted density of weight
(w=-1,s=2)
which behaves asymptotically as

\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}\mathcal{T}(z,\bar{z})=\frac{z}{\bar{z}^{3}}\partial_{\bar{\hat{z}}}^{2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}(0,0)+\mathcal{O}\Big(|z|^{-3}\Big).

(B.14)

Here again, in the limit of large radius that we are considering, the subleading terms in these asymptotic expansions do not contribute to the boundary term (
B.12
). Finally, a careful calculation shows that the contribution of the leading order terms vanishes due to the angular integration.

3.7

Let
\{p^{\mu}_{i}\}
be a collection of momenta satisfying momentum conservation,
\sum_{i}p^{\mu}_{i}=0

\mathcal{S}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{i}(q\cdot p_{i})\ln|q\cdot p_{i}|

(B.15)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(z,\bar{z})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{i}q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p_{i}\;\ln\Big[(|p^{0}_{i}+p^{3}_{i}|\,|z^{2}|\Big(1+F_{i}(z,\bar{z})\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{i}q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p_{i}\;\ln\Big(|p^{0}_{i}+p^{3}_{i}|\Big)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{i}q(z,\bar{z})\cdot p_{i}\;\ln\Big(1+F_{i}(z,\bar{z})\Big)

(B.18)

One sees that both terms in (

\mathcal{S}

The Killing metric on

\displaystyle g_{Killing}=\text{Re}\Big[\text{Tr}\big[(g^{-1}dg)\odot(g^{-1}dg)\big]\Big]

We have

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\big[(g^{-1}dg)\odot(g^{-1}dg)\big]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\big[(N^{-1}dN)^{2}\big]+\text{Tr}\big[L^{-1}dL)^{2}\big]+2\,\text{Tr}\big[(N^{-1}dN)\odot(dL\,L^{-1})\big]

and direct computation gives:

\displaystyle N^{-1}dN

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\omega^{-1}d\omega&\;\omega d\zeta\\
0&\;\frac{1}{2}\omega^{-1}d\omega\end{pmatrix}

(C.1)

\displaystyle L^{-1}dL

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{i}{2}d\alpha&\quad 0\\
d\beta-ie^{i\tfrac{\alpha}{2}}\beta\,d\alpha&\quad-\frac{i}{2}d\alpha\end{pmatrix}

(C.2)

\displaystyle dL\,L^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{i}{2}d\alpha&\;0\\
e^{-i\alpha}d\beta&\;-\frac{i}{2}d\alpha\end{pmatrix}

(C.3)

and thus

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\big[(L^{-1}dL)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\,d\alpha^{2}

(C.4)

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\big[(N^{-1}dN)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\omega^{-2}d\omega^{2}

(C.5)

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\big[(N^{-1}dN)\odot(dLL^{-1})\big]

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\omega^{-1}\,d\omega d\alpha+\omega e^{-i\alpha}\,d\zeta d\beta

(C.6)

and therefore

\displaystyle g_{Killing}=-\frac{1}{2}\,d\alpha^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\,\omega^{-2}d\omega^{2}+\omega\left(e^{-i\alpha}\,d\zeta d\beta+e^{i\alpha}\,d\bar{\zeta}d\bar{\beta}\right)

(C.7)

\mu_{Haar}=\sqrt{\det\big(g_{Killing}\big)}\,d^{6}X=\frac{1}{2}\,\omega\,d\omega\,d\zeta_{1}\,d\zeta_{2}\,d\alpha\,d\beta_{1}\,d\beta_{2}\,,

(C.8)

where
\zeta=\zeta_{1}+i\,\zeta_{2}
and
\beta=\beta_{1}+i\,\beta_{2}

Appendix E
Continuous-spin representations of the Poincaré group

We here briefly recap the “continuous-spin” UIRs of the Poincaré group (for more details, see e.g. the reviews
[
81
,
82
]
and refs therein). Before that, it is useful to describe the UIRs of the massless little group, i.e. the UIRs of the Euclidean group (see e.g.
[
101
, Chapter 9]
for a basic introduction).

E.1
Unitary irreducible representations of the Euclidean group

The Euclidean group
ISO(2)=SO(2)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a semidirect product of the rotation group
SO(2)
acting on the Abelian normal subgroup
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Therefore, the corresponding UIRs are again obtained from Wigner’s method of induced representations.

The first step of this procedure is to pick an eigenvalue of the translation generators of the subgroup
\mathbb{R}^{2}\subset ISO(2)
: this is nothing but a vector
\vec{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The second step amounts to pick a UIR of the stabiliser of this vector. Accordingly, there are two cases to distinguish:

1.

\underline{\vec{\pi}=\vec{0}}:
The degenerate case
\vec{\pi}=\vec{0}
corresponds to representations which are unfaithful UIRs of
ISO(2)
but which are faithful UIRs of
SO(2)
instead. Therefore, the UIRs are labeled by a single number
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and they pick a phase
e^{i\lambda\theta}
under a rotation of angle
\theta
. This label may be called the “helicity”. This label
\lambda
is clearly an integer for single-valued representations and a half-integer for double-valued representations of
ISO(2)
, i.e.
\lambda\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
for UIRs of
\widetilde{ISO}(2)
.

2.

\underline{\vec{\pi}\neq\vec{0}}:
The generic case
\vec{\pi}\neq\vec{0}
corresponds to faithful UIRs of
ISO(2)
. Since the stabiliser in
SO(2)
of a plane vector is the identity, the second step in the method of induced representations is absent. Therefore, these UIRs of
ISO(2)
are only labeled by the norm
\mu:=|\vec{\pi}|\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
(since the orbits of a vector in the plane under rotations are labeled by the radius of the vector).

Several remarks are in order because they will be instrumental for the identification of continuous-spin modes inside wavefunctions of massless BMS particles in Section
6.4
.

Remark E.1
.

In practice, the UIRs in the second case are carried by square-integrable functions on the circle of radius
\mu
in the plane of vectors
\vec{\pi}
. However, as always there are infinitely many equivalent realisations of a given UIR. For instance, the UIR can be represented by wavefunctions
\Psi(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
on the plane with support on the circle
(\pi_{1})^{2}+(\pi_{2})^{2}=\mu^{2}
. Equivalently, in dual space they are square-integrable solutions
\Psi(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
of the Helmholtz equation

(\partial^{2}_{\beta_{1}}+\partial^{2}_{\beta_{2}}+\mu^{2})\Psi=0\,.

(E.1)

Remark E.2
.

Furthermore, one may equivalently consider realisations of the same UIRs of
ISO(2)
in terms of vector (or tensor) fields on the plane. Therefore, although these fields may have non-vanishing helicities, they would nevertheless carry an equivalent UIR, by assumption. This point will be important later on. For instance, consider for a given

E.2
Massless representations of the Poincaré group

The Poincaré little group of a null momentum
p_{\mu}
is isomorphic to the Euclidean group,
\ell_{p}\simeq ISO(2)
.
The step 2 in Theorem
4.1
implies that the massless UIRs of
ISO_{0}(3,1)
are induced from UIRs of the Euclidean subgroup
ISO(2)\subset SO_{0}(3,1)
. Accordingly, one may introduce a vector
\vec{\pi}
describing the spinning degrees of freedom. As explained above, there are two cases to distinguish:

1.

\underline{\vec{\pi}=\vec{0}}:
These UIRs of the little group
ISO(2)
are unfaithful. In fact, they are genuine UIRs of
SO(2)
labeled by a single integer or half-integer
\lambda\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
according to whether the representation is single or double valued. They induce UIRs of the Poincaré group
ISO_{0}(3,1)
which are the usual massless representations, where
\lambda
is the helicity.

2.

\underline{\vec{\pi}\neq\vec{0}}:
These UIRs of
ISO(2)
are labeled by the norm
\mu=|\vec{\pi}|\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The corresponding UIRs of the Poincaré group
ISO_{0}(3,1)
are the continuous-spin representations. Their name originates from the fact that they are labeled by a continuous parameter:
\mu
. The only extra information is whether the UIR of
ISO_{0}(3,1)
is single or double valued.
