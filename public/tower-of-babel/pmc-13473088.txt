Foliar nutrient concentrations and antioxidant enzyme activities in wheat grown in the cerrado as influenced by nitrogen sources and plant growth-promoting bacteria
Authors: Marcelo Cordeiro de Abreu, Marcos Vinícius Garbiate, Munir Mauad, Cleberton Correia Santos, Silvana de Paula Quintão Scalon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biological nitrogen fixation is a promising strategy to reduce reliance on synthetic nitrogen fertilizers. This study evaluated foliar inoculation with Methylobacterium symbioticum (Blue N™) and Azospirillum brasilense (Masterfix Gramineas™), alone or combined, in wheat (Triticum aestivum L. cv. TBIO Calibre) under Cerrado abiotic stresses and nitrogen (N) volatilization losses. Field trial (2024 season) with five treatments over basal NPK 15.15.15: T1(no urea aplication); T2 (+ 72 kg ha⁻¹ N-urea+NBPT); T3(+ 250 mL ha⁻¹ Masterfix Gramineas™); T4 (+ 333 g ha⁻¹ Blue N™); T5(+ 250 mL ha⁻¹ Masterfix Gramineas™ + 333 g ha⁻¹ Blue N™) at V3 stage. Co-inoculation (T5) increased foliar N (6.6%), phosphorus (P) (7.5%), potassium (K) (12.8%), zinc (Zn) (12.5%), manganese (Mn) (3.1%) and boron (B) (11.1%) versus T1; A. brasilense alone (T3) increased K (10.4%), Zn (5.5%) and B (122.2%), representing the greatest boron accumulation among all treatments; urea+NBPT (T2) only excelled in chlorophyll b (15.9%). Peroxidase activity was reduced Urea+NBPT (T2) (87.7%), A. brasilense alone (T3) (79.7%), M. symbioticum alone (T4) (46.8%) and co-inoculation (T5) (93%) compared to T1. Foliar co-inoculation of A. brasilense + M. symbioticum optimizes nutrient uptake, strengthens antioxidant defenses and enhances the physiological response capacity of wheat under field conditions, offering a sustainable alternative to synthetic nitrogen in wheat grown in the Brazilian Cerrado.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) ranks among the world’s most vital cereals, accounting for 27.6% of global production and 41.5% of international grain trade. For 2025, worldwide wheat output is projected to reach 809.7 million ton1. In contrast, Brazil anticipates a domestic harvest of 7.69 million ton, supplemented by imports totaling 6.63 million ton2. By 2050, an additional 198 million ton of wheat production will be required to satisfy the demands of a global population projected to reach 9.8 billion3. Wheat cultivation remains constrained by abiotic stresses, including drought and elevated temperatures, which are prevalent in tropical regions1. Climate change exacerbates these challenges by altering surface precipitation patterns, temperatures, humidity levels, and wind regimes, thereby disrupting these climatic variables. These perturbations promote growth in plant evapotranspiration, which accelerates water deficits and heightens plant water demand4, while also elevating the frequency of wildfires and intensifying drought severity5, the principal driver of agricultural losses6. Such stresses impair photosynthesis, disrupt electron transport, compromise membrane integrity, and elevate the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), culminating in photoinhibition and the inactivation of proteins and enzymes within the Calvin cycle7, alongside diminished availability of macro- and micronutrients to plants.

In Brazil, wheat production is increasingly prominent in the Midwest region, where it follows soybean cultivation in crop rotation. Nevertheless, this area confronts substantial obstacles stemming from adverse climatic conditions, soil characteristics, and nitrogen fertilization practices, all of which curtail productive efficiency8. Nitrogen (N) is indispensable for the synthesis of proteins, chlorophyll, and metabolites9. However, improper application diminishes productivity and quality, while concurrently augmenting greenhouse gas emissions10. The timing of top-dressing applications is pivotal, as soil moisture and water availability directly influence volatilization losses – a recurrent issue in tropical regions prone to periods of intense heat and dry air that occur during the rainy season11.

To mitigate such losses, technologies including urea treated with N-(n-butyl) thiophosphoric triamide, which is urease inhibitor (e.g., NBPT) are recommended. Furthermore, biofertilizers harboring plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB), such as Azospirillum brasilense and Methylobacterium symbioticum, can enhance nutrient assimilation and attenuate stress impacts12,13. The hypothesis posits that the deployment of PGPB and NBPT can optimize foliar nutrient dynamics and activate antioxidant enzymes, thereby alleviating the repercussions of adverse climatic conditions on cultivation. Accordingly, the objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of nitrogen fertilization management and PGPB application on antioxidant enzyme activity and nutrient concentrations in wheat.

Materials and methods

The experiment was conducted during the 2024 cropping season at the Experimental Farm of Coamo Agroindustrial Cooperativa, located in Dourados, Mato Grosso do Sul, Brazil (22°19’32.268"S 54°49’50.952"W, elevation 400 m). The predominant climate is classified as Aw according to the Köppen system, characterized by hot summers and dry winters, with maximum temperatures observed in December and January, and minimum temperatures between May and August, an annual average precipitation of 1,428 mm, and an annual mean temperature of 22.7 °C14. Monthly precipitation and average temperatures recorded during the experimental period are presented in Fig. 1, based on data provided by EMBRAPA15. Chemical and particle-size analysis of the 0.0–0.20 m layer revealed the following: pH (CaCl₂) 5.13; 33.65 g dm⁻³ organic matter; 12.13 mg dm⁻³ phosphorus (P) (Mehlich-1); 5.3 mmol_c dm⁻³ potassium (K); 63.8 mmol_c dm⁻³ calcium (Ca); 23.4 mmol_c dm⁻³ magnesium (Mg); 136.90 mmol_c dm⁻³ cation exchange capacity (CEC); 30.04 mg dm⁻³ sulfur (S); 0.50 mg dm⁻³ boron (B); 21.22 mg dm⁻³ copper (Cu); 48.37 mg dm⁻³ iron (Fe); 192.34 mg dm⁻³ manganese (Mn); 2.17 mg dm⁻³ zinc (Zn); and 67.57% base saturation. The soil exhibited a very clayey texture, with 641 g kg⁻¹ clay, 309 g kg⁻¹ sand, and 50 g kg⁻¹ silt. The experiment followed a randomized block design with five treatments and four replications, comprising 20 experimental units. Each plot consisted of 15 rows, 20 m in length, spaced 0.17 m apart. The usable area comprised the nine central rows, excluding 2 m from each end. The treatments evaluated were T1 [basal fertilization with single-granule 15.15.15 NPK applied in the seeding furrow, no urea aplication], T2 [basal 15.15.15 NPK in the seeding furrow, supplemented with 72 kg ha⁻¹ N (Excellen™ urea containing NBPT)], T3 [basal 15.15.15 NPK in the seeding furrow + foliar application of 250 mL ha⁻¹ Masterfix Gramineas™ (A. brasilense) at wheat growth stage V3], T4 [basal 15.15.15 NPK in the seeding furrow + foliar application of 333 g ha⁻¹ Blue N™ (M. symbioticum) at wheat growth stage V3], and T5 [basal 15.15.15 NPK in the seeding furrow + combined foliar application of 333 g ha⁻¹ Blue N™ (M. symbioticum) and 250 mL ha⁻¹ Masterfix Gramineas™ (A. brasilense) at wheat growth stage V3]. The wheat cultivar used was TBIO Calibre, an ultra-early cycle, short-statured variety with reduced tillering, well-suited to tropical cultivation17. Sowing occurred on 22 May 2024 using a Baldan seeder, with 17 cm row spacing, calibrated to 70 seeds m⁻¹ linear (411 seeds m⁻²). Fertilization followed soil analysis and technical recommendations for the crop17. Basal fertilization consisted of 150 kg ha⁻¹ of single-granule 15.15.15 NPK applied in the planting furrow. Crop emergence was recorded on 29 May 2024 (7 days after sowing, DAS). Top-dressing N and/or PGPB applications – A. brasilense (250 mL ha⁻¹ Masterfix Gramineas™) and M. symbioticum (333 g ha⁻¹ Blue N™) - were performed on 12 June 2024 (21 DAS) at the tillering stage (V3). Foliar applications were conducted using a CO₂-pressurized backpack sprayer equipped with Jacto 110.015-ADI nozzles, a 3 m boom with six spray tips, and a spray volume of 150 L ha⁻¹.

Climatic conditions by ten-day period throughout the wheat cropping cycle.

Crop management adhered to technical recommendations for wheat17. Pre-plant desiccation involved a single application of glyphosate (Roundup Original Mais™; 3.5 L ha⁻¹). Disease control comprised three fungicide applications: Viovan™ (0.7 L ha⁻¹), Fox Xpro™ (0.5 L ha⁻¹), and a final application of Aproach Power™ (0.6 L ha⁻¹) combined with Unizeb Gold™ (3 kg ha⁻¹), based on monitoring. No insecticide applications were required due to drought conditions. Harvest was performed manually at physiological maturity (R8), followed by natural drying, threshing, and manual cleaning.

Chlorophyll a and b indices were measured in the morning, one week after treatment application, at 28 DAS, using a Falker chlorophyll meter on the flag leaves of 50 plants per plot18.

For antioxidant enzyme activity analysis, 20 flag leaves per plot were collected at anthesis, wrapped in aluminum foil, labeled, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Enzyme extracts were prepared from 0.3 g of tissue macerated in 6 mL of solution containing 0.3 g polyvinylpyrrolidone in 100 mL of 0.2 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), followed by centrifugation at 12,400 rpm for 20 min. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD) activities were determined according to Giannopolitis and Ries19 and Broetto20, respectively. Total protein was quantified using the Bradford21 method with Coomassie Blue G-250, absorbance at 595 nm, and a bovine serum albumin standard curve.

Foliar concentrations of P, N, K, Ca, Mg, S, Cu, Zn, Mn, B, and Fe were assessed from 50 flag leaves collected per plot at flowering. Samples were oven-dried at 65 °C to constant weight, ground in a Wiley mill, and analyzed following Malavolta et al.22.

Data were subjected to analysis of variance (F-test; p ≤ 0.05), and treatment means were compared using Tukey’s test (p ≤ 0.05) in RStudio (v. 4.4.1).

Results and Discussion

Overall, the application of PGPB enhanced foliar nutrient concentrations in wheat and enhanced the plant physiological response capacity under the climatic conditions recorded during the growing cycle, primarily through elevated SOD and POD activities, particularly from flowering onward, thereby corroborating our initial hypothesis. Moreover, certain beneficial responses were more pronounced with the combined use of A. brasilense and M. symbioticum, indicative of a synergistic biological effect.

Foliar analysis revealed significant increases in N, P, K, Mn, and B concentrations under the NPK + A. brasilense + M. symbioticum treatment (T5), whereas Mg and Fe exhibited significant reductions (Table 1), however, the greatest B accumulation was observed in T3 (A. brasilense alone). Calcium concentrations remained unaffected by the treatments. Magnesium declined in the combined NPK + A. brasilense + M. symbioticum treatment (T5) relative to NPK alone, while S concentrations did not differ from the reference treatment. For Cu and Zn, the same combination yielded the highest values compared to NPK.

Across all treatments, macronutrient (except N) and micronutrient concentrations fell within recommended ranges for wheat23: P (2.5–3.5 g kg⁻¹), N (20–34 g kg⁻¹), K (15–30 g kg⁻¹), Ca (2.5–8 g kg⁻¹), Mg (1.5–4.0 g kg⁻¹), S (1.5–3.5 g kg⁻¹), Cu (5–20 mg kg⁻¹), Zn (25–70 mg kg⁻¹), Mn (25–150 mg kg⁻¹), B (5–15 mg kg⁻¹), and Fe (10–250 mg kg⁻¹) (Table 1). The highest N and K concentrations were recorded in T2 (NPK + top-dressed N) and T5 (NPK + A. brasilense + M. symbioticum), whereas the highest P concentration occurred in T4 (NPK + A. brasilense), showing no significant difference from T2 and T5 but superiority over the remaining treatments (Table 1).

Nitrogen constitutes an essential component of Rubisco, which in C3 species such as wheat accounts for over 50% of nitrogen in the photosynthetic apparatus24,25. Furthermore, N participates in cellular differentiation, tissue formation26, tillering27, and grain protein synthesis (albumin, globulin, gliadin, and glutenin), determinants of flour technological quality.

The elevated N concentrations in T2 and T5 may be ascribed to the top-dressing of N (NBPT-protected urea) in T2, providing a rapidly available nitrogen source that increased foliar levels. In T5, a positive association between the two bacterial species enhanced N availability to the crop beyond that achieved with individual applications (Table 1).

Certain Methylobacterium species colonize the phyllosphere28, accessing organic compounds such as methanol released via stomata during pectin metabolism in cell wall synthesis. In return, these microorganisms supply nitrogen to the host29. Such bacteria delay foliar senescence, prolonging photosynthetic activity, as observed by Valente et al.30, in wheat. Conversely, A. brasilense produces growth-promoting phytohormones, fixes nitrogen by stimulating nitrate reductase activity, and enhances root development, thereby expanding the absorption surface for water and nutrients31. Hungria et al.32, reported up to 31% yield increases in Azospirillum spp. - inoculated wheat, asserting that these bacteria can meet 12–79% of plant nitrogen requirements, whereas Cassán et al.33, estimate a lower contribution of 5–18%.

The highest P concentration was observed in T4 (NPK + A. brasilense at V3), statistically equivalent to T2 and T5 (Table 1). Beyond nitrogen fixation, Azospirillum spp. solubilize phosphates32,34,35, produce diverse phytohormones33, increase root branching and biomass, elevate root hair density36, and consequently expand soil exploration volume, contributing to nutrient and water acquisition – an explanation for elevated P in treatments involving this genus. Phosphorus uptake occurs primarily via ion-root contact and diffusion; thus, processes promoting root proliferation and greater soil volume exploration are particularly advantageous in highly weathered tropical soils with high P adsorption, as in the present study.

Phosphorus is indispensable for synthesizing high-energy phosphate compounds such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and diphosphate (ADP), and is implicated in numerous physiological functions, including energy accumulation and transfer, photosynthesis, respiration, and cellular differentiation and expansion. In wheat, phosphorus deficiency reduces plant height, tillering, development, spike length, grains per spikelet, and grain mass37.

Mirroring nitrogen, the highest K concentrations were recorded in T2 and T5 (Table 1). Potassium (K⁺) and nitrate (NO₃⁻) uptake exhibit synergy, attributed to improved charge balance38,39, alongside K⁺ activation of enzymes involved in NO₃⁻ assimilation40. Additionally, PGPB may enhance plant development by stimulating nitrate reductase activity41. Potassium ranks as the second most absorbed nutrient in wheat, surpassed only by N. It plays pivotal roles in protein synthesis, sugar transport, photosynthesis, cellular osmotic regulation, cytoplasmic cation–anion balance, and stomatal opening and closure24,42.

No significant treatment effects were observed on Ca concentrations (Table 1). Conversely, the highest Mg concentrations occurred in T1 and T4, contrasting with K, which exhibited the lowest levels in these treatments (Table 1). Antagonism between Mg²⁺ and K⁺ during uptake likely explains elevated Mg in treatments with reduced K (Table 1). This antagonism may relate to the smaller ionic radius of Mg compared to K, despite its larger hydrated radius. Moreover, distinct transport mechanisms—mass flow for Mg and diffusion for K can yield substantial concentration differences in soil solution, particularly in the rhizosphere43, suggesting a pronounced antagonistic effect of K on Mg uptake. Ding et al.44, observed a more marked K influence on Mg absorption in rice than vice versa.

Magnesium is vital for numerous plant physiological and biochemical processes, including photosynthesis, carbohydrate partitioning, and protein synthesis, all impacting growth and yield formation45. As the central ion in chlorophyll, Mg activates over 300 enzymes, including Rubisco24.

Foliar sulfur concentrations were highest in T1 and T2, statistically equivalent to T4 and T5, with the lowest in T3 (Table 1). Isolated foliar M. symbioticum or its combination with A. brasilense elicited no significant sulfur differences relative to full NPK at sowing (T1) or NPK at sowing plus NBPT-protected urea top-dressing (T2) (Table 1).

Except for Cu, PGPB application - singly or combined - yielded Mn, Fe, B, and Zn concentrations equal to or exceeding the control, underscoring their role in enhancing micronutrient translocation and mobilization (Table 1). PGPB significantly contribute to soil nutrient solubilization and plant availability46. Inoculation elevated Mn, Fe, B, and Zn in wheat, suggesting improved micronutrient translocation. Comparable findings were reported by Hana et al., (2012) in wheat. Cassán and Díaz-Zorita47 documented increased B in A. Brasilense inoculated plants, attributing this to exopolysaccharide and siderophore production, which mobilize soil-bound B and enhance plant availability. Here, the highest foliar B occurred in T3 (A. brasilense-inoculated), followed by T4 and T5 (M. symbioticum alone or combined with A. brasilense, respectively) (Table 1).

There is no conclusive evidence of a single, specific mechanism underlying the foliar co-inoculation of A. brasilense and M. symbioticum in wheat. However, current research supports that the most plausible synergy arises from the functional complementarity between these bacteria: A. brasilense enhances hormonal signaling and root growth, increasing nutrient acquisition from the soil, while M. symbioticum, adapted to the phyllosphere, improves nitrogen metabolism, photosynthesis, and nitrogen use efficiency following foliar application. The simultaneous action of these processes tends to enhance nutrient uptake and assimilation compared to single inoculation12,30,48,49.

In the case of A. brasilense, even when applied via foliar spraying, there is evidence that the bacterium promotes systemic signaling, stimulating root growth and increasing the plant’s capacity for nutrient uptake from the soil. This effect occurs through the production of phytohormones, particularly auxins, which expand the soil volume explored by roots and enhance the uptake of both macro and micronutrients50.

For M. symbioticum, a recent study indicates that its foliar application can enhance nitrogen metabolism under reduced chemical N inputs, increase photosynthetic capacity, and function in the phyllosphere as a bacterium associated with biological nitrogen fixation. Furthermore, a recent review on Methylobacterium highlights phytohormone production/modulation, nitrogen fixation, and ethylene regulation through ACC deaminase (1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate deaminase) as key mechanisms operating in the foliar environment51.

Chlorophyll a index remained unaffected by treatments, whereas chlorophyll b peaked with NBPT-urea (Table 2). Chlorophyll b, an accessory pigment in the antenna complex, protects against oxidative stress and thylakoid protein complex disassembly, averting photoinhibition52,53– a response also noted by Sherin et al.54, in wheat.

Regarding protective metabolism, total protein content and SOD and POD activities were treatment-dependent. Protein content peaked with M. symbioticum (T4), differing significantly from A. brasilense (T3) (Table 3). Under this condition, elevated Fe may relate to its involvement in diverse proteins, including energetic ones like ferredoxin, a carrier in photosystem I55.

ROS are naturally produced in plants, but in quantities that the plant’s own antioxidant defense system is able to eliminate or neutralize59. However, under stress conditions — such as drought — there is an overproduction of oxidants, and antioxidant activity is often insufficient given the intensity of exposure to the stressful event. In general, when a plant is exposed to adverse conditions, complex signaling events occur that activate various defense pathways. The initial molecular signaling occurs through hormonal modulation, in which the ABA (abscisic acid) biosynthesis pathway is activated, regulating the expression of genes responsive to water stress60.

The activation of the defense metabolism, represented here by SOD and POD, is also variable, as its increase depends on other efficient mechanisms of action, as previously mentioned, such as hormonal regulation, the activity of non-enzymatic compounds, and even stomatal adjustments. Under stress conditions, the plant naturally redirects energy toward the activation of protective metabolic processes. However, we suggest in our study that the inoculation of microorganisms may modulate this response, potentially reducing the plant’s natural energy expenditure by signaling activation pathways and alleviating ROS-mediated oxidative demand.

Recent advances in plant redox biology have reinforced the dual role of reactive oxygen species (ROS) as both damaging agents and indispensable signaling molecules, a perspective that directly supports the interpretation of our enzymatic data. Ali59, demonstrated that ROS — including O₂•⁻, H₂O₂ and OH• — are generated in a compartment-specific and tightly regulated manner across chloroplasts, mitochondria, and peroxisomes, where their concentrations are finely tuned to balance signaling functions and prevent cellular damage. Critically, these authors showed that antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and guaiacol peroxidase (GPX) are dynamically modulated by ROS–RNS (reactive nitrogen species) crosstalk, with nitric oxide (NO) capable of either enhancing or inhibiting their activities depending on the intensity and nature of the stress signal. This regulatory plasticity is consistent with the treatment-dependent SOD and POD responses observed in the present study, where inoculation with A. brasilense and M. symbioticum modulated antioxidant enzyme activity in a non-linear fashion, reflecting fine adjustments in redox homeostasis rather than a simple proportional stress response. Furthermore, Ali et al.59, highlighted that the primary mode of ROS signaling involves oxidative post-translational modifications (oxPTM) of proteins — including cysteine oxidation and S-nitrosylation — which dynamically regulate transcription factors and stress-responsive genes. This molecular framework supports the notion that PGPB-mediated modulation of the antioxidant system in wheat operates through sophisticated redox signaling networks, integrating hormonal, enzymatic, and non-enzymatic components to attenuate oxidative damage under field stress conditions59.

Overall, PGPB application increased foliar nutrient concentrations in wheat and enhanced the plant physiological response capacity under the climatic conditions recorded during the growing cycle. Notably, reduced POD activity in PGPB- and NBPT-treated plants relative to the unfertilized control (T1) suggests that these strategies alleviated oxidative demand, with T1 exhibiting the highest POD activity as a compensatory defense response in the absence of inoculants. SOD activity was similarly unaffected by most treatments; however, T4 (M. symbioticum alone) was the only treatment to significantly reduce SOD activity relative to T1, while T3 (A. brasilense alone) showed numerically the highest SOD values, though without statistically significant difference from T1, likely associated with higher foliar B concentrations. These results corroborate our initial hypothesis and suggest a synergistic biological effect, particularly with the combined use of A. brasilense and M. symbioticum.

Reduced SOD activity in T4 is ascribed to lower Zn, a SOD enzymatic cofactor61,62. Declining Zn under this condition stems from elevated Fe, reflecting Fe–Zn competition63, corroborated here in wheat. Conversely, SOD increased in T3, linked to higher B — a finding echoed by Sülüş and Leblebici64 in Carthamus tinctorius L., where elevated B enhanced this antioxidant enzyme, aiding ROS elimination under stress. Generally, SOD represents the first defense line, converting superoxide anions to hydrogen peroxide65.

Elevated POD in T1 followed by T4 correlates with higher foliar Mg, potentially activating this protective pathway. Magnesium chelation of protoporphyrin IX (PPn) yields magnesium-protoporphyrin IX (Mg-PPn), boosting the enzyme and metabolizing hydrogen peroxide66. Notably, treatment-dependent pathways predominated for ROS detoxification, often inversely related, as between SOD and POD.

PGPB regulate both the production and scavenging of ROS, which function as intracellular signaling molecules. SOD catalyzes the conversion of O₂•⁻ − into H₂O₂, while POD is involved in its subsequent detoxification67. Furthermore, PGPB can directly regulate the expression of antioxidant system genes, including SOD, CAT and APX, through the activation of redox signaling pathways and stress-related transcription factors68,. Thus, changes in the activity of these enzymes reflect a fine-tuned adjustment of redox homeostasis rather than a simple linear response to stress. In addition, certain PGPB, such as A. brasilense, contribute to the activation of ACC deaminase, which reduces ethylene synthesis under stress conditions69. Furthermore, inoculation with A. brasilense has been reported to regulate the expression of stress-responsive genes such as DREB2, which activates antioxidant defense signaling pathways, responses that collectively indicate attenuation of stress-related damage in inoculated plants70. Concurrently, auxin (Aux) signaling interacts with ROS in a bidirectional manner: on one hand, elevated ROS levels can influence the expression of genes related to auxin biosynthesis, transport, and signaling, as well as the stability of Aux/IAA (indole-3-acetic acid) repressor proteins and the activity of Auxin Response Factor (ARF) transcription factors; on the other hand, auxins modulate the expression of antioxidant enzymes, contributing to redox homeostasis under stress conditions71. In this context, PGPB such as A. brasilense play a relevant role, since their capacity to produce IAA not only promotes root architecture and nutrient uptake, but also amplifies stress-responsive hormonal and redox signaling, potentially enhancing the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and POD in the host plant.

Based on two independent field studies, complete replacement of synthetic nitrogen fertilizers by PGPB in wheat cannot be recommended without risk to yield and grain quality. Valente30, demonstrated that foliar application of M. symbioticum maintained yield and flour technological quality under a 28% nitrogen reduction (180 to 130 kg ha⁻¹), but over a substantial mineral nitrogen base. Shaaban et al.49, across two growing seasons, reported that inoculation combined with 50% of the recommended nitrogen dose matched the yield and grain protein content of the full-dose uninoculated treatment; however, maximum values were consistently observed when inoculation was combined with the full nitrogen dose. Thus, although PGPB represent a promising strategy for enhancing nitrogen use efficiency, current evidence indicates that integrated management combining bacterial inoculation with mineral fertilization is still necessary to achieve optimal yield and grain quality in wheat. Advances in this area depend on a better understanding of genotype–microorganism interactions, evaluation of a broader range of cultivars, and definition of more efficient application timings and doses under field conditions.

Conclusions

Overall, the deployment of A. brasilense and M. symbioticum emerges as a promising practice that, beyond enhancing nutrition and metabolic adjustments, aligns with sustainable development goals. The absence of yield data, due to operational setbacks during harvest, is acknowledged as a limitation of the study. Future studies should directly assess the impact of these microorganisms on photosynthetic metabolism, grain yield, and economic returns, in order to elucidate their roles across wheat physiological processes in the Cerrado.
