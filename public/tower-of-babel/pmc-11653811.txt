Dietitians as innovators: a deductive-inductive qualitative analysis
Authors: Sarah J Hewko, Julia Freeburn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrontline health professionals are well-placed to develop and implement beneficial innovations. Evidence supports the clinical and financial benefits of Registered Dietitian (RD)-led improvement initiatives, but we know little about how RDs perceive of innovation or of themselves as innovators. The objectives of the study were to gain an understanding of: 1) how RDs define innovation; 2) who RDs perceive as innovative; 3) whether RDs feel prepared to innovate, and; 4) to what extent work context impacts RDs’ capacity to innovate at work.MethodsAll RDs employed in Canada were eligible to participate. Semi-structured interviews were conducted and a deductive-inductive approach was applied to qualitative analysis. Specifically, Scott & Bruce’s (1994) Path Model of Individual Innovation in the Workplace was first applied as a coding structure.ResultsRespondents (n = 18) exhibited a pro-innovation disposition and a gendered perception of innovation. Few felt their preparatory education prepared them to be innovators in the workplace. All components of Scott & Bruce’s model were supported. Inductive codes were categorized into five themes, including: benefits, dietetics-specific, health care system, technology and individual characteristics.ConclusionsResearchers have previously raised concerns about gendered perceptions of innovation; our results support the legitimacy of these concerns. RDs reported entering the workforce unprepared to be innovative. While the applicability of Scott & Bruce’s model among RDs was confirmed, deficits in the model were noted beyond what would be expected due to context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-12095-5.Keywords: Qualitative research, Dietitian, Innovation, Organizational, Roles, Gender, Canada

Introduction

We know that innovativeness should be highly valued in knowledge workers by both employers and consumers of services provided by knowledge workers [1]. What differentiates knowledge workers from other types of workers is that their most basic work task is thinking. Their work depends on pre-existing communal and individual knowledge and on their ability to apply it, with the potential that new knowledge will be generated [2]. Medical personnel, lawyers, accountants, teachers and professors are all examples of knowledge workers [3]. These workers must continuously expand their expertise, adopt novel ideas, take risks, respond to changes in best practice and be comfortable with ambiguity [1]. Registered Dietitians (RDs) are knowledge workers that, for the most part, work within complex healthcare organizations, whose processes and structure often impede both the adoption and spread of innovation [4, 5]. Because most existing research exploring innovation among health professionals has focused on either nurses or physicians [6], we know little about RDs’ perceptions of innovation or of themselves as innovators. We do, however, know that quality improvement initiatives led by RDs can lead to reduced infection rates [7], decreased lengths of stay [7–9], fewer screening form errors [8] and improved rates of documented malnutrition diagnoses by providers [9, 10]. Unfortunately, current organizational and human resource practices provide little encouragement or support for health professionals’, including RDs’, innovation efforts [11].

Historically, the majority of studies conducted on workplace innovation have been in male-dominated industries; as a result, women have frequently been underestimated as drivers of innovation [12, 13]. Risk-taking, in particular, is often considered central both to innovation and masculinity; despite this, evidence indicates that women are equally likely to take risks when the definition of risk-taking is expanded to include feminine risk-taking behaviours in the workplace, such as asking for help with a task [14]. Both Belghiti-Mahut et al. [12] and Morgenroth et al. [14] have explored the relationship between gender and innovation and what is clear is that males are more rewarded for risk-taking behaviours in the workplace [12, 14]. Women are more likely to demonstrate innovativeness by effectively adapting a universal innovation to the local context. Their innovations are more likely to be social and/or incremental than technological and/or radical; this is not attributable to any essential biological differences between the sexes, but to socialization and gendered incentives for different types of innovation. By defining, studying and measuring innovation through a gendered lens that emphasizes risk-taking, we fail to capture and quantify the impacts of feminine innovations [12].

Many definitions of innovation have been proposed in the literature and while most appear gender-neutral, their operationalization and measurement favour masculine forms of innovation and innovation leadership (e.g., attributing innovation at a company in an empirical study to the (frequently male) Chief Executive Officer, when innovation activities are carried out by gender diverse teams) [12]. While we have adopted Yuan and Woodman’s [15] definition for this research, where innovation is “an employee’s intentional introduction or application of new ideas, products, processes, and procedures to his or her work role, work unit or organization” (p. 324), we wanted to determine how aligned this definition was with RDs’ definitions. Innovative approaches in dietetics can be difficult to implement as health professionals’ have established ways of working that are reinforced through lengthy training and education, regulation and legislation, and by professional associations [16]. RDs may have difficulty purposefully critiquing the established ways of working and envisioning creative, new ways of working [17–20]. Despite these barriers, RDs, as healthcare professionals, are well-situated and have the needed expertise to develop and implement innovations that will improve patient care [21]. Staff at the frontline are particularly well-placed to develop beneficial innovations, as their procedural and professional experiences and their relationships with patients and colleagues give them access to information that is unavailable to those who are further removed from service delivery [6, 22]. Additionally, innovations may be appraised more favourably when the connection between the innovator’s personal training and experience and the innovation (whether product, process or idea) is clear [15].

Our objectives in this exploratory study were to gain an understanding of: 1) how RDs define innovation; 2) who RDs perceive as innovative, and 3) how prepared RDs feel to be innovative. Knowing that identification of the factors (organizational and individual) that facilitate innovation in the work place has been the focus of significant previous study, we sought, instead, to uncover broader understandings of innovation in the setting of dietetics and to elicit richer narratives about how RDs understand and experience innovation in their work-lives.

Methods

In this manuscript, we are reporting the results of the second phase of a two-part, multi-method study. The study included a survey (phase 1) and semi-structured interview (phase 2). The survey was designed to help us identify factors most predictive of health professionals’ innovative output and included an invitation to participate in a semi-structured interview to explore the topic of RDs as innovators. Two-hundred and fifty-eight dietitians (258) responded to our survey and 18 responded to the interview invitation. The survey details, including which validated tools were incorporated into the survey, distribution approach and results have been previously reported [23]. All survey respondents and, thus, all respondents were RDs working in Canada. RDs who expressed interest in participating in a semi-structured interview were contacted by e-mail to arrange a date and time for a virtual interview that would take no more than 30 min (see Supplementary Materials for interview questions). All respondents received a $25 e-gift card to a Canadian chocolatier as a thank you for their participation. The interviews took place in January of 2020.

All interviews were conducted virtually over Zoom™ and were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Each transcript was reviewed by SH and JF for accuracy before being uploaded into MAXQDA2022 (VERBI Software, 2021) for coding. Hennink and Kaiser concluded, following a systematic review of empirical tests, that data saturation in qualitative research is achievable with 9–17 interviews [24]. Similar to Baumeister et al. [25] and Chun et al., [26] who each employed a deductive-inductive approach to better understand the perspectives of health professionals, we began coding deductively using an established model relevant to our research. It was only once we had completed the assignment of codes deduced from the model that we inductively formulated codes to capture themes not reflected in the model.

For the deductive analysis, we applied Scott & Bruce’s Path Model of Individual Innovation in the Workplace [27], which has been cited more than 4,000 times. In this model, which the authors initially tested in a sample of US-based scientists, technicians and research and development engineers employed in the industrial sector, leadership (leader-member exchange, leader role expectations), work group (team-member exchange), and individual-level variables (intuitive problem-solving style and systematic problem-solving style) are identified as directly predictive of innovative behaviour. In leader-member exchange theory, leadership is conceptualized as a process centered on the interactions between leaders and their followers [28]. Leadership, work group and individual-level variables are also identified as contributing indirectly to innovative behaviour through their impact on psychological climate factors, which include support for innovation and resource supply. Although team-member exchange was not statistically significantly related to perceptions of psychological climate or innovative behaviour in Scott & Bruce’s analysis, we retained this factor in our code structure [27]. After SH and JF independently assigned deductive codes, we re-reviewed the transcripts and independently created inductive codes. Coding for all transcripts was finalized in a meeting between SH and JF, with consensus achieved for all codes after discussion (where required). Multiple codes could be (and were) assigned to the same pieces of text (data segments) when appropriate [29]. We coded both negatives and positives associated with each code (e.g., for Psychological Climate – Resource Supply, we coded when a lack of resources was noted and when respondents identified having had access to resources for innovation-focused activities). Preliminary codes that appeared in fewer than three separate interviews were considered unsupported and deleted. Collaboratively, SH and JF categorized inductive codes into themes for analysis.

Reflexivity

Multiple perspectives were represented on the research team. Dr. Sarah Hewko (SH), an RD with more than 15 years of experience, both as a clinical RD and manager of clinical RDs in the Canadian healthcare system, is the principal investigator for the research; she did not participate in the interviews as she is personally acquainted with some of the interview respondents. Academically, she has an interdisciplinary background and has completed a Master of Health Administration and a Doctorate in Nursing, in addition to her dietetic preparatory training. Dr. Jo-Louise Huq contributed to the development of the semi-structured interview protocol and conducted the interviews. Dr. Huq has an interdisciplinary background and has consulted to and worked in the Canadian healthcare system. She has completed a Masters of Business Administration (Public Administration concentration) and a Doctorate in Strategic Management and Organization, with her doctoral research conducted in the healthcare system.

Ms. Julia Freeburn was a dietetic intern at the time of the study and became involved in the project at the point of analysis. All three researchers are white, Canadian-born women and are from middle-class backgrounds.

Results

We had 18 respondents complete semi-structured interviews, all of whom also completed the online survey (see results here [23]). To maintain confidentiality, we did not collect demographic information from respondents. However, the mix of interview respondents was observed to be similar to the group that completed the survey (i.e., ~ 95% presented as women and there was disproportionately high representation from Alberta-based RDs). None of the respondents primary employment was in food service, public health or private practice.

Defining innovation and the connection between creativity and innovation

Even though most of the dietitians we interviewed had not received formal education or training in innovation or related subjects, they were able to provide intuitive definitions of innovation and connected innovation to creativity in interesting ways. A third of respondents used some variation of “thinking outside the box” in their definition. The same proportion indicated within their definition that innovation improves things, such as by making them better or more useful. The word “new” was commonly employed in definitions, with a “new way” of doing or approaching things being the most common usage. Keeping up with what is current, whether that be in the research or in technological advances, was included in the definition of close to a third of respondents. None of the respondents limited their definition to the invention of tangible products and/or the creation of novel intellectual property. Near half of respondents felt that innovation could not exist without creativity. Creativity was seen as important throughout the process of innovation, from idea generation to implementation.

Identifying RD-created or RD-implemented innovations

No two dietitians identified the same innovation when asked to identify an innovation created by and/or implemented by an RD. Respondents identified a mix of patient-facing and RD-facing innovations. Among the patient-facing innovations reported were: computer and smart phone food applications that track intake (such as MyFitnessPal https://www.myfitnesspal.com); scheduled opportunities to “Walk and Talk” with a dietitian; continuous glucose monitors for people with diabetes; protected mealtimes, and; unique food products for people requiring texture-modified diets. Some of the noted innovations, such as teaching tools that concretely explained carbohydrate counting and cooking classes to demonstrate patterns of healthy eating, were noted to improve the experience of both RDs and their clients.

Among the reported RD-facing innovations were: nutrition care process terminology (NCPT); creation of RD educator and research RD positions; facilitation of quarterly, health authority-wide RD meetings, and; development of a document to guide long-term care menu planners planning menus based on the 2019 Canada Food Guide. Some innovations, such as continuous glucose monitors, food service management software and food intake tracking apps were technological. Social innovations identified by respondents included health authority-wide RD meetings and Walk and Talk with a dietitian. Many were process innovations, such as the implementation of NCPT, a priority intervention criteria tool to facilitate triaging patients by need, a protocol for nurse titration of tube feeds according to Propofol dosing and the introduction of a multidisciplinary family conference early in the treatment of child/adolescent overweight and obesity.

Identifying RD innovators

All but one of the individuals mentioned by respondents when asked to name the most innovative RD they knew personally or were aware of was referred to with she/her pronouns. Near one-third of respondents named an academic, with some highlighting their nominee’s novel research and others highlighting innovative teaching methods. In research, P1 noted that her nominee was “really excellent as a researcher to engage people who are in practice to get their voice within her research.” Teaching-wise, P6 noted that her former professor had “teamed up with a theatre prof at the university to have a joint course where the theatre student earns a credit for medical acting and the nutrition students get to practice physical assessment on them.” Near one-fifth identified an RD working in media, whether that involved appearing on television news programs, producing their own podcast or creating audiovisual content. For example, P13 noted that her nominee “was quite innovative in seeing that social media and YouTube was really the way of the future in promoting the world of dietitians…she’s done a really good job of making a media presence and showing that dietitians can be cool, which I think is innovative.” Several respondents highlighted innovation among their colleagues, for example P1 notes that her nominee was able to “give people that time, to find funding to buy out their time to work on projects and such.”

Identifying the most innovative people

Near 40% of nominees for most innovative person you know or are aware of outside of dietetics were companies. Quintessential tech companies, such as Apple and Microsoft, were seen as having the “tenacity to see things differently and not being satisfied” P4. Other companies, such as Passion Planner and 4Ocean were recognized for their ethics, with P15 noting that 4Ocean “look at a situation and everybody else goes ‘it’s hopeless’ and they’re actually doing something.” Of the individuals identified, almost two-thirds were male. Elon Musk was the only public figure named twice by respondents, while Brené Brown and Stephen Hawking were each named once. Musk was noted by P17 to create “a solution to a problem that didn’t look like it was being addressed by anyone else” in his consideration for rocket waste in the SpaceX program. A third of individuals named were family members of respondents, whether parent, spouse, sibling or child. Linking risk-taking with innovation, P8 noted that a family member “took a big risk” in switching from a government job to start their own business.

Results of deductive analysis

Overall, there was support for all components of Scott & Bruce’s Path Model of Individual Innovation in the Workplace (see Table 1) [27]. For context, a common source for codes supporting leadership and work group predictors of individual innovation at work, were responses to our last question (see Supplemental Material for the interview guide): “Is there anything else you think we need to know about dietetics and innovation?” Codes supporting individual-level predictors were less concentrated and were identified in responses to many of the questions in the interview guide. Codes related to psychological climate factors predicting individual innovation at work were most frequently found in the responses to “If you were to introduce something new in your work unit or program, do you feel your organization would be receptive?” And last, the question most likely to yield responses coded to our outcome variable of individual innovation at work, was “Can you identify an innovation created by and/or introduced/implemented by an RD?” Of the predictors of individual innovation at work, psychological climate factors were discussed both by the largest number of respondents (all of them) and the most frequently (reflected by the number of times coded). Resource supply, which included reference to the availability of money, time and opportunities (e.g., training, mentorship), was the single-most frequently coded code in the study. Of the factors hypothesized to contribute directly to innovative work behaviour and indirectly to psychological climate, work group factors, specifically team-member exchange, were the most frequently coded and were coded in the all but one interview transcript.

Benefits

Benefits to patients were more frequently mentioned than those to RDs. Respondents indicated that innovations boosted the applicability of nutrition recommendations and that they led to fewer errors (e.g., patients receiving foods they are allergic to or foods of inconsistent texture on texture-modified diets). Innovations that standardized approaches to medical nutrition therapy (MNT), such as NCPT and triage tools, were identified as boosting the confidence and skills of novice clinicians. The application of NCPT, in particular, was noted to improve documentation of MNT. Improved documentation, in turn, was said to facilitate the conduct of research on-site that could quantify the impacts of RD-delivered MNT. Additional benefits for dietitians related to time saved, such as with innovations that automatically completed complex calculations; this gave RDs more time to focus on edifying and impactful tasks.

Dietetics-specific

This thematic category included the most sub-codes. Within the category, characteristics of dietetics as a profession was discussed by near all respondents and was the most frequently coded in the transcripts. The relative size of the profession was noted as a reason RDs often felt marginalized in their workplaces and the broader health sector; it also meant many RDs worked in units or on teams where they were the only RD and their direct supervisor was not an RD. As part of this, respondents noted a general misunderstanding of RD roles, strengths and potential contributions, both within other health professions and within the broader public. Several of the other sub-codes were closely connected to characteristics of dietetics, such as volunteering to advance the profession, which is more necessary in small professions with inadequate pooled resources to fund well-resourced national or provincial associations. Underestimation of dietitians and the need to create awareness of innovation in dietetics may be (at least in part) the result of the small size of the profession.

Respondents were explicitly asked if they felt their education prepared them to be innovative in their work, which is likely why three-quarters of respondents discussed characteristics of dietetic education in relation to innovation. Overall, responses were mixed, with some indicating that that the breadth of dietetic practice covered in preparatory programs gave them the skills to adapt their practice effectively across diverse contexts and settings and others indicating that they did not feel prepared to be innovative after having completed their education. Some of those who had been working for a longer period as an RD indicated that they could not recall enough about their education to comment. Multiple respondents did report that their employer supported them to enrol in internally-offered process improvement training(s). Most felt that opportunities to be innovative were more common in the internship or practicum than during the academic part of their dietetic program. How much innovation was encouraged was noted to be dependent on the preceptor to whom you were assigned and on the program you had enrolled in. Clinical nutrition training was reported as being particularly unconducive to innovation, with requirements to “read and regurgitate” (P17) and to apply “math equations” (P7) and formulas in clinical rotations. The breadth of dietetic training was noted to be a facilitator of innovative thinking, as RDs in training are exposed to several distinct areas of practice and, frequently, to multiple practicing RDs as preceptors. As per P8, her broad educational and practical experiences helped her to realize that she didn’t “have to do it the same as other people have done it just because that's how they do it. Like if I see a better way of doing things for me, like that's something that I could pursue.”

Health care system

Near all respondents identified political and institutional barriers to innovation. This was the most frequently coded sub-code within the health care system theme. Barriers were both external and internal to the organization of employment. Externally, provincial barriers were noted to impede innovation-supporting collaborations.1 Territorialism or “turf wars” across professions, which often exist outside an individual organizational context, were also noted to stifle opportunities for innovation. Internal to organizations, policies, procedures and clauses in union collective agreements were noted to curb innovation. Bureaucratic processes (i.e. “all the different layers and levels of approvals” P6) were noted as taking significant time and energy to navigate, leading to RD frustration and exhaustion. This is particularly significant when considering that organizational climate (resource supply – including time) was the most coded of the inductive factors identified by Scott & Bruce.(27) In general, innovations that exclusively impacted the work of RDs were reported to be the easiest to implement (P8). Multiple respondents noted that workload measurement [30] and the focus on time spent on patient-facing activities squelched their capacity for innovation. For example, RDs may have quotas for percent time spent on patient-facing activities, number of patients seen per day, or requirements to see patients (triaged by need) within a specific time period after a consult is received. The introduction of the most recent Canada’s Food Guide in 2019 by the federal government was noted as a trigger for innovation as organizations and advocacy groups whose resources were based on Canada’s Food Guide messages had to be (quickly) re-vamped.

Near three-quarters of respondents indicated that the size of their place of work and/or the number of RDs employed in the organization impacted on RD capacity for innovation. Benefits and drawbacks were reported for both small and large organizations. Organizations that employed more RDs sometimes had more internal supports for RD innovation, such as dedicated RD educators, while smaller organizations were unlikely to have such resources. Larger organizations were noted to have stricter expectations for consistent practice across sites, which was noted to hamper attempts to implement practice changes. RDs employed in sole-charge positions, where they were the only RD working in their facility, reported having greater freedom to implement changes in their practice, particularly when the change did not impact other health providers. One aspect of working for a large organization that was reported as being supportive of innovation was that “you can always find an early adopter because we’re so big” P14. A downside was that in such large organizations, it was difficult to communicate effectively across all sites.

Technology

More than half of respondents highlighted the connection between technology and innovation. Staying up-to-date with technology, including social media content creation, was seen as being important in order to maintain relevance and to maximize RD influence. Technology was not universally seen as improving patient care, with P11 noting that patients will “use it when it first comes out. It's awesome. And everybody is loving it. Yeah yeah sis boom bah. But it's shiny and new for a while and then it's gone.”

Individual characteristics

Last, the theme of individual characteristics adds to the individual-level factors identified in Scott & Bruce’s [27] model. They incorporated only problem-solving style and not other aspects of personality that may predispose someone to innovative behaviour. More than 80% of respondents indicated that innovation depended, at least in part, on the characteristics of the individual. Characteristics associated with innovators included: the desire to always “do things better” (P5, P9, P11, P15), “giving back” to the community (P8, P9), being “ahead of the trend” (P7), consistently seeking new challenges (P8), seeking novel experiences (P15), political savvy (P15), being open to change (P14), and persevering after hitting a dead end (P7).

Discussion

After having completed this study, we have gained an understanding of how RDs define innovation, who they perceive as innovative and how prepared they feel to be innovative. We were also able to determine the applicability of a broadly universal model of employee innovation [27] within the dietetic setting and which aspects of RD work-lives were not reflected within that model. Resource supply – as it applied to the availability of money, time and opportunities – was the single-most coded code in the study. The lack of resources in the healthcare system is a long-standing, universal concern and has been discussed in the literature as having negative impacts on many important outcomes, including, among others: nurse workload [31], ICU patient mortality rates [32] and the well-being of health professionals [33, 34], Challenges with sufficient resourcing in healthcare are likely to worsen over time as, globally, countries struggle with inflation, climate change and aging populations.

The most frequently used phrase used to define innovation among our respondents was “thinking outside the box.” Notably, none of our respondents identified risk-taking as a key part of a definition of innovation; this may be because near all of our respondents were female. A gendered perception of innovation was evident in our study. Some RDs did describe technological innovations such as food service management software and continuous glucose monitors when highlighting innovations they use in their RD work, but the innovation they were highlighting was typically the application (and not the development) of these technologies within their practice and the context in which they worked. Additionally, when asked to think broadly of the most innovative person they knew or knew of, almost two-thirds identified a male innovator. Others, who named companies, identified companies with famous male leaders/founders (e.g., Microsoft – Bill Gates, Apple – Steve Jobs). Importantly, however, none of our respondents explicitly highlighted gender as a factor impacting on their perception of or experience with innovation. A future qualitative study, conducted in a sample of health professionals with more male representation, would be of value.

There was an apparent pro-innovation disposition among respondents, with many indicating that innovations, by definition, improve things. Inherent to the process of innovation is that some innovations, or iterations of innovations, make things worse rather than better [35]. A pro-innovation disposition has been previously noted in healthcare [36], despite a common belief that failure is a fundamental part of innovation processes [37].

Based on our findings, innovation training for RD students during the academic portion of their preparatory programs is lacking. While there is agreement that innovative methods must be encouraged and applied in the delivery of health professionals’ education [38–40], less has been published [41–43] on the benefits or logistics of delivering innovation training within the curriculum of preparatory programs in the health professions. Findings from part one of this study indicate that individual characteristics (such as conscientiousness) are significantly more predictive of individual RD innovativeness than job-specific or organization-level predictors [23], and personal characteristics are not typically as modifiable as group or organization-level factors. Chaytor et al. [44] (2012) explored change in conscientiousness among students between year one and year two of a UK undergraduate medical program and concluded that conscientiousness is a stable trait that is unlikely to be modified through teaching. Notably, this study did not include an intervention; rather, the studied medical students participated in standard medical training, in which they were exposed to more education in professionalism between year one and two and would have had more direct contact with patients in that time frame [44]. Javaras et al. [45] (2019) reviewed several psychological interventions to increase conscientiousness; in general, the interventions considered in the review entailed repeat practice of thought processes and behaviours observed among highly conscientious individuals. These interventions tend to be undertaken in the context of psychological treatment and are most effective when participants begin at a lower level of conscientiousness [45]. Thus, if dietetic programs have an explicit goal of producing dietitians capable of and motivated toward innovation, the best strategy may be to incorporate personality testing at the point of admission to the preparatory program. Across employment sectors, personality tests are a prevalent component of selection processes [46]. The Big Five Inventory (BFI) [47], for example, may be completed by candidates applying to dietetic programs to screen for conscientiousness. More research is needed to determine if incorporating such screening measures would be of value in dietetics as individual personality factors can have positive impacts on one desirable characteristic (such as innovativeness among RDs) while also having negative impacts on other desirable characteristics (such as flexibility) [48].

Having employed a deductive-inductive analysis of our data, we can see that all of the factors and pathways to individual innovation in the workplace (both direct and indirect)) included in Scott & Bruce’s [27] model were represented in our data (see Table 1). Unlike in Scott & Bruce’s analysis, where team-member exchange was found to have a nonsignificant relationship with individual innovation, team-member exchange was more strongly represented in our data than were leadership factors or individual-level factors. This may be because, across all employment sectors, there is an increasing expectation for collaborative work within organizations with flatter hierarchical structures [49]; Scott & Bruce’s [27] model was developed in the 1990s. Alternately, it may reflect an ongoing difference between the industrial sector and health sector in terms of the importance of team relationships for individual innovation. It is no surprise that psychological climate factors, including both support for innovation and resource supply, were so heavily represented in our data. Health care can scarcely be mentioned without commenting on resource scarcity, whether it be the increasing percentage of GDP expended on health [50], inadequate infrastructure to meet the needs of aging populations [51] or progressively worsening shortages of health professionals [52]. These interviews were conducted just prior to the COVID-19 pandemic (in January of 2020) and so our respondents had not yet had to deal with the consequences of widely reported supply chain issues in their workplaces [53], such as limited access to personal protective equipment or hand sanitizer.

For most of our inductive themes (see Table 2), it was unsurprising that they were not reflected in Scott & Bruce’s model. Benefits, for example, would have been outside of the scope of their model, while the dietetic-specific and healthcare system themes would not have been relevant in the industrial sector, where the original study was conducted [27]. The individual-level category of factors in Scott & Bruce’s model is where it was found most lacking, as it incorporated only decision-making styles (intuitive and systematic) [27]. As noted in our prior publication from the first part of this two-part study, many individual-level factors, including personality traits such as openness, conscientiousness, motivation, and self-efficacy (among others), have been linked to individual innovativeness [23]. Importantly, the most notable finding from our quantitative analysis of the survey data was that the large majority (~ 82%) of explained variance in self-reported innovation in role by dietitians could be explained by individual-level factors, with the personality factors of conscientiousness and openness having the most significant relationship with RD innovation [23]. All of our respondents for these interviews had completed the quantitative survey and so were part of the analytic sample for the previous analysis. For this reason, it was expected that many would discuss individual characteristics of innovators at some point in their interview. In future quantitative testing of Scott & Bruce’s model, in any work setting, we would recommend that researchers incorporate additional individual-level variables such as the Big Five personality traits [47], which include conscientiousness and openness. Unfortunately, there is currently little to guide health administrators and managers seeking to hire innovative dietitians, who may want to incorporate assessments of innovativeness into their human resources processes.

Strengths

Our sample size was more than adequate to ensure data saturation. We successfully tested a validated model of individual-level employee innovation with a novel set of respondents. The interviewer, Dr. Jo-Louise Huq, an expert in healthcare innovation and experienced qualitative researcher had no previous relationship with the respondents. At both the transcription and coding stage, tasks were completed independently by two researchers (SH and JF). The final list of inductive themes was determined collaboratively by SH and JF. The identified themes echoed many of the findings from the first (quantitative) part of this two-part study.

Limitations

There was an overrepresentation of respondents from the province of Alberta, which may have biased results to be more reflective of the perceptions of RDs working in that province. However, many RDs complete their training outside of the province of their future employment, which makes the collective viewpoints of respondents on innovation education in preparatory programs more relevant to the broader Canadian RD experience. Additionally, we did not have representation from key settings of RD employment including public health, food service or private practice. It is highly likely that perceptions of innovation differ across settings of RD employment.

Conclusion

Our study results confirm concerns raised in the literature about gendered perceptions of innovation. While gender was not explicitly highlighted in relation to innovation by our respondents, the large majority who identified the most innovative individual they knew or knew of, named a male innovator. There was wide agreement among respondents that the academic portion of RD preparatory programs did not prepare them to be innovative in the workplace. More research is needed before concrete recommendations can be made that would enhance the innovation competence of newly graduated RDs. We confirmed that Scott & Bruce’s Path Model of Individual Innovation in the Workplace was applicable within the health care setting and among RDs. However, deficits in the model were noted beyond what would be expected due to context. Specifically, the addition of individual-level variables predictive of individual innovation, such as personality traits, would likely strengthen the model, regardless of the setting of application. Further research is needed to better understand how gender impacts upon individual definitions and perceptions of innovation. A similar qualitative study in a health profession with more gender balance would be of value.
