Bioelectric calcium transport and activation in mammalian cells using field-focused DNA-carbon nanotube meshes
Authors: Haodong Li, Yuyao Li, Jia Liao, Changming Bao, Xiaodi Cao, Xinping Gao, Wenjian Luo, Tao Ding, Weiming Lin, Xizhi Deng, Hongwu Tang, Mingxing Xie, Sisi Jia, Chunhai Fan, Le Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrical signaling in biology is ionic. Safe modulation needs interfaces that couple electrons to ions at soft membranes without injury. We present DNA-FOCUS, a membrane-conformal, DNA-programmed carbon-nanotube biointerface that self-assembles on living cells, weaving DNA-wrapped SWCNTs into an ultraflexible mesh. A subvolt bias is condensed into nanometer hotspots at the membrane, lowering access resistance and engaging endogenous calcium-permeable ion channels. Subvolt stimulation drives sustained Ca2+ influx across multiple lineages, with voltage-sensitive dye and impedance readouts showing stronger coupling. Responses occur without excess ROS or loss of viability. The focused field also enables gentle electroporation for plasmid delivery, augments mechano-electrical transduction, and modulates neuronal excitability. Transcriptional analysis and functional validation confirm NFAT activation, and in natural killer cells, it boosts perforin and IFN-γ release with higher tumor killing. This programmable, substrate-free interface supports low-voltage electro-modulation for high-throughput analytics and therapeutic augmentation.

INTRODUCTION

Electrical signaling lies at the heart of physiology, yet it differs fundamentally from solid-state electronics: Currents in living tissue are carried by hydrated ions, not by electrons (1–3). Any artificial stimulator must therefore bridge two dissimilar regimes—electron flow in conductors and ion flow in aqueous media—while conforming to the softness and curvature of the plasma membrane (4–6). At biologically tolerable, macroscopic low voltages, much of the applied potential is dissipated across the electrical double layer and access resistances, and unfocused fields do little to raise transmembrane potential (7–9). Conversely, conventional planar electrodes and electrolyte-gated sensors, mounted on rigid substrates, overcome dispersion and Debye screening only by driving hundreds of millivolts (or even volts) through bulk electrolyte (10, 11). Such high fields diffuse beyond their targets, heat the medium, alter pH, and trigger off-target gene programs; lowering the bias to a biologically safe range renders physically gated channels largely unresponsive (12, 13). What is needed is a soft, substrate-free interface that marries field-effect precision with ionic transport at physiologically benign voltages.

Single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) appear almost tailor-made for the task. Their nanometric diameter and high aspect ratio create a geometric singularity that concentrates the electric field into localized, high-intensity hotspots, while their exceptional intrinsic electrical conductivity minimizes resistive losses to enable efficient charge injection (14–17). Beyond these electronic attributes, their inherent mechanical flexibility facilitates intimate conformal contact with soft biological membranes (18). Moreover, SWCNTs exhibit a wide electrochemical safety window and chemical inertness, ensuring stable operation in physiological environments (19). Realizing that promise in living systems has been hampered, in part, by deployment modes in which spray- or drop-cast CNT films reaggregate into micron-scale bundles, limiting conformal coverage and, in some regimes, altering membrane mechanic, while rigid-substrate tethering places CNTs beyond the membrane’s Debye length; in both cases, Debye screening and capacitive shunting attenuate electron-ion coupling and waste the applied bias (20–22).

Here, we introduce the DNA-FOCUS, a DNA-programmed field-oriented CNT ultraflexible sheath that self-assembles in situ on living cells. Dendritic DNA framework network (dDFn) inserts into the outer leaflet, utilizing a hybridization-chain reaction (HCR) to weave DNA-wrapped SWCNTs into an ultraflexible mesh flush with the bilayer. This mesh converts a subvolt bias—delivered into a localized field that directly activates calcium-permeable ion channels—amplifying calcium influx without detectable cell cytotoxicity and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Downstream, NFAT programs are activated, and, in natural killer (NK) cells, perforin/interferon-γ (IFN-γ) release and tumor cell apoptosis are enhanced. Crucially, the interface also supports gentle, low-injury electroporation for plasmid entry and augments mechano-electrical transduction under optical tweezers and the modulation of neuronal excitability for bioelectronic applications—establishing a versatile, low-voltage toolkit for bioelectric control. By uniting programmable DNA self-assembly with membrane-proximal field focusing, DNA-FOCUS enables safe, low-voltage electro-modulation of native ion channels in a high-throughput, cell-addressable format for ion channel analytics, regenerative medicine, and immunotherapy.

RESULTS

Design and in situ assembly of a field-focused DNA-CNT mesh

To create a soft, substrate-free interface that bridges electron flow in conductors with ion flow across curved, aqueous membranes, we engineered a DNA-programmed field-oriented CNT ultraflexible sheath (DNA-FOCUS). SWCNTs by virtue of their sub–2-nm-diameter, ultrahigh carrier mobility and low intrinsic resistance assemble into continuous conductive networks on the plasma membrane. Under an applied subvolt bias, this mesh generates local field gradients that drive Ca2+ influx and physically gated channels pathways. We propose a positive feedback mechanism (Fig. 1A): The SWCNT network acts as a field-concentrating mesh, lowering local membrane resistance and concentrating the applied bias into nanometer-scale hotspots, which, in turn, recruit further channel opening and amplify Ca2+ entry, ultimately triggering downstream gene activation.

The self-assembly of this system was performed directly on the surface of living cells (Fig. 1B). First, a cholesterol-modified DNA framework (DF) was anchored onto the cell membrane to serve as a nucleator. Subsequently, dDFn was grown in situ from the nucleator via a specific HCR. Each dendritic branch terminates in a sequence complementary to GT(20)-wrapped SWCNTs, guiding their orderly, in situ integration into a continuous, flexible mesh that conforms tightly to the lipid bilayer. Fluorescence microscopy imaging and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging (Fig. 1C and fig. S1) clearly confirmed the success of this multistep assembly, showing the progression from the ring-like fluorescence of the DF to the solid fluorescence of the dDFn and culminating in the uniform coverage of the DNA-FOCUS composite on the cell surface. Together, these data establish that DNA-SWCNTs can self-assemble via a programmable scaffold and couple directly to living membranes, thereby forming the DNA-FOCUS biointerface.

Meanwhile, finite-element modeling predicts that a modest macroscopic bias of 0.8 V cm−1 is compressed by the DNA-FOCUS into localized field hotspots at the membrane interface—reaching intensities tens of times stronger than the applied field alone (Fig. 1D and fig. S2). To validate this field effect, we cultured CTX TNA2 cells in a free-standing indium tin oxide (ITO)/Ag-AgCl chamber (Fig. 1E) designed to maintain constant substrate (Rsub/Csub) and system (Rsys/Csys) impedances and then applied a controlled 0.8 V cm−1 bias (fig. S3). Under static stimulation, the calcium indicator Fluo-4 acetoxymethyl ester (Fluo-4 AM) imaging showed only a minimal response in unmodified cells, whereas DNA-FOCUS cells exhibited a rapid, sustained Ca2+ influx (Fig. 1F and movies S1 and S2). Quantitatively, the peak fluorescence change (ΔF/F) (Fig. 1G) rose from 0.02 (background) to 0.62, demonstrating ultra-enhanced calcium ion transport at subvolt stimulation and confirming efficient coupling between the DNA-FOCUS and cellular calcium channels.

Following stimulation, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis showed robust up-regulation of calcium-responsive regulators, specifically calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase I (CAMK1), nuclear factor of activated T cells 1 (NFATC1), and the AP-1 complex component Fos proto-oncogene (FOS). Concurrently, we observed increased expression of calcium ion channels genes, including Piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (PIEZO1) and transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V members 1 and 4 (TRPV1 and TRPV4) (Fig. 1H and fig. S4). Collectively, these data confirm that triggers robust calcium signaling cascades accompanied by the transcriptional up-regulation of key calcium channel genes. The DNA-FOCUS thus functions dually as a highly conductive membrane interface and a nanoscale field concentrator, lowering activation thresholds for endogenous channels and enabling ultra-efficient, subvolt calcium signaling in living cells.

Growth control and interface characterization of DNA-FOCUS

To construct a membrane scaffold that can subsequently template the CNT mesh, we first assembled a cholesterol-tagged tetrahedral DF from four single strands (Fig. 2A and fig. S5). Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) revealed a clean, stepwise shift from monomers to the final tetrahedron (fig. S6), confirming that the strands fold with high fidelity. This structural precision is essential, as the rigid geometry relies on a three-vertex base to furnish multivalent cholesterol anchors and a well-defined spatial registry for downstream growth (23, 24). Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) showed that the fluorescence of DF cholesterol recovers five times more slowly than that of a cholesterol-modified single strand (Fig. 2B), while quantitative fits indicate a markedly reduced diffusion constant; the slower recovery reflects stronger membrane tethering and lower lateral mobility, traits that keep initiators in place long enough to support extended hybridization (Fig. 2C). Live-cell imaging over 2 hours agreed: The single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) signal faded to <88%, whereas DF retained >95% of its surface intensity (Fig. 2, D and E), demonstrating superior resistance to endocytosis and shedding.

Leveraging that stability, we introduced complementary hairpins H1 and H2 so that the membrane-bound DF could trigger an isothermal HCR (Fig. 2F). PAGE analysis of reaction aliquots captured a progressive smear toward higher molecular weight (Fig. 2G), confirming successful polymer growth in solution. This confirms that the reaction builds an extended dendritic architecture on the membrane, providing a spacious, mechanically coherent scaffold for subsequent SWCNT capture (fig. S7). Time-lapse confocal microscopy of Cy5-labeled hairpin H2 on live cells recorded a monotonic, ~2.3-fold rise in surface fluorescence over 120 min (Fig. 2H), and quantitative plots fit to a single-exponential growth constant, underscoring that HCR proceeds efficiently in the crowded membrane environment (Fig. 2I). Together, these data establish a dense, three-dimensional dendritic DF that is mechanically coupled to the lipid bilayer yet extends tens of nanometers into the extracellular space—an ideal pre–DNA-FOCUS scaffold for orderly, site-specific capture of DNA-wrapped SWCNTs.

To leverage DNA’s programmability for in situ assembly of the DNA-FOCUS, we noncovalently wrapped SWCNTs with GT(20) oligonucleotides, cloaking each nanotube in a biocompatible, sequence-defined shell (figs. S8 to S10) (17, 25, 26). Atomic force microscopy (AFM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and SEM confirmed well-dispersed DNA-SWCNTs (Fig. 2J, top, and figs. S11 to S13), and TEM of DNA–gold nanoparticles labeled SWCNTs pinpointed DNA anchoring sites along the tube surface (Fig. 2J, bottom, and figs. S14 and S15), demonstrating that DNA modification endows DNA-SWCNTs with robust self-assembly capabilities (26).

To test whether the density of the DNA-FOCUS layer could be tuned in situ, we varied the HCR time after GT(20)-SWCNT addition and examined membrane coverage at 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 hours. Confocal microscopy of Cy3-labeled SWCNTs (fig. S16) and Cy5-labeled dDFn showed that isolated nanotube puncta present at 0.5 hours gradually merged into a continuous sheath by 2 hours, while the underlying dDFn fluorescence remained stable—evidence of layer-by-layer growth rather than scaffold turnover (Fig. 2K). Integrated SWCNT fluorescence increased steadily across the time course (Fig. 2L). Crucially, control experiments confirmed that negligible SWCNT adsorption occurred in the absence of membrane-anchoring DNA, verifying the specificity of the assembly (fig. S17). Furthermore, the DNA-FOCUS biointerface demonstrated robust stability; confocal imaging at 4 hours postassembly revealed sustained surface localization, with cross-sectional intensity profiles confirming that the SWCNT mesh remained distinctly bound to the plasma membrane (fig. S18) (27). Raman spectra provided complementary chemical confirmation: Radial-breathing (150 to 180 cm−1) and D/G band (1585 to 1600 and 2570 to 2595 cm−1) peaks sharpened and intensified in parallel (Fig. 2M and fig. S19). These concordant optical and spectroscopic trends verify time-dependent, ordered recruitment of CNTs to the membrane framework. To evaluate how the evolving DNA-FOCUS alters electrical coupling, we recorded real-time impedance on a real-time cell analysis (RTCA) platform (schematic, Fig. 2N, left) (28). As assembly progressed, the cell index declined stepwise (Fig. 2N, right), indicating that the growing CNT mesh presents an increasingly low-impedance shunt across the sensing electrodes. This finding was consistently corroborated by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy. Specifically, cells featuring the fully assembled DNA-FOCUS (2-hour HCR) exhibited a markedly smaller semicircular arc in the Nyquist plot compared to controls, directly confirming that the mature DNA-FOCUS mesh substantially lowers the interfacial impedance at the cell membrane (fig. S20). Functionally, this translated into a stronger field effect: Following a 0.8 V cm−1, 100 s pulse, the voltage-sensitive dye FluoVolt reported a ΔFmax/F of 0.028 at 0.5 hours, rising to 0.102 at 2 hours (Fig. 2O). Crucially, we verified that the DNA-FOCUS biointerface does not perturb cellular physiology in the absence of stimulation. Resting potential measurements using FluoVolt showed no significant deviation between DNA-FOCUS and controls (fig. S21). Similarly, Fluo-4 AM imaging confirmed that basal intracellular calcium levels remained stable, effectively ruling out spontaneous ion leakage (fig. S22). Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings further corroborated these findings, demonstrating preserved resting potentials comparable to untreated cells (fig. S23). These data confirm that the DNA-FOCUS biointerface achieves membrane integration with negligible impact on basal cellular physiology. Thus, the same HCR growth dial that controls CNT loading concomitantly tunes membrane depolarization, linking structural adjustment with electro-functional output. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HCR time offers precise, on-cell control of DNA-FOCUS thickness, conductivity, and field responsiveness—establishing a versatile route to tailor bio-electronic interfaces before stimulation experiments.

Subvolt calcium dynamics under static and oscillatory electrical stimulation

On the basis of the recruitment curve of the DNA-FOCUS on living cells, we selected 2-hour HCR assembly time with more notable changes in membrane potential for subsequent electrical simulations to ensure maximal CNT density and robust electro-ionic coupling (subsequent experiments have defaulted to this treatment). Human umbilical cord endothelial cells (HUVECs), CTX TNA2, and Jurkat cells were then exposed to under two subvolt paradigms across a 1-cm chamber: static (0 to 0.8 V cm−1 in 85 s, followed by a 100-s hold) and oscillatory (square wave, 0.8 V cm−1 peak-to-peak, 2 Hz, 0.5-s duty). Real-time Fluo-4 AM imaging provided four kinetic indices—the fraction of responding cells, the peak ΔF/F (ΔFmax/F), the onset time Tonset (time to ΔF/F > 0.01), and time to peak Tpeak.

Under a single static subvolt pulse (0 to 0.8 V cm−1, 100 s) (Fig. 3A), DNA-FOCUS coating uniformly intensified calcium entry in the three lineages examined: The fraction of responsive cells, the peak amplitude (ΔFmax/F), and the integrated fluorescent signal all exceeded those of unmodified controls (Fig. 3, B to D). Superimposed on this global gain, each cell type retained its own kinetic fingerprint driven by distinct channel repertoires. HUVEC reacted earliest (Tonset ≈ 15 s versus 23 s) and reached the largest peak (ΔFmax/F ≈ 0.25 versus 0.04) (Fig. 3, E to H). Pharmacological interrogation points to the TRP channel family as the primary mediator of this rapid influx. The substantial attenuation by GsMTx4 confirms the recruitment of mechanosensitive pathways—likely involving mechanically activated TRP isoforms alongside a contribution from PIEZO1—thereby validating the role of these polymodal sensors in transducing the field-focused stimulus (fig. S24). In contrast, CTX TNA2 astrocytes gave the greatest responder percentage (64% versus 49%) and a higher peak (≈0.27 versus 0.09), Tpeak stretched to 270 s (Fig. 3, E to H). To determine the mechanism underlying this distinct calcium signature, we performed inhibitor assays. The results showed that the L-type calcium channel blocker Verapamil almost completely abolished the signal, identifying VGCCs as the primary responders to depolarization in this lineage (fig. S24). Jurkat showed the most modest enhancement: Tonset lengthened to 32 s, and the peak rose only to ≈0.10 (Fig. 3, E to H) and remained largely insensitive to both VGCC and TRP inhibitors (fig. S24). Instead, their activity pattern—confirmed by susceptibility to thapsigargin-induced depletion—points to a store-operated calcium entry mechanism, which relies on capacitive influx rather than the high-gain amplification of the excitable channels (note S1 and fig. S25) (29–31).

Switching to an oscillatory waveform (square wave, 0.8 V cm−1 p-p, 2 Hz, 0.5-s duty) (Fig. 3I) preserved the overall enhancement while sharpening the temporal kinetics (32–34). DNA-FOCUS increased the responder fraction and ΔFmax/F in every lineage (Fig. 3, J to L). Specifically, HUVEC peaks climbed further to ≈0.30 and Tpeak to 119 s. CTX TNA2 maintained an elevated peak (≈0.21) with contracted temporal profiles, and Jurkat cells exhibited a markedly faster onset (Tonset fell to 21 s) (Fig. 3, M to P). This kinetic facilitation is consistent with our three-state cyclic Markovian modeling, which suggests that static fields rapidly drive channels into a nonconducting inactivated trap (13, 35). In contrast, the off phase of the oscillatory drive facilitates channels to recover from inactivation and transition back to the closed state, thereby maintaining a high open probability for subsequent pulses (note S2 and fig. S26). This simulations imply that the DNA-FOCUS biointerface under oscillatory mode could effectively mitigate channel desensitization, enabling a more synchronized and responsive calcium entry compared to static stimulation.

Together, these results show that the DNA-FOCUS serves simultaneously as a highly conformal, conductive membrane biointerface and a field concentrator: It lowers activation thresholds for endogenous channels, amplifies Ca2+ influx under either bias regime, and preserves lineage-specific electrophysiological signatures. The degree of enhancement derives from the coupling efficiency of the DNA-FOCUS membrane interface, whereas the residual heterogeneity reflects intrinsic differences in channel density and Ca2+-handling machinery across the three cell types.

Transcriptional programs and cytoskeletal remodeling downstream of activation

To clarify how the DNA-FOCUS influences molecular circuitry downstream of the calcium burst, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on HUVEC 24 hours after subvolt stimulation of DNA-FOCUS and untreated controls (fig. S27). With |log2 fold change| > 1 and q value < 0.05 as thresholds, the most prominent changes clustered in ion transport and cell migration pathways: up-regulation of the Ca2+-activated cation channel transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily M member 5 (TRPM5), carbonic anhydrase (CA9), and transport components solute carrier family 35 member F4 (SLC35F4) and G protein–coupled receptor 146/17 (GPR146/GPR17) points to enhanced membrane depolarization. Simultaneous induction of kinesin family member 19 (KIF19), adrenomedullin (ADM), fos proto-oncogene, AP-1 transcription factor subunit (FOS), and transforming growth factor beta 2 (TGFB2), which drive microtubule-actin rearrangement and mitogen-activated protein kinase/small mothers against decapentaplegic signaling, supports a rise in migratory capacity (Fig. 4A). Reactome enrichment analysis mapped these changes to AP-1, Reelin, and leukotriene receptor pathways (Fig. 4B), signaling axes that collectively integrate Ca2+ influx with proliferation, migration, and immune modulation (36, 37). Gene Ontology analysis supported this view: Biological process terms were dominated by lipid transport, Rho family guanosine triphosphatase signaling, and actin-bundle assembly; cellular component terms by apical plasma membrane and adherens junction clusters; and molecular function terms by G protein–coupled receptor, cytokine, and growth factor activities (figs. S28 and S29) (38, 39). In combination with the electrophysiology and imaging data, these transcriptomic changes suggest a cross-scale cascade in which DNA-FOCUS–focused Ca2+ entry drives AP-1–dependent transcription and reprograms cytoskeletal and paracrine outputs while curbing selected metabolic transporters—molecular hallmarks that can be harnessed to steer low power electro-therapies toward proliferation, migration, or immunoactivation end points.

On the basis of the activation of signaling pathways related to cell growth and proliferation observed in the gene expression profile, we directly assessed cellular proliferation using a 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay. Click chemistry labeling showed a marked rise in EdU-positive nuclei after DNA-FOCUS treatment (Fig. 4C, EdU-647 labeling newly synthesized DNA). The fraction of EdU-positive cells climbed from 48 ± 6 to 83 ± 2% (Fig. 4D, top), and the integrated EdU signal increased ~1.3-fold (Fig. 4D, bottom), suggesting that amplified Ca2+ influx serves as a potent mitogenic cue that accelerates DNA synthesis and cell cycle progression (40). This finding provides potential of DNA-FOCUS in tissue regeneration.

Because elevated calcium ion transients can remodel the cytoskeleton, we visualized filamentous actin (F-actin) with fluorescein isothiocyanate–phalloidin. Controls exhibited long, oriented, smooth actin fibers, whereas DNA-FOCUS cells reorganized into a more complex, multidomain network (Fig. 4E, top). The orientation of actin fibers was further analyzed using ImageJ software, where the same color indicates the same orientation in the pattern. The pattern of control cells showed a single color, indicating a high degree of consistency in the orientation of cytoskeletal F-actin. In contrast, the DNA-FOCUS group displayed a pattern with multiple colors, suggesting reorganization of cytoskeletal F-actin (Fig. 4E, bottom). Automated orientation analysis revealed similar anisotropy values (Fig. 4F). Orientation mapping and anisotropy analysis demonstrated a significant drop in global alignment, consistent with Ca2+-driven actin reorganization.

Given that the mechanism of DNA-FOCUS is closely linked to ion channels, we further examined the protein expression levels of key ion channels. Immunofluorescence revealed differential up-regulation of PIEZO1 and TRPV1 in DNA-FOCUS cells (Fig. 4, G to I), implying that the conductive nano-milieu preferentially reinforces stretch-activated signaling.

Last, we confirmed that these physically induced Ca2+ transients translate into functional downstream gene activation using an NFAT-responsive green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter system (Fig. 4J). The GFP signal intensity scaled with the HCR assembly time, with the 2-hour condition showing an approximately 1.4-fold greater response than the 0.5-hour condition. This demonstrates that downstream transcriptional reprogramming is tunable and quantitatively dependent on the DNA-FOCUS mesh density (Fig. 4, K and L, and fig. S30). This proves that the DNA-FOCUS can effectively couple external physical stimuli to transcriptional reprogramming, as validated by the significant increase in GFP signal. Collectively, these results establish that the DNA-FOCUS–induced field enhancement is a versatile and powerful principle for controlling a wide spectrum of cellular processes.

Biocompatibility and extended functions—poration, signal amplification, and network activity

After successfully constructing the DNA-FOCUS biointerface, we evaluated its biocompatibility and investigated its applications in modulating various cellular functions. To evaluate possible downstream consequences of subvolt stimulation through the DNA-FOCUS, we first examined viability 24 hours after the pulse (Fig. 5A). Calcein acetoxymethyl ester (Calcein-AM) and propidium iodide (PI) staining revealed no excess PI-positive nuclei in the coated cohort, and quantitative counts confirmed that survival remained above 90% in both groups (Fig. 5B), indicating that the field focused interface is cytocompatible under the applied bias (27). Traditional electrical stimulation (ES) may lead to oxidative stress, so intracellular ROS was monitored with a fluorescent probe (Fig. 5C and fig. S31). Basal ROS did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 5D), confirming that the subvolt regime avoids redox damage. To rigorously exclude physical membrane compromise, we further assessed lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release, a standard enzymatic marker for membrane leakage. LDH levels in the DNA-FOCUS group remained comparable to untreated control groups (fig. S32), ruling out structural disruption. Complementing this static integrity, FRAP analysis confirmed that the DNA-FOCUS biointerface retains dynamic membrane fluidity. Although the DNA-FOCUS coating slightly extended the fluorescence recovery time—reflecting a modest increase in surface viscosity due to the nanotube mesh—the robust recovery of the signal demonstrates that lipid mobility is preserved rather than rigidified (fig. S33). Collectively, these data confirm that the DNA-FOCUS biointerface is chemically safe, structurally nondisruptive, and dynamically compatible with living membranes.

Next, we explored the biointerface’s capability to enhance gene delivery. DNA-FOCUS concentrates the electric field at the cell membrane to induce membrane permeabilization, thereby enabling electroporation (Fig. 5E). It was validated by an in situ gold deposition assay, where the conductive mesh significantly promoted the surface reduction of gold ions (fig. S34) (41). The results of electroporation showed that, when a weak electric field was applied, cells equipped with the DNA-FOCUS exhibited far greater transfection efficiency of mEGFP-Lifeact-7 than the control group, as evidenced by robust GFP expression (Fig. 5, F and G, and fig. S35). Building on this physical basis, we further demonstrated that the transfection efficiency is quantitatively tunable by the interface architecture. Prolonging the HCR assembly time from 0.5 to 2 hours—effectively increasing the SWCNT density—resulted in a saturation-dependent rise in EGFP expression (fig. S36). This confirms that the density of field-focusing elements directly dictates the permeabilization outcome. Crucially, to distinguish physical poration from biological uptake, we performed the delivery in the presence of Dynasore, a potent dynamin inhibitor that blocks endocytosis (42). The transfection efficiency remained statistically unchanged compared to the vehicle control, proving that plasmid entry is driven by direct physical electroporation rather than endocytic pathways (fig. S37). Last, we substantiated this mechanism via thermodynamic modeling of pore energetics (note S3 and fig. S38) (43–47). The intensified local field lowers the free energy barrier required for pore expansion, effectively shifting the pore population into a high-flux zone. This theoretical evidence aligns with the experimental data, confirming that the interface physically lowers the energetic cost of membrane permeabilization. This demonstrates that the DNA-FOCUS can serve as a highly effective tool for enhancing electroporation.

We next investigated the impact of the DNA-FOCUS biointerface on cellular responsiveness to mechanical stimuli. Using optical tweezers, we applied a precise, periodic mechanical force to the cells via a microbead and monitored the intracellular Ca2+ concentration in real time with the Fluo-4 AM calcium indicator (Fig. 5H). The results showed that, in response to the same mechanical force, DNA-FOCUS–modified cells produced a markedly amplified calcium influx signal compared to the control group (Fig. 5, I and J). To quantify this sensitization, we titrated the optical trap force across a physiological range (peak forces ≈ 20, 40, and 60 pN). Control cells exhibited only a marginal increase in calcium entry with rising force, and the DNA-FOCUS group displayed force-dependent escalation in response intensity (fig. S39). These data indicate that the interface effectively couples external mechanical load to the membrane, thereby sensitizing native mechanotransduction pathways to physical inputs.

Last, to validate the biointerface’s potential in more complex biological systems, we applied it to cultured neural networks and recorded their electrical activity using multielectrode arrays (MEAs) (20, 48). The results revealed that neural networks treated with DNA-FOCUS displayed substantially enhanced spontaneous electrical activity, with firing frequencies and the number of active electrodes far exceeding those of the control group (Fig. 5, K to M). Quantitative analysis confirmed that DNA-FOCUS significantly increased both the total number of spikes and the mean firing rate of the neural network (Fig. 5, N and O). This suggests the DNA-FOCUS effectively lowers the activation threshold of neurons, enhancing network-level communication and demonstrating its potential for neuromodulation. In summary, these results demonstrate that the DNA-FOCUS is a biocompatible and powerful multifunctional biointerface, which can be effectively used to enhance gene delivery, amplify mechanosensing signals, and potentiate the electrical activity of neural networks.

Immunomodulation with DNA-FOCUS—boosting NK cell cytotoxicity

To assess whether the DNA-FOCUS can be translated into an immunotherapeutic context, we selected the NK92MI/Ramos as a stringent, contact-dependent killing model (Fig. 6A). NK92MI cells were first enveloped by the DNA-FOCUS and given a single subvolt conditioning pulse (0.8 V cm−1, 100 s); they were then cocultured with Ramos targets for 12 hours, after which cytolysis and cytokine release were quantified.

Live/dead imaging demonstrated a clear stepwise escalation of cytotoxicity. Ramos monocultures stained almost exclusively with Calcein-AM, indicating intact membranes. Coculture with untreated NK92MI cells introduced scattered PI nuclei, whereas NK92MI ES substantially increased the PI count. The full NK92MI@DNA-FOCUS ES regimen converted most Ramos cells to PI positive, yielding dense red fields (Fig. 6B). Quantitative analysis showed apoptosis rates of 2 ± 1% for Ramos alone, 12 ± 1% for NK92MI, 38 ± 3% for NK92MI ES, and 66 ± 5% for NK92MI@DNA-FOCUS ES (Fig. 6C), confirming that DNA-FOCUS and subvolt priming act additively to enhance tumor clearance.

To explore the potential mechanism by which DNA-FOCUS and ES enhance the killing effect of NK cells, we detected the secretion levels of various cytokines related to NK cell activation (Fig. 6D). The results showed that the secretion levels of tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α), perforin, granzyme B, and IFN-γ in the NK92MI@DNA-FOCUS ES group were significantly higher than those in the NK92MI and NK92MI ES groups. Beyond soluble mediators, we investigated the contact-dependent machinery essential for immunological synapse formation. Immunofluorescence staining with the conformation-specific antibody m24 revealed a substantial up-regulation of active LFA-1 integrin on the DNA-FOCUS–modified membrane (fig. S40). Notably, this enhanced integrin activation did not alter the overall conjugation frequency between NK and tumor cells, which remained statistically comparable to controls (fig. S41) (49). This suggests that the DNA-FOCUS biointerface potentiates the functional quality of the synapse. Furthermore, this functional gain occurred without inducing cellular stress or exhaustion, as evidenced by the stable expression levels of heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), and T cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3) (fig. S42) (50–52).

Collectively, these data indicate that the DNA-FOCUS boosts NK cell cytotoxicity through a coordinated enhancement of both secretory and synaptic machinery. The subvolt stimulation effectively triggers the release of key effector molecules—perforin, granzyme B, TNF-α, and IFN-γ—while simultaneously up-regulating the active conformation of LFA-1 to improve the functional quality of the immunological synapse. Crucially, this electro-priming achieves functional potentiation without inducing cellular stress and exhaustion. Thus, the DNA-FOCUS biointerface provides a safe and effective strategy to amplify NK cell effector potency via low-voltage physical modulation.

DISCUSSION

This work establishes a general strategy for safe, low-voltage control of ionic signaling: a DNA-programmed, ultraflexible CNT mesh that self-assembles on living cells and focuses weak external bias into nanometer-scale membrane hotspots. The resulting SWCNT mesh concentrates a gentle field, lowers access resistance, and boosts Ca2+ entry, activating NFAT/AP-1 transcription and accelerating proliferation. In immune settings, the same subvolt stimulus elevates perforin and IFN-γ release, driving a significantly enhanced killing efficacy against targets—demonstrating energy-efficient, label-free enhancement of effector function without sacrificing viability. Furthermore, the versatility of DNA-FOCUS is demonstrated by its applicability to efficient plasmid delivery via gentle electroporation, the facilitation of mechano-electrical transduction, and the potentiation of network-level neuronal communication for precise neuromodulation.

By combining field-effect precision with programmable DNA self-assembly, the DNA-FOCUS delivers stimulation free of rigid substrates or high-voltage pulses, positioning it as a versatile bridge between soft bioelectronics and cell engineering (53, 54). The inherent programmability of the DNA-FOCUS further enables modular functionalization—ranging from cell-selective ligands and environment-responsive motifs to payload integration—offering a highly adaptable toolkit for diverse biological contexts (55). Together, these attributes frame the DNA-FOCUS not merely as a tool for ionic control but as a foundational interface for regenerative electrotherapy, precision immunomodulation, and next-generation soft bioelectronic applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials and reagents

High-purity purified SWCNTs (SuperPureTubes) (~99%) were purchased from NanoIntegris. All DNA oligonucleotides, including cholesterol-modified and fluorophore-labeled strands, were synthesized and purified by Sangon Biotech. The cell lines used in this study, including HUVECs, CTX TNA2, Jurkat, NK-92MI, Ramos, and SH-SY5Y, were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures. Cell culture reagents, including basal media [Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), RPMI 1640, α-MEM, and DMEM/F12], fetal bovine serum (FBS), and supplements, were primarily purchased from Gibco, with the exception of the EGM-2 Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 BulletKit, which was obtained from Lonza. Commercial kits for functional assays, such as the Fluo-4 AM calcium indicator, membrane potential assay kit, ROS fluorescence detection kit, Click-iT EdU Cell Proliferation Kit, RNA extraction and reverse transcription qPCR (RT-qPCR) reagents (TRIzol, PureLink RNA Mini Kit, High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit, PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for cytokine quantification, were all acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific. ITO-coated glass slides and poly(dimethylsiloxane) (PDMS) for device fabrication were obtained from commercial sources.

Preparation and characterization of DNA-SWCNTs and dDFn

DNA-dispersed SWCNTs were prepared by mixing pristine SWCNT films with ssDNA (L1) in tris-boric acid–EDTA buffer containing 100 mM NaCl, followed by probe sonication (3 W) in an ice bath for 2 hours and sequential purification via ultracentrifugation (20,000g) and ultrafiltration (100-kDa MWCO) to eliminate aggregates and excess DNA. The concentration of the dispersed SWCNTs was determined spectrophotometrically (ε632 = 0.036 liters mg−1 cm−1), and their monodispersity and structural integrity were verified using ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared absorption spectroscopy, confocal Raman microscopy (785-nm excitation), TEM, SEM, and AFM, which revealed characteristic RBM, G, and G′ bands and a well-exfoliated morphology. DNA tetrahedral frameworks were assembled by annealing equimolar mixtures of four constituent strands (S1 to S4) from 95° to 20°C in Mg2+-containing buffer, while HCR hairpin probes (H1 and H2) were prepared by heat denaturation and slow cooling; the successful assembly of these DNA structures was confirmed by native PAGE.

Construction and characterization of DNA-FOCUS

To engineer the DNA-FOCUS on the cell surface, a programmable stepwise assembly was performed. First, DF assembled from equimolar mixtures of S1 to S4 strands (1 μM) were incubated with cells (1 × 106 to 5 × 106 cells/ml) in serum-free medium at 37°C for 30 min to anchor them into the plasma membrane via hydrophobic interaction. After washing three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove unbound DF, the HCR was initiated by adding H1 and H2 hairpin probes (1 μM each) in 2× SSC buffer containing 10% dextran sulfate, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 2 hours to grow extended DNA scaffolds. Subsequently, DNA-dispersed SWCNTs were added to the cell culture and coincubated for 30 min at room temperature to hybridize with the HCR scaffolds, followed by three washes with PBS to remove unbound nanotubes.

For characterization, the dynamic stability of the anchored structures was assessed using FRAP on a confocal microscope (60× oil objective); a defined region (2 to 5 μm) was photobleached using a 488-nm laser at 70 to 90% power for 0.5 to 1 s, and recovery was monitored at 1% laser power every 0.1 to 1 s. To visualize the surface morphology, cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 1 to 2 hours, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30 to 100%), dried via critical point drying, and sputter-coated with a 5- to 15-nm gold layer before imaging by SEM at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. The surface density of SWCNTs was quantified by confocal Raman microscopy using a 60× water immersion objective, where spectra were acquired with a 5-s integration time (three accumulations) to detect the SWCNT G-band intensity. In addition, the assembly process was monitored in real time by recording cellular impedance changes using an xCELLigence RTCA system with 20,000 cells seeded per well in E-Plates.

Membrane potential measurement

To quantitatively assess dynamic changes in cell membrane potential, FluoVolt was used following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, target cells (1 × 105 to 5 × 105 cells per well) were cultured at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were gently rinsed once with Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS). The voltage-sensitive fluorescent dye concentrate was diluted in assay buffer to the 1 to 5 μM. A total of 100 μl of the dye solution was added to each well, cells were incubated at 37°C in the dark for 30 to 60 min, and fluorescence signals were recorded using a fluorescence microscope (excitation, ~490 nm; emission, ~515 nm). To capture changes in membrane potential, ES was applied poststaining during time-lapse fluorescence acquisition.

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed to measure the resting membrane potential of the cells. Borosilicate glass pipettes (outer diameter 1.5 mm, inner diameter 0.86 mm; Sutter Instrument) were fabricated using a micropipette puller (Sutter Instrument). The recording pipettes were filled with an intracellular solution containing 140 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 10 mM Hepes, and 1 mM EGTA, with pH adjusted to 7.3 to 7.4 using KOH. The cells were bathed in an extracellular solution containing 130 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2·2H2O, 1 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 15 mM glucose, and 10 mM Hepes, with pH adjusted to 7.3 to 7.4 using NaOH. Recordings were acquired using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier, and a Digidata 1440A digitizer (Molecular Devices) was mounted on an upright microscope. After forming a giga-ohm seal and rupturing the membrane to establish the whole-cell configuration, the amplifier was switched to current-clamp mode with zero current injection (I = 0 pA) to record the stable membrane potential.

Device fabrication and ES-coupled calcium imaging

To perform ES, a custom cell culture device was fabricated by bonding a PDMS chamber (cured at 80°C for 2 hours) to a cleaned ITO-coated glass substrate (15 to 25 Ω/square) via oxygen plasma treatment (50 W, 60 s). Cells cultured within this device were loaded with the calcium indicator Fluo-4 AM (1 to 5 μM) in HBSS buffer at 37°C for 20 to 45 min, followed by a 30-min de-esterification step. The device was then integrated into a three-electrode system (ITO working electrode, Pt counter electrode, and Ag/AgCl reference electrode) connected to a CHI660E electrochemical workstation. To dissect the calcium signaling mechanisms, cells were subjected to pharmacological inhibition before stimulation: For ER store depletion, cells were incubated with 1 to 2 μM thapsigargin for 15 to 30 min; for ion channel blockade, cells were pretreated with the Verapamil, Capsazepine, and GsMTx4 under similar physiological conditions. Intracellular calcium dynamics were captured via time-lapse fluorescence microscopy and quantified as relative fluorescence changes (ΔF/F).

RNA-seq and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with RNA integrity assessed by an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). Sequencing libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw data were processed using fastp to remove low-quality reads and adapters. Clean reads were mapped to the human reference genome using HISAT2, and gene counts were obtained using HTSeq-count. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2. Subsequently, GO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses were conducted to identify significantly enriched functional pathways.

Cell proliferation assay

Target cells were seeded in culture plates 5 × 105 cells per well. EdU solution was added to the culture medium to a final concentration of 10 μM, and cells were incubated at 37°C for a preset period (1 to 4 hours) to allow incorporation of EdU into newly synthesized DNA. After EdU labeling, cells were washed once with PBS. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 15 to 20 min. Cells were washed two to three times with PBS. Next, permeabilization was performed (0.1 to 0.5% Triton X-100) for 15 to 20 min, and cells were washed again. The Click-iT reaction cocktail, containing Alexa Fluor 647 azide and reaction buffer, was prepared according to the kit instructions. This reaction mixture was added and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Upon completion of the Click reaction, cells were washed two to three times with PBS to remove unreacted reagents. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 μg/ml) for 5 to 15 min, followed by a final wash. Fluorescence imaging was conducted using a fluorescence microscope (excitation, ~650 nm; emission, ~668 nm).

F-actin staining

F-actin staining was performed using the Alexa Fluor 488 Phalloidin kit. Adherent cells cultured on glass coverslips at 60 to 80% confluency were washed once with PBS and then fixed with 4% PFA in PBS for 10 to 20 min. After fixation, cells were washed three times with PBS, 5 min each. To permeabilize the cell membrane and allow phalloidin entry, cells were incubated with PBS containing 0.1 to 0.25% (v/v) Triton X-100 at for 5 to 10 min, followed by three additional washes with PBS, 5 min each. According to the kit instructions, fluorescent phalloidin stock solution was diluted in PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) to the 200 nM final concentration and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 20 to 60 min. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS, 5 min each, to remove unbound probe. For nuclear counterstaining, cells were incubated with DAPI solution (1 μg/ml) for 5 to 10 min, followed by PBS washing. Last, coverslips were mounted on glass slides with antifade mounting medium.

Immunofluorescence staining

Adherent cells cultured on optically compatible culture plates (5 × 104 to 2 × 105 cells) were first rinsed once with PBS. Cells were then fixed with freshly prepared 4% PFA in PBS for 15 to 20 min. After fixation, cells were washed three times with PBS, 5 min each wash. Cells were incubated in PBS containing 0.1 to 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 10 to 15 min. Following permeabilization, cells were washed three times with PBS. Blocking was performed with PBS containing 5% BSA. Then, primary antibodies diluted 1:500 in PBS containing 1% BSA were applied, and cells were incubated overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber or for 1 to 2 hours. After primary antibody incubation, cells were washed three to five times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 (PBST), 5 to 10 min each wash. Subsequently, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies diluted 1:200 in PBS with 1% BSA were added, and cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 45 to 60 min. Cells were then washed thoroughly three to five times with PBST to remove unbound secondary antibodies. Nuclear counterstaining was performed by incubating cells with DAPI solution (1 μg/ml) for 5 to 10 min, followed by PBS wash. Last, coverslips were mounted onto glass slides using an antifade mounting medium. Multichannel fluorescence images were acquired and analyzed using a fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate excitation and emission filter sets.

Functional gene reporter

The activation of downstream calcium signaling was evaluated using an NFAT-responsive GFP reporter assay. Cells were transfected with the pNFAT-PTA-GFP-Neo plasmid (1 to 5 μg/ml) using Lipofectamine 3000. Following the kit instruction, cells were cultured for 24 to 36 hours to allow for reporter gene expression. GFP fluorescence was subsequently imaged, and the fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Cell viability assessment

To evaluate cell viability, Calcein-AM and PI dual-staining was performed. Cultured cells (1 × 105 to 5 × 105 cells/ml) were gently washed twice with PBS to remove residual medium. A staining solution was then prepared by diluting Calcein-AM stock [1 mM in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)] and PI stock (1 mg/ml in PBS) with PBS or serum-free medium to final concentrations of ~1 to 2 μM for Calcein-AM and ~1 to 5 μg/ml for PI. Each well received 100 to 200 μl of this dual-staining solution, followed by incubation at room temperature (20° to 25°C) in the dark for 15 to 30 min. Immediately after incubation, fluorescence imaging was conducted using a fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate filter sets to detect green fluorescence from Calcein (excitation, ~490 nm; emission, ~515 nm), indicating intracellular esterase activity and membrane integrity (live cells), and red fluorescence from PI (excitation, ~535 nm; emission, ~617 nm), indicating compromised membranes (dead cells). Cell viability was quantified by counting green fluorescent (live) and red fluorescent (dead) cells and calculating the percentage of live cells relative to the total.

ROS fluorescence detection

An ROS fluorescence detection kit was used following the manufacturer’s protocol. Target cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. The ROS-sensitive fluorescent probe stock solution (dissolved in DMSO) was diluted with PBS to the 5 to 10 μM, and 100 μl of the working solution was added to each well. Cells were incubated at 37°C in the dark for 30 to 60 min. Fluorescence signals were acquired and quantified using a confocal fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate excitation (~495 nm) and emission (~529 nm) filters.

Electroporation

To evaluate the field-enhanced membrane permeabilization, DNA-FOCUS–modified cells were incubated with a mEGFP-Lifeact-7 (1 to 5 μg/ml). A defined square-wave pulse (10 V, 5 ms) was applied using an electrical stimulator, followed by a 50-ms stabilization phase at 0 V. After 24 hours of culture to allow protein expression, transfection efficiency was quantified by measuring the mean fluorescence intensity of the intracellular GFP signal using ImageJ software.

Optical tweezers-based mechanical stimulation

To investigate Ca2+ responses triggered by mechanical stimulation, we used an integrated optical tweezers-fluorescence microscopy system that enables simultaneous cellular manipulation and calcium imaging. Target cells were seeded on 35-mm glass-bottom dishes at a density suitable for single-cell observation and manipulation. Cells were loaded with Fluo-4 AM by incubating at 37°C for 30 to 45 min, followed by washing with HBSS and a further 15- to 30-min incubation to allow complete de-esterification of the AM esters. The optical trapping system used a near-infrared continuous-wave laser (1064 nm) focused through a high–numerical aperture objective (100× oil immersion) to generate an optical trap with a controllable power range at the sample plane (2 W). Mechanical stimulation was delivered via bead-mediated indentation: A functionalized and fluorescently labeled polystyrene microsphere (diameter, 1 to 3 μm) was optically trapped and directed to contact a specific site on the cell surface for a predefined duration. During the mechanical stimulation, time-lapse fluorescence imaging conducted in parallel excitation was provided by a 488-nm laser, and emission was collected through a 500- to 550-nm bandpass filter. Image analysis software was used to define ROIs and extract the mean fluorescence intensity, which was then expressed as relative fluorescence change (ΔF/F).

MEA recording

Extracellular electrophysiological activity was recorded using a 4 × 4 MEA system. MEA chips were coated with poly-d-lysine to promote adhesion, and cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells/ml. After stabilization in the recording headstage (37°C, 5% CO2), wide-band voltage signals were acquired and band-pass filtered to isolate spiking activity from local field potentials and noise. Spikes were identified using a defined voltage threshold. The data were processed to generate raster plots and spatial activity heatmaps, with network functionality quantified by the total spike count per minute and the mean firing rate (hertz) across active electrodes.

NK cell cytotoxicity assay

NK cell–mediated cytotoxicity was assessed using a dual-fluorescence assay. Ramos target cells labeled with Calcein-AM (0.5 to 2.5 μM) were cocultured with NK-92MI effector cells for 4 hours at 37°C. PI (1 to 5 μg/ml) was added 15 min before the endpoint to label dead cells. The cytotoxicity rate was calculated as the percentage of PI-positive dead cells relative to the total target cell count identified by fluorescence microscopy. For cytokine analysis, NK-92MI cells were electrically stimulated and incubated for 24 hours. The supernatants were collected to quantify the secretion of TNF-α, IFN-γ, granzyme B, and perforin using commercial ELISA kits. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and concentrations were determined using a four-parameter logistic regression model.

Quantification of gene expression via RT-qPCR

To quantify the transcriptional levels of genes of interest, total RNA was extracted from harvested cells using TRIzol reagent. The concentration and purity of the RNA were assessed via UV spectrophotometry (NanoDrop), ensuring an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0. Subsequently, 1 to 2 μg of high-quality total RNA was treated with deoxyribonuclease I to eliminate genomic DNA contamination and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit under standard conditions. qPCR was performed using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix on a real-time PCR system with specific primers designed to span exon-exon junctions. The cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 to 60 s. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative cycle threshold (2−ΔΔCq) method, normalizing the data to the housekeeping gene GAPDH.

Finite element simulation

To quantify the field-focusing effect of the DNA-FOCUS mesh, a simplified two-dimensional stationary electrostatic model was constructed using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.3. The computational domain was defined as a 100 μm–by–100 μm square filled with extracellular medium. The cell was modeled as a concentric circular structure with a radius of 20 μm, incorporating a 10-nm-thick plasma membrane, and the two-dimensional model represents a cross-sectional approximation of the experimental system. To represent the surface nanotube modification, a geometric script was used to stochastically generate 2000 rectangular SWCNT elements within an annular region (inner radius: 20 μm; outer radius: 25 μm) surrounding the cell. Each SWCNT element (width: 0.5 nm; random length: 400 to 1000 nm) was assigned a random position and orientation (0° to 360°) to simulate a disordered conductive network. A Boolean union operation was applied to the SWCNT elements to resolve geometric overlaps. Material properties were assigned to specific domains with the following relative permittivity (εr) and electrical conductivity (σ) values: extracellular medium (εr = 80, σ = 1 S/m), cell membrane (εr = 8.57, σ = 3 × 10−7 S/m), cytoplasm (εr = 154.4, σ = 0.3 S/m), nuclear membrane (εr = 28, σ = 6 × 10−3 S/m), nucleus (εr = 52, σ = 1.35 S/m), and CNTs (εr = 10,000, σ = 1 × 108 S/m). The CNTs were treated as highly conductive effective media to capture their field-focusing behavior, rather than to represent their intrinsic dielectric response. The simulation used the electric currents interface to solve for electric potential (V) and electric field (E = −▽V) in conductive media. Boundary conditions were set by applying an electric potential of 0.8 V to the top boundary and grounding (0 V) the bottom boundary. The domain was discretized using a free triangular mesh. To accurately resolve the multiscale geometry—spanning from micrometers (cell) to subnanometers (CNTs)—a custom mesh sequence with local refinement was applied. Extremely fine mesh elements (maximum element size < 1 nm) were used for the SWCNT and membrane domains, while the bulk extracellular and intracellular regions were meshed with a coarser setting (maximum element size of 33 μm, minimum element size of 5 μm). To minimize numerical artifacts associated with sharp corners and mesh-dependent field singularities, field enhancement was quantified using percentile-based metrics rather than the absolute maximum. The stationary solution was computed to evaluate the spatial distribution and magnitude of the local electric field (E) at the cell-material interface. To enable robust comparison between conditions and avoid overinterpretation of absolute field magnitude, results were additionally reported as a normalized field enhancement factor, η = |E|/|E0|, where E0 is the background field obtained from the corresponding control model without SWCNTs under the same boundary conditions.

Cell culture and differentiation

All cell lines (HUVEC, CTX TNA2, Jurkat, NK-92MI, Ramos, and SH-SY5Y) were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures and maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. HUVECs were cultured in EGM-2 BulletKit on fibronectin-coated vessels, while CTX TNA2, Jurkat, Ramos, and SH-SY5Y cells were maintained in DMEM, RPMI 1640, or DMEM/F12 basal media, respectively, supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. NK-92MI cells were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 12.5% FBS, 12.5% horse serum, 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and recombinant human interleukin-2. For neuronal differentiation, SH-SY5Y cells were sequentially induced with 10 μM all-trans retinoic acid (5 to 7 days) followed by brain-derived neurotrophic factor (50 ng/ml; 5 to 7 days) in low-serum medium. All cells were routinely passaged at 80% confluence or appropriate suspension densities and confirmed mycoplasma free.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise noted, data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0. Normality and variance homogeneity were assessed via Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s/Bartlett’s tests, respectively. Two-group comparisons were analyzed using unpaired Student’s t test, while multiple-group comparisons used one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post hoc correction. Pearson correlation was used for association analysis, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.
