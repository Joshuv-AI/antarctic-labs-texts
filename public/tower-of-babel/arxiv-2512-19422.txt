. Thus by (i) and (ii) we see that
\alpha\mathcal{R}^{*}\delta\mathcal{L}^{*}\gamma\mathcal{R}^{*}\beta
.

Similarly, define
\delta=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}n-p+{1}&\cdots&n\\
a_{1}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{array}\right)
and
\gamma=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}n-p+1&\cdots&n\\
b_{1}&\cdots&b_{p}\end{array}\right)
. Clearly,
\delta
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
. Moreover, notice that
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\delta
,
\mathop{\rm ker}\nolimits\,\delta=\mathop{\rm ker}\nolimits\,\gamma
,
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\gamma=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\beta
.

Thus by (i) and (ii) we have
\alpha\mathcal{L}^{*}\delta\mathcal{R}^{*}\gamma\mathcal{L}^{*}\beta
. Hence, by (
[
8
]
, Proposition 1.5.11) it follows that
\alpha\mathcal{D}^{*}\beta
. The proof is now complete.

∎

Lemma 2.12
.

On the Schröder semigroup
\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}

(
n\geq 4
)
, we have
\mathcal{D}^{*}=\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}=\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}
.

Proof.

The sufficiency follows from the converse of the proof of (iv) in the above theorem, while for the necessity, we have to prove that
\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}\neq\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}
. Take

\alpha=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2&3\\
{2}&3\end{array}\right)\text{ and }\beta=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2&4\\
{2}&4\end{array}\right).

Now define
\delta=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}2&4\\
{2}&3\end{array}\right).
Then clearly
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\delta
and
\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\delta=\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\beta
, and so
\alpha\mathcal{L}^{*}\delta\mathcal{R}^{*}\beta
. i.e.,
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}
.

On the other hand if we have
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}
, then it means there must exist
\gamma\in\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
such that
\alpha\mathcal{R}^{*}\gamma\mathcal{L}^{*}\beta
. However, this means that
\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha=\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\gamma=\{2,3\}
and
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\gamma=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\beta=\{2,4\}
, which is impossible. The result now follows.
∎

Lemma 2.13
.

On the semigroups
{RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p)
and
K(n,p)
(

\mathcal{D}^{*}=\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}=\mathcal{L}^{*}\circ\mathcal{R}^{*}\circ\mathcal{L}^{*}.

Proof.

The proof is the same as the proof of the above lemma.

Lemma 2.14
.

The Schröder semigroup
\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
is not left abundant for all

.

Proof.

Consider
\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}2\\
1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}\ \text{for all }n\geq 2

\mathcal{L}^{*}-\,class
of

{L^{*}}_{\alpha}=\Bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}2\\
1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\{2,3\}\\
1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\{2,3,4\}\\
1\end{pmatrix},\dots,\begin{pmatrix}\{2,3,\dots,n\}\\

3
Rank properties

For a semigroup
S
and a nonempty subset of
S
say
A
, the
smallest subsemigroup
of
S
that contains
A
is denoted by
\langle A\rangle
and is referred to
as subsemigroup generated by
A
. If
A
is a finite subset of
S
such that
\langle A\rangle
equals
S
, then
S
is called a
finitely-generated semigroup
. The
rank
of a finitely generated semigroup
S
is defined and denoted as:

\text{rank}(S)=\min\{|A|:\langle A\rangle=S\}.

If the set
A
consists only of idempotents in
S
, then
S
is said to be an
idempotent-generated semigroup
(equivalently, a
semiband
), and the idempotent-rank is denoted by
\text{idrank}(S)
. For a more detailed explanation of the rank properties of a semigroup, we refer the reader to
[
10
,
11
]
. There are various classes of transformation semigroups, whose ranks have been investigated, see for example
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
25
,
26
]
. In
[
26
]
, the ranks of the Schröder monoids
\mathcal{LS}_{n}
and
\mathcal{SS}_{n}
, as well as the ranks of their respective certain two-sided ideals and their Rees quotients, have been investigated. In fact, these semigroups have been shown to be idempotent-generated, and their ranks have been investigated as well. Our aim is to compute the rank of the two-sided ideal
K(n,p)
of the Schröder semigroup
\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
, thereby obtaining the rank of
\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
as a special case. Firstly, we note the following well-known result about decreasing maps
[
15
,
22
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all order decreasing partial maps
\alpha
and
\beta
on
A\subseteq[n]
,
F(\alpha\beta)=F(\alpha)\cap F(\beta)=F(\beta\alpha)
.

Proof.

If
\alpha
or
\beta
is zero (i.e., the empty map), the result follows. When
\alpha
and
\beta
are nonzero order decreasing partial maps, the proof is the same as the proof of Lemma 2. 1. 3 in
[
22
]
.
∎

We initiate our examination of the rank properties of the semigroup
S\in\{K(n,p),\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}\}
by first introducing the following definition about injective elements in
J^{*}_{p}
for any
1\leq p\leq n-1
.

Definition 3.2
.

An injective map
\alpha
in
J^{*}_{p}
is said to be a
requisite element
if it is of the form:

\alpha_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}2&\cdots&i&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\\
1&\cdots&i-1&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix},

where
1<i<a_{i}<a_{i+1}<\cdots<a_{p}\leq n
.

Remark 3.3
.

If
\alpha_{i}
is a requisite element, then observe that for each
1\leq i\leq p
,

\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha_{i}=\{2,\ldots,i,a_{i},\dots,a_{p}\}=\{2,\ldots,i\}\cup F(\alpha_{i}).

Moreover,
\alpha_{i}
is unique in
L^{*}_{\alpha_{i}}
, in the sense that no two
\alpha_{i}
’s belong to the same
\mathcal{L}^{*}-
class. However, an
\mathcal{R}^{*}-
class may contain more than one requisite element. In fact, in
J^{*}_{n-1}
all the
n-1
requisite elements belong to a single
\mathcal{R}^{*}-
class.

We immediately have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
\alpha_{i}
is the unique requisite element in
L^{*}_{\alpha}
in
J_{p}^{*}

(
1\leq p\leq n-1
)
, then there exists
\beta\in R^{*}_{\alpha}
such that
\alpha=\beta\alpha_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{i}
be the unique requisite element in
L^{*}_{\alpha}
in
J^{*}_{p}
, where
\alpha
is as expressed in (
6
). Now since
\alpha_{i}\in L^{*}_{\alpha}
then
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha_{i}
, and so
a_{j}=j
for all
1\leq j\leq i-1
, so that
\alpha
and
\alpha_{i}
are:

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i-1}&A_{i}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
1&\cdots&i-1&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\alpha_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}2&\cdots&i&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\\
1&\cdots&i-1&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix},

respectively.
By order preservedness property, it is clear that
\min A_{j}<\min A_{j+1}
for all
1\leq j\leq i-1
, and since
2\leq\min A_{1}
, it follows that
j+1\leq\min A_{j}
. Thus, the map
\beta
defined as:

\beta=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i-1}&A_{i}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
2&\cdots&i&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}.

Furthermore, notice that
\mathop{\rm ker}\nolimits\,\alpha=\mathop{\rm ker}\nolimits\,\beta
, and so
\beta\in R^{*}_{\alpha}
. Clearly,
\beta\alpha_{i}=\alpha
.

The proof is now complete.
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
be as expressed in (
6
). Then

(i)

if
a_{1}\neq 1
, then
\alpha
is idempotent-generated;

(ii)

if
a_{1}=1
, then
\alpha
is a product of idempotents and the unique requisite element in
L^{*}_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
be as expressed in (
6
).

(i.)
Suppose
a_{1}\neq 1
and let
U=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}:\,1\not\in\,\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha\}
. Then it is not difficult to see that
U
is a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
, which is isomorphic to
\mathcal{LS}_{n-1}
. Hence by [
[
26
]
, Lemma 3.2],
U
is idempotent generated.

(ii.)
Now suppose
a_{1}=1
. Thus, by Lemma
3.4

\alpha
can be expressed as

\alpha=\beta\alpha_{i},

for some
\beta\in R^{*}_{\alpha}
, where
\alpha_{i}
is the unique requisite element in
L^{*}_{\alpha}
. To be precise,

\beta=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{i-1}&A_{i}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
2&\cdots&i&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\alpha_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}2&\cdots&i&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\\
1&\cdots&i-1&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix}.

Notice that in the
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\beta
,
a_{1}=2\neq 1
, and so
\beta\in U
. Thus, by (i)
\beta
is idempotent-generated, as stipulated.
∎

Consequently, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.6
.

The semigroup
\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
is generated by its idempotents and requisite elements.

Notice that in the proof of Theorem
3.5
,
|\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha|=h(\alpha)=h(\beta)=h(\alpha_{i})=p
for all
1\leq i\leq p
. Based on this, we have the following result.

Lemma 3.7
.

Every element in
\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
of height
p
can be expressed as a product of idempotents and requisite elements in
\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
, each of height
p
.

Now let
M(p)=\{\alpha\in{RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p):\alpha\text{ is a requisite element}\}
and
E({RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p)\setminus\{0\})
be the collection of all nonzero idempotents in
{RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p)
. Then we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.8
.

For
1\leq p\leq n-1
, we have

(i)

|M(p)|=\binom{n-1}{p-1}
;

(ii)

|E({RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p))\setminus\{0\}|=\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n-1}{\binom{n-1}{r}}{\binom{r-1}{p-1}}
.

Proof.

(i.)
Given that
1
must be included in every image set that contains
p
images, we can then choose the remaining
p-1
images from the available
n-1
elements in
\binom{n-1}{p-1}
distinct ways, as stipulated.

(ii.)
The result follows from Remark
2.19
.
∎

Now, let
G(p)=M(p)\cup(E({RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p))\setminus\{0\})
. The next result shows that the subset
G(p)
of
{RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p)
is the minimum generating set of
{RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p)\setminus\{0\}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\alpha
,
\beta
be elements in
{RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p)\setminus\{0\}
. Then
\alpha\beta\in G(p)
if and only if
\alpha,\,\beta\in G(p)
and
\alpha\beta=\alpha
or
\alpha\beta=\beta
.

Proof.

Suppose
\alpha\beta\in G(p)
. Thus, either
\alpha\beta\in E({RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p)\setminus\{0\})
or
\alpha\beta\in M(p).
We consider the two cases separately.

Case i.
Suppose
\alpha\beta\in E({RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p)\setminus\{0\})
.
Then

p=f(\alpha\beta)\leq f(\alpha)\leq|\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha|=p,

p=f(\alpha\beta)\leq f(\beta)\leq|\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\beta|=p.

This ensures that

F(\alpha)=F(\alpha\beta)=F(\beta),

and so
\alpha,\,\beta\in E({RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p)\setminus\{0\})\subset G(p)
, which implies that
\alpha,\,\beta\in G(p)
and
\alpha\beta=\alpha
.

Case ii.
Now suppose
\alpha\beta\in M(p)
. Thus
\alpha\beta
is a requisite element which has the form

\alpha\beta=\begin{pmatrix}2&\cdots&i&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\\
1&\cdots&i-1&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix},

where
1<i<a_{i}<a_{i+1}<\cdots<a_{p}\leq n
. This means that
\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha=\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha\beta
and
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\beta=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha\beta
. Thus,

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}2&\cdots&i&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\\
2\alpha&\cdots&i\alpha&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\beta=\begin{pmatrix}2\alpha&\cdots&i\alpha&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\\
1&\cdots&i-1&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix},

since
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha=\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\beta
.
The claim here is that
\alpha
must be an idempotent. Notice that either
2\alpha=1
or
2\alpha=2
. In the former, we see that
1=2\alpha\in\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\beta
which is a contradiction. Therefore
2\alpha=2
which implies
j\alpha=j
for all
2\leq j\leq i
. Thus
\alpha
is an idempotent, that is,

\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}2&\cdots&i&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\\
2&\cdots&i&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\beta=\begin{pmatrix}2&\cdots&i&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\\
1&\cdots&i-1&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,
\beta\in M(p)\subset G(p)
and
\alpha\in E({RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p)\setminus\{0\})\subset G(p)
and also
\alpha\beta=\beta.

The converse is obvious.
∎

The proof follows from Lemmas
3.9
&
3.8
.

and its two sided ideal

. Now for

be the collection of all requisite elements in

, and let

Then we have the following lemmas.

Lemma 3.11
.

For

,
|G(p)|=\binom{n-1}{p-1}+\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n-1}{\binom{n-1}{r}}{\binom{r-1}{p-1}}

.

Lemma 3.12
.

For
0\leq p\leq n-3
,
J^{*}_{p}\subset\langle J^{*}_{p+1}\rangle
. In other words, if
\alpha\in J^{*}_{p}
then
\alpha\in\langle J^{*}_{p+1}\rangle
for
1\leq p\leq n-3
.

Proof.

Using Theorem
3.5
, it suffices to prove that every element in
G(p)
can be expressed as a product of elements in
G(p+1)
. That is to say every idempotent of height
p
can be expressed as product of idempotents of height
p+1
, and every requisite element, can be expressed as product of requisite elements of height
p+1
. Thus, we consider the elements of
E(J^{*}_{p})
and
M(p)
separately.

i.
The elements in
E(J^{*}_{p})
:

Let
\epsilon\in E(J^{*}_{p})
be expressed as:

\epsilon=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{p}\\
t_{1}&\cdots&t_{p}\end{pmatrix},

where
t_{i}=\min A_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq p
. Notice that since
1\not\in\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\epsilon
, then
1\not\in A_{1}
, and so
t_{1}\neq 1
. This implies that
\epsilon\in E(U)
, where
U=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}:\,1\not\in\,\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha\}
, which is isomorphic to the large Schröder monoid
\mathcal{LS}_{n-1}
, and so, the proof follows from [
[
26
]
, Lemma 3.7].

ii.
The elements in
M(p)
:

Let
\alpha_{i}
be a requisite element of height
p
, which has the form:

\alpha_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}2&\cdots&i&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\\
1&\cdots&i-1&a_{i}&\cdots&a_{p}\end{pmatrix},

where
1<i<a_{i}<a_{i+1}<\cdots<a_{p}\leq n
.
Now, since
p\leq n-3
, it implies that
(\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha_{i}\cup\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha_{i})^{c}
contains at least two elements, specifically
c
and
d
. We may suppose without loss of generality that,
d<c
.
Now let
A=\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha_{i}\cup\{d\}
and
B=\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha_{i}\cup\{c\}
. Now define
\beta
and
\gamma
as follows:

For
x\in A
and
y\in B

x\beta=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x,&\hbox{if $x\neq d$;}\\
d,&\hbox{if $x=d$}\end{array}\right.,

and

y\gamma=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y\alpha_{i},&\hbox{if $y\neq c$;}\\
c,&\hbox{if $y=c$}\end{array}\right..

Notice that
\beta\in E(J^{*}_{p+1})\subset G(p+1)
, and it is not difficult to see that
\gamma
is a requisite element in
M(p+1)\subset G(p+1)
. One can now easily show that
\alpha_{i}=\beta\gamma
.

The proof of the lemma is now complete.
∎

Consequently, we have the following result.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
K(n,p)
be as defined in (
4
). Then for
1\leq p\leq n-2
, we have the

\text{rank }K(n,p)=\binom{n-1}{p-1}+\sum\limits_{r=p}^{n-1}{\binom{n-1}{r}}{\binom{r-1}{p-1}}.

Proof.

Notice that by Lemma
3.12
,
\langle J^{*}_{p}\rangle=K(n,p)
for all
1\leq p\leq n-2
. Notice also that,
\langle E({RSS}_{n}^{\prime}(p)\setminus\{0\})\cup M(p)\rangle=J^{*}_{p}
. The result now follows from Theorem
3.10
.
∎

It is important to note that Lemma
3.12
does not cover the case
p=n-2
, meaning that the assertion
J^{*}_{n-2}\subset\langle J^{*}_{n-1}\rangle
is not true. This can be demonstrated by first noting that every element
\alpha
in
J^{*}_{n-1}
is an injective map, because
\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha=[n]\setminus\{1\}
. However, in
J^{*}_{n-2}
there are maps which are not necessarily injective. For example, a map
\alpha
with
\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,\alpha=[n]\setminus\{1\}
and
|\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,\alpha|=n-2
, and cannot be generated by injective maps, since injective maps alone can only generate injective maps. Therefore, we need to investigate the generating set of
J^{*}_{n-1}
, and its relationship with
G(n-2)
.

The elements of
J^{*}_{n-1}
are obviously injective, isotone and decreasing maps from
[n]\setminus\{1\}
into
[n]
, and are of two types:
requisite elements
\alpha_{i}
of the form

\alpha_{i}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}{2}&3&\cdots&i-1&i&i+1&\cdots&n\\
{1}&2&\cdots&i-2&i-1&i+1&\cdots&n\end{array}\right)~~(2\leq i\leq n),

(10)

and the unique idempotent

\epsilon=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}{2}&\cdots&n\\
{2}&\cdots&n\end{array}\right),

which is the unique left identity on
\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
, but not an identity. Perhaps we may call the semigroup
\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
, a
left monoid.
It is also clear that
J^{*}_{n-1}
has only 1
\mathcal{R}^{*}-
class and
n

\mathcal{L}^{*}-
classes. Obviously, there are
n-1

\mathcal{L}^{*}-
classes each containing a unique requisite element. Thus,
G(n-1)=M(n-1)\cup\{\epsilon\}=J^{*}_{n-1}
, and so
|G(n-1)|=n
. We now have the following:

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
\alpha
,
\beta
be elements in
\langle J^{*}_{n-1}\rangle
. Then
\alpha\beta\in G(n-1)
if and only if
\alpha,\,\beta\in G(n-1)
and
\alpha\beta=\alpha
or
\alpha\beta=\beta
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Lemma
3.9
.
∎

Lemma 3.15
.

The rank
\langle J^{*}_{n-1}\rangle=n
.

Proof.

The result follows from Lemma
3.14
and the fact that
|G(n-1)|=|J^{*}_{n-1}|
.
∎

The elements of
M(n-2)
are generally of the form:

{\alpha}_{i,k}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}{2}&\cdots&i&i+1&\cdots&k-1&k+1&\cdots&n\\
{1}&\cdots&i-1&i+1&\cdots&k-1&k+1&\cdots&n\end{array}\right)\text{ }(2\leq i<k\leq n).

(11)

Notice that
1,\,k\not\in\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,{\alpha}_{i,k}
and
i,k\not\in\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,{\alpha}_{k,i}
. Also, the element

{\epsilon}_{1,k}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}{2}&\cdots&k-1&k+1&\cdots&n\\
{2}&\cdots&k-1&k+1&\cdots&n\end{array}\right)

(12)

is a partial identity in
G(n-2)
for all
2\leq k\leq n
. Notice also that
1,\,k\not\in\mathop{\rm Dom}\nolimits\,{\epsilon}_{1,k}
and
1,k\not\in\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\,{\epsilon}_{1,k}
.

The subsequent lemma demonstrates that the requisite elements together with the partial identity
\epsilon_{1,2}
in
G(n-2)
are not required to be included in any minimal generating set of
\langle J^{*}_{n-2}\cup J^{*}_{n-1}\rangle
. However, the requisite elements in
G(n-1)
suffice to be included in any generating set of
\langle J^{*}_{n-2}\cup J^{*}_{n-1}\rangle
.

Lemma 3.16
.

\langle G(n-2)\rangle\subseteq\langle\left(G(n-2)\setminus(M(n-2)\cup\{\epsilon_{1,2}\})\right)\cup G(n-1)\rangle=\langle J^{*}_{n-2}\cup J^{*}_{n-1}\rangle=\mathcal{SS}^{\prime}_{n}
.

Proof.

It is enough to show that
M(n-2)\cup\{\epsilon_{1,2}\}\subset\langle G(n-1)\cup(E(J^{*}_{n-2})\setminus\{\epsilon_{1,2}\})\rangle
.
Now let
{\alpha}_{i,k}\in M(n-2)
be as expressed in (
11
). So, take the idempotent
{\epsilon}_{1,k}\in G(n-2)
and
\alpha_{i}\in G(n-1)
as expressed in (
12
) & (
10
), respectively; and observe that for any
i<k
, we have:

\displaystyle{\epsilon}_{1,k}\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}{2}&\cdots&i&i+1&\cdots&k-1&k+1&\cdots&n\\
{2}&\cdots&i&i+1&\cdots&k-1&k+1&\cdots&n\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}{2}&3&\cdots&i&i+1&\cdots&n\\
{1}&2&\cdots&i-1&i+1&\cdots&n\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}{2}&\cdots&i&i+1&\cdots&k-1&k+1&\cdots&n\\
{1}&\cdots&i-1&i+1&\cdots&k-1&k+1&\cdots&n\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle={\alpha}_{i,k}.

Similarly, observe that if
i=2
, then

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}{2}&3&\cdots&n\\
{1}&3&\cdots&n\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}{2}&3&\cdots&n\\
{1}&3&\cdots&n\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{3}&\cdots&n\\
{3}&\cdots&n\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle={\epsilon}_{1,2}.

The result now follows.
∎

Finally, we conclude the paper with the following result:

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
\mathcal{SS}_{n}^{\prime}
be as defined in (
3
). Then the rank
\mathcal{SS}_{n}^{\prime}=3n-4
.

Proof.

Clearly
(G(n-2)\setminus(M(n-2)\cup\{\epsilon_{1,2}\}))\cup G(n-1)
is the minimum generating set of
\langle J^{*}_{n-2}\cup J^{*}_{n-1}\rangle=\mathcal{SS}_{n}^{\prime}
, and so by Lemmas
3.15
&
3.8
we see that

\displaystyle\text{ rank }\mathcal{SS}_{n}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=|G(n-2)|-|M(n-2)|-1+|G(n-1)|

\displaystyle=\binom{n-1}{n-2}+\sum\limits_{r=n-2}^{n-1}{\binom{n-1}{r}}{\binom{r-1}{n-3}}-\binom{n-1}{n-2}-1+n

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{r=n-2}^{n-1}{\binom{n-1}{r}}{\binom{r-1}{n-3}}+n-1=3n-4,

as stipulated.
∎
