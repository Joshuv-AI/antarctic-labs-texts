The effect of mass shootings on voter turnout in the United States
Authors: Kelsey Shoub, Kevin Morris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mass shootings occur with horrifying frequency in the United States. Many point to electoral politics as a potential path to change this. Here, we test whether mass shootings increase electoral participation using the most granular dataset to date. We draw information on mass shootings from the Gun Violence Archive and estimate national block group-level turnout using four snapshots of the national voter file. Using a regression discontinuity in time design, we find that mass shootings mobilize local voters (especially in heavily Democratic areas) but do not shift presidential vote choices. Furthermore, we show that pre-election mass shootings were associated with increased support for a 2016 Californian gun reform ballot initiative. Our results show the (geographically bounded) salience of mass shootings for local political engagement and ballot reform efforts.

INTRODUCTION

Mass shootings, including school shootings, occur with horrifying frequency in the United States, with more than 400 mass shootings occurring on average each year since 2014 according to the Gun Violence Archive (GVA). Not only do such events irreparably alter the lives of those who survived, their loved ones, and the loved ones of those who lost their lives, but they also have a deep impact on their local communities more broadly. Such tragedies affect teen mental health (1), the emotions of those in the surrounding area (2), and the public’s nonvoting political and civic engagement, such as seeking information on gun violence (3). Further, there is evidence that opinions on gun control policies change after mass shootings (4, 5), at least temporarily, with evidence pointing to such changes being conditional on one’s previous political orientation (6, 7). Expanding on and building from these previous studies, we focus on how and whether Americans turn to a tool uniquely situated to effectuate policy change in a liberal democracy: namely, the ballot.

Specifically, we ask whether mass shootings affect Americans’ turnout and, separately, for whom—or what—they vote. The latter question(s) is particularly interesting given that mass shootings could shift aggregate vote shares through two mechanisms: On the one hand, they might be especially mobilizing for specific groups, such as Democrats; on the other hand, mass shootings could cause minds and opinions (and, therefore, electoral preferences) to change. With respect to this final mechanism, there is a question of whether a potential change of opinion carries all the way to a change in party support, which is particularly sticky, or whether it is isolated to specific, closely related, policies. In turn, this has implications for those seeking policy changes to address mass shootings around what types of approaches might be most effective.

Why might mass shootings affect voter turnout? Theories of policy feedback, specifically around community contact, provide some clues. Developed in conversation with studies of personal and proximal contact with the criminal legal system [e.g., (8–10)], theories of policy feedback [e.g., (11–13)], and place-based mobilization (14), community contact is “diffuse contact an individual has with the police via community incidents, word of mouth, and/or the media,” which is “mediated through an individual’s relationship with their community” [(15), p. 460]. For community contact to (potentially) be translated into action, four conditions need to be met: (a) They are not universally experienced, but rather those residing in specific geographic areas experience the effects of a policy or event, while those in geographically distinct areas do not—at least not in the same way; (b) not all members of the community have direct personal or proximal contact with the event or policy; (c) a narrative exists that links the event or policy-targeted government (in)action and decision-making; and (d) that narrative—or one closely related to it—communicates a blueprint for responsive action, likely promoted by a social movement and/or interest group. Previous studies have examined this with respect to police killings [e.g., (15–17)], local police surveillance (18), and school closures in Chicago (14). In each case, narratives exist that tie government decisions, policies, and/or actions to an event or outcome. In turn, these studies show that the public’s behavior changes following events or policy changes that are felt throughout their local communities: Voter turnout increases [e.g., (15, 17)], who and/or what they vote for shifts [e.g., (15, 14)], and the rates at which they reach out to government change [e.g., (16)].

For the community contact framework to apply to mass shootings, we first must establish that a narrative exists that ties an event to government (in)action along with some pathway between understanding mass shootings as, at least in part, the result of government (in)action and voting in response. With respect to school shootings and public mass shootings more generally, narratives and frames placing the blame of the frequent occurrence of mass shootings on government have increased. Some point to a lack of access to mental health care, others point to a need for stricter gun control, and many point to both as root causes [e.g., (19, 20)]. Furthermore, during the last decade, gun control advocacy groups, such as Everytown for Gun Safety and the Brady Campaign to Prevent Gun Violence, have not only lobbied for policy change but also urged the public to register and turnout to vote, as well as cast their ballot for gun control measures and related candidates (see https://www.everytown.org/vrg-tier3cto6-p/; https://elections.bradyunited.org/take-action). A reflection of these changes can be seen in the composition of letters written to the editor in newspapers around gun control, where more letters on this topic have been written over time and the balance has shifted toward a majority of letters being “anti-gun” rather than “pro-gun” since about 2010 (21). Together, this means that a narrative exists connecting government policy—or lack thereof—to shootings such that we expect to see an increase in voter turnout following a mass public shooting.

We should note that we are not the first to ask whether shootings affect turnout, as voting is a crucial way the public can make their voice heard. A small number of previous studies have examined potential turnout effects at the county level, finding null effects across studies (22–24). However, some have documented an increase in Democratic vote share following mass shootings (23, 24), with a more recent paper arguing that those studies mis-specified their models, such that when corrected there is no relationship (25). But a null effect, measured at the county level, does not necessarily mean there is no effect: We argue that if there are effects, they are most likely to be observed at a more granular, sub-county level. Previous studies looking at the effects of school shootings on student mental health (1) and the effects of mass shootings on emotions (2), highlight that effects are highly localized. Similarly, other work examining proximity to other negative events or policy changes and turnout, which find effects, uses more granular estimates—sub-county estimates—either at the Census block group level or by voting precinct (14–16). Simply because effects are localized does not mean they do not matter; rather, they contextualize the effects of elections, such as whether and when we might be likely to see reform of different types occur.

To assess the impact of public mass shootings on local voter turnout, we compile the most fine-grained dataset yet brought to bear on this question. We draw on almost a half-billion individual-level voter turnout records (aggregated to the block group-level) from the 2014–2020 federal elections and information on public mass shootings across the United States from the GVA. For reference, the median county has more than 26,000 residents, while the median block group has closer to 1300 residents. This level of granularity is crucial as it allows us to examine sub-county dynamics, which theory (discussed above) and previous work on related topics [e.g., (1, 2, 14–16)] highlight as critical for associated tests. Using these block group data, we then leverage the as-if random timing of public mass shootings around election day in a regression discontinuity in time (RDiT) framework to test whether shootings affected turnout in the areas close to a shooting in the days leading up to the 2016 and 2020 presidential elections. We nonparametrically select a bandwidth around the election day cut point (26) and identify some 806 block groups within a half-mile of a shooting occurring in the 51 days before an election and 388 in the 51 days after. Putting this into perspective, more than a million Americans lived in a block group that was within a half-mile of a mass shooting in the 51 days leading up to either the 2016 or 2020 election. We find that mass shootings increased turnout dramatically at the local level. By the time we incorporate block groups 10 miles away from a shooting, however, our effects drop to zero, implying highly geographically constrained turnout effects.

Extending beyond simply whether turnout changes, we examine in what ways it might change. More specifically, we wonder whether mass shootings make it more likely that candidates supporting gun reform or gun reform ballot initiatives prevail at the ballot box. There are two pathways by which mass shootings might influence election outcomes. They might change for whom (or, in the case of ballot initiatives, for what) a voter casts her ballot by increasing the salience and importance of gun reform (27, 28); they might also selectively mobilize individuals already disposed to support a given candidate or initiative (29, 30). Further, within potential changes in who or what voters might cast their ballot for, it is possible that we only observe a shift when specific policies are considered, such as ballot initiatives, as previous research indicates that abstract and general opinions around gun control and reform are both polarized and ossified but that there can be general (31–33), bipartisan support for specific proposals (34, 35). To that end, there are three extensions we pursue. First, we test whether mass shootings are associated with shifts in presidential candidates’ vote shares at the precinct level; this, we argue, would provide support for the idea that mass shootings lead voters to select a different candidate. We fail to uncover any support for the hypothesis that mass shootings systematically shift voters toward one candidate or another in presidential contests.

Second, we ask whether mass shootings are especially mobilizing in neighborhoods where we expect narratives about the need for an increased role for government in gun control are most likely (that is, where voters already overwhelmingly support Democratic candidates). Here, we have clear results: The effects of mass shootings dramatically increase turnout in the most heavily Democratic areas, but have hardly any effect on turnout in places where Donald Trump won more than 20% of the vote.

Finally, we leverage a ballot initiative from California in 2016 to directly test whether voters were more likely to support background checks when exposed to a mass shooting shortly before the election. While we lack the statistical power to make causal claims in this context, we do find evidence that the ballot initiative garnered more support in precincts near pre-election mass shootings than in those near post-election ones, ceteris paribus. The same was not true of other liberal ballot initiatives that year. Our results are thus highly consistent with a causal narrative in this test.

In short, we find that voter engagement increases in presidential election years in the areas immediately surrounding where a mass shooting occurred. This provides important context for the null effects uncovered in previous studies (22–24); rather than mass shootings having no effect on turnout, the effects are just too geographically bounded to be identified in county-level models. While we find no evidence that voters systematically shift toward one party’s presidential candidate, we do find that the turnout effects are concentrated in heavily Democratic-leaning areas, leading us to conclude that mass shootings, on net, likely (marginally) benefit Democratic candidates. That pre-election shootings are associated with higher support for gun reform initiatives, however, indicate that mass shootings can perhaps shift voters’ policy preferences. Additionally, our findings highlight that geographic and temporal proximity play a key role, at least with respect to turnout: Only those that are closest change their behavior, and our RDiT design can estimate effects only for areas near mass shootings shortly before an election; there may be other longer-term effects as well. Practically, this implies that there may be greater local electoral will for change following mass shootings such that local pushes for change after a mass shooting—to the extent feasible—may be more successful than national or state approaches, and greater will to pass specific ballot initiatives or gun reform policies than to support Democratic candidates. Moreover, many politically relevant units like city council districts are geographically compact; the effects we uncover here indicate that although mass shootings might not influence countywide results, they might nevertheless have implications for local elections.

RESULTS

Study 1: Changes in voter turnout?

Throughout this project, we rely on data from the GVA to identify all public mass shootings, and repeated snapshots of the national voter file data from L2 Political. For more detailed information about these data, please see Materials and Methods. As we mentioned above, gun violence and mass shootings are widespread in the United States. Figure 1 makes this clear: In the 6 months on either side of the 2020 election, there were 693 mass shootings in 39 states. We show the analogous map for the 2016 election in the Supplementary Materials.

Mass shootings within 6 months of 2020 election.

We use the estimated turnout for each block group in the country in 2016 and 2020 to test the effects of mass shootings on turnout. We expect effects to be especially large in areas close to a mass shooting, and for this effect to decay as we look at areas further away. We use an RDiT framework, which relies on the assumption that, within a narrow window of dates, the timing of mass shootings is effectively random. Put differently, we assume that block groups very near a shooting before an election are similar to those very near one after the election—and that the election itself was unrelated to the timing of the shooting. The block groups near a pre-election mass shooting are considered “treated” (the mass shooting might have influenced the political behavior in these places), while those near a post-election shooting are considered “controls.” Block groups near shootings before and after an election are considered treated. Since a mass shooting that occurs after election day cannot influence turnout, any discontinuity in turnout between the pre- and post-election block groups can be attributed to the public mass shooting.

Figure 2 presents an initial look at the potential relationship between mass shootings and voter turnout. It shows a binned scatterplot of the full set of the data, where light gray dots indicate block groups within a half-mile of a public mass shooting, and the red line represents a linear function fit to the data on either side of the cut point (i.e., election day). We use a local polynomial of 1 and adjust for block group racial characteristics, median income, median age, population density, and education. We also include an election-year dummy and adjust for each block group’s turnout in the midterm election 2 years prior [for more on the use of covariates in regression discontinuity designs (RDDs), please see the Supplementary Materials]. This figure shows that turnout is higher among block groups that are treated (i.e., experience a mass shooting) in the days leading up to the election, with a visible discontinuity. However, the figure does not indicate whether this shift is statistically significant nor does it allow us to examine how the relationship might change as the radius around the mass shooting changes. It also relies on an arbitrary bandwidth of 6 months around the election that is not driven by the underlying properties of the data. Although the plot does demonstrate some seasonality—mass shootings are more common in the summer and, therefore, in the pre-election period—we nevertheless remain well powered with an 82% power to detect a turnout effect of 5 percentage points (the SD of turnout for treated block groups is 18 points) (36).

Having established the likelihood of an effect on turnout in Fig. 2, we turn now to formally estimating causally identified models. We use a modern regression discontinuity approach (26), which allows for nonparametric bandwidths and correct ordinary least squares (OLS) adjustments using the same characteristics detailed in the previous paragraph, to increase statistical power and precision. As multiple block groups can be within the geographical threshold of a single shooting—i.e., multiple block groups can be treated by the same shooting—standard errors are clustered by shooting ID. In Fig. 3, we present a plot of the point estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for these models, reestimated for a range of distances from a mass shooting. At the far left, we test the discontinuity among block groups within 0.25 miles of a mass shooting before or after the election. We then gradually expand that buffer until reaching a 10-mile radius. We present the results of the robust, bias-adjusted models. Coefficients, sample sizes, and other metrics for the models presented here are available in the Supplementary Materials.

Figure 3 makes a number of things clear. First, the local average treatment effect (LATE) of a public mass shooting on turnout is geographically concentrated but dramatic. Block groups closest to shootings saw their turnout substantially increase—by almost 10 percentage points—with those slightly further away seeing turnout increasing by 3 to 4 percentage points. While geographically concentrated, they are quite large effects relative to traditional get out the vote efforts, such as mailers, phone banking, and door-to-door canvassing, which tend to have turnout effects below 4 percentage points [see (37) for meta-analyses of these interventions].

Further, this relationship begins to decay immediately; by five miles from a shooting (that is, in block groups whose centroids are approximately 5 miles from a shooting), the point estimate is not significant, and it drops to zero by the 10-mile mark. This finding provides some insights into why our findings—a statistically significant and substantively large increase in turnout following a shooting—differ from previous studies [e.g., (22–24)]. Furthermore, this radius is consistent with the effect of school shootings on student mental health, where the authors used a 5-mile radius as the definition of affected versus non-affected schools (1).

In short, we find that public mass shootings locally increase voter turnout. This differs from previous studies (22–24), indicating that these previous null results were due to an undetectable effect—not that they had identified a “true null.” We note here, and show in the Supplementary Materials, that these results are robust to different bandwidths around the cut point, to using the first difference in turnout (that is, the change in turnout from 2 years prior) as our dependent variable, to different local polynomials in the regression estimates, and to the use of different definitions of “public mass shootings” and bandwidths around election day. Further, placebo models indicate that the cut point of election day is meaningful (there is no discontinuity if we shift the cut point away from election day) and that using turnout from the previous election results in nonsignificant results (a mass shooting shortly before the 2016 election, for instance, should be and is unrelated to 2014 turnout).

Study 2: Shifting partisan mobilization, partisan support, or neither?

Up to this point, our analysis reveals increased voter turnout rates in presidential election years at the local level attributable to mass shootings. However, this does not tell us whether there are changes in the composition of who turns out to vote or how they voted. There is some debate around whether mass shootings increase Democratic vote share [(23, 24), but see (25)]. The remainder of this paper engages the question of whether this mobilization might have impacts on who is elected, or what policies are passed by ballot initiative. We begin with the question of candidates’ vote share, in this section, before turning to ballot initiatives, in the next.

By way of reminder, increased turnout attributable to mass shootings might change aggregate partisan vote shares in two distinct ways. First, mass shootings might affect who voters vote for at the ballot box, either by encouraging them to party switch or to lean one way over another if they are true independents (27). But this is a high bar to meet—partisan identities are considerably more stable than most policy preferences (38), and party-switching has notably declined in recent decades (39). This is especially true in the gun control and gun rights arena, as attitudes and behaviors have polarized over time (31, 32). Second, even if mass shootings do not shift individual vote choices, they might nevertheless have partisan implications if the turnout increases we observed in the previous section are concentrated in certain types of neighborhoods. Mass shootings might disproportionately mobilize certain groups, such as partisans of one party, either generally or when they exist in communities with each other (29). This is precisely how “community contact” is theorized to operate: When communities have narratives that allow them to link phenomena to political actors, they can be mobilized (15). If these narratives are more readily available in communities generally aligned with a particular party, that party might benefit. In the case at hand, it seems that communities sympathetic to narratives in the Democratic party platform (that the government ought to do more to regulate guns) should be especially mobilized by mass violence.

To examine these possibilities, we explore the effects of mass shootings on within-geographic-unit presidential vote share, which requires us to shift from a unit of observation at the block group level to voter precincts, as precincts are the lowest geographical level at which election returns are reported (40, 41). While precincts are often larger than block groups, they are far smaller than counties: In 2016, the median precinct had 602 ballots cast, while the median county had more than 10,000. The Voting and Election Science Team (VEST) makes election results available at the precinct level (42, 43), along with precinct geographic boundary data, allowing us to calculate precincts’ presidential Democratic vote share. Additionally, we calculate precinct demographics, used in OLS adjustments to the RDiT, as the population-weighted average of the block groups comprising the precinct. Here, we limit this analysis to the precincts in which mass shootings actually occurred. Our focus is narrowed to just those precincts where mass shootings occurred, as these are the most likely to show an effect; without a local in-precinct effect (similar to that observed in block group turnout), there is no need to test for any spatial decay in these effects.

In Fig. 4, we present the regression discontinuity plot of Democratic vote share, similar to Fig. 2 above. Figure 4 clarifies two things. First, we can see that mass shootings are overwhelmingly concentrated in Democratic-leaning areas. Hillary Clinton won 81% of the two-way vote share in precincts home to a mass shooting that occurred within a year of the 2016 election; Joe Biden’s comparable number was 79%. Second, we see no visual evidence that the Democratic candidate systematically won more (or less) of the votes in precincts near a pre-election mass shooting than near a post-election one. A formal regression discontinuity model corroborates this. The model provides a relatively precisely estimated null effect of 0.3 points (95% CI: −3.3 to 2.9). We therefore conclude that, in aggregate, mass shootings did not shift presidential vote shares within precincts.

As discussed above, within-precinct shifts are not the only way mass shootings might influence total vote shares across the electorate. If mass shootings are particularly mobilizing in neighborhoods that tend to support one party, they might increase that party’s total vote share. Similarly, even if they are uniformly mobilizing wherever they occur, but they are more likely to occur in some types of neighborhoods, they might shift vote totals. Given the highly Democratic nature of the precincts in which mass shootings occur, Fig. 4 indicates this might well be the case.

To test whether mass shootings are particularly mobilizing in areas home to certain populations, we turn back to the block group models in which turnout is our dependent variable. Here, however, we split our sample in two, based on whether the block group leaned toward Democrats or Republicans (block groups are assigned the vote share of the precinct in which their centroid falls). We split our sample at the median Democratic vote share. About 50% of block groups were in precincts where Clinton or Biden won more than 80% of the vote; thus, when we talk about “Republican-leaning” block groups, we mean this in a relative sense. Fewer than 15% of treated block groups are in precincts that Trump won.

Figure 5 breaks out the estimated treatment effects of a mass shooting, by distance, according to whether the block group was in a precinct that was more or less Democratic than average. The estimated effects of mass shootings on turnout in more Democratic areas are presented in the left-hand panel; those in less-Democratic areas are in the right-hand one. The results are clear and unambiguous: The turnout effects of mass shootings are entirely concentrated in areas where more voters support the Democratic presidential candidate. We find only very weak evidence of any mobilizing effect at all in block groups where Trump won more than 20% of the two-way vote share.

Together, we conclude that, on net, mass shootings likely marginally increase the total number of votes won by Democratic presidential candidates. We thus add considerable nuance to previous work finding a general increase in Democratic vote share (23, 24) at the county-level; we argue that this is likely not driven by within-precinct effects, but is instead due to the fact that mass shootings only appear to mobilize voters (and to mobilize them dramatically, at the local level) in neighborhoods where Democratic candidates win at least four of every five ballots that are cast.

Study 3: Shifting support for gun control reform?

Just because there was no shift in the Democratic vote share does not necessarily imply that there was no shift in support for a specific related policy. Remember, changing the party for which one votes is a high bar (38, 39, 44). However, there seems to be some bipartisan support for specific policies, even within the gun reform space (34). Anecdotally, we saw this tension in 2024, where majorities in some states—such as Arizona, Missouri, and Florida—both voted for Donald Trump (45), the Republican nominee for president, and for enshrining abortion access and rights (46), a position typically associated with the Democratic party but that provided voters a specific and targeted way to engage on an issue.

Now we turn to this question through a narrower examination of how voters cast their ballot when they could register support for a specific policy, examining whether mass shootings might be linked to calls for increased gun control. While previous studies have shown no change in Democratic (or Republican) party vote share (22, 25), others have shown that public opinion around gun control changes, temporarily and conditionally (6, 7). As such, and potentially as a reflection of those (temporary) changes in opinion, one might expect vote share on gun control initiatives to change. While one recent paper (35) indicates that a high-profile mass shooting with multiple fatalities in Washington State increased support for a gun reform ballot initiative, we know little about how or whether the “average” mass shooting shifts support (only about half of mass shootings involve a fatality, according to our analysis of the GVA data).

To examine this, we look to California in 2016 and the vote on (and approval of) Proposition 63. Prop 63 was a gun control ballot initiative that proposed mandatory background checks for ammunition purchases and a ban on large-capacity ammunition magazines (47). We expect that precincts near a mass shooting before election day in 2016 voted in favor of the ballot initiative at higher rates, other things being equal, than those precincts near a shooting after the election.

Because of a test that was only on the ballot in 2016, the relative rarity of public mass shootings, and a focus on a single state, we cannot use the same RDiT design. We continue, however, to compare small geographic units that were exposed to a shooting before the election—and, thus, when it could influence electoral behavior—to those exposed to a shooting after the election, when it of course could not influence support for the ballot initiative. In California, there were 10 mass shootings in the 2 months before the 2016 election, and 6 in the 2 months afterward. We use OLS to control for observable characteristics, and by limiting the set of precincts to ones that were exposed to a mass shooting in either the 2 months before or after the 2016 election, we also implicitly control for unobservables associated with being near a mass shooting within a single 4-month period. We thus borrow from the logic of RDDs.

While we do expect mass shootings to be related to support for gun control ballot initiatives, we do not expect them to be related to support for other ballot initiatives. We thus include three “placebo” regressions, exploring whether precincts near a mass shooting before the election supported other liberal ballot initiatives (Proposition 62, which would abolish the death penalty; Proposition 64, which would legalize recreational marijuana; and Proposition 67, which would ban single-use plastic bags) at higher rates than the precincts near a post-election mass shooting. Significant differences in these measures between the precincts near mass shootings before and after the election would indicate that they have different underlying political preferences even after accounting for the other covariates, weakening any interpretation of a relationship between mass shootings and support for Prop 63. Results shown in Table 1 support our hypothesis that precincts near pre-election mass shootings are more supportive of gun reform (but not other liberal issues) than precincts near post-election mass shootings. The effect size is fairly large: Proximity to a pre-election mass shooting was associated with a 3.5 point increase in support for the measure (the SD for support in these precincts was about 6.4 points). These results hold when controlling for both observable characteristics and unobservable characteristics associated with being near a mass shooting. Further, as we show in the Supplementary Materials, the relationship between proximity to a mass shooting and support for Prop 63 decreases as we expand the radius around the mass shootings to include precincts further away.

While we refrain from calling this relationship causal, as it can only test for a statistical relationship, our approach borrows from the RDiT framework and is consistent with a causal story. Future work should find ways to explore more credibly whether this relationship is indeed causal.

DISCUSSION

Public mass shootings have become a tragically frequent event in the United States. While previous research has shown changes in engagement with gun policy (3), public opinion (4–7), emotions (2), and mental health (1), previous work looking at voter turnout specifically found no relationship with the occurrence of a mass shooting (23, 22). However, community contact theory [e.g., (15)] tells us that we should expect to see an increase in turnout after a shooting, and that this would locally translate into changes in the vote share of related ballot initiatives. To evaluate the impact of public mass shootings on local voter turnout, and vote share of a gun control measure on the 2016 California ballot, we leverage unusually rich data on voter turnout at the block group and precinct levels, respectively.

We first show that public mass shootings occurring shortly before a general election in a presidential election year substantively and significantly increase voter turnout. However, as previous studies have shown with respect to local emotional responses (2) and affects on teen mental health following mass shootings (1), this effect is geographically limited, attenuating by approximately 5 miles from the site of a mass shooting. These geographic bounds explain, in part, why this granular approach identifies turnout effects, whereas previous studies that had to examine turnout at the county level found null effects (22–24). The localized nature of these effects—in line with what a community contact framework, and previous work on police killings [e.g., (15)] and school closures (14), would lead us to expect—explains the conflicting findings of this study and previous studies. Our results provide an important extension to past work, finding that mass shootings have null results on county-level turnout rates (22, 23), and indicate that mass shootings might well have implications for geographically compact elections, like those for city councils.

As we note throughout this piece, whether individuals cast a ballot is not the only question of interest. So too is whether a voter changes how they cast their ballot, or whether supporters of a particular party are disproportionately mobilized. We find no evidence of systematic shifts toward presidential candidates of a particular party at the precinct-level, undermining the hypothesis that voters’ preferences—at least with respect to candidates—shift in response to mass shootings. That said, some of this might be due to “ceiling effects”; the average mass shooting occurred in a precinct where the Democratic candidate for president won 79% of the two-way vote share. Nonetheless, we do demonstrate that turnout effects are concentrated in heavily Democratic areas; there is little evidence of a turnout effect in areas where Democrats received less than 80% of the two-way vote share. Taken as a whole, we interpret these effects as support for the notion that community contact mobilizes in areas where narratives already exist linking the local event to electoral politics (that is, where voters are already “bought into” the framing around mass shootings and elections used by Democratic party activists), but do not themselves shift partisan allegiances, at least in the short term testable in the RDiT framework.

We then ask whether proximity to a mass shooting in 2016 in California was linked to an increase in vote share for Prop 63, which instated mandatory background checks for ammunition purchases and a ban on large-capacity ammunition magazines upon passage. While this involved looking at only one ballot initiative from a single state and was not a causal test, we nevertheless uncover convincing evidence that, other things being equal, precincts near pre-election mass shootings supported the ballot initiative at higher rates than precincts near post-election shootings. Future studies should expand the number of initiatives examined and the number of states studied to test the generalizability of these results, as well as seek ways to more credibly test whether this relationship is indeed causal.

While we demonstrate a relationship between mass shootings and turnout and then vote share of a related ballot measure, we do not and cannot test the possible mechanisms through which these relationships operate. An explanation is that social movements, interest groups, civic organizations, and political parties mobilize following events such as public mass shootings. In turn, they may actively engage in voter registration and turnout efforts that lead to increases in voter turnout. Alternatively or additionally, they may simply have previously built the infrastructure to which those in local communities affected by a mass shooting then turn.

The RDiT framework allows us to explore the effect of mass shootings on turnout only in areas treated shortly before an election. Future work should explore the temporal durability of these effects, and the conditions under which they might last longer. Similarly, the RDiT approach does not allow us to take into account broader factors—like the increase in mass shootings in 2020 generally—that influence treated and control observations alike. Future work should explore how factors like the number of times a mass shooting occurs in the days, months, and years leading up to an election influence participation. We also note that registration deadlines in the United States mean that ours are likely lower-bound estimates; if there are individuals who would be spurred to political action by a mass shooting in the days leading up to the election, but were not already registered to vote (many states require voters to register at least a month before the election), registration rules might well have prevented them from casting a ballot.

Regardless, together, these results show a localized increase in turnout following a public mass shooting event that occurs shortly before a general election in a presidential election year, as well as supportive evidence that vote share on gun control initiatives is linked to such events as well. In each case, geographic proximity plays a crucial role, with only those in relatively close proximity altering their behavior. This result sheds some light on broader conversations around why there is relatively little change in gun policy across the United States. Localized results here imply that there may be greater local electoral will for change following mass shootings that dissipates as the related constituency grows—and that there might be greater will for specific change, such as through particular ballot initiatives, rather than a diffuse endorsement for abstract reform. This is in line with the work on polarization around gun reform at different levels of specificity (31, 32, 34, 35).

Finally, while our results are of course most relevant to discussions surrounding mass shootings and gun policy, they nevertheless offer some insight into the disjuncture between policy preferences and partisan vote choices. Namely, that we find no evidence of candidate switching but do find evidence consistent with a causal relationship between mass shootings and support for the gun reform ballot initiative indicates that it might be easier to convince voters to support specific policy reforms that are not aligned with their party of choice than to switch parties. Given the passage of 12 “progressive” ballot initiatives in 2024 in states carried by Trump that (among other things) raised the minimum wage, codified abortion access, and expanded workers’ sick leave (48), questions along these lines are particularly timely and deserve further attention from researchers.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Identifying public mass shootings and treated block groups

Staff at the GVA (https://www.gunviolencearchive.org/) generously provided us with a spreadsheet including every tracked shooting occurring in the 6 months before or after the 2016 and 2020 election. These data include a number of characteristics that we use in our analysis. Principally, we use their definition of mass shootings as incidents with “a minimum of four victims shot, either injured or killed, not including any shooter who may also have been killed or injured in the incident.” This definition is also used by the Mass Shooting Tracker (https://www.massshootingtracker.site/). In doing so, we draw a distinction between “mass murder” events, defined as either three-or-more or four-or-more deaths excluding the gunman, and “mass shootings” (49).

The GVA data also include a text field with fairly standardized “tags” about each shooting, which we use to identify (and exclude) shootings that were related to domestic violence, or which took place in the home. As we show in the Supplementary Materials, our results do not depend on one specific definition of a mass shooting: They are generally consistent whether we look at shootings with three victims (though the effects are unsurprisingly smaller, as mass shootings with fewer victims likely garner less attention on average) or five victims.

A more restrictive definition would also exclude gun violence linked to criminal activity, such as gang-related shootings, due to the perception that victims were perhaps not “innocent people” [(23), p. 810]. We include gang-related shootings, as these shootings are consistent with the theory of community contact, in which residents might feel themselves to be less safe because of the shooting and therefore take political action like voting, but as we show in the Supplementary Materials, our results hold when gang-related shootings are excluded.

The GVA data also include the latitudes and longitudes of shootings. We use these to measure how close each shooting is to “neighborhoods” around the country. We use Census block groups as neighborhoods. We determine how close each block group is to each shooting by measuring the geodesic distance between block groups’ geographic centroids and the incidents. Block group centroids, and shapes, come from Census Tiger shapefiles. To access those shapefiles, use the following URL: https://www.census.gov/geographies/mapping-files/time-series/geo/tiger-line-file.html. The distance between a mass shooting and a block group is considered 0 for the block group in which a shooting occurred, regardless of its distance from the centroid.

Measuring voter turnout

In recent years, measuring voter turnout using registered voter files has become increasingly common. The L2 voter file (https://www.l2-data.com/) contains information about nearly every registered voter in the country, including whether they participated in a given election and the geographic coordinates of their home residence. We use these records to estimate the number of ballots cast in each block group in the country in each year. We use snapshots following the 2014, 2016, 2018, and 2020 elections (turnout estimates from the 2014 and 2018 midterms are used as control variables for the models of 2016 and 2020 turnout, respectively). Interested researchers can purchase these files from L2 directly; we also note that the block group ballot estimates for the 2014–2020 federal elections can be found in the replication materials of (15) here: https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/DMG0XD.

We calculate turnout rates by dividing the number of ballots by the number of potential voters in each block group, estimated as the citizen voting age population (CVAP). CVAP estimates come from the US Census Bureau (https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial-census/about/voting-rights/cvap.html).

Estimating the regression discontinuity

Our central causal identification strategy relies on what is called a “regression discontinuity (in time) design” (RDiT), a specific type of RDD. RDDs rely on assumptions about the data-generating process to make causal claims, and less on parametric statistical ones, and are widely used in the social sciences (50). In all RDDs, researchers identify some “cut point,” where units on one side of the cut point are treated by some intervention, while those on the other side of the cut point are not. (For the sake of simplicity, we here describe “sharp” RDDs, though we recognize that the cut point is not always as simple as we make it sound. That said, all RDDs do leverage a cut point or threshold of some sort.) In our study, the cut point is election day: For neighborhoods near a pre-election mass shooting, the mass shooting has the potential to influence turnout rates, because election day is in the future. They are thus considered treated. Turnout rates in neighborhoods near post-election mass shootings, of course, cannot be influenced by the mass shooting, and these units are thus our controls.

We adopt the continuity framework in this project (51). This framework relies on the assumption that, in our range of dates, the function describing turnout rates would be continuous across the cut point if the treatment had no effect. This assumption, however, is not directly testable: We cannot observe a world in which the mass shootings did not occur (or in which election day was somehow postponed). Nevertheless, we can assess the plausibility of the assumption in a number of ways: We can look to see whether the function describing turnout is usually continuous by using placebo cut points. We can also test whether there are discontinuities in other characteristics of the treated and control units—things like education rates, income levels, and ages—at the cut point; if the cut point is really exogenous, as we assume, there should not be discontinuities in these measures. As we show in the Supplementary Materials, there is good reason to believe that the assumptions necessary for making causal claims using our design are very plausible.

Other demographic controls for turnout models

By making OLS adjustments in RDDs, we can improve the power and precision of our models. In our models testing the effects of mass shootings on voter turnout, we control for other block group characteristics using OLS in rdrobust::rdrobust() in R (26). These characteristics are the share of the block group that is white, Black, Latino, or Asian; median income; median age; population density; share of the population with some college education; turnout in the midterm election 2 years earlier; and an election-year dummy. With the exception of previous turnout, all block group characteristics come from the 5-year American Community Survey estimates ending with the election year. Previous turnout is calculated using the same procedure as that described in the “Measuring voter turnout” section.

Throughout our analyses, we use previous turnout in a block group as a control. This presents something of a problem, however: Block group boundaries are redrawn following the decennial census, so determining the 2018 turnout for block groups in 2020 is not possible. For this reason, throughout our analysis, we follow (15) by using the 2010–2019 block group configurations for the 2020 analyses. This means that all CVAP and demographic estimates come from the 2015–2019 ACS, rather than the 2016–2020 ACS (which would apply to the redrawn block group definitions).

National precinct models

In our models testing the effects of mass shootings on precinct-level vote shares, we rely on data from the VEST Team (42, 43). We identify precincts home to mass shootings by merging the precinct shapefiles with the geocoded GVA shootings.

Unfortunately, ACS data are not provided at the precinct-level. Precinct demographics are thus population-weighted block group estimates. Each Census block in the nation is assigned to the precinct in which its centroid falls. We then assign each block the attributes of its block group (the lowest level at which most demographic data are reported). We then take the population-weighted mean of each characteristic across the blocks that fall inside each precinct.

Finally, we note that our focus on precinct-level vote share rather than individual vote is practical decision: The secret ballot and geographically aggregated data prevent us from being able to observe individual behavior. This presents a problem of ecological inference: Our finding that precinct vote shares do not shift due to a mass shooting could be due to either true null effects or individuals being differentially moved (that is, mass shootings shift some voters toward Republicans, and others toward Democrats) in ways that cancel out. Either way, the precinct-level null results indicate that small geographies do not systematically shift toward one party or another.

Convenience voting and the potential impacts of COVID-19

In recent years more and more voters have taken advantage of “convenience” voting methods, allowing them to cast their ballot before election day. According to our analysis of the Election and Voting Administration Survey administered by the federal Election Assistance Commission, 55% of participants voted in person on election day in 2016, and that dropped to just 31% in 2020. This means that the idea of “election day” as a single day is a bit murky. However, because voters in every state could cast their ballot on election day (either at a polling place or by depositing their mail ballot in a drop box or elections office), the treatment remains theoretically viable for all voters.

These trends were exacerbated in 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic affected both the crime rate throughout the nation and the manner in which voters cast their ballots. While most “public” crime (with the exception of homicides) declined during the early months of COVID, there was an increase in domestic violence rates (52). Mass shootings similarly increased in 2020 relative to earlier years (53). This could lead to saturation effects, or other unanticipated effects. At the same time, voters changed how they cast their ballots: The “cost” of voting in person increased in 2020 for voters scared of contracting the virus, leading to a surge in early and mail voting. Because in-person election day voting would be more costly in 2020 than in 2016, we might expect to see (and, as we show in the Supplementary Materials, do see) smaller treatment effects in 2020 than in 2016. Nevertheless, these unique factors in 2020 do not threaten our central identification strategy, as there is no evidence that they affect block groups near pre-election mass shootings differently than those near post-election ones. Please see the Supplementary Materials for more on this discussion.

California ballot initiative

Information on precinct-level results comes from the California Statewide Database (see https://statewidedatabase.org/election.html). As before, we calculate distance by measuring the distance between a mass shooting and each precinct’s centroid.

We also control for other characteristics that might relate to support for gun control: median income, racial demographics, share with some college, median age, and population density. These characteristics are calculated in the same way as those for the national precinct models, described above.

We also control for the share of registered voters in each precinct who are Democrats. These figures come from the registered voter file, which include the partisan affiliation of all voters in California.

Finally, we also control for Obama’s vote share in 2012, and support for Prop 36 in 2012, which reformed the three-strike law. Determining the 2012 vote share in each 2016 precinct is not entirely straightforward. Helpfully, the Statewide Database provides cross-walks, allowing us to determine what share of each 2012 (and 2016) precinct was in each Census block. We can thus estimate the number of ballots cast for each candidate in each block. Let us imagine that Barack Obama and Mitt Romney each received 100 votes in Precinct B2012. If 70% of Precinct B2012 was in block α and 30% was in block β, we would estimate that Obama and Romney each received 70 votes in block α and 30 votes in β (to be sure, the total number of ballots each candidate received in block α or β might be higher, if parts of these blocks were also in other precincts). Now, let us imagine that 50% of block α was in Precinct C2016; we thus estimate that block α contributed 15 votes from each candidate to the vote totals in Precinct C2016.
