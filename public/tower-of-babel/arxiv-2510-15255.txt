Estimating the Number of Primes In Unusual Domains
Johnathan Cai, Ryan Diehl, William Gasarch, Ian Kim

Abstract
The Prime Number Theorem states that the number of primes in { 1 , … , x } \{1,\ldots,x\} , denoted π ⁡ ( x ) \pi(x) , is approximately x ln ⁡ ( x ) \frac{x}{\ln(x)} . In this paper, we investigate the distribution of primes for domains other than ℕ {\mathbb{N}} . First we look at 𝔸 d = { x : x ≡ 1 ( mod d ) } {\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}=\{x\colon x\equiv 1\pmod{d}\} . We give a heuristic argument to form a conjecture on the number of congruence monoid primes in 𝔸 d {\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}} that are ≤ x \leq x . We then provide empirical evidence that indicates our conjecture is close but may need some correction. Second, we do similar calculations for the Gaussian Integers. Third, we discuss the difficulty of these types of questions for quadratic extensions of 𝖹 {\sf Z} .



Keywords:
Prime Number Theorem, Gaussian Integers, Circles, and Primes.

2
Congruence Monoids

2.1
Definitions For Congruence Monoids

Def 2.1

[Prime]
Let
{\mathbb{D}}\subseteq\ {\mathbb{N}}
. A
prime
in
{\mathbb{D}}
is a number
p\in{\mathbb{D}}
such that

p>1
and the only positive divisors of
p
that lie in
{\mathbb{D}}
are
1
and
p
itself.

Def 2.2

[Congruence Monoid Prime]
Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
d\geq 2
. Define the set

{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}=\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}\colon n\equiv 1\pmod{d}\}.

An element
p\in{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}
is called a
congruence monoid prime
if
p\neq 1
and, whenever
p=ab
for some
a,b\in{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}
, then either
a=1
or
b=1
. Factorizations involving elements outside
{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}
are not considered.

Examples

Lets look at
{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$4$}}}}
.

1.

We write down the first few elements:

1,5,9,13,17,21,25,29,33,37,41,45

2.

All of the numbers that are prime in
{\mathbb{N}}
are prime in
{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$4$}}}}
.
So thats
5,13,17,29,37
.

3.

What about the numbers that are not prime in
{\mathbb{N}}
?

9 is prime. Even though
9=3\times 3
note that
3\notin{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$4$}}}}
.

21 is prime. Even though
21=3\times 7
, note that
e\notin{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$4$}}}}
.

25 is not prime since
25=5\times 5
and
5\in{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$4$}}}}
.

33 is not primes since
33=3\times 11
and
3\notin{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$4$}}}}
.

45 is not prime since
45=5\times 9
and
5,9\in{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$4$}}}}
.

4.

Note that there are numbers that are not primes in
{\mathbb{N}}
but are primes in

{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$4$}}}}
.

We want to define an analog of
\pi(x)
for
{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}
. We first carefully define
\pi(x)
.

Def 2.3

\pi(x)
is the number of elements of
\{1,\ldots,x\}
that are prime, i.e.,

\pi(x)=\#\{x\in\{1,\ldots,x\}\colon p\text{ is prime}\}.

Note that the domain of interest is
\{1,\ldots,x\}
.
We define
\pi_{d}(x)
, the analog of
\pi(x)
for
{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}
, noting that
the domain of interest is
{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}\cap\{1,\ldots,x\}
.

Def 2.4

[Congruence Monoid Prime Count]

\pi_{d}(x)
is the number of elements of
{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}\cap\{1,\ldots,x\}
that are prime in
{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}
, i.e.,

\pi_{d}(x)=\#\{x\in{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}\cap\{1,\ldots,x\}\colon p\text{ is prime in ${\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}$}\}.

2.2
Congruence Monoid Prime Estimation

How does
\pi_{d}(x)
compare to
\pi(x)
?

•

The domain for
\pi_{d}(x)
is smaller than that for
\pi(x)
.
This suggests that
\pi_{d}(x)\leq\pi(x)
.

•

There are primes in
{\mathbb{A_{\hbox{$d$}}}}
that are not primes in
{\mathbb{N}}
. Hence
this suggests that
\pi(x)\leq\pi_{d}(x)
.

Balancing these effects, we propose the estimation

\pi_{d}(x)\approx\frac{x}{d\ln(x)^{1/d}}.

Our empirical results from computational simulations support this approximation, and its accuracy can be quantified by the normalized ratio

R_{d}(x)=\frac{\pi_{d}(x)}{\,x/\bigl(d\ln(x)^{1/d}\bigr)}.

Values
R_{d}(x)\approx 1
indicate close agreement between the model and observed data.

3
Integral Domains

When studying primes in integral domains careful definitions of

unit
,
prime
, and
irreducible
are needed.

Def 3.1

An integral domain is a triple
{\mathbb{D}}=(D,+,\times)
such that
(1)
D
is a set,
(2)
+
and
\times
are maps from
D\times D
into
D
, and
(3)
(D,+,\times)
satisfies the following properties:

1.

D
is closed under
+
and
\times
.

2.

+
and
\times
are both commutative and associative.

3.

\times
is distributive over
+
. Hence, for all
a,b,c\in D
,

a\times(b+c)=a\times b+a\times c
.

4.

There exists an element
0\in D
such that, for all
a\in D
,
a+0=0+a=a
.

5.

For every
a\in D
there exists
b\in D
such that
a+b=0
. We usually
denote
b
by
-a
.

6.

There exists an element
1\in D
such that, for all
a\in D
,
a\times 1=a
.

7.

For all
a,b\in D
, if
ab=0
then either
a=0
or
b=0
.

Def 3.2

Let
{\mathbb{D}}
be an integral domain.

1.

An element
u\in{\mathbb{D}}
is a
unit
there exists
v\in{\mathbb{D}}
such that
uv=1
.
The units of
{\sf Z}
are
\{-1,1\}
.

2.

An element
p\in{\mathbb{D}}
is
prime (in
{\mathbb{D}}
)
if (a)
p
is not a unit, and
(b) if
p
divides
ab
then either
p
divides
a
or
p
divides
b
.
The primes of
{\sf Z}
are the usual primes and their negations. For example, 3 and
-7
are
both primes in
{\sf Z}
.

3.

An element
r\in{\mathbb{D}}
is
irreducible (in
{\mathbb{D}}
)
if (a)
r
is not a unit, and
(b) if
r=ab
, then either
a
or
b
is a unit.
In
{\sf Z}
irreducibles and primes are the same. This equivalence is false in some other integral
domains.

Def 3.3

Let
\alpha\notin{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then
{\sf Z}[\alpha]
is the set
\{a+b\alpha\colon a,b\in{\sf Z}\}

4
Gaussian Prime Estimation

Def 4.1

The
Gaussian Integers
is the set
{\sf Z}[i]
where
i=\sqrt{-1}
.

The Gaussian Integers are an integral domain. Hence the definitions of units, primes, and
irreducible from Definition
3.2
apply to them.
In the Gaussian integers
(a) the units are
\{1,-1,i,-i\}
, and
(b) primes and irreducibles are the same.

We want to study an analog of the Prime Number Theorem for the Gaussian Integers. Recall that the usual Prime Number Theorem is about the number of primes in

\{1,\ldots,x\}
. Since
{\mathbb{N}}
is not an integral domain, but
{\sf Z}
is, lets rewrite that as the prime number
theorem taking into account that the domain is
{\sf Z}
:

The number of primes in
\{y\in{\sf Z}\colon 0\leq|y|\leq x\}
is approximately
\frac{x}{\ln(x)}
.

We take
0\leq|y|
since in the usual Prime Number Theorem we do not count both a prime and its
negation. For example, we don’t count both 7 and
-7
as primes.

For the Gaussian Integers we need (1) a notion of size analogous to absolute value for
{\sf Z}
,
(2) a way to not count
p
,
-p
,
ip
, and
-ip
.

Def 4.2

1.

Let
a,b\in{\sf Z}
. Then the
norm
of
a+bi
is
a^{2}+b^{2}
.
This is denoted by
N(a+bi)
.
This will not be our notion of size; however,
\sqrt{N(a+bi)}
will be.

2.

Let
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The
norm circle
of radius
r
in the set

\{a+bi\colon\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\leq r\}.

Using the norm circle provides a clear and finite boundary, making it possible to study the distribution of
Gaussian primes up to a specific size, analogous to counting up to
x
in the Prime Number Theorem.
There is one more issue: we only look at
a,b\geq 0
since that avoids the problem of counting
a prime four times because of units.

Def 4.3

\pi_{G}(r)
is the number of primes in
a+bi\in{\sf Z}[i]
such that
(a)
a,b\geq 0
, and (b)
a+bi
is in the norm circle of radius
r
.

We empirically found the estimate
\pi_{G}(r)\approx\frac{r^{2}}{2\ln r}

5
Other Integral Domains

We list integral domains and discuss if analogs of the Prime Number Theorem can
be asked and what issues might arise.
Much of the information in this section is from Weintraub
[
2
]
.

1)
{\sf Z}[\sqrt{-d}]
where
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
is squarefree. The standard norm is

N(a+b\sqrt{-d})=a^{2}+db^{2}
.
There are two possible questions to ask:

•

Approximate the
number of primes of the form
a+b\sqrt{-d}
where
a,b\geq 0
and

a^{2}+db^{2}\leq r
, or

•

Approximate the
number of primes of the form
a+b\sqrt{-d}
where
a,b\geq 0
and

a^{2}+b^{2}\leq r
.

2)
{\sf Z}[\sqrt{d}]
where
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
is squarefree. Asking about primes in
this integral domain is problematic for several reasons.

2a) For some values of
d
this is the wrong question.

In Section
4
we looked at
{\sf Z}[i]
. Why not
{\sf Z}[\frac{i}{2}]
?
Lets start with
{\mathbb{Q}}(i)
. We look for an integral domain
{\mathbb{D}}
such that

{\sf Z}[i]\subseteq{\mathbb{D}}\subseteq{\mathbb{Q}}(i)
and
(roughly):
{\mathbb{Q}}
is to
{\sf Z}
as
{\mathbb{Q}}(i)
is to
{\mathbb{D}}
.

Def 5.1

Let
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
and
{\mathbb{D}}_{2}
be integral domains such that
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathbb{D}}_{2}
.

1.

x\in{\mathbb{D}}_{2}
is
integral over
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
if
x
is the root of a monic polynomial with
coefficients in
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
.

2.

The set of elements of
{\mathbb{D}}_{2}
that are integral over
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
is the

integral closure of
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
in
{\mathbb{D}}_{2}
.

Example 5.2

1.

{\sf Z}
is the integral closure of
{\sf Z}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

2.

{\sf Z}[i]
is the integral closure of
{\sf Z}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}[i]
.

3.

{\sf Z}[\sqrt{5}]
is
not
the integral closure of
{\sf Z}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{5}]
:

\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
is integral over
{\mathbb{D}}
—it satisfies
x^{2}-x-1=0
.

4.

The integral closure of
{\sf Z}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{5}]
is
{\sf Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}]
.

5.

Let
d
be a square free integer.

(a)

If
d\equiv 2,3\pmod{4}
then the integral closure of
{\sf Z}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d})
is
{\sf Z}[\sqrt{d}]
.

(b)

If
d\equiv 1\pmod{4}
then the integral closure of
{\sf Z}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{d})
is

{\sf Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}]
.

Def 5.3

Let
d
be a squarefree integer.
We define
{\mathbb{O}}(\sqrt{d})
as follows:

1.

If
d\equiv 2,3\pmod{4}
then
{\mathbb{O}}(\sqrt{d})={\sf Z}[\sqrt{d}]
.

2.

If
d\equiv 1\pmod{4}
then
{\mathbb{O}}(\sqrt{d})={\sf Z}[\frac{\sqrt{d}+1}{2}]
.

Here is the right question to ask:

is there an analog of the Prime Number Theorem for
{\mathbb{O}}(d)
?

2c) Number of Units.

1.

If
d\geq 1
then
{\mathbb{O}}(\sqrt{d})
has an infinite number of units. This makes it hard
to phrase an analog of the Prime Number Theorem.

2.

{\mathbb{O}}(\sqrt{-1})
has four units (
\pm 1
,
\pm i
).
{\mathbb{O}}(\sqrt{-3})
has six units
(the six roots of unity). For all squarefree naturals
d\geq 5
,
{\mathbb{O}}(\sqrt{-d})
has
two roots of unit (
\pm 1
). Hence for these an analog of the Prime Number
Theorem may be possible. But see the next item.

2d) For some
{\mathbb{O}}(\sqrt{d})
primes and irreducibles are not the same. This may cause problems.

2e) For some
d

{\mathbb{O}}(\sqrt{d})
is not a unique factorization domain. This may cause problems.

We are
not
saying that formulating an analog of the Prime Number Theorem
in
{\mathbb{O}}(\sqrt{d})
is impossible; however, there are some difficulties to overcome.

3) What about adding cube-roots or higher fractional powers? What about adding more
irrationals? These get into issues far harder than those encountered
for quadratic extension.
