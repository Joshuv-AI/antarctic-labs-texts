Predictors of return to work status among patients with autoimmune encephalitis at one year post-discharge: a prospective observational study
Authors: Yejie Peng, Linxue Hu, Yuhui Wang, Yan Liang, Dezhi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutoimmune encephalitis (AE) is a group of centrally mediated inflammatory disorders characterized by symptoms such as seizures, cognitive deficits, psychiatric symptoms, and movement disorders, often requiring prolonged hospitalization and rehabilitation. Although studies have explored the prognosis of patients with autoimmune encephalitis (AE), the return to work (RTW), a crucial indicator of quality of life, remains under-researched. This study aims to fill this gap by investigating the RTW status and its influencing factors among AE patients one year post-discharge, providing new evidence for clinical practice and facilitating optimized rehabilitation management and improved quality of life for these patients.MethodsThis prospective observational study was conducted from August 2021 to April 2024 and included 124 AE patients who were recruited from the neurology department of a tertiary hospital in Southwest China. Demographic, disease-related, and work-related questionnaires as well as a social support rating scale (SSRS)and the Modified rankin scale (mRS) were used to collect data. The RTW status and current work characteristics of the patients were assessed via telephone interviews one year after discharge. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the predictors of RTW status.ResultsApproximately 46.2% of AE patients returned to work within one year after discharge. Logistic regression analysis revealed that AE patients who were the primary contributors to family income, had fewer children, had high levels of social support, or had low mRS scores were more likely to return to work.ConclusionsAt one year post-discharge, the RTW rate among AE patients was significantly low, influenced by the complex cultural context of Chinese society. Early identification of low-risk RTW patients and targeted interventions to enhance work motivation and readiness are essential for supporting successful RTW in AE populations.

Background

Autoimmune encephalitis (AE) is a neuroimmune disorder that is characterized by the presence of aberrant antibodies against the neuronal cell surface, synaptic antigens, or intracellular antigens [1, 2]. Currently, there are no accurate data on the global incidence of autoimmune encephalitis. An epidemiologic study in the United States indicated that the incidence rate of AE from 1995 to 2015 was 0.8 of 100,000 [3]. The most common subtype of AE is anti-NMDAR encephalitis, which accounts for approximately 80% of AE cases [4, 5]. Recovery to independent living occurs in the majority of patients with antibody-mediated subtypes of AE [6]. Additionally, recent studies have reported that 80% of AE patients have favorable outcomes [7, 8]. AE patients with favorable outcomes have the potential to reshape their lives. Individuals who temporarily leave their original work positions due to illness or injury can return to work (RTW) by returning to their previous or similar roles, taking a new position or otherwise fulfilling job responsibilities on a part-time or full-time schedule [9]. Currently, RTW is widely studied in populations such as cancer patients and those with cardiovascular diseases [10]. RTW serves as a crucial indicator of recovery and reintegration into normal life for patients, significantly contributing to improvements in their physical and mental health, alleviating their economic burden, and enhancing their quality of life. Therefore, for patients diagnosed with AE, returning to work may reduce the incidence of depression, increase quality of life, alleviate the economic burden on both patients and their families, and decrease societal costs [11, 12].

Research on RTW among AE patients is relatively limited [13, 14], particularly in the context of prospective studies and specific cultural settings. AE is a complex neurological disorder that has long-term impacts on cognitive function, emotional state, and social adaptation, thereby significantly impairing the employability of patients. However, most current research focuses on disease treatment and short-term rehabilitation outcomes. There is still a lack of systemic studies – targeted analyses across different cultural contexts – on how patients can successfully RTW.

In China, the societal emphasis on work and the economic pressures faced by families create unique challenges for AE patients attempting to RTW. Chinese society generally views work not only as a source of income but also as a reflection of personal value and social status. Therefore, losing the ability to work may cause severe psychological and social stress for patients and their families. Additionally, China’s healthcare system and economic environment differ from those in other countries, leading to varying resource limitations and support systems for patients during their rehabilitation and RTW processes.

Furthermore, China’s competitive labor market and the limited policies and support measures for employing rehabilitated patients by enterprises further exacerbate the difficulties that AE patients face with respect to RTW. Therefore, from both a theoretical and practical perspective, it is important to perform a systematic study to examine the predictors of RTW, social support models, and rehabilitation strategies tailored to China’s cultural and social context. By filling this research gap, more targeted RTW programs can be provided for AE patients, and the findings of this study can offer scientific evidence for governments, medical institutions, and enterprises to formulate relevant policies.

This study aims to address this gap by investigating the RTW status of AE patients at one year postdischarge and identifying the factors influencing their ability to RTW. By providing evidence-based insights, this research seeks to provide guidance to healthcare professionals and policymakers regarding the critical factors that need to be addressed to support AE patients in successfully returning to work.

Methods

Research design and setting

This prospective observational study included 124 AE patients who were recruited via convenience sampling from the neurology department of a tertiary hospital in southwestern China between August 2021 and April 2024. The sample size was estimated using the rough estimation method proposed by Kendall [15], which suggests a sample size of 5 to 10 times the number of variables. Considering a 20% attrition rate, approximately 90 to 180 study subjects were expected. Ultimately, 124 study subjects were included in the analysis.

Participants

The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (1), met the diagnostic criteria for autoimmune encephalitis (AE), suggested by Graus et al. in 2016 [16], which include: ① acute or subacute onset (< 3 months) of one or more of the following symptoms: (a) symptoms of the limbic system: psychiatric symptoms, memory deficit, seizure; (b) encephalitis syndrome: clinical manifestations of diffuse or multifocal brain damage; (c) clinical manifestations of basal ganglia and/or diencephalon/hypothalamus involvement; (d) psychiatric disorder that does not qualify as a non-organic disease by a psychologist; ② with or without CSF (cerebrospinal fluid) pleocytosis, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features of encephalitis, or electroencephalogram (EEG) with epileptic or slow-wave activity; ③ CSF and blood serum antibody testing positive for anti-NMDAR antibodies based on a cell-based assay (CBA)(Euroimmun, Lübeck, Germany); CSF and/or blood serum antibody testing positive for other neuronal surface antibodies (CBA): patients with positive antibodies only in the serum need to have typical clinical symptoms and/or high antibody titers (>1:32) (2) at least 18 years old, (3) employed and not retired before diagnosis, and (4) voluntarily participated in the study after providing informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a diagnosis of other major psychiatric disorders, (2) other malignancies, or (3) other severe medical conditions.

Measurements

Demographic, disease-related, and work-related questionnaire

The study utilized a self-designed questionnaire to collect demographic, disease-related, and work-related information. The demographic information assessed herein included age, sex, education level, marital status, and number of children. The disease-related data assessed herein included the subtype of AE, clinical manifestations, and hospitalization duration. The work-related information assessed herein included the nature of employment and pretreatment income. The participants’ RTW status was assessed through a single yes/no item: “Have you returned to work?” [14].

Social support rating scale (SSRS)

The Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) was developed by Xiao [17]. This scale includes 10 items across three dimensions: objective support (items 2, 6, and 7), subjective support (items 1, 3, 4, and 5), and utilization of social support (items 8, 9, and 10). Within the scale, items 1 through 4 and 8 through 10 are rated on a 4-point scale. Item 5 is comprised of five sub-questions, each scored from 1 to 4 points, reflecting varying levels of support ranging from no support to full support. For items 6 and 7, a score of 0 is assigned if “no source” is indicated, whereas the sum of the sources is recorded if “the following sources” are specified. The total score ranges from 22 to 66, with higher scores indicating higher levels of social support. The SSRS has been widely utilized across many disciplines in China, and the internal consistency reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s α) for this scale is 0.867.

Modified Rankin scale (mRS)

The modified Rankin Scale (mRS) is a widely recognized and validated tool for assessing the functional status and degree of disability among patients following cerebrovascular events. In recent years, the mRS has also been increasingly applied to patients with AE [18, 19]. This total score on this scale ranges from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating greater dependence on others and more severe disability.

Data collection

Prior to enrollment, eligible participants were provided with a detailed explanation of the study’s objectives and procedures, and they were ensured of their right to withdraw at any time without compromising their treatment. They were also informed that all the data would be presented in an aggregated statistical format, with no personal information disclosed, thus guaranteeing the anonymity and confidentiality of the survey. After providing written informed consent, the participants completed the demographic, disease-related, and work-related questionnaires as well as the SSRS and mRS. These measures were completed independently and in a quiet room before the patients were discharged. Standardized explanations were provided to the participants as necessary. The researchers reviewed the completeness of the questionnaires on site. At one year postdischarge, researchers collected data on RTW status, current job characteristics, and mRS scores via telephone interviews. The telephonic assessment of the modified mRS was conducted using a standardized scoring sheet, and all assessors underwent formal training to ensure inter-rater reliability. Training was validated through pre-assessment calibration exercises, ensuring consistency in scoring across all evaluators.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26.0. Normally distributed continuous data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent samples t test. Nonnormally distributed continuous data are expressed as medians (P25, P75) and were compared using the Mann‒Whitney U test. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test; alternatively, when the expected count was less than 1, Fisher’s exact test was applied for between-group comparisons. In the univariate analysis, the independent variables were individually assessed for their association with the dependent variable. Those variables that demonstrated significant associations (defined as p < 0.05) were subsequently selected for inclusion in the multivariate logistic regression model. The multivariate model was then used to identify the independent predictors of the dependent variable, while adjusting for potential confounding effects. The threshold for statistical significance was a two-sided P value < 0.05. To further investigate the independent predictive roles of various variables for the dependent variable and to adjust for the effects of potential confounding factors, we conducted a multivariate logistic regression analysis on the selected variables. In the model construction process, we first controlled for all potential confounding factors (such as age, gender, comorbidities, and baseline disease severity) and then sequentially included other significant variables. The model assumptions were tested as follows: ①Normality of Residuals: The normality of residuals was confirmed through residual distribution plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test; ②Multicollinearity: The absence of significant multicollinearity among variables was confirmed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) test (VIF < 10);③Goodness-of-Fit: The goodness-of-fit of the model was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p > 0.05).The results indicate that the model fits well and shows no signs of overfitting.

Return to work

The results of the follow-up assessment revealed that 54 (46.2%) AE patients had returned to work at one-year postdischarge; 38 of these patients (70.4%) returned to their previous jobs, 10 patients (18.5%) returned to the same jobs but switched positions, and 6 patients (11.1%) changed jobs (Table 2).

Discussion

This study represents a pioneering exploration of work resumption patterns and predictors among patients with AE, aiming to fill a critical gap in understanding their long-term vocational recovery. We found that 46.2% of patients with AE successfully returned to work within one year after discharge, whereas previous research reported that 71% of patients were able to RTW and have a normal life two years after discharge [20]. This discrepancy may be partly attributed to differences in follow-up duration, as patients may have more opportunities to recover and RTW over a longer time frame. However, the fact that only half of the patients returned to work within one year highlights the complexity and prolonged nature of the recovery process in patients with AE [21]. In China, the mean direct cost per AE patient is 94,129 renminbi (RMB; equivalent to 14,219 United States dollars [USD]), with an average direct medical cost of 88,373 RMB (13,349 USD). The average inpatient cost per patient with AE was 86,810 RMB (13,113 USD) [22]. This substantial financial burden forces patients with AE to RTW as soon as possible after discharge to alleviate the financial strain on their families. For context, patients with other neurological disorders demonstrate markedly higher RTW rates: 68.8% at 3 months post-stroke [23] and 52.8% at 6 months post-viral meningitis [24]. This comparison underscores the disproportionately severe impact of AE on occupational function.

Furthermore, the study highlighted that approximately 18.5% of AE patients had changed jobs within one year following discharge, with 11.1% transitioning to entirely different occupations. The univariate analysis revealed that among the 53 patients who successfully RTW, those engaged in physically demanding occupations prior to disease onset accounted for the lowest proportion (7.4%). While this finding did not reach statistical significance—potentially due to limitations such as sample size—it nevertheless suggests that occupational type (particularly manual labor) may pose significant challenges to patient rehabilitation and RTW outcomes. This finding suggests that AE patients undergo a thorough reassessment of their work capabilities and make career adjustments on the basis of the type of work and the work environment, thus reflecting a dynamic and adaptive process influenced by both personal and external factors [25]. For example, patients with physically demanding jobs, such as construction workers, laborers, or manual laborers, face increased challenges in returning to such roles owing to the physical limitations caused by their disease and its sequelae. Similarly, professionals such as surgeons, pilots, or other high-stress professionals may struggle to reintegrate into their previous occupations, as the cognitive and neurological impairments associated with AE can affect decision-making, memory, and attention, which are critical for these high-stakes professions. The study also revealed that many AE patients perceive their previous work environment as a potential threat to their physical or mental health, leading them to seek out less strenuous and healthier occupations. This change may be driven by a desire to reduce physical strain, minimize psychological stress, and create a more supportive and accommodating work environment—factors that are often critical for the long-term recovery and sustainability of AE patients [26]. Additionally, the physical and cognitive limitations imposed by the disease may result in significant barriers to adapting to or performing previous jobs, thus forcing patients to explore alternative career paths that better align with their current abilities and health status. These findings underscore the need for tailored support and vocational rehabilitation programs that address not only the immediate medical needs of AE patients but also their long-term career goals and adaptability. Employers, healthcare providers, and policymakers should work collaboratively to design flexible work arrangements, provide vocational training, and create supportive work environments that accommodate the unique needs of AE patients, thereby facilitating a smoother and more successful RTW process. Such initiatives are essential to ensuring that AE patients can regain their independence, maintain their sense of identity, and achieve sustained social and economic integration following their illness.

The results of the multivariate analysis revealed that having no children and being the primary breadwinner of the family were influential factors affecting the RTW of AE patients at one year postdischarge. The that AE patients without children are more likely to RTW than those with children contradicts previous research [25]. Patients without children often assume fewer caregiving responsibilities at home, allowing them to devote more time and energy to their recovery process. In contrast, patients with children may need to allocate their energy between caring for their children and their own recovery, which could influence their recovery progress and potentially delay their return to work. Furthermore, patients who are the primary financial contributors to their families may be under significant economic pressure, which prompts them to return to work even when they have not fully recovered. This economic pressure not only affects patients’ mental state but also may push them to re-enter the workforce prematurely, thereby increasing health risks. While this economic drive might provide motivation for patients to RTW, it also highlights the urgent need to find a balance between economic needs and health needs [27].

The results of this study indicate that higher levels of social support are associated with a greater likelihood of AE patients returning to work. AE patients of working age primarily require support from their families, workplaces, and medical institutions. Emotional support from relatives can prevent the development of negative emotions in patients, reduce internal emotional harm caused by the disease, and promote positive progression of the disease, thereby increasing the likelihood of returning to work [28]. As Buy et al. [29] reported, early contact and communication between the workplace and the patient, as well as assistance in coordinating positions and providing accommodations, can help patients readjust to work. Professional vocational rehabilitation (work assessments, work analysis, job placement, etc.) also promotes work readiness among stroke patients. In a study from the Netherlands [30], 86% of patients successfully returned to work after undergoing vocational rehabilitation for 4 months, with average working hours increasing by 5.3 h compared with before the intervention, and 64% of patients were still employed 3 to 6 years later. Given that social support is a controllable influencing factor, clinical healthcare professionals should focus on mobilizing patients’ social support systems and enhancing interactions and cooperation among the medical system, community, workplace, and patients’ families. Through multidisciplinary collaboration and effort, the goal is to maximize the patient’s ability to achieve work-related values [31].

The results of this study indicated that a lower mRS score at one year postdischarge was a key factor influencing the RTW status among AE patients. A lower mRS score not only reflects significant recovery in the patient’s functional ability but also suggests a more favorable overall condition in terms of physical, cognitive, and emotional readiness to return to work. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies, emphasizing the importance of functional independence for vocational rehabilitation [32, 33]. Patients with a higher mRS score [34, 35] may be in stable condition but still have significant functional impairments, potentially leading to physical or cognitive limitations when attempting to return to work. This phenomenon underscores the necessity of early intervention and rehabilitation programs, including personalized occupational therapy and cognitive rehabilitation, to help patients progressively regain their work capabilities. However, the study also revealed that among patients who did not return to work (n = 63), 65.1% (n = 41) achieved an mRS score of < 3 one year after discharge. Further follow-up of this subgroup demonstrated that despite favorable neurological recovery, factors such as anxiety, depression, fear of disease recurrence, and self-perceived fatigue remained major barriers to occupational reintegration. These findings indicate that future long-term outcome assessments for AE patients should incorporate psychological and subjective well-being measures alongside traditional functional evaluations.

Strengths and limitations

This study represents the first exploration of predictive factors for the return-to-work (RTW) rates among patients with AE, filling a gap in the literature. However, several limitations were identified in the current research. First, the sample was drawn exclusively from Chengdu, a city with relatively developed economy and society. This geographical limitation may have led to sampling bias and limited the generalizability of the findings to regions with different levels of economic development. Therefore, future studies should consider conducting multicenter surveys involving patients from areas with varying economic conditions to enhance the representativeness and external validity of the results. Second, this study only assessed RTW rates one year after the completion of treatment. While this time point provides valuable insights into initial employment outcomes, longer follow-up periods, such as two and five years, are necessary to explore the dynamic changes in work conditions over time. Future research should incorporate extended follow-up durations to comprehensively analyze long-term trends and influencing factors of RTW. While efforts were made to standardize assessments and train evaluators, potential variability in telephonic assessments cannot be fully excluded. Third, this study did not thoroughly explore the impact of cognitive impairment on patients’ ability to RTW. As one of the core sequelae of AE, cognitive impairment may significantly hinder vocational rehabilitation through multiple dimensions, such as executive function and information processing speed. Future research should employ standardized neuropsychological assessments (e.g., MoCA, WAIS) to quantitatively evaluate the association between cognitive impairment and RTW rates, job type, and work efficiency, thereby providing more targeted evidence for rehabilitation interventions.

Despite these limitations, they do not undermine the primary findings of the study. Instead, they highlight areas for further investigation. Addressing these issues in future research will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms influencing RTW in AE patients and contribute more robust evidence for clinical practice and policy formulation.

Conclusions

This study sheds light on the key social and individual factors underlying the RTW rates among patients with AE, emphasizing the importance of social support, household income dynamics, and neurological function in the vocational rehabilitation process. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for developing personalized intervention strategies, particularly when optimizing the family background and social support network of patients. Future research should aim to replicate these findings in diverse geographic and socioeconomic contexts to validate their generalizability. Additionally, exploring broader factors such as employment type (full-time vs. part-time) and long-term outcomes will offer more comprehensive guidance for the vocational rehabilitation of patients with autoimmune encephalitis. Furthermore, extending follow-up periods to assess long-term RTW and occupational stability will help refine intervention and support strategies, ultimately improving patients’ quality of life and long-term social reintegration.
