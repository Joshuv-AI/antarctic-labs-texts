The spectra of graph substitutions
Thomas Hirschler, Wolfgang Woess

Abstract
. Let ( X , E X ) (X,E_{X}) and ( V , E V ) (V,E_{V}) be finite connected graphs without loops. We assume that V V has two distinguished vertices a , b a,b and an automorphism γ \gamma which exchanges a a and b b . The V V -edge substitution of X X is the graph X ⁡ [ V ] X[V] where each edge [ x , y ] ∈ E X [x,y]\in E_{X} is replaced by a copy of V V , identifying x x with a a and y y with b b or vice versa. (The latter choice does not matter; it yields isomorphic graphs). The aim is to describe the spectrum of X ⁡ [ V ] X[V] in terms of the spectra of X X and V V . Instead of the spectra of the adjacency matrices, we consider the versions which are normalised by dividing each row by the row sum (the vertex degree). These are stochastic, reversible matrices, and our approach applies more generally to reversible transition matrices corresponding to arbitrary positive edge weights invariant under γ \gamma . We write P P for the transition matrix over X X and Q Q for the one over V V . Together, they induce the matrix P ∗ P_{*} over X ⁡ [ V ] X[V] . There is not a nice and compact formula which says how to transform the spectra of P P and Q Q into the spectrum of P ∗ P_{*}\, . The results depend on issues like whether X X has circles of even length and on the eigenvalues of the restriction of Q Q to V ∖ { a , b } V\setminus\{a,b\} , which are classified into 4 possible types, each to be handled differently. Also quite subtle is the issue of determining the multiplicities of the eigenvalues of P ∗ P_{*} in terms of the input.

More formally this may be described as follows:

. We will think of this as putting an orientation (arrow) on each edge,

consider the graph

, where of course the edges are

.
Then we define the identification map as the graph homomorphism

(1.1)

\displaystyle\pi:E_{X}\times V\to X\cup(E_{X}\times V^{o}),

\displaystyle\pi(e,a)=e^{a}\,,\;\pi(e,b)=e^{b}\,,\;\pi(e,v)=(e,v)\;\text{for}\;v\in V^{o}\,,

so that

.

Thus, the total conductances of the substituted network are

\displaystyle m_{X[V]}(x)

\displaystyle=m_{X}(x)m_{V}(a)\quad\text{for }\;x\in X\,,\and

\displaystyle m_{X[V]}(e,v)

\displaystyle=a_{X}(e)m_{V}(v)\quad\text{for }\;(e,v)\in E_{X}\times V^{o}\,.

associated with the network

on

.

Several colleagues asked whether this has not been done before. The answer appears to be “no”
with the small exception of Thm. 9.89 in the second author’s book

In the present paper, we assume from §

We also assume that the subgraph

of

We start in §
2
with general
facts about the Green function of a reversible stochastic matrix, that is, the resolvent matrix.
Typical equations for the latter as well as related functions can be obtained in
terms of walk generating functions in the complex variable

, valid a priori in the domain of
convergence of these power series from where they extend analytically as rational functions.
A crucial role is played by

. This leads to

of the equations

, where

frequencies of

.

In §
3
, we classify the eigenvalues of

as well as of

into 4 types,
respeptively, depending on the corresponding eigenspaces. According to the type, the eigenfunctions may
(or may not) be extended to eigenfunctions of

. The more important ones are those of

which lead to eigenfunctions that are

on the set of vertices

,
that is, those which have

In §
4
, we show that the extension constructions of §

possible eigenfunctions which are nodal (

) on

\mathsf{spec}(Q_{V^{o}})

.

We then summarise in §
5
, describing the entire spectrum of

and
the multiplicities of the eigenvalues. The latter are subtle and depend on the types of
the eigenvalues, and an exceptional set of candidate eigenvalues has to be excluded when

is a tree or posseses just one cycle which has odd lentgh.

In §

, we present a few basic examples along with their
computational details, checking each time that the resulting sum of the eigenvalue
multiplicities of

is what is has to be, namely
|X|+|E_{X}|\bigl(|V|-2\bigr)
.

The final, short §
7
contains a brief discussion on spectrally (non-)dominant
vertices, an outlook on infinite graphs
including self-similarity, as well as an outlook on substituting a manifold, and concludes
with additional references concerning the affinity of graph substitution with graph products.

What follows is formulated in terms of

We start with a little general lemma that will be used later.

(2.1) Lemma.

For every
\lambda\in[-1\,,\,1]
there are a bipartite graph
(X,E_{X})
and an associated
reversible transition matrix
P
such that
\lambda\in\mathsf{spec}(P)
.

Proof.

The graph is the
2N
-circle with vertices
x_{0}\,,\dots,x_{2N-1}
and edges

[x_{i}\,,x_{i+1}]
with indices modulo
2N
, where
N\geq 2
. For a parameter

,
the edge conductances are
a(x_{i}\,,x_{i+1})=1
for

and
a(x_{2N-1}\,,x_{0})=a
.
We write
P_{a,2N}
for the resulting transition matrix, and we want to locate its second largest
eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
for

(the largest one is
\lambda_{0}=1

In the standard case

\lambda_{1}=\cos\frac{\pi}{N}

If

then this means that the edge

is deleted, and the graph is reduced to
the line graph with

is simple random walk on that graph with reflecting
end-vertices, compare with
[
23

\lambda_{1}=\cos\frac{\pi}{2N-1}
.
By continuity, for every
\lambda\in J_{N}=\bigl[\cos\frac{\pi}{N}\,,\,\cos\frac{\pi}{2N-1}\bigr]

there is

such that
\lambda_{1}(P_{a,2N})=\lambda
. Now

\bigcup_{N=2}^{\infty}J_{N}=[0\,,\,1)
. Therefore each
\lambda\in[0\,,\,1)
appears as

\lambda_{2}(P_{a,2N})
for some

and

. Of course,
\lambda=1
is always an eigenvalue, and since
the

∎

For

It can be viewed as a matrix over

The norm = spectral radius of the operator

\lambda_{0}(P)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}p^{(n)}(x,y)^{1/n}\,,

where for

The following is well-known.

(2.2) Lemma.

If

The spectral gap

For a stochastic, reversible Markov chain transition matrix such as
P
on
X
, the
spectral gap

is
|\lambda_{1}|\,
, where
\lambda_{0}=1
and
\lambda_{1}
are the largest and second
largest eigenvalues of
P
. The name is related to (minus) the Laplacian
I-P
,
where
|\lambda_{1}|
is the distance of its second eigenvalue from the bottom of its spectrum.
It is a well known basic consequence of the spectral representation
of
P
that this is a key quantity for understanding the speed of convergence of the Markov chain
distributions (the rows of
P^{n}
) to equilibrium
(the normalised measure
m_{X}
).

We now address the question whether for
P_{*}
on
X[V]
, the second largest eigenvalue
has to be a solution of the equation
\varphi(\lambda_{1}^{*})=\lambda_{1}\,
, or whether
the “degenerate” case
\lambda_{1}^{*}\in\mathsf{spec}(Q_{V^{o}}
may arise.
For this, it is natural to assume that
X
has at least three vertices, in which case it
is an easy exercise to show that
|\lambda_{1}|<1
. Recall that we assume that
V\supset\{a,b\}

strictly. The following is a corollary to Proposition
2.16
.

(5.3) Corollary.

If
|X|\geq 3
and
V^{o}
is connected then
\lambda_{1}^{*}\notin\mathsf{spec}(Q_{V^{o}})
, and it is
the largest solution of the equation
\varphi(\lambda^{*})=\lambda_{1}\,
.
In particular,
\lambda_{1}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\,
.

Furthermore, if
\lambda_{1}\geq 0
the statement remains true without assuming connectedness of

V^{o}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.16
when
V^{o}
is connected,
\varphi(z)
is continuous in the interval

[\lambda_{0}(Q_{V_{o}})\,,\,1]
with
\varphi(1)=1
and
\varphi\bigl(\lambda_{0}(Q_{V^{o}})\bigr)=-1
.
Since
|\lambda_{1}|<1
there must be solutions of
the equation
\varphi(\lambda^{*})=\lambda_{1}\,
in the open interval

(\lambda_{0}(Q_{V_{o}})\,,\,1)
. We take the largest solution. Thus must be
\lambda_{1}^{*}
.
It is not contined in
\mathsf{spec}(Q_{V^{o}})
, being larger than the spectral radius of that
matrix. And in that interval,
\psi(z)>0
, concluding the proof.

If
\lambda_{1}>0
then by Proposition
2.16
, the equation has a unique solution
in
[\lambda_{0}(Q_{V\!-b})\,,\,1]
, and this must be
\lambda_{1}^{*}\,
.
∎

Note that when Corollary
5.3
applies, maybe even beyond the assumption of
connectedness of
V^{o}
, one gets
\lambda_{1}^{*}>0
, an subsequent iteration of the substitution
does no more require that assumption.

Final remarks and outlook

(A) It is desirable to compute many further examples to complete the picture.

(B) A vertex

In relation with the study of

Namely, in almost all cases (and at least when

\mathsf{spec}(Q_{V^{o}})

in
[

]
the adjacency matrix

is considered, while our results concern the normalised
version

applies equally to the restriction

of

to
V^{o}
, and the embedding lemmas
3.3
,
3.11
,
3.13
and

3.14
also remain unchanged; indeed, the requirement that the corresponding

only depends on
the edge weights (conductances) of

.

In the papers of

]
