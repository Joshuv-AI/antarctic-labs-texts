The role of crystallinity in the synthesis of hydroxysodalite from natural kaolins
Authors: Arouna Nsangou, Hervé Barye Tatang, Jacques Richard Mache, Dayirou Njoya, Jean Aimé Mbey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the influence of kaolinite crystallinity on the direct synthesis of hydroxysodalite from kaolin. Three natural samples, codified KM, KE, and DA, were characterized and treated in 6 N sodium hydroxide with or without thermal activation. The mineralogical and structural evolution were analysed using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), thermo-gravimetric and differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC). The crystallinity of the kaolinite within each raw sample was assessed via the Hinckley Index (HI) and P0 factor. The resulting crystallinity order is as follows: DA > KM > KE. The conversion into hydroxysodalite is assessed via a conversion ratio (CR) of 55%, 45% and <2% respectively for KE, KM and DA. These conversions ratios inversely correlated the crystallinity order, indicating that lower crystallinity favors the conversion. Comparison with synthesis using the metakaolin from each raw clay, revealed that thermally activated samples produced more crystallized hydroxysodalites highlighting the role of structural organization as a key factor for the conversion. Overall, this study demonstrates that lower crystallinity favors the direct conversion of raw kaolinite into hydroxysodalite.

Introduction

The kaolin group are a family of clay minerals that include kaolinite, nacrite, halloysite, and dickite, all of which belong to the 1 : 1 phyllosilicate group.1 The use of kaolinite for hydroxysodalite synthesis has been widely reported.2–4 Kaolin is a clay mainly containing kaolinite as clay mineral. This mineral is particularly suitable for hydroxysodalite synthesis due to its high alumina and silica content.5 Hydoxysodalite is a porous hydrophilic mineral that is used in several domains, including water desalination and purification;6 gas separation, moisture resistance; heavy metal removal and catalyst carriers.7–9 The use of kaolin, an abundant clay mineral, is significantly cost effective in comparison to expensive synthetic chemical reagents like sodium silicate and sodium aluminate.10 In the synthesis of hydoxysodalite from kaolinite, several experimental parameters are known to influence the yield, the structure, and the type of zeolite formed. Specifically, these parameters include the nature of the silica/alumina source;11 the synthesis time;12 the temperature and the concentration;13,14 the hydrothermal conditions,15 and the microwave power.4,16 At a fundamental level, the Si/Al ratio15,17 remains a key factor in hydroxysodalite synthesis. In the Microwave assisted synthesis, it is shown that both reaction time and microwave energy affect the structure of the product while also demonstrating that kaolin becomes more reactive after its conversion into metakaolin.4,18 Most synthesis routes involve a preliminary dehydroxylation step to produce amorphous metakaolinite. This thermal conversion is typically carried out between 600 °C to 700 °C which is energy consuming.4,14 From a sustainability perspective, material synthesis should minimize environmental impact, making the optimization of experimental conditions essential to reduce the carbon footprint. Regarding structural properties, the crystallinity of kaolinites has been extensively studied.19–22 Generally, kaolinites with fewer structural defects exhibit higher crystallinity.19,20 Notably, the most common defects are those affecting layer stacking, which weaken hydrogen bonding.19

Although the effects of the silica/alumina source,23 sodium hydroxide concentration,14 synthesis time, and hydrothermal conditions on hydroxysodalite formation are well documented, the specific role of the crystallinity in the direct conversion of kaolinite mineral to hydroxysodalite remains underexplored. To address this gap, the present study investigates the influence of kaolinite crystallinity on its direct conversion to hydroxysodalite.

Materials and methods

Three kaolin samples from the localities of Mayouom (KM),24 Etoa (KE),25 and Afanoyoa (DA) in Cameroon were used in this study. The samples were wet sieved through a 45 µm mesh, followed by air-drying at (28 ± 1) °C to constant weight before being ground and stored in plastic bags prior to their use for the synthesis and the analyses. Their metakaolins were obtained by heating each sample in a muffle furnace at a rate of 10° min−1 up to 650 °C. The sample were left for one hour stay at 650 °C before being cool down in the furnace up to ambient. The resulting metakaolins were labelled KE1, KM1, and DA1. Throughout the synthesis process, analytical-grade sodium hydroxide (99% purity) was used.

Hydroxysodalite synthesis

The synthesis was carried out by mixing 20 g of each sample with 200 mL of 6 N sodium hydroxide solution. The obtained dispersions were left to stand under ambient conditions for 15 days, after which they were filtered. The collected cakes were repeatedly washed with distilled water to remove excess sodium hydroxide, then dried to constant mass in an oven at 50 °C. The dried products were labelled KM60, KE60, and DA60 for those deriving from the raw kaolins, and KM61, KE61, and DA61 for those deriving from the metakaolins.

Chemical analysis

Chemical analysis for major elements in the raw kaolins were determined by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) using an ICP-OES Agilent device at the Service d’Analyse et de la Roche et du Minéral (SARM) in Nancy, France. The samples were fused in lithium metaborate (LiBO2) and dissolved in nitric acid prior to the analysis. The detection limit (dl) values are: 0.5% (SiO2); 0.02% (Al2O3); 0.01% (Fe2O3); 0.0005% (MnO); 0.02% (MgO); 0.035% (CaO); 0.03% (Na2O); 0.01% (K2O); 0.001% (TiO2) and 0.05% (P2O5). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectra of the samples were acquired in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode using a Bruker Optik Alpha-p Fourier transform spectrometer at the University of Yaoundé I. The powder clay sample (≤45 µm) was deposited directly on the ATR crystal, the pressure anvil is lower and the recording is started. The spectra, recorded from 4000 cm−1 to 400 cm−1 with a scanning step of 4 cm−1, are obtained as an accumulation of 40 scans.

FTIR spectra were used for the crystallinity parameter related to the extent of interlayer hydrogen bonding, known as the P0 factor.27,28 For the P0 factor evaluation, the relative intensity ratio of the vibrations of interlayer hydroxyls in the kaolinite at 3620 cm−1 and 3696 cm−1 are used for the calculation as indicated in eqn (3). X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The XRD patterns of the raw samples and their corresponding metakaolins were recorded at the University of Lorraine using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer equipped with a 1D LynxEye linear detector. Diffraction patterns were obtained using Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. Scanning was performed at a step rate of 0.035° every 3 seconds over a 2θ range of 2.5° to 70°. The diffraction patterns of the synthesized products were recorded at the University of Liège using a Bruker AXS D8 Advance diffractometer, equipped with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) and operating under voltage and current of 40 kV and 40 mA. These patterns were recorded at a step rate of 0.02° every 2 seconds over a 2θ range of 5° to 70°.

Thermal analysis (TGA-DSC)

Thermal analysis was performed under air circulation using a coupled DSC-TGA equipment LINSEIS STA PT-1000 device. For each sample, approximately 10 mg were placed in an alumina crucible and heated at a rate of 10 °C min−1 from room temperature up to 800 °C.

Results and discussion

Chemistry, mineralogy, and crystallinity of raw kaolins

The oxide percentages obtained from the chemical analysis of the sieved samples are presented in Table 1. These values deviate from the theoretical ratio of 1.17 for ideal kaolinite, with the deviation being most pronounced in KE. Such a deviation is typically associated with an enrichment in silicate minerals or increased aluminium vacancy29 which could contribute to structural defects. The obtained values are consistent with previous studies on these samples.25,30

The XRD analysis of the raw kaolins (KE, KM, and DA) shows strong reflections at 7.14 Å and 3.57 Å respectively associated to the 001 and 002 reflections of kaolinite (ICDD 14-164) (Fig. 2a). The differences in reflection intensities indicate the following relative crystallinity order: KE < KM < DA. Associate minerals are quartz (ICDD 46-1045), muscovite 2M1 (ICDD 6-263), and goethite (ICDD 29-713), the latter being better evidenced in KE. The sharp spectral resolution of DA, combined with its SiO2/Al2O3 ratio closest to 1.17, suggests that the kaolinite mineral within the DA sample, may have a chemical formula closer to the ideal chemical formula for kaolinite in comparison to the kaolinite mineral in the other samples.

Characterization of the raw kaolins (a) FTIR, (b) XRD.

The FTIR analysis of KM, KE, and DA clays (Fig. 2b) reveals characteristic bands at 3696 cm−1, 3676 cm−1, 3650 cm−1, and 3620 cm−1 which are due to stretching of hydroxyl groups in kaolinite.31,32 In particular, for DA and KM the obvious presence of the doublet stretching at 3676 cm−1 and 3650 cm−1 for the interlayer –OH bonds (which is not obvious in KE) is indicative of good structural organization in these samples.22,30 The absorption band near 1635 cm−1, associated to hydration water,33 is more intense in KE, suggesting a larger amount of physically bonded water molecules at the surface of this sample. Furthermore, Si–O bond vibrations from various silicate minerals are observed around 1109 cm−1, 1024 cm−1, 997 cm−1, 795 cm−1, 694 cm−1, and 468 cm−1 (ref. 30, 33 and 34). The deformation vibration of the Al–OH bond is evidenced by the band at 908 cm−1, while the 535 cm−1 band corresponds to the deformation vibration of Si–O–Al.24,30,33

Thermal analysis curves (TGA-DSC) of the samples show the release of physisorbed water below 100 °C (Fig. 3a). Around 289 °C, KE exhibits an additional thermal event associated to the transformation of goethite into hematite. The onset of mullite formation appears as exothermic event at 941 °C (KE) and around 1012 °C (KM and DA). The difference in this onset being is due to crystallinity differences among the samples.35 At approximately 530 °C, dehydroxylation of the kaolinite mineral occurs, leading to metakaolinite. This temperature, which varies among samples, reflects the influence of crystallinity on dehydroxylation behavior.21,22 The dehydroxylation temperatures are in the order DA > KM > KE. This order agrees with stronger cohesive energy in DA compared to KM and KE as related to the extent of interlayer hydrogen bonding from the P0 values (Table 1). Additionally, disparities in mass losses are observed and are correlated to kaolinite purity in the samples as suggested by the kaolionite content (Table 1). Beyond 900 °C, the observed exothermic phenomenon is attributed to the onset of mullite formation, which is typical in thermally treated kaolinite.

Thermal characterization (a) DSC, (b) TGA.

HI and P0 factor (Table 1) show the following crystallinity order DA > KM > KE, which is consistent with previous deductions from XRD patterns and FTIR stretching of structural OH groups. Given that crystallinity is influenced by the structural defects, then the crystallinity order also aligns with defect order within the samples, showing that DA have the least defect degree while KE has the highest defect degree. It is then suggested that crystallinity difference may induce influence on their direct conversion to hydroxysodalite.

Mineralogical analysis after calcination at 650 °C

XRD patterns of the fired products from KM, KE, and DA (Fig. 4a–c respectively) confirm the amorphization which is evidenced by the disappearance of characteristic kaolinite reflections (7.14 Å and 3.58 Å) and the appearance of a broad amorphous hump in the 2θ range between 15° and 35°. The associate minerals, muscovite and quartz, remain unaffected after firing at 650 °C.

XRD patterns (a–c) and FTIR spectra (d) of samples heated at 650 °C.

Associate mineral such as muscovite or quartz are unaffected while appearance of some hematite reflections are observable.

FTIR analysis of the fired samples at 650 °C reveals the disappearance of bands corresponding to hydroxyl bonds stretching in the kaolinite36 (Fig. 4d). This observation clearly evidence dehydroxylation and this is further supported by changes in the FTIR bands of Al–O and Si–O bonds at 1040 cm−1, 782 cm−1, 687 cm−1 and 432 cm−1. Thus, the calcination at 650 °C for 1 hour successfully induced structural reorganization to metakaolinite.

Direct conversion of the raw kaolinite to hydroxysodalite: mineralogical evolution and thermal behavior

FTIR spectra of the samples treated with 6 N sodium hydroxide are presented in Fig. 5. The high-wavenumber region shows a marked decrease in the intensity of interlayer hydroxyl stretching bands. This reduction is more pronounced in KE and KM, likely due to interlayer hydroxyl interaction with the soda solution inducing local environment modification. Additionally, vibration bands associated to Si–O and Al–O bonds are altered, indicating that local environment of these bonds has change and this is probably due to change in the mineral network. These changes agree with structural modifications through destabilization of the three-dimensional framework caused by acid–base reactions on both silanol and aluminol hydroxyl. DA, which exhibits minimal spectral changes, suggests that structural order may be a key parameter influencing the reactivity of potentially acidic hydroxyl groups in the kaolinite minerals.

FTIR spectra of raw kaolins treated in 6 N sodium hydroxide.

On the XRD patterns of the products (Fig. 6), new reflections are noticeable. These reflections are particularly prominent in the KE60 and KM60 samples. Coupled with the FTIR observations, these new reflections suggest a progressive transformation toward a new crystalline phase, identified on the XRD patterns (Fig. 6) as hydroxysodalite (ICSD-413496, hydroxysodalite 8 : 2 : 2).10,37 The sodalite reference file ICDD 20-1070 also confirmed the sodalite structure through the reflections at 3.65 Å, 6.34 Å, 2.56 Å and 2.13 Å which are characteristics of sodalite structure. The DA sample shows less significant changes in its XRD pattern, indicating lower conversion to hydroxysodalite. This low conversion was associated to its high crystallinity. The presence of reflections associated to kaolinite in the product, indicates that the conversion is partial or kinetically low (some kaolinite remain unaffected). Also the amorphous hump between 25° and 40° (2 theta scale) for KE60, in particular, is associated to poorly crystalline region in the products. The amorphous hump is better expressed in KE60 than in KM60, probably due to difference in kaolinite dissolution from KE and KM which agree with the hypothesis of increase resistance to direct dissolution of the kaolinite which increasing kaolinite crystallinity. It should be quoted that associated minerals such as quartz and muscovite remain unaffected in accordance to the findings of Fan et al. (2025)38 which indicate that these minerals are not converted in sodalite.

XRD patterns of the treated raw samples in 6 N sodium hydroxide.

The thermal behaviour of the products obtained after kaolin treatment with 6 N sodium hydroxide reveals additional endothermic events on their DSC curves (Fig. 7a) around 139 °C and 229 °C. These events are clearly observed in KE60 and KM60 and correspond to approximate mass losses of 5% and 4%, respectively, on the TGA curves (Fig. 7b). They are attributed to the release of structural water from the newly formed phases. The almost absence of such events in the DA60 curves was associated to low conversion of the kaolinite in the DA sample under the applied experimental conditions. The lowering of the dehydroxylation temperature, along with the reduction in the exothermic signal associated with mullite formation beyond 900 °C, confirms structural modifications in KE and KM. These changes are less evident in DA and are consistent with the conclusion of low conversion of the kaolinite in DA sample. The minimal transformation of DA aligns with the influence of its pristine crystalline order, which hinder its reactivity. In contrast, the less ordered KE and KM samples undergo conversion into a new structure capable of trapping water molecules. As identified by XRD, hydroxysodalite is the newly formed phase, characterized by a cage-like framework within which water molecules are retained.

Crystallinity influence on direct conversion of the raw kaolins

The evaluation of the conversion ratio from raw kaolins to hydroxysodalite reveals the following order: DA (<2%) < KM (45%) < KE (55%) (Table 1). This ranking follows the same trend as the crystallinity (be it from HI or P0). Indeed, as the crystallinity of the kaolinites increases, a lower conversion rate is evidence from the low reductions in the intensity of hydroxyl bands (FTIR, Fig. 5) and kaolinite reflections (XRD, Fig. 6). This was attributed to the strong cohesion resulting from extensive hydrogen bonding in the interlayer region of highly crystalline samples. Such a cohesion likely limits the action of sodium hydroxide toward network breakage and reorganization to sodalite. In contrast, the presence of structural defects in less crystalline kaolinite may facilitate sodium insertion and enhance the dissolution of the kaolinite prior to reorganization. Higher crystallinity leads to a well-organized structure with tightly packed layers, restricting chemical interaction with sodium hydroxide. It is then concluded that lower crystallinity induces increase conversion of kaolinite to hydroxysodalite in sodium hydroxide.

Conversion evolution of metakaolins related to kaolins

To confirm the effectiveness of direct conversion, the respective metakaolins of the kaolins were treated under identical alkaline conditions. FTIR spectra of the treated metakaolins clearly show differences in the wavenumbers of Si–O and Al–O vibrational bands compared to those of the directly treated kaolinites (Fig. 8a). Additionally, new bands near 3500 cm−1 and 1633 cm−1, associated with water vibrations, are more prominent in the products derived from metakaolins. Also, variations in the intensity of siloxane and aluminoxane bands indicate structural differences between the products obtained from raw kaolins and those from metakaolins. These differences are mainly associated to improve crystallization of the sodalite sample when using metakaolin.

FTIR spectra (a) and XRD patterns (b) of products derived from metakaolins and kaolins treated for 15 days in 6 N sodium hydroxide solution.

Considering the raw sample conversion, a convenient choice of poorly crystalline kaolinite for hydroxysodalite making can led to reduce production cost through the suppression of the conversion step of kaolinite to metakaolinite. However, longer time of reaction or higher sodium hydroxide concentration may be needed for improved crystallization of the products.

The XRD patterns of products from metakaolins treated with 6 N sodium hydroxide (Fig. 8b) reveal improved crystallinity compared to the products derived from raw kaolin. This improved crystallinity is evidenced by a most obvious presence of additional reflections, indicating a more ordered structure for product derived metakaolin. Also, another evidence, for crystallinity difference can be view through the difference in hydroxysodalite reflection width at half maximum, which is inversely proportional to the thickness of the coherent scattering domain from Scherrer equation.39 The difference in crystallinity of products from raw kaolins and metakaolins is attributed to their reactivity. The amorphous nature of metakaolinite within the metakaolin justify the increase reactivity. The increase dissolution of silica and alumina upon sodium hydroxide treatment from this amorphous phase, promote the crystalline reorganization toward zeolite-like structures. In contrast, the ordered structure of kaolinites in the raw kaolins result in reduce dissolution which reduce the availability of silica and alumina units and hence, slowing and limiting the crystalline growth from dissolved species.

The DSC curves (Fig. 9a) and TGA curves (Fig. 9b) of metakaolins and their derived products further confirm conversion into similar phases. The effectiveness of this conversion is demonstrated by the appearance of endothermic events in the DSC curves of the products, which are absent in the starting metakaolins. Another indicator of conversion is the shift in mullitization temperature: initially observed around 1007 °C in DA1 and KM1, it is lowered between 960 °C and 900 °C in the derived products. The endothermic events in the DSC curves of the products are located around 170 °C and 191 °C, corresponding to the release of coordinated water molecules within the structure. The variation in release temperature likely reflects differences in the strength of coordinating interactions between the water molecules and the sodalite framework. The corresponding mass losses from the TGA curves are 22% for DA61, 18% for KE61, and 13% for KM61 (Fig. 9b). The almost similar mass losses and identical mean release temperature (170 °C) for DA61 and KE61 suggest structural and hydration similarities. KM61 exhibits the lowest percent of water content and the highest mean temperature (191 °C) for water release. It is suggested that the KM61 structure have smaller cages for water retention in comparison to DA61 and KE61. The smaller cage dimensions in KM61 allow stronger interactions of water molecules with the sodalite structure.

Conclusion

The objective of this study was to analyze the role of the kaolinite crystallinity in the direct synthesis of hydroxysodalite from kaolin under alkaline conditions. Hydroxysodalite was synthesized from three kaolins KM, KE, and DA. The analysis of the starting material confirmed that kaolinite is the main mineral in all the samples. Crystallinity indexes, including the HI and the P0 factor, indicate improved structural organization ranking as follows: DA > KM > KE highlighting crystallinity difference among the samples. After treatment in 6 N sodium hydroxide solution, a decrease in the intensity of hydroxyl-related bands was observed, along with the appearance of new reflections in the XRD patterns. These changes were associated to hydroxysodalite formation and further confirmed by thermal analysis through reduced dehydroxylation and reduced mullite formation. These transformations were most pronounced in KE, followed by KM, and then DA which evidenced the influence of the starting kaolinite crystallinity on the conversion process. The calculated conversion ratios, from their XRD patterns, further confirm the crystallinity influence on the synthesis and it is concluded that lower crystallinity is favorable to the direct transformation of kaolinite into hydroxysodalite. In contrast, starting from metakaolins, the conversion is better pronounced and lead to a more uniform presence of hydroxysodalite. This reactivity difference is related to the amorphous structure of the metakaolinite phase which favours reactions with sodium hydroxide for improved conversion to hydroxysodalite and more efficient crystalline growth resulting in improved crystallization of the hydroxysodalite. From all the above, it is demonstrated that a convenient choice of a kaolin made of poorly crystalline kaolinite is a key toward reduced costs arising from the direct production of hydroxysodalite from raw kaolins.
