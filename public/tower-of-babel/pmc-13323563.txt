Comprehensive Endotyping of Patients With Angina and Nonobstructive Coronary Arteries: A Report From the J‐CMD
Authors: Masahiro Hada, Tsunekazu Kakuta, Hiroki Ueno, Yoshihisa Kanaji, Eisuke Usui, Tatsuhiro Nagamine, Kodai Sayama, Takumi Watanabe, Takahiro Watanabe, Hikaru Shimosato, Riko Murakami, Takashi Mineo, Shunta Hiroi, Kaisei Hosokawa, Taishi Yonetsu, Tetsuo Sasano, Morihiko Takeda, Kenichi Tsujita, Hiroaki Shimokawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAngina with nonobstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA) is frequently driven by coronary vasospasm and coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD). Despite recommendations for comprehensive invasive testing to phenotype ANOCA, prospective data on the prevalence and interplay of these endotypes remain limited. We aimed to investigate the prevalence, clinical correlations, and overlap of epicardial vasospasm, microvascular spasm, and CMD, defined by a coronary flow reserve <2.0.MethodsWe analyzed the data from a prospective, multicenter nationwide registry of the J‐CMD (Japanese Association of Coronary Microvascular Dysfunction; UMIN000047609). Patients with ANOCA underwent standardized intracoronary functional testing, including acetylcholine‐based spasm provocation and coronary flow reserve assessment. The distribution of the endotypes (epicardial vasospasm, microvascular spasm, isolated CMD, and CMD with spasm) was investigated according to sex and age tertiles.ResultsOf 947 patients (55.6% women), epicardial vasospasm was the most common diagnosis (44.2%), followed by microvascular spasm (16.5%), CMD with spasm (8.7%), and isolated CMD (5.0%). Substantial overlap was evident; concurrent CMD and any vasospasm were more frequent in women than in men (10.8% versus 6.0%, P=0.011). While microvascular spasm was more prevalent in women, CMD was associated with older age, female sex, hypertension, and diabetes. Intriguingly, the prevalence of these endotypes varied with age in women, a pattern not observed in men.ConclusionsAmong patients with ANOCA, coronary vasospasm and CMD were prevalent and frequently coexisted, demonstrating distinct sex‐ and age‐related patterns. These findings offer novel insights into the heterogeneous pathophysiology of ANOCA and reinforce the importance of comprehensive invasive assessment in establishing a precise diagnosis and guiding targeted therapy.Keywords: angina with nonobstructive coronary arteries, coronary microvascular dysfunction, coronary spasm, invasive diagnostic procedureSubject Categories: Clinical Studies, Coronary Circulation, Ischemia, Physiology

A substantial proportion of patients undergoing coronary angiography have no obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD), a condition termed angina with nonobstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA). 1 , 2 Contrary to historical perceptions of a benign prognosis, patients with ANOCA experience substantial morbidity, impaired quality of life, and increased health care use. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Importantly, ANOCA is associated with an elevated risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including death, myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure, underscoring the critical need for accurate diagnosis and targeted management strategies. 7

The underlying pathophysiology of ANOCA appears to be heterogeneous, with coronary vasospasm and coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) recognized as the 2 major endotypes. 8 , 9 Coronary vasospasm can affect the epicardial coronary arteries (epicardial vasospasm [EVS]), the coronary microcirculation (microvascular spasm [MVS]) or both. CMD, on the other hand, is characterized by impaired coronary blood flow regulation at the microvascular level, leading to an inability to augment blood flow in response to myocardial demand. 10 These distinct pathophysiological mechanisms often necessitate different therapeutic strategies. 7 Therefore, accurate endotyping is crucial for guiding appropriate, mechanism‐based therapy.

In recognition of this, current international guidelines recommend a comprehensive interventional diagnostic procedure (IDP) for patients with ANOCA. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 This approach involves a functional assessment of the coronary circulation, combining physiological measurements such as coronary flow reserve (CFR) and the index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR) with provocative testing for vasospasm using agents like acetylcholine. Although this comprehensive evaluation is the gold standard, its use is largely limited to specialized centers, resulting in scarce large‐scale data on the prevalence, characteristics, and overlap of IDP‐defined endotypes. Moreover, the influence of demographic factors, such as sex and age, on the distribution of these endotypes is not fully understood, although women are known to be disproportionately affected by ANOCA. 15

The J‐CMD (Japanese Association of Coronary Microvascular Dysfunction) registry is a large‐scale, prospective, multicenter registry, enrolling patients from 32 expert coronary physiology centers across Japan to systematically investigate ANOCA endotypes using a standardized protocol. 16 The J‐CMD registry represents a unique and powerful resource to provide robust, real‐world data on the mechanisms of ANOCA.

In this initial report from the J‐CMD registry, we aimed to:

Determine the prevalence of EVS, MVS, and CMD (defined as CFR <2.0) in a large, contemporary cohort of patients with ANOCA;

Determine the prevalence of EVS, MVS, and CMD (defined as CFR <2.0) in a large, contemporary cohort of patients with ANOCA;

Characterize the clinical profiles and overlap patterns of these endotypes; and

Characterize the clinical profiles and overlap patterns of these endotypes; and

Investigate the relationship between endotype distribution and patient demographics, particularly sex and age.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data underlying this article will be shared on reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Study Population

We analyzed patients enrolled between August 2022 and September 2025 in the prospective, multicenter cohort, J‐CMD registry (UMIN000047609). This registry prospectively and consecutively enrolls patients presenting with chest symptoms suggestive of myocardial ischemia that are not explained by obstructive epicardial CAD. All patients underwent clinically indicated invasive coronary angiography and comprehensive coronary function testing, including physiological assessment using bolus thermodilution and spasm provocation testing, according to the Japanese Circulation Society guidelines 14 (Graphic Abstract). It is important to note that enrollment was not limited to patients fulfilling the strict definition of angina (which requires both symptoms and preexisting objective evidence of ischemia). Instead, the registry reflects a real‐world population where invasive testing was performed on the basis of clinical suspicion to establish a definitive diagnosis of ANOCA endotypes. A total of 1285 patients were enrolled in the registry. For the present analysis, we focused on the ANOCA population by excluding patients with obstructive epicardial CAD, defined as diameter stenosis ≥50% or fractional flow reserve ≤0.80 (n=301), as well as patients without physiological assessment of left anterior descending artery (LAD) (n=37). Patient‐reported symptoms were assessed using Seattle Angina Questionnaire‐19. 17 All patients enrolled in this registry provided written informed consent specific to the registry, in addition to the routine informed consent for cardiac catheterization. The study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Catheterization Protocol

In this registry, patients with angina were prospectively enrolled and underwent IDP, including microvascular functional assessment using bolus thermodilution and spasm provocation test. 18 , 19 Coronary angiography was performed primarily via radial artery, with femoral or brachial when necessary. Either a 5‐ or 6‐French guiding catheter was used for the assessment. The coronary spasm provocation test was conducted using intracoronary administration of acetylcholine or ergonovine in both the left and right coronary arteries. 14

For microvascular functional assessment, a pressure–temperature sensor–tipped guidewire (Pressure Wire Aeris, Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA) was used. Intracoronary nitrate (100–200 μg) was administered before each physiological measurement. The wire was advanced to the distal segment of the interrogated vessel. Three bolus injections of 3‐mL saline were performed to obtain thermodilution curves for resting mean transit time. Hyperemia was induced by either intravenous or intracoronary administration of a hyperemic agent (eg, adenosine, adenosine triphosphate, or nicorandil). 14 Although the specific agent was not systematically recorded, adenosine triphosphate was used in the majority of cases. During stable hyperemia, mean hyperemic arterial pressure and mean distal coronary pressure were measured to calculate fractional flow reserve. Hyperemic mean transit time was obtained in the same manner as resting mean transit time. The IMR was calculated as the product of mean distal coronary pressure and hyperemic mean transit time. CFR was determined by the thermodilution method as the ratio of resting mean transit time to hyperemic mean transit time. Pressure tracings were analyzed using fully automated offline software (Coroflow, Coroventis Research AB, Uppsala, Sweden). Spasm provocation testing is generally performed first; however, in this study, the sequence of testing was not specified and was left to the discretion of the operator.

Definition of Diagnosis

In this analysis, ANOCA endotypes were determined on the basis of IDP, defined as follows:

EVS: Chest pain accompanied by transient, total, or subtotal focal occlusion (>90% stenosis) of a coronary artery with signs/symptoms of myocardial ischemia (chest pain and ischemic ECG change) or 90% diffuse vasoconstriction induced in ≥2 contiguous segments of a coronary artery. 16

EVS: Chest pain accompanied by transient, total, or subtotal focal occlusion (>90% stenosis) of a coronary artery with signs/symptoms of myocardial ischemia (chest pain and ischemic ECG change) or 90% diffuse vasoconstriction induced in ≥2 contiguous segments of a coronary artery. 16

MVS: Chest pain and ECG change without angiographic criteria for EVS.

MVS: Chest pain and ECG change without angiographic criteria for EVS.

CMD: CFR <2.0.

CMD: CFR <2.0.

For clarity, IDP‐based diagnoses were grouped as follows; nonspasm, EVS, MVS, isolated CMD, and CMD with spasm. We applied this classification scheme to assess symptoms and to evaluate the prevalence of CMD across sex and age. Structural CMD was defined as CFR <2.0 and IMR ≥25, whereas functional CMD was defined as CFR <2.0 and IMR <25.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical data were expressed as numbers and percentages and compared using the χ2 test. The normality of the distributed values was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were compared using Student's t test or 1‐way ANOVA, as appropriate. Continuous data with a nonnormal distribution were expressed as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Data with a normal distribution were presented as mean±SD. Patient characteristics and physiological findings were compared between sexes and each diagnosis (Tables 1 and 2). Seattle Angina Questionnaire scores (angina frequency, physical limitation, quality of life, and summary score) were categorized as severe (<60), moderate (60 to <80), mild (80 to <100), and none/complete (100), and compared across diagnostic groups. 17 We examined the distribution of each diagnosis by sex and age. The cohort was stratified by age tertiles, and the proportions of diagnoses were compared across these groups. Correlations between age and physiological indices were assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient (Figure S1).

RESULTS

Spasm Provocation Agents and Spasm Type Distribution

Acetylcholine was used as the spasm provocation agent in 820 patients (86.6%), whereas ergonovine was used in 105 patients (11.1%). Epicardial and microvascular spasm were observed more frequently with acetylcholine than with ergonovine (epicardial vasospasm, 52.9% versus 26.5%; microvascular spasm, 20.7% versus 18.6%). The overall distribution of spasm types differed significantly between the 2 agents (χ2=37.7, P<0.001).

EVS was diagnosed in 30.0% of patients as single‐vessel involvement and in 19.4% as multivessel involvement. Because multivessel spasm provocation testing was performed at the operator's discretion and not systematically in all patients, the prevalence of multivessel EVS should be interpreted with caution. The distribution of EVS classification (single vessel versus multivessel) differed significantly between sexes (χ2=6.70, P=0.035), with women exhibiting a lower prevalence of single‐vessel EVS than men (26.6% versus 34.3%), while multivessel EVS occurred at similar rates in both sexes (20.1% versus 18.6%).

Characteristics of Patients and IDP Findings Between Sexes

This study included 947 patients (527 women [55.6%] and 420 men [44.4%]). Table 1 summarizes patient characteristics and IDP results by sex. Female patients tended to be older than male patients (age 64 [interquartile range, 55–75] versus 62 [interquartile range, 52–74] years, P=0.052) and had lower rates of diabetes and history of smoking than male patients. The prevalence of MVS was higher in female patients (23.9% versus 15.0%, P=0.003), whereas the prevalence of EVS was lower in female patients than in male patients (46.7% versus 52.9%). Median CFR was significantly lower in female patients (3.40 [interquartile range, 2.40–4.80] versus 3.70 [interquartile range, 2.50–5.20], P=0.032). CMD tended to be more frequently diagnosed in women (15.2% versus 11.7%, P=0.141). IMR was comparable between sexes. Fractional flow reserve was significantly higher in female patients (0.92 [interquartile range, 0.90–0.95] versus 0.90 [interquartile range, 0.87–0.94], P<0.001).

Patients and Lesion Characteristics According to IDP‐Based Phenotyping

In the total cohort, CMD was diagnosed in 13.6% (129 patients). Patients and lesion characteristics stratified by the presence of CMD are demonstrated in Table 2. Patients with CMD were significantly older and tended to be more frequently women, with a higher prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, history of ischemic heart disease, and chronic kidney disease. The prevalence of hyperlipidemia was comparable between the patients with and without CMD. The correlation between age and the physiological indices (CFR and IMR) in each sex is shown in Figure S1. Both indices had significant correlations across diagnostic groups with age in both sexes.

The characteristics stratified by the diagnosis on the coronary spasm provocation test are shown in Table 3. EVS was diagnosed in 49.4% (468 patients), and MVS in 20.0% (189 patients). Patients diagnosed as EVS were frequently men, whereas those with MVS were more frequently women. Age was comparable between EVS and MVS. In terms of vessel territory where spasm was diagnosed, EVS was diagnosed in 40.8% of patients with left main trunk/LAD involvement, 13.2% with left circumflex artery, and 18.8% with right coronary artery. MVS was diagnosed in 37.0% with left main trunk/LAD, 0.5% with left circumflex artery, and 3.9% with right coronary artery, respectively. Among patients with CMD, coexistence of EVS was observed in 38.0% and that of MVS was detected in 25.6%.

DISCUSSION

The present analysis of a large, prospective, multicenter registry of patients with ANOCA undergoing comprehensive IDP provides several key findings. First, a definitive underlying pathology, either coronary vasospasm or CMD, was identified in the majority of patients, with EVS being the most prevalent endotype. Second, a significant overlap exists between CMD and coronary vasospasm, a finding particularly prominent in women. Third, the distribution of these endotypes varied significantly by sex and age; MVS was more prevalent in younger women, while CMD was associated with older age and traditional cardiovascular risk factors. These findings underscore the heterogeneity of ANOCA and highlight the critical importance of a systematic, guideline‐recommended diagnostic approach for elucidating its complex pathophysiology.

Prevalence and Overlap of ANOCA Endotypes

The present study reveals that EVS was the most common single endotype, present in 44.2% of patients. This high prevalence is consistent with previous reports on vasospasm and may reflect not only ethnic differences in susceptibility among East Asian populations but also methodological variations in diagnostic testing, which remain a subject of ongoing discussion. 20 , 21 The high prevalence of EVS likely reflects the study population, which consisted of patients with angina but no functionally significant epicardial stenosis and was predominantly composed of Japanese individuals, a population with a historically higher prevalence of coronary vasospasm. 3 In addition, although the testing sequence was not prespecified, spasm provocation testing is often performed before vasodilator‐based functional assessment in Japan, 14 as prior vasodilator administration may attenuate spasm responses. These factors may have contributed to the higher observed prevalence of EVS than the previous study. 22 The distribution of spasm sites in the present study differed from that reported in the prior Japanese multicenter study. 23 The earlier study primarily enrolled patients with a high pretest probability of vasospastic angina, whereas the J‐CMD registry included a broader ANOCA population, including those with suspected microvascular dysfunction. This broader phenotypic spectrum may have influenced the distribution of spasm sites. The prevalence of CMD (defined as CFR <2.0) was 13.6%, aligning with findings from other major studies such as the CorMicA (Coronary Microvascular Angina) trial. 7 Importantly, the present study demonstrates a substantial overlap between these endotypes. The coexistence of any vasospasm with CMD was observed in nearly 10% of the entire cohort and was more frequent in women than in men (10.8% versus 6.0%). This finding challenges the conventional view of ANOCA endotypes as mutually exclusive entities and suggests that multiple pathophysiological mechanisms could coexist in a single patient. Clinically, these patients with an “overlap syndrome” may represent a particularly challenging cohort to manage, potentially requiring combination therapies that target both vasospasm and CMD. 24

Influence of Sex and Age

A striking finding of our study is the distinct age‐related distribution of endotypes in women, a pattern not observed in men. MVS was significantly more common in younger women, while the prevalence of CMD increased with age. The predilection of MVS for younger women may suggest a role for hormonal factors or a unique predisposition to endothelial hyperreactivity in this group, independent of classical atherosclerotic risk factors. In contrast, the association of CMD with older age, female sex, hypertension, and diabetes aligns with the concept that CMD is part of a systemic microvascular disease spectrum driven by traditional cardiovascular risk factors. 10 The lack of a clear age‐related trend in men suggests that the pathogenic drivers of ANOCA may differ fundamentally between the sexes. These sex‐specific patterns underscore the inadequacy of a uniform approach to ANOCA and highlight the need for diagnostic and therapeutic strategies tailored to individual patient demographics.

Symptoms According to IDP‐Based Phenotyping

Our analysis of patient‐reported symptoms also supports the value of endotyping. The significant differences in angina frequency across the groups, as assessed by the Seattle Angina Questionnaire, demonstrate that distinct ANOCA mechanisms are associated with varying symptom burdens. This link between endotype and symptom severity not only reinforces the clinical relevance of the diagnosis but also suggests its potential utility in risk stratification and guiding therapeutic decision making. 7

Clinical Implications

Our findings strongly reinforce current guideline recommendations for performing comprehensive IDP in patients with ANOCA. 12 Relying on clinical symptoms alone is insufficient to determine the underlying endotype, as demonstrated by the significant differences in Seattle Angina Questionnaire angina frequency across our diagnostically distinct groups. Identifying the specific mechanism via IDP, whether EVS, MVS, CMD, or an overlap, is an essential prerequisite for implementing endotype‐guided therapy. For instance, a patient with isolated EVS would be an ideal candidate for high‐dose calcium channel blockers, whereas one with CMD might derive greater benefit from statins and angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors. Patients with overlap endotypes would logically require a multifaceted therapeutic approach. By providing a definitive diagnosis, this invasive approach can help break the cycle of repeated testing, reduce health care costs, and, most importantly, improve patient outcomes and quality of life, as demonstrated in the CorMicA trial. 6 , 7

Extending comprehensive coronary function testing to patients with obstructive CAD represents an important next step, as coexisting vasomotor and microvascular dysfunction may substantially influence prognosis after revascularization. In addition, the J‐CMD registry is designed with planned long‐term follow‐up of up to 5 years, which will allow evaluation of the prognostic impact of these endotypes.

Limitations

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, the observational nature of the registry means that our findings demonstrate associations but not causality. Second, the cohort was recruited from specialized centers performing IDP, which may introduce a selection bias toward patients with more severe or refractory symptoms. Consequently, the observed prevalence of these endotypes may not be generalizable to the broader ANOCA population. Third, our definition of CMD relied solely on a CFR <2.0. Incorporating additional indices, such as IMR, might have yielded different prevalence rates and identified a different subgroup of patients with microvascular dysfunction. Fourth, the present study was conducted exclusively in a Japanese population. Given the known ethnic differences in the prevalence of vasospasm, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to other ethnic groups. A large‐scale, international prospective registry is needed to confirm these findings, investigate ethnic variations, and further refine personalized therapeutic strategies. Fifth, the sequence of spasm provocation testing and microvascular functional assessment was not standardized and was left to operator discretion in this real‐world registry. Because vasoactive agents, including nitrates, may attenuate vasospastic responses, the testing order could have influenced the results, although washout periods were routinely implemented. Sixth, physiological assessment was limited to the LAD, whereas spasm provocation testing was performed in both coronary systems. This mismatch may have introduced bias and could result in underestimation of microvascular dysfunction in non‐LAD territories. Finally, this report is a cross‐sectional analysis focused on diagnostic endotypes. Forthcoming longitudinal data from the J‐CMD registry will be crucial for determining the prognostic implications of each endotype and evaluating the efficacy of tailored management strategies.

CONCLUSIONS

In this large‐scale registry of patients with ANOCA evaluated with a comprehensive, guideline‐recommended protocol, we were able to demonstrate that coronary vasospasm and CMD are common, frequently overlapping conditions with distinct sex‐ and age‐related patterns. These findings highlight the complex, heterogeneous nature of ANOCA and strongly reinforce the importance of invasive functional testing to move beyond a simple exclusion of obstructive CAD. Accurate endotyping is the cornerstone of a personalized management strategy, paving the way for mechanism‐based therapies aimed at improving both symptoms and long‐term outcomes in this challenging patient population.
