Enablers and barriers for allied health professionals working in the Australian aged care or adult disability sector: a scoping review
Authors: Kristy Robson, Jodie Kleinschafer, Elsa Dent, Stephanie Nelson, Rahul Ganguly
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ageing population and increasing rates of chronic disease create many challenges to the delivery of high-quality care for older adults and adults with disabilities. Allied health professionals provide a critical role in supporting quality of life and independence in both the aged care and the adult disability sectors, yet recruitment and retention of allied health professionals in these sectors remains a major health workforce concern. This scoping review aimed to identify the enablers and barriers influencing allied health professionals to work in the aged care or adult disability sectors in Australia, as well as to highlight successful strategies for their recruitment and retention.MethodsThis scoping review was conceptualised using the Population Concept Context Framework and conducted using methods as described by Arksey and O’Malley 2005 and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). A search of electronic databases, including Medline, Emcare, CINAHL, Scopus, Psych Info, university platforms EbscoHost and ProQuest Health and Medicine, ProQuest Business and grey literature databases was undertaken initially in March-June 2023 and repeated in August 2024. Content and thematic analysis of the key findings were employed to provide a contextualised and comprehensive understanding of study aim.ResultsTwenty papers met the inclusion criteria, including 5 cross-sectional studies, 13 qualitative studies, 1 mixed-method study, and 1 senate enquiry. From the included articles several workforce related enablers and barriers, related to allied health professionals working in the aged care and adult disability sectors, were reported. As a result of the thematic analysis, four overarching themes were identified that incorporated both enablers and barriers, including: (a) supporting clinicians to work in diverse and complex environments, (b) developing the pipeline to build workforce capacity, (c) navigating system-wide challenges, and (d) appreciating the added complexity of rural practice.ConclusionsFindings from this review provide sector employers, government, and policy makers with an understanding of the potential enablers and barriers encountered by allied health professionals working in the aged care and disability sectors in Australia. The review suggests that some barriers could become enablers when appropriately supported, which highlights the need for future research on this topic. This information may also help to inform the National Allied Health Workforce Strategy in Australia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13713-6.Keywords: Workforce, Allied health, Recruitment and retention, Aged care, Adult disability

Background

Allied health professionals belong to a group of professions who assist with the diagnosis of health conditions and provide evidence-based interventions to maintain and optimise the physical, social and mental health and wellbeing of the individual [1]. As such, allied health professionals play an important role in supporting the quality of life and functional health of adults living with disabilities and older adults accessing aged care services [2, 3]. However, over the past two decades, the complexity of assisting older people and adults living with disabilities in Australia has increased due to social policy changes alongside the ageing population and increases in comorbidities. Both the aged care and adult disability sectors have a central role in focusing on the individual’s quality of life and have similar approaches to social and economic policy frameworks within Australia [4]. The introduction of the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) has been the most significant social policy reform to better support individuals, including adults with disabilities [5]. Similarly, the Australian Royal Commission into Aged Care [6], now reflected in the new Aged Care Act 2024 [7], called for a rights-based, person-centred approach that enables older Australians to live active and meaningful lives with self-determination [6, 8]. As such, allied health professionals working in both these sectors have similar roles but also experience similar workforce challenges.

These challenges are evident broadly across both community-based aged care and residential aged care homes (RACHs), where allied health professionals play a critical role in supporting reablement and independence [2, 9]. While recruitment and retention issues persist across both settings, RACH’s are particularly impacted due to systemic barriers such as restrictive funding models and limited integration of allied health into core care teams [10, 11]. Additionally, older people accessing RACHs are comparatively much older - around half are now aged ≥ 84 years [9], and have increasingly complex care needs, with nearly 90% of Australian aged care residents either frail or pre-frail [12, 13]. In the disability context, the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) has transformed disability support services to better support people with disability to gain greater independence and improved quality of life [14]. Consequently, the NDIS places increased emphasis on individualised, person-centred care, with the person with disability as the primary decision-maker rather than services determined and directed by state and territory agencies [15]. In this changing context, allied health professionals are seen as essential to the healthcare team in supporting the health and wellbeing of individuals accessing both aged care and adult disability services. Furthermore, allied health professionals are one of the largest groups of NDIS registered providers, resulting in an increased workload burden on this group [16, 17]. However, there is a significant workforce shortages of allied health professionals in both these sectors in Australia, due to recruitment and retention challenges, and poor distribution of available workers [6, 9]. This is particularly problematic in rural areas where more than a quarter of the Australian population live; however, they have poorer health outcomes and access to healthcare than their metropolitan counterparts [18]. In addition to recruitment and retention challenges, barriers to accessing healthcare are reportedly due to geographic spread and low population density, limited infrastructure, and higher service delivery costs, potentially delaying access to preventive and primary healthcare [19].

In the Australian aged care sector, there are a number of structural barriers to providing quality outcomes by allied health professionals [10]. While aged care funding models in Australia have recently changed to focus more on promoting restorative and reablement care [20], which is a key component of allied health practice, specific allied health funding has not substantially increased to support greater involvement of allied health, particularly in RACHs. This lack of funding is a major issue given that the allied health workforce is essential for delivering the high-value, evidence-based and multifaceted care that older adults require [21, 22]. Moreover, the existing national shortage of allied health professionals in aged care [23, 24], contributes to lengthy wait times for therapy services, which compromises the safety and wellbeing of older Australians [25]. To combat this issue, an estimated additional 38,000 allied health professionals are needed over the next ten years to provide a minimum standard of care for older Australians [26].

The adult disability sector faces similar challenges. The diversity of needs and the evolving NDIS landscape demand a flexible and skilled allied health workforce [27]. Yet there also remains a shortage of allied health professionals working in the adult disability sector [28]. It should also be noted that these two sectors are not mutually exclusive, with adults with disability under the age of 65 living in RACHs. Despite the significant allied health workforce shortage in both the aged care and adult disability sectors, research on the topic is only emerging [22, 29]. There remains a distinct lack of knowledge regarding the workforce related enablers and barriers faced by allied health professionals employed in these sectors. Given the increasing workforce need, such knowledge is urgently needed to inform public health policies and interventions to recruit and retain Australia’s allied health workforce in the aged care and adult disability sectors. Therefore, the aim of this scoping review was to explore the enablers and barriers that influence allied health professionals working in the aged care and adult disability sectors in Australia and identify any strategies to support sustaining this workforce.

Methods

Protocol and registration

Scoping studies are a useful way of examining the available evidence and contextualising current knowledge in a field. This review used Arksey and O’Malley’s [30] five step methodological framework to guide the process and incorporated the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [31]. We used the Population Concept Context Framework [32] to identify and formulate our research question and registered the protocol with the Open Science Framework [33]. The original protocol involved reviewing articles from a range of specified countries; however, as there were sufficient articles identified, the focus of this review was reframed to examine the Australian context for an in-depth analysis, given the specific policy and social system in this context.

Eligibility criteria

Population

No definitive list of allied health professions exists. For this review, we used the allied health professions as listed by the Allied Health Professions Australia [34, 35] and the Australian Government Department of Health and Aged Care [36] as a starting point and included studies involving allied health professions commonly involved in the Australian aged care or adult disability sector, with a re-enablement focus (Table 1). Professions related to medicine nursing, dentistry, pharmacy, radiation therapy, paramedicine, and sonography were not included. Allied health assistants and allied health students were included.

Inclusion criteria

All full-text journal articles, reports and reviews from journals, government documents or industry websites or sources were considered eligible for inclusion. Due to the Australian context and the specific policy reforms of My Aged Care and the National Disability Health Insurance Scheme which have specific impacts for Australian allied health professionals working in these sectors, the review was limited to the English language and included studies involving Australian settings. In line with the rollout of these reforms, the review was limited to a date restriction from 2013, when My Aged Care was implemented, to August 2024. For studies that included a mix of practice areas, only data explicitly related to aged care or adult disability sectors were extracted and analysed. Studies were excluded during full-text review if relevant data specific to these sectors could not be clearly distinguished.

Exclusion criteria

Protocols, conference abstracts and proceedings, editorials and commentaries were excluded, as were studies with a primary focus on clinical treatment or outcomes and studies that did not have a focus on the aged care or adult disability sector.

Search strategy

A comprehensive electronic search of databases covering health and business was initially conducted in March-June 2023 and re-run again in August 2024 to include any recent studies. The following databases were searched through the Charles Sturt University library: MEDLINE and Emcare [OVID], Scopus, PsychINFO and CINAHL, Business Source Complete, Consumer Health Complete, Business Health Elite, Health Source – Consumer Edition, Psychology and Behavioural Science Collection and SocINDEX [EBSCO Host] and the ProQuest Health & Medicine (including Nursing & Allied Health Database, Public Health Database, Psychology Database, Consumer Heath Database, Career Technical Education Database: Health & Medicine, Healthcare Administration Database, Research Library: Health & Medicine), and ProQuest Business platforms.

Using the population, concept and context framework, search terms and synonyms were used to create a search strategy in consultation with a senior university librarian (Appendix 1). Key word searching was used, and terms were mapped to Subject Headings. Synonyms were combined with the Boolean operator “OR” for parallel concepts, and then “AND” to link concepts. Truncation characters were used. An example of the search strategy is shown in Appendix 2. A grey literature search for additional studies was conducted in Mednar and Google Scholar (see Appendix 3 for details). The Google Scholar search was performed using advanced search techniques and was limited to the first ten pages of results to ensure relevance. Additionally, the references of included studies were screened by hand for additional references.

Literature selection

Search results from each platform or database were imported into EndNote and then individually imported into Covidence systematic review software. Removal of duplicates occurred automatically at importation by the Covidence software. During the initial round of screening in 2023, titles and abstracts were independently screened by at least two authors (KR, SN, JK, ED) according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, which had an inter-rater reliability proportional agreement of between 93% and 96%. Any further studies uploaded in August 2024 were screened by only one author (KR or SN) at the title and abstract stage; however, all full-text screening was conducted in duplicate. All conflicts were resolved by team discussion to achieve consensus. Relevant full-text studies were then reviewed independently by two authors (ED, RG, JK, SN), with an inter-rater reliability proportional agreement of between 78% and 92%. Conflicts at this stage were resolved by a third author (KR). Data extraction was undertaken by at least two authors (KR, JK, ED) using a custom extraction template developed within Covidence. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion to resolve any conflicts. The following information was collected: author, year, country, aims, study design, discipline, sector type, recruitment method and participant characteristics, study inclusion criteria, barriers and enablers, findings related to reducing barriers, findings related to enhancing recruitment and retention of allied health professionals in the adult aged care or adult disability sector. Whether a study included a focus on barriers and/or enablers was determined implicitly by the research team via the screening process of the full-text paper (if it could not be determined from the title and abstract). Any uncertainty was resolved by team discussion as outlined above.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data extraction and synthesis was a two-stage process beginning with content analysis before moving onto thematic analysis [37]. The content analysis provided insight into the characteristics and contexts of the studies included in the review, which also highlighted what had been and had not been investigated. The thematic analysis provided a more in-depth analysis of the content of each paper and the common themes that emerged from the papers. It also allowed for the contrast and comparison of findings across the different studies (as characterised in the content analysis).

The content analysis was undertaken to determine the categories of enablers and barriers identified in the included studies in order to represent clusters of responses [37, 38]. Using the simple three step approach, as described by Elo & Kyngas [38], a deductive analytical process of coding and identification of categories was conducted by one author (JK) and then checked with the broader team. Studies that reported only quantitative findings were included in the content analysis to capture relevant workforce-related enablers and barriers.

The thematic analysis followed a recursive process, involving familiarisation and coding of the data, searching for and reviewing themes, and writing up the analytic narrative process [39, 40]. While the study by Roberts et al. [22] further informed the process of coding the qualitative data and theme development. This analytic approach provided a method for organising and interpreting the data to identify common patterns among the identified papers, creating a more comprehensive understanding of the topic of interest. Thematic analysis was applied only to studies with qualitative data to explore more nuanced perspectives and recurring patterns in greater depth. The thematic analysis of the extracted data was conducted by one author (KR), then the themes were considered and refined in a whole team discussion, ultimately leading to the identification of four overarching themes. Coding and theme development were undertaken using Microsoft® Excel® and Word®.

This scoping review was conducted by an interdisciplinary team of five academics, including three allied health professionals (physiotherapy, exercise physiology and podiatry) with extensive experience providing aged care and disability services [41, 42]. Given the professional backgrounds and extensive sector experience of the research team, we acknowledged the potential for interpretive bias. To enhance reflexivity, coding and theme development were undertaken through an iterative and collaborative process involving regular team discussions. These discussions served to challenge assumptions, validate interpretations, and ensure that thematic findings were grounded in the data rather than influenced by pre-existing views.

Results

A total of 1814 studies were identified through database searching, with a total of 20 studies identified for inclusion in the review (See Fig. 1). No assessment was made of the quality of the studies as this is not an objective of a scoping review.

Barriers and enablers identified in the review

The most frequently mentioned enablers across the sectors were access to relevant professional development, which was highlighted as an enabler in ten papers [22, 25, 44, 47, 50, 52–54, 56, 57]; positive exposure to the aged care and adult disability sectors during pre-registration training [25, 43, 45, 55, 56]; and professional satisfaction [25, 45, 46, 50, 51, 55]. The most frequently mentioned barriers across both sectors were: the underutilisation of allied health professionals in the aged care and disability sectors [11, 22, 29, 43, 44, 48–51], and a lack of professional development opportunities for allied health professionals working in these areas [11, 27, 44, 46, 49, 50, 54, 57]. The aged care and adult disability sectors were perceived as “unappealing” with lower rates of pay for allied health professionals [11, 43, 45, 51–53, 55] and a lack of positive exposure to these sectors during pre-registration training [22, 43, 49, 51, 55, 56].

Factors that were highlighted as barriers by studies with a focus on aged care only included: access to medical records and difficulties communicating across and between healthcare functions [46–48, 51], the administrative burden associated with delivering allied health services within the sector [46, 48, 50], the inability to provide care as desired [11, 46, 51], professional isolation [11, 44, 49, 51] and the lack of empirical support for allied health interventions in aged care [11, 29, 44, 49]. Identified enablers for allied health professionals specifically focused on the aged care sector included resources such as equipment and space [11, 29].

Thematic analysis

Listing enablers and barriers highlights their relevance, but content analysis alone cannot capture the complexity of these sectors or the interconnectedness between issues. For this reason, we then conducted a thematic analysis of the data extracted from the 20 included papers and identified four broad themes: a) Supporting clinicians to work in diverse and complex environments; b) Developing the pipeline to build workforce capacity; c) Navigating system wide challenges; and d) Appreciating the added complexity of rural practice. The themes presented below were informed by the content analysis findings (Table 3), with key barriers and enablers mapped across themes to highlight their interconnection and influence on allied health workforce outcomes. Additionally, as some of the barriers and enablers are interdependent, they are discussed together.

Supporting clinicians to work in diverse and complex environments

The opportunity to work with complex and diverse caseloads was a key enabler that encouraged allied health professionals to work in either the aged care or adult disability sectors. This often meant working in multidisciplinary teams or having strong working relationships with other health professionals to enable quality outcomes for clients [47, 52, 55]. While staff enjoyed this aspect of their work, some indicated that this complexity could overwhelm professionals transitioning into the sectors, particularly if they are not adequately prepared [55]. This theme is closely aligned with barriers and enablers identified in the content analysis, such as access to professional development, supervision, burnout, and low remuneration (Table 3). The studies highlighted that one way to better support allied health professionals in managing this complexity and reducing burnout was through regular and timely access to quality professional development or mentoring [44, 52, 53], which is not always available, particularly for allied health professionals working in the aged care sector [29, 49, 50]. Importantly, the ability to access additional financial support to attend relevant professional development was seen as an important driver of retention, given the lower levels of remuneration often associated with these sectors [53].

Regular access to professional development was also seen as particularly important for reducing isolation and increasing support mechanisms for allied health professionals [54], as well as effectively transitioning new graduates into the sector [49]. However, a key limiting factor is the current funding models, which often prioritise billable hours over non-billable professional development [27, 44, 56]. Access to professional development that builds relevant skills and awareness of sector-specific risks was viewed as critical for retention [43, 44, 57].

The ability to work flexibly and with autonomy was seen as a highly valued aspect of working in both the aged care and adult disability sectors [52, 53]. When there was a lack of flexibility, this posed significant barriers to retention, particularly given the predominantly female workforce, often with caring responsibilities [52]. While autonomy was seen as a key enabler, the studies highlighted the lack of sector specific, evidenced clinical guidelines, assessment tools and access to resources to inform clinical practice, limited the ability of allied health professionals to independently provide consistency / continuity of care, and advocacy for their role in providing clinical services in the aged care and adult disability sectors [29, 44, 57]. It was also highlighted that the motivation of allied health professionals to work in these sectors differed depending on the individual circumstances; therefore, tailoring recruitment and retention strategies was important, rather than implementing a one-size-fits-all approach [53].

Developing the pipeline to build workforce capacity

Several studies in this review highlighted the importance of effectively developing the aged care and adult disability pipeline from student to clinician, to ensure sustainability within the sectors. Content analysis categories such as limited positive student placements and a lack of confidence entering the sector (Table 3) are reflected in this theme, which highlights challenges in attracting and preparing new graduates. These sectors were seen as unattractive, with graduates often applying out of necessity rather than genuine interest [55]. Limited exposure to high quality, positive experiences during student placements also contributes to reduced awareness of the benefits of working in these sectors and requires appropriate investment [43, 44, 55]. Limited exposure through placements reduces students’ confidence and reinforces stereotypes, which shapes career decisions [25, 55]. For new graduates who enter these sectors, the limited undergraduate exposure challenges their ability to easily transition into these sectors and impacts retention [48, 56].

Leveraging the multidisciplinary scope of practice in these sectors, is one way to better support students to prepare for aged care and adult disability sectors through specific inclusion within the undergraduate curriculum [57]. Scaffolding undergraduate curricula to develop students’ understanding of sector complexity is essential. Drawing on students’ lived experiences with older adults may also foster more positive learning and preparation [25, 45, 55]. Furthermore, ensuring that undergraduate interprofessional practice experiences are authentic and highlighting the scope of practice in both the aged care and adult disability space can provide students with an understanding of the positive benefits of working with these clients [43, 44, 55]. However, there needs to be appropriate supervisor support and funding mechanisms to facilitate high quality supervision models in these sectors [55]. This is likely to result in new graduates being better prepared for the transition into these sectors, while enabling more positive attitudes and confidence and facilitating greater recruitment and retention opportunities [56, 57]. For this process to be effective, academic staff and clinicians must have the appropriate training and support to develop and deliver genuine interprofessional experiences [56].

Additionally, targeting a curriculum that focuses on understanding the aged care and disability business and funding models was also seen as important for undergraduate students to transition into the workforce [27]. Finally, given the structure of the current workforce in the aged care and adult disability sectors, one study also identified the need to ensure that allied health assistant programs have the necessary skills to effectively work with allied health professionals within these contexts [22]. This was seen as particularly important in the areas of appropriately recognising clinical risk, managing competing demands and interacting with the team, given the nature of the work [22].

Navigating system wide challenges

Several studies highlighted a broad range of system level factors that contribute to the challenges of effective recruitment and retention of allied health clinicians in both the aged care and disability sectors. This theme draws on barriers including funding structures, underutilisation of allied health roles, and administrative complexity, as shown in Table 3. It is well known that these sectors are not attractive choices, given the lower pay rates and the often negative perceptions around career identity and career status, particularly in aged care compared to other allied health areas [43, 52, 53]. Particularly within the aged care sector, there is also a perception that allied health professionals are underutilised due to a lack of integration into the sector, which often limits the scope of practice and a sense of fulfilment due to being undervalued or not being seen as a necessary part of the team by the wider organisation [29, 43, 47, 48]. This is also constrained by limited career structures for allied health professionals in these sectors [53], which often results in clinicians leaving clinical roles for management positions, thus creating clinical service gaps [52]. In addition to common broader organisational factors, especially in the aged care sector, there may be a lack of clear processes, policies, and communication pathways to support allied health professionals to effectively integrate into multidisciplinary teams [29, 46, 47]. Supportive management structures that foster a positive work culture is likely to be a useful enabler for allied health to participate in multidisciplinary practice [50]. Funding models often produce a part-time, siloed workforce, leading to high staff turnover and poor continuity of care [11, 49, 51]. Additionally, the structure of funding models in some cases have led to questionable ethical reporting, where there has been a disincentive to report improvement in client outcomes [49]. System reform to embed allied health workforce as employees, particularly in aged care facilities may foster greater multidisciplinary practice and provide greater continuity of care for clients, which could lead to better retention of the workforce [11, 51], with one study highlighting that staff were optimistic with the new funding policy changes moving to a more strengths-based approach [49].

Both the aged care and disability sectors are considered structurally complicated, which creates regulatory complexity and additional administrative burdens on clinicians [46, 57]. This results in a high burden of administration and compliance tasks at the expense of service provision [46, 50]. While there is an acceptance that quality services are required, the additional administration burdens and costs associated with compliance impact the retention of allied health professionals in these sectors [57]. Additionally, inflexible funding models have also been shown to limit innovative approaches, risking sustainability and creating barriers for appropriate engagement and oversight of the assistant workforce, particularly in the aged care sector [22, 27, 57]. The flexibility to instigate new models of care was seen as a stronger motivator to build job satisfaction and workforce retention among allied health professionals [51]. Adding to the challenges associated with these specific sectors is the constant change occurring at the policy and system levels, which results in difficulty with workforce planning and development [27]. Pressure for allied health professionals to keep up to date with the changing regulatory landscape can result in clinicians exiting these sectors and leaving vulnerable people with limited options [46, 57]. This is also often compounded by a high percentage of part-time workers [47].

The studies in this review also highlighted that funding structures in these sectors also tended to limit a more person-centred approach and could result in low-value care options [53, 57]. Aged care and disability funding structures often created tension between the client and clinician and limited the autonomy of allied health professionals to make effective decisions with a focus on reablement, particularly in aged care [29, 44, 48]. Furthermore, the nature of services is changing with increasing focus on consumer choice, often increasing the time required to spend with clients navigating these choices, which may not be billable and can result in some services no longer being provided due to financial viability [27]. The inability of aged care funding models to influence a greater utilisation of the assistant allied health workforce with appropriate professional oversight, such as that seen in the disability sector, also limits the type and scope of allied health services provided in the aged care sector, further impacting professional satisfaction and retention [22, 29, 44].

Appreciating the added complexity of rural practice

The uniqueness of rural practice was also identified as influencing recruitment and retention in several studies in this review. The rural-specific barriers and enablers such as long travel distances, professional isolation, and flexible work arrangements (Table 3) inform this theme. Professional isolation in rural areas was a significant issue and was often exacerbated in both the aged care and disability sectors [52]. Conversely, incorporating systems that provided professional support and development to allied health professionals in rural locations was seen as a key enabler for retention [52]. The additional complexity associated with accessing relevant services, referral pathways and the consistency of service provision also created increased burdens on rural allied health professionals as they navigate resource allocation and prioritisation of care, all of which impact retention [27, 46, 52, 56]. Having to travel long distances and spend several nights away when providing services rurally and remotely was also seen as a barrier to retention, particularly for a predominantly female workforce [52, 53].

Due to the nature of rural health practice, there were additional impacts on both allied health professionals and clients engaging in aged care and adult disability services as a consequence of geographic distances, socio-economic factors, and the sociocultural diversity of rural Australia [54]. These challenges require clinicians to spend more time identifying alternative and flexible approaches that support their broad client base [54]. However, the burdens faced by rural allied health professionals and the resulting impacts on clients, have been compounded by limited recognition at a government level that these rural challenges require tailored policy and funding responses [54].

Innovative and flexible approaches were seen as a driver to support rural recruitment and retention of allied health professionals [46]. For example, private-public business models were seen as an opportunity to minimise the impact of service provision in rural locations as they enabled greater access to more allied health professionals and services, particularly in geographic locations where there were challenges with sustainability due to the population size of the community [48]. However, system-level barriers, such as poor inter-organisational communication, appointment management, and medical record access, can undermine these approaches [47, 48] and have limited effectiveness. Therefore, greater system flexibility is required to promote the benefits of working in rural areas to allied health professionals, such as the capacity to leverage off existing structures and networks to create an individualised service that can best support the clinician and provide effective health outcomes to aged care and adult disability clients within that community, rather than a one-size-fits-all all approach [54].

Discussion

The scoping review investigated enablers and barriers influencing allied health professionals working in the aged care and adult disability sectors. However, none of the studies specifically focused on identifying successful strategies that supported the recruitment and retention of allied health professionals in these two sectors. The synthesised findings from the review were grouped across four overarching themes: a) Supporting clinicians to work in diverse and complex environments, b) Developing the pipeline to build workforce capacity, c) Navigating system-wide challenges, and d) Appreciating the added complexity of rural practice. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first scoping literature review that examined barriers and enablers for allied health professionals working in the aged care or adult disability sector in Australia. The review findings offer policymakers, health-service leaders, and other stakeholders insights into the factors that enable and inhibit the recruitment, engagement, and retention of allied health professionals in these care sectors.

The systemic complexity of aged care and adult disability sectors emerged as a key barrier to recruiting and retaining allied health professionals. This reflects international evidence, with a recent umbrella review highlighting poor supervision, lack of recognition, unclear roles, and value misalignment as key drivers of workforce turnover across healthcare and welfare sectors in high-income countries [58]. Our findings also align with Peterson et al. [59], who identified a limited evidence base for effective allied health retention strategies, particularly in rural areas, and called for structural and policy reform. Within the aged care sector, increasing rates of chronic disease require more specialised staff care to support the complex needs of older people, creating further pressure on an already strained system [60]. Allied health professionals can make a valuable contribution in assisting older people with these complex needs, but more effective integrated models of care are required that reduce fragmented or ad hoc approaches that often have high administration and regulatory burden [61, 62]. As highlighted in the Royal Commission into Aged Care, access to allied health care, which is critical to maintaining the mobility and quality of life of older Australians in residential aged care, is limited and may be influenced by funding arrangements [6]. In a qualitative study undertaken by Muelenbroeks et al. [11], they found that Australian allied health professionals working in residential aged care facilities perceived that under-funding of allied health services led to an assessment-only focus rather than preventative or supportive care interventions. The introduction of the Aged Care Act 2024 further reinforces the need for multidisciplinary, person-centred care [7]. The Act introduces a rights-based framework and promotes a single-entry assessment model, both of which create an opportunity to strengthen the role of allied health professionals in supporting reablement, functional independence, and quality of life for older people [7].

The common nature of the workforce in these sectors, largely comprising contract or part-time staff, was also considered to contribute to further professional isolation and siloing for the allied health workforce, potentially limiting their ability to actively engage in multidisciplinary teams. These findings align with the recently released National Allied Health Workforce Strategy, which identifies aged care and disability as priority areas requiring urgent workforce attention [8]. The strategy calls for more sustainable workforce models that embed allied health professionals within multidisciplinary teams, highlighting the need for stronger career pathways, enhanced rural access, and improved supervision, particularly in settings where allied health roles remain undervalued or fragmented [63]. Individualised funding models, while promoting choice, can create competition that discourages collaboration and increases administrative burden, diverting time from clinical care [31, 37, 38, 64]. Additionally, the recent Disability Royal Commission has also been critiqued for overlooking the essential role of allied health in disability services, reinforcing the perception of under-recognition within national policy [65]. Emerging policy changes require the allied health workforce to adapt rapidly [27] but inadequate funding models, especially those limiting collaborative practice, contribute to professional isolation, burnout and inconsistent care, hindering staff retention in both sectors.

The review findings highlighted limited informal and formal support structures for allied health professionals as one of the significant barriers to long-term retention. The participants in several studies in this review indicated that a lack of professional supervision, limited career progression opportunities, and limited opportunities to network with other professionals were among the primary reasons for not staying in their jobs. The ability to access and receive support for regular professional development and mentoring is a key enabler for both recruitment and retention, particularly for new graduates and practitioners working in rural communities, in other studies [66–68]. The review indicated the importance of receiving quality supervisor support to work effectively in a multidisciplinary environment. Mentorship to navigate complex settings and support career planning and progression was also highly valued in these sectors [47, 52, 55]. The ability to access mentors and support during induction and other work phases has been shown to build a sense of belonging among employees [69]. Conversely, findings from this review highlighted that the absence of mentorship and structured workplace support led to staff burnout and attrition over time [55]. It would seem that a focus on quality mentoring or career coaching is not common practice in the disability and aged care sectors, as highlighted by a number of studies [27, 70–73]. Additionally, the challenges, benefits, and importance of professional support for allied health professionals working in rural and remote contexts have also been reported in a Australian context [74]. The implementation of an Allied Health Professional Enhancement program within rural and remote regions of Queensland, Australia, is reported to be of benefit to allied health professional participants, their health service and health care service delivery. Although effective supervision through the program was reported to be affected by time, technology and organisational factors [75, 76]. Organisational and funding structures that support ongoing professional development and mentorship can build workforce capability and improve outcomes, satisfaction, and retention [77, 78]. Further research on the effects of mentoring, structured career pathways, and regular professional networking on job retention and satisfaction of allied health professionals working in these sectors should be considered [21, 25], a finding also highlighted in the broader literature [79, 80].

The provision of quality placement experiences for allied health students has been a long-term strategy to address workforce issues across a range of sectors, particularly in rural areas [81, 82]. However, student perceptions of certain areas of the workforce, such as aged care, can greatly influence their decision to apply for roles in this sector [45, 83]. Schlatter et al. [84] reinforce this, noting that students are more likely to choose roles that offer structured support, mentorship, and professional growth, features often lacking or inconsistently provided in aged care and disability placements. Additionally, the structural policy changes occurring in the disability space require allied health graduates to possess a range of skills and knowledge to transition effectively into this complex sector [29]. The findings of this review indicate that university programs may not provide undergraduate allied health students with adequate positive exposure to the variety and benefits of working in the aged care sector. Such exposure could help to counter negative attitudes [45, 51] and the perceived complexity of systems in adult disability sectors [27]. Furthermore, the review found that some allied health professionals expressed dissatisfaction with their professional degree program regarding their level of preparation to work in these sectors [44, 55], with entry-level qualifications often inadequate for transition into sectors that have complex service and funding structures [27, 62]. As a result, new graduates may need additional support and mentorship, which could be difficult to provide under current funding models that prioritise billable hours [27, 42, 54, 55]. The recent NDIS National Workforce Plan report calls for better connection between universities, government and disability providers to recognise opportunities within the NDIS and to establish and support positive student NDIS placements [85]. Further research is required to understand the career decisions and pathways allied health students consider and what pre- and post-employment strategies would better support them in effectively transitioning into these sectors.

Factors that facilitated or impeded allied health professionals working in the aged care and adult disability sectors tended to be multifaceted, malleable, dynamic, and interlinked, depending on the complexity of the working conditions, rurality, and organisational setting [86–88]. Aspects such as flexible work conditions, job autonomy, collaborative multidisciplinary team structure, career progression opportunities, and timely access to continuing professional development programs were identified as enablers of the employment retention of allied health professionals. While factors such as large clinical caseloads, administrative burden, negotiating complex systems and funding models, large travel distances to deliver services, and inadequate services acted as barriers to the employment and retention of allied health professionals. Although some of these barriers and enablers can be seen more broadly among allied health professionals working in other sectors, they are exacerbated within the aged care and disability sectors due to the range of system level issues and stigma faced. However, there is limited longitudinal research on the multidimensional aspects, including personal, social, and organisational factors, that facilitate long-term employment of the allied health workforce in this sector and more research of this type is required to understand the evolving enablers and barriers of recruitment and retention. Cross-sectional quantitative surveys and qualitative interviews, conducted at one point in time, were the most used methods. To address the current shortage of allied health practitioners, there is a National Allied Health Workforce Strategy currently in progress, collecting evidence to understand factors that influence allied health workforce supply, demand and quality [63]. Nevertheless, further research examining the career journeys of allied health professionals in long-term employment may be beneficial in enhancing our understanding of the multifaceted factors relating to the recruitment and retention of this population. Understanding what allied health professionals value, alongside strategies for addressing known barriers, can inform responsive recruitment policy initiatives that can be pilot trialled before large-scale implementation.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this scoping review is that it is the first review of its type to examine the range of factors associated with the recruitment and retention of allied health professionals in the ageing and/or adult disability sectors across Australia. Although this review utilised multiple databases and grey literature, searching other databases may have yielded other relevant published papers relevant to the aims of this scoping review. Also, due to resource constraints, title and abstract screening for the updated search was conducted by a single reviewer, which may have introduced a small degree of selection bias, although all full-text screening was conducted in duplicate. In addition, allied health encompasses a broad range of professions. Due to the limited studies identified, there may be enablers and barriers unique to specific allied health groups that have not been identified. Furthermore, this review only included studies that focused on an Australian context. As well as the limited number of papers identified and the complexity across both the aged care and adult disability sectors, and diversity across allied health professions, findings reported should be viewed with caution when considering sector and profession specific considerations and from a global perspective. No studies were found that focused explicitly on successful strategies to support recruitment and retention more broadly across the aged care and adult disability sectors.

Future research

The current research landscape offers limited insight into the characteristics and motivations of allied health professionals who choose careers in aged care and disability. Two studies briefly mentioned age and gender demographics (e.g., the ‘phenotypical’ allied health professional who stayed working in aged care had a mean age of 38 years and was most likely female). Future research should explore the motivations and backgrounds of allied health professionals working in these sectors, including the reasons why they chose to work in these sectors and what choices and supports would have facilitated their entry into and ongoing practice in these sectors.

Future research regarding appropriate models of professional development is also needed, given current approaches may not be entirely sufficient to equip allied health professionals to care for the specific health needs of older adults and adults with disabilities. It is noteworthy that some early work has been undertaken implementing and trialling a professional support program in rural and remote Australia [75, 76]. However, there is a need to investigate other professional development models to determine which best incentivise allied health professionals’ participation in the aged care and disability sectors and to identify the most effective tools and approaches for allied health professionals working in these sectors. Additional research is needed to evaluate the impact of early positive exposure to these sectors in undergraduate allied health programs and how this may increase the likelihood of allied health professionals choosing aged care or adult disability care as a career path. This is important given limited undergraduate exposure to working in the aged care and adult disability sectors may hinder later engagement. Our review also noted a distinct lack of research on allied health professionals from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) and First Nations backgrounds. Thus, future research is needed to explore whether allied health professionals from these backgrounds face unique challenges working in the aged and disability sectors.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this review is the first to synthesise data on the enablers and barriers for allied health professionals working in the aged care and adult disability sections in Australia. Key enablers for allied health professionals include flexible work arrangements, working in a multidisciplinary team environment, and an opportunity to work with diverse and complex caseloads where adequate support is available. In comparison, key barriers include lower rates of pay, a lack of career progression opportunities, high administrative burden and negative perceptions of the aged care and adult disability sectors. The findings from this review provide sector employers, government, and policy makers with an understanding of the potential enablers and barriers encountered by allied health professionals working in the aged care and adult disability sectors. The review suggests that barriers could become enablers when appropriately supported, which highlights the need for future research on this topic. This information may also help to inform the National Allied Health Workforce Strategy in Australia.
