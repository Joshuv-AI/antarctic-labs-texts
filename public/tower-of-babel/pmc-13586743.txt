Adherence to French guidelines on cuff leak test use across French intensive care units: a national survey
Authors: Aimé Sao Cordiez, Benjamin Popoff, Dominique Vodovar, Gaëtan Beduneau
Published: 2026

Introduction

Post-extubation laryngeal oedema occurs in 1–30% of intubated intensive care unit (ICU) patients and may require early reintubation [1]. In 2016, French guidelines recommended performing a cuff leak test (CLT) to predict post-extubation laryngeal oedema (grade 2+) [1], a recommendation strengthened in patients with at least one risk factor for laryngeal oedema, in whom CLT should be performed to reduce extubation failure. In case of failed CLT test (low or absent leak volume), guidelines recommend that corticosteroids should probably be prescribed and initiated at least 6 h before extubation to ensure efficacy. These French recommendations closely mirror the 2017 American guidelines, which similarly advocate CLT in high-risk patients and corticosteroids after a failed test [2].

As these recommendations are now over 10 years old, our objective was to assess adherence to French guidelines in real-life practice.

Material and method

We conducted a prospective, web-based survey in accordance with the CHERRIES guidelines (Supplemental material 1). A 32-item questionnaire addressed ICU characteristics, extubation practices including CLT use, and corticosteroid prescription (Supplemental material 2).

French ICUs were identified through lists on the Collège des Enseignants en Médecine Intensive et Réanimation (CEMIR) and Collège National des Enseignants en Anesthésie-Réanimation (CNEAR) websites. Overall, 317 ICUs were contacted to present the study and identify a local investigator per unit.

In May 2025, the survey was emailed to the designated practitioners of the 178 ICUs that agreed to participate, with a 7-week completion period and reminders sent to non-responders at weeks 2 and 4.

Continuous and categorical variables were expressed as median [interquartile range] and counts (percentages), respectively. The study protocol was approved by the Rouen University Hospital review board (reference E2024-82), and the e-survey was hosted on LimeSurvey (version 6.13.2).

CLT indications varied markedly across ICUs (Fig. 1B). Sixteen ICUs (14.0%) performed CLT routinely, irrespective of risk factors for post-extubation laryngeal oedema, while four (3.5%) never performed CLT. Seventy-nine ICUs (69.3%) performed CLT when at least one established risk factor for post-extubation laryngeal oedema was present, whereas 15 (13.2%) based CLT solely on non-established risk factors.

Among ICUs reporting CLT use based on established risk factors, the median number of factors used to indicate a CLT was 3 [[2], [3], [4]]. The most frequently reported were traumatic (57.0%), difficult (49.1%), and prolonged endotracheal intubation (44.7%) (Fig. 1B). Non-established risk factors mainly involved upper airway injury of surgical or circumstantial origin.

Among the 110 ICUs using CLT, 81 (73.6%) administered systemic corticosteroids after a failed test, and 72 (65.5%) systematically repeated the CLT after failure, regardless of corticosteroid use. Most ICUs (79/96; 82.3%) delayed extubation by at least one day after a failed CLT (14 aberrant responses excluded) (Fig. 1C).

Discussion

Adherence to French guidelines regarding CLT use in patients meeting extubation criteria was highly heterogeneous. Approximately one-sixth of ICUs adhered to the recommendation to probably (Grade 2+) perform routine CLT, while another one-sixth was non-compliant, either never performing CLT or basing its indication on non-established risk factors. The remaining ICUs applied CLT indications variably according to established risk factors, consistent with a previous international survey [3].

Adherence to corticosteroid administration after a failed CLT was high. However, extubation was reported as delayed by at least one day in more than eight out of ten ICUs, despite French guidelines allowing extubation as early as 6 h after corticosteroid administration. Although data on the impact of delayed extubation remain limited, it may be associated with increased complications, including ventilator-associated pneumonia, unplanned extubation, and prolonged ICU length of stay [4].

This survey's strengths include its high response and completion rates, supporting good external validity. The 317 ICUs contacted were consistent with recent national publications [5], with approximately one-third responding overall. One limitation is that the study relied on only one respondent per ICU, which may reflect individual rather than unit-level practice. Additionally, question phrasing may have led to a partially quantitative assessment, potentially introducing inaccuracies.

Conclusion

This study reveals limited adherence to national guidelines and considerable heterogeneity in cuff leak test use across French ICUs. Furthermore, a failed test commonly leads clinicians to postpone extubation by at least one day, despite guidelines supporting earlier extubation once corticosteroids have been administered. Together, these findings highlight the need for further studies to better define the clinical role and optimal use of this test in guiding extubation decisions.
