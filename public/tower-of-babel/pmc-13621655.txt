Wound infections due to factory gunpowder and pyrotechnic explosions in patients with burn injury: risk factors and antimicrobial resistance
Authors: Jinfu Li, Yueying Fan, Xiangong Jiao, Ming Zou, Zhangjia Jiang, Cheng Li, Shiqiang Jiang, Chiyu Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA substantial obstacle in the treatment of burn patients is the management of burn wound infection (BWI). The objective of this investigation was to assess the antimicrobial susceptibility test results and distribution characteristics of microorganisms isolated from the burn wounds of patients affected by the factory gunpowder and pyrotechnic explosions.MethodsThis tertiary burn center conducted a 5-year retrospective observational study of the clinical and laboratory characteristics of pathogenic microorganisms through drug susceptibility tests, species distribution, and timing of wound infections. Two groups of patients were compared: patients with burn wound infection (BWI) versus patients without burn wound infection (Non-BWI).ResultsThe study enrolled 313 individuals (median age: 47 years), of whom 286 (91.37%) were male. One hundred twenty‑four (39.6%) developed BWI. Multiple regression analysis showed that inhalation injury, length of stay (LOS), and percentage total body surface area (%TBSA) were independent risk factors for developing BWI. The most frequently isolated bacteria were Acinetobacter baumannii (31.6%) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (10.9%). The median time from admission to first pathogen isolation was 12 days (IQR: 4–25 days) for Gram‑negative bacteria, 20 days (IQR: 10–30 days) for Gram‑positive bacteria, and 35 days (IQR: 18.5–59.5 days) for fungi.ConclusionBWI occurred in 39.6% of gunpowder-related burn patients. Independent risk factors were inhalation injury (OR 4.86), larger %TBSA (OR 1.08), and prolonged LOS (OR 1.09). Gram-negative pathogens predominated (74.4%), with A. baumannii (31.6%) showing near-complete resistance. Gram-negative infections emerged earlier (median 12 days) than Gram-positive (20 days) or fungi (35 days). Based on these findings, our burn unit now initiates early surveillance cultures at day 7 post-admission for high-risk patients, prioritizes empiric Gram-negative coverage (specifically anti-Acinetobacter therapy) in the first two weeks, and has implemented enhanced infection control protocols targeting A. baumannii transmission.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14140-6.Keywords: Burn wound, Infection, Gunpowder, Epidemiology, Risk factors, Pathogen, Resistance

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), burns remain a major global public health problem, causing an estimated 120,000 deaths each year, with the vast majority occurring in low‑ and middle‑income countries [2–5]. Infections in burn patients significantly contribute to sepsis and septic shock [6–8]. Sepsis is the primary cause of death after burns, responsible for around 73% of deaths during the first 5 days post-burn. The risk of infection and death increases with greater percentage total body surface area (%TBSA) involvement and with the presence of high bacterial loads on the wound surface, alongside burn‑induced immune suppression [9, 10]. In addition, the widespread use of antimicrobials has driven the emergence of multidrug-resistant pathogens, and infections caused by these organisms are associated with delayed wound healing, longer hospital stays, and higher mortality in burn patients [11–17]. Antibiotic resistance is a global public health priority [11–17]. Multidrug-resistant infections delay wound healing, prolong hospital stays, and increase mortality in burn patients [15]. By 2050, antimicrobial resistance is projected to cause 10 million annual deaths worldwide, with cumulative global economic losses reaching $100 trillion [16, 17].

Because the pathogens and medication resistance rates of various burn types vary greatly throughout the globe, recognizing the primary microorganisms usually discovered in burn units is critical for efficient infection therapy. In China, Liuyang is the national center of the firecracker industry. Firecrackers are a traditional industry with mostly manual production, and workers are frequently in close contact with gunpowder, which can cause catastrophic injury in the event of an accident. In addition, some individuals are injured through misuse of pyrotechnics during celebrations. Thus, gunpowder explosion injuries are the leading cause of large-scale burn victims in Liuyang City and have historically been associated with high fatality rates and limited systematic data analysis. In this study, we use the terms gunpowder and pyrotechnic in a related but not identical sense. Gunpowder is a combustible particulate mixture (traditionally of potassium nitrate, charcoal, and sulfur) that provides the explosive charge for many fireworks and firecrackers. Pyrotechnic products for celebration typically combine gunpowder with additional oxidizers, colorants, binders, and structural components to create visual and sound effects. In Liuyang, a major firecracker manufacturing center in China, workers are frequently exposed to large quantities of gunpowder during largely manual production, while members of the general public are exposed to pyrotechnic devices during festivals and celebrations. This retrospective cohort study collected clinical data from 313 patients with factory gunpowder or celebratory pyrotechnic explosion injuries admitted to Liuyang People’s Hospital between January 2018 and December 2023, building on our previous six-year analysis of 279 pyrotechnic‑related burn patients from the same center [4]. This study aimed to compare the clinical characteristics and risk factors between patients with and without burn wound infection (BWI vs. Non-BWI) and to characterize the microbial epidemiology, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and temporal distribution of pathogens isolated from burn wounds in patients with factory gunpowder and pyrotechnic explosion injuries treated at Liuyang People’s Hospital in Hunan Province.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

Inclusion criteria were patients with burn injuries caused by gunpowder and pyrotechnic explosions who were admitted to the burn unit for the first time and underwent wound bacteriological culture. Exclusion criteria were death or transfer to another hospital within 72 h of admission; non-burn causes such as chronic persistent infection or scar cosmetic surgery; severe autoimmune disorders or extended use of immunosuppressive medications (e.g., glucocorticoids); refusal to comply with medical advice or voluntary discharge due to mental disorders or special circumstances; and insufficient clinical data.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Liuyang People’s Hospital. Given the retrospective design of this study, which involved the analysis of existing data without direct patient interaction, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the aforementioned ethics committee.

Electronic hospital and laboratory information systems were employed to extract data from medical records. The data extraction process included demographic characteristics (age, gender), percentage total body surface area (%TBSA), depth and anatomical site of injuries, inhalation injury, tracheotomy, length of stay (LOS), timing of the first positive bacteriological culture result, isolate identification, antibiotic resistance status, surgical interventions, and outcomes.

In addition, pre‑existing comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic cardiovascular or pulmonary diseases, chronic kidney disease, and other chronic illnesses documented in the medical records) were collected.

Definitions

The “Wallace rule of nines” (as defined by the 1970 National Burn Symposium) was employed by senior burn clinicians to evaluate the %TBSA of burn patients following their admission [18]. Burn depth is classified into three levels in our investigation. First-degree burns were superficial burns that affect only the epidermis, exhibiting erythema, no blisters, and evident discomfort. Blisters that extend to the lower layer of the skin (dermis) were classified as second-degree burns and are categorized into superficial and profound degrees based on the thickness and thinness of the blister walls. The full-thickness burn was referred to as third-degree burn. The pain was not immediately apparent; the skin develops a leathery, rigid, and inelastic texture [19]. All definitions were based on standard clinical criteria used in burn care.

Microbiology

Wound cultures were obtained upon admission for all patients and subsequently at regular surveillance intervals (every 3–5 days) in accordance with the burn unit’s standard protocol. Additional swabs were collected whenever clinical signs of infection appeared, such as increased exudate, discoloration, odor, or delayed graft healing.

A total of 1,058 wound swabs were collected from 313 patients during the study period. This corresponded to a median of 3 swabs per patient (IQR: 2–5). Of these, 407 swabs (38.5%) were culture-positive and 651 (61.5%) yielded no pathogenic growth. Wound samples were collected using sterile rayon swabs with transport medium (e.g., Amies). Swabs were plated within 2 h onto blood agar, MacConkey agar, and Mueller-Hinton agar, incubated at 35–37 °C for 18–24 h, and processed using the Phoenix 100 automated system for identification and susceptibility testing.

Bacterial strain screening rules

Only the initially isolated strain from the same anatomical site of the same patient at different time points was documented; thus, the study included the same strain obtained from various specimen types of the same patient and distinct strains isolated from the same anatomical site of the same patient.

Medium

Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA) for drug susceptibility test, and MHA containing 5% defibrillated sheep blood for Streptococcus. All culture media were purchased from bioMérieux (Marcy-l’Étoile, France).

Drug susceptibility test

The Phoenix 100 automatic microbial identification and drug susceptibility analysis system (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was used. The MIC approach was implemented to ascertain drug sensitivity. The 2016 Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) standards were implemented in the selection of antibiotics and the interpretation of drug sensitivity results. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) results were interpreted according to CLSI (or EUCAST) clinical breakpoints used in our laboratory. For example, MIC values < 1 µg/mL were interpreted as susceptible, while MIC values ≥ 4 µg/mL were interpreted as resistant, following standardized thresholds for each antimicrobial agent.

Staphylococcus aureus(S. aureus)ATCC25923, Escherichia coli (E. coli)ATCC 25,922, Pseudomonas aeruginosa(P. aeruginosa)ATCC27853、Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) ATCC700603 were used as quality control strains.

Patient demographics and clinical details

Due to the small number of celebratory firework injuries, their data were incorporated into the statistical analysis together with patients who had factory gunpowder injuries. Among the celebratory firework cases, there were 17 male patients (of 286 total males), 6 female patients (of 27 total females), and 22 children (of 313 total children).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) for descriptive statistics, survival curve generation, and graphical presentations, and SPSS version 27.0 (IBM SPSS Software, USA) for univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. All statistical analyses were reviewed and verified by a professional medical statistician to ensure correct data interpretation. The Shapiro-Wilk test and Q-Q diagrams were implemented to verify the normality of the data. The mean and standard deviation (SD) were employed to report variables that were normally distributed, while the median and interquartile range (IQR) were employed to denote non-normally distributed variables. Independent t or Mann-Whitney tests were used for continuous variables, and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

The prospective risk factors of BWI were assessed using univariate analysis. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was implemented to identify independent risk factors for BWI. Variables with a P-value of less than 0.05 were incorporated. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were computed. All statistical outputs were cross-checked between the two software packages for consistency. The Kaplan-Meier method was employed to generate survival curves, and a P value of less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

The Department of Burn and Reconstructive Surgery admitted 2418 burn patients from 2018 to 2023. Eight of the 373 patients who sustained explosive injuries perished, while 21 were transferred to other institutions. Furthermore, 31 patients did not undergo wound bacterial culture. Consequently, this investigation comprised 313 qualified participants, which accounted for 12.9% of the total burn patient population. Associated frequency and risk factors for BWI

Of the 313 patients, 124 (39.6%) were diagnosed with BWI (Burn Wound Infection group) and 189 (60.4%) were in the Non-BWI group. The positive rate of culture was 38.5% (407/1058) among 1058 swab specimens, and 266 strains of pathogenic bacteria were identified. The frequency of infection did not differ significantly between males and females. The frequency of BWI was markedly elevated by the combination of full-thickness burns (P < 0.001). The risk of infection increased as the amount of %TBSA increased, and the majority of patients (75.1%) sustained burns that were less than 30% TBSA. Patients with more than 50% TBSA experienced a 100% infection rate. The head and neck (86.3%) and extremities (both upper and lower limbs, 96.8%) were the most frequently burned anatomical sites, followed by the trunk (40.3%), buttock (20.1%), and perineal region (2.2%). Perineal burns occurred due to the seated working position of factory workers during gunpowder explosions. In total, over half of the patients had multiple burn sites. Inhalation injury was present in 207 patients, of whom 115 (55.6%) developed BWI. In comparison to other patients, those who underwent three or more surgeries were at a threefold increased risk of BWI. The median LOS of patients with BWI was 50.5 days, which was substantially prolonged than that of patients without BWI (P < 0.001). Burn wound infection occurred in 9 of 27 female patients (33.3%), compared with 115 of 286 males (40.2%). There was no statistically significant difference in BWI frequency between females and males (P = 0.470).

BWI was associated with age, depth of burn, %TBSA, anatomical segments, inhalation injury, tracheotomy, operations, and LOS, as demonstrated by univariate analysis (Table 2). The multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that inhalation injury, %TBSA, and LOS are independent risk factors for BWI (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis: inhalation injury and BWI

To further explore the relationship between inhalation injury and burn wound infection, we stratified patients into four groups based on the presence or absence of inhalation injury and BWI. Among the 313 patients, 115 (36.7%) were in the Inhal/BWI group, 92 (29.4%) in the Inhal/Non-BWI group, 9 (2.9%) in the NoInhal/BWI group, and 97 (31.0%) in the NoInhal/Non-BWI group. The frequency of BWI was significantly higher in patients with inhalation injury (55.6%, 115/207) compared to those without inhalation injury (8.5%, 9/106; P < 0.001).

Among the Inhal/Non-BWI group, several protective factors were observed compared to the Inhal/BWI group. These patients had significantly lower median %TBSA (10% [IQR: 5–18] vs. 21% [IQR: 12–35], P < 0.001), fewer full-thickness burns (18.5% vs. 68.7%, P < 0.001), shorter median hospital stays (18 days [IQR: 12–28] vs. 52 days [IQR: 38–80], P < 0.001), and lower rates of tracheotomy (0% vs. 42.6%, P < 0.001). Multivariate analysis confirmed that among patients with inhalation injury, larger %TBSA (OR 1.12 per 1% increase, 95% CI 1.05–1.19, P < 0.001) and full-thickness burns (OR 8.34, 95% CI 3.21–21.67, P < 0.001) were independent predictors of BWI development. These findings suggest that inhalation injury alone does not guarantee wound infection; rather, the combination of inhalation injury with larger burn size and deeper burns substantially increases BWI risk.

Pneumonia as a cofactor in inhalation injury patients

Among the 207 patients with inhalation injury, 78 (37.7%) developed pneumonia during hospitalization. The frequency of BWI was significantly higher in inhalation injury patients with pneumonia (71.8%, 56/78) compared to those without pneumonia (47.3%, 61/129; P = 0.001). In contrast, among the 106 patients without inhalation injury, only 6 (5.7%) developed pneumonia, and all 6 had BWI (100% vs. 7.0% in those without pneumonia, P < 0.001).

Multivariate analysis restricted to patients with inhalation injury showed that pneumonia (OR 3.21, 95% CI 1.58–6.52, P = 0.001), larger %TBSA (OR 1.09 per 1% increase, 95% CI 1.03–1.15, P = 0.002), and full-thickness burns (OR 5.67, 95% CI 2.34–13.72, P < 0.001) were independent predictors of BWI. Among the 92 Inhal/Non-BWI patients, only 12 (13.0%) had pneumonia, whereas among the 115 Inhal/BWI patients, 66 (57.4%) had pneumonia (P < 0.001). These findings suggest that pneumonia may be an important cofactor linking inhalation injury to subsequent burn wound infection, possibly through systemic inflammatory effects or prolonged respiratory support requirements.

Time of appearance of first infection

Infection-free survival was defined as the time from admission to the first positive wound culture (i.e., onset of BWI). At 60 days, the overall infection-free survival rate was 38%. In general, approximately 50% of patients who eventually developed BWI did so within 10 days of admission. The median time to infection decreased substantially as burn depth and %TBSA increased. Patients who underwent tracheotomy or sustained inhalation injury developed infections earlier than those without these factors. These patterns are shown in the Kaplan–Meier curves (Fig. 2), which compare infection-free survival across subgroups. Correlation of isolated bacteria with burn characteristics

A total of 266 pathogenic isolates were identified from culture-positive burn wound samples. Among these, Acinetobacter baumannii was the most common organism (84 isolates, 31.6%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (29 isolates, 10.9%), Escherichia coli, Proteus mirabilis, and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia.

When correlated with anatomical site, the majority of isolates originated from burns of the extremities, which were the most frequently injured anatomic region in this cohort (96.8% of patients had extremity burns). Approximately 62% (≈ 165/266) of all isolates were recovered from extremity wounds, consistent with their high exposure and surface area. Burns involving the head and neck—present in 86.3% of patients—accounted for approximately 20% (≈ 53/266) of isolates, while the trunk and buttock/perineal regions contributed smaller proportions.

Burn depth demonstrated a clear relationship with bacterial patterns. Full-thickness burns accounted for 46.3% of patients, and these wounds yielded a disproportionately higher number of Gram-negative isolates. An estimated 70–75% of all Gram-negative bacteria (≈ 100/139) were isolated from full-thickness burns, whereas Gram-positive organisms—including Staphylococcus haemolyticus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Staphylococcus aureus—were more frequently recovered from partial-thickness or mixed-depth wounds (≈ 40% of Gram-positive isolates, ≈ 50/127).

Overall, these findings indicate that deeper burns (full-thickness) and burns of highly exposed areas (extremities, head/neck) are more likely to harbor Gram-negative pathogens, particularly A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae, while Gram-positive bacteria are more often isolated from superficial or mixed-depth wounds.

In this cohort of 313 patients with gunpowder- or pyrotechnic-related burns, the overall frequency of burn wound infection (BWI) was 39.6% (124 patients). Independent risk factors for BWI identified by multivariate logistic regression were inhalation injury (OR 4.86, 95% CI 1.68–14.09, P = 0.004), longer length of stay (OR 1.09 per day, 95% CI 1.06–1.13, P < 0.001), and larger %TBSA (OR 1.08 per 1% increase, 95% CI 1.00–1.16, P = 0.040). The most common anatomic sites of injury were the extremities (96.8%) and head/neck (86.3%). Gram-negative bacteria accounted for 74.4% of all first isolates from infected wounds, with Acinetobacter baumannii (31.6%) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (10.9%) being the most frequent pathogens. Antimicrobial resistance among Gram-negative isolates was extensive, with A. baumannii showing near-complete resistance (≥ 96%) to most antibiotics except cefotaxime (46.4% resistant), minocycline (53.6%), and colistin (56.0%). All Gram-positive isolates remained susceptible to vancomycin and linezolid. The median time to first positive culture was 18 days (IQR 9–30), with Gram-negative infections appearing earlier (median 12 days) than Gram-positive (20 days) or fungal infections (35 days). The 60-day infection-free survival rate was 38%, with infection occurring earlier in patients with inhalation injury, tracheotomy, deeper burns, or larger %TBSA

The resistance test results indicated that Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae were highly resistant to the majority of antibiotics (see Table 5). Stenotrophomonas maltophilia is typically resistant to antibiotics other than Compound sulfanilamide, Levofloxacyne, and ceftazidim. Discussion

This study met its objectives by identifying the demographic characteristics of patients with gunpowder- and pyrotechnic-related burns, determining the risk factors associated with burn wound infection (BWI), and describing the microbial spectrum and antimicrobial resistance patterns of wound isolates. We found that Acinetobacter baumannii was the most frequently occurring pathogen, and that inhalation injury, larger %TBSA, and prolonged hospitalization were independently associated with BWI. The overall frequency of BWI in this cohort was 39.6%, and the timing of Gram-negative, Gram-positive, and fungal isolation followed clear temporal patterns.

The aggregate frequency of BWI was 39.6%, which is consistent with the 38.5% reported by Corcione et al. [20–23]. Additionally, the collective frequency of positive samples was 38.5% (407/1058), which is lower than the 51.2% reported by Zhou et al. [24]. The absolute risk factors that influenced burn wound infection in the univariate and multivariate analysis of our study were inhalation injury, %TBSA, and length of hospital stay (LOS). These findings are consistent with those of Strassle et al. [25] and Jaimes et al. [26], who also identified inhalation injury and larger TBSA as independent risk factors for burn wound infection. Despite being initially sterile following thermal injury, burn lesions inevitably become colonized by microorganisms. A clear correlation was observed between the %TBSA and microbial colonization in our analysis, which is in accordance with previous reports [24]. Burn lesion colonization is linked to extended hospitalization. It is difficult to determine whether bacterial colonization is a cause or consequence of a prolonged hospital stay, and it is highly likely that the relationship is reciprocal. Taneja, N., et al. also observed a correlation between the duration of hospital stay and the frequency of burn lesion infection [27].

An important question raised by our data is why 92 patients with inhalation injury did not develop BWI. Our subgroup analysis revealed that these patients had significantly smaller burns (median TBSA 10% vs. 21%), fewer full-thickness burns (18.5% vs. 68.7%), and shorter hospital stays (18 vs. 52 days) compared to those with inhalation injury who developed BWI. None of the Inhal/Non-BWI patients required tracheotomy, whereas 42.6% of BWI/Inhalation+ patients did. These findings indicated that inhalation injury was a marker of injury severity but not a direct cause of wound infection. Patients with milder burns may tolerate inhalation injury without progression to wound infection, whereas those with larger and deeper burns are at substantially higher risk.

Further analysis revealed that pneumonia played a significant role among patients with inhalation injury. Among the 92 patients in the Inhal/Non-BWI group, only 13.0% had pneumonia, compared to 57.4% of the Inhal/BWI group. Multivariate analysis confirmed pneumonia as an independent predictor of BWI in this subgroup (OR 3.21, 95% CI 1.58–6.52). This finding suggested that pneumonia whether due to the inhalation injury itself or secondary to respiratory support might promote systemic inflammation or prolong hospitalization, thereby increasing susceptibility to wound infection. Alternatively, pneumonia and BWI may share common risk factors, including greater injury severity and prolonged intensive care.

In our investigation, Acinetobacter spp. was the most frequently occurring pathogen recovered from burn lesions, comprising 31.6% of all isolates. The hypothesis of a large frequency of Acinetobacter SPP is supported by additional investigations [28–30]. In the past few years, Acinetobacter baumannii isolates have become a substantial source of wound infection in our burn unit in China. Ibrahim et al. [31] reported 85% multidrug resistance in A. baumannii from burn patients in Iran, with high susceptibility to colistin (85.9%) and polymyxin B (100%). In contrast, our isolates exhibited near-complete resistance (up to 100%) to most antibiotics, with the lowest resistance observed for cefotaxime (46.4%), minocycline (53.6%), and colistin (56%). Acinetobacter baumannii and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia were notable for their extensive antibiotic resistance among the gram-negative bacteria. Vancomycin and linezolid were effective against all Gram-positive microorganisms.

The microbial profile of burn wounds typically shifts over time. Early after injury, Gram-positive bacteria originating from the patient’s skin flora or the hospital environment predominate. Subsequently, Gram-negative organisms—including those of gastrointestinal origin, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli—become more prevalent, consistent with reports by Ladhani et al. [32] and van Duin et al. [33, 34]. Later in the clinical course, particularly following broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, fungal colonization and infection may emerge. Our findings aligned with this established temporal pattern, demonstrating a transition from Gram-positive to Gram-negative bacteria and, subsequently, to fungi. Clinically, the earlier emergence of Gram‑negative infections (median 12 days) compared to Gram‑positive infections (median 20 days) suggested that empiric antibiotic coverage should prioritize Gram‑negative pathogens in the early post‑injury period.

The frequency of invasive sepsis caused by burn wound colonization has been significantly reduced by interventions such as early excision [35]. Moreover, the microbiological environment of burn lesions has been significantly altered by the implementation of strict infection control protocols, the use of topical and systemic antibacterial medications, and early skin transplantation [36].

The mortality rate in the present cohort was 4.2%, which is lower than the 7.5% reported in our previously published six-year study (Li et al.). Several factors may account for this difference. First, improvements in burn care at our center after 2018—including earlier excision, enhanced infection-control protocols, and more standardized resuscitation practices might have contributed to lower mortality. Second, the current cohort contained a slightly higher proportion of patients with smaller %TBSA burns and fewer catastrophic multi-region injuries, which might also explain the reduced mortality. Finally, differences in follow-up duration and case-capture during the two study periods might have led to variations in observed in-hospital death rates. Our previous six-year study (Li et al. [4]) focused on the general epidemiology and prognosis of burn injuries in China, reporting that older age, larger %TBSA, and inhalation injury were associated with higher mortality. The present study extended those findings by specifically examining burn wound infections in gunpowder- and pyrotechnic-related burns, identifying independent risk factors for BWI (inhalation injury, %TBSA, and LOS), characterizing the temporal distribution of pathogens, and detailing antimicrobial resistance patterns not previously reported.

The very low detection of Pseudomonas aeruginosa in our study differed from trends commonly seen in burn units worldwide. Several factors might explain this finding. First, our burn center has adopted strict water-supply control and environmental disinfection policies since 2018, which may have reduced environmental reservoirs of P. aeruginosa. Second, most injuries in this cohort resulted from high-temperature pyrotechnic explosions, which often produce relatively dry wounds with less moisture retention conditions less favorable for P. aeruginosa colonization. Third, local epidemiology in northern China might differ from that of other regions, with A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae serving as the predominant Gram-negative pathogens in our facility. These factors likely contributed to the near absence of P. aeruginosa isolates. Fungal infections did not occur in our cohort; however, we included literature on fungal pathogens to emphasize the importance of monitoring for such infections in long-stay burn patients, as they are clinically recognized complications in other centers.

Study limitations

However, this investigation also has certain deficiencies. In the initial phase, we collected clinical features and demographic information from patients affected by either factory gunpowder explosions or celebratory pyrotechnic devices, along with data on bacterial isolates and antibiotic resistance patterns in the ward. Nevertheless, our ability to gain a comprehensive understanding of the microbiological characteristics that are distinctive to burns caused by pyrotechnic explosives was limited by the fact that we did not compare them to other types of burns. Secondly, the bacterial specimens lacked a precise and comprehensive molecular identification and homology analysis. Additionally, this research was retrospective and restricted to a single site, rendering it incapable of examining pathogen resistance patterns over a multitude of treatment periods. It is imperative to take these constraints into account when interpreting our findings, and additional clarification is required through future research.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that burn wound infection in gunpowder- and pyrotechnic-related burns is independently predicted by inhalation injury (OR 4.86), larger %TBSA (OR 1.08 per 1% increase), and prolonged length of stay (OR 1.09 per day). Notably, inhalation injury alone does not guarantee infection—patients with smaller burns (median %TBSA 10% vs. 21%) and fewer full-thickness burns (18.5% vs. 68.7%) remained infection-free despite inhalation injury, indicating that inhalation injury is a marker of injury severity rather than a direct cause. Gram-negative bacteria account for 74.4% of BWI isolates, with Acinetobacter baumannii (31.6%) as the dominant pathogen showing near-complete resistance to most antibiotics, while all Gram-positive isolates remained susceptible to vancomycin and linezolid. The earlier emergence of Gram-negative infections (median 12 days) compared to Gram-positive (20 days) and fungal infections (35 days) provides a critical temporal window for empiric antibiotic targeting. Based on these findings, our burn unit has implemented three specific protocol changes: (1) surveillance wound cultures initiated at day 7 post-admission for patients with %TBSA > 20% or inhalation injury; (2) empiric antibiotic coverage prioritizing Gram-negative pathogens, specifically anti-Acinetobacter therapy, during the first two weeks post-injury; and (3) enhanced infection control measures targeting A. baumannii transmission, including contact precautions and environmental disinfection. These evidence-based interventions provide a practical framework for reducing BWI morbidity in this unique burn population.
