Family Caregiving and Psychosocial Support in Hospice and Palliative Care: A Social Work Perspective
Authors: Jung-won Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFamily caregiving and psychosocial support are essential components of high-quality hospice and palliative care. This review examined family caregiving and psychosocial support in hospice and palliative care from a social work perspective. Specifically, it reviewed the current evidence on family caregiving and psychosocial interventions for family caregivers, re-examined the evolving role of hospice social workers, and discussed implications for future research, practice, and policy in hospice social work. Evidence has indicated that psychosocial interventions effectively reduce caregivers’ psychological distress, depression, and anxiety while improving their quality of life and family communication. However, caregiving burden remains a multidimensional challenge that cannot be adequately addressed through psychosocial interventions alone, highlighting the need for structural and community-based support. The review also found that hospice social workers are key providers of psychosocial counseling, caregiving burden assessment, resource coordination, communication facilitation, and decision-making support. Nevertheless, role ambiguity, workforce shortages, and limited institutional recognition remain significant barriers to the delivery of comprehensive family-centered hospice care. Strengthening the evidence base for hospice social work will not only enhance the professional recognition of hospice social workers but also improve the quality of hospice and palliative care by advancing comprehensive patient- and family-centered care.Keywords: Caregivers, Hospice care, Palliative care, Social work, Social support

INTRODUCTION

Hospice and palliative care is a healthcare service designed to alleviate physical symptoms among patients with life-threatening illnesses while addressing the psychosocial needs of patients and their families. Its primary goals are to relieve suffering and enhance the quality of life (QOL) of patients and their families [1]. From this perspective, hospice and palliative care views not only the patient but also the family as the unit of care. Accordingly, reducing the burden on family members caring for terminally ill patients and supporting their adaptation are essential for improving the quality of patient care [2]. Therefore, hospice and palliative care emphasizes that, in addition to symptom management and relief of patients’ suffering, reducing family caregiving burden and providing psychosocial support are key components for improving the QOL of patients and their families [3].

In South Korea, the Act on Decisions on Life-Sustaining Treatment for Patients in Hospice and Palliative Care or at the End of Life (commonly referred to as the Life-Sustaining Treatment Decision Act) was enacted and implemented in 2016, marking a significant milestone in recognizing the government’s responsibility to provide hospice and palliative care services to patients and their families. Under this legislation, patients with terminal illnesses are entitled to receive comprehensive hospice and palliative care delivered by an interdisciplinary team of healthcare professionals, including physicians, nurses, and social workers [4]. However, hospice and palliative care services do not replace family caregivers with healthcare professionals. Family members remain the primary providers of day-to-day care and are indispensable partners throughout the care process. As patients’ illness trajectories and health conditions change, family caregivers often experience substantial psychosocial challenges, and their caregiving capacity and adaptation significantly influence patients’ QOL [5]. Thus, family caregivers are not only members of a caregiving unit who share the psychological distress and social burden associated with caring for a terminally ill patient but also key contributors to improving the patient’s QOL.

From a social work perspective, family caregiving and psychosocial support are fundamental components of holistic hospice and palliative care. Hospice and palliative care extends beyond symptom management, encompassing relationships, loss, and bereavement. Thus, care should address not only patients’ suffering but also the emotional and psychosocial needs of family caregivers. Moreover, family caregivers’ psychosocial well-being significantly influences medical decision-making, the quality and continuity of caregiving, and patient outcomes, thereby underscoring the importance of supporting families throughout the illness trajectory. Accordingly, family caregiving and psychosocial support constitute core domains of hospice social work and provide the foundation for patient- and family-centered care.

Despite their recognized importance, research examining family caregiving and psychosocial support from a social work perspective remains limited. Therefore, this study aims to review family caregiving and psychosocial support in hospice and palliative care from a social work perspective. Specifically, it examines current evidence on family caregiving and psychosocial interventions for family caregivers. Drawing on this review, this study re-examines the role of hospice social workers by highlighting their achievements and the challenges they face and provides implications for future research, practice, and policy in hospice social work.

RESEARCH TRENDS IN FAMILY CAREGIVING

Research on family caregiving in hospice and palliative care has increasingly focused on identifying the challenges that family caregivers experience throughout the caregiving process, examining the factors associated with caregiving burden, and exploring strategies to alleviate it. Studies have generally conceptualized family caregiving burden in three major dimensions: (1) physical burden, (2) changes in family roles and daily life, and (3) caregiver burnout associated with prolonged caregiving.

First, a substantial body of research has documented family caregivers’ physical fatigue and chronic stress. Family caregivers of terminally ill patients commonly report sleep disturbances, persistent psychological tension, accumulated fatigue, and impaired immune functioning [6]. Previous studies have consistently shown that longer caregiving duration is associated with greater physical symptoms and higher levels of stress among family caregivers [7,8]. Second, research has highlighted the increased caregiving responsibilities and disruptions to daily life experienced by family caregivers [9,10]. Caring for a terminally ill family member often requires substantial adjustments in family roles. As caregiving responsibilities increase, caregivers may reduce their working hours or leave employment altogether, as well as restrict their social activities and personal lives [11]. These role changes have been associated with not only financial hardship but also social isolation and diminished QOL [12]. Third, a growing body of literature has examined caregiver burnout resulting from prolonged caregiving [13,14]. Family caregivers who provide care over extended periods frequently experience emotional exhaustion, feelings of helplessness, and declining motivation to continue caregiving. Evidence suggests that such burnout develops gradually and often begins to accumulate even before the patient’s death, indicating that caregiving burden is not a temporary challenge but rather a progressive process that intensifies over time.

Building on these findings, recent international studies have increasingly adopted the conceptual framework of caregiving burden, originally developed by Zarit et al. [15], to identify its determinants more systematically. Rather than viewing burden as a single psychological outcome, this framework defines caregiver burden as a multidimensional construct encompassing physical, psychological, social, and financial strain arising from prolonged caregiving demands and the interaction between caregiving responsibilities and available coping resources. Within this framework, caregiving duration, illness-related uncertainty, and emotional strain have consistently emerged as major predictors of caregiving burden. In contrast, Korean research has primarily focused on the caregiving experiences and family roles of caregivers of patients with terminal cancer. This finding emphasizes the need to conceptualize caregiving not only as an individual responsibility but also as a sustained and structurally embedded burden requiring social and policy-level support [10]. More recent research has expanded the focus beyond caregiving burden to examine family caregivers’ unmet needs. For example, Usai et al. [16] conducted a systematic review of 17 studies on hospice and palliative care and found that family caregivers most frequently reported unmet needs related to practical caregiving assistance, emotional support, and information regarding illness and caregiving. These findings suggest that reducing family caregiving burden requires a comprehensive approach that extends beyond psychosocial interventions to include information provision, caregiving support services, and effective connections to community resources.

PSYCHOSOCIAL INTERVENTIONS FOR FAMILY CAREGIVERS: EVIDENCE OF EFFECTIVENESS

Family members of terminally ill patients frequently experience emotional distress, changes in family roles, communication difficulties, and anticipatory grief throughout the caregiving process [17,18]. These challenges not only affect caregivers’ well-being but also directly affect the quality of patient care and the QOL of both patients and their families. Accordingly, psychosocial interventions for family caregivers are not limited to alleviating psychological distress; they are integral to holistic hospice and palliative care because they enhance the QOL of both patients and family caregivers.

Current psychosocial hospice and palliative care interventions for family caregivers can be broadly classified into four categories: (1) family counseling and emotional support, (2) family meetings and communication interventions, (3) caregiving education and information provision, and (4) anticipatory grief and bereavement preparation. These interventions are designed to alleviate caregivers’ emotional distress, facilitate family communication and shared decision-making, provide the knowledge and practical information necessary for caregiving, and support families in preparing for loss and accepting death in a healthy and adaptive manner. Recently, systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating the effectiveness of psychosocial interventions for family caregivers have become increasingly common. For example, Wu et al. [23] conducted a systematic review of 34 studies examining psychosocial interventions designed to reduce psychological distress among family caregivers in hospice and palliative care. Their findings indicated that more than half of the included studies reported significant reductions in psychological distress among family caregivers after these interventions. Yan et al. [24] conducted a meta-analysis of 16 randomized controlled trials evaluating the effects of hospice and palliative care interventions on depression, QOL, and caregiving burden among family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer. Their results demonstrated that hospice and palliative care–based psychosocial interventions were effective in reducing depression and improving QOL among family caregivers. However, these interventions did not significantly reduce caregiving burden.

Collectively, these systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that psychosocial interventions for family caregivers are effective in reducing psychological distress and depression while improving QOL. However, caregiving burden is a multidimensional construct shaped by factors such as caregiving duration and intensity, changes in family roles, and the availability of social support [24,25]. Consequently, psychosocial interventions alone may be insufficient to substantially reduce caregiving burden. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive social work approaches that combine psychosocial interventions with structural and community-based support to address family caregivers’ complex needs.

ROLE OF HOSPICE SOCIAL WORKERS: ACHIEVEMENTS AND CHALLENGES

The literature has demonstrated that the growing recognition of family caregiving and psychosocial support in hospice and palliative care has strengthened the role and professional identity of hospice social workers. Based on previous research, hospice social workers’ core responsibilities can be grouped into four key domains.

First, they provide psychosocial support and counseling by assessing caregivers’ psychosocial needs, facilitating emotional adjustment, and helping family caregivers cope with emotional challenges such as anxiety, depression, guilt, and helplessness [26]. Second, they assess caregiving burden and unmet needs and connect families to financial assistance, community resources, and formal caregiving services [27]. By recognizing caregiving burden as a social and structural issue rather than solely an individual responsibility, they help establish support systems that reduce the burden on family caregivers. Third, hospice social workers serve as coordinators between families and healthcare professionals by facilitating communication, conveying families’ values and preferences to the healthcare team, and mediating conflicts to promote collaborative decision-making [28,29]. Fourth, they support families throughout the treatment process and the life-sustaining treatment decision-making process by facilitating psychosocial preparation, family communication, consensus-building, and ongoing adjustment to guilt, regret, and anticipatory grief [27,28]. Through these roles, hospice social workers enhance family caregivers’ emotional well-being and resilience while promoting continuity of care. Their psychosocial interventions, resource coordination, and communication support contribute to reducing caregiver burden, improving family caregivers’ QOL, and advancing patient- and family-centered hospice and palliative care.

Despite the importance of these roles, hospice social work practice continues to face several crucial challenges [26-30]. First, role ambiguity and excessive workload remain major concerns. Hospice social workers’ responsibilities vary across institutions according to organizational structures, interdisciplinary team characteristics, and individual practice scopes. In addition to counseling, they frequently engage in administrative tasks, resource coordination, and interdisciplinary meetings, leaving limited time for professional psychosocial interventions. Thus, clearer role definitions and standardized practice guidelines, as well as reduced administrative burdens, are required to enable a greater focus on psychosocial care and family support. Second, workforce shortages and institutional limitations hinder the implementation of effective practice. Despite steady increases in the number of patients receiving hospice and palliative care, the number of hospice social workers has not increased proportionally. Consequently, opportunities to provide comprehensive counseling and sustained psychosocial support for family caregivers remain limited. Therefore, establishing evidence-based staffing standards and expanding the hospice social work workforce are essential for improving service quality. Third, hospice social workers’ professional contributions remain insufficiently recognized within the current healthcare system. Existing reimbursement and evaluation systems inadequately reflect psychosocial counseling and interventions, resulting in limited institutional recognition of their value. Strengthening professional competencies, standardizing practices, and developing evaluation and reimbursement systems that appropriately recognize psychosocial interventions are essential for enhancing the professional status of hospice social workers and the quality of hospice and palliative care.

DISCUSSION

This review highlights that family caregiving and psychosocial support are not peripheral aspects of hospice and palliative care, but fundamental components of high-quality, patient- and family-centered care. As the needs of patients and their families have become increasingly complex, the role of hospice social workers has expanded beyond providing emotional support to encompass family assessment, resource coordination, communication facilitation, and decision-making support. These findings underscore the need to strengthen the evidence base for hospice social work and translate these research findings into clinical practice and policy.

The findings of this review have several important implications for future research, practice, and policy in hospice social work. Future research should adopt a longitudinal perspective that follows families throughout the illness trajectory, from diagnosis and caregiving to end-of-life care and bereavement, to strengthen the professional role of hospice social workers and promote evidence-based practice. Such an approach would provide a more comprehensive understanding of family adaptation over time and help clarify the roles required of hospice social workers at different stages of care. Future studies should also evaluate the long-term effectiveness of social work interventions using outcomes such as QOL, caregiving sustainability, healthcare utilization, bereavement adjustment, and family functioning. Furthermore, the development of family-centered outcome indicators and context-specific evidence reflecting Korea’s hospice care system, family culture, and healthcare environment will be essential for demonstrating the effectiveness and value of hospice social work.

The continued accumulation of such evidence has important implications for both practice and policy. It can provide a stronger foundation for recognizing family caregiving and psychosocial support as core components of hospice and palliative care while supporting the development of evidence-based practice guidelines, standardized job descriptions, and appropriate staffing standards for hospice social workers. Moreover, it can strengthen family-centered care by promoting professional competencies in family counseling, decision-making support, and interdisciplinary collaboration.

Ultimately, establishing a stronger evidence base will not only enhance the professional recognition of hospice social workers but also improve the quality of hospice and palliative care. Continued efforts to integrate research evidence into practice and policy are essential for advancing patient- and family-centered care and ensuring that patients and their families receive comprehensive psychosocial support throughout the illness trajectory.
