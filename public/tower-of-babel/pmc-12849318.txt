Patterns and indications of caesarean sections in a tertiary care hospital: a Robson Ten-Group Classification analysis
Authors: Madhumita Nigam, Nisha Gusain, Richa Upadhyay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe study aimed to analyse the caesarean section (CS) rates in a tertiary care centre using Robson’s Ten-Group Classification System (TGCS) to identify contributing factors and inform strategies for reducing CS rates.MethodsThis retrospective study was conducted at Tertiary care Hospital Pune India, during August–December 2023. Data were collected on all live and stillbirths with a birth weight ≥ 500 g or gestational age ≥ 28 weeks. CS rates were classified according to Robson’s 10-group classification, and statistical analysis was performed to determine the relative contribution of each group.ResultsThe total number of deliveries was 2493, of which 1345 were CS (53.95%). The highest contribution to CS was from Group 5 (previous CS, term, cephalic), accounting for 37.92% of all CS. Group 2 (nulliparous, induced or pre-labour CS) contributed 34.42%. The lowest CS rates were seen in Groups 1 and 3 (spontaneous labour, nulliparous and multiparous). Groups 6 to 10, representing breech, multiple pregnancies, and preterm deliveries, had near 100% CS rates.ConclusionThe study highlights a high CS rate, mainly due to repeat CS and induced labours. The Robson classification proved effective in identifying key groups contributing to the CS rate. Reducing unnecessary CS, promoting vaginal birth after caesarean (VBAC), and refining induction protocols could help lower CS rates. Future efforts should also focus on improving labour management and monitoring.Keywords: Caesarean section, Robson’s classification, Primary caesarean, Repeat caesarean, Vaginal birth after caesarean (VBAC), Obstetric classification, Delivery outcomes, Pre-labour caesarean, Induced labour, Tertiary care centre

Introduction

Caesarean section (CS) is a life-saving surgical intervention that plays a critical role in modern obstetric care. However, in recent decades, there has been a significant global rise in CS rates, sparking concern among healthcare professionals and policymakers. In 1985, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended that the ideal rate of CS should be between 10 and 15%, as higher rates are not associated with improved maternal or neonatal outcomes. Despite this, many countries, especially in developing regions and tertiary care centres, continue to report CS rates well above this threshold. Several factors have contributed to this upward trend, including increasing maternal age, better access to antenatal care, changes in clinical practice, medico-legal concerns, and patient preferences. While some of these are valid medical reasons, others may reflect a pattern of over-medicalization of childbirth [1]. To address this, a need arose for a standardised classification system to objectively assess and monitor CS practices. In 2001, M.S. Robson proposed the Ten-Group Classification System (TGCS), which was later endorsed by WHO (2014) and FIGO (2016) as a global standard. The Robson classification categorises women into ten mutually exclusive and totally inclusive groups based on five obstetric variables: parity, gestational age, onset of labour, foetal presentation, and number of foetuses. This system is widely praised for its simplicity, reproducibility, and clinical relevance, and it serves as a powerful tool to identify target areas for intervention to reduce unnecessary caesarean deliveries [2, 3].

Rationale of the study

Tertiary care hospitals often report higher CS rates due to their role as referral centres for high-risk pregnancies. Therefore, it is essential to analyse the contributing factors in such settings to develop context-specific strategies for improving obstetric care [4].

Objectives

To classify caesarean sections using Robson’s Ten Group Classification System.

To identify the major contributors to the overall CS rate.

To audit current CS indications and explore opportunities to optimise delivery practices.

Practicality of the study

This study provides valuable insights into the pattern and indications of caesarean sections in a tertiary care setting using Robson’s Ten Group Classification System. By identifying the groups that contribute most significantly to the overall CS rate—particularly Group 5 (previous CS) and Group 2 (nulliparous, induced or pre-labour CS)—the study highlights key areas for targeted intervention. These findings can help inform hospital protocols, support the implementation of evidence-based guidelines for labour management, and promote strategies such as encouraging vaginal birth after caesarean (VBAC) and judicious use of induction. The study also serves as an effective audit tool for healthcare providers, administrators, and policymakers to monitor trends, improve clinical decision-making, and ultimately reduce unnecessary caesarean deliveries without compromising maternal or neonatal outcomes. In resource-limited, high-volume government settings, such standardised evaluation is especially practical for improving care quality and optimizing resource allocation [5, 6].

Discussion

This study utilised the Robson Ten Group Classification System (TGCS) to analyze caesarean section (CS) rates at a tertiary care referral hospital. The overall CS rate observed was 53.95%, significantly higher than the WHO-recommended rate of 10–15% and the national average reported by NFHS-5 (21.5%). This elevated rate is largely attributable to the hospital’s role as a referral centre that receives a high volume of complicated and high-risk pregnancies [7, 8].

Group-wise contributions and key findings

The highest contributor to the CS rate was Group 5 (multiparous women with a previous CS, term, cephalic presentation), accounting for 37.92% of all CS, with a group-specific CS rate of 99.6%. This finding aligns with other studies (e.g., Jacob KJ et al., Ray et al.) and global trends, highlighting the growing burden of repeat caesarean sections [9]. The low rate of Vaginal Birth After Caesarean (VBAC) in this study (only 2 cases attempted) underscores the need for greater awareness, training, and supportive infrastructure to promote VBAC where appropriate. Group 2 (nulliparous, term, cephalic, induced or pre-labour CS) was the second largest contributor, making up 34.42% of all CS, with a group-specific CS rate of 65.4%. This is notably higher than global averages and suggests possible overuse or premature resort to caesarean delivery in induced or non-progressive labours. Contributing factors may include misdiagnosis of labour progress, increased use of induction agents, and a cautious approach to managing first-time pregnancies. Groups 1 and 3 (spontaneous labour in nulliparous and multiparous women, respectively) showed lower CS rates (17.5% and 5.7%), consistent with global findings, indicating that spontaneous onset of labour is generally well managed [10, 11]. In contrast, Groups 6 to 9, though small in number, had near 100% CS rates, reflecting standard obstetric practices for breech, multiple pregnancies, and abnormal lies, which carry increased risks with vaginal delivery. Group 10 (preterm, cephalic presentation) contributed to 9.89% of total CS, with a 53.6% group-specific rate. This reflects the cautious management of preterm births, often involving early intervention to reduce neonatal morbidity and mortality. This is expected in a tertiary centre with a high proportion of medically complicated pregnancies [12].

Comparison with global and national data

When compared with the WHO Multicountry Survey and national data, the present study’s rates in Groups 2, 5, and 10 are significantly higher. This indicates a need to review clinical decision-making, particularly for primary CS and repeat CS cases, and to develop protocols for induction, labour monitoring, and VBAC implementation. Utility of Robson’s Classification: Robson’s classification proved to be an effective tool in identifying the most contributing groups to the overall CS rate and guiding quality improvement strategies. However, its limitation lies in not accounting for maternal comorbidities, foetal conditions, or emergency versus elective CS. Modifications or additional subgroup analyses may provide a more nuanced understanding in complex referral settings [13, 14].

Implications for clinical practice

The findings of this study have several important implications for obstetric practice and maternal health care in tertiary settings:

Promotion of Vaginal Birth After Caesarean (VBAC): With Group 5 (previous CS, term, cephalic) being the largest contributor to the overall CS rate, encouraging and supporting VBAC through proper counselling, strict eligibility screening, and adequate intrapartum monitoring could substantially reduce repeat caesarean deliveries. Refinement of Induction and Pre-labour Caesarean Protocols: The high contribution of Group 2 (nulliparous women with induced labour or pre-labour CS) highlights the need to re-examine induction practices. Standardised induction protocols, adherence to evidence-based guidelines, and patience with the latent phase of labour may reduce unnecessary surgical interventions. Improved Labour Management and Monitoring: Strengthening intrapartum care with the use of partograms, continuous foetal monitoring, and timely obstetric interventions can help optimise labour outcomes, particularly in Groups 1 and 3 (spontaneous labour). Risk-based Clinical Decision-Making: For high-risk groups (breech, multiple pregnancies, and preterm), individualised decision-making is essential to balance maternal and neonatal safety while avoiding overuse of caesarean delivery.Regular Audit Using Robson’s Classification: Institutionalizing routine audits based on Robson’s TGCS can help hospitals identify the major drivers of caesarean deliveries, track trends over time, and implement targeted interventions. Policy and Training Implications: Training of obstetricians and midwives on VBAC, safe induction methods, and evidence-based intrapartum care is necessary. At the policy level, establishing quality improvement initiatives and maternal safety bundles could help reduce unnecessary CS [15].

Strengths

This study applied the internationally accepted Robson Ten Group Classification System (TGCS), allowing meaningful comparison with both national and global datasets. The data were collected from a tertiary care hospital, ensuring inclusion of both low- and high-risk pregnancies. The study provides contemporary data (2023), contributing recent evidence on caesarean section patterns in India.

Limitations

Being a retrospective study, data were dependent on the accuracy and completeness of medical records. The study was conducted at a single centre, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings. Factors such as patient preferences, medico-legal issues, and institutional protocols were not assessed but may have influenced CS rates.

Conclusion

This study reveals a high caesarean section rate (53.95%) at a tertiary care centre, primarily driven by Group 5 (previous CS) and Group 2 (induced or pre-labour CS). The findings highlight the need to address the overuse of caesarean sections, especially in nulliparous women and repeat CS cases. Strategies to reduce unnecessary CS, such as promoting Vaginal Birth After Caesarean (VBAC) and refining induction protocols, are essential. The Robson classification effectively identified key contributing groups but could be improved by considering maternal comorbidities and emergency versus elective CS. Future efforts should focus on reducing unnecessary interventions and improving labour management.
