Trapezius flap for reconstruction of malignant transformation of a chronic occipital burn scar with skull involvement in a 70-year-old man: a case report
Authors: Abdul Ghafar Ghayur, Mohammad Shafiqi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Chronic burn scars may undergo malignant transformation into a Marjolin’s ulcer, most commonly a squamous cell carcinoma. Advanced lesions involving the calvarium present significant oncologic and reconstructive challenges.Case presentation:A 70-year-old man presented with a chronic non-healing occipital ulcer that developed 2 years after a full-thickness thermal burn caused by boiling water. The injury had been managed only with repeated dressings, resulting in complete scalp alopecia and persistent ulceration. Biopsy confirmed basaloid squamous cell carcinoma, and computed tomography demonstrated occipital bone invasion without intracranial extension or distant metastasis. Wide oncologic excision of the scalp and involved calvarium was performed until healthy dura was exposed. The resulting defect was reconstructed using a pedicled lower trapezius myocutaneous flap. Histopathology confirmed basaloid squamous cell carcinoma with involvement of the deep resection margin. At a 6-month follow-up, the flap remained viable, with satisfactory wound healing and no clinical evidence of recurrence.Clinical discussion:Reconstruction of extensive posterior scalp defects with calvarial involvement is technically demanding. Although free tissue transfer is often preferred for large scalp defects, regional flaps remain valuable in elderly patients or when microsurgical reconstruction is not feasible. In this case, the pedicled lower trapezius myocutaneous flap provided reliable vascularized tissue, durable coverage of the exposed dura, and satisfactory functional and reconstructive outcomes.Conclusion:Early biopsy of chronic burn scars is essential to detect malignant transformation. The pedicled lower trapezius myocutaneous flap represents a safe and effective reconstructive option for extensive occipital scalp defects following oncologic resection with calvarial involvement.

Introduction

The scalp is a special part of the skin that covers the skull[1]. Alopecia is a common problem after burns, which poses a real challenge to the reconstructive surgeon[2].

In the scar tissue of chronic ulcerative wounds, neoplastic changes are a well-known process[3]. This condition appears often in post-burn scars but can also appear after other types of scars[4]. We use the term “Marjolin’s ulcer” to describe this rare and aggressive cutaneous malignancy that appears in previously traumatized and chronically inflamed skin[5]. Marjolin’s ulcers occur in 0.8–2% of burn scars, most commonly in those who have had deep partial- or full-thickness burns left to heal by secondary intention[6]. Compared with sun exposure-associated skin malignancies, scar-associated carcinomas have a higher incidence of regional metastases, a worse prognosis, and greater mortality[7]. They most commonly occur on the lower limbs, and the scalp is a relatively uncommon site for scar-related carcinoma[7]. Oncologic resection of scalp malignancies often causes complex soft tissue and bone defects that pose challenges to reconstructive surgeons due to the need for durable coverage, restoration of contour, and protection of intracranial structures[9]. Due to the lack of flexibility of scalp tissue and the need to protect vital structures such as the dura, specific planning for soft tissue and bone coverage is necessary[10]. Large defects often require loco regional or free flap reconstruction to achieve stable closure, minimize complications, and provide both function and appearance[11].

In this article, we present a rare case of a Marjolin’s ulcer in a 70-year-old man who was burned 2 years ago with boiled water on his head that now penetrated the scalp and invaded the underlying bone.

The present case is particularly noteworthy because the patient developed malignant transformation following prolonged, inappropriate conservative treatment of a full-thickness occipital burn. Furthermore, successful reconstruction was achieved using a pedicled lower trapezius myocutaneous flap after wide oncologic excision, including calvarial resection, avoiding the need for microsurgical free tissue transfer in an elderly patient with limited resources.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[12].

Clinical history

A 70-year-old man presented to our plastic surgery department with a chronic, non-healing ulcer on the occipital scalp that had gradually enlarged over approximately 2 years. The lesion had developed following a full-thickness thermal burn caused by boiling water, which involved the occipital scalp and resulted in permanent alopecia.

Immediately after the burn injury, the patient received only repeated local dressing changes without definitive surgical treatment, such as excision and skin grafting. Because he lived in a remote rural area with limited access to specialized healthcare, the wound remained untreated for nearly 2 years, during which the wound developed progressive ulceration, intermittent bleeding, and gradual enlargement.

On clinical examination, an approximately 8 × 8 cm ulcerative lesion was present on the occipital scalp. The ulcer had irregular, everted margins, friable granulation tissue, contact bleeding, necrotic areas, and an indurated base, raising a strong suspicion of malignant transformation (Fig. 1A). No clinically palpable cervical lymphadenopathy was detected. Complete staging investigations showed no evidence of regional or distant metastasis.

Before definitive tumor resection, an excisional biopsy was performed to confirm the diagnosis. Histopathological examination demonstrated basaloid squamous cell carcinoma. The biopsy specimen showed involvement of the deep (basal) margin, indicating persistent tumor extension and the need for definitive wide oncologic excision.

Following histopathological confirmation, a wide oncologic resection was therefore planned.

The involved scalp and calvarial bone were resected using an osteotome until the healthy, intact dura mater was exposed. The dura remained completely intact, without tumor invasion, and therefore did not require reconstruction (Fig. 1B).

The resulting composite posterior scalp defect was reconstructed using a pedicled lower trapezius myocutaneous flap.

The flap was designed according to the skin defect and elevated together with the lower portion of the trapezius muscle.

The flap was based on the descending branch of the transverse cervical artery, which served as the dominant vascular pedicle. Careful pedicle dissection provided an adequate arc of rotation, allowing tension-free transfer of the flap to the occipital defect while maintaining excellent vascularity.

The donor site was closed primarily in layers (Fig. 3A,B). Because the dura remained intact after calvarial resection, no cranioplasty or additional reconstruction of the bony defect was performed. The well-vascularized trapezius muscle provided durable biological coverage of the exposed dura and eliminated the need for immediate bony reconstruction.

Postoperative recovery was uneventful.

Healing occurred by primary intention without flap necrosis, wound infection, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, hematoma, or donor-site complications.

Skin sutures were removed 2 weeks after surgery.

Final histopathology confirmed basaloid squamous cell carcinoma with involvement of the deep (basal) margin (R1); the closest peripheral margin was 4 mm. No lymphovascular or perineural invasion was identified. No gross residual tumor was identified intraoperatively, and the patient was referred to the oncology service for further management.

At the 6-month follow-up, the flap remained completely viable, with excellent contour and stable wound coverage (Fig. 3C). No wound breakdown, flap-related complications, or clinical evidence of local recurrence were observed.

The patient has been enrolled in a structured surveillance program consisting of clinical examinations every 3 months during the first postoperative year; however, follow-up has been primarily clinical because repeated CT imaging was limited by access and financial constraints.

Clinical discussion

Marjolin’s ulcer is a malignant degeneration arising in chronic wounds and scar tissue, most commonly in long-standing burn scars that fail to heal, thereby demonstrating the aggressive potential of cancerous transformation following delayed treatment of chronic burn injuries[13].

The pathogenesis of Marjolin’s ulcer is thought to be multifactorial and includes chronic inflammation, repeated tissue injury, impaired local immune surveillance, and continuous cellular regeneration, all of which contribute to malignant transformation over time. Deep full-thickness burns that heal by secondary intention or receive inadequate treatment are particularly associated with an increased risk of malignant degeneration[14].

In the present case, the patient sustained a full-thickness thermal burn of the occipital scalp caused by boiling water. Because he lived in a remote, rural area with limited access to specialized healthcare, the wound was managed only with repeated dressings for approximately 2 years without definitive surgical reconstruction, resulting in persistent ulceration and subsequent malignant transformation.

Cases of Marjolin’s ulcer localized to the scalp with penetration into the underlying bone are extremely rare in the literature, with recent reports documenting such invasive behavior[15].

Histopathological examination confirmed basaloid squamous cell carcinoma. Final pathology demonstrated involvement of the deep (basal) surgical margin, whereas the closest peripheral margin was free, with a clearance of 4 mm. No distant metastasis was identified during staging investigations. These findings emphasized the locally aggressive behavior of the tumor, with predominant deep invasion into the calvarium.

Large defects of the scalp and neurocranium, after extensive soft-tissue and bone resection, pose challenges to reconstructive surgeons due to the need for durable coverage, adequate vascular supply, and restoration of contour, which often require microsurgical free-tissue transfer for optimal outcomes[16].

Several reconstructive techniques have been described for posterior scalp defects following oncologic resection. Selection depends on defect size, calvarial or dural involvement, patient comorbidities, operative time, and the availability of microsurgical expertise. In the present case, the lower trapezius myocutaneous flap was selected because the defect was located in the occipital region after wide excision and calvarial resection with exposed dura. Considering the patient’s advanced age (70 years), the need for a shorter operative time, and the availability of a reliable regional flap with excellent vascularity, the trapezius flap represented a practical alternative to microsurgical free tissue transfer while providing durable multilayer coverage and satisfactory cosmetic and functional outcomes.

In this patient, a pedicled lower trapezius myocutaneous flap was selected because the defect was located in the posterior scalp and required reliable vascularized soft-tissue coverage over the exposed dura. The flap was based on the descending branch of the transverse cervical artery, which provided reliable perfusion and an adequate arc of rotation to reach the occipital defect[19,20]. Compared with local scalp flaps, the trapezius flap provides greater tissue reach and bulk for larger posterior defects, while a split-thickness skin graft would not provide comparable, durable, vascularized coverage over exposed dura. Microsurgical free tissue transfer, including latissimus dorsi and anterolateral thigh flaps, remains an important option for extensive composite scalp defects and can provide substantial tissue volume and reach; however, it requires microsurgical expertise, microvascular anastomosis, and generally greater operative complexity[18]. In our patient, the pedicled lower trapezius flap provided adequate reach, reliable vascularized coverage, and a relatively straightforward regional reconstructive option without microvascular anastomosis, making it appropriate given the posterior location of the defect, the patient’s age, and the available resources[19–21].

At the 6-month follow-up, the flap remained completely viable without wound dehiscence, flap necrosis, infection, or cerebrospinal fluid leakage. The patient reported no functional limitations related to neck or shoulder movement, and satisfactory cosmetic and functional outcomes were achieved. No clinical evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis was observed during this follow-up period.

Although the patient returned for follow-up beyond 6 months with complete flap viability and stable wound coverage, the six-month documented follow-up is insufficient to assess long-term oncological control.

Because the final histopathological examination demonstrated involvement of the deep (basal) surgical margin, the patient was counseled regarding the increased risk of local recurrence and enrolled in a close surveillance program. Follow-up consists of regular clinical examinations every 3 months during the first 2 years, with imaging studies performed whenever recurrence is clinically suspected. Long-term surveillance is particularly important because Marjolin’s ulcers are associated with higher recurrence and metastatic rates than conventional cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma[22].

Although several reconstructive techniques have been described, including split-thickness skin grafting, local rotational flaps, cranioplasty combined with scalp flaps, and microsurgical free tissue transfer, reports describing reconstruction with a pedicled lower trapezius myocutaneous flap are extremely limited. Our case further supports the trapezius flap as a reliable regional reconstructive option for extensive posterior scalp defects following oncologic resection, particularly in elderly patients and resource-limited settings.

This case is rare and clinically valuable because it demonstrates malignant transformation of a chronic full-thickness occipital burn scar into basaloid squamous cell carcinoma (Marjolin’s ulcer) with invasion of the occipital bone, successfully managed by radical oncologic excision and reconstruction using a pedicled lower trapezius myocutaneous flap. To the best of our knowledge, reports describing this combination of full-thickness scalp burn, calvarial invasion, and successful reconstruction with a pedicled trapezius flap remain extremely limited in the current literature.

Furthermore, this case highlights the importance of early biopsy of any chronic non-healing burn wound, timely referral to specialized centers, meticulous oncologic resection, appropriate reconstructive planning, and long-term postoperative surveillance. These principles are essential for reducing morbidity, minimizing recurrence, and improving long-term patient outcomes.

Conclusion

Early recognition and biopsy of chronic non-healing burn scars are essential to prevent delayed diagnosis of Marjolin’s ulcer and its potentially devastating local invasion.

This case demonstrates that wide oncologic excision, including involved calvarial bone, followed by reconstruction using a pedicled lower trapezius myocutaneous flap, can provide reliable, durable, and well-vascularized coverage for extensive posterior scalp defects while avoiding the complexity of microsurgical free tissue transfer in selected elderly patients.

Furthermore, this report emphasizes the importance of multidisciplinary management and long-term oncologic surveillance in patients with Marjolin’s ulcer of the scalp.
