Effects of increasing doses of wood vinegar on feed intake, digestibility, ingestive behavior, nitrogen balance and serum biochemistry in sheep
Authors: Maria Alyne Coutinho Santos Nogueira, Emerson Marcos Nascimento Silva, Maria Mirna Gonçalves Oliveira, Raiane Mikaeli Nunes da Costa, Elisomar André da Silva, Rayara Silva de Freitas, Mateus Gonçalves Bezerra, José Gustavo Lima de Almeida, Thiago Luís Alves Campos de Araújo, Salenilda Soares Firmino, Alexandre Santos Pimenta, Dorgival Morais de Lima Júnior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective was to evaluate the effects of increasing doses (0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 mL/day) of eucalyptus wood vinegar (EWV) on intake, apparent digestibility, water balance, nitrogen balance, ruminal parameters, serum biochemistry, and ingestive behavior of sheep fed a high-concentrate diet. Five castrated male Santa Inês sheep, with an average age of 35 months and an initial body weight of 55.8 ± 9.0 kg, were used in a 5 × 5 Latin square design. The experimental period lasted a total of 100 days, distributed across five consecutive 20-day periods. In each period, 14 days were allocated for adaptation and six days for data collection. Increasing EWV levels increased (P < 0.05) the intake of dry matter (DMI), organic matter (OM), crude protein (CP), non-fibrous carbohydrates (NFC), and total digestible nutrients (TDN), but no effect (P > 0.05) was observed on the apparent digestibility of DM (81.97%), OM (83.92%), CP (81.98%), NFC (92.44%), and NDF (43.34%) in the sheep diet. There was no effect (P > 0.05) of increasing EWV doses on feeding (163 min/day) and rumination (310 min/day) times, pH values (6.86), water balance (48.29%), or ruminal N—NH3 (9.84 mg/dL) concentrations in sheep. Increasing EWV doses increased (P < 0.05) nitrogen (N) intake and the amounts of N absorbed and retained by the animals. A decrease (P < 0.05) in serum lactate levels was observed with increasing EWV doses supplied to the animals. A dose of up to 20 mL/day of EWV is recommended for sheep fed high-concentrate diets.Keywords: Liquid smoke, N balance, Phytogenic, Pyroligneous extract, Pyrolysis

Introduction

Lamb production in tropical environments is quite challenging, mainly due to the low forage intake capacity of these animals, combined with their high susceptibility to parasitism. Furthermore, muscle anabolism, which is characteristic of the growth phase, imposes high nutritional demands on lambs. In this context, the use of concentrate ingredients (i.e., grains and their derivatives) in the diet of growing sheep is an important strategy to meet the high nutritional requirements of these animals.

The presence of starch-rich grains has made lamb diets increasingly energy-dense (Carlis et al., 2023); however, the presence of highly fermentable starch in the rumen can increase the occurrence of metabolic disorders (i.e., acidosis, rumenitis, laminitis, and liver abscesses) in ruminants (Christodoulopoulos, 2025; Gouvêa et al., 2022). Therefore, the use of antibiotics that modulate ruminal fermentation, particularly when fiber inclusion is a limiting factor in feedlot diet formulation, represents an alternative to promote productivity, health, and well-being in feedlot lambs (Casagrande et al., 2024; Polizel et al., 2021). Reis et al. (2018) found that the inclusion of monensin in the diet of sheep with acute ruminal lactic acidosis exerted a prophylactic effect by decreasing the rate of ruminal pH decline and reducing ruminal acidosis in sheep.

Antibiotics that modulate rumen fermentation have faced several restrictions on their use in countries across Europe, America, and Asia (Acosta et al., 2025). Claims of induction of antibiotic resistance, together with alignment with One Health guidelines, have led to measures prohibiting or drastically reducing the use of antibiotics as rumen fermentation modulators (Enshaie et al., 2025). In this context, there is a need to validate alternatives capable of maintaining production performance and animal well-being in high-concentrate diets for ruminants (Ibeagha-Awemu et al., 2025). Essential oils, tannins, flavonoids, and other phytogenic compounds have shown promising results as feed additives, including as modulators of ruminal fermentation in sheep (Nastoh et al., 2024). In an extensive meta-analysis, Silva et al. (2025) reported that phytogenic additives did not interfere with nutrient intake but increased the digestibility of crude protein in sheep diets.

Wood vinegar (syn. pyroligneous extract, liquid smoke, pyroligneous acid) is a liquid resulting from the condensation of part of the smoke generated during the burning of lignocellulosic materials for charcoal production (Grewal et al., 2018). It has several applications due to its antimicrobial, antiparasitic, antioxidant, healing, and anti-inflammatory properties (Gama et al., 2024; Mota et al., 2025; Souza et al., 2018). The presence of acetic acid and other carboxylic acids, in addition to phenolic compounds, are the main factors responsible for its bioactive properties (Pimenta et al., 2018). Recently, eucalyptus wood vinegar (EWV) was evaluated as an additive in diets for sheep receiving high-concentrate diets, and linear increases in fiber intake and apparent crude protein digestibility were observed with the supplementation of 144 mg of total phenols/day (Melo et al., 2025). However, further studies are needed to strengthen the evidence regarding the effects of EWV as a phytogenic additive for sheep fed high-grain diets.

Therefore, it is hypothesized that EWV may improve nutrient utilization in sheep while maintaining stable ruminal pH and biochemical markers, even in animals fed high-concentrate diets. Thus, the objective was to evaluate the effects of increasing doses (0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 mL/day of EWV, equivalent to 24.6, 49.2, 73.8, and 98.3 mg/day of total phenols, respectively) on intake, digestibility, ingestive behavior, nitrogen balance, and serum biochemistry of sheep receiving high-concentrate diets.

Material and methods

Animals and treatments

Five castrated male Santa Inês sheep, with an average age of 35 months and an average body weight of 55.8 ± 9.0 kg, were used. The animals were housed in individual metabolic cages equipped with feeders, water troughs, and feces and urine collectors. The experimental period lasted a total of 100 days, distributed across five consecutive 20-day periods. In each period, 14 days were allocated for adaptation, followed by six days dedicated to experimental data collection.

Before the experimental period, the animals were treated with Ripercol (Zoetis, USA) against endo- and ectoparasites. In addition, the animals were adapted to a high-concentrate diet, beginning with a forage-to-concentrate ratio of 50:50 until reaching the experimental diet with an 18:82 forage-to-concentrate ratio. After the initial adaptation, the animals remained on an 18:82 forage-to-concentrate ratio throughout the experimental period.

A Latin square design was used, with five animals and five treatments (0, 5.0, 10.0, 15.0, and 20.0 mL of EWV/animal/day). The diet was formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of castrated male sheep at maintenance, according to NRC guidelines (National Research Council, 2007). The concentrate consisted of ground corn grain, soybean meal, wheat bran, mineral premix (Ovinofós®, DSM-Firmenich, Brazil), calcitic limestone, and sea salt (Table 1).

Wood vinegar

The crude extract was obtained through the carbonization of wood from a hybrid of Eucalyptus urophylla and Eucalyptus grandis (Eucalyptus urograndis – clone GG100). The material was subsequently subjected to double vacuum distillation for purification. The procedures for production, purification, and chemical characterization of the wood vinegar were described by Pimenta et al. (2023).

EWV was administered 30 min after feed delivery (8:30 a.m. and 4:30 pm.), directly into the animals' mouths, using disposable syringes to ensure daily delivery of the tested doses. The total dosage was divided into two daily administrations, following the feeding schedule. For animals that did not receive EWV, 20 mL of water was administered via syringe as a placebo solution.

Total phenol content of EWV

The total phenol content was quantified according to the methodology proposed by Meda et al. (2005), using the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. For this purpose, 0.5 mL of EWV (20 ppm) and 2.5 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (0.2 N) were added to a test tube. After approximately 5 min, 2 mL of sodium carbonate (75 g/L) were added, and the tubes were left to stand for 2 h under light exposure.

Subsequently, absorbance was measured using a Gehaka UV-340 G spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 760 nm, using methanol as a blank. The obtained values were extrapolated from a calibration curve prepared with standard gallic acid (20 to 200 ppm), and the results were expressed as grams of gallic acid equivalent (GAE)/100 mL of EWV (Table 2).

The determination of total flavonoids was also performed according to the methodology described by Meda et al. (2005), with adaptations. For this purpose, a 5 mL aliquot of EWV (20 ppm) was mixed with 5 mL of aluminum chloride (2%) in a test tube and, after 10 min, absorbance was measured using a Gehaka UV-340 G spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 415 nm. The sample itself, diluted in methanol without the addition of AlCl3, was used as a blank. Quantification was performed using a quercetin standard curve (5 to 50 mg/L), and the results were expressed as grams of quercetin equivalent (QE)/100 mL of EWV (Table 2).

Intake and apparent digestibility

Nutrient intake was determined as the difference between the amount of nutrients offered and the amount of nutrients present in the orts. For this purpose, daily weighings of the feed and corresponding orts were performed, and samples of these materials were collected during the collection period and frozen at −10 °C for subsequent chemical analysis. The feed was provided ad libitum.

For apparent digestibility, the total fecal collection method was used. During the collection days, feces were removed daily from the plastic trays and weighed, and a homogenized aliquot corresponding to 10% of the total excreted every 24 h was collected. The samples were stored in a freezer at −10 °C for subsequent analysis.

Chemical composition of feed, orts, and feces

Frozen samples of feed, orts, and feces were thawed and subsequently pre-dried in forced-air circulation ovens at 55 °C for 72 h. The samples were then ground in a knife mill fitted with a 1 mm sieve, packed in hermetically sealed plastic bags, and properly identified.

Samples of feed, orts, and feces were analyzed for their chemical composition according to AOAC (2019) methods for the determination of dry matter (DM), mineral matter (MM), crude protein (CP), and ether extract (EE). Neutral detergent fiber corrected for ash and protein (NDFap) and acid detergent fiber were determined according to the methodology of Van Soest et al. (1991), adapted by Mertens (2002) and Licitra et al. (1996). Non-fibrous carbohydrates (NFC) were calculated according to Detmann and Valadares Filho (2010), while total digestible nutrients (TDN) were estimated using the methodology proposed by Weiss (1999).

Water balance

Total water intake was measured as the difference between the amount of water offered and the amount remaining, discounting water losses through evaporation. Before being supplied to the animals, the water was weighed using 10-L polyethylene buckets, and during the collection period, both offered and leftover water were weighed every 24 h. To estimate evaporative losses, a bucket containing the same amount of water was placed in a strategic location out of reach of the sheep, and the average evaporation loss was quantified as the difference in weight over a 24-h period to correct water intake values.

Dietary water intake was estimated based on the moisture content of the feed and orts after chemical analysis (corrected for evaporation losses). The amount of water excreted was estimated from the volume of water present in fecal and urinary excretions.

Nitrogen balance

To determine nitrogen balance, the total urine collection method was used with the aid of plastic trays. During the collection period, 20 mL of sulfuric acid solution (50%w/w) was added to the trays to prevent nitrogen volatilization. Urine excreted over a 24-h period was weighed, and a 10% aliquot of the total volume was collected after filtration through gauze and frozen at −10 °C for subsequent total nitrogen analysis according to AOAC (2019). Nitrogen balance was determined from the difference between total dietary nitrogen intake and total nitrogen excreted through feces and urine.

Rumen fluid

On the penultimate day of each collection period, 100 mL of ruminal fluid was collected from each animal using an esophageal probe coupled to a vacuum pump (model 131, Prismatec, Brazil) at 0, 3, and 6 h after the morning meal. The first jet of rumen fluid was discarded to avoid contamination with saliva. Immediately after collection, the fluid was filtered through double-layer cotton gauze, and pH was measured using a digital potentiometer (model PG 1800, Gehaka, Brazil). After pH measurement, the samples were stored in 80 mL polyethylene containers containing 1 mL of hydrochloric acid solution (2 N) and frozen for subsequent determination of ammoniacal nitrogen (N—NH3) according to the methodology described by Detmann et al. (2012).

Serum biochemistry

Blood samples were collected approximately 4 h after the morning feeding (12:00 a.m.) by jugular venipuncture using a vacuum collection system and tubes without anticoagulant. After collection, the samples were immediately centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and stored at −10 °C. Subsequently, the samples were thawed at room temperature and analyzed for concentrations of protein (g/dL), albumin (g/dL), globulin (g/dL), urea (mg/dL), creatinine (mg/dL), cholesterol (mg/dL), triglycerides (mg/dL), alanine aminotransferase (U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (U/L), and gamma-glutamyl transferase (U/L) using commercial kits (Labtest, Brazil) and a semi-automatic biochemical analyzer (model LB-B200, Bioplus, Brazil), according to the manufacturer's recommendations.

Blood samples intended for leukocyte counting were collected in tubes containing anticoagulant, and the counts were performed using a hemocytometer. Hematological analyses followed the guidelines described by Jain (1993).

Ingestive behavior

The animals were observed continuously for 24 h (from 07:00 to 06:55 the following day) using the instantaneous scan sampling method proposed by Martin and Bateson (2021), with observations recorded at 5-min intervals. The behavioral variables evaluated were idle time (IT; total time during which the animal was neither feeding nor ruminating), feeding time (FT; total time spent feeding), and rumination time (RT; total time spent ruminating).

Cud chewing activity was determined according to the methodology described by Eustáquio Filho et al. (2016). The number of chews per cud (chews/cud) and the time spent chewing each cud (s/cud) were recorded using digital stopwatches simultaneously with the behavioral observations.

For cud counting, three rumination cuds were observed during each of three daily periods: 7:00–12:00, 12:00–18:00, and 18:00–24:00. Feeding and rumination efficiencies, expressed as grams of DM and NDF per unit of time, were calculated by dividing intake by the total time spent feeding and/or ruminating over the 24-h period, according to the methodology described by Bürger et al. (2000).

Discussion

The eucalyptus wood vinegar evaluated here contains 491.7 mg GAE/100 mL of total phenols.

Increasing doses of EWV increased DM and OM intake in sheep, possibly due to the secondary metabolites present in this phytogenic additive, which may stimulate feed intake and dilute the proportion of phenolic compounds in the consumed DM (Xie et al., 2023). EWV is rich in phenolic compounds such as guaiacol, phenol, and cresols, among others, which possess bactericidal and anti-inflammatory properties (Pimenta et al., 2018). It is suggested that EWV acted selectively by reducing bacterial growth and organic acid production in the rumen of sheep. We speculate, its anti-inflammatory properties may have favored ruminal epithelial health, improving organic acid absorption and helping to prevent disorders associated with high-concentrate diets. Compounds with anti-inflammatory activity can modulate the ruminal environment by regulating the expression of pro-inflammatory genes and anti-inflammatory cytokines, thereby promoting rumen health and improving animal performance (Upadhaya & Kim, 2017). Melo et al. (2025) also described increases in DM and OM intake with increasing EWV doses in sheep diets. The increases in crude protein (CP), non-fibrous carbohydrates (NFC), and total digestible nutrients (TDN) intake were likely a consequence of the positive effect of EWV on dry matter intake. Similarly, Vinyard et al. (2021) reported increased organic matter intake in confined sheep receiving diets containing 15% grape residue, a feedstuff rich in phenolic compounds. In this context, increases in protein and energy intake, especially in animals receiving high-concentrate diets, are associated with improved performance (Nascimento et al., 2020) and are particularly relevant during feedlot adaptation protocols (Silvestre et al., 2023).

The apparent digestibility of DM (81.97%) and OM (83.92%), as well as the TDN content (84.18%), were high and were not influenced by EWV supplementation. Phenolic compounds are known to modulate degradation and fermentation rates, thereby affecting ruminal dry matter disappearance kinetics (Vastolo et al., 2025). However, they may not influence total nutrient digestibility, as observed by Kazemi and Valizadeh (2021) when evaluating pomegranate by-products rich in phenolic compounds in sheep diets. Therefore, we speculate that increasing doses of phenolic compounds affected the kinetics of particle disappearance in the rumen (Hassan et al., 2020). However, when considering total tract digestibility (rumen and intestines), no effect of EWV was observed, possibly due to the relatively low doses evaluated. Similarly, Liu et al. (2018) reported that phenolic compounds altered ruminal DM degradation without affecting the apparent digestibility of dietary DM.

The time spent ruminating, rumination efficiencies, and chewing behavior of sheep were not altered by increasing EWV supplementation, likely due to the similar intake and digestibility of dietary NDF among treatments. Likewise, Melo et al. (2025) reported no effect of increasing EWV doses on dietary NDF digestibility or chewing dynamics in sheep. It is likely that the intake of physically effective NDF (peNDF), provided by Tifton 85 hay, was sufficient to maintain rumen motility and health in confined animals (Pereira et al., 2023). Thus, it can be suggested that the use of EWV in high-concentrate diets allowed the maintenance of masticatory activity, saliva production, and ruminal pH regulation (Castillo-Lopez et al., 2021). The observed rumination time (310.6 min/day), proportion of time spent ruminating (11.6%), and cud-chewing activity (50.93 chews/cud and 38.15 s/cud) indicate normal rumination patterns consistent with healthy animals.

On the other hand, phenolic compounds can affect the rate of particle disappearance (Qomariyah et al., 2021) and have recognized effects on rumination frequency (Bottegal et al., 2025). However, the magnitude of these effects depends on ruminal pH and the starch content of the diet (Monjezi et al., 2022).

The water balance of the sheep was not influenced by increasing EWV doses, possibly due to the similarity in total water intake and water excretion among treatments. However, a slight effect of EWV on urinary water excretion was observed, potentially related to increased diuresis resulting from urine acidification. EWV has an acidic pH (3.41), which may influence not only ruminal pH (Moya et al., 2011) but also blood and urinary pH. Hydrogen ions (H+) are exchanged for sodium ions (Na+) in the renal tubules, resulting in sodium accumulation and stimulation of greater urinary water excretion (Bobulescu & Moe, 2009).

Nitrogen (N) intake was positively influenced by the effect of EWV on dry matter intake in sheep; however, the concentration of ammoniacal nitrogen (N—NH3) in the rumen averaged 9.84 mg/dL and did not vary with increasing EWV doses. The similar ruminal N—NH3 concentrations may be associated with the lack of an effect of EWV on the digestibility of dietary crude protein (CP) in sheep (Zanin et al., 2025). On the other hand, our results showed increases in N absorption and retention, likely due to the greater N intake associated with increasing EWV doses. This hypothesis is supported by the absence of an effect of EWV on nitrogen excretion by the animals. Similarly, dietary supplementation with Cistanche deserticola at levels corresponding to 2% and 4% of dry matter intake promoted greater feed intake and nitrogen retention in grazing male sheep (Liu et al., 2024). The relatively stable fecal N excretion observed in the present study is also consistent with the findings of Pan et al. (2025). According to Silva et al. (2019), N recycling and retention mechanisms in ruminants are strongly influenced by the amount of nitrogen consumed by the animals.

The effects of phenolic compounds on the digestibility of the nitrogenous fraction of the diet and on improving nitrogen utilization by animals are widely reported in the literature (Ahmed et al., 2024), often accompanied by reductions in plasma urea concentrations (Deng et al., 2024). However, no effects of EWV were observed on biomarkers of protein metabolism, including urea, total protein, albumin, and globulin concentrations. Therefore, we speculate that metabolizable protein supply was similar among sheep regardless of the EWV dose offered.

Serum indices provide essential information regarding the metabolic characteristics and health status of animals, serving as important markers for the assessment of physiological and pathological conditions (Hussein et al., 2020). Thus, the maintenance of serum creatinine concentrations and liver enzyme activities (AST, ALT, and GGT) reinforces the safety of EWV as a feed additive, with no evidence of hepatotoxic effects. These findings agree with those of Bashtani et al. (2025), who evaluated the inclusion of up to 18% grape pomace in feedlot lamb diets. The stability of blood metabolites suggests preservation of metabolic homeostasis, highlighting the potential application of EWV in commercial lamb production systems while also contributing to more sustainable feeding practices in ruminant nutrition.

Serum lactate concentrations decreased despite the increase in NFC intake, indicating that EWV may influence ruminal fermentation processes, particularly the biochemical reduction of propionate to lactate. Specifically, the phenolic compounds present in EWV may have negatively affected the ruminal degradation of prolamins in the corn endosperm (Zhao et al., 2021), potentially reducing pyruvate production derived from ruminal starch fermentation. Ruminal lactate is produced through enzymatic reduction of ruminal pyruvate under conditions of excess reducing coenzymes (Ungerfeld, 2020); therefore, a reduction in propionate production may have contributed to the modulation of ruminal fermentation and pH stability.

Similar effects on ruminal fermentation patterns and the ruminal environment have been reported with the use of phenolic compounds and tannins, which are capable of modulating microbial fermentation (Zhao et al., 2021). Likewise, Benchaar et al. (2007), when evaluating essential oils containing eugenol, observed a tendency toward increased ruminal pH in dairy cows fed high-grain diets. These findings suggest that phytogenic compounds modulate ruminal fermentation through their effects on the ruminal microbial community, particularly on acetate- and propionate-producing bacteria, which play major roles in fermentation and acid production within the rumen (Rivera-Chacon et al., 2022).

The main limitation of this study is the small number of animals used. Although this limitation was partially mitigated by the experimental design, a larger number of animals could have generated more robust responses. The use of mature animals in our study may also decrease the magnitude of the treatment effects due to metabolic differences in nutrient partitioning and utilization. Additional limitations include the lack of more robust data on ruminal fermentation patterns, particularly regarding short-chain fatty acid production and microbial protein synthesis. We believe that further studies with a larger number of lambs and evaluation of ruminal parameters and carcass and meat quality may strengthen the indications for the use of eucalyptus wood vinegar.

Conclusion

Supplementation with up to 20 mL/day of EWV (491.7 mg GAE/100 mL) increases crude protein and total digestible nutrient intake in sheep but does not affect ruminal parameters or ingestive behavior. Furthermore, supplementation with 20 mL/day of EWV increases nitrogen intake, absorption, and retention while decreasing serum lactate concentrations. Therefore, a dose of 20 mL/day of EWV is recommended for sheep fed diets with a forage-to-concentrate ratio of 18:82. Further studies are needed to evaluate higher EWV doses and their effects on growing lambs.
