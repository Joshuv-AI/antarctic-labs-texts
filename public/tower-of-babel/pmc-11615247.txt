SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6-Omicron causes less damage to the Drosophila heart and mouse cardiomyocytes than ancestral Nsp6
Authors: Jun-yi Zhu, Jin-Gu Lee, Guanglei Wang, Jianli Duan, Joyce van de Leemput, Hangnoh Lee, Wendy Wenqiao Yang, Zhe Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA few years into the COVID-19 pandemic, the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron strain rapidly becomes and has remained the predominant strain. To date, Omicron and its subvariants, while more transmittable, appear to cause less severe disease than prior strains. To study the cause of this reduced pathogenicity we compare SARS-CoV-2 ancestral Nsp6 with Nsp6-Omicron, which we have previously identified as one of the most pathogenic viral proteins. Here, through ubiquitous expression in Drosophila, we show that ancestral Nsp6 causes both structural and functional damage to cardiac, muscular, and tracheal (lung) tissue, whereas Nsp6-Omicron has minimal effects. Moreover, we show that ancestral Nsp6 dysregulates the glycolysis pathway and disrupts mitochondrial function, whereas Nsp6-Omicron does not. Through validation in mouse primary cardiomyocytes, we find that Nsp6-induced dysregulated glycolysis underlies the cardiac dysfunction. Together, the results indicate that the amino acid changes in Omicron might hinder its interaction with host proteins thereby minimizing its pathogenicity.Subject terms: Mechanisms of disease, Glycobiology

Introduction

In the fifth year of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, many places have returned to pre-pandemic normalcies, even though the threat lingers. SARS-CoV-2 Omicron and its subvariants continue to be the predominant strain in the US and Europe1,2. Whereas early in the pandemic, newly emerged SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern led to increased infection rates, hospitalizations, and deaths3, epidemiological studies have found Omicron to be less virulent compared to the prior lineages4–15. SARS-CoV-2 Omicron has been shown highly capable of evading neutralization and to cause breakthrough infections16,17. Despite increased population immunity due to prior infections and vaccination efforts, Omicron appears more transmissible; indeed, its sub-lineages have dominated in much of the world since its emergence1,2,18,19. Many variants have been identified in the Omicron spike protein which facilitates virus entry into host cells and tissues, and in its nucleocapsid protein which is important for viral replication20–22. Notably, cleavage of the Omicron spike is less efficient, rendering it less susceptible to fusion than previous lineages23, and to favor ACE2-mediated TMPRSS2-independent endosomal cell entry instead18,21,22,24; whereas previous lineages depend on both host factors25. Moreover, animal studies consistently find reduced replication and viral presence in the lungs with relatively increased replication and viral presence in the respiratory tracks, like nasal cavities, following SARS-CoV-2 Omicron infection21–24,26. The reduced viral presence in the lungs is associated with decreased body weight loss, reduced lung pathology and inflammation, and improved survival compared to infection with ancestral SARS-CoV-2 lineages22–24,26,27. Explants of human lung and bronchial tissues, similarly have shown increased replication and viral presence in upper (lung parenchymal, nasal) compared to lower (bronchi) respiratory tracts21,28,29. All these changes could contribute to increased Omicron viral presence in the upper airway, and facilitate viral transmission via airborne droplets6,21,30. Aside from its own nucleocapsid, SARS-CoV-2 relies heavily on host mechanisms to replicate. This hijacking of host pathways to favor virus replication causes tissue pathogenicity in addition to pathogenic effects resulting from immune and inflammatory responses.

We have previously shown that ancestral SARS-CoV-2 proteins Orf6 and Nsp6 are among the most pathogenic and in flies are responsible for respiratory and cardiac damage associated with COVID-1931,32. Here, we focus on Nsp6, since it is encoded by one of the hypervariable regions of the SARS-CoV-2 genome33. Our prior study used heart-specific expression (4xHand-Gal4) of ancestral SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6 in Drosophila, and found that Nsp6-mediated glycolysis pathway disruption underlies the cardiac pathology associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection in flies34. In the current study, we compared Nsp6-Omicron to the ancestral Nsp6. By ubiquitous expression (Tub-Gal4) of the Nsp6 variant in Drosophila, we could examine heart, muscle, and lung (trachea). Furthermore, we validated the glycolysis-induced heart damage related findings in mouse primary cardiomyocytes.

Results

Nsp6-Omicron versus ancestral SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6

First, we compared the sequences and structures of the SARS-CoV-2 ancestral Nsp6 (sequence NC_045512, Wuhan-Hu1 isolate) and the Nsp6-Omicron variant (sequence obtained from GISAID35). This revealed the three amino acid deletion (L-S-G; Δ106–108) and a single-amino acid variant (I189V) in Nsp6-Omicron previously described in sequences from Omicron in South Africa36. The previous report37 suggested that the global structure of Nsp6 is not altered by the triad deletion, in particular concerning the transmembrane core; however, protein interaction, such as with host proteins, might still be affected37. Based on the overall less severe illness following infection with SARS-CoV-2 Omicron (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention), we would predict that these variants in Nsp6 confer reduced pathogenicity.

To verify our hypothesis that the Omicron variant induced fewer pathogenic effects due to the Nsp6 mutations, we first expressed ancestral Nsp6 (Wuhan-Wu-138;) and Nsp6-Omicron (B1.1.529) genes in HEK 293T cells and analyzed their effects on cell viability. We found that both Nsp6 variants exhibited similar protein levels (Fig. 1a and Supplemental Fig. 1) and additionally contributed to reduced cell viability, in line with our previous findings for ancestral Nsp631; however, the ancestral Nsp6 was significantly more toxic than the Nsp6-Omicron variant (Fig. 1b). Because HEK 293T cells tend to form spheres even when attached, which can obscure clear visualization of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), we opted to use the monkey kidney cell line COS-7 for its advantageous morphology. COS-7 cells spread out well on the culture surface, making them ideal for observing the shape of the ER. Both HEK 293T and COS-7 cell lines are derived from kidney tissue, with HEK 293T being a human cell line and COS-7 a monkey cell line. Despite these differences in morphology, the distribution of the protein NSP6 is not expected to vary significantly between these two cell types, making COS-7 a suitable alternative for our studies. Both showed ER and perinuclear localization in COS-7 cells (Fig. 1c–e and Supplementary Fig. 2). This initial test supports the notion of reduced pathogenic effects by Nsp6-Omicron compared to ancestral Nsp6.

Pathogenicity in vivo; lethality and survival in flies

To investigate the pathogenicity of Nsp6-Omicron compared to ancestral Nsp6 in vivo, we employed Drosophila. We generated UAS-based transgenic fly lines that carried the Nsp6 gene (either the ancestral or Omicron variant) driven by the Tubulin enhancer (Tub-Gal4) to achieve ubiquitous expression from embryonic through to adult developmental stages. By using a balancer (Tm3, Sb) for the Gal4 line, following the cross with UAS-Nsp6, progenies will either express Nsp6 ubiquitously, driven by the Tub-Gal4 enhancer in the UAS>Gal4 configuration, or they will not express Nsp6 and will instead exhibit Stubble (Sb) bristles as a distinct feature. If the gene-of-interest does not affect early lethality, progenies will show a 1:1 ratio for these two groups. However, flies that expressed ancestral Nsp6 showed >50% lethality at eclosion [calculated by ((Sb − full length bristles)/Sb) x 100] (Fig. 2a, b). Flies that expressed Nsp6-Omicron showed only minor, albeit still significant, early lethality compared to control progenies (Fig. 2a, b). To rule out the possibility that the reduced lethality was due to lower protein expression of Nsp6-Omicron, we examined the protein levels in Drosophila indirect flight muscle. We found no difference in expression levels between flies expressing ancestral Nsp6 and those expressing Nsp6-Omicron (Supplemental Fig. 3a). For flies that emerged as adults (i.e., eclosed), we measured the lifespan. Those that expressed ancestral Nsp6 flies displayed markedly reduced lifespans compared to control flies, with only a minor reduction observed in flies that expressed Nsp6-Omicron (Fig. 2c). Like our in vitro finding, Nsp6-Omicron appears much less toxic than its ancestral variant in our in vivo Drosophila model system.

Cardiac pathology in flies

Next, we examined the specific pathology associated with Nsp6 expression. First, we looked at morphological and functional aspects of the adult fly heart. The hearts of flies that expressed ancestral Nsp6 showed marked structural disorganization, evident in significantly reduced cardiac myofibrial density (Fig. 3a, b). We also observed increased deposition of Pericardin (Fig. 3a, d). Pericardin is a type IV collagen that plays a critical role in maintaining Drosophila cardiac tissue integrity, its overabundance indicates pathophysiological fibrosis39–42. Flies that expressed Nsp6-Omicron showed no obvious structural damage (Fig. 3a), no significant changes in cardiac myofibrillar density (Fig. 3b), and only a minor increase in cardiac collagen deposition (Fig. 3d).

Furthermore, we found that the expression of ancestral Nsp6, but not Nsp6-Omicron, caused significant loss of mitochondria (mitochondrial area) (Fig. 3c, e). When we assessed cardiac function using optical coherence tomography (OCT), the hearts from flies that expressed ancestral Nsp6 had clear defects visible in the orthogonal view (Fig. 3f) associated with a significantly increased heart period (Fig. 3g). While Nsp6-Omicron seemed to affect the heart to some extend based on the orthogonal view of the OCT (Fig. 3f), we detected no significant change in heart period compared to control flies (Fig. 3g).

Altogether these findings demonstrate that SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6-Omicron exerts minimal effect on the heart, i.e., we only detected a minor increase in cardiac collagen deposition, whereas, the presence of the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6 led to severe structural and function cardiac defects.

Muscular pathology in flies

Aside from the heart, we examined the effect of the Nsp6 variants on muscle structure and function, specifically the morphology of the indirect flight muscle. Neither the ancestral nor the Omicron variant of Nsp6 caused significant morphological changes in muscle fiber (Fig. 4a, Actin; and Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). However, like the heart, we found significantly reduced mitochondria (mitochondria number) in muscle from flies that expressed ancestral Nsp6, but not from those with Nsp6-Omicron expression (Fig. 4a, b). This was correlated with significantly reduced ATP in these muscles (Fig. 4c). To examine the effect of this on muscle function, we applied a climbing assay as a measure of locomotion. In line with the mitochondria findings, flies that expressed ancestral Nsp6, but not Nsp6-Omicron, showed a significant climbing defect (Fig. 4d). The ancestral Nsp6 flies also displayed a held-up wing position, a phenotype indicative of damaged muscle43, that was not observed in the Nsp6-Omicron or control flies (Fig. 4e, f).

Altogether, these findings show that SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6-Omicron does not significantly affect muscle tissue, whereas presence of the ancestral Nsp6 variant dramatically affects muscle mitochondria and function.

Tracheal pathology in flies

Since SARS-CoV-2 is a respiratory virus with a primary effect on the lungs, next we tested the pathological effect of the Nsp6 variants on the Drosophila tracheal system. The fly tracheas are structurally and functionally similar to the human lung44–47. In control larvae, the tracheal system consists of a central branch and multiple classes of terminal branches (Fig. 5a). In fly larvae that expressed ancestral Nsp6 the tracheas displayed significantly reduced branching (Fig. 5b). Notably, the central and class I terminal branches were not affected, only the class II terminal branches (Fig. 5a). Even though, we observed minor branching changes in the larvae that expressed Nsp6-Omicron, no significant reduction in tracheal branching was detected (Fig. 5a, b).

Effect on glycolysis in flies

Previously we identified increased glycolysis as an important contributor in Nsp6-mediated cardiac pathology34. Therefore, we next examined if the diminished phenotypes observed in flies that expressed Nsp6-Omicron, might be due to a reduced effect on the glycolysis pathway (Fig. 6a). First, we measured the relative expression levels of genes involved in glycolysis; Pgi, Pfk, Pglym78, and Eno were significantly increased in flies that expressed ancestral Nsp6, but not in those that expressed Nsp6-Omicron (Fig. 6b–e). In addition, we measured Phosphoglucose isomerase (Pgi) activity. Pgi is a key enzyme at the start of the glycolysis pathway that interconverts Glucose-6-phosphate and Fructose-6-phosphate. We found that ancestral Nsp6 significantly increased Pgi activity, but not Nsp6-Omicron (Fig. 6f). We also measured NADH levels, a metabolite produced from NAD+ through glycolysis. Ancestral Nsp6 increased NADH levels, but not Nsp6-Omicron (Fig. 6g).

Cardiac pathology and effect on glycolysis in mouse primary cardiomyocytes

Finally, we determined whether our findings in fly translated to a mammalian model. We isolated primary cardiomyocytes from E12.5 mouse embryonic hearts and transfected the cell cultures with the SARS-CoV-2 ancestral Nsp6 or the Nsp6-Omicron transgene. Mice with a disrupted glucose metabolism frequently show hypertrophy within their cardiac phenotype48,49, therefore we checked the cardiomyocytes for signs of hypertrophy. SARS-CoV-2 ancestral Nsp6, but not Nsp6-Omicron, significantly increased Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) and type B natriuretic peptide (BNP)—both established markers of cardiac hypertrophy49,50—compared to primary cardiomyocytes from the control group (Fig. 7a, b). Primary cardiomyocytes transfected with either SARS-CoV-2 ancestral or Omicron Nsp6 exhibited significantly increased Ca2+ absorption intervals when compared to the control cultures, but Nsp6-Omicron exhibited fewer defects compared to ancestral Nsp6 (Fig. 7c, d). Ca2+ waves are critical to the muscular contraction and relaxation cycles in the heart and thus for its function. Moreover, the relative expression level of genes involved in glycolysis (Gpi1, Pfkm, Pgam2, and Eno1) (Fig. 7e) were significantly increased after ancestral Nsp6, but not Nsp6-Omicron, transgene expression (Fig. 7f–i).

Together, these results confirm that, like our findings in fly, SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6-Omicron causes less cardiac pathology in primary mouse cardiomyocytes than the ancestral Nsp6, i.e., no sign of hypertrophy but evidence of altered Ca2+ waves present. These differences in pathology could be explained by Nsp6-Omicron’s lack of effect on the glycolysis pathway.

Discussion

Viral pathogenicity determined by specific proteins

Epidemiological studies consistently find that SARS-CoV-2 Omicron sub-lineages are less virulent compared to ancestral strains, albeit that the extent of pre-existing immunity contributing is not always clear4–15 (Disclaimer: This is a selection of the latest epidemiological studies, with more published continuously). Despite growing immunity attributed to prior infection and/or vaccination, the Omicron sub-lineages seem highly adept at evading the immune response. Thus, changes in virus entry (infection) and mutations that confer immune escape have been studied as likely underlying causes20,21. A spike mutation might enhance receptor binding and entry into cells or even alter the preferred entry mechanism. The latter has been shown for SARS-CoV-2 Omicron which unlike its ancestral lineages favors non-TMPRSS2-mediated entry21, thus potentially targeting different host tissues. Moreover, several accessory proteins have been shown to affect virus replication in addition to pathogenesis associated with inflammation51,52; suggesting that lineage variants in these proteins might modulate virulence. Here we looked beyond the immune system and inflammation, at virus proteins that confer pathogenicity by hijacking host pathways; likely to favor viral replication which heavily depends on the host cell systems53. We show that mutations in the SARS-CoV-2 nonstructural protein Nsp6 may also convey reduced pathogenesis and contribute to SARS-CoV-2 Omicron reduced virulence.

The concept of specific viral proteins driving pathogenicity in the host has been established for a variety of viruses. For instance, Vpr, Tat, and Nef have been identified as the main pathogenic proteins encoded by the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1) genome. Each of these proteins has been shown to play an important role in modulating disease severity54 and their pathogenic mechanisms have been well studied, with important contributions from flies. Such as the interaction of HIV-1 Nef with JNK and NF-kappaB signaling, which was identified in Drosophila55 and later confirmed in human monocytes56. Fly studies resulted in the discovery of the role of HIV-1 Tat protein in modifying host cell microtubule polymerization and kinetochore dynamics57, which was confirmed in human cells and then led to the discovery of TAT and Tubulin interactions that result in apoptosis58. The individual Zika virus protein NS4A was identified in fly to cause microcephaly, similar to the clinical phenotype in humans following Zika virus infection59. NS4A interacts with and inhibits Ankle2, a conserved protein required for cell division in brain development in both Drosophila and humans59. In addition, NS4A induced fly eye growth restriction through interaction with the JAK/STAT signaling pathways60. Similarly for SARS-CoV-2, we previously identified Nsp6 and Orf6 among its most pathogenic proteins in both human cells (HEK 293T)31 and in Drosophila32,34. Drosophila’s success as a system for studying human viruses stems from the viral dependence on host metabolic pathways, which are highly conserved between fly and human. In fact, for several interactions discovery in fly precedes that in mammals61. Notably, over 80% of the human proteins in the SARS-CoV-2 host interaction network are conserved in the fly32,62. There remain challenges in producing physiologically relevant results from Drosophila studies, especially concerning the correlation with in vivo infection tissue and cell type tropism in mammals.

SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6 pathogenicity in ancestral and Omicron lineages

Given that individual virus proteins can confer pathogenicity, we hypothesized that mutations in these proteins contribute to changes in pathogenic severity. In the case of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron, the variants in its pathogenic proteins, induced altered host interactions that contribute to its reduced virulence compared to the ancestral lineages. Hypervariable regions, those with significantly increased frequencies of in-frame indels, have been identified in the SARS-CoV-2 genome and suggest adaptive selection; the regions have been found in sequences that encode the spike, nucleocapsid, and accessory proteins, as well as the Nsp3 and Nsp6 nonstructural proteins33. Previously, we found Nsp6 to be one of the most pathogenic SARS-CoV-2 proteins31,32,34. The three amino acid deletion (Δ106–608) and single amino acid variant (I189V) gained by Nsp6-Omicron, do not result in major protein structural changes37. We hypothesized, however, that these variants might still affect host interactions. Indeed, we found that, whereas expression of ancestral Nsp6 caused substantial structural and functional damage to the fly heart, muscle, and lungs, Nsp6-Omicron only had minimal effect. We previously linked SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6-induced cardiac damage to its interaction with and dysregulation of the glycolysis pathway. Here we show that Nsp6-Omicron does not significantly alter glycolysis. Moreover, mouse primary cardiomyocytes expressing either ancestral Nsp6 or the Nsp6-Omicron variant validated the cardiac findings made in fly. Altogether these findings support the notion that the Omicron variants in Nsp6 confer reduced pathogenicity and contribute to this lineage’s overall reduced virulence. A recent study used molecular dynamics simulations to research the consequences of the variants in Nsp6-Omicron; their findings suggest the variants alter Nsp6 interaction with the lipid bilayer, which might hamper autophagy as a means to favor viral replication and maturation37. Another study found that Nsp6 is associated with ER stress-induced autophagy and the region 81–120 is the key region which is responsible for autophagy induction63. ER stress can activate alternative signaling pathways that impact cellular metabolism, including the upregulation of aerobic glycolysis64. The three amino acid deletion (Δ106–608) can attenuate the phenotypes induced by ancestral Nsp663. These findings demonstrate the difference in severity of the pathogenic effects of ancestral Nsp6 and Nsp6-Omicron on various tissues. They encourage further study to determine Nsp6 virus – host interactions are directly affected by the amino acid variants in Nsp6-Omicron.

Determining and tracking viral protein pathogenicity across lineages and variants

Growing evidence, including the findings presented herein, indicates that mutations outside of the spike protein could be driving SARS-CoV-2 pathology and severity20. Further emphasizing the importance of tracking variants in all proteins of emerging SARS-CoV-2 lineages, i.e., structural, accessory, and nonstructural proteins—to recognize the danger of newly emerging variants and identify the viral proteins responsible. Recently published studies provided an elegant example of how to study viral proteins and variants therein. The researchers introduced accessory (Orf) protein deletions51,52 or even specific mutations in the spike protein52 to the same SARS-CoV-2 viral background, and then investigated virus replication and the variant pathogenic effects in K18-hACE2 mice. This work revealed that in addition to viral pathogenesis (weight loss, and lung inflammation and pathology), specific SARS-CoV-2 accessory proteins significantly contribute to viral replication51,52. These insights are highly valuable; however, the approach is extremely time consuming and costly. Therefore, we propose the fly system to provide a rapid (and cheap) initial screen to keep track of emerging SARS-CoV-2 lineages and determine which pathogenic protein variants should be watched most closely. Especially with the newly emerging and recombining Omicron sub-lineages, which make it difficult to predict the outcomes of ongoing surges, even now, more than four years following the start of the pandemic.

Methods

Protein sequence and structure comparative analysis

To identify protein sequence variants in Nsp6, the SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6-Omicron variant sequence (B1.1.529) was obtained from GISAID and compared to the SARS-CoV-2 reference sequence (i.e., Wuhan-Hu1 isolate38, NCBI Accession ID: NC_045512). For prediction of the three dimensional protein structures of the SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6 variants RoseTTAFold65 on the ColabFold platform66 was used, with the “msa_method = mmseqs2” parameter for the homology search.

Cell lines, plasmids, and transfection

The human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T were purchased from ATCC and maintained in DMEM medium (Corning cellgro) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin–streptomycin (10 units/ml). Using a Q5 Site-Directed Mutagenesis kit (New England Biolabs) the mammalian expression plasmid for Nsp6-Omicron was generated by mutation of nucleotides in pCMV6-Nsp6-mCh (previously generated31) to obtain the triple amino acid deletion (Δ106–108) and isoleucine to valine mutation (I189V); these are the variants in Nsp6-Omicron compared to the ancestral Nsp6 protein (Fig. 1A). Transfection of the HEK 293T cells was performed with TransIT-293 (Mirus).

Western blotting

The HEK 293T cells were harvested 24 h post-transfection and lysed in the Nonidet P-40 lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM sodium chloride, 2 mM magnesium chloride, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, and a protease inhibitor mixture. After centrifugation at 16,100 × g for 10 min to remove insoluble materials, the cleared cell lysates were heated with Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad #1610747) at 95 °C for 10 min. Following, immunoblotting was performed according to standard protocol. NSP6 proteins were Flag-tagged and immunoblotted by flag antibody. Fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody (IRDye 680RD Goat anti-Mouse IgG, LI-COR #926-68070 used at 1:5,000 dilution) were used for detection. The fluorescent bands were imaged by a LI-COR Odyssey infrared imager using the software (Image Studio Version 5.2) provided by the manufacturer (LI-COR).

Cell viability assay

HEK 293T cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells/well in an opaque-walled white 96-well plate. After 24 h of incubation, 150 ng control plasmid (pCMV6-mCh), or ancestral Nsp6 or Nsp6-Omicron gene expressing plasmid was transfected into the cells. Following 48 h expression at 37 °C, cell viability was determined using a CellTiter-Glo 2.0 Assay (Promega). Briefly, a volume of CellTiter-Glo reagent equal to the volume of cell culture medium present in each well was added and mixed for 30 min at room temperature on an orbital shaker. Then, the luminescent signal was detected by the Spark Multimode Microplate Reader (Tecan) with 10 ms integration time, using the SparkControl application.

Cell imaging

COS-7 cells (African green monkey kidney fibroblast-like) were purchased from ATCC and maintained in DMEM medium (Corning cellgro) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and Penicillin–Streptomycin (10 units/ml). These are commonly used for protein subcellular localization studies due to their large size. To observe the localization of ancestral Nsp6 and Nsp6-Omicron, the COS-7 cells were seeded at 1.5 × 105 cells/well in a 35 mm dish (ibidi GmbH, Germany) coated with fibronectin. A total of 1 μg plasmid, 0.5 μg ancestral Nsp6 or Nsp6-Omicron gene plasmid (pCMV6-mCh, mCherry) with 0.5 μg ER marker plasmid (mCitrine-ER5; from Michael Davidson through Addgene), were co-transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Airyscan confocal microscopy (ZEISS) with a 100 nm resolution was performed 20 h post-transfection. Cells were washed with phosphate buffered saline (1× PBS) and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Fixed cells were washed and mounted using Fluoromount-G Mounting Medium, with DAPI nuclear labeling (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were imaged on an LSM 900 confocal microscope equipped with an Airyscan detector array (ZEISS) with ZEN operating and acquisition software (blue edition v3.1; ZEISS).

Fly strains

All fly stocks were reared and kept on standard food (Meidi, LLC) at 25 °C. The following fly lines were obtained from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center (Indiana University, IN): w1118 (# BL-3605), Tubulin-Gal4/TM3, Sb (# BL-5138). w1118 was used in the crosses and served as the control fly.

DNA cloning and generation of transgenic ancestral Nsp6 / Nsp6-Omicron fly strains

The ancestral Nsp6 plasmid was previously generated32 by PCR-amplification of human codon-optimized SARS-CoV-2 Nsp6 (pDONR233 vector; from Fritz Roth, through Addgene) to obtain the cDNA fragment, which was then assembled into the pUASTattB vector. Using a Q5 Site-Directed Mutagenesis kit (New England Biolabs) the fly expression plasmid for Nsp6-Omicron was generated by mutation of nucleotides in pUASTattB-Nsp6-mCh to obtain the triple amino acid deletion (Δ106–108) and isoleucine to valine mutation (I189V); these are the variants in Nsp6-Omicron compared to the ancestral Nsp6 protein. The transgene (ancestral Nsp6/Nsp6-Omicron) was then introduced into a fixed chromosomal docking site by germline transformation to generate UAS-Nsp6 overexpression transgenic flies. Fly injections were performed by Rainbow Transgenic Flies (Camarillo, CA).

Fly lethality at eclosion

To assay the effect of viral genes on viability, a balancer system was used. UAS transgene flies were crossed with the Tub-Gal4/TM3, Sb line. Offspring either carried UAS-transgene OE/TM3, Sb which carries the balancer chromosome resulting in orange eye color, shortened (stubbly; Sb) hairs on the back, and no transgene expression, or they carried UAS-transgene OE/Tub-Gal4, resulting in expression of the UAS-transgene driven by Tub-Gal4 and transgenic flies with the typical red eyes and long hair. Embryo progenies were collected and allowed to develop under standard conditions. Lethality at eclosion (adult emergence from pupa stage) was based on the percentage of flies with UAS-transgene expression (red, long) that failed to emerge as adults, relative to siblings that did not express the UAS-transgene construct (orange, short). The result was presented as a Mortality Index calculated eclosion [calculated by ((stubble − full length bristles)/stubble) × 100]. A total of ~300 flies from 6 repeats (~50 flies per repeat) per genotype were assayed.

Adult fly survival assay

Flies were kept at 25 °C, standard conditions, providing an optimal temperature for UAS-transgene expression. Adult male flies were maintained in vials in groups of 20 or fewer. The number of alive flies in each group was recorded every second day. Fly lifespan is typically 60–70 days for wild-type flies in the laboratory. The assay ended when no survivors were left for any of the fly lines. Per genotype, 100 flies were assayed.

Fly heart imaging

Adult female flies (4-day-old) were dissected and fixed for 10 min in 4% paraformaldehyde in 1× PBS. Dissected flies were incubated in block solution containing 0.1% Triton X-100 plus 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 1× PBS for 40 min, then incubated with Alexa Fluor 647 phalloidin (Thermo Fisher; 1:1000 in block solution) and mouse anti-Pericardin antibody (EC11, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank; 1:1000 in block solution) or mouse anti-ATP5a antibody (ab14748, Abcam; 1:1,000 in block solution) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the flies were washed three times with 1× PBS, then imaged or for immunostaining incubated with secondary Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse antibody (Thermo Fisher; 1:1000 in block solution) for 2 h at room temperature, and then washed three times with 1× PBS. Confocal imaging was performed using a ZEISS LSM 900 with Airyscan 2 and a 63× Plan-Apochromat 1.4 N.A. oil objective with ZEN operating and acquisition software (blue edition v3.1; ZEISS). For quantitative comparisons of fluorescence intensity, common settings were chosen to avoid oversaturation and then applied across images for all samples within an assay. Per genotype, 8 female (4-day-old) flies were imaged.

Fly heart analysis

For quantitative comparisons, we analyzed eight flies (female, 4-day-old) per genotype. ImageJ software67 (version 1.49) was used to process the images. The Z-stack projections were screened and image levels containing cardiac myofibrils were selected for analysis, while avoiding the ventral muscle layer that underlies the heart tube. The cardiac myofibril number was quantified by using the MyofibrilJ plugin for Fiji67 (version 1.53q). The entire heart region in segment A2 was selected using the freehand selection function in Fiji to count the number of cardiac myofibrils. For quantitation, cardiac myofibrillar density was calculated as the cardiac myofibril number divided by the size of the heart region. Pericardin deposition was measured in heart segment A2 based on the fluorescent intensity. The size of the area covered by mitochondria in heart segment A2 was measured. The percentage of mitochondrial coverage was calculated by dividing the area covered by mitochondria by the total area of the heart segment. For quantitative comparisons of fluorescence intensity, common settings were chosen to avoid oversaturation and then applied across images for all samples within an assay. Per genotype, 8 female (4-day-old) flies were imaged. Cardiac myofibril density and Pericardin deposition were each normalized to the values obtained in the control flies.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) measurements and analysis of cardiac function

Cardiac function in adult Drosophila was measured using an optical coherence tomography (OCT) system (Bioptigen Inc.). For this, 4-day-old male flies were anesthetized by carbon dioxide (CO2) for 3–5 min and gently placed on a plate with petroleum jelly (Vaseline) for immobilization with the dorsal aspect facing the OCT microscopy source, then rested for at least 10 min to ensure the fly was fully awake. For each genotype, eight flies were used. The OCT orthogonal view of the heart provides accurate and real-time measurements. This was used to record adult fly heart rhythm and heart wall movement at the same position, i.e., the cardiac chamber in the abdominal segments A2 of each fly. For each fly, measurements were obtained at three different positions within abdominal segment A2, these were averaged to obtain the cardiac diameter for that fly. M-mode images recorded the heart wall movement during the cardiac cycle. ImageJ software67 (version 1.49) was used to process the images. The diastolic dimension and systolic diameter were processed, measured, and determined based on three consecutive heartbeats. The heart rate was determined by counting the total number of beats that occurred during a 15-s recording.

Fly indirect flight muscle imaging

Adult female flies (4-day-old) were dissected and fixed for 30 min in 4% paraformaldehyde in 1× PBS. Indirect flight muscle fibers from both wings were assayed. Dissected muscle fibers were incubated in block solution containing 0.1% Triton X-100 plus 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 1× PBS for 40 min, then incubated with Alexa Fluor 647 phalloidin (Thermo Fisher; 1:1000 in block solution) and primary antibody ATP5a (ab14748, Abcam; 1:1000 in block solution), kettin (1B8-3D9; Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank; 1:100 in block solution), and actinin (2G3-3D7; Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank; 1:50 in block solution) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the flies were washed three times with 1× PBS, then imaged; or, for immunostaining incubated with secondary Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse antibody (Thermo Fisher; 1:1000 in block solution) for 2 h at room temperature, and then washed three times with 1× PBS. Confocal imaging was performed using a ZEISS LSM 900 with Airyscan 2 and a 63× Plan-Apochromat 1.4 N.A. oil objective with ZEN operating and acquisition software (blue edition v3.1; ZEISS). For quantitative comparisons of fluorescence intensity, common settings were chosen to avoid oversaturation and then applied across images for all samples within an assay. ImageJ software67 (version 1.49) was used to process the images. Eight flies were analyzed for each genotype.

ATP measurements in fly

The ATP levels were determined using the ATP Bioluminescent Assay Kit (Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For each sample, one female fly (4-day-old) was homogenized in 6 M guanidine-HCl (SRE0066, Sigma-Aldrich). Samples were boiled for 3 min, cleared by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 5 min, and diluted at 1:10,000. The colorimetric reaction was measured at 450 nm on a Spark multimode microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland; SparkControl software, v2.3). Eight samples were assayed per genotype.

Fly climbing assay

Adult female flies (4-day-old) were monitored by analyzing their performance to climb 6 cm in a horizontal tube within 14 s. A successful attempt was scored as 1, and failure to reach the top (6 cm line) as 0. Each fly was assessed five times to calculate the average climbing score. Per genotype, 20 flies were analyzed.

Fly wing observation

Wing position was observed using a ZEISS Stemi 305 Stereo Zoom microscope with 5:1 zoom. A total of ~300 flies from 6 repeats (~50 flies per repeat; female, 4-day-old) per genotype were counted manually. Representative images were performed using a ZEISS Ste-REO Discovery.V12, modular stereo microscope with 12× zoom and an AxioCam 208 color (camera 1.3.3.).

Fly tracheal branch imaging

3rd instar larvae were dissected and fixed for 20 min in 4% paraformaldehyde 1× PBS. Tracheal branch imaging was performed using a ZEISS Ste-REO Discovery.V12, modular stereo microscope with 12× zoom and an AxioCam 208 color (camera 1.3.3.). Tracheal branches were manually identified and counted in the larval segments A3-A5 and averaged by segment for each larva. ImageJ software67 (version 1.49) was used to process the images. Eight larvae were analyzed for each genotype.

Fly quantitative RT-PCR analysis

RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) from one adult fly (female, 4-day-old). RNA purity and concentration were determined using a Nanodrop-1000 (Thermo Scientific). Total RNA (1 μg) was reverse transcribed using Superscript IV (Invitrogen). SYBR Green based real-time qPCR (Power Cyber Mastermix; Applied Biosystems) was performed on a StepOne Plus machine (Applied Biosystems) using gene-specific primer pairs (Integrated DNA Technologies): Pgi-Forward (5’ ATT CGC ACA CAC TGC AAA CC); Pgi-Reverse (5’ CAG CAG TAT CTC GCC ATC GT); Pfk-Forward (5’ TCT GTG GCA CTG ACA TGA CC); (Pfk-Reverse: 5’ TAT GCA TCA CAG CTA GGC GG); Pglym78-Forward (5’ CCA CAT ATT GCA GTC GCA GC); Pglym78-Reverse (5’ AAT GCC CGT GGG CAG ATT TA); Eno-Forward (5’ GAA GGT CAA CCA GAT CGG CA); Eno-Reverse (5’ GAA CGA GTC CTC GGT CTC AC); Gapdh-Forward (5’ GAT TTG TTG TTG GCC GCA GT); Gapdh-Reverse (5’ GAA GTG GTT CGC CTG GAA GA). Quantitative values were determined using the 2-DDCT method and normalized to Gapdh as the endogenous reference gene. Four samples were assayed per genotype.

Pgi activity and NADH level measurement in fly

The enzymatic activity of Phosphoglucose isomerase (Pgi) and NADH levels were determined using the Phosphoglucose Isomerase Colorimetric Assay kit (Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For each sample, one female fly (4-day-old) was homogenized and diluted at 1:50. The colorimetric reaction was measured at 450 nm on a Spark multimode microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland; SparkControl software, v2.3). Four samples were assayed per genotype.

Mice

We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. The procedures for animal use were compliant with all relevant ethical regulations and approved by the University of Maryland School of Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Wild-type C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). Male and female mice were paired and observed for pregnancy by the presence of the vaginal plug (E0.5). At E12.5, pregnant female mice were euthanized, and embryos were collected for primary cardiomyocyte isolation.

Mouse primary cardiomyocyte isolation and culture

Mouse primary cardiomyocytes were isolated from eight E12.5 embryonic hearts using the Pierce Primary Cardiomyocyte Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, E12.5 embryonic hearts were collected and then minced individually. After three washes with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS), the minced heart tissue was treated with lysis buffer at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 30 min. Prewarmed cardiomyocyte culture medium—DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 mg/ml streptomycin, and cardiomyocyte growth supplement (from the Pierce Cardiomyocyte Isolation Kit; Thermo Fisher Scientific)—was added to each heart cell lysate to neutralize the lysis reaction. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, the cardiomyocytes were resuspended in cardiomyocyte culture medium, and then seeded in a Matrigel-coated 6-well culture plate at 1 × 106 cells/well density and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After 48 h in culture, the cardiomyocytes were transfected with control pcDNA3 plasmid, SARS-CoV-2 ancestral Nsp6, or Nsp6-Omicron plasmid using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen) overnight. The next morning, culture medium was replaced with fresh medium and the cells were cultured for another 48 h. The cells were collected for further morphological and molecular analysis.

Mouse RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and RT-PCR

RNA was extracted from mouse primary cardiomyocytes using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) and reverse transcribed using q-script cDNA Supermix (QuantaBio Biosciences) following the manufacturer’s guidelines and protocol. RNA samples were analyzed for quality and quantity using a NanoDrop ND-1000 Spectrophotometer. Next, expression of the following genes was analyzed in mouse E12.5 primary cardiomyocytes: ANP, BNP, Gpi1, Pfkm, Pgam2, and Eno1 with β-actin as an endogenous reference gene. Primer sequences used: ANP-Forward (5’ ACC TGC TAG ACC ACC TGG AG); ANP-Reverse (5’ CCT TGG CTG TTA TCT TCG GTA CCG G); BNP-Forward (5’ GAG GTC ACT CCT ATC CTC TGG); BNP-Reverse (5’ GCC ATT TCC TCC GAC TTT TCT C); Gpi1-Forward (5’ CCA TCA AGG TGG ACG GCA AAG A); Gpi1-Reverse (5’ CCG TGA TGG ATT TGC CAG TGT AC); Pfkm-Forward (5’ CTG TTC GCT CTA CCG TGA GGA T); Pfkm-Reverse (5’ TTG GAA CCA CCT TGA CCA GTC C); Pgam2-Forward (5’ CTG GAA TGA GGA GAT CGC ACC T); Pgam2-Reverse (5’ ATT CCA GTG GGC AGG TTC AGC T); Eno1-Forward (5’ TAC CGC CAC ATT GCT GAC TTG G); Eno1-Reverse (5’ GCT TGT TGC CAG CAT GAG AAC C); β-actin-Forward (5’ GTG ACG TTG ACA TCC GTA AAG A); β-actin-Reverse (5’ GCC GGA CTC ATC GTA CTC C). The control pcDNA3 plasmid treated cells served as control to the cardiomyocytes transfected with SARS-CoV-2 ancestral Nsp6 or Nsp6-Omicron. RT-PCR was performed with a Step One Plus System (Applied Biosystem, Grand Island, NY), using SsoFast EvaGreen Supermix (Bio-Rad) with a total of 20 μl reaction volume: 2 μl cDNA, 4 μl SsoFast EvaGreen Supermix, 2 μl F/R primer mix, and 12 μl water, followed by a standard touchdown PCR protocol. Quantitative values were determined using the 2-DDCT method. Three independently cultured cardiomyocyte samples were assayed per genotype.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using PAST software (Natural History Museum, Norway). Mean values are presented along with their standard deviation (SD). Data was first tested for normality by using the Shapiro–Wilk test (a = 0.05). Normally distributed data were analyzed either by Student’s t test (two groups) and Bonferroni comparison to adjust the P value or by a one-way analysis of variance followed by a Tukey–Kramer posttest for comparing multiple groups. Nonnormal distributed data were analyzed by either a Mann–Whitney test (two groups) or Bonferroni comparison to adjust P value or a Kruskal–Wallis H-test followed by a Dunn’s test for comparisons between multiple groups. Statistical significance is defined as P < 0.05. Survival data was analyzed by Fisher’s Exact test at 50% lethality. To ensure the validity and reliability of the results, consistent genetic backgrounds and ages of the animals were maintained throughout the experiments. All experiments will be conducted on females used per experiment. Each experiment will be repeated three times to ensure biological reproducibility. The Drosophila crosses will be performed independently, and similar controls will be used in each separate study to ensure reproducibility across genotypes. Environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, and Drosophila food will be kept constant. Details for sample size and replicates used for quantitation and the statistical tests applied to determine significance have been provided in the figure legends.
