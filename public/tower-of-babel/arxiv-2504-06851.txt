Mixing trichotomy for random walks on directed stochastic block models
Alessandra Bianchi, Giacomo Passuello, Matteo Quattropani

Abstract
. We consider a directed version of the classical Stochastic Block Model with m ≥ 2 m\geq 2 communities and a parameter α \alpha controlling the inter-community connectivity. We show that, depending on the scaling of α \alpha , the mixing time of the random walk on this graph can exhibit three different behaviors, which we refer to as subcritical, critical and supercritical. In the subcritical regime, the total variation distance to equilibrium decays abruptly, providing the occurrence of the so-called cutoff phenomenon. In the supercritical regime, the mixing is governed by the inter-community jumps, and the random walk exhibits a metastable behavior: at first it collapses to a local equilibrium, then, on a larger timescale, it can be effectively described as a mean-field process on the m m communities, with a decay to equilibrium which is asymptotically smooth and exponential. Finally, for the critical regime, we show a sort of interpolation of the two above-mentioned behaviors. Although the metastable behavior shown in the supercritical regime appears natural from a heuristic standpoint, a substantial part of our analysis can be read as a control on the homogenization of the underlying random environment. MSC 2020: primary: 60J10, 05C80, 05C81. Keywords: random digraphs, mixing time, mixing trichotomy, cutoff phenomenon, metastability. Acknowledgments. 1.
Introduction

1.1.
Mixing trichotomy: a phase transition in the mixing behavior

Phase transitions are among the most interesting and well-studied phenomena in statistical physics. In the broadest sense, a
phase transition
is an abrupt change in behavior of a system as a consequence of a slight variation in its parameters. In the mathematical literature, there are plenty of simple probabilistic models that present such a behavior, so it would be worthless to even try to do a small account here. Let us rather focus on a single, striking example of a phase transition phenomenon arising in the study of (large) finite Markov chains: the
cutoff phenomenon
. In a few words, a Markov chain is said to
exhibit cutoff
if the decay of the distance to equilibrium of the chain—measured, e.g., in total variation—takes place in an abrupt manner: there exists some
t
(which depends on the size of the system) such that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, at time
(1-\varepsilon)t
its distribution is arbitrarily far from the equilibrium one, while at time
(1+\varepsilon)t
it is arbitrarily near. In this sense, the “parameter” inducing the phase transition here is
time
, and for this reason it is often said that this is an example of a
dynamical
phase transition.

Despite an increasing amount of work on the subject, the cutoff phenomenon is still far from being completely understood, and the research of simple conditions (i.e., easy-to-check and model independent) guaranteeing the presence of a cutoff is still very active.
In such a landscape, a quite natural line of investigations lies in considering
parametric perturbations
of Markov chains that exhibit a cutoff. More explicitly, focusing the analysis on a particular choice of chain and a method of implementing the perturbation, it is interesting to quantify the
robustness
of the cutoff phenomenon with respect to the
strength
of the perturbation.

In this setup, the mixing time of the dynamics may exhibit an additional phase transition, governed by the strength of the perturbation and referred to as a
mixing trichotomy
.
This involves the identification of a
subcritical
regime,
where the perturbation is sufficiently weak to preserve the mixing behavior of the original chain, contrasted with a
supercritical
regime, where the “cutoff picture” is disrupted, leading to
smooth
rather than
abrupt
mixing.
As in the broader framework of statistical physics, the description of such a phase transition is typically accompanied by the identification of a
critical
regime, at the interphase between the other two, where the system exhibits some sort of intermediate behavior.

Among the models for which such a phenomenon has been rigorously proved, we recall: Glauber dynamics for the Ising model
[
LS13
,
LS14
]
, random walks on dynamic graphs
[
AGHH19
,
AGHHN22
,
CQ21b
]
, random walks with reset (and the so-called PageRank dynamics)
[
CQ21a
,
VS21
]
, Ehrenfest urns with multi-type particles
[
Qua24
]
, mass redistribution models
[
CQS24
]
.
In particular, although the models in
[
AGHH19
,
CQ21a
,
CQ21b
]
are very different from each other, they all share a common feature: in the supercritical regime, the total variation distance over time—properly rescaled—converges to an exponential function. Moreover, in all these examples, such an exponential decay of the distance to equilibrium can be read from the point of view of the trajectory of the process: the arrival at equilibrium is due to the occurrence of a certain event, and the time of the first occurrence of the latter event is (asymptotically) exponentially distributed. From a high-level perspective, this is the cartoon underlying another classical phenomenon in statistical physics, known as
metastability
: the system is trapped in a
local equilibrium
up to some (large) exponential time in which the global equilibrium is eventually reached. In other words, looking through the lens of the
mixing trichotomy approach
, one might be tempted to see metastability and cutoff as “opposed” phenomena.

To make this idea more convincing, it is worth recalling that the emergence of a cutoff
is often explained in terms of
concentration phenomenon
.
Conversely, the memorylessness of the exponential distribution, which characterizes metastable behaviors,
can be seen as the complete opposite of
concentration
.
The goal of this paper is to make the heuristic picture discussed so far as explicit as possible by means of a simple (but natural) model.

1.2.
Mixing time of random walks on graphs with a community structure

In recent years, random walks on random graphs have been extensively
studied on various random graph models, becoming a prototypical example
of Markov chains that exhibit the cutoff phenomenon.
Notable contributions include the establishment of the cutoff for
random walks on regular random graphs
[
LS10
]
, on the giant component of the Erdös-Rényi graph
[
BLPS18
]
,
on the configuration model
[
BLPS18
,
BHS17
,
BHLP19
]
,
on all Ramanujan graphs
[
LP16
]
, and on random lifts
[
BL22
,
C22
]
.

A significant part of these investigations has also focused
on the directed setting, which is particularly challenging due to
the non-reversibility of the dynamics and the poor knowledge
of the stationary distribution.
This framework, which is closely related to our work,
was first explored in
[
BCS18
,
BCS19
]
,
where the cutoff was established for random walks on the directed configuration model and for a broad class of sparse Markov chains,
and later extended to the case of heavy-tailed degrees
[
CCPQ23
]
, to PageRank dynamics
[
CQ21a
]
, and to the directed Chung-Lu model
[
BP25
]
. Other results in the same spirit have also been obtained in
[
BL22
,
Dub24a
,
Dub24b
]
.

A common thread in all these works is the characterization of the mixing time of the random walk on the graph
in terms of the entropy production rate (or simply, the
entropy
) of the random walk on its
local weak limit
.
The cutoff time is then shown to correspond
to the entropic time
t_{\textup{ent}}
(see Eq. (
2.3
) for a formal definition),
which corresponds to the logarithm of the size of the graph divided by the entropy.

The first extension of this approach to random graphs with many communities is due to Ben-Hamou
[
BH20
]
, who studied the
non-backtracking
random walk on a variant of the configuration model incorporating a 2-community structure.
In
[
HSS25
]
, the authors extended the analysis to the simple random walk and allowed multiple communities.
These results reveal
that the community structure can create a configurational bottleneck
in the set of random walk trajectories, depending on the strength
of interactions between communities, and disrupt the entropic picture.
In fact, the mixing behavior displays a phase transition
among a subcritical regime, where the interaction strength is sufficiently high so that
the random walk exhibits cutoff at the same time as in the single-community case,
and a supercritical regime, where the low interaction strength results in a smooth–rather than abrupt–mixing, and the convergence to equilibrium is driven by the occurrence of the inter-community transitions. In both these works, the intensity of inter-community connections is modeled via a parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
, and the critical scaling for this parameter is shown to correspond to the inverse of the entropic time of a single community. Nevertheless, in
[
BH20
,
HSS25
]
the authors do not attempt a refined analysis of the total variation distance profile in the supercritical regime, being their focus on the cutoff/non-cutoff transition.

In this work, our aim is to extend their results to another class of directed random graphs with community structures and also to complete
trichotomy picture
by providing sharp results on the total variation profile in the critical and supercritical cases.

To this end, we will consider a block model with
m>1
communities,
constructed from
m
independent directed Erdős–Rényi random graphs on
n
vertices (the
communities
) and a random rewiring procedure governed by a parameter

\alpha\equiv\alpha_{n}\in[0,1]
, which introduces connections between these communities. The smaller
\alpha
, the sparser the inter-community connectivity.
Specifically, we will work in a
weakly sparse
regime, setting the probability of connection for the Erdős–Rényi communities to

p\equiv p_{n}:=\lambda\log(n)/n
, for a constant
\lambda>1
, and assuming
that each edge is
rewired
so as to point to a different community with probability
\alpha\gg(\lambda n\log n)^{-1}
.
On the one hand,
the choice
\lambda>1
ensures that, before the rewiring, each community is strongly connected with high probability (see, e.g.,
[
CF12
]
). On the other hand, the requirement
\alpha\gg(n\log n)^{-1}
is needed for the communities to be typically connected, even though such connections may be sparse.

We will then analyze the convergence to the equilibrium
of a random walk on the resulting graph, letting
the interaction parameter
\alpha
, or rather the sequence
\alpha_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, vary on
[0,1]
.
As a main result, as stated in Theorem
2
, we will show that
the dynamics exhibits a mixing trichotomy with a critical regime at
\alpha^{-1}\asymp t_{\textup{ent}}
, in line with the findings in
[
BH20
,
HSS25
]
.
For higher values of
\alpha
, the system is in a subcritical regime with cutoff at time
t_{\textup{ent}}
, as described in Eq. (
2.5
), while for lower values of
\alpha
,
the system enters a supercritical regime where the cutoff behavior is disrupted.
In this regime, we will then identify two relevant timescales.

(i)

The first corresponds to the attainment of a local equilibrium within the starting community, which causes a sharp drop in the total variation distance from
1
to
(m-1)/m
at time
t_{\textup{ent}}
(see Eq. (
2.7
)).

(ii)

The second is a larger timescale of order
\alpha^{-1}
, which governs the convergence to the global equilibrium through a smooth exponential decay of the total variation distance (
2.8
).

At criticality, where the two timescales are of the same order,
the dynamics exhibits an intermediate behavior,
as illustrated on the rightmost side of Figure
1
(see Eq. (
2.6
)).
This can be roughly interpreted as a competition between achieving local equilibrium within a community
and the first passage across two communities. We will provide an extended interpretation of this result in Section
2.3
.

2.
Model and results

In this section, we introduce the necessary notation and preliminaries,
and we state our main results.
We begin in Section
2.1
by providing a precise definition
of the graph model. Then, in Section
2.2
, we review
key concepts essential for stating and interpreting our main result, Theorem
2
, which is presented and discussed formally in Section
2.3
.
In Section
2.4
, we comment on our choice of the graph model and explore potential extensions of the framework considered in this paper. The proof of the main result is developed in several sections, from Section
3
to Section
7
. A detailed road map that describes the structure of this technical part is provided in Section
2.5
.

2.1.
The graph model

We consider the following model, which we call
Directed Block Model
and denote by
{\rm DBM}(n,m,p,\alpha)
:

(1)

Consider
m\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
independent directed Erdős–Rényi random graphs with
n
vertices and connection probability
p\in(0,1)
, that is, any ordered couple of vertices presents an oriented edge with probability
p
. We call these graphs
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1}),

\dots,

G_{m}=(V_{m},E_{m})
. More precisely, for each
i\leq m
, the vertices in
V_{i}
will be labeled by the integers in
[n]
, with the superscript
(i)
identifying their community of membership.

(2)

For each edge of each graph, throw a coin with a success probability
\alpha\in[0,1/2)
. If it is a head, rewire the edge as follows: if the edge is in the graph
G_{i}
with
i\leq m
and it goes from
x^{(i)}
to
y^{(i)}
, for
x,y\in[n]
, remove the edge
(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})
, choose

j\in[m]\setminus\{i\}
uniformly at random, and let the new edge be
(x^{(i)},y^{(j)})
.

We call
G=(V,E)
such a graph on the
mn
vertices.
Notice that, rather than a technical constraint, the requirement
\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
is a physical assumption that guarantees some sort of
community structure
, since the majority of edges out-going a given vertex point towards its same community (i.e., are not
rewired
).
Throughout the paper, we will write
\mathbb{P}
(resp.
\mathbb{E}
) to denote the probability law (resp. expectation) of the two-step graph generation process just described.
As usual in the random graph literature, we will be interested in the asymptotic regime in which
n\to\infty
, and all the asymptotic notation will refer to that limit. We will say that an event occurs with high probability (or simply w.h.p.), if the probability of its occurrence is a function of
n
that converges to
1
in the limit
n\to\infty
.
The dependence of
\alpha
on
n
is the main object of the analysis, since we want to understand how the mixing behavior depends on the relation between
\alpha
and
n
.

In what follows, we will make the following assumptions on the parameters of the model.

•

We will consider
p=\frac{\lambda\log(n)}{n}
for some
\lambda>1
to ensure that each graph is strongly connected with high probability (see Section
3
below).

•

We will also assume
\lambda\asymp 1
, to have a logarithmic average degree. Due to the result in
[
BP25
]
, this assumption ensures that the random walk in each of the graphs (before the rewiring) exhibits a cutoff with high probability.

•

We will consider
m\asymp 1
.

Let us now introduce some further notation. For each vertex
x\in V
, we denote by
D_{x}^{+}
the out-degree of
x
,
and write

D_{x}^{+}=O_{x}^{+}+I_{x}^{+}\,,

where

\begin{split}O_{x}^{+}:=\#\{\mbox{out-edges of }x\mbox{ pointing to other graphs}\}\\
I_{x}^{+}:=\#\{\mbox{out-edges of }x\mbox{ not affected by the rewiring}\}\end{split}

Similarly, we call
D_{x}^{-}
the in-degree of
x\in V
.

Notice that while the out-degree
D_{x}^{+}
is the same before
and after the rewiring, the in-degree
D_{x}^{-}
could be different,
and for this reason, we introduce the symbol
D_{x,i}^{-}
, to denote the degree of a vertex
x\in V_{i}
before the rewiring procedure. It will also be convenient to define the function
c:V\to\{1,\dots,m\}
that maps each vertex to its community.

We will first consider the random walk on
G_{i}
for
i\leq m
, i.e., on a graph
before
the rewiring, and for any probability distribution
\mu
on
V_{i}
, we write
\mathbf{P}_{\mu}^{G_{i}}(\cdot)
for its law when the initial position of the walk has distribution
\mu
. Recall that the latter is a
random
law, since it depends on the realization of the environment
G_{i}
, and for this reason we will refer to it as
quenched law
. If the graph
G_{i}
turns out to be strongly connected and aperiodic (which is the case with high probability), we will call
\pi_{i}
the stationary distribution of the random walk on
G_{i}
.

Similarly, we will consider the random walk on the whole graph
G
(after the rewiring procedure), and we will denote by
\mathbf{P}_{\mu}^{G}(\cdot)
its law when the initial position of the walk has distribution
\mu
(on
V
) and
\pi
is its stationary distribution, if unique.
We will show below that, in the setting we are dealing with, the measures
\pi
and
(\pi_{i})_{i\leq m}
are w.h.p. unique.
To avoid ambiguities, we will conventionally set them to coincide with the uniform distribution (on the corresponding vertex sets) in the unlikely event that they are not uniquely defined.
Let us finally clarify that we consider a discrete-time evolution. For this reason, we will consider times as integers (possibly taking their integer part).

2.4.
Some comments on the graph model

As mentioned in the Introduction, this work aims to establish a simple yet natural framework
for studying the mixing trichotomy induced by the presence of a bottleneck in the state space.
In this sense, the model we consider is somehow the
minimal one
to this aim, both in terms of notation and of technicalities required for a rigorous proof. However, it would be possible to extend our findings to more general versions of the model presented in Section
2.1
. In this section, we present some remarks in the direction of such generalizations.

Weakening the assumptions on
\lambda

Let us stress that our assumptions on the connectivity parameter
\lambda
are not expected to be
sharp
to prove a mixing trichotomy. For example,
\lambda>1
is sufficient but not necessary to guarantee the strong connectivity of communities, and it would be enough to have
(\lambda-1)\log(n)\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty

[
CF12
]
.
On the other hand, we do not expect
\lambda\asymp 1
to be sharp either; rather, we anticipate that any
\lambda=n^{o(1)}
would yield similar results. In fact, as long as
\lambda=n^{o(1)}
, it should be possible to show
that the random walk on a single community exhibits a cutoff at the entropic time, which itself forms a divergent sequence in terms of
n
.
However, since this observation is not rigorously stated in any of the
aforementioned works, we retain this generalization here.

Less rigid rewirings

We chose to define the rewiring procedure in the model as being strictly tied to the “labels” of the vertices. The same result can be obtained assuming that the edges are rewired uniformly at random. Although this would require only minor adjustments, it would introduce additional notation to describe the rewiring process. To maintain clarity, we choose to omit this generalization. We believe that the argument presented in the paper is robust to more general non-singular rewiring mechanisms, provided that the rewiring is uniform among the other communities.

Removing dependencies between inter- and intra-community out-degrees

The construction in Section
2.1
, introduces, for every
x\in V
, a correlation between the random variables
O_{x}^{+}
and
I_{x}^{+}
, which is not present in the context of the classical stochastic block model.
However, by randomizing the number of vertices of the graph, it is possible to remove this kind of dependencies. In particular,
assuming that each community has a number
N\sim\text{Pois}(n)
of vertices, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that

O_{x}^{+}\sim\text{Bin}(D_{x}^{+},\alpha)\sim\text{Pois}(n\alpha p)

and
I_{x}^{+}\sim\text{Bin}(D_{x}^{+},1-\alpha)\sim\text{Pois}(n(1-\alpha)p)
. Moreover, the last two variables turn out to be independent as desired.
Being
|N-n|=O(\sqrt{n})
w.h.p., the arguments given in the proofs remain valid and lead to the same results.

Heterogeneous communities

We believe that, with some extra work, the results presented in this work could be generalized to the case
in which the communities have different intra-community connectivities, say
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}
, with
\min_{i\leq m}\lambda_{i}>1
, different rewiring parameters
(\alpha_{i,j})_{i,j\leq m}
with
\max_{j,k\leq m}\frac{\alpha_{i,j}}{\alpha_{i,k}}\lesssim 1
, or with different sizes, say
n_{1},\dots,n_{m}
, with
\max_{i,j\leq m}\frac{n_{i}}{n_{j}}\lesssim 1
.
In particular, while the result in Theorem
2
should remain unaffected by the heterogeneity of the
\lambda_{j}
’s, we believe that in the latter two cases the matrix
Q
has to be modified,
and the (super)critical exponential profile should change to a more general mixing profile, depending on the asymptotic behavior of the corresponding auxiliary Markov chain.
Also notice that when the community sizes are different, the rewiring procedure described in Section
2.1
is not well defined, and should be suitably modified, e.g., by allowing a rewired edge to point to a uniformly random vertex in the new chosen community, as mentioned above.

Diverging number of communities

Although not the focus of our study, Theorem
2
suggests that even when the number of communities is slowly diverging, the characterization of the critical and supercritical regimes should remain unchanged.
However, if
m\gg 1
grows sufficiently fast,
the mixing behavior of the model could undergo significant changes.
For instance, choosing the parameters
(\alpha,m,\lambda)
so that
the average number of rewired edges within each community is
\asymp\log(m)
, we expect a cutoff behavior even in the subcritical regime
\alpha^{-1}\gg t_{\textup{ent}}
.

In particular, rescaling the time by
\alpha
, we expect that the dynamics
is well approximated by a simple random walk on a coarse-grained graph obtained by collapsing each community to a single point and erasing multiple edges.
In that case, the coarse-grained graph is precisely a
directed Erdős–Rényi random graph on
m
vertices in the weakly sparse regime,
for which cutoff is now well known to take place.
In conclusion, in that setting and for
\alpha^{-1}\gg t_{\textup{ent}}
,
we expect to observe a cutoff on the timescale
t_{{\rm ent},m}=\alpha^{-1}\log(m)/\textup{H}_{m}\gg 1
, where

\textup{H}_{m}\asymp\log\log(m)
is the row-entropy of the random walk on the coarse-grained graph.

2.5.
Organization of the proof

The proof of Theorem
2
is articulated on several parts, depending on the different regimes for the parameter
\alpha
. We now provide a road map to the forthcoming sections.
We start in Section
3
by presenting some preliminary facts that will be needed throughout the analysis of both the sub- and supercritical case. In Section
4
we deal with the subregion of the
supercritical regime where
t_{\textup{ent}}\ll\alpha^{-1}\ll\sqrt{n}\log^{-2}(n)
, which we will call
weakly supercritical
. In this region, a control on the total variation distance to equilibrium for both the timescales appearing in Eq. (
2.7
) and Eq. (
2.8
) can be obtained by relying on the analysis of the so-called
annealed random walk
, which will be introduced in Section
3
. Section
5
is the technical core of our work, and it deals with the remaining subregion of the supercritical case, i.e.
\sqrt{n}\log^{-2}(n)\lesssim\alpha^{-1}\ll\lambda n\log(n)
, which we will refer to as
strongly supercritical
.
In this case, arguments based on
annealed random walks

are doomed to fail, requiring us to employ alternative technical tools
to establish a form of
homogenization property
of the graph.
This, in turn, offers a level of control over the total variation distance comparable to that achieved in Section
4
for the
weakly supercritical
case.
Section
6
deals with the analogue of the results in the previous sections but for the subcritical and the critical case. Here, the arguments can be seen as an adaptation of those in
[
BCS18
,
CCPQ23
,
BP25
]
to the DBM case. Finally, in Section
7
we collect all the estimates obtained throughout the paper and conclude the proof of Theorem
2
.

3.
Approximations and auxiliary processes

In this section we aim at presenting some background material and the first statements about the behavior of the random walk on
short timescales
, i.e., for
t\ll\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}
. These results will be used later in the paper in all three regimes.
In particular, in Section
3.1
we present some approximation of the stationary distribution of the
local
random walks in terms of the in-degree sequences; in Section
3.2

9
.

3.1.
Approximating the stationary distribution

After the statement of Theorem
1
, we claimed that the proof does not require explicit knowledge of the stationary distribution. Nevertheless, a uniform first-order approximation for the latter in our setup has been obtained by Cooper and Frieze,
as stated by the following theorem.

Theorem 3

(
[
CF12
]
)
.

The local stationary distributions are approximated at first order and uniformly by the (normalized) local in-degree sequences. In formula,

(3.1)

\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V_{i}}\left|\frac{\pi_{i}(x)}{\frac{D_{x,i}^{-}}{pn^{2}}}-1\right|\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{\hskip 8.19447pt\mathbb{P}\hskip 8.19447pt}0\,.

The latter result is partially based on the fact that, given the choice
\lambda>1
, uniform concentration properties for the degrees of vertices of
G
are true.

Proposition 4
.

There exist two positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that, w.h.p. ,

\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V_{i}}\max\{D_{x}^{+},D_{x}^{-},D_{x,i}^{-}\}\leq C_{2}\lambda\log(n)\quad\text{and}\quad\min_{i\leq m}\min_{x\in V_{i}}\min\{D_{x}^{+},D_{x}^{-},D_{x,i}^{-}\}\geq C_{1}\lambda\log(n)\,.

Proof.

The thesis follows immediately from standard concentration bounds for binomial random variables and an application of the union bound.
∎

As a consequence we may conclude that w.h.p. the stationary distribution
\pi_{i}
is uniform, up to corrections bounded away from zero and infinity, and this will be helpful in the characterization of the stationary distribution of the random walk on the whole
G
.
This fact can then be used to deduce the following byproduct of Theorems
1
,
3
, and Proposition
4
, which allows us to control the
\ell^{\infty}
-distance to equilibrium of local random walks.

Corollary 5
.

Let
S=3t_{\textup{ent}}\log(n)
. Then

(3.2)

\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x,y\in V_{i}}\left|\frac{\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X_{S}\in y)}{\pi_{i}(y)}-1\right|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Proof.

Recall the subadditivity property of the total variation distance, which implies that

(3.3)

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V_{i}}\|\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X_{2kt_{\textup{ent}}}\in\cdot)-\pi_{i}\|\leq{\rm e}^{-k}\right)=1-o(1)\,.

Then, by choosing
k=\lfloor\frac{3}{2}\log(n)\rfloor
,

(3.4)

\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V_{i}}\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X_{S}\in\cdot)-\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1})\,,

and the desired result follows by Theorem
3
and Proposition
4
.
∎

3.2.
Local neighborhoods are tree-like

The digraphs
(G_{i})_{i\leq m}
and
G
are w.h.p. locally tree-like
in the following sense: the out-neighborhood
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}({4\varepsilon t_{\textup{ent}}})
of depth
{4\varepsilon t_{\textup{ent}}}
(growing with
n
) of any vertex
x\in V
, locally look like trees for small
\varepsilon
, as the next proposition clarifies.

Lemma 6
.

For a digraph
S=(V,E)
, let
\textit{Tx}(S):=|E|-|V|+1
denote the tree-excess of
S
and, for
\varepsilon>0
,

(3.5)

\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}:=\{\forall x\in V,\textit{Tx}(\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}({4\varepsilon t_{\textup{ent}}}))<2\}.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
denote the SRW on
G_{i}
or
G
and, for
t\leq{2\varepsilon t_{\textup{ent}}}
, consider the set and related event

(3.6)

\displaystyle V^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\left\{x\in V\mid\textit{Tx}(\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}({4\varepsilon t_{\textup{ent}}}))=0\right\},\qquad\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\{\max_{x\in V}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\notin V^{\star}_{\varepsilon})\leq 2^{-t}\}\,.

For a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
(independent of
n
), it holds
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon}\right)=1-o(1)
.

Proof.

The proofs for
G_{i}
follow
[
BP25
, Lemmas 6 and 7]
and can adapted to
G
.
∎

This means that out-neighborhoods of depth
{4\varepsilon t_{\textup{ent}}}
are at most trees with an extra edge, and if they are not trees, each step of SRW (up to
{2\varepsilon t_{\textup{ent}}}
steps) w.h.p. halves the quenched probability to have extra edges.

3.3.
The annealed random walk

A key analysis tool is the so-called
annealed random walk
.
Let us recall its definition and outline its construction in our setting.

By annealed random walk one simply means the non-Markovian process in which the underlying random graph is constructed together with the random walk on it.
More precisely, at time
t=0
all vertices are
unrevealed
, in the sense that its out-neighbors are unknown. Then, for any
t\geq 0
, assuming
X_{t}=x\in V
,

(1)

If
x
is revealed, go directly to step (3).

(2)

If
x
is unrevealed, reveal it by generating its out-neighborhood, i.e.:

(i)

for each vertex
y\in V_{c(x)}
toss a coin with success probability
p
;

(ii)

for each
y\in V_{c(x)}
which resulted in a success in (i), toss a coin with success probability
\alpha
;

(iii)

for each
y\in V_{c(x)}
which resulted in a success in (i) and a failure in (ii), add an arrow from
x
to
y
;

(iv)

for each
y\in V_{c(x)}
which resulted in a success both in (i) and (ii), sample a label
i
u.a.r. in
\{1,\dots,m\}\setminus\{c(x)\}
and add an arrow from
x
to the vertex in
G_{i}
corresponding to
y
(i.e., the vertex with the same label of
y
).

(3)

Let
X_{t+1}
be one of the out-neighbors of
x
sampled u.a.r..

Clearly, such a random process will eventually stop to reveal new vertices. Moreover, the joint law of the revealed vertices coincides with the law
\mathbb{P}
. The striking feature of the annealed random walk is that it coincides with the expectation of the quenched law, i.e., for any probability distribution
\mu
on
V
and
t\geq 0

(3.7)

\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{\mu}\big((X_{0},\dots,X_{t})\in\cdot\big)=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{P}_{\mu}^{G}\big((X_{0},\dots,X_{t})\in\cdot\big)\big]\,.

In what follows we will be interested in the event

(3.8)

\mathcal{C}_{t}=\{(X_{0},\dots,X_{t})\text{ is cycle-free}\}\,,

where by cycle-free we simply mean that the random walk does visit any vertex more than once.

Lemma 7
.

For any
x\in V
, if
t\ll\sqrt{n}
,

(3.9)

\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(X_{t}=z,\mathcal{C}_{t})=\frac{{Q}^{t}(c(x),c(z))}{n}\left(1+O\left(\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}\right)\right)\,,\qquad\forall z\in V\,,

where

(3.10)

{Q}^{t}(i,j)=\frac{1+(m\textup{{1}}_{i=j}-1)(1-\frac{m}{m-1}\alpha)^{t}}{m},\qquad i,j\leq m,\quad t\geq 0,

Remark 8
.

Notice that (
3.10
) corresponds to the
t
-step transition probability of the Markov chain with transition matrix
Q
as in (
2.9
). This can be verified computing the powers of the diagonal form of
Q
.

Proof.

Fix
z_{0}=x\in V
and
z_{t}=z\in V
. We will say that a sequence
(z_{1},\dots,z_{t-1})
in
V
is
cycle free
if
(z_{0},\dots,z_{t})
does not contain repeated vertices.
Start by writing

(3.11)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(X_{t}=z,\mathcal{C}_{t})&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(z_{1},\dots,z_{t-1})\in V^{t-1}\\
\text{cycle-free}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\frac{\textup{{1}}_{\{z_{i}\to z_{i+1}\}}}{D^{+}_{z_{i}}}\right]\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(z_{1},\dots,z_{t-1})\in V^{t-1}\\
\text{cycle-free}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\mathbb{P}(z_{i}\to z_{i+1})\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{{D}^{+}_{z_{i}}}\Big|\textup{{1}}_{\{z_{i}\to z_{i+1}\}}=1\right].\end{split}

Conditionally on
\textup{{1}}_{\{z_{i}\to z_{i+1}\}}=1
, the out-degree of
z_{i}
has the same distribution as
1+D
, where
D\sim\text{Bin}(n-2,p)
. Then

(3.12)

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{{D}^{+}_{z_{i}}}\Big|\textup{{1}}_{\{z_{i}\to z_{i+1}\}}=1\right]=\frac{1}{np}\left(1+o\left(n^{-1}\right)\right)\,,

and as a consequence

(3.13)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(X_{t}=z,\mathcal{C}_{t})&=\left(1+o\big(\tfrac{t}{n}\big)\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(z_{1},\dots,z_{t-1})\in V^{t-1}\\
\text{cycle-free}\end{subarray}}(np)^{-t}\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\mathbb{P}(z_{i}\to z_{i+1})\,.\end{split}

Moreover,

\mathbb{P}(z_{i}\to z_{i+1})=\begin{cases}p\frac{\alpha}{m-1}&\text{if }c(z_{i})\neq c(z_{i+1})\\
p(1-\alpha)&\text{if }c(z_{i})=c(z_{i+1})\end{cases}\,,

and therefore

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(X_{t}=z,\mathcal{C}_{t})&=\left(1+o\big(\tfrac{t}{n}\big)\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(z_{1},\dots,z_{t-1})\in V^{t-1}\\
\text{cycle-free}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n^{t}}\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\left(\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\textup{{1}}_{c(z_{i})\neq c(z_{i+1})}+(1-\alpha)\textup{{1}}_{c(z_{i})=c(z_{i+1})}\right)\,.\end{split}

Since the term within the brackets is bounded above by
1
uniformly in
(z_{i},z_{i+1})
, we could take the sum over all the possible sequences
(z_{1},\dots,z_{t-1})\in V^{t}
, so having
n^{t-1}
rather than
(n-2)\cdots(n-t)
summands. Overall we get

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(X_{t}=z,\mathcal{C}_{t})&=\left(1+O\big(\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}\big)\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(z_{1},\dots,z_{t-1})\in V^{t-1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n^{t}}\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\left(\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\textup{{1}}_{c(z_{i})\neq c(z_{i+1})}+(1-\alpha)\textup{{1}}_{c(z_{i})=c(z_{i+1})}\right)\,,\end{split}

and since
t=o(\sqrt{n})
, the prefactor does not change the first order term.
Now we notice that the sum over
V^{t-1}
collapses into a sum over
[m]^{t-1}
. More precisely, using the shorthand notation
c_{i}=c(z_{i})
, for
i\leq t
and defining

J(c_{0},\dots,c_{t})=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\textup{{1}}_{c_{j+1}\neq c_{j}}\,,

we have

(3.14)

\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(X_{t}=z,\mathcal{C}_{t})=\left(1+O\big(\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}\big)\right)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c_{1},\dots,c_{t-1})\in[m]^{t-1}\\
J(c_{0},\dots,c_{t})=k\end{subarray}}\Big(\frac{\alpha}{m-1}\Big)^{k}\Big(1-\alpha\Big)^{t-k}\,.

Notice that
the quantity expressed by the double sum above can be be interpreted as the probability that the Markov chain with transition matrix as in Eq. (
2.9
)
is in
c_{t}=c(z)
at time
t
.
Therefore

(3.15)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(X_{t}=z,\mathcal{C}_{t})&=\frac{1}{n}\,{Q}^{t}(c(x),c(z))\left(1+O\left(\frac{t^{2}}{n}\right)\right)\,.\qed\end{split}

Lemma
7
can be strengthened to a statement valid for the quenched law of the SRW
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, provided that we consider sufficiently small times, as it will be shown in the next subsection.

Proposition 9
.

For any
\alpha=\alpha_{n}\in[0,1]
, let
1\ll t\ll\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}
. Then,

(3.16)

\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V}\left|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})-{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)\right|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,,

where,
{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)
is defined as in Eq. (
3.10
).

Proof.

Notice that, since
m\asymp 1
, it suffices to prove (
3.16
) for a fixed
i\leq m
.
We first consider the case in which
\alpha\ll 1
. Let
K=\lfloor\log^{2}n\rfloor
and call
\cE
the event that all the vertices in the graph have out-degree at least
C_{1}\log(n)
and
\cD
the event in which each vertex has a tree-excess at most
1
in its out-neighborhood of height
4
. Recall that, thanks to Proposition
4
and Lemma
6
,
\mathbb{P}(\cD\cap\cE)=1-o(1)
.
Then

(3.17)

\mathbb{P}(\textup{{1}}_{\cE\cap\cD}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})\geq{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)+\delta)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\textup{{1}}_{\cE\cap\cD}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})^{K}]}{({Q}^{t}(c(x),i)+\delta)^{K}}.

Consider now
K
annealed random walks
(X^{(\ell)})_{\ell\leq K}
starting at some
x\in V
. We let the walks evolve—all starting from
x
—one after the other, for a time
t
. Clearly, these walks are not independent, but each is independent of the previous ones
conditionally on the environment discovered so far
.
Let us define the family of events
(B_{\ell})_{\ell\leq K}
. For each
\ell\leq K
,
B_{\ell}
is the event that:

(i)

X^{(j)}_{t}\in V_{i}
for each
j\leq\ell
;

(ii)

all the out-degrees of the vertices visited by the first
\ell
walks are at least
C_{1}\log(n)
(cf. Prop.
4
);

(iii)

the out-neighborhood of
x
of height
4
discovered by the first
\ell
trajectories has a tree excess at most
1
.

With this definitions we have

(3.18)

\mathbb{E}[\textup{{1}}_{\cE\cap\cD}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})^{K}]\leq\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(B_{K})=\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(B_{1})\prod_{{\ell}=2}^{K}\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(B_{{\ell}}|B_{{\ell}-1})\,.

We start by noting that, given
B_{{\ell}-1}
, the event
B_{{\ell}}
is contained in the union of the following events:

(1)

For all times
s\leq 4
the
\ell
-th trajectory is always in a vertex already visited by one of the previous walks.
Since we are working on the events (ii) and (iii), for any couple
(x,y)
there are at most
2
paths of length
4
joining
x
to
y
and each such path has a weight
\leq(C_{1}\log(n))^{-4}
. The probability of the event described above is thus upper bounded by

8K(C_{1}\log(n))^{-4}=o(1)\,.

(2)

The event in (1) does not occur, i.e., there exists some
s\leq 4
such that the walk visits an unvisited vertex, and there exists a time
s^{\prime}\in(s,t]
at which the trajectory intersects again one of the previous walks (including itself); this happens with probability less than

\frac{Kt^{2}}{n}=o(1)\,.

(3)

None of the events above occurs, and at time
s=4
the walk is out of
V_{c(x)}
.
Since
\alpha\ll 1
, this happens with probability at most

1-(1-\alpha)^{4}=o(1)\,.

(4)

None of the event above occurs, yet at time
t
the
\ell
-th trajectory is found in
V_{i}
; thanks to Lemma
7
, this happens with probability at most

\displaystyle\max_{s\leq 4}\max_{y\in V_{c(x)}}\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{y}(X_{t-s}\in V_{i},\mathcal{C}_{t-s})

\displaystyle=\max_{s\leq 4}{Q}^{t-s}(c(x),i)\left(1+O\left(\frac{t^{2}}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{{s_{\varepsilon}}\leq 4}{Q}^{t-s}(c(x),i)+o(1)

\displaystyle={Q}^{t}(c(x),i)+o(1)\,,

where the first identity comes from the fact that, by symmetry, the annealed probability on the l.h.s. is independent of
y\in V_{c(x)}
; the second one follows from the fact that
{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)\leq 1
and
t^{2}\ll n
;
the third one uses that
t\gg 1
.

In conclusion, uniformly over
{\ell}\leq K
we have
\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(B_{\ell}|B_{{\ell}-1})={Q}^{t}(c(x),i)+o(1)
.
Thanks to the choice of
K
, we conclude that, being
\delta>0
fixed,

(3.19)

\mathbb{P}(\textup{{1}}_{\cE\cap\cD}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})\geq{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)+\delta)\leq\left(\frac{{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)+o(1)}{{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)+\delta}\right)^{K}=o(n^{-1}).

Therefore

(3.20)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\big(\max_{x\in V}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})\geq{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)+\delta\big)\leq\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathbb{P}(\textup{{1}}_{\cE\cap\cD}\max_{x\in V}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})\geq{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)+\delta)+\mathbb{P}(\cE\cup\cD)=o(1)\,.\end{split}

To prove a uniform lower bound on
\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})
, one can consider the events

(3.21)

\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{x,i,\delta}=\left\{\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})\leq{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)-\delta\right\}

and

(3.22)

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{x,j,\delta}=\left\{\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{j})\geq{Q}^{t}(c(x),j)+\frac{\delta}{m-1}\right\}\,.

Clearly, for any
i\leq m
,
x\in V_{i}
and
\delta>0
,
\cup_{j\neq i}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{x,j,\delta}\supseteq\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{x,i,\delta}
.
Therefore,

(3.23)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\big(\min_{x\in V}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})\leq{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)-\delta\big)&=\mathbb{P}\big(\cup_{x\in V}\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{x,i,\delta}\big)\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}\big(\cup_{j\neq i}\cup_{x\in V}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{x,j,\delta}\big)\\
&\leq m\max_{j\leq m}\mathbb{P}\big(\cup_{x\in V}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{x,j,\delta}\big)\,.\end{split}

Since
m\asymp 1
and since the probability on the r.h.s. of (
3.23
) coincides, replacing
\delta
with
\frac{\delta}{m-1}
, with the one on the l.h.s. of (
3.20
), we conclude that

(3.24)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\big(\min_{x\in V}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})\leq{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)-\delta\big)&=o(1)\,.\end{split}

Therefore, in the case
\alpha\ll 1
, (
3.16
) follows from (
3.20
) and (
3.24
) and a union bound over
i\leq m
.

We are left to consider the case
\alpha\asymp 1
. In this case,
Q^{t}(j,i)=\frac{1}{m}+o(1)
for any
t\gg 1
. We can argue as above, but rather than the events (1), (2), (3) and (4), we can consider the events (1), (2) and (4’), where

(4’)

The events (1) and (2) do not occur, yet at time
t
the
j
-th trajectory is found in
V_{i}
; thanks to Lemma
7
, this happens with probability at most

\displaystyle\max_{s\leq 4}\max_{y\in V}\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{y}(X_{t}\in V_{i},\mathcal{C}_{t})

\displaystyle=\max_{s\leq 4}\max_{j\leq m}{Q}^{t-s}(j,i)\left(1+O\left(\frac{t^{2}}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}+o(1)\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Weakly supercritical regime

In this section and in the following one, we approach the regime
\alpha^{-1}\gg t_{\textup{ent}}
.

4.1.
First jump across two communities

We now consider the first time at which the random walk traverses a rewired edge, that is

(4.1)

\tau_{\textup{jump}}=\min\{t>0:c(X_{t})\neq c(X_{t-1})\}\,.

Letting
z_{0}=x
, and arguing as in the proof of Lemma
7
we obtain

(4.2)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}>t,\mathcal{C}_{t})]&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(z_{1},\dots,z_{t})\in V_{c(x)}^{t}\\
\text{cycle-free}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\mathbb{P}(z_{i}\to z_{i+1})\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{{D}^{+}_{z_{i}}}\Big|\textup{{1}}_{\{z_{i}\to z_{i+1}\}}=1\right]\\
&=(1-\alpha)^{t}\left(1+O\big(\tfrac{t^{2}}{n}\big)\right).\\
\end{split}

We now show that, if
t
is (twice) the entropic time then, w.h.p.—and uniformly over the starting position—the quenched probability to see a jump to another community before
t
is small. Before stating the proposition, we need a preliminary lemma that serves as a bootstrap for the forthcoming Proposition
11
.

Lemma 10
.

If
\alpha\ll 1
, for any constant
a\in\mathbb{N}
,

(4.3)

\max_{x\in V}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq a)=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.

Proof.

Let
\cE
again denote the event that all the vertices in the graph have out-degree at least
C_{1}\log(n)
.
It is enough to prove that

(4.4)

\max_{x\in V}\frac{O_{x}^{+}}{D_{x}^{+}}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.

To see the validity of the estimate in the latter display, we use Bennett’s inequality, which gives, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0

(4.5)

-\log\mathbb{P}(O_{x}^{+}\geq\varepsilon D_{x}^{+}\,,\,\mathcal{E})\geq-\log\mathbb{P}(O_{x}^{+}\geq\varepsilon C_{1}\log(n))\gtrsim\varepsilon\log(\varepsilon\alpha^{-1})\log(n)\,.

Then (
4.4
) follows by Proposition
4
, the fact that
\log(\varepsilon\alpha^{-1})\gg 1
, and a union bound.
∎

Proposition 11
.

Let
\alpha^{-1}\gg t_{\textup{ent}}
. Then for
T\ll\min\{\alpha^{-1},\sqrt{n}(\log(n))^{-2}\}
,

(4.6)

\max_{x\in V}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq T)=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.

Proof.

We proceed by the same line of argument as in the proof of Proposition
9
. Notice that in this case there is no loss of generality in assuming
T\gg 1
, since the (random) map
T\mapsto\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq T)
is deterministically increasing for any choice of
x\in V
. Let
K=\lfloor\log^{2}n\rfloor
and call
\cE
the event that all the vertices in the graph have out-degree at least
C_{1}\log(n)
and
\cD
the event in which each vertex has a tree-excess at most
1
in its out-neighborhood of height
4
. Then

(4.7)

\mathbb{P}(\textup{{1}}_{\cE\cap\cD}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq T)\geq\delta)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\textup{{1}}_{\cE\cap\cD}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq T)^{K}]}{\delta^{K}}.

Consider now
K
annealed random walks
(X^{(\ell)})_{\ell\leq K}
starting at some
x\in V
.
Let us define the family of events
(B_{\ell})_{\ell\leq K}
. For each
\ell\leq K
,
B_{\ell}
is the event that:

(i)

\inf\{s\geq 1\mid c(X^{(j)}_{s})\neq c(x)\}\leq T
, for all
j\leq\ell
;

(ii)

all the out-degrees of the vertices visited by the first
\ell
walks are at least
C_{1}\log(n)
;

(iii)

the out-neighborhood of
x
of height
4
discovered by the first
\ell
trajectories has a tree excess at most
1
.

With this definitions we have

(4.8)

\mathbb{E}[\textup{{1}}_{\cE\cap\cD}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq T)^{K}]\leq\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(B_{K})=\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(B_{1})\prod_{{\ell}=2}^{K}\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(B_{{\ell}}|B_{{\ell}-1})\,.

Let us start by noting that
given
B_{{\ell}-1}
, the event
B_{\ell}
is contained in the union of the following four events:

(0)

The
{\ell}
-th trajectory jumps to some
y\in V
such that
c(y)\neq c(x)
at some time
s\leq 4
(the probability of this event is
o(1)
thanks to Lemma
10
);

(1)

For all
s\leq 4
the
{\ell}
-th trajectory is always in a vertex already visited by one of the previous walks; thanks to (iii), for any
y
there are at most
2
paths of length
4
joining
x
to
y
, moreover, thanks to (ii), this happens with probability at most
8K(C_{1}\log(n))^{-4}=o(1)
;

(2)

There exists some
s\leq 4
such that the walk visits an unvisited vertex, and there exists a time
s^{\prime}\in(s,T]
at which it
intersects again one of the previous walks (including itself); this happens with probability less than
\frac{KT^{2}}{n}=o(1)
;

(3)

None of the event above is verified, yet before time
T
the
{\ell}
-th trajectory jumps to some
y\in V
such that
c(y)\neq c(z)
; thanks to Eq. (
4.2
), this happens with probability at most

\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq T,\mathcal{C}_{T})(1+o(1))=o(1)\,.

In conclusion, we have
\mathbb{P}^{\textup{an}}_{x}(B_{\ell}|B_{{\ell}-1})=o(1)
. Thanks to the choice of
K
, we conclude, for
\delta>0
fixed,

(4.9)

\mathbb{P}(\textup{{1}}_{\cE\cap\cD}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq T)\geq\delta)\leq\left(\frac{o(1)}{\delta}\right)^{K}=o\left(n^{-c}\right)\,,\qquad\forall c>0\,.

Therefore

(4.10)

\mathbb{P}(\max_{x\in V}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq T)\geq\delta)\leq\mathbb{P}(\textup{{1}}_{\cE\cap\cD}\max_{x\in V}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq T)\geq\delta)+o(1)=o(1)\,.

∎

4.2.
Local equilibrium: a first timescale

In what follows we will sometimes commit a slight abuse of notation by lifting
\pi_{i}
to a probability measure on the entire vertex set
V
.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\alpha^{-1}\gg t_{\textup{ent}}
. Then, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
and
T\ll\min\{\alpha^{-1},\sqrt{n}(\log(n))^{-2}\}
,

(4.11)

\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V_{i}}\max_{t\in[(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}},T]}\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\pi_{i}\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.

Proof.

Fix
i\leq m
, and notice that there is no loss of generality in assuming
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Let
\mathcal{J}=[(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}},T]
. By the triangle inequality

(4.12)

\begin{split}\max_{t\in\mathcal{J}}&\max_{x\in V_{i}}\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\pi_{i}\|_{\rm TV}\\
&\leq\max_{t\in\mathcal{J}}\max_{x\in V_{i}}\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)\|_{\rm TV}+\max_{t\in\mathcal{J}}\max_{x\in V_{i}}\|\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\pi_{i}\|_{\rm TV}\\
&\leq\max_{x\in V_{i}}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq T)+\max_{x\in V_{i}}\|\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X_{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}\in\cdot)-\pi_{i}\|_{\rm TV}\,,\end{split}

where the second inequality can be deduced by coupling the random walk on
(G_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
and
G
in the natural way.
Thanks to Proposition
11
and Theorem
1
, respectively, we may maximize the two terms on the r.h.s. over
i\leq m
and
x\in V_{i}
and get the desired upper bound.
∎

In conclusion, Theorem
12
shows that for
T\ll\min\{\alpha^{-1},{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\log(n)^{-2}}\}
the dynamics is trapped in the local equilibrium corresponding to the starting community.

4.3.
Global equilibrium: a second timescale

In the section, we are going to show that each
T\gg\alpha^{-1}

provides an upper bound on the mixing time of the SRW on our digraph.
The claim will be divided into two parts
depending on the value of
\alpha
. In particular, here we will focus on the window
t_{\textup{ent}}\ll\alpha^{-1}\ll\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}
,
while we postpone to Section
5
the discussion of the strongly supercritical regime, where
\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}\lesssim\alpha^{-1}\ll n\lambda\log(n)
.
As it will be clear along the proofs, the two regimes require different tools and techniques.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\alpha
be such that
t_{\textup{ent}}\ll\alpha^{-1}\ll\sqrt{n}{\log(n)^{-2}}
. If
T
is such that
T\gg\alpha^{-1}
, then

(4.13)

\max_{x\in V}\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{T}\in\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.

Moreover, it holds
\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\pi_{i}}-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,
.

Proof.

Start by fixing
T\ll{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\log(n)^{-2}}
. The thesis will hold for general
T
by monotonicity.
Notice also that there is no loss of generality in replacing
T
by
T+2t_{\textup{ent}}
. We use the deterministic bound

(4.14)

\begin{split}\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{T+{2t_{\textup{ent}}}}\in\cdot)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}&=\left\|\sum_{y\in V}P^{T}(x,y)P^{2t_{\textup{ent}}}(y,\cdot)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}\\
&=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{y\in V_{i}}P^{T}(x,y)P^{2t_{\textup{ent}}}(y,\cdot)-\frac{\pi_{i}}{m}\right)\right\|_{\rm TV}\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|\sum_{y\in V_{i}}P^{T}(x,y)P^{2t_{\textup{ent}}}(y,\cdot)-\frac{\pi_{i}}{m}\right\|_{\rm TV}\,.\end{split}

Notice that, thanks to Proposition
9
, we can bound

(4.15)

\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V}\left|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{T}\in V_{i})-\frac{1}{m}\right|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Let us fix
i\leq m
and focus on the total variation distance on the r.h.s. of (
4.14
). We have

(4.16)

\begin{split}&\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V}\left\|\sum_{y\in V_{i}}P^{T}(x,y)P^{2t_{\textup{ent}}}(y,\cdot)-\frac{\pi_{i}}{m}\right\|_{\rm TV}\\
&\leq\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V}\left\|\sum_{y\in V_{i}}P^{T}(x,y)\left(P^{2t_{\textup{ent}}}(y,\cdot)-\pi_{i}\right)\right\|_{\rm TV}+\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V}\Big|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{T}\in V_{i})-\frac{1}{m}\Big|\\
&\leq\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V}\sum_{y\in V_{i}}P^{T}(x,y)\left\|P^{2t_{\textup{ent}}}(y,\cdot)-\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\\
&\leq\max_{i\leq m}\max_{y\in V_{i}}\left\|P^{2t_{\textup{ent}}}(y,\cdot)-\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,,\end{split}

where both in the second and in the third inequalities we used (
4.15
), and the last asymptotic bound follows from Theorem
12
. Plugging (
4.16
) into (
4.14
) we deduce that

\max_{x\in V}\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{T+2t_{\textup{ent}}}\in\cdot)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.

Then,

(4.17)

\begin{split}\left\|\pi-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}&\leq\sum_{x\in V}\pi(x)\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{T+2t_{\textup{ent}}}\in\cdot)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}\\
&\leq\max_{x\in V}\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{T+2t_{\textup{ent}}}\in\cdot)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.\end{split}

This concludes the proof.
∎

5.
Strongly supercritical regime

If
\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}\lesssim\alpha^{-1}\ll\lambda n\log(n)
, the relevant timescale, beyond the scale of
t_{\textup{ent}}
, is
\alpha^{-1}\gtrsim\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}
. We cannot rely on the approximation obtained in Proposition
9
to control the random walk behavior of such a timescale.
For this
strongly supercritical
regime, we need to generalize the estimates obtained for the
weakly supercritical
regime in Section
4
using a different set of tools. In particular, the following two statements provide the analogue of Proposition
9
and Theorem
13
.

Proposition 14
.

For
\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}\lesssim\alpha^{-1}\ll\lambda n\log(n)
, there exists some
C\gg 1
such that, if
t\leq C\alpha^{-1}
,

(5.1)

\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V_{i}}\left|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})-{Q}^{t}(i,i)\right|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,,

where,
{Q}^{t}(c(x),i)
is defined as in Eq. (
3.10
).

Theorem 15
.

For
\sqrt{n}{\log(n)^{-2}}\lesssim\alpha^{-1}\ll\lambda n\log(n)
, if
T
is such that
T\gg\alpha^{-1}
, then

(5.2)

\max_{x\in V}\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{T}\in\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.

Moreover,
\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\pi_{i}}-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.

As mentioned above, despite the clear analogy with Proposition
9
and Theorem
13
, the proofs of Proposition
14
and Theorem
15
exploit a different line of argument, based on quasi-stationary distributions, which also shows the emergence of homogenization for such small values of
\alpha
. In order to facilitate the reading, before entering the details, we provide a brief account of the organization of the rest of this section.

Organization of the section

The rest of the section is divided into five parts. In Section
5.1
we introduce the notion of
gates
. In words, a
gate
is a vertex that has an edge that points toward another community. Clearly, in the strongly supercritical regime, gates are rare, since only few vertices have such inter-community connections. With this idea in mind, in Section
5.2
we provide a control on the first time the random walk visits the set of gates. In particular, using the framework of
quasi-stationary distributions
, we show that such a first visit is well approximated by an exponential random variable and characterize its expectation. In Section
5.3
we use the understanding of the hitting time of the set of gates to couple the random walk with a toy process which enjoys some sort of
renewal
property which makes it simpler to analyze. Finally, in Section
5.4
we use this coupling to complete the proof of Proposition
14
and Theorem
15
.

5.1.
The gates

Fixed a community
i\leq m
, the idea is to couple the random walk on
G
started at some
x\in V_{i}
with a simpler process on
V_{i}
, up to the first time when the random walk moves to another community. To provide further details, it is necessary to first introduce some additional notation.
We we call
gates of
V_{i}
the subset of vertices in
V_{i}
having at least a rewired out-edge, i.e.,

(5.3)

\mathcal{G}_{i}\coloneqq\{y\in V_{i}\mid O_{y}^{+}>0\}\,.

In the regime
\alpha^{-1}\gtrsim\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}
, this set turns out to be small, in the sense that it has a small stationary value, as explained by the next result.

Lemma 16
.

If
\alpha^{-1}\gg\lambda\log(n)
,

(5.4)

\max_{i\leq m}\left|\frac{\pi_{i}(\mathcal{G}_{i})}{\alpha\lambda\log(n)}-1\right|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.

Proof.

For
i\leq m
, thanks to Theorem
3
,
\pi_{i}(\mathcal{G}_{i})
is w.h.p. well approximated by
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}\frac{D_{x,i}^{-}}{n^{2}p}
.
The latter is a sum of random variables taken on a random set, but it is not difficult to show that it concentrates around its expectation. Indeed, for
k>0
it holds

(5.5)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\big||\mathcal{G}_{i}|-\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{G}_{i}|]\big|>k)&\leq\frac{\mathbb{V}\hskip-1.0pt{\rm ar}(|\mathcal{G}_{i}|)}{k^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{x\in V_{i}}\mathbb{V}\hskip-1.0pt{\rm ar}(\textup{{1}}_{\{O_{x}^{+}>0\}})}{k^{2}}\\
&=(1+o(1))\frac{n(\lambda\alpha\log(n))(1-\lambda\alpha\log(n))}{k^{2}}\,,\end{split}

where we used that, for
x\in V_{i}
, we have
\mathbb{P}(x\in\mathcal{G}_{i})=1-(1-\alpha p)^{n}=\lambda\alpha\log(n)(1+o(1))
.
Choosing
k
such that
\sqrt{n\lambda\alpha\log(n)}\ll k\ll n\lambda\alpha\log(n)
, we get that

(5.6)

\mathbb{P}\left(\big||\mathcal{G}_{i}|-\lambda n\alpha\log(n)\big|>k\right)=o(1).

Fixed any
\varepsilon>0
,
using the Chernoff bound, we obtain

(5.7)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}&\left(\bigg\{\bigg|\sum_{x\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}{D_{x,i}^{-}}-|\mathcal{G}_{i}|\lambda\log(n)\bigg|>\varepsilon|\mathcal{G}_{i}|\lambda\log(n)\bigg\}\cap\bigg\{\Big||\mathcal{G}_{i}|-n\alpha\lambda\log(n)\Big|<k\bigg\}\right)\\
&\leq\max_{\delta\in[-\frac{1}{10},\frac{1}{10}]}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{y=1}^{n\alpha\lambda\log(n)(1+\delta)}{D_{y,i}^{-}}-n\alpha(\lambda\log(n))^{2}(1+\delta)\right|>(1+\delta)\varepsilon n\alpha(\lambda\log(n))^{2}\right)\\
&\leq 2\exp\left\{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}n\alpha(\lambda\log(n))^{2}}{4}\right\}\,.\end{split}

Notice that the event
\{x\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\}
does not depend on
D_{x,i}^{-}
, and hence we can rely on the classical bound for the sums of i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables. Then, choosing
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{\log(n)}
suffices to make the estimate in (
5.7
) vanish. The desired result then follows by combining (
5.6
) and (
5.7
).
∎

5.2.
First visit time to gates

We now introduce the so-called
quasi-stationary distribution
, that is, the long-run distribution of the walk on
G_{i}
conditioned to the event of not having hit the set
\mathcal{G}_{i}
yet. Let
[P_{i}]_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}
be the sub-Markovian kernel in which the rows and columns indexed by the vertices in
\mathcal{G}_{i}
have been removed. Then, called
\mathfrak{l}_{i}
the largest eigenvalue of
[P_{i}]_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}
, by the Perron-Frobenius theorem there exists a probability distribution
\mu^{\star}_{i}
which is a left eigenvector for
[P_{i}]_{\mathcal{G}_{i}}
associated to the eigenvalue
\mathfrak{l}_{i}
. In particular, the hitting time of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
for the simple random walk on
V_{i}
(with kernel
P_{i}
) started at
\mu^{\star}_{i}
is
exactly
geometrically distributed with parameter
\mathfrak{l}_{i}
. This
\mathfrak{l}_{i}
can also be characterized at first order by the expected hitting time of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
starting at
\pi_{i}
. All these fact are summarized by the following proposition due to Aldous
[
Ald82
]
.

Theorem 17

(Cf. Prop. A.1 and Lemma A.2 in
[
QS23
]
)
.

Let
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Markov chain on a finite state space
\Omega
with transition matrix
\Pi
and unique stationary distribution
\rho
, fully supported on
\Omega
. Let
\partial\in\Omega
be a
target state
. Then, there exist a unique probability distribution
\mu_{\star}
on

\Omega\setminus\partial
and a unique
\mathfrak{l}\in(0,1)
such that

(5.8)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbf{P}_{\rho}\left(W_{t}=x\mid\tau_{\partial}>t\right)=\mu_{\star}(x)\,,\qquad\forall x\in\Omega\setminus\partial\,,

and

(5.9)

\mathbf{P}_{\mu_{\star}}\left(\tau_{\partial}>t\right)=(1-\mathfrak{l})^{t}\,,\qquad\forall t\geq 0\,.

Moreover, observing that
\mathfrak{l}=(\mathbf{E}_{\mu_{\star}}[\tau_{\partial}])^{-1}
, it holds

(5.10)

\left|\frac{\mathbf{E}_{\mu_{\star}}[\tau_{\partial}]}{\mathbf{E}_{\rho}[\tau_{\partial}]}-1\right|=\left|\frac{\mathfrak{l}^{-1}}{\mathbf{E}_{\rho}[\tau_{\partial}]}-1\right|\leq\frac{20}{3}\frac{t_{\rm mix}(2+\log\mathbf{E}_{\rho}[\tau_{\partial}])}{\mathbf{E}_{\rho}[\tau_{\partial}]}\,,

where

(5.11)

t_{\rm mix}=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\mid\max_{x\in\Omega}\|\Pi(y,\cdot)-\rho\|_{\rm TV}\leq(2{\rm e})^{-1}\right\}\,.

Additionally, consider a sequence of Markov chains with
\Omega_{N}=[N]
, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
If

(5.12)

\lim_{N\to\infty}T\times\rho(\partial)=0\,,

where

(5.13)

T=t_{\rm mix}\times\log\left(1/\min_{x\in\Omega_{N}}\rho(x)\right)\,,

then, the expected hitting time of
\partial
, starting at stationarity can be estimated asymptotically by

(5.14)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}_{\rho}[\tau_{\partial}]}{R/\rho(\partial)}=1\,,

where

(5.15)

R=\sum_{t=0}^{T}{\mathbf{P}}_{\partial}(W_{t}=\partial)\,.

In our setting, the
target state
is actually a
target set
, namely
\mathcal{G}_{i}
. This is not a big issue: indeed, if one considers the Markov kernel
\tilde{P}_{i}
on
\tilde{V}_{i}=(V_{i}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{i})\cup\partial
, where the state
\partial
represent the merging of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
into a single state and the transitions are set to

(5.16)

\tilde{P}_{i}(x,y)=\begin{cases}P_{i}(x,y)&x,y\neq\partial\\
\sum_{z\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}P_{i}(x,z)&x\neq\partial\text{ and }y=\partial\\
\sum_{z\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}\frac{\pi_{i}(z)}{\pi_{i}(\mathcal{G}_{i})}P_{i}(z,y)&x=\partial\text{ and }y\neq\partial\\
\sum_{z\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}\frac{\pi_{i}(z)}{\pi_{i}(\mathcal{G}_{i})}P_{i}(z,v)&x=\partial\text{ and }y=\partial\end{cases}\,,

then one can realize at once that the restriction of
\tilde{P}_{i}
to
V_{i}\setminus\partial
coincides with the restriction of
P_{i}
to
V_{i}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{i}
. Moreover, by direct computation, it is possible to see that, as soon as
P_{i}
has a unique stationary distribution
\pi_{i}
, then
\tilde{P}_{i}
has a unique stationary distribution
\tilde{\pi}_{i}
satisfying

(5.17)

\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}\pi_{i}(x)&x\neq\partial\\
\pi_{i}(\mathcal{G}_{i})&x=\partial\end{cases}\,.

For each
i=1,\dots,m
, we take the choice
\Pi=\tilde{P}_{i}
.
Replacing

Moreover, thanks to Lemma

, the inter-community jump ending each iteration is approximately uniformly distributed among the other communities. Therefore, if
(\tau_{\textup{jump}}^{({\ell})})_{{\ell}\leq C^{2}}
denotes the sequence of the lengths of the first
C^{2}
iterations, we can couple the sequence
\big(c(X_{\tau_{\textup{jump}}^{({\ell})}})\big)_{\ell\leq C^{2}}
with the trajectory (of length
C^{2}
) of the process with transition matrix
Q
at a total-variation cost bounded by
1-C^{2}o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. The latter goes to
1
as soon as
C
diverges sufficiently slowly. At this point the desired result follows at once by noting that if, for any
t\geq 0
, we sample i.i.d. geometric random variables of parameter
\alpha
up to the first time in which their cumulative sum is above
t
(hence, sampling the jump times), and then we sample the path of a simple random walk on the complete graph, we can entirely reconstruct the trajectory of process with transition matrix
Q
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
15
.

The proof of this fact follows the same line of argument as in Theorem
13
.
In our setting, Eq. (
4.15
) follows from Proposition
14

for any
T\ll C\alpha^{-1}
, where
C\gg 1
is as given
in Proposition
14
.
The remainder of the argument proceeds through the same steps, leading to the validity of Eq. (
5.2
)
for any
\alpha^{-1}\ll T\ll\sqrt{C}\alpha^{-1}
. Finally, by the monotonicity of the distance to equilibrium,
the result holds for any
T\gg\alpha^{-1}
.
∎

6.
Critical and subcritical regime

In this section, we analyze the mixing behavior of the random walk in the regime
\alpha^{-1}\lesssim t_{\textup{ent}}
.
This range of
\alpha
includes both the subcritical and critical cases, described in Eqs. (
2.5
)
and (
2.6
).
The proofs of these results are adaptations of the techniques developed in
[
BCS18
,
BP25
]
to the current setting
with multiple communities.
Since it will be useful later, in what follows we take
\alpha^{-1}=Ct_{\textup{ent}}
, for
C>0
. We again stress that Eq. (
2.5
) will hold only for
C\to 0
.

6.1.
Concentration results for random walk paths

The first random object to study is given by the following definition. For each oriented path
\mathfrak{p}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{t})
in
G
, we define the quenched probability
mass
of
\mathfrak{p}
as

\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p})\coloneqq\prod_{s=0}^{t-1}P(x_{s},x_{s+1})\,.

We can state the following result, which can be written in the shape of a Law of Large Numbers for the variables
\log(P(X_{s},X_{s+1}))
.

Theorem 29

(Quenched LLN)
.

Let
t=\Theta(t_{\textup{ent}})
and
\theta\in(0,1)
be such that there exists
\rho>0
,
\rho\neq 1
, satisfying
\log\theta=\rho\textup{H}t(1+o(1))
.
Then

(6.1)

\max_{x\in V}\left|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}\left(\textup{{m}}(X_{0},X_{1},\dots,X_{t})>\theta\right)-\textup{{1}}_{\{\rho>1\}}\right|\,\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{\hskip 8.19447pt\mathbb{P}\hskip 8.19447pt}0.

Proof.

The proof follows by a straightforward adaptation of the proof of
[
BP25
, Theorem 3]
which, in turn, is an adaptation of the proof of
[
BCS19
, Theorem 4]
.
Indeed, all vertex out-degrees have the same law,
\text{Bin}(n,p)
, and the proof in the above mentioned references does not rely on the details of the graph structure but only on the out-degree distribution and the fact that the random walk is w.h.p. non-backtracking on the timescale
\log(n)
.
∎

This result has the same shape of Eq. (
2.5
) and is the core of the cutoff result. In fact, it is easy to observe that
\theta={\rm e}^{-\textup{H}t_{\textup{ent}}}=n^{-1}
provides a threshold for the concentration of the mass of the paths with length
t_{\textup{ent}}
. That specific choice of
\theta
is not covered by the theorem above, but in
[
BP25
]
it is possible to find a quenched CLT refinement, valid in this critical regime, to describe the cutoff window under a suitable hypothesis.

The second ingredient is provided by the following family of paths, which allows us to capture typical features of SRW paths.

Definition 30

(Nice paths)
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and

(6.2)

{h_{\varepsilon}}\coloneqq 2\varepsilon t_{\textup{ent}},\quad\quad{s_{\varepsilon}}\coloneqq(1-\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}},\quad\quad t\coloneqq{s_{\varepsilon}}+{h_{\varepsilon}}+1=(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}+1.

We say that a path
\mathfrak{p}=(x,x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{t-1},y)
of length
t
from
x
to
y
is
nice
if:

(i)

the entire path is such that
\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p})\leq\frac{1}{n\log^{3}n}
;

(ii)

the sub-path
(x_{{s_{\varepsilon}}+1},\dots,x_{t-1},y)
is the unique path in
G
of length at most
{h_{\varepsilon}}
from
x_{{s_{\varepsilon}}}
to
y
;

(iii)

the sub-path
(x,x_{1},\dots,x_{{s_{\varepsilon}}})
is contained in the random tree
\mathcal{T}_{x}({s_{\varepsilon}})
constructed as follows:

Fix a realization of
G
and a root node
x\in V
.
Let
\mathcal{G}^{0}=\mathcal{T}^{0}:=\{x\}
. Then, for
\ell\geq 1
:

(1)

Let
\mathcal{E}^{\ell}\coloneq\{(v,y):v\in\mathcal{G}^{\ell-1},y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}(1)\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\ell-1}\}
,
be the set of edges which have not been visited by the first
\ell-1

iterations, and with tails in
\mathcal{G}^{\ell-1}
.

(2)

Choose
e=(v,y)\in\mathcal{E}^{\ell}
such that, if
\mathfrak{p}_{x,v}
is the unique path from
x
to
v
in
\mathcal{G}^{\ell-1}
,

\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p}_{x,v})(D^{+}_{v})^{-1}
is not below
{\rm e}^{-(1+\varepsilon)\textup{H}{s_{\varepsilon}}}
and it is maximal among
(v,y)\in\mathcal{E}^{\ell}
,
and
v
is at distance at most
{s_{\varepsilon}}-1
from
x

(use a deterministic criterion to break ties).

If such edge does not exist, stop the procedure and set
\mathcal{T}_{x}({s_{\varepsilon}})\coloneqq\mathcal{T}^{\ell-1}
;

(3)

Generate
\mathcal{G}^{\ell}
by adding
e
to

\mathcal{G}^{\ell-1}
;

(4)

Generate
\mathcal{T}^{\ell}
by adding
e
to
\mathcal{T}^{\ell-1}
if it results in a tree,
otherwise set
\mathcal{T}^{\ell}=\mathcal{T}^{\ell-1}
.

For every fixed
x,y\in V
we define

(6.3)

P_{\rm nice}^{t}(x,y)\coloneqq\text{prob. of
following a nice path $x\rightsquigarrow y$ of length $t$}.

As mentioned above, nice paths w.h.p. host typical trajectories of the SRW:

Proposition 31
.

Fix
\beta>1
and
t=\beta t_{\textup{ent}}
. Then, uniformly in a starting position in
V^{\star}_{\varepsilon}
, defined in Eq. (
3.6
), the SRW w.h.p. follows nice paths. More precisely,

\max_{x\in V^{\star}_{\varepsilon}}\Big(1-\sum_{y\in V}P_{\rm nice}^{t}(x,y)\Big)=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Proof.

Thanks to Lemma
6
, the requirements (ii) and (iii) of Definition
30
are w.h.p. satisfied. Then, letting

\theta=({n\log^{3}n})^{-1}
,

(6.4)

\max_{x\in V^{\star}_{\varepsilon}}\Big(1-\sum_{y\in V}P_{\rm nice}^{t}(x,y)\Big)\leq\max_{x\in V^{\star}_{\varepsilon}}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}\left(\textup{{m}}(X_{0},X_{1},\dots,X_{t})>\theta\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

and the l.h.s. (namely, the cost of requirement (i) ) vanishes by Theorem
29
.
∎

6.2.
Bounds on the total variation profile

With the latter tools at hand and with the help of Proposition
32
below, which is a rewriting of
[
BP25
, Prop. 3]
, it is possible to bound effectively the distance to equilibrium.

Proposition 32
.

Let
\alpha^{-1}\lesssim t_{\textup{ent}}
,
x\in V
, and
t={s_{\varepsilon}}+{h_{\varepsilon}}+1
as in Definition
30
.
Then,

(6.5)

\mathbb{P}\left(P_{\rm nice}^{t}(x,y)\leq(1+\delta)\nu_{c(x)}(y)+\frac{\delta}{n},\,\forall x,\in V^{\star}_{\varepsilon},\forall y\in V,\right)=1-o(1),\qquad\forall\delta>0,

where
{Q}^{{s_{\varepsilon}}+1}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in Eq. (
3.10
) and, for
i\leq m
,

(6.6)

\nu_{i}(y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{w\in V}{{Q}^{{s_{\varepsilon}}+1}(i,c(w))}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(w,y).

Proof.

The proof mimics
[
BP25
, Prop. 3]
.
Let
x\in V^{\star}_{\varepsilon}
, as defined in Eq. (
3.6
), and let
\mathcal{F}
denote the partial environment generated by the tree
\mathcal{T}_{x}({s_{\varepsilon}})
and
\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}({h_{\varepsilon}})
, the in-neighborhood of
y
of depth
{h_{\varepsilon}}
. We split
P_{\rm nice}^{t}(x,y)
in
m
different addends.
For
i=1,\dots,m
, let

P_{{\rm nice},i}^{t}(x,y)=\sum_{z\in V^{+}_{\mathcal{F}}(i)}\sum_{v\in V^{-}_{\mathcal{F}}}\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p}_{x,z})\frac{1}{D^{+}_{z}}\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p}_{v,y})\textup{{1}}_{\{z\to v\}}\textup{{1}}_{\{\mathfrak{p}\textup{ is a nice path}\}},

where
V^{+}_{\mathcal{F}}(i)
is the set of vertices
at depth
{s_{\varepsilon}}
in
\mathcal{T}_{x}({s_{\varepsilon}})\cap V_{i}
, and
V^{-}_{\mathcal{F}}
is the set of vertices in
\partial\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}({h_{\varepsilon}})
such that there exists a unique path of length
{h_{\varepsilon}}
to y.
Then, it holds
P_{\rm nice}^{t}(x,y)=\sum_{i\leq m}P_{{\rm nice},i}^{t}(x,y).

Setting
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(\cdot)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\cdot\,|\mathcal{F})
, we have that, for
z,v\in V
,

(6.7)

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\textup{{1}}_{\{z\to v\}}}{D^{+}_{z}}\,\big|\mathcal{F}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\textup{{1}}_{\{z\to v\}}}{D^{+}_{z}}\,\big|\mathcal{F},\textup{{1}}_{\{z\to v\}}=1\right]\mathbb{P}\left((z,v)\in E\right)=\frac{p_{zv}}{np}(1+o(1)).

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[P_{{\rm nice},i}^{t}(x,y)\,|\,\mathcal{F}\,]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in V^{-}_{\mathcal{F}}}\sum_{z\in V^{+}_{\mathcal{F}}(i)}\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p}_{x,z})\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\textup{{1}}_{\{z\to v\}}}{D^{+}_{z}}\,\big|\mathcal{F}\right]\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p}_{v,y})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in V^{-}_{\mathcal{F}}}\sum_{z\in V^{+}_{\mathcal{F}}(i)}\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p}_{x,z})\frac{p_{zv}}{np}\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p}_{v,y})(1+o(1)).

Since the random walk performs w.h.p. a nice path with support concentrated on the vertices of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{x}({s_{\varepsilon}})
(Proposition
31
), and thanks to Proposition
9
, for
i\leq m
, w.h.p. it holds

(6.8)

\left|\sum_{z\in V^{+}_{\mathcal{F}}(i)}\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p}_{x,z})-{Q}^{{s_{\varepsilon}}}(c(x),i)\right|=o(1).

Then, observing that
\frac{p_{zv}}{p}={Q}(c(z),c(v))
, it holds

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}[P_{{\rm nice},i}^{t}(x,y)\,|\,\mathcal{F}\,]}

\displaystyle\leq{Q}^{s_{\varepsilon}}(c(x),i)\sum_{v\in V^{-}_{\mathcal{F}}}\frac{{Q}(i,c(v))}{n}\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p}_{v,y})(1+o(1))

\displaystyle\leq{Q}^{{s_{\varepsilon}}}(c(x),i)\sum_{w\in V}\frac{{Q}(i,c(w))}{n}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(w,y)(1+o(1)).

Summing over
i\leq m
, we conclude

(6.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[P_{\rm nice}^{t}(x,y)\,|\,\mathcal{F}\,]\leq\sum_{w\in V}\frac{{Q}^{{s_{\varepsilon}}+1}(c(x),c(w))}{n}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(w,y)(1+o(1))=\nu_{c(x)}(y)(1+o(1)).

The proof then continues as in
[
BP25
, Prop. 3]
, employing Eq. (
6.9
)
and a suitable Bernstein’s concentration inequality,
and averaging over the partial environment
\mathcal{F}
to obtain the thesis.
∎

We now proceed with our analysis, pursuing an upper bound and a lower bound for the distance to stationarity at times
t
greater than
\beta t_{\textup{ent}}
for
\beta>1
, and smaller than
\beta t_{\textup{ent}}
for
\beta<1
, respectively.

6.2.1.
Upper bound

We start stating the following straightforward lemma.

Lemma 33
.

Let
\alpha^{-1}=Ct_{\textup{ent}}
. Then for each
j=1,\dots,m
, it holds
\pi(V_{j})=\frac{1}{m}(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))
.

Proof.

By definition of stationarity, for each
j=1,\dots,m
, and for
t=(\log(n))^{2}
,

(6.10)

\left|\pi(V_{j})-\frac{1}{m}\right|\leq\sum_{x\in V}\pi(x)\left|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{j})-\frac{1}{m}\right|\leq\max_{x\in V}\left|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{j})-\frac{1}{m}\right|,

which is
o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
by Proposition
9
.
∎

Choose now a time
t\geq\beta t_{\textup{ent}}
, for
\beta>1
. Applying Proposition
32
, and later Proposition
31
, for every
\delta>0
it holds

(6.11)

\begin{split}\max_{x\in V^{\star}_{\varepsilon}}\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\nu_{c(x)}\|_{\textup{TV}}&\leq\max_{x\in V^{\star}_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{y\in V}\left[(1+\delta)\nu_{c(x)}(y)+\frac{\delta}{n}-P_{\rm nice}^{t}(x,y)\right]\\
&\leq\max_{x\in V^{\star}_{\varepsilon}}\left(1-\sum_{y\in V}P_{\rm nice}^{t}(x,y)\right)+2\delta=2\delta+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).\end{split}

Then, thanks to Lemma
6
and Eq. (
6.11
), for
\ell=3\log\log(n)
,

(6.12)

\max_{x\in V}\|P^{t+\ell}(x,\cdot)-\nu_{c(x)}\|_{\textup{TV}}\leq\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{\ell}\notin V^{\star}_{\varepsilon})+\max_{x\in V^{\star}_{\varepsilon}}\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\nu_{c(x)}\|_{\textup{TV}}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Since, for every
w\in V
, it holds
\sum_{i=1}^{m}{{Q}^{{s_{\varepsilon}}+1}(i,c(w))}=1
, we can define

(6.13)

\nu\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}=\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{w\in V}P^{h_{\varepsilon}}(w,\cdot).

Employing Lemma
33
and later Eq. (
6.12
), it holds

(6.14)

\begin{split}\left\|\nu-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}&=\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu_{i}-\sum_{z\in V}\pi(z)P^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(z,\cdot)\right\|_{\rm TV}\\
&=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{z\in V_{i}}{\pi(z)}\left[\nu_{i}-P^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(z,\cdot)\right]\right\|_{\rm TV}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\\
&\leq\max_{i\leq m}\max_{z\in V_{i}}\left\|P^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(z,\cdot)-\nu_{c(z)}\right\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).\end{split}

This means that
\nu
constitutes a good proxy for
\pi
.
Then, by the triangle inequality, and thanks to Eqs. (
6.12
) and (
6.14
), we can conclude the following upper bound

(6.15)

\begin{split}\max_{x\in V}\left\|P^{t+\ell}(x,\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}&\leq\max_{x\in V}\left\|P^{t+\ell}(x,\cdot)-\nu_{c(x)}\right\|_{\rm TV}+\max_{i\leq m}\left\|\nu_{i}-\nu\right\|_{\rm TV}+\left\|\nu-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}\\
&\leq\max_{i\leq m}\left\|\nu_{i}-\nu\right\|_{\rm TV}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).\end{split}

6.2.2.
Lower bound

Let
t\leq\beta t_{\textup{ent}}
, for
\beta<1
.
We are going to show that the law of
X_{t}
at a time
t\leq{\beta t_{\textup{ent}}}
, is w.h.p. concentrated on a set with cardinality
o(n)
.
To this end, let
\theta=n^{-\beta(2-\beta)}
and let

S_{x}\coloneqq\left\{y\in V:\text{ there exists a path $\mathfrak{p}$ of length $t$ such that }\textup{{m}}(\mathfrak{p})\geq\theta\right\}.

We have
|S_{x}|\leq\theta^{-1}=n^{\beta(2-\beta)}=o(\frac{n}{\log(n)})
, since, for
\beta<1
, we have
\beta(2-\beta)<1
. Moreover, it holds

-\frac{\log\theta}{\textup{H}t}=2-\beta>1,
and we conclude the following lower bound:

(6.16)

\begin{split}\min_{x\in V}\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}&\geq\min_{x\in V}\left[\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in S_{x})-\pi(S_{x})\right]\\
&\geq\min_{x\in V}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}\left(\textup{{m}}(X_{0},\dots,X_{t})\geq n^{-\beta(2-\beta)}\right)-\max_{x\in V}\pi(S_{x})\\
&=1-o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),\end{split}

where the last inequality holds combining Theorem
29
with the observation that

\max_{x\in V}\pi(S_{x})=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,,

thanks to Theorem
3
and Proposition
4
.

7.
Proof of the main result

7.1.
Supercritical regime

In this regime, the proof of Theorem
2
is split into two parts, depending on the chosen timescale. We first analyze the case
t\asymp t_{\textup{ent}}
, proving Eq. (
2.7
), and then move to the case
t\asymp\alpha^{-1}
, proving Eq. (
2.8
).

7.1.1.
Relaxation to a local equilibrium

Let us first assume

t_{\textup{ent}}\ll\alpha^{-1}\ll\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}
. We will sometimes commit a slight abuse of notation by lifting
\pi_{i}
to a probability measure on the entire vertex set
V
.
Let
t=\beta t_{\textup{ent}}
for some
\beta<1
.
By Theorem
1
,

(7.1)

\min_{i\leq m}\min_{x\in V_{i}}\|\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X^{i}_{t}\in\cdot)-\pi_{i}\|_{\rm TV}=1-o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,,

In particular, for every
\delta>0
and
x\in V_{i}
, it must hold

(7.2)

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in V}\left|\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X^{i}_{t}=y)-\frac{1}{m}\pi_{i}(y)\right|\geq 1-\frac{m-1}{2m}-\delta+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

otherwise
there would exists a
\delta>0
such that

(7.3)

\begin{split}\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X^{i}_{t}\in\cdot)-\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}&\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in V}\left|\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X^{i}_{t}=y)-\frac{1}{m}\pi_{i}(y)\right|+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{m-1}{m}\sum_{y\in V}\pi_{i}(y)\\
&<1-\frac{m-1}{2m}-\delta+\frac{m-1}{2m}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)=1-\delta+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),\end{split}

which is in contradiction with (
7.1
).
As a consequence, using the characterization of
\pi
given in Theorem
13
, and using that, by Proposition
11
,

(7.4)

\begin{split}\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X_{t}^{i}\in\cdot)\right\|_{\rm TV}&\leq\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V_{i}}\check{\mathbf{P}}_{x}(X_{t}{\neq}X_{t}^{i})\\
&\leq\max_{i\leq m}\max_{x\in V_{i}}\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(\tau_{\textup{jump}}\leq t)=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),\end{split}

we have that, for every
\delta>0
and
x\in V_{i}
,

(7.5)

\begin{split}\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}&=\left\|\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X^{i}_{t}\in\cdot)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}\right\|_{\rm TV}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in V}\left|\mathbf{P}^{G_{i}}_{x}(X^{i}_{t}=y)-\frac{1}{m}\pi_{i}(y)\right|+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{y\in V}\pi_{j}(y)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\\
&\geq 1-\frac{m-1}{2m}-\delta+\frac{m-1}{2m}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)=1-\delta+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).\end{split}

Let now
\beta>1
. By definition of total variation distance

(7.6)

\begin{split}\min_{i\leq m}\min_{x\in V_{i}}\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\pi\|_{\rm TV}&\geq\min_{i\leq m}\min_{x\in V_{i}}\left|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in V_{i})-\pi(V_{i})\right|\\
&=\frac{m-1}{m}\left(1-\frac{m}{m-1}\alpha\right)^{t-1}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,,\end{split}

where we have used Proposition
9
and the characterization of
\pi
given in Theorem
13
.
By our choice
t=\beta t_{\textup{ent}}\ll\alpha^{-1}
, one gets

(7.7)

\min_{i\leq m}\min_{x\in V_{i}}\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\pi\|_{\rm TV}\geq\frac{m-1}{m}-o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.

For what concerns the upper bound, for
\beta>1
, let us fix some
\gamma\geq 0
, possibly depending on
n
, and
\varepsilon>0
such that
(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}+\gamma\leq\beta t_{\textup{ent}}
. By splitting over the vertex on which the SRW sits at time
(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}
, and over the community of such vertex one gets, for
x\in V
,

(7.8)

P^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}+\gamma}(x,\cdot)=\sum_{y\in V}P^{\gamma}(x,y)P^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(y,\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{y\in V_{i}}P^{\gamma}(x,y)P^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(y,\cdot)\,.

Using Theorem
12
, we can write

(7.9)

\displaystyle\max_{x\in V}\left\|P^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}+\gamma}(x,\cdot)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}P^{\gamma}(x,V_{i})\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Let us now focus on the case
t_{\textup{ent}}\ll\alpha^{-1}\ll\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}
. By Proposition
9
we get

(7.10)

\displaystyle\max_{x\in V}\left\|P^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}+\gamma}(x,\cdot)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}{Q}^{\gamma}(c(x),i)\pi_{i}\right\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Thanks to Theorem
13
and recalling the definition in (
3.10
), we then obtain

(7.11)

\begin{split}\max_{x\in V}\left\|{P^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}+\gamma}(x,\cdot)-\pi}\right\|_{\rm TV}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|{Q}^{\gamma}(c(x),i)-\frac{1}{m}\right|+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\\
&=\frac{(m-1)(1-\frac{m}{m-1}\alpha)^{\gamma}}{m}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).\end{split}

Taking
\gamma\ll\alpha^{-1}
and by monotonicity, we conclude that

(7.12)

\max_{x\in V}\left\|{P^{\beta t_{\textup{ent}}}}(x,\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}\leq\frac{m-1}{m}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Then, putting together Eqs. (
7.5
), (
7.7
), and (
7.12
), Eq. (
2.7
) follows.
Similarly, in the case
\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}\ll\alpha^{-1}\ll\lambda n\log(n)
, instead of Proposition
9
and Theorem
13
, we use Proposition
14
and Theorem
15
, leading to the same result.

7.1.2.
Convergence to the global equilibrium

Let us first assume

t_{\textup{ent}}\ll\alpha^{-1}\ll\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}
. Using Eq. (
7.6
), with
t=\beta\alpha^{-1}
, for
\beta>0
,

(7.13)

\min_{i\leq m}\min_{x\in V_{i}}\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\pi\|_{\rm TV}\geq\frac{m-1}{m}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\beta m}{m-1}}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,.

For the upper bound, consider now, in Eq. (
7.11
),
\gamma=t-(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}\sim\beta\alpha^{-1}
. Then, again by monotonicity, we have

(7.14)

\max_{x\in V}\|\mathbf{P}^{G}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\pi\|_{\rm TV}\leq\frac{m-1}{m}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\beta m}{m-1}}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\,,

concluding the proof. Similarly, in the case
\sqrt{n}\log(n)^{-2}\ll\alpha^{-1}\ll\lambda n\log(n)
, instead of Proposition
9
and Theorem
13
, we use Proposition
14
and Theorem
15
, leading to the same result. This proves Eq. (
2.8
).

7.2.
Subcritical regime

The upper bound in Eq. (
2.5
) (which is non-trivial only for
\beta>1
) can be obtained observing that in the subcritical case
\alpha^{-1}\ll t_{\textup{ent}}
, it holds

(7.15)

{{Q}^{{s_{\varepsilon}}}(i,\cdot)}=\frac{1}{m}(1+o(1)),\qquad\forall i\leq m,

Then, recalling the definitions in Eqs. (
6.6
) and (
6.13
), it follows
\max_{i\leq m}\left\|\nu_{i}-\nu\right\|_{\rm TV}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. This, plugged in Eq. (
6.15
),
concludes the upper bound.

The lower bound in Eq. (
2.5
) (which is non-trivial only for
\beta<1
) is precisely Eq. (
6.16
).

7.3.
Critical regime

We fix
C>0
and
t=\beta t_{\textup{ent}}
, for some
\beta>0
,
\beta\neq 1
. We want to prove Eq. (
2.6
). For
\beta<1
, the bound is, again, precisely given by Eq. (
6.16
).
Then, we consider
\beta>1
. By a trivial bound and Proposition
9
,

(7.16)

\begin{split}\min_{x\in V}\left\|P^{\beta t_{\textup{ent}}}(x,\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}&\geq\min_{x\in V}\left|P^{\beta t_{\textup{ent}}}(x,V_{c(x)})-\pi(V_{c(x)})\right|\\
&=\tfrac{m-1}{m}\left(1-\tfrac{m}{m-1}\alpha\right)^{\beta t_{\textup{ent}}}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\\
&=\tfrac{m-1}{m}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\beta}{C}\frac{m}{m-1}}(1+o(1))+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).\end{split}

In the what follows we will prove the following result.

Lemma 34
.

For every
0<\varepsilon<\beta-1
,

(7.17)

\max_{x\in V}\left\|P^{\beta t_{\textup{ent}}}(x,\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}\leq\tfrac{m-1}{m}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\beta}{C}\frac{m}{m-1}}(1+o(1))+\frac{6m\varepsilon}{C}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Since
\varepsilon>0
can be taken arbitrarily small, this lemma closes the discussion for
\beta>1
, finishing the proof of Theorem
2
.
To prove it, it is useful to consider, for every
i\leq m
, the measures

\pi_{V_{i}}(x)=\frac{\pi(x)\textup{{1}}_{\{x\in V_{i}\}}}{\pi(V_{i})}\,,\qquad x\in V.

Lemma 35
.

Let
\alpha^{-1}=Ct_{\textup{ent}}
, for
C>0
. Then

(7.18)

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\leq m}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{i}}P^{\varepsilon t_{\textup{ent}}}(y,\cdot)-{\pi_{V_{i}}}\right\|_{\rm TV}\leq\frac{4m\varepsilon}{C}\right)=1-o(1).

Proof of Lemma
35
.

Using the inequality
|a+b|\geq|a|-|b|
, for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(7.19)

\begin{split}\Bigg\|\frac{1}{mn}&\sum_{y\in V}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,\cdot)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{V_{i}}\Bigg\|_{\rm TV}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{z\in V_{j}}\frac{1}{m}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{j}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,z)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\pi_{V_{i}}(z)}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{j}^{\complement}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,z)\right|\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{z\in V_{j}}\frac{1}{m}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{j}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,z)-\pi_{V_{j}}(z)\right|-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{j}^{\complement}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,z)\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{m}\left[\sum_{z\in V_{j}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{j}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,z)-{\pi_{V_{j}}(z)}\right|-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{j}^{\complement}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,V_{j})\right].\end{split}

On the other hand, for every
j\leq m
it holds

(7.20)

\begin{split}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{j}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,\cdot)-{\pi}_{V_{j}}\right\|_{\rm TV}\!\!\!\!&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z\in V}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{j}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,z)-{\pi}_{V_{j}}(z)\right|\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum_{z\in V_{j}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{j}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,z)-{\pi}_{V_{j}}(z)\right|+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{j}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,V_{j}^{\complement})\right].\end{split}

Putting Eqs. (
7.19
) and (
7.20
) together, it follows

(7.21)

\begin{split}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{m}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{i}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,\cdot)-{\pi}_{V_{i}}\right\|_{\rm TV}\leq\left\|\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{y\in V}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,\cdot)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_{V_{i}}\right\|_{\rm TV}+\Phi(\varepsilon),\end{split}

where

(7.22)

\begin{split}\Phi(\varepsilon)&\coloneqq\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{n}\Big(\sum_{y\in V_{j}^{\complement}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,V_{j})+\sum_{y\in V_{j}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,V_{j}^{\complement})\Big)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{j}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,V_{j}^{\complement}).\end{split}

Invoking Proposition
9
, we get

\left|\Phi(\varepsilon)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{Q}^{h_{\varepsilon}}(j,[m]\setminus\{j\})\right|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Using that, for
t_{\textup{ent}}=\alpha^{-1}/C
, and for each
j\leq m
,

(7.23)

\begin{split}{Q}^{h_{\varepsilon}}\left(j,[m]\setminus\{j\}\right)&=\frac{m-1}{m}\left(1-\left(1-\tfrac{m}{m-1}\alpha\right)^{h_{\varepsilon}}\right)\\
&=\frac{m-1}{m}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-\frac{m}{m-1}\frac{2\varepsilon}{C}}(1+o(1))\right)\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{C}(1+o(1)),\end{split}

As a consequence, w.h.p.
\Phi(\varepsilon)\leq\frac{3\varepsilon}{C}
.
Recalling that
\pi=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{V_{j}}(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))
, thanks to Lemma
33
, from Eq. (
7.21
) we conclude that w.h.p. it holds

(7.24)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left\|\sum_{y\in V_{j}}\frac{1}{n}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,\cdot)-{\pi}_{V_{j}}\right\|_{\rm TV}\leq\left\|\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{y\in V}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}+\frac{3\varepsilon}{C},\end{split}

which is at most
\frac{3\varepsilon}{C}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, thanks to Eq. (
6.14
). Then, w.h.p., each term of the sum is bounded by
\frac{4m\varepsilon}{C}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
34
.

We consider a time
t=\beta t_{\textup{ent}}
,
where
\varepsilon>0
is chosen such that
\beta>1+\varepsilon
. For
x\in V
,
it holds

(7.25)

\begin{split}\left\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}&\leq\left\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}{Q}^{t}(c(x),j)\pi_{V_{j}}\right\|_{\rm TV}\!\!\!\!+\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{m}{Q}^{t}(c(x),j){\pi_{V_{j}}}-\pi\right\|_{\rm TV}.\end{split}

Being
\pi=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi(V_{j}){\pi_{V_{j}}}
, taking the maximum over
x\in V
, the second summand provides

(7.26)

\begin{split}\max_{x\in V}&\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{m}{Q}^{t}(c(x),j){\pi_{V_{j}}}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi(V_{j}){\pi_{V_{j}}}\right\|_{\rm TV}=\max_{x\in V}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|{Q}^{t}(c(x),j)-\pi(V_{j})\right|\\
&\leq\max_{x\in V}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|{Q}^{t}(c(x),j)-\frac{1}{m}\right|+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)=\frac{m-1}{m}\left(1-\frac{m}{m-1}\alpha\right)^{t}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),\end{split}

where we have employed Lemma
33
and the triangle inequality in the second line. This will provide the leading term.
We now consider the first summand.
By Proposition
9
, it holds

(7.27)

\max_{x\in V}\max_{j\leq m}\left|P^{(\beta-1-\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(x,V_{j})-{Q}^{(\beta-1-\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(c(x),j)\right|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Then

(7.28)

\begin{split}&\max_{x\in V}\left\|P^{\beta t_{\textup{ent}}}(x,\cdot)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}{Q}^{\beta t_{\textup{ent}}}(c(x),j)\pi_{V_{j}}\right\|_{TV}\\
&\leq\max_{x\in V}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{z\in V_{i}}P^{(\beta-1-\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(x,z)\left[P^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(z,\cdot)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}{Q}^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(i,j)\pi_{V_{j}}\right]\right\|_{\rm TV}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\\
&\leq\max_{i\leq m}\max_{z\in V_{i}}\left\|P^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(z,\cdot)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}{Q}^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(i,j)\pi_{V_{j}}\right\|_{\rm TV}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\\
&\leq\max_{i\leq m}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{{Q}^{{s_{\varepsilon}}+1}(i,k)}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{k}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,\cdot)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}{Q}^{(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}}(i,j)\pi_{V_{j}}\right\|_{\rm TV}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\end{split}

where the third inequality follows from Eq. (
6.12
) (recall that, according to Definition
30
,
(1+\varepsilon)t_{\textup{ent}}={s_{\varepsilon}}+{h_{\varepsilon}}+1
) Using the semigroup property of
{Q}(\cdot,\cdot)
,
we can bound the last expression in Eq. (
7.28
) by

(7.29)

\begin{split}&\max_{i\leq m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}{Q}^{{s_{\varepsilon}}+1}(i,k)\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{k}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,\cdot)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}{Q}^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(k,j)\pi_{V_{j}}\right\|_{\rm TV}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\\
&\leq\max_{k\leq m}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in V_{k}}P^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(y,\cdot)-\pi_{V_{k}}\right\|_{\rm TV}+\left\|\pi_{V_{k}}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}{Q}^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(k,j)\pi_{V_{j}}\right\|_{\rm TV}\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),\end{split}

Now w.h.p. we can bound the first summand in the last display with
\frac{4m\varepsilon}{C}
by Lemma
35
. The second summand has the same magnitude: reasoning in the spirit of Eqs. (
7.26
) and (
7.23
), it holds

(7.30)

\begin{split}\max_{k\leq m}\left\|{\pi_{V_{k}}}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}{Q}^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(k,j){\pi_{V_{j}}}\right\|_{\rm TV}&=\max_{k\leq m}\frac{1}{2}\left|1-{Q}^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(k,k)\right|+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\neq k}{Q}^{{h_{\varepsilon}}}(k,j)\\
&=\max_{k\leq m}\frac{m-1}{m}\left(1-\left(1-\tfrac{m}{m-1}\alpha\right)^{h_{\varepsilon}}\right)\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{C}(1+o(1)).\qed\end{split}
