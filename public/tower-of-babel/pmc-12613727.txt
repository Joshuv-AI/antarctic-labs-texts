The systemic wrinkled skin phenotype involves aberrant expression and variation of genes related to the oxidative stress and extracellular matrix in Xiang pigs
Authors: Fengbin Hu, Tingting Xu, Shihui Huang, Xiaoli Liu, Lijuan Wu, Honghui Zhang, Xi Niu, Jiafu Wang, Xueqin Ran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA phenotype characterized by age-dependent hairless, thicker and systemic wrinkled skin has been discovered from Chinese native Xiang pig herd, which is similar to the wrinkle skin phenotype and hereditary periodic fever syndrome observed in Shar-Pei dog. Our aim was to elucidate the functional mechanisms associated with these systemic wrinkle phenotype.ResultsGene expression changes in wrinkled skin were first explored via RNA-seq technology. There were 699 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in wrinkled skin, with 272 downregulated genes and 427 upregulated genes. Most of the upregulated genes were significantly centered at pathways that generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as carbon and lipid metabolism, the citrate cycle and the oxidation-reduction process, leading to excessive ROS production and/or DNA damage in both the epidermal and dermal compartments of the skin. The downregulated genes were involved mainly in extracellular matrix (ECM) signaling. The most decreased genes were focused on several ECM components, including COL1A1, COL21A1, COL6A3, and ELN, likely related with ECM disorders. Moreover, 831 SVs and 26 CNVs were found in the gene regions of 335 DEGs according to the genomic resequencing data from the same wrinkled skin and control samples. These SVs and CNVs contributed to the deletion, insertion, duplication and inversion in exons, introns and regulatory regions of the DEGs. We then used F2 crossbreds by using Large White pig and the wrinkled Xiang pig as parents to verify the skin features and gene expression changes. Approximately 26% of F2 individuals exhibited a wrinkled skin phenotype, whereas the skin on F1 hybrids remained unchanged, suggesting autosomal recessive inheritance. The disorganized and thinner collagen bundles in the dermis of wrinkled skin were very similar to those of the parents, and many genes were shared between F2 pigs and the parents, including 137 DEGs corresponding to wrinkle scores, 23 oxidative stress-related genes, nine aging-related genes, and four ECM genes.ConclusionsThe systemic wrinkled skin phenotype of pigs involves aberrant gene expression and genetic variations related to oxidative stress and ECM components.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12236-y.Keywords: Skin, Oxidative stress, Extracellular matrix, Wrinkles, Pig

Background

The skin represents the largest organ covering the outer layer of the animal body, offering a protection barrier against environmental damage, and losses of body fluid and temperature [1, 2]. Skin fibroblasts are capable of synthesizing and depositing all of the extracellular matrix (ECM) components into the extracellular space [3], which contains collagens, elastic fibers and ground substances (e.g., glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), proteoglycans (PGs), and glycoproteins). Fibroblasts maintain the ECM homeostasis by modulating of degrading enzymes and crosslinking enzymes (such as metalloproteinases and lysyl oxidase) and in response to extrinsic and intrinsic stimuli [2, 3]. The health and architecture of skin are strongly affected by its regular organization and high contents of collagen and elastic fibers. Collagens are complex supermacromolecules that endow the dermis with unique tensile strength. Collagen type I and type III, accounting for 70% and 10% of the skin dry weight, respectively, are the most enriched protein in human skin [2] and are involved in a series of signaling pathways via the interplay between cell-surface receptors [3]. Elastic fibers constitute approximately 3% of the dermal dry weight in humans [3], while plenty of ground substances fill in the uncellular space of the skin.

Skin aging and wrinkle formation are ineluctable and multifactorial due to several chronological and environmental factors, which lead to phenotypic alterations in the skin together with structural/functional transformation in both the epidermal and dermal layers [4–6]. Chronological skin aging is highly related to the gene regulation, accompanied by a visible phenotype of fine wrinkles and moderate skin pigmentation in humans [1]. Senescent cells are persistently produced in the aged skin, which result in a decline in tissue function, pathological changes and skin diseases. Senescent fibroblasts release a mixture of molecules known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), including matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and miRNAs. Senescent fibroblasts are also characterized by DNA damage, cell cycle arrest, inhibiting capacity to protein quality impairment, proliferation, mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and inflammation [1]. In those disorders, ROS activate transcription factors, such as NF-κB and activating protein-1 (AP-1), which stimulate the expressions of MMPs, leading to the degradation of most ECM components, especially collagen, and remodeling of collagen architecture and aggravating the formation of cutaneous wrinkles [7]. It is determined that the collagen density decreases from 81% in young reticular dermis to 58% in aged skins [8]. Moreover, senescent fibroblasts in the dermis accompany with the disorganized ECM caused by the accumulation of collagen and elastotic fragments due to the incomplete degradation of those fibers [1, 9]. It is reported that disruptions of those fibrous net contribute to fine wrinkles, and deposits of disorganized elastin cause deep and permanent wrinkles [7, 10]. In addition, the activation of NF-κB by ROS also stimulates proinflammatory cytokines to induce inflammatory responses in the skin [11–13].

Pigs are important economic animals that provide protein and energy for human beings and constitute a large share of meat consumption worldwide. Owing to the anatomical and physiological similarities of skin architecture to that of humans, a well-established model of pig is used for preclinical researches of skin aging and skin grafts [14–16]. One miniature pig, Bama pig in China, has been established as burn models to evaluate pathophysiology and to screen burn drugs [17, 18].

Notably, unlike human wrinkles in advanced age, most endemic pig breeds in China harbor deep forehead wrinkles in the shape of a rhombus starting from birth, which is thought to be a marker for pig breed defining. Furthermore, small herds of Xiang pigs appear systemic wrinkles on the body-wide as early as two months of age in addition to those wrinkles on the forehead. Numerous genomic variations from the wrinkled skin of these pigs have been distinguished on the basis of genome sequencing and screening technologies [19]. Similarly, a genetic mutation is identified to cause the wrinkled skin phenotype and hereditary periodic fever syndrome in Shar-Pei dogs [20]. It is then postulated that the wrinkled skin phenotype in Xiang pigs might result from the changes of gene expression important to cutaneous architecture and fibroblasts senescence. In the present research, the distinct expression profiles of the wrinkled skin were determined via RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) technology, and correlation of genomic variations with these gene expression changes were further explored via construction of genetic resource pedigrees. As the earliest skin wrinkles appear in the childhood stage, we hypothesized that the wrinkled skin phenotype might be associated with ECM component disorder, oxidative stress stimuli or cutaneous cellular aging.

Results

Gene expression profiles in wrinkled skin

To compare the expression profiles of genes, transcriptomes of twelve skin samples were sequenced from the cases with wrinkled skin and controls with the normal skin of Xiang pigs via RNA-seq technology. The skin cDNA libraries generated a total of 903 million pairs of raw reads and 893 million pairs of clean reads after quality control and filtering, and 83.14% of the unique clean reads from every library were mapped to the genomic sequence of Sscrofa11.1 (Supplementary Table S1), suggesting high quality sequencing data for gene expression analysis.

After filtering genes with CMP values less than 1 and from two samples, a total of 13,399 genes were identified (Supplementary Table S2). Of those, gene numbers with CPM values less than 100 were counted to be 11,813 and 11,751 in the control and case groups (Fig. 1A), accounting for 88.16% and 87.70%, respectively. While the amounts of those with CPM greater than 1000 were only 47 and 53 in the control and case groups with percentages of 0.35% and 0.40%, respectively. Of note, eleven collagen genes were expressed at a high level with a CPM greater than 1000 (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table S3), including nine genes (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL12A1, COL3A1, COL5A1, COL5A2, COL6A1, COL6A2, and COL6A3) in both groups together with COL14A1 in control, and COL4A1 in the case group. Moreover, keratin genes (KRT5 and KRT71) and ECM components such as FN1 (fibronectin 1) and HSPG2 (heparan sulfate proteoglycan 2) also appeared in the cohort of top 30. It indicated that these genes might provide a basic foundation for the skin architecture.

Differentially expressed genes in the wrinkled skin

A substantial number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected after comparing the expressed genes between the wrinkled and normal skin of Xiang pigs. A total of 699 genes were recognized as DEGs shared by the three programs, of which 992, 2169, and 907 genes were detected by DESeq2, edgeR and NOISeq programs, respectively (Fig. 1C). These DEGs harbored 272 downregulated and 427 upregulated genes, as showed in the volcano plot of the wrinkled versus normal skin comparisons (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Table S4), such as FMO3 (log2FC = 5.65, padj = 3.69E-5, FDR = 6.87E-07), and KRT79 (log2FC = −8.19, padj = 6.54E-7, FDR = 2.48E-10). The broad differences between two groups were further analyzed via principal component analysis (PCA). A clear clustering between the case and control groups were contribute to PC1 (Fig. 1D), which interpreted a percentage of 68% of the total differences in the datasets. Similarly, the heatmap presented in Fig. 1F showed the hierarchical clustering based on gene expression, in which all wrinkled samples clustered together.

Enrichment analysis of DEGs

To explain the biological effects of those genes with diverse expression patterns, 699 DEGs were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses, and 70 signaling pathways and 191 GO terms were gathered via cutoff of the corrected P value ≤ 0.05 (Supplementary Table S5). The primary pathways and GO terms were metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation pathways, and 141 oxidative stress-related genes were enriched (Supplementary Table S6), including biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, glycerophospholipid and butanoate metabolism, glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, PPAR signaling pathway, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), peroxisome, process of oxidation-reduction, oxidative stress response, etc. In addition, other pathways and GO terms significantly clustered in the ECM, such as extracellular matrix organization, hyaluronic acid binding, cell-matrix adhesion, collagen-activated tyrosine kinase receptor signaling pathway, laminin binding, mitochondrial matrix, etc. Then those 39 genes engaged in ECM pathway and GO terms were listed (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S7), which consisted of 15 downregulated and 24 upregulated genes, respectively, such as COL1A1, with a log2FoldChange of −2.53.

Screening of aging-related genes from DEGs

As both variations in metabolism in fibroblast and ECM components from fibroblast are largely affected the cellular aging [21], the DEGs were further overlapped with skin aging genes deposited in the NCBI Gene database [22]. Total of 100 DEGs were identified as aging-related candidate genes, including 79 upregulated and 21 downregulated genes (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table S8). Pathway analysis revealed that these 100 aging-related DEGs were significantly gathered in 57 signaling pathways, such as AMPK, PPAR, FoxO, adipocytokine, glycerolipid metabolism, apelin and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways, etc. And 112 GO terms were focused on extracellular matrix, the response to oxidative stress, various metabolic processes, hyaluronic acid binding, as well as oxidoreductase activity, etc. (Supplementary Table S8). After association analysis by String, the center of the protein network was pointed to PPARG with a node degree of 23, indicating that its interplay with 23 proteins contributes jointly to a shared common function (Fig. 2C). And eight factors were recognized as hub genes under the threshold of a node degree greater than 10, such as ADIPOQ, APOE, CD44, COL1A1, FABP4, FGF2, LEP, and PPARG (Fig. 2D).

The genomic variations involved in the differentially expression of genes

To reveal the influences of genomic variations on the gene expression in wrinkled skin, we identified whole-genome structural variation (SV) and copy number variation (CNV) via our previous genomic resequencing data containing the same wrinkled and control samples of Xiang pigs [19, 23], and focused on the DEGs-related SVs and CNVs (Supplementary Tables S9, S10). A total of 831 SVs and 26 CNVs were detected around or within 335 DEGs, including 726 deletions (DELs), 54 tandem duplications (DUPs), 43 insertions (INSs), 8 inversions (INVs), 11 gains and 15 losses. The effects of these SVs and CNVs on host genes were further investigated via the Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) online. A 312 bp deletion in TMPRSS4 resulted in the loss of the stop coding sequence. These nine SVs (4 DELs, 3 INVs, and 2 DUPs) led to both coding sequence and intron variants in eight genes, such as ABCA12, ABLIM1 and SPART. There were 647 deletions in introns that were predicted to cause intron truncation. A total of 43 duplications and 4 inversions led to intron variants; 40 insertions led to the intron elongation; eight deletions led to the 3`-UTR truncation variants in the genes KCNK1, LIMK2, PFKFB1, RCN1, EMCN, and GNAI1, CRLF3, DENND11; forty-two SVs (37 DELs, 4 DUPs, 1 INS) led to 5`-UTR truncation or elongation variants in 40 genes such as COL21A1, ACACA and APOLD1; and 37 SVs (29 DELs, 5 DUPs, 2 INSs, 1 INV) led to downstream variants of 32 genes, such as ACADL, ADCY5 and DHCR24. In addition, ten gains of CNVs were featured as integral gene duplications, which were predicted to increase the dosage of protein-coding genes, including PLEKHG1, AKAP12, FHOD3, PDHB, CYP4F22, CLMP, SPRP, MAPK9, CASP14, TMEM132A, DMTN, ELMOD3, RETSAT and ATOH8. Total of seven CNVs might result in copy losses of seven entire genes, including TINCR, INAVA, OBSL1, GPX4, SLC2A4, DMTN, and MYL11. A CNV overlapping two exons of CLSTN2 yielded changes of exons 1 and 2 of the amplicons. Furthermore, both CNV losses involving the MFNG and VCAN genes might result in truncated variants of the 5`-UTR and coding region. A duplication overlapping 11 exons of ABCA12 was assumed CNVs of exons 24–33 amplicons. Interestingly, gene expression in wrinkled skin was largely affected by genetic variation of all SV types and CNVs except for the 5`-UTR compared with that in normal controls (Fig. 2E).

Construction and gene expression patterns in genetic pedigrees

Construction and histological examination of genetic pedigrees

To verify the association between the changes in gene expression and the phenotype of wrinkles, we developed genetic resource pedigrees by taking Large White pigs and Xiang pigs with wrinkled skin as parents. These two breeds were chosen to designate two extreme parents for the performance of Large White pigs without obvious wrinkles on the body versus wrinkled-skin Xiang pigs with many wrinkles on the body and forehead. The F1 hybrids presented as wrinkle-free and white coat or white coat with a few black spots. However, the wrinkle performances of F2 individuals were highly diverse, indicating the occurrence of segregation in the wrinkle phenotype (Fig. 3). Some of them appeared wrinkles on the forehead and body-wide with a black coat (similar to the F0 parent of the Xiang pig with wrinkled skin), whereas other F2 pigs were wrinkle-free on the body-side with a white coat similar to their parent Large White pig. Interestingly, we obtained some pigs possessing mixed performances of two parents, which presented white coats but carrying many wrinkles on the forehead and body-wide. The percentage of pigs with wrinkles on the body-side was counted as to be approximately 26%, while nearly all of the F2 pigs had wrinkles on the forehead. The systemic wrinkles on the body showed autosomal recessive inheritance and were not linked with the wrinkle phenotype on the forehead. The wrinkle scores on the F2 body were further evaluated via an in-house script, which ranged from 0.097 to 0.232 (Supplementary Table S11).

On the other hand, the histological characteristics of the wrinkled skin in F2 pigs were the same as those of the parent of Xiang pig with wrinkled skin [19] (Fig. 4). The thickness of the wrinkled skin in F2 with systemic wrinkles was significantly greater (8.17 ± 1.22 mm) than that in F2 without wrinkles on the body (Fig. 3M). After observation via H&E staining (Fig. 4), the epidermal layer was much undulating with deep ridges viewed from the external surface of the wrinkled skin from F2 and contained fewer nucleated keratinocytes especially in the granular and spinous layers. By performing Masson’s trichrome staining which dyes collagen as blue, it was noticed that the collagenous bundles in the reticular dermis of the wrinkled skin of F2 pigs were thin and tangled with each other in an irregular shape, whereas the bundle orientation in F2 without wrinkles on the body was more parallel-oriented with regular collagen deposition (Fig. 5H and J). Taken together, these data suggested a qualitative impairment in wrinkled skin, as evidenced by decreased collagen deposition in both F2 pigs and the natural Xiang pigs with systemic wrinkles on the body.

Gene expression patterns in wrinkled skin from F2 pedigrees

The gene expression profiles of the skin of 19 F2 progeny were detected leading to 12,553 protein-coding genes. The expression levels in CPM values were used for correlation analysis with skin wrinkle scores, and 764 genes were significantly correlated with the wrinkled skin phenotype, with 402 positively related genes (ρ = 0.325 ~ 0.667) and 362 negatively related genes (ρ = −0.325 ~ −0.679) under a fair to moderate relationship based on the previous threshold (Supplementary Table S12) [24]. In particular, 137 of them were DEGs detected from the case and control groups, including nine aging-related genes (AKAP12, DMD, FOXO6, IL15, PENK, PGR, SFRP1, SLC1A3, and SNCG), four ECM genes, such as TNN (tenascin N, binding to integrin), SFRP1 (secreted frizzled related protein 1, which functions as a modulator of Wnt signaling), GPC6 (glypican 6, which acts as a cell surface coreceptor for extracellular matrix proteins), and ECM2 (extracellular matrix protein 2), and 23 oxidation stress-related genes, such as ALDH1A2 (aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member A2), FA2H (fatty acid 2-hydroxylase), FMO3 (flavin containing dimethylaniline monoxygenase 3) and PCYOX1 (prenylcysteine oxidase 1).

Validation of DEGs in wrinkled skin by quantitative RT‒PCR

The quantitative RT-PCR was applied to compare the expression levels of 21 DEGs, which included six up- and 15 downregulated genes in wrinkled skin samples versus controls. Among those selected DEGs, twenty were ECM-coding genes, and the other gene (PPARG) was included in both aging-related and oxidation stress-related genes. In Fig. 5, the expression profiles of 21 DEGs were presented, including the most abundant genes, COL1A1, COL4A1 and COL6A3, as well as the less frequent genes, CCN4 and TNN. And the relative expression levels determined via RT‒qPCR were consistent with their CPM values from the sequencing data obtained by RNA-seq, with a moderate positive correlation (Pearson’s correlation coefficient γ = 0.4, P < 0.01).

Discussion

The Xiang pig is a Chinese indigenous breed characterized by a small stature and excellent meat quality [25]. We previously reported the cases of systemic wrinkled skin in Xiang pigs, which is similar to other researches on the wrinkled skin phenotype in Shar-Pei dogs [20], and HGPS syndrome in humans [26], and found that many genomic structural variants were probably related to the systemic skin wrinkles [19]. To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms involved in genetic wrinkled skin in Xiang pigs, in the present study, we carried out a comparative analysis of the expression profiles via the RNA-seq method. We detected 13 399 genes that were expressed in skin (Supplementary Table S2). Most genes ranged from 1 to 100 CPM and accounted for 88.16% and 87.70% of total genes in case and control groups, respectively. The percentage of genes with a CPM higher than 1000 was only about 0.35% in control and 0.40% in wrinkled skin. Of those, many collagen genes, such as COL1A1, COL1A2, COL12A1, COL14A1, COL3A1, COL4A1, COL5A1, and COL6A1, were the most highly expressed genes with CPMs greater than 1000 (Supplementary Table S3). The high expression pattern of collagen genes in Xiang pig is much similar with Chenghua pigs [27].

Differential analysis revealed that 699 DEGs were identified from wrinkled skins compared with controls. Previous studies have indicated that skin aging is the result of multiple factors and pathways working together, including oxidative stress, degradation of the ECM, and a proinflammatory SASP [28]. The DEGs were enriched in response to oxidative stress, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, etc. It was inferred that these DEGs might play crucial roles in the process of skin wrinkles formation in Xiang pigs through oxidative stress and ECM degradation.

Oxidative stress in the skin provides a key role in promoting chronological aging [21]. The ROS can be produced intracellularly in various cell compartments as byproducts of aerobic reactions during various cellular processes, such as metabolism, apoptosis, and proliferation [21, 29]. However, excessive production of ROS may disrupt the redox balance in cell, suppressing cellular antioxidant capacity and leading to a response to oxidative stress [21]. In our study, most upregulated genes in wrinkled skin were enriched in those cellular processes that generate ROS, such as carbon and lipid metabolism, citrate cycle, and reaction of oxidation‒reduction (Supplementary Table S6). The overexpression of these genes may lead to ROS generation and/or DNA fracture in the cutaneous epiderms and derma. For example, the thioredoxin‒interacting protein, edcoded by TXNIP, with an increase of 2.42‒folds in wrinkled skin, interplays with thioredoxin to change redox reactions, excessive production of ROS [30].

It is clear that decreases in the collagen content, and strength will result in skin wrinkles in humans [31]. Collagen loss is caused by an increase in ROS, inflammation, and the activity of proteolytic enzymes in the extracellular matrix of skin [7]. Furthermore, the upregulation of MMPs can depress the amount of collagenous and elastic fibers, which are also involved in the augmentation of skin wrinkles [7]. In this study, seven collagen and an elastin gene were differentially expressed. Compared with those in controls, the expression levels of COL1A1, COL21A1, COL6A3, and ELN genes in wrinkled skin decreased by 82.74%, 76.11%, 57.03% and 73.61%, respectively (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table S4). Although the expression levels of COL4A1, COL4A2, COL5A3, and COL6A6 increased in wrinkled skin, the total expression levels of these seven collagen genes were only 21.2% of those in control samples. Collagen type I is the most broadly distributed collagen and is the primary ingredient in the skin [2]. It serves as a tissue support, maintaining the tissue architecture and ensuring its basal functions [32]. This result revealed that the loss of those collagens, especially COL1A1 and elastin, might be one of the triggering factors for the incidence of systemic wrinkles on Xiang pigs.

The ECM is a stereoscopic network of large extracellular molecules including GAGs, PGs, and glycoproteins [3]. Glycoproteins such as fibronectin, laminins or tenascins, as well as glycosaminoglycans, contribute to filling the ECM. The ECM provide mechanical and biochemical supports to mammalian cells, regulate cellular differentiation, proliferation and migration, and participate in intercellular communication [33], which is pivotal in skin development [34]. Because individual ECM component typically interact with other ECM macromolecules, abnormal gene expression in ECMs is often associated with grievous developmental dysplasia [34]. In the present study, we found that 39 DEGs, including 15 downregulated and 23 upregulated genes, were significantly enriched in the ECM (Supplementary Table S7). The upregulated genes were involved mainly in the regulation of cell adhesion, response to stimuli and developmental procedures. The high expression of these genes in those cell processes might result in aberrant ROS generation and/or DNA fracture in the skin of Xiang pigs. On another hand, many glycoprotein genes, including LAMA3, MEGF9, LTBP1, LTBP2, TNN, FREM1, and FGFR2 were significantly downregulated in the wrinkled skins of Xiang pigs. LAMA3, MEGF9, LTBP1, LTBP2, and TNN are factors that harbor EGF-like domains and are involved in the development and injury response of tissues. In addition, proteins containing EGF-like domains also change the function of connective tissues and extracellular matrix formation [35]. Mutations in laminin chains can lead to skin diseases, such as junctional epidermolysis bullosa [36]. And then, FREM1 is involved in the interactions between the epithelium and mesenchyme [37]. Variations in FREM1 gene are related to several disorders, such as subepidermal blistering [38]. Fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) are expressed in many tissues and are involved in both organogenesis and homeostasis. The disappear of FGFs receptors, such as FGFR2, in keratinocytes leads to keratinocyte hyperproliferation [39]. In addition, we found that four ECM genes, ACAN, VCAN, CCN4, and WNT5A, were also significantly downregulated in the wrinkled skin of Xiang pigs. Proteoglycans (PGs), such as aggrecan (ACAN) and versican (VCAN), have a large core protein with several glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chains covalently attached, and contribute to the formation of the ECM [40, 41]. The macromolecule PG/GAG complexes are necessary to regulate the hydration process of skin [42]. It is known that a genetic mutation causing Familial Shar-Pei fever with a wrinkled skin appearance is associated with excessive hyaluronan deposit in dog skin [20]. In the present study, low expression of ACAN and VCAN in wrinkled skin might obstruct the formation of a complete PGs aggrecan/versican-hyaluronan matrix, likely leading to the accumulation of hyaluronan, which was possibly involved in the pathogenesis of wrinkles and inflammation in the skin of Xiang pigs. Both CCN4 and WNT5A are components of the Wnt signaling pathway. The CCN4 physically binds with numerous receptors and proteins to regulate cell proliferation, including ECM proteins, such as fibronectin [43]; growth factors, such as FGFs [44]; and proteoglycans [45]. Recent data have shown that loss of CCN4 increases cell proliferation [46]. As such, deficiency of those growth factors, CCN4 and WNT5A promotes the epidermal and/or dermal cell proliferation, which might associate with thickening of the wrinkled skin in Xiang pigs [19].

To screen genes related to the wrinkled skin phenotype, we mapped the DEGs with the NCBI gene database taking aging as keyword. In total, 100 DEGs were identified as ageing-related DEGs associated with the wrinkled phenotype (Supplementary Table S8). These aging-related DEGs were enriched mainly in the metabolic, PPAR, extracellular matrix, as well as peroxidase activity (Supplementary Table S8). These crucial DEGs may provide key information for understanding the mechanisms involved in the skin wrinkles of Xiang pigs. After PPI network analysis, eight core genes, were regarded as hub genes potentially influencing the wrinkled skin phenotype, including COL1A1 and PPARG etc. (Fig. 2D). They can interact with most aging-related genes. The PPARγ is initiated by numerous ligands, containing the oxidized fatty acids [47, 48]. Of note, PPARγ can accelerate the differentiation of terminal epiderms [48]. It is known that PPARγ is expressed in human dermal fibroblasts, and PPARγ agonists (troglitazone) can inhibit the production of ECM proteins, such as collagen type I and fibronectin [49]. And another PPARγ agonist, Rosiglitazone, is confirmed to stimulate the expression of MMP-3 and MMP-13 in chondrocytes [50]. In the wrinkled skin of Xiang pigs, the PPARG gene was stimulated (log2FC = 2.75), and MMP-28 was increased significantly (log2FC = 1.17). It is known that MMP-28 is highly expressed by keratinocytes in human skin [51], and overexpression of MMP-28 reduced drastically the basolateral ECM content in epithelial cells and several ECM proteins are disappeared, including collagen type IV, laminin and fibronectin [52]. It is speculated that PPARG might have a pivotal role in the process of wrinkles formation in Xiang pigs via indirectly manner.

We used Xiang pigs with a wrinkled skin phenotype and Large White pigs as parents to construct genetic pedigrees. The skins of F1 hybrids were white in color and wrinkle-free, whereas approximately 26% of F2 individuals exhibited wrinkled skin phenotype, suggesting a manner of autosomal recessive inheritance. And then, a phenotype value of the wrinkled skin was utilized to associate with the generic CPM values of 12 553 proteins in the skins from 19 F2 pigs. There was a total of 764 genes significantly correlated with the wrinkled skin phenotype (Supplementary Table S12). It was worth noting that 137 of them were DEGs between control and wrinkled skin of Xiang pigs, including 23 oxidation stress related genes, nine aging-related genes, four ECM genes (Supplementary Table S12). The results from hybrid pedigrees confirmed the partial abnormal expressed genes in the wrinkled skin of Xiang pigs.

Genetic variation is one of the most notable chronological factors leading to cutaneous aging. The genetic basis of age-related diseases has been identified, such as Hutchinson‒Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS). These syndromes are mostly inherited in an autosomal recessive manner and are characterized by defects in DNA replication and transcription [53]. The cause of classic progeria (such as HGPS) is due to the heterozygous pathogenic mutation of c.1824 C >T in the LMNA gene, which creates a cryptic splice site [54]. Additionally, a large number of genes are involved in the aging progress [55–57]. For example, a 16.1 kb duplication correlates with HAS2 over-expression and Familial Shar-Pei Fever in dogs [20]. In our study, we detected 831 SVs and 26 CNVs involved in 335 DEGs via genomic SV and CNV detection from wrinkled and control samples of Xiang pigs. These genes with SVs and CNVs were gathered dominantly in the cell components, pathways involving the extracellular matrix, metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation. Most SVs were located on the introns of the genes leading to intron truncation or elongation. A few of these genes were located in the 3`-UTRs and 5`-UTRs regions of the genes. Majority of the SV breakpoints overlapped with transposable elements (TEs), including SINEs, LINEs, LTRs, etc. (Supplementary Table S9). The presence or absence of TEs may result in mutations in gene regulatory regions and introns, which may provide or eliminate regulatory sequences for adjacent genes at new integration sites, potentially affecting gene expression regulation [58]. In addition, TEs can bind to RNA polymerase II and significantly inhibit promoter activity, thereby blocking the transcription of certain genes and leading to exon jumping, frameshift, premature termination of downstream codons, and possibly resulting in truncated protein products [59, 60]. For example, we found that 21 SVs lost SINEs or LINEs in regulatory regions or introns of 14 genes in wrinkled skin, such as a 316 bp deletion in intron 8 of CAT, and 304 bp deletions in upstream regulatory regions for HSD17B4. Moreover, we investigated the variants of genes involved in the extracellular matrix, and identified 139 SVs and 4 CNVs among 47 DEGs, such as a 1420 bp duplication in intron 3 of COL21A1. Significantly, the truncated variants of coding region of VCAN caused by CNV-VCAN might undoubtedly contribute to the downregulation of those genes in wrinkled skin [23, 61]. Many genes were affected by two or more SVs and/or CNVs (Supplementary Table S9, S10). These SVs and CNVs may contribute to changes in gene expression prompted by genotypes of variations positively or negatively [60–62].

Conclusion

The differentially expressed genes played crucial roles in the systemic wrinkle phenotype of Xiang pigs. The compared transcriptome dataset contributed to the identification of 699 DEGs, and nearly half of the DEGs harbored variation and transposable elements in gene regions. On the basis of the constructed genetic pedigrees, we determined 137 DEGs related to wrinkles containing 23 oxidative stress-related genes, nine aging genes and four ECM genes, improving our understanding of the genetic basis for the formation of systemic wrinkles in pigs. These findings may aid in the development of early clearance for individuals with genetic defects and new therapies for skin health and wrinkle control in pigs.

Methods

Animal samples

Total of twelve female skin samples were collected from the Dachangdi pig breeding farm, Congjiang, Guizhou, China, including six normal Xiang pigs (XP) and six Xiang pigs with wrinkled shin (WXP) at twelve months and 70 ± 5 kg body weight, which were selected randomly with the similar skin performance. After being sacrificed via the standard commercial slaughter method, a piece of skin (approximately 2 cm × 3 cm) on the back of the last rib was collected immediately and placed into liquid nitrogen to extract total RNA.

The genetic pedigrees were constructed using purebred male Large White pigs (RRID: NSRRC_0004) and female Xiang pigs with a wrinkled skin phenotype as parents on the basis of previous reports [63, 64]. Three boars in the parent generation (F0) were Large White pigs, and six sows in the F0 generation were Xiang pigs with wrinkled skin. Total of 54 F2 progeny from three F1 boars and ten F1 females were produced with 26% of which presented wrinkles on the body side. Skin samples from nineteen F2 pigs were collected as previously described for RNA-sequencing detection. At the age of 12 months and with a body weight of 115 ± 11 kg, the pigs living at the commercial farm were taken to a slaughter house where they were electrically stunned, and their skins were sampled before the carcasses were processed following industry standards.

Histological assay

The skin samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution and immersed in paraffin. Each sample was cut into consecutive sections with a thickness of 5 μm via a microtome and then stained with either hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or Masson’s trichrome according to previously described protocols [65].

Wrinkle score evaluation

A digital image of a pig was captured via a Canon EOS70D camera. The pixel points of those images were evaluated using an in-house python (RRID: SCR_024202) script based on the OpenCV-bxl library (RRID: SCR_015526) as previously described [66]. In brief, the colored image was transformed into a gray-level image after denoising, and the external contours and internal textures of the wrinkles were then extracted. The wrinkle score was calculated as the wrinkle pixel value divided by its external contour pixels (Supplementary Figure S2).

mRNA library construction and RNA-seq

The skin tissue was homogenized in liquid nitrogen into powder, and total RNA was extracted via TRNzol reagent (Invitrogen) with an RNA integrity number (RIN) from 8.0 to 8.7. The construction of cDNA libraries and procedure of RNA sequencing were performed based on previous report [67] via Illumina HiSeq X10 Platform (BGI, China) to produce paired-end reads of 150 bp on the basis of the manufacturer’s standard protocols.

Differential expression gene analysis

After quality detection via FastQC (v11.8, RRID: SCR_014583) [68], the raw data were filtered by Trimmomatic (v0.39, RRID: SCR_011848) [69]to remove the low-quality reads and obtain the clean reads. The clean reads were then aligned to the reference genomic sequence of Ssrofa 11.1 [70] via STAR software (RRID: SCR_004463) [71]. The gene amounts were calculated by the Subread featurecounts (v2.0.0, RRID: SCR_012919) [72], and each gene expression level was standardized by counts per million (CPM) values [62]. Of those, genes in CPM value less than 1 and from less than two samples were filtered out. A principal component analysis (PCA) was performed by Sangerbox [73], and the differential expression profiles were investigated between groups via the DESeq2 (v1.26, RRID: SCR_000154) [74], edgeR (v3.28, RRID: SCR_012802) [75] and NOISeq (v2.30, RRID: SCR_003002) [76] programs. The thresholds of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were taken as: genes with padj ≤ 0.01, false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.01, probability (Prob) ≥ 0.95, and log2FoldChange log2FC ≥ 1.00 or ≤ −1.00.

Functional enrichment of DEGs and correlations with wrinkle scores

Both KEGG (RRID: SCR_012773) and GO (RRID: SCR_002811) analyses were performed via Webgestalt (RRID: SCR_006786) [77] and KOBAS v3.0 (RRID: SCR_006350), respectively [78]. Furthermore, the correlation of the CPM values of the DEGs with the wrinkle scores of F2 individuals was evaluated using SPSS v27 (RRID: SCR_002865) via Spearman’s rank correlation test.

Gene expression validation by RT‒qPCR

These 21 genes (Supplementary Table S13) were chosen for validation by RT-qPCR using the same aliquot of total RNA for RNA-seq detection. The primers used for the detection of target genes were designed by Primer3plus online (RRID: SCR_003081) [79] with the housekeeping genes GAPDH and β-actin, used as interior controls. Real-time PCR was conducted on a CFX Connect real-time system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), with a total volume of 20 µL containing 10.0 µL of 2 × Talent qPCR PreMix (Tiangen, China), 1 µL of cDNA, and 0.4 µL of 10 µM primer. The amplification conditions of quantitative PCR were as follows: 33 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 20 s, annealing at 60 °C for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. The mRNA expression of these target genes was first normalized via the ratio to that of the interior control genes and then assessed via the 2-ΔΔCt method [80], and the different levels of gene expression between groups were determined through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS (RRID: SCR_002865), and P < 0.05 was considered significantly different. The results were presented as the means ± standard deviations calculated via GraphPad Prism (RRID: SCR_002798).
