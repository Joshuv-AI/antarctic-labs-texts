From eigenvector nonlinearities to eigenvalue nonlinearities
Elias Jarlebring, Vilhelm P. Lithell

Abstract
Over the past decades, transformations between different classes of eigenvalue problems have played a central role in the development of numerical methods for eigenvalue computations. One of the most well-known and successful examples of this is the companion linearization for polynomial eigenvalue problems. In this paper, we construct a transformation that equivalently reframes a specific type of eigenvalue problem with eigenvector nonlinearities (NEPv) into an eigenvalue problem with eigenvalue nonlinearities (NEP). The NEPv class considered consists of nonlinearities expressed as sums of products of matrices and scalar functions, where the scalar functions depend nonlinearly on the eigenvector. Our transformation defines scalar eigenvalue nonlinearities through a polynomial system, resulting in NEP nonlinearities of algebraic type. We propose methods to solve the polynomial system, one of which involves a multiparameter eigenvalue problem (MEP). We adapt well-established NEP solvers to this setting, with the most effective strategy being a combination of deflation and a locally quadratically convergent iterative method. The efficiency and properties of the approach are illustrated by solving a problem related to a modification of a Gross-Pitaevskii equation (GPE). The simulations are reproducible and publicly available.

Introduction

We consider problems with eigenvector nonlinearities (NEPv), where the nonlinearities appear as functions mapping vectors to scalars.
Let
A_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric matrix, and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consider the NEPv problem

(1a)

\displaystyle\lambda Ev=(A_{0}+({a_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}}v)^{2}a_{1}{a_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}}+\cdots+({a_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}}v)^{2}a_{m}{a_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}})v,

(1b)

\displaystyle 1=||v||_{B}^{2}:={v^{\mathrm{T}}}Bv,

with the goal of finding eigenpairs
(\lambda,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
that satisfy (
1

The condition (
1b
) imposes normalization on the eigenvector with respect to the symmetric positive definite matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
The left-hand side matrix
E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is also assumed to be symmetric positive definite.
With the techniques in this article, we will show how this problem can be transformed into an equivalent problem with eigenvalue nonlinearities (NEPs) of the form

(2)

(A_{0}-\lambda E+\mu_{1}^{2}(\lambda)a_{1}{a_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}}+\cdots+\mu_{m}^{2}(\lambda)a_{m}{a_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}})v=0,

where
\mu_{1}(\lambda),\dots,\mu_{m}(\lambda)
are scalar functions of the eigenvalue.
In this way, our technique enables us to reframe the NEPv as an equivalent NEP, allowing us to leverage efficient methods for these problems; see, e.g.,

Transformation

2.1
A parametrized polynomial system

We derive the equations defining the NEP (
2
),
by considering a particular transformation
of (
1
) that eliminates the presence of
v
, and instead, the nonlinearity appears only in terms of the scalars

\mu_{i}=a_{i}^{T}v,\;\;\;i=1,\ldots,m,

which are later interpreted as functions of
\lambda
.
By multiplying (
1a
) from the left with
(\lambda E-A_{0})^{-1}
we obtain a formula for
v

consisting of a linear combination of
\lambda
-dependent vectors

(7)

v=\mu_{1}^{3}f_{1}(\lambda)+\cdots+\mu_{m}^{3}f_{m}(\lambda),

where the functions
f_{1}(\lambda),\dots,f_{m}(\lambda)
are defined by

f_{i}(\lambda):=(\lambda E-A_{0})^{-1}a_{i},\;\;\;i=1,\ldots,m.

By applying the normalization condition (
1b
) on the vector
v
, we obtain

(8)

\begin{split}1&={v^{\mathrm{T}}}Bv\\
&=(\mu_{1}^{3}f_{1}(\lambda)^{T}+\cdots+\mu_{m}^{3}f_{m}(\lambda)^{T})B(\mu_{1}^{3}f_{1}(\lambda)+\cdots\mu_{m}^{3}f_{m}(\lambda))\\
&=\sum_{i,j\leq m}\mu_{i}^{3}\mu_{j}^{3}f_{i}(\lambda)^{T}Bf_{j}(\lambda).\end{split}

Hence, the normalization gives a scalar condition involving the
m
scalar functions
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}
, as well as the scalar
\lambda
.

We can obtain further polynomial relations between the scalars by multiplying equation (
7
) from the left by
a_{\ell}^{T}
, leading to

(9)

\mu_{\ell}=a_{\ell}^{T}v=\mu_{1}^{3}a_{\ell}^{T}f_{1}(\lambda)+\cdots+\mu_{m}^{3}a_{\ell}^{T}f_{m}(\lambda),

for any
\ell=1,\ldots,m
.
To state these relations more concisely, let
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the vector

(10)

\mu=[\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m}]^{T},

and let
\mu^{p}
, denote element-wise exponentiation of
\mu
.
Let
A_{m}
be the matrix

(11)

A_{m}:=\left[a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}.

Using this new notation, the relations (
8
) and (
9
) are summarized in a more concise way in the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Let
G(\lambda)
be a matrix whose elements depend on the parameter
\lambda
, defined by

(12)

G(\lambda):={A_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}}(\lambda E-A_{0})^{-1}B(\lambda E-A_{0})^{-1}A_{m},

and similarly let
H(\lambda)
be

(13)

H(\lambda):={A_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}}(\lambda E-A_{0})^{-1}A_{m}.

If
(\lambda,v)
is an eigenpair of (
1
), and
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue of the pencil
A_{0}-\lambda E
, then the following equations are satisfied

(14a)

\displaystyle{(\mu^{3})^{\mathrm{T}}}G(\lambda)\mu^{3}-1=0,

(14b)

\displaystyle H(\lambda)\mu^{3}-\mu=0.

This system has
m+1
unknowns and
m+1
equations. For the purpose of obtaining a transformation that defines functions
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}
implicitly, we reduce the system. Although many options are possible, for our purposes, we can select
m-1
rows in (
14b
). This is formally represented as the multiplication with
P\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-1)\times m}
, which is a submatrix of the identity matrix, yielding the equations

(15a)

\displaystyle{(\mu^{3})^{\mathrm{T}}}G(\lambda)\mu^{3}-1=0,

(15b)

\displaystyle P\left(H(\lambda)\mu^{3}-\mu\right)=0.

In the next section we will use these equations to define the functions
\mu_{1}(\lambda),\dots,\mu_{m}(\lambda)
. Conditions on the existence of implicit functions can be determined from the Jacobian of the system of equations, which in this case is explicitly given by the following formula.

Proposition 2
.

The Jacobian of the reduced system (
15
) with respect to
\mu
at a point
(\lambda,\mu)
, where
\lambda
is not an eigenvalue of the pencil
A_{0}-\lambda E
, is given by

(16)

J(\lambda,\mu)=\begin{bmatrix}6{(\mu^{3})^{\mathrm{T}}}G(\lambda)\operatorname{diag}(\mu^{2})\\
P(3H(\lambda)\operatorname{diag}(\mu^{2})-I)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},

where
\operatorname{diag}(\mu^{2})
denotes the diagonal matrix whose diagonal elements are those of
\mu^{2}
.

Proof.

Fix
\lambda
to be as above.
Since
G(\lambda)
is symmetric, we have

(17)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\left[{(\mu^{3})^{\mathrm{T}}}G(\lambda)\mu^{3}\right]={(\mu^{3})^{\mathrm{T}}}(G(\lambda)+{G(\lambda)^{\mathrm{T}}})3\operatorname{diag}(\mu^{2})=6{(\mu^{3})^{\mathrm{T}}}G(\lambda)\operatorname{diag}(\mu^{2}),

which gives the first row in (
16
).
For the remaining entries in (
16
), consider the derivative with respect to
\mu_{\ell}
,
1\leq\ell\leq m
, of row
k
of (
14b
)

(18)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu_{\ell}}\left(h_{k1}\mu_{1}^{3}+\dots+h_{km}\mu_{m}^{3}-\mu_{m}\right)=3h_{k\ell}\mu_{\ell}^{2}-\delta_{\ell m},\quad 1\leq k\leq m,

with
\delta_{ij}
denoting the Kronecker delta.
Then the Jacobian of (
14b
) with respect to
\mu
becomes

(19)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\left[H(\lambda)\mu^{3}-\mu\right]=3H(\lambda)\operatorname{diag}(\mu^{2})-I,

and selecting
m-1
rows from this Jacobian by application of
P
gives the remaining rows in (
16
).
∎

2.2
Equivalence with implicit functions

In the previous section, we constructed a polynomial system from the NEPv (
1
), involving
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m},\lambda
and
m
equations.
This polynomial system does not contain the solution
v
.
Since we have
m+1
variables and
m
equations, the implicit function theorem can be applied whenever the Jacobian is non-singular.
This leads to implicit functions
\mu_{1}(\lambda),\ldots,\mu_{m}(\lambda)
, that define a NEP (
2
), whose solutions coincide with the solutions to (
1
), in a neighborhood of a given non-singular point.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{\mu})
be a solution to the polynomial system (
15
) and assume the corresponding system Jacobian (
16
) is nonsingular.
Then there exist unique functions
\mu(\lambda)={[\mu_{1}(\lambda),\dots,\mu_{m}(\lambda)]^{\mathrm{T}}}
, continuous in a neighborhood of
\tilde{\lambda}
, denoted
D\subset\mathbb{R}
, such that
(\lambda,\mu(\lambda))
satisfies (
15
) for all
\lambda\in D
, and
\tilde{\mu}=\mu(\tilde{\lambda})
.
For any
(\lambda_{\ast},v_{\ast})
such that
\lambda_{\ast}\in D
, the following two statements are equivalent.

•

The pair
(\lambda_{\ast},v_{\ast})
is a solution to (
1
).

•

The pair
(\lambda_{\ast},v_{\ast})
is a solution to (
2
) with the functions defined by
\mu
and
||v_{*}||_{B}^{2}=1
.

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness of continuous functions
\mu_{i}(\lambda)
,
i=1,\dots,m
, and a neighborhood
D\subset\mathbb{R}
, having the stated properties follows from the implicit function theorem.
Every solution to (
1
) is a solution to (
2
) by construction, and it remains to show that the solutions of (
2
) are solutions to (
1
).
Assume that
(\lambda_{\ast},v_{\ast})
is an eigenpair of (
2
), with
\lambda_{\ast}\in D
.
It is sufficient to show that we have the equalities

(20)

\mu_{i}^{2}(\lambda_{\ast})=({a_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}}v_{\ast})^{2},\quad i=1,\dots,m.

Since
(\lambda_{\ast},v_{\ast})
is an eigenpair of (
2
), we have

(21)

v_{\ast}=(\lambda E-A_{0})^{-1}\left(\mu_{1}^{2}(\lambda_{\ast})({a_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}}v_{\ast})a_{1}+\dots+\mu_{m}^{2}(\lambda_{\ast})({a_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}}v_{\ast})a_{m}\right).

Applying the normalization condition, together with multiplying from the left with
{a_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}}
, for
i=1,\dots,m
, and extracting
m
rows using the matrix
P
, yields

(22a)

\displaystyle{[\mu(\lambda_{\ast})^{2}\cdot\xi(v_{\ast})]^{\mathrm{T}}}G(\lambda_{\ast})[\mu(\lambda_{\ast})^{2}\cdot\xi(v_{\ast})]-1=0,

(22b)

\displaystyle P\left(H(\lambda_{\ast})[\mu(\lambda_{\ast})^{2}\cdot\xi(v_{\ast})]-\xi(v_{\ast})\right)=0,

where we define
\xi(v_{*}):={[{a_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}}v_{*},\dots,{a_{m}^{\mathrm{T}}}v_{*}]^{\mathrm{T}}}
.
Since
\lambda_{\ast}\in D
,
\mu(\lambda_{\ast})
must satisfy both (
22
) and (
15
) simultaneously.
Hence, we have
\mu(\lambda_{\ast})=\xi(v_{\ast})
.
∎

.

In the illustrative example above, we observe that singularities occur at points

) for
m>2
can be obtained by means of a change of variables, and a reformulation as a multiparameter eigenvalue problem (MEP).

Consider the change of variables
w:=\mu^{3}
.
Then the solutions of the polynomial system (
15
) are contained in those of a new system

(34a)

\displaystyle{w^{\mathrm{T}}}G(\lambda)w-1=0,

(34b)

\displaystyle P\left((H(\lambda)w)^{3}-w\right)=0,

obtained by cubing both sides of (
15b
).
The solutions to (
34
) can in turn be extracted from the solutions of an associated MEP constructed in the following way.
We will use a companion linearization, a technique very common for polynomial eigenvalue problems, see, e.g.,
[
33
]
, but also polynomial multiparameter eigenvalue problems
[
35
]
.
Notice that (
34a
) is equivalent to the system of equations

(35)

\begin{bmatrix}-1&{w^{\mathrm{T}}}{G^{\mathrm{T}}}\\
w&-I_{m\times m}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
w\end{bmatrix}=0,

where
w
appears only linearly in the coefficient matrix.
Furthermore, denoting the
k
th row of
H(\lambda)
by
{h_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times m}
, the
k
th row of (
34b
) is equivalent to the system of equations

(36)

\begin{bmatrix}-w_{k}&0&{h_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}}w\\
{h_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}}w&-1&0\\
0&{h_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}}w&-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
{h_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}}w\\
({h_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}}w)^{2}\end{bmatrix}=0,

where
k=1,\dots,m-1
, assuming that
P
discards the last equation in (
15b
).
Together, (
35
) and (
36
) form
m
equations, where the
m
unknowns
w=[w_{1},\dots,w_{m}]
appear only linearly in the coefficient matrices.
This means that
(
34
)
can be reformulated to the form of a general MEP, i.e., a problem of the form

(37a)

\displaystyle(A_{1,0}+w_{1}A_{1,1}+

\displaystyle\dots+w_{m}A_{1,m})x_{0}=0,

(37b)

\displaystyle(A_{2,0}+w_{1}A_{2,1}+

\displaystyle\dots+w_{m}A_{2,m})x_{1}=0,

(37c)

\displaystyle(A_{m,0}+w_{1}A_{m,1}+

\displaystyle\dots+w_{m}A_{m,m})x_{m-1}=0,

where
x_{0}
is the vector
{[1,w]^{\mathrm{T}}}
,
x_{k}={[1,{h_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}}w,({h_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}}w)^{2}]^{\mathrm{T}}}
, for
k=1,\dots,m-1
, and the matrices
A_{1,0},\ldots,A_{m,m}
are constructed correspondingly from (
35
) and (
36
).
In this way, the solutions to the polynomial system can be obtained by solving the MEP (
37
), and subsequently reversing the change of variables.

Remark 7

(Singular points)
.

In Section
3.1.3
, we showed, for
m=2
, that around points where
\mu_{2}=0
, corresponding to
\lambda=\lambda_{*}
,
we have the asymptotic behavior

\mu_{2}^{2}(\lambda)=O(|\lambda-\lambda_{*}|^{2/3})
. A consistent conclusion can be drawn from the above construction using MEPs.
Eigenvalue solutions to MEPs are generically analytic functions of the elements of the coefficient matrices
A_{1,0},\ldots,A_{m,m}
, which in turn are generically analytic functions of
\lambda
. Therefore, near a point
w_{i}=0
, we have

\mu_{i}^{2}=w_{i}^{2/3}=O(|\lambda-\lambda_{*}|^{2/3}).

Implementation details

In order to combine the techniques described in the previous sections into an effective strategy for solving (
1
), several implementation aspects need to be developed. In this section and the simulation section (Section
5
) we provide these details, which are also implemented and publicly available in an online repository
1
1

1

https://github.com/lithell/nepv-nep.git
. The simulations are implemented in Julia, specifically using
NEP-PACK
which is a software package for NEPs
[
24
]
.

4.1
Numerical methods for the polynomial system

The scalar functions
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}
are implicitly defined as
solutions to algebraic systems, e.g., in the general case (
15
), or as in Corollary
4
for
m=2
. To apply the techniques in this article, these systems have to be solved efficiently and reliably. For
m=2
, we have an explicit form (Corollary
4
), although this seemingly cannot be naturally generalized to
m>2
.
Computationally solving algebraic systems of equations is the main topic of the field of computational algebraic geometry.
The Julia package
HomotopyContinuation.jl

[
10
]

is one powerful tool that has emerged from this field.
We performed simulations with this tool, but an alternative approach based on the linearization, as described in Section
3.2
, turned out to be preferable in practice.

In Section
3.2
, we illustrated how the algebraic system could be linearized (in the sense of companion linearization) to a MEP.
This opens up the possibility to use methods and software for MEPs, see e.g.,
[
37
]
and the references therein, as well as the Matlab software package
MultiParEig

[
38
]
.
Since we needed functionality from the Julia ecosystem
we could not directly use
MultiParEig
, but instead implemented a subset of the functionality for our setting. A fundamental technique for MEPs is the construction of
the generalized eigenvalue problems (GEPs)

(38)

w_{i}\Delta_{0}x=\Delta_{i}x,\quad i=1,\dots,m,

where
\Delta_{0},\ldots,\Delta_{m}
are matrices constructed from Kronecker products, derived from an operator determinant associated with the MEP (
37
).
The eigenvalues of (
37
) can in turn be extracted from those of the GEPs (
38
).
Formally, (
38
) is a family of generalized eigenvalue problems, with a shared eigenvector.
In practice, the consideration of only one of these generalized eigenvalue problems is often sufficient.
This yields one set of eigenvalues of the MEP, and the remaining eigenvalues can be computed, e.g., using Rayleigh quotients.
In our specific case, we chose to solve the GEP associated with the
\Delta_{i}
-matrix having the smallest condition number.
Again similar to
MultiParEig
, as a post-processing procedure, we carried out Newton refinement to improve the quality of the solutions.

Remark 8

(Avoiding recomputation of
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}
)
.

Note that the evaluation of
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}
requires substantial computational effort,
stemming from both the evaluation of the coefficients
G(\lambda)
and
H(\lambda)
as well as the solution to the MEP (
37
).
In the implementation in the context of a NEP-solver, e.g., as described in Section
4.2
, we
may need to repeatedly evaluate
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}
. In order to avoid recomputation, we use a look-up table to store already computed
\mu
-values.

4.2
Adaption of iterative methods for NEPs

The literature and results regarding numerical methods for NEPs of the general form

(39)

M(\lambda)v=0,

where
M(\lambda)\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, is extensive, see, e.g.,
[
34
]
, or the summary
[
20
]
for a more recent review of the field.
Consequently, there are many efficient methods for different use-cases.
We now motivate our choice of method, which we will subsequently discuss in more detail.

A large number of methods depend on the function
M(\lambda)
being holomorphic or meromorphic in some domain
\Omega
of the complex plane.
This class consists of, among other methods, contour-integral based methods
[
9
]
[
3
]
, Krylov methods based on series expansion of
M(\lambda)
(so-called infinite Arnoldi methods)
[
27
]
[
15
]
[
18
]
, and approaches based on rational approximation
[
20
]
[
17
]
.
Crucially, for meromorphic problems, these methods depend on knowing the location of the singularities a priori.
In our application, this is not the case, and determining the locations of the singularities of
\mu^{2}_{1}(\lambda),\dots,\mu^{2}_{m}(\lambda)
numerically is expensive.
Hence, we have not chosen to consider any of these methods for use in our application.

There are also a number of adaptions of Newton’s method to nonlinear eigenvalue problems, see, for instance,
[
20
,
34
]
or
[
39
]
. Although convergence of
Newton’s method can only be guaranteed locally, Armijo steplength control (which is used in our setting) can be adapted to improve the convergence basin
[
29
]
.
This method class consists of locally convergent iterative schemes, whose main computational cost is the need to repeatedly solve an iteration-dependent linear system, involving the Jacobian matrix of the extended system

(40)

\begin{bmatrix}M(\lambda)v\\
{c^{\mathrm{H}}}v-1\end{bmatrix}=0,

for some vector
c\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
not orthogonal to the eigenvector
v
.
A common approach to reduce this computational cost is to consider so-called quasi-Newton methods.
In these methods, (parts of) the Jacobian matrix are kept constant throughout the iterations, meaning precomputed factorizations can be used to speed up the solution of the linear system.
In
[
25
]
, several choices of approximate Jacobians are considered.
Generally, the authors conclude that we can only expect local linear convergence to simple eigenvalues, where the convergence factor is proportional to the distance from the eigenvalue to some fixed shift parameter,
\sigma\in\mathbb{C}
.
In our setting, the value in terms of computation time, of using a (partially) fixed Jacobian is less substantial due to the fact that every evaluation of
\mu_{1}^{2},\dots,\mu_{m}^{2}
requires the solution of
m
(iteration-dependent) linear systems.
Instead, we have opted to use a locally quadratically convergent Newton scheme, specifically the augmented Newton method; see, e.g.,
[
39
]
.
To allow the possibility of computing several eigenvalues, we will combine this with a deflation method to remove already computed eigenvalues from the solution set.
The deflation procedure will be described in Section
4.3
.

Newton’s method generates a sequence of eigenvalue and eigenvector approximations
(\lambda_{k},v_{k})
,
k=1,2,3,\dots
, and proceeds by solving the system

(41)

\begin{bmatrix}M(\lambda_{k})&M^{\prime}(\lambda_{k})v_{k}\\
{c^{\mathrm{H}}}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v_{k+1}-v_{k}\\
\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda_{k}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}M(\lambda_{k})v_{k}\\
{c^{\mathrm{H}}}v_{k}-1\end{bmatrix}.

The augmented Newton method is obtained by equivalently writing these relations as the steps

(42)

\displaystyle M(\lambda_{k})u_{k+1}=M^{\prime}(\lambda_{k})v_{k},

(43)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k+1}=\lambda_{k}+\frac{1}{{c^{\mathrm{H}}}u_{k+1}},

(44)

\displaystyle v_{k+1}=\frac{1}{{c^{\mathrm{H}}}u_{k+1}}u_{k+1}.

In this way, the augmented Newton method operates on vectors of length
n
, but is equivalent to Newton’s method in exact arithmetic.

In the setting of our numerical simulations, we consider a discretization of a differential equation leading to
n\gg 1
, such that
A_{0}
is a large and sparse matrix. Moreover, we assume that
m\ll n
, such that
M(\lambda_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
has a
low-rank structure originating from the nonlinear terms in (
2
).
To avoid explicitly forming the rank-one matrices (which are dense matrices) involved in evaluating
M(\lambda_{k})
in (
42
), we will employ the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury (SMW) formula, see, e.g.,
[
19
, Section 2.1.3]
.
The SMW formula states that the inverse of the rank-
m
update to
A_{0}
required in solving (
42
), can be computed by the identity

(45)

(A_{0}+U_{k}{U_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}})^{-1}=A_{0}^{-1}-A_{0}^{-1}U_{k}(I_{m\times m}+{U_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}}A_{0}^{-1}U_{k})^{-1}{U_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}}A_{0}^{-1},

where we have defined
U_{k}=[\mu_{1}(\lambda_{k})a_{1},\dots,\mu_{m}(\lambda_{k})a_{m}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.
Notice that this means that the computation of
u_{k+1}
in (
42
) can be performed in
1+m
sparse
n\times n
linear solves, instead of one dense linear solve.
In practice, using (
45
) is much more efficient for
m\ll n
, both with respect to computation time and memory limitations.
All reported results from the numerical simulations in Section
5
utilize (
45
) for the solution of linear systems involving low-rank terms.

4.3
Deflation

In the numerical simulations in Section
5
we will employ a deflation technique developed in
[
16
]
, based on the concept of invariant pairs for NEPs introduced in
[
29
]
. For simplicity, we consider the index-one case.
The background necessary for constructing the deflation procedure is now briefly summarized, where we still consider the general setting of (
39
).
The deflation procedure defines an extended NEP one dimension larger.
The eigenvalues of the extended NEP are eigenvalues of the original NEP. The converse holds for all eigenvalues except for the deflated eigenvalue, which can be viewed as removed from the solution set.
For the definition of the extended problem, we will require the concept of minimal invariant pairs.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
M(\lambda)
in (
39
) has the structure
M(\lambda)=M_{1}f_{1}(\lambda)+\dots+M_{m}f_{m}(\lambda)
, where
M_{1},\dots,M_{m}
are matrices, and
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
are scalar functions of the eigenvalue.
An invariant pair of (
39
) is then defined to be a pair
(X,S)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
such that

(46)

M_{1}Xf_{1}(S)+\dots+M_{m}Xf_{m}(S)=0,

where
f_{i}(S)
,
i=1,\dots,m
, are understood to be matrix functions.
In our case,
f_{i}=\mu_{i}^{2}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, which are generically analytic. Hence, the matrix functions
\mu_{i}^{2}(S)
are well-defined.
The columns of
X
form a basis for an invariant subspace of (
39
), and by means of a Schur decomposition of
S
, one can show that
S
shares its eigenvalues with (
39
).
In
[
16
]
, invariant pairs are used to construct a deflation technique, by expanding a previously computed invariant pair one column at a time.
We compute vectors
v,u
and a scalar
\lambda
such that the pair
(\widehat{X},\widehat{S})
defined by

(47)

(\widehat{X},\widehat{S})=\left(\begin{bmatrix}X&v\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}S&u\\
0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}\right),

is also minimal, which is ensured by an orthogonality condition.
This orthogonality condition means we avoid reconvergence to an already computed eigenvalue.
If
(X,S)
is a minimal invariant pair of (
39
), it is possible to show that all extended minimal pairs of the form (
47
) are equivalent by similarity transform to such pairs with
u,v,\lambda
defined as the solutions to the extended NEP

(48)

\begin{bmatrix}M(\lambda)&U(\lambda)\\
{X^{\mathrm{H}}}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v\\
u\end{bmatrix}=0,

with
||v||+||u||\neq 0
, and
U(\lambda)
defined by

(49)

U(\lambda)=M(\lambda)X(\lambda I-S)^{-1}.

In this way, additional eigenvalues of the original problem (
39
) can be extracted from those of (
48
), while avoiding reconvergence.
The foundational functionality of the above deflation technique is available in
NEP-PACK
.

5.1
Discretization of a PDE eigenvalue problem

In order to illustrate the performance and properties
of the approach, we now study a
GPE-inspired nonlinear eigenvalue problem stemming from the discretization of the following problem,
similar to the problem given in
[
23
, Section 2.3]
.
Consider the two-dimensional nonlinear eigenvalue problem of finding
(u,\lambda)\in L^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{R}
such that

(50)

-\Delta u(x,y)+p(x,y)u(x,y)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}^{3}(u)\psi_{i}(x,y)=\lambda u(x,y),

with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions and
m=5
.
Normalization of the solution is enforced with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm, i.e., we require
||u||_{L^{2}}=1
.
We consider the square domain
\Omega=[-1,1]\times[-1,1]
, and define the functions
\phi_{i}
and
\psi_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,5
, by

(51)

\phi_{i}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\psi_{i}(x,y)u(x,y)\,d\Omega,\quad\psi_{i}(x,y)=c_{i}e^{-\sigma_{i}[(x-x_{i})^{2}+(y-y_{i})^{2}]},

where we have selected the parameters to be
c_{i}=45
,
\sigma_{i}=6
, for
i=1,\dots,5
, and the centers of the Gaussians
\psi_{i}
were selected as
(x_{1},y_{1})=(0.4,-0.6)
,
(x_{2},y_{2})=(0.6,0.3)
,
(x_{3},y_{3})=(0.1,0.6)
,
(x_{4},y_{4})=(-0.5,0.4)
,
(x_{5},y_{5})=(-0.4,-0.4)
.
The positions of the centers is essentially arbitrary, but were chosen in an approximately circular configuration in order to illustrate problem properties.
The potential function
p(x,y)
is chosen to model a superposition of a harmonic trapping potential and an optical lattice, common in experiment
[
23
]
, although the parameters are chosen differently for illustration purposes.
More precisely, we take the potential to be
p(x,y)=p_{\text{h}}(x,y)+p_{\text{opt}}(x,y),
where the harmonic oscillator potential
p_{\text{h}}
is given by

p_{\text{h}}(x,y):=16\left(\gamma_{x}^{2}x^{2}+\gamma_{y}^{2}y^{2}\right)
,
with
\gamma_{x}=1,\gamma_{y}=2
, and the optical lattice potential
p_{\text{opt}}
is

p_{\text{opt}}(x,y):=64\left(\sin(4\pi x)^{2}+\sin(4\pi y)^{2}\right)
.
With a uniform mesh,
\Delta x=\Delta y=:h
, and
(N+2)^{2}
total points, we define the discretized solution vector
v\approx[u(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,u(x_{N},y_{1}),\dots,u(x_{1},y_{N}),\dots,u(x_{N},y_{N})]^{T}
for the
N^{2}
interior points.
We set
L_{N^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}\times N^{2}}
to be the standard two-dimensional discrete Laplacian stencil of size
N^{2}
, i.e.,

L_{N^{2}}=D_{2,N}\otimes I+I\otimes D_{2,N},

with
D_{2,N}=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\text{tridiag}(1,-2,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, and we let
V
be the diagonal matrix

V=\operatorname{diag}(p(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots p(x_{N},y_{1}),\dots,p(x_{1},y_{N}),\dots,p(x_{N},y_{N}))
.
The integrals are approximated with the two-dimensional trapezoidal rule.
We arrive at the discrete problem of finding
(v,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{N^{2}}\times\mathbb{R}
such that

(52a)

\displaystyle\left(h^{2}(-L_{N^{2}}+V)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}({a_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}}v)^{2}a_{i}{a_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}}\right)v=\lambda Ev,

\displaystyle||v||_{B}^{2}=1,

with

.

5.2
Simulation results

We apply our approach to the NEPv (
52
), leading to the NEP given by

(53)

\left(h^{2}(-L_{N^{2}}+V)+\sum_{i=1}^{5}\mu_{i}^{2}(\lambda)a_{i}{a_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}}\right)v=\lambda Ev,

where
\mu_{1}^{2}(\lambda),\dots,\mu_{5}^{2}(\lambda)
are defined as in Section

and the equation selection in (
15b
) is chosen to discard the last equation.
For the simulation we selected
N=256
, i.e., the problems (
52
) and (
53
) are of dimension
n=256^{2}=65536
.
The problem (
53
) is solved by the augmented Newton method for nonlinear eigenvalue problems from Section
4.2
.
To compute several eigenvalues of (
53
), we deflate already computed eigenvalues as described in Section
4.3
.
The augmented Newton method requires the derivative of (
53
) with respect to
\lambda
.
Since the derivatives of
\mu_{1}^{2},\dots,\mu_{5}^{2}
are not directly available, these are approximated by standard second-order finite differences, which do introduce additional approximation error, but seemingly do not substantially influence the performance.
We selected the stopping tolerance in the NEP solver to be
5\cdot 10^{-12}
, with respect to the relative residual norm of (
53
), i.e., the quality of the approximation of the pair
(\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{v})
is quantified by

(54)

\frac{\|M(\tilde{\lambda})\tilde{v}\|}{\|\tilde{v}\|}.

For illustration we also studied the relative residual for the NEPv, i.e.,

(55)

\frac{\|A(\tilde{v})\tilde{v}-\tilde{\lambda}\tilde{v}\|}{\|\tilde{v}\|}.

All simulations were performed on a system with a 3.9GHz 8-core Intel i5-8265U processor and 8GB of memory, running Debian GNU/Linux 12.
The results of the simulation are presented in Figure
3
and
5
.
Performance metrics from the simulation can be found in Table
1
.
The functions
\mu_{1}^{2},\dots,\mu_{5}^{2}
for this example are visualized in Figure

.

We observed rapid convergence to all nine computed eigenvalues once the solver entered the asymptotic regime.
As expected, the deflation procedure allowed us to robustly compute several eigenvalues of (
52
), even when the eigenvalues were not well separated.
In such a situation, a locally convergent NEPv solver applied directly to (
52
) might struggle to distinguish between the eigenvalues unless several different starting guesses are used across multiple runs.
Clearly, instead solving the equivalent NEP, when combined with deflation, allows us to largely circumvent such difficulties.

Studying Figure

\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{5}
due to Remark
7
.
This behavior is expected and can be explained by studying Figure
5

For the eigenmodes corresponding to these eigenvalues, the Gaussians
\psi_{i}(x,y)
, corresponding to
\mu_{i}^{2}(\lambda)
, are nearly centered on the zero-valued interface between positive and negative parts of the eigenfunctions.
Hence, the
L^{2}
inner product between
\psi_{i}(x,y)
and the eigenmode is small, by the symmetry of
\psi_{i}(x,y)
.
Consequently, by the definition of
\mu_{1}^{2},\dots,\mu_{5}^{2}
, we also expect these functions to be small for these eigenvalues.
The fact that we robustly found these eigenvalues, despite being close to singularities, can be seen as a favorable aspect of the method.

Another observation from Figure
4
is that this problem seems to have unique
\mu
-values. That is, although the polynomial system that is solved for every
\lambda
has many complex solutions, there seems to only be one real-valued solution. This means that the potential complication with multi-valued
\mu
-functions is not a concern for this specific application.

Iteration times and a quantification of the number of linear solves are given in Table

. We observe that the deflation technique is successful in this situation, since we can compute 9 eigenvalues, and the number of iterations per eigenvalue is not substantially increasing. The CPU time per eigenvalue does increase slightly. This can be partially explained by having to solve additional systems when using the extended NEP (
48
). However, the variation in CPU time is more influenced by the number of Armijo steps required, e.g.,
for eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}
and eigenvalue
\lambda_{5}
that have the same number of iterations, but differ significantly in terms of the number of linear solves.

can be performed together using five linear solves.
All linear solves are sparse.

6
Conclusions and outlook

In this work, we have illustrated how a class of NEPvs can be transformed into a NEP in a way that enables the use of NEP solvers. The transformation involves a polynomial system of equations that must be solved each time the NEP is accessed. We propose several strategies for solving this system. For the case
m=2
, we derive an explicit solution, while for the general case
m>2
, we propose a transformation leading to a MEP that can be reliably solved with MEP methods.

The approach seems generalizable in several ways. The fact that
we use scalar terms involving squares is due to the appearance of such nonlinearities in the motivating applications, and it can be naturally adapted to other polynomials, again leading to polynomial systems.
The strategy in this paper can also be adapted for more general choices of functions, although this will lead to a nonlinear system that is not necessarily polynomial.

A more general message of the results in this paper is the existence of a transformation from NEPv to NEP. In the proposed approach, evaluating the functions
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}
requires substantial computational effort. Further research on the construction of similar functions may lead to alternative transformations with lower computational cost. For example, we note that
H(\lambda)
is the transfer function of a linear dynamical system (and
G(\lambda)
is related to a second-order system). Therefore, the evaluation could potentially be made more efficient by precomputing a reduced-order model
[
2
,
8
]
.

We note that the proposed approach is, to a large extent, independent of the choice of NEP solver. While we have suggested a specific solver in this paper, other options may be more appropriate for different problem types. For instance, if the singularities of the functions
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}
can be determined without substantial computational effort, this may enable the use of rational approximation methods. See Section
4.2
for further discussion.

The theoretical construction presented in this paper is applicable to an arbitrary number of terms, i.e.,
m\gg 1
. However, when
m
is large, the polynomial system becomes computationally restrictive to solve. Although other approaches than the one proposed here may be used to handle this, we expect the polynomial system to eventually become the dominating complication.
Note that the GPE corresponds to the case
m=n
and is therefore not solvable with the current approach, at least not without further research.
The simulations in Section
5
suggest several such research directions.
The
\phi
-functions in (
50
) can be interpreted as localized weighted averages of the wave function.
This suggests to iteratively update the
\psi
-functions in a fashion similar to the SCF iteration.
More knowledge about the approximation properties, in relation to the GPE, of an approach using a small number of nonlinear terms would be insightful in applying such an iterative technique.
