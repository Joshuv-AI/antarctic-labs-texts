Carbon ion stripper for NSTRI tandem accelerator
Authors: M Mohseni Kejani, F Ghasemi, F Abbasi Davani, Sh Sanaye Hajari, M Lamehi Rachti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tandem accelerator project at the Nuclear Science and Technology Research Institute (NSTRI) aims to design and construction a 1.7 MV accelerator for AMS applications. Alongside the design and construction of the high voltage power supply for the intended accelerator, the accelerating tube including the low-energy section, the stripper section, and the high-energy section is also being developed. This paper focuses specifically on the design of the stripper section for a carbon ion beam. In the design of this section, by excluding interactions involving charge states of (q, q–2) and (q, q + 2), a total of 14 interactions between charge states (q, q + 1) and (q, q–1) have been considered. After deriving the interaction cross-sections at the desired energy, the optimal geometry was introduced, and the charge exchange efficiency was calculated for different gas throughputs. At a gas throughput of 9 Standard Cubic Centimeter per Minute (SCCM), an equilibrium thickness of 2.3 × 1016 cm−2 was achieved, which ensures charge exchange equilibrium for the formation of various carbon charge states within the designed geometry. Results indicate that for charge states 1+, 2+, and 3+, the 14C/12C stripping ratios are 1.22, 1.01, and 0.89 respectively, while the corresponding ratios for 14C/13C are 1.52, 1.09, and 0.76. These variations highlight the importance of considering isotopic mass effect on stripping efficiency in AMS-based radiocarbon measurements. The optimized geometry of the stripper section was constructed and experimentally tested. Pressure measurements taken in different regions showed excellent agreement with the gas-flow simulation results, validating the accuracy of the simulation. Furthermore, the calculated carbon charge-state fractions were validated with experimental measurements from another reference at 1.7 MeV in nitrogen under equilibrium-thickness conditions. The results reveal a close match between the calculated fractions in this study and the experimental data, further supporting the reliability of the model and the credibility of the results. The results demonstrate that this optimized geometry and structure are capable of achieving the required atomic density for reaching the equilibrium thickness of the carbon beam, while the overall length is reduced to approximately one-third of that in conventional stripper designs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42751-1.Keywords: radiocarbon dating, tandem technique, charge exchange interaction, gas stripper, equilibrium thicknessSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Archaeology is an interdisciplinary science focused on reconstructing past lifestyles, social structures, and technologies through the study of material remains. A key challenge is accurately dating these findings, which is essential for understanding historical and cultural developments. Dating methods, including Optically Stimulated Luminescence (OSL)1–10, dendrochronology11–14, and radiocarbon (14C) dating11–14, help estimate artifact ages and provide insights into past environmental and climatic changes10,15. Radiocarbon dating, for example, enables age estimation up to about 55,000 years by analyzing the decay of carbon-14 isotopes, which are absorbed by living organisms16. When an organism dies, carbon exchange ceases, and 14C decays with a half-life of approximately 5,700 years. To enhance accuracy, corrections such as calibration17–19 and isotopic fractionation20–23 are applied. Radiocarbon dating methods, including beta counting24–27, gas proportional counting28–30, and Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS)26,31–34, measure the ratio of 14C to stable carbon isotopes. AMS, offering high sensitivity for small samples and ancient ages, is the preferred method for radiocarbon dating but requires a tandem accelerator35.

The tandem accelerator is a type of electrostatic accelerators36. Electrostatic accelerators are the simplest type of ion accelerators37. This kind of accelerators provide the ability to achieve beams with very good energy resolution (typically in the range of 10−438, which is why they are among the most widely used accelerators in research fields, particularly in analysis. The ion source, high-voltage power supply, and accelerating tube are the main components of electrostatic accelerators.

In a tandem accelerator, unlike single-stage accelerators, high-voltage terminals are used twice to achieve higher energy for ions39. One of the key features of this type of accelerator is the placement of its ion source outside the high-voltage section, allowing for easy replacement of the ion beam produced from different samples for the analysis of various specimens39. In the ion source suitable for this type of accelerator, negative ions are generated. These ions are accelerated by the high-voltage potential as they pass through the low-energy section of the accelerating tube. When these ions pass through the charge exchange cell and reach the high-voltage terminal, they lose their electrons and become positive ions. The positive ions then accelerate toward the zero potential39.

The most important difference between a tandem accelerator and other single-stage electrostatic accelerators is the stripper section. This section is located in the middle of the accelerating tube, between the low-energy and high-energy sections, and is responsible for converting negative ions to positive ions36. For the stripper section of the tandem accelerator, there are two main options: using a carbon foil-based stripping system40–43 or a gas-based stripping system44–48. If stable beam transfer and achieving higher current are prioritized, the gas-based stripping system is more commonly used36. On the other hand, if achieving higher charge state and consequently higher beam energy is the priority, the carbon foil-based stripping system is used36. The main challenge with the gas-based stripping system is the leakage of gas to the sides of the accelerating tube, which disrupts the vacuum needed for particle acceleration. Meanwhile, the primary challenge with the carbon foil system is the short lifespan of the foil when using heavy ions and the reduction in beam current after stripping36.

Archaeology in Iran is very important due to its ancient history, diversity, and richness of archaeological artifacts. Physics and Accelerators Research School (PARS) at Nuclear Science and Technology Research Institute (NSTRI) is active in the development of both radiofrequency49–52 and electrostatic accelerators53,54. Also, numerous analyses have been carried out at NSTRI in the field of archaeology55–67. These analyses were mainly conducted using the single-stage accelerator available at NSTRI. However, given the applications of the tandem accelerator, which allows for precise and rapid dating of small samples, the design and construction of a tandem accelerator at the NSTRI, in collaboration with Shahid Beheshti University (SBU), has been initiated. This accelerator, which will be used for accelerating hydrogen and carbon ion beams, will have applications in element identification analysis or ion implantation using hydrogen ion beam, and radiocarbon dating. The power supply for this accelerator has been built and is capable of producing a voltage of 1.7 MV53. Carbon stripping and cross-section of interactions

As the C⁻ ion beam passes through the nitrogen gas environment, the carbon negative ions collide with the gas molecules and the charge exchange (Ch. Ex.) process begins. In this process, the carbon ions can convert from one charge state to others, including negative ions, neutrals, and positive ions. These continuous charge state changes are influenced by various interactions, such as electron capture, electron detachment, and ion recombination.

In tandem accelerators with applications in radiocarbon dating, efficient conversion of the negative carbon beam to positive ones is essential for entering the acceleration section. Therefore, all potential interactions occurring in the stripper section must be carefully considered. This consideration includes calculating the interaction rates and determining the final distribution of ions produced after passing through the stripper section. As shown in Fig. 4, the energy values in these plots are expressed in units of MeV per amu (atomic mass unit). This implies that the cross-section values differ for various carbon isotopes due to their mass differences. The resulting variation is significant and must be taken into account in the calculations.

According to Eq. (1), the amount of charge exchange is directly influenced by the gas number density (ngas) and the interaction path length (x). In the early stages, as these two parameters increase, the probability of collisions between the carbon ions and gas molecules increases, and as a result, the charge exchange rate also rises. However, this increasing trend will not continue permanently. After reaching a certain level of gas number density and interaction thickness (ngas.x), an equilibrium is established among all the carbon species (negative, neutral and positive). At this point, further changes in gas number density or interaction path length will have poor effect on the composition of the carbon species, as the rate of conversion between different ions reaches a dynamic equilibrium. This specific thickness is referred to as the equilibrium thickness85,86. Determining the exact value of this point is one of the key objectives in charge exchange calculations because optimal design of the charge exchange cell requires achieving conditions that maximize the conversion efficiency of negative carbon ions to positive ones, without causing unnecessary increases in gas injection rate that could lead to issues such as gas leakage into the accelerating tube.

The natural abundances of carbon isotopes are highly unequal; 12C and 13C account for approximately 98.93% and 1.07% of stable carbon, respectively, while 14C is present at about 1.2 × 10⁻12 relative to 12C in the atmosphere87. When using a tandem accelerator for radiocarbon dating, the negative carbon ion beam (C⁻) undergoes charge exchange in the stripper section, resulting in the formation of positively charged carbon ions. In the specific case of the NSTRI tandem accelerator, the beam is accelerated to an energy of 1.7 MeV before entering the stripper section.

These isotopes are being accelerated under the same voltage, arrive at the stripper section with different velocities due to their mass differences. This velocity variation directly influences the charge exchange cross-sections for each isotope, affecting their probabilities of undergoing electron detachment or electron capture interactions.

Geometry and design assumption

The stripper section in a tandem accelerator is positioned between the low-energy and high-energy sections of the accelerating tube, with its primary function being the conversion of negative ions to positive ions. In the gaseous stripper system, a suitable gas is injected into this section to achieve the required atomic density for the charge exchange process. As the gas number density increases, the probability of interaction between negative ions and gas molecules rises, leading to a higher conversion rate of these ions into positive ones. This process continues until the equilibrium thickness is reached. After reaching this equilibrium thickness, further increases in gas number density will not affect the amount of charge exchange.

However, since ion acceleration requires maintaining a high vacuum level of around 10−6 mbar in the accelerating tube36, controlling gas leakage from the charge exchange cell to these regions becomes an important challenge. One possible solution to prevent gas leakage is the use of a physical window to separate the stripper section from the accelerating tube. However, this approach presents serious challenges, as the presence of the window in the beam path results in a significant loss of beam intensity and emittance growth.

Therefore, the design of the stripper section must ensure that the maximum gas number density in the beam path is achieved with the minimum gas injection rate, while also minimizing gas leakage into the accelerating tube. To achieve this, the appropriate geometric design of the Charge Exchange cell (Ch. Ex.) and the distribution of gas inside it play a critical role. The authors reported the optimization of the charge exchange section dimensions aimed at minimizing gas leakage into the accelerator tube in Reference89. The calculations related to this optimization were performed using the Free Molecular Flow interface in COMSOL Multiphysics software90. This software uses the Angular Coefficient Method for vacuum-related calculations, which enables precise examination of pressure distribution and gas leakage.

In the Angular Coefficient Method91, the gas flow is calculated by integrating the flux from all surfaces that fall within the line-of-sight of a specific surface. In other words, each surface can receive gas from other surfaces within its line-of-sight, and the gas transfer between different surfaces depends on their angle and distance from each other. This method is highly accurate and efficient for flow calculations in the free molecular regime, where collisions between gas molecules are negligible.

As a result, for vacuum simulations, such as the stripper section in a tandem accelerator, which operates in the molecular flow regime, this method can be used to model gas leakage and pressure distribution accurately within the accelerating tube.

As shown in Fig. 6, the stripper section has its own dedicated vacuum pump, while each of the high energy and low energy sections of the accelerating tube have a separate ones. In this simulation, the nitrogen gas throughput considered 10 SCCM (Standard Cubic Centimeter per Minute). The vacuum pump speeds for both the accelerating tube sections and the stripper section are set to 300 L/s, with the gas temperature maintained at 20 °C. As a result, the optimized dimensions for the charge exchange section are presented in Table 4, in accordance with reference89. According to Eq. (1), by having the cross-section values for the possible interactions in the stripper section and determining the appropriate geometric dimensions, the efficiency of the charge exchange process can be calculated for each gas throughput. This calculation is carried out using simulations in COMSOL Multiphysics, coupling two interfaces: Free Molecular Flow and Charged Particle Tracing. In this process, first, using the Free Molecular Flow interface and considering the geometry of the cell and vacuum pumps, the required amount of gas for the charge exchange process is injected into the stripper section. Then, under steady-state conditions, the gas number density distribution along the accelerator beam path is determined. This step is particularly important for controlling the amount of injected gas and preventing its adverse effects on low- and high-energy sections of the accelerating tube. Once the steady-state gas distribution is obtained, the Charged Particle Tracing interface is employed to simulate the motion of carbon ions through the stripper section. This interface uses the Monte Carlo method to account the interactions such as Coulomb collisions and charge exchange processes. The collision cross-section is a key parameter in these simulations, as it directly influences the mean free path and determines the probability of each interaction occurring.

By integrating these two interfaces, the interaction cross-sections and the gas number density distribution along the beam path are used to compute the occurrence rate of each charge exchange processes listed in Table 3. This enables a realistic estimation of trajectories and charge state of carbon ions within the stripper section.

Experimental setup

In order to experimentally investigate the pressure behavior and validate the simulation results, the stripper section of the NSTRI tandem accelerator was constructed and assembled. After the construction of the stripper section and its associated connections, the section was mounted onto two 100-mm vacuum T-joints, serving as a representative of the low and high energy sections of the accelerating tube. This configuration enabled pressure readings at different positions, thereby providing a criterion for evaluating the amount of gas leakage into the low- and high-energy sections of the accelerating tube. In this experiment, the pressure was measured at three main regions: the stripper section, the charge-exchange cell, and the accelerating tube. Due to the geometric symmetry of the setup, pressure was measured on only one side of the accelerating tubes (low-energy and high-energy sections) and taken as representative of the opposite side; this serves as a diagnostic for gas leakage from the stripper region into the accelerating tubes. The general view of the experimental setup illustrated in Fig. 9.

For maintaining appropriate vacuum conditions during the experiments, three independent pumping systems were employed for the stripper cell, the stripper section, and the accelerating tube ensuring separate vacuum control in each part. To ensure the proper functioning of the vacuum gauges and to estimate their measurement error, two ports were incorporated into the stripper section, enabling the chamber pressure to be monitored from two independent locations. The gauges were pre-labeled and tested under both low- and high-vacuum conditions. In the first stage, only the rotary pump was operated, and after one hour the pressure values were recorded at low vacuum condition. Subsequently, with the turbomolecular pumps activated and the system reaching high vacuum after two hours, the pressure was measured again. Results

Using the optimized dimensions of the stripper section and the available cross-section values for the interactions listed in Table 3, calculations were performed to determine the final distribution of different charge states for each carbon isotope (12C, 13C, and 14C) after passing through this section. These calculations were conducted to evaluate the impact of different gas throughput on the charge exchange process, with the goal of identifying the equilibrium gas thickness at which the distribution of charge states reaches a steady state.

For each gas throughput, the charge state distribution of carbon ions after exiting the stripper section was calculated, and the behavior of each isotope under different gas conditions was analyzed. Due to the variations in the isotope’s velocities and consequently in the interaction cross-sections, it was observed that the efficiency of producing each charge state differs among the isotopes. As expected, due to the low cross-section values of the interactions leading to the formation of carbon ions with charge states higher than 4+, such charge states were not produced during the charge exchange process. This confirms that the probability of generating such highly charged carbon ions using a gas stripper system is extremely low, and therefore, gas strippers are not practical for achieving these charge states. In Monte Carlo simulations, the number of m successful outcomes in N independent trials follows a binomial distribution. Therefore, the estimated fraction has a standard deviation of 92. Across all Monte Carlo calculations performed, the maximum relative statistical error was found to be 1.58%, which is sufficiently small for the objectives of this study.

To determine the equilibrium thickness in the stripper section, a dimensionless parameter, Alpha (α), was defined. This parameter is the ratio of the charge exchange efficiency at a given gas throughput to that at the next higher rate, and it was calculated for each charge state of carbon isotopes across various gas throughputs. The concept of equilibrium thickness is based on the assumption that, at a certain point, further increases in gas number density no longer affect the charge exchange efficiency and the α value gradually approaches to 1. Therefore, when the α value for all charge states of a particular isotope reaches 1, the corresponding gas thickness can be considered the equilibrium thickness for that isotope.

The variation of the α value as a function of gas throughput for different carbon isotopes is presented in Fig. 14. Ultimately, the equilibrium thickness of nitrogen gas for the entire system was defined as the highest value among the equilibrium thicknesses of all isotopes. This approach ensures optimal and complete charge exchange performance across all carbon isotopes.

Conclusion

The main advantages of AMS based radiocarbon dating over other methods include the need for only a very small amount of sample, high sensitivity for detecting very old samples, and the ability to separate and remove interferences caused by other isotopes. A tandem accelerator is required to apply this method. The power supply for NSTRI tandem accelerator has already been developed and is capable of generating a voltage of 1.7 MV.

In this paper, following the steps of derivation of cross section of interactions and simulating the charge exchange process has resulted in an increased charge exchange efficiency and the determination of the equilibrium thickness.

The initial step in designing the stripper system is to accurately derive the cross-sections of the relevant interactions within the stripper region. To achieve this, various data sources providing cross-section values were reviewed and compared at the desired energy. Considering the relationship between ion energy and velocity, as well as the mass differences among carbon isotopes, it was found that the cross-sections for each interaction differ among isotopes during the charge exchange process. Therefore, these differences should be carefully considered in the calculations.

The charge exchange interactions for all three carbon isotopes (12C, 13C, and 14C) were calculated using a coupled approach that combines the Free Molecular Flow and Charged Particle Tracing interfaces in COMSOL Multiphysics software. The purpose of these calculations was to determine the equilibrium gas thickness required for efficient stripping of the carbon beam. After designing the optimal geometry of the stripper section, the equilibrium thickness was carried out at a gas throughput of 9 SCCM, resulting in a value of 2.3 × 1016 cm−2.

Accurately determining the ratio of stable carbon isotopes (12C and 13C) to 14C is fundamental for radiocarbon dating by AMS. Since ion velocity depends on both energy and mass, carbon isotopes with different masses exhibit slightly different velocities at the same energy, which in turn leads to differences in their charge-exchange cross-sections. This research indicates that, after reaching the equilibrium gas thickness in the stripper section, the 14C/12C stripping ratios for charge states 1+, 2+, and 3 + are 1.22, 1.01, and 0.89, respectively, while the corresponding 14C/13C ratios are 1.52, 1.09, and 0.76. These results demonstrate the inherent isotopic fractionation occurring during the stripping process. In practical AMS radiocarbon dating, such differences are corrected through calibration against standards; nevertheless, the present findings highlight the intrinsic nature of these effects and emphasize the necessity of careful calibration to ensure accurate 14C quantification.

Additionally, the calculated results were validated with measurements from reference86, showing excellent agreement between the model and the experimental data. This further confirms the reliability of the calculation approach used in this study.

Finally, the optimized geometry of the stripper section was constructed and experimentally tested. The pressure was measured in the low-energy section, stripper section, charge-exchange cell, and high-energy section. The obtained values were compared with the simulation results, and this comparison confirmed the validity of the simulation, as the measured pressure values showed good agreement with the simulated results.

The results demonstrate that this optimized geometry and structure are capable of achieving the required atomic density for reaching the equilibrium thickness of the carbon beam, with a length approximately one-third that of common stripper sections used in tandem accelerators.
