Translation and validation of the Norwegian ABCD Risk Questionnaire to assess cardiovascular disease knowledge and risk perception prior to myocardial infarction
Authors: Virginia De Martin Topranin, Gunhild Brørs, Julie Caroline Sæther, Eva Gerdts, Siri Vangen, Anja Bye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe aim of this study was to translate the Attitudes and Beliefs about Cardiovascular Disease (ABCD) Risk Questionnaire into Norwegian and assess its psychometric properties among individuals with a history of myocardial infarction.DesignThe study adopted a cross-sectional design. The original questionnaire was translated into Norwegian and adapted for use in the target population. The Norwegian version was pilot tested in a sample of patients and then validated in the target population.SettingNorway, using a web-based solution to collect data.PatientsA random sample of Norwegian individuals <85 years old with a history of myocardial infarction and no cardiovascular disease before their first myocardial infarction.Main outcome measuresInternal consistency was tested using Cronbach’s α and test–retest reliability using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Difficulty and discrimination indices were determined for the Knowledge scale. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to assess structural validity of the Risk scale.ResultsData for 746 participants (mean age, SD: 66.4, 10.3 years), of which 26.9% females were analysed. The Norwegian version showed satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α 0.73–0.79) but modest test–retest reliability (ICC 0.35–0.64). The Knowledge scale showed moderate difficulty (0.39–0.84) and good discrimination power (0.44–0.60). The one-factor model CFA for each scale achieved acceptable fit, and the four-factor model showed moderate fit (root mean square error of approximation=0.05, standardised root mean squared residual=0.07, Comparative Fit Index=0.91, Tucker-Lewis Index=0.88).ConclusionsThe Norwegian translated ABCD Risk Questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties and can be considered a useful instrument for assessing knowledge and risk perception among individuals with a history of myocardial infarction.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease accounts for approximately one third of global deaths, with coronary heart disease (CHD) being the leading cause of mortality both in women and men.1 Although mortality and incidence of cardiovascular disease have decreased in Norway, more than 40 000 individuals are diagnosed with a CHD condition every year and 1 in 5 persons is either affected by the disease or at high risk requiring preventive treatment.2 Thus, CHD remains a major health concern despite being largely preventable with lifestyle interventions or preventive medical treatment.

Among other factors, individuals’ knowledge and risk perception related to CHD are crucial for adopting and maintaining a healthy lifestyle to reduce CHD risk. Evidence suggests that accurate knowledge of CHD risk and its consequences increases the likelihood of individuals taking preventive measures to minimise the risk.3 Assessing and enhancing awareness of CHD may therefore hold particular importance within certain patient populations. Individuals who have experienced a myocardial infarction constitute a vulnerable group that can provide critical insights into existing gaps in CHD awareness. A thorough understanding of these individuals’ knowledge and risk perception of CHD prior to their first myocardial infarction has important implications for prevention and care, such as informing public health campaigns for primary prevention. Furthermore, improving CHD awareness in this population has the potential to strengthen adherence to evidence-based prevention strategies, thereby reducing the likelihood of recurrent events.

Most studies assessing knowledge and perception of CHD risk relied on non-validated tools. Non-validated tools may produce biased results, as their questions might fail to accurately and consistently reflect individuals’ perspectives or assess the constructs they are intended to measure. Woringer and colleagues developed a brief, validated questionnaire to assess knowledge, risk perception, beliefs and attitudes regarding cardiovascular disease, the Attitudes and Beliefs about Cardiovascular Disease (ABCD) Risk Questionnaire.3 Unlike previous validated tools, the ABCD Risk Questionnaire covers all these aspects related to cardiovascular disease awareness.3 The authors demonstrated a good agreement between perceived risk measured by the questionnaire and predicted cardiovascular disease risk.3 The ABCD Risk Questionnaire has been translated and culturally adapted into several languages demonstrating good psychometric properties.47 Furthermore, the questionnaire has been validated in a larger sample of the general population in the UK which resulted in an edited version including a new section about smoking behaviour.8 Overall, the ABCD Risk Questionnaire is a reliable and valid survey tool. However, the properties of this questionnaire have not been evaluated in specific patient populations, such as individuals who have experienced a myocardial infarction. Moreover, a version of the ABCD Risk Questionnaire is not available in any Scandinavian language.

Hence, the aim of this study was to (1) translate the ABCD Risk Questionnaire into Norwegian, (2) adapt it for use among individuals with a history of myocardial infarction and (3) assess the psychometric properties of the questionnaire in this population.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study adopted a cross-sectional design using a web-based solution to collect data. The translated questionnaire has been piloted at a Norwegian university hospital, while the rest of the data collection took place digitally in Norway. The study adhered to the COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health status Measurement INstruments (COSMIN) taxonomy throughout the validity process.9 To ensure appropriate reporting the study was performed in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.10

Participants

A random sample of Norwegian female and male individuals <85 years old who have experienced a first-ever myocardial infarction during the last 3 years were invited to participate in the study. Participants were not eligible to participate if they had been diagnosed with myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke or if they underwent percutaneous coronary intervention before their first myocardial infarction.

The Norwegian translated ABCD risk questionnaire

According to the original version, the Norwegian version of the ABCD Risk Questionnaire includes 28 items and constitutes one Knowledge of Risk and Prevention scale (eight items, here called Knowledge), and four Risk scales, namely: (1) Perceived Risk of Heart Attack or Stroke (five items), (2) Perceived Benefits and Intentions to Exercise (six items), (3) Perceived Benefit and Healthy Eating Intentions (four items) and (4) Benefits and Intentions to Stop Smoking (five items). The Knowledge scale consists of the response options true/false/do not know. Correct responses are scored as 1, whereas 0 is assigned to incorrect or ‘don’t know’ answers. A higher sum score indicates more knowledge about cardiovascular disease risk and prevention. The Risk scales are scored on a 4-point scale that ranges from 1=strongly disagree to 4=strongly agree and a score of 0 is applied to ‘non applicable’ answers. A higher sum score indicates higher perception of risk, higher perceived benefits and readiness for change. Questionnaire items and scoring system are presented in table 1.

Translation procedure

We obtained permission from the authors to translate the ABCD Risk Questionnaire8 into Norwegian. First, two native Norwegian speakers with a very good command of English independently translated the ABCD Risk Questionnaire into Norwegian. The two versions were reconciled by a third person resolving any discrepancies. The reconciled version was back translated into English by a native English speaker blind to the original version and any inconsistencies were discussed. Next, representatives from user participation organisations, including healthcare professionals, provided their comments on the translated questionnaire. The preliminary translation was then reviewed by a professional proofreader (Semantix Translations Norway AS), who conducted harmonisation across languages and suggested changes until a consensus with the research team was reached.

Pilot testing

The interim translation of the questionnaire was pilot tested on a convenience sample of Norwegian-speaking individuals who had recently experienced a myocardial infarction, recruited from the Rehabilitation Clinic at St Olavs Hospital. Current guidelines recommend a pilot sample of 10–15 participants.11 However, saturation of responses was achieved after eight participants, and pilot testing was therefore deemed satisfactory. Sociodemographic characteristics of participants included in the pilot-testing are presented in online supplemental file 1, table S1. Participants completed the questionnaire digitally on a secure computer administered by the research team. Immediately after, an individual interview was conducted. Participants were asked whether each part of the questionnaire was: (1) difficult to answer, (2) confusing, (3) difficult to understand and (4) upsetting/offensive. In addition to any comments, the participants were asked to reword the question. This step resulted in a number of changes to the questionnaire and therefore, responses collected during pilot testing were not included in the final analysis. Pilot-testing participants were not invited to participate in the subsequent data collection.

Cross-cultural adaptation

If feedback or suggestions were provided by the participants during the interview, these suggestions were considered when revising the questionnaire. The verb tense of the Risk scale was modified from present to past to adapt the questionnaire to our target population. For instance, item 9 ‘It is likely that I will have a heart attack or stroke some time in my life’ was modified to ‘It was likely that I would have a heart attack or stroke some time in my life’.

Sociodemographic measures

Participants were also asked to report some sociodemographic characteristics in the questionnaire, including sex, age, smoking status, family history of myocardial infarction, any diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis, ethnicity, native language, education level, income, occupation, marital and living status. We considered biological sex (ie, assigned at birth) in the present study. Thus, we refer to ‘female’ and ‘male’ participants according to the Sex and Gender Equity in Research guidelines.12

Data collection

Participants were identified through the Norwegian Myocardial Infarction Registry, which provides a complete overview of all individuals experiencing myocardial infarction in Norway every year. We used the digital platform eFORSK to recruit participants, collect their informed consent and administer the questionnaire. eFORSK is an independent information and communication technology that offers solutions for electronic data collection. According to current guidelines, we aimed to include as large a sample as possible.13 A respondent-to-item ratio of 15:1 was applied, yielding a target sample of 420 participants. Given the web-based data collection, a participation rate of 42–46% was anticipated,14 necessitating about 1000 invitations. To further account for deceased participants and incomplete responses requiring exclusion, a larger number of invitations was distributed. A total of 1948 questionnaires were distributed to eligible participants through Helsenorge.no or secure digital mailbox between February and May 2025. A flowchart of the exclusion process is shown in figure 1. Participants received two reminders 7 and 14 days after the first invitation. In addition, all participants were invited to take part in the retest of the questionnaire 21 days after their initial completion. Each participant was assigned a unique identifier on completing the questionnaire. In cases where a participant submitted multiple responses, only the earliest entry was retained for analysis.

Statistical analyses

Data were first checked for completeness and questionnaire responses that had two or more blanks in at least one of the questionnaire scales were excluded. The remaining blanks were treated as missing data. Descriptive results were presented as mean with SD for continuous variables and frequencies with percentage of total responses for categorical variables. Normality was assessed through visual inspection of the data distribution using histograms and Q–Q plots, which did not reveal any substantial deviations from normality. Except for descriptive statistics, the response option ‘not applicable’ was treated as missing data in the analysis. ‘Not applicable’ responses on the scale Benefits and Intentions to Stop Smoking mostly reflect structural missingness determined by participant smoking status. To ensure consistency across scales, the full sample was retained for the main analysis. Floor effect (lowest possible score) and ceiling effect (highest possible score) were estimated for each Risk scale excluding missing and ‘not applicable’ values. A threshold of 15% in the lowest and highest possible score was considered to define a floor and ceiling effect, respectively.15 A sum score for each scale was calculated for every participant and then averaged across participants. Items 12, 18, 22 and 28 were reverse coded for the analysis.

Internal consistency was tested using Cronbach’s α, Guttman’s lambda 6, Revelle’s beta coefficient and McDonald’s ω. A value between 0.70 and 0.95 was considered acceptable.16 Mean interitem correlations, corrected item–total correlations and Cronbach’s α if item deleted were calculated. Values between 0.20 and 0.40 were considered optimal for mean interitem correlations.17 Items with corrected item–total correlations <0.30 were flagged for review, and α if item deleted was examined to judge whether removal improved reliability. Item removal is usually justified when dropping such item increases α and the item has a corrected item–total correlations <0.300.18 Test–retest reliability was assessed using Spearman correlations (ρ) and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). Based on the COSMIN criteria, values ≥0.70 were considered sufficient.16

Because the Knowledge scale consists of factual items assessing knowledge about cardiovascular risk and prevention, traditional consistency and reliability indices are not optimal indicators of measurement quality. Therefore, we assessed item difficulty and discrimination instead for the Knowledge scale. As previously performed by Guo et al,4 the difficulty index was determined by dividing the number of correct responses by the total number of responses; a higher index indicating an easier item. The ideal range of difficulty is between 0.20 and 0.80. To assess how well each item differentiates between high-performing and low-performing participants, corrected item-total correlations were calculated. A correlation value greater than 0.40 reflects good discriminatory ability.

A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using the maximum likelihood estimator was performed to assess the structural validity of the questionnaire. Missing data were handled using full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation, which incorporates all available data without casewise deletion, providing unbiased parameter estimates under the missing at random assumption. Model fit was evaluated using the χ2 test, the Tucker-Lewis and Comparative Fit Indexes (TLI and CFI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and the standardised root mean squared residual (SRMR). Model fit was considered acceptable if CFI or TLI >0.95 and RMSEA <0.06 or SRMR <0.080.1 In line with previous validations of the ABCD Risk Questionnaire, the Knowledge scale was not included in this analysis as it uses a different response score compared with the Risk scale. First, a one-factor model was tested for each scale to ensure that the translation did not distort the internal structure of each scale and verify that items in each translated scale still reflected one underlying construct. Correlated residuals were assessed for each one-factor model and values <0.20 were deemed acceptable. One-factor models were modified by sequentially adding correlated residuals based on modification indices until an acceptable model fit was reached. Next, we fitted a four-factor model to the Risk scales according to Bowyer et al8 to test whether the structure of the original questionnaire was preserved in the Norwegian translated version. The analysis was repeated allowing residuals to correlate within each scale. To assess the impact of ‘not applicable’ responses, two sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, the CFA was repeated excluding participants with any ‘not applicable’ response on the non-smoking scales (ie, Perceived Risk of Heart Attack or Stroke, Perceived Benefits and Intentions to Exercise and Perceived Benefit and Healthy Eating Intentions), here called complete-case analysis (n=177). Second, the CFA was repeated restricting the sample to people who smoked previously and people who smoke currently only (n=482), to evaluate the impact of structural missingness on the smoking-related scale.

Significance level was set at 0.05. All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software V.4.3.119 with the packages ‘dplyr’ (V.1.1.4), purr (V.1.2.1) and ‘tidyr’ (V.1.3.1) for data preparation, ‘psych‘ (V.2.6.1), ‘Hmisc‘ (V.5.2–5) and ‘lavaan‘ (V.0.6–21) for data analysis, and ‘semPlot’ (V.1.1.8) for data visualisation.

Patient and public involvement

Representatives from user participation organisations, that is, the Norwegian Health Association, the Norwegian Women’s Public Health Association and the Norwegian Research Centre for Women’s Health were involved from the early planning phase of the project. They contributed to shaping the study aims and ensuring that the project addressed issues relevant to patients and practical needs within healthcare services. Representatives from user participation organisations were actively involved in the translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the questionnaire, providing feedback on wording and cultural appropriateness to ensure clarity, relevance and acceptability for the target population. Patients representative of the target population participated in pilot testing of the questionnaire to assess comprehensibility, relevance and to ensure the questions were not upsetting or offensive. Patients’ feedback helped assess the time required to complete the questionnaire and the burden of participation in the study. Representatives from user participation organisations will play an important role in dissemination and implementation of the findings. They will contribute to decisions regarding which results are most relevant to patient communities and how these should be communicated, including format and channels of dissemination.

Results

Characteristics of the sample

In total, 754 (38.7% of total invitations) participants completed the online questionnaire and 410 participated in the retest. On checking for completeness, data from 746 (401 for the retest) participants were considered for the final analysis. Mean (SD) age for the total sample was 66.4 (10.3) years, of which males 65.7 (10.3) years and females 68.3 (10.3) years. Proportion of missing data was ≤0.5% across all sociodemographic variables of both the main and retest samples. Internal consistency and reliability

All the Risk scales showed satisfactory internal consistency (table 4). Cronbach’s α coefficients ranged from 0.73 to 0.79 across scales, above the threshold of 0.70.16 Good internal consistency was also confirmed by McDonald’s ω values between 0.82 and 0.85. The corrected item-total correlations were acceptable except for item 27 in the Benefits and Intentions to Stop Smoking scale (item-total correlation: 0.16), which was also found to substantially increase the Cronbach’s α if deleted. Items 12 and 23 also increased α if deleted but did not have low corrected item-total correlations (0.33 and 0.40 respectively). Item-level results of the internal consistency analysis are shown in online supplemental table S5.

The results of the test–retest reliability analysis are presented in table 5 and online supplemental table S6. At scale level, Spearman ρ ranged between 0.45 and 0.63, indicating moderate correlations between test and retest. However, none of the Risk scales showed sufficient ICC. At item level, several items showed poor reliability and some had both weak correlation and low ICC (ie, items 15, 16, 19, 23, 24, 26 and 27).

Item difficulty and discrimination for the Knowledge scale

The Knowledge scale showed a 64.3% mean proportion of correct responses across participants. The difficulty index ranged from 0.39 to 0.84, indicating moderate difficulty. Item 3 and 5 looked particularly easy for participants, whereas item 8 had the highest difficulty level, possibly owing to the negative statement in this item. The discrimination coefficients ranged from 0.44 to 0.60, which can be interpreted as good discrimination power. Complete results are presented in online supplemental table S7.

Construct validity

First, a one-factor model was fit for each scale. The original model fit was acceptable for the scale Perceived Benefit and Healthy Eating Intentions, while only one adjustment adding correlated residuals was needed to achieve a satisfactory fit for all the other scales (table 6). Despite the acceptable fit, the standardised residual variances remained large for almost all items in all models (online supplemental table S8).

We also calculated the estimated correlations between the latent factors in the model (figure 2 and online supplemental table S10). We did not observe high positive correlations (>0.70) which indicates good discriminant validity. Factor 1, corresponding to the scale Perceived Risk of Heart Attack or Stroke showed a very low correlation with the other factors (between 0.02 and 0.09), suggesting this construct is relatively independent. The other factors had moderate correlations, indicating related but distinct constructs.

Compared with the main sample, the complete-case sample excluding participants with any ‘not applicable’ response on the non-smoking scales was significantly younger, had higher educational attainment, higher household income and a higher proportion of employed individuals (online supplemental table S11). The complete-case CFA showed somewhat deteriorated model fit compared with the main analysis (RMSEA=0.07, SRMR=0.09, CFI=0.87, TLI=0.84), suggesting partially informative missingness on these scales. The smoking-related CFA showed minimal change in fit indices (RMSEA=0.06, SRMR=0.08, CFI=0.89, TLI=0.86), supporting the adequacy of FIML for handling structural missingness on the smoking-related scale. Complete results of both sensitivity analyses are reported in online supplemental table S12.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to translate the ABCD Risk Questionnaire into Norwegian as well as assess the psychometric properties of the questionnaire among persons that suffered from myocardial infarction. The main findings are: (1) we performed a robust translation of the questionnaire and cultural adaptation to the Norwegian context according to the guidelines, (2) all Risk scales showed satisfactory internal consistency, (3) the Knowledge scale showed moderate difficulty and good discrimination power and (4) the one-factor model CFA for each scale achieved acceptable fit after allowing a single modification each, and the hypothesised four-factor model showed moderate fit.

Response distribution

The proportion of missing responses was low across all items, suggesting a good comprehension of the items. There were indications that ‘not applicable’ responses not associated with smoking status were not missing completely at random, as the complete-case sample was systematically younger and of higher socioeconomic status compared with the full sample.

Two items showed floor effects and six items showed ceiling effects. Given that the sample consisted of individuals at high cardiovascular risk, the prevalent ceiling effects are not surprising, as this group would be expected to have heightened risk perception regarding cardiovascular disease. A ceiling effect can signal that the scale does not offer enough high-end response options,15 making it difficult to differentiate participants with particularly high risk perception. On the other hand, the items showing floor effects may suggest that certain aspects of cardiovascular risk perception remained low in this high-risk group, potentially pointing to areas where targeted education could be beneficial.

Internal consistency and reliability

The Norwegian version of the ABCD Risk Questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, indicating that the items within each of the Risk scales measured a coherent underlying construct. The Norwegian version of the questionnaire showed slightly lower α coefficients but consistent ω coefficients with those reported in the original validation of the questionnaire (α, 0.84–0.86; ω, 0.82–0.85).8 Cronbach’s α was slightly lower also compared with previous language adaptations of the ABCD Risk Questionnaire initially developed by Woringer et al3 (Dutch version, 0.84–0.93; Chinese version, 0.80–0.94; Hungarian version, 0.76–0.95).4 5 7 Overall, these results suggest that the internal structure of the instrument is preserved in the Norwegian context.

At the item level, our study showed lower values of inter-item correlations across all Risk scales compared with the original validation of the questionnaire.8 These attenuated correlations could be due to a lower variability in responses, possibly owing to a more homogenous sample in the present study. Removal of item 27 ‘If I stopped smoking, it would have reduced my chances of having a heart attack or stroke’ seemed justified based on the corrected item-total correlations and α if item deleted. In the original questionnaire, item 27 did not show any issues compared with the other items in the scale.8 This inconsistency could be related to the characteristics of our sample rather than the questionnaire structure and thus, further investigation is needed before applying any changes.

The test–retest reliability of the Norwegian ABCD Risk Questionnaire was moderate, showing acceptable Spearman’s ρ values, but only modest ICC estimates across the Risk scales. The ICC values below the conventional threshold of 0.70 suggest that the stability of the instrument over time was lower than desirable.16 The suboptimal test–retest reliability may be partly attributable to learning and recall effects after the first administration. Participants may have reflected on their initial responses, leading to altered answers during the second administration. In addition, exposure to the content during the first completion may have changed participants’ awareness or understanding of the construct, thereby introducing genuine score change rather than measurement error. Alternatively, the time window (ie, 3 weeks) between administrations might have been too long, allowing for true variation in awareness, thereby affecting the reliability estimates. In a previous study assessing the psychometric properties of the Chinese ABCD Risk Questionnaire, a 2-week interval was used to calculate test–retest reliability obtaining acceptable ICC estimates (0.788, 95% CI 0.527 to 0.902).4 On the other hand, it is unlikely that the cardiac event itself or the cardiac rehabilitation course has influenced the test–retest reliability. The time interval between the myocardial infarction and the participation in the study was more than a year for all participants and individuals who experience a myocardial infarction in Norway are usually invited to participate in the cardiac rehabilitation course shortly after the event. Overall, the moderate reproducibility suggests that the tool is not suited to detect individual-level changes, but it still could be useful to describe group-level differences. Future research should re-examine test–retest reliability using a shorter retest interval to minimise temporal variability and improve measurement precision.

Item difficulty and discrimination for the knowledge scale

Overall, difficulty and discrimination indices for the Knowledge scale in the Norwegian version were lower than what was found in a previous study (difficulty, 0.44–0.85; discrimination, 0.57–0.71).4 This suggests that the items were generally more challenging for our participants and less effective in discriminating knowledge levels compared with the Chinese version.4 These findings could be due to a more homogenous sample and differences in awareness of cardiovascular risk factors between the two populations. Some evidence shows that persons with higher cardiovascular risk often have lower knowledge and awareness of cardiovascular risk factors and symptoms.20 21 Restricting our study to individuals with a history of myocardial infarction and thereby at higher cardiovascular risk might have resulted in an average lower knowledge and awareness regarding cardiovascular disease compared with the more diverse population in the Chinese study.4 On the other hand, subtle linguistic or contextual differences introduced during translation cannot be ruled out and further refinement of specific items could enhance the discriminatory power of the Norwegian version.

Construct validity

When one-factor models were examined separately for each scale, model fit was satisfactory following minimal modifications, which suggests that each scale captured a coherent construct. Nevertheless, high residual variances, correlated errors and large modification indices persisted, indicating that items within each scale shared variance beyond the underlying factor. This may be due to similarities in item wording or content overlap and thus further refinement of specific items may improve construct structure and overall model fit in this population.

Our confirmatory four-factor model demonstrated less acceptable fit compared with the original validation of the ABCD Risk Questionnaire (RMSEA=0.06 and SRMR=0.05, CFI=0.95, TLI=0.94),8 even after allowing residual covariances within each scale. While acceptable RMSEA and SRMR values indicated that the model captured the overall structure, the low CFI and TLI possibly reflected model misspecifications in the adapted version. High standardised factor loadings indicated that items related well to their underlying constructs, but the factors did not fully account for item-level variability as shown by the large residuals. Overall, it seems that the latent structure is less robust in the Norwegian translated version compared with the original one. However, these results might not necessarily reflect translation issues, but rather cultural differences or sample characteristics that influence how participants interpret and respond to the items. Previously, the ABCD Risk Questionnaire has indeed only been validated in samples of the general population rather than patient cohorts.

Strengths and limitations

This study represents the first validation of the ABCD Risk Questionnaire in the Norwegian context and among persons with a history of myocardial infarction, thereby extending the use of the questionnaire into patient populations. The present study was conducted in a large sample, which increases statistical power, precision of estimates and generalisability. Moreover, the random invitation strategy reduced the risk of selection bias arising from convenience samples, which was commonly seen in previous validation studies of the ABCD Risk Questionnaire. The validation process adhered to COSMIN guidelines, ensuring a comprehensive assessment of measurement properties.

Some limitations should also be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. The low participation rate may still limit representability. Despite random invitations, participation was voluntary, which may have introduced self-selection bias. A total of eight participants (1.1%) were excluded due to two or more blanks. Although no systematic differences were identified between excluded and included participants on available baseline characteristics, the missing data mechanism cannot be formally verified and a small degree of selection bias cannot be entirely excluded. Although FIML estimation was used to handle missing data, the complete-case sensitivity analysis showed a degree of fit decline, suggesting that missingness on the non-smoking scales may be partially informative. If participants systematically rated items as inapplicable for reasons related to their health or socio-economic status, residual bias in parameter estimates for the non-smoking scales cannot be fully excluded and their structural validity in this population should be interpreted with caution.

The Norwegian version of the ABCD Risk Questionnaire was adapted for use among individuals with a history of myocardial infarction. Despite the consequent sample’s relative homogeneity ensuring internal comparability, it also limits generalisability to populations with different characteristics. The modest model fit and large residual variances suggest that some items may require further refinement or cultural adaptation. The proportion of people who smoke in our sample was low and thus psychometric estimates for the scale Benefits and Intentions to Stop Smoking should be interpreted with caution. Low participation may result in unstable internal consistency and reliability, reduced validity evidence and limited generalisability. Since the scale Benefits and Intentions to Stop Smoking is relevant only for people who smoke, computation of a single overall sum score for the entire questionnaire is not appropriate and scales should rather be interpreted independently.

Conclusions

The Norwegian version of the ABCD Risk Questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties and can be considered a useful instrument for assessing knowledge and risk perception of cardiovascular disease risk among individuals with a history of myocardial infarction. Despite some limitations, the tool showed acceptable internal consistency and structural validity in line with COSMIN standards. Considering the moderate reproducibility, the questionnaire is not recommended for individual follow-up. At group level, this brief, validated questionnaire is suitable for both research and practical applications, including population-based surveys and educational programme evaluations. Future research should focus on further improving the questionnaire’s psychometric properties by refining items with suboptimal fit. Comparing the questionnaire results with risk prediction models score such as Systematic COronary Risk Evaluation (SCORE)2 or actual cardiovascular outcomes would help to assess its ability to discriminate participants and its sensitivity to change following targeted interventions.
