Late posttonsillectomy haemorrhage, differences in unilateral versus bilateral tonsillectomy, a retrospective epidemiological multicentric study
Authors: Jan Vodicka, Viktor Chrobok, Martin Chovanec, Milan Urik, Bretislav Gal, Richard Salzman, Rami Katra, Petr Skopek, Jan Kolin, Zdenek Knizek, Hana Dolezalova, Libor Sychra, Patrik Bursa, Lenka Jetmarova, Silvia Berkova, Pavel Strejcek, Josef Hajek, Zdenek Kucera, Jakub Syrovatka, Tomas Kostlivy, David Slouka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTonsillectomy (TE) is one of the most performed head and neck surgeries. Patients range from children to seniors, and there is a wide range of indications and various complications related to TE. The most feared complication is post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage (PTH). Multicentric retrospective study, conducted between 2014 and 2018, includes data from 8166 patients who underwent TE. Among these patients, 3889 were men (47.62%) and 4277 were women (52.28%), with a mean age of 29.06 years. For this study, we analysed a total of 14,030 unilateral tonsillectomies (resected tonsils), of which 2,302 were performed primarily as unilateral surgeries, while 11,728 were part of bilateral procedures. Late post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage (LPTH) was significantly associated with the type of surgery performed (unilateral versus bilateral procedure; p < 0.0001). In the unilateral group, bleeding occurred in 217 cases (9.43%), while in the bilateral group, it was noted in 1062 cases (18.11%). When analysing the total sample (14030 unilateral tonsillectomies), the incidence of LPTH was found to be 9.12%. The occurrence of bleeding in our sample was influenced by several factors, including hospital choice, age, and gender (p < 0.0001). Additionally, the use of anticoagulants was associated with bleeding (p = 0.0449), as was antiplatelet medication (p = 0.0160). However, factors such as the spectrum of indications, surgical technique, surgeon experience, and the use of electrocoagulation during surgery did not impact LPTH. The severity of bleeding was linked to the use of anticoagulants (p = 0.0002) and antiplatelet medication (p = 0.0404). Our study highlighted the importance of methodology when studying LPTH as the outcome of unilateral and bilateral TE procedures. LPTH occurrence was most influenced by the choice of hospital and the patient’s age and gender. The severity of bleeding was affected by the use of anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents. The manner in which LPTH was treated did not affect the likelihood of LPTH recurrence.Keywords: Tonsillectomy, Haemorrhage, Late post-tonsillectomy haemorrhageSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Tonsillectomy is one of the most performed head and neck surgeries1,2. Patients range widely in age from young children to seniors3–5. The indications for this procedure are diverse, including common diagnoses such as peritonsillar abscess (PTA), chronic tonsillitis (CHRTON), recurrent tonsillitis (RECTON), and sleep-disordered breathing; most often obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA). Additionally, there are rarer indications, including halitosis, malocclusion, dysphagia, trauma, elongated styloid process, nephropathy, and psoriasis4,5. In most cases, we perform elective, planned TE, whereas when a PTA is present, an immediate TE, referred to as “a hot,” is preferred6–8.

There are several complications associated with TE. Early complications include post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage (PTH), pain, nausea, vomiting, and dehydration. Delayed complications can involve issues such as jaw and teeth problems, dysphagia, scarring in the palatal arch area, perforation of the soft palate, swallowing difficulties (including velopharyngeal insufficiency), infections of the upper airways, emphysema, sensory symptoms, altered sense of smell and taste, voice, and speech problems9–14.

The most urgent complication is PTH15–17. PTH can be classified based on timing: early PTH occurs within the first 24 h, while LPTH (late post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage) arises several days after the procedure18,19. PTH involves bleeding in an unsecured airway, and in some cases, it can be so severe that surgical revision is necessary. Intense PTH may lead to aspiration or haemorrhagic shock20.

It is well-established that PTH remains an important and current issue in head and neck surgery. The literature includes studies focusing on TE as a standalone procedure21,22 and TE performed as part of larger surgical procedures. This is often the case in paediatric surgeries, such as adenotonsillectomy, OSA surgeries, or uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP); PTH can present a problem in extensive oncological surgeries23,24.

The aforementioned surgical conditions can influence the risks and complications of TE. As a result, in order to eliminate other confounding variables, most studies on this subject focus on isolated TE rather than TE performed as part of a larger surgical intervention.

PTH is classified into two types: primary, known as early, occurring within 24 h of TE, and secondary, referred to as secondary, late, or delayed, with a time frame of over 24 h following the TE. Studies are available from large populations of separate adult cohorts25–27, cohorts of children28–30 and mixed population cohorts31,32, reporting the most often days with occurrence of PTH33–35. Most of the studies in the research of PTH are retrospective12,21,36–40. In our review of prospective studies, we only found two cases, both of which had smaller sample sizes7,41.

Current scientific data are inconclusive regarding the best TE technique. The literature does not indicate that there are significant differences between cold steel TE and TE performed by different devices such as a monopolar knife, by coblation, or using argon plasma coagulation, ultrasonic devices, and radiofrequency1,22,42–44. Similarly, to date there is no consensus on the association between the use of NSAID analgesics (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory analgesics drugs) after surgery and postoperative bleeding45–47. Opinions furthermore differ on the association between the indications for TE and postoperative bleeding48–50 or on the effect of antibiotics on postoperative bleeding49,51,52. The literature agrees on the negative impact of age29,53,54, smoking status55–57 and male gender58–60.

Most studies have a single-centre design, with cohort sizes ranging from hundreds of patients in individual studies to thousands of participants in large meta-analyses7,41. To date, no research has compared multiple independent hospitals or clearly stated results from the research where are unilateral TE (UTE) and bilateral TE (BTE) together. A significant limitation of the available literature is the heterogeneity of the reported data, which prevents the establishment of clear recommendations with corresponding levels of evidence.

The aim of this work was to assess the risk factors for PTH occurrence and severity in more than 8,000 patients through a nationwide multicentre retrospective study.

Methods

Group of patients

This retrospective epidemiological multicentric study included 12,318 patients who underwent palatine tonsil surgery between 2014 and 2018. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the data were collected from 18 independent hospitals, including tertiary and regional facilities. The study received approval from the Ethical Committee of University Hospital in Pilsen under protocol code 326/24. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

The cohort consisted of 6413 men (52.06%) and 5905 women (47.94%), with ages ranging from 1 to 94. The average age of the group was 23.76, with a median age of 20.00. Due to not meeting the inclusion criteria, 4152 patients were excluded from the study (for details, please see the flowchart in Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study. TE: tonsillectomy, TT: tonsillotomy, UPPP: uvulopalatopharyngoplasty, UTE: unilateral tonsillectomy, BTE: bilateral tonsillectomy.

The exclusion criteria included: patients who underwent only a tonsillotomy, those with an oncologic diagnosis requiring TE, cases where TE was part of more extensive surgery (e.g., uvulopalatopharyngoplasty), instances of PTA with a prior peritonsillar incision during outpatient care, and cases with incomplete documentation.

In the final sample, 8166 respondents underwent unilateral or bilateral extracapsular TE. Among them, 3889 were men (47.62%), and 4277 were women (52.28%), with a mean age of 27.33 years and a median age of 25. For the purposes of this study, we analysed 14,030 unilateral TEs (for details, see the Methods section below).

The sample was divided into two subgroups: unilateral tonsillectomy (UTE) and bilateral tonsillectomy (BTE). The UTE group contained 2302 patients (28.19%), while the BTE group had 5864 patients (71.81%). Gender and age distribution are shown in Fig. 2.

Methods used

For the purpose of this study, we classified patients into two distinct subgroups: UTE (n = 2302) and BTE (n = 5864), subgroups exhibited differences in bleeding rates, with UTE bleeding incidents recorded at 9.43% and BTE at 18.11% (detailed results are available in the Results section).

To provide a comprehensive overview of all 8166 patients, we calculated data for 14,030 unilateral TEs (resected tonsils), which demonstrated an overall bleeding rate of 9.12%. Of this total, 2302 TEs were performed as primary, unilateral procedures, while 11,728 TEs were part of bilateral interventions.

The classification of bleeding is divided into five stages based on severity:

Stage A: defined as “anamnestic,” this stage refers to situations in which patients report a history of bleeding but do not present with bleeding at the time of examination. Care involves observation, rest, applying a cold neck bandage, and administering intravenous haemostatics (e.g., Etamsylatum).

Stage B: This stage involves the presence of a clot in the tonsillar bed or mild bleeding that can be managed by a tampon compression. Next care includes observation, rest, a cold neck bandage, and intravenous haemostatics (e.g., Etamsylatum).

Stage C: In this stage, there is active bleeding that requires electrocoagulation, performed under local or general anaesthesia. Next care comprises observation, blood substitutes according to the patient’s state, rest, a cold neck bandage, and intravenous haemostatics (e.g., Etamsylatum).

Stage D: This stage is characterized by dramatic bleeding, with the potential risk of permanent consequences or death. Care must be administered under general anaesthesia and may include electrocoagulation, suturing within the pharynx (e.g., palatal arches or tonsillar bed), or tamponade of the pharynx. Aftercare involves intensive care unit monitoring, blood substitutes according to the patient’s state, a cold neck bandage, and intravenous haemostatics (e.g., Etamsylatum).

Stage E: This final stage refers to bleeding following the death of the patient.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SW SAS (Cary, NC, USA), graphs performed using SW STATISTICA (StatSoft, Inc., Tulsa, OK, USA).

Descriptive statistics such as frequencies, mean, standard deviation, variance, median, minimum, and maximum were calculated to describe the investigated parameters. Box plots, pie charts, and histograms were used to present the data graphically.

Nonparametric ANOVA tests, including the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and the Kruskal-Wallis test, were utilized to compare age and the number of bleedings among the studied groups. The Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was employed for frequency comparisons.

The relationships between the variables were assessed using Spearman’s correlation.

The time of bleeding after surgery was analysed using the Kaplan-Meier survival method, and comparisons were made using the Log-rank test. A risk function was employed to assess the probability of bleeding on specific days following surgery. The clinical impact of various factors was evaluated using the Hazard Ratio within a Cox Regression Hazard Model. Additionally, classification and regression trees (C&RT) were utilized as a multivariate analysis tool to identify protective and risk groups for post-surgery bleeding. The optimization of the Cox Regression Hazard Model served as the foundation for C&RT, with a minimum sample size of 50 patients established for the analysis.

Prognostic factors for second bleeding were assessed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and their clinical impact was evaluated through Odds Ratios.

Statistical significance was set at 5%.

Results

For clarity, the results are structured by individual risk factors, each presented in a separate subsection.

Occurrence of bleeding

The total sample had two subgroups: UTE and BTE. 2302 unilateral TEs (28.18%) were performed in the UTE subgroup, while 5864 bilateral TEs (71.81%) were conducted in the BTE group. The overall group included 14,030 unilateral TEs.

LPTH was statistically significantly associated with the type of surgery (UTE vs. BTE; p < 0.0001). In the one-sided group, there were 217 cases of bleeding (9.43%), while in the both-sided group, there were 1062 cases (18.11%).

An analysis of the total sample, which included 14,030 unilateral TEs overall, revealed that 1279 patients experienced LPTH (9.12%). Discussion

In the literature, the reported incidence of post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage varies considerably, largely because of differences in definitions of what constitutes a bleeding event. Saravakos and Hartwein reported that “depending upon the definition of PTH, the reported rates vary from 1% to 40%” in their single-institution study, where the overall incidence was 11.5%61. Similarly, Liu et al. noted that published rates of post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage range between 0.28% and 20%, again depending on the methodology and the definition of bleeding used62. Some studies restrict haemorrhage to clinically significant events requiring intervention, while others also include minor or anamnestically reported bleeding. Our methodology intentionally applied an inclusive definition, classifying even self-reported minor bleeding (Stage A) as late post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage. This approach likely explains why our overall incidence is somewhat higher compared with certain single-centre studies, but it provides a comprehensive representation of real-world practice in a nationwide cohort.

The large amount of data are presented in this nationwide retrospective study. We therefore structured the Discussion section by individual risk factors and highlighted the main findings in the same order as in the Results section.

Occurrence of bleeding

At present, there are more than 14,000 scientific papers on the topic of TE and over 3000 that discuss PTH. There is a general consensus on categorizing bleeding into two types: early (or primary) and late (or secondary) bleeding. Some studies focus solely on TEs performed for specific diagnoses63–65, while others compare tonsillectomies with multiple diagnoses26,27,38,66. There are papers that focus solely on unilateral TEs6,35,38 or bilateral procedures21,33,39,67,68. Unfortunately, some papers describe their methodologies in this point of view unclearly8,27,36,37,64,69. In our review of the literature, we found only one paper discussing the comparison between UTE and BTE, authored by Fauck et al.70, which focused solely on PTA. We did not find any studies addressing PTH following UTE versus BTE across various indications for TE. Unilateral TEs are often performed for PTA but they are also performed for other diagnoses. In our work, we analysed 279 cases from 18 hospitals.

In the scientific literature, we encountered a study indicating that the general prevalence of PTH is approximately 6.65%. This study suggested that the difference in PTH rates between unilateral and bilateral procedures is not statistically significant. The authors noted that the lack of significance may be influenced by the differing statistical power of the few studies included in their analysis71. In contrast, our findings show strong statistical differences between UTE and BTE. We found that the rate of LPTH was 9.43% following UTE but increased to 18.11% after BTE (p < 0.0001), hazard ratio 2.025 with a confidence interval of 1.750–2.343). This suggests that the type of tonsillectomy—unilateral versus bilateral—is one of the most significant variables of interest in our study (for details, see Fig. 7). Our results align with the findings of Fauck et al.70 and Hahn et al.35.

Differences of LPTH occurence between UTE and BTE.

In our sample, we identified variations in the relationships between different variables and LPTH in the UTE and BTE groups. To comprehensively evaluate both groups and in consideration of the reasons and results outlined previously, we have opted to examine the entire sample for our study’s objectives. Details on the procedures are provided in the Methods section. The findings for both UTE and BTE are presented to address the evidence of undesirable heterogeneity in the assessment.

In our study, we found that the occurrence of LPTH ranged between hospitals from 3.10% to 18.60% among the total group of unilateral procedures. The choice of hospital proved to be a significant factor (p < 0.0001), although this factor may be partially influenced by the patient demographics. Notably, the ENT departments for children were in the group of the lowest incidence of LPTH. To our knowledge, there are no existing studies in the scientific literature that compare LPTH across anonymised hospitals.

Age was analysed as a continuous variable, which was found to be a statistically significant factor associated with the occurrence of LPTH in the total group (p = 0.0022). This finding is aligned with findings by Kim et al.53 and Spektor et al.29. When we divided the patients into adult and children (aged < 18) groups, we observed a similar trend in the LPTH incidence curve, albeit with lower absolute numbers (see details in Fig. 8). The higher occurrence of LPTH in the adult group was statistically significant (p < 0.0001). We identified a cut off age of 15 years in the total group (p < 0.0001). Notably, children aged 5–9 were represented as a “protected group” in the overall sample, with a p-value of < 0.0001 and a hazard ratio of 0.377, a confidence interval of 0.377–0.493. Similar results were published by Sarny et al.26. Conversely, the age group of 25–29 emerged as the information about anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy was also collected highest risk group in the total sample (p < 0.0001), with a hazard ratio of 1.433 and a confidence interval of 1.231–1.668, similar to the findings of Windfuhr et al.72 and Skoloudik et al.73. Further details can be found in Table 3. Our data therefore provide a direct comparison of pediatric and adult patients, showing that age is an important independent risk factor for LPTH. This comparison also helps to explain part of the variability between hospitals with different patient populations.

Curves representing the LPTH incidence in adults and children.

Gender was identified as a statistically significant factor in the overall group (p < 0.0001). Men had a hazard ratio of 1.405, with a confidence interval of 1.067 to 1.850. (For details see Table 3.) These findings are in accordance with the research of Coordes et al.58, Hsueh et al.59, and Ikoma et al.60.

When analysing bleeding occurrences based on the indication for surgery, we did not find a statistically significant association (p = 0.1930). This is consistent with the studies by Johnston et al.74, Hoddeson et al.4, and Seshamani et al.49. Seshamani’s work indicated that the indications for surgery do not influence the occurrence of LPTH; rather, factors such as the severity of tonsillitis or prolonged antibiotic use in the past year are significant.

Further examining the impact of surgical indications for TE on the incidence of LPTH revealed two noteworthy differences. In the PTA subgroup (details provided in Table 3), the occurrence of LPTH in “a hot” TEs compared to “planned” TEs for PTA was 8.36% versus 11.06% in the total group (p = 0.0067). In our study, the total group had a hazard ratio of 0.741 for “a hot” TEs, with a confidence interval of 0.596 to 0.922. This finding is consistent with Ahmad et al.75 but contradicts the study by Tsikopoulos et al.63. Skoloudik et al.73, who studied a cohort of group of 1492 patients, indicated that PTA poses a greater risk than other indications for tonsillectomy.

Seshamani et al.49 examined the effects of blood thinners on surgical outcomes. They found an increased likelihood of complications after surgery. In our research, anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents were statistically linked to post-tonsillectomy bleeding, though without significant clinical impact. For anticoagulants p = 0.046, and p = 0.0181 for antiplatelet agents; hazard ratio 1.169, confidence interval 1.003–1.362 for anticoagulants, and hazard ratio 1.847, confidence interval 1.110–3.073 for antiplatelet agents. Hazkani et al.76 compared unfractionated heparin with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) and concluded that both can be effectively used in chronically anticoagulated patients without a higher rate of LPTH events, though these patients require multidisciplinary care. Spyropoulos et al.77 supported this finding, highlighting the significant benefits of LMWH, including predictable pharmacokinetics, minimal need for monitoring, and convenient subcutaneous administration.

The literature presents conflicting results regarding the influence of doctor experience on the occurrence of LPTH. In our sample, we found no significant effect, with a p-value of 0.5319. Most TEs were conducted by doctors certified for independent practice: senior doctors (Level 3) performed 9532 cases (68.02%), while certified doctors (Level 2) handled 2432 cases (17.33%). Only 2055 cases (14.65%) were performed by doctors under supervision. Muratori et al.78 reported a similar overall risk of postoperative complications for tonsillectomies performed by residents compared to those conducted by certified surgeons. Leader et al.79 likewise concluded that TEs performed by residents under experienced supervision do not result in a higher incidence of LPTH. In contrast, Sarny et al.26 observed a lower incidence of LPTH following operations performed by junior doctors. These findings contradict the results of Hinton-Bayre et al.80 and Xu et al.66, which described a higher rate of LPTH following TEs performed by junior surgeons.

In agreement with other authors, such as Russo et al.1, Bäck et al.42, and Hinton-Bayre et al.80, our study found that the surgical technique and the use of electrocoagulation during TEs did not prove to be a statistically significant for postoperative bleeding in our group (p = 0.4190; p = 0.9123, respectively). However, the use of suture during TE was associated with a statistically significant result (p = 0.0020), yielding a hazard ratio of 1.393 with a confidence interval of 1.128 to 1.720. Most ENT departments do not routinely use sutures in the oral cavity during TE. Suture is typically employed only in cases where perioperative bleeding cannot be addressed solely by tampon compression or electrocoagulation. In fact, nearly half of the ENT departments monitored did not use any type of suture during surgery or during the treatment of LPTH over the observed years.

TE is a common procedure with a surprisingly high rate of morbidity11,12,48. Given this, we chose to examine some uncommon factors that may influence the occurrence of bleeding. Specifically, we investigated whether the day of the week and the year of surgery affected bleeding rates. Interestingly, the highest-risk days were found to be Tuesday, Wednesday, and Thursday (p < 0.0001). Additionally, we discovered a correlation between surgical years and the rate of LPTH (p = 0.0291). The clinical differences in LPTH rates were relatively small across the years—2014 had an 8.63% rate, 2015 had 8.01%, 2016 had 9.01%, 2017 had 9.94%, and 2018 had 10.23%—it is important to note that each ENT department organizes its operations differently throughout the week based on the types of surgeries it typically performs. Only acute surgeries, such as TE “a hot”, are performed on weekends, thus rendering these findings without significant clinical impact.

Severity of bleeding

The topic of post-tonsillectomy bleeding severity has been the focus of numerous studies. In their 2007 work, Lowe et al.81 examined whether hospitalisation was necessary for the treatment of LPTH. Evans et al.82 defined LPTH as lasting longer than one minute. Windfur et al.27 and Tomkinson et al.25 investigated cases of bleeding that required general anaesthesia, whereas Wei et al.83 did not provide a definition for the severity of LPTH in their study. Kominek et al.18 emphasized the inconsistencies present in the scientific literature regarding this topic.

Several authors, including Inzuka et al.84, Dharmawardana et al.85, and Negm et al.86, presented a staging of post-tonsillectomy bleeding severity that is similar to the staging presented here. While these studies addressed factors related to LPTH occurence, they did not report on the variables influencing the bleeding grade. In our sample, we identified the use of anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, and surgical technique as variables associated with bleeding severity (p = 0.0002; p = 0.0404; p = 0.0065, respectively). However, factors such as gender, age, and indications for surgery had no impact on the severity of LPTH (p = 0.4403; p = 0.8133; p = 0.1024, respectively). Additionally, the comparison between PTA “a hot " and “planned TE” showed no significance (p = 0.0913). Further details can be found in Table 4.

Recurrent bleeding

Experiencing more than one bleeding incidence after a TE is considered rare. Hussain et al.87 reported that 18.75% of cases in their sample involved a first LPTH. Liu et al.62 found a rate of 14.29%, while Bhattacharyya et al.88 reported 25%. In our study, the data is in agreement with these findings: a second LPTH occurred in 213 patients, representing 16.65% of cases following the first LPTH and 1.52% of all TEs. This second bleeding happened within 15 days of the first incident. A third LPTH was observed in 26 patients, accounting for 2.03% of cases with the first bleeding, 12.21% of TEs resulted in two bleeding incidences, and 0.19% of all TEs resulted in three bleeding incidents. In our study, approximately 70% of first and second LPTH cases were treated under general anaesthesia, while 100% of third bleeds were managed this way (details can be found in Table 5).

Our study highlights an essential point: the chosen procedure for addressing the first bleeding—whether hospitalisation vs. home treatment and observation vs. revision—was not statistically significantly associated with the risk of a second bleeding (p = 0.7735; p = 0.2106, respectively). Details in Table 5.

In contrast, the use of anaesthesia showed statistical relevance to the incidents of bleeding. In our total patient sample, the use of local anaesthesia in the first bleeding solution was associated with a 1.67 times higher risk of second bleeding (p = 0.0464; confidence interval 1.0280–2.7229). The application of electrocoagulation and tamponade during the revision did not show a significant association with the risk of recurrent bleeding (p = 1.000; p = 0.8016). Furthermore, the use of sutures during revision was linked to a hazard ratio of 0.03572 (p = 0.014, confidence interval 0.1501–0.8503).

In our analysis of different treatment modalities for postoperative bleeding we examined various procedures. LPTH means bleeding from extensive wounds to unsecured airways. Management varies by workplace, and as one-day surgery becomes more prevalent, there is a clear trend toward providing maximum of care in the home environment. Data from 18 independent hospitals are involving in our study, we found 94 patients (7.35% of 1279 cases) with grade “A” LPTH were monitored at home. The statistical analysis indicated that home monitoring did not impact their healing process (p = 0.7735) in this study sample.

Limits of the study

We consider the multicentric nature of this study and the high volume of respondents are significant strengths. However, we acknowledge a limitation in the study design related to the heterogeneity of treatment approaches in independent hospitals. Furthermore, another noteworthy limitation is the potential underdiagnosis of LPTH, which remains an inadequately assessed variable. Given the large patient population, it is plausible that some individuals may not come to medical care after one small episode of grade “A” bleeding. In addition, the overall incidence of haemorrhage may appear higher compared with some other reports, which is largely due to methodological differences in how post-tonsillectomy bleeding is defined across studies. Our study intentionally adopted an inclusive definition, also recording anamnestically reported minor episodes, which increases sensitivity but at the same time limits direct comparability with cohorts using narrower criteria. Finally, perioperative management of anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy was not standardized across centres, which may also have contributed to variability in bleeding outcomes.

Conclusions

In our study involving 18 independent hospitals, we highlighted the importance of methodology when studying LPTH as the outcome of unilateral and bilateral TE procedures. We found that the factors significantly associated with the likelihood of late postoperative bleeding included the choice of hospital, as well as patient age and gender. The use of blood thinners negatively affected the severity of this bleeding. Additionally, the likelihood of repeated bleeding following the first surgical revision was decreased using general anaesthesia and suture in the tonsillar bed. Interestingly, the palatine tonsils appear to be the only paired organ in the human body where bilateral removal is the most common surgical procedure, and bleeding is a major concern. The probability of experiencing bleeding after a bilateral procedure is nearly doubled, a fact that is logical but not adequately highlighted in the literature.
