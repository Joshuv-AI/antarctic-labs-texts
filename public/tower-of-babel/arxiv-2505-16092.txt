Spontaneous generation of athermal phonon bursts within bulk silicon causing excess noise, low energy background events and quasiparticle poisoning in superconducting sensors Preprint: AIP/123-QED
C.L. Chang, Y.-Y. Chang, M. Garcia-Sciveres, W. Guo, S.A. Hertel, X. Li, J. Lin, M. Lisovenko, R. Mahapatra, W. Matava, D.N. McKinsey, P.K. Patel, B. Penning, M. Platt, M. Pyle, Y. Qi, M. Reed, I. Abstract
Solid state phonon detectors used in the search for dark matter or coherent neutrino nucleus interactions (CE ν \nu NS) require excellent energy resolution (eV-scale or below) and low backgrounds to meet their science objectives. Unfortunately, an unknown source of phonon bursts (the low energy excess, or “LEE”) both dominates all other above threshold background sources and produces shot noise from sub-threshold bursts which greatly exceeds all fundamental noise sources. In this paper, we measure these phonon bursts for 12 days after cool down in two nearly identical multi-phonon sensor channel 1cm 2 silicon detectors which differ only in the thickness of their substrate (1 mm vs 4 mm thick). We find that both the correlated shot noise and near threshold shared LEE relax with time since cooldown. Additionally, we show that both shot noise and LEE rates scale linearly with substrate thickness. When combined with previous measurements of other silicon phonon detectors with different substrate geometries and mechanical support strategies, these measurements strongly suggest that the dominant source of both above and below threshold LEE is the bulk substrate. By monitoring the relation between bias power and excess phonon shot noise we estimate that ε = < E 2 > < E > \varepsilon=\frac{<E^{2}>}{<E>} for sub-threshold noise events is 0.68 ± 0.38 0.68\pm 0.38 meV. In our final dataset, we report a world-leading energy resolution of 258.5 ± \pm 0.4 meV in the 1mm thick detector. Simple calculations suggest that these Si substrate phonon bursts are likely the dominant source of quasi-particle poisoning in superconducting qubits operated in well shielded and vibration free environments.



I
Motivation

Solid state phonon detectors with excellent energy resolution and low backgrounds are a key technology with applications in fundamental physics and beyond. For example, the search for light dark matter candidates
Kuflik et al. 2016
;
Kuflik et al. 2017
;
Hochberg et al. 2014
;
Hochberg et al. 2015
;
Hall et al. 2010
requires detectors with low noise and backgrounds to probe new regions of parameter space. Similarly, the detection of rare eV-scale Coherent Elastic Neutrino Nucleus Scattering (CE
\nu
NS) from nuclear reactors requires both low threshold and well controlled backgrounds
Billard et al. 2017
;
Rothe et al. 2019
. Finally, high resolution phonon sensors can be used as a veto for sensor-coupled backgrounds in photon coupled rare event searches (e.g. dark photon or axion searches)

Romani et al. 2024
.

At present, the field lacks an understanding of the fundamental processes setting the background environment and energy resolution of such detectors. For nearly the past decade, low threshold phonon detectors have observed orders of magnitude more events below several hundred eV than expected from background radioactivity or cosmic ray interactions
Adari et al. 2022
;
Baxter et al. 2025
, events often called the “low energy excess” (or LEE). These excess events couple both directly to a detector’s sensor films (“singles” LEE), and more problematically, to a detector’s phonon system (“shared” LEE)
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
;
Angloher et al. 2024a
. Similarly, our collaboration has observed excess noise in our phonon sensors, consistent with sub-threshold events in the phonon system (“correlated” or “phonon” noise) and our phonon sensors themselves (“uncorrelated” noise)
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
.

Superconducting qubits and similar devices have observed quasiparticle bursts in their superconducting films
Mannila et al. 2022
including correlated bursts seemingly caused by phonon bursts in the substrate
Yelton et al. 2025
. While potential connections to LEE-like phenomena have been proposed
Baxter et al. 2025
, the precise link between observations in qubits and low threshold particle detectors remains unclear.

A variety of explanations have been proposed for observations in low threshold phonon detectors, including the relaxation of thermal stress in the metal films used to construct phonon sensors
Romani 2024
, or defects in the detector’s bulk crystal
Nordlund et al. 2024
or on its surface. This work probes the scaling of these backgrounds and noise terms with the detector volume (or equivalently mass).

II
Experimental Setup

Here, we measure background events and noise in two low threshold athermal phonon detectors with different substrate thicknesses and identical phonon readout sensors (see Fig.
1
). One of these two detectors is 1 mm thick (mass: 0.233 grams, resistivity: 70 k
\Omega
/cm
2

\Omega
/cm
2

Irwin et al. 1995

Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025

Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025

Anthony-Petersen et al. 2024

Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
;
Angloher et al. 2024a
;
Romani 2023
, our detectors use two identical phonon readout channels to separate noise and backgrounds which couple to the detector phonon system (and therefore to both sensors essentially equally) from noise and backgrounds which couple more directly to the sensors themselves (and therefore almost exclusively to one sensor or the other). Our detectors are housed in an IR and EMI shielded set of housings on the mixing chamber stage of a dilution refrigerator, and are read out using DC SQUID array amplifiers. While we took strenuous precautions to ensure that these detectors underwent identical processes, including fabricating, dicing, transporting, storing and running these detectors together, it is possible that unknown systematics could preclude a direct comparison of these detectors based on their thicknesses.

III
Measurement and Modeling of Detector Noise

To characterize our detectors’ noise over time, we take 2 hours of continuous data following each calibration dataset, and remove periods with abnormally small bias power (from periods with abnormally high environmental vibration or after large energy depositions). Additionally, to select periods without above threshold events that would contaminate and bias our noise, we find the largest magnitude pulse in the entire 200 ms period using an optimal filter, and select periods for which this largest fit magnitude pulse has a negative amplitude, i.e. the largest event in the trace is a statistical fluctuation rather than a real energy deposition. In total, 3.9% of the 200ms traces pass these very strict noise selection criteria and are used to estimate the noise Cross power Spectral Density (CSD, see supplementary material section
C
for further discussion of our cuts). We convert this CSD into the power domain by applying a three-pole responsivity model
Lindeman et al. 2011
;
Irwin and Hilton 2005
for each channel (i.e.
\partial P/\partial I(f)
) that is estimated from IV and
\frac{\partial I}{\partial V}
measurements as in Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
.

Similar to our observations in Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
, we observe that our noise is significantly in excess of modeled TES noise, both in the individual channel Power Spectral Densities (PSDs), and in noise which is correlated between the two channels of the detector (which should be statistically consistent with zero). The broad spectrum correlated noise is consistent with shot noise from many sub-threshold phonon events in the substrate, i.e. it has the same shape and energy partition (50% left, 50% right) as substrate-interacting calibration photons (Fig.
3
Top Right). The magnitude of this broad correlated noise appears to scale with the detector volume, i.e. the correlated noise is four times larger in the 4 mm detector than in the 1 mm detector (Fig.
3
Top Right). The correlated noise peaks in the 100Hz-1kHz range are harmonics of the vibrational resonant modes of the hanging wire bond-detector system.

After removing these correlated noise terms, we find that there is an additional quasi-flat uncorrelated noise term, in significant excess of the expected level of thermal fluctuation noise in the TESs, but in some tension with the flat shot noise model of Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
(see supplementary material section
D
for further discussion).

We additionally find that the bias power (i.e. the power applied to the TES to keep it in transition) of our TESs increases over time (see Fig.

B

IV
Above Threshold Shared Backgrounds

In addition to this phonon shot noise from
below
threshold events, we observe a high rate of
above
threshold background events (often called the “Low Energy Excess,” or LEE
Adari et al. 2022
;
Baxter et al. 2025
). To investigate these LEE events, we recorded 2 or 12 hour periods of continuous background data following each calibration run, and triggered this dataset offline using an optimum filter optimized to search for events with a channel energy partition and pulse shape consistent with that of substrate absorbed calibration photons as in Ref.
Chang et al. 2025
. In Fig.
5
bottom left, a scatter plot shows the energy deposited in each channel without a constraint on the partition. Identical to observations in Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
, the LEE backgrounds split into 2 classes: “shared” events with a equal channel partition and a pulse shape identical to substrate absorbed photons, and “singles” events where nearly all energy is deposited in a single phonon sensor channel. Singles have a pulse shape with a fast rise, but a slow fall consistent with an energy deposition that saturates a subset of the athermal phonon sensors that are read out in parallel within a channel. Each event is then fit with a multi-channel optimum filter whose pulse shape and channel partition are fixed to the average of each LEE class (singles left, singles right, shared), and classified according to lowest
\Delta\chi^{2}
.

We see that the rate of above-threshold shared background events scales with the detector mass, as it does for below threshold phonon shot noise (the y-axis has been normalized by substrate mass). However, the rate of these above threshold shared events appears to be constant with respect to time at high energies. At lower energies, where statistics are better, the rate of shared LEE events does appear to decrease over time.

As in Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
, our singles appear to originate within the aluminum QET films, and we additionally observe that their rate decreases with time (see further discussion in supplementary materials section
F
). These observations give additional support to the proposal that the dislocation mediated relaxation of thermal stress in these aluminum QET films is responsible for these singles.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
;
Romani 2024

V
Discussion

Our observation of above threshold backgrounds and phonon shot noise which scales with the mass of our detector substrate leads us to conclude that these phonon bursts originate within the detector substrate itself. Through comparisons to detectors with different masses, surface-to-volume ratios, and holding methods,
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
;
Chang et al. 2025
;
Alkhatib et al. 2021
;
Angloher et al. 2023
we can reject possible LEE scalings with the sidewall area, total surface area, or the holding method, either above (see Fig.
5
) or below threshold (see supplementary materials section
H
). We also conclusively reject the hypothesis that our aluminum sensor films constitute our dominant source of phonon bursts,
Romani 2024
but note that such a source could contribute at a subdominant level.

Such a mass scaling suggests that defects in the detector substrate are responsible for LEE and excess phonon noise observations, and is compatible with the model proposed in Ref.
Nordlund et al. 2024
. The strong rate vs. time since cooldown dependence we observe in our noise data is easily explained by assuming that the relevant excited states are thermally populated at 300K ( >> 1 meV), and then tunnel to lower energy states at mK temperatures. The observation of time dependence in above threshold LEE rates, both here and previously
Angloher et al. 2023
, is more difficult to explain given their eV energy scales are much higher than 300K
k_{B}
. A more complex model, perhaps involving the avalanche-like relaxation of many lower energy states, is needed to explain this time dependence in above threshold data.

The low energy phonon bursts we observe may also constitute the quasiparticle bursts and “poisoning” observed in superconducting qubits and similar devices. We can estimate the reduced quasiparticle density
x_{qp}=n_{qp}/n_{cp}=n_{qp}/(4\times 10^{6}\mu\mathrm{m}^{-3})
Martinis 2021
created by such phonon bursts by assuming that a constant power density
\rho_{p}
of phonon events originating in the substrate is evenly divided over a
\sim
100 nm thick ground and qubit layer which covers essentially all of one face of a superconducting qubit chip. We assume that quasiparticle dynamics in the qubit and ground plane are dominated by recombination (rather than trapping), and that the system is in steady state, such that

\displaystyle\frac{dx_{qp}}{dt}=0=-rx_{qp}^{2}+g

(5)

where
g
is the dimensionless steady state quasiparticle generation rate and
r\approx(20\mathrm{ns})^{-1}
is the quasiparticle recombination rate in aluminum
Martinis 2021
. We assume a
t_{chip}=
1 mm thick chip, a
t_{Al}=
100 nm thick aluminum layer and a power density of
\rho_{p}\approx
1 fW / (1 cm
{}^{2}\times
1 mm), giving

\displaystyle x_{qp}=\sqrt{\frac{g}{r}}=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{p}}{\Delta_{Al}n_{cp}}\frac{t_{chip}}{t_{Al}}\frac{1}{r}}\approx 7\times 10^{-8}

(6)

which is similar to the reduced quasiparticle density observed by well shielded silicon devices

Phonon Collection Efficiency

To monitor the detectors’ phonon collection efficiency over time, we measure the responsivity
\partial P/\partial I
of each sensor using IV sweeps and
\partial I/\partial V
measurements at the operating point
Irwin and Hilton 2005
. We then calculate an average current-domain trace for events where a single photon is absorbed in the detector’s phonon system
i(t)
. Combining this with the responsivity, we can then calculate the phonon collection efficiency

\displaystyle\epsilon=\frac{E_{TES}}{E_{\gamma}}=\frac{1}{E_{\gamma}}\int p(t)dt=\frac{1}{E_{\gamma}}\int\mathcal{F}^{-1}\Big(\frac{\partial P}{\partial I}\mathcal{F}(i(t))\Big)dt

(7)

where
E_{\gamma}
is the energy of the photon hitting the detector and
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
are Fourier and inverse Fourier transforms, respectively. We find that our phonon detectors’ phonon collection efficiencies remain roughly constant over time (See Fig.
6
).

Supplementary Materials Section B
Effects of Mixing Chamber Cooling

In the steady state, we assume that two terms effect the bias power
P_{0}
of our devices: the cooling of the TES through electron-phonon coupling, and parasitic power from any number of sources incident on our TESs.

\displaystyle P_{0}=K_{ep}(T_{c}^{5}-T_{bath}^{5})-P_{par}

(8)

Here,
K_{ep}
gives the electron-phonon coupling in a certain TES operating at a temperature
T_{c}
and connected to a bath of temperature
T_{bath}
.

We operate our mixing chamber (MC) at far below the
T_{c}
of our TESs, such that even relatively large shifts in our mixing chamber and therefore bath temperature should have minimal impact on our TES cooling. Our detector housing is connected to our MC with several very high force gold plated copper to gold plated copper joints, and our detector is thermally connected to our housing with multiple gold wire bonds, which should very efficiently thermalize our detectors. Given our MC temperature drops from about 20 to 6 mK over the course of our run, we would expect a roughly 1% cooling power shift, assuming our detectors are at essentially the same temperature as our MC.

Our large (
\>4
x) noise shift over time gives additional evidence that the mixing chamber temperature does not dominate our system’s dynamics. As an absolute upper limit, if our sensor’s bath temperature decreased from the sensor
T_{c}
to a temperature of zero over the course of our datataking campaign, we would expect a maximum of a factor of 2 shift in noise due to shifting Thermal Fluctuation Noise (TFN) caused by this implausibly large shift in the bath temperature.
Watkins 2022
Given we observe a noise shift of more than twice that magnitude, and as such an enormous temperature shift is highly implausible in any case, we can safely discount the possibility that shifting bath temperature causes our noise shift. (The characteristic phonon pole and correlated noise provide additional evidence against this model.)

We conclude that the monotonic increase in TES bias power over time is dominated by a decrease in
P_{par}
, and that the observed behavior of our noise and bias power is not dominated by shifts in the temperature of our fridge or detector.

Supplementary Materials Section C
Data Quality and Single/Shared Discrimination Cuts

As in Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
, we apply quality cuts to all data series to ensure the detector is operating stably at a well characterized bias point. Additionally, we apply two special purpose cuts: a cut to ensure that traces with a large event in them are not used to construct noise estimates, and a cut which discriminated between shared and singles events in our background data.

Briefly, we cut on pre-pulse baseline, slope (pre-pulse baseline minus post-pulse baseline), and a low frequency
\chi^{2}
statistic (only considering frequencies below 50 kHz), using the same general philosophy as in Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
. Unlike in Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
, we see significant vibration coupled noise around several hundred Hz (see Fig.
7
), and use the pre-pulse baseline and slope cuts to remove events in which this noise dominates. Our low frequency
\chi^{2}
cut primarily removes pileup events, and events which were triggered at the incorrect time.

Figure 7:
The baseline cut in a representative 2 hour dataset on the 1 mm detector’s left channel. The approximately minute scale variations in the detector baseline are due to shifting vibration noise in the detector, which appears at roughly intervals and intensities in all datasets. The scattered points with very high baselines are events where a pulse occurs in the pre-pulse region (e.g. pileup). In the intervals with no points, we stop taking continuous data, and characterize the detector state by taking IV and dIdV measurements to characterize the TES state and photon calibration data to characterize the detector response.

For the noise analysis, we apply an additional cut intended to remove traces with large pulses that might dominate the noise if not removed. We do this by finding the largest magnitude (positive or negative) event in the each channel’s trace by using an unconstrained 1x1 OF, and only passing traces for which the largest event is negative in both channels, i.e. the largest event is consistent with a statistical fluctuation rather than a true event. In this sense, we select traces which are consistent with being dominated by pure noise.

Detail of the PSD of the 1 mm left and right channels with and without the PSD amplitude cut applied. Note that between the vibration induced peaks, the PSD amplitude cut does not change the broadband phonon shot noise (see text). Also note that the vibration-coupled noise causing the sharp peaks appears to couple more strongly to the right channel in some peaks.

Figure 9:
Average current domain noise (arbitrary scale) in the Right channel of the 1 mm detector between 160 and 190 Hz as a function of time, showing events passing (pink) and failing (blue) the “PSD Amplitude” cut in this frequency range.

For the noise analysis, we also apply a more selective “PSD amplitude” cut to reduce vibration-induced peaks in the observed PSD and CSD which were not cut by our relatively loose baseline and slope cuts (see Fig.
8
). These narrow resonance vibration-induced peaks (Q
\sim 10^{4}
) can be directly excited by mechanically thumping the fridge, and slowly fade away when the pulse tube is temporarily turned off (we cannot run for more than approximately 10 minutes in this configuration, and therefore take data with the pulse tube on). We also observe variation over time in the noise environment (see Fig.
9
), presumably as the vibration source shifts into and out of resonance with the fridge/hanging detector mechanical system. We also observe that these peaks are power coupled: they disappear when the TES channel is superconducting or normal and therefore insensitive to changes in the incident power (but still sensitive to current or electronically coupled noise). Interestingly, on specific peaks, the right channel of the 1 mm detector appears to be significantly more strongly coupled to the noise source than the left channel. We interpret this to mean that the mechanism injecting power into our TESs does not always couple through the detector’s phonon system (which would equally couple to the two channels), and instead at least partially couples directly to individual channels. One possible explanation would be that the electrical wire bonds connecting to each channel vibrate and deposit energy directly into the TESs, with different damping constants for the wire bonds to each channel.

To perform a more selective cut of these high-vibration periods of time, we average the PSD of individual traces in a set relatively narrow (
\sim
30 Hz wide) frequency bins, and cut events for which the average in any bin exceeds a threshold. By varying the level of this threshold, we can more or less aggressively remove periods of high vibration noise. Because of the relatively low passage of this cut, we only apply it during the noise analysis. The findings in this paper do not depend on whether or not this PSD amplitude cut is applied, e.g. the level of measured phonon noise is the same with or without the PSD amplitude cut, as is the shift in the phonon noise over time.

For the background analysis, we discriminate singles from shared events by comparing the
\chi^{2}
values for 2x1 OFs with different event typologies (as in Ref.

The uncorrelated noise obviously deviates from the TES noise + flat shot noise model we proposed in Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
, and deviates in seemingly opposite ways in the 1 mm and 4 mm detectors. Several possibilities might explain this deviation from the expected form:

•

Phonon collection position dependence that changes solely the relative partition of energy between the channels will produce uncorrelated shot noise with an average phonon pulse shape from subthreshold phonon LEE relaxation events. Optical calibration energy distributions are consistent with a
\sim
4% fractional energy position dependence in the 4 mm device. However, this likely overestimates the true position dependence for uniformly distributed events throughout the bulk of the substrate since the optical calibration photons are preferentially absorbed on the phonon sensor instrumented surface. In the 4 mm device, the excess measured uncorrelated noise is consistent with a
8\times 10^{-37}
W
2
/Hz flat noise possibly from localized relaxation in the aluminum fins plus additional shot noise with a phonon shape due to a 2% fractional energy position dependence.

The excess shot noise above the phonon collection bandwidth but below the TES sensor pole seen in the 1 mm device can not be understood with such a simple model. Potentially, models where the position dependence changes both the pulse shape and the energy partition between the 2 channels could model the rise in excess uncorrelated noise found at higher frequencies in the 1 mm device. This explanation is not entirely self-consistent; we both expect and see more position dependence in the 1 mm thick device in the photon calibration, yet see less uncorrelated noise at all frequencies.

•

Our power-to-current responsivity model (
dP/dI
) might be inaccurate. We use standard models for this responsivity
Lindeman et al. 2011
;
Irwin and Hilton 2005
which accurately predict current-domain pulse shapes for energy impulses in our TESs from photons
Romani et al. 2024
, giving us some reassurance that we are accurately modeling TES responsivity. However, our QETs (which have a different structure from TES photon sensors) could have additional thermal dynamics that do not couple as expected to our
\partial I/\partial V
excitations. For example, “third” poles seen in our
\partial I/\partial V
could couple to current through the TES in addition to the thermal couplings proposed in Ref.
Lindeman et al. 2011
. Directly probing our TESs’
dP/dI
through e.g. exciting our TESs with a low energy (
\sim 300
meV) photon source could probe this potential systematic.

•

We do not attempt to model the Internal Thermal Fluctuation Noise (ITFN)
Lindeman et al. 2011
caused by stocastic energy flucations between the independent thermal degrees of freedom in our 3 pole TES dynamical model. Given that our observed noise (even after “decorrelation”) dominated our modeled TFN noise by close to an order of magnitude, we find it unlikely that this ITFN noise due to additional heat capacities could be dominant.

Ultimately, we cannot pinpoint the origin of this effect, and are therefore reluctant to draw strong conclusions about the origin of our uncorrelated noise. We do note that the uncorrelated noise is roughly equal in magnitude for both 1 mm and 4 mm channels around 3-5 kHz, where neither correlated phonon noise nor TES noise should be dominant. This suggests that a common noise source (e.g. originating within the sensors themselves) causes this noise in all channels, however, without an accurate noise model, we feel that this observation is inconclusive.

Without a well supported model for this uncorrelated noise, we fear that attempts to measure changes in the uncorrelated noise over time will be subject to unknown systematics. Attempting to do this measurement by subtracting the TES noise and averaging the uncorrelated noise between 3 and 5 kHz (where presumably there is not too much interference from either phonon or TES noise) does appear to show a reduction in the uncorrelated noise over time (see Fig.
14
). However, we are unable to draw strong conclusions about this apparent effect, and caution that it is likely due to systematic effects (e.g. phonon noise which we cannot subtract due to position dependence) rather than a true reduction over time in the uncorrelated noise.

Shot Noise From Energy Spectra

Generically, we can extend the results of Eqns.
1
and
3
to account for arbitrary event spectra
\frac{\partial R}{\partial E}(E)
by integrating

\displaystyle P_{par}=\int_{\varepsilon_{l}}^{\infty}E\frac{\partial R}{\partial E}(E)dE

(25)

\displaystyle S_{p}=2\int_{\varepsilon_{l}}^{\infty}E^{2}\frac{\partial R}{\partial E}(E)dE

(26)

where
\varepsilon_{l}
is the lowest energy event the detector is sensitive to (set by e.g. the superconducting bandgap
\Delta_{Al}
).

For an exponential event distribution

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R}{\partial E}(E)=\frac{R_{0}}{\varepsilon_{0}}e^{-E/\varepsilon_{0}}

(27)

we find

\displaystyle P_{par}=R_{0}e^{-\varepsilon_{l}/\varepsilon_{0}}(\varepsilon_{l}+\varepsilon_{0})

(28)

\displaystyle S_{p}=2R_{0}e^{-\varepsilon_{l}/\varepsilon_{0}}(2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{l}+\varepsilon_{l}^{2})

(29)

\displaystyle\frac{S_{p}}{2P_{par}}=\frac{2\varepsilon_{0}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{l}+\varepsilon_{l}^{2}}{(\varepsilon_{l}+\varepsilon_{0})}

(30)

Fitting a line to the
S_{p}
vs.
P_{par}
data, we would fit
2\varepsilon_{0}
or
\varepsilon_{l}
for
\varepsilon_{l}>>\varepsilon_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{l}<<\varepsilon_{0}
respectively.

A power law distribution

\displaystyle\frac{\partial R}{\partial E}(E)=R_{1}\bigg(\frac{E}{E_{1}}\bigg)^{-\kappa}

(31)

where
E_{1}
is an energy which serves for normalization only yields

\displaystyle P_{par}=\frac{R_{1}\varepsilon_{l}^{2}}{2-\kappa}\bigg(\frac{\epsilon_{l}}{E_{1}}\bigg)^{2-\kappa}

(32)

\displaystyle S_{p}=2\frac{R_{1}\varepsilon_{l}^{3}}{3-\kappa}\bigg(\frac{\epsilon_{l}}{E_{1}}\bigg)^{3-\kappa}

(33)

\displaystyle\frac{S_{p}}{2P_{par}}=\epsilon_{l}\frac{2-\kappa}{3-\kappa}

(34)

for
\kappa>3
.

Note that while here we integrate up to infinity, these integrals are essentially unaffected by integrating up to a finite cutoff
\varepsilon_{h}
as long as
\varepsilon_{h}>>\varepsilon_{l}
and
\varepsilon_{h}>>\varepsilon
for exponential distributions. Equivalently, we can say that the ultimate shot noise we measure does not strongly depend on how we cut high energy events, as long as we cut very high energy events which do not belong to the underlying exponential or power law distribution (e.g. saturated events from cosmic rays, high energy LEE). For scale, our
\varepsilon_{l}
(and
\varepsilon
in the exponential distribution scenario) should be around the aluminum bandgap
2\Delta_{Al}\approx
0.7 meV, and our high energy cutoffs will be on the order of
\sigma_{p}
, i.e. hundreds of meV, decisively putting us in the regime where the severity of our high energy event cut does not strongly set the correlated noise level we measure. We have explicitly tested this by accepting events for the noise analysis with a varying energy cutoff, and have confirmed that the ultimate correlated noise level does not vary with different energy level cuts, as long as the very largest events are cut.

Supplementary Materials Section F
Singles Rate over Time

As in Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
, we have studied the pulse shape of the singles pulses observed in the background dataset and compared them to the aluminum direct hit events in the calibration dataset, and conclude as we did in Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
that the background singles events originate within the aluminum films.

In addition to these pulse shape studies, we have measured the rate of singles events over time. To do this, we trigger singles events on a single channel optimal filter, using the averaged pulse shape of above threshold singles events as a template. We then reconstruct the energy these events deposit in our TESs, assuming this same average template, and converting from a current-domain amplitude in the TES as measured by our optimal filter to an energy by applying a current responsivity model
\partial P/\partial I(f)
as in Refs.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
;
Romani et al. 2024
. As described in the main text, we discriminate these events from shared events by comparing the
\chi^{2}
values for two channel, one amplitude (2x1) optimal filters assuming both singles and shared responses.

Figure 12:
(Left) Spectra of singles events observed in the 1 mm and 4 mm detectors. Energies correspond to an absorbed energy in the TES. (Right) Singles rate between 0.7 and 1.8 eV over time, corresponding to the orange band in the left panel.

Comparing the left and right singles spectra for the 1 and 4 mm devices, we see differences in the rate and energy scale of singles observed in different channels. Presumably, some of these differences are due to saturation effects, which would be expected to distort spectra at different energies and relative to each other. As we discuss in Ref.
Anthony-Petersen et al. 2025
, our singles appear to deposit energy at a single point within a specific QET’s aluminum fins, with energy unevenly shared between the several TESs closest to this event. These different TESs receive different amounts of energy, and therefore saturate at different energies, leading to an artificially broadened and inherently energy dependent pulse shape. We observe evidence of energy dependence in these pulse shapes, and by fitting a pulse model composed of a delta-function and saturated component find that the average singles pulse shape becomes less saturated and more delta-like at lower energies, consistent with this model.

However, some channel-to-channel variation might remain even if the saturation related effects could be compensated for. In principle, some film-to-film variation of singles energy and rate might be expected, given these singles appear to originate with the aluminum QET films. This might be due to, for instance, the different thermal histories of the 1 mm and 4 mm aluminum films, as thicker substrates would be expected to remain cooler during aluminum deposition.
