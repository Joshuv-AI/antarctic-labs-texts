Meso-diverticular band causing small bowel obstruction in an infant: a case report and literature review

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Meckel’s diverticulum (MD) is the embryonic remnant of the proximal end of the omphalomesenteric duct, named after Johann Friedrich Meckel of Germany[1]. It is a congenital pouch on the wall of the distal ileum that occurs in approximately 2% of the population. The most common congenital anomaly of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, it is also a true diverticulum containing all three histological layers[2]. It may harbor ectopic gastric or pancreatic tissue in about 50% of cases. It is often discovered incidentally during surgery for other clinical conditions, and it has a lifetime clinical complication rate of 4%[3,4]. Although usually asymptomatic, in some cases it can cause complications in the form of ulceration, hemorrhage, intussusception, intestinal obstruction, perforation and, very rarely, vesico-diverticular fistulas and tumors[5,6]. Generally, these complications, especially bleeding, are more common in the pediatric age group than in adults[5]. We report a rare complication of MD in an infant, which was successfully managed with open surgery, and the patient was discharged in good condition.

Case presentation

A 3-month-old male infant from central Ethiopia presented with a 4-day history of crampy abdominal pain, bilious vomiting, and progressive abdominal distension. He had not passed stool since the onset of symptoms and had poor oral intake. Over the preceding day, he became increasingly lethargic and developed a subjective fever. There was no prior history of a similar illness. He was delivered at a local clinic with an unremarkable perinatal history.

On presentation, the child appeared acutely ill and lethargic. Peripheral pulses were not palpable, and he had cool, clammy extremities with delayed capillary refill. He was afebrile on presentation and required 2 L/min of oxygen via nasal cannula to maintain adequate oxygen saturation. Abdominal examination revealed marked abdominal distension with hyper-tympanic percussion notes. No palpable mass, organomegaly, or clinical evidence of intra-abdominal fluid collection was identified. Digital rectal examination was unremarkable, with no palpable mass or blood on the examining finger.

In the emergency department, a nasogastric (NG) tube was inserted for decompression, and the patient was resuscitated with 40 mL/kg of normal saline. Empiric intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole were initiated, and urinary catheterization was performed. The NG tube drained a large volume of bilious output. Following resuscitation, peripheral pulses became palpable, urine output improved, and capillary refill and mental status normalized.

Laboratory tests were then obtained. Complete blood count revealed a white blood cell count of 11.2 × 103/µL with 58% neutrophils; hemoglobin and platelet counts were within normal limits. Blood urea nitrogen was elevated at 69 mg/dL, while serum creatinine was 0.6 mg/dL. Serum electrolytes were within normal ranges. A plain abdominal radiograph demonstrated multiple centrally located dilated bowel loops (Fig. 1). Given the clinical and radiological findings suggestive of an acute small-bowel obstruction requiring urgent surgical intervention, further diagnostic investigations were deferred as they would have delayed definitive management without significantly altering the treatment plan. Therefore, an urgent exploratory laparotomy was undertaken.

Under general anesthesia, exploratory laparotomy was performed through a supraumbilical transverse incision. Intraoperatively, the small bowel was markedly dilated, while the colon was collapsed. An approximately 8 cm segment of ileum was found entrapped beneath a fibrous band extending from the mesentery to the antimesenteric border of the ileum (Fig. 2). The entrapped ileal segment, located 22 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve, was frankly gangrenous (Fig. 3a). Following reduction of the entrapped bowel, the obstructing structure was identified as a 3 cm-long MD arising approximately 30 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve (Fig. 3b). The diverticulum originated from the antimesenteric border of the ileum and was connected to the adjacent mesentery by a short fibrous meso-diverticular band. The diverticulum appeared grossly healthy except for mild inflammation at its tip, with no palpable mass or gangrenous change. No evidence of malrotation, volvulus, or intussusception was identified intraoperatively. The patient remained hemodynamically stable intraoperatively with adequate urine output, suggesting that the shock noted at presentation was more consistent with hypovolemia rather than sepsis. Consequently, the gangrenous segment of the ileum, together with the MD, was resected, and a primary end-to-end anastomosis was performed. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged in stable condition on postoperative day 7. Histopathologic examination confirmed chronic inflammatory changes in the MD and gangrenous terminal ileal tissue. The patient was seen at the surgical referral clinic and was doing well, gaining weight, and remaining symptom-free at three follow-up visits over the 2 months following surgery.

Discussion

MD is lined mainly by typical ileal mucosa, as in the adjacent small bowel. However, ectopic gastric mucosa (most common), pancreatic, duodenal, and colonic mucosa; Brunner’s glands; hepatobiliary tissue; and endometrial mucosa may be found, usually near the tip[5]. The most frequent complication of MD in adults is obstruction due to intussusception or an adhesive band, whereas in pediatric patients GI bleeding is the most common complication. Generally, the risk of complications decreases with increasing age, and there are no predictive factors for the development of complications. The main mechanism of bleeding is acid secretion from the ectopic mucosa, leading to ulceration of the adjacent ileal mucosa[5].

The presentation of MD with small bowel obstruction can be challenging because its symptoms mimic other causes of obstruction, such as adhesions or hernias. It is especially difficult to diagnose unless bleeding is the presenting symptom. Imaging studies, particularly contrast-enhanced CT, can be helpful in diagnosing this condition, but the definitive diagnosis is often made during surgical exploration[8]. MD results in small bowel obstruction through a variety of mechanisms. The common ones are volvulus of the intestine around the fibrous band attaching the diverticulum to the umbilicus, intussusception with a diverticulum acting as the lead point, entrapment of the intestine by a meso-diverticular band, and strictures secondary to chronic diverticulitis[9,10,11]. A meso-diverticular band is an embryologic remnant of the vitelline artery that may rarely extend from the tip of a MD to the mesentery, creating a closed loop within which bowel can become entrapped. In rare cases, small bowel obstruction can be caused by knotting of the diverticulum over a segment of the bowel, an internal hernia, Litter’s hernia, or impaction of bezoars, stool, or enteroliths[12–15]. Knotting of the bowel occurs when one part of the bowel snares itself around another part of the bowel, entrapping it and causing obstruction[10]. An internal hernia is a rare cause of small bowel obstruction and is defined as a condition in which a viscus protrudes through an opening within the abdominal cavity. Whatever the cause of obstruction may be, the presentation is strikingly similar. The patient typically presents with features of small bowel obstruction, such as absolute constipation, spasmodic abdominal pain, vomiting, which may be bilious, and abdominal distention[16].

Clinical diagnosis of MD can be challenging and usually requires supplementary imaging. Although plain abdominal X-ray is often the first imaging modality used, it lacks the specificity needed to determine the primary cause of obstruction. Therefore, computed tomography (CT) is considered the primary diagnostic modality. This may be supplemented by a Meckel’s scan, especially when clinical suspicion is high and CT findings are equivocal. Definitive diagnosis and management are achieved through laparoscopy or laparotomy[17].

Bowel obstruction is an unusual presentation of MD in pediatrics, and among the few cases of bowel obstruction in children, meso-diverticular bands are quite rare[17]. Generally, lower GI hemorrhage is the most common presentation in children with a symptomatic MD, while in adult cases of MD, obstruction is the most frequently reported presenting symptom[16,18]. Intestinal obstruction due to MD is the second most common presentation in children, following bleeding[18]. Children often present with dark red or maroon stools or stools containing blood or mucus, whereas adults usually present with melena and crampy abdominal pain. The bleeding is typically painless, and it may be brisk or massive, giving the stool a bright red, brick-red, or black[16]. Our case involved a healthy MD attached to the small-bowel mesentery with a short fibrous band, apparently a meso-diverticular band. A mesodiverticular band is an embryologic remnant of the vitelline circulation that carries the arterial supply to the MD. In the event of an error of involution, a patent or nonpatent arterial band persists and extends from the mesentery to the apex of the antimesenteric diverticulum. This creates a snare-like opening through which bowel loops may herniate and become obstructed[19]. The band extending from the mesentery to the tip of the MD creates a gate for an internal hernia[20].

Although management is usually surgical, the type of procedure can range from simple diverticulectomy to small bowel resection, depending on the integrity of the diverticular base and the morphology of the diverticulum. The prognosis is usually good except in a few cases that present late. In a small cohort study in China of 20 children with intestinal obstruction caused by a meso-diverticular band, all patients survived except 1 who died of total colonic aganglionosis[21]. There is also a report of sudden death from small bowel incarceration due to a meso-diverticular band[22].

At laparotomy, an MD with an inflamed tip that was adherent to the mesentery by a short band was identified. The diverticulum, adherent to the adjacent mesentery, formed an internal hernia through which a loop of ileum had become entrapped. What makes this case unusual is that the MD was neither elongated nor associated with a fibrous band attaching it to the anterior abdominal wall, which is the more common mechanism of bowel obstruction in MD. In addition, a meso-diverticular band is a very rare cause of small-bowel obstruction in pediatrics, and most reports to date are in adults[13,14,19–21].

Although the management is generally similar to that for other causes of bowel obstruction associated with MD, this article highlights a rare underlying mechanism. It further broadens scientific understanding of the diverse presentations and pathophysiological mechanisms of MD. Additionally, it emphasizes the importance of prompt surgical exploration in patients with evidence of bowel obstruction, irrespective of the exact etiology, as early intervention is essential for improving patient outcomes and reducing morbidity.

Conclusion

A short meso-diverticular band alone can form an internal hernia, resulting in bowel strangulation and gangrene. Early surgical exploration remains crucial in unstable patients, as timely intervention can be lifesaving and result in favorable outcomes.
