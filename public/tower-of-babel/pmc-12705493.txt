Cardiac Arrest Caused by Tracheal Intubation During Anesthesia Induction: A Case Report
Authors: Haiyun Gu, Haikun Zhang, Le Cao, Jinxiang Yu, Tao Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCardiac arrest during peri‐intubation is rare, necessitating timely identification of its cause and the implementation of precise diagnostic and therapeutic interventions. The purpose of the report is to pay attention to peri‐intubation cardiac arrest and to advocate for proactive management strategies to avert severe outcomes.Keywords: anesthesia induction, cardiac arrest, peri‐intubation cardiac arrest, tracheal intubation, visual laparoscopic

Introduction

Endotracheal intubation under general anesthesia is a routine anesthetic procedure. Among its complications, cardiac arrest is the most severe [1], called peri‐intubation cardiac arrest (PICA). PICA is one of the most severe complications of endotracheal intubation. Existing studies suggest that the risk of PICA is associated with pre‐intubation hypotension, hypoxemia, and hemodynamic instability [2]. Currently, most studies focus on emergency departments, intensive care units (ICUs), and general wards [2, 3, 4], with a lack of dedicated statistical data on the incidence of PICA in the operating room and the absence of standardized risk assessment models. Both direct laryngoscopy and laryngoscope‐guided endotracheal intubation carry inherent risks of eliciting vagally‐mediated reflexes through epiglottic mechanoreceptor stimulation. Prior literature indicates that cardiac arrest during direct laryngoscopy is associated with vagal stimulation [5, 6], suggesting it is a potential cause of PICA.

Case History

A 35‐year‐old male was admitted to a general surgery clinic with a right inguinal groin mass of one‐year duration. The patient has a medical history of good health, 175 cm, 85 kg. There are no other significant symptoms. The patient has a history of smoking and no significant past medical history.

Preoperative anesthesia assessment of the patient: Cardiac function class I; Mallampati airway classification II. Physical examination and examination items showed no abnormalities. American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification: Class I. The rest of the physical examination was unremarkable.

At 08:20, the patient was admitted to the operating room. Three‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring was initiated, showing a heart rate (HR) of 72 beats per minute, non‐invasive blood pressure (BP) of 123/74 mmHg, and pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 98%. Peripheral venous access was established in the patient's right upper arm. The patient was preoxygenated with a face mask for 5 min, at which point the SpO2 reached 100%.

At 08:40, the anesthetic induction began with the following intravenous injections: 25 mg of etomidate, 10 mg of cisatracurium besilate, and 70 μg of sufentanil. The patient was then provided face mask ventilation for about 4 min, during which the heart rate remained around 67 beats per minute; intermittent non‐invasive blood pressure (NIBP) monitoring recorded values of 135/73 mmHg. After ensuring adequate muscle relaxation, the preparation for endotracheal intubation was made.

At 08:44:31, when the glottis was exposed using a visual laryngoscope, the ECG suddenly showed a continuous, long, intermittent missed beat, and the heart rate dropped to 30 beats per minute, followed by cardiac arrest. After the surgery, the monitor recorded that the heart had stopped beating for about 8 s (Video S1). The initial consideration was that the endotracheal intubation procedure might have stimulated the vagus nerve, leading to vagal bradycardia. The decision was made not to intubate and to withdraw the laryngoscope to remove the stimulus immediately. The heart rhythm then recovered to sinus rhythm, and the heart rate stabilized around 45 beats per min. The patient continued to be ventilated with the face mask. After trying to gently insert the visual laryngoscope again, a long interval with bradycardia occurred once more.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The differential diagnosis of cardiac arrest during intubation includes primary cardiac arrest, anaphylactic shock, and airway trauma, such as laryngospasm, bronchospasm, or mucosal injury. The patient had no history of preexisting cardiac conditions or documented allergies to anesthetic agents or neuromuscular blocking agents. Following anesthetic induction, there were no clinical manifestations of generalized urticaria or angioedema, and video laryngoscopy revealed no evidence of iatrogenic airway trauma. Based on this comprehensive assessment, we promptly ruled out these three potential etiologies as causative factors. The observed cardiac arrest has been directly associated with laryngoscopic manipulation, with vagal reflex activation postulated as the underlying pathophysiological mechanism.

Upon re‐identification of cardiac arrest prodrome, the laryngoscope was quickly withdrawn, and 0.5 mg of atropine was administered intravenously. Subsequently, the heart rate increased to around 70 beats per minute. Under video laryngoscopic visualization, 3 mL 2% lidocaine was sprayed to the supraglottic region using a 5 mL syringe to achieve topical anesthesia. After the anesthesia was deepened with an additional intravenous injection of 10 mg(5 mL) etomidate, intubation was successful.

Conclusion and Results

During the second intubation attempt, as the patient developed recurrent bradycardia, we administered anticholinergic agents to augment HR, deepened anesthetic depth, and performed topical anesthesia to mitigate laryngoscopic stimulation, ultimately achieving successful endotracheal intubation. There were no further episodes of arrhythmia during the surgery, which proceeded smoothly. The total surgical duration was 1 h. The patient was extubated in the Post‐Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) postoperatively and awakened without reporting any discomfort. While PICA may stem from multifactorial etiologies, we confirm that laryngoscopic manipulation was a significant contributing factor in this event.

Discussion

In this case, following initial endotracheal intubation complicated by cardiac arrest, the patient received etomidate intravenous injection, and topical lidocaine was applied to the throat. Subsequent reintubation was performed without recurrent cardiac arrest.

Endotracheal intubation stress reaction mainly refers to laryngoscopy placement, endotracheal intubation stimulation of the base of the tongue, epiglottis, throat, and mucosa in the airway, activation of the sympathetic adrenergic system and renin‐angiotensin system, resulting in the excitation and reflex of the sympathetic or parasympathetic nervous system [7]. Most clinical manifestations are hemodynamic changes such as increased heart rate, elevated blood pressure, or arrhythmia. In a tiny number of patients, a vagal reflex occurs during endotracheal intubation, which is characterized by bradycardia, decreased blood pressure, and even cardiac arrest [8, 9].

PICA has a variety of potential causes, such as underlying heart disease, electrolyte imbalance, hypovolemia, hypoxia, and vagus nerve stimulation. According to the age and physical condition of the patients, we summarized the following reasons [4, 10, 11, 12].

Airway Management

Laryngeal mask airway(LMA) can minimize complications by avoiding contact with the glottis and trachea, reducing stimulation of the vocal cords and tracheal mucosa, and not activating the receptors in the epiglottis, vocal cords, and tracheal mucosa, thereby reducing the excitation of the sympathetic vagus nerve and contributing to hemodynamic stability. However, with poor tightness, patients with reduced lung compliance and increased airway resistance are at a greater risk of air leakage with LMA. Given the patient's weight (BMI = 27.8, near obesity), there was a higher risk of air leakage with LMA. Considering the safety and stability of the operation, we chose endotracheal intubation to control ventilation, which is a possible cause of PICA.

Drug Dose

As demonstrated in preclinical research, sufentanil is a potent analgesic with a high therapeutic index. In this case, the patient received 70 micrograms of sufentanil, a dose within the therapeutic range. However, due to individual differences, vagal reactions that may be caused by too high or too low an opioid dose cannot be ruled out. In addition, the calculated minimum dose of cisatracurium besilate, based on the patient's weight, was 12.75 mg, but only 10 mg was administered. Despite allowing sufficient time for the muscle relaxants to act, the low dose did not produce adequate muscle relaxation, leading to an exaggerated vagal response at intubation. Between the induction of anesthesia and endotracheal intubation, no inhaled or intravenous anesthesia drugs were used to maintain the depth of anesthesia, which may result in insufficient depth of anesthesia.

Laryngoscope

Laryngoscopic stimulation of the base of the tongue and epiglottis can enhance vagal nerve activity, triggering bradycardia and cardiac arrest. Compared with the traditional direct laryngoscope, the visual laryngoscope minimizes throat trauma and aligns better with the anatomical structure, potentially lessening compression. However, the effectiveness is operator‐dependent [13]. A systematic analysis of more than 200 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), most of which had an undefined risk of bias in at least one area, and all of which had a high risk of implementation bias and detection bias, revealed: Video laryngoscopy demonstrated superior first‐pass success rates and reduced complications compared to direct laryngoscopy, particularly in ICU settings and among novice clinicians; however, direct laryngoscopy was associated with a lower incidence of arterial hypotension [14, 15, 16]. These findings were largely derived from studies involving adults undergoing elective surgery requiring endotracheal intubation, but differences in clinical context and potential confounding, particularly confounding by indication, may limit the generalizability of the conclusions.

Blood Volume

Fasting prior to general anesthesia can lead to relative blood volume deficiency, an established risk factor for cardiac arrest. The suprathalamic nucleus and paraventricular neurons secrete too much vasopressin, which leads to contraction of vascular smooth muscle that easily triggers vagal [17]. Therefore, rehydration before inducing anesthesia maybe a key measure to prevent PICA, but a 2022 RCT in critically ill patients demonstrated no significant difference in cardiac arrest rates between groups receiving a 500‐mL intravenous fluid bolus versus no fluid bolus (1.7% vs. 1.5%) [18].

Once a PICA occurs, it is crucial to implement prompt management strategies, including the use of anticholinergic agents (e.g., atropine), deepening the level of anesthesia, and applying topical anesthesia, to stabilize the patient and enable safe reintubation. In this case, we aborted the intubation immediately because the patient's heart rate quickly returned to normal and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was not performed. However, the heart rate reverted to bradycardia during the second attempt. In response to the potential problems mentioned in the previous analysis, we responded promptly: first, atropine was administered to stabilize hemodynamics and reduce vagus overreaction, followed by deeper anesthesia and pharyngeal surface anesthesia to reduce the irritation of the intubation.

Postoperatively, we monitored patients' vital signs and state of consciousness to identify and address any complications related to PICA in a timely manner. Both PACU observation and postoperative follow‐up showed that the patient recovered well and had no memory of the procedure.

Cardiac arrest is a critical complication that may arise during intubation. It is typically triggered by factors such as vagal nerve overactivation and irritation of the upper airways. Consequently, when PICA actually occurs in the clinic, it is crucial to implement proactive management strategies, which may include the use of anticholinergic agents, increasing the depth of anesthesia, and applying local infiltration anesthesia.

In summary, this case highlights the importance of reporting PICA to help prevent potentially catastrophic events and improve our understanding and management of the condition.
