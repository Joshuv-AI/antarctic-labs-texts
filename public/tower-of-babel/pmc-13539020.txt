Comparative Postoperative Outcomes After Rotator Cuff Repair in Tobacco and Nontobacco Nicotine Users
Authors: M Lane Moore, Alejandro M Holle, William H Rankin, Collin L Braithwaite, Eugenia Lin, Joseph C Brinkman, Jeffrey D Hassebrock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Tobacco use is a known risk factor for poor healing after rotator cuff repair (RCR), but the effects of nontobacco nicotine products remain unclear.Purpose:To compare postoperative complications among nontobacco nicotine users, tobacco users, and nonusers undergoing RCR.Study Design:Cohort study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:Using the PearlDiver database, the authors identified patients who underwent arthroscopic RCR with ≥2 years of follow-up. Three 1:1 propensity-matched comparisons were conducted: nontobacco nicotine users versus nonusers (n = 4844), tobacco users versus nonusers (n = 19,182), and nontobacco nicotine users vs tobacco users (n = 4882). Outcomes included 90-day complications and 2-year revision surgeries.Results:Nontobacco nicotine users had mildly higher rates of lysis of adhesions than controls (0.7% vs 0.4%; OR, 1.95; P = .033) but no other significant differences. Tobacco users showed increased rates of acute kidney injury (AKI), pneumonia, infections, emergency department (ED) visits, readmissions, and revision surgery compared to controls. Compared to tobacco users, nontobacco nicotine users had moderately strong lower risks of pneumonia (0.6% vs 1.2%; OR, 0.46), ED visits (8.8% vs 13.6%; OR, 0.62), and readmissions (0.8% vs 1.5%; OR, 0.54) (all P < .01).Conclusion:While nontobacco nicotine use was associated with a modestly increased risk of lysis of adhesions after RCR, it did not significantly elevate the risk of major medical complications, infections, or revision surgery when compared to nonnicotine users. In contrast, tobacco users demonstrated consistently worse postoperative outcomes across multiple domains, including higher rates of AKI, pneumonia, surgical site infection, ED visits, readmissions, and revision procedures. Notably, when compared directly to tobacco users, nontobacco nicotine users had significantly lower rates of pneumonia, ED utilization, and readmissions. These findings suggest that although nontobacco nicotine products may not be entirely benign, their perioperative risk profile appears to be less severe than that of traditional tobacco use.Keywords: rotator cuff repair, nontobacco nicotine, tobacco use, postoperative complications, lysis of adhesions

Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (RCR) is a commonly performed orthopaedic procedure, with approximately 180,000 surgeries conducted annually in the United States.2,20 Despite improvements in technology and technique, RCR is associated with a relatively high retear rate, with studies reporting retear rates ranging from 9.5% to 63.2% after primary repair and revision surgery rates of 3.8% to 15.4%. 3 Multiple patient-related factors have been implicated in poor outcomes after RCR, including older age, biological healing capacity, tear size and morphology, fatty infiltration of the rotator cuff muscles, level of work activity, and medical comorbidities such as diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, and osteoporosis.11,13,14,22 Among these, tobacco use is a well-established modifiable risk factor. 4 Nicotine and other constituents of combustible tobacco have been shown to impair tendon healing by reducing tissue perfusion, inhibiting tenocyte function, and increasing susceptibility to infection, ultimately contributing to worse functional outcomes and higher revision rates. 6

As the public has become increasingly aware of the harmful effects that tobacco smoking can have, there has been a surge in popularity of alternative nicotine delivery vehicles including e-cigarettes, vaping products, and nicotine pouches.1,7 Unlike combustible tobacco, these products do not contain some of the harmful compounds and by-products such as carbon monoxide and tar, which has significantly contributed to the public perception that they are a safer and healthier alternative. However, the impact that these alternative nicotine products have on orthopaedic surgical outcomes has remained underexplored.

To date, the literature on nontobacco nicotine use in orthaopedic surgery is limited and has primarily focused on spine surgery and total joint arthroplasty.19,21 Prior animal studies have demonstrated that nicotine alone, independent of other tobacco-related toxins, can impair bone and soft tissue healing by promoting vasoconstriction, reducing collagen synthesis, and inhibiting fibroblast proliferation. 6 More recently, large administrative database studies have suggested that nontobacco nicotine use may increase the risk of various postoperative complications. Notably, a study by Lawand et al 12 evaluated postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing arthroscopic RCR who used nontobacco nicotine products. Their analysis revealed higher rates of 90-day emergency department (ED) visits and increased narcotic usage at both 90 days and 3 years postoperatively. However, the study examined a limited set of complications and did not directly compare outcomes with those of patients who used traditional tobacco products.

Given the rising prevalence of nontobacco nicotine use and the well-known risks associated with tobacco, there is a pressing need to determine whether these new nicotine modalities carry similar surgical risks. The present study aimed to evaluate postoperative outcomes after arthroscopic RCR in 3 patient populations: users of nontobacco nicotine products, users of traditional tobacco products, and matched controls who use neither. We hypothesized that nontobacco nicotine users would exhibit fewer adverse outcomes than tobacco users but more than nonusers.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study was conducted using the PearlDiver Mariner Database (PearlDiver Inc), a commercially available US administrative claims database that includes deidentified patient data spanning from 2010 to the third quarter of 2023. The study utilized the M170Ortho data set, which contains information on approximately 170 million patients. As PearlDiver data are compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, institutional review board approval was not required for this analysis.

Cohort Definitions

Patients who underwent arthroscopic RCR were identified using Current Procedural Terminology code 29827. Only patients with a minimum follow-up of 2 years after the index procedure were included. For patients with multiple RCR procedures recorded, only the earliest instance was analyzed. Individuals who underwent concomitant major shoulder procedures at the time of RCR were excluded, except for those who had concurrent subacromial decompression or distal clavicle excision.

Patients were categorized into 3 groups: (1) nontobacco nicotine users, (2) combustible tobacco users, and (3) controls with no recorded nicotine or tobacco use. Cigarette smokers were defined by International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) code F17.210 (“nicotine dependence, cigarettes, uncomplicated”). The nontobacco nicotine cohort consisted of patients with a preoperative diagnosis of nicotine dependence (ICD-10 F17 group), excluding those specifically diagnosed with dependence on cigarettes (F17.21), chewing tobacco (F17.22), or other tobacco products such as nicotine patches or gum (F17.29). Patients with diagnoses indicating concurrent use of multiple nicotine or tobacco products were excluded. Patients were eligible to be included in the control group if they had none of the abovementioned diagnoses.

Propensity Score Matching

Three separate 1:1 propensity score matching analyses were conducted: nontobacco nicotine users (n = 4844) were matched to nonnicotine users, tobacco users (n = 19,182) were matched to nonnicotine users, and nontobacco nicotine users (n = 4882) were compared directly with tobacco users (n = 4882). Matching variables included age, sex, and Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI), and specific comorbid conditions including diabetes, obesity, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease, liver disease, anemia, depression, thyroid disorders, rheumatoid arthritis, coagulopathy, and nutritional deficiencies (Table 1). In addition, alcohol use disorder and illicit drug use were included as covariates given their known association with nicotine use and postoperative outcomes. These variables were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature identifying risk factors for postoperative complications after RCR. Standardized mean differences <0.1 were used to confirm adequate balance between matched cohorts.

Outcome Measures

Postoperative complications occurring within 90 days of RCR were assessed across all matched groups. These included myocardial infarction, acute kidney injury (AKI), pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, deep vein thrombosis, urinary tract infection, sepsis, surgical site infection, ED visit, readmission, wound dehiscence, and irrigation and debridement. Longer-term outcomes within 2 years of surgery were also evaluated, including revision RCR, lysis of adhesions, manipulation under anesthesia, and total shoulder arthroplasty.

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons of continuous variables between groups were made using Welch t tests, while categorical variables were evaluated using chi-square tests. Complications between groups were compared using the chi-square test. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported for each complication. Given the higher prevalence of substance use in nicotine-using populations, alcohol and illicit drug use (identified via ICD-10 codes) were also included as covariates when matching. Any other patient characteristics that differed significantly between groups were likewise controlled for. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant, and a lower bound of the 95% confidence interval >1.00 was required to report an increased association. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) within the PearlDiver platform.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

After matching, the final cohorts included 4844 nontobacco nicotine users, 19,182 tobacco users, and equivalent numbers of matched nonnicotine controls. Nontobacco nicotine users had a mean age of 57.1 ± 9.8 years, were 55.3% male, and had a mean ECI of 4.9 ± 3.3. There were no significant differences in age, sex, or ECI between matched controls and nontobacco nicotine users. Tobacco users had a mean age of 56.4 ± 9.4 years, were 49.8% male, and had a mean ECI of 5.4 ± 3.3. There were no significant differences in age, sex, or ECI between matched controls and tobacco users. Similarly, there were no differences in age, sex, or ECI between matched smokers and nontobacco nicotine users.

When analyzing specific comorbidities, tobacco users demonstrated significantly lower rates of obesity (52.6% vs 54.5%; P < .001) and hypertension (78.4% vs 79.4%; P = .023) when compared with controls. Otherwise, the rates of the included comorbidities were not significantly different between the 3 groups. All participant descriptive and comorbidity information among the cohorts is displayed in Table 1.

Nontobacco Nicotine Use Versus Control

Compared to matched controls, nontobacco nicotine users demonstrated a modestly significantly higher rate of lysis of adhesions at 2 years of follow-up (0.7% vs 0.4%; OR, 1.95; 95% CI, 1.08-3.50; P = .033). However, there were no significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of other 90-day medical complications, ED visits, readmissions, or need for revision surgery at the 2-year postoperative time frame (Tables 2 and 3, Figure 1). Tobacco Use Versus Control

In contrast, tobacco users, when compared to their matched controls, exhibited significantly increased odds of multiple adverse outcomes, including myocardial infarction (0.3% vs 0.2%; OR, 1.52; 95% CI, 1.04-2.23; P = .036), AKI (0.7% vs 0.5%; OR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.17-2.00; P = .0019), pneumonia (1.2% vs 0.9%; OR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.13-1.68; P = .002), surgical site infection (1.8% vs 1.4%; OR, 1.31; 95% CI, 1.11-1.54; P = .001), ED visits (14.9% vs 8.8%; OR, 1.82; 95% CI, 1.71-1.94; P < .001), readmissions (1.5% vs 1.0%; OR, 1.47; 95% CI, 1.22-1.77; P < .001), irrigation and debridement (1.2% vs 1.0%; OR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.06-1.56; P = .013), revision surgery (3.5% vs 2.4%; OR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.27-1.61; P < .001), and total shoulder arthroplasty (0.8% vs 0.6%; OR, 1.30; 95% CI, 1.02-1.65; P = .039) (Tables 2 and 3, Figure 2).

Nontobacco Nicotine Use Versus Tobacco Use

When directly comparing nontobacco nicotine users to tobacco users, nontobacco nicotine use was associated with a moderately strong lower risk of pneumonia (0.6% vs 1.2%; OR, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.30-0.73; P < .001), fewer ED visits (8.8% vs 13.6%; OR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.54-0.70; P < .001), and reduced readmission rates (0.8% vs 1.5%; OR, 0.54; 95% CI, 0.37-0.80; P = .003). There were no significant differences between the nontobacco nicotine and tobacco user groups in rates of other complications, including infection, revision surgery, and reoperation (Tables 2 and 3, Figure 3).

Discussion

The primary finding of this large, propensity-matched analysis is that nontobacco nicotine users do not experience the broad spectrum of postoperative risks historically associated with traditional tobacco use after arthroscopic RCR. Aside from a modest increase in lysis of adhesions at 2 years, nontobacco nicotine users demonstrated no significant elevation in major medical complications, infections, ED utilization, readmissions, or revision procedures when compared with nonusers. In contrast, tobacco users exhibited consistently worse outcomes across nearly every postoperative domain, including higher rates of acute medical complications, surgical site infections, ED visits, readmissions, and revision surgery. Furthermore, when directly compared, nontobacco nicotine users had substantially lower rates of pneumonia, ED utilization, and readmissions than smokers, underscoring a distinctly more favorable perioperative profile. These findings indicate that while alternative nicotine products are not entirely without risk, their impact on postoperative recovery appears markedly less harmful than that of traditional tobacco.

The differential postoperative risk between tobacco and nontobacco nicotine users demonstrated in this analysis aligns with emerging evidence from other orthopaedic subspecialties. Prior work has consistently linked combustible tobacco smoking with poorer postoperative outcomes in RCR, including increased complication rates, impaired tendon healing, and higher revision risk.4,17,18 Our findings reaffirm these associations, demonstrating increased rates of myocardial infarction, AKI, pneumonia, surgical site infection, ED utilization, readmissions, and revision procedures among tobacco users compared with matched controls.

By contrast, the literature surrounding nontobacco nicotine use in orthopaedic surgery remains limited. A recent study found that nontobacco nicotine users had higher 90-day ED utilization and increased postoperative narcotic requirements after RCR; however, the analysis did not include major medical complications or direct comparison with traditional smokers. 12 The current results expand on those observations, demonstrating that use of e-cigarettes, vaping products, or nicotine pouches is not associated with increased infection, cardiopulmonary complications, or revision surgery when compared to nonusers. Moreover, the existing literature evaluating nontobacco nicotine or smokeless tobacco use in orthopaedic surgery consistently shows a risk profile that is intermediate between traditional smoking and nicotine abstinence. Studies in arthroplasty, spine surgery, and RCR have demonstrated that nontobacco nicotine users experience higher rates of certain postoperative complications and greater health care utilization than nonusers, yet substantially fewer medical and surgical complications than cigarette and chewing tobacco users.5,8,16,19,21

Although the mechanisms differentiating the effects of tobacco and nontobacco nicotine products remain incompletely understood, several biological pathways provide plausible explanations for the patterns observed in this study. Combustible tobacco contains numerous cytotoxic and vasoactive compounds that impair microvascular perfusion, suppress immune cell function, and disrupt collagen organization, mechanisms strongly implicated in delayed tendon-to-bone healing and increased susceptibility to infection.4,6 In contrast, nontobacco nicotine products lack many combustion-related toxins but still deliver pharmacological doses of nicotine, which exerts its own detrimental effects on soft tissue biology. Nicotine-induced vasoconstriction reduces local perfusion, prolongs the inflammatory phase of healing, and alters fibroblast activity and collagen synthesis6,9 These processes create a hypovascular, pro-inflammatory, and structurally disorganized repair environment that may predispose patients to postoperative stiffness, offering a biologically coherent explanation for the higher rate of lysis of adhesions observed among nontobacco nicotine users in this cohort. Additionally, prior rotator cuff and tendon animal models have demonstrated that nicotine exposure delays tendon-to-bone healing and impairs collagen maturation, further supporting the plausibility of a fibroproliferative response after nicotine exposure.6,10,15 The comparatively favorable cardiopulmonary profile seen among nontobacco nicotine users relative to smokers likely reflects the absence of carbon monoxide–mediated hypoxia and lower systemic inflammatory burden characteristic of combustible smoke inhalation, even as nicotine itself continues to exert localized effects on tissue healing.

As nontobacco nicotine products become increasingly prevalent, it is important for orthopaedic surgeons to understand their nuanced perioperative implications. Our findings indicate that nontobacco nicotine use should not be considered equivalent to smoking, yet it is also not entirely benign. Preoperative counseling should reflect this more balanced risk profile, and clinicians should routinely assess not only whether patients use nicotine, but also the specific form and delivery method. These results further support targeted smoking cessation strategies, in which transitioning patients away from combustible tobacco, even if complete nicotine cessation is not immediately achievable, may meaningfully reduce postoperative risk.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to administrative database analyses. Identification of nicotine use relied on ICD-10 coding, which may underestimate true prevalence. The database cannot distinguish between varying doses or delivery mechanisms of nontobacco nicotine. Additionally, ICD-10 coding cannot be used to definitively claim that the patients included in the analysis did not cease nicotine consumption for a period around their surgical procedure. Clinical variables such as tear morphology, fixation technique, and rehabilitation protocols were unavailable. Residual confounding may persist despite propensity matching. Additionally, the nontobacco nicotine cohort was substantially smaller than the tobacco cohort, which may have resulted in reduced statistical power to detect small but clinically relevant differences in postoperative complications within this group, thereby increasing the risk of type 2 error. Finally, the findings represent associations rather than causality.

Conclusion

While nontobacco nicotine use was associated with a modestly increased risk of lysis of adhesions after RCR, it did not significantly elevate the risk of major medical or surgical complications when compared to those of nonnicotine users. In contrast, tobacco users demonstrated consistently worse postoperative outcomes across multiple domains, including higher rates of AKI, pneumonia, surgical site infection, ED visits, readmissions, and revision procedures. Notably, when compared directly with tobacco users, nontobacco nicotine users had significantly lower rates of pneumonia, ED utilization, and readmissions. These findings suggest that although nontobacco nicotine products may not be entirely benign, their perioperative risk profile appears to be less severe than that of traditional tobacco use.
