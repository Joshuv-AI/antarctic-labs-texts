Multidisciplinary Approach to Management of Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy With Severe Left Ventricular Outflow Obstruction in Pregnancy
Authors: Aditya Bhave, Gem Mohan, Lindsey Couture, Gyanendra Sharma, Juliet Yirerong
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Pregnancy can worsen symptoms of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and increase the risk of complications. Severe left ventricular outflow tract obstruction with gradient >100 mm Hg poses a high risk of deterioration during pregnancy. We present a patient with hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy in the third trimester of pregnancy with a left ventricular outflow tract gradient >100 mm Hg and discuss successful interdisciplinary management.

A 37-year-old woman (G7P6006) at 28.3 weeks gestational age was referred to Maternal-Fetal Medicine because she had worsening exertional dyspnea, occasional lightheadedness, and 2 syncopal episodes within the prior 2 weeks. Her past medical history was significant for recurrent syncope attributed to hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM) diagnosed 3 months prior to pregnancy, at which time metoprolol tartrate 50 mg twice daily was initiated. Syncopal episodes had not recurred since then until the current presentation. There was no family history of HOCM or sudden cardiac death. She had not received preconception counseling as pregnancy was unplanned. She smoked 1 pack daily and used recreational drugs until 2 months prior to conception. Transthoracic echocardiogram 2 months prior showed an ejection fraction (EF) of 60%-65%, severe asymmetric septal hypertrophy (2.7 cm), and a significant resting left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) peak gradient of 68 mm Hg.

Question 1: What Are the Antepartum Management Considerations in a Pregnant Patient With HOCM?

Guidelines recommend continuing beta-blocker therapy in pregnancy and initiating it in patients who develop symptoms related to LVOT obstruction or arrhythmias.1 Atenolol is the 1 beta blocker that is not recommended in pregnancy due to fetal growth retardation risk.2 Mavacamtem, a recently approved cardiac myosin inhibitor for NYHA functional class II-III HOCM, is also not recommended in pregnancy due to potential fetal toxicity in animal studies.3

Monitoring the fetus for bradycardia and growth restriction is crucial throughout pregnancy. Comprehensive assessment involves fetal echocardiogram with detailed evaluation of function and rhythm and anatomic growth scans.

Furthermore, preconception counseling is vital for patients with HOCM who are considering pregnancy. Patients should be educated on the risk of maternal cardiac complications and genetic transmission to their offspring.

For our patient, metoprolol tartrate was increased to 75 mg twice daily at the initial visit. She was referred urgently to cardio-obstetrics for further evaluation. However, she failed to show up for this and subsequent follow-up visits.

Question 2: What Are the Complications That Could Arise in a Pregnant Patient With HOCM?

Pregnancy is usually well tolerated by patients with HOCM, yet risks increase with severe LVOT obstruction, diastolic dysfunction, moderate or severe systolic dysfunction, and pulmonary hypertension. Complications include heart failure, sustained ventricular tachycardia, maternal death, atrial fibrillation, syncope, preeclampsia, neonatal death, and preterm birth.4

Patients with HOCM are particularly susceptible to changes in preload, afterload, contractility, and heart rate, all of which can be affected by labor, delivery, and anesthesia. Guidelines recommend echocardiograms be performed in the second and third trimesters, especially because most complications occur near the end of pregnancy.1 Key indices to monitor are changes in EF, valve function, and LVOT gradient.

At 34 weeks gestational age, the patient presented to the emergency department with chest discomfort, cough, and dyspnea. Vitals showed blood pressure of 145/93 mm Hg, heart rate of 104 beats/min, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 100% on room air. A chest x-ray showed mild pulmonary edema. Guidelines suggest that whereas vaginal delivery is preferred among most patients with HOCM, caesarean delivery should be considered in patients with severe LVOT obstruction.1 In consultation with cardio-obstetrics, obstetrics, and anesthesiology, the decision was made to proceed with early caesarean delivery at 35 weeks gestational age via low-transverse caesarean section. She was counseled against future pregnancies.

Diuresis was initially avoided despite pulmonary edema due to concern for worsening LVOT gradient with reduced preload; however, low-dose intravenous furosemide was initiated at 20 mg daily on hospital day 2 when her dyspnea worsened, and pro–B-type natriuretic peptide had risen from 3,983 to 6,659 pg/mL. Her urine output was closely monitored. Metoprolol was also increased to 100 mg twice daily to achieve a goal heart rate of 60-70 beats/min.

Question 3: What Are the Intraoperative Management Considerations During Delivery, Especially With Anesthesia?

Spinal anesthesia, commonly used in caesarean delivery, is generally safe but can lead to hypotension, potentially worsening outflow obstruction in HOCM.5 Therefore, blood pressure should be diligently monitored and vasodilators avoided. In cases of HOCM-related hypotension, phenylephrine is recommended to enhance afterload and mitigate outflow obstruction.6 Alternatively, pure vasoconstrictors such as vasopressin can be considered.

An anesthesiologist with cardio-obstetrics experience assisted with our case. Hemodynamics were monitored closely via an arterial line. The patient received 2 L lactated Ringers solution to maximize preload, supported by phenylephrine and vasopressin via central venous access to maintain afterload. Tubal ligation was also performed during the procedure. Our patient gave birth to a live baby girl weighing 2.19 kg with Apgar scores of 8 and 9. The patient was monitored postoperatively in the cardiac intensive care unit while phenylephrine and vasopressin were weaned off.

Question 4: What Are the Considerations in the Management of Postpartum Preeclampsia in Patients With HOCM Who Have a Severely Increased LVOT Gradient?

On postoperative day 1, the cardiac intensive care unit team was concerned about possible postpartum preeclampsia and HELLP—hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets—syndrome. Two hypertensive readings of 158/94 mm Hg and 160/80 mm Hg were recorded. Her hemoglobin had dropped from 8.9 to 7.2 g/dL despite a reported 600 mL blood loss intraoperatively. Haptoglobin measured under 30 mg/dL; lactate dehydrogenase was 1,516 U/L; platelets had dropped from 269,000 to 158,000 per mL, creatinine rose from 1.04 to 1.75 mg/dL, and aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase increased to 159/337 U/L from 19/33 U/L.

Preeclampsia diagnosis involves new onset hypertension (SBP ≥140 mm Hg or DBP ≥90 mm Hg on 2 occasions at least 4 hours apart) associated with the new onset of any of the following: thrombocytopenia (platelets <100,000/mL); renal insufficiency (serum creatinine >1.1 mg/dL or a doubling of creatinine without other renal disease); impaired liver function (elevated transaminases to twice normal concentration); pulmonary edema; new onset headache unresponsive to medication without alternate causes or visual symptoms.7 The diagnostic criteria for HELLP syndrome includes lactate dehydrogenase ≥600 IU/L, aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase greater than twice the upper limit of normal, and platelets <100,000 per mL.7

Magnesium sulfate is preferred for prevention of eclampsia in the intrapartum and postpartum periods. However, caution is needed for patients with HOCM given the potential of worsening dynamic outflow obstruction due to vasodilation. For acute severe hypertension in preeclampsia, intravenous labetalol, hydralazine, and oral nifedipine are common choices.7 A goal blood pressure <150/100 mm Hg is reasonable in the postpartum period.8

Due to LVOT gradient concern, magnesium sulfate was withheld. Intravenous lorazepam was kept at bedside for use in the event of seizure. The patient reported interval improvement of dyspnea. She was transfused 1 U of packed red blood cells to maintain hemoglobin >8 g/dl. Though nifedipine and hydralazine can cause reflex tachycardia that can worsen LVOT gradient, we initiated oral nifedipine and not labetalol because she was already on metoprolol.

On postoperative day 2, several of her preeclampsia/HELLP syndrome–defining labs had improved. She was able to ambulate and urine output was adequate. She developed a severe headache on postoperative day 3 that was thought to be related to either preeclampsia or nifedipine adverse effect. Reassuringly, laboratory markers of preeclampsia continued to improve. Nifedipine was therefore discontinued, and metoprolol dose was increased. Headache resolved with nifedipine discontinuation. Transthoracic echocardiogram performed on this day revealed a hyperdynamic systolic function, EF >70%, and a resting LVOT peak gradient of 62 mm Hg (Figure 3). The right ventricular systolic pressure was 51 mm Hg. Question 5: What HOCM Management Options Should Be Considered Beyond the Postpartum Period?

For patients with HOCM and persistent symptoms despite optimal medical therapy, septal reduction therapy should be considered. Septal myectomy is preferred over alcohol ablation among younger patients such as ours because of the lower risk of conduction block requiring permanent pacemaker placement and a lower likelihood of reintervention secondary to residual obstruction. Alcohol ablation is also less effective if resting gradient is ≥100 mm Hg and septal thickness is ≥30 mm.6 First-degree relatives should undergo electrocardiogram and echocardiogram screening and sudden cardiac death risk assessment should occur every 1-2 years.6

At her cardio-obstetrics follow-up 2 weeks postpartum, her metoprolol was adjusted to 100 mg twice daily, targeting a heart rate of 60-70 beats/min. She reported palpitations for which an event monitor was ordered for sudden cardiac death risk stratification. She was counseled on screening her children and was referred to cardiothoracic surgery for evaluation for possible future septal myectomy. Mavacamtem was not considered because she would have needed multiple follow-up echocardiograms on therapy but had a questionable history of keeping her clinic appointments. Her appointment with cardiothoracic surgery is scheduled, and prolonged cardiac rhythm monitoring is in progress.

Conclusions

Pregnant women with HOCM and severe LVOT obstruction are at an increased risk of complications throughout pregnancy. An interdisciplinary team involving Cardiology, obstetrics, cardio-obstetrics, anesthesiology, maternal-fetal medicine, and neonatal intensive care experts is essential for optimal maternal and fetal outcomes. Maximizing preload, augmenting afterload, and avoiding increases in contractility and heart rate are crucial to avoid worsening of LVOT obstruction.
