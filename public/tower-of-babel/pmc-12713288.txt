Speech feature identification model for depressed individuals with suicidal ideation based on autobiographical memory
Authors: Ying Zhu, Qianlan Yin, Huijing Xu, Fang Xiao, Qian Jiang, Meng Liang, Qi Cheng, Taosheng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicidal ideation in depression is a critical predictor of suicide risk, yet its objective and early identification remains a significant challenge. Current machine learning models often fail to distinguish specific markers of suicidality from the general symptoms of severe depression. This study aimed to develop and validate a multimodal model, integrating vocal features and autobiographical memory, to specifically distinguish depressed patients with suicidal ideation from those without.MethodsThis study enrolled 88 patients with depression, who were divided into three groups based on depression severity and the presence of suicidal ideation: mild depression without suicidal ideation(mD-NSI), moderate depression with suicidal ideation(MD-SI), and severe depression with suicidal ideation(SD-SI). Methodologies included the Autobiographical Memory Test (AMT), clinical scales (BDI-II, OGMQ), and comprehensive vocal feature extraction. We used repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) for group comparisons and developed machine learning models, primarily Random Forest, for various classification tasks.ResultsSignificant differences were found in autobiographical memory; patients with suicidal ideation demonstrated significant overgeneralization, retrieving fewer specific memories than those without. Acoustically, individuals with suicidal ideation exhibited distinct vocal patterns, including reduced prosodic variation and altered spectral energy, indicated by features like Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), spectral centroid, and zero-crossing rate. A Random Forest model achieved high accuracy (AUC up to 1.00) in classification. Crucially, model interpretability analysis (SHAP) revealed that the predictive importance of features shifted depending on the clinical comparison: autobiographical memory scores were key for distinguishing the initial presence of suicidal ideation, whereas depression severity scores became more prominent when differentiating between moderate and severe cases who were already suicidal.ConclusionThe integrated analysis of vocal features and autobiographical memory, validated through an interpretable machine learning model, offers a powerful and objective approach for predicting suicidal ideation in depression. This multimodal method not only effectively differentiates patients with and without suicidal ideation but also provides novel insights into the shifting cognitive and physiological markers of suicide risk. It represents a significant step towards developing precise, clinically applicable tools for early risk identification and timely intervention.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07635-0.Keywords: Depression, Suicide, Speech, Autobiographical memory, Machine learning

Introduction

Globally, over 322 million individuals suffer from major depressive disorder (MDD), a leading cause of disability and disease burden [1].Individuals with MDD frequently grapple with diminished self-esteem and depressive moods that, over time, can intensify into a pervasive sense of pessimism and world-weariness [2]. With recent studies suggesting a potential rise in its prevalence [3], and its position as the fourth leading cause of human suffering and impaired social development, the imperative for early detection and effective treatment of depression has become a critical focus of societal concern [4].The most tragic outcome of depression is suicide, which inflicts immense suffering upon families and carries profound societal consequences [5]. Despite significant investment and attention directed toward mental health over the last two decades, suicide persists as a major global public health issue. Statistical data indicates that between 65% and 80% of individuals with depression experience suicidal ideation, with the lifetime incidence of suicide attempts falling between 15% and 25% [6]. While suicide is preventable, for every completed suicide, there are 10 to 20 attempts [7]. The challenge is compounded by individuals who meticulously plan their suicide, thereby evading medical and familial oversight. This underscores the critical need for identifying high-risk individuals and deploying early interventions. However, progress in suicide risk prediction has remained largely stagnant for the past 50 years, a stagnation potentially due to the lack of a comprehensive theoretical framework explaining the transition from suicidal ideation to action [8]. Suicidal behavior is a complex, progressive process, often initiated by heightened sensitivity to external stressors, which culminates in suicidal thoughts, attempts, and, tragically, completion [9]. As a key stage primarily driven by psychological factors, suicidal ideation presents a crucial window for intervention [10]. Given that approximately 50% of individuals who die by suicide had a clinical diagnosis of depression, the accurate prediction of suicidal ideation within this population is paramount for effective suicide prevention [11].

Traditional methods for assessing suicide risk, such as psychological scales and clinical interviews, are often limited by their reliance on a patient’s willingness to self-disclose. Furthermore, clinical judgments are inherently subjective, influenced by the practitioner’s expertise, personal biases, and cultural background. These limitations underscore an urgent need to identify objective biomarkers. The search for such indicators is an active area of research, with recent systematic reviews highlighting the potential of neurophysiological measures. For example, specific components of event-related potentials (ERPs), like the P3, have been suggested as promising neural markers for identifying individuals at high risk and improving predictive accuracy [12]. This broader shift towards objective assessment informs the rationale for exploring more nuanced methodologies, such as the Autobiographical Memory Test (AMT). Autobiographical memory, which is central to our sense of self, involves the intricate processes of storing and retrieving information about personal life events [13]. The AMT, developed by Williams and colleagues, employs emotional cue words to elicit these memories [6]. Research consistently shows that individuals with depression exhibit a distinct pattern of autobiographical recall known as Overgeneral Memory (OGM), where they struggle to retrieve specific memories and instead recall categorical events [14]. OGM is recognized as a stable cognitive marker in depression, influencing its onset, course, and prognosis [15]. Moreover, it is considered a significant vulnerability factor for suicidal behavior, contributing to the emergence of suicidal ideation [16] The similar impairments in memory specificity observed in both severely depressed individuals and suicide attempters suggest that OGM may be a shared vulnerability factor, playing a pivotal role in the development of suicidal thoughts in those with depression.

The Autobiographical Memory Test (AMT) provides a structured yet relatively spontaneous method for collecting language data, which is a rich source of information for diagnosing mental disorders [17]. Beyond the content of speech, the acoustic characteristics of the voice itself offer a non-invasive and clinically accessible window into a patient’s psychological state [18]. Studies have identified that individuals with depression often exhibit specific vocal markers, including increased amplitude, and reduced variation in fundamental frequency (F0) [19]. Furthermore, alterations in spectral energy have been correlated with the severity of depression [20]. However, while the link between depression and vocal features is increasingly studied, research specifically targeting the vocal signatures of suicidality within depression remains markedly insufficient. This reflects a broader gap: although the correlation between depression and suicide is well-established, research on suicide as a distinct phenomenon is less extensive than that on depression itself, and studies that seek to differentiate the risk of suicide within a depressed population using objective markers are particularly scarce.

This scarcity leads to a critical issue in current research, especially in the application of machine learning. Most existing studies focus on a binary classification: distinguishing depressed individuals from healthy controls [21, 22] As a result, when these models are applied to suicide risk, they often fail to disentangle the acoustic features of severe depression from the specific markers of active suicidal ideation. A model may achieve high accuracy simply by identifying severe depression—a known risk factor—rather than isolating the unique acoustic signature of suicidality itself. In essence, current machine learning models for suicide risk do not adequately differentiate their predictions from those of general depression models. Furthermore, many of these studies rely on tasks that lack a strong psychological grounding for eliciting speech related to self-representation and memory, which are central to the suicidal crisis. This leaves a critical gap for psychologically informed experiments that can isolate the specific vocal characteristics of suicidal thinking.

Building upon these identified gaps, the present study aims to move beyond simple prediction and address a more fundamental challenge: disentangling the predictive features of suicidal ideation from those of depression severity. Current models often fail to clarify whether they identify suicidality itself or merely severe depression, a known but non-specific risk factor. To overcome this, we designed a three-group study to create distinct analytical comparisons. Our primary research question is: Can we identify objective biomarkers (vocal, memory) that are specific to the presence of suicidal ideation, rather than being confounded by the severity of depressive symptoms? We hypothesize that by systematically comparing groups that differ primarily in suicidal ideation versus those that differ in depression severity, we can isolate a core set of features uniquely characteristic of the suicidal state. This approach will allow us to build a more precise and interpretable identification model, clarifying the distinct contributions of cognitive and vocal markers to suicide risk within depression.

Materials and methods

Participants

Patients with depression were recruited from a psychiatric specialty hospital between June 2024 and March 2025. During clinical diagnostic process, physicians recruited eligible patients based on their conditions, informed them of the study’s specific content and requirements, and obtained signed consent forms. Inclusion criteria: patients diagnosed with depression by a psychiatrist, aged 16–45 years, with at least a junior high school education, and able to read Chinese comfortably. Exclusion criteria: presence of other psychiatric comorbidities, throat diseases, or electroconvulsive therapy within the past week. Patients with drug abuse, addiction, or a history of mental illness, were also excluded. Participants in this study were divided into three groups based on their level of depression and suicidal ideation: (1) 26 individuals with mild depression (and below) without suicidal ideation (BDI-II: 0–19, and a score of 0 on items 4 or 5 in the BSI-CV)for the past week); (2) 35 individuals with moderate depression with suicidal ideation (BDI-II: 20–28, and a score other than 0 on items 4 or 5 in the BSI-CV for the past week); (3) 27 individuals with severe depression with suicidal ideation (BDI-II:29–63, and a score other than 0 on items 4 or 5 in the BSI-CV for the past week).

All participants provided with informed consent, and were assured of their privacy and the right to withdraw from the study at any time. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the 904th Hospital of the Chinese PLA Joint Logistics Support Force, with the approval number: [SFYEC-202403-C9].

Study design

This study utilized cue words from the classic AMT paradigm, comprising randomly presented positive, neutral, and negative categories. Each cue word was displayed for 60 s before the screen automatically advanced. Participants were instructed to recall and narrate a specific personal experience based on the cue word. Subsequently, they self-evaluated the specificity and emotional intensity of their recall on a scale. The AMT procedure was programmed using E-Prime 3.0 and displayed on a Lenovo L1710A LCD monitor. The experiment was conducted in a quiet and comfortable behavioral laboratory. Participants were required to remain alert and focused throughout the task. After receiving standardized instructions, they completed a practice session with neutral cue words (e.g., table, car, clothing, television, spring) until they could proficiently narrate a specific event based on a cue. The formal experiment commenced once proficiency was achieved.

During the experiment, nine cue words were randomly presented to participants, with three positive, three negative, and three neutral words. Each word was displayed for 60 s. Participants were asked to recall a specific personal experience based on the cue word and narrate it. Final evaluations of the participants’ recall results were conducted by two psychology graduate students, who assessed the specificity of the narratives and categorized them as either specific or generalized memories. Specific memories were defined as events occurring within a day, with specific times, locations, and contexts. Generalized memories included the following categories: (1) categorical memories: events or situations occurring multiple times without a clear date; (2) extended memories: events lasting more than one day; (3) repetitive memories: events or situations repeated across different cue word responses; (4) no memory: failure to recall any event; and (5) semantic memories: non-contextual events, such as overall feelings or impressions of the cue word. The number of specific memories was used as a scoring metric, with higher scores indicating lower levels of memory generalization and vice versa.

Assessments

Demographic and antipsychotic medication data

Clinical demographic information was collected, including gender, marital status, education level, family economic situation, per capita monthly family income, parents’ education levels, family residence, only child status, family background, history of mental illness in close relatives, major life events during upbringing, oral ulcer symptoms, gastrointestinal symptoms, family relationships, perceived family stress, and psychological stress experienced in the past month. Since antipsychotic medications can affect voice through extrapyramidal side effects, the doses of these medications were checked and compared across groups [23]. Because voice quality may be affected by antipsychotic medications, the total doses of antipsychotic medications were converted to aripiprazole-equivalent doses for summarization [23]. Patients taking any medications other than antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, antidepressants, and benzodiazepines were categorized as taking “other medications”.

Scales

The Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) [24]was utilized to assess the severity of participants’ depressive symptoms. The BDI-II is a 21-item self-report measure that evaluates the intensity of depression over the preceding two weeks. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 0 to 3), yielding a total score between 0 and 63. The scores are interpreted as follows: 0–13 indicates minimal or no depression, 14–19 indicates mild depression, 20–28 indicates moderate depression, and 29–63 indicates severe depression.The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the Chinese version of the scale is 0.94 [25].

The Over-general autobiographical memory questionnaire (OGMQ) was used to assist in observing the degree of generalization of autobiographical memory in the subjects. The OGMQ is a 19-item self-report scale designed to evaluate the specificity of autobiographical memory. An example item is, “I can recall a specific event that I personally experienced during my middle school years.” Responses are recorded on a four-point Likert scale (1 = ‘completely agree’, 2 = ‘somewhat agree’, 3 = ‘somewhat disagree’, 4 = ‘completely disagree’). Total scores range from 19 to 76, with higher scores being proportional to the severity of OGM. The established cutoff scores are: 19–37 for no autobiographical memory deficit, 38–55 for moderate deficit, and 56–76 for severe deficit. Individuals with severe OGM are considered a high-risk population for depression and may require clinical intervention. The questionnaire’s items were developed based on the Autobiographical Memory Test (AMT) [6] and the Sentence Completion for Events from the Past Test (SCEPT) [11].

The Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation-Chinese Version (BSI-CV) [26] was used to evaluate participants’ suicidal ideation. The BSI-CV is a 19-item self-report inventory that assesses the severity of thoughts about life and death and suicidal ideation. Items are rated on a 3-point scale (0–2), with total scores ranging from 0 to 38. A score other than 0 on item 4 or 5 is indicative of the presence of suicidal ideation. Higher total scores reflect more severe suicidal ideation. Participants are asked to respond to each item in reference to two distinct time frames: the past week and the period during which they felt most depressed in their lives. The Chinese version of the scale has good internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.870), structural validity (χ2/df = 3.550, RMSEA = 0.053, GFI = 0.944, CFI = 0.970, NFI = 0.958, IFI = 0.970) and criterion-related validity (r (OGMQ, AMT) = 0.870; r (OGMQ, BDI) = 0.507).

Vocal feature extraction

Voice recordings were conducted in a quiet professional studio. Participants sat 50 cm away from the computer screen, with the recorder positioned 20 cm from their mouths. As a crucial preprocessing step to control for variations in recording loudness across participants, all audio files were first normalized. Following standard procedures, we used the ‘Scale intensity’ command in Praat to scale each recording to a uniform root-mean-square (RMS) intensity of 70 dB. This normalization was performed prior to the extraction of any acoustic features, as described below. This ensures that the extracted features are not biased by differences in microphone placement or vocal effort.Voice recordings were captured using a Roland R-26 portable recorder with a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz, 16-bit quantization, and dual-channel recording.After segmenting the recordings, acoustic features were extracted using PRAAT and Python software packages. These features, detailed in Table 6, primarily included pitch-related metrics (e.g., mean_pitch, error_pitch, change_p), key spectral measures (spectral_centroid, spectral_bandwidth, spectral_roll-off, Root-mean-square energy, Zero-crossing rate), and timbral qualities of the short-term power spectrum represented by a total of 40 Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), ranging from MFCC1 to MFCC40. For the final analysis, a single summary statistic (the mean) was computed for each of these features over the entire duration of a speech segment. This process yielded a single value per feature for each segment, ensuring that all 40 MFCCs were treated as individual features in the model, which is consistent with the data presented in Table 6.

Statistical analysis

Prior to all parametric tests, the normality of data distribution for all dependent variables within each group was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. All variables met the assumption of normality or approximate normality, justifying the use of analysis of variance (ANOVA).Task behavioral performance data were organized and evaluated using Eprime 3.0 software. Acoustic features were extracted using the open-source PRAAT software and Python toolkits. SPSS 26.0 software was employed for statistical analysis. Metric data were described using means (M) and standard deviations (SD), while single-factor analysis of variance was used for intergroup comparisons. Categorical data were described using frequencies and percentages, with chi-square tests for intergroup comparisons. For all repeated-measures analyses of variance, partial eta-squared 𝜂2𝑝 was used to indicate effect size. The Mauchly sphericity test determined whether data met the sphericity assumption. Data violating this assumption were corrected using the Greenhouse-Geisser method for degrees of freedom and p-values. For indicators showing significant main effects of group or group-by-group interaction effects, Pearson correlation analysis further assessed correlations between suicidal ideation and these indicators. A p-value lower than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Machine learning modeling was conducted using Python to identify suicidal ideation and depression severity in patients. Based on recent empirical recommendations, six commonly used machine learning classifiers were employed [27]: random forest, logistic regression, support vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbor (KNN), naïve Bayes, and XGboost. To prevent overfitting, 10-fold cross-validation was used to develop classification models. Subsequently, the hyperopt Python library was utilized to select optimal hyperparameters for each classifier through iterative tuning. After hyperparameter optimization, 1000 bootstrap analyses were performed to verify the final model’s reliability, and the key parameters for our classifiers were established. The Logistic Regression model used a ‘liblinear’ solver with L2 regularization. The Support Vector Machine (SVM) employed a radial basis function (RBF) kernel. The Random Forest was configured with 100 estimators and ‘sqrt’ for max features. The XGBoost model was set with a ‘binary: logistic’ objective. The Gaussian Naive Bayes model utilized the default var_smoothing for numerical stability. Specifically for the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), the architecture comprised a Conv1D layer (64 filters, kernel size 3, ReLU activation), followed by a MaxPooling1D layer, a Flatten layer, a Dense hidden layer (64 neurons, ReLU), and a sigmoid output layer. This model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 and binary cross-entropy as the loss function.

For performance comparison, several metrics were calculated for each classifier, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with 95% confidence intervals and mean values. Due to class imbalance, the weighted F1 score was used as the primary evaluation metric, calculated as the weighted average of each class’s F1 score. The F1-weighted score and AUROC are critical for comprehensive model evaluation, especially in imbalanced datasets, as they avoid assessment distortions caused by majority-class biases. The optimal model was selected based on the highest scores of these two metrics. If results were inconsistent, the model with the higher AUROC value was chosen. SHapley Additive exPlanations(SHAP) were used for model interpretability. All processes were implemented in Python using SMOTE, hyperopt, and SHAP packages. Machine learning and feature disentanglement strategy

To achieve our primary goal of isolating SI-specific features, we employed a sequential, multi-stage machine learning strategy:

Stage 1: Identifying Depression-Related Features. We first trained two separate Random Forest models on Dataset C (mD-NSI vs. SD-SI) and Dataset B (MD-SI vs. SD-SI). SHAP values were calculated for both models to generate ranked lists of the top 20 most influential features. The logic is that features critical for distinguishing depression severity (from Dataset B) that are also critical for distinguishing the two clinical extremes (from Dataset C) are highly likely to be strong markers of the depression continuum itself. Therefore, features that appeared in the top ranks of both lists were conceptually flagged as “depression-related confounders.”

Stage 2: Training and Validating the Suicidal Ideation-Specific Model. Next, a definitive Random Forest model was trained on Dataset A (mD-NSI vs. MD-SI). This dataset provides the cleanest possible comparison for the presence versus absence of suicidal ideation, with minimized variance in depression severity.

Stage 3: Hypothesis Validation. The final step was to validate our core hypothesis. We applied SHAP analysis to the model trained on Dataset A. Our objective was to determine if this model would preferentially and heavily rely on features not previously flagged as “depression-related confounders.” If the model prioritized features like AMT scores over known depression markers like BDI-II, it would provide strong evidence that we have successfully built a model that is truly sensitive to suicidal ideation, rather than merely detecting severe depression.

For model development and evaluation, we implemented a rigorous data-splitting and validation protocol. Each dataset was partitioned into a training set (80%) and a testing set (20%) using stratified sampling. This approach ensured that the proportion of clinical subgroups in both the training and testing sets accurately reflected their distribution in the original dataset, which is crucial for handling potential class imbalances. Specifically, for our primary model trained on Dataset B(N = 62), the training set comprised 50 samples, and the testing set comprised 12 samples; Dataset C(N = 53), the training set comprised 42 samples, and the testing set comprised 11 samples. We did not set aside a separate, fixed validation set. Instead, we employed a more robust 10-fold cross-validation technique on the training set for hyperparameter tuning and internal model validation. This method maximizes the use of our available data and provides a more reliable estimate of model performance by averaging results over 10 different train-validate splits, thus mitigating the risk of overfitting and the potential bias of a single, arbitrary validation split. The final performance of the optimized model was then assessed on the completely unseen Dataset A set.

Results

Based on BSI-CV and BDI-II results, they were divided into 26 individuals with mild depression (and below) without suicidal ideation (mD-NSI), 35 individuals with moderate depression with suicidal ideation (MD-SI), and 27 individuals with severe depression with suicidal ideation (SD-SI).Chi-square tests and one-way analysis of variance showed no significant differences among the three groups in gender, marital status, family economic situation, per capita monthly family income, parents’ education levels, family residence, only child status, family background, history of mental illness in close relatives, major life events during upbringing, oral ulcer symptoms, gastrointestinal symptoms, family relationships, perceived family stress, or medication dosages. This suggests that study results were largely unaffected by demographic factors. Descriptive statistics for the demographic characteristics of the three groups are presented in Table 1.

Comparison of acoustic features

Machine learning prediction models

The model included scale features such as OGMQ and BDI-II. AMT features included the number of specific and generalized memories in response to different types of cue words, as well as total scores for specific and generalized memories. Additionally, acoustic features with significant differences identified through repeated-measures analysis of variance were selected. The collected dataset was divided into three groups: mD-NSI and MD-SI(Dataset A), MD-SI and SD-SI(Dataset B), MS-NSI and SD-SI (Dataset C).This specific grouping was fundamental to our feature disentanglement strategy, a multi-stage approach designed to systematically isolate predictors of suicidal ideation (SI) from the confounding effects of depression severity. Specifically, Datasets B and C were strategically employed to first identify features strongly associated with the depression continuum itself. By analyzing markers that distinguish both varying levels of depression (Dataset B) and the clinical extremes (Dataset C), we could operationally define them as “depression-related confounders.” Subsequently, our primary predictive model was trained on Dataset A, which provides a controlled comparison for the presence versus absence of SI while minimizing variance in depression scores. This design allows for a more robust validation of SI-specific markers, with a full description of the methodology detailed in the Methods section.

First, the performance of six machine learning models was compared using Dataset C to identify the optimal classifier for suicidal ideation. As detailed in the performance metrics table, four models achieved identical, top-tier results: the Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, Random Forest, and XGBoost models all yielded an accuracy of 0.9917 ± 0.0250. The Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) also demonstrated strong performance with an accuracy of 0.9667 ± 0.0553, while the Naïve Bayes model showed the lowest performance among the group at 0.9583 ± 0.0559. The confusion matrices presented in Fig. 2 offer a more granular, per-class breakdown of these results, illustrating the models’ robust ability to correctly identify cases in each category.The close convergence of performance among the top models strongly suggests that the multimodal feature set employed in this study—integrating autobiographical memory, clinical scales, and acoustic features—is exceptionally powerful and highly discriminative for the classification task. Given this parity in predictive power, the final selection was based on the critical attribute of interpretability. A primary goal of this research is not only to predict suicidal ideation but also to understand which cognitive and vocal features are the most significant markers. Random Forest (RF) provides a distinct advantage in this regard. It has a built-in mechanism for ranking feature importance, which seamlessly integrates with advanced explanatory methods like SHAP analysis (as used in Fig. 3) to illuminate the model’s decision-making process. This contrasts with models like Support Vector Machines, which often function as less transparent “black boxes.” Therefore, despite the parity in performance metrics, the Random Forest model was selected as the optimal classifier for its superior interpretability, which is essential for achieving the study’s goal of feature disentanglement.

Discussion

This study investigated the vocal features, autobiographical memory, and suicidal ideation in patients with depression, revealing significant differences in autobiographical memory and vocal features between patients with and without suicidal ideation. The findings indicate that suicidal ideation in patients with depression is closely associated with overgeneralized autobiographical memory. Patients exhibit lower specific memory recall scores and higher generalized memory scores when recalling self-related events. Additionally, vocal feature analysis revealed significant differences in multiple acoustic features, such as Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC), fundamental frequency (F0), and pitch error, with patients showing more monotonous voices and reduced pitch variation. Machine learning models, particularly the random forest model, demonstrated excellent performance in predicting suicidal ideation, suggesting that the combination of vocal features and autobiographical memory can effectively identify patients at high risk of suicide. The correlation between suicidal ideation and depression severity, as well as the potential link between vocal features and suicide risk, holds important clinical implications. Through machine learning models, we can identify predictive factors of suicidal ideation in patients with depression, providing new insights for early warning systems of suicide risk.

The relationship between autobiographical memory and suicidal ideation

A primary finding of this study is the significant correlation between the severity of suicidal ideation and the overgeneralization of autobiographical memory (OGM). Specifically, patients with depression and co-occurring suicidal ideation, particularly those in the severe group, exhibited a significantly impaired ability to retrieve specific episodic memories of personal experiences (indicated by lower specificity scores) and instead showed a tendency toward broad, generic recollections (indicated by higher generalization scores). This pattern was consistent across positive, neutral, and negative emotional cues and was especially pronounced in response to positive cues.

This result is highly consistent with seminal research in the field, reaffirming that OGM is a stable cognitive marker for depression and suicide risk [6, 28]. Further, our findings refine the current understanding by elucidating a graded difference in OGM across varying severities of suicidal ideation. Notably, the exacerbated OGM observed in patients with suicidal ideation when recalling positive events aligns with the conclusions of research on suicide attempters [29], suggesting that impaired retrieval of positive past experiences may be a unique cognitive deficit in populations at risk for suicide. OGM is not merely a decline in memory function but may represent a maladaptive cognitive avoidance strategy. According to the “functional avoidance” hypothesis, patients unconsciously adopt a generalized recall style to avoid the specific details associated with negative emotions [6]. While this mechanism may offer temporary protection from emotional distress, in the long term, it deprives individuals of the ability to draw strength from past successes to solve current problems. The difficulty in retrieving positive memories, in particular, can reinforce negative cognitive schemas [30], leading to heightened feelings of hopelessness and pushing individuals toward the brink of suicide.This process aligns with advanced models of OGM, such as the Capture and Rumination, Functional Avoidance, and Impaired Executive Control (CaR-FA-X) model, which posits that OGM is a maladaptive cognitive style preventing effective emotional processing of past events [31].Furthermore, the impaired ability to access specific positive autobiographical memories, especially those involving social connection, may exacerbate feelings of “thwarted belongingness”—a core component of the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide—thereby providing a direct cognitive pathway to the development of suicidal desire [32].

Acoustic features significantly associated with suicidal ideation

The detailed statistical results of the repeated-measures ANOVA for all 49 acoustic features are summarized in Table 6, which is located in the Appendix for easy reference. This study identified a series of acoustic features significantly associated with suicidal ideation. Compared to the group without suicidal ideation, the speech of patients with suicidal ideation exhibited differences across multiple dimensions, particularly in Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), spectral centroid, and zero-crossing rate. Collectively, changes in these acoustic parameters point to a more monotonous vocal pattern with reduced prosodic variation and abnormal energy distribution. These results are consistent with the monotonous speech and slower speech rate observed in patients with depression by Yamamoto et al. [33]. They also support recent studies by Yünden et al. [34] and Wang et al. [21], which concluded that features like MFCCs are highly valuable in distinguishing individuals at risk for suicide from healthy controls using machine learning.

Speech constitutes a physiological process meticulously governed by the nervous system, and its fluctuations can serve as an objective indicator of an individual’s internal condition. Suicidal ideation is frequently accompanied by pronounced anhedonia, psychomotor retardation, and emotional insensitivity. These cardinal symptoms directly influence the central nervous system’s regulation of the vocal apparatus (e.g., laryngeal muscles, respiratory system), resulting in a diminished fundamental frequency (F0) range and alterations in the distribution of resonant frequencies, as evidenced by MFCCs [35]. Consequently, the acoustic distinctions observed in this study may be construed as an objective manifestation of the underlying psychopathological state of suicidal ideation upon vocal behavior. However, it is noteworthy that the extent of alterations in particular acoustic parameters noted in our study may vary from those observed in other research. Such discrepancies likely arise from differences in the composition of the samples studied, the linguistic and cultural contexts, as well as the experimental methodologies employed.

The efficacy of machine learning models combining autobiographical and acoustic features for the detection of suicidal ideation

A core innovation of this study lies not merely in creating a predictive model, but in implementing a multi-stage analytical strategy designed to disentangle the specific biomarkers of suicidal ideation (SI) from the confounding effects of depression severity. By leveraging our three-group design and SHAP analysis, we moved beyond a “black box” approach to reveal the distinct roles of different feature sets.To provide the most definitive proof of this disentanglement, we conducted a decisive comparative analysis. A new model was constructed using only the features previously identified as “depression-related confounders” (henceforth, “Model Dep”), and its performance was directly compared against our primary model, which uses the putative SI-specific features (“Model SI”). When both were tasked with classifying suicidal ideation on Dataset A (mD-NSI vs. MD-SI), the results, as shown in Table 5, were unequivocal. The near-random performance of Model Dep (AUC ≈ 0.49) was not a failure, but rather a crucial piece of evidence, powerfully demonstrating that features for depression severity are insufficient for this specific task. In stark contrast, the exceptional performance of Model SI (AUC ≈ 0.93) validates our entire methodological framework. This substantial performance gap serves as the ultimate validation of our hypothesis, confirming that we successfully isolated a feature set that is not only predictive but highly specific to suicidal ideation.

Our analysis began with Stage 1: Identifying Depression-Related Confounders. By comparing the feature importance of models trained on Dataset B (MD-SI vs. SD-SI) and Dataset C (mD-NSI vs. SD-SI), we identified a core set of “depression-related confounders.” These features, including BDI-II, AMT-General, Perceived psychological stress, and several MFCCs, were consistently ranked as highly important for distinguishing varying levels of depression severity. This is clinically intuitive: BDI-II is a direct measure of depressive symptoms, while overgeneral memory (AMT-General) and flattened vocal prosody (reflected in MFCCs) are well-established cognitive and physiological correlates of worsening depression. Flagging these features was a critical first step in preventing our final model from simply being a proxy for a depression severity detector.

Interestingly, this comparative analysis also unearthed a unique set of predictors. We observed that features such as change_p (pitch variability), Family of origin, Self-assessment of family stress, Symptoms of oral ulcer, educational level, and Mel-frequency cepstral coefficient 10 were important for distinguishing the clinical extremes (Dataset C: mD-NSI vs. SD-SI) but were notably less important when comparing two groups already experiencing SI (Dataset B: MD-SI vs. SD-SI). This finding carries significant meaning. These features do not merely track the linear progression of severity; instead, they appear to signify the factors associated with the fundamental transition from a state of mild depression without SI to one of severe depression with SI. The inclusion of psychosocial factors like family stress and background (Family of origin, Self-assessment of family stress) suggests that the vulnerability to this major clinical shift is tied to an individual’s socio-environmental context. Meanwhile, the emergence of specific acoustic (change_p, MFCC10) and physiological (Symptoms of oral ulcer) markers may signal the initial onset of the profound psychomotor and somatic changes that accompany the development of suicidality.

Finally, we conducted Stage 3: Hypothesis Validation by analyzing the definitive SI-specific model trained on Dataset A (mD-NSI vs. MD-SI). This dataset offered the cleanest comparison for the presence versus absence of SI, with minimized variance in depression severity. As hypothesized, the SHAP analysis for this model revealed that it preferentially and heavily relied on features not previously flagged as “depression-related confounders.” Specifically, cognitive markers like AMT-Concrete and AMT-General emerged as top predictors. This provides strong evidence that when the overwhelming signal of severe depression is controlled for, the impaired ability to retrieve specific autobiographical memories becomes a primary, specific indicator of the suicidal state. This successfully validates our approach, demonstrating that we have built a model that is truly sensitive to the cognitive signatures of suicidal ideation, rather than merely detecting severe depression. In summary, our disentanglement strategy successfully differentiated three classes of biomarkers: (1) general depression confounders, (2) vulnerability factors for the transition to a severe suicidal state, and (3) SI-specific cognitive markers, painting a far more precise and clinically useful picture of suicide risk within depression.

Compared to studies relying solely on acoustic features [35]or traditional clinical scales [36]for prediction, the multimodal integration strategy of this study demonstrates a clear advantage. It overcomes the limitations of subjective self-reporting inherent in traditional clinical assessments and transcends the potential narrowness of single objective indicators. By employing the SHAP method, similar to that used in studies like Zhou et al. [37], we not only built a high-accuracy “black box” model but also successfully rendered its predictive logic transparent and interpretable, greatly enhancing its credibility and utility in clinical decision-making. The success of machine learning stems from its ability to capture complex, non-linear relationships in high-dimensional data that are imperceptible to human observers. Suicidal ideation is a complex phenomenon driven by multiple factors that a single indicator can hardly encompass. The high efficiency of our model can be attributed to its simulation of a comprehensive “clinical diagnostic reasoning” process, considering memory indicators that reflect deep cognitive patterns, incorporating vocal indicators that reveal instantaneous physiological states, and integrating contextual clinical and psychosocial factors (e.g., depression severity, social support). This data-driven, multi-pronged approach naturally approximates the true complexity of the condition more closely than any single method, thereby achieving more accurate risk identification.

Strengths, Limitations, and future directions

The notable strength of this study is its innovative multimodal research paradigm, which successfully combines cognitive psychology, acoustic analysis, and artificial intelligence to provide a new objective and quantifiable tool for the early identification of suicidal ideation. Nevertheless, this study has several limitations.First, the relatively small sample size (N = 88), a consequence of the challenges in recruiting well-phenotyped participants from specific clinical subpopulations, may limit the generalizability of the findings and increase the risk of model overfitting. To mitigate this, we employed a principled feature selection strategy, including only features with theoretical relevance or significant statistical differences between groups, and utilized robust 10-fold cross-validation to validate our model’s performance. Still, future research with larger, more diverse samples is crucial to verify the robustness of our findings. Second, its cross-sectional design prevents the capture of the dynamic fluctuations of suicidal ideation over time. Third, the acoustic data were collected in a quiet laboratory setting, which differs from real-life scenarios filled with noise and emotional variability.

To address these limitations, future research should focus on the following directions. First, expanding the sample size and diversity is crucial to verify the robustness of the current findings across different populations. Second, longitudinal studies should be conducted to explore how dynamic changes in vocal and memory features might predict the imminent onset of a suicidal crisis, using time-series analysis methods. Third, data collection should be attempted in more ecologically valid, real-world environments (e.g., via smartphones or wearable devices) to enhance the model’s real-world applicability.Future predictive models could integrate additional dimensions of physiological data, such as heart rate variability and facial micro-expressions, to construct a more comprehensive and precise integrated warning system for suicide risk. The ultimate goal is to translate this technology into a convenient daily monitoring tool, enabling early and proactive intervention for high-risk individuals.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully moves beyond a traditional “black box” predictive model by implementing a multi-stage analytical strategy that definitively disentangles the specific biomarkers of suicidal ideation (SI) from the confounding effects of depression severity. We demonstrated through SHAP analysis that key predictive features dynamically shift depending on the clinical comparison: cognitive markers like Overgeneral Memory were paramount in identifying the presence of SI, whereas affective-symptomatic markers such as the BDI-II score were most critical for differentiating depression severity once SI was established. This process revealed that acoustic features serve as a stable physiological foundation across the depressive spectrum, Overgeneral Memory (OGM) acts as a core cognitive indicator for the emergence of SI, and the degree of affective distress (BDI-II) marks disease exacerbation. Ultimately, our work provides a more precise, mechanistically-informed framework for suicide risk assessment, paving the way for multimodal diagnostic tools that can distinguish between risk status, cognitive shifts, and stages of affective distress.
