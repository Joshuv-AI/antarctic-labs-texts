Physics-informed Gaussian Processes as Linear Model Predictive Controller with Constraint Satisfaction
Jörn Tebbe 1, Andreas Besginow 1, Markus Lange-Hegermann 1

Abstract
Model Predictive Control evolved as the state of the art paradigm for safety critical control tasks. Control-as-Inference approaches thereof model the constrained optimization problem as a probabilistic inference problem. The constraints have to be implemented into the inference model. A recently introduced physics-informed Gaussian Process method uses Control-as-Inference with a Gaussian likelihood for state constraint modeling, but lacks guarantees of open-loop constraint satisfaction. We mitigate the lack of guarantees via an additional sampling step using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampling in order to obtain safe rollouts of the open-loop dynamics which are then used to obtain an approximation of the truncated normal distribution which has full probability mass in the safe area. We provide formal guarantees of constraint satisfaction while maintaining the ODE structure of the Gaussian Process on a discretized grid. Moreover, we show that we are able to perform optimization of a quadratic cost function by closed form Gaussian Process computations only and introduce the Matérn kernel into the inference model.

Introduction

Controlling technical systems is a central part of modern industrial applications.
The physical limits of real world systems have to be considered in order to provide safe operation
[
15
]
.
Model Predictive Control
[
12
]
is a compelling framework for this, which requires a model of the system which can be derived from first order principles and/or data-driven approaches.
This model, e.g. a Gaussian Process
[
11
]
, projects the system under given inputs into the future and we obtain the optimal control input via a constrained optimization scheme.
The optimization is constrained on the dynamics, the initial state and input, and on input and state constraints for the considered horizon.

Another approach of MPC is the Control-as-Inference
[
7
]
paradigm which suggests obtaining the desired control input for the next time step by solving a probabilistic inference problem instead of an optimization.
In recent work, such a scheme was introduced using Gaussian Processes
[
14
]
.
As a Bayesian method, a Gaussian Process prior uses an inductive bias on the modeled functions and provides closed predictive distribution formulas under the assumption of Gaussian noise.

For the Control-as-Inference scheme, the authors used a Gaussian Process prior for Ordinary Differential Equations, which model the underlying control system.
The control input is obtained via conditioning the Gaussian Process on a desired reference. The obtained marginal predictive distributions provide each point in the output space with strict positive probability mass which makes this framework unsuitable for regression bounded by given constraints. Although the authors choose the mean of the predictive distribution as the control input, it is not guaranteed that the mean satisfies the given constraints.

A better choice to model bounded functions, which is required for MPC, is pushing full probability mass inside the constraints. This procedure is well known in the Gaussian Process literature for ordinary regression
[
4
]
.
The easiest approach is warping
[
13
]
, due to its preservation of the Gaussian likelihood.
Unfortunately, the nonlinear transformation breaks the covariance structure of the linear Ordinary Differential Equation kernel which means that both mean and samples no longer have to fulfill the Ordinary Differential Equation.
Therefore, we proceed to use bounded likelihood formulations which break Gaussianity, therefore require approximate solutions like Monte Carlo approximations, but retain the Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process prior unchanged.

The Monte Carlo methods use samples from the posterior and apply accept/reject sampling for the trajectories in favor of the constraints. This naive approach lacks efficiency, since the probability mass of the constrained space will degrade with growing dimension of the Gaussian Process. We therefore chose an advanced sampling strategy such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
[
10
]
which is able to approximate small subsets of the output space.

As the authors in
[
14
]
only use Squared Exponential kernel functions as part of their latent Gaussian Process prior for the parameterizing functions, the sampling for constraint satisfaction has to break the thereby induced smoothness. As a remedy, we propose to use Matérn type kernel functions with suitable smoothness parameter in order to obtain more flexible functions which are able to fulfill the constraints.
Moreover, we want to optimize cost functions, which are often of quadratic type. These cost functions provide easy to compute control laws
[
12
]
for linear Model Predictive Control problems. In a similar manner, we provide a way of incorporating quadratic cost functions in a Control-as-Inference fashion for Gaussian Processes.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we provide our considered Model Predictive Control problem and recall Control-as-Inference via Gaussian Processes. In Section
3
we provide our methodology for the choice of a better kernel, an additional reweighting as adaptation of the cost function and a subsequent sampling step for open-loop constraint satisfaction. In Section
4
we provide two numerical examples which validate our method, while we conclude in Section
5
.

Problem formulation and Preliminaries

2.1
Problem formulation

We want to solve the optimal control problem

\displaystyle\min_{u(t)}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N}(x_{\text{ref}}(t_{i})-x(t_{i}))^{2}+\|u\|

(1a)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\partial_{t}x

\displaystyle=Ax+Bu,

(1b)

\displaystyle x(t_{0})

\displaystyle=x_{0},

(1c)

\displaystyle x_{\min}

\displaystyle\leq x(t)\leq x_{\max}\quad\forall t\in[t_{0},t_{N}],

(1d)

\displaystyle u_{\min}

\displaystyle\leq u(t)\leq u_{\max}\quad\forall t\in[t_{0},t_{N}].

(1e)

with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{x}}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}\times n_{u}}
as system and control matrices, respectively
[
12
]
. The objective (
1a
) is a quadratic cost function for a given reference with regularization of the control function. The linear Ordinary Differential Equation (
1b
) and the initial state (
1c
) are assumed to be known in advance. For ease of notation, we assume that the state bounds (
1d
) and input bounds (
1e
) are constant. The proposed methods also work for time-varying bounds which we validate in section
4
. We assume that the control system
(A,B)
is controllable.

For Model Predictive Control, this problem is solved repeatedly between two time steps
t_{i}
and
t_{i+1}
, while the computed control action is applied for
t_{i+1}
to
t_{i+2}
.

2.2
Gaussian Processes

A Gaussian Process (GP)
[
11
]

g=\mathcal{GP}(\mu,k)
is a stochastic process with the property that all function evaluations
g(t_{1}),\ldots,g(t_{n})
are jointly Gaussian.
Such a Gaussian Process is fully characterized by its mean function
\mu(t)
and covariance function
k(t,t^{\prime})
.
We can condition the Gaussian Process prior on data
\mathcal{D}=(T,Z)
,with
T\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}
and
Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{z}}
, with a Gaussian likelihood and obtain the posterior via Bayes’ rule

p(f|T,Z,\theta)=\frac{p(Z|f,T,\theta)p(f,T,\theta)}{p(T,Z,\theta)}

(2)

with hyperparameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
.
Since both prior and likelihood are Gaussian, the product is also Gaussian. We thus obtain again a Gaussian Process as posterior distribution which is again fully defined by its mean and covariance which are derived as

\displaystyle\mu^{*}

\displaystyle=\mu(t^{*})+K_{*}^{\top}(K+\sigma_{n}^{2}I)^{-1}(z-\mu(t))

(3a)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{*}

\displaystyle=K_{**}-K_{*}^{\top}(K+\sigma_{n}^{2}I)^{-1}{K_{*}}

(3b)

with covariance matrices
K=(k(t_{i},t_{j}))_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
K_{*}=(k(t_{i},t^{*}_{j}))_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{*}}
and
K_{**}=(k(t^{*}_{i},t^{*}_{j}))_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{*}\times n_{*}}
for predictive positions
t^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{*}}
with noise variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}
.
The noise variance does not have to be constant (homoscedastic), but can be input dependent (heteroscedastic), i.e.
\sigma_{n}^{2}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}}
. The noise variance describes the noise we expect on a given datapoint
(t_{i},z_{i})
.
In our case we use the time
t
as input and concatenate control state and input as one variable, i.e.
(x,u)=z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}}
with
n_{z}=n_{x}+n_{u}
, for the output of the Gaussian Process. Therefore, we obtain a predictive distribution of size
n_{z}\cdot n_{*}
.

Hyperparameters are commonly introduced via the covariance function, for example the Squared Exponential
covariance function often includes signal variance
\sigma_{f}^{2}
and smoothness parameter
\ell^{2}
:

k_{\text{SE}}(t,t^{\prime})=\sigma_{f}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{(t-t^{\prime})^{2}}{2\ell^{2}}\right)

(4)

As the function
k_{\text{SE}}
is infinitely many times differentiable, so are the samples of the Gaussian Process almost surely.
Another class of covariance functions is the Matérn family

\displaystyle k_{\nu}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{f}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2r+1}|t-t^{\prime}|}{\ell}\right)v(x)

\displaystyle v(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{r!}{(2r)!}\sum_{i=0}^{r}\frac{(r+i)!}{i!(r-i)!}\left(\frac{2\sqrt{2r+1}|t-t^{\prime}|}{\ell}\right)^{r-i}

for
\nu=r+\frac{1}{2}
and
\ell>0
. The Gaussian Process prior
\mathcal{GP}(0,k_{r+\frac{1}{2}})
is
r
times mean squared differentiable
[
11
]
, in contrast to its samples which are not necessarily
r
times differentiable.
Note, that the Squared Exponential kernel is obtained as a Matérn kernel for
\nu\to\infty
and is therefore infinitely many times differentiable.

2.3
Linear Ordinary Differential Equation GPs

The class of Gaussian Processes is closed under linear operations,
i.e. applying a linear operator
\mathcal{L}
to a Gaussian Process
g
as
\mathcal{L}g
is again a Gaussian Process.
This ensures that realizations of the Gaussian Process
\mathcal{L}g
lie in the image of the linear operator
\mathcal{L}

[
5
]
.
We demonstrate the procedure for constructing so-called Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Processes — Gaussian Processes that strictly satisfy the underlying system of linear homogeneous ordinary differential equations (
1b
).
We subtract
(\partial_{t}x)I
and combine state
x
and input
u
by stacking it in one variable
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}}
to reformulate the system in (
1b
) as:

\displaystyle 0=H\cdot z=\begin{pmatrix}A-\partial_{t}xI|B\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}x\\
u\end{pmatrix}

(5)

We can algorithmically factor
H\in\mathbb{R}[\partial_{t}]^{n_{x}\times n_{z}}
into three matrices such that
Q\cdot H\cdot V=D
, where
D\in\mathbb{R}[\partial_{t}]^{n_{x}\times n_{z}}
is called Smith Normal Form and
Q\in\mathbb{R}[\partial_{t}]^{n_{x}\times n_{x}},V\in\mathbb{R}[\partial_{t}]^{n_{z}\times n_{z}}
invertible
[
9
]
.
All matrices are defined over the polynomial ring
\mathbb{R}[\partial_{t}]
i.e. their entries are polynomials in
\partial_{t}
.

We then construct a prior latent Gaussian Process
\tilde{g}=\mathcal{GP}(0,\tilde{K})
with
\tilde{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{z}\times n_{z}}
being a matrix of latent kernels, using the simple construction rules presented in Table 1 of
[
2
]
. The construction is based on the diagonal entries of
D
which are
0
or
1
for controllable systems.
We obtain a Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process by applying the linear operator
V
to this
latent
Gaussian Process
\tilde{g}
via

V\tilde{g}=\mathcal{GP}\left(\mathbf{0},V\cdot\tilde{K}\cdot\hat{V}^{\top}\right)

(6)

where
\hat{V}
is the operator
V
applied to the second argument (
t^{\prime}
) of the covariance functions
k_{i}
in
\tilde{K}
.
We thereby guarantee that the realizations of the resulting Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process
V\tilde{g}
in Equation (
6
)
strictly
satisfy the system in Equation (
5
), as detailed in
[
2
]
.
The Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process in Equation (
6
) can be trained and conditioned on datapoints as any regular Gaussian Process.

2.4
Control as Inference with Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Processes

Recall the optimal control problem (
1
). This problem is solved in
[
14
]
via a Control-as-Inference scheme using a Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process.
The minimization of (
1a
) is obtained via the correspondence of optimality in Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces and the GP posterior mean (
3a
)
[
14
]
.
The ODE constraint (
1b
) is fulfilled exactly via the Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process structure.
For the initial condition constraint (
1c
), we use the initial point
(t_{0},z_{0})=(t_{0},(x_{0},u_{0}))
as datapoint in the conditioned dataset
\mathcal{D}
with a heteroscedastic noise of the numerical jitter
\sigma_{n}^{2}(t_{0})=10^{-8}
.
The two constraints (
1b
) and (
1c
) require no additional tuning.

The critical constraints are the state and input constraints (
1d
) and (
1e
) which are in
[
14
]
modeled as datapoints
(t_{i},z_{i})
with
z_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(z_{\text{max}}+z_{\text{min}})
and heteroscedastic noise variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}(t_{i})=\frac{1}{2}(z_{\text{max}}-z_{\text{min}})
.
This retains the property to compute the Gaussian Process posterior in closed form at the price of no guarantees to fulfill the constraints.

Moreover, the hypothesis space for the optimization (
1a
) is determined by the Squared Exponential kernel used in
[
14
]
. In the following, we provide approaches to improve the Control-as-Inference scheme by different latent kernel functions and additionally steering of the optimization function towards a quadratic cost function.

3
Methodology

3.1
Matérn covariance functions for LODE-GPs

The choice of the latent covariance function of the Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process determines the degree of regularization we provide for the optimal control problem.
The benefit of using Matérn kernels is that the control function does not have to be smooth but can exhibit more control advantage behavior.
We provide a characterization of the function space we can model with Gaussian Processes with Matérn kernels.

Theorem 1

The RKHS of a Matérn Gaussian Process
\mathcal{H}_{k_{\nu}}
on
X\subset\mathbb{R}
,
\nu>m\geq 0
with
\nu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2}
is dense in
C^{m}
.

Proof (Sketch):
The Matérn Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space
\mathcal{H}_{k_{\nu}}
is equivalent to the Sobolev space
W^{s,2}
and is, due to the Sobolev embedding theorem and standard density arguments, dense in
C^{m}
.
\blacksquare

Using the above property, we now show that Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Processes with a latent Matérn kernel are dense in the solution space of the underlying differential equation. For this purpose, we denote the relevant columns of
V
, i.e. the columns belonging to zero diagonal entries in
D
, by
P\in\mathbb{R}[\partial]^{q\times p}
as a parametrization of the solution space.

Theorem 2

For the parameterization
P\in\mathbb{R}[\partial]^{q\times p}
as above, and Matérn kernel
k_{\nu}
with
\nu\geq m+h+\tfrac{1}{2}
it holds that
P\cdot(\mathcal{H}_{k_{\nu}})^{p}
is dense in
\text{sol}_{C^{m}}(H)
.

The proof for Theorem
2
uses a similar argument as
[
6
, Proposition 3.5]
and standard density arguments as those in Theorem
1
.

Therefore, the mean of the Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process with latent Matérn kernel is able to approximate arbitrary solutions of given differentiability.
In particular, this result shows that all possible
mean functions
are dense in the solution space.
This theorem allows us to use Matérn kernels in the Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process for our Control-as-Inference scheme.

3.2
Cost Function optimization

In Model Predictive Control we optimize arbitrary cost functions, but most of them are of quadratic type

\displaystyle J(x,u)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{p}(x-x_{\text{ref}})^{T}E(x-x_{\text{ref}})

(7a)

\displaystyle\qquad+(u-u_{\text{ref}})^{T}F(u-u_{\text{ref}})

(7b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{p}(z-z_{\text{ref}})^{T}\begin{pmatrix}E&0\\
0&F\end{pmatrix}(z-z_{\text{ref}})

(7c)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{p}(z-z_{\text{ref}})^{T}G(z-z_{\text{ref}})=J(z)

(7d)

with
E,F,G
being positive definite matrices of respective sizes.
The authors in
[
14
]
only provide optimality in the RKHS norm, given by the LODE-Kernel, which is hard to interpret. Therefore, we reweigh the Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process posterior
\mathcal{GP}(\mu^{*},\Sigma^{*})
with the normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(z_{\text{ref}},S)
with
S
being proportional to
G^{-1}
, therefore a positive definite matrix. The matrix
S
is thereby acting in the same manner as the inverse of
G
in (
7
). We see this, as the log-likelihood of
\mathcal{N}(z_{\text{ref}},S)
is proportional to (
7d
) with
S=G^{-1}
.

The reweighting is done via multiplication of the GP’s predictive posterior
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{*},\Sigma^{*})
with the multivariate normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(z_{\text{ref}},S)
. The product is proportional to the normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{opt}},\Sigma_{\text{opt}})
with

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{opt}}=

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\text{opt}}({\Sigma^{*}}^{-1}\mu+S^{-1}z_{\text{ref}})

(8a)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\text{opt}}=

\displaystyle({\Sigma^{*}}^{-1}+S^{-1})^{-1},

(8b)

due to
[
11
, Appendix A.2]
.
Thereby we obtain a normal distribution which still satisfies the linear ODE structure on the discretized grid.

This reweighting is possible for arbitrary cost functions, which are given via (log-)likelihoods, losing the property that the product of both distributions yields a Gaussian.

We may now sample from the normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\text{opt}},\Sigma_{\text{opt}})
for the sake of constraint satisfaction.

3.3
Constraint satisfaction via sampling

A natural way of achieving constraint satisfaction, is combining the Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process posterior with the uniform likelihood

\mathcal{U}(z,z_{\min},z_{\max})=\begin{cases}\@add@centering\frac{1}{z_{\max}-z_{\min}}&\text{ if $z\in[z_{\min},z_{\max}]$}\\
\centering 0&\text{ else}\end{cases}

(9)

on the constraints
[z_{\min},z_{\max}]
. We obtain

\mathcal{TGP}(z|t^{*})=\frac{V\tilde{g}(t^{*})\cdot\mathcal{U}(z,z_{\min},z_{\max})}{\int_{z_{\min}}^{z_{\max}}V\tilde{g}(t^{*}|f)\mathrm{d}f}

(10)

via Bayes’ rule. This distribution is the well known truncated normal distribution
[
1
]
.
Since calculating the denominator in (
10
) is analytically intractable, we approximate this distribution via Monte Carlo sampling.
A naive way is i.i.d. sampling from the Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process posterior and rejecting samples which are not in the feasible interval given by the uniform distribution (
9
).
As i.i.d. sampling provides vanishing acceptance rates with growing dimension of the multivariate normal distribution, we require more efficient sampling strategies.

A sampling strategy which, by construction, provides acceptance rate
1
is Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
[
10
]
.
This sampling strategy leverages the sample distribution to a Hamiltonian dynamical system which is then simulated on paths which have a constant value of the Hamiltonian.
Exploiting the invariance structure of Hamiltonian systems and the normal distribution properties, the path of the Hamiltonian is simulated only inside the constraints resulting in proposed samples which satisfy the given linear constraints, given that the initial sample has support inside the constraints.
Exact Hamiltonian Monte Carlo uses the fact that, for truncated Gaussians, the simulated Hamiltonian path can be integrated exactly with particle collisions at the constraint boundaries
[
10
]
. The algorithm produces a Markov-Chain which is, up to discretization error, a sample of a Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process if the chain is initiated with a sample feasible to the constraints.
We choose this via the truncated mean

\bar{\mu}(t)=\begin{cases}z_{\min}\quad\text{if }\mu_{\text{opt}}(t)<z_{\min}\\
z_{\max}\quad\text{if }\mu_{\text{opt}}(t)>z_{\max}\\
\mu_{\text{opt}}\quad\text{ else}\end{cases}

(11)

which does not necessarily satisfy the LODE structure. This choice is motivated by the result of
[
1
]
which states that the optimal function based on its Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space for a truncated normal distribution is the posterior mode, which in our case is
\bar{\mu}
.
Thereby we provide an initial sample which is close to the Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process distribution and the optimal choice for a truncated normal distribution.
In similar contexts, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo is often referred to be one of the most efficient sampling methods for truncated multivariate normal distributions
[
8
,
3
]
.
We now show that, as the Linear Ordinary Differential Equation Gaussian Process is again a Gaussian Process which produces normal distributions, the truncation via uniform likelihoods keeps the linear Ordinary Differential Equation structure.

Theorem 3

The support of the distribution
\mathcal{TGP}
(
10
) fulfills the ODE structure given by (
1b
) and the constraints given by (
1d
) and (
1e
).

Proof 3.4
.

This theorem follows directly from Bayes’ rule in (
10
). Since
V\tilde{g}
has full support on solutions of the linear ODE, so does the posterior which is restricted on the constraints by the uniform likelihood.

We choose Hamiltonian Monte Carlo as a sampler on a discretization of
\mathcal{TGP}(z|t)
, because it has an acceptance rate of
1
.
We provide our proposed methodology in Algorithm

.

Conclusion

We provided a Control-as-Inference method with constraint satisfaction guarantees for feasible Model Predictive Control problems.
We do this by restricting the inference model, a linear ODE based Gaussian Process to input and state constraints via truncating the predictive multivariate Normal distribution. We prove that samples of the resulting distribution still satisfy the ODE. Additionally, we have shown that the Matérn kernel is a reasonable choice for the Control-as-Inference scheme, both from a theoretic view by proving a density theorem and from a practitioner’s view in our experiments. Moreover, we provided a method to incorporate quadratic cost functions into the control scheme.
