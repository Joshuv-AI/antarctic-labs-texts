Cardiac Myxoma Causing Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Obstruction and Syncope in Rural Tanzania
Authors: Alina Murji, Eva Mujuni, Shija Mahenda, Bashir J Nyangasa, Cody Cichowitz, Patrick S Ngoya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCase SummaryA 20-year-old woman presented with recurrent syncope to a health center in rural Tanzania. Point-of-care ultrasound revealed a cardiac mass. The patient was transferred to a regional referral hospital, where a comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation revealed a left ventricular mass causing intermittent left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. Surgical candidacy was confirmed, and she underwent surgical resection at the national hospital.Take-Home MessagesIn resource-limited settings, point-of-care ultrasound, transthoracic echocardiography, and computed tomography contribute to the initial diagnosis and surgical evaluation for patients presenting from remote areas with surgical disease. Multidisciplinary teamwork and clear evaluation and referral protocols are essential to improve access to cardiac surgery.Key words: cardiac surgery, global cardiology, referral pathways, ventricular myxoma

History of Presentation and Differential Diagnosis

A 20-year-old woman in rural Tanzania presented to her local health center with recurrent exertional syncope. Point-of-care ultrasound detected a cardiac mass, prompting transfer to Bugando Medical Center, a regional referral hospital 420 km away. Examination was unremarkable, except for a systolic ejection murmur at the left upper sternal border that worsened with Valsalva. The initial differential diagnosis included left ventricular thrombus, myxoma, lipoma, sarcoma, and metastatic tumor.

Investigations and Management

Transthoracic echocardiography showed a large heterogeneous mass in the left ventricle attached to a stalk near the apical portion of the intraventricular septum; the mass extended into the left ventricular outflow tract and prolapsed into the ascending aorta during systole (Figures 1A and 1B, Videos 1 and 2). Cardiac computed tomography showed a lobulated, hypodense, moderately enhancing mass with focal areas of fat density, attached to the intraventricular septum and measuring 5.0 cm in maximum planar diameter (Figures 1C and 1D).

Given the size, embolic risk, and hemodynamic consequences of the left ventricular mass, a multidisciplinary team comprising cardiologists, cardiothoracic surgeons, and radiologists converged to plan immediate and definitive care. In Tanzania, a low-income country of ∼72 million people, the Jakaya Kikwete Cardiac Institute is the only center capable of cardiac surgery. Given that the distance from Bugando Medical Centre to Jakaya Kikwete Cardiac Institute is 1,200 km, a comprehensive evaluation for surgical candidacy was performed and discussed with the treating surgeon (BN). Additional imaging was performed to evaluate for underlying noncardiac primary malignancy or metastases that would preclude cardiopulmonary bypass and was reviewed for surgical feasibility.

The patient underwent successful surgical resection of the cardiac mass, and a diagnosis of left ventricular myxoma was made based on histopathology (Figures 1E and 1F). She was discharged home after an uncomplicated recovery and returned to full activity and university studies without limitations.

Discussion

This case highlights current challenges and opportunities in treating rare, highly morbid surgical cardiac diseases in remote regions of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Left ventricular myxomas account for 0.6%-4% myxomas.1 Diagnostic evaluation of rare pathology, including cardiac tumors, in resource-limited settings requires creativity and teamwork. Clinicians often rely on low-quality data (ie, point-of-care ultrasound, transthoracic echocardiography, and computed tomography) as opposed to transesophageal echocardiography or cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging, which are recommended in guidelines.2 As was seen with our patient, multiple, costly referrals over long distances are often required, and patients frequently do not have access to medical transport (ie, ambulances). Finally, cardiac surgery remains cost-prohibitive; the total costs were ∼$3,500 USD or 3 times the Tanzanian per capita gross domestic product.

This case represents an exception. Most patients living in remote areas of LMICs in need of cardiac surgery succumb to potentially treatable disease.3 Clear, pragmatic, and context-relevant diagnostic and referral pathways, that are easy to use for frontline clinicians, are needed to optimize use of cardiac surgery. More efficient referrals from frontline facilities, coupled with standardized evaluations of surgical appropriateness and feasibility at regional referral hospitals, will help alleviate critical access bottlenecks to cardiac surgery. Significant investment in health systems interventions is needed to meet the growing burden of cardiovascular disease in LMICs.
