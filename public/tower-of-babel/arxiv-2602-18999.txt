The Strong Spectral Property and the Jacobian Method for Weighted Laplacian Matrices
Minerva Catral, Shaun Fallat, Himanshu Gupta, Jephian C.-H. Lin

Abstract
Strong matrix properties, roughly speaking, refer to generic conditions on a matrix such that its spectral perturbation and pattern perturbation interact nicely to cover a neighborhood in the ambient space. With a rich history, these strong properties originate from various fields, including the inverse eigenvalue problem, the sign pattern problem, and structural graph theory. In this paper, we introduce a new strong property, the strong spectral property for weighted Laplacian matrices (SSPWL), and establish the corresponding Supergraph and Bifurcation lemmas. Instead of the space of symmetric matrices, the SSPWL considers the ambient space spanned by all weighted Laplacian matrices. Moreover, we provide a detailed study comparing the Jacobian Method and some strong properties, leading to a full understanding between these two techniques used in different problems. Using these tools, we identify the potential boundaries of the spectral regions of weighted Laplacian matrices associated with connected graphs on 4 4 vertices, extending the analysis from the previous work [S. M. Fallat, H. Gupta, and J. C.-H. Lin. Inverse eigenvalue problem for Laplacian matrices of a graph. SIAM J. Matrix Anal. Appl. , 46:1866–1886, 2025]. In addition, this analysis can be used to identify the absolute algebraic connectivity of such small ordered graphs, and we establish the existence of strong weighted Laplacian matrices for several graph families.



Keywords:

Inverse eigenvalue problems, graphs, weighted Laplacian matrices, strong property, Jacobian Method, elimination ideal.

05C50, 15A18, 05C22, 15B57, 65F18.

Introduction

The strong properties, though they appeared as different names over time, have a long impact on matrix theory and its applications to the inverse eigenvalue problem, the sign pattern problem, and structural graph theory. Roughly speaking, the strong properties are generic conditions on a matrix to ensure its spectral perturbation (that preserves the spectrum, ordered multiplicity list, or inertia, etc.) and the pattern perturbation (that preserves signs or zero/nonzero pattern of the entries) interact nicely so that every nearby matrices in the ambient space can be reached by a combination of these two perturbations. A typical example is the strong spectral property in the inverse eigenvalue problem of a graph (IEP-
G
). Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices and let
\mathcal{S}(G)
be the set of all
n\times n
real symmetric matrices whose off-diagonal
i,j
-entry is nonzero if and only if
\{i,j\}\in E(G)
, while the diagonal entries can be any real number. We consider two perturbations. The spectral perturbation is
A\mapsto Q^{\top}AQ
, where
Q
is any orthogonal matrix near
I
. The pattern perturbation is the perturbation around
A
in
\mathcal{S}(G)
. Geometrically, the matrix
A
has the strong spectral property (SSP) if and only if the tangent spaces of the two perturbations generate the ambient space of symmetric matrices.

By the inverse function theorem, the SSP implies that every matrix near
A
can be reached by a combination of the two perturbations. Specifically, the Supergraph lemma states that a spectral perturbation of
A
can reach any nearby pattern. In contrast, the Bifurcation lemma states that, using the pattern perturbation,
A
can reach any nearby spectrum. The SSP and related theorems were introduced in
[
6
,
5
,
16
]
.

Strong matrix properties have a long history and have appeared in various fields. In 1971, Arnold
[
2
]
studied matrices whose entries depend on given parameters and considered the cases where a small perturbation of the parameters is enough to reach all possible Jordan canonical forms of nearby matrices. Arnold called such a matrix family depending on the parameters a versal deformation, which can be viewed as the prototype of several strong properties. One year later, Arnold
[
3
]
introduced the hypothesis of transversality for operators on a membrane. This idea was then adopted by Colin de Verdière as the strong Arnold hypothesis for operators defined on a manifold and matrices associated with a graph
[
8
]
. Using the strong Arnold hypothesis, Colin de Verdière defined a graph parameter
\mu(G)

[
10
,
9
]
, which was later referred to as the Colin de Verdière parameter.

The Colin de Verdière parameter
\mu(G)
is defined as the largest multiplicity of the second smallest eigenvalue over the discrete Schrödinger operators on a graph that satisfies the strong Arnold hypothesis. Despite its linear algebra nature, the parameter has a strong connection to the topological properties of the graph. It is known that
\mu(G)\leq 1
if and only if
G
is a path,
\mu(G)\leq 2
if and only if
G
is outerplanar,
\mu(G)\leq 3
if and only if
G
is planar, and
\mu(G)\leq 4
if and only if
G
is linklessly embeddable
[
10
,
9
,
22
]
. Moreover,
\mu(G)
is shown to be minor monotone, which means
\mu(G)\leq\mu(H)
if
G
is a minor of
H
. Therefore, by the graph minor theorem (see, e.g.,
[
13
]
), the minimal forbidden minors of
\mu(G)\leq k
are a finite set of graphs for any
k
.

The strong Arnold hypothesis is usually called the strong Arnold property (SAP) in the IEP-
G
literature. Motivated by the parameter
\mu(G)
, the other minor monotone parameter
\xi(G)
was developed based on the SAP
[
4
]
. Later, the strong multiplicity property (SMP) and the strong spectral property (SSP) were introduced and related theorems were established to solve the IEP-

for graphs up to
5

[
6
,
5
,
16
]
. For more details on the IEP-

and several strong properties, see, e.g.,
[

,
11
,
12
,
1

The strong properties appeared in many different fields under different names. In the nonnegative inverse eigenvalue problem (NIEP), Laffey studied the extreme nonnegative matrices and showed that matrices with some kind of strong property cannot be extreme
[
23
]
. In the sign pattern problem, the Jacobian Method and the Nilpotent-Centralizer Method were introduced to recognize a spectrally arbitrary sign pattern, while the assumptions of these two methods can also be viewed as some strong properties
[

,

,

Let
G
be graph on
n
vertices. Define
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
to be the set of all weighted Laplacian matrices associated with

\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
consists of all
n\times n

such that

a_{i,j}\begin{cases}<0&\text{if }\{i,j\}\in E(G),\\
=0&\text{if }\{i,j\}\notin E(G),\ i\neq j,\\
-\sum_{k:k\sim i}a_{i,k}&\text{if }i=j.\end{cases}

Any matrix

The combinatorial and weighted Laplacian matrices of a graph are central objects in combinatorics and spectral graph theory, serving as primary tools for understanding structural and connectivity properties of graphs. For a fixed graph

with spec
(A)=\{0,\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}

The inverse eigenvalue problem for Laplacian matrices of a graph (IEPL) was first formulated in
[
15
]

[
15
]

In this paper, we introduce a new strong property, the strong spectral property for weighted Laplacian matrices (SSPWL). We build the Supergraph lemma and the Bifurcation lemma for the SSPWL. We show that the Jacobian Method also works for weighted Laplacian matrices. We prove that the Jacobian matrix associated with a matrix

has full rank if and only if
A
has all eigenvalues distinct and

has the SSPWL. This provides a full understanding of the Jacobian Method, the Nilpotent-Centralizer Method, and the strong properties.

Strong Spectral Property for Weighted Laplacian Matrices

In this section, we develop, akin to previous such properties, a strong matrix property specific to weighted Laplacian matrices associated with a graph. We refer to this matrix property as the strong spectral property for weighted Laplacian matrices, or SSPWL for short. This property provides a key tool for investigating the IEPL.

As noted above for any graph
G
, it is well known that
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
is singular and that
A\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
. This common null vector requires special attention when considering the matrix perturbations that result from the so-called strong matrix properties, in part due to the fact that any such desired perturbation must also have the all-ones vector
\mathbf{1}
in its null space. To this end, we consider the projection
H_{n}
from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}^{\perp}
. In this case, we have
H_{n}=I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}J_{n}
, where
J_{n}
is the
n\times n
matrix of all ones. When
n
is obvious from the context, we write
H=H_{n}
and
J=J_{n}
.

We begin by defining the strong spectral property for matrices in
S_{L}(G)
and then follow with several facts and consequences concerning this strong property in the remainder of this section.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
. We say
A
has the
strong spectral property for weighted Laplacian matrices
(or
SSPWL
) if
X=O
is the only symmetric matrix such that
A\circ X=I\circ X=O
and
H(AX-XA)H=O
.

Clearly, the SSPWL resembles the standard strong spectral property for symmetric matrices (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
6
]
); however, the requirement that
H(AX-XA)H=O
adds a level of difficulty when trying to determine when such a matrix
A
has the SSPWL. To address this extra layer of difficulty, we now state some auxiliary propositions that will be used frequently.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
X
be an
n\times n
matrix. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

HXH=O
.

(2)

P_{1}^{\top}XP_{2}=O
, where
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are some matrices with
\operatorname{Col}(P_{1})=\operatorname{Col}(P_{2})=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}^{\perp}
.

(3)

N_{1}^{\top}XN_{2}=O
, where
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are the vertex-edge incidence matrix of some trees.

(4)

(\mathbf{e}_{i_{1}}-\mathbf{e}_{j_{1}})^{\top}X(\mathbf{e}_{i_{2}}-\mathbf{e}_{j_{2}})=0
for all pairs
i_{1}\neq j_{1}
and
i_{2}\neq j_{2}
.

Proof.

Observe that
\operatorname{Col}(H)=\operatorname{Col}(P_{1})=\operatorname{Col}(P_{2})=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}^{\perp}
. Therefore, there are matrices
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
such that
HL_{1}=P_{1}
and
P_{1}L_{2}=H
. Similarly, there are matrices
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
such that
HR_{1}=P_{2}
and
P_{2}R_{2}=H
. Consequently, if
HXH=O
, then
P_{1}^{\top}XP_{2}=L_{1}^{\top}HXHR_{1}=O
, and if
P_{1}^{\top}XP_{2}=O
, then
HXH=L_{2}^{\top}P_{1}^{\top}XP_{2}R_{2}=O
. Thus,
(1)
and
(2)
are equivalent.

Statement
(3)
is a special case of Statement
(2)
.
Statement
(3)
is equivalent to
E^{\top}XE=O
, where
E
is a matrix with
\binom{n}{2}
columns of the form
\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}
. Since
\operatorname{Col}(H)=\operatorname{Col}(E)
, Statement
(4)
may be viewed as a special case of Statement
(2)
since the vectors
\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}
with
i\neq j
span the subspace
\{\mathbf{1}\}^{\perp}
.
∎

Now we introduce the verification matrix to check whether a given matrix has the SSPWL.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
and
P
a matrix with
\operatorname{Col}(P)=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}^{\perp}
. Define the
SSPWL verification matrix of

with respect to

as

whose rows are indexed by

such that the row of

corresponding to

is a vector corresponding to the strictly upper triangular part of

. Here

is the matrix whose two entries corresponding to the edge

are
1
while all other entries are

. By default, the rows and columns of

are sorted by lexicographic order.

Example 2.4
.

Consider the matrices

A=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&-0.5&0&0\\
-0.5&1.5&-1&0\\
0&-1&2.5&-1.5\\
0&0&-1.5&1.5\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }P=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\
-1&0&0\\

with

. Let
e_{1}=\{1,3\}
,
e_{2}=\{1,4\}
,
e_{3}=\{2,4\}

be the matrix

are

while all other entries are

,
H(AX-XA)H=O
if and only if

P^{\top}(AX-XA)P=\sum_{i=1}^{3}c_{i}P^{\top}(AE_{e_{i}}-E_{e_{i}}A)P=O.

Since

is a skew-symmetric matrix, we focus on the entries in the strict upper triangular part, which has three entries. This leads to the verification matrix

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
. Then

has the SSPWL if and only if

has full row rank. The statement is independent of the choice of

Corollary 2.6
.

The linear map
X\mapsto HXH
from
\{X\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R}):I\circ X=O\}
to
\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
is a bijection.

Proof.

It is straightforward to see that the map is linear. We first verify that this map is injective by establishing that its kernel is
\{O\}
. If
HXH=O
, then

(\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j})^{\top}X(\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j})=0

by

2.2
, which implies that the

-entry of
X
is zero since

. Since

and

are arbitrary,
X=O
.

Since

\dim(\{X\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R}):I\circ X=O\})=\dim(\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R}))=\binom{n}{2},

this injective map is indeed a bijection.
∎

2.1
Manifolds and Perturbations

We first note that the intuitive extension of the strong spectral property (SSP) does not apply in the case of weighted Laplacian matrices. Let
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. Recall that the strong spectral property
[
5
,
6
]
is equivalent to the transversal intersection at
A
between the manifolds

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}(G),\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{Q^{\top}AQ:Q\text{ orthogonal}\}.

Thus, a natural analog would be the transversality at
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
between
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. Unfortunately, the all-ones matrix
J
is always in the normal spaces of both
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
at
A
. The tangent space of
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
at
A
is the span of
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
. Each of the matrix in the tangent space has each row sum equal to zero, so
J
is orthogonal to the tangent space. On the other hand,
AJ-JA=O
indicates that
J
is in the normal space of
\mathcal{M}_{2}
at
A
. Therefore, in this extended version no matrix will have the strong property.

The reason for this behavior is because there is too much freedom in the ambient space
\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
so that
J
is always in the normal space. If we shrink the ambient space, then the normal spaces shrink as well and could possibly intersect trivially.

Instead, we consider two sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{L}(G),\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{Q^{\top}AQ:Q\text{ orthogonal},\ Q\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}\}.

Observe that there is a slight change from
\mathcal{M}_{2}
to
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{2}
since
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is not included in the new ambient space
\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

We now consider some foundational analysis concerning the sets

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{1}

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{2}
, from the matrix perturbation point of view. Let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
. Consider the function

(1)

with codomain

, where

.

Let
G
be a graph on

Assume

is an immediate consequence by applying basic properties of orthogonal complementary subspaces.

Diagonalize
A
as
Q^{\top}AQ=D
so that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbf{1}
is the first column of
Q
and the
1,1
-entry of
D
is zero. Now we may write
AY-YA=O
as
D(Q^{\top}YQ)-(Q^{\top}YQ)D=O
. Moreover,
Y\in\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
is equivalent to
Q^{\top}YQ
has its first row and column zero. This matrix commutes with
D
if and only if the
i,j
-entry of
Q^{\top}YQ
is zero whenever the
i,i
-entry and the
j,j
-entry of
D
are distinct. Thus,
Q^{\top}YQ
has
\binom{m_{1}}{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{q}\binom{m_{i}+1}{2}
free entries.
∎

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
F
be the function defined in
Equation

1
and follow the same hypotheses as in
Proposition

2.7
.
Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

\dot{F}
is surjective.

(2)

\operatorname{range}(F)=\operatorname{range}(F_{B})+\operatorname{range}(F_{K})
.

(3)

\operatorname{range}(F_{B})^{\perp}\cap\operatorname{range}(F_{K})^{\perp}=\{O\}
.

(4)

A
has the SSPWL.

Proof.

Statements
(1)
,
(2)
, and
(3)
are equivalent by applying basic linear algebra facts.

To see that Statements
(3)
and
(4)
are equivalent, we use contrapositive statements. Suppose
X\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is a nonzero matrix such that
A\circ X=I\circ X=O
and
H(AX-XA)H=O
. By
Corollary

2.6
,
HXH\in\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
is a nonzero matrix. Thus,
HXH
is a nonzero matrix in
\operatorname{range}(F_{B})^{\perp}\cap\operatorname{range}(F_{K})^{\perp}
from the above definitions.

On the other hand, suppose
Y\in\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
is a nonzero matrix in
\operatorname{range}(F_{B})^{\perp}\cap\operatorname{range}(F_{K})^{\perp}
.
Since
Y\in\operatorname{range}(F_{B})^{\perp}
, there is a matrix
X\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
such that
A\circ X=I\circ X=O
and
Y=HXH
. By
Corollary

2.6
,
X
is nonzero. Since
Y\in\operatorname{range}(F_{K})^{\perp}
and
AH=HA=A
,

O=AY-YA=A(HXH)-(HXH)A=H(AX-XA)H.

Therefore,
X
is a nonzero matrix such that
A\circ X=I\circ X=O
and
H(AX-XA)H=O
.
∎

We are now in a position to establish the so-called Supergraph lemma and the Bifurcation lemma associated with this strong matrix property for weighted Laplacian matrices. Both of these results utilize the inverse function theorem, which is quoted below from
[
16
, Theorem 2.4]
.

Theorem 2.10

(Inverse function theorem)
.

Let
U
and
W
be finite-dimensional vector spaces over
\mathbb{R}
. Let
F
be a smooth function from an open subset of
U
to
W
with
F(\mathbf{u}_{0})=\mathbf{w}_{0}
. If
\dot{F}\Big|_{\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{0}}
is surjective, then there is an open subset
W^{\prime}\subset W
containing
\mathbf{w}_{0}
and a smooth function
T:W^{\prime}\rightarrow U
such that
T(\mathbf{w}_{0})=\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
F\circ T
is the identity map on the set
W^{\prime}
.

We begin with the Supergraph lemma for weighted Laplacian matrices.

Lemma 2.11

(Supergraph lemma)
.

Let
G
be a spanning subgraph of the graph
H
. Suppose
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
has the SSPWL. Then there is a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(H)
such that

(1)

\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(A)
,

(2)

A^{\prime}
has the SSPWL, and

(3)

\|A^{\prime}-A\|
can be chosen arbitrarily small.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
. Consider the function
F
as in
Equation

1
. Since
A
has the SSPWL,
\dot{F}
is surjective. By
Theorem

2.10
, for any
M\in\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
close to
A
, there are
B^{\prime}
and
K^{\prime}
such that
F(B^{\prime},K^{\prime})=M
. Choose
M
as the matrix obtained from
A
by perturbing its zero entries in
E(H)\setminus E(G)
into any small negative values and adjusting the diagonal entries to ensure
M\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(H)
. Thus,
e^{-K^{\prime}}Ae^{K^{\prime}}+B^{\prime}=M
. When the perturbation from
A
to
M
is small enough, the corresponding
B^{\prime}
is also small by continuity. Then
A^{\prime}=e^{-K^{\prime}}Ae^{K}=M-B^{\prime}
will have the same pattern as
M
, so
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(H)
. Furthermore,
A^{\prime}
has the same spectrum as
A
by similarity. Since this perturbation is small,
A^{\prime}
still has the SSPWL.
∎

We now verify the Bifurcation lemma for weighed Laplacian matrices with this strong matrix property.

Lemma 2.12

(Bifurcation lemma)
.

Let
G
be a graph. Suppose
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
has the SSPWL. Then there is
\epsilon>0
such that for any matrix
M\in\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|M-A\|<\epsilon
, a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
exists such that

(1)

\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(M)
and

(2)

A^{\prime}
has the SSPWL.

Proof.

Consider the function
\tilde{F}(B,K)=e^{-K}(A+B)e^{K}
. Observe that
\tilde{F}
and
F
are the same when
B=O
or
K=O
. Then
\tilde{F}
also satisfies
Propositions

2.7
,
2.8
and
2.9
. Since
A
has the SSPWL, the derivative of
\tilde{F}
is surjective. By
Theorem

2.10
, for any
M\in\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
close to
A
, there are
B^{\prime}
and
K^{\prime}
such that
\tilde{F}(B^{\prime},K^{\prime})=M
. Thus,
e^{-K^{\prime}}(A+B^{\prime})e^{K^{\prime}}=M
. Then, since
B^{\prime}
is a small perturbation of
A
, it follows that
A^{\prime}=A+B^{\prime}=e^{K^{\prime}}Me^{-K^{\prime}}
is in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
and has the same spectrum as
M
.
∎

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
G
be a tree. Then
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
has the SSPWL implies that
\operatorname{spec}(A)
is composed of distinct elements.

Proof.

Let
n=|V(G)|
and
m=|E(G)|
. Let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
be a matrix with the SSPWL and
F
the corresponding function as in
Equation

1
. According to
Proposition

2.9
, a necessary condition for

to have the SSPWL is that

\dim(\operatorname{range}(F_{B}))+\dim(\operatorname{range}(F_{K}))\geq\dim(\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R}))=\binom{n}{2}.

By
Corollary

2.8
, we have

\dim(\operatorname{range}(F_{B}))=m\text{ and }\dim(\operatorname{range}(F_{K}))=\binom{n}{2}-\binom{m_{1}}{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{q}\binom{m_{i}+1}{2}.

Since
G
is a tree,
m=n-1
. Since
G
is connected,
m_{1}=1
. Therefore, the necessary condition becomes

n-1+\binom{n}{2}-\sum_{i=2}^{q}\binom{m_{i}+1}{2}\geq\binom{n}{2},

which is equivalent to
n-1\geq\sum_{i=2}^{q}\binom{m_{i}+1}{2}
. This happens only when
m_{i}=1
for all
i
.
∎

Let

On the other hand, the combinatorial Laplacian matrix

.

3
Equivalence to the Jacobian Method

In this section, we introduce the Jacobian Method for verifying the SSPWL and show that for every connected graph
G
, there is a matrix in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
with the SSPWL. The idea of the Jacobian Method for inverse eigenvalue problems was adopted from the sign pattern problem
[
14
,
19
,
16
]
, and a key idea, to be verified, is to certify that the nonzero entries of a matrix have full control of the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial and consequently the spectrum.

We start with an example to demonstrate the Jacobian Method before proceeding with the theoretical details.

Example 3.1
.

Let
G=K_{1,4}
and

A=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{4}w_{i}&-w_{1}&-w_{2}&-w_{3}&-w_{4}\\
-w_{1}&w_{1}&0&0&0\\
-w_{2}&0&w_{2}&0&0\\
-w_{3}&0&0&w_{3}&0\\
-w_{4}&0&0&0&w_{4}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Let
s_{k}=s_{k}(A)
be the sum of all
k\times k
minors of
A
. By direct computation, we have

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle=2(w_{1}+w_{2}+w_{3}+w_{4}),

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=3(w_{1}w_{2}+w_{1}w_{3}+w_{1}w_{4}+w_{2}w_{3}+w_{2}w_{4}+w_{3}w_{4}),

\displaystyle s_{3}

\displaystyle=4(w_{1}w_{2}w_{3}+w_{1}w_{2}w_{4}+w_{1}w_{3}w_{4}+w_{2}w_{3}w_{4}),

\displaystyle s_{4}

\displaystyle=5w_{1}w_{2}w_{3}w_{4},

and

\det(A-xI)=s_{0}(-x)^{5}+s_{1}(-x)^{4}+\cdots+s_{5}.

Note that
s_{1}=1
and
s_{5}=0
. Then, the Jacobian matrix
J_{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial s_{i}}{\partial w_{j}}\end{bmatrix}
is

\begin{bmatrix}2&2&2&2\\
3(w_{2}+w_{3}+w_{4})&\cdots&\cdots&3(w_{1}+w_{2}+w_{3})\\
4(w_{2}w_{3}+w_{2}w_{4}+w_{3}w_{4})&\cdots&\cdots&4(w_{1}w_{2}+w_{1}w_{3}+w_{2}w_{3})\\
5w_{2}w_{3}w_{4}&5w_{1}w_{3}w_{4}&5w_{1}w_{2}w_{4}&5w_{1}w_{2}w_{3}\end{bmatrix}.

If
J_{A}
is invertible with some positive
w_{1},\ldots,w_{4}
, then the inverse function theorem implies that
w_{i}
’s have full control of
s_{k}
’s, which has the same effect as the Supergraph lemma (
Lemma

2.11
) and the Bifurcation lemma (
Lemma

2.12
).

The graph
K_{1,4}
has
m=4
edges and
\overline{m}=6
missing edges. Note that the Jacobian matrix in
Example

3.1
is of order
m=4
, while the verification matrix is of order
\overline{m}=6
. At first glance, the Jacobian Method and the SSPWL appear rather different, but they lead to the same conclusion. In the following, we show that they are equivalent provided that
A
has distinct eigenvalues.

Given a square matrix
M\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, we define
s_{k}(M)
to be the sum of all its
k\times k
principal minors. Then the characteristic polynomial of
M
can be written as

\det(M-xI)=s_{0}(M)(-x)^{n}+s_{1}(M)(-x)^{n-1}+\cdots+s_{n}(M).

For each
k
, define the matrix
\nabla s_{k}(M)
whose
i,j
-entry is the partial derivative of
s_{k}(M)
with respect to the
i,j
-entry. (Note that here
M
is not necessarily symmetric and the
i,j
-entry and the
j,i
-entry are treated as different variables.) If
M(t)
is a trajectory of matrices where
s_{k}(M(t))
is constant, then by the chain rule, we have

0=\frac{d}{dt}s_{k}(M(t))=\left\langle\nabla s_{k}(M(0)),\dot{M}(0)\right\rangle.

That is,
\nabla s_{k}(M(0))
is orthogonal to the tangent of
M(t)
at
t=0
.

Example 3.2
.

Let

M=\begin{bmatrix}1&\framebox{2}&3\\
4&5&6\\
7&8&9\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }E(t)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
t&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

For
M
, we have

s_{2}=(1\cdot 5-\framebox{2}\cdot 4)+(5\cdot 9-6\cdot 8)+(1\cdot 9-3\cdot 7).

By changing the entry with value
2
,
s_{2}
will change correspondingly with a ratio of
-4
. Following a similar idea for all other entries gives

\nabla s_{2}(M)=\begin{bmatrix}14&-4&-7\\
-2&10&-8\\
-3&-6&6\end{bmatrix}.

Consider the matrix
M(t)=E(t)^{-1}ME(t)
with
M(0)=M
. Since similarity does not alter the characteristic polynomial,
s_{2}
is constant. By direct computation, we have

M(t)=\begin{bmatrix}1+2t&2&3\\
4+4t-2t^{2}&5-2t&6-3t\\
7+8t&8&9\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }\dot{M}(0)=\begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\\
4&-2&-3\\
8&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

As expected, we have
\left\langle\nabla s_{2}(M),\dot{M}(0)\right\rangle=0
.

The following proposition, which was proved in
[
18
]
, provides an interesting and systematic way to compute
\nabla s_{k}
. We use the notation
\nabla\det(M-xI)
to denote a matrix whose
i,j
-entry is the partial derivative of
\det(M-xI)
with respect to the
i,j
-entry. Let
X^{\rm cof}
be the cofactor matrix of
X
, the transpose of the adjugate matrix
X^{\rm adj}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
M\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
\nabla s_{k}=\nabla s_{k}(M)
for
k=1,\ldots,n
. Then the total derivative of
\det(M-xI)
with respect to the entries is

\nabla\det(M-xI)=\nabla s_{1}(-x)^{n-1}+\nabla s_{2}(-x)^{n-2}+\cdots+\nabla s_{n}=(M-xI)^{\rm cof}.

Example 3.4
.

Let
M
be as in
Example

3.2
. Direct computation yields

(M-xI)^{\rm cof}=\begin{bmatrix}(5-x)(9-x)-48&-(4(9-x)-42)&32-7(5-x)\\
-(2(9-x)-24)&(1-x)(9-x)-21&-(8(1-x)-14)\\
12-3(5-x)&-(6(1-x)-12)&(1-x)(5-x)-8\end{bmatrix},

which can be expanded as

(-x)^{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}+(-x)^{1}\begin{bmatrix}14&-4&-7\\
-2&10&-8\\
-3&-6&6\end{bmatrix}+(-x)^{0}\begin{bmatrix}-3&6&-3\\
6&-12&6\\
-3&6&-3\end{bmatrix}.

Thus,
\nabla s_{2}(M)
is shown as the coefficient of
(-x)^{1}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
M\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
\nabla s_{k}=\nabla s_{k}(M)
for
k=1,\ldots,n
. Then
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
and
\{I,M^{\top},\ldots,(M^{\top})^{n-1}\}
span the same subspace. Consequently,
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
is linearly independent if and only if the minimal polynomial of
M
has degree
n
.

Proof.

To simplify notation, let
M_{x}=M-xI
, treated as a matrix over the field of rational functions
\mathbb{R}(x)
, and consider its characteristic polynomial

\det(M_{x}-tI)=p_{0}(x)(-t)^{n}+p_{1}(x)(-t)^{n-1}+\cdots+p_{n}(x).

By the Cayley–Hamilton theorem, the inverse of
M_{x}
can be expressed as

M_{x}^{-1}=\frac{1}{p_{n}(x)}(p_{0}(x)(-M_{x})^{n-1}+p_{1}(x)(-M_{x})^{n-2}+\cdots+p_{n-1}(x)I).

Now, it is known that

M_{x}^{\rm cof}=(M_{x}^{\rm adj})^{\top}=\det(M_{x})(M_{x}^{\top})^{-1}.

Thus, since
p_{0}(x)=1
and
p_{n}(x)=\det(M_{x})
, we have

M_{x}^{\rm cof}=(-M_{x}^{\top})^{n-1}+p_{1}(x)(-M_{x}^{\top})^{n-2}+\cdots+p_{n-1}(x)I.

Expanding the powers of
-M_{x}^{\top}
on the right-hand side, we may write

M_{x}^{\rm cof}=r_{0}(x)(M^{\top})^{n-1}+r_{1}(x)(M^{\top})^{n-2}+\cdots+r_{n-1}(x)I

for some coefficients
r_{0}(x),\dots,r_{n-1}(x)
.
Equating this expression for
M_{x}^{\rm cof}
with the formula in
Proposition

3.3
, each element in
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
is in the span of
\{I,M^{\top},\ldots,(M^{\top})^{n-1}\}
.

On the other hand, we claim that
r_{k}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
k
. This would imply that
\{r_{0}(x),\ldots,r_{n-1}(x)\}
is a basis of the space of polynomials of degree less than
n
, and therefore, each element of
\{1,(-x),\ldots,(-x)^{n-1}\}
can be written as a linear combination of
\{r_{0}(x),\ldots,r_{n-1}(x)\}
. Consequently, by comparing the two expressions for
M_{x}^{\rm cof}
, each element in
\{I,M^{\top},\ldots,(M^{\top})^{n-1}\}
is in the span of
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
, showing that the two sets have the same span. We now examine the leading term of
r_{k}(x)
. By direct computation,

r_{k}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}p_{i}(x)\cdot(-1)^{n-1-i}\binom{n-1-i}{k-i}(-x)^{k-i}.

Note that
p_{i}(x)
is the sum of all
i\times i
principal minors of
M_{x}
, so its leading term is
\binom{n}{i}(-x)^{i}
. Thus, the leading term of
r_{k}(x)
is

(-x)^{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{n}{i}\cdot(-1)^{n-1-i}\binom{n-1-i}{k-i}=(-1)^{n-1}x^{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{n}{i}\binom{-(n-k)}{k-i}.

Observe that the summation is the coefficient of
y^{k}
in
(1+y)^{n}(1+y)^{-(n-k)}=(1+y)^{k}
, which is
1
. Therefore, the leading term of
r_{k}(x)
is
(-1)^{n-1}x^{k}
. This completes our argument showing that
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
and
\{I,M^{\top},\ldots,(M^{\top})^{n-1}\}
span the same subspace.

If the minimal polynomial is of degree less than
n
, then the dimension of the span of
\{I,M^{\top},\ldots,(M^{\top})^{n-1}\}
is less than
n
, indicating that
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
is linearly dependent. If the minimal polynomial is of degree
n
, then the dimension of the span of
\{I,M^{\top},\ldots,(M^{\top})^{n-1}\}
is
n
, implying that
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
is a linearly independent set.
∎

We need one more proposition before we provide the connection between strong properties and the Jacobian matrix.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be subspaces of a finite dimensional ambient space
W
. Let
\alpha
be a basis of
T_{2}
and
\beta
a basis of
N_{2}=T_{2}^{\perp}
. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1)

T_{1}+T_{2}=W
.

(2)

The orthogonal projection from
N_{2}
to
T_{1}
is injective.

(3)

The matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{j}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}
for
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\alpha
and
\mathbf{b}_{j}\in\beta
has full column rank.

Proof.

The equivalence between
(1)
and
(2)
is straightforward. Next, we verify that
(2)
and
(3)
are equivalent. Let
A=\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{j}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}
for
\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{j}\in\alpha
and
P=\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{j}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}
for
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\alpha
and
\mathbf{b}_{j}\in\beta
. Note that
A
is invertible since
\alpha
is a basis. Thus,
A^{-1}P
is the matrix representation of the orthogonal projection from
N_{2}
to
T_{1}
with respect to the bases
\beta
and
\alpha
. Therefore, the projection is injective if and only if
A^{-1}P
has full column rank. Since
A
is invertible,
A^{-1}P
has full column rank if and only if
P
has full column rank.
∎

3.1
Jacobian Method and the SSPWL

Given a matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)\subseteq\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
, the SSPWL considers two sets:
\mathcal{M}_{1}=\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, the set of all matrices of the form
Q^{\top}AQ
, where
Q
is any orthogonal matrix with
Q\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
. The Jacobian Method relies on the Jacobian matrix

J_{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{d(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n-1})}{d({\rm free\ entries})}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that
s_{n}=0
for any matrix in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
and
\frac{ds_{n}(A(t))}{dt}=0
if
A(t)\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
for all
t
, so the Jacobian matrix has fewer rows in the weighted Laplacian problem. Also note that the free entries here indicate the changes
A+tE_{e_{j}}
where
e_{j}
is an edge of
G
and
E_{e_{j}}
is the matrix whose
2\times 2
induced principal submatrix on
e_{j}
is

\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix}

and other entries are zero. By the chain rule,

J_{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle\nabla s_{i},E_{e_{j}}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}.

For SSPWL, we need one extra fact to reach our goal, utilizing properties of symmetric matrices. Recall that
H=I-\frac{1}{n}J
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
G
be a connected graph and
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
. Let
\nabla s_{k}=\nabla s_{k}(A)
. Then
\nabla s_{n}
is a multiple of
J
, so
H\nabla s_{n}H=O
. Moreover, the collection of matrices
\{H\nabla s_{1}H,H\nabla s_{2}H,\ldots,H\nabla s_{n-1}H\}
spans a subspace in
\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
, and its dimension equals
n-1
if and only if the eigenvalues of
A
are distinct.

Proof.

Since
G
is connected,
\operatorname{null}(A)=1
and the kernel of
A
is
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}
. By applying
Proposition

3.3
with
x=0
, we have
\nabla s_{n}=A^{\rm cof}
. Moreover,
A^{\rm cof}
is a multiple of
J
since
AA^{\rm cof}=AA^{\rm adj}=\det(A)I=O
and
A^{\rm cof}
is symmetric. Necessarily,
H\nabla s_{n}H=O
. Since
HMH
is the orthogonal projection of a symmetric matrix
M
onto
\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
, the set
\{H\nabla s_{1}H,H\nabla s_{2}H,\ldots,H\nabla s_{n-1}H\}
spans a subspace in
\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

Let
A=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\lambda_{q}P_{i}
be the spectral decomposition of
A
, where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{q}
are the distinct eigenvalues of
A
while
P_{i}
is the projection matrix onto the corresponding eigenspace. Without loss of generality, we may assume
\lambda_{1}=0
and
P_{1}=\frac{1}{n}J
. Note that
P_{1}P_{k}=P_{k}P_{1}=O
for any
k\neq 1
implies that the rows and columns are orthogonal to
\mathbf{1}
for any
k\neq 1
. Therefore,
HP_{k}H=P_{k}
for
k\neq 1
.

Also, it is known that
\{P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{q}\}
,
\{I,A,\ldots,A^{n-1}\}
,
\{\nabla s_{1},\nabla s_{2},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
span the same subspace in
\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
by
Lemma

3.5
. By applying
M\mapsto HMH
to every element, along with the facts that
HP_{1}H=O
,
HP_{k}H=P_{k}
for
k\neq 1
, and
H\nabla s_{n}H=O
, we have

\operatorname{span}\{P_{2},P_{3},\ldots,P_{q}\}=\operatorname{span}\{H\nabla s_{1}H,H\nabla s_{2}H,\ldots,H\nabla s_{n-1}H\}.

Moreover, its dimension is
n-1
if and only if
q=n
, that is, the eigenvalues of
A
are distinct.
∎

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
G
be a connected graph,
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
and
J_{A}
the associated Jacobian matrix. Then
J_{A}
has full rank if and only if the eigenvalues of
A
are distinct and
A
has the SSPWL.

Proof.

Let
\nabla s_{k}=\nabla s_{k}(A)
. We consider two cases. Firstly, when the eigenvalues of
A
contain repeated elements,
\{H\nabla s_{1}H,H\nabla s_{2}H,\ldots,H\nabla s_{n-1}H\}
is a linearly dependent set by
Proposition

3.7
. Observe that
\left\langle\nabla s_{i},E_{e_{j}}\right\rangle=\left\langle H\nabla s_{i}H,E_{e_{j}}\right\rangle
since
E_{e_{j}}\in\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
HE_{e_{j}}=E_{e_{j}}H=E_{e_{j}}
. Therefore, the rows of
J_{A}
are dependent and
J_{A}
does not have full row rank.

Secondly, when the eigenvalues of
A
are distinct, by
Proposition

3.7
the set
\{H\nabla s_{1}H,H\nabla s_{2},\ldots,H\nabla s_{n-1}H\}\subset\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
is independent. Let
F
be the function as in
Equation

1
. Consider a trajectory
A(t)=e^{-K(t)}Ae^{K(t)}
with
K(0)=O
and
A(0)=A
. Since
s_{k}
is a constant for any
A(t)
, we have
\left\langle\nabla s_{k},\dot{A}(0)\right\rangle=0
. By properly choosing
K(t)
, we may obtain
\dot{A}(0)
to be any element in
\operatorname{range}(F_{K}))
, so
\nabla s_{k}
is orthogonal to any element in
\operatorname{range}(F_{K})
. Moreover,

\left\langle H\nabla s_{k}H,K^{\top}A-AK\right\rangle=\left\langle\nabla s_{k},H(K^{\top}A-AK)H\right\rangle=\left\langle\nabla s_{k},K^{\top}A-AK\right\rangle=0

for any
K^{\top}A-AK\in\operatorname{range}(F_{K})^{\perp}
since
K^{\top}A-AK\in\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Thus,
H\nabla s_{k}H\in\operatorname{range}(F_{K})^{\perp}
for each
k
. By
Corollary

2.8
,
\dim(\operatorname{range}(F_{K})^{\perp})=n-1
. Therefore,
\{H\nabla s_{1}H,H\nabla s_{2}H,\ldots,H\nabla s_{n-1}H\}
is a basis of
\operatorname{range}(F_{K})^{\perp}
. On the other hand,
\{E_{e_{j}}\}
for nonzero entries
e_{j}
is a basis of
\operatorname{range}(F_{B})
. Recall that
A
has the SSPWL if and only if
\operatorname{range}(F_{B})+\operatorname{range}(F_{K})=\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
by
Proposition

2.9
. The result now follows from
Proposition

3.6
.
∎

3.2
Every Connected Graph Realizes a Matrix with SSPWL

In this subsection, we show that connectedness is necessary and sufficient for a graph to admit a matrix with the SSPWL. To prove this, we rely on the equivalence of a matrix having the SSPWL and having a full rank Jacobian matrix (
Theorem

3.8
).

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
T
be a tree on
n
vertices with variable edge weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1}
such that
w_{n-1}
is a weight on a pendant edge. Let
T^{\prime}
be the tree on
n-1
vertices obtained from
T
by removing the pendant edge and leaf corresponding to the weight
w_{n-1}
. For
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
, let

p_{A}(x)=\det(A-xI)
be the characteristic polynomial of
A
where the coefficient of
(-x)^{n-i}
is
s_{i}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1})
, the
i
th symmetric function of the eigenvalues of
A
. Let
J_{A}
be the Jacobian matrix of
A
with
i,j
-entry given by
\frac{\partial s_{i}}{\partial w_{j}}
evaluated at the weights
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1})
of
A
. Then
J_{A}
evaluated at
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2},0)

is of the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}J_{A^{\prime}}&\ast\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}&c\end{array}\right],

where
J_{A}^{\prime}
is the Jacobian matrix with respect to the matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T^{\prime})
with edge weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2}
and
c=nw_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{n-2}
.

Proof.

For
i,j<n-1
, the
i,j
-entry of
J_{A}
is
\frac{\partial s_{i}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2},w_{n-1})}{\partial w_{j}}
. Note that the value of this expression when
w_{n-1}
is set equal to 0 is the same as
\frac{\partial s_{i}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2},0)}{\partial w_{j}}
; that is, if
g_{i}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2})=s_{i}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2},0)
, then

\frac{\partial g_{i}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2})}{\partial w_{j}}=\left.\frac{\partial s_{i}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2},w_{n-1})}{\partial w_{j}}\right|_{w_{n-1}=0}.

On the other hand, if
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T^{\prime})
as described in the statement of the Lemma, then
A^{\prime}
is the order
n-1
submatrix of the matrix obtained from
A
with
w_{n-1}=0
. Thus,
p_{A^{\prime}}(x)=\det(A^{\prime}-xI)
where the coefficient of
(-x)^{n-1-i}
is

s_{i}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2})=g_{i}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2})
. Thus, for
i,j<n-1
, the
i,j
-entry of
J_{A}
when evaluated at
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2},0)
is equal to the
i,j
-entry of
J_{A}^{\prime}
. It is left to prove that the last row of
J_{A}
when evaluated at
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2},0)
is
[\ 0\ \cdots\ 0\ |\ c\ ]
, where
c=nw_{1}\cdots w_{n-2}
. Since the entries in this row are equal to

\left.\frac{\partial s_{n-1}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1})}{\partial w_{j}}\right|_{w_{n-1}=0},\ j=1,\dots,n-1,

we are done once we verify that

s_{n-1}(w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1})=nw_{1}\cdots w_{n-1},

(2)

which follows from the Weighted Matrix-Tree Theorem (see, e.g.,
[
21
]
).
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
n\geq 2
. For any tree
T
on
n
vertices, there exist
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1}>0
such that
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
with these edge weights satisfies
\det J_{A}\neq 0
.

Proof.

We prove the result by induction on
n
. For
n=2
, choose any
w_{1}\neq 0
. For
n=3
,
T=P_{3}
and any
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
satisfies

A=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}&-w_{1}&0\\
-w_{1}&w_{1}+w_{2}&-w_{2}\\
0&-w_{2}&w_{2}\end{bmatrix},\ J_{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\
3w_{2}&3w_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus,
\det J_{A}\neq 0
for any
w_{1},w_{2}
with
w_{1}\neq w_{2}
.

For the purpose of an induction argument, assume that the result holds for all trees on fewer than
n
vertices. Suppose
T
is a tree on
n
vertices with variable edge weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1}
such that
w_{n-1}
is a weight on a pendant edge, and
T^{\prime}
is the tree on
n-1
vertices with weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2}
, that is,
T^{\prime}
is obtained from
T
by removing the pendant edge and leaf. Let
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
and denote by
A_{0}
the matrix obtained from
A
by setting
w_{n-1}=0
. By
Lemma

3.9
,

J_{A_{0}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}J_{A^{\prime}}&\ast\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}&c\end{array}\right],

where
J_{A^{\prime}}
is the Jacobian matrix with respect to the matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T^{\prime})
with edge weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2}
and
c=nw_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{n-2}
. Using the induction hypothesis, there exist positive weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2}>0
such that
\det J_{A^{\prime}}\neq 0
. Hence,
\det J_{A_{0}}=\det J_{A^{\prime}}\cdot c\neq 0
. With this choice of weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2}
, consider
\det J_{A}
as a polynomial in
w_{n-1}
, and call this polynomial
q(w_{n-1})
. We have
q(0)\neq 0
. Since
q
is a continuous function of
w_{n-1}
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
q(\varepsilon)\neq 0
. Then the matrix
A_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
with weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-2},w_{n-1}=\varepsilon
has
\det J_{A_{\varepsilon}}\neq 0
.
∎

Theorem 3.11
.

The set
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
admits a matrix with SSPWL if and only if
G
is connected.

Proof.

Suppose
G
is not connected. Assume
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
has the form
A=\begin{bmatrix}B&O\\
O&C\end{bmatrix}
. By selecting
X=\begin{bmatrix}O&J^{\top}\\
J&O\end{bmatrix}
, where
J
is the all-ones matrix of appropriate size, it follows that
A\circ X=I\circ X=O
and
H(AX-XA)H=O
. Therefore, every matrix in
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
loses the SSPWL if
G
is disconnected.

For sufficiency, assume that
G
is connected. Then
G
contains a spanning tree
T
. By
Lemma

3.10
, there exists
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
such that
\det J_{A}\neq 0
. Hence, by
Theorem

3.8
,
A
has the SSPWL. The result now follows from the Supergraph lemma
Lemma

2.11
.
∎

The next result demonstrates that starting with an SSPWL matrix whose graph is a tree, appending an edge results in an SSPWL matrix for all but a finite number of weights that are assigned to the new edge.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
T
be a tree on
n
vertices. For
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
,
w>0
and
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, define

\widehat{A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}A+w\mathbf{e}_{j}\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}&-w\mathbf{e}_{j}\\
\hline\cr-w\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\top}&w\end{array}\right]\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(\widehat{T}),

where
V(\widehat{T})=V(T)\cup\{n+1\}
and
E(\widehat{T})=E(T)\cup\{j,n+1\}
. If
A
has the SSPWL, then
\widehat{A}
has the SSPWL for all but a finite number of values of
w>0
.

Proof.

Let
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1}
be the weights on the edges of
T
and consider
\det J_{\widehat{A}}
as a polynomial in
w
(treating
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1}
as constants), call this polynomial
q(w)
. By
Lemma

3.9
,
q(0)=\det J_{A}\cdot nw_{1}\dots w_{n-1}
. Since
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(T)
has the SSPWL, the eigenvalues of
A
are distinct by
Corollary

2.13
and thus
\det J_{A}\neq 0
by
Theorem

3.8
. Thus
q(0)\neq 0
, and since
q
is a polynomial of degree at most
n
,
q(w)\neq 0
, except for at most
n
values of
w
(see also the proof of Lemma
3.10
). Thus,
\det J_{\widehat{A}}\neq 0
except for at most
n
values of
w
.
∎

4
Stars,
K_{n}-e
, and Graphs on Four Vertices

In this section, we apply the analysis on strong weighted Laplacian matrices and the corresponding Jacobian Method from the previous sections to two specific graph families and carefully review (relative to some of the work in
[
15
]
)
the spectral regions in
S_{L}
for connected graphs on 4 vertices. For short, we call a matrix that has the SSPWL as a
strong
matrix; otherwise, it is a
weak
matrix.

4.1
Stars

In this subsection, we focus on strong weighted Laplacian matrices whose graph is a star, namely
K_{1,n-1}
.
Let

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}\sum_{i}w_{i}&-w_{1}&\cdots&-w_{n-1}\\
\hline\cr-w_{1}&w_{1}&&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
-w_{n-1}&0&&w_{n-1}\\
\end{array}\right]

be a weighted Laplacian matrix that fits the star. Then a basic computation reveals that the sum of all
k\times k
minors of
A
can be written as

s_{k}=(k+1)\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\prod_{i\in\alpha}w_{i},

where
\alpha
is a
k
-subset of
\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}
for
k=1,2,\ldots,n-1
.

Given a vector
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n-1})^{\top}
, we let

t_{k}(\mathbf{w})
denote the
k
th elementary symmetric function of the entries of
\mathbf{w}
, for
k=1,2,\ldots,n-1
. Then it follows that
s_{k}=(k+1)t_{k}(\mathbf{w})
. Furthermore, if
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{j}
is a
j
-subset of
\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}
, then the notation
\mathbf{w}^{(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{j})}
denotes the sub-vector obtained from
\mathbf{w}
by removing the entries
w_{i_{1}},w_{i_{2}},\ldots,w_{i_{j}}
.

Then, basic differentiation implies that

\frac{\partial s_{k}}{\partial w_{i}}=(k+1)t_{k-1}(\mathbf{w}^{(i)}),

where
k=1,2,\ldots,n-1
and
i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
(note:
t_{0}:=1
). In this case, it follows that the Jacobian matrix with respect to the matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{1,n-1})
with edge weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1}
is the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
matrix equal to

J_{A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}2&2&\cdots&2\\
3t_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)})&3t_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{(2)})&\ldots&3t_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{(n-1)})\\
4t_{2}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)})&4t_{3}(\mathbf{w}^{(2)})&\ldots&4t_{2}(\mathbf{w}^{(n-1)})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
nt_{n-2}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)})&nt_{n-2}(\mathbf{w}^{(2)})&\ldots&nt_{n-2}(\mathbf{w}^{(n-1)})\\
\end{array}\right].

To determine a criterion regarding the invertibility of
J_{A}
, we consider
\det(J_{A})
. In this case applying elementary column operations to eliminate the 2’s in row 1, it follows that
\det(J_{A})
is equal to

\hskip-28.45274pt2\det\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}3(t_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{(2)})-t_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}))&3(t_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{(3)})-t_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}))&\ldots&3(t_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{(n-1)})-t_{1}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}))\\
4(t_{2}(\mathbf{w}^{(2)})-t_{2}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}))&4(t_{2}(\mathbf{w}^{(3)})-t_{2}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}))&\ldots&4(t_{2}(\mathbf{w}^{(n-1)})-t_{2}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}))\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
n(t_{n-2}(\mathbf{w}^{(2)})-t_{n-2}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}))&n(t_{n-2}(\mathbf{w}^{(3)})-t_{n-2}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}))&\ldots&n(t_{n-2}(\mathbf{w}^{(n-1)})-t_{n-2}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}))\\
\end{array}\right],

where the latter matrix has size
(n-2)\times(n-2)
. In fact, the entries of this latter matrix are of the form

(k+2)(t_{k}(\mathbf{w}^{(j)})-t_{k}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)}))
, where
k=1,\ldots,n-2
and
j=2,3,\ldots,n
. A straightforward algebraic simplification involving symmetric functions reveals

t_{k}(\mathbf{w}^{(j)})-t_{k}(\mathbf{w}^{(1)})=(w_{1}-w_{j})t_{k-1}(\mathbf{w}^{(1,j)}),

where

k=1,\ldots,n-2
and
j=2,3,\ldots,n
and with the convention that
t_{0}:=1
.
Applying the multi-linearity of the determinant function implies

\det(J_{A})=2\cdot\left(\prod_{j=2}^{n-1}(w_{1}-w_{j})\right)\det(B),

where the entries of
B
are of the form
(k+2)t_{k-1}(w^{(1,j)})
. Hence, we can apply similar arguments as above to establish that

\det(J_{A})=(n-1)!\cdot\prod_{i<j}(w_{j}-w_{i}).

With
Corollaries

2.13
and
3.8
, this formula implies
Theorem

4.1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Choose the matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{1,n-1})
with edge weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n-1}
. Then

A
has the SSPWL if and only if the edge weights
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n-1}
represent distinct positive numbers.

4.2
The Complete Graph Minus an Edge

The case of a complete graph is simple in that all weighted Laplacian matrices are strong. However, removing a single edge does allow for some weak matrices which are characterized below. To this end,
let
e=\{1,n\}.
Suppose that
A=[a_{ij}]\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{n}-e)
does not have the SSPWL. Then there exists
c\neq 0
such that
X=cE_{1n}
satisfies
H(AX-XA)H=O
, or equivalently,
H(AE_{1n}-E_{1n}A)H=O
. Note that
a_{1n}=0
since
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{n}-e)
. By direct computation,
AE_{1n}-E_{1n}A
is

\begin{array}[]{cl}&\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}0&0\ \cdots\ 0&a_{11}\\
\hline\cr a_{2n}&&a_{21}\\
\vdots&O&\vdots\\
a_{n-1,n}&&a_{n-1,1}\\
\hline\cr a_{nn}&0\ \cdots\ 0&0\end{array}\right]-\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}0&a_{n2}\ \cdots\ a_{n,n-1}&a_{nn}\\
\hline\cr 0&&0\\
\vdots&O&\vdots\\
0&&0\\
\hline\cr a_{11}&a_{12}\ \cdots\ a_{1,n-1}&0\end{array}\right]\\
&\\
=&\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}0&-a_{2n}\ \cdots\ -a_{n-1,n}&a_{11}-a_{nn}\\
\hline\cr a_{2n}&&a_{12}\\
\vdots&O&\vdots\\
a_{n-1,n}&&a_{1,n-1}\\
\hline\cr a_{nn}-a_{11}&-a_{12}\ \cdots\ -a_{1,n-1}&0\end{array}\right].\end{array}

By
Proposition

2.2
,
H(AE_{1n}-E_{1n}A)H=O
is equivalent to
(\mathbf{e}_{i_{1}}-\mathbf{e}_{j_{1}})^{\top}(AE_{1n}-E_{1n}A)(\mathbf{e}_{i_{2}}-\mathbf{e}_{j_{2}})=0
for arbitrary
i_{1}\neq j_{1}
and
i_{2}\neq j_{2}
.
By taking
(i_{1},j_{1})=(1,2)
and
(i_{2},j_{2})=(1,3)
, we see that
a_{2n}=a_{3n}
. By switching
j_{2}
to
4,\ldots,n-1
, we get
a_{2n}=a_{3n}=\cdots=a_{n-1,n}
. Similarly, by taking
(i_{1},j_{1})=(2,n)
and
(i_{2},j_{2})=(3,n),\ldots,(n-1,n)
, we have
a_{12}=a_{13}=\cdots=a_{1,n-1}
.
Since
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{n}-e)
, we know
a_{11}=-a_{12}-\cdots-a_{1,n-1}=-(n-2)a_{12}
and
a_{nn}=-a_{2n}-\cdots-a_{n-1,n}=-(n-2)a_{2n}
. Finally, we consider
(i_{1},j_{1})=(1,2)
and
(i_{2},j_{2})=(1,n)
and get

a_{12}-a_{2n}-(a_{11}-a_{nn})=(n-1)(a_{12}-a_{2n})=0,

which implies
a_{12}=a_{2n}
.

In summary,
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{n}-e)
is a weak matrix if and only if

a_{2n}=\cdots=a_{n-1,n}=a_{12}=\cdots=a_{1,n-1}=-\alpha

for some
\alpha>0
. These computations and
[
15
, Lemma 2.4]
prove Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
e=\{1,n\}
and
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{n}-e)
. Then
A
does not have the SSPWL if and only if it is of the form

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}(n-2)\alpha&-\alpha\mathbf{1}_{n-2}^{\top}&0\\
\hline\cr-\alpha\mathbf{1}_{n-2}&B+2\alpha I_{n-2}&-\alpha\mathbf{1}_{n-2}\\
\hline\cr 0&-\alpha\mathbf{1}_{n-2}^{\top}&(n-2)\alpha\rule{0.0pt}{9.68745pt}\end{array}\right]

where
B\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(K_{n-2})
and
\alpha>0
. Furthermore,

\operatorname{spec}(A)=\{0,(n-2)\alpha,n\alpha,\mu_{2}+2\alpha,\dots,\mu_{n-2}+2\alpha\}

(3)

where
\{0,\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{n-2}\}=\operatorname{spec}(B)
.

4.3
Connected Graphs of Order Four

In this subsection, we characterize all weak matrices for connected graphs of order four and use them to find the potential boundaries of the spectra. To be more precise, for each connected graph of order four
G
, we study the spectra of matrices
A\in\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
under the normalization that
\operatorname{tr}(A)=2m
, where
m=|E(G)|

5
Jacobian Methods and Other Strong Properties

As mentioned earlier in
Section

3
, the Jacobian Method was introduced as a tool to study spectrally arbitrary sign patterns. This method was then extended to the Nilpotent-Centralizer Method and the non-symmetric strong spectral property (nSSP). In this section, we make a comprehensive study on the Jacobian Methods under different settings.

5.1
Jacobian Method and the nSSP

A
sign pattern

P
is a matrix whose entries are in
\{+,-,0\}
. The
quantitative class
of
P
, denoted by
\mathcal{Q}(P)
, is the set of all matrices whose entries follow the signs of the corresponding entries in
P
. Note that the arguments in this subsection also hold for zero-nonzero patterns, while we only mention the sign patterns. Let
A\in Q(\mathcal{P})\subseteq\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. According to
[
16
, Section 5]
the nSSP is equivalent to

T_{1}+T_{2}=W,

where

•

T_{1}=\operatorname{span}(\mathcal{Q}(P))
,

•

T_{2}=\{-LA+AL:L\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\}
,

•

W=\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

The Jacobian Method under this setting relies on the Jacobian matrix

\displaystyle J_{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle{\frac{d(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})}{d({\rm free\ entries})}}\end{bmatrix}

and is a powerful tool in certifying a spectrally arbitrary sign pattern
[
14
,
19
,
16
]
.
Let
e_{j}
be the indices of the nonzero entries and
E_{e_{j}}
the
0
-
1
matrix whose entry at
e_{j}
is
1
and
0
otherwise. Then, by the chain rule,

J_{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle\nabla s_{i},E_{e_{j}}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
A\in Q(\mathcal{P})\subseteq\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and
J_{A}
its associated Jacobian matrix. Then
J_{A}
has full row rank if and only if the minimal polynomial of
A
has degree
n
and
A
has the nSSP.

Proof.

We consider two cases. Firstly, when the minimal polynomial of
A
has degree less than
n
, the dimension of the span of
\{I,A^{\top},\ldots,(A^{\top})^{n-1}\}
is less than
n
, so
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
is linearly dependent by
Lemma

3.5
. Therefore, the rows of
J_{A}
are dependent and
J_{A}
does not have full row rank.

Secondly, when the minimal polynomial of
A
has degree
n
,
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
is linearly independent by
Lemma

3.5
. Also,
T_{2}
is known to have codimension
\dim(T_{2}^{\perp})=n
. Therefore,
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
is a basis of
N_{2}=T_{2}^{\perp}
. On the other hand,
\{E_{e_{j}}\}
for nonzero entries
e_{j}
is a basis of
T_{1}
. The result now follows from
Proposition

3.6
.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Theorem

5.1
explains why the Nilpotent-Jacobian Method in
[
14
]
, used for showing that a pattern is spectrally arbitrary, requires a nilpotent matrix of index
n
. The paper
[
19
]
which introduced the Nilpotent-Centralizer method assumes the same condition. However, as explained in
[
16
]
, nilpotency of index
n
is not required with the nSSP condition to verify that a pattern is spectrally arbitrary.

5.2

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)\subseteq\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. According to
[
16
, Proposition 1.1]
the SSP is equivalent to

T_{1}+T_{2}=W,

where

•

T_{1}=\operatorname{span}(\mathcal{S}(G))
,

•

T_{2}=\{K^{\top}A+AK:K\in\operatorname{Skew}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\}
,

•

W=\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

The Jacobian Method under this setting relies on the Jacobian matrix

J_{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle{\frac{d(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})}{d({\rm free\ entries})}}\end{bmatrix},

where the free entries include the diagonal entries and the entries corresponding to edges.
Let
e_{j}
be the indices of the free entries and
E_{e_{j}}
the
0
-
1
matrix with the entry at
e_{j}
equal to
1
and all other entries are
0
. Note that
E_{e_{j}}
has a
1
when
e_{j}
is diagonal and two
1

e_{j}
is off-diagonal. Then, by the chain rule,

J_{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle\nabla s_{i},E_{e_{j}}\right\rangle\end{bmatrix}.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)\subseteq\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
and let

be its associated Jacobian matrix. Then

has full rank if and only if the eigenvalues of

are distinct and

has the SSP.

We consider two cases. Firstly, when the eigenvalues of

contain repeated elements, the dimension of the span of
\{I,A,\ldots,A^{n-1}\}
is less than

, so
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
is a linearly dependent set by
Lemma

3.5
. Therefore, the rows of
J_{A}
are dependent and
J_{A}
does not have full row rank.

Secondly, when the eigenvalues of
A
are distinct,
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
is a linearly independent set by
Lemma

3.5
. Since
A
is symmetric, each
\nabla s_{k}
is also symmetric. Since
T_{2}
is known to have codimension
\dim(T_{2}^{\perp})=n
, with respect to the ambient space
\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore,
\{\nabla s_{1},\ldots,\nabla s_{n}\}
is a basis for
N_{2}=T_{2}^{\perp}
. On the other hand,
\{E_{e_{j}}\}
for nonzero entries

is a basis of
T_{1}
. The result now follows from
Proposition

3.6
.

, respectively, and one may observe that the Jacobian matrix computed in
Example

5.4
can be obtained from these matrices by recording the entries and multiplying by a factor
2
for each off-diagonal entry.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we introduce the strong property, SSPWL, for the weighted Laplacian matrix. This is the first time that a strong property is considered on a specific ambient space
\operatorname{Sym}_{\mathbf{1}}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
, other than the standard spaces
\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
or
\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. The Supergraph lemma and the Bifurcation lemma are established in order to study the inverse eigenvalue problem for Laplacian matrices of a graph.

In parallel with studying the SSPWL, the Jacobian Method is established as an equivalent condition for SSPWL, provided that the eigenvalues are all distinct. This provides an alternative method for verifying the SSPWL. For sparse graphs, this method is, in general, easier than checking the SSPWL by definition. In particular, for trees, the Jacobian Method and the SSPWL are exactly the same, and the Jacobian matrix is of order
n-1
, while the verification matrix of SSPWL is of order
\binom{n-1}{2}
.

Using these tools, we detect the potential boundaries for the set of spectra given by
\mathcal{S}_{L}(G)
when
G
is a connected graph with
4
vertices. In
[
15
]
, the authors suspected that the boundaries for
\mathsf{Paw}
,
C_{4}
,
K_{4}-e
are linear, and our computations support this claim.

[

G

\hat{a}(G)

\lambda_{2}
among matrices

with
\operatorname{tr}(A)=2m
. That is, it is the right-most points in each picture in
Figure

1
, so the absolute algebraic connectivity is solved for these graphs. In
[
17

, the absolute algebraic connectivity for trees is considered, and the basic properties of the matrix that achieve the absolute algebraic connectivity as a simple eigenvalue are studied. With the simulation in
