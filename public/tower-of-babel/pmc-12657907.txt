Clinical study of an integrated sensor system for detection and classification of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)
Authors: Seongmun Kim, Thi Hang Dang, Haewan Cho, Sungmin Shin, Seungup Seo, Sanghyun Lee, Hyungki Min, Jagannath Malik, Franklin Bien
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) is traditionally diagnosed via Polysomnography (PSG), which relies on multiple wired sensors in an unfamiliar hospital setting. In this study, a compact home-sleep-test system is proposed, integrating a fringing-field capacitive sensor for wireless respiratory-effort monitoring system(, and a nasal airflow sensing system (2 cm×2 cm) with an connected 2 cm × 1 cm temperature sensor (both wired to the processing unit). A customized signal-processing algorithm was developed to denoise both channels and automatically identify apnea and hypopnea events. Validation with subjects (n = 31) demonstrated performance metrics (SN = 0.846, SP = 0.944, Precision = 0.917, and Accuracy = 0.903, ) in classifying OSA severity. By combining novel capacitive fringing-field sensing and temperature-based airflow measurement into a largely wireless wearable, a practical and accurate alternative to traditional PSG for at-home OSA detection is offered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26119-5.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Biomedical engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering, Lifestyle modification

Introduction

Sleep occupies approximately one-third of an individual’s lifetime, playing a crucial role in physical and mental well-being. However, during sleep, issues such as improper posture and respiratory problems often hinder effective ventilation, leading to sleep-disordered breathing (SDB)1–3. Prolonged SDB not only disrupts sleep architecture but also poses significant health risks, contributing to various comorbidities including diabetes, dementia, and cardiovascular disease4–6. Continuous monitoring of sleep disturbances becomes imperative to mitigate the adverse effects of SDB. Among the spectrum of sleep disorders, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) stands out as a prevalent condition characterized by recurrent cessation of airflow during sleep. These interruptions, lasting 10 s or longer due to airway obstruction, signify the need for intervention to ensure uninterrupted breathing7–9. The diagnosis of sleep apnea follows guidelines set forth by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), with the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) serving as a diagnostic index10,11. An AHI > 5 events/hour is diagnostic of OSA.

Currently, the gold standard for assessing SDB remains in-lab polysomnography (PSG), a comprehensive sleep study conducted exclusively in clinical settings12–14. However, the cumbersome process of PSG, involving the attachment of numerous sensors to monitor sleep quality, has logistical and financial challenges15,16. Consequently, there is a need for a sleep-monitoring sensor system that is cost-effective and easily accessible for general users at home.

Efforts to address these challenges have led to the development of portable sleep monitoring devices, such as those offered by ResMed, equipped with a reduced but essential set of sensors for home use. Despite their convenience, the high cost of ownership (ApneaLink, Alice PDx, Nox T3s, Grael PSG and so on) still limits accessibility and affordability for general users. Additionally, reliance on nasal airflow measurements using nasal cannulas poses limitations, as sensor displacement can lead to inaccurate readings17.

In context of diagnosing and measuring sleep apnea through PSG; respiratory effort (chest, abdomen), nasal airflow, and blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) are crucial biometrics used to identify respiratory abnormalities. Consequently, several research groups are trying to develop wearable devices that can measure respiratory signals at home, overcoming the limitations of PSG, which requires multiple sensors attached to body, and must be performed in a clinical environment. Photoplethysmography(PPG) is utilized to measure SpO2 by analyzing light reflection and transmission through blood vessels18. This allows the detection of sleep apnea by monitoring SpO2 levels, as healthy individuals typically maintain levels above 90%, whereas a drop in these levels indicates respiratory issues during sleep19,20. However, PPG measurements are limited to areas with abundant capillaries, such as the fingertips and earlobes, and are highly susceptible to ambient light interference.

Strain belt sensors, commonly known as respiratory effort sensors, measure respiratory signals through chest movements during breathing. These sensors detect changes in electrical resistance caused by the expansion and contraction of the chest and abdomen during respiration21,22. Since they rely on changes in tension and pressure on the sensor, strain belts must be tightly fitted around the chest or abdomen, often causing discomfort over long periods and potential measurement inaccuracies due to sensor displacement caused by movement. However, positional changes during sleep and extended wear exacerbate discomfort and inaccuracies. To address the limitations of belt-type respiratory sensing, piezoelectric sensor–based methods have been proposed23,24. In this approach, a flexible material converts chest volume changes into mechanical strain, which is measured as an electrical signal. Although sensor miniaturization improves portability, signal quality deteriorates with slight shifts in attachment, and performance varies with material properties and skin contact conditions. Wearable, skin-attached methods also suffer from sensor detachment or displacement due to patient movement. To overcome this, noncontact radar sensors have been investigated25,26. Radar systems emit signals from a fixed position above the subject and calculate the time delay of reflections caused by chest motion to distinguish inhalation from exhalation. However, accuracy is highly sensitive to sleep posture and body orientation, and the bulky equipment and fixed installation requirements limit practical use in home-sleep-test (HST) settings.

Respiratory airflow signals are generally measured from the nose or mouth. Monitoring thermal or humidity changes in the airflow during breathing, provides insight into respiratory patterns27–29. Airflow measurement through the mouth necessitates sensor placement near the mouth, whereas nasal airflow is measured near the nostrils or the philtrum, often using masks or humidity-sensitive sensors. Typically, Humidity sensors employ capacitive sensing. However, sensitivity shows non-linear characteristics beyond 60% humidity, leading to lower accuracy at higher humidity levels30. Respiratory sensors using capacitance sensing are often belt-type devices that detect pressure changes resulting from chest expansion during breathing31,32.

In this paper, we propose an integrated sensor system for measurement airflow and respiratory signals(Fig. 1a). The system utilizes a novel fringing field-based capacitive sensor, and an embedded on-chip temperature sensor capable of measuring temperature variation due to nasal airflow as shown Fig. 1b-c. The respiratory signal is measured from capacitance variations of the fringing field, which correlate with abdominal expansion and contraction during the respiratory cycle, thus capturing the respiratory effort signal (Fig. 1c). Similar methods for non-contact monitoring of cardiopulmonary activity through electromagnetic fields have been suggested33. However, restrictions on subject movement, stationary measurement environments, and the large size of traditional equipment limit daily-life applications. Here, a miniature, capacitive fringing-field sensor has been developed for respiratory measurement, designed for extended use without recharging. The sensor operates at low frequency in the range of a few hundreds kHz, and able to detect small variations in the order of pF, due to in respiration cycle. The sensor system measures respiratory signals in real-time and supports wireless data transmission to a mobile application, enabling data collection, algorithmic analysis, and classification. An additional temperature sensor measures nasal temperature variations during respiration for improved accuracy in detecting OSA and hypopnea. This system is positioned under the nose to detect a temperature change of during breathing, measuring the airflow signal. Based on the measured respiratory signals, a level-adaptive noise reduction method is applied to reduce measurement errors due to changes in the measurement environment. An algorithm distinguishes between deep-breathing and apnea periods, providing quantitative measurement of OSA event durations (Fig. 1d). This system is suitable for continuous sleep-state monitoring.

Results

Respiration capacitive sensor and temperature sensor

The fringing effect is utilized to detect objects within the fringing-field of a capacitive sensor. When an object enters this field, the effective permittivity changes, producing a corresponding variation in the sensor’s capacitance. This principle is commonly employed in touchscreen technology34. When the human body enters the fringing field, mutual capacitance () forms as the body acts as a ground reference, causing the sensor’s self-capacitance () to decrease. The proposed sensor exploits this effect to detect volumetric changes in the torso during breathing: inhalation reduces the body‑to‑sensor distance, increasing Cₘ and thus decreasing , while exhalation produces the opposite trend, enabling respiratory signal capture. Figure 2a illustrates an experimental setup replicating realistic measurement conditions. A skin-mimicking biomaterial ( = 50) was used to emulate human tissue near the sensor35. CST simulations (CST Studio Suite, Simulia, France) were performed to calculate capacitance as the biomaterial‑sensor gap varied from 2 mm to 15 mm. As shown in Fig. 2b, the measured capacitance approached the air‑gap value ( =0.22pF) at larger distances, confirming that diminishes with increased separation. Based on these simulation insights, the sensor was fabricated (Fig. 2a) and tested by varying the distance to a human finger. Capacitance was measured using a Precision LCR Meter (4285 A, Agilent Technologies, USA). Results (Fig. 2c) matched simulations: at a 1 mm gap, decreased to approximately 2.19 pF from the air-state baseline ( = 2.3 pF). This agreement confirms that when applied to the abdomen or chest, breathing-induced skin-sensor distance changes reliably encode respiratory effort. Discrepancies between simulated and measured values are attributed to parasitic capacitances from surrounding printed circuit board(PCB) traces and components. As a body-worn medical device, safety is critical. The sensor must remain adhered for extended monitoring ( ≥ 4 h), so the fringing-field intensity is kept low. At a 2 mm distance, the field strength is approximately 2.07 × 10³ V/m (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Information Figure S1a–b). To assess potential biological effects, Specific Absorption Rate (SAR) was evaluated at a reference input power of 1 mW, yielding 0.00702 W/kg. This is well below the 0.08 W/kg limit for whole-body exposure, fully complying with international SAR regulations (Supplementary Information Text S2 and Supplementary Information Figure S2)36. Furthermore, the temperature sensor exhibited consistent and reliable responses both across temperature changes and during prolonged measurements at a constant temperature (Fig. 2e). This performance confirms its suitability for monitoring nasal airflow during respiration.

Breathing system with respiration effort sensor, airflow sensor and algorithm

To measure respiratory effort, a circuit employing the capacitance‑to‑digital conversion (CDC) technique was utilized, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. This circuit detects variations in capacitance values caused by changes in the distance between the sensor and the skin during respiration. As the external capacitance changes during breathing, it is converted into analog to digital conversion (ADC) output codes according to Eq. (5). The CDC interface has a high resolution of 0.887 aF/LSB, enabling it to accurately detect changes on the order of a few femtofarads that occur during respiration. Additionally, respiratory airflow is measured using an internal sensor embedded within the chip, eliminating the need for any external airflow transducer. The ADC output is transmitted to an external application via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), as shown in Fig. 3a. The nasal airflow sensor measures temperature changes during inhalation and exhalation. As shown in Fig. 3b, temperature rises during exhalation and falls during inhalation, remaining slightly below core body temperature with consistent cyclic changes. The temperature output is calculated using Eq. (4), based on a predefined offset (a), slope (b), and the measured code value. In tests with the sensor placed on the philtrum, the respiratory temperature swings measured °C, demonstrating adequate sensitivity to both inhalation and exhalation. The combined effort-and‐airflow measurement system is designed for ultra‐low power, consuming approximately 1.05 mW, making it suitable for prolonged monitoring such as overnight sleep studies. The respiratory effort channel tracks capacitance variations due to mid‑abdomen displacement. Since the sensor response is maximal at the steepest gradient of that motion, experiments were performed to locate the point of greatest sensitivity. The maximum capacitance change was observed at 9 cm above the navel (see Supplementary Information Text S3 and Figure S3b, c). To validate dynamic response, a motorized linear actuator was used to vary the sensor–skin distance periodically; the measured effort waveform matched the imposed displacement profile (Supplementary Information Text S3 and Figure S3d, e). System validation under OSA‐like conditions was then carried out using real subject data (Fig. 3c). In true OSA events, airflow remains unchanged while effort amplitude falls by > 90% for over 10 s. Because it is impractical to suppress effort by 90% in a healthy volunteer, we designed a scenario that reduced baseline breathing amplitude by ≈ 50%. The proposed system successfully detected this simulated apnea, confirming correct operation under pathophysiological conditions.

The validation of the proposed system: airflow and respiratory effort signal while sleeping by subject

To verify the sleep‑apnea differentiation capability and accuracy of the proposed system, a clinical trial was conducted. As in the simulated conditions, the system measured signals occurring during sleep apnea and identified abnormal events using peak‑to‑valley detection algorithms by MATLAB(R2021a, Mathwork, USA). Thirty‑one subjects (23 male, 8 female) were recruited to evaluate the efficacy of the proposed system (Table 1). To assess the system’s performance in distinguishing between sleep apnea and normal breathing, experiments were conducted on both the general population and subjects with suspected sleep apnea. The general population group consisted of individuals who may snore but have no suspected apnea symptoms, while the sleep apnea group included those diagnosed with or suspected of having severe apnea. Table 1 outlines the characteristics of the subjects. The AHI was obtained using the reference device during the clinical trial. All subjects were fitted with the proposed system and the reference device (strain belt, nasal cannula, PPG sensor) before sleep, with simultaneous measurement initiated at sleep onset and terminated upon awakening (Supplementary Information Text S4). Figure 4a shows the overall sleep results for a single subject. The reference device analyzed sleep apnea according to AASM guidelines and reported an AHI of 49, indicating severe apnea. Event times correspond to all apnea and hypopnea periods identified by the reference device. Per AASM criteria, hypopnea is defined by amplitude of signal ≥ 70% reduction in airflow and effort signals plus an SpO2 drop below 90% (from a baseline > 95%), while apnea is defined by the amplitude ≥ 90% reduction in airflow for > 10 s. The ResNasal and ResChest signals measured by the reference device were used to mark these events. The interval ( > 10s) denotes an apnea episode, with brief recovery breaths at t2 before apnea recurs. The proposed system’s nasal airflow and effort signals exhibited similar trends to the reference signals (Supplementary Information Figure S5). Signals from the proposed system were preprocessed to align event timestamps with the reference data. Because abnormal events are embedded in normal breathing, respiration rate and volume were derived from peak-to-peak analysis of airflow and effort waveforms (Supplementary Information Text S5). Inhalation (Insp) and exhalation (Exp) peaks were identified to compute tidal volume, and intervals with volumes below a set threshold were classified as OSA or hypopnea per AASM rules. The event intervals detected by the proposed system closely matched those from ResNasal and ResChest. Correlation analysis yielded for effort and for airflow (Fig. 4b, c). Beyond correlation, performance metrics—sensitivity (SN), specificity (SP), accuracy (Acc), precision, and F₁-score—were calculated to evaluate detection quality (Supplementary Information Text S6)37. Table 2 shows that airflow and effort channels both achieved high specificity, but sensitivity was lower (0.76 for effort, 0.61 for airflow), impacting overall accuracy and F₁-score. Low sensitivity reflects missed events, especially in subjects with frequent apneas. Standard PSG and multi-channel reference devices integrate multiple physiological signals rather than a single metric. Consequently, fusing airflow and effort signals improved detection, achieving SN = 0.84, SP = 0.94, and (Fig. 4d). Figure 5a illustrates the system’s ability to distinguish apnea from normal breathing using an AHI threshold of < 15 events/hour38. The true labels represent measurements from the reference device, while the predicted labels are outputs from the proposed system. The confusion matrix shows 67.7% of cases correctly identified as normal (21/31) and 26.8% correctly identified as apnea (8/31), resulting in high specificity (0.94), perfect precision (0.92), a strong F₁-score (0.89), and overall accuracy of 0.90 (Table 2). The system can also stratify OSA severity (Supplementary Information Text S6). Figure 5b compares severity classifications by the proposed system against those from the reference device39. Both normal and severe groups achieved 100% sensitivity, whereas mild and moderate groups showed lower sensitivities (64% and 50%, respectively), largely due to class imbalance and the AHI = 15 threshold dividing those categories (Table 1). For example, subjects with AHI = 14 and AHI = 16 exhibit similar respiratory patterns but fall into different severity bins. Overall, these results demonstrate that the proposed system can reliably distinguish normal versus apneic breathing and accurately classify OSA severity levels, confirming its clinical applicability.

Discussion

A diverse array of home‑based sleep apnea screening technologies has been developed in recent years; however, many remain reliant on wired systems, thoracic belts, or rigid probes, which compromise both user comfort and signal integrity in Table 3. Peripheral arterial tonometry (PAT)‑based devices, such as the WatchPAT 200 and 300, are approved medical devices that utilize pressure‑enhanced finger photoplethysmography (PPG) to infer respiratory events40,41. While high sensitivity has been reported for obstructive apnea detection, these systems do not provide direct measurements of respiratory effort and require dedicated finger‑mounted sensors. Wearables based solely on PPG, including wrist‑worn and ring‑type devices, have been engineered to reduce bulk but remain vulnerable to motion‑induced artifacts42,43 and peripheral perfusion variability—particularly during hypopnea when signal amplitude is low. Multimodal systems integrating PPG, accelerometry, and acoustic sensing via smartphones have been proposed to enhance detection accuracy, yet their reliability is compromised by dependence on device positioning and ambient noise44. Meanwhile, impedance belts and piezoelectric sensors monitor thoracoabdominal motion directly, but issues such as slippage, constriction, and discomfort during extended overnight wear are frequently reported45.

To address these limitations, we developed a dual‑sensor configuration that integrates two complementary modalities into a fully cable‑free, patch‑based platform. A fringing‑field capacitive sensor was affixed to the abdomen to monitor respiratory effort, while a miniature thermistor‑based probe was positioned near the nares to measure nasal airflow via temperature fluctuations. Using this multimodal approach, we achieved 90.03% classification accuracy, 84.6% sensitivity, 94.4% specificity, and an F₁ score of 0.88 for obstructive apnea and hypopnea differentiation, demonstrating performance comparable or superior to prior uni‑ and multi‑modal systems (Table 3). Compared to PAT + PPG systems, our architecture enables direct respiratory effort measurement without finger sensors. In contrast to PPG‑only wearables, skin‑conformal abdominal patches reduce motion artifacts and eliminate reliance on peripheral perfusion. Unlike smartphone‑assisted systems, our design does not depend on user placement or environmental acoustics. By replacing belts and rigid probes with lightweight, flexible patches, we also improved user comfort, mechanical stability, and long‑term signal fidelity. Nevertheless, certain limitations remain. Differentiation of central and mixed apneas cannot be achieved with effort and airflow alone; future iterations will require integration of SpO2 sensing and sleep‑stage classification. Validation using ApneaLink-Air as a reference standard in a non‑clinical setting—and an imbalance in subject severity—may introduce bias, so large‑scale trials with simultaneous PSG are warranted for clinical generalizability. Medical‑device certification typically demands multi‑channel monitoring (airflow, effort, SpO2, body position), exceeding our dual‑channel prototype. Finally, formal human‑factors evaluations—including SUS/VAS comfort scoring, donning/doffing time, and adherence metrics—will be essential for real‑world usability and regulatory approval. In conclusion, we present a novel, fully wireless, patch‑based platform combining fringing‑field capacitive sensing and temperature‑differential airflow measurement for unobtrusive home screening of sleep‑disordered breathing. Future work will expand sensor modalities, validate against PSG in diverse cohorts, and conduct comprehensive usability studies to support clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Capacitive sensor-based respiratory effort monitoring

The proposed capacitive sensor employs a coplanar electrode configuration, leveraging fringing fields generated by an excitation signal between the electrodes. To improve the sensor’s material detection performance, periodic perturbations were introduced to maximize the fringing fields (Supplementary Information Text S1). The sensor’s electrical properties were evaluated via CST simulation. The sensor detects variations in dielectric properties within the fringing field based on capacitance changes. On-chip temperature sensor for nasal airflow measurement

Humidity and temperature around the nose and mouth rise during exhalation and fall during inhalation with normal respiration. This cyclical variation can be exploited to monitor respiratory patterns. To this end, we integrated a temperature sensor chip based on a PTAT (Proportional to Absolute Temperature) architecture, capable of measuring from − 40 to 100 °C. Sensor performance was characterized in a temperature–humidity chamber (TC3‑ME, Jeio‑tech, South Korea), where its sensitivity to controlled temperature steps was recorded. System for operating wireless system

A CDC methodology was implemented to detect respiration‑induced capacitance changes in the picofarad to femtofarad range, forming the core of a system with both hardware and software components. The hardware integrates a fringing‑field capacitive sensor, a Bluetooth Low Energy module (ANNA‑B112, u‑box) for wireless data transmission, power‑management circuitry (LDL112PVR, STMicroelectronics, Netherlands), a 24‑bit delta‑sigma ADC with high resolution (SB08, SB‑Solutions, Inc., South Korea), and a charging circuit (AW32002, awinic, China). Sensor outputs are digitized by the ADC and relayed via BLE to the software, which handles data acquisition, signal processing, and analysis in real time. Study design

A clinical trial was conducted to evaluate the performance of the proposed system in measuring and distinguishing sleep-apnea signals. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology (UNIST; reference number UNISTIRB-24-006-A). As this approval was obtained outside of a hospital setting, clinical oversight by a medical institution was not involved. Thirty-one subjects (23 male, 8 female) exhibiting a range of sleep-related symptoms—from healthy sleepers to individuals with pronounced snoring or severe sleep apnea—were recruited. Participants were grouped by gender and symptomatology. Each subject was monitored overnight using three devices capable of recording respiratory effort and airflow signals. All measurements were initiated at sleep onset and terminated upon spontaneous awakening.

Experimental environment and protocol

Participants in this study underwent overnight sleep-apnea assessment in exchange for compensation. Each participant was simultaneously fitted with the ApneaLink-Air (ApneaLink, ResMed, USA) as the reference standard and with the proposed dual-sensor system designed to capture respiratory effort and airflow signals. Both systems were affixed using biocompatible medical patches (DuploMed TP-21401, Lohmann-tapes, Germany) to ensure stable placement on the mid-abdomen and cheek. Data were recorded continuously via a dedicated mobile application. In total, 31 subjects wore both the proposed system and the reference device for periods of 2–8 h in a controlled laboratory setting to evaluate sleep-disordered breathing. Data processing and classification

The average tidal volume is calculated using the peak-to-peak measurement method when determining apnea and hypopnea46–48. If the tidal volume decreases beyond a certain threshold compared to the standard, it is identified as an abnormal breathing interval according to the AASM guidelines. Using the ApneaLink-Air and the proposed system, the breathing patterns of subjects during sleep were measured. Normal breathing periods and OSA symptom intervals were distinguished. The severity is categorized as Normal, Mild, Moderate, and Severe based on the AHI.
