A Unified Framework for Locality in Scalable MARL
Sourav Chakraborty, Amit Kiran Rege, Claire Monteleoni, Lijun Chen

Abstract
Scalable Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) is fundamentally challenged by the curse of dimensionality. A common solution is to exploit locality, which hinges on an Exponential Decay Property (EDP) of the value function. However, existing conditions that guarantee the EDP are often conservative, as they are based on worst-case, environment-only bounds (e.g., supremums over actions) and fail to capture the regularizing effect of the policy itself. In this work, we establish that locality can also be a policy-dependent phenomenon. Our central contribution is a novel decomposition of the policy-induced interdependence matrix, H π H^{\pi} , which decouples the environment’s sensitivity to state ( E s E^{\mathrm{s}} ) and action ( E a E^{\mathrm{a}} ) from the policy’s sensitivity to state ( Π ⁡ ( π ) \Pi(\pi) ). This decomposition reveals that locality can be induced by a smooth policy (small Π ⁡ ( π ) \Pi(\pi) ) even when the environment is strongly action-coupled, exposing a fundamental locality-optimality tradeoff. We use this framework to derive a general spectral condition ρ ⁡ ( E s + E a ​ Π ​ ( π ) ) < 1 \rho(E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi))<1 for exponential decay, which is strictly tighter than prior norm-based conditions. Finally, we leverage this theory to analyze a provably-sound localized block-coordinate policy improvement framework with guarantees tied directly to this spectral radius.

1
Introduction

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) is a powerful paradigm for controlling complex, decentralized systems. However, its application is fundamentally challenged by the curse of dimensionality: the joint state and action spaces (
\mathcal{S}=\prod_{i}\mathcal{S}_{i}
,
\mathcal{A}=\prod_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}
) grow exponentially with the number of agents,
n
. This problem places MARL in the category of “succinctly described” but intractable MDPs
(
1
;
14
)
, rendering standard, centralized learning algorithms computationally infeasible.

A highly effective approach to overcome this barrier is to exploit the local interaction structure inherent in many networked systems. This line of work, often termed Networked MARL
(
15
;
17
;
7
)
, assumes agents are situated on a graph and that their local transitions and rewards depend only on a local neighborhood. These foundational papers show that such systems often exhibit an Exponential Decay Property (EDP), where the influence of distant agents on an agent’s value function decays exponentially with graph distance. This property is the theoretical bedrock that justifies truncating computations to a small
\kappa
-hop neighborhood, enabling scalable algorithms whose complexity depends on the neighborhood size, not the full network.

A critical question, however, is when this EDP holds. In the discounted setting, one gets EDP for “free” due to a
\gamma<1
multiplicative factor. In the more challenging average-reward (
\gamma=1
) setting, prior work has established the EDP by proving a Dobrushin-style weak coupling condition
(
15
)
. This condition, however, is policy-independent - it relies on a worst-case (action-supremum) bound on the environment’s influence. This bound is inherently conservative. It can fail to certify locality in a system where the environment is strongly action-coupled, even if the agents’ policies are “smooth” (e.g., highly exploratory) and effectively break this strong coupling.

This paper’s central thesis is that locality is best viewed depending on both the environment and the policy. Our main contribution is a new theoretical framework that formally decouples the sources of influence. We introduce a novel decomposition of the one-step interdependence matrix,
C^{\pi}
, that separates the environment’s “physics” from the policy’s “logic”:

C^{\pi}\preceq E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi)

Here,
E^{\mathrm{s}}
and
E^{\mathrm{a}}
measure the environment’s sensitivity to changes in state and action, respectively, while
\Pi(\pi)
measures the policy’s sensitivity to changes in state (Proposition
1
). This decomposition reveals that a smooth policy (a small
\Pi(\pi)
) can induce locality even in an environment with strong action-based coupling (a large
E^{\mathrm{a}}
).

This framework allows us to derive a new, more general condition for locality: the system exhibits exponential decay if the spectral radius
\rho(H^{\pi})<1
(Theorem
3
). This is strictly tighter than prior norm-based, policy-independent tests. We connect this theory to practice by showing that for standard entropy-regularized policies
(
3
)
, the temperature
\tau
serves as a direct, practical knob to control
\Pi(\pi)
and thus manage the system’s locality (Lemma
10
). This exposes a fundamental locality-optimality tradeoff.

Finally, we leverage this theory to analyze a deterministic, oracle-based policy improvement framework (Section
5
). By decoupling the analysis of spatial approximation from the complex analysis of stochastic sampling, we provide a clean, provably-sound justification for localized algorithms. We prove that the error of our localized framework is bounded deterministically by the tail of a Neumann series governed by
\rho(H^{\pi})
, completing the link from our fundamental theory to a provable algorithmic guarantee. We decsribe additional related work in Appendix
A
.

2
Setup and preliminaries

We consider
n
agents with local state spaces
\mathcal{S}_{i}
and local action spaces
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, both finite and nonempty. The joint state and action spaces are
\mathcal{S}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{S}_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{i}
. At each discrete time
t
, the system is in state
S_{t}=(S_{t}^{(1)},\dots,S_{t}^{(n)})\in\mathcal{S}
. We denote a state excluding an agent
i
by
s_{-i}
meaning
s=(s_{i},s_{-i})
. Under a policy
\pi
, a joint action
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
is sampled from
\pi(\cdot\,|\,S_{t})
.

The next state
S_{t+1}
is drawn from a transition kernel
P(\cdot\,|\,S_{t},A_{t})
that factorizes
P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}P_{j}(s^{\prime}_{j}\mid s,a)
, where
P_{j}(\cdot\,|\,s,a)
is a probability distribution on
\mathcal{S}_{j}
. This is a general formulation that, notably, allows the next state of agent
j
,
s^{\prime}_{j}
, to depend on the entire global state
s
and the entire joint action
a
. This setup is more general than prior work on theoretical networked MARL (e.g.,
(
16
;
9
)
), which often assume a priori structural locality. For instance, prior models restrict the kernel to
P_{j}(s^{\prime}_{j}\mid s_{N_{j}},a_{j})
, thereby assuming that
j
’s next state is affected only by states of agents in its neighborhood on a network.

We adopt this more general model precisely because our goal is to derive locality from first principles, not to assume it. We do not impose a graph on the environment’s dynamics. Instead, our analysis will be phrased in terms of operator bounds to show that locality - and the exponential decay property it implies - is an emergent property of the system. This allows us to recover prior networked models as a special case (i.e., where the interaction matrices are sparse), while also revealing new, policy-dependent sources of locality.

A policy is of product form. For each agent
k
, there is a stochastic map
\pi_{k}(\cdot\,|\,s)
on
\mathcal{A}_{k}
and the global policy
\pi(a\,|\,s)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\,|\,s)
. If the policy depends on a subset of state coordinates (instead of global state
s
), we specify the subset using observation scopes
O_{k}\subseteq[n]
such that
\pi(a\mid s)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s_{O_{k}})
. When
O_{k}=[n]
, we note that
s=s_{O_{k}}
. In some examples we will consider localized observation scopes, but the general theory does not require this.

The policy–induced state transition kernel is

P^{\pi}(s^{\prime}\mid s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}P_{j}(s^{\prime}_{j}\mid s,a)\Big)\Big(\prod_{k=1}^{n}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s_{O_{k}})\Big).

We write
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\,\cdot\,|\,S_{t}=s]
for expectation with respect to one step of this kernel. We work in the more challenging average reward setting and prove guarantees for the discounted setting as a corollary in the Appendix.

We measure sensitivity using coordinatewise oscillations. For a bounded function
f:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, the
i
–oscillation is

\delta_{i}(f)=\sup_{x,y\in\mathcal{S},\ x_{-i}=y_{-i}}|f(x)-f(y)|.

The vector
\delta(f)=(\delta_{1}(f),\dots,\delta_{n}(f))
will be used to track how changes in individual coordinates propagate through linear operators and use the shorthand
\|\delta(f)\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}\delta_{i}(f)
. This seminorm - often used in Dobrushin-type arguments for interacting particle systems and Glauber dynamics
(
2
;
11
)
- has a simple interpretation: it is the Lipschitz constant of
f
with respect to Hamming changes at a single coordinate. (See
E.3
for details)

We consider three families of sensitivities. The first measures how much changing one coordinate of the current state can change the next–state marginal of a given agent when the action is held fixed. For
j,i\in[n]
, we denote the total variation distance between two probability measures using
TV(\cdot,\cdot)
and define

E^{\mathrm{s}}_{j\leftarrow i}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}:s_{-i}=s^{\prime}_{-i}\\
a\in\mathcal{A}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{TV}\Big(P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a),P_{j}(\cdot\mid s^{\prime},a)\Big)

The second measures how much changing one agent’s action can change the next–state marginal of another agent when the state is held fixed. For
j,k\in[n]
define

E^{\mathrm{a}}_{j\leftarrow k}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in\mathcal{S}\\
a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}:a_{-k}=a^{\prime}_{-k}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{TV}\Big(P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a),P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a^{\prime})\Big)

The third measures how sensitive each agent’s local action distribution is to a change in one coordinate of the state. For
k,i\in[n]
define

\Pi_{k\leftarrow i}(\pi)=\sup_{s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}:s_{-i}=s^{\prime}_{-i}}\mathrm{TV}\Big(\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid s_{O_{k}}),\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid s^{\prime}_{O_{k}})\Big)

The
n\times n
matrices
E^{\mathrm{s}}
and
E^{\mathrm{a}}
are properties of the environment. The
n\times n
matrix
\Pi(\pi)
depends on the policy. We will sometimes suppress the policy in the notation when there is no ambiguity. Note that if agent
k
does not observe
i
then the corresponding entry of
\Pi
is zero.

The policy-induced one-step marginal influence of
i
on the next–state marginal of agent
j
is

C^{\pi}_{j\leftarrow i}=\sup_{s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}:s_{-i}=s^{\prime}_{-i}}\mathrm{TV}\big(P^{\pi}_{j}(\cdot\mid s),P^{\pi}_{j}(\cdot\mid s^{\prime})\big),\quad P^{\pi}_{j}(\cdot\mid s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a)\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s_{O_{k}}).

The matrix
C^{\pi}=[C^{\pi}_{j\leftarrow i}]
is the policy–induced interdependence matrix. It governs how one step of the kernel maps oscillations of functions.

This policy-dependent definition is a critical departure from prior approaches. A common alternative for measuring influence is to define a single, policy-independent matrix that captures the worst-case coupling of the environment. This is typically done by taking a supremum over all joint actions, rather than an expectation (or “mixture”) over the policy
\pi(\cdot\mid s)
. While this action-supremum approach has the advantage of being a fixed property of the environment, it is fundamentally conservative. It conflates the “physics” of the environment with the “logic” of the policy.

Consider an environment where the next state
P_{j}
is extremely sensitive to a neighbor’s action
a_{k}
. An action-supremum bound would conclude the system is tightly coupled (i.e., not local), even if the agent’s policy
\pi_{k}
is “dumb” and never chooses that action, or is completely insensitive to the state
s_{i}
. By defining
C^{\pi}
as an expectation over the policy, our definition correctly measures the actual one-step influence of the closed-loop system (environment + policy). This precision is what allows us to decouple these effects, which is the subject of the next section.

3
Policy–induced influence and locality

In this section, we introduce the policy-induced influence operator and show it leads to exponential decay. Proofs are deferred to the appendix.

The first result is a decomposition of the policy–induced interdependence. This inequality formalizes the informal reasoning from the introduction. A perturbation at
i
propagates to the next state of coordinate
j
along two one-step routes. The direct route is through the environment: even if the action is fixed, the conditional update
P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a)
may change when
s_{i}
changes. The indirect route goes through the policy and back into the environment: changing
s_{i}
changes the distribution of each local action
a_{k}
, and changing
a_{k}
may alter the next-state distribution of
S^{\prime}_{j}
. The matrices
E^{\mathrm{s}},E^{\mathrm{a}},\Pi(\pi)
quantify these two routes. The policy-induced interdependence matrix
C^{\pi}
aggregates their net effect. The next proposition makes this decomposition precise.

Proposition 1

(Decomposition of policy–induced influence)
.

For any policy
\pi
of product form and any synchronous dynamics
P(\cdot\mid s,a)
on a finite state space, the interdependence matrix
C^{\pi}
of the policy–induced kernel satisfies the entrywise bound
C^{\pi}\ \preceq\ E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\,\Pi
. In particular, for each pair of indices
i,j
,
C^{\pi}_{j\leftarrow i}\ \leq\ E^{\mathrm{s}}_{j\leftarrow i}\ +\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}E^{\mathrm{a}}_{j\leftarrow k}\,\Pi_{k\leftarrow i}(\pi)
.

Informally, the proof separates the two routes of influence using the triangle inequality. A detailed construction using a coupling argument is provided in Appendix
B.1
.

To build intuition, we discuss a quantitative example before moving ahead. Let
n=2
agents have binary states and actions,
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{0,1\}
. Suppose agent 1 is unaffected by anything (
P_{1}
is constant). Agent 2’s next state is
deterministically
set by agent 1’s action, regardless of state:
P_{2}(s^{\prime}_{2}=1\mid s,a)=\mathbf{1}\{a_{1}=1\}
.
This environment has maximal action-based coupling. The sensitivities are:

•

E^{\mathrm{s}}=0
(state
s
has no direct effect on the next state).

•

E^{\mathrm{a}}_{2\leftarrow 1}=1
(flipping
a_{1}
from 0 to 1 flips the next state
s^{\prime}_{2}
from 0 to 1, a
\mathrm{TV}
distance of 1). All other
E^{\mathrm{a}}
entries are 0.

An “action-supremum” analysis (from prior work) would stop here. It sees
E^{\mathrm{a}}_{2\leftarrow 1}=1
and concludes the system is globally coupled, as the worst-case influence is 1.

Now, consider a policy
\pi
where agent 1 is “sleepy” and barely reacts to its state,
s_{1}
. Let
\pi_{1}(\cdot\mid s_{1})
change by a small amount
\alpha
(in
\mathrm{TV}
distance) when
s_{1}
flips. Assume
\pi_{1}
doesn’t observe
s_{2}
, so
\Pi_{1\leftarrow 2}=0
.

\Pi_{1\leftarrow 1}(\pi)=\sup_{s_{-1}=s^{\prime}_{-1}}\mathrm{TV}(\pi_{1}(\cdot\mid s),\pi_{1}(\cdot\mid s^{\prime}))=\alpha.

We can now compute a bound on the true policy-induced influence
C^{\pi}_{2\leftarrow 1}
(measuring how a change in
s_{1}
affects
s^{\prime}_{2}
):

C^{\pi}_{2\leftarrow 1}\leq E^{\mathrm{s}}_{2\leftarrow 1}+\sum_{k=1}^{2}E^{\mathrm{a}}_{2\leftarrow k}\,\Pi_{k\leftarrow 1}(\pi)=0+(E^{\mathrm{a}}_{2\leftarrow 1}\Pi_{1\leftarrow 1}+E^{\mathrm{a}}_{2\leftarrow 2}\Pi_{2\leftarrow 1})=(1\cdot\alpha)+(0\cdot 0)=\alpha.

Thus, if the policy is insensitive to the state (small
\alpha
), our bound certifies that the system is only weakly coupled, even when the environment’s “action channel”
E^{\mathrm{a}}
is not. Our bound also captures the opposite failure mode, where smoothing the policy is ineffective (See Appendix
E.1
).

The second result states that a bound on the interdependence matrix controls how one step of the synchronous kernel acts on coordinatewise oscillations. This is the synchronous analogue of classical Dobrushin–type bounds.

Lemma 2

(Oscillation bound via
H^{\pi}
)
.

Let
T^{\pi}f(s)=\mathbb{E}[f(S_{t+1})\mid S_{t}=s]
under the synchronous update
with product-form policy
\pi(a\mid s)=\prod_{k}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s_{O_{k}})
and factorized kernel

P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)=\prod_{j}P_{j}(s^{\prime}_{j}\mid s,a)
.

For
i\in[n]
and bounded
f:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\delta_{i}(T^{\pi}f)\ \leq\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}H^{\pi}_{j\leftarrow i}\,\delta_{j}(f)
where
H^{\pi}\ \coloneqq\ E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi)
. Equivalently, in vector form, we write
\delta(T^{\pi}f)\ \leq\ \big(H^{\pi}\big)^{\!\top}\,\delta(f)
.

The next theorem turns one–step bounds into global decay. It gives a spectral condition on the matrix
H^{\pi}=E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi)
that, if satisfied uniformly over a policy class, ensures contraction and average–reward solvability. This solvability is centered on the average-reward Poisson equation, which is the Bellman equation for this setting. This equation helps us find a relative value function (or differential value function),
h^{\pi}
, which represents the long-term, transient advantage of starting in a particular state relative to the long-term average reward
\bar{r}^{\pi}
. The equation is given by:

h^{\pi}(s)\ =\ \big(r^{\pi}(s)-\bar{r}^{\pi}\big)\ +\ \mathbb{E}[h^{\pi}(S_{t+1})\mid S_{t}=s],

or, written in operator form,
h^{\pi}-T^{\pi}h^{\pi}=r^{\pi}-\bar{r}^{\pi}
.

Our Poisson decay result in the following theorem shows that if the system is well-behaved (i.e.,
\rho(H^{\pi})<1
), then the solution
h^{\pi}
to this equation inherits the locality of the reward. Specifically, the sensitivity of the value function at one coordinate,
\delta_{i}(h^{\pi})
, is bounded by the (decayed) sensitivities of the reward function,
\delta(r^{\pi})
.

Theorem 3

(Policy–uniform synchronous contraction and Poisson decay)
.

Suppose there is a policy class
\Pi_{\mathrm{pol}}
such that for some
\lambda\in[0,1)
,
\rho\big(E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi)\big)\ \leq\ \lambda
for all
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{pol}}
.

Then for all
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{pol}}
and all bounded
f
,
\delta\big((T^{\pi})^{t}f\big)\ \leq\ \big((E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi))^{\top}\big)^{t}\,\delta(f)
.

In particular, there is
C\geq 1
independent of
t
and
\pi
such that
\|\delta((T^{\pi})^{t}f)\|_{\infty}\leq C\lambda^{t}\|\delta(f)\|_{\infty}
for all
t\geq 0
. The average–reward Poisson equation

h^{\pi}-T^{\pi}h^{\pi}\ =\ r^{\pi}-\bar{r}^{\pi}\qquad\text{with }r^{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a}r(s,a)\prod_{k}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s),

has a solution unique up to an additive constant, and

\delta(h^{\pi})\ \leq\ \big(I-(E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi))^{\top}\big)^{-1}\,\delta(r^{\pi}).

This theorem is geometry–free in the sense that it provides a global contraction condition,
\rho(H^{\pi})<1
, without assuming any underlying network structure. The connection to spatial locality becomes explicit when we assume
H^{\pi}
is sparse. If we assume the system has an interaction graph
G
such that
E^{\mathrm{s}}
,
E^{\mathrm{a}}
, and
\Pi(\pi)
are all sparse with respect to
G
, then
H^{\pi}
is also sparse. In this case, the matrix power
\left((H^{\pi})^{\top}\right)^{t}
has non-zero entries
(i,j)
only if there is a path of length at most
t
from
j
to
i
in
G
.

This directly implies the exponential decay property:

•

The solution to the Poisson equation,
\delta(h^{\pi})
, is bounded by the Neumann series
\sum_{t}\left((H^{\pi})^{\top}\right)^{t}\delta(r^{\pi})
.

•

The influence of an agent
j
on
i
is a sum over paths, which decays at the spectral rate
\lambda^{t}
with path length
t
.

•

Therefore, the
\kappa
-local truncation error - the error from ignoring agents more than
\kappa
hops away - is bounded by the tail of this series, which decays exponentially in
\kappa
.

This framework recovers and generalizes the locality results in prior work
(
16
;
9
)
. Those analyses start with a graph
G
and propose a sufficient condition for locality based on a policy-independent, worst-case (action-supremum) matrix
C
, such as requiring
\|C\|_{\infty}<1
(details in Appendix
B.4
).

Our result reveals this to be a conservative special case. Their norm-based condition (
\|C\|_{\infty}<1
) is simply one way to certify that our more fundamental spectral radius condition (
\rho(H^{\pi})<1
) holds. Our analysis is sharper, as it correctly identifies that locality can be policy-induced (via a small
\Pi(\pi)
) even when the environment’s worst-case coupling (a large
E^{a}
) is strong.

We briefly discuss alternatives to our choice of definition. One could take a supremum over actions in the definition of policy–induced influence. This means that the matrix is now policy independent but ignores the actual policy–state dependence and is too conservative whenever action coupling is strong but the policy changes slowly with the state (as discussed in Section
2
). One could also attempt to analyze the average–reward Poisson equation starting from discounted problems and letting the discount tend to one. This route hides the role of the environment and the policy in the contraction and yields weaker constants in problems where discounting is not a modeling choice. The direct contraction analysis is both sharper and closer to the algorithms we ultimately care about.

Our framework’s core finding that locality is governed by the spectral radius of
H^{\pi}
is robust. As we demonstrate in Appendix
B.6
, the same analysis extends directly to the asynchronous update model (e.g., Glauber dynamics), where only one agent updates at a time step.

Extension: Discounted setting

We have focused on the average-reward setting (
\gamma=1
), as this is the most challenging case where no ”automatic” contraction is given.Our entire framework, however, applies directly to the standard
\gamma
-discounted setting. In that case, the one-step Bellman operator
T^{\pi}_{\gamma}
contracts oscillations by
\gamma(H^{\pi})^{\top}
. The governing spectral radius is therefore
\lambda=\gamma\cdot\rho(H^{\pi})
.This is a strictly tighter bound than the standard
\lambda=\gamma
used in most discounted analyses. Our framework thus shows that the true decay rate is a product of the ”trivial” temporal decay (
\gamma
) and the ”structural” decay (
\rho(H^{\pi})
) we have characterized.This confirms that our analysis is not just a special case for average-reward, but a more general theory of locality that sharpens the bounds for the discounted setting as well.

Let’s use an example: Assume
\gamma=0.99
(a very patient agent, so the default bound is weak). Assume our system is very weakly coupled, so
\rho(H^{\pi})=0.5
. Prior work would say to get an error of 0.01, we need
0.99^{\kappa}\leq 0.01
, so
\kappa\approx 458
. They would require a 458-hop neighborhood. Our work would say, “The true decay rate is
\lambda=0.99\times 0.5=0.495
. To get an error of 0.01, we need
0.495^{\kappa}\leq 0.01
, so
\kappa\approx 7
. Thus, we say “We can certify the algorithm with a 7-hop neighborhood, while the standard analysis would (incorrectly) demand 458 hops.”

4
The Algorithmic Link: Controlling Interdependence

Our analysis so far has revealed a fundamental, geometry-free condition for locality: the system is well-behaved if the spectral radius of the policy-induced influence matrix is less than one. This raises the question: how can an algorithm use this insight to keep the system well-behaved (local)?

The environment’s “physics” matrices,
E^{\mathrm{s}}
(state-based influence) and
E^{\mathrm{a}}
(action-based influence), are fixed. The only knob an algorithm can turn is the policy sensitivity matrix,
\Pi(\pi)
. If we can control
\Pi(\pi)
, we can
induce
locality even when
E^{\mathrm{a}}
is large. All proofs are in Appendix
C
.

We now formally introduce the agent’s objective. We consider the standard entropy-regularized average reward objective. The goal is to find a policy
\pi
that maximizes:

J_{\tau}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi},a\sim\pi(\cdot|s)}\left[r(s,a)-\tau\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s_{O_{k}})\right]

Here,
d^{\pi}
is the stationary distribution of the system under
\pi
,
r(s,a)
is the global reward, and
\tau>0
is the temperature parameter, which weighs the importance of maximizing reward against maximizing policy entropy (i.e., exploration).

The well-known solution to this problem is the softmax policy
(
3
;
5
)
. The policy for each agent
k
is parameterized by a logit function
g_{k}
, which in practice will be our estimate of the local Q-function or advantage function.

Definition 4

(Softmax Policy)
.

A policy
\pi
is a temperature-
\tau
softmax policy if for each agent
k
, there is a logit function
g_{k}:\mathcal{S}_{O_{k}}\times\mathcal{A}_{k}\to\mathbb{R}
such that:
\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s_{O_{k}})\propto\exp\big(g_{k}(s_{O_{k}},a_{k})/\tau\big)

This policy class gives us a controllable knob: temperature
\tau
mediates the policy’s randomness.

Intuitively, a “random” (high
\tau
) policy should be less sensitive to small changes in its state input. If all actions are almost equally likely, why care if the state changes slightly? The following lemma makes this intuition precise. We first define the “logit Lipschitz constant,”
L_{k\leftarrow i}
, which measures the maximum sensitivity of agent
k
’s logits to a change in agent
i
’s state.

Definition 5

(Logit Lipschitz Constant)
.

For a logit function
g_{k}(s_{O_{k}},\cdot)
, the one-coordinate logit Lipschitz constant with respect to state
i
is:
L_{k\leftarrow i}\coloneqq\sup_{s_{-i}=s^{\prime}_{-i}}\big\|g_{k}(s_{O_{k}},\cdot)-g_{k}(s^{\prime}_{O_{k}},\cdot)\big\|_{\infty}
where the supremum is over all states
s,s^{\prime}
that differ only on coordinate
i
.

This
L_{k\leftarrow i}
term is an intrinsic property of the logit function
g_{k}
. The next lemma, proved in Appendix
C
, connects this to the policy sensitivity
\Pi(\pi)
via the temperature
\tau
.

Lemma 6

(Softmax Temperature Controls
\Pi(\pi)
)
.

For a temperature-
\tau
softmax policy, the policy sensitivity matrix
\Pi(\pi)
is bounded for all
k,i\in[n]
by:
\Pi_{k\leftarrow i}(\pi)\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{L_{k\leftarrow i}}{2\tau}\right\}

This lemma provides the link between theory and algorithm design by giving an upper bound on
\rho(H^{\pi})
. Let
L=[L_{k\leftarrow i}]_{k,i}
. Since
H^{\pi}=E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi)
and all matrices are nonnegative,

H^{\pi}\ \preceq\ E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\,\frac{L}{2\tau}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\rho(H^{\pi})\ \leq\ \rho\!\left(E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\,\frac{L}{2\tau}\right).

The spectral monotonicity follows from Perron–Frobenius. Hence, we have essentially shown the abstract problem of controlling
\Pi(\pi)
to be equivalent to tuning
\tau
for the entropy-regularized objective. This reveals a locality-optimality tradeoff:

•

We can simply make
\tau
large. As
\tau\to\infty
, we have
\Pi(\pi)\to 0
, which in turn means
H^{\pi}\to E^{\mathrm{s}}
. If we assume the
E^{\mathrm{s}}
is stable (i.e.,
\rho(E^{\mathrm{s}})<1
), then a high-entropy policy guarantees locality. This justifies using a small, computationally cheap truncation radius
\kappa
.

•

Letting
\tau\to 0
allows the policy to become deterministic and greedy. The policy becomes sensitive to its inputs, which can cause the entire system to become globally coupled (
\rho(H^{\pi})\geq 1
), breaking the exponential decay property.

A practical, localized algorithm can start with a high
\tau
(system will be local; can run cheap algorithms on
\kappa=1
) and then anneal
\tau
towards zero, potentially needing to increase
\kappa
as the policy becomes more optimized and the system’s coupling increases. See Appendix
E.2
for a quantitative example.

5
A Provable Framework for Localized Policy Improvement

We now integrate the results developed so far into a complete framework for
localized policy improvement
. The starting point is conceptual rather than algorithmic. Most analyses of multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL)—especially stochastic ones such as actor–critic—intertwine spatial truncation error (from limiting attention to local neighborhoods) with stochastic approximation error (from finite samples and noisy updates). This coupling makes it difficult to tell whether a poor bound arises from the algorithm or from the structure of the environment.
We therefore take a step back and study an
oracle model
in which all expectations under
\pi
(i.e.,
r^{\pi}
,
H^{\pi}
) are known exactly. This idealization isolates the purely structural aspect of locality. Within this setting, we can quantify precisely how the truncation radius
\kappa
, the decay rate
\lambda
, and the policy smoothness (via
\tau
) interact.

For each policy
\pi
, we associate a directed
support graph

G^{\pi}
on vertices
[n]
by drawing an edge
i\!\to\!j
whenever
H^{\pi}_{j\leftarrow i}>0
. This graph captures which coordinates can directly influence each other in one step of the policy–environment dynamics. When
H^{\pi}
is sparse—e.g., due to limited observation scopes or weak couplings—this graph has small degree, and a
\kappa
-hop neighborhood covers only a small subset of agents. All our algorithms will exploit this structure to compute local approximations by message passing on
G^{\pi}
.

Phase 1: Localized evaluation via message passing

The first phase aims to approximate the (generally global) Poisson solution
h^{\pi}
, which encodes the long-term value landscape of the policy. The challenge is that
h^{\pi}
depends on all agents through the transition kernel. Our approach constructs a
localized surrogate

\widehat{h}_{\kappa}^{\pi}
that agrees with
h^{\pi}
up to an exponentially small bias outside a
\kappa
-hop neighborhood, and provides an explicit certificate for its accuracy.

Step 1: computing a locality certificate.

Recall from Theorem
3
that the oscillation vector
\delta(h^{\pi})
can be written as a Neumann series in powers of
(H^{\pi})^{\top}
applied to
b^{\pi}=\delta(r^{\pi})
. Truncating this series at depth
\kappa
yields the computable
certificate

\widehat{\delta}^{(\kappa)}\ \coloneqq\ \sum_{t=0}^{\kappa}\!\big((H^{\pi})^{\top}\big)^{t}\,b^{\pi}.

Intuitively,
\widehat{\delta}^{(\kappa)}_{i}
measures how far the effect of a reward perturbation can propagate to coordinate
i
within
\kappa
steps. Since
(H^{\pi})^{\top}
has nonzeros only along edges of
G^{\pi}
, this truncated sum can be computed by
\kappa
rounds of local message passing on
G^{\pi}
.

Step 2: constructing a surrogate value.

Using this certificate, we define a truncated Poisson approximation

\widehat{h}_{\kappa}^{\pi}\ =\ \sum_{t=0}^{\kappa}(T^{\pi})^{t}(r^{\pi}-\bar{r}^{\pi})\ -\ c_{\kappa},

where
c_{\kappa}
is an arbitrary constant (the value function is defined up to shift). The surrogate satisfies
\delta(\widehat{h}_{\kappa}^{\pi})\leq\widehat{\delta}^{(\kappa)}
by construction.

Theorem 7

(Localized evaluation: certificate and bias)
.

Let
\pi
be a product policy with
H^{\pi}=(E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi))^{\top}
and assume
\|(H^{\pi})^{t}\|_{\infty\to\infty}\leq C\lambda^{t}
for some
\lambda<1
. Then:

1.

Certificate.

The true sensitivity satisfies

\delta(h^{\pi})\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(H^{\pi})^{t}b^{\pi}
,
and the truncated certificate
\widehat{\delta}^{(\kappa)}
gives a uniform bound

\|\delta(h^{\pi})-\widehat{\delta}^{(\kappa)}\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ \tfrac{C}{1-\lambda}\lambda^{\kappa+1}\|b^{\pi}\|_{\infty}.

2.

Locality.

Each component
\widehat{\delta}^{(\kappa)}_{i}
depends only on nodes within
\mathrm{Ball}_{\kappa}(i)
in
G^{\pi}
and can be computed by
\kappa
message-passing rounds.

3.

Bias.

The corresponding truncated Poisson solution satisfies

\|\widehat{h}_{\kappa}^{\pi}-h^{\pi}\|_{\infty}\leq\tfrac{C}{2(1-\lambda)}\lambda^{\kappa+1}\|\delta(r^{\pi})\|_{1}
.

Hence, increasing
\kappa
expands the radius of communication, while increasing
\lambda
(tighter coupling) slows exponential decay. Together they control the bias–cost tradeoff in local evaluation.

Given
\widehat{h}_{\kappa}^{\pi}
, the
localized surrogate advantage

\widehat{A}_{\kappa}^{\pi}(s,a)\ =\ r(s,a)-\bar{r}^{\pi}+\sum_{y}P(y\mid s,a)\widehat{h}_{\kappa}^{\pi}(y)-\widehat{h}_{\kappa}^{\pi}(s)

acts as a locally computable substitute for
A^{\pi}
.

Phase 2: Localized policy improvement via block–coordinate KL updates

The second phase performs policy improvement.
Updating all agents simultaneously can cause instability because each agent’s update changes the stationary distribution
d^{\pi}
that the others depend on.
To ensure monotonic progress, we adopt a
block–coordinate
approach, updating one agent at a time while keeping others fixed.

For a given agent
k
, define the expected local advantage logits

g_{k}^{\pi}(s,a_{k})\ =\ \E_{a_{-k}\sim\prod_{j\neq k}\pi_{j}(\cdot\mid s_{O_{j}})}\big[\widehat{A}_{\kappa}^{\pi}(s,(a_{k},a_{-k}))\big].

We then perform a
KL–proximal
update around the current policy:

\pi_{k}^{\mathrm{new}}(\cdot\mid s_{O_{k}})\ \coloneqq\ \arg\max_{\mu\in\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{k})}\big\{\langle\mu,g_{k}^{\pi}(s,\cdot)\rangle-\tau\,\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid s_{O_{k}}))\big\}.

(1)

This has the closed-form solution

\pi_{k}^{\mathrm{new}}(\cdot\mid s_{O_{k}})\propto\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid s_{O_{k}})\,\exp(g_{k}^{\pi}(s,\cdot)/\tau)
,
which preserves the product structure and provides an interpretable temperature-controlled step size.

Appendix A
Related work

Our work addresses the curse of dimensionality in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL). The challenge is that the global state and action spaces (
\mathcal{S}=\prod_{i}\mathcal{S}_{i}
,
\mathcal{A}=\prod_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}
) grow exponentially with the number of agents,
n
. This problem falls into the category of “succinctly described” MDPs
(
1
)
, which are known to be computationally intractable in the general case, even with structural assumptions
(
14
)
.

To achieve scalability, researchers have explored various structural assumptions.
One common approach in general MARL is to use function approximation
(
19
;
10
)
or assume independent learners
(
18
)
, though the latter can suffer from non-stationarity
(
12
)
. Other related areas include
Factored MDPs

(
6
;
4
)
, which assume local states but typically a global action, and
Weakly Coupled MDPs

(
13
)
, which assume agents’ transitions are independent and coupling only occurs through the reward. Our work differs from these by focusing on systems with local, coupled transitions, which is common in networked systems.

This paper is most related to the line of work on scalable Networked MARL
(
15
;
17
;
8
)
. These foundational papers were the first to show that if the system’s local transitions and rewards depend only on a local graph neighborhood, the system exhibits an Exponential Decay Property (EDP). This property, where influence decays exponentially with graph distance, justifies
\kappa
-hop truncation and enables scalable algorithms.

A critical distinction lies in how this EDP is proven. In the challenging average-reward (
\gamma=1
) setting, these works establish the EDP by proving a Dobrushin-style weak coupling condition*
(
15
)
. This condition is policy-independent, it relies on a worst-case (action-supremum) bound on the environment’s influence, requiring
\|C\|_{\infty}<1
.

This paper builds directly on that foundation, but identifies that a policy-independent bound is inherently conservative. It can fail to certify locality in systems where the environment’s action-based coupling (
E^{\mathrm{a}}
) is strong, even if the policy itself is smooth and breaks this coupling.

Our central contribution is a more general and fundamental characterization of locality.

Proofs from Section

This appendix contains complete proofs of the results in the main text and an extension to asynchronous updates. All total variation distances are normalized: for probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on a finite set,
\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\nu)=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{x}|\mu(x)-\nu(x)|
. For a bounded function
f
on a product space, the coordinatewise oscillations are
\delta_{i}(f)=\sup_{x,y\in\mathcal{S},\ x_{-i}=y_{-i}}|f(x)-f(y)|
and
\delta(f)

\delta_{i}(f)
.

B.1
Proof of Proposition
1

Fix
i,j
and two states
s,s^{\prime}
that agree off coordinate
i
. Write

\mu_{j}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big(\prod_{k}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s)\Big)P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a),\qquad\nu_{j}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big(\prod_{k}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s^{\prime})\Big)P_{j}(\cdot\mid s^{\prime},a).

We claim

\mathrm{TV}(\mu_{j},\nu_{j})\ \leq\ E^{\mathrm{s}}_{j\leftarrow i}\ +\ \sum_{k}E^{\mathrm{a}}_{j\leftarrow k}\,\Pi_{k\leftarrow i}(\pi).

The decomposition uses the triangle inequality and a coupling argument.

Define an intermediate distribution

\tilde{\mu}_{j}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big(\prod_{k}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s^{\prime})\Big)P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a).

By the triangle inequality,

\mathrm{TV}(\mu_{j},\nu_{j})\ \leq\ \mathrm{TV}(\mu_{j},\tilde{\mu}_{j})\ +\ \mathrm{TV}(\tilde{\mu}_{j},\nu_{j}).

We bound
\mathrm{TV}(\tilde{\mu}_{j},\nu_{j})
first. The measures
\tilde{\mu}_{j}
and
\nu_{j}
are mixtures with the same mixing weights
w(a)=\prod_{k}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s^{\prime})
. By convexity of total variation,

\mathrm{TV}(\tilde{\mu}_{j},\nu_{j})\leq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}w(a)\,\mathrm{TV}\big(P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a),P_{j}(\cdot\mid s^{\prime},a)\big)\leq\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{TV}\big(P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a),P_{j}(\cdot\mid s^{\prime},a)\big)=E^{\mathrm{s}}_{j\leftarrow i}.

We now bound
\mathrm{TV}(\mu_{j},\tilde{\mu}_{j})
. This is the difference between two mixtures with the same components and different mixing weights
p(a\mid s)=\prod_{k}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s)
and
p(a\mid s^{\prime})=\prod_{k}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s^{\prime})
, but the components
P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a)
can be far apart for different
a
. We upper bound this difference by a coupling that changes actions along one coordinate at a time and uses the action sensitivity of the kernel.

Let
(A,A^{\prime})
be a coupling of
p(\cdot\mid s)
and
p(\cdot\mid s^{\prime})
constructed as follows. For each agent
k
, couple the marginals
\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid s)
and
\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid s^{\prime})
by a maximal coupling so that

\mathbb{P}[A_{k}\neq A^{\prime}_{k}]\ =\ \mathrm{TV}\big(\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid s),\pi_{k}(\cdot\mid s^{\prime})\big)\ =\ \Pi_{k\leftarrow i}(\pi).

Take these couplings independent across
k
, which is possible because the policy is of product form and the coordinates are independent under each product marginal.

Conditional on
(A,A^{\prime})
, couple next–state coordinates for agent
j
by a maximal coupling of
P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,A)
and
P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,A^{\prime})
. Then

\mathbb{P}[S^{\prime(j)}\neq\tilde{S}^{\prime(j)}\mid A,A^{\prime}]\ =\ \mathrm{TV}\big(P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,A),P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,A^{\prime})\big).

Taking expectation,

\mathrm{TV}(\mu_{j},\tilde{\mu}_{j})\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{TV}\big(P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,A),P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,A^{\prime})\big).

For any
a,a^{\prime}
differing on a set
D\subseteq[n]
of action coordinates, the triangle inequality and the definition of
E^{\mathrm{a}}
imply

\mathrm{TV}\big(P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a),P_{j}(\cdot\mid s,a^{\prime})\big)\ \leq\ \sum_{k\in D}E^{\mathrm{a}}_{j\leftarrow k}.

Applying this bound with the random pair
(A,A^{\prime})
and taking expectations yields

\mathrm{TV}(\mu_{j},\tilde{\mu}_{j})\ \leq\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}E^{\mathrm{a}}_{j\leftarrow k}\,\mathbb{P}[A_{k}\neq A^{\prime}_{k}]\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}E^{\mathrm{a}}_{j\leftarrow k}\,\Pi_{k\leftarrow i}(\pi).

Combining the two pieces finishes the proof:

\mathrm{TV}(\mu_{j},\nu_{j})\ \leq\ E^{\mathrm{s}}_{j\leftarrow i}\ +\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}E^{\mathrm{a}}_{j\leftarrow k}\,\Pi_{k\leftarrow i}(\pi).

By the definition of
C^{\pi}_{j\leftarrow i}
as a supremum over
s,s^{\prime}
with
s_{-i}=s^{\prime}_{-i}
, the same bound holds for
C^{\pi}_{j\leftarrow i}
.

By Lemma

B.4
Spatial decay as a corollary of sparsity

Suppose that there is an undirected graph on
[n]
with maximum degree
\Delta
such that
E^{\mathrm{s}}_{j\leftarrow i}=0
and
E^{\mathrm{a}}_{j\leftarrow k}=0
whenever
j
and
i
are nonneighbors and
j
and
k
are nonneighbors. Suppose also that
\Pi(\pi)_{k\leftarrow i}=0
whenever
k
and
i
are nonneighbors. Then the matrix
H^{\pi}=(E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi))^{\top}
has the same sparsity pattern as the graph. For any two sets of coordinates at graph distance at least
d
, the corresponding block of
(H^{\pi})^{t}
vanishes for
t<d
. If
\rho(H^{\pi})\leq\lambda<1
, then
\|(H^{\pi})^{t}\|_{\infty\to\infty}\leq C(\Delta)\lambda^{t}
and the influence of a perturbation decays exponentially in graph distance. This recovers the locality phenomenon used by synchronous truncated algorithms as a corollary of the geometry–free spectral condition.

B.5
Average–reward localized evaluation and improvement (synchronous)

We record a consequence for synchronous localized policy evaluation and policy iteration. Let
\mathrm{Ball}_{\kappa}(i)
denote the set of coordinates within distance
\kappa
of
i
in a given sparsity graph. Suppose the sparsity conditions in the previous subsection hold and
\rho(E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi))\leq\lambda<1
uniformly over
\pi
in a policy class. Then there is a synchronous policy evaluation scheme that approximates the average–reward Poisson solution

using only local computations on
\kappa

. A synchronous localized policy iteration that uses these local evaluations to perform local improvements enjoys a performance loss bounded by the same exponential rate, under standard boundedness and irreducibility assumptions. The details follow from the resolvent bound for

\mathbb{P}[S_{t+1}=s^{(j\to y)}\mid S_{t}=s]=\nu_{j}\sum_{a}\Big(\prod_{k}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid s)\Big)P_{j}(y\mid s,a)\quad\text{for }y\in\mathcal{S}_{j}.

Define
E^{\mathrm{s}}
,
E^{\mathrm{a}}
, and
\Pi(\pi)
as before and set

M^{\pi}=(I-\mathrm{diag}\,\nu)+\mathrm{diag}\,\nu\,(E^{\mathrm{s}}+E^{\mathrm{a}}\Pi(\pi)).

Then for all bounded

\delta(K^{\pi}f)\leq(M^{\pi})^{\top}\delta(f)
, and if
\rho(M^{\pi})\leq\lambda<1
uniformly over the policy class then
\|\delta((K^{\pi})^{t}f)\|_{\infty}\leq C\lambda^{t}\|\delta(f)\|_{\infty}
and the average–reward Poisson equation has a solution with
\delta(h^{\pi})\leq(I-(M^{\pi})^{\top})^{-1}\delta(r^{\pi})

The proof is identical to the synchronous case, with the additional
(I-\mathrm{diag}\,\nu)

B.7
Extension of Theorem
3
to the weighted case

Since proofs for these are similar to the unweighted case, we skip rederiving in full detail.

Fix

and
W=\mathrm{diag}(w)
. For any bounded

and

\delta_{i}^{w}(T^{\pi}f)\ =\ w_{i}\,\delta_{i}(T^{\pi}f)\ \leq\ w_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}H^{\pi}_{j\leftarrow i}\,\delta_{j}(f)\ =\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(W^{-1}H^{\pi}W\big)_{j\leftarrow i}\,\delta_{j}^{w}(f).

This is the entrywise weighted one–step contraction. Iterating gives the multi–step bound. For the Poisson resolvent, one repeats the proof of Proposition A.
with the seminorm

The truncated series bound and the uniform power bound over a compact policy class are identical to the unweighted case after replacing

Proofs from Section

We bound the policy sensitivity matrix

\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\nu)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mu-\nu\|_{1}

Lemma 8

(Softmax Lipschitz constant in total variation)
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\tau>0
. For
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, define

\mathrm{soft}_{\tau}(u)_{a}\ \coloneqq\ \frac{\exp(u_{a}/\tau)}{\sum_{b=1}^{m}\exp(u_{b}/\tau)}\,,\qquad a\in[m].

Then

\mathrm{TV}\big(\mathrm{soft}_{\tau}(u),\ \mathrm{soft}_{\tau}(v)\big)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2\tau}\,\|u-v\|_{\infty}.

Moreover, the constant
1/(2\tau)
is sharp: for each
\tau
and
m\geq 2
there exist
u\neq v
attaining equality.

Proof.

Write
p=\mathrm{soft}_{\tau}(u)
and
q=\mathrm{soft}_{\tau}(v)
. By the mean value theorem on the line segment
w(t)=v+t(u-v)
,
t\in[0,1]

\|p-q\|_{1}\ =\ \left\|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{soft}_{\tau}(w(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{1}\ \leq\ \int_{0}^{1}\left\|D\mathrm{soft}_{\tau}(w(t))\,(u-v)\right\|_{1}\mathrm{d}t,

where
D\mathrm{soft}_{\tau}(w)
is the Jacobian. For
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
r=\mathrm{soft}_{\tau}(w)
,

D\mathrm{soft}_{\tau}(w)\ =\ \frac{1}{\tau}\,\big(\mathrm{diag}(r)-rr^{\top}\big).

Hence, using the induced operator norm from
\ell_{\infty}
to
\ell_{1}
,

\|p-q\|_{1}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\tau}\,\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big\|\mathrm{diag}(r(t))-r(t)r(t)^{\top}\big\|_{\infty\to 1}\right)\,\|u-v\|_{\infty},\qquad r(t)=\mathrm{soft}_{\tau}(w(t)).

We claim that for every probability vector
r
,

\big\|\mathrm{diag}(r)-rr^{\top}\big\|_{\infty\to 1}\ \leq\ 1.

(2)

This yields
\|p-q\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\tau}\|u-v\|_{\infty}
and therefore

\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\leq\frac{1}{2\tau}\|u-v\|_{\infty}
.

It remains to prove (
2
). Let
J(r)=\mathrm{diag}(r)-rr^{\top}
and fix
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\|\delta\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. Then

(J(r)\delta)_{i}\ =\ r_{i}\big(\delta_{i}-\langle r,\delta\rangle\big),\qquad i\in[m],

hence

\|J(r)\delta\|_{1}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\,\big|\delta_{i}-\mu\big|\ =\ \mathbb{E}_{I\sim r}\big[\,|\delta_{I}-\mu|\,\big],\qquad\mu\coloneqq\langle r,\delta\rangle\in[-1,1].

For fixed
\mu
, the map
\delta\mapsto\mathbb{E}[|\delta_{I}-\mu|]
is convex and is maximized over
\{\delta:\|\delta\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}
at extreme points
\delta\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}

\{\pm 1\}

\delta

\mu

equals

\alpha(1-\mu)+(1-\alpha)(1+\mu)\ =\ 1-\mu^{2},\qquad\alpha=\tfrac{1+\mu}{2},

and the maximum over
\mu\in[-1,1]
is

, attained at

. Therefore
\|J(r)\delta\|_{1}\leq 1
for all
\delta
with
\|\delta\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, proving (

uniform on

\delta
with half of the entries

∎

We now translate Lemma

Definition 9

(Local logits and per-coordinate logit Lipschitz constants)

Lemma 10

(Softmax temperature controls

Under the setup above, for all

\Pi_{k\leftarrow i}(\pi)\ \leq\ \min\!\left\{\,1,\ \frac{L_{k\leftarrow i}}{2\tau}\,\right\}.

Appendix E
Miscellaneous Results

E.1
Example: A Coupled System where Policy is Ineffective

Our framework also captures the failure mode, where policy smoothing is ineffective. Suppose the environment has strong
direct state-based coupling
, where one agent’s state physically determines another’s.

Consider two agents, a Leader (agent 1) and a Follower (agent 2). Suppose the Follower’s next state
s^{\prime}_{2}
is
deterministically
set to be a copy of the Leader’s
current
state
s_{1}
, regardless of any actions taken.

P_{2}(s^{\prime}_{2}=1\mid s,a)=\mathbf{1}\{s_{1}=1\}.

This models a system with direct, physical state-to-state influence (e.g.,
s^{\prime}_{2}
is a sensor reading of
s_{1}
). The environment’s sensitivities are:

•

E^{\mathrm{s}}_{2\leftarrow 1}=\sup_{s_{-1}=s^{\prime}_{-1},a}\mathrm{TV}(P_{2}(\cdot\mid s,a),P_{2}(\cdot\mid s^{\prime},a))=\mathrm{TV}(\delta_{0},\delta_{1})=1
.

•

E^{\mathrm{a}}=0
(actions have no effect on
s^{\prime}_{2}
).

We can now analyze the policy-induced influence of agent 1 on agent 2,
C^{\pi}_{2\leftarrow 1}
:

C^{\pi}_{2\leftarrow 1}\leq E^{\mathrm{s}}_{2\leftarrow 1}+\sum_{k=1}^{2}E^{\mathrm{a}}_{2\leftarrow k}\,\Pi_{k\leftarrow 1}(\pi)=1+(0\cdot\Pi_{1\leftarrow 1}+0\cdot\Pi_{2\leftarrow 1})=1.

In this case, the true influence
C^{\pi}_{2\leftarrow 1}
is exactly 1, and our bound correctly reflects this. The policy sensitivity
\Pi(\pi)
is rendered completely irrelevant because the action-coupling matrix
E^{\mathrm{a}}
is zero.

Our framework correctly shows that no amount of policy smoothing (i.e., making
\Pi(\pi)
small by increasing
\tau
) can create locality when the environment’s direct state-to-state physics (
E^{s}
) already creates a tight, off-diagonal coupling. The system’s influence matrix
H^{\pi}
will have an off-diagonal entry of 1, making
\rho(H^{\pi})\geq 1
and breaking the condition for spatial decay.

E.2
Example: policy-induced locality in a nonlocal-looking system.

Consider a hub–and–spoke system with
n\geq 3
agents.

Let
P_{1}
be constant and, for
j>1
,

P_{j}(s^{\prime}_{j}=1\mid s,a)=\mathbf{1}\{a_{1}=1\}
. Then
E^{\mathrm{s}}=0
and

E^{\mathrm{a}}_{j\leftarrow 1}=1
for all
j>1
(others zero). A policy-independent,
action-supremum certificate declares the system non-local (
\|C\|_{\infty}=1
)
(
16
)
.

Under a temperature-
\tau
softmax for agent 1 with logit Lipschitz constants
\{L_{1\leftarrow i}\}
,
Lemma
10
gives
\Pi_{1\leftarrow i}\leq L_{1\leftarrow i}/(2\tau)
and hence

\rho(H^{\pi})\ \leq\ \rho\!\Big(E^{\mathrm{a}}\frac{L}{2\tau}\Big)\ =\ \frac{1}{2\tau}\sum_{j>1}L_{1\leftarrow i}\ \leq\ \frac{(n-1)L_{\max}}{2\tau}.

Thus locality is certified whenever

, and if the hub ignores its inputs (

.
This illustrates how locality can be

, while worst-case, policy-independent tests cannot detect it.

E.3
Properties of the Coordinatewise Oscillation Seminorm

Let
V=B(\mathcal{S})
be the vector space of all bounded real-valued functions on the finite state space
\mathcal{S}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{S}_{i}
.

Definition 17

(Coordinatewise Oscillation)
.

For a function
f\in V
and a coordinate
i\in[n]
, the
i
-oscillation is:

\delta_{i}(f)=\sup\Big\{\,|f(x)-f(y)|:x,y\in\mathcal{S},\ x_{-i}=y_{-i}\,\Big\}.

We define the function
p:V\to\mathbb{R}
as the maximum oscillation:

p(f)=\left\lVert\delta(f)\right\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{i\in[n]}\delta_{i}(f).

Proposition 18
.

The function
p(f)=\left\lVert\delta(f)\right\rVert_{\infty}
is a seminorm on the vector space
V
.

Proof.

To prove that
p(f)
is a seminorm, we must verify three properties:

1.

Non-negativity:

p(f)\geq 0
for all
f\in V
.

2.

Absolute Homogeneity:

p(cf)=|c|p(f)
for all
f\in V
and scalar
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.

Subadditivity (Triangle Inequality):

p(f+g)\leq p(f)+p(g)
for all
f,g\in V
.

1. Non-negativity:

The absolute value
|f(x)-f(y)|
is always non-negative. The supremum of a set of non-negative numbers,
\delta_{i}(f)
, is also non-negative. The maximum of a set of non-negative numbers,
p(f)
, is therefore non-negative.

2. Absolute Homogeneity:

For any
f\in V
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\delta_{i}(cf)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{-i}=y_{-i}}|(cf)(x)-(cf)(y)|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{-i}=y_{-i}}|c\cdot(f(x)-f(y))|

\displaystyle=|c|\cdot\sup_{x_{-i}=y_{-i}}|f(x)-f(y)|=|c|\cdot\delta_{i}(f).

Taking the maximum over all
i
:

p(cf)=\max_{i\in[n]}\delta_{i}(cf)=\max_{i\in[n]}\big(|c|\cdot\delta_{i}(f)\big)=|c|\cdot\max_{i\in[n]}\delta_{i}(f)=|c|\cdot p(f).

3. Subadditivity:

For any
f,g\in V
:

\displaystyle\delta_{i}(f+g)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{-i}=y_{-i}}|(f+g)(x)-(f+g)(y)|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{-i}=y_{-i}}|(f(x)-f(y))+(g(x)-g(y))|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x_{-i}=y_{-i}}\big(|f(x)-f(y)|+|g(x)-g(y)|\big)\quad\text{(by the triangle inequality for $\mathbb{R}$)}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x_{-i}=y_{-i}}|f(x)-f(y)|+\sup_{x_{-i}=y_{-i}}|g(x)-g(y)|\quad\text{(by a standard property of suprema)}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}(f)+\delta_{i}(g).

Now, taking the maximum over all
i
:

p(f+g)=\max_{i\in[n]}\delta_{i}(f+g)\leq\max_{i\in[n]}(\delta_{i}(f)+\delta_{i}(g)).

For any
i
, we know
\delta_{i}(f)\leq\max_{j}\delta_{j}(f)=p(f)
and
\delta_{i}(g)\leq\max_{j}\delta_{j}(g)=p(g)
. Thus:

p(f+g)\leq\max_{i\in[n]}(p(f)+p(g))=p(f)+p(g).

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 19

(Why it is a seminorm, not a norm)
.

A norm requires
p(f)=0\iff f=0
. For our
p(f)
, if
f(x)=c
for some non-zero constant
c
, then
f\neq 0
. However, for any
i
and any pair
x,y
with
x_{-i}=y_{-i}
,
f(x)=c
and
f(y)=c
, so
|f(x)-f(y)|=0
. This implies
\delta_{i}(f)=0
for all
i
, and thus
p(f)=0
. Since
p(f)=0
for non-zero constant functions,
p(f)
is a seminorm. In fact,
p(f)=0\iff f\text{ is a constant function.}

Proposition 20
.

The value
p(f)=\left\lVert\delta(f)\right\rVert_{\infty}
is the (best) Lipschitz constant of
f
with respect to the Hamming distance
d_{H}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

Let
K=p(f)
. We must show that for any
x,z\in\mathcal{S}
,
|f(x)-f(z)|\leq K\cdot d_{H}(x,z)
.

Case 1:
d_{H}(x,z)=1
.

If
d_{H}(x,z)=1
, then
x
and
z
differ in exactly one coordinate, say
j
. This means
x_{-j}=z_{-j}
. By the definition of
\delta_{j}(f)
:

|f(x)-f(z)|\leq\sup_{x^{\prime}_{-j}=y^{\prime}_{-j}}|f(x^{\prime})-f(y^{\prime})|=\delta_{j}(f).

By the definition of
p(f)
,
\delta_{j}(f)\leq\max_{i}\delta_{i}(f)=p(f)=K
.
Therefore,
|f(x)-f(z)|\leq K=K\cdot d_{H}(x,z)
. This also shows that
K
is the best Lipschitz constant for pairs at distance 1.

Case 2:
d_{H}(x,z)=k>1
.

Let the coordinates where
x
and
z
differ be
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}
. We can construct a path from
x
to
z
by changing one coordinate at a time. Let
z^{(0)}=x
, and let
z^{(t)}
be the state obtained by changing the first
t
coordinates in
I
from their values in
x
to their values in
z
.
For example,
z^{(1)}
is identical to
x
except
z^{(1)}_{i_{1}}=z_{i_{1}}
.
In general,
z^{(t)}
and
z^{(t+1)}
differ only in coordinate
i_{t+1}
. Thus,
d_{H}(z^{(t)},z^{(t+1)})=1
.
The full path is
x=z^{(0)},z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(k)}=z
.

Using the triangle inequality, we “telescope” the sum:

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(z)|

\displaystyle=|f(z^{(0)})-f(z^{(k)})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\big(f(z^{(t)})-f(z^{(t+1)})\big)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}|f(z^{(t)})-f(z^{(t+1)})|.

For each term in the sum,
z^{(t)}
and
z^{(t+1)}
differ in exactly one coordinate, so
d_{H}(z^{(t)},z^{(t+1)})=1
. From Case 1, we know:

|f(z^{(t)})-f(z^{(t+1)})|\leq p(f)=K.

Substituting this into the sum:

|f(x)-f(z)|\leq\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}K=k\cdot K.

Since
k=d_{H}(x,z)
, we have shown:

|f(x)-f(z)|\leq K\cdot d_{H}(x,z).

This holds for all
x,z\in\mathcal{S}
, so
p(f)
is the Lipschitz constant of
f
w.r.t.
d_{H}
.

Optimality.

Let L be any constant such that
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L
,
d_{H}(x,y)
for all
x,y
.
Fix
i
and
x,y
with
x_{-i}=y_{-i}
; then
d_{H}(x,y)=1
, so
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L
.
Taking the supremum over such pairs gives
\delta_{i}(f)\leq L
, hence

p(f)=\max_{i}\delta_{i}(f)\leq L
. Therefore
p(f)
is the least Lipschitz constant.
∎
