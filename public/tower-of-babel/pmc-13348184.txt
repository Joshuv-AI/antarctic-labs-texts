Draft genome sequencing of Proteus mirabilis strain Indica (ST286) reveals antimicrobial resistance and virulence determinants
Authors: Sovon Acharya, Parmanand Kushwaha, Shailesh Desai, Soham Biswas, Langamba Angom Longjam, Biswaroop Chatterjee, Munindra Ruwali, Sachinandan De, Lakshminarasimhan Krishnaswamy, Prashanth Suravajhala, Gyaneshwer Chaubey

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProteus mirabilis is a Gram-negative, motile bacterium that typically colonizes the human gastrointestinal tract as a commensal organism. Under favorable conditions, particularly in hospitalized or immunocompromised individuals, as an opportunistic pathogen, causing a range of infections including catheter-associated urinary tract infections, wound infections and bacteremia. Its pathogenic potential is linked to a combination of virulence traits such as urease production, swarming motility, biofilm formation and the ability to acquire antimicrobial resistance determinants, which together facilitate persistence, tissue invasion and treatment failure. In this study we aim to understand the genomic attributes of a P. mirabilis clinical isolate from India.MethodsThe bacterial isolate was recovered from a clinical sample, followed by antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) using standard methods. Whole-genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform. De novo genome assembly and annotation were conducted, followed by multilocus sequence typing (MLST), virulence sequence typing and analysis of the resistome, virulome and mobile genetic elements using established bioinformatics pipelines. In silico protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was performed to identify key functional hubs associated with pathogenicity.ResultsWGS confirmed the multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotype of the isolate, revealing a diverse array of antimicrobial resistance genes, including blaTEM−1B. The virulome analysis identified multiple fimbrial adhesins, hemolysins and toxin-encoding genes, collectively supporting adhesion, biofilm formation and host tissue damage. Multilocus sequence typing assigned the isolate to a previously unreported sequence type, designated ST286 in the PubMLST database., Virulence sequence typing indicated the profile as vST-1157 by the PubMLST database. Protein–protein interaction network analysis identified the hemolysin genes hpmA and hpmB, along with the fimbrial operon regulator mrpA, as major hub nodes with extensive connectivity. These hubs were strongly linked to multiple interaction partners, indicating their central role in mediating host–pathogen interactions, inflammation and tissue damage.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive genomic insight into a novel multidrug-resistant P. mirabilis strain Indica, with a unique MLST profile isolated from India. The study also reveals the presence of antimicrobial resistance and virulence associated genes. The identification of hemolysin and fimbriae-associated hub genes as central nodes within the interaction network underscores their pivotal role in driving P. mirabilis virulence and host pathogen interaction. Also underscores the importance of genomic surveillance in geographically under represented areas. These findings provide a network-level perspective on P. mirabilis pathogenic mechanisms and highlight key virulence hubs as potential targets for therapeutic intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05127-z.Keywords: Proteus mirabilis, blaTEM-1B, Antimicrobial resistance, Virulence evolution, Whole-genome sequencing (WGS), Subunit Vaccine, Antibody, Bacteriophage

Introduction

P. mirabilis is a Gram-negative bacterium of the family Enterobacteriaceae, widely recognized for its dual identity as both a harmless commensal of the human gut and a significant opportunistic pathogen. While its presence in the gut microbiota is often benign, under certain conditions, it can breach anatomical barriers and cause a range of severe infections in humans [1, 2]. Among these, urinary tract infections (UTIs) are its most well-known manifestation, particularly complicated cases and those associated with long-term indwelling catheters, known as catheter-associated UTIs (CAUTIs) [1–3]. The bacterium’s ability to thrive in the urinary tract is a consequence of its unique virulence traits, which facilitate colonization, persistence and tissue damage. Beyond UTIs, P. mirabilis has been implicated in more serious systemic infections, including wound infections, bacteremia and septicemia, which can be life-threatening, especially in vulnerable patient populations such as the elderly, diabetics, cancer patients and immunocompromised individuals [4, 5]. Understanding the genomic basis of its transition from a commensal to a virulent pathogen is crucial for developing effective therapeutic and preventative strategies.

The pathogenic success of P. mirabilis is rooted in its sophisticated and coordinated expression of multiple antibiotic resistance (ABR) genes, virulence factors and mobile genetic elements (MGEs). Its most distinguishing feature is the production of urease, an enzyme that hydrolyzes urea into ammonia and carbon dioxide [6]. This enzymatic activity significantly raises the pH of urine, creating a hostile alkaline environment for other bacteria while promoting the precipitation of magnesium and calcium ions [7]. This process leads to the formation of crystalline biofilms and struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate) stones, which can cause severe kidney damage, block urinary catheters and render antibiotic treatment ineffective [8, 9]. This unique mechanism is a primary reason for the persistent and recurrent nature of P. mirabilis UTIs. Another hallmark of this bacterium is its characteristic swarming motility, a collective form of cell movement across solid surfaces [10]. This process involves a reversible differentiation of short, flagellated vegetative cells into elongated, hyperflagellated “swarmer” cells. This swarming behavior allows P. mirabilis to rapidly ascend the urinary tract, a critical step in the pathogenesis of pyelonephritis (kidney infection) [11]. Furthermore, swarming is intricately linked to biofilm formation, a complex process where bacteria adhere to surfaces, such as urinary catheters and encase themselves in a protective matrix [12]. This biofilm matrix provides a physical barrier against host immune defenses and antibiotics, contributing to chronic infections. The ability of P. mirabilis to adhere to host cells is mediated by a diverse repertoire of fimbrial adhesins. These include the mannose-resistant Proteus (MR/P) fimbriae and the P. mirabilis fimbriae (PMF), which bind to specific receptors on uroepithelial cells, facilitating colonization and preventing the bacteria from being flushed out by urine flow [13]. Other important virulence factors include hemolysins, which damage host cell membranes and release essential nutrients like iron and proteases, which can degrade host tissues and evade the immune system. Additionally, the bacterium’s robust iron acquisition systems are essential for its survival in the iron-limited environment of the urinary tract, allowing it to scavenge iron from host proteins like transferrin and lactoferrin [14]. The collective and synergistic action of these virulence factors allows P. mirabilis to successfully colonize, invade and cause severe disease when host defenses are compromised.

Historically, P. mirabilis was considered more susceptible to antibiotics compared to other Enterobacteriaceae like Escherichia coli or Klebsiella pneumoniae. However, over the past few decades, it has rapidly emerged as a significant reservoir of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [15]. This has transformed it from a manageable pathogen into a multidrug-resistant (MDR) threat, severely complicating therapeutic options. A major concern is the widespread prevalence of β-lactamases, enzymes that break down the β-lactam ring of antibiotics such as penicillins and cephalosporins. These include TEM and CTX M-type extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), which have been globally disseminated, leading to treatment failures. While ESBLs have received considerable attention, the growing threat of carbapenemases (e.g., NDM, KPC) in Enterobacteriaceae is also a grave concern, with some rare reports of their presence in Proteus species further highlighting the escalating crisis [16]. Beyond β-lactamases, P. mirabilis has acquired resistance to multiple other antibiotic classes. Genes encoding aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (e.g., aph genes), sulfonamide resistance genes (e.g., sul1, sul2) and tetracycline efflux pumps (tet genes) are frequently reported [9]. The emergence of plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (qnr variants) is also a growing issue, limiting the use of a key class of antibiotics for treating UTIs [17]. The coexistence of these resistance determinants on MGE allows a single isolate to be resistant to multiple drugs, a hallmark of MDR strains. This rapid acquisition of resistance traits has been a major driver of the increasing morbidity and mortality associated with P. mirabilis infections [18].

The rapid evolution of P. mirabilis from a commensal to a MDR pathogen is largely driven by horizontal gene transfer (HGT), a process that allows bacteria to acquire new genetic material from other organisms or vice versa. HGT is mediated by MGEs such as plasmids, transposons and bacteriophages. Plasmids are extrachromosomal DNA molecules that can carry both virulence and resistance genes, enabling their rapid transfer between different bacterial species. The acquisition of large plasmids can fundamentally change a bacterium’s phenotype in a single event, making it both more virulent and more drug-resistant [19]. Genomic islands, often referred to as pathogenicity islands (PAIs), are another key mechanism. These are clusters of genes, often with virulence-related functions, that are integrated into the host chromosome and show evidence of HGT. Comparative genomic studies have revealed that pathogenic strains of P. mirabilis contain large PAIs that are absent in their commensal counterparts [20]. The co-localization of resistance genes and virulence factors on these MGEs underscores a key evolutionary dynamic: the traits that enable a bacterium to survive in a hospital environment (AMR) are often acquired alongside the traits that allow it to cause disease (virulence), leading to the co-evolution of these two critical phenotypes.

Despite the global recognition of P. mirabilis as a clinically relevant pathogen, there is a significant lack of comprehensive genomic data from India. The country faces a substantial burden of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram negative infections, driven by high population density, extensive antibiotic use, and inadequate infection control practices [21]. While surveillance efforts in India have predominantly focused on ESBL and carbapenemase-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae, the rising clinical significance of P. mirabilis warrants urgent genomic characterization [22]. Very few studies were performed on the WGS data of Indian P. mirabilis isolates, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of their resistome, virulome and evolutionary dynamics. In this study, we aimed to bridge this knowledge gap by providing a detailed whole-genome characterization of a blaTEM−1B​ harboring P. mirabilis isolate from India. Through the integration of genomic approaches encompassing resistome and virulome profiling, MGE characterization and comparative genomics, this study provides new insights into the genetic mechanisms underlying the evolution of this species from a benign commensal to a multidrug-resistant and highly virulent pathogen. Our findings highlight the critical role of genomic surveillance in monitoring the emergence of high-risk clones and emphasize its value in guiding more effective strategies for diagnostics, therapeutic interventions and infection control both in India and globally.

Methods

Isolation and identification of the bacteria

A bacterial isolate was recovered in 2024 from discarded diagnostic culture plates obtained from the Microbiology Department of a tertiary-care hospital in West Bengal, India. The isolate was subcultured on MacConkey agar, where it exhibited non-lactose-fermenting colonies. Based on preliminary phenotypic characteristics, the isolate was provisionally suspected to belong to the Acinetobacter baumannii complex. Species-level identification was subsequently confirmed using conventional biochemical tests and automated identification systems, followed by the whole-genome sequencing (WGS) for definitive characterization. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of IQ City Medical College & NH Hospital, Durgapur, India (Approval No. IQMC/IEC/Project/17/28) for the genomic characterization of Acinetobacter baumannii. During subsequent laboratory analysis, the isolate was found, through WGS, to be P. mirabilis rather than A. baumannii. The sequencing data and downstream analyses presented in this manuscript, therefore, pertain to P. mirabilis. Since the isolate in this study was recovered from discarded clinical culture plates without access to any patient-identifiable information. According to the International Ethical Guidelines for Health-related Research Involving Humans (CIOMS-WHO, 2016) and the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) guidelines, such use of anonymized bacterial isolates without any associated personal data does not require individual patient consent, as it does not constitute human subject research.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using automated broth microdilution (Vitek® 2 Compact system; bioMérieux) and the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method. Results were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2024 guidelines [23].

Bacterial DNA Extraction and Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS)

Genomic DNA was extracted from an overnight bacterial culture using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method as described by Doyle and Doyle (1987). Briefly, bacterial cells were lysed in CTAB extraction buffer, followed by protein removal using chloroform–isoamyl alcohol extraction. DNA was then precipitated with isopropanol, washed with ethanol, and resuspended in nuclease free water [24]. DNA quality and concentration were assessed prior to sequencing. Library preparation was carried out using the QIAGEN® QIAseq FX DNA Library Kit and sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with a paired-end 2 × 150 bp configuration.

Genome assembly, annotation and bioinformatic analysis

Raw paired-end sequencing reads were assessed for quality using FastQC [25] with default parameters, including evaluation of per-base sequence quality, GC content, sequence duplication levels and adapter contamination. Adapter trimming and quality filtering were performed using FastP [26] with default settings, applying a minimum Phred quality score cutoff of Q20, automatic adapter detection and removal of reads shorter than 50 bp after trimming. High-quality filtered reads were assembled de novo using Unicycler v0.8.4.0 [27] in short-read mode, optimized for Illumina data, using default parameters. Assembly quality metrics, including total assembly length, N50, number of contigs, GC content and genome completeness, were assessed using QUAST [28] with default settings. Genome annotation was initially performed using Prokka [29] with default bacterial parameters (kingdom Bacteria), followed by cross-validation using the Rapid Annotation using Subsystem Technology (RAST) server [30] with standard annotation settings to ensure consistency and functional accuracy. The assembled genome sequence has been deposited in the NCBI database under BioProject ID: PRJNA1206681. Antimicrobial resistance genes were identified using tools available through the Center for Genomic Epidemiology, including ResFinder [31] and the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) [32], applying default thresholds of ≥ 90% sequence identity and ≥ 60% minimum coverage. Mobile genetic elements (MGEs) were detected using ISEScan with standard HMM-based detection parameters, while genomic islands were identified and visualized using IslandViewer4 [33] employing integrated prediction methods (IslandPick, SIGI-HMM and IslandPath-DIMOB). For molecular typing, multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was performed using the PubMLST database [34] with default allele and sequence type assignment parameters. Additional functional annotation and comparative genomic analyses were conducted using the Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center (BV-BRC) platform [35] with default analytical pipelines. Virulence-associated genes were identified by querying predicted proteins against the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) [36] using default BLAST parameters. Pairwise sequence similarity analyses were performed using NCBI BLASTn/BLASTp with an E-value cutoff of 1e − 5. Protein structure prediction was carried out using AlphaFold [37] with default model parameters for single-sequence prediction.

Variant calling and SNP analysis

High-confidence variant calling was performed using DeepVariant [38], a deep learning based variant caller developed by Google, which applies convolutional neural networks to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and small insertions and deletions from next-generation sequencing data. Briefly, quality-filtered paired-end Illumina reads were aligned to a suitable Proteus mirabilis reference genome using BWA-MEM [39] and the resulting alignments were sorted and indexed using SAMtools [40]. Duplicate reads, if present, were marked prior to variant calling to minimize bias. DeepVariant was executed in short-read mode, optimized for Illumina sequencing data, using default parameters recommended for bacterial genomes. The tool converts aligned reads into image-like representations capturing local sequence context, base quality, mapping quality and read depth, which are subsequently processed by a trained neural network to predict variant genotypes. Variant calls were generated in Variant Call Format (VCF) and included biallelic and multiallelic SNPs as well as short indels. Post-calling filtering was applied to retain high-confidence variants based on quality metrics, including variant quality score (QUAL), genotype quality (GQ) and read depth. Summary statistics such as variant type distribution, base substitution patterns, transition-to-transversion (Ti/Tv) ratio, indel size distribution and depth-of-coverage profiles were computed using standard bioinformatics tools and custom scripts. These metrics were used to assess variant call reliability and to characterize genome-wide patterns of genetic variation in P. mirabilis strain Indica.

Comparative genomics and phylogenetic analysis

To understand the evolutionary context, comparative genomics was performed against reference uropathogenic and environmental P. mirabilis genomes. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) was calculated to assess genetic relatedness through the autoMLST 2.0 [41]. Core genome-based phylogeny was constructed using concatenated alignments of orthologous genes and visualized using iTOL [42].

Identification of secretion systems

Bacterial secretion systems were identified using TXSScan, implemented within the MacSyFinder framework, which detects and classifies secretion system gene clusters based on curated Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) and genomic context. The draft genome assembly of Proteus mirabilis strain Indica was screened for the presence of Type I, Type VI secretion systems (T1SS, T6SS) using default parameters. Detected secretion system components were further inspected to assess completeness and organization of the predicted gene clusters. In parallel, virulence-associated secretion system genes were cross-validated using the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) to identify components with established roles in pathogenicity [43].

Results and discussion

Isolation, identification and genomic characterization led to annotation, yielding a draft genome of ~ 3.89 Mbp

Illumina NovaSeq sequencing generated approximately 19.1 million high-quality paired-end reads. WGS of the Proteus mirabilis isolate yielded a draft genome of approximately 3.89 Mbp. Genome assembly resulted in a highly contiguous draft genome. Annotation using Prokka identified 3,655 predicted genes. A map of the genome (Fig. 1) provided a visual overview of these features, including GC content, GC skew and the location of predicted genes. De novo assembly of the Proteus mirabilis strain Indica genome resulted in a high-quality draft assembly comprising 46 contigs, with 39 contigs ≥ 1 kb and 31 contigs ≥ 5 kb. The total assembled genome size was approximately 3.89 Mb, with a GC content of 38.65%, consistent with previously reported P. mirabilis genomes. The largest contig measured 431,158 bp and the assembly showed strong continuity, reflected by an N50 value of 216,256 bp and an L50 of 7, indicating that half of the genome was contained within seven contigs. The N90 and L90 values were 50,048 bp and 18, respectively. Notably, no ambiguous bases (N’s) were detected in the assembly, indicating the high quality of the sequencing data and assembly process. Overall, these metrics indicate a robust draft genome suitable for downstream comparative genomic, resistome and virulome analyses.

Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST) analysis identifies a novel sequence type (ST286)

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) analysis demonstrated that P. mirabilis strain Indica belongs to a previously undescribed sequence type, designated ST286 in the PubMLST database. The distinct allelic profile across the seven housekeeping genes (atpD, dnaJ, mdh, pyrC, recA and rpoD) confirms that this isolate represents a genetically divergent lineage within the P. mirabilis population. The identification of a novel ST highlights the ongoing genetic diversification of P. mirabilis and reflects the dynamic population structure of this opportunistic pathogen. The detection of ST286 in a clinical isolate from India is of particular relevance, given the limited availability of genomic data for P. mirabilis from this region. This finding suggests the presence of locally evolving lineages that may not be captured by existing global datasets. The designation of this isolate as P. mirabilis strain Indica provides a reference for future molecular epidemiological investigations and comparative genomic analyses. Overall, the emergence of a novel sequence type underscores the importance of continued MLST-based surveillance to monitor the evolution and dissemination of P. mirabilis lineages with potential clinical and epidemiological significance. Virulence sequence typing indicated the profile as vST-1157 by the pubMLST database.

Resistome profiling and comparison with the antibiotic sensitivity test data

The AST of P. mirabilis strain Indica was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method and results were interpreted according to CLSI guidelines. The isolate exhibited variable susceptibility across the tested antibiotics. Measurable zones of inhibition were observed for ceftazidime (18 mm), ciprofloxacin (18 mm), gentamicin (12 mm), cefepime (16 mm), imipenem (16 mm) and meropenem (16 mm), indicating retained activity of selected β-lactams, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides and carbapenems. In contrast, no zone of inhibition was observed for co-trimoxazole (Supplementary data 4).

WGS based resistome analysis of P. mirabilis strain Indica revealed a diverse repertoire of acquired antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes, indicating substantial resistance potential to multiple clinically relevant antibiotic classes. The presence of these resistance determinants highlights the increasing therapeutic challenge posed by P. mirabilis isolates. The genome harbored the blaTEM-1B gene, encoding a β-lactamase capable of conferring resistance to β-lactam antibiotics, including penicillins (e.g., ampicillin and amoxicillin). In addition to β-lactam resistance, genes encoding aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, including aph(6)-Id and aph(3′)-Ib, were identified (Fig. 2). These enzymes confer resistance to streptomycin by enzymatic modification that prevents binding to the bacterial ribosome. Resistance to sulfonamides was mediated by the presence of sul1 and sul2, which encode alternative dihydropteroate synthase variants that bypass drug inhibition in folate biosynthesis pathways. The cat gene, responsible for chloramphenicol acetyltransferase–mediated drug inactivation, was also detected. Furthermore, the genome encoded resistance determinants against tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones, including tet(J), which mediates resistance via efflux of tetracycline and doxycycline. The presence of the qacE gene suggests potential tolerance to quaternary ammonium compounds, which are commonly used as disinfectants in healthcare environments.

Comparative resistome analysis

Comparative resistome analysis was performed on 19 Proteus mirabilis isolates from India, revealing the presence of multidrug resistance determinants. Tetracycline resistance was the most dominant feature, with tet(J) detected in 18/19 isolates (94.7%), while tet(A), tet(M) and tet(J)_2 were each identified in 1/19 isolates (5.3%). Fluoroquinolone resistance genes were common, with qnrD1 present in 7/19 isolates (36.8%), qnrD3 in 5/19 (26.3%) and qnrD2 in 1/19 (5.3%). Aminoglycoside resistance genes were widespread and diverse, including ant(3ʺ)-Ia in 4/19 isolates (21.1%), aadA2, aph(3ʺ)-Ib and aph(6)-Id each in 2/19 (10.5%), while aac(3)-IId, aac(6ʹ)-Ib, aadA1, aadA3, aadA5, armA, aph(3ʹ)-Ia and aph(3ʹ)-VI were each detected in 1/19 isolates (5.3%). Sulfonamide and trimethoprim resistance genes were also observed, with sul1 detected in 4/19 isolates (21.1%), sul2 and sul3 each in 1/19 (5.3%) and dfrA1 present in 7/19 isolates (36.8%), while dfrA14 and dfrA17 occurred in 1/19 (5.3%) each. Notably, clinically significant β-lactamase genes were detected at lower frequencies, including blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-10, each in 1/19 isolates (5.3%), indicating the presence of carbapenemase- and ESBL-associated resistance in the Indian P. mirabilis population (Fig. 3). Collectively, these data indicate that Indian P. mirabilis isolates exhibit complex resistomes dominated by tetracycline and fluoroquinolone resistance, along with the sporadic but concerning emergence of carbapenem resistance determinants (Fig. 3). The evolutionary expansion of antimicrobial resistance in Proteus mirabilis is evident when comparing strain Indica with the reference strain HI4320. HI4320 represents the first whole-genome sequenced clinical P. mirabilis isolate, originally collected in 1982 and sequenced in 2007, providing an important baseline for understanding long-term genomic evolution. Reanalysis of the P. mirabilis strain HI4320 genome revealed the presence of only two antibiotic resistance genes, including cat and tet(J), reflecting a largely susceptible resistome characteristic of that era. In contrast, P. mirabilis strain Indica exhibits a substantially expanded resistome encompassing multiple resistance determinants across several antibiotic classes (Supplementary data 1). This marked difference highlights the cumulative impact of decades of antibiotic exposure and clinical selective pressure, underscoring the progressive evolution of P. mirabilis from relatively susceptible lineages into contemporary multidrug-resistant strains with increased clinical relevance.

Virulence repertoire of Proteus mirabilis strain Indica

Genome annotation of Proteus mirabilis strain Indica revealed a broad and functionally diverse repertoire of virulence-associated genes, providing molecular evidence for its pathogenic potential. The virulence profile indicates the coordinated involvement of multiple mechanisms that collectively support host colonization, persistence and tissue damage (Table 1). A prominent feature of the genome was the abundance of fimbrial and pili-associated adhesins, including fim, pil, pmf, and autotransporter genes, which are central to surface attachment and biofilm formation. These traits are critical for the establishment and persistence of infection, particularly in the urinary tract. The presence of a complete urease operon (ureA–G) is consistent with the characteristic urease activity of P. mirabilis and supports its role in urinary alkalinization, crystal formation and catheter-associated urinary tract infections. Genes encoding flagellar components and chemotaxis proteins (fli, flg, mot) were also identified, reflecting the swarming motility phenotype that facilitates ascension and dissemination within the host. In addition to adhesion and motility, the genome encoded several toxins and proteases, including hpmA and hpmB (hemolysin) and zapA (metalloprotease), which are implicated in host tissue damage and immune evasion. The identification of multiple iron acquisition systems, such as TonB–ExbB/ExbD complexes, heme uptake receptors and siderophore-associated genes, suggests an enhanced capacity to acquire essential nutrients under iron-limited host conditions. Furthermore, genes involved in lipopolysaccharide and capsule biosynthesis (wzx,wzy,,lpx ) were detected, which may contribute to serum resistance and protection against host immune responses. The virulence repertoire further included components of Type III, Type V and Type VI secretion systems, indicating a versatile secretion capacity for the translocation of enzymes and effector proteins. Genes associated with quorum sensing and biofilm regulation, including luxS, pga and bcs, were also present, supporting coordinated bacterial behavior and enhanced persistence. Collectively, these findings indicate that P. mirabilis strain Indica possesses a multifactorial virulence arsenal, integrating adhesion, biofilm formation, urease activity, motility, toxin production, nutrient acquisition and immune evasion, which together likely underlie its clinical persistence and pathogenic versatility.

Prediction of MGEs in P. mirabilis strain Indica

Using IslandViewer4, eight genomic islands were identified in the genome of Proteus mirabilis strain Indica, several of which contained clusters of antimicrobial resistance and virulence-associated genes, highlighting the pronounced genomic plasticity of this isolate. These islands represent potential hotspots for HGT, underscoring their role in the co-evolution of resistance and pathogenic traits (Fig. 4). Notably, an IS91 family insertion sequence was detected on contig 37 in close proximity to the blaTEM-1B gene. Given the rolling-circle transposition mechanism of IS91 elements, this co-localization suggests a potential platform for the mobilization and dissemination of β-lactam resistance determinants, emphasizing the contribution of mobile genetic elements to the evolving resistome of P. mirabilis (Table 2).

Protein–protein interaction network of P. mirabilis strain Indicashowing urease, fimbrial and hemolysin clusters as key virulence hubs

The STRING interaction network of the P. mirabilis strain Indica (Fig. 5A and B) isolate revealed distinct functional clusters of virulence-associated proteins. A urease module (ureA–E) showed dense interconnections, underscoring the importance of urease in urinary tract colonization and stone formation. A fimbrial operon cluster (mrpA–H) formed another well-defined module, reflecting its role in adhesion and biofilm formation. Hemolysin genes (hpmA and hpmB) emerged as central hubs with extensive links to both metabolic and virulence proteins, suggesting coordinated functions in host damage, nutrient acquisition and immune evasion. Peripheral proteins, including zapA, were less connected but indicated specialized roles in tissue invasion. Collectively, the network highlights a modular but interconnected virulence architecture that equips the isolate for persistence in hostile host environments (Fig. 5A).

Whole-genome phylogenetic analysis

Whole-genome phylogenetic analysis based on concatenated core gene alignments revealed the evolutionary placement of P. mirabilis strain Indica within the genus Proteus. In the maximum-likelihood tree generated using autoMLST2, strain Indica clustered robustly within the P. mirabilis clade, clearly separated from other Proteus species and closely related genera such as Providencia and Xenorhabdus. The node supporting the placement of strain Indica within the P. mirabilis lineage was associated with high bootstrap support, indicating strong confidence in its phylogenetic assignment. The tree topology further demonstrated distinct species-level clustering among Proteus taxa, with strain Indica forming a well-supported branch within the P. mirabilis group, consistent with its MLST-based identification as a novel sequence type (ST286). Overall, the phylogenetic analysis confirms the taxonomic identity of strain Indica and highlights its evolutionary distinctiveness within the P. mirabilis population (Fig. 6).

Genome-wide variant analysis

Genome-wide variant analysis of P. mirabilis strain Indica, performed relative to a reference genome, revealed a total of ~ 26,000 genetic variants, predominantly comprising biallelic single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Among the detected variants, biallelic SNPs constituted the majority, whereas insertions, deletions and multiallelic variants were present at substantially lower frequencies. Analysis of nucleotide substitutions showed a non-random distribution of base changes, with transitions occurring more frequently than transversions. Consistent with this pattern, the transition-to-transversion (Ti/Tv) ratio was 2.80, indicative of a high-confidence variant dataset and typical of bacterial whole-genome comparisons. Indel analysis demonstrated that most insertion and deletion events were short in length, with a strong enrichment of indels ≤ 5 bp, suggesting localized sequence variation rather than large structural rearrangements. Depth-of-coverage analysis across variant sites revealed a high and relatively uniform sequencing depth, with the majority of variants supported by substantial read coverage, further supporting the reliability of variant calls. Collectively, these findings indicate that genomic divergence in P. mirabilis strain Indica is largely driven by point mutations and small indels, reflecting ongoing microevolution within the species (Fig. 7).

Conclusion

Genomic characterization of P. mirabilis strain Indica (ST286) provides valuable insight into the molecular features of a clinically relevant Indian isolate, addressing the current paucity of genomic data from this region. The isolate harbors a broad repertoire of virulence and antimicrobial resistance determinants, including blaTEM-1B, while lacking carbapenemase genes. The co-localization of resistance genes with insertion sequence elements and the presence of multiple genomic islands comprising antimicrobial resistance determinants, hypothetical proteins and mobility-associated genes highlight the role of genomic plasticity and horizontal gene transfer in ongoing adaptation. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of continued genomic surveillance of P. mirabilis to monitor the emergence and dissemination of high-risk lineages in clinical settings.

Future perspectives and limitations

From a public health perspective, the emergence of multidrug-resistant P. mirabilis lineages with expanded virulence capacity raises concerns for global dissemination and clinical management. Integrating genomic surveillance with antimicrobial stewardship is essential to anticipate these threats. Region-specific monitoring in India is particularly critical, given the country’s high antibiotic consumption, dense hospital environments and rising burden of MDR infections. Immunoinformatics-based approaches can leverage genomic data to identify vaccine candidates, while phage therapy and monoclonal antibodies offer promising alternatives to traditional antibiotics. At the same time, stewardship programs must carefully balance the use of existing antibiotics to slow the selection pressure driving resistance. However, our work is void of a few limitations, viz. (a) First, the analysis was based on a limited sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to broader P. mirabilis populations or other clinically relevant pathogens. Second, while genomic and regulatory RNA features were characterized, detailed host–pathogen interaction studies were not performed, leaving gaps in understanding how these regulatory elements influence bacterial survival and immune evasion.
