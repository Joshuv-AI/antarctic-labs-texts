Multi-Scale Land Use Impacts on Fossil Fuel-Related CO 2 Emissions in the United States
Jason Hawkins

Abstract
Anthropogenic greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions exhibit spatial variation owing to differences in development patterns, local climate, economic composition, energy sources, and other factors. Many of these factors - and therefore their contribution to GHG production - are influenceable through spatial planning and economic policy. Recent advances in environmental data reporting and climate flux measurement have produced high fidelity GHG emissions estimates at detailed spatial and temporal resolutions. Using one such dataset (Vulcan v3.0 1-km gridcell estimates of fossil-fuel CO2 for the U.S.), we explore the relationship between land use features and CO2 emissions. Analysis is conducted at multiple scales (neighbourhoods and metropolitan areas) to explore scale law effects. Using a data-driven propensity score approach, we develop doubly robust causal estimands for the effects of multiple land use features on CO2 emissions by sector. Preliminary results suggest that per capita transportation emissions are not significantly affected by local population density after controlling for metropolitan population density factors. However, results are likely influenced by the way transportation emissions are allocated in the Vulcan dataset. Results for scope 2 residential electricity and non-electricity energy are also considered in the study.



Keywords:
Transportation-land use interactions, Climate and land use policy, Machine learning causal inference

Introduction

Engineering and economic policy focus on how we can influence human activity through technology, investment, and behavioural shifts to maximize societal wellbeing. One focus area is the impact of human activity on global climate change due to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Quantifying these emissions and their spatial variation at a sufficiently granular scale to formulate policy analyses has historically faced constraints imposed by data availability. In most cases, emissions factors are applied to proxy measures – e.g., total vehicle miles travelled may be estimated by travel mode and mode-specific emissions factors used to estimate total transportation GHG emissions for a region. Recent advances in data availability and processing have produced high fidelity GHG emissions estimates at detailed spatial and temporal resolutions. These data make use of atmospheric inversion techniques to reconcile bottom-up sectoral estimates with top-down ensembles of atmospheric carbon 14 (C-14) estimates
(
Basu et al. 2020
)
. However, the effective leveraging of these data for regional climate policy analysis requires a sound basis in the planning and econometrics literature.

As of 2007, greater than half the world now lives in urban areas
(
Ritchie et al. 2024
)
. In the United States, the rate is greater than 83 percent, up from 64 percent in 1950
(
Ritchie et al. 2024
)
. As such, while this study will focus on all regions of the United States, our discussion will often use an urban framing. At a high level, there are two perspectives taken to urban GHG analysis in the literature. The first measures variation between metropolitan areas, primarily focused on variation in climate and regulation. For example,
Glaeser and Kahn 2010
find a negative association between GHG emissions and land use regulations. The second perspective takes a narrower focus on neighbourhood design.
Nichols and Kockelman 2014
;
Nichols and Kockelman 2015
and
Norman et al. 2006
are examples of this approach, which consider the life cycle energy use and GHG emissions associated with residential density and building typology decisions. A central hypothesis in the present work is that both perspectives are necessary, but neither is sufficient, to form effective climate policy for regions. Drawing on urban science
(
West 2017
)
, many urban features follow power law relationships as a function of population. For example,
(
Newman and Kenworthy 1989a
)
conducted a seminal study of the relationship between population density and transportation energy use across global cities, finding that high density Asian cities tend to consume less energy per capita than lower density North American cities.
Bettencourt et al. 2007
show that economic activity tends to scale superlinearly, while infrastructure length scales sublinearly with population size. That is, cities exhibit agglomeration and scale economies with infrastructure. These findings focus on quantitative population scaling but provide no insight into qualitative heterogeneity across the distribution, which could help to translate built form features into policy recommendations. Linking the inter-city and intra-city perspectives, a fundamental question is the relative contributions of neighbourhood-scale design features compared to incremental changes at the city scale. As a concrete example, consider a neighbourhood characterized by mixed use development and streets with pedestrian-centric design features such as low vehicular speeds and protected bicycle lanes. If this neighbourhood is situated in an otherwise low density automobile-centric city, it is unlikely to benefit from high frequency transit and access to other urban amenities. Thus, residents will likely continue to use low occupancy private vehicles despite neighbourhood built form and desire larger dwellings as a substitute for access to public spaces.

This paper makes use of a fossil-fuel CO2 dataset for the United States (Vulcan v4.0) to provide two contributions to the literature. We first study urban scaling at multiple spatial scales. These descriptive results are used as a motivation for causal estimates of the impact of land use features on sectoral CO2 emissions production. Section

gives a summary of the relevant land use planning literature that motivates the empirical analysis, as well as situating the study in the existing literature measuring the relationship between urban structure and GHG emissions. Section

5

Section

Literature

Land use is the foundation for GHG reduction in urban areas
(
Ramaswami et al. 2020
)
. Increasing density is critical to moving people more efficiently on public transit and providing access to goods and services within feasible walking and cycling distances. In addition to transportation outcomes, compact urban form also means reduced land, material, and energy consumption for buildings. While population density is often used as a proxy measure for built form, many other features affect total annual vehicle kilometres travelled (VKT), travel mode choice, and associated GHG emissions. Compact cities with increased density allow for a diversity of services and uses, accessible destinations, and interconnected streets and have 20-40 percent less VKT than low-density, sprawling cities
(
Ewing et al. 2014
)
.
Ewing and Cervero 2010
classify these features into five categories, often termed “the 5Ds of land use”: population
d
ensity, land use
d
iversity, street
d
esign,
d
estination accessibility, and
d
istance to transit.
Duranton and Turner 2018
find an elasticity of VKT with population density between -0.07 and -0.10. However, their density measure does not isolate for the effect of other built form features. Of the 5Ds, destination accessibility is found in the planning literature to have the largest impact on VKT
(
Ewing and Hamidi 2014
;
Stevens 2017
)
. Density is associated with the other 5D features and can act as a proxy for them. The elasticity of VKT with density alone is only -0.04 on average, whereas the literature suggests that the average effect of density, when used as a proxy for all 5Ds, is closer to -0.3
(
Stevens 2017
)
.

Moving beyond general density strategies to specific compact growth strategies is necessary to produce major reductions in household GHG emissions. Using household energy estimates for the 125 largest metropolitan statistical areas in the United States,
Lee and Lee 2014
find that doubling the population-weighted density of a metropolitan area is associated with a 48 percent reduction in household travel emissions and a 38 percent reduction in residential energy emissions. Note that these results do not mean that population density must be doubled across the metropolitan area. The concentration of density in specific areas can have a similar effect. If only 60 percent of new population growth were directed into compact growth areas, the cumulative effect would be equivalent to those same people driving a fuel-efficient hybrid vehicle in business-as-usual development patterns
(
Ewing et al. 2009
)
. In another study by
Lee and Lee 2020
finds strong support for planned density, which focuses development along commercial and transit corridors .
Suzuki et al. 2013
demonstrate this difference through a comparison of the linear density along major transit corridors in Curitiba, Brazil and unplanned density in Sao Paulo, Brazil.

Among the first studies to examine the relationship between built form and environmental impacts was by
Newman and Kenworthy 1989b
. They estimate gasoline consumption as a function of population density for several global cities (Figure
1
), finding that higher density cities tend to have lower associated gasoline consumption rates per capita. Their work has led to numerous subsequent studies that refine different dimensions of the comparison.
Ewing et al. 2018
expand the analysis to 157 US urbanized areas and perform multivariate analysis to explore confounding effects on the density and gasoline consumption (i.e., auto dependence) relationship. They find that the localized density of individual neighbourhoods is less critical than relative accessibility across the region. In a study for Toronto, Canada,
Pushkar et al. 2000
also find that regional land use structure has a greater influence on transportation GHG emissions than neighbourhood design features. Focusing on non-transportation factors,
(
Liu et al. 2017
)
find that ecosystems services (i.e., nitrogen and phosphorus purification, crop production, water supply, and soil retention) in China are controlled by anthropogenic activity and sociodemographic variation at a 12 km scale, but physical environmental effects are dominant at an 83 km scale. These findings are a first hint at the need for a multi-scale perspective on the relationship between land use and GHG outcomes.

Glaeser and Kahn 2010
and
Zheng et al. 2011
confirm the finding that denser built form leads to lower carbon footprints in the United States and China, respectively. However, the policy interpretation provided by Glaeser and Kahn is perplexing. They argue that land use regulations in lower-emitting cities in temperate climates tend to push households towards hotter and lower density cities, such as those in Texas and Arizona. Their implication is that public policy should encourage development in temperate climates and discourage urban growth in hotter climates. The practicality and costs associated with moving entire urban populations into existing high density urbanized areas is given short shrift in their analysis. Such macro analyses tend to discount planning measures to shift neighbourhoods across the intra-urban built form – i.e., redevelop high GHG-emitting neighbourhoods to shift them toward the development patterns in lower GHG-emitting neighbourhoods in the same urban area. Local planning solutions should recognize regional context and not rely on a global optimization assumption, which allocates the national population based on climatic conditions absent its significant transaction costs.

Jones and Kammen 2014
find lower household carbon footprints (HCFs) in urban areas and higher HCFs in outlying suburbs. They find a weak but positive effect from population density up to a threshold. Larger metros in the US have high HCFs, except in New York and Los Angeles. They hypothesize that outlying suburbs bump up the HCF values for many metros. Large principal cities have lower HCFs than smaller ones, but larger/denser suburbs have higher HCFs than smaller ones. They included in their model the number of vehicles per household, annual household income, carbon intensity of electricity, number of rooms (a proxy for home size, which is not available for zip codes), natural log of persons in the household, and log of population density of about 3,000 persons per square mile is reached, after which mean HCF declines logarithmically, leveling out at a lower limit of about 30 tCO2 per household (35 percent below average) at densities over 50,000 persons per square mile. Food tends to be a much larger share of emissions in urban cores, where transportation and energy emissions tend to be lower, and in rural areas, where household size tends to be higher.

Fragkias et al. 2013
provide countervailing results, finding that contrary to their theoretical expectation, larger cities are not more emissions-efficient than smaller ones. Their analysis is based on comparing two attributional approaches: production-based and consumption-based estimation. Production-based estimation attributes emissions to the production source. This approach tends to downweight urban contributions because production sectors (e.g., oil and gas extraction) tend to occur in non-urban areas. Their production-based estimates attribute 30-40 percent of total GHG emissions to urban areas in the United States. In contrast, consumption-based estimation attributes GHG emissions to the consumption source, leading to a higher (60 percent) attribution to urban areas. They use an earlier version of the GHG dataset used in the present study, which aggregates results to 10 km grid cells. Note also that their analysis unit is metropolitan areas, so the variance between production and consumption perspectives is largely a function of the total population in urban areas and GHG-intensive industrial activity generally being located outside large metropolitan areas. This caveat does not discount the role of cities in addressing climate change. As noted by
Dasgupta 2021
, the ”mobility of nature” means that environmental impacts are often felt well outside the point of activity (extraction). Rather, it highlights the need for more robust methods to capture the spatial nature of production-consumption interactions. For example, research using the detailed Hestia dataset compiled for Salt Lake City found that CO2 emissions from electricity generation often occur at remote locations, decoupling the spatial association between emissions and urban population patterns for this sector
(
Patarasuk et al. 2016
)
. In contrast, the same study found a slightly sublinear population density effect for on-road emissions.

The literature highlights the complex relationship between built form, household characteristics, and GHG emissions. Research in Helsinki, Finland finds that households in the denser urban core have higher life-cycle emissions than their suburban counterparts when considering transportation, dwellings, and goods consumption
(
Heinonen and Junnila 2011
)
. Their results give 14.7 and 12.0 life-cycle tCO2eq per capita for urban core and suburban households, respectively. However, this difference likely relates to differences in the pattern of household goods consumption. Rather than being a function of built form, the difference is largely driven by urban core households having higher incomes (about 33 percent higher than suburban households). Translating these results to the US also faces significant challenges as the energy consumption profiles of American and European cities are quite different
(
Mindali et al. 2004
)
. Most US cities exhibit the opposite relationship between income and density, and this effect is likely magnified by suburban households consuming goods at a higher rate than urban households due to the larger size of their dwellings
(
Murphy 2018
)
. In addition, the suburbs of Helsinki have a residential built form closer to that of central Minneapolis – only one-fifth of dwellings are single-family detached in the suburbs of Helsinki.
Wiedenhofer et al. 2013
give a more comparable example using Australian data. In that study, direct GHG emissions were higher in the suburbs due to longer travel times by private vehicle. However, indirect GHG emissions from goods consumption were generally lower for these households because they must spend a larger portion of their household income on transportation costs, leaving less income available for goods consumption.

In summary, we note that most existing studies are correlational rather than causal in their specification. Studies consider either local area built form or metropolitan land use effects, with we found no study that provides a comprehensive analysis of effects at multiple spatial scales. We address the causal identification gap through doubly robust generalized propensity score estimation. Multiple spatial scales are considered through the inclusion of land use variables defined at the local (block group) and metropolitan (core-based statistical area) scales. Furthermore, we distinguish between sources to understand the various effects of land use variables on emissions.

3
Data

A core dataset for this work is the Vulcan v4.0 fossil fuel-based CO2 (FFCO2) estimates constructed by
Gurney et al. 2020
. The Vulcan dataset comprises 1 km grid cell FFCO2 estimates for the United States between 2010-2021. The data are further disaggregated into the following source categories: on-road, nonroad, airport, commercial marine vessels, railroads, electricity production, residential nonpoint buildings, nonroad, airport, commercial nonpoint buildings, commercial point sources, industrial point sources, industrial nonpoint buildings, and cement. Estimates are provided at an hourly resolution, though we aggregate these estimates to annual totals. The Vulcan dataset has been refined and validated across multiple studies
(
Gurney et al. 2024
;
Gurney et al. 2020
;
Patarasuk et al. 2016
)
. It includes confidence bands derived from the uncertainty of the underlying input datasets. Validation includes comparison against three other estimates for the US domain: the CDIAC, EDGAR (v4.2 and v4.3) and the USEPA
(
Basu et al. 2020
)
. Vulcan v3.0 and the atmospheric-derived estimate for 2010 agreed to within 1.4% but departed significantly from the other three aggregated totals. Though the uncertainty bounds overlapped, the USEPA central estimate was 6% lower than the total Vulcan v3.0 FFCO2 emissions. The ODIAC dataset provides gridded estimates comparable to Vulcan v3.0
(
Oda et al. 2018
)
. In validation work,
Gurney et al. 2020
find that 2011 estimates for ODIAC and Vulcan are 1,453.5 TgC/year and 1,553.8 TgC/year, respectively, or a difference of 7.6%. The Vulcan estimates are further calibrated against atmospheric FFCO2 measurement based on NOAA’s air sampling network.
Gurney et al. 2021
compare Vulcan results to reports by 48 US cities and find these cities underreported their Scope 1 emissions by about 18.3 percent (range of -145.5 to +63.5 percent). If extrapolated to all US cities, this amounts to 123.5 percent of all California Scope 1 emissions by their estimates.

Recently, a supplemental dataset was released for scope 2 emissions for the 2019-2021 time period
(
Gurney et al. 2024
)
. Scope 2 emissions represent the GHG associated with electricity consumption by households and industry. Vulcan scope 2 data are a downscaling of US balancing Authority estimates by
de Chalendar and Benson 2021
.
de Chalendar and Benson 2021
uses economic input-output accounts to distribute electricity supply to household and industry sources of demand.
Gurney et al. 2024
then downscale these results to census block groups using floor area and energy intensity factors. CBG estimates are validated by comparison with an EIA electricity consumption survey.

A second core dataset is the EPA SmartLocation Database (EPA-SLD)
(
Chapman et al. 2021
)
. This dataset summarizes census block group (CBG)-level statistics related to demographics, employment, and built form variables for the United States, including measures of the 5Ds. The use of publicly available datasets (such as Vulcan and EPA-SLD) facilitates open science and extension of the proposed work by other researchers. The spatial analysis unit will be census block groups (CBG), such that grid cell data must be re-allocated among CBG. This allocation will be based on an areal interpolation and assumption of homogeneity within grid cells. For example, a CBG containing two grid cells will be assigned the weighted average of their statistic values as a function of the proportion of each grid cell area contained within that CBG (see Figure
2
). We used a similar approach in previous work on household consumption in the US to generate a uniform grid population density measure
(
Hawkins and Habib 2021
)
. Areal interpolation is a common technique in spatial analysis fields
(
Goodchild et al. 1993
;
Tsutsumi and Murakami 2014
;
Comber and Zeng 2019
)
. We chose to aggregate grid cells rather than CBG because the aggregation affects GHG estimates only. CBG statistics include both intensive (rate) and extensive (count) variables, which must be treated differently when performing areal interpolation. An auxiliary variable, population density, was used to apportion GHG emissions between CBGs because CBGs with a higher population density have more residents within a unit area.

4
Methods of Analysis

Using CBG-level observations of emissions, we are interested in the causal effect of the 5D land use metrics on FFCO2 emissions by sector. In this study, we focus on the emissions from transportation, residential electricity, and residential non-electricity sources. Among the causal identification strategies available for observations data, propensity score weighting is selected as the preferred option. This choice is motivated by several factors. The lack of a quasi-experimental interruption and panel observations of treatment variables limits the available options. The continuous treatments would be well suited to an instrumental variable (IV) approach, but identifying multiple suitable variables is a barrier to implementing IV. Propensity score weighting rather than matching is suitable in this case because there are multiple continuous treatments. Propensity score weighting lets us incorporate the propensity scores into either a doubly robust approach or regression model.

4.1
Confounder Variable Models

The first step in the DR approach is to determine appropriate propensity score variables. Selection is based on a combination of available data, prior knowledge, and data-driven inference. In addition to land use features available in the EPA Smart Location database, we compile county-level climate data available from NOAA including precipitation, temperature minimum and maximum, and heating and cooling degree days. We also use 5-year (2018-2023) American Community Survey data to capture variations in sociodemographics. Residential energy and electricity demand will be driven by heating and cooling needs and income. Marginal GHG emissions associated with electricity consumption will be a function of the grid mix, which we capture through state-level eGrid emissions factors. Transportation emissions are correlated with mode choice through vehicle ownership, household size, and other demographic variables. They are correlated with VKT through income and other demographic variables. Variables for considering in the propensity score are then filtered using correlation heatmap plots (available upon request) to remove highly correlated variables. From these subsets of variables, we use a data-driven approach to select the propensity score variables for each treatment.

Li et al. 2024

We use Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART), described further below, for the confounder model. A simple heuristic to quantify variable importance using BART is to count the number of times a variable is included in the regression trees. A plot of conditional prediction can be constructed by pruning trees from the BART model not containing the variable and calculating the corresponding R
2

We approximate their approach by partitioning the treatment space into deciles, then estimating models conditional upon treatment variable range. This approach gives us marginal contributions to prediction accuracy for each variable and the ten deciles. We sum these marginal contributions based on intuition paralleling that of
Li et al. 2024

We set a threshold of 0.05 representing variables describing at least 0.5% of the outcome variation on average across deciles. This threshold was deemed conservative and tested during the propensity score balancing step.

Propensity Score Balancing

We construct the propensity scores to ensure balance between treatment levels. Propensity score construction for continuous treatments is a challenging task. Many of the diagnostic tests developed for binary treatments are not applicable. Using the WeightIt package, we test parametric (CBPS) and nonparametric (NPCBPS) covariate balancing propensity score
(

4.3
Outcome model

A limitation of much causal research is its reliance on linear and logarithmic effect scaling. While these are appealing assumptions in their simplicity, they may not adequately capture the non-linear relationship between the treatment variable of interest and the outcome. Machine learning approaches address linearity and parametric critiques but suffer from their own set of limitations arising from their lack of casual interpretation. BART is an approach that balances the flexibility of machine learning against the interpretability of classical causal econometric methods.

Causal inference in the absence of controlled or natural experimental data is a challenge. Many causal methods for observational data rely on modelling the treatment assignment, then the outcome variable conditional on its treatment assignment and confounding covariates. As demonstrated in
(
Hill 2011
)
, BART provides a method to precisely model the outcome response surface . Consider a binary treatment variable
Z
and outcome variable
Y
, where the difference
Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)
is the difference between outcomes with treatment
(Z=1)
and without treatment
(Z=0)
. We do not observe both outcomes in observational studies, so the standard focus is on average treatment effects defined over a sample or population. The treatment ignorability assumption underlying causal inference relies on: 1) an unconfoundedness assumption,
Y\left(0\right),Y\left(1\right)\ \upmodels Z|X
, where
X
is a vector of pretreatment confounding variables and
\upmodels
denotes conditional independence; and 2) a common support (overlap) assumption,
0<Pr(Z=1|X)<1
. Causal estimation then relies on evaluating the response surface at both conditions,
E(Y|X,Z=1)
and
E(Y|X,Z=0)
. This estimation becomes complex when one considers that Y may not be linearly related to X and the distribution of X may be different across treatment groups. Hill (24) argued that the simple implementation, precision, and robustness of BART estimates outweigh the benefits of alternative nonparametric approaches that are more strictly consistent under certain sets of regularity conditions.
The BART causal identification strategy relies on specifying the difference with treatment for a general function
Y\ =\ f\left(z,x\right)+\epsilon

The method can be extended to a continuous treatment z by making predictions for treated observations (where a prediction is a posterior interval) both at their observed treatment values and given no treatment. The difference in outcomes is then a measure of causal effect.

Spatial Scaling Results

We present results for the relationship of FFCO2 emission outcomes with population density using standard log-log plots, interpretable as power law relationships. The same analysis was also conducted for the 5Ds of land use, with results omitted for brevity. Figure

6.1
Transportation Treatment Effects

Interpreting transportation results has a significant caveat due to the allocation assumption made in the Vulcan dataset. Vulcan allocates emissions to their point of generation. In the case of stationary sources, this approach is reasonable. However, transportation emissions are the outcome of trip generation at the origin location, which may be distant from the location of emissions. As such, CBG with desirable 5D features (high density, access to transit, etc.) also typically have high through traffic volumes that will skew transportation results. We discuss an approach to address this allocation assumption at the end of this paper, but leave it as a limitation of results herein.

The ATE transportation results are provided in Figure
6
as percent changes in per capita FFCO2 emissions for a one percent change in the treatment variable. By taking a Bayesian approach to inference we are able to specify distributions on the ATEs. We find no effect for population density but with a large variation. Land use diversity at the local level tends to increase FFCO2 emissions, whereas variations in the remaining land use variables tend to decrease FFCO2 emissions. Our ATE estimates for roadway design and destination accessibility are skewed to the left and right, respectively, such that the mean estimates are far from the bulk of the distribution. I.e., the mean estimates differ significantly from their median counterparts. Contrary to expectations, our ATE for transit suggests being further from transit results in lower FFCO2, likely due to the allocation approach taken in Vulcan and discussed above.

7
Conclusions

This study comprised two parts. The first focused on whether power laws exist in the FFCO2 emissions for various sectors at two spatial scales: CBSA (representing metropolitan areas) and CBG (representing neighbourhoods). We confirmed prior results by
Fragkias et al. 2013
that sublinear power law gains do not exist for total FFCO2 across metropolitan areas based on updated Vulcan data. However, we find such a relationship for scope 2 residential electricity emissions (0.85 with
R^{2}=0.83
). The second part of the study used a doubly robust (DR) causal inference strategy based on non-parametric propensity scores to esimate average treatment effects (ATE) for transportation, residential non-electricity energy, and residential electricity FFCO2 emissions and relevant 5D land use treatment variables.

There are two important extensions to this work that limit the current results. First, transportation GHG emissions are assigned to their point of production rather than home locations, such that treatment effects do not correspond to the effect of land use at the home location.
Glaeser and Kahn 2010
used the 2001 National Household Travel Survery (NHTS) to assign GHG emissions to home locations using the 5-digit home zipcode of the respondent. However, there are significant limitations to this approach: (1) the public 2017 NHTS does not provide zipcode ids, (2) there is significant land use heterogeneity at the 5-digit zipcode level (land areas may be anywhere from 5 to 300 km
2
), and (3) observations are sparse for many areas. A study might generate a synthetic population and calibrate between Vulcan- and NHTS-derived estimates. Second, there may be correlations between GHG production across outcomes (e.g., non-electricity energy emissions low when electricity energy emissions are high) that could be captured through a Copula model structure. However, our model estimation experience in the present work suggests that it would be challenging to identify the parameters of such a complex model.
