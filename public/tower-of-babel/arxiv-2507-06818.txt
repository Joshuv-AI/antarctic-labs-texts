The Bispectrum of Intrinsic Alignments: II. Precision Comparison Against Dark Matter Simulations
Thomas Bakx †,1, Toshiki Kurita 2,3, Alexander Eggemeier 4, Nora Elisa Chisari 1,5, Zvonimir Vlah 6,7,8

Abstract
We measure three-dimensional bispectra of halo intrinsic alignments (IA) and dark matter overdensities in real space from N-body simulations for halos of mass 10 12 − 10 12.5 ​ M ⊙ / h 10^{12}-10^{12.5}M_{\odot}/h . We show that their multipoles with respect to the line of sight can be accurately described by a tree-level perturbation theory model on large scales ( k ≲ 0.11 ​ h k\lesssim 0.11\,h /Mpc) at z = 0 z=0 . For these scales and in a simulation volume of 1 Gpc/ h h , we detect the bispectrum monopole B δ ​ δ ​ E 00 B_{\delta\delta E}^{00} at ∼ 30 ​ σ \sim 30\sigma and the two quadrupoles B δ ​ δ ​ E 11 B_{\delta\delta E}^{11} and B δ ​ δ ​ E 20 B_{\delta\delta E}^{20} at ∼ 25 ​ σ \sim 25\sigma and ∼ 15 ​ σ \sim 15\sigma , respectively. We also report similar detection significances for the lowest order multipoles of B δ ​ E ​ E B_{\delta EE} and B E ​ E ​ E B_{EEE} , although these are largely driven by stochastic contributions. We show that the first and second order EFT parameters are consistent with those obtained from fitting the IA power spectrum analysis at next-to-leading order, without requiring any priors to break degeneracies for the quadratic bias parameters. Moreover, the inclusion of higher multipole moments of B δ ​ δ ​ E B_{\delta\delta E} greatly reduces the errors on second order bias parameters, by factors of 5 or more. The IA bispectrum thus provides an effective means of determining higher order shape bias parameters, thereby characterizing the scale dependence of the IA signal. We also detect parity-odd bispectra such as B δ ​ δ ​ B B_{\delta\delta B} and B δ ​ E ​ B B_{\delta EB} at ∼ 10 ​ σ \sim 10\sigma significance or more for k < 0.15 ​ h k<0.15\,h /Mpc and they are fully consistent with the parity-even sector. Furthermore, we check that the Gaussian covariance approximation works reasonably well on the scales we consider here. These results lay the groundwork for using the bispectrum of IA in cosmological analyses.

In order to successfully extract all cosmological and astrophysical information from large-scale structure, these developments must be accompanied by an improved theoretical understanding of the statistical properties of galaxy populations on cosmological scales.

In stark contrast to the (primary) anisotropies of the cosmic microwave background, the distribution of LSS is highly non-Gaussian due to nonlinear gravitational evolution of the initial density perturbations. Hence, the statistics of LSS are not captured by only the two-point correlation function and linear theory does not suffice except at very large separations. This has triggered a surge in the study of statistics of LSS beyond the power spectrum, the simplest of which is the bispectrum, i.e. the Fourier transform of the three-point correlation function

2.2
Flat Sky Projection

Not all five degrees of freedom of the shape field can be discerned by an observer at a fixed location. Instead, only two degrees of freedom in
g_{ij}
are observable. They constitute a spin-2 field on the sky which is commonly decomposed into E- and B-modes in Fourier space. Throughout this work we will use the flat-sky approximation to construct projected observables. To recap the findings of

32

(10)

\gamma_{ij,I}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{P}^{ijkl}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})g_{kl}(\mathbf{x}).

(11)

\gamma_{\pm 2}(\mathbf{x}):=(\mathbf{M}^{\pm 2}_{ij})^{*}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})\gamma_{ij,I}(\mathbf{x})

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{ij}^{(\pm 2)}(\hat{\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{m}_{i}^{\pm}\mathbf{m}_{j}^{\pm};

(13)

where
\mathbf{m}_{1}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}
,

\mathbf{m}^{\pm}:=\mp\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\mathbf{m}_{1}\mp\mathrm{i}\mathbf{m}_{2})

2.3
Bispectrum Multipoles

The bispectrum of tensor fields admits a multipole decomposition as detailed in Paper I. There it was argued that a separable local parallel-plane Yamamoto estimator requires the use of
associated
Legendre polynomials to define the multipole moments
(
38
;
39
)
. In that work, it was also shown that differences between the use of ordinary and associated Legendre polynomials are small and that associated Legendre polynomials appear better suited for
B_{\delta\delta E}
(at least in real space, which is the case we restrict to here). For that reason, we will only consider associated Legendre multipoles (ALMs) here. We thus also omit the superscript ‘A’ on the multipoles that was present in Paper I to distinguish between ordinary and associated Legendre multipoles, and simply refer to the ALMs as multipoles. Specifically, defining the even and odd projectors

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{XYZ}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}(\{\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{i}\})_{\text{even}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{m_{X}}_{\ell_{1}}(\mu_{1})\mathcal{L}^{m_{Y}}_{\ell_{2}}(\mu_{2})\mathcal{L}^{m_{Z}}_{m_{Z}}(\mu_{3});

(24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{XYZ}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}(\{\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{i}\})_{\text{odd}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{m_{X}}_{\ell_{1}}(\mu_{1})\mathcal{L}^{m_{Y}}_{\ell_{2}}(\mu_{2})\mathcal{L}^{m_{Z}}_{m_{Z}}(\mu_{3})

\displaystyle\times\big(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot(\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{1}\times\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{2})\big),

the multipoles are given as

\displaystyle B^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}_{XYZ}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}}{4\pi}\int\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}\mathrm{d}\xi\bigg(\mathcal{P}_{XYZ}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}(\{\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{i}\})

(25)

\displaystyle\times B_{XYZ}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},\mu_{1},\xi)\bigg)

with the normalization constant

N_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}=(2\ell_{1}+1)(2\ell_{2}+1).

(26)

Here the integration is over the angles
\mu_{1}
and
\xi
as first introduced in
(
40
)
, which describe the orientation of the bispectrum with respect to the line of sight. This orientation requires only two angles to be specified, because statistical isotropy demands that there is no dependence on any azimuthal rotations around the line of sight.
The
\mu_{i}
-dependence is captured via the associated Legendre polynomials
\mathcal{L}^{m}_{\ell}(\mu_{i})
. We only need two independent multipole indices to capture all angular dependence, since the third angle
\mu_{3}
is always linearly dependent on the other two. In Paper I, we also showed in App. B that a double expansion in powers of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
is equivalent to the one in terms of spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell m}(\mu_{1},\xi)
.

3.3
Discreteness and Binning Effects

It is customary to replace the discrete sum over wavevectors in the estimator (
27
) by a continuous integration, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{\Delta}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{q}_{i}\in\text{bin }i}\delta^{K}(\mathbf{q}_{123})B(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},\mathbf{q}_{3})

(34)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{V_{\Delta}}\int_{k_{1,2,3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{q}_{1,2,3}\,\delta^{D}(\mathbf{q}_{123})B(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},\mathbf{q}_{3});

where

(35)

This integration extends both over the angles that specify the orientation of the triangle as well as the ‘radial bin’ that specifies the length of wavevectors. We will refer to this as the ‘integral approximation’

7

7

Crucially, we do not replace the volume

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V_{\Delta}}

\displaystyle\int_{k_{1,2,3}}\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{q}_{1,2,3}\,\delta^{D}(\mathbf{q}_{123})B(\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{2},\mathbf{q}_{3})

(36)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}\mathrm{d}\xi\,B(k^{\text{eff}}_{1},k^{\text{eff}}_{2},k^{\text{eff}}_{3},\mu_{1},\xi).

We assume that the likelihood

(
28

28

\displaystyle=0.129,\quad\sigma_{2,2}^{\text{fid}}=0.008;

5
Conclusions and Discussion

We have seen that the tree-level bispectrum in the EFT of IA accurately captures the rich scale- and angle dependence of the bispectrum of IA. In particular, a five-parameter model involving four deterministic bias parameters occurring in the second order ‘TATT’ expansion and an additional noise term provides a good fit to the different bispectra considered in Section
4
up to
k_{\text{max}}=0.11\,h/\text{Mpc}
. Moreover, the parameters that are shared with the power spectrum of matter and shape fields match those from the power spectrum analysis, and the noise parameter is close to its Poisson expectation. This is a nontrivial achievement, given that the cumulative SNR of all bispectra in a single volume of
V=1\,(\text{Gpc}/h)^{3}
is around
\approx 30
(cf. Figure
3
) for
k_{\text{max}}=0.11\,h/\text{Mpc}
.

Note that simpler models for the scale dependence of intrinsic alignment bispectra fall short here; for example according to NLA-inspired models
(
19
;
36
)
there is no B-mode and we would have
B_{\delta\delta B}=0
. However, we have clearly demonstrated that this bispectrum is nonzero. In addition, our work indicates that bispectra with more than one shape field are strongly dependent on the noise amplitude
\alpha_{\text{gg}}^{1\delta}
. This contribution was missing in the earlier second-order (i.e. ‘TATT’) alignment models of
(
35
;
60
)
. We emphasize that the importance of this contribution will only be exacerbated as we consider more realistic samples with lower number densities and lower alignment amplitudes. To this end, it is reassuring that several different bispectra can in principle be combined in order to jointly constrain noise amplitudes. This is also important when attempting to use the bispectrum of IA for mitigation purposes in cosmic shear surveys. Generally speaking, other models for IA which are inspired by the galaxy bias expansion are also capable of providing physically motivated templates for the IA bispectrum that can be used in conjunction with two-point statistics. This may allow for more efficient nuisance parameter choices
(
59
)
. Alternatively, using the Lagrangian framework,
(
61
;
62
)
showed that a perturbative description of intrinsic alignments can be used to place conservative constraints on the amplitude of matter fluctuations
S_{8}\equiv\sigma_{8}(\Omega_{m}/0.3)^{0.5}
.We anticipate that the bispectrum of IA can be a useful tool in directly constraining the intrinsic alignment of galaxy samples when spectroscopic redshifts are available
(
26
;
27
)
.

Moreover, we have seen that higher multipoles are instrumental in breaking degeneracies amongst second order bias parameters (cf. Figure
6
). This could not be established by only considering the SNR of these multipoles, as we did in Paper I. The different multipoles thus provide an efficient way of incorporating information from the full, anisotropic bispectrum
B_{\delta\delta E}
. We also found that when adding bispectra with two shape fields, constraints on bias parameters improved significantly (cf. Figure
5
). However, this conclusion may depend strongly on the aforementioned sample properties such as alignment amplitude and shot noise.

Our results motivate a more in-depth study of joint constraints wrought from the power spectrum and bispectrum of IA for a more realistic halo or galaxy sample. This can be done by downsampling halos from an N-body simulation and randomizing their orientations to mimic misalignment of the LRG with respect to its host
(
63
;
64
)
, or by considering a galaxy population from a large-scale hydrodynamical simulation such as
(
65
)
. We will explore these options in future work.

Theory Expressions for Tree-level Tensor Bispectrum

The tree-level tensor bispectrum contains a deterministic and a stochastic piece.
The
n
-th order term in Fourier space for the deterministic piece takes the form

S_{ij}^{(n)}(\mathbf{k})=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}^{3}\mathbf{p}_{n}\,\mathcal{K}_{ij}^{(n)}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots\mathbf{p}_{n})\delta^{D}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{p}_{1,n})\delta^{(1)}(\mathbf{p}_{1})\dots\delta^{(1)}(\mathbf{p}_{n})

(55)

where

\mathcal{K}_{ij}^{(n)}=\mathcal{K}_{ij}^{\text{s},(n)}+\mathcal{K}_{ij}^{\text{g},(n)}

(56)

is the decomposition into trace- and trace-free parts and
\delta^{(1)}
is the linear matter density field. The total deterministic bispectrum at tree-level considered in this work is thus

\displaystyle B_{ijklrs}^{\alpha\beta\gamma,\text{det,tree}}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},\mathbf{k}_{3})

\displaystyle=2\,\mathcal{K}_{ij}^{\alpha,(1)}(\mathbf{k}_{1})\mathcal{K}_{kl}^{\beta,(1)}(\mathbf{k}_{2})\mathcal{K}_{rs}^{\gamma,(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})P_{L}(k_{1})P_{L}(k_{2})+\text{2 perm.}

(57)

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are either
\delta
or g. Since we only consider the matter density field in the scalar sector, the scalar kernels are trivial. Explicitly, the first order kernels are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{ij}^{\delta,(1)}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij};

(58)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{ij}^{\text{g},(1)}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=b_{1}^{\text{g}}\big(\frac{\mathbf{k}_{i}\mathbf{k}_{j}}{k^{2}}-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}\big)=b_{1}^{\text{g}}\mathcal{D}_{ij}(\mathbf{k});

where in the second line we introduced the trace-free Fourier space operator
\mathcal{D}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})=\mathbf{k}_{i}\mathbf{k}_{j}/k^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}
.
The second order kernels are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{ij}^{\delta,(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}F_{2}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2});

(59)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{ij}^{\text{g},(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})

\displaystyle=\text{TF}\bigg[c_{1}^{\text{g}}\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{k}_{12,i}\mathbf{k}_{12,j}}{k_{12}^{2}}F_{2}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})\bigg)+c_{2,1}^{\text{g}}\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{k}_{12,i}\mathbf{k}_{12,j}}{k_{12}^{2}}F_{2}(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2})-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathbf{k}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{k}_{2}}{k_{1}^{2}k_{2}^{2}}(\mathbf{k}_{1,i}\mathbf{k}_{1,j}+\mathbf{k}_{2,i}\mathbf{k}_{2,j})\bigg)

\displaystyle+c_{2,2}^{\text{g}}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathbf{k}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{k}_{2}}{k_{1}^{2}k_{2}^{2}}(\mathbf{k}_{1,i}\mathbf{k}_{2,j}+\mathbf{k}_{2,i}\mathbf{k}_{1,j})\bigg)+c_{2,3}^{\text{g}}\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{k}_{1,i}\mathbf{k}_{1,j}}{2k_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\mathbf{k}_{2,i}\mathbf{k}_{2,j}}{2k_{2}^{2}}\bigg)\bigg]

where
\mathbf{k}_{12}=\mathbf{k}_{1}+\mathbf{k}_{2}
and
F_{2}
is the usual the second order scalar kernel.

In the notation of
(
33
)
, the coefficients
c_{1}^{\text{g}},c_{2,a}^{\text{g}}(a=1,2,3)
belong to the trace-free parts of the operators

\Pi^{[1]}_{ij},\Pi^{[2]}_{ij},(\Pi^{[1]})^{2}_{ij},\Pi^{[1]}_{ij}\text{Tr}(\Pi^{[1]}_{ij})

(60)

respectively.
We work with a redefinition of shape bias coefficients which is given by

\displaystyle b_{1}^{\text{g}}

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{\text{g}};\qquad b_{2,1}^{\text{g}}=c_{2,1}^{\text{g}}+c_{2,2}^{\text{g}}+c_{2,3}^{\text{g}};\qquad b_{2,2}^{\text{g}}=c_{2,3}^{\text{g}}+\frac{20}{7}c_{2,1}^{\text{g}};\qquad b_{2,3}^{\text{g}}=c_{2,3}^{\text{g}}.

(61)

The B-mode does not depend on
b_{2,2}^{\text{g}}
, which automatically implies that bispectra containing any B-mode do not depend on
b_{2,2}^{\text{g}}
at tree level (since the B-mode starts at second order). We also use the relation to another basis used consisting of the operators
b_{\delta K},b_{K^{2}}
and
b_{t}
, which is

b_{\delta K}=\frac{1}{3}(2b_{2,1}^{\text{g}}+b_{2,3}^{\text{g}}),\quad b_{K^{2}}=b_{2,1}^{\text{g}}-b_{2,3}^{\text{g}},\quad b_{t}=\frac{7}{8}(b^{\text{g}}_{2,3}-b^{\text{g}}_{2,2}).

(62)

We will need the co-evolution relations for the second order parameters in terms of
b_{1}^{\text{s}}
and
b_{1}^{\text{g}}
, which are derived under the assumption that the Lagrangian higher order bias parameters are zero. They read

b_{2,1}^{\text{g}}=(b_{1}^{\text{s}}-1)b_{1}^{\text{g}};\qquad b_{2,2}^{\text{g}}=(b_{1}^{\text{s}}-20/7)b_{1}^{\text{g}};\qquad b_{2,3}^{\text{g}}=b_{1}^{\text{s}}b_{1}^{\text{g}}.

(63)

For the stochastic piece, all the relevant correlators were collected in Paper I. The only nonzero stochastic contribution we can form with the stochastic shape contributions

g_{ij}^{\text{stoch}}(\mathbf{x})\qquad\supset\qquad\epsilon_{ij}(\mathbf{x})\text{ (first order)};\qquad\epsilon_{ij}^{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\delta(\mathbf{x}),\quad\epsilon^{\Pi}(\mathbf{x})\text{TF}(\Pi^{[1]})_{ij}(\mathbf{x})\text{ (second order) }

(64)

and which occurs in the projected tree-level bispectrum is

\langle\epsilon_{ij}(\mathbf{k}_{1})\epsilon_{kl}^{\delta}(\mathbf{k}_{2})\rangle^{\prime}=\alpha_{\text{gg}}^{1\delta}\Delta_{ij,kl}^{\text{TF}}+\mathcal{O}((k/k_{\text{NL}})^{2});

(65)

where

\Delta_{ij,kl}^{\text{TF}}=\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\frac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}

(66)

is pair-symmetric and pair-traceless.

B.2
Undoing chi-squared bias when using the diagonal sample covariance

Using a noisy estimate for the covariance in a Gaussian likelihood can lead to underestimated errors on parameters if not properly taken into account. In particular, following
(
46
)
we see that in order to construct an unbiased estimator of the true log-likelihood

\chi^{2}_{\text{true}}=-2\log\mathcal{L}=\sum_{L,L^{\prime}}^{k_{\text{max}}}(\hat{\bar{B}}_{L}-B_{L}^{\text{th}})(C^{\text{true}}_{LL^{\prime}})^{-1}(\hat{\bar{B}}_{L^{\prime}}-B_{L^{\prime}}^{\text{th}})

(68)

we cannot use the inverse of the diagonal sample variance of Eq. (
32
). Indeed,

\bigg\langle\sum_{L,L^{\prime}}^{k_{\text{max}}}(\hat{\bar{B}}_{L}-B_{L}^{\text{th}})(\hat{C}_{LL^{\prime}})^{-1}(\hat{\bar{B}}_{L^{\prime}}-B_{L^{\prime}}^{\text{th}})\bigg\rangle=\sum_{L,L^{\prime}}^{k_{\text{max}}}\langle(\hat{\bar{B}}_{L}-B_{L}^{\text{th}})(\hat{\bar{B}}_{L^{\prime}}-B_{L^{\prime}}^{\text{th}})\rangle\langle(\hat{C}_{LL^{\prime}})^{-1}\rangle=\frac{N_{r}-1}{N_{r}-N_{L}-2}\langle\chi^{2}_{\text{true}}\rangle

(69)

where in the second equality we used the independence of the sample mean and sample variance to factor the expectation value
(
47
)
and
N_{L}
is the number of multipoles. The Hartlap prefactor needs to be applied to every block in multipole space in order to get an unbiased estimate of the true precision matrix, where the size of the sample covariance is
N_{L}\times N_{L}
(assuming that the true covariance is diagonal in triangle space). This factor is greater than unity, indicating that error bars will be underestimated if this correction is not applied. In the limit where
N_{r}\gg N_{L}
, this prefactor approaches 1 as expected.

Naively, one might expect that a similar correction should be applied to the goodness-of-fit statistic, that is, to obtain an unbiased estimator of

\bar{\chi}_{\text{true}}^{2}=\frac{1}{N_{r}}\sum_{L,L^{\prime},r}^{k_{\text{max}}}(\hat{B}^{r}_{L}-B_{L}^{\text{th}})(C^{\text{true}}_{LL^{\prime}})^{-1}(\hat{B}^{r}_{L^{\prime}}-B_{L^{\prime}}^{\text{th}}),

(70)

we should multiply the ‘naive’ expression (where the hat indicates that we are using a sample covariance)

\hat{\bar{\chi}}^{2}=\frac{1}{N_{r}}\sum_{L,L^{\prime},r}^{k_{\text{max}}}(\hat{B}^{r}_{L}-B_{L}^{\text{th}})(\hat{C}_{LL^{\prime}})^{-1}(\hat{B}^{r}_{L^{\prime}}-B_{L^{\prime}}^{\text{th}})

(71)

by the reciprocal of Eq. (
33
). However, this intuition is not correct. The reason is that while the sample mean
\hat{\bar{B}}_{L}
and the sample covariance
\hat{C}_{LL^{\prime}}
are independent, the same is not true for the individual realizations
\hat{B}^{r}_{L}
and the expectation does not factorize (this property was used in
(
46
)
). However, we can still compute the expectation value of Eq. (
71
) (when the covariance is taken to be the uncorrected block diagonal sample variance from Eq. (
32
)). To wit, after some algebra we obtain

\hat{\bar{\chi}}^{2}=\hat{\chi}^{2}+\frac{1}{N_{r}}\sum_{L,L^{\prime},r}^{k_{\text{max}}}(\hat{B}^{r}_{L}-\hat{\bar{B}}_{L})(\hat{C}_{LL^{\prime}})^{-1}(\hat{B}^{r}_{L^{\prime}}-\hat{\bar{B}}_{L^{\prime}}).

(72)

without taking the expectation value, where

\hat{\chi}^{2}=\sum_{L,L^{\prime}}^{k_{\text{max}}}(\hat{\bar{B}}_{L}-B_{L}^{\text{th}})(\hat{C}_{LL^{\prime}})^{-1}(\hat{\bar{B}}_{L^{\prime}}-B_{L^{\prime}}^{\text{th}}).

(73)

However, when
\hat{C}
is the sample covariance, the second term in Eq. (
72
) is
independent of the data
and simply reads

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{r}}\sum_{L,L^{\prime},r}^{k_{\text{max}}}(\hat{B}^{r}_{L}-\hat{\bar{B}}_{L})(\hat{C}_{LL^{\prime}})^{-1}(\hat{B}^{r}_{L^{\prime}}-\hat{\bar{B}}_{L^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{r}}\sum_{L,L^{\prime}}^{k_{\text{max}}}(\hat{C}_{LL^{\prime}})^{-1}\sum_{r}(\hat{B}^{r}_{L}-\hat{\bar{B}}_{L})(\hat{B}^{r}_{L^{\prime}}-\hat{\bar{B}}_{L^{\prime}})

(74)

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{r}-1}{N_{r}}\sum_{L,L^{\prime}}^{k_{\text{max}}}(\hat{C}_{LL^{\prime}})^{-1}\hat{C}_{LL^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N_{T}N_{L}(N_{r}-1)}{N_{r}}

after plugging in Eq. (
32
). Thus, after taking the expectation value of Eq. (
72
) and using Eq. (
69
) we obtain

\langle\hat{\bar{\chi}}^{2}\rangle=\langle\hat{\chi}^{2}\rangle+\frac{N_{T}N_{L}(N_{r}-1)}{N_{r}}=\frac{N_{r}-1}{N_{r}}\bigg(\frac{N_{r}\langle\chi_{\text{true}}^{2}\rangle}{N_{r}-N_{L}-2}+N_{T}N_{L}\bigg).

(75)

Similarly, in case of using true covariance
C^{\text{true}}
, the analog of Eq. (
72
) still holds, and after taking expectation values on both sides and using
\langle\bar{\chi}_{\text{true}}^{2}\rangle=N_{T}N_{L}
we obtain

\langle\bar{\chi}_{\text{true}}^{2}\rangle=\langle\chi_{\text{true}}^{2}\rangle+\frac{N_{T}N_{L}(N_{r}-1)}{N_{r}}\implies N_{r}\langle\chi_{\text{true}}^{2}\rangle=N_{r}\langle\bar{\chi}_{\text{true}}^{2}\rangle-N_{T}N_{L}(N_{r}-1)=N_{T}N_{L}.

(76)

Finally, after inserting the above equality into the expression for
\langle\hat{\bar{\chi}}^{2}\rangle
and dividing by the number of degrees of freedom
N_{T}N_{L}
we infer that

\hat{\mu}_{\text{red}}\equiv\langle\hat{\bar{\chi}}_{\text{red}}^{2}\rangle=\frac{\langle\hat{\bar{\chi}}^{2}\rangle}{N_{T}N_{L}}=\frac{N_{r}-1}{N_{r}}\bigg(1+\frac{1}{N_{r}-N_{L}-2}\bigg),

(77)

where the hat on
\hat{\mu}_{\text{red}}
indicates that the block diagonal sample covariance is used in
\hat{\chi}^{2}
. Typically, when
N_{r}\gg N_{L}
and
N_{r}\gg 1
the difference between Eq. (
77
) and unity is
\mathcal{O}(N_{L}/N_{r}^{2})\ll 1
. However, this correction can be at the percent level when e.g.
N_{r}=20
and
N_{L}=5
. More importantly, the variance of
\hat{\bar{\chi}}^{2}
is

17

To be more precise, the correct distribution of
\hat{\chi}^{2}
is
(
66
)

where all the

are independent and
T^{2}
-distributed. As such, the variance of the sum is given by (see e.g.

That is, the scatter in the reduced chi-squared computed

smaller
when using the sample covariance than when using a noiseless covariance. To our knowledge, this result has not been appreciated in the literature thus far. On the other hand, if one does not sum the individual realizations but rather uses the mean data vector (with the appropriately rescaled covariance), the above calculation shows that the expectation of
