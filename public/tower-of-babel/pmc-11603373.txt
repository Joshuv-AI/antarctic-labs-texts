High performance, pH-resistant membranes for efficient lithium recovery from spent batteries
Authors: Yafei Su, Huawen Peng, Xufei Liu, Jiapeng Li, Qiang Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCation separation under extreme pH is crucial for lithium recovery from spent batteries, but conventional polyamide membranes suffer from pH-induced hydrolysis. Preparation of high performance nanofiltration membranes with excellent pH-resistance remains a challenge. Here we synthesize a high performance nanofiltration membrane (1,4,7,10-Tetraazacyclododecane (TAD)−1,3,5-Tris(bromomethyl)benzene (TBMB) thin film composite membranes (TFCMs)) with excellent pH-stability through interfacial quaternization reaction between TAD and TBMB. Due to the high stability of “C-N” bonds in TAD-TBMB TFCMs, its separation performance is stable even after 70 days immersion in concentrated acid (3 M H2SO4, HNO3, or HCl) and base (3 M NaOH), which is at least 15 times more stable than benchmark commercial membranes. The membrane shows an overall separation performance (11.3 L m−2 h−1 bar−1 (LMHB), RCo2+: 97% in 2 M H2SO4) due to the size sieving and the intensified charge repulsion, outperforming many of the state-of-the-art membranes. Finally, the TAD-TBMB TFCM remains stable during 30-days continuous nanofiltration of 2 M H2SO4 and leachate (2 M H2SO4, ions: 6.2 g L−1) from spent batteries.Subject terms: Polymers, Soft materials

Introduction

Lithium-based new energy is rising rapidly to combat global greenhouse gas emissions and accomplish carbon neutrality1, leading to the increasing use of lithium batteries in electric vehicles, consumable electronics and energy storages1–5. It is anticipated that lithium batteries will share 70% of the rechargeable battery market in 20256,7, giving rising to $139.3 billion global market by 20266,8. Consequently, about 250,000 tons of spent batteries (cathode material accounts for 40%) are being discharged as solid wastes every year9, which not only pollute environment but also exaggerate lithium shortage. For example, the lithium demand of new energy industry will exceed the current mining by 204010–12. Thus, recycling methods with high efficiency, eco-friendliness and economic feasibility are crucial for the sustainable development of new energy13–16.

The pyrometallurgy-based method of spent batteries recycle17, such as that practiced in Umicore and Sony-Sumitomo, involves high temperature (1473-1723 K) and loss of Li and Mn in slag or fly ash18–20. For the widely used hydrometallurgy method18,21, cathodes of spent batteries are leached by concentrated acid (e.g. 2–3 M H2SO4) and/or base (e.g. 28 wt% ammonia), resulting in an acidic/base solution containing mixed ions such as Li+, Ni2+, Co2+, and Mn2+ 21,22. Then these ions are separated by solvent extraction or precipitation23, requiring multiple neutralization treatments and/or large amounts of organic solvents such as Cyanex272 or P50723,24. Given these constraints, efficient separation of ions at extreme pH (<0 or >14) conditions is urgently demanded24,25. Recently, polyamide nanofiltration membranes are widely studied for separating mono and divalent cations at mild pH conditions (pH 2-11)26–33, such as the Mg2+/Li+ separation which is crucial for lithium extraction from salt lake brines34–38. These membranes were synthesized from the interfacial polymerization of amine monomers with acryl monomers39–43, and contain amide bonds that degrade rapidly under extreme pH conditions44–47. Thus conventional polyamide membranes are inapplicable for ion separation from anode leachates containing concentrated acids. Other acid-stable membranes such as polysulfonamide48–50, triazine polyamine51–53 and polyurea membranes54–56 are emerging, but membranes stable in both the acid and base conditions are very limited, and they suffer from low permeance (<5 LMHB). Thus, the preparation of nanofiltration membranes featuring high separation performance and excellent pH-stability is of significant importance, which remains a formidable challenge requiring advanced chemistry for membrane synthesis.

This work synthesized high performance, pH-resistant ion separation membranes, and explored them to recycle lithium from spent batteries. A TAD monomer was designed to react with TBMB at the water-mesitylene interface, resulting in TAD-TBMB TFCMs linked by stable “C-N” bonds instead of amenable amide bonds. As such, the performance of TAD-TBMB TFCMs remains stable after being soaked in 3 M acid solution (70 days) and 3 M NaOH solution (70 days), 15 times more stable than MPS-34 membrane that is the most widely used acid-resistant commercial membrane. The membrane remains stable in 30 days continous filtration of the leachate solution (2 M H2SO4, 6.2 g L−1 ions) of spent battery cathodes.

Results and discussion

Characterizations of TAD-TBMB TFCMs

Figure 1a schemes the “TAD-TBMB” interfacial Hoffmann alkylation reaction, whereby “C-N” bonds form between the two monomers and build up the network. A thin film formed on the water-mesitylene interface containing the two monomers (Fig. 1b), which verifies the occurrence of “TAD-TBMB” interfacial reaction. Chemical structures of the film were stable after 10-days immersion in 3 M H2SO4 (Supplementary Fig. 1) or 3 M NaOH, which is due to the high pH-resistance of the formed “C-N” bonds (Supplementary Fig. 2). The high stability of “C-N” bonds is consistent with literature reports57. Then the “TAD-TBMB” interfacial reaction was conducted on polyacrylonitrile supporting membranes to prepare TFCMs. The effect of reaction time on surface morphology, crosslinking degree and thickness of TFCMs was studied (Fig. 1c–i).

As shown by arrows in Fig. 1c, d, defects are seen on TAD-TBMB TFCMs formed in the first 4-h reaction, while such defects are absent from membranes formed in 8-h reaction (Fig. 1e). Moreover, nanoaggregates appear on the membrane surface and grow bigger with increasing the reaction time to 10 h (Fig. 1f) and 20 h (Fig. 1g). Figure 1h shows that the surface roughness of TFCMs decreases from 19.8 nm to 7.4 nm during the first 8-h reaction, and then increases to 9.7 nm during the 8–20 h reaction. Meanwhile, the reaction degree of TAD increases rapidly during the first 8-h reaction, and then increases slowly in the subsequent 8–20 h reaction (Fig. 1h). The membrane thickness shows a similar dependence on reaction time, i.e., the thickness increases rapidly in the first 8-h reaction and then slowly (Fig. 1i, Supplementary Fig. 3). Based on these results, the two-stage formation of TAD-TBMB TFCM was schemed (Fig. 1j). A root-like discontinuous layer forms in the Stage I (0–4 h) and the active layer grows epitaxially on the root-like sites to seal defects (>4 h). Considering both the permeance and Co2+ rejection (RCo2+), TAD-TBMB TFCMs were prepared at 8 h reaction time and 2 wt% TAD concentration (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Figure 2a shows that the TAD-TBMB TFCM reveals new peaks at 1210 and 702 cm−1, which are assigned to “C–N” and “C–Br” bonds57,58, respectively. In addition, the Br mass content in the TAD-TBMB TFCMs decreases with increasing the TAD-TBMB reaction time (Supplementary Fig. 5). These result confirms the occurrence of TAD-TBMB interfacial reaction, which is consistent with the XPS characterization of the TAD-TBMB TFCMs (Supplementary Fig. 6). The zeta potential of TAD-TBMB TFCM is stable with increasing pH from 7 to 10, while the zeta potential of PEI-TMC TFCMs decreases rapidly with increasing pH (Fig. 2b). This is because: 1) the TAD-TBMB TFCM contains no hydrolysable groups that yields negative COOH groups, and 2) its positive charge arises from the quaternary ammonium that is more stable. The molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) of TAD-TBMB TFCM is 353 Da (Fig. 2c), and its mean pore size is 4.2 Å (Supplementary Fig. 7). Both values are close to that of PEI-TMC TFCM, a control membrane widely used for cations separation59,60. The surface hydrophilicity of the TAD-TBMB TFCM is slightly better than PEI-TMC TFCM (Fig. 2d).

Nanofiltration performance of TAD-TBMB TFCMs

As seen from Fig. 3a, permeance of TAD-TBMB TFCMs for different feed solution are close (11.3-12.5 LMHB). Meanwhile, the membrane shows high rejection (~95%) to divalent cations (Ni2+, Co2+, Mn2+, Ba2+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Cu2+) and relatively low rejection (~40%) to monovalent ions (Li+, Na+ or K+.). The positively charged TAD-TBMB membrane exert a stronger repulsion to divalents ions than to monovalent ions, contributing to the higher rejection of divalent ions as a result of charge repulsion effect. In addition, the monovalent ions in the feed have lower hydration energy and are prone to dehydration during the nanofiltration process, thus the membrane (mean pore size: 0.42 nm) allow the pass of dehydrated monovalent ions with smaller effective sizes61. The slightly higher permeance of monovalent ions than divalent ions can be explained by enhanced concentration polarization according to the osmotic pressure model62. Moreover, both the permeance and salt rejection of TAD-TBMB TFCM are stable versus 1–5 bar pressure (Supplementary Fig. 8) and 30-days nanofiltration (Supplementary Fig. 9). Then the TAD-TBMB TFCMs was continuously filtrated by Co2+/Li+, Ni2+/Li+ and Mn2+/Li+ mixtures dissolved in 2 M H2SO4 and neutral water, respectively (Fig. 3b). For the acidic feed, the Co2+/Li+, Ni2+/Li+ and Mn2+/Li+ selectivity are 26.5, 22.3 and 19.9, respectively, which are >1 times higher than corresponding selectivity (11.3, 11.0, 8.4) when the feed is neutral (pH=7). The membrane was protonated in acid condition (Scheme in Fig. 2b), which enhanced its positive charge and electrostatic repulsion toward divalent cations. Thus the rejection to divalent ions (Co2+, Ni2+, Mn2+) increases from ~95% (in pH 7 feed) to ~97% (in 2 M H2SO4, Supplementary Fig. 10a), thus increasing the M2+/Li+ selectivity. Meanwhile, permeance of TAD-TBMB TFCM is ~12.6 LMHB (in pH 7) and ~11.3 LMHB (in 2 M H2SO4, Supplementary Fig. 10b). The rejection of Co2+ of the TAD-TBMB TFCM can maintain ~94% when the ion concentration increases to 10 g L−1 (Supplementary Fig. 11). As seen from Fig. 3c, very limited membranes (solid symbols) are stable in both the acid and base conditions. Compared to these membranes, permeance of TAD-TBMB TFCM is at least ~2 times higher than the rest of reported membranes while its rejection to divalent ions are close, which renders the TAD-TBMB TFCM the first TFCM showing high separation performance combined with broad pH-stability.

Acid and alkali resistance of the TAD-TBMB TFCMs

Acid-resistance of TAD-TBMB TFCMs was further compared to PEI-TMC TFCMs and three commercial membranes (DK, NF270 and MPS-34) that are widely used for ion separation63. All membranes were immersed in 3 M H2SO4 and tested at different time intervals. As shown in Fig. 4a, RCO2+ of the TAD-TBMB TFCM slightly decreases from 97.8% to 96.8% and 94.7% after 20 days and 70 days immersion in 3 M H2SO4, respectively. By contrast, RCO2+ of PEI-TMC, DK, NF270 and MPS-34 membranes decreases from ~90% to 42.6%, 53.3%, 28.9%, and 81.1%, respectively, after 20 days immersion in 3 M H2SO4. Correspondingly, permeance of PEI-TMC, DK, NF270 and MPS-34 membranes increases significantly after 20-days immersion, while the permeance of TAD-TBMB TFCM is stable (Fig. 4b). As such, the acid stability of TAD-TBMB TFCMs is 26.4, 19.6, 33.7, 15.0 times higher than PEI-TMC, DK, NF270 and MPS-34, in terms of the decrease in salt rejection (ΔR). The poor stability of control membranes is due to the pH-induced hydrolysis of amide bonds in them45, while the pH-resistance of TAD-TBMB TFCM arise from the high stability of “C-N” linking bonds. Indeed, surface morphologies of TAD-TBMB TFCMs are stable after 20-days immersion in 3 M H2SO4 (Fig. 4c), while defects appear on surfaces of PEI-TMC, DK, NF270 and MPS-34 TFCMs (Fig. 4d–g) compared to their pristine morphology (Supplementary Fig. 12). Noteworthy, both the surface morphology and separation performance of TAD-TBMB TFCM are also stable in other acids such as HCl (Supplementary Fig. 13) and HNO3 (Supplementary Fig. 14) that are frequently used in batteries recycle. FTIR spectra of the TAD-TBMB TFCMs remain the same with increasing the immersion time in 3 M H2SO4 (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Table 1), 3 M HCl (Supplementary Fig. 15a) and 3 M HNO3 (Supplementary Fig. 15b), while the -CO-NH- (dash rectangle) in the PEI-TMC TFCMs degraded obviously (Fig. 4i). Moreover, both the MWCO and mean pore sizes of the TAD-TBMB TFCMs are stable versus H2SO4 immersion (Supplementary Fig. 16). The membrane is even stable in 3 M H2SO4 at elevated temperature of 353 K (Supplementary Fig. 17), which is beneficial for filtrating high temperature mixture64.

In literature, most acid-resistant membranes were evaluated by immersing them in acid solution56,65, yet in practical application membranes are continuously filtrated by acidic solution. We further tested the TFCMs in the continuous filtration. Figure 5a shows that the RCo2+ of TAD-TBMB TFCM slightly decreases from ~98.2% to 97.8% after 30 days of continuous filtration of CoCl2 solution (2 M H2SO4). While for the PEI-TMC TFCM, its RCo2+ decreases from 96.4% to 31.2% after 30-days filtration with 2 M H2SO4 feed solution (Fig. 5b). In this regard, the TAD-TBMB TFCM is 65 times more stable than PEI-TMC TFCM during the contious filtration. In addition, the TAD-TBMB TFCMs also shows good stability in base conditions as well (Supplementary Fig. 18). Thus the TAD-TBMB TFCMs highlight excellent pH-resistance in the continuous filtration of feed containing concentrated acid/base.

Lithium extraction from spent batteries

The TAD-TBMB TFCM was explored in lithium extraction from spent batteries. First, mixtures containing different ratios of Li+, Ni2+, Co2+, Mn2+ ions were prepared according to the compositions of widely used cell chemistries (LiNi0.33Co0.33Mn0.33O2, LiNi0.5Co0.2Mn0.3O2, LiNi0.6Co0.2Mn0.2O2, LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2)9,23,66 of lithium batteries. As shown in Fig. 6a, the permeance of TAD-TBMB TFCM is ~8.4 LMHB, and the rejection for divalent cations (RM2+) is maintained ~97%. After the nanofiltration, Fig. 6b shows that the M2+/Li+ mass ratio decreases from ~8.5 (in the feed) to ~0.5 (in the permeate), which demonstrates good M2+/Li+ selectivity for acidic feed solution.

A representative spent battery (LiNi0.5Co0.2Mn0.3O2) was selected for Li2CO3 extraction. As shown in Fig. 6c, cathode materials of the spent battery obtained from company were leached by 2 M H2SO4, yielding an acidic leachate solution containing various metal ions. The leachate solution was filtrated by two-stage nanofiltration, whereby the Li+ content in the permeate increases from 13.6% (feed) to 76.5% (1st permeate) and 98.9% (2nd permeate), respectively (Fig. 6d, Supplementary Fig. 19). The 2nd permeate was then neutralized and precipitated with Na2CO3 to extract high purity (99.1%) Li2CO3 product (Fig. 6e), which was characterized by XRD to confirm its structures (Fig. 6f). Importantly, the TAD-TBMB TFCM shows stable permeance and M2+/Li+ selectivity during 30-days continuous filtration of the practical leachate of spent battery (Supplementary Fig. 20). In addition, the TAD-TBMB TFCM also shows good separation performance in filtrating the spent cathode leachate with lower acidity (Supplementary Fig. 21), which proves its versatility in treating mixtures with broad pH. Given the commercial availability of monomers for preparing the high-performance TAD-TBMB TFCMs, this membrane is promising compared to acid-resistant membranes available on markets (Supplementary note 1, Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

A high performance and pH-resistant nanofiltration membrane was engineered via the TAD-TBMB interfacial alkylation, and explored to recycle lithium from the leachate of spent batteries under practical conditions. The overall nanofiltration performance of TAD-TBMB TFCM ranks the best in terms of the permeance and ion selectivity. More importantly, it represents the first and only membrane that shows not only high separation performance, but also broad pH-resistance (3 M H2SO4-3 M NaOH) under both the static immersion and cross-flow filtration conditions. On the basis of these merits, high purity (99.1%) Li2CO3 compound was extracted from practical leachates of spent lithium batteries.

Methods

Materials and reagents

Polyacrylonitrile (PAN, ~50 KDa) and polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF, ~50 KDa) membranes were purchased from Separate Equipment Company in Beijing. 1,4,7,10-Tetraazacyclododecane (TAD, ≥97%), 1,3,5-Tris(bromomethyl)benzene (TBMB, ≥ 98%), polyethylene glycol (PEG, Mw: 200, 400, 600, 1000, 1500 Da) were procured from Aladdin Reagent Company in Shanghai. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, 99%) was purchased from Wokai Pharmaceutical Company in Shanghai. 1,3,5-Trimethylbenzene (AR), chloroform (AR), NaOH (99%), Na2CO3 (99%), Li2CO3 (≥99%), H2SO4 (GR), HCl (AR), HNO3 (GR), MgCl2 (AR), LiCl (≥99%), NaCl (≥99.5%), KCl (≥99.5%), CaCl2 (≥96%), MgSO4 (≥99%), CuCl2 (≥98%), CoCl2 (≥99.7%), MnCl2 (≥99%) and NiCl2 (≥98%) were all acquired from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Company in Beijing. The cathode material of spent lithium battery was purchased from Chang’an Outai Renewable Resources Recycling Management Department in Guangdong. The deionized (DI) water was prepared by Water Purification System with conductivity of 15 MΩ.

Preparation of TAD-TBMB free-standing membranes

TAD (0.4 g, 2.3 mmol) and SDS (0.02 g, 0.07 mmol) were dissolved with 20 mL water. Subsequently, a TBMB solution, comprising 0.3 g (0.8 mmol) dissolved in 20 mL mesitylene, was carefully layered atop the TAD/SDS mixture solution. Following a 15-min interval, a free-standing TAD-TBMB TFCM was successfully formed at the interface between the water and mesitylene interface.

Preparation of acid-resistant TAD-TBMB TFCMs

A PAN ultrafiltration membrane was firmly placed within a holder module (clamp blind), upon which a 2 wt% TAD aqueous solution (10 mL) containing 0.1 wt% SDS was dispensed for 2 h. Afterward, the excess aqueous solution was removed, and the substrate was allowed air-dried at ambient temperature. Next, a 1.5 wt% TBMB solution in mesitylene (10 mL) was carefully poured over the substrate and kept for 8 h at 303 K. The resulting TAD-TBMB TFCMs underwent drying at 323 K for 20 min, followed by rinses with mesitylene and alcohol, and were stored in DI water for subsequent testing.

Preparation of alkali-resistant TAD-TBMB TFCMs

The PVDF ultrafiltration membrane was immersed in PEG solution (400 Da, 20 wt%) for 12 h to improve its hydrophilicity. Then the PVDF membrane was washed and firmly placed within a holder module, and a TAD aqueous solution (7 wt%, containing 0.1 wt% SDS, 10 mL) was poured onto the PVDF membrane and kept for 2 h. Then the excess aqueous solution on the surface of PVDF membrane was removed, and the PVDF membranes was air-dried at ambient temperature. Next, a TBMB solution in mesitylene (1.5 wt%, 10 mL) was poured over the PVDF membrane and left undisturbed (8 h, 303 K). The resulting TAD-TBMB TFCM was dried (323 K, 20 mins), rinsed with mesitylene and alcohol, stored in DI water.

Li2CO3 extraction from real spent battery (LiNi0.5Co0.2Mn0.3O2)

The cathode materials (10 g) of spent lithium-ion batteries were dispersed in 500 mL of 2 M H2SO4 (2 h, 353 K). The leaching solution was filtered for 2-3 times to remove undissolved impurities, and the resulting leaching solution was further filtrated by nanofiltration. The collected permeate (~400 mL) was tuned to pH 7, concentrated (to ~150 mL) by rotary evaporation, and added with Na2CO3 (10 g) at 353 K to obtain Li2CO3 precipitates.

Nanofiltration

Nanofiltration was conducted under cross-flow mode (flow rate: 0.3 L min−1). The feed temperature and feed pressure in all nanofiltration tests were kept at 303 K and 4 bar, if not specified. All membranes (effective area: 3.14 cm2) were pre-pressured for 2 h at 4 bar before testing. Characterizations

ATR-FTIR (Attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy) analysis was performed on the Bruker Vertex 70 spectrometer, spanning the wavelength range of 4000–400 cm−1. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) was conducted by Bruker AV400 using deuterated Dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 (DMSO) as solvent. XPS (X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy) measurements were conducted using the Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi instrument. EA (Organic elementary analysis) employed the Thermo FLASH2000 equipment. FESEM (Field emission scanning electron microscope) imaging was carried out at 3 kV and 10 µA using the SU8010 model from Japan. Surface roughness was characterized by SPM (Scanning probe microscope, 9700-SPM, Shimadzu, Japan) in tapping mode, with at least three replicates per membrane. Zeta potential measurements across a pH range of 2–10.5 were conducted using the SurPASS analyzer from Anton Paar, Austria, in 1 mM KCl solution. Water contact angles were determined using the OCA 15EC instrument from dataphysics. TOC-L (Total organic carbon values) equipment (Shimadzu, Japan) was used to assess total organic carbon under TC-IC mode.
