Therapy Optimization in mHSPC: Insights from a Multidisciplinary Uro-Oncology Expert Panel
Authors: André Mansinho, João Carlos Gramaça, Rodrigo Ramos, Pedro Miguel Baltazar, José Palma dos Reis, Ricardo Fernandes, Mariana Malheiro, Alina Rosinha, Sofia Amorim, Ricardo Borges, Isaac Braga, Andreia Capela, Diogo Nunes Carneiro, Rui Dinis, André Ferreira, Arnaldo Figueiredo, Sílvia Lopes, Pedro Silvestre Madeira, Helena Magalhães, Hélder Mansinho, Sara Meireles, Carolina Pereira, Cidália Pinto, Chiara Rodrigues, Ana Spencer, Carolina Carvalho, Tiago Tomás, Carlos Rabaça

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) is a disease state characterized by the presence of distant metastases detectable on conventional imaging while the tumor remains responsive to androgen deprivation therapy (ADT). It represents an earlier, treatment-sensitive phase that precedes the development of metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), the terminal stage of the disease. The addition of therapies beyond traditional ADT to achieve more sustained tumor regression by combining different mechanisms of action and targeting different cancer fuelling pathways has spurred the concept of treatment intensification in prostate cancer. This approach may involve the addition of androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) to ADT—hereafter referred to as “treatment intensification”—or the addition of ARPIs plus chemotherapy to ADT—hereafter referred to as “treatment optimization.” Randomized controlled trials have consistently shown improved patient outcomes with the evolution from ADT alone to ADT plus ARPI, and more recently to ADT plus ARPI plus docetaxel, and demonstrated that treatment intensification decisions in mHSPC have clinically meaningful implications. However, in the absence of comparative efficacy data between therapy options, the treatment choice for a given patient will depend on patient- and disease-related factors, with the goal of improving outcomes while minimizing the cumulative toxicity of therapies, particularly when using combination strategies. In this article, a panel of 28 experts—8 urologists and 20 oncologists—with recognized experience and expertise in the management of advanced prostate cancer discussed the role of treatment intensification in mHSPC and which patients are more likely to benefit from each treatment strategy.Keywords: Androgen deprivation therapy, Androgen receptor pathway inhibitor, Metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer, Therapy intensification, Therapy optimization, Uro-oncology

Introduction

According to GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, prostate cancer is the second most common cancer and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death among men worldwide, with approximately 1.5 million new cases and 397,000 deaths [1]. It is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in men in nearly two-thirds of countries and ranks as the fourth most commonly diagnosed cancer overall, accounting for 7.3% of all cases [1]. Portugal follows this trend, with prostate cancer ranking third in incidence (7529 new cases; 10.8% of all new cancer cases) and sixth in mortality (2083 deaths; 6.2% of all cancer-related deaths) in the country [2].

Metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) is a disease state characterized by the presence of metastases on conventional imaging modalities (bone scan, computed tomography [CT], and/or magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) but in which the disease remains responsive to androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) [3]. mHSPC precedes metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), the terminal stage of the disease. In Western countries, although most patients present with localized disease, the incidence of newly diagnosed (de novo) metastatic prostate cancer has been increasing and is currently estimated to range from 5% to over 70%, depending on geographic region and screening practices. Notably, these cases account for more than half of prostate cancer-related deaths [4–6]. In fact, the incidence of advanced stage disease, including mHSPC, has been increasing by approximately 5% per year in recent years [3].

The treatment goals in mHSPC are to prolong survival through long-term suppression of tumor proliferation, delay progression to castration resistance, reduce symptomatic disease burden, and improve quality of life (QoL). ADT has been the mainstay of treatment for mHSPC; however, progression to castration resistance typically occurs within 1 year of use of ADT alone [7–12], and thus guidelines recommend the use of ADT monotherapy only when combination therapy is not feasible [13, 14]. The additional use of therapies beyond traditional ADT to achieve more sustained tumor regression by combining different mechanisms of action and targeting different cancer fuelling pathways has spurred the concept of treatment intensification in prostate cancer. This approach may involve the addition of androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs)—such as abiraterone acetate, apalutamide, darolutamide, and enzalutamide—to ADT, hereafter referred to as “treatment intensification,” or the use of triple combination therapies with ADT, an ARPI, and chemotherapy, hereafter referred to as “treatment optimization.”

As randomized controlled trials began to demonstrate improved patient outcomes in mHSPC with combination therapies, treatment intensification options have become increasingly varied and were incorporated into prostate cancer guidelines as standard of practice [14, 15]. The phase III CHAARTED [16, 17] and STAMPEDE [18] trials were the first to demonstrate that upfront addition of docetaxel to the ADT backbone yielded improvements in median overall survival (OS) over ADT alone in mHSPC, particularly in patients with high-volume disease (Table 1) [16, 19]. Additional OS improvements compared to ADT alone were subsequently shown for the ARPIs abiraterone in the LATITUDE trial [20], enzalutamide in the ENZAMET [21–23] and ARCHES [7, 24] trials, and apalutamide in the TITAN trial [25] (Table 1), with favorable safety profiles. A positive OS trend was also observed for darolutamide plus ADT versus ADT alone in the ARANOTE trial [26] (Table 1). More recently, triple combination therapies with ADT, docetaxel, and either abiraterone (PEACE-1 trial) [27] or darolutamide (ARASENS trial) [28, 29] have shown improved survival outcomes in patients with mHSPC (Table 1) and may become standard of care for some patients, as already stated in the respective guidelines, which recommend the use of triple therapy with ADT in combination with docetaxel and either abiraterone acetate plus prednisone or darolutamide in selected patients with metastatic presentations [14, 15].

The findings of these studies collectively show improved patient outcomes with the evolution from ADT alone to ADT plus ARPI, and more recently to ADT plus ARPI plus docetaxel, and demonstrate that treatment intensification decisions in mHSPC have clinically meaningful implications. However, in the absence of comparative efficacy data between the various options, the choice of treatment for a given patient will depend on patient- and disease-related factors, with the goal of improving outcomes while minimizing the cumulative toxicity of therapies, particularly when using combination strategies.

As several questions remain about how best to translate clinical trial findings into clinical practice and which patients are more likely to benefit from different treatment strategies, a panel of 28 experts—8 urologists and 20 oncologists—with recognized expertise in the management and treatment of advanced prostate cancer convened in person in central Portugal in June 2024 to discuss the role of treatment intensification in mHSPC, based on their clinical experience. During the meeting, the experts were presented with a series of questions on the management of patients with mHSPC, which they discussed openly based on their clinical practice. The questions and the experts’ perspectives are presented in the Discussion section of this manuscript. At the end of the meeting, the experts were presented with several mHSPC patient profiles. For each case, they discussed their clinical approach and voted on a preferred treatment strategy, with the aim of identifying the most consensual approach for each profile. These discussions and voting results are reported in the Clinical Case section of the manuscript.

Ethical Approval

This article is a review of previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. Therefore, approval by an ethics committee was not required.

All members of the expert panel provided consent to participate in this study and are also the authors of this manuscript.

Discussion

As of today, treatment intensification is unquestionably the standard of care for patients with mHSPC. Overall, the evidence indicates that (1) the addition of ARPI to ADT provides an OS/radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS) benefit compared to ADT for most, if not all, patients with mHSPC [7, 9, 20, 22, 25]; (2) the addition of docetaxel to ADT provides an OS/rPFS benefit compared to ADT for patients with mHSPC presenting with high-volume, de novo, or relapsed disease and low-volume plus de novo disease [10, 16, 19]; and (3) the addition of abiraterone or darolutamide to ADT plus docetaxel provides an OS/rPFS benefit for high-volume patients compared to ADT plus docetaxel, but not compared to ADT plus ARPI, given the lack of evidence from direct comparisons [27, 28]. For low-volume or metachronous disease, the numbers are small and preclude definitive conclusions.

However, questions remain regarding the management of mHSPC due to inconclusive or insufficient data from pivotal studies. The experts identified three key questions that they believe remain open for debate with the currently available data, which are discussed next: What is the primary goal when deciding for treatment optimization with a triple combination therapy (ADT + ARPI + docetaxel)? What disease-related criteria can be used in clinical practice to select patients for triple combination therapy? What patient characteristics may modulate the treatment decision when considering the use of triple combination therapy? These questions and their discussion are highlighted with clinical case scenarios at the end of the manuscript.

What is the primary goal when deciding for treatment optimization with a triple combination therapy (ADT + ARPI + docetaxel)?

The treatment paradigm in advanced prostate cancer has evolved from a therapy sequencing approach to one emphasizing the use of the most effective agents early in the disease course. This shift is driven by growing evidence that providing the most potent available therapies upfront, rather than reserving them for later disease stages, yields the greatest survival gains.

It is now undisputed and established in guidelines that single-agent androgen blockade (or androgen deprivation therapy, ADT) is not sufficient in the treatment of mHSPC and must always be combined with ARPI, regardless of disease characteristics. The current debate centers on when to add docetaxel chemotherapy to this double blockade, rather than when to add an ARPI to the ADT plus docetaxel combination, which was what was actually investigated in clinical trials. Nevertheless, it is generally agreed that in prostate cancer, the most effective treatment should always be given upfront, even if this means using most of the available therapies.

It is widely accepted that the primary goal of triple optimization is the overall survival benefit provided by the triple regimen. Time to pain progression is also an important factor; however, in the absence of evidence directly comparing ADT plus ARPI with triple regimens, it cannot serve as a basis for decision-making. Therefore, in the setting of pain management, the focus should be on palliative radiotherapy and effective analgesic strategies to achieve pain control.

What disease-related criteria can be used in clinical practice to select patients for triple combination therapy?

As randomized clinical trials evaluating the combination of ADT plus docetaxel have consistently shown a significant survival benefit in patients with high-volume mHSPC, subsequent trials aimed to specifically enroll and study this patient population. Although the PEACE-1 trial did not fully achieve this goal due to several protocol amendments over time, ARASENS was explicitly designed to enroll patients who, according to current clinical practice, would be suitable candidates for ADT plus docetaxel. As a result, the ARASENS cohort largely comprised individuals with more aggressive disease features.

The evidence available from clinical trials such as PEACE-1 and ARASENS can only inform the selection of patients with mHSPC for treatment intensification with triple therapy based primarily on disease volume criteria. Recently, the STOPCAP M1 collaboration conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis including 2261 patients from the GETUG-AFU15, CHAARTED, and STAMPEDE trials to evaluate docetaxel plus ADT versus ADT alone in mHSPC and to determine which patient subgroups may benefit most from the addition of chemotherapy to the ADT treatment backbone [30]. The study showed a meaningful benefit for patients with low-volume synchronous disease (8% absolute difference in 5-year PFS and 8% absolute difference in 5-year OS), high-volume metachronous disease (11% absolute difference in 5-year PFS and 10% absolute difference in 5-year OS), and particularly high-volume, synchronous disease (12% absolute difference in 5-year PFS and 12% absolute difference in 5-year OS). Conversely, patients with low-volume metachronous disease did not seem to benefit from this strategy (− 1% absolute difference in 5-year PFS and 0% absolute difference in 5-year OS) and should therefore be managed differently [30].

However, criteria related to disease volume and timing of metastatic presentation as sole drivers of decision-making can be questioned, as they are context dependent.

Disease Volume

Disease volume refers to the extent of metastatic spread and translates into the classification of the disease as low or high volume. The CHAARTED trial defined high-volume disease as at least one of the following criteria: ≥ 4 bone metastases, with at least one outside the vertebral column or pelvis, or visceral metastases [16]. This classification helped to differentiate patients on the basis of disease burden, with an impact on treatment decisions and outcomes observed in the trial [16, 31]. However, the CHAARTED definition of high-volume disease has some limitations. Starting with the heterogeneity in disease burden among patients: the requirement of either visceral metastases or ≥ 4 bone metastases with at least one outside the axial skeleton groups together patients with potentially very different disease burdens. For example, a patient with isolated liver metastases and a patient with four bone metastases are both considered high-volume despite potentially different prognoses and treatment responses. On the other hand, this definition lacks precision for intermediate cases, such as patients with ≤ 3 bone metastases without visceral involvement but extensive and bulky lymph node disease, who may have a poor prognosis and potential for complications such as hydronephrosis, fistulas, or bowel obstructions, who do not fit into the high-volume category. There are also imaging issues associated with the CHAARTED criteria. When the study was conducted, traditional imaging modalities such as bone and CT scan were standard, which have limitations in detecting small or early metastases. Imaging advances since then, particularly the use of new imaging techniques such as advanced MRI and prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PSMA PET/CT), are much more sensitive and can detect smaller or lower volume metastases that may have previously gone undetected. This raises the potential for overclassification, as with more sensitive imaging, patients may now be diagnosed with metastatic lesions that would not have been visible using older techniques and implies that patients with what might have been considered low-volume disease by older imaging standards could be reclassified as high-volume by current standards.

It should also be noted that low-volume synchronous disease is not a homogeneous entity. The classification of low-volume disease is highly dependent on the stage at which it is assessed, and with the widespread adoption of PSMA PET, it is likely that there is a significant proportion of stage migration. Patients previously classified as having localized disease, which would traditionally be treated with curative intent, may now, when presenting a few months later with “metachronous” disease, be reclassified as having synchronous low-volume disease, similar to what has happened in non-metastatic (M0) prostate cancer. This group of patients represents a gray area in prostate cancer management and warrants careful consideration of the benefits versus potential harms of intensified treatment, especially in the context of evolving imaging technologies.

These limitations show that the current high- versus low-volume cutoffs are somewhat imprecise and may not fully capture the biological aggressiveness of the disease and suggest the need for more refined criteria that can account for the broad spectrum of metastatic disease presentation, particularly as diagnostic imaging continues to evolve. These may incorporate, not only the number and location of metastatic lesions but also additional disease-related features, such as the presence of visceral metastases, burden and distribution of bone involvement, timing of metastatic presentation (de novo vs. recurrent disease), and biological or molecular characteristics associated with prognosis and treatment response. Another important step is the harmonization of disease volume definitions across clinical trials, as the use of different criteria has complicated cross-trial comparisons and the interpretation of evidence, and prospective validation of refined definitions in contemporary cohorts treated with current doublet and triplet regimens. In addition, advanced imaging modalities, particularly PSMA PET/CT, may contribute to a more accurate characterization of metastatic burden and may help inform future refinements of disease-volume classification.

Timing of Metastatic Presentation

Although the timing of metastatic presentation (i.e., de novo/synchronous versus recurrent/metachronous disease) may be considered for therapy selection, it is not a biological or absolute concept but rather a conventional one.

Historically, synchronous disease has been associated with poor survival outcomes. In the long-term analysis of the GETUG-AFU 15 trial, patients with metastatic disease after failure of local treatment (metachronous disease) had a significantly longer median OS than patients with metastases at diagnosis (synchronous disease): 83.1 versus 46.5 months (p = 0.015) [32].

However, if a given population has limited uptake of screening programs or limited access to primary care, patients may present with indolent disease that is diagnosed only when it becomes clinically significant. In this context, the timing of presentation may not provide meaningful insight into disease behavior or aggressiveness. Consequently, it is questionable whether this should be considered a legitimate criterion for therapeutic decision-making in all cases.

Gleason Score

Disease aggressiveness, as determined by the Gleason score, is another factor traditionally used to guide therapeutic decisions. The LATITUDE trial provided important insights into how the Gleason score, along with other factors, helps define high-risk disease. The trial, which evaluated the addition of abiraterone acetate and prednisone to standard ADT in patients with newly diagnosed mHSPC, defined high-risk disease as at least two of the following criteria: ≥ 3 bone metastases; visceral metastases; or Gleason score ≥ 8 [20]. Its results showed that treatment with ADT alone in de novo mHSPC with high-risk features was associated with poor survival outcomes due to rapid progression to metastatic castration-resistant state (mCRPC), and that the addition of abiraterone and prednisone to the ADT backbone significantly improved OS and delayed progression to castration resistance, suggesting the importance of early treatment intensification for patients with high-risk features [20, 33].

However, the use of Gleason score and high-risk criteria in clinical practice to inform treatment decisions is not unanimous among the experts. Some question the reliability of the Gleason score as a marker of biological aggressiveness and instead prioritize disease volume and clinical criteria/patient-specific factors such as comorbidities, performance status (PS), and age/life expectancy for treatment decisions.

It should also be noted that, although the ARASENS trial was designed to include a similar population to the CHAARTED trial, neither ARASENS nor PEACE-1 used disease risk or volume as formal criteria for selecting or stratifying patients, instead focusing on metastatic burden [27, 28]. However, treating patients with low-volume synchronous disease is different if their Gleason score is 6 or 7 versus ≥ 8. Patients with a lower Gleason score (6–7) may have a more indolent disease course, and potentially benefit from less intensified treatment approaches, whereas patients presenting with a higher Gleason score (8–10) have a more aggressive and rapidly progressive disease that may benefit from treatment intensification.

This body of evidence emphasizes that the treatment strategy in mHSPC is multifactorial and should be patient-tailored according to the specific therapy, disease, and patient characteristics.

Emerging evidence suggests that genetic alterations in tumor suppressor genes such as TP53, RB1, and PTEN—collectively termed aggressive-variant prostate cancer-associated tumor suppressor genes (AVPC-TSGs)—may influence treatment decisions in mHSPC. A retrospective study by Pedrani et al. demonstrated that patients harboring these mutations exhibit androgen insensitivity and poorer outcomes, potentially benefiting from intensified regimens including ARPIs and chemotherapy [34]. Complementing this, the TRIPLE-SWITCH trial (SWOG/CCTG-PR26) is investigating the addition of docetaxel to ARPI-based therapy in patients with suboptimal PSA response, aiming to personalize triplet therapy based on early resistance markers, including circulating tumor DNA and tissue biomarkers [34]. These findings support a biomarker-driven approach to escalate treatment in genetically high-risk mHSPC populations.

What patient characteristics may modulate the treatment decision when considering the use of triple combination therapy?

ECOG PS

Patients who are fit and have a good overall health status (i.e., ECOG PS 0 or 1) and patients with a poor ECOG PS (i.e., ECOG PS 2) primarily due to the disease rather than underlying comorbidities or frailty might be candidates for upfront triple therapy. Evidence indicates that if a patient is fit for chemotherapy, there is no benefit in postponing it because of the impact on clonal selection and metabolic pathways, and because the patient may not be fit to receive it later. As demonstrated by Fizazi and Gillessen, because prostate cancer predominantly affects elderly men, when patients progress to mCRPC they will be older and may have worsening comorbidities or increased frailty that render them ineligible to receive chemotherapy [14]. Therefore, they are more likely to tolerate chemotherapy if it is administered during the hormone-sensitive phase rather than at a later stage of the disease. Optimizing treatment earlier with triple therapy can thus lead to significantly better outcomes. On the other hand, studies in mCRPC have shown that the activity of docetaxel after abiraterone is lower than anticipated, providing evidence that first-line chemotherapy leads to better outcomes than second-line chemotherapy. In the study by Mezynski et al., patients who received docetaxel after first-line abiraterone had a prostate-specific antigen (PSA) decline ≥ 50% of only 26% and a median time to PSA progression (PSA-PFS) of 4.6 months [35]. In the study by Schweizer et al., men who received abiraterone before docetaxel had a significantly higher risk of progression than those who only received docetaxel (PSA decline ≥ 50% of 38% vs. 63%; p = 0.02 and median PSA-PFS of 4.1 vs. 6.7 months; p = 0.002) [36]. In the TAX 327 study, men who received first-line docetaxel had a PSA decline ≥ 50% of 45% and a median PSA-PFS of 7.7 months [37].

In addition, the CHAARTED trial showed that, after completing 1 year of chemotherapy with docetaxel, patients experienced a significant improvement in QoL, including better symptom control, reduced pain, and overall enhanced physical functioning, which is particularly relevant for younger patients, for whom QoL is a crucial consideration, as it allows them to return to a high level of daily activity, work, and social engagement [16].

Comorbidities

Comorbidities are an important consideration in the management of mHSPC, as this patient population is typically elderly and therefore more likely to present with concurrent medical conditions. When determining the most appropriate treatment strategy, the type of comorbidities is a more critical consideration than the number of comorbidities.

Depending on the type of comorbidities, patients may benefit more from a less intensive treatment strategy than triple therapy, given the increased risk of treatment-related toxicities and complications associated with intensified regimens. In such cases, a more conservative approach tailored to the patient’s overall health status, functional reserve, and life expectancy may help optimize the balance between treatment efficacy and tolerability, while preserving the patient’s QoL.

Age

Patients with a longer life expectancy will be better candidates for early optimized treatment upfront, i.e., triple therapy, because they are more likely to benefit from the trade-off between prolonged survival, delayed progression to castration resistance, and improved long-term outcomes versus treatment toxicity and side effects. For patients with limited life expectancy, preserving QoL may take precedence over long-term outcomes, and less intensive treatments may be more adequate.

The experts consider that there is no formal age cutoff to define eligibility for chemotherapy. The decision should be made on a case-by-case basis and according to the patient’s functional rather than chronological age. If a patient has a good overall health status and is physically fit and willing to receive triple therapy with upfront chemotherapy, this should be an option regardless of chronological age. This stance is also endorsed by the International Society of Geriatric Oncology (SIOG), which considers that men aged ≥ 75 years with prostate cancer should be managed according to their individual health status rather than age [38]. The Geriatric-8 screening tool can be used to make this assessment and inform management decisions, and the various treatment options should be discussed with the patient, explaining the trade-off between effective treatment and adverse effects.

Patient Preference

The patient’s preferences, expectations, and social and family context should be considered when making therapeutic decisions. Patients should be informed about the potential risks and side effects of chemotherapy so that they can make an informed decision.

mHSPC patient profiles: clinical cases

The clinical management of mHSPC requires an individualized approach that takes into account patient and disease characteristics, the patient’s overall health status, and treatment goals. To illustrate the complexity of treatment decisions and the importance of tailoring therapeutic strategies to individual characteristics and needs, the expert panel discussed the management of various mHSPC patient profiles using clinical case scenarios. Starting from a baseline case, disease and patient characteristics were progressively modified, and the impact of these changes on therapeutic decisions and the choice between ADT monotherapy, double therapy, or triple therapy was discussed. Ultimately, the experts’ responses were compiled into a heat map providing a comprehensive overview of the national approach to mHSPC treatment (Fig. 1).

For patients with synchronous high-volume disease and metachronous low-volume disease, the experts generally agree that triple therapy and double therapy are the most appropriate treatment strategies, respectively. This consensus is based on clinical trial data and on the recognition that synchronous high-volume disease is more aggressive and requires a more intensive upfront approach, while metachronous low-volume disease has less aggressive biology and does not appear to benefit from adding chemotherapy to the ADT treatment backbone.

However, the therapeutic approach for patients with metachronous high-volume disease and synchronous low-volume disease is less straightforward and lacks the same level of expert consensus. This is due to limited trial data specific to these settings, as well as greater clinical heterogeneity within these subgroups. These cases warrant individualized treatment decisions based on disease and patient characteristics and will be discussed next.

Metachronous High-Volume Disease

The management of relapsed high-volume disease requires careful clinical consideration.

A 67-year-old patient with initial total PSA of 27 ng/mL, Gleason score of 7 (4 + 3), International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade 3, submitted to radical prostatectomy 3 years ago, currently presenting with biochemical recurrence, PS 0, six lesions on bone scan (including one in the femur), no evidence of visceral or lymph node involvement, PSA of 19 ng/mL, and no symptoms, is considered a young and fit patient. In such cases, a combination of ADT, an ARPI, and docetaxel is a recommended treatment option. However, while triple therapy might provide clinical benefit, the treatment decision must take into account the patient’s ability to tolerate chemotherapy and the risk of associated toxicities.

The experts agree that treatment in this scenario is not straightforward, and both double and triple therapy may be appropriate. Given that the patient is likely to require chemotherapy at some point during the disease course, initiating chemotherapy earlier may offer greater long-term benefit. The relatively young age and aggressive features of the disease—evidenced by a high PSA and multiple bone metastases only 3 years post-prostatectomy—support consideration of triple therapy. However, since the patient is currently asymptomatic, the potential impact of chemotherapy-related side effects on QoL must also be weighed, which may favor a double regimen as an initial approach. Ultimately, the decision should be individualized and made in collaboration with the patient, carefully balancing the risks and benefits of each therapeutic strategy.

If this patient presents with visceral metastases, symptoms, or comorbidities, the treatment approach should be reassessed on the basis of those clinical factors.

Visceral Metastases

In the presence of visceral metastases, the experts widely agree that triple therapy is the preferred treatment approach. Younger patients with visceral involvement are generally considered suitable candidates for chemotherapy due to the aggressive disease biology and poorer prognosis typically associated with visceral spread.

While chemotherapy is associated with potential adverse events—most notably febrile neutropenia—these can be effectively mitigated through the use of primary prophylaxis with granulocyte colony-stimulating factors (G-CSFs), in accordance with current supportive care guidelines.

Deferring chemotherapy at this stage carries the risk that disease progression and clinical deterioration may later render the patient ineligible for more intensive treatments. As demonstrated in several studies, outcomes with first-line chemotherapy are superior to those achieved when chemotherapy is administered in later lines. The CHAARTED trial further showed that, although patients receiving ADT plus docetaxel experience an initial decline in QoL during chemotherapy, this is followed by a recovery and eventual improvement over time [39], an important consideration, particularly for younger and more active patients.

For these reasons, the experts recommend that long-term treatment planning and sequencing prioritize the use of upfront triple therapy, clearly explaining to the patient the benefits and potential drawbacks of this approach.

Moreover, patients with visceral metastases typically exhibit faster disease progression and require second-line therapy earlier than those without visceral involvement. Consequently, they may not have sufficient time to benefit from the full sequence of available treatments. Delivering the most effective therapy as early as possible is therefore critical to optimizing both survival and quality of life in this population.

Symptoms

When patients present with symptoms (such as bone pain), symptom control becomes a key consideration alongside survival outcomes. As there is no clear data indicating that triple therapy might improve outcomes in this setting, both doublet and triplet regimens may be considered, with particular emphasis on integrating palliative radiotherapy and effective pain management strategies to address QoL. While both options are possible, the experts generally favor a triple therapy approach for these patients.

Comorbidities

If the previously described 67-year-old patient were to present with a PS of 1 and significant comorbidities—such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetic chronic kidney disease (CKD), a history of ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), or others—concerns would arise regarding the patient’s ability to tolerate the toxicity associated with intensive chemotherapy. In such cases, a less intensive approach—such as a combination of ADT and an ARPI—may be preferred to balance efficacy and safety. Nevertheless, triple therapy may be considered for patients with significant life expectancy.

Close clinical monitoring of these patients is essential, especially regarding cardiac and renal function, to detect early signs of treatment-related complications and adjust therapy accordingly.

Synchronous Low-Volume Disease

Synchronous low-volume disease represents a highly heterogeneous clinical entity. Although these patients share a low metastatic burden at diagnosis, they may differ substantially in terms of biological disease aggressiveness, response to ADT, and the presence or absence of comorbidities. Furthermore, the definition of “low volume” varies across clinical trials and real-world settings—particularly with the widespread adoption of PSMA PET—which further contributes to this heterogeneity. This variability complicates treatment decision-making and limits the generalizability of clinical trial findings, underscoring the need for individualized therapeutic strategies based on both patient- and disease-specific factors.

A 55-year-old man with an ECOG PS of 0, no comorbidities, an initial PSA of 18 ng/mL, Gleason score 7 (3 + 4), no lymph node involvement, and three bone metastases confined to the pelvic and lumbar spine would be considered a good-prognosis patient. Given his young age, excellent PS, and absence of comorbidities, treatment should focus on achieving durable disease control while preserving quality of life. For this patient profile, the experts generally agree that a reasonable approach is doublet therapy (ADT plus an ARPI) combined with palliative radiotherapy, given the lack of evidence for ablative therapy in this setting. This regimen could be maintained for approximately 2 years, followed by clinical and imaging reassessment to guide further management.

Patients with Good Prognosis and Comorbidities

If the previously described patient had a limited life expectancy (e.g., ≥ 80 years), ECOG PS of 1, and comorbidities as previously mentioned, the expert panel would still recommend double therapy (ADT + ARPI) combined with radiotherapy, with reassessment of PSA levels after 2 years to evaluate the potential for treatment de-escalation.

Achieving an undetectable PSA level within the first few months of treatment is considered an important prognostic marker. Evidence from the TITAN and ARASENS trials indicates that exceptional responders (i.e., those who achieve a very significant PSA reduction) have significantly better OS and rPFS outcomes [40, 41]. Therefore, if an undetectable PSA is achieved early after starting treatment (with the definition of “early” still open for debate), further treatment intensification may not be necessary.

In patients with multiple comorbidities, some experts may also consider ADT monotherapy, as the addition of an ARPI may not provide a significant survival or QoL benefit when weighed against potential adverse effects.

Patients with Poor Prognosis

A 55-year-old man with ECOG PS of 0, no comorbidities, an initial PSA of 143 ng/dL, Gleason score of 9 (4 + 5), no lymph node involvement on CT scan, and three bone metastases (including one in the femur) has several poor prognostic features, including a high Gleason score, elevated PSA, and bone metastases, particularly in long bones such as the femur, which increases the risk of skeletal-related complications (e.g., fractures and pain). The de novo presentation further suggests an aggressive disease course, despite being considered low volume. Given these high-risk features, the experts unanimously recommend a triple regimen of ADT, an ARPI, and docetaxel to maximize survival outcomes and delay disease progression.

Patients with Poor Prognosis and Comorbidities

For a patient with a similar high-risk disease profile but advanced age (e.g., 81 years), ECOG PS of 1, and the aforementioned relevant comorbidities, the approach should be more conservative. Despite the poor prognostic features, the limited life expectancy and comorbidities justify avoiding intensive chemotherapy.

In such cases, most experts recommend double therapy with ADT and an ARPI, followed by clinical and biochemical reassessment after three months. This strategy allows for early evaluation of treatment response and timely therapeutic adjustments, while balancing disease control and quality of life.

Conclusion

The present work was intended as a reflection on how to optimize the management of mHSPC in clinical practice based on the insights and experience of a group of experts involved in the treatment of these patients. Its primary aim was to stimulate discussion and share clinical perspectives that may help inform clinical practice and future research, rather than to establish formal practice recommendations. For this reason, no formal dissemination or implementation of recommendations arising from this work is planned.

On the basis of current evidence and expert-driven discussion, high disease volume is the most important disease-related criterion when deciding whether to intensify treatment with triple therapy in a given patient, particularly in those with aggressive disease features such as visceral metastases. For patients who are eligible for docetaxel in this setting, earlier administration of the drug increases the likelihood of eligibility and provides the patient with another life-prolonging therapy.

Patients with synchronous low-volume disease are a highly heterogeneous group. Those with high-risk features such as Gleason score ≥ 8, bulky tumors (T4), or extensive and/or bulky nodal involvement may benefit from treatment intensification with triple therapy.

For patients with low-volume metachronous disease, treatment optimization with triple therapy is not recommended because chemotherapy is associated with non-negligible toxicity and its benefit in this subgroup remains uncertain.

In patients with reduced life expectancy, even in the absence of absolute exclusion criteria for docetaxel, preserving QoL may take precedence over long-term outcomes, and less intensive treatment options may be more appropriate.

In the absence of head-to-head comparisons between triple and double regimens, it is critical to incorporate patient preferences into the decision-making process, as there is no definitive evidence regarding differences in outcomes between these strategies.

The safety and drug–drug interaction profile are an important consideration in the choice of the ARPI for the triple regimen, particularly when considering long-term administration. The fact that abiraterone has a higher risk of cardiotoxicity compared to third-generation anti-androgens (apalutamide, enzalutamide, darolutamide), while darolutamide has a more favorable safety profile and fewer drug–drug interactions (DDI) [42], should be considered in the therapy choice.

Overall, the experts agree that the preferred approach for high-volume patients eligible for chemotherapy is ADT combined with an ARPI and docetaxel, and that docetaxel should not be given without an ARPI. For low-volume mHSPC, therapy should be risk-adapted considering individual patient and disease characteristics.
