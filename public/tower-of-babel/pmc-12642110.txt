Using protection motivation theory to explain monkeypox vaccination intention among men who have sex with men in China
Authors: Chen Xu, Jiechen Zhang, Huifang Xu, Ying Gao, Shangbin Liu, Lulu Xu, Fan Hu, Gang Xu, Ying Wang, Yong Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMonkeypox (Mpox) has become a Public Health Emergency of International Concern. Men who have sex with men (MSM) are the main population of Mpox infection. Vaccines are considered as the most effective methods for preventing and controlling Mpox epidemics. This study aimed at constructing a structural equation model (SEM) of the Mpox vaccination intention based on protection motivation theory (PMT) among MSM in China, so as to provide a strategic basis for relevant interventions.MethodsA multi-center cross-sectional survey was conducted from November 2023 to March 2024 among 2481 MSM from six provinces (Shanghai, Guangdong, Xinjiang, Shaanxi, Yunnan, and Liaoning) in China. Participants completed a questionnaire evaluating PMT components, Mpox-related knowledge, risk perception, and vaccination intention. The SEM was constructed and evaluated whether it could effectively measure the protection motivation of MSM.ResultsA total of 2403 valid questionnaires were collected, with an effective rate of 96.86%. Most participants were young MSM, and 2,093 (87.1%) expressed high vaccination intention. The hypothetical SEM exhibited acceptable model fit, with the chi-square/degrees of freedom ratio (CMIN/DF) = 9.410, p < 0.001, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.059, goodness-of-fit index (GFI) = 0.911, comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.929, normed fit index (NFI) = 0.921, relative fit index (RFI) = 0.912, incremental fit index (IFI) = 0.929, and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) = 0.920. The PMT constructs including maladaptive rewards, response efficacy, self-efficacy, and response costs were directly associated with vaccination intention and mediated the association between knowledge score, risk perception, and vaccination intention. The construct of maladaptive rewards was the strongest predictor.ConclusionsThe majority of MSM had the intention to receive the Mpox vaccination. It is suggested that the vaccination program should focus on young MSM in response to the Mpox outbreak in the future. The PMT constructs can be applied to predict Mpox vaccination intention among MSM. Future Mpox vaccination promotion strategies can be based on the PMT constructs of maladaptive rewards, response efficacy, self-efficacy, and response costs to improve the Mpox vaccination intention of the MSM population.

Background

Monkeypox (Mpox) is a zoonotic disease caused by the Mpox virus (MPXV), which has a clinical presentation similar to smallpox. A human case was first detected in 1970 in the Democratic Republic of Congo [1]. Since its discovery, the disease has remained endemic in Central and Western Africa [2]. There are two distinct clades of the virus: clade I, formerly known as the Central African clade (subclades Ia and Ib), and clade II, known as the Western African clade (subclades IIa and IIb). Clade I is associated with higher case fatality rates and more severe disease [3]. The global Mpox outbreak in 2022–2023 has primarily been driven by Clade IIb. In contrast, the current resurgence of Mpox infections in Africa during 2024 predominantly involves Clade I [4]. Given the rapid increase of Mpox cases worldwide, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared the global Mpox outbreak a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) on July 23, 2022 [5]. The PHEIC was declared over in May 2023 after a sustained decline in global cases. However, Africa is witnessing a resurgence of Mpox infections, with 13 countries reporting 17,541 cases and 517 deaths in 2024. The WHO declared the Mpox outbreak a PHEIC for the second time on August 14, 2024 [6]. As of May 2024, 97,745 confirmed cases and 203 deaths have been reported from 116 countries worldwide [7]. Since the first confirmed case in September 2022, 1601 confirmed cases have been reported between June and November 2023 in China, with no severe cases or deaths [8].

Mpox virus is mainly transmitted through sexual contact. Most cases occurred in individuals who engaged in high-risk sexual behaviors, such as condomless sex or sex with multiple partners. Notably, more than 90% of cases were concentrated in men who have sex with men (MSM), which includes gay, bisexual, and other men who engage in same-sex sexual behavior, regardless of their sexual orientation or identity [9]. China had the largest MSM population globally, estimated at 8,288,536 individuals in 2018, accounting for about 1.73% of adult men aged 18–64 [10]. Given the large population size and the eased public health measures against Mpox, MSM faced significant public concern about the risk of a domestic outbreak.

Vaccination is considered as the most effective method for preventing and controlling Mpox epidemics, with an estimated effectiveness of 85% [11]. Previous studies showed that vaccinated people have a 5.2-fold lower risk of Mpox than unvaccinated people [12]. The MPXV is classified within the Orthopoxvirus genus of the Poxviridae family, sharing genetic similarities with other viruses in the Orthopoxvirus group [13]. Two smallpox vaccines are currently available to prevent MPXV infection in the USA: ACAM2000 (Emergent Product Development Gaithersburg, MD, USA) and JYNNEOS (Bavarian Nordic, Hellerup, Denmark) [14]. LC16m8 is another third-generation vaccine manufactured by Kaketsuken (Kumamoto, Japan) [3]. China has not yet approved internationally licensed Mpox vaccines. However, as the Mpox epidemic evolves, these vaccines may be considered for emergency vaccination programs targeting high-risk populations in the future. Concurrently, China is actively developing domestic Mpox vaccines. Notably, in September 2024, a live attenuated Mpox vaccine, independently developed by the Shanghai Institute of Biological Products Co., Ltd., entered the clinical trial stage (Registration Number: CTR20250074) [15]. Individuals who received smallpox vaccination in the past may have partial immunity to Mpox [16]. Although vaccines are essential to controlling infectious diseases [17], people in most countries are reported to be hesitant about vaccination according to a global report [18]. There are many factors that affect the intention to be vaccinated, such as knowledge of the disease, risk perception of the disease, fear of vaccine side effects, the cost of time and money, the availability of vaccines, and so on [19–21]. The factors related to vaccination intention are important for policymakers to guide effective intervention strategies and improve vaccination acceptance [22].

Behavioral research identified four key factors driving vaccine uptake: necessary knowledge, an enabling environment, social influences, and individual motivation [23]. The Protection Motivation Theory (PMT), established by Rogers in 1975 and revised in 1983 on the basis of health belief theory [24], provides a comprehensive explanation of behavior change from the perspective of motivation. Compared to other theories, PMT emphasizes the cognitive mediation process that involves threat appraisal and coping appraisal [25]. Threat appraisal centers on evaluating threats and factors that increase or decrease the likelihood of maladaptive responses. Coping appraisal focuses on evaluating the ability to cope with and mitigate the threatened danger [26]. By addressing both threat and coping simultaneously, PMT offers a dual-perspective approach to understanding health behaviors, making it particularly applicable to vaccine-related decision-making. The PMT has been widely used and confirmed in many health behavior fields, especially vaccination-related research [27–33]. For example, a previous study applied a structural equation modeling approach and found the PMT constructs were useful in predicting the intention of COVID-19 vaccination [31]. Another study examined the intention to receive a seasonal flu vaccination among American adults and showed that PMT constructs accounted for 62% of the variance in intention [32]. There was a study using the PMT theory to explain the intention to initiate human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination among MSM in China [33].

To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies using the PMT to explore the predictors of Mpox vaccination intention among MSM. The purpose of this study was to construct a structural equation model (SEM) of the Mpox vaccination intention based on PMT constructs, and to evaluate whether it can effectively measure the protection motivation of MSM, so as to provide a theoretical basis for the development of vaccination interventions. In this study, we hypothesized that (1) PMT constructs were directly associated with vaccination intention and (2) Mpox-related knowledge and risk perception can be directly associated with vaccination intention or indirectly associated with vaccination intention through PMT variables.

Methods

Study population and eligible criteria

A national multi-center cross-sectional survey using questionnaires was conducted from November 2023 to March 2024 in six geographic regions (Shanghai in East China, Guangdong Province in South China, Yunnan Province in Southwest China, Shaanxi Province and Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region in Northwest China, Liaoning Province in Northeast China). The participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) males over 18 years of age; (2) having had male sexual activity in the last 6 months; (3) being permanent local residents (residence for more than 6 months). Questionnaires that took less than 5 min to complete, had incorrect answers to quality control questions, or had an IP address that is not the place of residence were excluded.

Recruitment and study procedure

The recruitment of participants was mainly done with the support of local non-governmental organizations (NGO). Due to the non-public and hidden status of MSM, a snowball sampling method was used in this study to recruit participants instead of random sampling [34]. Approximately 5–10 eligible MSM were selected as “seeds” by local NGO, and they continued to recruit or recommend more research subjects within their personal social and sexual networks and so on, until the sample size reached the expected value. The selection of “seeds” followed criteria to ensure effective and representative sampling. First, “seeds” had to meet the study’s definition of MSM and be respected and trusted within MSM community, with credibility demonstrated through leadership roles, such as NGO members or community volunteers. Second, “seeds” were required to have close ties to hidden MSM to access private and less accessible groups. Third, “seeds” were selected to represent different MSM subgroups to ensure diversity. Fourth, “seeds” needed a large and diverse social network, defined as connections with at least 20–30 individuals across different subgroups. Lastly, “seeds” were chosen for their ability and enthusiasm to recruit others, demonstrating strong communication skills and a proactive attitude.

Data collection and quality control

NGO staff in each region were appointed as investigators. The investigators underwent standardized training to ensure they fully understood the survey’s purpose, content, methods, and precautions. After recruitment, participants were invited to local NGO workplaces to complete the surveys via a web-based platform named Wenjuanxing (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd, Changsha, China). To minimize the risk of duplicate recruitment, several preventive measures were implemented. First, participants were explicitly informed during the recruitment process that they should only complete the survey once, even if they were invited by multiple networks. Second, the survey platform recorded each participant’s WeChat ID as a unique identifier, preventing individuals from submitting multiple responses. Third, NGO staff members, who were familiar with the participants, oversaw the recruitment process and monitored for potential duplicate participation.

The questionnaire was anonymous and self-administered by the participants, and the investigators offered technical support and provided non-directive guidance when needed. A data quality monitoring team was established during data collection to regularly check the data and provide feedback within 48 h. After the data collection was complete, researchers carried out the steps of data cleansing and processing, including checking for outliers, missing data, logical errors, and duplicate entries, and making necessary corrections and adjustments. Participants were provided with a transportation subsidy of 80 RMB in cash (about US $10.96) as an incentive to enhance survey compliance. The subsidy was provided only after the participants had completed the questionnaire in full compliance with the study’s requirements.

Variables and measurements

The first draft of the questionnaire was designed based on the previous literature [27–33, 35–39] and guidance from experts. A number of MSM were invited to conduct a pilot survey, and the questionnaire was modified according to their feedback to improve readability and acceptability. The final questionnaire consisted of five parts: demographic characteristics, Mpox-related knowledge, risk perception, Mpox vaccination hesitation scale, and vaccination intention.

Demographic characteristics included age, education level, marital status, occupation, monthly income, residence status, and sexual orientation. Mpox-related knowledge was measured by 12 Mpox-related questions including severity, transmission, symptoms, and precautions (e.g., “Mpox is a viral infectious disease,” “Animals or people infected with Mpox are the source of infection”) [40, 41]. The options for each question were “true = 1” or “false = 0”. A total score was derived by tallying the correct answers to the 12 questions. A higher score indicated better knowledge of Mpox, with a total score ranging from 0 to 12. Risk perception of Mpox was measured by two items (e.g., “you feel susceptible to Mpox.”), with responses ranging from “very agree = 1” to “very disagree = 5” (range: 2–10). A composite score was calculated by summing up the scores of the two items. A higher score implied a higher level of risk perception [42].

The Mpox vaccination hesitation scale was developed based on the Protection Motivation Theory. The scale consisted of four dimensions, namely maladaptive rewards, response efficacy, response costs, and self-efficacy [37]. Maladaptive rewards, including intrinsic and external rewards, were used to measure the acceptance of factors that hindered vaccination intention from the individuals and external circumstances, respectively. Response efficacy was used to assess the perceived acceptance of the benefits of vaccination. Response costs indicated the subjective and objective difficulties and obstacles that might be faced in the decision-making and behavioral process of vaccination. Self-efficacy indicated confidence in overcoming barriers to getting vaccinated. The scale had 22 items, and each item was scored on a five-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating “very disagree” and 5 indicating “very agree”. The response efficacy (four items) and self-efficacy (seven items) were positively scored, while the maladaptive rewards (seven items) and response costs (four items) were negatively scored. The total scores of each dimension were calculated after the scores of maladaptive rewards and response costs were inversely assigned. The higher the score, the stronger the motivation to protect. The scale was proven to be a valid and reliable tool for assessing the vaccination intention among MSM in China [43]. The vaccination intention was assessed by a single item. Participants were asked “How willing are you to get vaccinated if there is a free vaccine to prevent Mpox?” A five-point Likert scale was used to assess the level of intention (from “very low” to “very high”). Participants who responded with “very high” and “high” were classified as the high vaccination intention group, while those who responded with “very low,” “low,” or “not sure” were classified as the low vaccination intention group [41]. This binary classification was applied consistently in all subsequent analyses.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed continuous data were described as median (inter-quartile range, IQR). Categorical data were described as frequency (percentage). The chi-square test was used to compare differences in basic sociodemographic information between the high vaccination intention group and the low vaccination intention group. Variables with significant differences were included as covariates in subsequent analyses. The hypothetical model was tested by SEM using the maximum likelihood method, a systematic instrument to evaluate the relationships within the theoretical model [44]. For the PMT constructs and other variables in the SEM, we calculated Cronbach’s α coefficients to assess the reliability of measurements, skewness and kurtosis to test the normality of each variable, and partial correlation coefficients to assess the relationship among the variables. Significant covariates identified in the sociodemographic analysis were incorporated into the SEM to control for potential confounding effects. Comparative, absolute, and residual aspects of fit were used to evaluate the fitting performance of the SEM. The ratio of chi-square and degrees of freedom (CMIN/DF) between 1 and 3, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) less than 0.08, goodness-of-fit index (GFI), comparative fit index (CFI), normed fit index (NFI), relative fit index (RFI), incremental fit index (IFI), and Tucker-Lewis index (TFI) greater than 0.9 indicated that the model was a goodness of fit [45]. Standardized regression coefficients were reported. A path diagram was applied to display the SEM. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and AMOS 28.0.

Results

Demographic of participants

A total of 2481 MSM were surveyed, and 2403 valid questionnaires were collected, with an effective rate of 96.86% (569 from Shanghai, 500 from Guangdong, 320 from Xinjiang, 199 from Shaanxi, 313 from Yunnan, 502 from Liaoning). The mean age of the surveyed population was 30.59 years, with a SD of 8.02. About half of them (51.1%) were between the ages of 25 and 34 years. Among them, 1888 (78.6%) had a college degree or above, 2035 (84.7%) were unmarried, and 1887 (78.5%) had a homosexual orientation. Additionally, 1008 (41.9%) and 1197 (49.8%) reported having unprotected anal intercourse and multiple sexual partners, respectively. A total of 2093 (87.1%) participants were categorized into the high vaccination intention group, while 310 (12.9%) were in the low vaccination intention group. There were significant differences in age group, marital status, monthly income, and homosexual orientation between the two groups (p < 0.05). The high vaccination intention group had a higher proportion of participants aged 25–34 years (51.7% vs. 46.8%), unmarried individuals (85.5% vs. 79.0%), and those with a homosexual orientation (79.6% vs. 71.0%). The low vaccination intention group had a higher proportion of participants with a monthly income between 3001 and 6000 RMB (41.3% vs. 34.5%). Structural equation model testing

Standardized coefficients for pathways in the SEM were summarized in Table 4 and depicted in Fig. 1. Firstly, maladaptive rewards (β = 0.237, p < 0.001), response efficacy (β = 0.106, p < 0.001), self-efficacy (β = 0.149, p < 0.001), and response costs (β = 0.055, p = 0.014) were directly associated with vaccination intention. Second, the knowledge score not only had a direct effect on vaccination intention (β = 0.040, p = 0.043) but also had an indirect effect on vaccination intention through maladaptive rewards (β = 0.178, p < 0.001), response efficacy (β = 0.171, p < 0.001), self-efficacy (β = 0.156, p < 0.001), and response costs (β = 0.069, p = 0.002). Third, risk perception was not directly related to vaccination intention (p = 0.295), but it was indirectly associated with vaccination intention through maladaptive rewards (β = 0.296, p < 0.001), response efficacy (β = 0.089, p = 0.001), self-efficacy (β = 0.225, p < 0.001), and response costs (β = 0.142, p < 0.001). The hypothetical model exhibited acceptable model fit with the CMIN/DF = 9.410, p < 0.001, RMSEA = 0.059, GFI = 0.911, CFI = 0.929, NFI = 0.921, RFI = 0.912, IFI = 0.929, TLI = 0.920 (Table 5). Discussion

The study employed the PMT combined with Mpox-related knowledge and risk perception to construct a SEM of Mpox vaccination intention among MSM in China. The results demonstrated the effictiveness of PMT constructs in understanding the determinants of Mpox vaccination intention. The proposed PMT-based SEM exhibited acceptable model fit with all variables—knowledge score, risk perception, maladaptive rewards, response efficacy, self-efficacy, and response costs—directly or indirectly influencing vaccination intention.

The results reported that 87.1% of MSM surveyed in this study had the intention of getting vaccinated. This value was similar to another study conducted in China, which showed that 84.7% of young MSM intended to receive Mpox vaccination [47]. One study in the Netherlands reported that 70% of MSM (56.6% under the age of 45 years) were interested in receiving the Mpox vaccine [48], and a French study showed that 33.6% of MSM (80.6% between 30 and 59 years old) expressed hesitancy to get vaccinated [49]. It was worth noting that most participants in these studies were young MSM. The majority of the MSM population in our study were also young people, with 93.7% under the age of 45. The higher prevalence of high-risk sexual behaviors among the young MSM highlighted the need to prioritize them in Mpox vaccination programs [50]. We speculated that age might be a reason for the high vaccination intention, as young people may be more receptive to emerging health services [51, 52].

The key finding of our study was that both the PMT threat appraisal pathway (maladaptive rewards) and coping appraisal pathway (response efficacy, self-efficacy, and response costs) were directly associated with Mpox vaccination intention. The results were consistent with previous studies about COVID-19 and HPV vaccination intention [31, 33]. Among all PMT variables, the construct of maladaptive rewards was the strongest predictor. The prominence of maladaptive rewards in predicting vaccination intention underscored the importance of identifying personal internal factors and external environmental factors that hinder motivation in public health messaging. Improving perceived response efficacy and self-efficacy contributed to high motivation of MSM to vaccinate against Mpox. When promoting the Mpox vaccination program, more information about the benefits of vaccination and how people overcome barriers to getting vaccinated should be promoted to encourage people to adopt self-protective behaviors [53]. Monetary cost, as an important part of response costs, was associated with taking the adaptive coping response. Free vaccination increased the prevalence of vaccination intention to a certain extent [39]. When developing the Mpox vaccination program, the price factor needs to be considered to increase the intention to be vaccinated.

Another important finding was that Mpox-related knowledge and risk perception had indirect effects by influencing other variables, suggesting that the acquisition of knowledge and perceived risk improve both threat appraisal and coping appraisal. Knowledge score could also directly improve the intention to be vaccinated, but the effect was relatively minor. The results were consistent with findings from recent studies on Mpox vaccination intention among MSM [54–57]. Research has demonstrated that MSM with a higher level of knowledge are more likely to perceive vaccination as effective and adopt protective behaviors (OR: 2.35, 95% CI: 1.51–3.66) [54]. MSM reported changing their sexual behaviors, like reducing the number of sex partners or one-time sexual encounters, since they learned about the Mpox outbreak [58]. Individuals’ concerns about their susceptibility to and the severity of a disease, along with their views on the benefits and obstacles of adopting behavioral changes, their self-efficacy, and the triggers prompting action, play a crucial role in shaping their health behavior adoption [56].

A significant concern is that large proportions of vaccine-related content on popular social media platforms are anti-vaccination messages [59]. Barriers such as stigma, vaccine accessibility, and misinformation have been shown to negatively impact vaccination motivation in this group [60]. Such misinformation may further contribute to vaccination hesitancy, especially among younger populations [61]. The government should strengthen the regulation of vaccine-related content on social media, combating misinformation and fostering a more accurate information environment. Improving the understanding of Mpox-related knowledge (e.g., source, route, precautions, etc.) and vaccines among MSM is essential to build trust in public health interventions and strengthen their conviction about the benefits of vaccination. Effective health communication strategies should focus on providing accurate, transparent, and accessible information, empowering MSM individuals to make informed decisions about vaccination and other protective measures.

In the general population, the willingness to receive the Mpox vaccine varies significantly across different countries, ranging from 29.30% in Romania to 76.4% in China [62, 63]. This highlights notable differences in vaccine acceptance between populations and regions. While the PMT-based SEM effectively explains vaccination intention among the Chinese MSM, its applicability to the general population or MSM in other countries may vary. The added value of focusing on MSM as a key population lies in their disproportionate risk of Mpox infection and their critical role in the transmission dynamics of the disease [64]. Public health interventions tailored to MSM not only help reduce Mpox incidence within this group but also contribute to community-level protection by interrupting transmission chains. This highlights the importance of developing models and strategies that address the unique needs and characteristics of key populations, which may not be captured in studies of the general population.

The study’s findings have several implications for designing effective public health interventions aimed at increasing Mpox vaccination intention among MSM in China [65]. Firstly, given the strong influence of maladaptive rewards, public health strategies should address both internal factors (e.g., believing one is healthy enough to not need vaccination or believing a clean lifestyle eliminates risk) and external factors (e.g., fear of social stigma or peer perceptions). To counter these barriers, campaigns could emphasize that Mpox vaccination is essential for everyone, regardless of health status or behavior, while normalizing vaccination to reduce stigma. Second, enhancing individuals’ belief in the effectiveness and safety of the Mpox vaccine is crucial. This can be achieved through transparent communication of vaccine research findings, efficacy rates, and success stories from vaccination campaigns. Third, interventions should aim to increase individuals’ confidence in their ability to obtain and receive the vaccine. This could involve simplifying the vaccination process, providing clear instructions on where and how to get vaccinated. Fourth, while knowledge alone has a limited direct effect, it plays a significant role in enhancing other motivational factors. Educational initiatives should aim to improve understanding of the Mpox vaccine’s design, function, and benefits, while tailoring messages to appropriately calibrate risk perceptions. Ensuring that the perceived severity and vulnerability to Mpox are accurately represented can activate motivational pathways and foster greater vaccine acceptance. Finally, in regions where MSM status is stigmatized or constrained by political or cultural limitations, individuals may face substantial barriers to accessing vaccines and treatment. These barriers include fears of discrimination, breaches of privacy, or legal repercussions associated with disclosing their sexual identity. Public health strategies should prioritize creating safe and inclusive environments for MSM by implementing policies ensuring confidentiality, providing anonymous vaccination services, and offering culturally sensitive training for healthcare providers. Advocacy efforts to promote the legal and social rights of MSM can further reduce stigma, enhance societal acceptance, and build trust in public health systems, ultimately improving vaccination uptake [65, 66].

There were some limitations under the consideration in interpreting the results of this study. Firstly, this study was a cross-sectional study, so causal relationships and temporal sequence cannot be explained. Longitudinal studies can be focused on determining causality in the future. Second, the snowball sampling method was used to recruit participants due to the concealment of MSM. It introduced selection bias, as participants recruited through social networks were more likely to share similar characteristics and thus limited the diversity within the sample and might have impacted the internal validity of the study. Future studies should consider alternative sampling methods, such as random sampling where feasible, to further reduce this limitation. Third, the generalizability of the findings was limited by the confined number of study sites, as the data were collected from specific regions and networks. This restriction may have hindered the applicability of the results to the broader MSM population, especially those outside the study regions. Future studies should expand the number and diversity of study sites, ensuring broader geographical and demographic coverage. Fourth, the large sample size resulted in the chi-square value of the SEM being highly sensitive, leading to a poor fit. To address this, we referred to additional indicators to evaluate the goodness-of-fit of the model as a whole. Fifth, the survey used a self-report questionnaire, which may have introduced reporting bias, as participants might have provided socially desirable answers rather than truthful responses. Lastly, our study primarily focused on exploring the impact of individual cognition on vaccination intention. Factors such as monetary cost, which might have also influenced vaccination intention, were not considered in this study. Future research could incorporate these factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of vaccination behaviors.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the majority of MSM had the intention to receive the Mpox vaccination. It is suggested that the vaccination program focus on young MSM in response to the Mpox outbreak in the future. The study underscores the applicability of the PMT in elucidating the factors influencing Mpox vaccination intention among MSM in China. The findings highlight the pivotal roles of maladaptive rewards, response efficacy, self-efficacy and response costs in directly shaping vaccination intention, while also illustrating the indirect pathways through Mpox-related knowledge and risk perception. Public health interventions aiming to increase vaccination intention in this population should strategically focus on these psychological constructs to effectively motivate protective health behaviors and mitigate the spread of Mpox.
