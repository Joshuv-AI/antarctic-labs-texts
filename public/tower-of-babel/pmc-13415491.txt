Retrospective Assessment of Food Noise Changes After Initiation of Injectable Semaglutide for Weight Management in the USA: The INFORM Survey
Authors: Timothy Arnaut, Shannon Duncan, Mads Faurby, Julie Hviid Hahn-Pedersen, Kajsa Kvist, Nele Steenackers, John B Buse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction‘Food noise’, persistent and intrusive thoughts about food that can disrupt daily life, has emerged as a concern for individuals with obesity, and often impacts quality of life. Recent anecdotal reports suggest that glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, such as semaglutide, may reduce food noise. Research is needed to characterize food noise in real-world populations and to quantify the impact of weight management treatments on its presentation and intensity. We present findings from the INFORM survey, which assessed perceived changes in food noise among US adults receiving injectable semaglutide for weight management.MethodsIndividuals recruited from Numerator’s opinion panel were asked to complete 22 multiple choice questions, including the five-item Food Noise Questionnaire (FNQ).ResultsIn total, 550 people participated in the survey. Respondents were predominantly women (86%) and white (79%), with a mean age of 53 years (standard deviation 10.8). Most had baseline body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2 (86%) and had used semaglutide for at least 4 months (81%). Based on recollection of food noise before initiating semaglutide, the median FNQ score was 13 [interquartile range (IQR) 10–16] out of a possible maximum score of 20. After initiation of semaglutide, the median FNQ score was 6 (IQR 3–10). Overall median change was −5 (IQR −10, −2). The proportion of participants agreeing/strongly agreeing with statements indicating the presence of food noise decreased from 47–63% before semaglutide to 15–20% after. These reductions were consistent across subgroups defined by treatment duration and BMI. Most respondents (83%) reported treatment satisfaction, particularly those with longer treatment duration, and the majority agreed that their mental health, self-confidence, lifestyle and habits had improved since initiating semaglutide.ConclusionFurther prospective, longitudinal research is warranted to fully characterize these apparent promising effects of semaglutide for weight management, and their relationship with clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03636-x.Keywords: Food noise, Obesity, Semaglutide, Survey, Treatment satisfaction

Key Summary Points

Introduction

The term ‘food noise’ has recently emerged to describe obsessive and intrusive thoughts about food. These persistent thoughts about food can occur independently of hunger or the presence of food-related environmental cues [1, 2]. Individuals experiencing food noise report that these thoughts can be incessant and burdensome, negatively affecting their mental well-being and quality of life [1, 2].

Food noise can be understood within the neurocognitive framework of obesity, whereby dysregulation of appetite and food-related reward processing may contribute to increased frequency and persistence of food-related thoughts [3]. This is supported by evidence of altered dopaminergic signaling and heightened food cue reactivity in individuals with obesity [4, 5]. Although the construct is new to scientific literature, food noise appears to be common, especially in the context of dieting or medical weight management. Multiple accounts from patients receiving glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) for weight management report a “quietening” or cessation of food noise associated with these medications [1, 2, 6]. This is supported by a recent survey of individuals receiving GLP-1RAs, in which some participants reported reduced food noise, alongside improved satiety and an increased ability to adopt healthier eating habits [7]. A previous 20-week, randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind trial of semaglutide 2.4 mg involving individuals with obesity also found significant reductions in food cravings, as measured by the Control of Eating Questionnaire, versus placebo [8].

Until recently, there was no validated tool for food noise. The Food Noise Questionnaire (FNQ) provided the first psychometrically valid means to measure food noise, defined in this case as “persistent, intrusive thoughts about food that are disruptive to daily life and make healthy behaviors difficult” [6]. The FNQ items were created following review of published literature and patient reports and refined by a panel of experts, resulting in five items scored on a 5-point Likert-type scale. Higher scores indicate greater food noise; maximum possible score is 20 points. The FNQ has been shown to have high internal consistency and test–retest reliability, in addition to good convergent and discriminant validity compared with other instruments [6]. Use of the FNQ in populations receiving weight-loss treatments is now required to determine its clinical utility.

The INFORM survey was used to examine perceived changes in food noise, measured retrospectively with the FNQ, among US individuals receiving injectable semaglutide for weight management. In addition, the survey was used to collect data on treatment satisfaction, well-being, and lifestyle following initiation of semaglutide, to contextualize food noise changes.

Methods

Recruitment, Eligibility Criteria and Survey Administration

Participants were recruited via Numerator (Chicago, IL, USA), which operates a proprietary, permission-based panel of several hundred thousand verified US households. Panelists opt in to participate and share information about their shopping behavior, opinions and demographic details, including age, gender, ethnicity, education level, household size and income bracket. The panel is balanced and weighted to the US census in terms of demographic factors such as age and sex. Via Numerator’s secure platform, panelists receive invitations to complete surveys and accumulate in-app rewards for survey participation.

Panelists who were pre-identified in an earlier survey as users of semaglutide for weight management were invited to participate in the INFORM survey. Eligible individuals were US adults who reported upon initiating the survey that they were currently using semaglutide for weight management; there were no formal exclusion criteria. There was no planned sample size, but all eligible participants who responded to the survey were included. Of the panelists available for this survey, 87% self-identified as women, 12% as men, and 1% as other.

Each respondent has a unique user ID, ensuring that they can only complete the survey once, thus avoiding duplicate responses. The survey was available to complete on a mobile device, and could be completed in more than one sitting. Responses were collected between 13 and 21 May 2025.

Study Design and Survey Content

INFORM was a cross-sectional survey designed collaboratively between Novo Nordisk and Numerator (see Supplementary file – INFORM survey). All data were self-reported by the respondents. The survey consisted of 22 multiple choice questions covering: duration of taking semaglutide and current dose; current weight, estimated weight before taking semaglutide and height; satisfaction with current weight management treatment; perceived changes in mental health, self-confidence, lifestyle and habits since initiating semaglutide; impact of food noise currently, and recollected impact of food noise before initiating semaglutide, using the FNQ [6].

Self-reported weight and height were used to estimate body mass index (BMI) prior to the initiation of semaglutide. Demographic details for the respondents were already recorded owing to their membership of the Numerator panel.

FNQ Questions, Scoring and Reporting of Data

The primary outcome of interest was changes in food noise, as scored using the FNQ. The items in the FNQ are:

‘I spend too much time thinking about food’

‘I spend too much time thinking about food’

‘I find myself constantly thinking about food throughout the day’

‘I find myself constantly thinking about food throughout the day’

‘My thoughts about food have negative effects on me and/or my life’

‘My thoughts about food have negative effects on me and/or my life’

‘My thoughts about food feel uncontrollable’

‘My thoughts about food feel uncontrollable’

‘My thoughts about food distract me from what I need to do’.

‘My thoughts about food distract me from what I need to do’.

Responses were scored as follows: strongly disagree (score: 0); disagree (score: 1); neither agree nor disagree (score: 2); agree (score: 3); strongly agree (score: 4). The total score for the FNQ was calculated by summing responses for the five items, with a maximum possible score of 20. Higher total scores indicate greater food noise. Here, we report the percentages of survey participants who selected each response when asked to consider food noise before and after initiation of semaglutide, along with mean and median changes in FNQ score.

Secondary outcomes related to treatment satisfaction, well-being, and lifestyle. Questions on these concepts had the same response options as the FNQ. We report the percentages of participants who selected each response when asked to consider the impact of receiving semaglutide for weight management.

No statistical testing was carried out, and descriptive statistics (sample size and percentage, mean with standard deviation, and median with interquartile range) were used to describe demographic data.

Subgroup Analyses

Results are reported for the full cohort, for subgroups defined by length of time receiving semaglutide for weight management (< 4 months, 4–12 months, and > 1 year), for subgroups with a starting BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 and < 30 kg/m2, and for subgroups of women and men.

Quality Control and Handling of Missing Data

Protocols in place on the Numerator platform ensured that a survey can only be completed once by a given panel member. All questions in the survey required a response before participants could move to the next question; for questions regarding demographic details, such as weight and height, participants had the option to select ‘Prefer not to answer’. Numerator uses data cleaning tools to quarantine responses that indicate straightlining, patterned responses, or speeding (completing the survey too quickly).

Ethical Approval

The Numerator panel is opt in and all members provide informed consent for Numerator to use their data, including for publication in a scholarly journal. All data are pseudonymized. The INFORM survey was approved by the legal team at Numerator. Institutional Review Board approval/waiver was not requested. This was considered to be unnecessary because this patient survey does not meet the definition of ‘research on human subjects’, as there was no direct communication between investigator and subject and no collection of identifiable private information [informed by federal regulation (FDA) 21 CFR 50 and 56].

Results

Population and Characteristics

The survey was distributed to 673 individuals who were using semaglutide for weight management, of whom 550 individuals (82%) completed the INFORM survey. Overall, the majority of respondents self-identified as women (n = 473; 86%). Mean age of the respondents was 53 years [standard deviation (SD) 10.8] and median age was 54 years [interquartile range (IQR) 45–62]. The majority of respondents self-identified as white (79%; n = 432), 13% (n = 71) self-identified as Black or African American, 6% (n = 35) as Hispanic, 1% (n = 6) as Asian, and the remaining 1% (n = 6) selected ‘Other’ for the question on ethnicity. Before initiation of semaglutide, 86% of respondents (n = 473) had an estimated BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 and the remainder (n = 71) had an estimated BMI < 30 kg/m2.

Duration of Semaglutide Use and Dose

Most individuals (81%; n = 447) reported that they had used semaglutide for weight management for at least 4 months (Table 1). Overall, 39% (n = 217) of respondents reported that they were receiving the 2.4-mg dose of semaglutide, and 65% (n = 106) of respondents who reported receiving semaglutide for more than a year indicated that they were receiving the 2.4-mg dose (Supplementary Fig. S1); 41% (n = 194) of individuals with an estimated BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 and 31% (n = 22) of those with an estimated BMI < 30 kg/m2 reported receiving the 2.4-mg dose. Overall, 41% (n = 194) of women and 30% (n = 23) of men reported receiving the 2.4-mg dose.

Food Noise

Overall FNQ scores were calculated for the 547 participants who answered all relevant questions in the survey. Based on participants’ recollection of food noise before initiating semaglutide, mean FNQ score was 12.7 [95% confidence interval (CI) 12.3–13.1; SD 5.0] and median score was 13 (IQR 10–16). After initiation of semaglutide, mean FNQ score was 6.6 (95% CI 6.2–7.0; SD 4.8) and median score was 6 (IQR 3–10). The mean change in FNQ score following semaglutide treatment was − 6.1 (95% CI − 6.6, − 5.6; SD 5.6), with a median change of − 5 (IQR − 10, − 2). Between 47% and 63% of respondents recollected that, before initiating semaglutide, they would have agreed or strongly agreed with each of the statements in the FNQ (Fig. 2). However, when asked to consider how they felt at the time of completing the survey, only 15–20% of respondents agreed/strongly agreed with each statement, highlighting the perceived reduction in the impact of food noise since initiation of semaglutide indicated by the reduction in FNQ score. The largest reductions were for the statements ‘I spend too much time thinking about food’ (63% believed that they would have agreed before initiation of semaglutide; 15% agreed after) and ‘I find myself constantly thinking about food throughout the day’ (62% would have agreed before; 16% agreed after).

Responses to the FNQ, by percentage of respondents agreeing with each statement. FNQ Food Noise Questionnaire

The same pattern was observed across groups defined by duration of semaglutide treatment. Respondents who had been receiving semaglutide for longer than 4 months were relatively more likely to recollect food noise having a negative impact prior to semaglutide initiation, and to report a lower current impact of food noise. However, there were large disparities between recollected food noise and current reported food noise even in individuals who had been receiving semaglutide for less than 4 months (Supplementary Table S1).

In general, larger reductions in the percentages of respondents agreeing or strongly agreeing with each statement were observed in the group with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 than in the group with BMI < 30 kg/m2 (Supplementary Table S1).

In general, food noise results for both women and men were similar to results for the full sample (Supplementary Table S1), with two notable disparities between these subgroups defined by sex. For the statement ‘My thoughts about food felt/feel uncontrollable’, 52% of women indicated that they would have agreed before initiation of semaglutide, but 14% agreed after; in the subgroup of men, 56% would have agreed before semaglutide initiation but 25% agreed after. For the statement ‘My thoughts about food distract(ed) me from what I need(ed) to do’, 46% of women would have agreed before semaglutide initiation and 13% agreed after, whereas 53% of men would have agreed before and 22% agreed after.

Treatment Satisfaction

Overall, 83% of respondents (n = 457) reported that they were satisfied with their weight management treatment (Fig. 3). This percentage was numerically higher in those with lower initial BMI [≥ 30 kg/m2, 82% (n = 387); < 30 kg/m2, 90% (n = 64)], and tended to increase with duration of time receiving semaglutide: 90% (n = 146) of those who had received semaglutide for more than a year expressed satisfaction with their weight management treatment (Supplementary Table S2). In the subgroups defined by sex, a slightly greater proportion of men [87% (n = 67)] than women [82% (n = 290)] reported treatment satisfaction.

Well-Being and Lifestyle

The majority of respondents agreed or strongly agreed that their mental health (64%; n = 352), self-confidence (76%; n = 417), lifestyle (76%; n = 420) and habits (80%; n = 438) had improved since initiating semaglutide (Fig. 3). These percentages tended to remain similar regardless of BMI (Supplementary Table S2). For all statements except ‘I developed a healthier lifestyle’, agreement was higher among respondents who had received semaglutide for more than 4 months than among those who had received it for 4 months or less. Slightly higher proportions of women than men reported improvements when answering these questions, notably for improvements in mental health [women: 65% (n = 307); men: 58% (n = 45)] and lifestyle [women: 78% (n = 369); men: 66% (n = 51)].

Discussion

Thus far, few published studies have quantified the effect of weight management interventions on food noise. In this survey-based study, we set out to capture the impacts of semaglutide treatment for weight management beyond purely clinical effects, and have shown that respondents reported various perceived treatment benefits, such as improvements in well-being. Notably, the reduced impact of self-reported food noise aligned with anecdotal evidence that GLP-1RAs may effectively limit or silence intrusive thoughts about food. The perceived treatment benefits in our study tended to increase with length of time receiving semaglutide; however, individuals who had been taking semaglutide for only a few months still reported these effects. Similarly, treatment benefits were perceived regardless of BMI upon initiation of semaglutide.

The median food noise scores recorded in the INFORM survey were higher than those reported by the cohort in the original analysis of Diktas et al. Respondents to our survey, most of whom had a BMI of 30 kg/m2 or higher, reported a median pre-semaglutide FNQ score of 13, whereas Diktas et al. found that individuals with a BMI of 25.0–29.9 kg/m2 or 30.0–39.9 kg/m2 had a median FNQ score of 7.0, and those with BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 had a median FNQ score of 9.5 [6]. The disparities between studies may be attributable to both patient selection and study design. It is plausible that individuals who have opted to use semaglutide for weight management may experience higher baseline food noise than the general population living with obesity. However, the design of the survey may also introduce recall bias by asking respondents currently receiving semaglutide to estimate food noise before the start of their treatment, leading to inflated estimates of baseline food noise. In addition to this, selection bias may have resulted in a survey population who were disproportionately likely to report treatment benefit, and therefore to rate baseline food noise as higher on average than the overall population receiving semaglutide for weight management.

Although our study was cross-sectional in nature, it supports the findings of previous longitudinal studies. In a phase 1, 6-week randomized controlled trial (RCT) that included scores on the Food Craving Questionnaire-State, the Food Craving Inventory, the Eating Inventory, the Power of Food Scale, and an appetite Visual Analogue Scale as secondary outcomes, tirzepatide was associated with reductions in appetite, hunger, cravings, tendency to overeat, and reactivity to environmental food cues, versus placebo [9]. In the STEP-5 phase 3 RCT, injectable semaglutide 2.4 mg improved craving control and control of eating, measured using the Control of Eating Questionnaire (CoEQ), over 104 weeks, compared with placebo [10]. In the STEP UP phase 3 RCT, semaglutide 7.2 mg was associated with improvements in craving control, emotional eating and uncontrolled eating versus placebo after 72 weeks, indicated by changes in scores on both the CoEQ domains and the Three-Factor-Eating Questionnaire (revised 18-item version 2) [11]. A 20-week RCT of oral semaglutide 50 mg versus placebo reported similar findings [12]. These effects are also observed in clinical practice. A real-world observational study found that people living with obesity reported a reduction in emotional eating and cravings over 3 months of receiving semaglutide [13], and in a qualitative interview-based study of people receiving weight loss medication, participants reported reductions in food noise [14].

Wider research into the concept of food noise is warranted, both in those receiving weight management interventions and more broadly in people living with obesity. In addition to the development of the FNQ, this research has been facilitated by the development and validation of a second instrument, the Ro Allison Indiana Dhurandhar Food Noise Inventory (RAID-FN) [15]. Developed to encompass a definition of food noise as “persistent thoughts about food that are perceived by the individual as unwanted and/or dysphoric and may cause harm, including social, mental, or physical problems”, and initially developed as a 29-item draft questionnaire [1], the validated version of the RAID-FN consists of seven items across the domains of preoccupation, persistence, and dysphoria. The instrument was found to have good internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and convergent validity [15]. Further testing and more widespread use of both the FNQ and RAID-FN are now required to assess concurrence or discrepancies between the two instruments. The INFORM survey used in our study was designed to be simple to understand and easy to administer. In addition to the questions from the validated FNQ [6], the inclusion of items relating to mental health and lifestyle was intended to capture further patient-relevant benefits of weight management treatment. This approach requires refinement ahead of conducting more extensive studies on food noise and other benefits that matter to people living with obesity.

The present study recruited a convenience sample of individuals using injectable semaglutide for weight loss and has clear limitations. In particular, the cross-sectional study design and single measurement time point taking place after initiation of semaglutide meant that responses regarding both baseline food noise and current well-being could be affected by recall bias. Treatment benefits were reported regardless of length of time receiving semaglutide; however, the observation that relatively greater benefits of semaglutide in terms of food noise reduction were reported by people who had been receiving it for longer must be interpreted with caution and tested in future research, to determine whether this is a true reflection of cumulative treatment benefit or arises in part from increases in recall bias over time. The study design was also limited by the potential for selection bias. The opt-in nature of the survey may have selected for individuals who held positive views of their semaglutide treatment; in addition, by selecting only individuals currently receiving semaglutide for weight management, the survey excluded individuals who had discontinued semaglutide for reasons of cost, adverse events or lack of benefit. This may have resulted in higher-than-average treatment satisfaction and perceived improvements in well-being. The potential for both recall and selection bias was a key reason why self-reported weight loss data captured in the survey are not reported in the present manuscript, particularly given that the analysis was not designed to assess or quantify any relationship between weight loss and food noise. This will be an important area for future research.

There were additional limitations relating to the study population and availability of data. The survey sample was disproportionately white and female, and data on key clinical factors relevant to obesity and receipt of weight management intervention, particularly baseline comorbidities, were not captured. Overall, these limitations mean that the study population cannot be considered representative of the wider US population receiving semaglutide for weight management. It is also important to note that the study lacked a control group, and that all outcomes were self-reported and largely based on subjective measures. The minimal clinically important difference for FNQ score remains to be defined; this will be an important step to contextualize the findings of future research using this instrument.

Future studies, including analyses with a prospective design, ideally RCTs, will be vital to assess the full real-world behavioral and psychological impacts of semaglutide for weight management, including how this links to relevant factors such as changes in diet. The results of our study, notably the finding that men reported smaller benefits than women for some lifestyle and well-being factors, require further research and highlight the importance of recruiting demographically representative populations for such analyses. Future studies should also build on the present analysis by incorporating the findings of ongoing research into the epidemiology, presentation and impacts of food noise [1]. In due course, comparisons between weight management medications will also be of benefit to guide treatment decision-making.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that receipt of semaglutide for weight management may be associated with reductions in food noise, in addition to improvements in self-confidence, mental health, lifestyle, and habits. Further research on the concept of food noise and its manifestations in people living with obesity and those on weight management treatment is warranted. More rigorous studies with a prospective design will help to characterize the potential beneficial effects of semaglutide on food noise reduction, and how this is related to clinical improvements such as weight loss.
