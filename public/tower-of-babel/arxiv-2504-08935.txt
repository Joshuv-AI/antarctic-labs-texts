Higher-Order Derivatives of First-Passage Percolation with Respect to the Environment
Ivan Matić, Radoš Radoičić, Dan Stefanica

Abstract
. We introduce and study derivatives in first-passage percolation with edge weights given by i.i.d. random variables supported on { a , b } \{a,b\} . We show that the variance of the passage time can be expressed in terms of these derivatives. We further analyze their structure and establish several fundamental properties and bounds. Our bounds for the lower Fourier levels on the torus model raise the prospect that, in dimensions 3 and higher, the variance may grow slower than any positive power of n n . Such growth would contradict the commonly held belief that the fluctuation exponent is positive.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Definition of the model

The first-passage percolation model was introduced by Hammersley and Welsh in
[
25
]
. Let
p\in(0,1)
and let
a
and
b
be two positive real numbers that satisfy
a<b
.
Consider the graph whose vertices are elements of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap[-2n,2n]^{d}
(with
d\geq 2
), where two vertices
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
and
(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
are connected by an edge if
|x_{1}-y_{1}|+\cdots+|x_{d}-y_{d}|=1
.

Let
W_{n}
denote the set of edges of this graph. The sample space is defined as
\Omega_{n}=\{a,b\}^{W_{n}}
. Each edge
e
of the graph is independently assigned a passage time of either
a
or
b
, with probabilities
\mathbb{P}(a)=p
and
\mathbb{P}(b)=1-p
.

For a fixed environment
\omega\in\Omega_{n}
and a path
\gamma
, the passage time
T(\gamma,\omega)
is defined as the sum of the values assigned to the edges of
\gamma
. For two fixed vertices
u
and
v
, the passage time
f(u,v,\omega)
is the random variable defined as the minimum of
T(\gamma,\omega)
over all paths
\gamma
connecting
u
to
v
.

When
v
is fixed, we will also use the notation
f_{n}(\omega)
,
f_{n}
, or simply
f
, in place of
f(0,nv,\omega)
.

For some of our results, we will only work on a simplified model from
[
13
]
that has more symmetry and fewer technical challenges: the percolation is considered on the torus
\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{d}
. We will use superscript
\tau
and write
f_{n}^{\tau}(\omega)
,
f_{n}^{\tau}
, or
f^{\tau}
to emphasize when we are working on this simplified torus model.
Formally, a
d
-dimensional torus is a graph whose vertices are elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{d}
and two vertices
u
and
v
are connected by an edge if and only if there is exactly one coordinate
k\in\{1,2,\dots,d\}
such that
u_{k}-v_{k}\equiv\pm 1
(mod
n
) and
u_{j}=v_{j}
for all
j\neq k
.
The set of admissible paths
\Gamma
consists of all paths that wrap around the torus in the direction
x_{1}
. Hence, the path
(s,v_{1},\dots,v_{m},e)
belongs to the set
\Gamma
if it is a path in the graph and if the starting and ending vertices
s
and
e
have all the coordinates the same except for the first coordinate. Their first coordinates are
s_{1}=0
and
e_{1}=n-1
. The random variable
f^{\tau}_{n}
is defined as the minimum of the passage times among all paths
\gamma
that consist of adjacent edges and that wrap around the torus exactly once in the direction
x_{1}
.
The function
f^{\tau}_{n}
is defined as

f^{\tau}_{n}(\omega)=\min\left\{T(\gamma,\omega):\gamma\in\Gamma\right\}.

A path
\gamma
is called
geodesic
if the minimum
f_{n}(\omega)
(or
f^{\tau}_{n}
, depending on which problem we are studying) is attained at
\gamma
, i.e. if
f_{n}(\omega)=T(\gamma,\omega)
.

1.2.
Definition of environment derivatives

If we denote by
W_{n}
the set of all edges, then the sample space is
\Omega_{n}=\{a,b\}^{W_{n}}
. We will often omit the subscript
n
when there is no danger of confusion. For each edge
j
and each
\omega\in\Omega
, we define
\sigma_{j}^{a}(\omega)
as the outcome from
\Omega
whose passage time over the edge
j
is changed from
\omega_{j}
to
a
, regardless of what the original value
\omega_{j}
was. The operation
\sigma_{j}^{b}
is defined in an analogous way. Formally, for
\delta\in\{a,b\}
, we define
\sigma_{j}^{\delta}:\Omega\to\Omega
with

\left[\sigma_{j}^{\delta}(\omega)\right]_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\omega_{k},&k\neq j,\\
\delta,&k=j.\end{array}\right.

If
\varphi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is any random variable, then the
first order environment derivative

\partial_{j}\varphi
is the random variable defined as

\partial_{j}\varphi=\varphi\circ\sigma_{j}^{b}-\varphi\circ\sigma_{j}^{a}.

For two distinct edges
i
and
j
, we will give the name
second order environment derivative
to the quantity
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi
. In general, if
S
is a non-empty subset of
W
, the operator
\partial_{S}\varphi
is defined recursively as

\partial_{S}\varphi=\partial_{S\setminus\{j\}}\left(\partial_{j}\varphi\right),

where
j
is an arbitrary element of
S
. The definition is independent on the choice of
j
, since a simple induction can be used to prove that for
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\}
, the following holds

\partial_{S}\varphi=\sum_{\theta_{1}\in\{a,b\}}\cdots\sum_{\theta_{m}\in\{a,b\}}(-1)^{\mathbf{1}_{a}(\theta_{1})+\cdots+\mathbf{1}_{a}(\theta_{m})}\varphi\circ\sigma_{s_{1}}^{\theta_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{s_{m}}^{\theta_{m}}.

The function
\mathbf{1}_{a}:\{a,b\}\to\{0,1\}
assigns the value
1
to
a
and
0
to
b
.

Let

\displaystyle\text{var}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\subseteq W,M\neq\emptyset}\left(p(1-p)\right)^{|M|}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{M}f\right]\right)^{2}.

(1)

Let us define Fourier level

as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{j}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\subseteq W,\;|M|=j\end{subarray}}\left(\mathbb{E}\big[\partial_{M}f\big]\right)^{2}.

Then, for each

\displaystyle=L_{k}(f)+R_{k}(f),\quad\text{where}

\displaystyle\quad L_{k}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(p(1-p))^{j}\Sigma_{j}(f)\;\text{ and }\;R_{k}(f)=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}(p(1-p))^{j}\Sigma_{j}(f).

Bound on the lower sum

On the torus model, we can prove that for fixed

In dimensions

\displaystyle=p(1-p)\cdot(b-a)^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{b}{ap}\right)^{1+\zeta}

1.5.
Bound on the remainder
R_{k}

The following result holds for general random variables. Hence, it applies both to
f
and
f^{\tau}
.

Theorem 3
.

For every integer
k\geq 1
, there exists a real constant
C
and an integer
n_{0}
such that
for
n\geq n_{0}
, and every random variable
f
on
\Omega_{n}
its remainder
R_{k}=R_{k}(f)
satisfies

\displaystyle R_{k}(f)

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\sum_{M\subseteq W,|M|=k,\|\partial_{M}f\|_{1}\neq 0}\frac{\left\|\partial_{M}f\right\|_{2}^{2}}{1+\left(\log\frac{\|\partial_{M}f\|_{2}}{\|\partial_{M}f\|_{1}}\right)^{k}},

(4)

where
\|g\|_{p}
is the
L^{p}
-norm of the function
g

\|g\|_{p}=\left(\int|g|^{p}\,d\mathbb{P}\right)^{1/p}=\left(\mathbb{E}[|g|^{p}]\right)^{1/p}.

1.6.
Conjectures about variance in higher dimensions

Theorem
2
casts the variance
conjecture in a new light, not by bringing it closer to resolution,
but by making it more mysterious. In dimensions
d\geq 3
, the lowest Fourier levels contribute at most
O(n^{1+\zeta-\zeta d})
to the variance. The value
\zeta
can be chosen to make the exponent of
n
negative. If the
widely conjectured bound
\mathrm{var}(f)=O(n^{2\chi})
with

\chi>0
holds in dimensions greater than or equal to
3
, then the divergence cannot come from the lowest levels. It must come entirely from higher Fourier
levels. This would be a striking phenomenon: the cumulative effect of higher-order interactions produces
polynomial growth. On the other hand, if higher Fourier levels are
also well-controlled, as we conjecture, particularly in dimensions

d\geq 3
where the
L^{2}
norms
\|\partial_{M}f\|_{2}
may decay
exponentially in
k
, then the variance itself would be
O(1)
,
implying
\chi=0
in higher dimensions. Either conclusion would be
remarkable, and both remain out of reach.

1.7.
Methods used in proofs and overview of literature

The inequality (
4
) in the case
k=1
was proved by Talagrand
[
39
]
. In
[

]
, Tanguy generalized Talagrand’s inequality to include second order environment derivatives. In
[
40
]
, the second order result was

\displaystyle\text{var}(f)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sum_{i}\|\partial_{i}f\|_{1+e^{-2s_{0}}}^{2}+\sum_{M\subseteq W,|M|=2,\|\partial_{M}f\|_{1}\neq 0}\frac{\left\|\partial_{M}f\right\|_{2}^{2}}{1+\left(\log\frac{\|\partial_{M}f\|_{2}}{\|\partial_{M}f\|_{1}}\right)^{2}}\right).

(5)

The bound (
5
) treats the
k=2
case for Boolean functions on the uniform discrete cube

\{-1,1\}^{n}
via the Bonami-Beckner semigroup. Tanguy
[
40
]
remarked that the proof technique could be extended to general
k
, while noting that the notation would become heavy. Further iteration of the semigroup machinery would likely introduce additional complexities in the first summation.

The results of Talagrand
[
39
]
and Benjamini, Kalai, and Schramm
[
13
]

show how inequalities with first order environment derivatives lead to bounds on the variance of the form
\text{var}(f_{n})\leq C\cdot\frac{n}{\log n}
and
\text{var}(f^{\tau}_{n})\leq C\cdot\frac{n}{\log n}
, for some constant
C
.

In dimension

, the right-hand side of (

) becomes
O(n)
, which
is consistent with the central limit theorem: the passage time
T(0,ne_{1})

is a sum of
n
i.i.d. variables with variance
\Theta(n)
, and the bound
is trivially satisfied. The bound becomes informative only in
d\geq 2
,
where the FPP variance is conjectured to be sublinear in
n
.

The conjectured upper bound for the variance in first-passage percolation is
C\cdot n^{2\chi}
, where
\chi
is an exponent that depends on the dimension.
Current predictions suggest that
\chi
is
\frac{1}{3}
in the two-dimensional case
[

]
.
A bound of

was established by Kesten in 1993

, and to date, there is no formal proof that

is strictly less than
\frac{1}{2}
.
In dimensions higher than two, even conjectural values for

remain unclear. According to
[
7
]
, there are reasons to believe that

remains strictly positive in all dimensions, though it tends to

as

The existence and location of a critical dimension, above which the variance ceases to grow as

[

]
and predicted in the physics literature
[

]
.
Our bound (

The coefficient

It is worth noting that the value

The TASEP model belongs to a class of exactly solvable models that can be analyzed using techniques from random matrix theory. In this setting, a central limit theorem has been proven, with the limiting

The best current variance bound for first-passage percolation is

Their approach relies on Talagrand’s inequality

Our Theorem

(6)

We conjecture that

However, these quantities remain difficult to analyze at present.

A complete understanding of the environment derivatives is equivalent to a complete understanding of the variance, due to (

Theorem

\text{var}(f)\leq C\frac{n}{\log n}

it can be used to derive certain

We will list in

The equation (

Talagrand, as well as Benjamini, Kalai, and Schramm, have previously employed the Fourier expansion of variance, but skillfully avoided dealing with the coefficients directly by relying on
clever bounding techniques. Similar techniques
and different forms of the variance equations were developed in
[

and
[

for Boolean circuits and functions.
We believe that there is a special value of
(
1
) because it expresses the coefficients in terms of environment derivatives.

In summary, the ultimate goal is to control the
L^{2}
-norms of the environment derivatives
\partial_{M}f
, as these norms are directly tied to the variance. At present, however, we are unable
to effectively bound these
L^{2}
-norms.

This paper makes progress in analyzing the environment derivatives. We introduce the concepts of essential and influential edges and study the relationship between these categories of edges. The results that follow make it possible to further clarify the anomalous changes of the geodesic that can happen with minimal changes in the environments. One of major benefits is the ability to
bypass intuitive representations–representations that become increasingly difficult to construct when the sets

M
contain more than a few elements.

The fundamental results about essential and influential edges that were derived in this paper paved a way for further study of environment derivatives and obtain almost sure bounds,
[
32
]
. The derivatives of order
1
are obviously bounded by
(b-a)
from above and
0
from below.
For
k\geq 2
, it is possible to construct examples where the environment derivative of order
k
are equal to
\binom{k-2}{\lceil\frac{k-2}{2}\rceil}(b-a)
and examples where the derivatives are

-\binom{k-2}{\lceil\frac{k-2}{2}\rceil}(b-a)
.
For
k\in\{2,3,4\}
these binomial coefficients turn out to be
1
,
1
, and
2
.
We can prove that for
k\in\{1,2,3,4\}
these examples are in some sense the worst case scenarios, i.e. that the environment derivatives are bounded by the above binomial coefficients.

We conjecture that these binomial coefficients are the actual bounds for all
k
, but we can’t prove this at the moment. We have an ongoing work at building computer-assisted proofs for almost sure bounds of higher order.

The proof of Theorem
3
relies on the Beckner–Bonami inequality from
[
11
]
and
[
14
]
, similar to Talagrand’s original approach. In our proof, we clearly separate probabilistic components from algebraic manipulations and extend the variance decomposition to gain higher powers in the denominator. The logarithmic improvement in the denominator is more transparent in our presentation due to this clearer separation between probability and algebra.

We modified Talagrand’s method by generalizing his operator
\Delta_{i}
(denoted
\rho_{i}
in
[
13
]
). Talagrand’s operator is defined as

\Delta_{i}f(\omega)=f(\sigma_{i}(\omega))-f(\omega),

where
\sigma_{i}(\omega)
denotes the environment in which the passage time over edge
i
is changed from its original value.
Our first-order environment derivative
\partial_{i}
is defined as

\partial_{i}f(\omega)=f(\sigma_{i}^{b}(\omega))-f(\sigma_{i}^{a}(\omega)).

This seemingly small change leads to significant improvements in clarity, particularly in identifying edges that belong to geodesics and those for which the environment derivatives are nonzero. In addition, the integration-by-parts formulas become much simpler with the operator
\partial_{i}
, as it is a more natural extension of the classical derivative than
\Delta_{i}
. If the denominator
(b-a)
were introduced to normalize the derivative, the ordering of terms would align with the numerator
f\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}-f\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}
of
\partial_{i}f
.

We generalize this environment derivative to higher orders, which allows us to make a tradeoff after applying the Beckner–Bonami inequality. This tradeoff improves the denominator from
\log n
to
(\log n)^{k}
,
but at the cost of introducing the
L^{2}
-norms
\|\partial_{M}f\|_{2}^{2}
of the higher-order environment derivatives into the numerator. As mentioned earlier, these
L^{2}
-norms are not easy to control.
We hope that other researchers will explore the theory of environment derivatives further, as they show promise for deeper understanding and improved bounds.

When the edge passage times are supported on
\{a,b\}
, as in the model studied in this paper, geodesics may not be unique. It is expected that there will be numerous sufficiently disjoint geodesics, which would imply a small number of influential edges. This, in turn, could make it easier to obtain bounds on
N_{2}
. The study of geodesics has produced several important results and highly credible conjectures. Notably, as the size of the environment grows, at least two infinite geodesics are expected to emerge
[
26
]
. Infinite geodesics are also known to coalesce with high probability
[
3
]
,
[

]
,
[

]

If the edge passage times are continuously distributed, geodesics are unique, and the event
A_{i}=\{\partial_{i}f\neq 0\}

coincides with the event that edge
i
is essential–that is that is, every geodesic passes through

.
Benjamini, Kalai, and Schramm studied the discrete case and encountered a major challenge: proving that the probability of
A_{i}
decays as
n^{-\xi}
.
If the event
A_{i}
occurs, we say that the edge

is influential. The authors of

]
proposed a simpler problem: prove that

.
This problem was resolved recently.
In the continuous setting, we now have bounds of the form

The first such results appeared in

Over the past 20 years, the method developed by Benjamini, Kalai, and Schramm has been successfully used to bound variances in numerous problems, many now categorized as superconcentration problems

or part of the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) universality class

In

The exponents

The models we study in this paper are discrete. However, there have been successful generalizations to models where graphs consist of points scattered in Euclidean space

2.
Essential and influential edges

We will distinguish four categories to which an edge of the graph can belong. These categories may overlap but are conceptually distinct. Most edges will not belong to any of them.

Definition 1
.

An edge
j\in W_{n}
is called essential on the environment
\omega
if every geodesic passes through
j
. We will denote by
E_{j}
the event that the edge
j

Definition 2

An edge

\hat{E}_{j}

\hat{A}_{j}

Proposition 7
.

Assume that

are real numbers. Then

\displaystyle\left(\exists\,(k_{a},k_{b})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\right)\quad ak_{a}+bk_{b}\in(0,\,b-a)\quad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{a}{b-a}\notin\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

Dividing

by
a
, the condition becomes

k_{a}+\frac{b}{a}k_{b}\in(0,\frac{b}{a}-1)
. Setting
\alpha=\frac{b}{a}-1>0
and substituting

,
n=k_{b}
(a bijection
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}
)
transforms (

) into the equivalent form

\displaystyle\left(\exists\,(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\right)\quad m+n\alpha\in(0,\alpha)\quad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{\alpha}\notin\mathbb{N}.

Case 1:
\alpha
is irrational.
By Dirichlet’s approximation
theorem, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a positive integer

n
with
n\alpha-\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor\in(0,\varepsilon)
.
Taking
\varepsilon=\min\{\alpha,1\}
and

Since
\alpha
is irrational,
\frac{1}{\alpha}
is also irrational,
hence not in
\mathbb{N}
.

Case 2:
\alpha=\frac{p}{q}
with
\gcd(p,q)=1
and

positive integers.
Multiplying

by

, the condition becomes

\displaystyle mq+np

\displaystyle\in(0,p).

(27)

Since
\gcd(p,q)=1

Hence (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A\cap\left\{\omega_{j}=\beta\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\beta)}{\mathbb{P}(\alpha)}\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{j}^{\alpha}\left(A\cap\left\{\omega_{j}=\beta\right\}\right)\right).

(30)

We now use (
30
) and (
29
) to prove the following two theorems.

Theorem 6
.

For every edge
i\in W_{n}
, the probabilities of the events
A_{i}
and
E_{i}
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{P}(E_{i}).

(31)

Proof.

Using (
21
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(A_{i}\cap\{\omega_{i}=a\}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(A_{i}\cap\{\omega_{i}=b\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(E_{i}\cap\{\omega_{i}=a\}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(A_{i}\cap\{\omega_{i}=b\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(E_{i}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(A_{i}\cap\{\omega_{i}=b\}\right).

(32)

We now use (
30
) and (
21
) to bound the second term on the right-hand side of (
32
).

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A_{i}\cap\left\{\omega_{i}=b\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(b)}{\mathbb{P}(a)}\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{i}^{a}\left(A_{i}\cap\left\{\omega_{i}=b\right\}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(b)}{\mathbb{P}(a)}\mathbb{P}\left(E_{i}\cap\left\{\omega_{i}=a\right\}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(b)}{\mathbb{P}(a)}\mathbb{P}\left(E_{i}\right).

(33)

The inequality in (
31
) is now a direct consequence of (
32
) and (
33
).
∎

Theorem 7
.

Assume that
\overrightarrow{v}\in W^{m}
has all distinct components. Assume that
j
is not a component of
\overrightarrow{v}
. Assume that
\overrightarrow{\gamma}
and
\overrightarrow{\delta}
are elements of
\{a,b\}^{m}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\partial_{j}\left(f\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\right)\neq 0\right\}\cap I_{\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\overrightarrow{\delta})}{\mathbb{P}(\overrightarrow{\gamma})}\mathbb{P}(A_{j}\cap I_{\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{\gamma}}).

(34)

Proof.

By (
29
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\partial_{j}\left(f\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\right)\neq 0\right\}\cap I_{\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\overrightarrow{\delta})}{\mathbb{P}(\overrightarrow{\gamma})}\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\left(\left\{\partial_{j}\left(f\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\right)\neq 0\right\}\cap I_{\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{\delta}}\right)\right).

It suffices to prove the set equality

\displaystyle\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\left(\left\{\partial_{j}\left(f\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\right)\neq 0\right\}\cap I_{\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle=A_{j}\cap I_{\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{\gamma}}.

(35)

Since
j
is not a component of
\overrightarrow{v}
, repeated application of the property (
8
) gives

\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\circ\sigma_{j}^{a}=\sigma_{j}^{a}\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}
and

\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\circ\sigma_{j}^{b}=\sigma_{j}^{b}\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}
. Hence

\displaystyle\partial_{j}\left(f\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle=f\circ\sigma_{j}^{b}\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}-f\circ\sigma_{j}^{a}\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}=(\partial_{j}f)\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}},

so

\displaystyle\left\{\partial_{j}\left(f\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\right)\neq 0\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\partial_{j}f)\circ\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\neq 0\right\}=\left(\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\right)^{-1}(A_{j}).

We now use the standard set-theoretic identity

g\!\left(g^{-1}(C)\cap D\right)=C\cap g(D)
, valid for every function

g:X\to Y
and all subsets
D\subseteq X
,
C\subseteq Y
. Combined
with
\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\!\left(I_{\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{\delta}}\right)=I_{\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{\gamma}}
, this yields

\displaystyle\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\!\left(\left(\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\right)^{-1}\!(A_{j})\cap I_{\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle=A_{j}\cap\sigma_{\overrightarrow{v}}^{\overrightarrow{\gamma}}\!\left(I_{\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{\delta}}\right)=A_{j}\cap I_{\overrightarrow{v},\overrightarrow{\gamma}},

which proves (
35
).
∎

3.
Variance decomposition

3.1.
Integration by parts

We will derive several theorems that hold for general random variables that are related to Boolean functions. The standard theory can be found in
[
34
]
. We are deviating in minor ways and we will end up with somewhat different formulas. We have defined the derivative
\partial_{i}
in a non-standard way to make our future results about
A_{i}
,
E_{i}
,
\hat{A}_{i}
, and
\hat{E}_{i}
the simplest possible. We will also deviate a little from the literature when we define the Fourier basis below. Due to these changes, and since the results we need are elementary, it is most convenient to re-derive them from scratch.

Theorems
8
and
9
are analogous to integration by parts formulas from calculus.

Proposition 14
.

For every random variable
\varphi
on
\Omega
, its expected value
\mathbb{E}[\varphi]
can be evaluated using
the equation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi\right]

\displaystyle=p\mathbb{E}[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}]+(1-p)\mathbb{E}[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}].

(36)

Proof.

Let us denote by
\mathbb{P}_{i}
the product measure on
\{a,b\}^{W\setminus\{i\}}
defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k\in W\setminus\{i\}}p^{\mathbf{1}_{a}(\omega_{k})}(1-p)^{1-\mathbf{1}_{a}(\omega_{k})}.

Let
\mathbb{E}_{i}
be the corresponding expected value. We use (
12
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varphi]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi\cdot 1_{\omega_{i}=a}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi\cdot 1_{\omega_{i}=b}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\cdot 1_{\omega_{i}=a}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}\cdot 1_{\omega_{i}=b}\right]

\displaystyle=p\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\right]+(1-p)\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}\right].

(37)

We can apply (
37
) to
\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}
and use (
7
).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}]

\displaystyle=p\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\right]+(1-p)\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}\right]

\displaystyle=p\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\right]+(1-p)\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\right].

In an analogous way we derive the equality
\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}\right]
. The equation (
37
) becomes (
36
).
∎

For
\omega\in\Omega
and
i\in W
, let us define

\displaystyle r_{i}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\sqrt{\frac{1-p}{p}},&\text{if }\omega_{i}=a,\\
\sqrt{\frac{p}{1-p}},&\text{if }\omega_{i}=b.\end{array}\right.

For each
i
, we have
\mathbb{E}[r_{i}]=0
and
\text{var}(r_{i})=1
.
For
S\subseteq W
, we define

\displaystyle r_{S}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in S}r_{i}(\omega).

Due to independence, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[r_{S}\right]=0
and
\text{var}(r_{S})=1
whenever
S\neq\emptyset
. Also, if
S
and
T
are different sets, then their dot product

\mathbb{E}\left[r_{S}r_{T}\right]
must be equal to
0
.
Therefore, the functions
\left(r_{S}\right)_{S\subseteq W}
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
; see Section 2 of
[
39
]
.

Theorem 8
.

For every nonempty
S\subseteq W
and every random variable
\varphi
, the expected value of
\varphi r_{S}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi r_{S}\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p(1-p)}^{|S|}\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{S}\varphi\right].

Theorem
8
is a special case obtained by placing
S=T
in the following more general result.

Theorem 9
.

For every two sets
T
and
S
with
T\subseteq S\subseteq W
, and every random variable
\varphi
, the expected value of
\varphi r_{S}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi r_{S}\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p(1-p)}^{|T|}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{T}\varphi\right)\cdot r_{S\setminus T}\right].

(38)

Proof.

Let us first consider the case
T=\{i\}
. An application of (
36
) results in

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi r_{S}\right]

\displaystyle=p\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi r_{S}\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\right]+(1-p)\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi r_{S}\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}\right]

\displaystyle=p\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\right)\left(r_{S}\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\right)\right]+(1-p)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}\right)\left(r_{S}\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-p\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1-p}{p}}r_{S\setminus\{i\}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+(1-p)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\frac{p}{1-p}}r_{S\setminus\{i\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p(1-p)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{b}-\varphi\circ\sigma_{i}^{a}\right)r_{S\setminus\{i\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p(1-p)}\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{i}\varphi r_{S\setminus\{i\}}\right].

The general result (
38
) follows by a straightforward induction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The coefficients of
f
in base
\left(r_{S}\right)_{S\subseteq W}
will be denoted by
a_{S}
. The coefficients satisfy

\displaystyle a_{S}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[fr_{S}\right]=\sqrt{p(1-p)}^{|S|}\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{S}f\right].

Except for the coefficient
a_{\emptyset}
, the random variables
f-\mathbb{E}[f]
and
f
have the same coefficients. This implies (
1
).
∎

3.2.
Beckner-Bonami inequality

We will use the following variant of Beckner-Bonami inequality.

Theorem 10
.

If
P_{L}f
is the projection of
f
to the
\text{span}\left\{r_{Q}:|Q|\leq L\right\}
, then for each
q\geq 2
, there exists a constant
\alpha=\alpha(p,q)>0
such that

\displaystyle\|P_{L}f\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\alpha L}\|f\|_{q^{\prime}}^{2},

where
q^{\prime}
is the conjugate of
q
.

Proof.

This is an easy consequence of a result proved in
[
39
]
. Proposition 2.2 on page 1580 of
[
39
]
states the following:
If
a_{S}=\mathbb{E}[fr_{S}]
, then for fixed
k
the following holds

\displaystyle\sum_{|S|=k}a_{S}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(q-1)^{k}\theta^{2k}\cdot\|f\|_{q^{\prime}}^{2},

(39)

where
\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}
.
Substituting
\beta=\frac{q-1}{p(1-p)}
and summing (
39
) for
k\in\{1,2,\dots,L\}
gives us

\displaystyle\|P_{L}f\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta^{L+1}-1}{\beta-1}\cdot\|f\|_{q^{\prime}}^{2}.

Clearly, we can find
\alpha
that depends only on
p
and
q
such that
\frac{\beta^{L+1}-1}{\beta-1}\leq e^{\alpha L}
.
∎

3.3.
Bound on the remainder
R_{k}

We now prove Theorem
3
that was stated in the introduction.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the family of those subsets of
W
that have cardinality at least
k
.
Let us introduce some notation that will simplify the writing. We will denote by
\mathcal{I}_{T}
the set of all subsets of
W
that contain
T
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{T}

\displaystyle=\left\{S\subseteq W:T\subseteq S\right\}.

We will often write
\mathcal{I}_{t}
instead of
\mathcal{I}_{\{t\}}
.

\displaystyle R_{k}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}a_{S}^{2}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}a_{S}^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{|S|}\cdot\sum_{i_{1}\in W}1_{i_{1}\in S}=\sum_{i_{1}\in W}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{I}_{i_{1}}}\frac{a_{S}^{2}}{|S|}\right).

The right-hand side can be further expanded using indicators.

\displaystyle R_{k}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}\in W}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{I}_{i_{1}}}\frac{a_{S}^{2}}{|S|(|S|-1)}\sum_{i_{2}\in W\setminus\{i_{1}\}}1_{i_{2}\in S}\right).

We can change the order of summations and obtain

\displaystyle R_{k}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}\in W}\left(\sum_{i_{2}\in W\setminus\{i_{1}\}}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{I}_{\{i_{1},i_{2}\}}}\frac{a_{S}^{2}}{|S|(|S|-1)}\right)\right).

(40)

We can now continue in the same way as in (
40
) until the number of indices becomes
k
.
The summation (
40
) becomes

\displaystyle R_{k}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\subseteq W,|M|=k}k!\cdot\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{I}_{M}}\frac{a_{S}^{2}}{|S|(|S|-1)(|S|-2)\cdots(|S|-k+1)}\right).

(41)

For each
S
with
M\subseteq S
, we apply (
38
) with
\varphi=f
and
T=M
to obtain

\displaystyle a_{S}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[f\cdot r_{S}\right]=\sqrt{p(1-p)}^{|M|}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{M}f\cdot r_{S\setminus M}\right].

(42)

Equations (
41
) and (
42
) imply

\displaystyle R_{k}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{M\subseteq W,|M|=k}k!\cdot\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{I}_{M}}\frac{(p(1-p))^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{S\setminus M}\right]^{2}}{|S|(|S|-1)(|S|-2)\cdots(|S|-k+1)}\right).

(43)

For fixed
M
, let us denote by
\Sigma(M)
the inner summation in (
43
). Formally,

\displaystyle\Sigma(M)

\displaystyle=k!\cdot\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{I}_{M}}\frac{(p(1-p))^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{S\setminus M}\right]^{2}}{|S|(|S|-1)(|S|-2)\cdots(|S|-k+1)}\right).

(44)

We will assume that the summation is restricted to the sets
M
for which
\|\partial_{M}f\|_{1}
is non-zero. Since we are working with finite sample space
in which every outcome has positive probability, the
L^{1}
-norm is zero only when the function
\partial_{M}f
is identically equal to
0
.
We split
\Sigma(M)
into two groups: the summation
H_{M}^{-}
corresponding to sets of sizes smaller than
L_{M}
; and the summation
H_{M}^{+}
corresponding to sets of sizes larger than or equal to
L_{M}
. The integer
L_{M}
will be determined later. If the number of elements of
S
is at least
k
, then the product of the numbers

|S|
,
|S|-1
,
\dots
,
|S|-k+1
in the denominator is greater than or equal to
k!
. Hence,

\displaystyle H_{M}^{-}

\displaystyle=k!\cdot\sum_{S\in\mathcal{I}_{M},k\leq|S|<L_{M}}\frac{(p(1-p))^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{S\setminus M}\right]^{2}}{|S|(|S|-1)(|S|-2)\cdots(|S|-k+1)}

\displaystyle\leq{(p(1-p))^{k}}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{I}_{M},k\leq|S|<L_{M}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{S\setminus M}\right]^{2}\right).

There is an obvious bijection between
\mathcal{I}_{M}
and the subsets of
W\setminus M
. Hence, we can do the substitution
Q=S\setminus M
and obtain the following bound for
H_{M}^{-}
.

\displaystyle H_{M}^{-}

\displaystyle\leq{(p(1-p))^{k}}\sum_{Q\subseteq W\setminus M,|Q|<L_{M}-k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{Q}\right]^{2}.

We will now use (
43
) to find an upper bound for
H_{M}^{+}
. The cardinalities of sets
S
are now bigger than or equal to
L_{M}
.
Therefore, the product of numbers
|S|
,
|S|-1
,
\dots
,
|S|-k+1
in the denominator is greater than or equal to
L_{M}\cdot(L_{M}-1)\cdots(L_{M}-k+1)
which is equal to
k!\cdot\binom{L_{M}}{k}
.
Therefore, the sum
H_{M}^{+}
satisfies

\displaystyle H_{M}^{+}

\displaystyle=k!\sum_{S\in\mathcal{I}_{M},|S|\geq L_{M}}\frac{(p(1-p))^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{S\setminus M}\right]^{2}}{|S|(|S|-1)(|S|-2)\cdots(|S|-k+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(p(1-p))^{k}}{\binom{L_{M}}{k}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{I}_{M},|S|\geq L_{M}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{S\setminus M}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p(1-p))^{k}}{\binom{L_{M}}{k}}\sum_{Q\subseteq W\setminus M,|Q|\geq L_{M}-k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{Q}\right]^{2}.

Let us now set our first requirement for
L_{M}

L_{M}\geq 2k
. Then, each of the numbers
L_{M}
,
L_{M}-1
,
\dots
,

and their product is at
least
L_{M}^{k}/2^{k}
. Therefore,
\binom{L_{M}}{k}\geq\frac{L_{M}^{k}}{2^{k}\cdot k!}
. The expansion (
43
) allows us to bound
R_{k}(f)
as follows.

\displaystyle R_{k}(f)

\displaystyle\leq(p(1-p))^{k}\sum_{M\subseteq W,|M|=k}\left(\sum_{Q\subseteq W\setminus M,|Q|<L_{M}-k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{Q}\right]^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\frac{2^{k}k!}{L_{M}^{k}}\sum_{Q\subseteq W\setminus M,|Q|\geq L_{M}-k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{Q}\right]^{2}\right).

(45)

The projection
P_{L_{M}-k}(\partial_{M}f)
of
\partial_{M}f
to the subspace spanned by
\{r_{Q}\}
for sets
Q
of cardinality strictly smaller than
L_{M}-k

\displaystyle P_{L_{M}-k}(\partial_{M}f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\subseteq W\setminus M,|Q|<L_{M}-k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{Q}\right]r_{Q}.

\displaystyle R_{k}(f)

\displaystyle\leq(p(1-p))^{k}\cdot\sum_{M\subseteq W,|M|=k}\left(\left\|P_{L_{M}-k}(\partial_{M}f)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\frac{2^{k}k!}{L_{M}^{k}}\sum_{Q\subseteq W\setminus M,|Q|\geq L_{M}-k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\partial_{M}f\right)r_{Q}\right]^{2}\right).

(46)

The second term on the right-hand side of (

L_{M}^{k}
in the denominator. We can afford the following generous bound

The inequality (

) becomes

\displaystyle R_{k}(f)

\displaystyle\leq(p(1-p))^{k}\sum_{M\subseteq W,|M|=k}\left(\left\|P_{L_{M}-k}(\partial_{M}f)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{2^{k}k!}{L_{M}^{k}}\left\|\partial_{M}f\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(47)

Since the cardinalities are strictly smaller than
L_{M}
, we can use Theorem
10
.
We will take

.
There exists a scalar
\alpha
such that

) as

. Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to
|\partial_{M}f|
and
|\partial_{M}f|^{1/2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\partial_{M}f|^{3/2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[|\partial_{M}f|\cdot|\partial_{M}f|^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|\partial_{M}f|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[|\partial_{M}f|\right]^{1/2}

(49)

Raising (
49
) to the
\frac{4}{3}
power and combining with (

\displaystyle\|P_{L_{M}-k}(\partial_{M}f)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\alpha L_{M}}\|\partial_{M}f\|_{2}^{4/3}\cdot\|\partial_{M}f\|_{1}^{2/3}

Using (

) into

\displaystyle R_{k}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\left(p(1-p)\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\|\partial_{M}f\|_{2}}{\|\partial_{M}f\|_{1}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}.

(52)

Define the function
\psi_{M}
as

\displaystyle\psi_{M}(L)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\alpha L}}{B_{M}}+\frac{1}{L^{k}},

(53)

we can re-write (

) as

\displaystyle R_{k}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\theta\sum_{M\subseteq W,|M|=k}\psi_{M}(L_{M})\cdot\|\partial_{M}f\|_{2}^{2}.

(54)

We now choose
L_{M}
, subject to
L_{M}\geq 2k
.
Observe that if

, then

be the real number such that

If we assume that

This choice satisfies

, as required, and immediately implies

, hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L_{M}^{k}}

\displaystyle<\frac{\left(3\alpha\right)^{k}}{\log^{k}B_{M}}.
