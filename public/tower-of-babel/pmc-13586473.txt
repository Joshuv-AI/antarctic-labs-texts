The evolution of anticoagulants for the prevention and treatment of deep vein thrombosis

It proceedeth from the restagnation of gross Blood, which being transmitted into the Veins, either by reason of the depending of the Part, or from some other pressure upon the Vessel, or else by its own grossness, proves unapt for Circulation. Then instead of continuing its current proportionably to the other Parts, it stops in the place and coagulates.

Richard Wiseman, 1676 1

While 17th-century physicians had a rudimentary understanding of the pathology of superficial and deep venous thrombosis, they had few options for treatment. Indeed, Wiseman quoted Hippocrates when suggesting they may actually serve some positive function and therefore should only be treated if painful or ulcerated – ‘Varices are not dangerous in themselves, they rather free the patient from imminent diseases, especially those of Melancholy’. 1 Where necessary, bloodletting and leeches formed the basis of therapeutic interventions, with treatment of underlying inflammatory conditions, like syphilis, a secondary consideration. Despite an evolving understanding of the mechanism of clot formation and its relationship to venous stasis, it was not until 1856 that Rudolf Virchow made the important connection between deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism. 2 As this phenomenon became more widely understood, bed rest and immobility were added to treatment regimens to prevent migration of the clot. Despite this, mortality rates remained high.

Little progress was made until the development of safe, commercially available heparin in the 1930s. 3 Despite the absence of early controlled trials, the benefits were obvious. A 9-year series of DVT treatment with intravenous heparin published in 1950 showed a reduction in fatal pulmonary embolism from 18% prior to heparin availability, to 0.4% once heparin treatment was introduced. 4 The subsequent introduction of warfarin into the anticoagulant armamentarium in the 1950s provided complementary therapy, with its oral formulation allowing prolongation of DVT treatment as an outpatient.5 –7

Prophylactic treatment was often discussed but was not practical with intravenous therapy. A landmark trial of subcutaneous heparin for surgical patients in 1972 led to its widespread introduction for the prevention of DVT. 8 The subcutaneous regimen involved twice daily dosing until the introduction of low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) in the 1980s allowed for a single daily dose. 9 But, despite the success of heparin thromboprophylaxis, the search for a better agent continued. In the 1990s, genetic engineering allowed the production of r-hirudin, a direct thrombin inhibitor originally detected in leeches.10,11 A series of trials showing the efficacy of r-hirudin in the treatment and prevention of DVT resulted, but it was never widely adopted for this purpose. It was expensive, it was not reversible, and in the end, it was another injectable substance with no clear benefits over heparin. What was really desirable was an effective oral agent.

Unlike the earlier anticoagulants which largely arose from serendipitous events, the origin stories of the direct-acting oral anticoagulant agents (DOACs) are unexciting. These drugs were actively sought and have been manufactured in laboratories after many years of deliberate, targeted research on two specific points in the coagulation pathway – thrombin inhibition and factor Xa inhibition. The first commercially available DOAC was dabigatran, a direct thrombin inhibitor. 12 It was swiftly followed by apixaban and a number of other factor Xa inhibitors, including rivaroxaban and edoxaban. These drugs were designed to replace warfarin, with faster onset, fewer food and drug interactions, and improved patient compliance, partly due to the lack of need for routine monitoring.13,14 Increasingly, they are also now being investigated as an alternative to LMWH in some operative settings. 15

The uptake of DOACs in medicine generally has been rapid, enhanced by increasingly protocolised care and national directives. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the National Health Service in the UK recommended that appropriate patients anticoagulated with warfarin be switched to a DOAC to minimise the need for monitoring. In just 2 months, 20,000 of the 164,000 warfarinised patients in the UK were switched to either apixaban or edoxaban. 16 International guidelines for DVT management were also swift to include DOACs at various stages of management, but in this rapidly evolving field, guidelines are complex and are constantly being revised.17,18 Current Australian guidelines continue to recommend LMWH as first-line therapy for DVT prophylaxis, 19 while recommending either apixaban or rivaroxaban for treatment of acute venous thrombosis in most adults. 20 The most recent (2024) statement on perioperative prevention of venous thrombosis is a French collaboration which runs to 80 pages and emphasises the need for institutional protocols to improve outcomes. 21

Meanwhile, new challenges in DVT management are occurring beyond the boundaries of national, or even international guidelines. In 2020, an occlusive left internal jugular vein thrombosis, usually seen only in cancer patients and a handful of rare conditions, was reported in a healthy astronaut aboard the International Space Station (ISS). This was an incidental finding in a research project, but it was decided that treatment was warranted. There was a limited supply of LMWH on board, no protamine and a finite number of syringes. The difficulty of accurately drawing up and administering injectable medications in a gravity-free environment added to the complexity of the problem. Despite these barriers, a careful dosage regimen was maintained until an urgent supply of apixaban could be delivered to the ISS.22 –24 Subsequently, on return to Earth, review of earlier scans revealed venous thrombus in the left internal jugular of another crew member and ‘stagnant’ flow in six others. 24 No definite risk factors have yet been identified, although upper limb trauma during extravehicular excursions and, ironically, pressure or injury from exercise harnesses are listed as possible contributors. Given that space travel to more remote locations is planned, with associated increases in evacuation times (ISS: 1 day, Moon: 6–14 days, Mars: not currently possible), NASA and the European Space Agency have now implemented a surveillance programme for internal jugular thrombus in astronauts. 25 To date, despite the ISS being continuously manned since 2000, there has only been one medical evacuation. This occurred earlier this year and, due to the manageable nature of the emergency, the crew was not evacuated for a few days. The descent itself took only 11 h, which was within expected parameters. 26

As experts in space medicine begin to draw up protocols for extraterrestrial situations, back here on Earth, the plethora of pharmacological agents and ongoing research projects will continue to challenge everyone involved in the creation of guidelines and protocols for all aspects of DVT prevention and treatment.
