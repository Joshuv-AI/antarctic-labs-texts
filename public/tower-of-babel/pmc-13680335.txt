A tutorial on selecting, translating, and reporting variance priors in Bayesian multilevel models
Authors: Liu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBayesian multilevel models are increasingly used in psychological, educational, and behavioral research, yet applied guidance on specifying priors for scalar variance components remains limited. This tutorial provides a practical workflow for identifying target variance components, selecting benchmark values, calibrating priors on variance and standard-deviation scales, translating them into software-compatible prior specifications, evaluating their implications through prior predictive checks, posterior comparison, sensitivity analysis, and transparent reporting. We review several commonly used prior families, including gamma, inverse-gamma, half-t, and half-Cauchy distributions, and clarify how their parameterizations relate across variance, precision (the reciprocal of variance), and standard-deviation formulations. The tutorial focuses on helping researchers align prior choice with substantive knowledge, model parameterization, software constraints, and sensitivity goals. As a worked example, we use a two-level analysis of TIMSS 2019 data to show how benchmark information from a non-overlapping reference sample can be calibrated into alternative variance-prior specifications and then evaluated in a smaller worked example sample. The empirical illustration is used as a pedagogical example to demonstrate the workflow and its associated checking procedures. We emphasize practical safeguards, including prior-density inspection, prior predictive checking, posterior comparison, convergence assessment, sensitivity analysis, and transparent reporting of prior assumptions. The tutorial concludes by summarizing practical, literature-grounded considerations for documenting prior rationale, implementation decisions, and robustness checks in Bayesian multilevel applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-026-03201-y.Keywords: Bayesian multilevel modeling, Variance priors, Scalar variance components, Prior calibration, Prior translation, Small-sample multilevel models

Introduction

Multilevel models are widely used to analyze hierarchically structured data in education, psychology, and the behavioral sciences, such as students nested within schools or repeated observations nested within individuals (Luo et al., 2021; McNeish et al., 2017; Snijders & Bosker, 2012). In Bayesian multilevel models, researchers need to specify prior distributions for model parameters, including regression coefficients and variance components such as residual and random-effect variances (Gelman, 2006; Gelman et al., 2014). Although variance priors are often treated as a technical detail, they can substantially affect posterior behavior when the number of higher-level units is limited or when variance parameters are weakly identified (Gelman, 2006; Hox & McNeish, 2020; McNeish, 2016a). In such settings, maximum likelihood estimation may yield unstable variance estimates or boundary estimates, which helps motivate closer attention to how prior distributions regularize variance parameters in Bayesian multilevel models (Browne & Draper, 2000, 2006; McNeish, 2016a, 2016b).

Choosing priors for variance components is often more difficult than choosing priors for fixed effects. Although variance components are defined on a nonnegative scale, the practical issue is not nonnegativity alone. Different prior families can differ in how much mass they allocate near zero, in skewness, and in tail behavior, and these differences can materially affect posterior behavior in weakly identified multilevel settings (Gelman, 2006; Gelman et al., 2014; McNeish, 2016a). Although right-skewness is common for some variance-prior families, it is not a general requirement for priors on variance parameters. Common options for scalar variance priors include gamma and inverse-gamma priors on the variance or precision scale, as well as half-t and half-Cauchy priors on the standard deviation scale (Gelman, 2006; Polson & Scott, 2012; van Erp & Browne, 2021). What counts as a “default” prior also varies across software and workflows; some defaults are built into software, whereas others persist through common tutorial examples or legacy practice (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2010; Bürkner, 2017). As a result, applied users may incorrectly assume that default-like choices are neutral or inconsequential, even though prior specification can meaningfully influence posterior summaries in small-sample multilevel settings (Gelman, 2006; McNeish, 2016a, 2016b). The extent of that influence depends on the relative informativeness of the prior and the likelihood and on how closely they align (Gelman et al., 2014; Kruschke, 2014).

This issue is especially important because commonly used variance priors are often described as weakly informative or diffuse even when their behavior can differ substantially across model structures, sample-size conditions, and hyperparameter choices (van Erp & Browne, 2021). Small-hyperparameter inverse-gamma priors, such as Inv-Gamma(0.01, 0.01), can place substantial mass near zero, whereas overly diffuse variance priors can assign substantial probability to implausibly large values; both patterns may affect estimation when the number of clusters is small or variance parameters are weakly identified (Chung et al., 2015; Gelman, 2006; McNeish, 2016a). Existing methodological work has shown that conclusions about variance priors depend on model structure, sample-size conditions, prior family, hyperparameter choice, and evaluation criteria such as bias, root mean square error (RMSE), and credible-interval coverage (Baldwin & Fellingham, 2013; Bolin et al., 2019; McNeish & Stapleton, 2016a; van Erp & Browne, 2021; Zitzmann et al., 2021). For applied researchers, however, this literature can be difficult to translate into concrete modeling decisions, especially when the practical task is to move from a benchmark variance estimate to a defensible prior specification.

This tutorial addresses that practical gap by providing a practical workflow for selecting, translating, implementing, checking, and reporting variance priors for scalar variance components in Bayesian multilevel models. The workflow is designed as a benchmark-informed framework that helps researchers connect plausible variance magnitudes to software-compatible prior specifications while keeping the role of substantive knowledge, model parameterization, software constraints, and sensitivity goals explicit. Other coherent approaches to prior specification, including formal expert elicitation, prior predictive elicitation, hierarchical prior construction, and fully external prior specification, may be more appropriate in some applications. The present tutorial focuses on a benchmark-informed route because applied researchers often need to translate prior studies, historical data, related datasets, or reference-sample information into defensible variance-prior specifications.

We concentrate on commonly used prior families, including gamma, inverse-gamma, half-t, and half-Cauchy distributions, and show how these specifications can be related across variance and standard-deviation parameterizations. To illustrate the workflow, we use a brief worked example based on Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) 2019 data. We present this example as a pedagogical illustration and emphasize practical safeguards such as prior predictive checking, sensitivity analysis, convergence assessment, and transparent reporting of prior assumptions. Throughout, we focus on scalar variance components because they are among the most common priors in applied multilevel modeling and provide a tractable setting for illustrating benchmark-informed calibration across scales. Accordingly, the present tutorial does not attempt to cover priors for covariance matrices of random effects or the broader class of multilevel models in which additional variance and covariance parameters arise. In the present tutorial, the primary concern is with limited numbers of higher-level units rather than cluster size per se, because higher-level variance components are often especially sensitive to estimation difficulties when the number of groups is small, although both features can matter for multilevel estimation in different ways (Maas & Hox, 2005; McNeish & Stapleton, 2016a).

Overview of common variance prior families

For scalar variance components in Bayesian multilevel models, the most practically relevant prior families can be organized according to the scale on which the prior is imposed. Some priors are specified on the variance or precision scale, such as chi-square, gamma, and inverse-gamma formulations. Others are specified directly on the standard-deviation scale, including half-t and half-Cauchy priors, with related alternatives such as half-normal priors also appearing in applied Bayesian workflows (Bürkner, 2017; Gelman, 2006; Gelman et al., 2014). Uniform priors are also sometimes used as bounded weakly informative alternatives, although their behavior depends strongly on the chosen bounds and should therefore not be treated as automatically neutral (Gelman, 2006; Gelman et al., 2014; McNeish, 2016a; Smid & Winter, 2020).

The prior families discussed in this tutorial were selected because they are widely encountered in applied Bayesian multilevel modeling and because, together, they illustrate the main implementation scales that applied researchers must navigate: variance, precision, and standard deviation. Gamma and inverse-gamma priors are useful for explaining variance- and precision-scale formulations, whereas half-t and half-Cauchy priors are widely used standard-deviation-scale options in modern Bayesian modeling. Uniform priors are included because they remain familiar bounded alternatives in applied work and provide a useful reminder that simple-looking bounds can carry substantive information. Other prior families, including half-normal priors and penalized-complexity priors, are also important alternatives, especially in modern hierarchical modeling, and the workflow principles developed here, including scale clarification, prior predictive checking, sensitivity analysis, and transparent reporting, can be applied to those specifications as well (Gelman, 2006; Gelman et al., 2014; Simpson et al., 2017).

Because variance parameters can be weakly identified in small-sample multilevel settings, and because prior families differ in how they allocate mass near zero and in the tails, the choice among these families matters both mathematically and substantively (Gelman, 2006; McNeish, 2016a, 2016b). These distinctions are consequential in practice because they can shape posterior regularization in meaningfully different ways when the number of higher-level units is limited.

Chi-square

The chi-square distribution is most useful here as a conceptual bridge rather than as the primary prior used in applied software. Under normal-data theory, sample variances are closely related to scaled chi-square distributions, which makes chi-square-based reasoning a natural starting point for constructing data-informed priors for variance components (Gelman et al., 2014; Mood et al., 1973). Its support is strictly positive, and its shape is governed by degrees of freedom, so it provides an intuitive way to think about both the location of a plausible variance value and the amount of information supporting that value. In practice, chi-square representations are often translated into more convenient prior families, particularly gamma or inverse-gamma formulations.

Gamma

The gamma distribution is a flexible positive distribution indexed by a shape parameter and either a rate or scale parameter, depending on the parameterization (Gelman et al., 2014; Johnson et al., 1995; Kaplan, 2023). In Bayesian hierarchical modeling, gamma priors are commonly used for variance-related quantities, especially when the model is parameterized in terms of precision. Their flexibility allows researchers to encode different degrees of informativeness: depending on the hyperparameters, a gamma prior can place considerable mass near zero or instead concentrate more strongly around a moderate positive range. This flexibility makes the gamma family useful when researchers wish to anchor a prior to a benchmark estimate while retaining a relatively transparent interpretation of location and spread.

Inverse-gamma

The inverse-gamma distribution has historically been one of the most common priors for variance parameters because of its conjugate role in normal models and its support on positive values (Forbes et al., 2011; Gelman, 2006; Gelman et al., 2014). However, its practical behavior depends heavily on its hyperparameters. In particular, small-hyperparameter specifications such as Inv-Gamma(0.01, 0.01), which are sometimes described as diffuse or weakly informative, can in fact place substantial mass near zero and may have undesirable consequences for variance estimation in small-sample multilevel settings (Gelman, 2006; McNeish, 2016a). For this reason, inverse-gamma priors should not be treated as default placeholders without examining the implied density and its substantive plausibility.

Half-t

Half-t priors are defined on the standard deviation scale and restrict support to positive values, making them attractive for variance-component modeling when software allows priors to be placed directly on standard deviations (Gelman, 2006; Gelman et al., 2014). Compared with inverse-gamma priors, half-t priors are often favored because they can provide regularization near zero while still allowing substantial tail mass for larger standard deviations. Their behavior is controlled by degrees of freedom and scale parameters: smaller degrees of freedom produce heavier tails, whereas larger degrees of freedom produce lighter tails. This makes the half-t family especially useful when researchers want a prior that is weakly informative but still responsive to an empirically plausible scale.

Half-Cauchy

Within the zero-location half-t family considered in this tutorial, the half-Cauchy distribution is obtained by setting the degrees of freedom to ν=1 (Gelman, 2006; Polson & Scott, 2012). More generally, both the half-t and half-Cauchy priors can be viewed as special cases of the broader folded-noncentral-t family. Like other half-t priors, it is defined on the positive scale, but it has especially heavy tails, which makes it relatively tolerant of large variance values while still concentrating substantial density near zero. This combination can be appealing when analysts want to avoid the strong near-zero behavior of some inverse-gamma specifications, although the heavy tails also mean that the scale parameter should be chosen thoughtfully rather than by default (Gelman, 2006; Gelman et al., 2014).

Uniform priors

Uniform priors are sometimes used on the variance or standard-deviation scale because they appear simple and “flat” over a bounded interval. Here, “flat” is used only as an informal descriptive term for constant density over a bounded interval, not as a synonym for the uniform family across prior-specification contexts. However, a bounded uniform prior is not noninformative in any general sense, because its lower and upper limits directly encode substantive assumptions about the parameter’s plausible range. In other words, uniform priors are typically specified by finite lower and upper bounds, and any moments are implied by those bounds rather than chosen directly as prior inputs. In small-sample settings, those bounds can materially affect posterior inferences, especially when the upper bound is chosen arbitrarily large. Accordingly, uniform priors are better understood as bounded weakly informative priors than as neutral defaults (Gelman, 2006; Smid & Winter, 2020).

Because these prior families differ in scale, shape, and tail behavior, they are not interchangeable even when they are all described as “weakly informative.” At the same time, several of these prior families are mathematically related. For example, the chi-square distribution is a special case of the gamma family, inverse-gamma priors can be derived from gamma formulations through reciprocal transformation, and, within the zero-location half-t family considered in this tutorial, the half-Cauchy is obtained by setting ν=1 (Koch, 2007; Mood et al., 1973; Polson & Scott, 2012). These relationships are useful because they help researchers organize benchmark information across prior families and software parameterizations while keeping the target parameter scale explicit. However, mathematical relationships across distributions do not imply that the resulting priors will have equivalent practical behavior once combined with the likelihood. Priors aligned on one feature, such as a median, mode, or upper quantile, may still differ in near-zero mass, tail behavior, prior predictive implications, and posterior regularization. The next section builds on these connections and introduces a practical transformation and calibration framework for constructing benchmark-informed variance priors.

Relationships and prior translation across families

Although these prior families differ in scale, shape, and tail behavior, several of them are mathematically related in ways that are directly useful for prior construction in Bayesian multilevel models. In practice, researchers often begin with a benchmark variance or standard deviation estimate, yet the target prior must ultimately be expressed in the parameterization required by a particular software environment. Accordingly, prior specification is often also a translation problem: researchers may need to move between variance and standard-deviation scales, between variance and precision formulations, or between closely related distribution families while keeping the prior scale substantively interpretable (Bürkner, 2017; Gelman, 2006; Gelman et al., 2014). We do not attempt to provide an exhaustive catalog of all possible distributional transformations. Instead, we focus on relationships that are especially useful for scalar variance components because they clarify how benchmark information can first be expressed conceptually on the variance or precision scale and then translated into the software-compatible forms used in practice. For clarity, gamma distributions are written using the shape-rate parameterization, Gamma (α,β), where α is the shape parameter and β is the rate parameter; the equivalent scale parameterization is obtained by setting θ=1/β (Gelman et al., 2014; Johnson et al., 1995).

Taken together, the chi-square to gamma, gamma to inverse-gamma, and half-t to half-Cauchy provide the backbone of the transformation workflow used in this tutorial. They are especially useful because they allow researchers to begin with a substantively interpretable benchmark, organize and calibrate that benchmark across common prior families, and then implement the resulting prior in software-specific parameterizations while retaining a clear link between prior scale and substantive meaning. The next section turns these relationships into a practical five-step workflow for choosing, calibrating, implementing, and evaluating variance priors in applied work.

A practical workflow for prior construction

The relationships described above provide the basis for a practical workflow for constructing variance priors in Bayesian multilevel models. In applied settings, the goal is usually not to identify a universally best prior family, but to translate available information into a prior that is substantively interpretable, compatible with the model parameterization, and transparent about the degree of regularization it imposes (Gelman, 2006; Gelman et al., 2014; McNeish, 2016a). For scalar variance components, this process can be organized into five steps. Table 1 provides a concise roadmap to this workflow, whereas the rationale, formulas, and practical considerations for each step are developed in the text that follows. In practical terms, the workflow moves from identifying the target parameter and implementation scale, to selecting a benchmark and level of prior informativeness, to conceptual calibration on the variance or precision scale, to software-compatible implementation on the scale required by the estimation environment, and finally to prior checking, posterior comparison, and transparent reporting.

Step 1. Identify the target variance components and implementation scale

The first step is to identify exactly which variance component is being assigned a prior and on which scale the prior must be specified. This is not merely a software detail: whether the prior is expressed on the variance, precision, or standard-deviation scale can materially affect its implied shape and should therefore be treated as an explicit modeling decision. Depending on the model and software, the relevant target may be a variance, a precision, or a standard deviation. This distinction matters because priors that appear similar conceptually can behave quite differently once expressed on different scales, and software defaults are often tied to a particular parameterization rather than to a general substantive rationale (Bürkner, 2017; Gelman, 2006; McNeish, 2016a). Accordingly, prior construction should begin by clarifying both the model component of interest and the scale required for implementation.

In practical terms, this step answers two questions: (1) What parameter am I placing a prior on, and (2) on what scale must that prior be implemented? Once those questions are answered, the remaining calibration steps become much more transparent.

Step 2. Choose benchmark values and a level of prior informativeness

The second step is to identify a benchmark value that reflects a plausible magnitude for the target variance component. Ideally, this benchmark should come from external evidence, such as prior studies, historical data, a related dataset, or a clearly separated reference sample. When such information is unavailable, a benchmark derived from a broader or preliminary analysis may still be useful, but it should be interpreted cautiously because it does not constitute fully independent prior information (Gelman, 2008; Gelman et al., 2020).

In practical terms, prior construction requires two inputs: a benchmark location that reflects a plausible magnitude for the target variance component and a choice about prior strength, that is, how strongly the prior should concentrate around that benchmark. Prior strength may be represented through a degrees-of-freedom-like quantity, a shape parameter, or another hyperparameter that governs concentration. Stronger specifications place more mass near the benchmark, whereas weaker specifications allow more dispersion around it.

This step is therefore not only about choosing a plausible value, but also about deciding how confidently that value should constrain the prior. In applied work, these concentration choices should be treated as provisional calibration decisions and checked before fitting the focal model. Analysts can compare a small set of plausible alternatives, such as weaker, moderate, and stronger concentration settings or heavier- and lighter-tailed specifications, and then use prior-density inspection and prior predictive checks to evaluate whether the implied parameter values and outcome variability are substantively reasonable.

For direct comparisons across prior families, researchers may wish to align a common summary feature, such as the prior median or an upper quantile, across specifications. These are not interchangeable calibration targets: matching the median aligns the central location of the prior, whereas matching an upper quantile constrains tail probability and the upper range of values treated as plausible. The appropriate choice therefore depends on the substantive goal of the calibration and should be stated explicitly. In practice, the most interpretable calibration target may also differ by prior family and by parameter scale. For example, mode-based calibration is often convenient for gamma-type priors on the variance scale, whereas median- or quantile-based calibration is often more interpretable for half-t-type priors on the standard deviation scale. What matters most is that the chosen calibration target be stated explicitly and linked to a substantively meaningful prior scale. Accordingly, on the variance scale, the benchmark primarily anchors the location of the prior, whereas the concentration parameter governs prior strength; on the precision scale, the corresponding interpretation follows through the induced transformation (Gelman, 2006; Gelman et al., 2014; van de Schoot et al., 2021).

Step 5. Inspect the implied prior, assess sensitivity, and report transparently

The final step is to examine what the calibrated priors imply before and after fitting the focal model. This can be done by plotting prior densities, checking summaries such as the mode, median, and upper quantiles, and, when possible, conducting prior predictive checks to determine whether the priors place reasonable probability on plausible outcome variability (Gelman et al., 2014; van de Schoot et al., 2014, 2021). It is also important to assess whether substantive conclusions change meaningfully under alternative but still reasonable prior choices, such as modest changes to benchmark location, prior strength, or prior family (Gelman, 2006; McNeish, 2016a, 2016b; Smid et al., 2020). Once the focal model has been fit using a simulation-based posterior sampler such as Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), transparent reporting should include posterior computation and convergence information, such as the number of chains and iterations, R^, effective sample size, and visual checks such as trace and autocorrelation plots when relevant. At a minimum, reporting should make clear which prior family was used, on which parameter scale it was imposed, how the hyperparameters were calibrated, what information informed the benchmark, and what sensitivity checks were conducted. Transparent reporting is especially important when defaults are overridden, approximated across parameterizations, or informed by benchmark values derived from related rather than fully external data.

Importantly, these five steps are not specific to any one prior family. Rather, they provide a common framework for translating benchmark information into gamma, inverse-gamma, half-t, or half-Cauchy specifications while keeping the calibration logic explicit. Taken together, the five steps provide a practical approach to constructing variance priors that is both technically coherent and substantively interpretable, provided that the benchmark, parameter scale, and degree of prior informativeness are clearly aligned. The worked example in the next section illustrates how this workflow can be implemented in practice using a multilevel analysis of TIMSS 2019 data.

The workflow outlined above is presented at a conceptual level, with emphasis on the mathematical relationships among common prior families and on how benchmark information may be translated across variance, precision, and standard deviation parameterizations. In the worked example that follows, implementation is shaped by the software environment and should therefore be understood as a software-compatible illustration of the broader workflow rather than as a set of exact variance-scale transforms.

Worked example: TIMSS 2019

To illustrate the tutorial framework in a concrete empirical setting, we used TIMSS 2019 data to show how benchmark information can be translated into software-compatible variance-prior specifications and then evaluated through prior inspection, prior predictive comparison, posterior comparison, and sensitivity analysis. In this worked example, standard-deviation-scale priors were calibrated by matching their median to the benchmark standard deviation. We used median-based calibration because the empirical illustration compared prior families with different skewness and tail behavior on the standard-deviation scale, including half-t and half-Cauchy priors whose standard location-0 forms have modes at zero. Under this setup, median matching provided a common and interpretable location target across prior families without requiring shifted-location specifications. Throughout this worked example, the non-overlapping reference sample was used only for benchmark construction, whereas the worked example sample was used for the focal prior comparison and evaluation.

Step 1. Identify the target variance components and implementation scale

We fit a two-level random-intercept model relating students’ science achievement to mathematics achievement at both the student and school levels. For pedagogical clarity, the worked example used the first plausible values for science and mathematics achievement and treated science achievement as the outcome and mathematics achievement as the predictor. This specification was chosen to provide a familiar and interpretable multilevel illustration rather than to assert a strong causal ordering, although mathematics and science achievement are typically positively associated in TIMSS data (Mullis et al., 2020; Singh et al., 2002). The focal worked example sample consisted of 400 students nested within 40 schools, and a separate non-overlapping reference sample was used later for benchmark construction. This setup was chosen to make the influence of variance-prior specification more visible under a smaller higher-level sample size while keeping the modeling framework familiar to applied researchers.

At Level 1, the predictor was students’ mathematics achievement centered within schools and standardized, so that the student-level effect represented within-school differences in mathematics achievement. At Level 2, the school-level predictor was the school mean of mathematics achievement, standardized across schools, so that it represented between-school differences in the academic environment. This setup corresponds to a manifest within–between decomposition of the mathematics predictor, which is sufficient for the present worked example but does not address latent decomposition approaches, such as the multilevel latent covariate framework proposed by Lüdtke et al. (2008). We also included the cross-level interaction between the student-level and school-level mathematics variables. In substantive terms, the model asked whether science achievement varied as a function of students’ own mathematics achievement, school-average mathematics achievement, and their interaction, while also allowing overall science achievement to vary across schools.

The model can be written as

Under the usual normality assumptions, u0j∼N(0,τ00) and εij∼N(0,σ2).

Within this model, the two scalar variance components of interest were the between-school random-intercept variance τ00 and the within-school residual variance σ2. Because the tutorial focuses on practical prior construction for scalar variance components, these two parameters provided a natural worked example: one higher-level variance component that is often more weakly identified in smaller samples, and one lower-level residual component that is typically more data-dominated.

Although these components can be described conceptually on the variance scale, the model was implemented in the brms package, which parameterizes these quantities on the standard-deviation scale for prior specification. Accordingly, the priors in the worked example were ultimately assigned to the between-school standard deviation, τ00, and the residual standard deviation, σ, rather than directly to τ00 or to precision parameters. This distinction is important because it determines how benchmark information must be calibrated into software-compatible prior specifications.

To ensure that the comparison focused on variance-prior choices, fixed-effect priors were held constant across prior conditions. Specifically, the intercept was assigned a Normal prior centered on the reference-sample outcome mean with standard deviation 100, and the regression coefficients were assigned Normal(0, 50) priors. The intercept prior was chosen to be broad relative to the observed TIMSS score scale while remaining centered on a plausible outcome location for the worked example. Because the predictors were standardized, the coefficient prior was also broad on the TIMSS score scale while avoiding an effectively flat specification. Thus, across prior conditions, the fixed-effect priors remained the same, whereas only the priors on the between-school and residual standard deviations varied across prior families.

The reference-sample centering of the intercept prior served a different role from the benchmark-informed variance priors used in the main comparison. The intercept prior was used as a broad location prior on the TIMSS outcome scale, was not estimated from the worked-example sample, and was held fixed across all prior conditions. It was therefore a background modeling choice rather than a prior-family condition under evaluation. By contrast, the variance-prior specifications were the focal inputs in the tutorial comparison and directly governed the amount of regularization applied to the between-school and residual standard deviations. This distinction clarifies why the prior-likelihood separation issues discussed in the worked example are most consequential for the benchmark-informed variance priors.

All analyses were conducted in R 4.4.1 using the R packages brms 2.22.0 and rstan 2.32.6 on a MacBook Pro with an Apple M3 Max processor and 48 GB unified memory. The worked example was fit using Stan’s Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithm with the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS), as implemented through the brms interface (Bürkner, 2017). The worked-example models were fit with four chains of 2,000 iterations each, including 1,000 warm-up iterations, with adapt_delta = .99 and max_treedepth = 15. Sensitivity models were fit with stricter sampler settings, using four chains of 4,000 iterations each with 2,000 warm-up iterations, adapt_delta =.995, and max_treedepth = 15, because initial runs indicated the need for more conservative tuning. These settings were used as practical starting values for this model, and adequacy was evaluated from the downstream convergence and sampler diagnostics.

Step 2. Choose benchmark values and a level of prior informativeness

The next step was to select benchmark values for the target variance components and decide how strongly the priors should be concentrated around them. In principle, benchmark information may come from prior studies, historical data, or other external evidence. In this worked example, we used a non-overlapping reference sample drawn from the broader TIMSS 2019 dataset while reserving a separate smaller-sample dataset for the focal prior comparison. This design provided an empirically grounded basis for benchmark construction while avoiding direct reuse of the same observations for both benchmark estimation and worked example model fitting.

Specifically, the reference sample consisted of 7,408 students nested within 233 schools, whereas the worked example sample consisted of 400 students nested within 40 schools. The same two-level random-intercept model described in Step 1 was fit to the reference sample using maximum likelihood, and the resulting variance-component estimates were used as benchmark values for prior calibration. Fitting the model to the reference sample yielded a benchmark between-school random-intercept standard deviation of 12.20 and a benchmark residual standard deviation of 46.08. On the variance scale, these corresponded to 148.77 and 2,123.38, respectively. These benchmark values served as empirical location targets for the subsequent prior specifications. Because these benchmark values were obtained from a reference-sample maximum-likelihood fit, their usefulness depends on how well that reference-sample estimate reflects the target variance structure. If the benchmark estimate is poor, biased, or inflated, the resulting benchmark-informed priors may pull posterior inferences in a correspondingly misleading direction. Accordingly, the reference-sample estimate should be treated as an empirical input for calibration rather than as a ground-truth value.

The use of a non-overlapping reference sample avoids direct reuse of the same observations for both benchmark estimation and focal model fitting, but it does not guarantee that the reference sample and worked example sample share the same variance structure. If the target sample differs meaningfully from the reference sample, benchmark-informed priors may stabilize estimation at the cost of shrinkage toward a misaligned value. For this reason, benchmark construction should be treated as a substantive assumption rather than as a neutral preprocessing step, and in applied settings benchmark information may be triangulated from prior studies, related datasets, expert elicitation, or data-splitting strategies when appropriate. When multiple sources of benchmark information are available, researchers should prioritize the most substantively comparable external evidence, examine whether the source and target samples are likely to share a similar variance structure, and treat disagreement across sources as a reason to calibrate and compare a range of plausible priors rather than a single benchmark (Garthwaite et al., 2005; Williams et al., 2021).

At this stage, it is important to distinguish benchmark location from prior informativeness. The benchmark values identify plausible magnitudes for the two variance components, but they do not by themselves determine how tightly the prior should be concentrated around those values. Accordingly, the worked example treated benchmark location, prior concentration, and tail behavior as related but distinct calibration decisions. The benchmark values anchored the location of the prior specifications, whereas shape parameters, degrees-of-freedom parameters, and scale settings governed how much dispersion or tail weight remained around those benchmark values.

In practice, these calibration settings should be chosen through an explicit calibration-and-checking process. One practical strategy is to define a small set of plausible alternatives, such as weaker, moderate, and stronger concentration settings for shape-based priors, or heavier- and lighter-tailed specifications for half-t priors, and then examine their implications through prior-density plots and prior predictive checks. If the prior predictive distribution assigns substantial probability to outcome patterns that are substantively implausible, a more concentrated prior, a lighter-tailed specification, or a different calibration target may be more appropriate. If the prior predictive distribution is unrealistically narrow, a weaker or heavier-tailed prior may better reflect uncertainty. These choices should be reported as analyst-specified calibration decisions and evaluated through sensitivity analyses.

For the main worked example, we chose a moderately concentrated setting for the between-school standard deviation and a more concentrated setting for the residual standard deviation. This asymmetry was intentional. Higher-level variance components are often less stable and less directly identified in smaller multilevel samples than lower-level residual variation, especially when the number of higher-level clusters is limited (Bolin et al., 2019; Hox & McNeish, 2020; McNeish & Stapleton, 2016a). Therefore, the worked example allowed greater prior dispersion for the between-school component while using a tighter calibration for the residual component. In addition, the half-t family was represented using a moderately heavy-tailed setting so that its behavior could be contrasted with both the more concentrated gamma-family specifications and the heavier-tailed half-Cauchy alternative (Gelman, 2006; Polson & Scott, 2012). At this stage, the emphasis is on the logic of the informativeness choice; the exact family-specific calibrations are introduced in Steps 3 and 4.

We also included a baseline reference condition that was not benchmark-calibrated in the same way as the four focal prior families. Specifically, the baseline condition used a user-defined weakly informative half-t(3, 0, 20) specification on the standard-deviation scale for both variance components. This baseline condition served as a less structured comparison point against which the benchmark-informed prior families could be evaluated.

In the present tutorial, this intermediate step serves as a conceptual bridge between the general relationships reviewed earlier and the standard-deviation-scale priors introduced in Step 4. It is also pedagogically useful because benchmark information is often easier to interpret on the variance scale, and many commonly discussed prior relationships are first presented in that parameterization, even when final implementation occurs elsewhere.

Step 4. Calibrate priors on the standard-deviation scale

The next step was to express the benchmark information as standard-deviation-scale priors suitable for software implementation. This step is especially important in the present worked example because the model was fit in brms, where priors for group-level standard deviations and the residual standard deviation are specified directly on the standard-deviation scale, not on the variance or precision scale. Thus, although Step 3 expressed the benchmark information in a conceptually useful variance-scale form, the fitted priors required a separate software-compatible calibration on the standard-deviation scale.

Common calibration target

A central issue in this step is the choice of a common calibration target across prior families. In the present tutorial, all four focal prior families were calibrated so that their median matched the corresponding benchmark standard deviation. Median matching is used here as a location-based calibration rule; upper-quantile matching would serve a different purpose by calibrating tail probability and the upper range of values treated as plausible. This median-based calibration was used for two reasons. First, it provides a common and interpretable location target across prior families with different skewness and tail behavior. Second, it is especially useful on the standard-deviation scale because some prior families commonly used in practice, particularly half-t and half-Cauchy priors with location 0, have their mode at zero. As a result, mode-based alignment would not yield a practically comparable family-wise calibration across all four prior families.

At the same time, matching the median does not make the priors practically equivalent. It aligns only one summary feature of each prior. The calibrated priors may still differ in near-zero mass, tail behavior, induced prior predictive variability, and posterior regularization once combined with the likelihood. For this reason, median matching should be understood as a transparent calibration device for organizing the comparison, not as a claim that the prior families preserve equivalent informativeness across parameterizations.

Data-informed versus analyst-specified inputs

At this stage, it is also important to distinguish data-informed benchmark values from analyst-specified calibration settings. The benchmark standard deviations were obtained from the non-overlapping reference sample and therefore served as empirical location targets for prior calibration. In contrast, the shape parameters for the gamma and inverse-gamma priors and the degrees-of-freedom parameter for the half-t prior were specified by the analyst to represent different levels of prior concentration and tail behavior. In the present worked example, shape = 12 was used to represent a moderate concentration setting for the between-school standard deviation, shape = 30 was used to represent a stronger concentration setting for the residual standard deviation, and df = 4 was used to represent a moderately heavy-tailed half-t prior. These values were chosen to produce a substantively interpretable and pedagogically clear comparison across prior families.

In applied analyses, analogous calibration settings should be selected by examining the substantive plausibility of the resulting prior distribution and by checking the implied prior predictive distribution on the outcome scale. For example, analysts may compare weaker, moderate, and stronger concentration settings, or heavier- and lighter-tailed specifications, before selecting the main prior condition. The present worked example follows this logic by using the main shape and degrees-of-freedom values as illustrative calibration choices and then evaluating their implications through prior-density inspection, prior predictive comparison, and sensitivity analyses.

Taken together, Step 4 completes the transition from benchmark identification to software-ready prior specification. It shows how a common benchmark can be used to organize multiple prior-family calibrations on the standard-deviation scale while preserving meaningful differences in concentration and tail behavior. The resulting calibrated priors then provide the basis for the prior inspection, prior predictive comparison, posterior comparison, and sensitivity analyses reported in the next step.

Step 5. Inspect the implied prior, assess sensitivity, and report transparently

With the priors calibrated, the final step was to examine what they implied before and after fitting the focal model, assess whether substantive conclusions were sensitive to reasonable alternative specifications, and report those choices transparently. In a tutorial context, this step is essential because prior construction does not end once hyperparameters have been specified. Even a mathematically valid prior may be a poor practical choice if it implies implausible outcome variation, places too much mass in unrealistic regions of the parameter space, or yields posterior behavior that is overly sensitive to arbitrary calibration settings. Accordingly, the present worked example evaluated the calibrated priors in four complementary ways: prior inspection, prior predictive checking, posterior comparison, and sensitivity and diagnostic assessment.

We began by inspecting the priors on the parameter scale itself. As shown in Fig. 2, the four benchmark-informed prior families were aligned to a common median on the standard-deviation scale, but they differed meaningfully in shape and tail behavior. The gamma and inverse-gamma priors concentrated relatively more mass near the benchmark values, whereas the half-t and especially the half-Cauchy priors retained heavier tails and therefore allowed larger standard deviations to remain more plausible a priori. This inspection established that the calibrations were not interchangeable, even though they were aligned to the same benchmark location.

We then examined the implications of these priors on the outcome scale through prior predictive checks. Figure 3 shows the prior predictive distributions for science achievement under the alternative prior conditions. Across conditions, the prior predictive distributions were centered in a broadly similar region of the observed outcome scale, indicating that the benchmark-informed calibrations were aligned to a common location target. At the same time, the heavier-tailed specifications implied greater dispersion, particularly for the half-Cauchy prior. This pattern is also reflected in Table 4, which summarizes the prior predictive means and standard deviations under each condition. For example, the median prior predictive standard deviation was smaller under the baseline and gamma conditions than under the half-t and half-Cauchy conditions, with the half-Cauchy prior showing the broadest upper range. These comparisons show that differences among prior families are not merely mathematical. They also affect the amount of variability implied for the observed outcome before fitting the model. Because benchmark-informed priors necessarily involve analyst choices about concentration and tail behavior, we also examined the robustness of the main findings through sensitivity analyses. Specifically, sensitivity analyses held the benchmark location fixed while varying prior concentration within the gamma family and tail behavior within the half-t family (see Supplementary Table B). Across these alternative settings, posterior summaries for the residual standard deviation were highly stable, and posterior summaries for the between-school standard deviation varied only modestly. In other words, although the exact interval widths and degree of shrinkage changed somewhat across alternative calibrations, the substantive pattern of results was unchanged. Convergence and sampler diagnostics were also satisfactory across sensitivity conditions (see Supplementary Tables B1 and B2). These results suggest that the benchmark-informed workflow can yield interpretable and stable inferences without depending on a single arbitrary hyperparameter choice.

Finally, this worked example illustrates the reporting principles that accompany prior construction in applied work. At a minimum, researchers should report the benchmark values used for calibration, the parameter scale on which the priors were specified, which hyperparameters were fixed and which were solved for, the rationale for the chosen level of prior concentration or tail behavior, prior predictive evidence showing what the priors imply on the observed-data scale, and sensitivity and diagnostic results demonstrating that the conclusions are not driven by a single unexamined specification. Thus, the TIMSS illustration shows that careful prior construction is not only a matter of choosing a prior family, but also of checking what that choice implies, evaluating whether inferences are robust to reasonable alternatives, and documenting those decisions clearly enough for readers to assess their substantive and methodological consequences.

Together, these five steps illustrate how benchmark-informed prior construction can be implemented as a transparent workflow that links substantive plausibility, mathematical calibration, software compatibility, and empirical evaluation.

Discussion

This tutorial presented a benchmark-informed workflow for specifying and evaluating variance priors in Bayesian multilevel models. The workflow organizes prior construction as a sequence of linked decisions: identifying the target variance components and implementation scale, selecting benchmark values, expressing those benchmarks conceptually on the variance scale, calibrating software-compatible priors on the standard-deviation scale, and evaluating their implications through prior inspection, prior predictive checks, posterior comparison, and sensitivity analysis (Gabry et al., 2019; Schad et al., 2021). Using TIMSS 2019 as a worked example, the tutorial illustrated how this workflow can be implemented transparently in a familiar applied setting.

A first contribution of the tutorial is the distinction it makes between benchmark location and prior informativeness. In the worked example, benchmark values were obtained from a non-overlapping reference sample and used to anchor the location of the prior specifications, whereas the concentration and tail behavior of the priors were chosen separately to yield an interpretable comparison in a smaller-sample multilevel setting. This separation is important in practice. A benchmark can provide a plausible magnitude for a variance component, but it does not by itself determine how concentrated the prior should be around that value. By making these decisions explicit, the tutorial clarifies how empirical information can inform prior location without implying that there is a single uniquely correct prior specification.

A second contribution is the distinction between conceptual calibration and software implementation. Step 3 showed how benchmark information can be expressed on the variance or precision scale using familiar analytical relationships, whereas Step 4 translated that same information into standard-deviation-scale priors suitable for estimation in brms. This distinction matters because many discussions of variance priors are framed on the variance scale, whereas software such as brms requires prior specification on the standard-deviation scale for group-level and residual scale parameters (Bürkner, 2017, 2018). The tutorial therefore bridges a common gap between general Bayesian theory and applied implementation by showing how the same benchmark information can be used in both contexts without treating the software-compatible priors as exact one-to-one transformations of the conceptual variance-scale forms.

The TIMSS worked example illustrates how the workflow can reveal differences among prior families even when they are aligned to a common benchmark location. The tutorial value of this comparison lies in showing that benchmark-matched prior families do not become interchangeable simply because they share a common location target. On the parameter scale, the gamma and inverse-gamma priors placed relatively more mass near the benchmark values, whereas the half-t and especially the half-Cauchy priors retained heavier tails. On the outcome scale, these differences translated into broader prior predictive dispersion under the heavier-tailed specifications, with the half-Cauchy prior showing the widest upper range. These patterns illustrate why prior-family choice is not only a mathematical issue. It also affects what the model considers plausible before observing the focal data. The TIMSS example therefore serves as an illustration of how the workflow operates: prior families aligned to the same benchmark can still imply different prior predictive behavior and different degrees of posterior regularization.

At the same time, posterior results in the worked example were relatively stable across prior conditions. For the between-school random-intercept standard deviation, posterior medians varied only modestly across the benchmark-informed prior families and the baseline reference condition. For the residual standard deviation, posterior medians were highly stable across conditions. The main differences appeared in interval width rather than posterior location: the gamma and inverse-gamma priors yielded somewhat tighter intervals for the between-school component, whereas the heavier-tailed half-t and half-Cauchy priors produced somewhat wider intervals. This pattern was most visible for the between-school random-intercept standard deviation, which is consistent with prior work showing that estimation of higher-level variance components becomes more difficult in multilevel models with a small number of clusters (Bolin et al., 2019; Maas & Hox, 2005; McNeish & Stapleton, 2016b). In this worked example, the choice of prior family mattered more for the degree of posterior regularization than for the basic substantive pattern of results. Because the TIMSS application does not provide known population values, these differences should not be interpreted as evidence that tighter intervals imply better performance in terms of bias, RMSE, or credible-interval coverage. Here, greater regularization should be understood as reducing implausible boundary behavior or excessive posterior dispersion when the likelihood is weak, whereas excessively concentrated priors can make posterior inferences too insensitive to the likelihood. When the benchmark location is misaligned with the target sample, this same regularization can also introduce bias by shrinking posterior variance estimates toward a value that is not substantively appropriate for the focal data. These considerations reinforce the importance of prior inspection, prior predictive checking, posterior comparison, and sensitivity analysis as connected parts of the workflow.

The fixed effects were also broadly stable across prior conditions. Because fixed-effect priors were held constant, this pattern should not be interpreted as a general test of fixed-effect prior sensitivity. Instead, it indicates that, under the present design, varying the variance priors primarily affected the targeted variance components while leaving the estimated regression coefficients comparatively stable. Together with the variance-component results, this suggests that benchmark-informed variance priors may meaningfully shape posterior uncertainty about variance components, even when fixed-effect summaries remain comparatively stable under the same design.

The diagnostic results further support this interpretation. Across prior conditions, the fitted models showed satisfactory convergence and sampler behavior, with no divergent transitions or maximum tree-depth warnings. Effective sample sizes were adequate overall, although the half-Cauchy condition showed somewhat smaller values than the more concentrated specifications. Sensitivity analyses led to a similar conclusion. When prior concentration was varied within the gamma family and tail behavior was varied within the half-t family, posterior summaries for the residual standard deviation remained highly stable and posterior summaries for the between-school standard deviation varied only modestly. These results suggest that the benchmark-informed workflow can yield interpretable and stable inferences without depending on a single arbitrary hyperparameter choice.

Taken together, the findings from the worked example suggest several practical considerations for applied researchers in education, psychology, and related fields. First, prior specification for variance components should be treated as a substantive modeling decision rather than as a default software option (Gelman, 2006). Second, when fully external prior information is unavailable, benchmark information from a related or separated source may still be used for provisional calibration, but it should not be treated as equivalent to independent prior knowledge and should be accompanied by explicit discussion of comparability, benchmark uncertainty, and sensitivity analyses. Third, the implementation scale matters. Researchers need to align their prior-construction logic with the parameterization required by the software they are using. Fourth, prior evaluation should not stop at listing hyperparameters. Prior densities, prior predictive implications, posterior summaries, diagnostics, and sensitivity analyses all contribute to transparent and credible Bayesian modeling practice (Gabry et al., 2019; Kruschke et al., 2021; Schad et al., 2021).

This reporting emphasis synthesizes existing work on variance priors, Bayesian workflow, and reporting standards rather than introducing de novo rules for prior selection (Gabry et al., 2019; Gelman, 2006; Kruschke et al., 2021; McNeish & Stapleton, 2016a). At a minimum, researchers should report the target variance components, the parameter scale on which priors were specified, the benchmark values used for calibration, which hyperparameters were fixed and which were solved for, the rationale for the chosen level of concentration or tail behavior, and the results of prior predictive and sensitivity analyses (Kruschke et al., 2021). Reporting these elements makes prior choice more transparent and allows readers to assess whether the analysis depends heavily on a single unexamined specification.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the present paper is a tutorial with one empirical worked example, not a general performance study of all variance-prior families across all multilevel settings. The TIMSS illustration shows how the workflow operates in one realistic application, but it does not establish universally optimal priors. Nor does it provide a formal comparison of bias, RMSE, or credible-interval coverage under known population values, as would be possible in simulation studies. Future simulation studies should compare alternative variance-prior families with respect to bias, RMSE, and credible-interval coverage under known population values and across a broader range of multilevel structures. Second, the benchmark values were drawn from a non-overlapping reference sample within the broader TIMSS 2019 dataset rather than from a wholly external study or expert elicitation exercise. This was appropriate for tutorial purposes, but future work could examine how the workflow behaves when benchmark information comes from genuinely external sources or from less certain prior knowledge. More broadly, the worked example conditions on benchmark values and calibration settings rather than propagating uncertainty in those quantities through hyperpriors. Accordingly, it should be understood as a benchmark-informed calibration strategy that fixes benchmark and concentration choices for tutorial purposes, rather than as a fully prespecified prior specification or a full hierarchical Bayes treatment of hyperparameter uncertainty. This choice was appropriate for a tutorial focused on transparent calibration, but it does not exhaust the range of coherent Bayesian strategies. In applications where benchmark information is uncertain, researchers may instead consider hierarchical specifications that place hyperpriors on calibration parameters, or alternative designs such as external elicitation, data splitting, or cross-validation to preserve clearer prior-likelihood separation.

Third, the worked example focused on a two-level random-intercept model. Future research should extend the same framework to more complex settings, including random-slope models, longitudinal multilevel models, generalized multilevel models, and latent-variable or measurement models. An important extension would be to multilevel models with covariance matrices of random effects, where prior specification must address not only individual variance components but also covariances among random effects. This also includes extensions in which predictors are decomposed using latent rather than manifest between-within formulations, so that prior specification must address not only scalar variance components but also the additional measurement and structural parameters introduced by such models. Fourth, although the sensitivity analyses strengthened the tutorial by varying concentration within the gamma family and tail behavior within the half-t family, future simulation studies could examine benchmark misalignment more systematically by varying both benchmark location and concentration under known population values.

Overall, this tutorial emphasizes a transparent workflow for constructing, implementing, checking, and reporting variance priors in Bayesian multilevel models. The TIMSS example shows that the central practical questions are where the prior is anchored, how concentrated it is, how it behaves under the software parameterization, and whether the resulting inferences are robust to reasonable alternatives. Framed this way, variance-prior specification becomes part of principled model building, with prior-density inspection, prior predictive checking, posterior comparison, sensitivity analysis, and transparent reporting serving as practical safeguards for applied Bayesian multilevel work.
