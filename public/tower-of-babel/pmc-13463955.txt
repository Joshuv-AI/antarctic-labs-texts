CD81 restricts decidual IGF2-mediated smooth muscle cell dedifferentiation during spiral artery remodeling in preeclampsia
Authors: Chenrui Cao, Yimin Dai, Zhiyin Wang, Yi Lei, Ning Gu, Hang Zhou, Honglei Duan, Li Shen, Taishun Li, Guangfeng Zhao, Dan Liu, Yali Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreeclampsia (PE) is a pregnancy-specific complication characterized by defective spiral artery remodeling, notably due to the abnormal retention of differentiated vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs). Although elevated CD81 levels in the placenta and maternal circulation contribute to this impairment, the definite mechanisms remain elusive. This study aimed to investigate how CD81 overexpression in extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs) contributes to defective VSMC dedifferentiation within uterine spiral arteries, as well as the involved molecular mechanisms.MethodsPlacental basal plates from severe preeclampsia (sPE) patients and non-PE controls were immunostained for CD81 and VSMC markers. A placental restricted CD81 overexpression mouse model was constructed to evaluate PE-like phenotype and placental pathology. Primary decidual stromal cells (DSCs) and natural killer (dNK) cells were applied to investigate how CD81-overexpressing EVTs interact with them to suppress VSMC dedifferentiation. Additionally, antagonistic and rescue experiments were conducted to identify the function of the key molecules involved.ResultsThe proportion of retained differentiated VSMCs in spiral arteries was significantly higher in sPE patients than in normal pregnancies, and was positively correlated with CD81 expression on EVTs. Similarly, pregnant mice with placental restricted CD81 overexpression exhibited PE-like phenotype and showed persistent VSMC retention in spiral arteries. In vitro, conditioned medium (CM) from DSCs pretreated with medium from CD81-overexpressing EVTs increased the expression of differentiated VSMC markers, which was associated with reduced insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2) levels in DSCs. Notably, exogenous IGF2 supplementation reversed this effect.ConclusionsCD81 upregulation on EVTs prevents VSMC dedifferentiation through a DSC-dependent mechanism. This study represents a pioneering effort to reveal the crucial role of interplay between EVTs, DSCs, and VSMCs in facilitating VSMC dedifferentiation, thereby enabling the completion of spiral artery remodeling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04965-y.Keywords: CD81, Preeclampsia, Extravillous trophoblast, Decidual stromal cell, Spiral artery remodeling, Vascular smooth muscle cell

Background

Preeclampsia (PE) is a pregnancy-specific disorder characterized by hypertension, proteinuria, and, in severe cases, multi-organ dysfunction occurring after 20 weeks of gestation. It remains a major contributor to maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality globally [1–3]. The fundamental pathophysiological feature of PE is defective remodeling of uterine spiral arteries, which is characterized by reduced muscle disruption and inadequate structural transformation, although the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms are not fully understood.

Uterine spiral arteries undergo profound morphological changes during normal pregnancy to create dilated, low-resistance vessels, which are essential for delivering oxygen and nutrients to support fetal development. This adaptive process involves the partial replacement of luminal endothelial cells (ECs) by invading extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs) and near-complete loss of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs). Numerous studies have demonstrated that insufficient replacement of endothelium by trophoblast is related to impaired trophoblast invasion. However, the mechanism underlying VSMC loss remains mysterious. While apoptosis has been suggested as a key driver of VSMC loss [4], conflicting evidence indicates that apoptosis within the vessel wall is rare [5, 6]. Instead, VSMCs appear to undergo phenotypic switching from a differentiated, contractile state to a dedifferentiated, synthetic state, allowing their migration away and withdrawal from the arterial wall [7, 8].

In our prior studies, we found that CD81, a transmembrane protein involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, adhesion and motility, was abnormally upregulated locally in placental syncytiotrophoblasts (STBs), cytotrophoblasts (CTBs) and EVTs and systemically in maternal circulation of severe PE (sPE) patients. Moreover, systemic administration of CD81-positive adenovirus (Ad-CD81) in rats induced PE-like symptoms and disrupted spiral artery remodeling [9], which may be due to decreased CTB invasion and enhanced endothelium activation. But whether CD81 affects the disappearance of VSMCs is unknown.

In the present study, we found that CD81 expression on EVTs was positively correlated with the retention of VSMCs surrounding spiral artery in sPE patients. Moreover, placental-restricted CD81 overexpression pregnant model mice exhibited PE-like phenotype and showed defective spiral artery remodeling with retained VSMCs. We demonstrated that elevated CD81 expression on EVTs prevented VSMC dedifferentiation in sPE patients by downregulating the production of insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF2) in decidual stromal cells (DSCs). Our findings underscore a previously unappreciated but essential role of DSCs as a key intermediary in mediating VSMC dedifferentiation, offering new insights into the cellular dynamics underlying spiral artery remodeling failure in PE.

Methods

Human studies

The diagnostic criteria for sPE were established based on the guidelines provided by the International Society for the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy (ISSHP) [10]. Fetal growth restriction (FGR), defined as birth weight below the 10th percentile, was diagnosed according to the international standards developed by the INTERGROWTH-21st project [11]. Individual birth weight percentiles adjusted for gestational age (in exact weeks and days) and fetal sex can be calculated using the freely accessible tool on http://www.intergrowth21.org. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Drum Tower Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School (2018-034-01 and 2021-214-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Four tissue samples (2 × 2 × 1 mm) were collected from the maternal side of the placenta near the umbilical cord insertion site immediately after delivery, avoiding areas of infarction or calcification. Serial sections from these samples were used for analysis. Only sections containing spiral arteries were included, whereas those without detectable arteries were excluded. A median of 5 vessels (range: 1–9) per placenta was analyzed. Abnormal remodeling of the decidual segment of spiral arteries can be detected by examining the placental basal plates [12]. Disruption of vessels was categorized into 3 grades based on the severity of structural impairment: preserved (minimal separation of the muscular layers), disorganized (partial disorganization of the media), and destroyed (extensive disorganization or complete loss of the media) (Additional file 4: Fig. 1A). Basal plates of the placental tissue samples collected from 45 sPE patients and 30 matched normotensive pregnant women were used for immunostaining. Table S1 (Additional file 4) presented the clinical profiles of the two study groups. The transformation status of spiral arteries in the basal plate sections was assessed in a blinded manner. All histological and immunostaining analyses were performed by investigators who were unaware of the clinical diagnosis, CD81 expression status, and group allocation, including during image acquisition and quantitative analysis. For every case, the number of spiral arteries exhibiting different degrees of transformation was recorded. The percentages of arteries with specific transformation features were calculated by dividing their count by the total vessel number, then multiplying by 100.

Immunostaining

Placenta, kidney, and other organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 2 μm. Placenta and other organ slices underwent hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, and kidney slices underwent periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining. For immunohistochemical analysis, paraffin sections of placental tissue samples underwent antigen retrieval and blocking prior to being incubated with primary antibodies targeting CD81, alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), Desmin, CK7, pan CK, smooth muscle 22 alpha (SM22α), IGF2, Vimentin and cleaved Caspase3 antibody at 4℃ overnight. Signals detection was performed using either anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG horseradish peroxidase (HRP) detection kit (hypersensitive DAB detection system, Typing), or a double dye test reagent (DS-0003, DS-0004, ZSGB-Bio) conjugated to HRP or alkaline peroxidase (AP), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.

For frozen placental sections and cultured cells, samples were briefly fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and incubated with antibodies against pan CK7, α-SMA, SM22a, IGF2, Vimentin, monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1), MCT4, CD31, p-mTOR and RhoA at 4℃ overnight. Fluorescent signals were visualized using fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. The slides were then counterstained with DAPI (Abcam), followed by imaging under a fluorescence microscope (DM6 B, Leica). Quantitative analysis of staining intensity was performed using ImageJ software (NIH). The antibodies used were listed in Table S2 (Additional file 4).

Mice

All animal manipulations in this study were approved by the Ethics Review Board for Animal Studies of the Affiliated Drum Tower Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School (2018010028). C57BL/6J mice, aged 8–12 weeks, weighed 18–20 g, were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University (Nanjing, China), and housed in a temperature and humidity-controlled room with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. The investigators responsible for assessing mouse phenotype and placental histopathology were blinded to group assignments.

Generation of transgenic mice with placental restricted CD81 overexpression

To generate transgenic mice, the placental-specific promoter ERVW-1 (endogenous retrovirus group W member 1) was used [13, 14]. A cDNA fragment encoding human CD81 (NM_004356.3) was subcloned (Hind III/ XbaI digestion) into pGL3-Basic plasmid. The ERVW-1 promoter cDNA sequence was then subcloned (XhoI/Hind III digestion) into the former pGL3-CD81 plasmid to obtain pGL3-ERVW-1 promoter-CD81 recombinant plasmid (Additional file 4: Fig. S2A). ERVW-1 promoter-driven CD81 expression was confined to trophoblastic cell lines HTR-8/SVneo, BeWo and JAR, and absent in non-trophoblastic cells (Additional file 4: Fig. S2B). The linear ERVW-1 promoter-CD81 fragment was excised from recombinant plasmid backbone using restriction enzymes NotI and BamHI, and microinjected into the pronuclei of DBA zygotes. The injected embryos were then transferred to pseudo-pregnant FVB females. A total of two transgenic mice with placental restricted CD81 overexpression (syn-CD81+ mice) were obtained. These were validated by PCR screening of genomic DNA from tail samples using a sense primer targeting the ERVW-1 promoter and an anti-sense primer for the CD81 cDNA (5’-AGGTGACCACGTCCACCTTT-3’ and 5’-GCGATGAGGATGTAGATGCCT-3’, respectively), yielding a 395 bp band (Additional file 4: Fig. S2C). In placentas from pregnant WT female mice mated with syn-CD81+ male mice (pregnant pl-CD81+ mice), 52.7% (89/169) were CD81+ and 47.3% (80/169) were CD81−, as determined by PCR screening of fetal tissues. Furthermore, CD81 expression was increased in the placentas from pregnant WT mice mated with syn-CD81+ male mice (Additional file 4: Fig. S2D-F), but not in other organs of the syn-CD81+ mice (Additional file 4: Fig. S2G-H). The CD81 protein content in CD81− placentas was slightly elevated, likely due to paracrine effects. For further studies, the syn-CD81+ founders were subsequently backcrossed to a C57BL/6J background for ten successive generations.

Blood pressure, proteinuria and doppler ultrasound in mice

Systolic blood pressure (SBP) measurements were obtained using a tail-cuff plethysmography system (BP-2010 A, Softron). Proteinuria was assessed by placing pregnant mice in individual metabolic cages, allowing for controlled urine collection. Additionally, uterine artery resistance indexes were conducted by Doppler ultrasound imaging (VINNO 6, Feiyinuo), following protocols established in our earlier research [14].

Primary cell isolation and culture

Decidual tissues for EVT isolation were obtained from normal pregnant women undergoing elective termination, while tissues for DSC and dNK cell isolation were obtained from normal pregnancies at 6–9 gestational weeks undergoing elective pregnancy termination. Collected tissues were finely cut into 1 mm3 fragments, and enzymatically digested with 100 µg/mL DNase I (DN25, Sigma Aldrich) and 1.0 mg/mL collagenase IV (C5138, Sigma Aldrich) at 37 °C for 1.5 h under gentle shaking.

The digested cell suspensions were sequentially purified using magnetic beads. EVTs were purified using PE-labeled anti-HLA-G antibody (SAB4700317, Sigma Aldrich), and dNK cells were purified using CD56 antibody (130050401, Miltenyi), following the respective manufacturer’s instructions. Purified EVTs and dNK cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (31800-022, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10270-106, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (450-201-EL, Wisent Inc.).

For isolating primary DSCs, the digested cell suspensions were pelleted by centrifugation at 500 × g for 10 min. The cell pellets were resuspended in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12, 319-085-CL, Wisent Inc.) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and cultured overnight. Floating cells were removed the next day, leaving the adherent cells as DSCs.

EVTs were transduced with either Ad-CD81 [9] or control adenovirus (Ad-CTL) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 200. The culture medium was changed after 24 h to remove the adenovirus, and the conditioned medium (CM) from Ad-CTL infected EVTs (EVT-CMCTL) and Ad-CD81 infected EVTs (EVT-CMCD81) were obtained after 72 h, centrifuged and stored at -80 °C. For CM treatment experiments, EVT-CM was added to the DSCs or dNK cells at 50% concentration. The culture medium was changed after 24 h to remove the EVT-CM, and the CM from EVT-CMCTL treated DSCs (DSC-CMCTL), EVT-CMCD81 treated DSCs (DSC-CMCD81), EVT-CMCTL treated dNK cells (dNK-CMCTL) and EVT-CMCD81 treated dNK cells (dNK-CMCD81) were obtained after 48 h, centrifuged, and stored at -80 °C for later use.

Cell lines

The HTR-8/SVneo (human extravillous trophoblasts in early pregnancy), JAR (Human choriocarcinoma cells) and HUVEC (human endothelial cells) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium; BeWo (Human chorionic cancer cells) and IK (human endometrial carcinoma cells) in DMEM/F12 medium; COS7 (renal tubular epithelial cells), MSCs (mesenchymal stem cells) and SW1353 (human chondrosarcoma cells) in DMEM medium (11966-025, Gibco), and MNNG/HOS (human osteosarcoma cells) in aMEM medium (12492-013, Gibco). All media were supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. When cells reached approximately 50% confluency, they were transfected with pGL3-ERVW-1 promoter-CD81 recombinant plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen), and cellular extracts were collected after 24 h for CD81 mRNA detection.

The T/G HA-VSMC cell line (XY9C1263, X-Y Biotechnology) was cultured in Smooth Muscle Cell Medium (SMCM, 1101, ScienCell Research Laboratories). Cells were passaged at a 1:3 ratio every 3 days. VSMCs were treated with EVT-CM, DSC-CM or dNK-CM at 50% concentration for 3 days. Following treatment, the VSMCs were subjected to immunofluorescence assays, flow cytometry, or other molecular measurements as described below.

RNA extraction and quantification of real time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted and analyzed by RT-qPCR as outlined in our previous publication [9]. The primer sequences used were listed in Table S3 (Additional file 4).

Western blotting

Western blotting was conducted according to established protocols [15]. The antibodies used were listed in Table S2 (Additional file 4). Images of the original, uncropped blots were listed in Additional file 2.

Dot blotting analysis of secreted proteins

PVDF membranes were activated in methanol for 1 min, rinsed in ddH₂O, and air-dried to a semi-dry state. EVT-CM was centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and 10 µL of supernatant was spotted onto the membrane and air-dried. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST, followed by incubation with primary and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (both 1:10,000). After washing, signals were detected using ECL and imaged with a chemiluminescence system.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The human IGF2 ELISA kit (RayBiotech) was used to quantify IGF2 levels according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Apoptosis detection

VSMCs incubated with EVT-CM, DSC-CM or dNK-CM were evaluated for apoptosis using the Annexin V-Alexa Fluor 647/PI apoptosis kit (FMSAV647-100, FcMACS) as previously described [16]. Flow cytometry was conducted on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), and the results were analyzed with CytExpert version 2.3 (Beckman Coulter).

Collagen gel contraction assay

VSMC contractility was assessed utilizing the Cell Contraction Assay Kit (CBA-021, Cell Biolabs, Inc) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. VSMCs pretreated with 50% DSC-CM, dNK-CM or IGF2 (R&D Systems, 292-G2) for 48 h were resuspended in SMCM at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL. A collagen lattice was generated by blending the cell suspension and ice-cold collagen gel solution at a 1:4 ratio. After that, a 0.5 mL mixture was dispensed into each well of a 24-well plate followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Then, 1.0 mL of 50% DSC-CM, dNK-CM, or SMCM containing either PBS or IGF2 was gently layered atop the gels for 24 h. To induce contraction, gels were carefully detached from the well edges using sterile pipette tips. The gel contractility was quantified by measuring surface areas using Image J software, and expressed as the contraction index ΔA/A0, where ΔA represents the reduction of gel area (ΔA = A0 − At) and A0 is the initial gel area at t = 0.

Transwell invasion assay

Cell invasion was assessed using Transwell assays as previously described [17]. Pre-treated VSMCs were resuspended (1 × 105/mL, 200 µL) and added to the upper chamber of Matrigel (1 mg/mL, 356234, BD Biosciences) -precoated polycarbonate filters (8 μm pore size, Millipore), and 800 µL respective CM was placed in the lower chamber. After a 24-hour incubation period, cells invaded through to the underside of the membrane were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and quantified. Three randomly selected visual fields per chamber were analyzed and averaged for quantification.

Wound-healing (migration) assay

Cell migration was evaluated using scratch assays as previously described [18]. VSMCs were seeded in 6-well plates, scratched, and then incubated with EVT-CM, DSC-CM, dNK-CM, or IGF2. Images were taken at consistent locations, and compared at designated time points, and the extent of migration was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Transcriptome analysis

Total RNA was extracted from DSCs pre-treated with three paired samples of EVT-CMCTL and EVT-CMCD81 using TRIzol reagent. Transcriptome sequencing analysis was conducted by 10 K Genomics (Shanghai, China). Differential gene analysis between treatment groups was performed using DESeq2. Genes exhibiting P value < 0.05 and absolute fold change ≥ 1.8 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). GSEA was applied to the ranked dataset encompassing all detected transcripts. RNA-seq data of DSCs were listed in Additional file 3.

Methylation-specific PCR (bisulfite genomic sequencing PCR)

Genomic DNA was extracted from DSCs and subjected to bisulfite conversion using the EpiTect Bisulfite Kit (59104, Qiagen). Unmethylated cytosines were converted to uracil, while 5-methylcytosines remained unchanged. The primer sequences used for methylation-specific PCR were designed by PyroMark Assay Design 2.0 software and listed in Table S4 (Additional file 4). To assess the methylation profile of the H19 DMR, the bisulfite-converted DNA was amplified, cloned, and subjected to sequencing. Analysis of the methylation status of the H19 DMR was determined by cloning and sequencing of bisulfite-treated DNA. The sequence obtained by cloning was analyzed with PyroMark Q96/48 ID (QIAGEN).

Rho GTPase pull-down assay

Rho GTPase activity was assessed using Active Rho Detection Kit (8820 S, Cell Signaling Technology) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Cultured VSMCs were lysed in extraction buffer to obtain total protein lysates containing both active (GTP-bound) and inactive (GDP-bound) forms of the GTPase. These lysates were incubated with a glutathione resin conjugated to a GST-tagged protein binding domain (PBD) specific to the downstream effector of the target GTPase, allowing selective capture of the active form. RhoA activation was specifically detected using agarose beads coupled with the Rhotekin binding domain. Inactive forms and other unbound proteins were eliminated using spin column washes. The GTP-bound (active) GTPase fraction was then eluted with SDS-PAGE loading buffer and subjected to western blotting for analysis.

Statistical analysis

The continuous variables with normal distribution are presented as mean ± SD, while the continuous variables with non-normal distribution are presented as median (interquartile range). The discrete variables were presented as n (%). Statistical significance was determined using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0, San Diego, CA, USA) and SPSS (version 22.0, Chicago, IL, USA). Student’s t test was applied for continuous variables with a normal distribution, and Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for continuous variables with non-normal distribution. Comparisons across multiple groups were tested by one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA. For the analysis of spiral artery transformation status between groups, a general linear mixed model with repeated measures was applied, allowing for multiple observations from the same patient. Spearman correlation was performed to assess the association between CD81 expression and the percentages of three different transformation statuses of spiral arteries, while Pearson correlation was performed to assess the association between CD81 expression and IGF2 expression. A value of P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Upregulation of CD81 on EVTs correlates with impaired spiral artery remodeling in sPE patients

Serial sections of placental basal plates from sPE patients (sPE, n = 45) and matched normotensive pregnant women (control, n = 30) were employed to evaluate the integrity of the vessel wall and the extent of smooth muscle disruption within spiral arteries (Fig. 1A and Additional file 4: Fig. S1B). The transformation status of the vessel wall was graded into three categories based on the severity of disruption according to a system described previously: preserved (intact muscular layer structure), disorganized (partial disorganization and separation of muscular layers with some muscle loss), and destroyed (extensive fragmentation and destruction of muscular layers, leaving little or no muscle remaining) [19–23]. Representative staining patterns were shown in Additional file 4: Fig. S1A, and the percentage distribution of the three transformation statuses across all spiral arteries was summarized in the clustered bar chart (Fig. 1B). Our data showed that 35.0% of vessels exhibited complete muscle destruction, 37.9% showed partially disorganized and separated muscle cells, and 27.1% preserved intact muscle in the control group. In contrast, only 21.4% of arteries showed complete destruction, 35.4% were disorganized, and 43.2% exhibited preserved muscle in the sPE group (F = 4.482, P = 0.013). Furthermore, the correlation between CD81 expression on EVTs and the percentage of the three transformation statuses across all subjects was explored (Fig. 1C), and the results showed that CD81 levels on EVTs were positively correlated with the percentage of preserved, non-transformed spiral arteries (r = 0.40, P = 0.0004), and negatively correlated with the percentage of destroyed, transformed spiral arteries (r=-0.33, P = 0.004) (Fig. 1D).

Placental restricted CD81 overexpression triggers PE-like features and defective spiral artery remodeling in pregnant mice

To distinguish the pathogenic contribution of elevated CD81 in sPE, we constructed a mouse model with placental-specific overexpression of CD81 using the ERVW-1 promoter as previously described [14]. Compared with pregnant WT mice (WT females mated with WT males), pregnant pl-CD81+ mice (WT females mated with syn-CD81+ transgenic males) exhibited strong CD81 expression in the placentas (Fig. 2A and Additional file 4: Fig. S2D-F). As shown in CD81+ placentas at GD15.5, the signals of CD81 were mainly localized to the junction zone, a region rich in trophoblast giant cells (TGCs) and spongiotrophoblasts (Fig. 2A), resembling human EVTs and cytotrophoblasts [24].

Compared with pregnant WT mice (n = 7), the SBP of pregnant pl-CD81+ mice (n = 7) elevated dramatically from GD 12.5 (114.1 ± 5.15 versus 101.9 ± 3.06 mm Hg, P = 0.0002), remained significantly elevated until GD 18.5 (118.0 ± 8.73 versus 100.1 ± 4.53 mm Hg, P = 0.0004), and returned to normal postpartum (Fig. 2B). Proteinuria of pregnant pl-CD81+ mice also significantly increased from GD 12.5 (2039 ± 718.9 versus 934.6 ± 190.1 µg, P = 0.002), persisted until GD 18.5 (2272 ± 1057 versus 1272 ± 375.8 µg, P = 0.0362), then declined to baseline after delivery (Fig. 2C). Although the numbers of live pups were comparable between two groups (Additional file 4: Fig. S2H-I), placental efficiency, measured by the ratio of fetal to placental weight, was significantly decreased in the pl-CD81+ group at GD 17.5 (Additional file 4: Fig. S2J), indicating FGR. Doppler ultrasound assessment demonstrated significantly elevated resistance indexes of uterine arteries in pregnant pl-CD81+ mice (Fig. 2D-E). Compared with WT mice, maternal kidneys harvested from pregnant pl-CD81+ mice at GD 17.5 exhibited glomerular hypertrophy, mesangial hypercellularity, and reduced Bowman’s capsule space of the glomerulus (Fig. 2F-G).

Then, HE staining of CD81+ and WT placentas was conducted, and the data showed that CD81+ placentas exhibited spiral arteries with smaller lumens, thicker vessel walls, and higher vessel-to-lumen ratios (Fig. 2H-I and Additional file 4: Fig. S3A). Cytokeratin 7 (CK7) staining of CD81+ placentas showed decreased trophoblast invasion (Additional file 4: Fig. S3B-C), consistent with Ad-CD81-infected pregnant rats [9]. Moreover, CD81+ placentas exhibited a significantly higher VSMC coverage in the walls of spiral arteries, demonstrated through co-immunostaining of CK7/pan CK and alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)/smooth muscle 22 alpha (SM22α) (Fig. 2J-L and Additional file 4: Fig. S3D-F). No significant differences were observed in the apoptosis of VSMCs from CD81+ and WT placentas, showed by TUNEL and cleaved Caspase3 staining (Additional file 4: Fig. S4A-B). Additionally, no changes in the alignment of the syncytiotrophoblast (SynT) bilayer was demonstrated by immunofluorescence staining for MCT1 (marker for SynT I) and MCT4 (marker for SynT II). Moreover, no morphological alterations were observed in the placental vascular networks within the labyrinth layer of CD81+ placentas (Additional file 4: Fig. S4C-D).

CD81-overexpressing EVTs inhibit VSMC dedifferentiation via a DSC-dependent rather than a dNK cell-dependent mechanism

CD81 consists of four transmembrane segments and two extracellular loops, with the larger one (large extracellular loop, LEL) recognized as the core function domain [25]. Notably, the LEL domain can be recombinantly produced while retaining its conserved biological activity [26]. To verify whether CD81 directly contributes to the retention of VSMCs within spiral arteries, the VSMCs were directly stimulated with recombinant LEL segment of human CD81 protein (rhCD81, 14244-HNCH, Sino Biological Inc.). We found that rhCD81 failed to alter the expression of key differentiation markers α-SMA, SM22a, myosin heavy chain 11 (Myh11), and Calponin or the dedifferentiation marker osteopontin (OPN) (Fig. 3A-D), and had no effect on apoptosis (Fig. 3E-F). Dot blotting analysis confirmed robust CD81 expression in EVT-CMCD81, whereas it was barely detectable in the control group (Fig. 3G). Subsequently, EVT-CMCTL and EVT-CMCD81 were applied to VSMCs, and no significant differences were detected in either differentiation/dedifferentiation marker expression (Fig. 3H-K) or apoptosis levels between the two groups (Fig. 3L-M). These results indicated that CD81 did not directly inhibit the dedifferentiation or apoptosis of spiral artery VSMCs.

As the predominant immune cell population in decidua during early pregnancy, decidual natural killer (dNK) cells initiate the early stage of uterine spiral artery remodeling before EVT invasion [27] and could secrete a variety of cytokines and growth factors to disrupt the structural integrity of VSMCs [8, 28]. As the most abundant non-immune cells within decidua, stromal cells, encircle the spiral artery walls and are well-positioned to influence vascular remodeling revealed by recent single-cell sequencing [29]. Given that both dNK cells and DSCs closely interact with EVTs and these interactions may alter their cytokine profiles, we examined their respective contributions to the loss of VSMC. However, dNK-CMCD81 had no impact on VSMC differentiation/dedifferentiation markers (Fig. 4A-D), contractility (Fig. 4F), migration and invasion (Fig. 4G-H), or apoptosis (Fig. 4E). In contrast, DSC-CMCD81 markedly increased the expression of differentiation markers (α-SMA, SM22a, Myh11, and Calponin), decreased the expression of dedifferentiation marker OPN (Fig. 4A-D), enhanced the contractility (Fig. 4F), and reduced the migration and invasion of VSMCs (Fig. 4G-H), without altering apoptosis (Fig. 4E). These findings indicated that, during spiral artery remodeling, CD81 suppresses VSMC dedifferentiation primarily through a DSC-dependent rather than a dNK cell-dependent mechanism.

Downregulation of DSC-derived IGF2 is a crucial mechanism by which CD81 suppresses VSMC dedifferentiation via mTOR signaling pathway

To elucidate the mechanism underlying DSC-dependent dedifferentiation of VSMCs, we performed transcriptome sequencing on DSCs treated with paired EVT-CM. Among the DEGs, IGF2 exhibited the most significant decrease (Fig. 5A). This finding was further validated by RT-qPCR and Western blotting, which showed a marked reduction in its incompletely processed forms (pro-IGF2 and big-IGF2), as well as the mature form (Fig. 5B-C). Notably, the precursor forms exhibit greater bioavailability than mature IGF2 and possess equal receptor-binding potential as mature IGF2 [30, 31]. A decreased level of secreted IGF2 was also observed in DSC-CMCD81 (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, immunostaining analyses demonstrated significantly reduced IGF2 levels in Vimentin-positive DSCs within the decidual stroma compartment (Fig. 5E and Additional file 4: Fig. S5A-C), and a negative correlation (r=-0.39, P = 0.006) between CD81 intensity on EVTs and IGF2 intensity on DSCs (n = 24 each group, Fig. 5F). Consistently, decreased IGF2 levels were observed in the decidual stroma of CD81+ placentas from the model mice (Fig. 5G and Additional file 4: Fig. S5D-E).

Notably, addition of recombinant LEL segment of CD81 directly decreased IGF2 expression in DSCs (Fig. 5H-I), indicating that the LEL segment is the key component responsible for the functional activity of CD81 within the EVT-CMCD81. Furthermore, the reduction of IGF2 in DSCs induced by EVT-CMCD81 was effectively restored by a CD81-specific neutralizing antibody (Fig. 5J-K).

As an imprinting gene, IGF2 is typically expressed in accordance with CpG methylation status of the imprinting control center (ICR) upstream of the H19 gene [32]. Thus, whether EVT-CMCD81 affects the expression level of IGF2 by regulating methylation status was explored. However, our data showed that EVT-CMCD81 had no effect on the methylation status at H19 differentially methylated region (DMR) (Additional file 4: Fig. S6A-B), and on the expression of DNA methyltransferase (DNMTs) [33] (Additional file 4: Fig. S6C) in DSCs. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further revealed that the mTOR signaling pathway, which is well-established for its involvement in promoting IGF2 production [34, 35], was downregulated in DSCs treated with EVT-CMCD81 (Additional file 4: Fig. S6D). Further Western blotting results indicated that both rhCD81 and EVT-CMCD81 significantly reduced the phosphorylation of mTOR, which could be reversed by neutralization of CD81 (Fig. 5L-M). To elucidate the role of mTOR signaling in this context, the mTOR activator MHY1485 and the mTOR inhibitor Rapamycin were utilized. The results showed that the reduction of IGF2 in DSCs caused by CD81 overexpression was reversed by MHY1485 (Fig. 5N-O), while the restoration of IGF2 following neutralization of CD81 was significantly inhibited by Rapamycin (Fig. 5P). Consistently, p-mTOR signaling in DSCs of placentas was significantly reduced in both sPE patients and transgenic mice (Additional file 4: Fig. S7A-D).

IGF2 from DSCs promotes VSMC dedifferentiation through inhibiting RhoA/ROCK1/MLC pathway

Then, recombinant human IGF2 protein was applied directly to stimulate VSMCs, and we found that IGF2 led to a pronounced downregulation of differentiation markers, accompanied by an upregulation of OPN (Fig. 6A-C), indicating that IGF2 actively induces the dedifferentiation process in VSMCs. Furthermore, the inhibitory effect of DSC-CMCD81 on VSMC dedifferentiation was partially restored by the addition of recombinant IGF2 (Fig. 6D-E), while neutralization of IGF2 blocked the dedifferentiation of VSMCs induced by DSC-CMCTL (Fig. 6F-G). Additionally, IGF2 impaired contractility (Fig. 6H), and enhanced the migration and invasion abilities of VSMCs (Fig. 6I-J).

Previous studies have reported that IGFs regulate the Rho GTPase signal pathway [36, 37], which is crucial for the phenotypic switching of VSMCs. Accordingly, we examined the small GTPase RhoA and its downstream effectors, including Rho-associated kinase 1 and 2 (ROCK1/2), and myosin light chain 2 (MLC2). Mechanistically, IGF2 reduced RhoA-GTP levels, decreased ROCK1/2 expression, and suppressed MLC2 phosphorylation in VSMCs (Fig. 6K-L). In addition, the increase in differentiation markers induced by DSC-CMCD81 was inhibited by the ROCK inhibitor Y27632 (Fig. 6M-N). Consistently, RhoA signaling in VSMCs of spiral arteries was significantly elevated in both sPE patients and transgenic mice (Additional file 4: Fig. S7E-H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that increased CD81 expression in EVTs inhibits DSC-derived IGF2, thereby preventing VSMC dedifferentiation by maintaining activation of the RhoA/ROCK1/MLC pathway.

Discussion

The most prominent manifestation of impaired spiral artery remodeling in PE is the failure of VSMCs to disappear within the arterial wall. In the current study, we demonstrate that increased retention of differentiated VSMCs is positively correlated with elevated CD81 levels on EVTs in both sPE patients and a placental‑specific CD81‑overexpressing mouse model. Mechanistically, this occurs because CD81-overexpressing EVTs inhibit IGF2 secretion from DSCs, thereby preventing VSMC dedifferentiation, rather than apoptosis, leading to incomplete remodeling.

We previously reported that in sPE patients, EVTs continuously overexpress CD81 with advancing gestation, which inhibits EVT migration/invasion and induces vascular endothelial cell activation in vitro, providing clues that elevated placental CD81 is associated with the onset of sPE [9]. Moreover, systemic administration of Ad-CD81, which successfully upregulated CD81 expression in placental trophoblasts, induced PE-like symptoms and impaired spiral artery remodeling in rats. Building on this, we employed a system to evaluate remodeling status by classifying spiral arteries into preserved, disorganized and destroyed VSMC phenotypes in placental basal plates, enabling a more comprehensive comparison between sPE patients and controls. Our analysis revealed that higher CD81 levels on EVTs were strongly associated with greater preservation of VSMCs and correspondingly more pronounced defects in spiral artery remodeling in sPE patients. Similarly, systemic and local pathologic evaluations revealed that the pregnant transgenic mice with placental-restricted CD81 overexpression recapitulated these human findings, exhibiting hypertension, proteinuria, FGR, and histological evidence of increased preservation of differentiated VSMCs in spiral arteries and decreased invasion of trophoblasts.

In exploring why VSMCs in spiral arteries failed to be eliminated under conditions of CD81 overexpression in EVTs, we discovered that impaired VSMC dedifferentiation, rather than apoptosis, was the primary cause, aligning with other recent reports [5, 38]. Typically, this dedifferentiation process in spiral artery remodeling is generally classified into sequential EVT-independent and EVT-dependent stages [8], and the former is driven largely by decidual leukocytes, particularly dNK cells, before EVTs invade the decidua [28, 39], while the latter pertains to the effects of EVTs after invasion. Prior studies have shown that the bioactivity of CM from co-cultured dNK cells and EVTs is lower than that from dNK cells or EVTs cultured separately [28], with reduced levels of Angiotensin-1 (Ang-1) and vascular endothelial growth factor-C (VEGF-C) [40]. These findings point to potential negative feedback loops between immune and trophoblast compartments that may restrict the localized release of pro-angiogenic factors, and highlight the importance of spatiotemporal regulation by different cell types in ensuring completion of spiral artery remodeling. Our in vitro co-culture model extended these insights by showing that CD81‑mediated inhibition of VSMC dedifferentiation was mediated by DSCs, not dNK cells, suggesting that EVT-induced changes in VSMC dedifferentiation were driven by complex interactions with DSCs. These findings highlight DSCs as important intermediary in EVT-VSMC crosstalk, and disturbances in this interaction could impair spiral artery remodeling and contribute to the pathogenesis of PE.

We identified DSC-derived IGF2 as a pivotal factor driving VSMC dedifferentiation during spiral artery remodeling. Given that VSMCs express receptors for multiple growth factors [41], IGF2 serves as an important ligand modulating their phenotypic plasticity. We further confirmed that IGF2 levels were markedly reduced in DSCs of placentas from both sPE patients and transgenic mice with placental-specific CD81 overexpression. This reduction was attributable to suppression of the mTOR signaling pathway in DSCs treated with EVT-CMCD81. Functionally, diminished IGF2 impaired VSMC dedifferentiation, whereas exogenous IGF2 supplementation restored it. Mechanistically, IGF2 suppression resulted in abnormalities in the RhoA/ROCK/MLC2 signaling axis, a pathway essential for maintaining the contractile phenotype, thereby contributing to pathological persistence of differentiated VSMCs in PE. Indeed, based on our data, IGF2 suppression, driven by maladaptive EVT-DSC signaling, may contribute to defective spiral artery remodeling and vascular dysfunction in PE.

Although we demonstrated that extracellular CD81 regulates IGF2 expression in DSCs via inhibition of mTOR signaling, the precise upstream receptors and molecular intermediates linking CD81 LEL engagement to intracellular signaling were not directly investigated and require further elucidation. In addition, whether circulating CD81 levels in early pregnancy can serve as a predictive biomarker for sPE remains to be determined and warrants validation in larger, well-designed longitudinal cohorts.

Collectively, our findings suggest that DSC‑dependent regulation of VSMC dedifferentiation plays an important role in spiral artery remodeling. In normal pregnancy, coordinated interactions among EVTs, DSCs, and VSMCs ensure timely phenotypic switching of VSMCs, enabling proper vessel remodeling to support placental perfusion. In PE, aberrant CD81 upregulation on EVTs disrupts this cellular interplay, suppressing DSC‑derived IGF2, locking VSMCs in a differentiated state, and ultimately compromising spiral artery remodeling. These insights elucidate a previously unrecognized EVT-DSC-VSMC regulatory axis, and suggest that targeting DSC‑mediated IGF2 signaling may represent a novel therapeutic avenue to restore vascular adaptation in PE.

Conclusions

Here, we demonstrate that sPE placentas exhibit impaired VSMC dedifferentiation within spiral arteries, positively correlating with CD81 levels on EVTs. Pregnant mice with placental restricted CD81 overexpression develop defective spiral artery remodeling with the persistence of differentiated VSMCs. Mechanistically, CD81-overexpressing EVTs inhibit VSMC dedifferentiation through a DSC-dependent pathway mediated by IGF2. Ultimately, our findings offer new insights into PE pathogenesis and open potential avenues for developing novel therapeutic strategies.
