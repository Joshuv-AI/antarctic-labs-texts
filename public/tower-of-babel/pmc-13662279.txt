Substrate energetics in non-templated DNA synthesis by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase
Authors: Katrina Mogannam, Andrej Lupták
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA polymerases typically use deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs) for DNA synthesis, yet the contribution of the γ-phosphate to catalysis remains unclear. Here, we examine the role of substrate energetics in enzyme-catalyzed polymerization using a fidelity-independent model polymerase, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT). Relative to dNTPs, we find that TdT utilizes the canonical deoxyribonucleoside diphosphates (dNDPs) with reduced rates and catalytic efficiencies, but diminished substrate bias. This lower specificity indicates that the γ-phosphate contributes primarily to nucleotide discrimination, rather than phosphodiester bond formation. Furthermore, our work shows that dNDPs and dNTPs with the same nucleobase exhibit comparable activation energies for the rate-limiting step during DNA synthesis, supporting a model in which the γ-phosphate is not required for lowering the energetic barrier for TdT-catalyzed polymerization. These findings define a mechanistic framework for understanding non-templated DNA synthesis and its application in enzymatic DNA synthesis technologies.

Introduction

DNA polymerases typically synthesize DNA using deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs), catalyzing a phosphoryl transfer reaction involving a nucleophilic attack by the 3′-hydroxyl of a DNA primer on the α-phosphate of an incoming nucleotide. The products are a new phosphodiester bond extending the DNA primer by a nucleotide and a molecule of inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi) (Fig. 1A) [1]. The γ-phosphate of the nucleotide is not directly involved in catalysis, but aids in driving polymerization as part of the pyrophosphate leaving group [2, 3]. Since only the α-phosphate is retained in the backbone of the DNA, a nucleoside diphosphate (NDP) can act as a substrate for polymerization, leaving the terminal β-phosphate as the leaving group (Fig. 1B). Previous work showed that DNA polymerases from A, B, and X families are capable of synthesizing DNA using dNDPs as substrates, and can use the phosphate (Pi) as a substrate for the reverse reaction, phosphorolysis [4–6]. Other enzymes that have been shown to utilize nucleoside diphosphates include polynucleotide phosphorylase (PNPase) [7], HIV-1 reverse transcriptase (RT) [8], RNA polymerase [9], and RNA dependent RNA polymerase of SARS-CoV-2 [10].

Forward and reverse reactions of DNA polymerization with (A) dNTPs and pyrophosphate (PPi), and (B) dNDPs and phosphate (Pi).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) is a family X polymerase known for its role in the nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) pathway during V(D)J recombination, promoting genomic diversity in vertebrate immune cell repertoires [11–14]. TdT catalyzes synthesis of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) independently of a template strand, randomly incorporating nucleotides onto the 3′-end of a DNA strand. The promiscuous nature of this enzyme allows it to catalyze polymerization of DNA or RNA, and also incorporate a variety of modified nucleotides, leading to its prominence in biotechnology applications, such as end labeling [15–22], de novo DNA synthesis [23–30], and other molecular purposes [31–42]. TdT lacks fidelity in that it does not use a template strand as a reference for incorporation of a specific nucleobase via Watson–Crick base pairing. However, in cells, TdT preferentially utilizes dGTP and dCTP over dTTP and dATP, resulting in GC-rich regions and more efficient annealing of single-stranded DNAs (ssDNAs) that are intermediates during immune cell NHEJ [43–45]. In contrast, in vitro experiments have revealed nucleotide preferences that depend on the divalent metal ion assisting TdT catalysis: dGTP > dATP > dTTP ≈ dCTP with Mg2+ [46].

Crystal structures of TdT as the apoenzyme and in complexes with a variety of bound substrates have revealed that the enzyme does not undergo large conformational changes during catalysis and lacks open and closed states, in contrast to template-dependent DNA polymerases [45]. The only major conformational change—a shift in Loop1—is associated with precatalytic complex formation with the primer strand [44]. High-resolution structures of the active site containing various nucleoside triphosphates or the pyrophosphate leaving group reveal no movement of the β- and γ-phosphates of a substrate before and after catalysis [2, 47, 48]. Additionally, structures of TdT before and after phosphodiester formation reveal base stacking of the incoming nucleotide with the last nucleotide of the DNA primer (Supplementary Fig. S1) [2]. However, most interactions with the incoming nucleotide occur at the phosphates, providing a molecular basis for the high activity but low nucleotide specificity of this enzyme (Fig. 2) [2, 49]. Conserved active site residues Lys338 and Arg336 contribute to the neutralization of the γ- and β-phosphate groups, respectively [47, 50, 51], and Asp343 and Asp345 coordinate the catalytic metal ion in the active site [2, 50]. There is no movement of these four active site residues observed when comparing structures of the apoenzyme, with primer bound, with NTP substrate bound, and as the holoenzyme [2, 47–49]. Only a single side chain, His342, shifts to coordinate to the last phosphodiester of the ssDNA primer and the γ-phosphate of a nucleotide substrate upon precatalytic complex formation, presumably aligning the two substrates for catalysis. His342 is found rotated, though still hydrogen-bonded to the distal phosphate of the pyrophosphate leaving group, in the product state of the reaction [2, 47, 49, 52]. Because the active site of the enzyme is largely preorganized, it is unlikely that any conformational changes contribute to the rate-limiting step of DNA synthesis. This template-agnostic enzyme is thus an ideal model for biochemical analysis of the energetics of phosphodiester bond formation in the absence of fidelity.

Even though TdT’s activity with a large variety of phosphorylated substrates is well documented, the contribution of the γ-phosphate to the enzymatic activity has not been investigated. Specifically, nucleoside diphosphates have yet to be evaluated. In the presumed catalytic mechanism of DNA synthesis by TdT, the catalytic divalent metal ion is coordinated by the β-phosphate of a nucleotide substrate or pyrophosphate via direct Coulombic interactions (Fig. 2). Therefore, we hypothesized that the presence of the β-phosphate on a nucleotide substrate is the most important determinant for catalytic activity. To test this hypothesis, we measured the kinetic and thermodynamic parameters of DNA synthesis from dNDP versus dNTP substrates, defining the contribution of the γ-phosphate to oligonucleotide synthesis with TdT.

Materials and methods

Materials

TdT was purchased from New England Biolabs. Deoxynucleotide triphosphates and diphosphates were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. Deoxynucleotide triphosphates were used as received, unless otherwise stated. DNA oligonucleotides were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies. All other chemicals were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich.

Primer sequence for incorporation reactions: /5IRD800/CCATGGACACGGCGAA or /5Cy5/CCATGGACACGGCGAA

Ion-exchange purification of dNDPs

Purification of dNDPs was carried out on an ÄKTApurifier UPC 10 using a DNAPac PA100 anion-exchange column. Nucleotides were injected at a neutral pH and eluted off the column with a gradient of 0–250 mM of KCl in six column volumes. Elution of dNMPs or dNTPs occurred at significantly lower and higher salt concentrations, respectively, for baseline resolution of the individual species. The collected dNDP fractions were desalted using an Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity Series II HPLC equipped with a diode array detector. Separations were performed using an Agilent Eclipse XDB-C18 column (eluting with 0%–1% acetonitrile in H2O over 6 min) with a flow rate at 4 ml/min. The nucleotides were dried and resuspended in 400 μl of D2O for NMR characterization, then concentrated as needed (Supplementary Fig. S2). The pH was adjusted to ∼7 with KOH.

Initial dNDP utilization test

We started each reaction by adding either 0.1 mM dNTPs or 0.2 mM dNDPs to a 16-nt 5′ end-labeled primer (5 nM) with 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM KCl, 0.1% Triton X-100 buffer, 5 mM MgCl2, and 0.1 units/µl of TdT. We allowed it to react for 5 min. A replicate left to react with 0.25 mM CoCl2 was added to confirm the influence of Co2+ on our reaction conditions.

Single-nucleotide incorporation assay

We combined a 16-nt 5′ end-labeled primer (5 nM) with 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM KCl, 0.1% Triton X-100 buffer, 5 mM MgCl2, and 0.1 units/µl of TdT. Reactions were prepared on ice for 40 s for a primer–enzyme complex to form and transferred to a heat block at final reaction temperature for no more than 10 s. The reaction was started by the addition of nucleotide substrates at indicated concentrations, and aliquots were withdrawn and quenched with equal volume of stop buffer (95% formamide, 25 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid). Samples were fractionated using a denaturing 15%–18% polyacrylamide gel. Gels were scanned in the IR800 dye channel on the LI-COR Odyssey CLx imager and the images were analyzed by drawing a line profile down each lane in ImageJ, and exporting the plot profile data to Excel to fit band intensities to a multi-Gaussian function. The number, amplitude, and width of Gaussians peaks used for band fitting varied for least sum-of-squared-differences fit of data from each experiment. The bands corresponding to the starting primer and the added nucleotide were easily distinguished, but required peak fitting for precise band intensity analysis, especially for weak bands neighboring strong peaks. We found that the Gaussian function modeled the peaks very well. For each nucleotide addition, the kinetics were calculated from initial rates of the fraction of the previous position extended.

Enzyme dilution experiment

Extension assays were performed at the same condition with a serial dilution of the enzyme stock to calculate saturating levels of enzyme to the labeled DNA primer at 5 nM concentration. A Prism analysis using “Michaelis–Menten” curve fitting of the data provided KM′ value, which assured our conditions are performed with enzyme in excess of our labeled DNA.

Arrhenius analysis

All kinetic data were plotted on ln(kobs) versus 1/T (K) graphs derived from the Arrhenius equation k = A · exp(−Ea/kT) linearized to ln(k) = ln(A) − Ea/kT, and the activation energy was extracted from the data by multiplying the slope of the linear portion of the graph (from 20°C to 37°C) by the Boltzmann constant.

Model fitting

All data were fit with the stated multi-Gaussian function models using the regression analysis in Excel. The final observed rates were fit in GraphPad Prism using the Michaelis–Menten equation model to obtain final values and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The final observed rates were additionally fit with the Michaelis–Menten equation fitting tool available as “MM-fit-v5.5.py” on Github, posted by JMB-Scripts [53]. This Python program provided equivalent final values observed with GraphPad Prism, and additionally provided the R2, standard error (SE), and relative standard error (%RSE) for each value. The activation energies were extracted directly from a line fit to the data with the reported errors being the SEs of the slope (multiplied by the Boltzmann constant).

Results

TdT utilizes dNDPs as substrates

To test substrate phosphorylation dependence on DNA synthesis, we first confirmed that TdT can utilize dNDPs as substrates. To directly compare our results to other DNA polymerases studied under similar reaction conditions, all subsequent experiments used a simplified buffer and Mg2+ as the sole catalytic divalent metal ion (Supplementary Fig. S3). TdT is known to follow Michaelis–Menten kinetics [54, 55], allowing us to perform single-nucleotide incorporation experiments for kinetic characterization with varying substrates (Supplementary Fig. S4). We measured the optimal enzyme–primer complex (Supplementary Fig. S5) to ensure that all primers were bound by the enzyme prior to nucleotide addition and measured the KM, Vmax, and the catalytic efficiency of TdT utilizing all four canonical dNTPs and dNDPs (Table 1).

The average KM for dNTPs was measured to be 7 µM, and, at 5 nM DNA primer and ∼30 nM TdT concentrations, the average Vmax and catalytic efficiency were 6 × 10−2 s−1 and 1.8 × 10−2 µM−1 s−1, respectively. All dNDP reactions were slower, with an average KM of 200 µM, and the average Vmax and catalytic efficiency of 2 × 10−3 s−1 and 9 × 10−6 µM−1 s−1, respectively. As expected from previous studies [46], KM values and catalytic efficiencies with dNTP substrates showed that TdT preferentially incorporates purines over pyrimidines (Supplementary Fig. S6). Surprisingly, the preference for purines disappears with dNDP substrates, revealing an altered order of KMs and catalytic efficiencies (Table 1). Additionally, the trend of the kobs curves exhibited more dispersion for dNTPs, compared to the dNDPs (Fig. 3A), showing that nucleobase discrimination during DNA synthesis is more pronounced with dNTPs and implying that the γ-phosphate contributes to the substrate bias of TdT (Fig. 3B and C).

Ea measurements of TdT with dNTPs and dNDPs

We further used presteady-state kinetic analysis to measure the activation energy (Ea) of DNA synthesis from dNDPs and dNTPs (Supplementary Fig. S7). We only considered temperatures in the linear range of the Arrhenius plot (20°C–37°C) and, because the active site of TdT is rigid, we assumed that the Ea measurements report on the rate-limiting step during phosphodiester formation. The TdT Ea values range from 46 to 70 kJ/mol (Table 2), much lower than templated DNA polymerases, which have dNTP Ea values between 90 and 130 kJ/mol [5, 56–59], whereas the average Ea for dNDPs for the Taq polymerase is 108 kJ/mol [5] (Supplementary Table S1). Surprisingly, the Eas tend to be similar for the same nucleobase regardless of phosphorylation (e.g. dADP and dATP), with differences spanning between 23 and 40 kJ/mol (Table 2 and Fig. 4). There is no apparent trend connecting Ea values of individual substrates to the respective KM, Vmax, or catalytic efficiencies, indicating that while the γ-phosphate contributes to substrate discrimination, it does not lower the energy barrier of the rate-limiting step for DNA synthesis.

Discussion

TdT is an outstanding model polymerase for studying the catalysis of phosphodiester bond formation because this unique enzyme lacks fidelity and does not undergo major conformational changes typical of templated DNA polymerases. The preorganized active site primarily interacts with atoms involved in catalysis, while making few interactions with the sugar and nucleobase of the incoming nucleotide [2, 45–47]. Taking advantage of these properties, we studied the chemistry of phosphodiester formation as a function of substrate energetics by comparing dNTP and dNDP utilization, revealing the contributions of the γ-phosphate. As expected, our measurements of TdT KM and Vmax values with dNTPs align with previously published work on the enzyme (Table 1) [18, 46]. Although the KMs of dNTPs are similar to templated polymerases, the rates are considerably lower and the enzyme is less processive [60, 61]. Based on the average KM and Vmax values, TdT utilizing dNTPs has ∼21-fold faster rate of DNA synthesis, ∼38-fold lower KM, and ∼1500-fold higher catalytic efficiency (Vmax/ KM) than with dNDPs. This amounts to a somewhat smaller dependence on substrate energetics than the average templated DNA polymerization (Supplementary Table S2). Furthermore, the substrate bias that the enzyme exhibits with dNTPs is greatly diminished with dNDPs (Fig. 3A). This is particularly notable with the variance in catalytic efficiencies among the diphosphorylated versus triphosphorylated nucleotides (Table 1), with the largest fold change in catalytic efficiency for dNDPs being ∼4 (dT/dC) and ∼37 for dNTPs (dG/dT), demonstrating that nucleobase discrimination is strongly influenced by the presence of the γ-phosphate.

While much of the active site of TdT is rigid [2, 47–49], His342 moves between two orientations, bridging the γ-phosphate of the incoming dNTP and the last phosphodiester of the primer in the precatalytic state, and swinging out to retain the hydrogen-bonding interaction with the pyrophosphate product (Fig. 2) and losing the interaction with the phosphodiester on the primer [2, 47, 49, 52]. Presumably, this interaction allows the product oligonucleotide to move by one nucleotide to become a substrate for the next catalytic cycle, with His342 then swinging back to bridge the two new substrates. However, His 342 does not appear to be essential for the function of this class of enzymes, because the substitution does not abolish phosphodiester synthesis, although variants of TdT with His342 replaced by Ala residues show lower activity [2, 52]. Furthermore, the presumed ancestral terminal deoxynucleotide transferase has a Gly at the analogous position, further underscoring its non-essential role in catalysis [62]. We anticipated that the hydrogen bond between His342 and the nucleotide substrate would not exist with dNDPs in the active site, reducing overall activity similarly to H342A activity with dNTPs [2, 52]. Our observations with diphosphorylated substrates, particularly the lower discrimination among the four canonical dNDP, fit well a model where His342 helps align the primer and the dNTP for the nucleophilic attack, but in absence of the γ-phosphate, this role is lost, allowing the active site to fit the four dNDPs with less discrimination, but at a cost of a higher KM and lower Vmax values.

When we measured the apparent activation energies for the rate-limiting step of the forward reaction, we expected the values for the di- and triphosphorylated substrates to differ significantly, as was previously seen with templated polymerases (Supplementary Fig. S8) [5, 9]. Surprisingly, we observed that the Ea values were similar for the same di- or triphosphorylated nucleoside, ranging from 46 to 70 kJ/mol (Table 2). Given that the ground-state energy difference between ADP and ATP is ∼32 kJ/mol [63], and assuming that the energy difference is similar between dNDPs and dNTPs, the transition-state energy we observed for this rate-limiting step is consistently and significantly higher for dNTPs than dNDPs (Fig. 4). These data show that the γ-phosphate does not contribute to lowering the energy barrier of the rate-limiting step of the DNA synthesis. We therefore conclude that it is unlikely that the transition state for the rate-limiting step is associated with an activated nucleotide substrate. Rather, we posit that the rate-limiting step is associated with activating the nucleophile, such as the formation of 3′-oxyanion (akin to γ catalysis in RNA-cleaving enzymes), or positioning of the nucleophile for an in-line attack at the α-phosphate of the incoming nucleotide, modulating the in-line fitness (α catalysis) of phosphate transesterification [64]. The consistent differences in the observed Eas for the same nucleobase then likely result from similar stacking of the nucleobase on the last nucleobase of the primer and the resulting similar positioning of the α-phosphate and the attacking oxyanion, regardless of whether the γ-phosphate is present or not.

Generally, we find that the activation energy of the rate-limiting step of polymerization with TdT is significantly lower than templated polymerases (Supplementary Table S1) [9, 56–59]. Given the observed activation energies, any other steps in DNA synthesis must have lower activation barriers. Considering that the mechanism of TdT differs from other DNA and RNA polymerases by catalyzing single-stranded primer extension in absence of a template strand, we propose that template-dependence results in an increase of the overall energy barrier of phosphodiester formation. In other words, the need for fidelity may lead to a higher transition state energy for template-dependent enzymatic DNA polymerization.

Our data show that substrate discrimination is distinct for dNTPs and dNDPs. This observation may be beneficial to enzymatic DNA synthesis (EDS) [65], because recent EDS methods have utilized TdT to synthesize DNA in step-wise manner, where the 3′-position of an incoming nucleotide substrate is hindered from extension until deprotection [27]. The efficiency of EDS is reliant on protein engineering to optimize the activity of TdT for unique modified nucleotide building blocks [66]. Our data reveal a potentially new approach of controlling DNA synthesis with the wild-type TdT by slowing down DNA synthesis and lowering substrate discrimination when utilizing dNDPs as substrates. These findings may be useful for single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) synthesis because fewer modifications may be needed for intentional nucleotide incorporation.
