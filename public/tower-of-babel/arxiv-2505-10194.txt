Passive Channel Charting: Locating Passive Targets using a UWB Mesh
Raffael Poeggel 1 {}^{\textbf{1}}, Maximilian Stahlke 1 {}^{\textbf{1}}, Jonas Pirkl 1 {}^{\textbf{1}}, Jonathan Ott 1 {}^{\textbf{1}}, George Yammine 1 {}^{\textbf{1}}

Abstract
Fingerprint-based passive localization enables high localization accuracy using low-cost UWB IoT radio sensors. However, fingerprinting demands extensive effort for data acquisition. The concept of channel charting reduces this effort by modeling and projecting the manifold of csi ( csi ) onto a 2D coordinate space. So far, researchers only applied this concept to active radio localization, where a mobile device intentionally and actively emits a specific signal. In this paper, we apply channel charting to passive localization. We use a pedestrian dead reckoning (PDR) system to estimate a target’s velocity and derive a distance matrix from it. We then use this matrix to learn a distance-preserving embedding in 2D space, which serves as a fingerprinting model. In our experiments, we deploy six nodes in a fully connected ultra-wideband (UWB) mesh network to show that our method achieves high localization accuracy, with an average error of just 0.24 m, even when we train and test on different targets.

I
Introduction

The widespread deployment and rapid expansion of IoT radio devices and infrastructure, such as WiFi and
uwb
(
uwb
), have transformed indoor environments into dense, radio-rich spaces
[
1
]
. At the same time, device-free, passive radio sensing enables the tracking of objects or individuals without requiring them to carry any devices
[
2
]
. Together, these developments create strong potential for a wide range of passive sensing applications, including monitoring, surveillance, presence detection, activity recognition, and even sensitive tasks like vital sign monitoring
[
3
,
4
]
. Such applications are especially relevant in areas like security
[
5
]
, personal health
[
6
]
, and elderly care
[
7
]
.

Most related work relies on
rssi
(
rssi
) measurements from various radio systems such as WiFi
[
8
]
, Bluetooth
[
9
]
, and
uwb
[
4
]
. However,
rssi
provides limited environmental information, resulting in restricted sensing accuracy
[
10
]
. To overcome this, recent approaches use channel state information (
csi
), which offers finer-grained insights into environmental effects such as reflections, scattering, and shadowing
[
11
,
12
,
10
,
8
]
. This richer data enables higher accuracy, especially with high-bandwidth systems such as
uwb

[
13
]
. For passive localization, analytic, i.e., non-data-driven methods extract reflections from a target, e.g., a person or automated guided vehicle
[
14
,
15
]
, or model the target’s impact on multipath propagation
[
16
]
. However, these methods typically require
los
(
los
) or detailed environmental models, limiting their applicability in cluttered or complex spaces. To enable localization in
nlos
(
nlos
) environments,
fp
(
fp
) can be used
[
17
]
. Unlike analytic methods,
fp
maps recorded
csi
directly to positions, making it robust to
nlos
conditions and environmental complexity. However,
fp
requires costly data acquisition with reference positions
[
18
]
. Environmental changes, e.g., movement of large objects, invalidate fingerprints, require updated reference data, and raise lifecycle management costs
[
19
]
.
Recently, the concept of
cc
(
cc
) was proposed to reduce or eliminate the need for reference data
[
20
]
, enabling simpler deployment and easier updates of
fp
models.
cc
models the manifold of
csi
and maps it to 2D coordinates using only radio signals, either in a self-supervised manner
[
21
]
or with additional context, such as physical motion assumptions
[
22
]
or low-cost sensor data
[
23
]
. However, prior work only applied
cc
to active localization with mobile transmitters.

Alongside Euchner et al.
[
24
]
, we propose the concept of
pcc
(
pcc
) for passive localization. While they apply
pcc
using a distributed Wi-Fi multi-antenna setup, we focus on a fully connected UWB mesh. Our approach localizes a mobile target based on its influence on environmental reflections and direct signal reflections. Due to the smooth movement of the target, the resulting variations in the radio signals remain smooth, allowing us to model the
csi
manifold and project it into spatial coordinates, analogous to active
cc
.
We implement velocity-based
cc

[
23
]
that estimates the target’s velocity using a
pdr
(
pdr
) system. Our experiments demonstrate that we achieve high localization accuracy, with a mean absolute error (MAE) of up to 0.24 m, even when training and testing on different individual targets. We also study the mesh configuration to provide insights for optimal system design.

II
Related Work

Active localization algorithms
such as classical Round-Trip Time (RTT) and Time Difference of Arrival (TDoA) multilateration
[
25
,
26
]
perform worse than
fp
methods in multipath-rich environments
[
27
]
. However,
fp
methods need
csi
databases with reference positions, which increase costs
[
28
]
. Unsupervised approaches solve this problem and remove the need for labeled training data
[
29
]
. Studer et al.
[
20
]
introduce
cc
as an unsupervised alternative to traditional
fp
.
cc
models the
csi
manifold and maps it to 2D coordinates through manifold-preserving dimensionality reduction. This enables fully unsupervised
fp
and captures environmental reflections. Many
cc
methods use distance matrices to represent spatial relationships in
csi
data
[
30
,
31
]
. Although
cc
lacks global consistency, Stahlke et al.
[
21
]
achieve it with geodesic distances derived from
csi
. Self-supervised
cc
reaches moderate localization accuracy
[
20
,
32
]
, but additional sensor data improves results. Low-cost sensors such as IMU or odometry estimate relative velocities. They provide inter-sample distances and help
cc
match the accuracy of supervised fingerprinting
[
23
]
.

Passive localization algorithms
locate people by using signal reflections and geometric models. Sensor noise reduces positioning accuracy. Zhang et al.
[
33
]
study geometric methods that reduce the effect of noise from a WiFi RSSI sensor array in
dfl
(
dfl
). Their methods support both single- and multi-target tracking. Ledergerber et al.
[
34
]
use high-bandwidth UWB systems to detect reflections and measure delays in CIR for outdoor tracking. Bocus et al.
[
15
]
adapt this to indoor environments. They remove clutter to isolate reflections from a target. Gentner et al.
[
16
]
use environmental reflections, assign
mpc
to virtual anchors, and solve localization with geometric models. These methods often require
los
or detailed maps, which limits their use in cluttered areas. Zhang et al.
[
35
]
improve accuracy in complex environments. They divide space based on statistical features, train local
dfl
models, and merge them with a variance-constrained method. Rao et al.
[
18
]
propose a low-complexity method. They use calibrated
csi
phases from a single link to run
dfl
and reduce hardware costs. Their approach depends on earlier phase calibration
[
36
]
and manual tuning. Recent unsupervised
dfl
methods use probabilistic RSSI models to estimate presence and position. Al-Husseiny et al.
[
37
]
apply a hidden Markov model. Hillyard et al.
[
38
]
extend this with a Bayesian method that adapts to changes. Kaltiokallio et al.
[
39
]
use Gaussian smoothing to increase accuracy and efficiency. These methods rely on iterative inference and do not scale well. They also depend on
los

rssi
links, which require dense sensor deployments and remain vulnerable to
mpc
interference.

Unlike all existing approaches, we draw inspiration from
cc
to achieve accurate localization of moving people by combining radio
csi
with additional information such as odometry measurements, and we integrate these ideas with the concept of passive localization.

III
Methodology

csi
dependents on the surrounding environment due to effects such as reflections, scattering, and blockage, and it changes smoothly w.r.t. the movement of the transmitter. As a result, the radio signals lie on a low-dimensional manifold corresponding to positions in space.
cc
leverages this property to create a mapping from high-dimensional
csi
data to spatial coordinates
[
20
]
. However, existing
cc
approaches have only been applied in active localization scenarios, where a target carries a transmitting device.

In passive localization, a target does not carry any active transmitter. Instead, the
csi
varies due to the target’s influence on the radio propagation, either by blocking existing paths or acting as a reflector, thereby affecting the signals in the environment. Due to the target’s smooth movement, the
csi
recorded by the static infrastructure also changes smoothly. Hence, as in active
cc
, the
csi
in passive setups lies on a spatial manifold. We extend this concept by projecting the manifold of static
csi
measurements to spatial positions, an approach we refer to as passive channel charting (PCC).

Fig.
1
illustrates the end-to-end positioning pipeline of our PCC method. We deploy a static UWB mesh in the environment, while a target moves within it, influencing the radio signals by blocking and reflecting them. Most
cc
algorithms estimate pairwise distances between measurements and learn a 2D embedding that preserves those distances. Following the approach of Stahlke et al.
[
23
]
, we estimate a distance matrix based on the target’s velocity, which we obtain using a
pdr
system attached via an
imu
(
imu
)
[
40
]
. We compute the distance between measurements by integrating the velocity over time:

d_{n,k}=\left\|\int_{t=t_{n}}^{t_{k}}v(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right\|_{2}\;,

(1)

where
v(t)
is the estimated velocity of the target at time
t
and
d_{n,k}
the distance between measurements
n
and
k
. To reduce the impact of
pdr
drift, we limit the integration window, resulting in a sparse distance matrix.

To learn the underlying manifold, we use a Siamese neural network
[
41
]
that embeds high-dimensional
csi
into 2D coordinates while preserving the estimated distances. The loss function for training is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathsf{d}}=\beta(d_{n,k}-\|f_{\theta}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}_{n})-f_{\theta}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle x$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle x$}}}_{k})\|_{2})^{2},

(2)

where
f_{\theta}(\cdot)
is the neural network parameterized by
\theta
,
\textstyle x
represents the input
cir
(
cir
) vectors,
||\cdot||_{2}
is the Euclidean distance, and
\beta
is a weighting factor. Shorter distances are weighted more heavily to account for increasing
pdr
drift over longer time spans. As the learned embedding preserves only relative distances, we transform the resulting local
cc
coordinate frame to the global coordinate frame using a small set of
csi
samples with known positions. Similar to
[
21
]
, we estimate
cc
coordinates for these reference samples and compute a linear transformation to the global positions via least squares estimation.

IV
Experimental Setup

This Section describes the
uwb
mesh (Sec.
IV-A
), the
pdr
system (Sec.
IV-B
), the Siamese Network configuration (Sec.
IV-C
), and the datasets (Sec.
IV-D
).

IV-A

uwb
Mesh

The system operates at a center frequency of 4.5 GHz (bandwidth = 499.99 MHz) with 6 DW1000-based nodes forming a mesh network. 4 nodes (0–3) act as transceivers, and 2 nodes (4, 5) act as receivers, forming a multi-static radar setup with 14 total links. A
tdma
(
tdma
) protocol assigns non-overlapping time slots for transmissions. The system records the
cir
for each link and limits it to 90
\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
(90 samples at 1
\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}
resolution), covering about 30 m of multipath propagation. The system records CIRs at 40
\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
.

IV-B

pdr

An IMU (XSense MTI 300) attached to the foot estimates a person’s velocity. We apply the Gaitmap toolset
[
42
]
for velocity estimation. As the IMU is mounted in a random orientation, we align roll and pitch using gravity and correct yaw with a principal component analysis. For trajectory estimation, we use angular velocity, acceleration, and magnetic field. We apply an Error-State Kalman Filter
[
43
]
together with Rauch-Tung-Striebel smoothing
[
44
]
to estimate the target’s velocity.

IV-C

Siamese network architecture

We use a ResNet-based Siamese network with 5.6 M parameters. Tab.
I
lists the full configuration, that includes all UWB links, i.e., number of antennas. The input dimensions vary depending on the setup. We start with a 1
\times
1 CNN layer to expand the channel dimension from 1 to 64. We then apply 9 ResNet blocks. After each ResNet block group (3 blocks), the network doubles the channel dimension and reduces the time and antenna dimensions with a stride of 2. Each CNN layer uses a kernel size of 3 and padding of 1. Global average pooling follows across the antenna and time dimensions, leaving a 256-dimensional channel vector. 2 linear layers with ReLU reduce this to the 2D target position.

V
Evaluation

We evaluate our
pdr
-based
pcc
for different
pdr
window sizes (Sec.
V-A
) and analyze how the mesh composition affects localization accuracy (Sec.
V-B
).

As an upper bound, we compare our method to supervised
fp
[
17
]
. The
fp
model uses the same Siamese network architecture (Sec.
IV-C
) and directly fits the output to the reference positions of the
csi
values using
mse
(
mse
) loss. We measure accuracy using
mae
(
mae
) and the 90th percentile of the error distribution (CE90), where the error is the Euclidean distance between the predicted and reference positions. Each model trains for 500 epochs using the AdamW optimizer
[
45
]
with a learning rate of 0.0001 and a batch size of 128. A learning rate scheduler reduces the learning rate by factor 2 if the training does not improve for 50 consecutive epochs. We train the models on data from 1 target and test on 2 others (P1, P2) to evaluate generalization. We fit a linear transformation to map the 2D manifold of the
pcc
method into absolute coordinates, for which we use 20 reference positions from the training set.

V-A

pdr
-based
pcc

We evaluate the
pdr
-based
pcc
for different window sizes on the fully connected UWB mesh, where all node links contribute to
pcc
. Tab.
II
shows the results; Fig.
3

Supervised FP achieves highest accuracy: CE90 = 0.31 m and MAE = 0.17 m for P1; CE90 = 0.23 m and MAE = 0.13 m for P2. The small 0.08 m gap between P1 and P2 shows strong generalization.
pcc
shows a similar pattern, with an average error difference of 0.07 m across window sizes, enabling target-independent localization.

pcc
reaches its best accuracy at a 40 s window: CE90 = 0.43 m and MAE = 0.21 m for P1; CE90 = 0.37 m and MAE = 0.26 m for P2. This results in a CE90 difference of 0.12 m for P1 and 0.14 m for P2 compared to supervised FP. Window size strongly impacts accuracy. Small windows (1–2 s) produce poor results: CE90 = 2.11 m, MAE = 1.43 m for P1; CE90 = 2.21 m,
mae
= 1.29 m for P2.

Fig.
4

The 2 s chart fails to recover the global geometry, though it preserves local structure. This matches prior findings
[
23

Overall, a trade-off exists between hardware cost and system complexity. A single-transmitter setup reduces synchronization effort and avoids

VI
Conclusion

To enable passive localization of moving people with an
uwb
mesh, we propose
pcc
, which builds
fp
models using only a few reference positions. Our
pdr
-based
pcc
approach achieves high accuracy, with CE90 up to 0.50 m on unknown targets, showing strong generalization.

We evaluated different
uwb
configurations, varying the number of nodes and their connections. We found that spatial distribution is as important as the number of links, creating a trade-off between hardware cost and link budget.
