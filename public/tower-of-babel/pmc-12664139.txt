Brown adipose tissue activation and cardiovascular risk following PD-1 antibody therapy in cancer patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Bingqian Lin, Duanyu Lin, Xiaodan Ou, Jinwen Wang, Shun Yu, Gan Chen, Junping Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate whether PD-1 antibody therapy modulates brown adipose tissue (BAT) activity and whether BAT activation is associated with cardiovascular risk parameters in cancer patients.Methods designRetrospective cohort study.SettingTwo tertiary hospitals in Fujian Province, China (Fujian Provincial Hospital and Fujian Cancer Hospital), between January 2019 and January 2024.ParticipantsSixty-eight cancer patients (54 males, 14 females) who received PD-1 antibody therapy (e.g., pembrolizumab, 200 mg Q3W) and underwent paired 18F-FDG PET/CT scans (pre-treatment and post-treatment, median interval: 3.2 months). Exclusion criteria: hypothyroidism (n = 3), severe renal insufficiency (n = 2), and incomplete PET/CT or laboratory data. Outcome measures: primary outcome: change in BAT activity (quantified by supraclavicular fossa SUVmax via PET/CT) pre- versus post-PD-1 therapy. Secondary outcomes: changes in VAT/SAT SUVmax, lipid profiles (triglycerides [TG], total cholesterol [TC], LDL-C, and HDL-C), arterial stiffness index (ASI), cardiac biomarkers (cTnT and NT-proBNP), and thyroid function (TSH, FT3, FT4).ResultsFollowing immunotherapy, BAT SUVmax exhibited a marked increase of 68.0%, whereas visceral and subcutaneous fat SUVmax rose by 29.6% and 30.8%, respectively (P < 0.001 for all). Significant dyslipidemia was observed post-treatment (P < 0.05): arterial stiffness index (ASI) increased by 17.0%, triglycerides by 22.6%, total cholesterol by 9.6%, and LDL-C by 9.7%. Notably, partial correlation analysis revealed an inverse relationship between BAT activity and ASI (r = − 0.321, P = 0.011). Multivariate linear regression identified ASI (β = − 1.208, P = 0.009) and high-density lipoprotein (β = − 1.074, P = 0.038) as independent predictors of BAT activity.ConclusionsPD-1 antibodies enhance BAT activity in cancer patients, and higher BAT activity is associated with reduced arterial stiffness. PET/CT-derived BAT SUVmax may serve as a novel biomarker for cardiovascular risk stratification during PD-1 antibody therapy.

Introduction

The development of tumor-related metabolic dysregulation represents an independent prognostic factor in cancer patients, manifesting as insulin resistance and sarcopenia in affected individuals, with TME-driven metabolic reprogramming serving as the principal underlying mechanism [1–4]. The TME, characterized by hypoxia, acidosis, and increased vascular permeability, forces tumor cells to rewire nutrient acquisition to meet bioenergetic demands, further exacerbating systemic metabolic dysfunction [5]. To survive in hypoxic and nutrient-deprived microenvironments, tumor cells reprogram their metabolic pathways to meet bioenergetic, biosynthetic, and redox requirements. This process, known as metabolic reprogramming, is considered a hallmark of cancer. It contributes to insulin resistance [6], with a particularly high prevalence in pancreatic and liver cancer cases [7]. Impaired browning of white adipose tissue (WAT) leads to elevated serum free fatty acid (FFA) levels [8], whereas protein dysregulation contributes to sarcopenia [9]. Meanwhile, immunotherapy has emerged as a clinically important modality in tumor treatment. Research has recently shifted its focus toward understanding how anti-tumor immunotherapy influences metabolic remodeling in cancer patients. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibodies, have revolutionized the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer, melanoma, and esophageal cancer [10]. However, their impact on metabolic remodeling in patients remains controversial. Existing evidence suggests PD-1 antibodies inhibit STAT3-driven fatty acid oxidation (FAO) in T cells, leading to elevated serum cholesterol and triglycerides—key risk factors for atherosclerosis [11]. Despite this, the effect of PD-1 antibodies on adipose tissue subpopulations (e.g., brown adipose tissue [BAT]) and subsequent metabolic cascades remains unexplored.

Brown adipose tissue (BAT) is a specialized adipose tissue that mediates thermogenesis via UCP1, and its activation is associated with improved insulin sensitivity and reduced atherosclerotic risk [12–16]. Studies demonstrate that cold exposure enhances cholesterol metabolism in hypercholesterolemic patients [17]. In the context of cancer, suppressed BAT activity is observed in individuals with tumors, whereas BAT hyperactivation is implicated in the induction of cancer cachexia [18]. Imaging investigations have demonstrated that programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) is predominantly expressed in murine BAT, not in WAT [19, 20], indicating a potential link between PD-1/PD-L1 signaling and BAT regulation. However, the current literature still lacks a systematic comparative analysis of PD-L1 expression across different adipose tissue subtypes (brown, white, and visceral adipose tissue). This knowledge gap limits our in-depth understanding of its tissue-specific regulatory functions. Furthermore, PD-1 antibody administration may modulate BAT functionality. Nevertheless, no prior research has directly examined the effect of PD-1 antibody dosing on BAT. Thus, we hypothesize that a key mechanism by which PD-1 antibodies influence metabolic remodeling is through the regulation of BAT activation kinetics.

Cardiovascular risk represents a critical comorbidity in cancer patients and recipients of immunotherapy. Substantial clinical evidence confirms that ICI-induced cardiovascular events, such as myocarditis, pericarditis, heart failure, and accelerated atherosclerosis, are implicated in elevated non-cancer-related mortality, both directly and indirectly via mechanisms, such as treatment interruption and worsening comorbid conditions. According to the largest study to date on immune-related cardiovascular adverse events [21, 22], patients treated with ICIs showed an 11-fold increased incidence of myocarditis compared with untreated controls. Animal studies have further confirmed that activation of BAT attenuates the progression of atherosclerosis [16], highlighting the therapeutic potential of BAT activation in combating this condition. Furthermore, a central objective of this study is to investigate the impact of PD-1 antibodies on arterial stiffness, as measured by the arterial stiffness index (ASI). ASI is calculated as [Total cholesterol (TC)-HDL-C]/HDL-C. Values below 4 are considered normal and indicate lower arterial stiffness and reduced cerebrovascular risk, whereas values ≥ 4 confirm arterial stiffness, with higher values reflecting greater severity. Therefore, we will expand our investigation into the effects of PD-1 antibodies on BAT activation and metabolic remodeling to include analyses of associations with cardiovascular events. This approach will help elucidate the mechanisms through which PD-1 antibodies modulate cardiovascular outcomes via the regulation of BAT activity.

In this study, we hypothesized that PD-1 antibodies regulate BAT activity, thereby influencing metabolic parameters and arterial stiffness. This retrospective cohort study aimed to: (1) quantify changes in BAT/VAT/SAT activity post-PD-1 therapy via PET/CT; (2) explore associations between BAT activation, lipid profiles, and arterial stiffness; and (3) identify predictors of BAT activity to inform cardiovascular risk stratification.

Methods

Patient selection criteria

This retrospective study enrolled cancer patients who received PD-1 antibodies at Fujian Provincial Hospital and Fujian Cancer Hospital between January 2019 and January 2024, as detailed in Fig. 1. All patients underwent at least one positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) scan before and after PD-1 antibody treatment. We analyzed demographic characteristics and laboratory results. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital (K2025-07-006, July 16, 2025). All patients were informed that consent to participate in the study and publish their data would be assumed on completion and submission of the study survey. In addition, since the study was retrospective, the data will be published anonymously.Fig. 1Flowchart of the enrollment of this study

Flowchart of the enrollment of this study

18F-FDG PET/CT analysis

After fasting > 4 h, patients received intravenous injection of 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG; radiochemical purity > 95%) at 3.7 MBq/kg. Patients then rested supine for 60 ± 15 min in a temperature-controlled room (20–25 °C). Whole-body PET/CT scans (vertex to mid-thigh) were acquired, followed by image reconstruction using CT-based attenuation correction and an ordered-subsets expectation maximization algorithm. Two blinded nuclear medicine physicians independently evaluated PET images. Maximum standardized uptake values (SUVmax) were measured at three sites: supraclavicular fossa (brown adipose tissue), perirenal area (visceral adipose tissue, VAT), and lumbosacral region (subcutaneous adipose tissue, SAT) using semi-quantitative analysis. Examination dates and corresponding daily mean outdoor temperatures were obtained from the China Meteorological Administration.

Clinical parameters

Following an 8-h overnight fasting period, peripheral venous blood samples were obtained under standardized conditions. Comprehensive biochemical analyses were performed, including quantification of: fasting plasma glucose (FPG), lipid profile parameters (triglycerides [TG], total cholesterol [TC], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]), thyroid function markers (thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH], free triiodothyronine [FT3], and free thyroxine [FT4]), and cardiovascular biomarkers (cardiac troponin T [cTnT] and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide [NT-proBNP]). Baseline demographic characteristics (sex and age) were documented, and anthropometric parameters (height and weight) were measured using calibrated instruments. Body mass index (BMI) was derived using the standard formula (weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp). Continuous variables underwent normality testing with Shapiro–Wilk tests. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t tests; non-normally distributed data are expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) with Mann–Whitney U tests for group comparisons. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies (%) and analyzed with χ2 tests. Non-normally distributed variables were log10-transformed for parametric analysis. Partial correlation analyses (adjusting for age and BMI) assessed relationships between SUVmax (brown, visceral, subcutaneous fat) and metabolic parameters. Multivariate linear regression identified independent predictors of BAT SUVmax. A two-sided P < 0.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

Between January 2019 and January 2024, a total of 68 cancer patients treated with PD-1 antibodies met the inclusion criteria. The cohort comprised of 54 males (79.4%) and 14 females (20.6%). Baseline metabolic parameters (presented as mean ± standard deviation) were: BAT SUVmax: 1.40 ± 0.40; visceral fat SUVmax: 0.98 ± 0.32; Subcutaneous fat SUVmax: 0.41 ± 0.12; arterial stiffness index (ASI): 3.35 ± 1.05 (Table 1). Treatment with PD-1 antibodies also significantly affected metabolic and cardiovascular parameters. The arterial stiffness index increased from 3.35 ± 1.05 to 3.92 ± 1.39 (P < 0.001), while lipid profiles showed elevated triglycerides (1.46 ± 0.97–1.79 ± 1.20 mmol/L, P < 0.05), total cholesterol (4.80 ± 1.17–5.26 ± 1.41 mmol/L, P < 0.05), and LDL-C (3.19 ± 0.85–3.50 ± 1.08 mmol/L, P < 0.05). In addition, significant increases were observed in cardiac troponin T (6.71 ± 6.46–8.58 ± 9.34 μg/L, P < 0.05) and TSH levels (3.31 ± 6.60–10.76 ± 28.70 mIU/L, P < 0.05), indicating systemic metabolic and endocrine changes post-treatment (Table 2).

Correlation analysis between SUVmax and blood lipid parameters

Variables showing significant associations (P < 0.05) in univariate analyses were included in further statistical modeling. To account for potential confounding factors, partial correlation analyses were conducted with adjustments for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), ambient temperature, and lipid profile parameters known to influence arterial stiffness indices.

Prior to PD-1 antibody therapy, BAT metabolic activity exhibited significant inverse correlations with both venous plasma glucose levels (r = − 0.301, P = 0.016) and free thyroxine (FT4) concentrations (r = − 0.293, P = 0.031). After treatment, the correlation pattern changed notably: BAT activity was negatively correlated with arterial stiffness index (r = − 0.321, P = 0.011) and positively correlated with free triiodothyronine (FT3) levels (r = 0.379, P = 0.003) (Table 3; Fig. 3). Correlation between Arterial Stiffness Index (ASI) and brown adipose tissue (BAT) SUVmax in cancer patients receiving PD-1 antibody treatment

All significant variables from the partial correlation analyses were incorporated into multivariable linear regression models, with BAT SUVmax serving as the primary outcome. The final adjusted model for the post-treatment group incorporated comprehensive covariates, including sex, age, ambient temperature, BMI, arterial stiffness index, and comprehensive lipid parameters (triglycerides [TG], total cholesterol [TC], HDL-C, and LDL-C). This robust analysis identified arterial stiffness index (β = − 1.208, P = 0.009) and HDL-C concentration (β = − 1.074, P = 0.038) as statistically significant independent determinants of BAT metabolic activity (P < 0.05 for both).

Correlation analysis between visceral adipose tissue SUVmax and blood lipid parameters

Before treatment, no significant correlations were observed between visceral adipose tissue (VAT) metabolic activity and ASI or any lipid parameters (TG, TC, HDL-C, or LDL-C; all P > 0.05). However, after PD-1 antibody administration, VAT activity was inversely correlated with free thyroxine (FT4; r = − 0.262, P = 0.047), but remained unrelated to ASI or lipid measures (P > 0.05; Table 4). Discussion

In contemporary oncology, PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies serve as cornerstone immune checkpoint blockade therapies However, treatment-induced dyslipidemia has emerged as a clinically significant concern. Although limited research has specifically addressed the metabolic effects of PD-1 antibodies, existing evidence suggests that signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) on T-cell surfaces promotes obesity progression. PD-1 antibodies inhibit PD-1/STAT3 signaling in T cells, which reduces STAT3-mediated fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and suppresses lipid catabolism, ultimately leading to elevated serum cholesterol levels [23]. Consistent with these findings, clinical studies have reported increased levels of triglycerides (TG), cholesterol (CHO), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) following PD-1 antibody administration. Therefore, routine monitoring of lipid metabolism is recommended for patients undergoing immunotherapy, and combination therapy with statins should be considered to improve therapeutic outcomes.

In mammalian systems, WAT represents the predominant form of adipose tissue and is responsible for leptin synthesis and secretion, in addition to expressing programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). Furthermore, growing evidence supports the expression of PD-L1 in BAT. This is corroborated by antigen blockade studies, which showed markedly reduced or absent radiotracer uptake in BAT of PD-L1-deficient mice [20]. Flow cytometric analysis revealed highest PD-L1 expression in CD45⁺ leukocytes but minimal in adipocytes from wild-type (WT) versus PD-L1-deficient mice [20]. Interscapular BAT injection in PD-L1 knockout (KO) mice yielded no significant PET/CT signal after 2 h [19]. Collectively, these findings indicate that PD-L1 enhances BAT activation.

Based on the findings of this study, we propose a potential mechanism, whereby PD-1 antibodies may influence BAT function by modulating the immune microenvironment within adipose tissue. Specifically, PD-1 antibodies likely disrupt the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling axis, thereby altering the infiltration and functional states of immune cells—such as macrophages and T lymphocytes—in adipose depots. This immune remodeling subsequently modulates the local cytokine milieu, ultimately impacting UCP1 expression and mitochondrial activity in BAT. These insights deepen our theoretical understanding of immuno-metabolic crosstalk and offer a novel perspective on how immune checkpoint inhibitors systemically regulate metabolic homeostasis.

However, direct evidence is lacking to support the modulation of BAT by PD-1 antibodies. Mechanistically, PD-1 antibodies bind PD-1 to disrupt PD-1/PD-L1 interactions, while PD-L1 antibodies target PD-L1 directly. We, therefore, hypothesize that PD-1 antibodies may promote BAT activation by counteracting PD-L1-mediated signaling blockade. Alternatively, ICI therapy may potentiate BAT activation through immune system disinhibition, which alters cytokine release profiles (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α). These changes may indirectly modulate central sympathetic tone or directly target adrenergic receptors on adipocytes.

This retrospective study included 68 cancer patients treated with programmed cell death protein 1 antibodies (PD-1 abs). We implemented a standardized BAT activity quantification protocol to ensure methodological rigor and clinical translational value. Key pre-scanning parameters—including ambient temperature (19–22 °C), fasting duration (> 6 h), and glycemic status—were rigorously controlled. The protocol specifically establishes two pivotal assessment timepoints: pre-treatment baseline and early therapy phase (4 weeks). This standardized framework provides a robust methodological foundation for dynamic BAT activity monitoring in future clinical applications. Maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) was measured by PET/CT in the supraclavicular, perirenal, and lumbogluteal subcutaneous adipose regions to assess the activity of brown adipose tissue (BAT), visceral adipose tissue (VAT), and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT). Significantly increased metabolic activity was observed in BAT, VAT, and SAT compared to baseline (all P < 0.05). A hypothesis is advanced that BAT activation occurs prior to changes in VAT and SAT activity, a sequence that can be demonstrated through longitudinal metabolic imaging. Previous studies have established associations between BAT activity and factors, such as age, body mass index (BMI), temperature, sex, and sympathetic nervous activity [13, 14, 24–30]. Multivariate linear regression identified atherogenic index (AI) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels as independent determinants of BAT activity. Located at 23°30′–28°22′N and 115°50′–120°40′E, Fujian Province has a subtropical monsoon climate characterized by long summers and mild winters. No significant difference in temperature (P > 0.05) was observed between PET/CT scanning sessions, which precluded meaningful analysis of thermal effects on BAT activity.

However, several case reports have described acquired generalized lipodystrophy (AGL) in patients following PD-1 antibody therapy. AGL is a rare disorder characterized by the loss of subcutaneous adipose tissue. This seems contradictory to the enhanced adipose tissue activity observed in this study. Although the mechanism underlying adipocyte destruction in AGL remains unclear, one case study proposed that PD-1 antibodies may break immune tolerance, leading to aberrant targeting of adipocytes, which in turn causes adipokine dysregulation and lipodystrophic manifestations [31]. It is, therefore, hypothesized that PD-1 antibodies might mediate dichotomous effects via different immune cell subsets: some subsets may induce adipocyte apoptosis and lead to white adipose tissue (WAT) atrophy, while others could promote brown adipose tissue (BAT) activation.

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality. Hyperlipidemia is a well-established risk factor for atherosclerosis, which itself underlies most cardiovascular pathologies. As a chronic inflammatory disease, atherosclerosis substantially contributes to mortality from coronary heart disease, with major risk factors, including hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, and smoking. In this study, the arterial stiffness index (ASI)—an internationally validated predictor of cardiovascular risk—was used to evaluate arterial rigidity. Although ASI typically increases with age, we observed a notable increase in ASI following immunotherapy in cancer patients. Importantly, partial correlation analysis revealed a significant inverse relationship between ASI and BAT activity (r = − 0.321, P = 0.011), while no such association was found with subcutaneous or visceral adipose tissue activity.

The central innovation of this study lies in the first systematic demonstration of PD-1 antibody-mediated activation of BAT within a clinical cohort while establishing an inverse correlation between BAT activity and arterial stiffness improvement. The primary contribution of this finding is the identification of a potential novel biomarker (BAT SUVmax) for early monitoring and stratification of immunotherapy-associated cardiovascular risk.

From a clinical perspective, our findings highlight the significant value of assessing BAT activity in patients receiving PD-1 antibody therapy. Future integration of PET/CT-derived BAT parameters with established cardiovascular risk scoring systems could enable the development of more precise risk prediction models, thereby informing personalized treatment strategies.

These results suggest that BAT activity, as assessed via whole-body PET/CT, may serve as a useful indicator for cardiovascular risk stratification and could inform anti-atherosclerotic treatment strategies, ultimately improving clinical outcomes. The application of BAT SUVmax as a biomarker in clinical practice not only enables the identification of patients with low baseline BAT activity prior to ICI therapy—who may benefit from intensified cardiovascular monitoring—but also suggests that longitudinal tracking of BAT activity dynamics through serial PET/CT scans may more effectively predict long-term cardiovascular risk progression and therapeutic efficacy. This approach will constitute a primary focus of our future research.

Furthermore, we explored potential mechanisms through which PD-1 antibodies may induce hypermetabolism in visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue (VAT/SAT). Similar to BAT regulation, this effect could be mediated via modulation of immune cell infiltration within adipose tissues or through systemic factors indirectly promoting white adipose tissue browning. Clinically, enhanced VAT/SAT metabolic activity may confer benefits such as improved systemic insulin sensitivity and synergistic effects with BAT activation. However, the long-term implications of altered lipid storage and redistribution warrant careful monitoring, particularly regarding potential dysregulation of adipokine secretion or paradoxical exacerbation of metabolic syndrome components in susceptible populations.

These finding gains further significance in light of previous work by Gotsman et al. [32], who demonstrated exacerbated atherosclerotic lesions in PD-L1 knockout mice, indicating that the PD-1/PD-L1 axis serves an atheroprotective role. Consequently, blockade of this pathway by PD-1 antibody therapy may heighten atherosclerotic risk.

Given these concerns, novel therapeutic approaches to mitigate atherosclerosis and prevent cardiovascular events are urgently needed. Both murine models [33, 34] and clinical studies [35–37] have confirmed the lipid-lowering capabilities of BAT, which reduces plasma triglycerides through enhanced fatty acid oxidation, thereby ameliorating obesity-related metabolic disorders. For example, Berbée et al. [16] showed that BAT activation via β3-adrenergic receptor stimulation protects hyperlipidemic mice from atherosclerotic damage. However, conflicting evidence exists, as Dong et al. [21] reported that BAT activation may not favorably influence cholesterol metabolism or atherosclerosis progression. Our results, showing a negative correlation between BAT activity and ASI, support the hypothesis that BAT activation may suppress atherosclerosis development.

Future research directions should prioritize validating the predictive value of BAT activity through prospective cohort studies and utilizing animal models to elucidate the specific molecular pathways through which PD-1 antibodies modulate BAT function. Concurrently, developing non-imaging-based methodologies for assessing BAT activity would significantly advance its clinical applicability. It must be emphasized that the observational nature of this study imposes inherent limitations, and the observed associations require further experimental validation.

A limitation of this study is the potential underestimation of BAT metabolic activity by PET/CT, highlighting the need for complementary biomarkers such as UCP1 expression in future studies and atherosclerotic risk.

Conclusions

PD-1 antibody therapy enhances BAT activity in cancer patients, and higher BAT activity is associated with reduced arterial stiffness. PET/CT-derived BAT SUVmax may serve as a novel biomarker for cardiovascular risk stratification during immunotherapy. Future prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and explore BAT-targeted interventions to improve cardiovascular outcomes in ICI-treated patients.
