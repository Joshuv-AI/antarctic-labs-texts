Home‐Based Opportunities and Self‐Determination in Youth: When Intellectual Disability Shapes the Impact of Context
Authors: Araceli Arellano, Cristina Cañete‐Massé, Clara Andrés‐Gárriz, Cristina Mumbardó‐Adam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHome is a key context to foster self‐determination. This study examined whether intellectual disability moderates the relationship between home‐based opportunities to engage in self‐determined actions and self‐determination levels.MethodMiddle and high school students with and without intellectual disabilities (aged 13–22) completed the AIR‐S questionnaire, which evaluates self‐determination capacity and opportunities at home. A moderation model was used to examine the relationship between home opportunities and self‐determination, as well as the moderating role of intellectual disability.ResultsA positive association was found between home‐based opportunities and self‐determination. Although intellectual disability was not a significant direct predictor of self‐determination, once opportunities were considered, it significantly moderated this relationship, indicating that the positive association between opportunities at home and self‐determination became progressively stronger as levels of intellectual disability increased.ConclusionsHome‐based opportunities play a critical role in fostering self‐determination. Intellectual disability conditions how strongly supportive environments translate into self‐determined actions.Keywords: home context, intellectual disability, opportunities, self‐determination, youth

According to Causal Agency Theory, individuals who are self‐determined—acting as causal agents—pursue goals that are freely chosen and their actions serve to reinforce and sustain their role as the primary agents of their own lives (Shogren, Wehmeyer, Palmer, Forber‐Pratt, et al. 2015; Shogren and Raley 2022). Self‐determination is defined by three essential characteristics: volitional action, agentic action and action‐control beliefs. Volitional actions refer to intentionally initiating behaviour based on personal preferences; agency involves purposefully directing actions to achieve specific goals; and action‐control beliefs encompass the confidence in one's ability and effort to successfully reach those goals (Shogren, Wehmeyer, Palmer, Forber‐Pratt, et al. 2015; Wehmeyer 2004). These characteristics are expressed through a range of interrelated skills—such as expressing preferences, making choices, solving problems, making decisions, setting and attaining goals, self‐regulating actions, self‐advocating, developing self‐awareness and self‐knowledge—which can be intentionally promoted through instruction and environmental support. Self‐determination has been identified as a critical developmental outcome across the life span, supporting identity formation, psychological well‐being, successful life transitions and overall quality of life (Deci and Ryan 2000; Wehmeyer et al. 2017; Lachapelle et al. 2005). Although research over the past decades has been largely focused on the field of disability, the theoretical frameworks and instructional approaches developed in this field are intended to be applicable to the general population, extending beyond disability status (Shogren et al. 2006).

Rather than being a fixed personality trait, self‐determination is increasingly understood as a dynamic and learnable goal that emerges through ongoing interactions between individual capacities and environmental opportunities. The socioecological model and more recent frameworks, such as the Shared Citizens Paradigm, emphasize that self‐determination should be understood as the product of multiple, interdependent factors rather than as an isolated individual attribute (Shogren et al. 2025). From this standpoint, personal characteristics—including cognitive abilities, motivation and perceived self‐efficacy—are expressed and developed through access to meaningful opportunities in everyday contexts across the lifespan. This interactional perspective is especially relevant when examining self‐determination in youth with intellectual disability. Despite the well‐documented benefits of self‐determination for adult outcomes—such as financial independence, employment and independent living (Wehmeyer and Palmer 2003; Martorell et al. 2008; Shogren, Wehmeyer, Palmer, Rifenbark, and Little 2015; Powers et al. 2012)—adolescents and young adults with intellectual disability consistently report lower levels of self‐determination than their peers without disabilities (Wehmeyer et al. 2010; Mumbardó‐Adam et al. 2017; Nota et al. 2007). While cognitive ability is relevant, it alone does not explain these disparities. Research increasingly points to socioecological barriers—including restrictive environments, overprotective parenting and low expectations—that limit opportunities to enact volitional and agentic actions (Shogren 2013; Shogren and Turnbull 2006; Stancliffe et al. 2011). In line with Self‐Determined Learning Theory (Mithaug et al. 2002), these disparities can be understood as reflecting differences in opportunities to act on existing capacities within everyday contexts (Cristescu et al. 2025; Mumbardó‐Adam et al. 2023).

Within this broader framework, the family environment emerges as a particularly influential setting for the development and expression of self‐determined behaviour during childhood and adolescence. As the primary context of daily life, the home provides essential opportunities for learning and practicing autonomy‐related skills, including decision‐making, goal‐setting and independent problem‐solving at home have been shown to significantly contribute to the development of self‐determined behaviour (Abery and Stancliffe 2003; Carter et al. 2013). Families are uniquely positioned to accompany and encourage their relatives with disabilities to practice and develop self‐determination within safe, natural and positive environments (Brotherson et al. 2008; Erwin et al. 2009; Palmer et al. 2013; Turnbull and Turnbull 2001; Zhang 2005). However, youth with intellectual disability often receive fewer such opportunities, frequently due to family concerns regarding safety, dependency or limited expectations of competence (Nota et al. 2007; Shogren and Turnbull 2006). From an ecological perspective (Bronfenbrenner 1979), opportunities for self‐determination within the family context are shaped by multiple, interacting factors, including characteristics of the family, support needs of the individual, the broader family environment or family expectations. Consistent with this, studies have identified several factors that shape parenting practices. These include personal and family characteristics, such as individual characteristics (e.g., type and severity of disability), family structure (Wehmeyer et al. 2011) and number of siblings and birth order (Vicente et al. 2018). Beyond these structural factors, parental beliefs and cultural expectations also play a central role, including the parental beliefs about stigma (Brotherson et al. 2008) and the cultural views on autonomy and interdependence (Shogren 2011; Zhang et al. 2010). Moreover, parental perceptions and expectations towards their children's development shape the opportunities and supports provided. These perceptions include perceptions of balance between individual and family quality of life (Taylor et al. 2019), expectations for the future (Holmes et al. 2018) and the importance attributed to self‐determination as a developmental goal (Arellano and Peralta 2013; Bigby et al. 2019). For instance, beliefs about the appropriate level of family involvement in decision‐making can either promote or constrain opportunities for children to act autonomously (Bigby et al. 2019; Taylor et al. 2019).

Despite growing scholarly interest, empirical studies rarely examine whether the relationship between family‐based opportunities and self‐determination operates differently depending on the presence of intellectual disability. In particular, while recent research in the Spanish context has shown that opportunities to engage in self‐determined actions at home are associated with higher levels of self‐determination (Vicente et al. 2020), it is still unclear whether the strength of this association differs according to the level of intellectual disability.

Addressing this gap requires moving beyond general comparisons to explicitly examine whether intellectual disability moderates the relationship between home‐based opportunities and self‐determination outcomes. Youth with intellectual disability—particularly those with higher support needs—may be more sensitive to variations in environmental supports. Examining intellectual disability as a moderator therefore allows us to extend prior mediation‐based research (Vicente et al. 2020) by focusing not only on why differences in self‐determination exist, but also on under which conditions home‐based opportunities are most impactful. By explicitly testing this moderating effect and addressing this gap, we aim to contribute to a more precise understanding of how supportive environments can be designed through a family‐centred approach that emphasizes the importance of empowering families with strategies, knowledge and resources to support their children's self‐determination.

Importantly, conceptualizing intellectual disability as a multicategorical moderator allows the examination of two related questions simultaneously: whether the association between opportunities at home and self‐determination differs between youth with and without intellectual disability and whether this association varies across levels of support needs within the intellectual disability group. From a socio‐ecological perspective, both comparisons are relevant, as contextual opportunities and environmental supports may differ not only according to disability status but also according to the intensity of support required. All in all, the research question that this study wants to address is: Does the association between opportunities at home and self‐determination vary as a function of intellectual disability status and levels of support needs? Based on previous research and the theoretical framework guiding this study, we hypothesize that opportunities at home are expected to be positively associated with self‐determination levels and that intellectual disability is expected to moderate this association, with stronger associations expected among youth with intellectual disability, particularly for those with higher support needs.

Method

Participants

Participants were intentionally recruited from schools or universities across different regions of Spain. Data was collected from 620 middle‐school and high‐school youths: 342 (55.2%) were students with intellectual disability enrolled in special education schools and 278 (44.8%) were students without intellectual disability enrolled in general education schools or universities. The demographic characteristics reported below refer to the total sample, including students with and without intellectual disability. On average, the students' ages ranged from 13 to 22 years (M = 16.86; SD = 2.06), the majority being male (58.1%). Most participants were originally from Spain (79.3%), whereas the remaining participants were from Latin American (10.8%), Eastern European (1.8%), West Asian (2.6%) and African countries (5.5%). Most of the students were enrolled in 9th (22.3%) or 10th grade (28.7%). Students enrolled in beyond compulsory education programs were either in 11th or 12th grade (5.6%), in vocational training programs (25%), universities (13.2%) or transition to adult life programs for students with disabilities (5.2%).

Additional demographic characteristics by group are reported as follows: Participants without intellectual disability had a mean age of 16.94 years (SD = 1.66), of whom 64.9% were male, whereas participants with intellectual disability had a mean age of 16.8 years (SD = 2.46), of whom 49.6% were male. Among students with intellectual disability, 119 (19.2%) had mild support needs, 149 (24%) had moderate support needs and 74 (11.9%) had severe support needs. Given the age of the participants, these diagnoses had been established prior to the publication of the DSM‐5 (American Psychiatric Association 2013) and were therefore based on the diagnostic criteria in use at the time of assessment, primarily DSM‐IV‐TR (American Psychiatric Association 2000), which primarily emphasized intellectual functioning.

Instruments

The AIR‐S measures a person's capacities and opportunities for self‐determination and is available in Student, Educator and Parent versions. For the purpose of this study, the Spanish online student version form was used (Mumbardó et al. 2018), which has 24 questions divided into two scales that gather data on the students' self‐reported capacities and opportunities to engage in self‐determined actions. The Capacity scale is in turn divided into two subscales and covers questions about the students' (1) ability related to self‐determination and (2) perceptions about performing self‐determined actions. The Opportunity scale is also composed of two subscales that measure (1) the students' perceptions of their opportunities at home to perform self‐determined actions and (2) opportunities at school. The scale was completed individually by participants using a computer or tablet. Items were presented one at a time and students responded using a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from never (1) to always (5). When needed, teachers provided support following the administration guidelines. The Opportunities at Home subscale assesses students' perceptions of the extent to which their family environment provides opportunities to engage in self‐determined actions. Example items include statements such as: ‘At home, I have learned how to make plans to meet my goals and to feel good about them’.

The AIR has been extensively used and in terms of validity, the original authors conducted a factor analysis that supported a four‐factor structure explaining 74% of the instrument variance (Mithaug et al. 2002). It has also demonstrated adequate test–retest reliability (0.74 after 3 months) and a strong internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha ranging from 0.89 to 0.99). The Spanish student version of the AIR Self‐Determination Scale has demonstrated robust psychometric properties in culturally and educationally comparable contexts. Specifically, the Spanish validation study provided evidence of adequate reliability, a well‐supported factorial structure with four first‐order and two second‐order dimensions and measurement invariance between youth with and without intellectual disability, supporting the appropriateness of this version for assessing self‐determination and contextual opportunities across diverse student populations (Mumbardó et al. 2018). In the present sample, the scale demonstrated high internal consistency overall (Cronbach's α = 0.903). Comparable reliability coefficients were observed when analyses were conducted separately by group, with Cronbach's alpha values of 0.908 for participants with intellectual disability and 0.900 for participants without intellectual disability.

Procedures

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the University of Barcelona ethics committee and all procedures were conducted in accordance with established ethical standards for research involving minors and young adults, including obtaining informed consent from participants and/or their legal guardians. General education schools, universities and special schools spread throughout Spain were intentionally contacted. To be included in this study, schools needed an Internet connection since the self‐determination survey was administered online. Out of the 48 schools contacted, 6 general education schools, 4 universities and 21 special schools agreed to participate in the study.

Regarding student selection, different procedures were followed for students with and without disabilities. Participants without intellectual disability were eligible if they were enrolled in mainstream secondary or post‐secondary education, were between 13 and 22 years of age and lived with their families at the time of data collection. Students with intellectual disability were eligible if they had a formal clinical diagnosis of intellectual disability established by accredited health or educational professionals in accordance with standardized diagnostic criteria, were between 13 and 22 years of age and lived with their families. Teachers were asked to identify students who met the predefined inclusion criteria and who, with appropriate support when needed, were expected to be able to understand and respond meaningfully to the questionnaire.

Once potential participants had been selected, consent from the parent or the participant, if they were of legal age, was obtained. Students were provided as much time as needed to complete the scale. Teachers were provided with detailed written guidelines prior to data collection specifying how to support the administration of the scale if needed. These guidelines instructed teachers to limit their role to clarifying vocabulary, explaining item meaning when requested and ensuring comprehension of the response format, while avoiding any form of guidance that could influence students' responses. The aim of these procedures was to promote as much standardization as possible across administrations. Although fidelity of implementation was not formally assessed, teachers were asked the profile of students with disabilities that had to be supported for the scale administration. A total of 163 students were given support in the scale administration, all of them with severe (n = 74) and moderate (n = 89) intellectual disability. Students could be provided with different kinds of support such as facilitating access to information (i.e., reading the questions) and understanding and interpreting the questions (i.e., giving synonyms for misunderstood words). If there were missing answers, teachers followed up with students to determine if they decided to leave the items unanswered (n = 4) or if they inadvertently skipped questions.

Data Analysis

To answer the study objectives, descriptive data were first computed to analyse the relationship between opportunities at home and self‐determination considering the levels of intellectual disability. Group differences in self‐determination across intellectual disability levels were examined within the regression framework by modelling intellectual disability as a multicategorical predictor using dummy coding, which provides inferential tests equivalent to one‐way ANOVA comparisons while allowing adjustment for covariates. Missing data were minimal (< 5%) across variables and were handled using listwise deletion. Then, a moderation model of the PROCESS Macro version 3.3 for SPSS (Hayes 2017) was performed to examine the direct relationship between opportunities to engage in self‐determined actions at home and the level of self‐determination, as well as the moderating role of having intellectual disability on this relationship. Self‐determination variable was calculated using the capacity score of the scale. Intellectual disability was modelled as a categorical moderator using dummy coding, with students without intellectual disability as the reference group. Three dummy variables (mild, moderate and severe intellectual disability) and their interactions with opportunities at home were included in the regression model. Age and gender were entered as covariates of the model to control for those variables. Standardized variables were used for this analysis. This model examines the association between opportunities at home (X) and self‐determination (Y) and tests whether this relationship varies as a function of a moderator variable (levels of intellectual disability), indicating the conditions under which the association is stronger or weaker (see Figure 1). A moderator variable M affects the strength and direction of the relationship between X and Y. Translated to the present model, a variable M is moderating the relationship of X on Y, when the effect of X on Y changes as M changes (Hayes 2017), in other words, when this effect is statistically different from 0.

The model was estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, including the interaction term between the predictor and the moderator. All reported effects are adjusted for the covariates. To obtain a robust inference, a nonparametric bootstrap procedure was applied using 5.000 resamples. Bias‐corrected 95% confidence intervals were computed for all relevant effects. Statistical significance was determined based on whether the confidence intervals excluded zero. Assumptions of linear regression were evaluated prior to the interpretation of the results. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values. Independence of errors was examined using the Durbin–Watson statistic. The normality and homoscedasticity of residuals were inspected through graphical analyses, including histograms, Q–Q plots and scatterplots of standardized residuals against predicted values. Potential multivariate outliers and influential observations were assessed using Mahalanobis distance and Cook's distance. Cases exceeding conventional thresholds were examined to determine their potential impact on the model estimates.

Visual inspection of histograms and normal probability plots indicated no substantial deviations from normality, supporting the use of regression‐based analyses. As depicted in Table 1, participants without intellectual disability had higher levels of opportunities at home than their peers with intellectual disability. However, regarding self‐determination levels, youth without intellectual disability and those with intellectual disability and mild support needs showed comparable levels of self‐determination, whereas youth with moderate and severe support needs exhibited lower levels. Although these descriptive statistics are presented to contextualize the sample, group differences in self‐determination were formally examined using regression‐based inferential analyses in which intellectual disability was modelled as a multicategorical predictor, providing statistical tests equivalent to one‐way ANOVA comparisons while allowing adjustment for covariates.

Second, before computing the moderation model, assumptions of linear regression were tested. Prerequisite analyses were conducted prior to the interpretation of the regression‐based moderation model. Assumptions of linear regression were evaluated by estimating an equivalent ordinary least squares regression model including opportunities at home, dummy‐coded intellectual disability variables, their interaction terms and covariates (age and gender). Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values. All tolerance values were above 0.40 and VIF values ranged from 1.03 to 2.35, indicating no concerns regarding multicollinearity. Independence of errors was evaluated using the Durbin–Watson statistic, which yielded a value of 2.14, falling within the acceptable range and indicating no autocorrelation among residuals. The normality and homoscedasticity of residuals were examined through graphical inspection, including histograms and normal probability (Q–Q) plots of standardized residuals, as well as scatterplots of standardized residuals against standardized predicted values. Visual inspection of these plots did not reveal substantial deviations from normality, linearity or homoscedasticity. Potential multivariate outliers and influential observations were assessed using Mahalanobis distance, Cook's distance, leverage values and standardized residuals. Eight cases exceeded conventional cut‐off values for Mahalanobis distance, indicating potential multivariate outliers. However, Cook's distance values for all cases remained well below the critical threshold (maximum Cook's D = 0.061), suggesting that none of these observations exerted undue influence on the regression coefficients or overall model fit. Examination of these cases indicated that their inclusion did not meaningfully influence parameter estimates or overall model fit and were consequently retained in the analysis. Besides, given the large sample size and the presence of interaction terms, the detection of some multivariate outliers was expected.

Finally, a moderation model was computed to respond to the research question of this study with opportunities at home to engage in self‐determined actions as the predictor (X), self‐determination levels as the outcome variable (Y), intellectual disability as the moderator (W) and age and gender as covariates (Figure 1).

Results indicated that the moderation model including intellectual disability as a multicategorical moderator was statistically significant, explaining a substantial proportion of variance in self‐determination (R 2 = 0.3328, F (9,610) = 33.81, p < 0.001). Regarding the covariates, age showed a small but statistically significant positive association with self‐determination (b = 0.0333, SE = 0.0163, t = 2.04, p = 0.041; BC CI [0.0025, 0.0645]), indicating that older participants reported slightly higher levels of self‐determination after controlling for opportunities at home, intellectual disability and the interaction terms. In contrast, gender was not significantly associated with self‐determination (b = −0.0108, SE = 0.0687, t = −0.16, p = 0.875; BC CI [−0.1455, 0.1228]). The standardized parameter estimates showed a statistically significant direct effect of opportunities at home on self‐determination (path c = 0.4262, SE = 0.0507, t = 8.41, p < 0.001), with the 95% bootstrap confidence interval not including zero (BC CI [0.3092, 0.5455]). These results indicate that greater opportunities to engage in self‐determined activities at home were associated with higher levels of self‐determination.

Regarding the main effects of intellectual disability, using students without intellectual disability as the reference group, students with mild intellectual disability showed slightly higher levels of self‐determination (b1 = 0.1827, SE = 0.0928, t = 1.97, p = 0.049), with a bootstrap confidence interval marginally excluding zero (BC CI [0.0102, 0.3486]). No statistically significant differences were observed for students with moderate intellectual disability (b2 = 0.0351, SE = 0.0843, t = 0.42, p = 0.677), as the bootstrap confidence interval included zero (BC CI [−0.1234, 0.1950]). In contrast, students with severe intellectual disability showed significantly lower levels of self‐determination compared to the reference group (b3 = −0.2237, SE = 0.1084, t = −2.06, p = 0.039), with a bootstrap confidence interval largely below zero (BC CI [−0.4648, 0.0122]).

Importantly, the interaction between opportunities at home and intellectual disability was statistically significant overall (ΔR 2 = 0.0215, F (3,610) = 6.54, p < 0.001), confirming the presence of a moderation effect. Examination of the interaction terms revealed that the moderation effect was not statistically significant for students with mild intellectual disability (b1 = 0.0636, SE = 0.0851, t = 0.75, p = 0.455; BC CI [−0.1523, 0.2977]). However, statistically significant moderated effects were observed for students with moderate intellectual disability (b2 = 0.3055, SE = 0.0901, t = 3.39, p = 0.001), with a positive bootstrap confidence interval (BC CI [0.1162, 0.4989]) and for students with severe intellectual disability (b3 = 0.3843, SE = 0.1113, t = 3.45, p = 0.001), also supported by a bootstrap confidence interval excluding zero (BC CI [0.1167, 0.6635]).

Conditional effects analyses further illustrated this moderating pattern. Opportunities at home were positively associated with self‐determination across all groups; however, the strength of this association increased with the severity of intellectual disability. Specifically, the effect of opportunities at home on self‐determination was significant for students without intellectual disability (b0 = 0.4262, SE = 0.0507, p < 0.001; BC CI [0.3092, 0.5455]), increased for students with mild intellectual disability (b1 = 0.4898, SE = 0.0683, p < 0.001; CI [0.3557, 0.6240]) and was substantially stronger for students with moderate (b2 = 0.7317, SE = 0.0744, p < 0.001; CI [0.5857, 0.8778]) and severe intellectual disability (b3 = 0.8105, SE = 0.0990, p < 0.001; CI [0.6162, 1.0049]). These findings indicate that while opportunities at home promote self‐determination in all students, their impact is particularly pronounced among those with higher levels of intellectual disability. The similarity between the slopes observed for students without intellectual disability and those with mild intellectual disability is consistent with the non‐significant interaction effect found for the mild support needs group.

To further examine the moderating effect of intellectual disability on the relationship between home opportunities and self‐determination, a graphical representation of the interaction was generated (see Figure 2). The scatterplot with fitted regression lines illustrates the association between home opportunities and self‐determination across different levels of intellectual disability (mild, moderate and severe). Across all groups, the relationship between home opportunities and self‐determination was positive, indicating that greater availability of opportunities at home is associated with higher levels of self‐determination. Importantly, the strength of this association increased as the level of intellectual disability increased. The slope of the regression line was steeper for individuals with more severe levels of intellectual disability, suggesting that these individuals are more sensitive to the presence of opportunities at home. In contrast, the relationship was weaker, although still positive, for individuals with lower levels of intellectual disability. These results visually support the significant interaction effect observed in the moderation analysis, indicating that the impact of home opportunities on self‐determination is not uniform across groups but varies as a function of support needs.

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the role of intellectual disability in the relationship between home‐based opportunities to engage in self‐determined actions and levels of self‐determination. Results add to the self‐determination field by strengthening the idea that context matters when it comes to scaffolding self‐determination (Shogren et al. 2025). Importantly, once home‐based opportunities were taken into account, intellectual disability did not show a direct statistically significant effect on self‐determination, suggesting that lower self‐determination levels are not inherently explained by the diagnostic condition itself, but rather by restrictions in access to meaningful opportunities. At the same time, the results indicate that, as the level of support needs increases, families require more structured guidance and support to effectively foster self‐determination skills, particularly in translating opportunities into self‐determined actions.

Consistent with previous research, results indicated that youth without intellectual disability reported significantly higher levels of opportunities to engage in self‐determined actions at home compared to youth with moderate and severe support needs (Mumbardó et al. 2018), whereas differences with youth with mild support needs were not statistically significant. Moreover, youth without intellectual disabilities and those with mild support needs showed comparable self‐determination levels, whereas youth with moderate or severe support needs exhibited significantly lower levels. Within the range of support that individuals with intellectual disabilities may require, those with mild support needs typically encounter fewer challenges and often possess greater personal skills and abilities compared to their peers with more significant support needs. As a result, they may exhibit behaviours and capabilities more similar to those of youth without intellectual disabilities. This pattern underscores the complexity of self‐determination, which is influenced by both individual capacities and environmental factors and highlights the central role of the home context in promoting self‐determined behaviour (Cristea and Ghergut 2023; Mumbardó et al. 2018; Vicente et al. 2020). Providing decision‐making, goal‐setting and real‐life practice opportunities at home has consistently been shown to facilitate self‐determination, particularly autonomy (Dean et al. 2021; Esteban et al. 2025; Cristea and Ghergut 2023; Mumbardó‐Adam et al. 2020). In this sense, the observed differences in self‐determination appear to be more closely related to differential access to opportunities at home than to intellectual disability status per se. Building on these differences, we examined how the presence of intellectual disability influences the relationship between home opportunities and self‐determination.

Our study demonstrates that intellectual disability does not act as a linear barrier to self‐determination but rather as a contextual factor that empirically explains variability in the strength of the relationship between opportunities at home and self‐determination across levels of support needs. More specifically, the positive interaction observed indicates that youth with intellectual disabilities—particularly those with moderate and severe support needs—are more responsive to variations in home‐based opportunities, such that increases in opportunities are associated with disproportionately larger gains in self‐determination. In other words, the presence of intellectual disability changes how home‐based opportunities translate into self‐determined behaviours. Although it is well established that families play a critical role in fostering self‐determination by providing opportunities for their children, doing so can be challenging in certain contexts. This finding aligns with prior research suggesting that, while families usually perceive self‐determination as a highly valued goal, they have different perspectives on how to promote it (Shogren and Turnbull 2006).

Previous research has identified a disconnection between families' beliefs about the importance of self‐determination and their actual practices in supporting it (e.g., Grigal et al. 2003; Pilnick et al. 2011). Consistent with the conditional effects observed in the present study, certain concerns and beliefs associated with intellectual disability might increase families' reluctance to offer opportunities to develop self‐determination. These issues include safety‐related concerns (Almack et al. 2009; Millar 2007), low expectations regarding competency (Hodgetts et al. 2017; Mitchell 2012) and perceptions of dependency (Nota et al. 2007; Shogren and Turnbull 2006). As support needs increase, the association between home‐based opportunities and self‐determination becomes stronger, which may reflect that families of youth with moderate or severe support needs face greater challenges in providing supportive opportunities and therefore require more structured guidance. For example, families with children who have moderate or severe support needs may be more concerned about providing opportunities for autonomy than families with children who have fewer support needs. Also, families may find it challenging to provide such opportunities, as doing so requires more specialized support and guidance. Thus, families' concerns typically intensify with the level of support required, particularly when they lack adequate resources and guidance. When unaddressed, such concerns may function as socioecological barriers, potentially leading to overprotective parenting practices and restrictive environments (Shogren 2013).

Beyond family practices, differences in prior experiences may also help explain why opportunities at home are more strongly related to self‐determination among youth with higher support needs. Young people with moderate or severe intellectual disability are more likely to grow up in highly structured environments, both at home and at school, which can limit their opportunities to make choices, set goals or act independently on a regular basis. As a result, when families deliberately provide opportunities for self‐determined action at home—an environment where autonomy has been shown to be most frequently supported (Mumbardó‐Adam et al. 2020)—these experiences may be especially meaningful because they represent less frequent and more visible opportunities for autonomy. This highlights the importance of providing families with appropriate support and guidance to address safety‐related concerns and foster self‐determination effectively and suggests that the development of self‐determination depends not only on isolated opportunities but also on the consistency of self‐determination supportive experiences across family and other educational contexts and coordinated efforts across settings.

It is also important to consider whether the observed moderating effect may be partly influenced by differences in how students with diverse profiles understand the content of the items they responded to. Individuals with moderate or severe intellectual disability may interpret abstract concepts such as choice, autonomy or goal‐setting in more concrete and context‐dependent ways. Rather than reflecting measurement bias, this may indicate different ways in which self‐determination is experienced and reported, often through supported and shared decision‐making. In this sense, increased opportunities at home may make self‐determined practices more visible and cognitively accessible, strengthening the association between opportunities and perceived self‐determination.

The moderating role of intellectual disability emphasizes that fostering self‐determination requires a focus on environmental factors, particularly opportunities within the home. Adopting a social‐ecological approach highlights that enhancing self‐determination involves not only supporting individual capacities but also transforming contextual conditions that may restrict participation, belonging and opportunities for exercising self‐determination (Shogren et al. 2025). Families who feel supported and confident in providing these opportunities—particularly when professionals explain the concept of self‐determination and offer practical guidance—are more likely to promote self‐determined actions effectively (Martínez‐Tur et al. 2018). Overall, our findings extend the literature by demonstrating that the presence of intellectual disability modifies the impact of home‐based opportunities on self‐determination. Taken together, these findings indicate that intellectual disability does not inherently limit self‐determination, but rather increases reliance on supportive, opportunity‐rich environments, underscoring the transformative potential of family‐centred interventions. Interventions should therefore not treat intellectual disability as a direct barrier but rather as a condition that amplifies the importance of supportive environments.

Implications for Practice

Considering these findings, family‐centred interventions are essential to empower families to promote self‐determined actions effectively, as they provide both conceptual clarity and practical guidance (Fàbregues et al. 2025). Such interventions should rest on several interconnected pillars. First, they should be anchored in daily family routines, generating structured, real‐life opportunities for youth to practice self‐determination‐related dimensions such as choice‐making, decision‐making and problem‐solving—within the activities that already make up family life, rather than through decontextualized training (Brotherson et al. 2008; Shogren, Wehmeyer, Palmer, Rifenbark, and Little 2015; Zhang et al. 2010). Second, interventions should address parental beliefs and expectations, both about their child's capacity for autonomous action and about their own competence to support it, given that parental self‐efficacy has been identified as a critical lever for reducing overprotection and enabling developmentally appropriate risk‐taking (Mumbardó‐Adam et al. 2023). Third, these efforts should combine experiential learning with structured incidental teaching of specific self‐determination skills, embedded in naturally occurring situations at home. Finally, sustained collaboration with professionals is essential, not only to help families identify and create opportunities for practice, but to progressively fade external support as youth with intellectual disability gain competence. These principles can inform the design of parent training and home‐based intervention models.

Limitations

This study is not exempt from some limitations that must be considered when interpreting results. First, we used convenience sampling, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, results should be interpreted as representative of the educational and regional contexts in which the study was conducted rather than of the wider population of youth with and without intellectual disability. Second, the cross‐sectional nature of the study prevents drawing causal inferences. Although the proposed model was theoretically grounded and describes a causal sequence based on previous research, the results should be interpreted with caution, as temporal or causal relationships cannot be empirically established. Future longitudinal or experimental studies would be necessary to more robustly examine how opportunities at home influence the development of self‐determination over time. Third, further information about the home context would have been helpful to better interpret the study results. Specifically, variables such as families' socioeconomic status, cultural background or regional characteristics were not collected, despite their potential influence on the opportunities provided at home. Future studies should endeavour to include these contextual variables in order to better understand how broader social and environmental factors shape youths' levels of self‐determination. Fourth, the participants involved, due to their age, had a diagnosis based on the former version of the DSM. Nowadays, in the DSM‐5 (American Psychiatric Association [APA] 2013), the operationalization of intellectual disability not only includes cognitive functioning, but also adaptive behaviour. Future studies could directly assess both cognitive functioning and adaptive behaviour to provide a more nuanced characterization of participants' support needs. Fifth, although teachers were provided with detailed guidelines on how to support the administration of the scale, the fidelity of this administration was not evaluated. This methodological limitation makes it difficult to empirically verify possible variability in the type or amount of assistance provided across participants, which may have influenced how items were interpreted and answered. Additionally, teacher support may have varied depending on students' support needs, potentially introducing heterogeneity in the data collection process. Future research should incorporate standardized training procedures and fidelity checks to minimize this source of methodological variability. Relatedly, while intellectual disability was modelled considering different levels of support needs, this category still encompasses a wide range of functional profiles. Although this heterogeneity was partially addressed by distinguishing mild, moderate and severe support needs, variability within these categories remains a limitation that should be acknowledged. Finally, this study relied exclusively on youths' self‐reports when assessing their levels of self‐determination and perceived opportunities at home. Although self‐report is central to the construct of self‐determination, given the strong influence of contexts highlighted in this study, future research should incorporate perspectives from other key informants, such as parents, teachers or support professionals, to better understand the complex ecological factors that underlie the expression of self‐determined behaviour (e.g., through proxy reports; Verdugo et al. 2021).

Conclusions

Our findings reinforce that context matters when it comes to supporting youth with intellectual disabilities in engaging in self‐determined actions. Importantly, the results suggest that lower levels of self‐determination are not primarily explained by the presence of intellectual disability itself, but rather by limited access to frequent and meaningful opportunities at home. In this sense, frequent and individualized opportunities at home are crucial to enhance self‐determination. However, families often face barriers to providing such opportunities, including safety concerns, low expectations or perceptions of dependency. These concerns, if unaddressed, may restrict opportunities for autonomy and participation. Family‐centred interventions are essential to help parents overcome fears and address specific concerns (Fàbregues et al. 2025). Professionals should provide families with clear and practical explanations of self‐determination (Martínez‐Tur et al. 2018) and offer strategies for embedding decision‐making, problem‐solving and goal‐setting into daily routines. Moreover, the findings indicate that youth with higher support needs may benefit disproportionately from enriched home‐based opportunities, highlighting the particularly strong influence of the family environment for this group. Finally, it is important to recognize that intellectual disability is not a fixed barrier but rather shapes how opportunities translate into self‐determined actions. Families of youth with higher support needs may benefit from more structured and intensive guidance, while those supporting youth with mild support needs may focus on expanding real‐life practice opportunities. By shifting the focus to opportunity‐rich environments and by working collaboratively with families and optimizing home‐based opportunities, we can empower youth with intellectual disabilities to exercise greater self‐determination, well‐being and successful life outcomes.
