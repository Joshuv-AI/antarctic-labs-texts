Markov Chain Variance Estimation: A Stochastic Approximation Approach
Shubhada Agrawal, Prashanth L.A, Siva Theja Maguluri

Abstract
We consider the problem of estimating the asymptotic variance of a function defined on a Markov chain, an important step for statistical inference of the stationary mean. We design a novel recursive estimator that requires O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) computation at each step, does not require storing any historical samples or any prior knowledge of run-length, and has optimal O ⁡ ( 1 n ) O(\frac{1}{n}) rate of convergence for the mean-squared error (MSE) with provable finite sample guarantees. Here, n n refers to the total number of samples generated. Our estimator is based on linear stochastic approximation of an equivalent formulation of the asymptotic variance in terms of the solution of the Poisson equation. We generalize our estimator in several directions, including estimating the covariance matrix for vector-valued functions, estimating the stationary variance of a Markov chain, and approximately estimating the asymptotic variance in settings where the state space of the underlying Markov chain is large. We also show applications of our estimator in average reward reinforcement learning (RL), where we work with asymptotic variance as a risk measure to model safety-critical applications. We design a temporal-difference type algorithm tailored for policy evaluation in this context. We consider both the tabular and linear function approximation settings. Our work paves the way for developing actor-critic style algorithms for variance-constrained RL.



1
Introduction

Consider an irreducible and aperiodic discrete time Markov chain (DTMC) on a finite state space. Given a function
f
, consider the partial sum

S_{n}(f):=\sum\nolimits_{k=0}^{n-1}f(X_{k}).
Under appropriate assumptions, it is well known that
\frac{S_{n}}{n}
converges almost surely to
\bar{f}
, the stationary expectation of
f
. Moreover, Central Limit Theorem (CLT) holds
(
Douc et al., 2018
, Chapter 21)
, and implies that for a positive constant
\kappa(f)
,

\frac{S_{n}(f)-n\bar{f}}{\sqrt{n}}\Rightarrow N(0,\kappa(f))\quad\text{ as }\quad n\rightarrow\infty,

where the above convergence is in distributions, and
N(0,\kappa(f))
denotes the Gaussian distribution with mean
0
and variance
\kappa(f)
. Since
\kappa(f)
corresponds to the variance in CLT, it is commonly referred to as asymptotic variance of the Markov chain.

The focus of the paper is to estimate
\kappa(f)
, which has been a long-standing area of research. Firstly, estimates of
\kappa(f)
play an important role in the statistical analysis of simulation outputs obtained from a Markov chain Monte Carlo experiment, for example, in statistical inference for the stationary mean,
\bar{f}
(see,
Schmeiser (1982)
;
Schruben (1983)
;
Carlstein (1986)
;
Kunsch (1989)
;
Glynn and Whitt (1992)
;
Chien et al. (1997)
;
Flegal and Jones (2010)
;
Wu (2009)
;
Nordman (2009)
;
Atchadé (2011)
;
Dingeç et al. (2015)
).

A second motivation comes from reinforcement learning (RL). In RL framework, the transition dynamics of the underlying MDP are unknown. The algorithm can obtain a sample of the MDP under any given policy, which specifies how actions are chosen in a given state. The traditional goal in an average reward RL problem is to find a policy that maximizes
\frac{S_{n}}{n}
. While the need to optimize over the average reward is well motivated, for applications in safety-critical domains, for example, healthcare or finance, it is also crucial to control adverse outcomes. As an example, one can consider the portfolio optimization problem, where the objective is to find an investment strategy that maximizes the return (expected value), while keeping the variability under control. As we will see later in Section
5.2
, a natural measure of variability in this setup is the asymptotic variance
\kappa(f)
. Estimating
\kappa(f)
is a vital sub-problem in mean-variance policy optimization, for instance, as a critic in an actor-critic framework, cf.
Prashanth and Ghavamzadeh (2016)
.

Estimating
\kappa(f)
, while practically relevant, is particularly challenging compared to estimating variance using independent and identically-distributed (i.i.d.) samples because of temporal correlations introduced due to the underlying Markov chain structure. The problem of estimating
\kappa(f)
has been extensively studied in literature. However, to the best of our knowledge, most of the existing estimators have limitations of being either non-recursive in that the estimator at step
n
cannot be expressed as a function of the various statistics computed at step
n-1
, necessitating re-computing the estimator from scratch at each step, or requiring to store a large number of historical observations, or having a sub-optimal rate of convergence of mean-squared estimation error to
0
, or lack finite sample guarantees, or some combination of these. We discuss the related literature in details in Section
1.2
. In this work, our goal is to design an estimator for
\kappa(\cdot)
that utilizes samples from the given Markov chain along a
single
trajectory, is recursive and memory-efficient, easy to compute, and has an optimal rate of convergence in mean-squared sense, with provable finite sample guarantees.

1.1
Contributions

We now briefly describe the main contributions of this work.

1.

We propose a novel recursive estimator (or algorithm) for estimating
\kappa(f)
that requires
O(1)
computation at each step, does not require storing any historical samples, and enjoys optimal
O(\frac{1}{n})
rate of convergence for the mean-squared error (MSE). Here,
n
refers to the total number of samples generated.

The proposed estimator does not require any prior knowledge of the run length. In addition, we establish finite sample bounds on MSE of the proposed estimator that hold for any run length. In an earlier version of this paper
Agrawal et al. (2024)
, we proposed an algorithm that required generating samples along two independent trajectories of the underlying Markov chain. But this may not always be feasible. In this version, we develop a novel
single-trajectory
algorithm.

2.

Our algorithm can be specialized to estimate the stationary variance of a Markov chain. It has similar convergence guarantees, as well as all the other desirable properties, even in this setting. Additionally, it can be used to design a recursive estimator for the variance in i.i.d. setting, which requires
O(1)
memory and computation at each step, and has an optimal
O(\frac{1}{n})
rate of convergence for MSE.

3.

In contrast to prior work on DTMC variance estimation, a key idea behind our approach is to use stochastic approximation (SA) for statistical estimation problems to develop estimators with provably-good finite sample guarantees. In particular, we work with an expression for the DTMC asymptotic variance in terms of the solution of the so-called Poisson equation (these solutions are called value functions). We use linear SA on this expression to develop an algorithm for variance estimation. Our algorithm uses Temporal Difference (TD) learning from prior work for estimating the value function
Zhang et al. (2021)
. We also obtain finite sample bounds for TD learning, improving the MSE bound of
Zhang et al. (2021)
from
O(\frac{\log n}{n})
to
O(\frac{1}{n})
, which is of independent interest.

The mean square guarantees are obtained by first establishing that the underlying matrix of our linear SA is Hurwitz, and then builds upon recent work
Zhang et al. (2021)
on finite sample mean square error bounds for linear SA. A novel step in our proof is the use of Poisson equation (again) to handle Markov noise, which enables us to shave off the multiplicative
\log n
factor in the error.

4.

We generalize our estimator in several directions.

(a)

First, we develop extensions of it to estimate the covariance matrix of a vector-valued function
f
.

(b)

Second, we extend our estimator to the settings where the underlying Markov chain has a large state space. Here, we consider linear approximations of the value function using a small number of feature vectors. We then use this approximate value function to estimate the asymptotic variance.

(c)

Finally, we consider the average reward RL framework in the risk-sensitive setting. We propose asymptotic variance as a natural measure of risk in contrast to the stationary variance studied in prior work. We then focus on evaluating the asymptotic variance of a given policy. We adapt our variance estimator to this setting, and obtain a novel TD like algorithm and establish its finite sample error bounds.

Batch Means (BM)

A popular estimator for
\kappa(f)
is the BM estimator
Conway et al. (1959)
;
Schmeiser (1982)
, which we informally describe next. Consider
n
observations of the function
f
evaluated on the
n
consecutive states of the underlying Markov chain, and divide these observations into
b
non-overlapping batches, each of size
m
(for simplicity, assume that
n=mb
). For
i\in(b)
, let
m_{i}
denote the sample mean of the observations from batch
i
. Then, the BM estimator corresponds to the sample variance of
m_{i}
scaled by the batch size
m
. BM and its variants have been extensively studied (see,
Schmeiser (1982)
;
Meketon and Schmeiser (1984)
;
Carlstein (1986)
;
Welch (1987)
;
Kunsch (1989)
;
Alexopoulos et al. (2007)
;
Meketon and Schmeiser (2007)
;
Alexopoulos et al. (2011)
). See
Politis et al. (1999)
for an introduction to the different estimators and their relations. Roughly speaking, here, the batch size
m
governs the bias, while the number of batches
b
governs the variance of the estimator.

Glynn and Whitt (1991)
establish limitations of BM in the settings where the number of batches
b
does not increase with the total number of samples
n
.
Glynn and Whitt (1992)
establish conditions that ensure the asymptotic validity of BM.

Chien et al. (1997)
show that BM with both
b
and
m
increasing with
n
, is asymptotically unbiased (as
n
increases), and converges to
\kappa(f)
in mean square. In addition, they also show an asymptotic equality for MSE of BM, establishing it to be
O(\frac{1}{n^{2/3}})
.

Overlapping BM

While BM may look wasteful, overlapping BM
Meketon and Schmeiser (1984)
;
Meketon and Schmeiser (2007)
uses
b
overlapping batches, each of size
m
, where the consecutive batches use just
1
additional observation, along with
m-1
previous (consecutive) ones. Here, total number of samples satisfy
n=m+b-1
. When
n
is known ahead of time,

Flegal and Jones (2010)
establish
O(\frac{1}{n^{2/3}})
MSE for overlapping BM estimator. Notably, overlapping BM has the same MSE as BM (order wise) since the bias of the two estimators is the same, and the variance of the overlapping BM is
\frac{2}{3}
times that of BM (improvement only by a constant factor). Thus, both BM and overlapping BM have a sub-optimal rate of convergence for MSE. See
Alexopoulos et al. (2011)
for a survey of results on BM and overlapping BM estimator.

In addition to being sub-optimal, another drawback of the classical and overlapping BM is that either their error guarantees are only satisfied at time
n
and are not anytime, or they are not recursive (sequential), i.e., one needs to re-compute the estimator at each time using computation that is linear in the number of observations. This is because the batch sizes are predetermined, and depend on
n
. Moreover, one requires storing all the previous observations in order to re-compute the estimator at each step, hence,
O(n)
computation at time
n
.
Wu (2009)
develop a recursive BM estimator (
O(1)
computation at each time) with increasing batch sizes that are independent of
n
. Their estimator does not require storing the past observations, improving the memory requirement to
O(1)
, and is asymptotically unbiased. However,
Wu (2009)
only give asymptotic guarantees, and establish a sub-optimal rate of
O(\frac{1}{n^{2/3}})
for MSE.
Yau and Chan (2016)
also propose a different recursive BM estimator with similar guarantees.

Other estimators

Several bootstrap-based estimators have also been proposed in literature
Kunsch (1989)
;
Liu et al. (1992)
;
Carlstein (1986)
;
Romano (1992)
;
Politis and Romano (1994)
. However, these are computationally intensive, and hence, we do not discuss them in details in this work.
Nordman (2009)
;
Lahiri (1999)
study the asymptotic rates for bias and variance of the different bootstrap-based estimators. We refer the reader to
Lahiri (2013)
for an introduction to this approach.
Schruben (1983)
develop standardized time series based estimators, extensively studied in
Goldsman and Schruben (1984)
;
Glynn and Iglehart (1988)
;
Goldsman et al. (1990)
;
Goldsman and Schruben (1990)
;
Foley and Goldsman (1999)
;
Aktaran-Kalayci et al. (2011)
.
Trevezas and Limnios (2009)
propose maximum likelihood estimator for
\kappa(f)
, and prove that it is strongly consistent and asymptotically normal.

Jackknifed versions of estimators

Since a significant contribution to MSE of BM and its variants is due to bias of the estimator,
Dingeç et al. (2015)
propose using jackknife
Tukey (1958)
to reduce it at a cost of modest increase in variance of the estimator. They develop jackknifed versions of BM and overlapping BM estimators with a significantly reduced MSE of
O(\frac{\log n}{n})
. However, these are not recursive, and require
O(n)
computation and
O(n)
memory at time step
n
, rendering them less practical.

Regenerative simulation

Another class of estimators that have been extensively studied at least since 1970s is the regeneration-based estimators. These depend on existence of regeneration points in the underlying process at which the process can be thought to probabilistically start afresh and evolve independent of the past. Note that in a finite state DTMC, the (random) times of successive visits of the Markov chain to a particular state can serve as the regeneration points. Hence, loosely speaking, a DTMC path can be thought of as several independent cycles pasted together. A number of past works have exploited this property of the underlying process to design efficient recursive estimators for
\kappa(f)
(see,
Crane and Iglehart (1974)
;
Glynn and Iglehart (1987)
;
Glynn and Iglehart (1993)
;
Henderson and Glynn (2001)
;
Glynn and Whitt (2002)
and
Glynn (2006)
for a nice exposition), which can be implemented in an online fashion using
O(1)
computation. Moreover, central limit theorems (CLTs) are established for these estimators, which establish an optimal asymptotic error rate of
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
(that is comparable to

O(\frac{1}{n})
MSE). However, to the best of our knowledge, finite time bounds of these estimators have not been established. While the CLT strongly suggests that one should be able to obtain a finite time bound of

O(\frac{1}{n})
MSE, the explicit dependence of the error on the parameters of the underlying chain is not known. In contrast, we propose an alternate approach to Markov chain variance estimation based on SA, and we present explicit finite time bounds on the MSE.

Poisson equations

Finally,
Douc et al. (2022)
resort to an alternative formulation for
\kappa(f)
involving solution to a certain Poisson equation, propose a coupled Markov chains-based estimator for
\kappa(f)
, and provide its asymptotic convergence guarantees. The work that is closest to ours is
Benton (2022)
which proposed a SA-based estimator for
\kappa(f)
, and established its asymptotic convergence. While their estimator is recursive, and requires
O(1)
memory and computation at each step, they do not provide either the rates of convergence, nor finite-sample bounds on MSE of the estimator. In the current work, we propose a different SA-based estimator, along with finite-sample guarantees on its MSE.

Note that the focus of this work is to estimate the asymptotic variance
\kappa(f)
. To this end, as we will see later, we design a TD-like linear SA algorithm. This problem of estimating
\kappa(f)
is very different from studying the variance of iterates of any particular algorithm like TD, or designing algorithms with minimum variance of the iterates themselves. These latter objectives too are well-studied in literature; cf.
Devraj and Meyn (2017)
;
Yin and Wang (2020)
;
Chen et al. (2020a)
;
Chen et al. (2020b)
;
Hao et al. (2021)
;
Zhu et al. (2023)
. In contrast to these orthogonal prior works, which focus on studying the variance of TD learning and its variants, we do
not
study the variance of a linear SA algorithm itself. We use linear SA simply as a tool for solving the variance estimation problem. To further elaborate on this distinction, for concreteness, consider the setting of Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), where the transition probabilities are known. Here, for evaluating a given policy, one can use value iteration (VI). Since the MDP is known, there is no variance associated with the iterates of VI. However, the asymptotic variance that we consider in this work is still well-defined, and exists due to inherent randomness associated with the probabilistic transitions of the MDP. This work focuses on estimating this asymptotic variance.

Later, in Section
5
, we present an application of the proposed estimator in the average reward RL setting. We postpone the related literature from RL to Section
5.3
.

1.3
Setup and Asymptotic Variance

In this subsection, we formally introduce the problem. To this end, we first define some notation. Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of Natural numbers,
\mathbb{R}
the set of Real numbers, and let
d\geq 1
. For a column vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
v^{T}
denote its transpose and let
\|v\|_{2}
denote the
\ell_{2}
-norm of
v
. Let
{\bf 1}
(or
{\bf e}
) and
{\bf 0}
denote the all ones and all zeros vectors in appropriate dimensions, respectively.

We now describe the underlying dynamics, followed by formally defining the asymptotic variance. For
S\in\mathbb{N}
, consider an irreducible and aperiodic DTMC,
{\mathcal{M}}
, on a finite state space
{\mathcal{S}}=\left\{1,\dots,S\right\}
with transition matrix
P
and a unique stationary distribution, denoted by
\pi

(
Levin and Peres, 2017
, Chapter 1)
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
X_{n}\in{\mathcal{S}}
denote the state at time
n
. Consider
f:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a function defined on state space of
{\mathcal{M}}
, and let
\bar{f}
denote its stationary expectation, i.e.,
\bar{f}:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[f(X)\right]
. Since
f(X)-\bar{f}
has stationary mean zero, there exists
V:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that solves the following Poisson equation
(
Douc et al., 2018
, Section 21.2)
:

f-\bar{f}{\bf 1}=V-PV.

Since
V
solves (
1.3
), observe that
V+c{\bf 1}
for any constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
is also a solution. Let
V^{*}
denote the solution normalized so that it is orthogonal to
{\bf 1}
in
\ell_{2}
norm, i.e.,
{\bf 1}^{T}V^{*}=0
. It is well-known that the set of solutions of (
1.3
) takes the form
S_{f}:=\left\{V^{*}+c{\bf 1}|c\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

(
Douc et al., 2018
, Section 21.2)
.

For a Markov chain starting in state
x_{0}
, the asymptotic variance is given by

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\operatorname{Var}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}f(X_{k})\bigg|X_{0}=x_{0}\right],

(2)

and is independent of the starting state (Proposition

below). This follows from geometric mixing of

. We denote this constant by

(or
\kappa
, since

is fixed). In this paper, we assume that

1
. Below, we first present equivalent formulations for
\kappa(f)
that will be useful in designing efficient algorithms for estimating it.

1.4
Stochastic Approximation: An Overview

Before proceeding to design an SA-based estimator for
\kappa(f)
, in this subsection, we give a brief overview of the SA updates we will repeatedly use throughout the rest of the paper. The earliest SA algorithms, which are iterative updates that can be used to solve for roots of certain functions, date back at least to
Robbins and Monro (1951)
. While the asymptotic convergence of SA algorithms has long been known
Borkar (2008)
, finite sample guarantees for linear SA algorithm have only recently been established
Srikant and Ying (2019)
;
Zhang et al. (2021)
. In this work, we will use SA for finding averages as well as roots of linear functions, which we discuss next.

Mean Estimation with Step Sizes

An SA algorithm to estimate
\Gamma:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[g(X)\right]
for a function
g
using samples
g(X_{0}),\dots,g(X_{n})
, corresponds to the following recursive update with an appropriately chosen step size
\alpha_{n}>0
, and an initial iterate
\Gamma_{0}
:

\Gamma_{n+1}=(1-\alpha_{n})\Gamma_{n}+\alpha_{n}g(X_{n}).

Observe that for
\Gamma_{0}=0
and
\alpha_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}
, this reduces to the classical empirical average estimator for mean. Moreover, if
X_{0},\dots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. according to
\pi
, then empirical mean is known to converge almost surely to
\Gamma
(from Law of Large Numbers) under mild regularity conditions. Similar convergence results hold for averages in (
1.4
) under certain regularity conditions on
\alpha_{n}
and data-generating distributions.

Linear SA

For
m>0
and
d>0
, let
\textsf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
and
{\gothfamily b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We want to estimate a solution
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to the equation
\textsf{A}\Gamma+{\gothfamily b}=0
using noisy observations
\textsf{A}_{1},\dots,\textsf{A}_{n}
and
{\gothfamily b}_{1},\dots,{\gothfamily b}_{n}
. An SA algorithm for this corresponds to the following update with a well chosen step size
\alpha_{n}>0
, as well as an initial iterate
\Gamma_{0}
:

\Gamma_{n+1}=\Gamma_{n}+\alpha_{n}(\textsf{A}_{n}\Gamma_{n}+{\gothfamily b}_{n}).

As we will see in the following sections, these update methods play a crucial role in arriving at a simple recursive estimator that satisfies all the desirable properties discussed earlier in Section
1
.

2
Variance Estimation: Tabular Setting

With the expressions for asymptotic variance derived in Proposition
1.1
above, in this section, we design an algorithm for estimating it. A natural first approach is to use the Monte Carlo technique for estimating
\kappa(f)
, for example, the Batch Means estimator and its variants, which proceed by appropriately truncating the infinite summation in (
1.1
). As discussed earlier in Section
1.2
, these approaches suffer from either being non-recursive, computationally demanding, having sub-optimal rates of convergence, or a combination of these. We refer the reader to Section
1.2
and Appendix
B.3
for a discussion.

2.1
The Algorithm

Since the equivalent formulation for
\kappa
in (
4
) involves an infinite summation, algorithms approximating it using a finite summation could possibly suffer from being non-adaptive as the vanilla Monte Carlo approaches. Instead, we design a fully-adaptive TD-type algorithm for estimating
\kappa
, using SA for a modification of the expression in (
5
) (Equation (
9
) below). This alternative representation helps us to design a linear SA algorithm with finite-sample guarantees on the mean (squared) estimation error. For a solution
V
of (
1.3
), let
\bar{V}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[V(X)\right]

Next, let

Since the feasible region in

.

Next, for the step-size constants

,
c_{2}
, and

(inputs to Algorithm

\displaystyle\eta:=\left(c^{2}_{1}+5+2c^{2}_{2}+10c^{2}_{3}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

, which immediately implies
{O}(\frac{1}{n})

Consider Algorithm

The above theorem follows from a more general result (Theorem

3.1
to that in the above theorem. We now present some remarks about the bound in Theorem
2.1
above.

First, observe that since
\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\kappa_{n}-\kappa(f))^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\Theta_{n}-\Theta_{\pi}\|^{2}_{2}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|V_{n}-V^{*}\|^{2}_{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\Theta_{n}-\Theta_{\pi}\|^{2}_{2}\right]
, the bound in Theorem
2.1
also holds for MSE in estimation of
\kappa(f)
as well as
V^{*}
.

Theorem
2.1
(a)
bounds the mean-squared estimation error of the proposed algorithm in case of constant step size. Though the first term in this bound decays exponentially fast as the number of iterations
n
increase, the other two terms are constant and become a bottleneck after sufficiently large
n
. This is a well-known behavior of SA algorithms with constant step sizes. In fact, this algorithm suffers similar drawback of being non-recursive as the vanilla Monte Carlo BM estimator. In order to ensure the mean-squared error smaller than
\epsilon^{2}
for
\epsilon>0
(or mean absolute error smaller than
\epsilon
), one needs to pick the step size
\alpha
as a function of
\epsilon
(also see Corollary
2.1.1
below). This drawback of being non-recursive is overcome by choosing the step size to be diminishing (as in Theorem
2.1
(b)
).

Second, it has been shown recently that linear SA with constant step size, in presence of Markov noise, suffers from an asymptotic bias (as
n\rightarrow\infty
); see
Huo et al. (2023)
;
Nagaraj et al. (2020)
. This is in contrast to the i.i.d. noise setting, where the asymptotic bias is shown to be
0
in
Mou et al. (2020)
;
Lakshminarayanan and Szepesvari (2018)
. This motivates us to study SA with diminishing step-size, considered in Theorem
2.1
(b)
.

Remark 2.3
.

For diminishing step size, Algorithm
1
achieves
{O}(\frac{1}{n})
rate of convergence (Theorem
2.1
(b)
). As in part
(a)
, the first term in the bound in part
(b)
decays faster, and the other two terms are rate-determining
O(\frac{1}{n})
terms. We believe that the rate of convergence of
\Omega(\frac{1}{n})
for MSE is tight. In the special setting of i.i.d. noise with parametric distributions, this follows from the Cramér-Rao lower bound
Nielsen (2013)
. We discuss a simple estimator that achieves this rate in i.i.d. setting in Appendix
C.4
, and also propose a SA-based recursive estimator achieving the same rate in i.i.d. setting in the following section (Section
2.3
).

Using bounds in Theorem
2.1
, the corollary below presents the number of iterations of Algorithm
1
needed to have the mean estimation error bounded by
\epsilon
. It follows from setting the mean-squared estimation error bounds in Theorem
2.1
to at most
\epsilon^{2}
. We refer the reader to Section

for a proof of Corollary
2.1.1
.

Corollary 2.1.1
.

To estimate
\kappa(f)
using the iterates
\kappa_{n}
generated by Algorithm
1
with diminishing step size, up to mean estimation error
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|{\kappa_{n}-\kappa(f)}\right|\right]\leq\epsilon
, we require

n=O\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)O\left(\frac{\|V^{*}\|^{2}_{2}}{\Delta^{4}_{1}}\right).

The sample complexity in Corollary
2.1.1
depends on
|S|
via
\Delta_{1}
, and
\|V^{*}\|_{2}
. Here,

V^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{|S|}
, and hence,
\|V^{*}\|^{2}_{2}=O(|S|^{2})
. The dependence of
\Delta_{1}
on

is more implicit, and relates to the mixing properties of the Markov chain induced by the policy. We make this dependence explicit in two specific examples. If the underlying Markov chain is a random walk on a complete graph (with
|S|
vertices), then
\Delta_{1}=O(1)
, and hence we get
{O}(|S|^{2})

\Delta_{1}=O({1}/{|S|^{2}})

{O}(|S|^{10})
in the sample complexity.

Remark 2.4
.

Using Jensen’s inequality, we have
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|{\kappa_{n}-\kappa(f)}\right|\right]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\kappa_{n}-\kappa(f))^{2}\right]},

which then gives a bound on the mean estimation error. In particular, for the diminishing step-sizes of the form
\alpha_{i}=\frac{\alpha}{i+h}
for all
i\geq 1
, Theorem
2.1
(b) gives

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|{\kappa_{n}-\kappa(f)}\right|\right]\leq{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Remark 2.5
.

Consider the problem of estimating the standard deviation,
\sqrt{\kappa(f)}
. Mean-squared error for the estimator
\sqrt{\kappa_{n}}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{n}}-\sqrt{\kappa(f)}\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{({\kappa_{n}}-{\kappa(f)})^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{n}}+\sqrt{\kappa(f)}\right)^{2}}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\kappa(f)}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left({\kappa_{n}}-{\kappa(f)}\right)^{2}\right],

which is at most
{O}(\frac{1}{n})
for
\alpha_{k}=O(\frac{1}{n})
.

The corollary below shows that Algorithm

in MSE. Moreover, the estimator satisfies all the desirable properties discussed in Section

Corollary 2.2.1
.

For

, consider a sequence of i.i.d. observations

3
Variance Estimation: Linear Function Approximation

When the underlying state and action spaces are large, estimating
V
function for each state-action pair may be computationally intractable. To address this, we consider evaluating an approximation of
V
by its projection on a linear subspace spanned by a given fixed set of
d
vectors
\{\tilde{\phi}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\phi}_{d}\}
,
where
\tilde{\phi}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
for each
i\in(d)
. More concretely, for
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
, and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we consider the following linear function approximation (LFA) of
V(s)
:

V_{\theta}(s):=\phi(s)^{T}\theta,\quad\text{where}\quad\phi^{T}(s):=[\tilde{\phi}_{1}(s)\dots\tilde{\phi}_{d}(s)],

\phi^{T}(s)
is the feature vector for state
s
, and
\phi(s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Recall that
S=|{\mathcal{S}}|
. Let
\Phi
be a
S\times d
matrix with
\tilde{\phi}_{i}
being the
i^{\text{th}}
column, and let

W_{\Phi}=\{\Phi\theta:\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

denote the column space of
\Phi
. Then,
V_{\theta}=\Phi\theta
, where
V_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times 1}
is an approximation for
V
using
\theta
.

Assumption 1
.

The matrix
\Phi
is full rank, i.e., the set of feature vectors
\{\tilde{\phi}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\phi}_{d}\}
are linearly independent. Additionally,
\|\phi(s)\|_{2}\leq 1
, for each
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
.

This is a standard assumption, and can be achieved by feature normalization, see
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy (1999)
;
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis (1996)
. We now introduce some notation that will be used in this section. Let
\theta_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the unique vector (if it exists) such that
\Phi\theta_{e}={\bf e}
. Define subspace
S_{\Phi,e}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\displaystyle S_{\Phi,e}:=\text{span}\left(\left\{\theta|\Phi\theta={\bf e}\right\}\right)=\begin{cases}\left\{c\theta_{e}|c\in\mathbb{R}\right\},\quad&\text{ if }{\bf e}\in W_{\Phi},\\
\{0\},\quad&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Let
E
be the subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is orthogonal complement (in
\ell_{2}
-norm) of
S_{\Phi,e}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle E=\begin{cases}\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:~\theta^{T}\theta_{e}=0\},\quad&\text{ if }{\bf e}\in W_{\Phi},\\
\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Observe that for
\theta\in E
,
\Phi\theta
is a non-constant vector. This is because there does not exist
\theta\in E
such that
\Phi\theta={\bf e}
. This follows since if
{\bf e}\in W_{\Phi}
, then the unique vector
\theta_{e}
such that

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the algorithm with

,
c_{2}
, and

c_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2\Delta_{2}}+\frac{\Delta_{2}}{2},\qquad\frac{5}{249}(5-2\sqrt{2})\Delta_{2}\leq c_{3}\leq\frac{5}{249}(5+2\sqrt{2})\Delta_{2},

and

c_{3}-\frac{498c^{2}_{3}}{7\Delta_{2}}+\frac{7\Delta_{2}}{498}\leq c_{2}\leq-3c_{3}+\frac{5}{83}\sqrt{498c_{3}\Delta_{2}-17\Delta^{2}_{2}}.

Recall that
\eta
was defined in (
15

\Theta^{*}
before (
20
). For a constant

,
\xi_{2}:=112B(1+\|\Theta^{*}\|_{2})^{2}

(a)

Let

for all

(b)

3.3
Approximation Error

Since we approximate the elements of the set
S_{f}
of
V
functions (defined after (

using (

may differ from

depending on the chosen approximation architecture.

In this section, we bound the squared approximation error:
(\kappa(f)-\kappa^{*})^{2}
. To this end, we first define the approximation error associated with approximating
V
using LFA. Since each element of

is a valid
V

by

-norm) of
\Phi\theta
from
S_{f}

Tsitsiklis and Van Roy (1999)
, i.e.,

\inf\nolimits_{V\in S_{f}}\|\Phi\theta-V\|_{D_{\pi}}
.
The minimum possible approximation error in
V
, due to the chosen architecture, is given by

\mathcal{E}:=\inf\limits_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\inf\limits_{V\in S_{f}}\|\Phi\theta-V\|_{D_{\pi}}.

This essentially captures the distance between the two sets,
S_{f}
and the column space of
\Phi
. The following proposition bounds the squared difference between
\kappa^{*}
and the asymptotic variance
\kappa(f)
, in terms of the approximation error for
V
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Given
d
basis vectors represented as columns of
\Phi
, there exists
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that

(\kappa^{*}-\kappa(f))^{2}\leq\frac{16~{\mathcal{E}}^{2}}{1-\lambda^{2}}.

Notice that
\mathcal{E}
, and hence the RHS above, equals
0
if the chosen basis functions are such that the span of these basis functions intersects with the set
S_{f}
of
V
functions, i.e., if the approximation architecture is capable of representing some value function from
S_{f}
. This is particularly true for the tabular setting, where
d=S
and the basis vectors are the standard basis in this dimension, spanning the entire space.

To prove Proposition
3.2
, we first bound the approximation error in estimation of
V
using the proposed Algorithm from Section
3.1
. This is analogous to that for the value function in
(
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy, 1999
, Theorem 3)
. Next, using this bound, we arrive at the bound in (
3.2
). We refer the reader to Section
E
for a proof of the proposition.

Our algorithm and analysis easily extend to the setting where the function

For

and

5
Applications in Reinforcement Learning

Consider an average reward reinforcement learning (RL) framework, where the transition dynamics of the underlying Markov decision process (MDP) are unknown
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis (1996)
;
Sutton and Barto (2018)
. An RL algorithm can obtain a sample of the MDP under a given policy, which specifies how actions are chosen in a given state. The traditional goal in an average reward RL problem is to find a policy that maximizes the long run average reward. While the need to optimize over average reward is well motivated, for applications in safety-critical domains like healthcare and finance, it is also crucial to control adverse outcomes. To address this, within the domain of risk-sensitive MDPs, several risk measures have been considered, namely variance
Sobel (1982)
;
Filar et al. (1989)
, conditional value-at-risk
Chow and Ghavamzadeh (2014)
, exponential utility
Whittle (1990)
, the general class of coherent risk measures
Tamar et al. (2015)
, and cumulative prospect theory
Prashanth et al. (2016)
, which is not coherent. The choice of the risk measure largely depends on the application at hand.

We consider variance as a risk measure. Several previous works incorporate variance in a constrained setting, where the goal is to maximize the average reward (which is an expectation), while ensuring a certain bound on the variance. This is the so-called “mean-variance tradeoff”, considered in the seminal work of Markowitz
Markowitz (1952)
, and later in MDP contexts, cf.
Mandl (1971)
;
Sobel (1982)
;
Filar et al. (1989)
. An alternative to such a formulation is to consider the exponential utility formulation, see
Arrow (1971)
;
Howard and Matheson (1972)
, where one optimizes the exponential. The constrained formulation is preferred over the exponential utility for two reasons. First, the mean-variance tradeoff can be controlled directly through a parameter that is a bound on the variance, while this trade-off is implicit in an exponential utility formulation. Second, the algorithms for the latter formulation do not extend easily when one considers feature-based representations and function approximation, see
(
L.A. and Fu, 2022
, Section 7.2)
for a detailed discussion.

Broadly, for a given policy, two different notions of variance are suggested for an average reward MDP in
Filar et al. (1989)
. The first notion is the asymptotic variance, while the second one is the per-period stationary variance. The latter has been studied in an RL setting in
Prashanth and Ghavamzadeh (2016)
, while the former has hardly been investigated in the literature, to the best of our knowledge. We use the asymptotic variance as a measure of the risk associated with a given policy, which is the variance of the random variable whose mean is typically optimized.
This asymptotic variance can be decomposed into two additive terms, where the first term coincides with per-step variance, while the other term involves correlations between states across time periods.
In a setting where the state sequence is independent, the second term is zero, while it is not in any non-trivial MDP. A mean-variance optimization formulation would consider maximizing the expectation of a random variable which represents the long-run average reward, and it is natural to consider the variance of this random variable; also see Section
5.2
.

In this section, we consider the problem of policy evaluation for asymptotic variance, where the goal is to estimate the asymptotic variance associated with the Markov chain induced by a given stationary policy. This problem of estimating the asymptotic variance is a vital sub-problem in mean-variance policy optimization, for instance, as a critic in an actor-critic framework, cf.
Prashanth and Ghavamzadeh (2016)
. For the discounted RL setting, a TD type algorithm for estimating variance has been proposed/analyzed in
Tamar et al. (2013)
, while a TD algorithm with provable finite sample guarantees, which caters to the variance risk measure in an average reward RL setting, is not present in the literature to the best of our knowledge.

The rest of this section is organized as follows. We begin by formally introducing the average reward RL setting and the problem setup in Section
5.1
. In Section
5.2
, we motivate the choice of asymptotic variance as a natural measure of risk associated with a given policy in this framework and discuss the related literature in Section
5.3
. We design a novel TD-like linear SA algorithm for policy evaluation in a tabular setting in Section
5.4
. Finally, we conclude in Section
5.5
with the finite sample error bounds for the proposed algorithm, proving
{O}(\frac{1}{n})
rate of convergence for the mean-squared error, where
n
is the time step. We defer the algorithm and its finite sample error bounds for the setting of linear function approximation in RL to Appendix
F
.

5.1
Setup

Consider an infinite-horizon, average-reward MDP specified by
({\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{A}},r,p)
, where
{\mathcal{S}}=\left\{1,\dots,\left|{S}\right|\right\}
denotes the finite state-space, and
{\mathcal{A}}=\left\{1,\dots,\left|{A}\right|\right\}
denotes the action space. At each time
k
, the agent is in state
S_{k}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, takes an action
A_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}}
, receives a reward
r(S_{k},A_{k})
, and transitions to state
S_{k+1}
. Here,
r:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and the next state
S_{k+1}
is sampled according to
p(S_{k},\cdot,A_{k})
, where,
p:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow[0,1]
, is the map that for states
s,s^{\prime}
and action
a
associates probability
p(s,s^{\prime},a)
with the transition from state
s
to
s^{\prime}
, when action
a
is taken.

A stationary policy
\mu:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\Sigma_{\left|{{\mathcal{A}}}\right|}
is a map from state space to a probability-simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{\left|{{\mathcal{A}}}\right|}
, i.e., it maps each state to a distribution over the actions. Given a stationary policy
\mu
and a state
x
, the associated probability of transitioning from state
x
to
x^{\prime}
is given by

P_{\mu}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum\nolimits_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\mu(a|x)p(x,x^{\prime},a).

Assumption 2
.

The sets
{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}
are finite. Under the stationary policy
\mu
, the induced Markov chain
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
with state space in
{\mathcal{S}}
and transition probabilities given by
P_{\mu}
is irreducible and aperiodic.

This is a standard assumption in literature (see
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy (1999)
;
Bertsekas (2012)
), and guarantees that each state is visited infinitely often. As a consequence of Assumption
2
, we have a unique stationary distribution associated with
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
, and the Markov chain starting from any initial distribution, converges to the stationary distribution geometrically-fast
(
Levin and Peres, 2017
, Section 4.3)
. In particular, let
\pi_{\mu}
denote the unique stationary distribution on
{\mathcal{S}}
that satisfies
\pi^{T}_{\mu}P_{\mu}=\pi^{T}_{\mu}
.

Next, let
X_{k}:=(S_{k},A_{k})
. Observe that the process
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}:=\left\{X_{k}\right\}
is also a Markov chain with finite states. Let its state space be denoted by
{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
. For any two states
x_{1}:=(s_{1},a_{1})
and
x_{2}:=(s_{2},a_{2})
in
{\mathcal{X}}
, the probability of transitioning from
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
under
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is given by

P_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}):=p(s_{1},s_{2},a_{1})\mu(a_{2}|s_{2}).

(27)

For
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
and
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
, define

d_{\mu}(s,a):=\pi_{\mu}(s)\mu(a|s).

Remark 5.1
.

Under Assumption
2
,
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
has a unique stationary distribution
d_{\mu}
defined above, and it mixes geometrically-fast. This follows from
(
Bhatnagar and Lakshmanan, 2016
, Proposition 1)
.

Notation

Let
D_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|{{\mathcal{S}}}\right|\left|{{\mathcal{A}}}\right|}\times\mathbb{R}^{\left|{{\mathcal{S}}}\right|\left|{{\mathcal{A}}}\right|}
be the diagonal array of stationary distribution
d_{\mu}
, i.e.,

D_{\mu}((s,a),(s,a)):=\begin{cases}d_{\mu}(s,a),&\text{ for }(s,a)\!\in\!{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}},\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

For vectors
x
and
y
in
\mathbb{R}^{\left|{{\mathcal{S}}}\right|\left|{{\mathcal{A}}}\right|}
, let

<x,y>_{D_{\mu}}:=x^{T}D_{\mu}y,

and
\|x\|_{D_{\mu}}:=x^{T}D_{\mu}x
denote the
D_{\mu}
weighted inner product and the induced norm, respectively.

Average Reward

The long-term per-step expected reward accumulated by a stationary policy
\mu
starting from state
s
is given by

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}r(S_{k},A_{k})|S_{0}=s\right].

(28)

Here, the expectation is with respect to both the transition dynamics of the environment, as well as the randomness in the policy
\mu
. It is well known that under Assumption
2
, this per-step expected reward in (
28
) is a constant that is independent of the starting state
Bertsekas (2012)
. We denote this quantity by
J_{\mu}
. It can be shown that

J_{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{d_{\mu}\!}\!\left[r(S,A)\right]=\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}},a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\pi_{\mu}(x)\mu(a|x)r(x,a).

(29)

Here,
\mathbb{E}_{d_{\mu}\!}\!\left[\cdot\right]
denotes the expectation when
(S,A)
is sampled according to the distribution
d_{\mu}
, which is defined after (
27
).

Asymptotic Variance

Observe that
r(\cdot,\cdot)
is a function defined on the state space of
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, which is fixed by the given policy
\mu
. In this work, we consider the problem of estimating the associated asymptotic variance,

\lim\limits_{T\rightarrow\infty}~\frac{1}{T}\operatorname{Var}\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{T-1}r(S_{k},A_{k})\bigg|S_{0}=s\right].

(30)

It can be shown to be a constant, independent of the starting state. We denote it by
\kappa_{\mu}
. Moreover (Proposition
1.1
),

\kappa_{\mu}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{Var}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}r(S_{k},A_{k})\bigg|(S_{0},A_{0})\sim d_{\mu}\right].

Q Function

Since
J_{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{d_{\mu}\!}\!\left[r(S,A)\right]
, there exists a solution of the following Poisson equation or Bellman equation
(
Douc et al., 2018
, Section 21.2)

Q_{\mu}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for each
(s,a)\in{\mathcal{X}}
, and for
P_{2}
given in (
27
),

\displaystyle r(s,\!a)\!-\!J_{\mu}=~

\displaystyle Q_{\mu}(s,a)-\!\sum\limits_{\!(\!s^{\prime}\!,a^{\prime}\!)\in{\mathcal{X}}\!}\!P_{2}((s,a)),\!(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}))Q_{\mu}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

(31)

Let
Q_{\mu}
be a solution to (
31
). Then,
Q_{\mu}+c{\bf e}
for any constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
is also a solution. Let
Q^{*}_{\mu}
denote the solution normalized so that it is orthogonal to
{\bf e}
, i.e.,
Q^{*T}_{\mu}{\bf e}=0
.

Remark 5.2
.

Note that the equivalent formulations for
\kappa_{\mu}
from Proposition
1.1
also hold with
r
,
J_{\mu}
,
Q_{\mu}
, and
d_{\mu}
replacing
f
,
\bar{f}
,
V
, and
\pi
, respectively.

In this section, our goal is to estimate the asymptotic variance associated with the Markov chain induced by a given stationary policy, i.e., estimate
\kappa_{\mu}
. Note that this goal is orthogonal to that of estimating the variance of iterates of any particular learning algorithm like temporal-difference (TD), or designing algorithms with minimum variance of the iterates of the algorithm, studied extensively in
Devraj and Meyn (2017)
;
Yin and Wang (2020)
;
Chen et al. (2020a)
;
Hao et al. (2021)
; see Section
1.2
for a discussion and an example highlighting this distinction.

5.2
Motivation for Asymptotic Variance

As discussed in the beginning of Section
5
, different notions of variance have been considered in the risk-sensitive RL literature. We now motivate the choice of asymptotic variance in a risk-sensitive setting

First, in the average reward setting, a classical goal is to optimize the long-term expected cumulative reward, which corresponds to expectation of the random variable
\sum_{k}r(S_{k},A_{k})
. The asymptotic variance considered in this work, corresponds to the variance of the same random variable. Since both the mean and variance of
\sum_{k}r(S_{k},A_{k})
summed up to
n
terms are
O(n)
, we equivalently consider
\frac{1}{n}
scaling of both the mean and variance to arrive at
J_{\mu}
and
\kappa_{\mu}
in (
28
) and (
30
), respectively.

Second, consider an investor receiving return
r(S_{k},A_{k})
on an investment at each time
k
. The total return of the investor in
n
steps is
\sum_{k=1}^{n}r(S_{k},A_{k})
. A risk-averse investor would aim to maximize the average cumulative return subject to its variance being small. The variance on the return at each time is
\mathbb{E}_{d_{\mu}\!}\!\left[(r(S,A)-J_{\mu})^{2}\right]
. If the sequence
\left\{r(S_{k},A_{k})\right\}_{k\geq 1}
were i.i.d. according to
d_{\mu}
, the variance of total reward over
n
steps is

n\mathbb{E}_{d_{\mu}\!}\!\left[(r(S,A)-J_{\mu})^{2}\right]
.
This corresponds to the first term in (
4
). However, since the returns are Markovian, and thus are correlated across time steps, this term captures only the variance over individual times, and does not capture the correlations across time steps. The second term in (
4
) captures the covariance of returns across time, and so the variance of the total reward
\sum_{k=1}^{n}r(S_{k},A_{k})
is approximately
n\kappa_{\mu}
. In other words, the per step variance can be modeled by
\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}[\sum_{k=1}^{n}r(S_{k},A_{k})]=\kappa_{\mu}
.

Finally, it is important to emphasize that prior research on variance-constrained average reward RL, cf.
Prashanth and Ghavamzadeh (2016)
, treats the first term in (
4
) as a surrogate for variance. In a setting where the state sequence is independent, the second term is zero, while in a Markovian context, the correlation between time steps is non-negligible, and should not be overlooked.

5.3
Related Literature

In this subsection, we briefly discuss the RL literature that is relevant to our work.

MDPs: Average Reward and Risk-sensitivity

MDPs have a long history. We refer the reader to classical books
Puterman (1994)
;
Bertsekas (2012)
for a textbook introduction to MDPs in general, and to
Howard (1960)
;
Blackwell (1962)
;
Brown (1965)
;
Veinott (1966)
;
Arapostathis et al. (1993)
for an introduction to average-reward MDPs, in particular. Risk-sensitive objectives have also been well-studied in the MDP setting. For instance, see
Sobel (1982)
;
Filar et al. (1995)
;
Mannor and Tsitsiklis (2013)
for variance in discounted and average reward MDPs,
Borkar and Meyn (2002)
;
Borkar and Jain (2010)
;
Whittle (1990)
for the exponential utility formulation,
Ruszczyński (2010)
for Markov risk measures,
Chow and Ghavamzadeh (2014)
for conditional value-at-risk (CVaR).
However, in a MDP setting, algorithms require complete knowledge of the underlying model, which may not be feasible in many practical applications.

Risk-neutral RL

In the risk-neutral RL setting, expected value is the sole objective. TD type algorithms have been proposed for policy evaluation in discounted as well as average reward settings, and their asymptotic convergence is shown in
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy (1996)
;
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy (1999)
, respectively. TD algorithms have also been used in actor-critic style algorithms for solving the problem of control, cf.
Konda and Tsitsiklis (2003)
;
Bhatnagar et al. (2009)
. Asymptotic convergence of the classical Q-learning algorithm was established in
Borkar and Meyn (2000)
;
Tsitsiklis (1994)
. In the non-asymptotic regime, finite-sample mean-square convergence bounds for classical discounted setting algorithms such as TD, TD(
\lambda
), n-step TD, and Q-learning, have been developed in
Chen et al. (2021)
. On the other hand, in the average reward setting, finite-sample bounds for TD are derived in
Zhang et al. (2021)
.

Risk-sensitivity in RL

In a risk-sensitive optimization setting, the goal is either to optimize the usual expected value objective, while factoring a risk measure in a constraint, or optimize a risk measure in the objective. Tail-based risk measures such as variance, CVaR are meaningful to consider as a constraint, while risk measures such as exponential utility and prospect theory can be considered as the optimization objective, since they consider the entire distribution. In the context of RL, variance as a risk measure has been studied earlier in a discounted reward MDP setting in
Mihatsch and Neuneier (2002)
;
La and Ghavamzadeh (2013)
, in a stochastic shortest path and discounted settings
Tamar and Mannor (2013)
;
Tamar et al. (2013)
;
Tamar et al. (2012)
;
Tamar et al. (2016)
, and in an average reward MDP setting in
Prashanth and Ghavamzadeh (2016)
. Exponential utility has been explored in an average reward RL setting earlier, see
Borkar (2010)
for a survey and
Moharrami et al. (2022)
for a recent contribution.
Huang et al. (2021)
;
Ding and Feinberg (2022)
;
Köse and Ruszczyński (2021)
study CVaR as a risk measure in the discounted setting. Other risk measures such as more general coherent risk measures and cumulative prospect theory have been explored in an RL setting in the literature, and some representative works include
Prashanth (2014)
;
Prashanth et al. (2016)
;
Markowitz et al. (2023)
;
Ruszczynski and Yang (2023)
.

Remark 5.3

Unlike in the discounted RL setting, we point out that the operators in the average reward RL framework are not contractive under any norm. In fact, as in

Remark 5.4

6
Conclusions and Future Work

We proposed a novel recursive estimator for the asymptotic variance of a Markov chain that requires
O(1)
computation at each step, does not require storing historical samples, and enjoys the optimal
O(\frac{1}{n})
rate of convergence for MSE. We use Stochastic Approximation to design the proposed estimator. Using Lyapunov drift arguments, and Poisson equation to handle the Markov noise in analysis, we established finite sample bounds on the performance of the proposed estimator. We generalized it in several directions, including covariance matrix estimation for vector-valued functions of a Markov chain and estimating the asymptotic variance of a given stationary policy in an average reward RL framework. Our tighter analysis using Poisson equations to handle the Markov noise improves the MSE bound for linear SA in presence of Markov noise, in general, and for policy evaluation problem in RL, in particular. We established the
{O}(\frac{1}{n})
rate of convergence of the proposed algorithm in the tabular and linear function approximation settings. We also characterized the approximation error in the latter setting. Below, we discuss a few directions for future research.

•

We discuss the dependence of the sample complexity in terms of the underlying Markov chain parameters such as the size of state space
S
and mixing time parameter
\Delta_{1}
in Corollary
2.1.1
. Deriving lower bounds on the sample complexity in terms of these problem dependent parameters is an important direction that remains open. Tight lower bounds could potentially serve as a guide to designing estimators with improved dependence on these parameters. This will be useful for arriving at efficient estimators in settings where
S
is large, for example, in modern RL applications.

•

Our estimator with diminishing step size, for example in Algorithm
1
with
\alpha_{k}=O(\frac{1}{k})
, uses multiple SA updates at each time, each with a step size differing from the other by only a constant (
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, and
c_{3}
). One of the limitations of this so-called single-time scale approach is the constraints on step sizes
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, and
c_{3}
in order to achieve
O(\frac{1}{n})
MSE bound. This is a well known limitation of single-time scale SA which can be overcome by using a multi-time scale SA. In multi-time scale SA, each SA update uses a different step size (in terms of its dependence on
k
at time
k
). However, deriving finite sample guarantees for multi-time scale SA is a challenging direction. Designing an estimator for
\kappa(f)
that uses multiple time scales, with finite sample guarantees on MSE is an interesting open direction for future research.

•

As we saw in Section
3
, when the state space is large, one approach to estimating
\kappa(f)
is to approximate the value function
V
using a given approximation architecture (linear in our setting), and use this approximate value function to arrive at an estimate for an approximation of
\kappa(f)
, which we call
\kappa^{*}
. In general, this approach and our LFA results can extend easily for nice enough infinite state Markov chains. However, a big challenge is to get an exact estimation for
\kappa(f)
in these settings. Even in finite but large state space settings, getting an exact estimation for
\kappa(f)
remains open.

•

In Section
1.2
, we mentioned that the asymptotic behavior of regenerative simulation based estimators for
\kappa(f)
has been extensively studied in literature. CLTs for these estimators have been proven, which can be used to establish an optimal asymptotic rate of
O(\frac{1}{n})
in MSE. Investigating the finite sample behavior of these estimators and comparing its performance to our SA-based estimator is an interesting direction for future research.

•

On the RL front, an immediate future direction is to design an actor-critic style algorithm for identifying a policy that maximizes the long-run average reward subject to an asymptotic variance constraint with provable finite sample guarantees. Our policy-evaluation algorithm (Algorithm
3
or Algorithm
4
) can serve as a building block for designing the critic in these algorithms.

B.2
Variance of Functions of a Discrete Time Markov Chain (DTMC)

Recall that
X=(X_{k}:k\geq 0)
is an irreducible and aperiodic DTMC on a finite-state space
{\mathcal{S}}
with transition probability matrix
P
and a unique stationary distribution
\pi
. Let
f:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any function, and let
\bar{f}:=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi(x)f(x)
denote its stationary expectation. Let
V
denote the solution to the Poisson equation for
f-\bar{f}
, i.e., for each
x\in{\mathcal{S}}
,
V
satisfies

V(x)-\sum\nolimits_{x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}P(x,x^{\prime})V(x^{\prime})=f(x)-\bar{f}.

Lemma B.1
.

Consider the Markov Chain starting in distribution
\pi_{0}
. The asymptotic variance of
f
,
\kappa(f)
, is a constant independent of the starting distribution of the Markov Chain
\pi_{0}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\kappa(f):=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}~\operatorname{Var}\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}f(X_{k})|X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}\right]=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}~\operatorname{Var}\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}f(X_{k})|X_{0}\sim\pi\right].

Proof.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, let
\bar{f}_{k}:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[f(X_{k})\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(X_{k})|X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}\right]
. Then,

\displaystyle\kappa(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}~\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[\left(\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}_{k}\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}_{k})^{2}\right]+\frac{2}{n}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i<k}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[\left(f(X_{i})-\bar{f}_{i}\right)\left(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}_{k}\right)\right].

(51)

Clearly,

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}_{k})^{2}\right]=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}~\sum\limits_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[(f(X_{t})-\bar{f})^{2}\right].

This follows from the following:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}_{k})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\frac{1}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})^{2}\right]-\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\frac{1}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}(\bar{f}-\bar{f}_{k})^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\frac{1}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})^{2}\right],

where we used that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}(\bar{f}-\bar{f}_{k})^{2}=0,

which follows from geometric mixing of the underlying Markov chain.

Let us now show that the second term in (
51
) converges to the right limit. Towards this, we first re-write it as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\frac{2}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i<k}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[(f(X_{i})-\bar{f})(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})\right]+(\bar{f}_{i}-\bar{f})(\bar{f}-\bar{f}_{k})\right),

and let the corresponding term under
\pi
be

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\frac{2}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i<k}\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[(f(X_{i})-\bar{f})(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})\right].

Let

T_{1}:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[(f(X_{i})-\bar{f})(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})\right]\quad\text{ and }\quad T_{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[(f(X_{i})-\bar{f})(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})\right].

Then,

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\frac{2}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i<k}(T_{1}-T_{2})+\frac{2}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i<k}(\bar{f}_{i}-\bar{f})(\bar{f}-\bar{f}_{k})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i<k}\left|{T_{1}-T_{2}}\right|+\frac{2}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i<k}\left|{\bar{f}_{i}-\bar{f}}\right|\left|{\bar{f}-\bar{f}_{k}}\right|

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\frac{2}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i<k}\left|{T_{1}-T_{2}}\right|+\frac{2}{n}~\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i<k}4f^{2}_{\max}C\alpha^{k},

(53)

where we used Lemma
B.4
to get the bound in
(a)
above, and recall that
f_{\max}:=\max_{x}f(x)
. Clearly, the second term converges to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Let us now bound the first term.

\displaystyle\left|{T_{1}-T_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\sum\limits_{y\in{\mathcal{S}}}\sum\limits_{z\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\pi_{0}(x)-\pi(x)\right)P^{i}(x,y)P^{k-i}(y,z)(f(y)-\bar{f})(f(z)-\bar{f})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 4f^{2}_{\max}\sum\limits_{z\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left|{\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\sum\limits_{y\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\pi_{0}(x)-\pi(x)\right)P^{i}(x,y)P^{k-i}(y,z)}\right|

\displaystyle=4f^{2}_{\max}\sum\limits_{z\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left|{\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\pi_{0}(x)P^{k}(x,z)-\pi(x)P^{k}(x,z)\right)}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{z\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left|{\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi_{0}(x)P^{k}(x,z)-\pi(z)}\right|f^{2}_{\max}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{z\in{\mathcal{S}}}\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi_{0}(x)\left|{P^{k}(x,z)-\pi(z)}\right|f^{2}_{\max}

\displaystyle=2L^{2}\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi_{0}(x)\|P^{k}(x,\cdot)-\pi\|_{\text{T.V.}}

\displaystyle\leq 2f^{2}_{\max}C\alpha^{k}.

Using the above bound in (

), we get that
\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|{\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\right|\leq 0
, proving the desired result.

Lemma B.2
.

Consider the Markov Chain starting in distribution
\pi_{0}
. For
j\in\mathbb{N}
and

\gamma(k):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[\left(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}\right)\left(f(X_{0})-\bar{f}\right)\right],

the asymptotic variance of

\displaystyle\kappa(f)

\displaystyle=\gamma(0)+2\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\gamma(k)=2\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi(x)(f(x)-\bar{f})V(x)-\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi(x)(f(x)-\bar{f})^{2}.

Proof.

From Lemma
B.1
, we have

\displaystyle\kappa(f):=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}f(X_{k})|X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}f(X_{k})|X_{0}\sim\pi\right].

We show that the r.h.s. in the above equation is same as the expression in the lemma. Recall that for any
k\geq 1
,
\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(X_{k})|X_{0}\sim\pi\right]=\bar{f}
. For simplicity of notation, we denote
\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[\cdot\right]
by
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[\cdot\right]

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}f(X_{k})|X_{0}\sim\pi\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[\left(\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum\limits_{i<k}\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[\left(f(X_{i})-\bar{f}\right)\left(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[\left(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}\right)\left(f(X_{0})-\bar{f}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=n\gamma(0)+2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)\gamma(k),

(54)

where
(a)
follows from Markov property. Dividing by

and taking limits, we get

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}f(X_{k})|X_{0}\sim\pi\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma(0)+\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{n-k}{n}\gamma(k)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\gamma(0)+\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\gamma(k)

(55)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}2\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{n-k}{n}\gamma(k)-\gamma(0)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}2\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma(k)-\gamma(0),

(56)

where
(a)
and
(b)

Since

for any constant

\displaystyle 2\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi(x)(f(x)-\bar{f})V(x)-\gamma(0)

\displaystyle=2\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi(x)(f(x)-\bar{f})\left(\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(X_{k})-\bar{f}|X_{0}=x\right]\right)-\gamma(0)

Lemma B.3
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gamma(k)
below,

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{n-k}{n}\gamma(k)=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma(k),

where

\gamma(k):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})(f(X_{0})-\bar{f})\right].

Proof.

The proof of this lemma follows along the lines of the proof of
[
Mou and Maguluri, 2020
, Lemma 3]
. However, we give the proof for completeness.
Let
f_{\max}:=\max\limits_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}f(x)
. Since
X
is an irreducible and aperiodic Markov chain on a finite state space, it mixes geometrically fast
Levin and Peres [2017]
, i.e., there exist constants
C>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,

\sup\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}~d_{TV}(P(X_{k}|X_{0}=x),\pi)\leq C\alpha^{k},

where
d_{TV}(\cdot,\cdot)
represents the total variation distance between the two input distributions. Below, we first show that
\gamma(k)
is bounded.

\displaystyle\left|{\gamma(k)}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[\left(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}\right)\left(f(X_{0})-\bar{f}\right)\right]}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\mathbb{E}_{\pi\!}\!\left[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}\right)\left(f(X_{0})-\bar{f}\right)\right]|X_{0}=x\right]}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi\!}\!\left[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}\right)|X_{0}=x\right]\left(f(x)-\bar{f}\right)\right]}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi\!}\!\left[\left|{\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(f(X_{k})-\bar{f}\right)|X_{0}=x\right]}\right|\left|{f(x)-\bar{f}}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 4f^{2}_{\max}C\alpha^{k},

(57)

where we used Lemma
B.4
to get the last inequality above.

Next, define

V_{1}:=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma(k),\quad\text{ and }\quad V_{2}:=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{n-k}{n}\gamma(k).

Consider the following:

\displaystyle\left|{V_{1}-V_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|{\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{k}{n}\gamma(k)}\right|\leq\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{k}{n}\left|{\gamma(k)}\right|\overset{(a)}{\leq}4f^{2}_{\max}C\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}k\alpha^{k}\leq 0,

Lemma B.4
.

Let
X=(X_{k}:k\geq 0)
be DTMC on a finite-state space
{\mathcal{S}}
with a unique stationary distribution
\pi
. Let
f:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any function, and let
\bar{f}:=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi(x)f(x)
denote its stationary expectation. Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, there exist constants
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
C>0
, such that for any initial distribution
X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}
, and
k>j
,

\left|{\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(X_{k})-\bar{f}|X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}\right]}\right|\leq 2f_{\max}C\alpha^{k},

and

\left|{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})(f(X_{j})-\bar{f})|X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}\right]}\right|\leq 4f^{2}_{\max}C^{2}\alpha^{k-j},

where
f_{\max}:=\max_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}f(x)
.

Proof.

This lemma follows from the geometric mixing of the underlying Markov chain. Parts of this lemma are borrowed from
Mou and Maguluri [2020]
.

Proving (
B.4
).
Consider the following inequalities:

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(X_{k})-\bar{f}|X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}\right]}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\sum\limits_{x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi_{0}(x^{\prime})\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}f(x)\left(\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=x|X_{0}=x^{\prime}\right)-\pi(x)\right)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi_{0}(x^{\prime})\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left|{f(x)}\right|\left|{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=x|X_{0}=x^{\prime}\right)-\pi(x)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq f_{\max}\sum\limits_{x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\pi_{0}(x^{\prime})\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left|{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=x|X_{0}=x^{\prime}\right)-\pi(x)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq f_{\max}~\sup\limits_{x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}~\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\left|{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{k}=x|X_{0}=x^{\prime}\right)-\pi(x)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2f_{\max}C\alpha^{k},

where the last inequality follows from the definition of total variation distance and geometric mixing of the underlying Markov chain.

Proving (
B.4
).
We now prove the other inequality using the geometric mixing of the Markov chain. Let
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[\cdot\right]
represent
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\cdot|X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}\right]
.

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})(f(X_{j})-\bar{f})\right]}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[(f(X_{j})-\bar{f})\mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})|X_{j}\right]\right]}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[|f(X_{j})-\bar{f}|\right]\left|{\sup\limits_{x\in{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})|X_{j}=x\right]}\right|.

(60)

Since (
B.4
) holds for any initial distribution
\pi_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|{\sup_{x}\mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})|X_{j}=x\right]}\right|=\sup_{x}\mathbb{E}\!\left[(f(X_{k-j})-\bar{f})|X_{0}=x\right]\leq 2f_{\max}C\alpha^{k-j}.

Using the above bound in (
60
),

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[(f(X_{k})-\bar{f})(f(X_{j})-\bar{f})\right]}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2f_{\max}C\alpha^{k-j}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}\!}\!\left[\left|{(f(X_{j})-\bar{f})}\right|\right]\leq 4f^{2}_{\max}C\alpha^{k-j},

proving the desired result.

∎

B.3
Discussion on Batched Mean Estimators

A natural first approach to estimate
\kappa(f)
is to use the Monte Carlo (MC) technique. For this, observe that

\kappa=\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\kappa_{m},\quad\text{where}\quad\kappa_{m}:=\operatorname{Var}[Z_{m}|X_{0}\sim\pi],\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{m}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}f(X_{k}).

A vanilla MC estimator, popularly known as Batched Means (BM) estimator, proceeds by generating a single large trajectory of the underlying Markov chain
\mathcal{M}
starting from any initial distribution
\pi_{0}
on
{\mathcal{S}}
. It then considers samples in non-overlapping buckets of a fixed length, say
m
. For
i\in\mathbb{N}
, let

Z^{(i)}_{m}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum\limits_{k=(i-1)m}^{i(m-1)}f(X_{k}).

BM method estimates the first and second moments of
Z_{m}
, defined in (
B.3
), using sample averages of
Z^{(i)}_{m}
across
b
buckets, say, and combines them to get an estimate for
\kappa_{m}
.

The asymptotic convergence of BM (as
b\rightarrow\infty
) to
\kappa_{m}
follows from the Ergodic Theorem for Markov chains
[
Douc et al., 2018
, Theorem 5.2.1]
. However, a drawback of BM is that approximating the limit in
\kappa
by the variance of a truncated random variable
Z_{m}
introduces a bias, which corresponds to
\left|{\kappa-\kappa_{m}}\right|
.
Chien et al. [1997]
established that the mean-squared estimation error for estimating
\kappa
using BM is
O(\frac{1}{m^{2}}+\frac{1}{b})
, where the
O(\frac{1}{m^{2}})
term corresponds to the bias. Thus, estimating
\kappa
with mean-squared estimation error at most
\epsilon^{2}
requires
m=O(\frac{1}{\epsilon})
, and
b=O(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}})
. This corresponds to a total of
mb=O(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{3}})
samples. While this bound can be improved by designing better MC estimators, see
Flegal and Jones [2010]
, MC estimators typically also suffer from being non-adaptive (or non-recursive), i.e.,
m
is a function of
\epsilon
- this necessitates restarting the procedure for different choices of
\epsilon
.

C.4
Estimating Variance: IID Setting

Consider
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
, independent samples from a distribution with mean
0
and an unknown variance
\sigma^{2}
. Further assume that the sampling distribution has bounded
4^{th}
moment. The goal is to estimate
\sigma^{2}
using these samples.

Since, in this setting,
\operatorname{Var}\left[X\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[X^{2}\right]
, let

\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}X^{2}_{j}

denote the estimate for
\sigma^{2}
using
n
samples. Clearly,
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}\right]=\sigma^{2}
, i.e.,
\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}
is an unbiased estimator.

Next, consider the following

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}-\sigma^{2})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n})^{2}+\sigma^{4}-2\sigma^{2}\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n})^{2}\right]-\sigma^{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}X^{4}_{j}+\sum\limits_{i\neq j}X^{2}_{i}X^{2}_{j}\right]-\sigma^{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[X^{4}\right]}{n}+\frac{n(n-1)\sigma^{4}}{n^{2}}-\sigma^{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[X^{4}\right]-\sigma^{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{1}}{n},

where
c_{1}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[X^{4}\right]-(\mathbb{E}\!\left[X^{2}\right])^{2}
. Thus, we have that the mean-squared estimation error in this setting is exactly
O(\frac{1}{n})
. In fact, Cramér-Rao lower bound
Nielsen [2013]
for the mean-squared error in estimating
\sigma^{2}
using an unbiased estimators is
O(\frac{1}{n})
, establishing that this rate cannot be improved in certain settings.

For
\sigma>0
, let
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
denote the Gaussian distribution with variance
\sigma^{2}
. If
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
,
c_{1}
equals
3\sigma^{2}
, and in this case we have that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}-\sigma^{2}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{n},\quad\text{ for $c>0$}.

Appendix G
Auxiliary Technical Lemmas

Lemma G.1
.

For any
\alpha>0
and
h\geq 2
, for all
k\geq 1
, and
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\alpha}{k+h}
or
\alpha_{k}=\alpha
, we have

\alpha_{k-1}-\alpha_{k}\leq\frac{3\alpha^{2}_{k}}{\alpha}.

Proof.

The result is trivially true for
\alpha_{k}
being a constant (
\alpha_{k}=\alpha
). Hence, we only provide a proof for the setting of a diminishing step-size (
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\alpha}{k+h}
). To this end, consider the following:

\displaystyle\alpha_{k-1}-\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{k+h-1}-\frac{\alpha}{k+h}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{(k+h)(k+h-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}_{k}}{\alpha\left(1-\frac{1}{k+h}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}_{k}}{\alpha}\left(1+\frac{2}{k+h}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\alpha^{2}_{k}}{\alpha},

where we used
\frac{1}{1-x}\leq 1+2x
for
x\leq\frac{1}{2}
in the first inequality above.
∎

Lemma G.2
.

For any
\alpha>0
, and either
\alpha_{k}=\alpha
for all
k\geq 0
, or for
h\geq 2
,
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\alpha}{k+h}
, for all
k\geq 0
, we have

\alpha_{k}\alpha_{k-1}\leq 2\alpha^{2}_{k}.

Proof.

This is trivially true for
\alpha_{k}=\alpha
. Below, we show for diminishing step sizes of the form
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\alpha}{k+h}
, for
h\geq 2
. Consider the following:

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}\alpha_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}_{k}}{1-\frac{1}{k+h}}\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}_{k}}{1-\frac{1}{h}}\leq 2\alpha^{2}_{k},

proving the lemma.
∎

Lemma G.3
.

For
b\geq 0
, and
c\geq 0
,

\min\limits_{x\in[0,c]}\left\{ax-b\sqrt{cx-x^{2}}\right\}=\frac{c}{2}\left(a-\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Let
f(x):=ax-b\sqrt{cx-x^{2}}
. Let
f^{\prime}(x)
denote the derivative of
f(x)
and
f^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)
denote the corresponding second derivative, both evaluated at
x
. Then,

f^{\prime}(x)=a-\frac{b(c-2x)}{2\sqrt{cx-x^{2}}},

and
f^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)>0
for
b\geq 0
. Solving for x such that
f^{\prime}(x)=0
, we get
x_{1}
defined below as the optimizer.

x_{1}:=\frac{c}{2}\left(1-\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}\right),

and
f(x_{1})
is the desired optimal value.
∎
