Real-world outcomes of mosunetuzumab use in relapsed/refractory indolent lymphomas: a CUBIC consortium analysis
Authors: Andrew P. Jallouk, Lorenzo Falchi, Ayushi Chauhan, Geoffrey Shouse, Swetha K. Thiruvengadam, Yazeed Sawalha, Sonia Godbole, Ajay Major, Reid W. Merryman, Shakthi T. Bhaskar, Allison M. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Mosun demonstrated high efficacy and low toxicity in a real-world population containing high-risk patients with indolent lymphomas.•No significant differences in response or survival were noted between trial-eligible and trial-ineligible patients treated with mosun.

Introduction

Bispecific antibodies have recently emerged as an effective treatment option for relapsed/refractory B-cell lymphomas.1 Mosunetuzumab (mosun) is a CD20 × CD3 bispecific antibody that is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration as a single agent for the treatment of relapsed/refractory follicular lymphoma after 2 lines of systemic therapy. This approval was based on results of a phase 2 study, which demonstrated a significantly improved complete response rate (CRR) compared with historical cohorts treated with the phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase inhibitor copanlisib.2,3 In this study, mosun was given as a fixed-duration therapy, with the potential to stop therapy after 8 cycles in patients obtaining a complete response.

Yet, despite their efficacy, bispecific antibodies may be associated with toxicities including cytokine release syndrome (CRS), immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), and severe infections. The optimal management of these toxicities remains uncertain, and safety concerns may limit access to bispecific antibodies in the community. Clinical trials have generally reported lower rates of these toxicities with mosun than with other CD20 × CD3 bispecific antibodies or chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy.4, 5, 6, 7 However, there are limited data available to assess the safety and efficacy of mosun therapy in a real-world setting, particularly in patients with poor performance status or comorbidities, which would render them ineligible for clinical trial participation.

Here, we present a real-world analysis of mosun use in relapsed/refractory indolent B-cell lymphomas using data from the Collaborative US Bispecifics Consortium (CUBIC).

Methods

Study design

We performed a multicenter retrospective study of real-world patients treated at 9 centers participating in the CUBIC. Patients aged >18 years with a diagnosis of non-Hodgkin lymphoma who received at least 1 dose of mosun as part of a standard-of-care treatment were considered eligible. Patients who received mosun as part of a clinical trial were excluded. Data cutoff was in September 2025, and all patients treated before that time were included in the analysis.

All centers obtained approval from their respective institutional review boards, and all studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Definitions

Tumor histology and patient clinical characteristics were determined locally at each institution. Prognostic scoring used the Follicular Lymphoma International Prognostic Index for patients with follicular lymphoma.8 POD24 was defined as progression of disease within 24 months of frontline treatment. Potential trial eligibility was determined by comparing laboratory and performance status data of each patient at the time of mosun initiation to the inclusion/exclusion criteria for the mosun registrational trial in follicular lymphoma.2 Specifically, trial eligibility was defined as follows: Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0 or 1, aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase ≤ 3× the upper limit of normal, total bilirubin ≤ 1.5× the upper limit of normal, platelet count ≥75 × 103/μL, absolute neutrophil count ≥1 × 103/μL, total hemoglobin ≥10 g/dL, and estimated creatinine clearance ≥60 mL/min. Patients who did not meet all of these criteria were considered trial ineligible.

Response assessment was performed locally per the Lugano 2014 criteria9 as the best response to mosun. Overall response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of patients achieving either a complete response or a partial response. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from mosun initiation to disease progression or death. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from mosun initiation to death. Toxicity grading and management, including the assessment of CRS and ICANS, were performed according to institutional guidelines.

Statistics

Descriptive statistics were calculated when applicable. For categorical variables, a χ2 test or Fisher exact test was used to compare patients for whom relevant data were available. For continuous variables, a Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare patient subgroups. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and patient subgroups were compared using a log-rank test. Multivariable survival analysis was performed using a Cox proportional hazards model, with age, sex, ECOG status, previous CAR T-cell therapy, number of previous lines of therapy, refractoriness to previous treatment, and POD24 as covariates. Nonrelapse mortality was calculated using the Fine-Gray method, with disease relapse as a competing risk. A 2-tailed P value of <.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics software, v. 31.0.0.0.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 119 patients were identified who received standard-of-care mosun between June 2022 and July 2025. The median age was 67 years (range, 33-92), and 61% of patients were male. Patients had received a median of 3 lines of therapy (range, 1-14) before mosun. Ninety patients (75.6%) had a diagnosis of indolent lymphoma. The remaining 29 patients (24.3%) had a diagnosis of aggressive lymphoma. Of patients with aggressive lymphomas, 13 (45%) had diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, 15 (52%) had mantle cell lymphoma, and 1 (3%) had follicular lymphoma grade 3B. In these patients, mosun was given as a single agent in 8 patients (28%) and in combination with polatuzumab vedotin in the remaining 21 patients (72%). Due to the small number of patients with aggressive lymphomas, as well as the heterogeneity in disease subtype and treatment regimen, these patients were excluded from subsequent analyses.

Of these patients, 59 (66%) had follicular lymphoma grade 1 to 2; 17 (19%) had follicular lymphoma not otherwise specified; 9 (10%) had follicular lymphoma grade 3A; and 5 (6%) had other types of indolent lymphoma, including 2 patients with low-grade B-cell lymphoma not otherwise specified and 1 patient each with nodal marginal zone lymphoma, extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue, and lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma. Twenty-one percent of patients with follicular lymphoma were low risk by the Follicular Lymphoma International Prognostic Index at the time of mosun administration, whereas 27% were intermediate risk, and 52% were high risk. Forty-three patients (48%) were refractory to their last line of therapy, and 39 patients (43%) had POD24. Of the patients with indolent lymphomas treated with mosun, 97% did not receive any other systemic therapy. One patient received mosun in combination with lenalidomide, 1 patient received mosun in combination with cyclophosphamide, and 1 patient received mosun in combination with zanubrutinib and rituximab. Eighty-four percent of patients received their first cycle of mosun entirely outpatient. All patients received standard step-up dosing. The median number of cycles was 8 (range, 1-22). The most common reasons for treatment discontinuation were completion of planned therapy (61%) and disease progression (17%). Other documented reasons for treatment discontinuation were poor tolerance (3%), recurrent infections (2%), second primary malignancy (2%), stroke (1%), and intraabdominal hemorrhage (1%).

Response and survival data for patients with indolent lymphomas are shown in Table 2. In the 85 evaluable patients, the ORR was 87%, and the CRR was 71%. Importantly, the 20 trial-ineligible patients also demonstrated high response rates, with an ORR of 78%, CRR of 61%, and no statistically significant differences compared with trial-eligible patients (ORR, P = .150; CRR, P = 295). In the 18 evaluable patients who had received previous CAR T-cell therapy, ORR was 83%, CRR was 56%, and there were no significant differences compared with patients who had not received previous CAR T cells (ORR, P = .596; CRR, P = .115). With a median follow-up of 17.0 months (range, 0.8-30.5), the median PFS and OS were not reached (Figure 1). The 12- and 18-month PFS estimates were 70.5% and 56.7%, respectively, and the 12- and 18-month OS estimates were 95.3% and 92.9%, respectively. There were no significant differences between trial-eligible and ineligible patients in terms of PFS (P = .147) or OS (P = .132). There were also no significant differences in survival between patients who had received ≤2 or >2 previous lines of therapy (PFS, P = .131; OS, P = .313) and patients who had or had not received previous CAR T-cell therapy (PFS, P = .294; OS, P = .243). Patients with ECOG status ≥2 had significantly worse OS (P = .004) but no difference in response rates (ORR 80% [P = .492]; CRR 60% [P = .634]) or PFS (P = .403) compared with patients with ECOG status <2. A multivariate analysis showed no significant differences in PFS or OS based on age (PFS, P = .762; OS, P = .792), sex (PFS, P = .269; OS, P = .968), number of previous lines of therapy (PFS, P = .406; OS, P = .956), previous CAR T-cell therapy (PFS, P = .252; OS, P = .104), refractoriness to previous treatment (PFS, P = .073; OS, P = .414), or POD24 (PFS, P = .747; OS, P = .517). In this analysis, ECOG status ≥2 remained associated with significantly worse OS (hazard ratio, 39.9; P = .013) but not PFS (hazard ratio, 2.0; P = .302). The cumulative incidence of nonrelapse mortality at 18 months was 2.2%. Toxicity data for patients with indolent lymphomas are shown in Table 3. Thirty patients (33%) developed CRS of any grade, of which 25 (83%) had a maximum grade of 1. Three patients (3%) developed CRS with maximum grade 2, and 1 patient (1%) developed CRS with maximum grade 3. Median time to onset of CRS was 16 days after the start of mosun therapy, with a range of 1 to 28 days. No patient had onset of CRS after 28 days. The median duration of CRS was 4 days, with a range of 1 to 5 days. In 12 patients (40%), CRS was managed entirely in the outpatient setting. Management strategies for CRS varied widely across patients and institutions, with the most common agents used including various combinations of acetaminophen, steroids, tocilizumab, and antibiotics. Only 1 patient (1%) developed ICANS, which began 2 days after initiation of mosun. It was treated with a single dose of IV dexamethasone and resolved after one day. The maximum ICANS grade was 1. There were no significant differences in CRS or ICANS rates or severity between trial-eligible and ineligible patients. Three patients (3.3%) required an intensive care unit stay at some point during their treatment course. A total of 24 patients (27%) developed infections after mosun therapy. The most common infections reported were COVID-19 (7 patients), pneumonia (6 patients), rhinovirus/enterovirus (4 patients), and sinusitis (3 patients). There was a higher rate of infections in trial-ineligible patients than in trial-eligible patients (45% vs 21%; P = .043).

Discussion

Our analysis incorporates data from 9 treatment centers and is one of the largest real-world studies of mosun therapy in indolent lymphomas to date. Importantly, our data set includes a significant proportion of trial-ineligible patients, comprising 26% of patients. Despite this high-risk population, we observed response and survival outcomes that compared favorably with those previously reported.

In the 3-year follow-up of the mosun registrational trial for follicular lymphoma, the observed CRR was 60%, and median PFS was 24 months.10 In our data set, the observed CRR was 71%, median PFS was not reached with a median follow-up of 17.0 months, and the estimated 18-month PFS rate was 56.7%. The more favorable response rates in our study may be due in part to their determination by investigators instead of central reviewers as in the clinical trial. Similarly, the rate of any-grade CRS was 44% in the registrational trial and 33% in our data set, with most cases being low grade and confined to cycle 1 in both studies. Although it is difficult to draw conclusions regarding optimal CRS management given the heterogeneity in treatment patterns across patients and institutions, 40% of patients who developed CRS in our study did not require hospitalization. This finding is in line with recent recommendations that grade 1 CRS from bispecific antibodies may be managed as an outpatient with acetaminophen and dexamethasone.11 Our study also demonstrated a strikingly low rate of ICANS, with only one grade 1 ICANS event, which responded rapidly to dexamethasone. Only 3 patients reported poor tolerance as a reason for treatment discontinuation, with the majority of treatment discontinuations due to completion of planned therapy (61%) or progression of disease (17%). Together, these findings support the continued use of single-agent mosun as an effective treatment option for relapsed/refractory follicular lymphoma, with a safety profile that is amenable to outpatient administration.

Infections have emerged as an important toxicity associated with bispecific antibodies, particularly with extended treatment courses. Most infections in our study were viral (Covid or rhinovirus/enterovirus) with additional reports of pneumonia and sinusitis. Importantly, mosun is typically given as a fixed-duration therapy, and the median duration of treatment in our study was 8 cycles, with most patients stopping treatment due to planned completion of therapy. Longer follow-up is needed to determine how infection risk evolves with time after stopping mosun therapy.

In our study, we noted an overall infection rate of 27%, which was comparable to the 20% infection rate reported in the mosun registrational trial. However, trial-ineligible patients had significantly higher rates of infection than trial-eligible patients. Together, these findings highlight the importance of close monitoring of patients to detect and treat infectious complications. This is particularly true for patients with risk factors associated with trial ineligibility, including poor performance status and cytopenias. Prospective studies to determine the role of prophylactic interventions (eg, antimicrobials, IV immunoglobulin) in preventing infection in high-risk patients are also needed. However, it is important to note that despite their higher infection rate, trial-ineligible patients had response rates and survival outcomes comparable to trial-eligible patients, suggesting that they should not be excluded from receiving mosun therapy. Similarly, although patients with poor performance status (ECOG ≥2) had worse OS than patients with better performance status (ECOG <2), they demonstrated similar response rates and PFS, suggesting that they likely benefit from mosun therapy and should not be excluded.

Despite its large, multicenter, real-world nature, our study has important limitations. These include relatively short follow-up, limiting our ability to accurately assess durability of response. In addition, although no significant difference in response rates or survival outcomes was noted between trial-eligible and ineligible patients, the number of trial-ineligible patients was relatively small, and the study is underpowered to detect minor differences in outcomes between these groups. The small number of patients with aggressive lymphomas and heterogeneity in treatment patterns and disease subtype also precluded meaningful analysis of this subset. Furthermore, it is important to note that the retrospective real-world nature of this study precludes the use of a standardized protocol for toxicity management and that treatment decisions made by individual physicians may not always reflect contemporary guidelines or standardized drug dosing strategies. In addition, our consortium consists primarily of academic centers, whose practice patterns and toxicity management strategies may differ from community sites and whose patient populations may not be broadly representative of those seen in the community.

In conclusion, response rates and survival outcomes with mosun therapy in our real-world study compared favorably with those reported in clinical trials, despite the inclusion of a substantial number of trial-ineligible patients. Mosun was associated with low rates of ICANS and high-grade CRS, supporting its use in the outpatient setting. Infections remain an important complication of mosun therapy, particularly in trial-ineligible patients. Longer follow-up and analysis of mosun use in different practice settings will further inform clinical practice.
