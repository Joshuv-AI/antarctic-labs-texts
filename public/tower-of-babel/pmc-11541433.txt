A Comprehensive Methodology for Investigating Cocurrent Spontaneous Imbibition in Coal
Authors: Yuejian Lu, Dameng Liu, Yidong Cai, Meng Cui, Youping Liu, Yingfang Zhou, Weisong Yin, Yu Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Visualizing and quantifying fluid distribution during spontaneous imbibition at the nanomicro scale is vital for understanding microfluid flow and dynamic wettability in coalbed methane (CBM) reservoirs, which could serve as a fundamental basis for optimizing the parameters of the hydraulic fracturing process. In this study, fluid distribution and flow behavior can be acquired by combining nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and in situ X-ray microcomputed tomography (μ-CT) technologies. Meanwhile, spontaneous imbibition stages were studied to analyze gas–water exchange efficiency. Additionally, dynamic wettability of gas–water was calculated during the process of spontaneous imbibition based on NMR. The results show that imbibition characteristics can be divided into three categories based on NMR. In type I, changes in imbibition fluid within nanopores are not obvious, while significant changes occur in micro pore-fractures, especially during the early stage of imbibition (0–2 h) for type I, which is almost the opposite of type II. Coal samples of type III exhibit low porosity and permeability, making it difficult for water to flow through the pores in coal due to capillary forces. The dynamic process of spontaneous imbibition can be divided into four stages using μ-CT equipment, with the third stage displaying the highest imbibition efficiency. This stage is characterized by frequent imbibition fluid exchange among various-scale fractures, which is related to the wettability and pore-fracture of the coal sample. A negative correlation between contact angle and T2g was observed, where hydrophobic samples corresponded to a smaller geometric average of T2g in the sample. In addition, the wettability of coal samples changed dynamically during the process of imbibition, with the contact angle gradually decreasing as imbibition time increased, which may be related to the formation of water film and hydration reactions.

Introduction

Coalbed methane (CBM), an unconventional resource with self-generation and self- accumulation characteristics,1,2 is approximately 95% stored at the surface of nanomicro pores in the coal matrix in an adsorption state, while the remaining 5% is stored in fractures in a free state.3,4 As an unconventional resource, CBM has broad prospects for development;5 however, the unclear understanding of the gas–water seepage mechanisms in the complex pore-fracture structures of coal reservoirs, as well as the lack of theoretical knowledge and technical expertise, continue to hinder the commercial development of CBM.6,7 CBM production is characterized by a “low single-well gas production rate and fast decline in the gas production of well”. In its natural state,8,9 hydraulic fracturing is an important technology to enhance the production of CBM,10−12 as shown in Figure 1. During hydraulic fracturing, the low flow-back efficiency of drilling fluid and fracturing fluid (generally below 30%), along with the content of irreducible water in the pore-fractures of coal, directly affects gas–water relative permeability.9,13,14 Additionally, water blocking and the Jamin effect, as the main damage mechanisms in low-permeability CBM reservoirs, seriously influence the results of gas flow in the reservoir due to the hydrophilicity of the pores and cleats.15−17 Considering the presence of water in coal reservoirs, wettability in methane–water–coal mixtures is a basic factor controlling the interrelations among gas–water distribution, CH4 adsorption, and diffusion mechanisms. Meanwhile, due to the development of nanoscale pores in coal reservoirs, a portion of the fracturing fluid tends to remain trapped within the pores and fractures of the coal matrix as a result of wettability and capillary forces. This entrapment adversely affects the desorption, diffusion, and seepage of gas, thereby impacting the recovery efficiency of CBM.18,19

Spontaneous imbibition is a key factor affecting flow-back efficiency. It refers to the process in which a wetting fluid displaces a nonwetting fluid under the influence of capillary forces.22,23 While substantial advances have been made in the characterization of spontaneous imbibition in coal over the past decade,24−60 Yuan et al. demonstrated that the permeability damage rate to the reservoir caused by spontaneous imbibition is positively correlated with the contact angle of coal samples, based on nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments and Darcy’s law.27 Additionally, Lu et al. illustrated that the relative permeability of CBM in coal follows a negatively decreasing exponential curve with respect to spontaneous imbibition time using fractal theory.18 Wu et al. studied the effect of spontaneous water imbibition in coal on gas desorption and diffusion, and found that water has a negative influence on methane desorption in coal overall, except when the imbibition ratio is in the range of 2 to 10%.30 Ghanbari and Dehghanpour proposed that water saturation in coal reservoirs is directly proportional to gas production during the process of CBM production, with capillary forces serving as a beneficial factor for desorption and flow in high-rank CBM reservoirs.31 While substantial advances have been made in the investigation of spontaneous imbibition in coal over the past decade, most research was based on a single method or experiment. There has been little emphasis on the dynamic wettability during spontaneous imbibition, and our understanding of the fundamentals of multiphase fluid flow in coal remains limited due to its complexity.

In this research, NMR and X-ray microcomputed tomography (μ-CT) technologies were combined to investigate the fluid flow behavior in various coals. This research can be summarized as follows: (1) Visualizing and quantifying fluid distribution during spontaneous imbibition at the nanomicro scale in coal; (2) Calculating the change in the contact angle of coal due to imbibition based on NMR and investigating the dynamic wettability of coal; (3) Analyzing the advantages and disadvantages of two methods in studying fluid flow during the process of spontaneous imbibition in coal.

Materials and Methods

Coal Sampling and Basic Information

Here, six coal samples were collected from three coal-bearing areas: the Junggar Basin, Qinshui Basin, and Wudong coal mine. These six selected coal blocks were transported to the laboratories for detailed analysis, including porosity, permeability, LF-NMR, and X-ray μ-CT. Maximum reflectance of vitrinite (Ro, max) was carried out with a Leitz MPV-3 photometer microscope, following the standards of GB/T 6948-1998.32 Additionally, the mineral content, proximate analysis, and wettability of the coal samples were tested, with the basic information on the various coal samples presented in Table 1. The contact angle of all coal samples (in a water/air system) is below 90°, ranging from 15 to 81.6°, indicating that all samples exhibit strong water-wet characteristics. Among them, the WD sample shows the strongest wettability, while the WY sample has the weakest wettability. Before the X-ray μ-CT and NMR experiments, wire-electrode cutting equipment was used to pretreat cylindrical coal samples that were identical in geometry (25 mm diameter and 50 mm height).

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Measurements

NMR has been widely used to identify fluids in porous media.33−35 In this study, the MesoMR-060H–I analytical instrument was used for measurements at Suzhou Niumag Analytical Instrument Co., Suzhou, China at 25 °C and 0.1 MPa. Before initiating NMR measurements, six cylindrical coal samples (diameter = 25 mm, length= 50 mm) were subjected to a 24-h oven-drying process at 105 °C to eliminate any residual immobile water within the samples.36 In addition, the surface of the coal samples (except at both ends of the cylinder) was wrapped in a heat shrink film to reduce imbibition fluid evaporation. To observe the dynamic of spontaneous imbibition, six time points for each coal sample were designed (15 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 10 h, and 24 hours, respectively). The detailed steps of the spontaneous imbibition experiments and NMR measurements are described as follows:

(1) Place the sample: Pretreated coal samples were set in an acrylic plastic tube with an imbibition liquid for the spontaneous imbibition experiment, as shown in Figure 2a. The liquid was imbibed into the pores and fractures of the coal due to capillary force and gravity.

Schematic diagram of the experimental setup for (a) spontaneous imbibition and (b) NMR measurement.

(2) Stop spontaneous imbibition: The imbibition water was removed after 15 min of spontaneous imbibition. This step controls whether continuous imbibition occurs or not.

(3) Identify the imbibition fluid: Transfer the sample to the NMR sample cell and turn on the NMR equipment. Observe the signal amplitude of T2 to characterize the distribution of the spontaneous imbibition fluid, as presented in Figure 2b.

(4) Continue spontaneous imbibition: Reposition the sample in the acrylic plastic tube to allow imbibition to continue.

(5) Cyclic steps: In the final step, repeat steps (1) to (3) under the same conditions after imbibition for 1, 2, 4, 10, and 24 h successively for six coal samples. Analyze changes in the T2 spectra during the entire spontaneous imbibition process.

Methodologies for X-Ray μ-CT

The μ-CT measurement of the XY1 sample was performed at CAS (the Institute of Mechanics, Chinese Academy of Sciences) using a Nano Voxel-3000 X-ray three-dimensional microscope with a spiral scanning mode. The size and the pretreatment process of the coal are similar to those described in our previous work.18 To visualize the process of spontaneous imbibition more clearly, potassium iodide solution (KI) was used as the working liquid due to its high density,37,38 with a mass percent concentration of 10%. In this work, the scanning configuration conditions for the Nano Voxel-3000 X-ray three-dimensional microscope are detailed in Table 2. The resulting voxel size of the 2D slices was 14.5 μm, and a total of 3630 slices were obtained in each imbibition measurement.

According to Yao et al.,42 the internal pores of coal can be divided into three types based on the T2 spectrum. The first peak, the second peak, and third peak corresponds to micropores, mesopores, and macropores/fractures, respectively. The micropore serves as the adsorption pore for CH4, while the mesopore and macropore/fracture act as seepage pores for CH4 in coal.47 Water distribution in the pores of coal samples can be measured based on T2 curves from NMR experiments. Furthermore, the process of water transport in coal at different stages of spontaneous imbibition can be dynamically monitored by analyzing the NMR T2 curves at different imbibition times. Figure 4 shows T2 amplitude–T2 curves of six coal samples after spontaneous imbibition for 15 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 10 h, and 24 h, respectively. In this figure, three peaks (Peak 1, Peak 2, and Peak 3) are exhibited for five coal samples, while the WD sample shows only two peaks. Peak 1 is in the range of 0.01–1 ms accounting for more than 95.8% of the T2 spectral area (except for sample WD) approximately, which implies the abundant imbibition liquid in the adsorption pores (micropores) of coal. Peak 2 is in the range of 1 to 100 ms, which indicates the imbibition liquid flow into seepage pores (mesopores) of coal. Peak 3 ranges from 100 to 1000 ms, which reflects a small amount of imbibition liquid imbibed into seepage space (macropores and fractures). Coal reservoirs with a large number of micronano pores have strong absorbability for methane, and have low permeability for water, indicating that hydraulic fracturing technology is necessary to improve the flow-back rate of fracturing fluid during CBM production. For the WD sample’s T2 curves, only two peaks are observed, with Peak 3 accounting for approximately 36.3% of the T2 spectral area. This phenomenon is mainly due to the presence of a large number of irregularly shaped pores and well-developed microscale fractures, as shown in the ion polishing-SEM images in Figure 5, allowing water to imbibe quickly into the coal pathways. Peak 2 and Peak 3 are almost absent for the GJ, CC, and WZX samples, which may be attributed to the poorly developed micropores and fractures in coal.

During the process of spontaneous imbibition, various imbibition processes can be found among different coal samples, which can be classified into three types: type I (sample WD), type II (samples GJ, WY, and WZ), and type III (samples CC and WZX). For type I, imbibition fluid changes in nanopores are not obvious, and show significant changes in micropore-fractures as shown in Figure 4, especially at the early stage of imbibition (0–2 h). For type II, imbibition regularity is almost the opposite of type I, with abundant liquid transport among nanopores. Coal samples of type III are tight with low porosity and low permeability, making it difficult for water to flow through the pores in coal due to capillary forces.

Imbibition Water Flow Behavior in Coal

Fluid transport law has a profound influence on energy dynamic balance of the CBM system, which is in direct relation to the exploration efficiency of CBM, and thus it is necessary to quantitatively research fluid flow behavior at different spontaneous imbibition stages. In this section, NMR and dynamic X-ray μ-CT technology were adopted to research the characteristics of imbibition fluid at different scales of CBM enrichment spaces and migration channels. In addition, by comparing the changes in imbibition fluid across various pores and fractures in coal samples, the appropriate application scenarios for each technology are discussed. To better analyze the fluid behavior differences at various imbibition times, fluid-changed NMR curves were obtained by subtracting the results of two consecutive imbibition times (0 to 15 min, 15 min to 1 h, 1 to 2 h, 2 to 4 h, 4 to 10 h, 10 to 24 h) as shown in Figure 6. In this figure, the WD samples exhibit the largest fluid changes in different scale pores (with changed T2 amplitude exceeding 70 p.u.), and the peak value moves further to the right, indicating fluid imbibition into larger pores as the imbibition time progresses, although the movement is limited. For the last 20 h (4–24 h) of imbibition in the WD coal sample, the fluid-changed NMR curves change from two peaks to three peaks (Figure 6a), indicating more frequent fluid exchange between pores and fractures. During the process of spontaneous imbibition, three other coal samples exhibited similar imbibition characteristics. The change in imbibition fluid in adsorption pores showed a trend of slight increase while remaining stable for different imbibition stages for each coal sample, which is one order higher than that of the WD coal sample. This phenomenon is mainly related to the adsorption pore number, percentage of adsorption pores, and connectivity.

According to this study, obvious differences in fluid flow behavior can be observed between the results of NMR experiments and in situ dynamic X-ray μ-CT (ISD-CT) experiments. The detailed results are shown in Figure 7a1–a6. The actual imbibition volume at different imbibition times can be obtained by calculating the difference between the various curves shown in this figure. For a better understanding of the fluid changes in the spontaneous imbibition channels of coal, fractures in the coal samples were classified into three groups based on their length: type-I fracture (length ranges from 1 to 3.5 mm), type-II fracture (length ranges from 3.5 to 6.5 mm), and type- III fracture (length ranges from 6.5 to 10 mm), The fluid flow behavior in coal based on ISD-CT is presented in Figures 7b–5f. In these figures, the blue zone represents the imbibition fluid flowing into the fractures, while the red zone indicates the fluid flowing out of these fractures in the coal. In the primary stage of imbibition, fluid is only imbibed into microfractures (particularly type-I fractures), with no fluid exiting these microfractures. Imbibition fluid flows into larger fractures as spontaneous imbibition progresses. Based on the changes observed in fractures of various scales, the process of spontaneous imbibition can be summarized into the following four key stages:

(1) Water was introduced into small imbibition channels (pores and microfractures) quickly under the joint action of buoyancy and capillary power, as shown in Figure 7b.

(2) Fluid continues to enter into larger imbibition channels, including type-II and type-III fractures, as presented in Figure 7c.

(3) Various scale imbibition channels show imbibition water coming in and out, and imbibition fluid moves intermittently, as shown in Figures 7d,e.

(4) Part of the imbibition fluid was imbibed into larger imbibition channels (macrofractures) from small imbibition channels (microfractures); in addition, a thin water film was formed on the surface of the macrofractures.

These results show that using ISD-CT to investigate imbibition fluid in coal has several advantages, such as large-scale coverage and real-time processing, allowing for quantitative analysis of the dynamic fluid distribution in different pore-fractures. Compared to the CT method, NMR can characterize fluid behavior in smaller pores, from nanopores to microfractures; in addition, it is simple to carry out imbibition experiments with NMR, and no special treatment of the imbibition fluid is required.

Based on the results of Figure 8a and eq 7, when calculating T2g of various imbibition stages, dynamic wettability can be characterized during the process of coal spontaneous imbibition, as shown in Figure 8b. The results show that the wettability of coal samples changed dynamically during the process of imbibition, with the contact angle gradually decreasing as imbibition time increased. This change depends on two factors: the water film and the hydration reaction. On one hand, imbibition water is adsorbed onto the walls of fractures, forming a water film resulting from disjoining pressure developed under the long-distance van der Waals adhesion force,49 as shown in Figure 9, which improves its hydrophilicity. On the other hand, the imbibition water reacts with certain hydrophilic minerals50 (such as clay minerals, as shown in Figure 5), further increasing the wettability of coal. It is worth noting that the wettability of various coal samples shows different dynamic changes, and the contact angle of the WZ sample shows the largest decline (from 74.9 to 57.7°) up to 23%. The CC coal sample shows the smallest rate of decline at 4% (from 76.5 to 73.5°), which is mainly attributed to the pore-fracture structure and the distribution of minerals in coal.

Experimental investigation shows that both NMR and ISD-CT offer valuable insights into the analysis of fluid flow behavior in coal, each with its unique set of advantages and limitations. The advantage of NMR is that the fluid distribution in smaller pores can be detected, and it is less time-consuming and easy to operate. For ISD-CT, it can visualize the dynamic process of fluid transcription in the porous medium and improve the reliability of the optimum result. The choice between these techniques should be guided by specific research objectives, characteristics of the coal sample under investigation, and available resources. A complementary approach, leveraging the strengths of both methods, may provide the most comprehensive understanding of fluid migration in coal matrices.

Conclusions

This work presents a novel combination of experimental methods to simultaneously investigate the process of spontaneous imbibition in a CBM reservoir. The advantages and disadvantages of NMR and IND-CT technologies have been discussed in the context of analyzing fluid flow behavior in coal. In addition, fluid distribution within the coal and various imbibition stages were studied, and dynamic wettability of the coal was assessed during the process of spontaneous imbibition.
