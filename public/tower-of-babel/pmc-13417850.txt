Efficacy and safety of tocilizumab in the treatment of Graves’ orbitopathy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jianhui Chen, Yongjian Chen, Huibin Huang, Honghong Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis meta-analysis aimed to explore the safety and efficacy of tocilizumab (TCZ) for moderate to severe steroid-resistant active Graves’ orbitopathy (GO).DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis of 1 randomized controlled trial and 6 cohort studies.Materials and methodsWe searched Pubmed, EMBASE and The Cochrane Library for relevant studies published before June 1, 2022. The primary outcome indicator was the change in clinical activity score (CAS). Secondary outcome indicators were a reduction in proptosis and correction of diplopia. Random effects models were used for all analytical models. The mean difference (MD) and standardized mean difference (SMD) of the combined effect sizes of these outcome indicators were calculated for continuous variables. The odds ratio (OR) of the combined effect size of these outcome indicators was calculated for the dichotomous variables. The study was registered with PROSPERO, CRD 42,022,381,596.ResultSeven studies (n = 161) were included in the meta-analysis. The analysis showed that treatment of moderate to severe steroid-resistant active GO patients with TCZ resulted in improvements in CAS [SMD 4.25, 95% CI(2.94, 5.57), P < 0.00001], proptosis [MD 2.13, 95% CI (1.45, 2.80), P < 0.00001] and diplopia [OR 6.00, 95% CI(2.93, 12.30), P < 0.00001].ConclusionOur results strongly suggest that the use of TCZ for the treatment of patients with moderate to severe GO is very beneficial in reducing CAS, improving proptosis, and correcting diplopia. However, the incidence of serious adverse events varied among the included studies. Future randomized controlled trials (RCT) are necessary for further exploration.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.PROSPEROCRD42022381596.Keywords: Graves’ orbitopathy, Tocilizumab, Treatment, Meta-analysis, Monoclonal antibody

Background

Thyroid eye disease (TED), also referred to as Graves’ orbitopathy (GO) in earlier literature, is an organ-specific autoimmune disease that severely affects patients’ appearance and quality of life. GO is closely related to Graves’ disease (GD). Most GO patients present with hyperthyroidism, and GO is the most common extra-thyroidal symptom of GD. However, there is a small percentage of GO patients who present with hypothyroidism or normal thyroid function [1, 2]. Some studies have reported that 30–50% of patients with GD present with clinical manifestations of GO. The most common clinical manifestations of GO are upper eyelid retraction, edema of the periorbital tissues and conjunctiva, and proptosis [3]. In the later stages of the disease, about 3 to 5% of GO patients develop corneal ulceration or compressive optic neuropathy, symptoms that can lead to vision loss [3]. Therefore, effective treatment methods are crucial for GO patients.

The treatment used for GO patients varies depending on the stage of the disease. The 2021 European Group on Graves’ orbitopathy (EUGOGO) guidelines state [4], GO patients with hyperthyroidism should be routinely treated with antithyroid drugs. Therefore, antithyroid medication is also a means of treating GO patients. Mild GO can be treated with topical treatments and systemic adjuncts such as smoking cessation, the use of artificial tears (especially in the presence of dry eye), ocular gels and the use of selenium supplements to control risk factors. Although selenium is currently widely used to treat mild GO, there is still a lack of evidence that selenium supplementation is beneficial for moderate to severe and active GO. If the quality of life is significantly impaired in GO patients, low-dose immunosuppressive therapy (active GO) or rehabilitative surgery (inactive GO) may be given after detailed communication and joint decision-making between the physician and the patient. For patients with active moderate-to-severe TED, intravenous glucocorticoids remain a standard initial therapy, and the 2021 EUGOGO guideline recommends intravenous methylprednisolone in combination with mycophenolate as first-line medical treatment. The 2022 ATA/ETA joint consensus statement also recognizes intravenous glucocorticoids as a key therapy for active moderate-to-severe TED and should be considered when describing current management strategies [4]. However, the higher the dose of glucocorticoids used, the higher the frequency of adverse events, including some potentially serious events such as severe cardiovascular disease and liver failure. However, GO patients in the advanced stages of the disease usually do not respond to immunosuppressive therapies and often require surgical intervention due to severe orbital tissue remodeling and/or fibrosis. In addition to the above treatments, in recent years, as researchers have further understood the molecular mechanisms of GO, several drugs targeting GO orbital fibroblasts receptors or inflammatory cytokines have been developed, such as tocilizumab (anti-IL-6R), teprotumumab (anti-IGF-1R), rituximab (anti-CD20), Etanercept (anti-TNF-α), K1-70™ (anti-TSHR), etc.

Tocilizumab (TCZ), a humanized monoclonal antibody against IL-6R that blocks IL-6 activity and signaling, is approved for use in rheumatoid arthritis. IL-6 is released by T lymphocytes and macrophages and is involved in the inflammatory response of the GO orbit as a pro-inflammatory cytokine, which also promotes the expression of Thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor by GO orbital fibroblasts [5]. In addition, IL-6 can stimulate B-cell activation and proliferation and secretion of antibodies, thus aggravating the autoimmune response of GO [6]. Therefore, we hypothesized that TCZ could reduce the proliferation and differentiation of memory B cells, thus reducing the level of immunoglobulins and slowing down the disease progression of GO. TCZ has been documented to significantly improve disease activity and severity in GO patients [7, 8]. Recently, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) investigated the efficacy of TCZ for GO and showed that most patients showed improvement in clinical symptoms and a reduction in clinical activity score (CAS) [9]. However, the results of these experiments vary and the reference indicators selected are diverse, thus no uniform conclusions can be drawn. A previous meta-analysis investigated the efficacy and harms of TCZ in treating patients with GO [10]. However, it included only one RCT and the data from the RCT was not published at that time, so it failed to assess the effectiveness and safety of TCZ in treating GO patients. With the increasing awareness of TCZ among physicians and the recent publication of new relevant studies, it is necessary to conduct a meta-analysis on the effects produced by TCZ for GO to further evaluate the effectiveness and safety of TCZ and provide a basis for treatment options for GO.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This study was conducted in accordance with the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement [11]. Two independent reviewers (CJH and CYJ) conducted a comprehensive search through PubMed, Embase, and The Cochrane Library, with the last references search updated in June 2022. The search end date (June 1, 2022) was defined a priori for this review; therefore, studies published after this date were not included in the quantitative synthesis. This study was not subjected to any language restrictions in the references search. The initial search strategy was constructed based on a combination of medical subject terms and free words, using the following search terms: Tocilizumab or Atlizumab or Monoclonal antibody, MRA or BAT-1806 or MSB11456 or RG-1569 or R-1569 or RO-4,877,533 or Actemra or Roactemra or RHPM-1 AND Graves Ophthalmopathy or Ophthalmopathy, Graves or Ophthalmopathies, Thyroid-Associated or Thyroid-Associated Ophthalmopathies or Thyroid Eye Disease or Disease, Thyroid Eye or Eye Disease, Thyroid or Thyroid Eye Diseases or Thyroid-Associated Ophthalmopathy or Dysthyroid Ophthalmopathy or Dysthyroid Ophthalmopathies or Ophthalmopathy, Dysthyroid or Graves Eye Disease or Disease, Graves Eye or Eye Disease, Graves or Graves Orbitopathy or Orbitopathy, Graves or Ophthalmopathy, Thyroid-Associated or Myopathic Ophthalmopathy or Myopathic Ophthalmopathies or Ophthalmopathy, Myopathic or Congestive Ophthalmopathy or Congestive Ophthalmopathies or Ophthalmopathy, Congestive or Edematous Ophthalmopathy or Edematous Ophthalmopathies or Ophthalmopathy, Edematous or Ophthalmopathy, Infiltrative or Infiltrative Ophthalmopathies or Infiltrative Ophthalmopathy. Study selection

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) study design: randomized controlled trial or cohort study; (2) population: patients with a clear diagnosis of moderate to severe steroid-resistant active GO; (3) intervention: at least one GO treatment was associated with TCZ; (4) outcome variables: one or more outcome variables should be included, including the presence of CAS, proptosis, and diplopia; (5) the study provides data available before and after TCZ treatment. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, letters, clinical practice guidelines, animal studies and conferences; (2) studies without sufficient data for analysis; (3) studies with duplicate data or repeated analyses. Any disagreement can be resolved by discussion and consultation with a third independent reviewer.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers (CJH and CYJ) independently screened the study, extracted information, and cross-checked based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. If disagreements were encountered, they were discussed and resolved or referred to a third independent reviewer (HHD) to assist in judgment. For missing or not directly available data, we contacted the original authors to add them. Information extracted included: (1) Basic information about the included studies: including authors’ names, year of publication, country, and contact information; (2) Basic characteristics of the study population: including sample size, age, gender, smoking status, GO severity, and GO duration; (3) Specific details of the intervention (dose and treatment regimen) and duration of treatment; (4) Key elements of the risk of bias evaluation; (5) Key data on outcome indicators of interest. The primary outcome indicator was CAS in each study, and the secondary outcome indicators were proptosis and diplopia. CAS was extracted as reported in the original studies (7-point or 10-point scale). Given potential differences in CAS scales across studies, CAS was synthesized using standardized mean difference (SMD). Diplopia was extracted and synthesized as a dichotomous outcome (improved/resolved vs not improved) based on each study’ s definition (e.g., complete or partial disappearance or improvement in grading such as the Gorman score).

The risk of bias for inclusion in the RCT was evaluated by 2 evaluators (CJH and CYJ) according to The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing risk of bias. The evaluation tool included seven aspects: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other biases, each of which was rated as “low risk”, “unclear”, and “high risk”. Newcastle-Ottawa’s risk of bias assessment tool for cohort studies, proposed in 2012, evaluates the quality of the original study of cohort studies. The evaluation tool consists of 3 aspects: selection, comparability and outcome measurement. All extracted data were cross-checked by two independent reviewers (CJH and CYJ), and any discrepancies were resolved by negotiation or by a third independent reviewer (HHB) to assist in judgment.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed in this study using Revman 5.4 software. Where the ending indicators were continuous type variables, mean difference (MD) and standardized mean difference (SMD) was used as the effect indicator. Point estimates and 95% CI were given for each effect size. Where the ending indicators were dichotomous variables, the odds ratio (OR) was used as the effect indicator. Point estimates and 95% CI were given for each effect size. For the inclusion of data for which the study mentioned median and extreme and minimal values, we transformed them according to the formula and then combined them for analysis. The heterogeneity of included studies was analyzed using the Χ2 test (test level α = 0.1) and evaluated in conjunction with the I2 statistic. Random effects models were used for all analytical models. If the heterogeneity test results I2 ≥ 50%, statistical heterogeneity exists among the studies’ results and further analysis of the sources of heterogeneity is required. Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding individual studies and then re-running the meta-analysis and assessing the difference between the excluded results and the original combined results. Potential publication bias of included studies observed by funnel plots. All data statistics were at a significant level of P ≤ 0.05 bilaterally.

Result

Study characteristics

A total of 120 records were generated after a study search of Pubmed (n = 55), EMBASE (n = 58) and The Cochrane Library (n = 7). All records are downloaded from the database and then imported into EndNote X9 software. A total of 46 duplicate records were removed through the software’s locate duplicate function. After assessing the article type, title, and abstract, 62 records were excluded from this study. Of the above studies, 23 were reviews, 18 were other studies, 13 were case reports, 3 were clinical trials, 3 were full text not found, 1 was a meta-analysis, 1 was a guideline, and 1 was a conference. Because the results of all three clinical trials were not posted and we contacted their investigators and did not obtain data. Therefore, we excluded these three clinical trials. This study then evaluated the full text of the 11 studies screened, removing studies that were underreported due to outcome data, and finally including 7 studies. The detailed process is shown in Fig. 1. Of the seven eligible studies, four were conducted in Spain [8, 9, 12, 13], and the remaining three in France [7], Switzerland [14], and the United States [15]. The included study population was patients with a clear diagnosis of moderate to severe steroid-resistant active GO. All studies examined CAS changes [7–9, 12–15]. Six studies examined changes in proptosis and correction of diplopia [7–9, 12, 14, 15]. Overall, data from 161 patients (125 women and 36 men) were evaluated in this study. The average age range was 47.9 to 55.6 years. A total of 71 of the 161 patients had a history of smoking. Table 1 summarizes the basic characteristics of the selected studies.

Study quality

Figure 2 shows the quality assessment of the included RCT studies according to the tools provided in the Cochrane Handbook. The included studies used double-blind methods and reported allocation concealment and random assignment. It had complete outcome indicators. The study protocol registration platform and registration number were provided. Positive and negative results were also reported [9]. Other sources of bias, such as intervention protocol and follow-up process, are also clearly described in this study [9]. Figure 3 provides a qualitative evaluation of the included cohort studies based on the risk of bias evaluation tool proposed by Newcastle-Ottawa in 2012 for cohort studies. All studies reported in detail the representation of the exposed group, the method of selecting the non-exposed group and the method of determining the exposure factors [7–9, 12–15]. All studies defined the values of the outcome indicators to be observed at the beginning of the study [7–9, 12–15]. Three studies did not describe how to adjust for other confounders [12, 14, 15]. None of the studies mentioned whether independent, blinded evaluations were performed and specifically how the outcome indicators were obtained [7–9, 12–15]. All studies mentioned the follow-up period and the follow-up time was adequate [7–9, 12–15]. And all included studies reported the occurrence of adverse events [7–9, 12–15].

Efficacy outcomes

For the effectiveness of TCZ in treating moderate to severe steroid-resistant active GO, CAS was selected as the primary outcome indicator for a meta-analysis of the seven included studies. The heterogeneity of the seven studies was high (Heterogeneity P < 0.0001, I2 = 89%), and meta-analysis was performed using a random effects model. The results showed that the combined sample size was 160 cases and the combined effect was statistically significant [SMD 4.25, 95% CI (2.94, 5.57)], Z = 6.36, P < 0.00001 (Fig. 4). The overall number of participants across all included studies was 161; however, 160 participants contributed to the CAS meta-analysis due to availability of extractable CAS data. When subgroup analysis was performed comparing administration by intravenous injection [SMD 4.27, 95%CI (2.92, 5.61)], Z = 6.22, P < 0.00001 (Heterogeneity P = 0.54, I2 = 0%) with administration by intravenous combined with subcutaneous injection [SMD 4.24, 95%CI (2.64, 5.83)], Z = 5.20, P < 0.00001 (Heterogeneity P < 0.00001, I2 = 92%), greater heterogeneity was observed between subgroups (Fig. 5). CAS was reported using either a 7-point or an extended 10-point scale in the included studies; therefore, CAS was pooled using SMD to account for scale differences. Given that only one RCT was available, design-stratified subgroup pooling (RCT vs. cohort) was not performed; heterogeneity was therefore explored and interpreted primarily in the Discussion.

For secondary efficacy outcomes, six studies [7–9, 12, 14, 15] measured a reduction in proptosis (mm). Meta-analysis was performed using a fixed-effects model because statistical heterogeneity was 0 (Heterogeneity P = 0.77, I²=0%). The pooled effect was statistically significant [MD 2.13 mm, 95% CI (1.45, 2.80), P < 0.00001] (Fig. 6).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

In this study, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding one study at a time and re-running the meta-analysis to assess whether the pooled results changed materially. The results show that the results of the sensitivity analysis are consistent with the main analysis and that the current conclusions are robust (Fig. 8). Because fewer than 10 studies were ultimately included in this study, funnel plots were not produced to detect the possibility of publication bias.

Adverse events

Adverse events of TCZ were reported in all seven studies (Table 2). Where available, we extracted the investigators’ assessment of causality. Adverse events that are known risks of IL-6 receptor blockade (e.g., infections, neutropenia/cytopenias, elevated liver enzymes, lipid abnormalities, and hypersensitivity/infusion reactions) were considered more plausibly related to TCZ, whereas events such as malignancy were reported with uncertain causality in the original study and may also reflect underlying risk factors (e.g., smoking, prior immunosuppression). There were 119 adverse events in 68 of 61 patients (42.2%), of which 6 (3.7%) had serious adverse events. Of the 6 serious adverse events reported, 3 (33.3%, 3/9) were cancer [14], 2 (13.3%, 2/15) were latent tuberculosis and acute pyelonephritis [9], and 1 (1.9%, 1/54) was anaphylactic shock with bronchospasm [8]. Of all minor adverse events, hypercholesterolemia (17 cases) was the most common. Neutropenia was observed in 12 patients, upper respiratory tract infection in 12 patients and fatigue in 10 patients. All other minor adverse drug events occurred in the following order: headache (9 cases), leukopenia (4 cases), thrombocytopenia (3 cases), cellulitis (3 cases), urticaria/pruritus (3 cases), mildly elevated cholesterol (2 cases), mild liver enzyme increase (2 cases), hypertransaminasemia (2 cases), gastroenteritis (2 cases), external otitis and otitis media (2 cases), urinary tract infections (2 cases), hyperlipidemia (1 case), costal osteitis (1 case), cutaneous rash (1 case), skin infection (1 case), gingival hyperplasia (1 case), delayed injection-site reaction (1 case), digestive upset, tinnitus, myalgia, and arthralgia (1 case). Adverse events were not consistently reported stratified by administration route (IV vs. subcutaneous), and the included studies were not designed to compare safety by route; therefore, route-specific differences could not be determined.

Discussion

GO has a long course, impairs vision and eye appearance, and seriously affects the physical and mental health and quality of life of patients. Therefore, people are eager to find a treatment option that is best for GO. TCZ is a new drug for the treatment of GO, and it has been included in the EUGOGO guidelines for second-line treatment [4]. However, due to the relatively short time TCZ has been on the market, there is not a lot of studies reporting its effectiveness and safety. Although there is a similar Meta-analysis, his study has certain flaws. The shortcoming of this meta-analysis is that they did not produce evidence from RCT to assess the effectiveness of TCZ in treating GO patients, because no appropriate articles were searched at the time and the only clinical trial included in the study did not have published data. Therefore, we retrieved published studies and performed a meta-analysis to more accurately estimate the value of TCZ use and treatment effects. Overall, the available evidence suggests that TCZ may be associated with improvements in patients with moderate-to-severe, steroid-resistant active GO; however, the certainty of evidence is limited because most included studies were observational.

CAS helps to assess GO disease progression and is one of the most important evaluation indicators for GO. In a study, it was shown for the first time that intravenous TCZ was effective in reducing the progression of disease activity in patients with GO [12]. Although Perez-Moreiras et al. [12] found that at 16 weeks, patients’ CAS decreased from an initial mean of 6.5 ± 1.29 to a mean CAS of 0.61 ± 0.85. However, at 40 weeks after the last treatment, the results did not last. Several included cohort studies and the single RCT provide evidence that TCZ is associated with improvements in disease activity and key ocular outcomes in active steroid-resistant GO. However, the durability of response after treatment discontinuation remains insufficiently characterized in the included evidence base. Although case reports and small case series (which were excluded from our quantitative synthesis) have described relapse after stopping TCZ in individual patients, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and are presented here only as contextual information rather than evidence of efficacy. Larger, well-designed studies with standardized long-term follow-up are needed to define optimal treatment duration and maintenance strategies [16, 17]. The EUGOGO guidelines also suggest that anti-inflammatory therapy is significantly less effective after 18 months of disease progression [4]. Therefore, future research is needed to define the optimal duration of treatment as well as the optimal time to stop the drug in order to achieve long-term remission of the disease. An RCT showed a response rate of 93.3% (95% CI; 70.1%-98.3%) for TCZ compared with the placebo group, which was 58.8% (95% CI; 36%-78.3%) (P=0.04) [9]. Bennedjaï et al. [7] reported that the mean change in CAS was more pronounced in the TCZ group compared to the rituximab group. It has also been shown that the mean change in CAS was significantly lower in the TCZ group compared to the GC group [18]. This suggests that TCZ may have a tendency to have stronger anti-inflammatory effects. Russell et al. [19] also reported that TCZ further reduced inflammatory activity after GO corticosteroid treatment. Our Meta-analysis also found a significant decrease in CAS compared to baseline levels, but the results are highly heterogeneous and need to be interpreted with caution. Therefore, we divided the administration methods into two subgroups, intravenous and intravenous/subcutaneous injections, to find the source of heterogeneity. However, analysis showed that different interventions were not the cause of high CAS heterogeneity. A sensitivity analysis showed the current conclusions to be robust. We did not find a source of heterogeneity after performing subgroup and sensitivity analyses. After team discussions, we concluded that the reason for the large heterogeneity lies in differences in study design. The main reason is the lack of randomization and blinding in cohort studies, which can produce more confounding factors due to their real-world data, such as patient populations, measurement times, and follow-up times that are often not standardized across studies. Therefore, the results may be biased. This diversity among studies leads to heterogeneity. Therefore, more RCTs are needed in the future to demonstrate the reliability of the results.

Cytokine production and inflammation, hyaluronic acid synthesis, adipogenesis, and myogenic fibrogenesis are the main pathological changes in GO. The combined effect of the above pathological changes leads to swelling of the orbital tissues (especially the extraocular muscles, the fat behind the eye), resulting in the typical clinical manifestation of GO, proptosis. Severe proptosis can prevent the eye from closing, resulting in exposure to the conjunctiva and cornea, which can lead to congestion, edema, corneal ulceration, and even blindness. So for GO, control of proptosis is also one of the important indicators for judging the efficacy of a drug. Several studies have shown that TCZ has good therapeutic effects in proptosis [7, 12, 14, 15]. In a case series report, it was also shown that TCZ has good results in GO patients with proptosis [20]. However, there are also studies that show conflicting results. Bennedjaï et al. [7] reported that only one of 10 patients showed improvement in proptosis after treatment with TCZ, while the remaining nine patients had no significant outcome after treatment. Also, Perez-Moreiras et al. [9] suggested that although there was a significant improvement in ocular protrusion in patients treated with TCZ, the improvement was not clinically significant. The differences in these studies may be related to the limited number of patients treated with TCZ and the different study populations. For our study, Fig. 6 shows the small diamond-shaped square falling to the right of the null line, i.e., favoring the endpoint side, and not intersecting with the null line. This means that the use of TCZ is able to reduce the manifestation of proptosis in GO patients compared to baseline. In our pooled analysis, TCZ treatment was associated with a statistically significant reduction in proptosis compared with baseline.

When a GO patient develops diplopia, it suggests that the patient has a malignant proptosis. Anti-thyroid medication alone is not enough in this case, but aggressive anti-inflammatory treatment is also needed. The first line of clinical anti-inflammatory treatment is intravenous methylprednisolone. However, some patients are not sensitive to this treatment or have contraindications. So, we need another drug to treat these patients who are not responding to corticosteroid therapy. TCZ is a good drug for this purpose. Maldiney et al. [21] found that three patients who initially responded partially or not to intravenous glucocorticoids had almost complete correction of diplopia after TCZ treatment. However, some studies also show conflicting results. Perez-Moreiras et al. [9] concluded that TCZ was not significantly effective in the correction of diplopia. We speculate that the discrepancy in the results of these studies may be due to the limited number of cases treated with TCZ. For our study, Fig. 7 shows that the small diamond-shaped square falls to the right of the null line, i.e., on the favorable endpoint side, and does not intersect with the null line. In the pooled analysis, TCZ use was associated with higher odds of diplopia improvement/resolution compared with baseline; however, these findings are largely derived from observational cohorts and should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

In our included studies, a total of 68 patients experienced adverse events, of which 6 patients experienced serious adverse events [8, 9, 14], and no patients died as a result of receiving TCZ. Except for serious adverse events, all reported adverse events were mild and no patients required special treatment or discontinuation of TCZ therapy. Moi et al. [14] reported three cases of serious adverse events, two with breast cancer and one with bladder cancer. However, they also noted that the causal relationship between these three cases of cancer and TCZ treatment is uncertain. Some other factors such as individual susceptibility factors, smoking and the use of immunosuppression prior to TCZ treatment may be more associated with the development of cancer. We should note that Perez-Moreiras et al. [9] reported 2 patients with latent tuberculosis and acute pyelonephritis, respectively. Another study reported a case of anaphylaxis with bronchospasm in a patient [8]. This all forced the suspension of TCZ treatment. Glucocorticoid shock therapy is currently the main treatment for GO, but high doses of glucocorticoid therapy often lead to a number of serious adverse events, such as cardiovascular occurrence, liver damage (liver failure), etc. Zang et al. [22] analyzed complete information on the occurrence of adverse events in 1045 GO patients receiving intravenous glucocorticoids and calculated morbidity and mortality rates of 6.5% and 0.6%, respectively. Längericht et al. [23] also showed that the overall incidence of glucocorticoid-induced adverse events affected 60–70% of participants. The difference in the probability of adverse events may be related to the size of the study population. In conclusion, we believe that in general, the use of TCZ for severe steroid-resistant active GO is desirable. The study was registered with PROSPERO, CRD 42022381596.

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have further evaluated tocilizumab and other biologic therapies for Graves’ orbitopathy/thyroid eye disease, including comparative and network meta-analytic approaches and broader outcome sets [24–27]. However, our review differs in several important aspects. First, we focused on a clinically relevant and homogeneous population—moderate-to-severe, steroid-resistant, active GO—in whom TCZ is commonly considered as a second-line option. Second, we prespecified and quantitatively synthesized three key outcomes (CAS, proptosis, and diplopia) using a conmework requiring extractable baseline and post-treatment data. Third, we included both an RCT and observational cohort studies and applied random-effects models to account for betweeno providing a structured summary of adverse events reported across the included clinical studies.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the number of patients included in the study was relatively small, thus introducing a higher risk of patient selection bias. Second, due to the limited reporting of the original study, this study only performed a subgroup analysis of patient CAS scores before and after treatment, and still could not completely exclude the effect of heterogeneity. Third, the cost of TCZ use is also critical to the treatment plan for GO patients but is not described in the study. Fourth, because the use of TCZ for GO is a novel treatment approach in recent years, there are fewer studies reported in the database. Only one of the studies selected in this study was an RCT, and other non-RCT studies may have led to an uneven selection of patients. Fifth, patient-reported QoL outcomes (e.g., GO-QOL) were not uniformly reported, which precluded quantitative synthesis and limited assessment of patient-perceived benefit. Additionally, other clinically relevant secondary outcomes (e.g., best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure, ocular motility, and early response after the first dose) were not consistently reported or were not available in an extractable format across the included studies, preventing quantitative synthesis of these endpoints. Sixth, because only one RCT was available and most evidence came from observational cohorts, we were unable to conduct meaningful subgroup meta-analyses by study design; additional RCTs are needed to enable more informative design-stratified comparisons in future updates. Finally, because our search was last updated on June 1, 2022, more recently published studies were not included and may further refine the pooled estimates; future updates incorporating newer evidence are needed.

Conclusion

In summary, the results of this Meta-analysis showed that overall treatment of GO with TCZ significantly improved the patients’ CAS scores, reduced proptosis, and corrected diplopia, which was beneficial to the patients. Also, most of the reported adverse events concerning patients were minor events, but a few serious adverse events occurred and need to be handled with caution. We conclude that TCZ is a safe and feasible treatment for patients with moderate to severe steroid-resistant active GO. However, due to the small number of cases treated with TCZ and the lack of comparison with other effective approaches, we need more larger sample, multicenter RCT support in the future to provide evidence to support the development of a therapeutic management approach for GO patients.
