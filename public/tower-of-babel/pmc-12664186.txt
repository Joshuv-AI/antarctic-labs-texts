Vortex formation of cardiac bilateral ventricles in fetuses with tetralogy of fallot assessed by blood speckle-tracking echocardiography
Authors: Yushan Liu, Dan Zhou, Yang Yang, Pan Yang, Shuhao Song, Shi Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo analyze the vortex characteristics of the cardiac bilateral ventricles in fetuses with tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) using a high–frame rate ultrasound with blood speckle-tracking (BST).MethodsA prospective cross-sectional study of 35 fetuses with TOF and 35 gestational age-matched controls was conducted. Fetuses with TOF were classified as mild to moderate TOF or severe TOF based on pulmonary annular diameter z-scores. The number, position, length, width, area and duration of the vortex in the bilateral ventricles were recorded by BST echocardiography. Correlations between the vortex characteristics and cardiac morphology parameters, as well as ventricular function, were assessed in fetuses with TOF.ResultsThe fetuses with TOF exhibited larger and longer duration vortex in the right ventricle (RV) than that of the controls (all P < 0.05). Linear regression analysis demonstrated that vortex area of RV was positively related to sphericity index (SI) of RV, isovolumic relaxation time (IRT) and myocardial performance index of RV in the fetuses with TOF (all P < 0.001). In fetuses with severe TOF, The vortex in RV was located closer to the cardiac apex and the vortex in the left ventricle (LV) was larger than that in normal fetuses (all P < 0.05). Vortex area of LV only in fetuses with severe TOF was slightly positively correlated with LV-IRT (P = 0.038).ConclusionsThe fetuses with TOF exhibited special vortex characteristics in RV, and some vortex characteristics, especially vortex area in RV, were closely related to RV anatomical and functional abnormalities. Further study is needed to explore the diagnostic and prognostic value of vortex characteristics in fetuses with congenital heart disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08428-9.Keywords: Blood speckle-tracking echocardiography, Vortex, Tetralogy of fallot, Fetus

Introduction

Blood flow occurs in different forms in the cardiovascular system, including laminar flow, turbulent flow or vortical flow. In fluid dynamics, a vortex refers to a flow structure characterized by the ordered rotational motion of fluid around a common axis. It essentially represents a localized concentration of fluid angular momentum. Vortex formation denotes the physical process by which such ordered rotational structures are generated within blood flow. Investigations of left ventricle (LV) diastolic phase hemodynamics have documented the characteristic formation of intraventricular vortex structures through rotational flow pattern analysis [1, 2]. Vortices formed during ventricular filling have specific geometric forms and anatomical locations, which are key determinants of directed blood flow during ejection. As persistent inertial flow patterns, cardiac vortices optimize systolic outflow via pressure-mediated fluid entrainment, achieving greater mass displacement than equivalent laminar jet flows [3]. Moreover, these vortices in ventricular diastole are demonstrated to play an important role in minimizing the impact of wall shear stresses (WSS) on the ventricular walls and keep conserving energy avoiding excessive energetic dissipation to optimize ventricular-arterial coupling [4]. Intraventricular blood flow is closely related to ventricular function, and abnormal diastolic vortex can be used as a new indicator of ventricular dysfunction [5, 6].

Blood speckle-tracking (BST) echocardiography, employing high-frame rate ultrasound with angle-independent tissue motion analysis, enables quantitative vortex mapping through myocardial speckle pattern correlation algorithms [7, 8]. The technical advantage of BST is it uses a best-matched algorithm to track and follow speckles formed within the myocardial wall, or the moving blood cells from one frame to the next in moving blood. Morphological visualization serves as the most intuitive method for vortex identification, based on either streamline patterns or velocity vector maps derived from BST. The core criterion involves observing the formation of closed-loop, elliptical, or spiral patterns in the streamlines over a relatively stable time interval, such as during the diastolic phase. This modality’s current clinical implementation primarily targets pediatric populations due to transducer frequency-penetration limitations [9, 10]. Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is currently the most common cyanotic congenital heart disease (CHD) [11]. Our group applied BST echocardiography to find abnormal vortex and turbulent flow observed in the ventricles and great arteries of fetuses with TOF [12]. However, the ventricles’ vortex characteristics in fetuses with TOF have not been studied in further detail. Thus, the aims of the current study were to analyze the vortex characteristics of the bilateral cardiac ventricles in fetuses with TOF using BST and to detect their relationships with cardiac morphology parameters and ventricular function. The deep-going study of intracavity blood flow patterns in fetuses with TOF can predict cardiac remodeling to provide new prenatal intervention strategies, optimized counseling and perinatal management.

Materials and methods

Patient population

A cross-sectional prospective research was conducted in the Department of Ultrasound, the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University in China between October 2023 and November 2024. Eligible participants comprised fetuses diagnosed with TOF, encompassing cases with pulmonary stenosis or atresia. Disease severity was classified as mild to moderate TOF(z-score >-4) or severe TOF (z-score <= -4) based on pulmonary annular diameter z-scores [13]. TOF diagnoses were validated through postnatal imaging, surgical intervention, or postmortem examination. All fetuses included in this study had their Crown-Rump Length (CRL) measured during first-trimester ultrasound examinations. This study utilized gestational age (GA) determined by first-trimester CRL as the benchmark for GA matching. The controls consisted of GA-matched fetuses exhibiting structurally normal hearts, uncomplicated placental function, and absence of congenital anomalies. Exclusion criteria involved: (1) multifetal gestations; (2) fetal growth restriction (estimated fetal weight (EFW) < 10th centile GA-adjusted, Hadlock criteria); (3) concurrent congenital heart defects, extracardiac malformations, or confirmed genetic disorders; (4) known chromosomal anomalies; (5) non-sinus cardiac rhythms; (6) significant maternal comorbidities including uncontrolled hypertension or diabetes. The protocol received written consent from all participants and was approved by the Ethics Committees of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University [(2018) Review No. 070].

Conventional echocardiography

Standard obstetric and fetal cardiac assessments were performed with a Voluson E10 ultrasound system (GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria) equipped with a RAB 4-8-D curved-array transducer. Standard fetal biometrics (biparietal diameter (BPD), head circumference (HC), femur length (FL) and abdominal circumference (AC)) were acquired. Hadlock algorithm [14] computed EFW incorporating all four parameters. Maternal-fetal covariates encompassed maternal age, body mass index (BMI), fetal heart rate (FHR), fetal EFW, GA and obstetric and menstrual history variability.

Cardiac morphometrics included: atrio-ventricular valve annuli diameters (tricuspid/mitral); biventricular end-diastolic inner dimensions (left ventricular internal diameter’s end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD)/ right ventricular internal diameter’s end-diastolic diameter (RVEDD)); semilunar valve systolic diameters. All measurements converted to GA-adjusted z-scores via published norms [15]. Ventricular sphericity index (SI) was quantified as ventricular longitudinal length-to-end-diastolic diameter ratios. Fetal cardiac functional evaluation was conducted using tissue Doppler imaging (TDI)-derived myocardial performance index (MPI) in all fetuses. For TDI, sample volumes were placed in the basal part of the mitral annulus and tricuspid annulus in a four-chamber view. The insonation angle between the ultrasound beam and ventricular wall orientation was maintained below 30°, with no manual angle correction applied during acquisition. Three key intervals were measured from the same cardiac cycle: isovolumic contraction time (ICT), ejection time (ET), and isovolumic relaxation time (IRT). MPI values for each ventricle were derived using the formula (ICT + IRT)/ET [16]. All measurements were obtained from the same cardiac cycle and were acquired by a certified fetal echocardiography specialist under a standardized protocol.

BST echocardiography

Fetal BST echocardiography was conducted using a GE Vivid E95 ultrasound platform (GE Healthcare Ultrasound, Austria) equipped with a 6 S phased-array transducer. High-resolution two-dimensional grayscale imaging was employed to delineate regions of interest (ROIs) within cardiac structures, specifically targeting the left and right ventricular walls in a standardized apical 4-chamber view. Color Doppler interrogation was optimized in four-chamber orientation to ensure comprehensive blood flow visualization throughout the cardiac cycle. Then BSI protocol was implemented with voxel dimensions (0.6 × 0.6 mm) and speckle tracking kernels (1.2 mm spatial resolution). Pulse repetition frequency in our study is 3.5 kHz. The speckle frame rate setting was targeted between 400 and 650 frames for a 2-second duration, and then the optimal intracardiac blood flow images were stored for further detailed analysis. All BST images were acquired from fetuses without significant fetal movement. The number of vortices was calculated as the sum of the vortices divided by the number of heart cycles.

Vortex spatial characteristics were quantified through vortex depth and transverse positional indices. Vortex depth represents vertical distance from the vortex centroid to the ventricular apex relative to the ventricular long axis length (Fig. 1B). Vortex transversal position represents the horizontal distance from the vortex centroid to the interventricular septal relative to the ventricular end-diastolic transverse diameter (Fig. 1B). Vortex positions were expressed as a decimal between 0 and 1, and 1 represents the closest position to the apex (depth) or lateral wall (transverse).

In vortex morphology parameters, Vortex length was determined by measuring the longitudinal length of the vortex relative to ventricular length, and vortex width as the horizontal width of the vortex relative to ventricular width (Fig. 1A). Vortex morphometric analysis included two key parameters: (1) SI, derived from the longitudinal-to-transverse dimension ratio (vortex length/width); (2) Area, obtained by tracing the outer ring of the clear vortex at maximum area using the software tracing tool and indexed to ventricular area (Fig. 1C). In vortex timing parameters, vortex duration was determined from frame-by-frame analysis of the first vortex appearance to its degradation. When vortices were visible over several phases of the cardiac cycle, the phase where the vortex had the largest dimension (main vortex) was selected for final analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software [version 24.0, IBM] and GraphPad Prism 8.0. The Shapiro-Wilk test of normality was conducted for the continuous variables. All the data are reported as mean with SDs or frequencies with percentages. For variables demonstrating non-normal distribution, descriptive statistics were reported using median with interquartile range (IQR), while between-group comparisons employed the Mann-Whitney U test. The study and control groups were compared with ANOVA, and Bonferroni correction is used to adjust multiple comparisons. Correlations between the vortex characteristics and cardiac morphology parameters and ventricular function were tested using Pearson correlation coefficients. Interobserver and intraobserver reliability analyses were conducted using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). For interobserver reliability assessment, a secondary sonographer independently quantified right ventricular vortex dimensions (area, length and width) in 24 randomly selected cases (12 controls, 12 fetuses with TOF), maintaining blinding to clinical status. Intraobserver reproducibility was determined through duplicate measurements by the primary sonographer across two sessions separated by one-day intervals, using identical analytical protocols. A probability value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant difference.

Results

Demographics

In total, 88 fetuses were initially enrolled in this study. Among them, 18 were excluded: 10 with poor image quality in the analysis of vortex, 5 with terminated pregnancy and 3 without follow­up. Finally, A total of 70 fetuses were finally included: 35 fetuses with TOF and 35 GA-matched normal fetuses. Pulmonary atresia refers to the complete obstruction of the pulmonary valve, resulting in no forward flow from the right ventricle to the pulmonary arteries. Pulmonary stenosis involves a partial obstruction at the level of the pulmonary valve, leading to restricted blood flow. The valve may be thickened, domed, or functionally narrow. Right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) obstruction is an umbrella term encompassing any anatomical or functional obstruction along the right ventricular outflow pathway, which includes subvalvular obstruction, valvular obstruction and supravalvular obstruction. Of the 35 fetuses with TOF, 40% (14/35) had pulmonary valve atresia, 34% (12/35) had pulmonary valve stenosis, and 26% (9/35) had RVOT obstruction (except for pulmonary valve stenosis and pulmonary valve atresia). The 14 fetuses with TOF accompanied by pulmonary valve atresia included in this study all had pulmonary valves, main pulmonary trunk and branches. And the blood supply to the main pulmonary trunk and branches was provided in reverse by the ductus arteriosus. There was no significant difference in the number of RV vortices between TOF groups and controls (Table 2). Two vortices were observed among 55/70 fetuses (79%, 26 TOF fetuses and 29 controls) in the RV. 12/70 fetuses (17%, 7 TOF fetuses and 5 controls) showed one vortex, and 3/70 fetuses (4%, 2 TOF fetuses and 1 control) exhibited three vortices. The additional vortices were casually found at the level of the tricuspid orifice. The fetuses with TOF exhibited significantly larger main vortex in the RV than that of the controls (P < 0.05), while these fetuses exhibited significantly longer vortex duration than that of the controls (P < 0.05).

There was no significant difference in the number of LV vortices between TOF groups and controls (Table 2). Two vortices were observed among 58/70 fetuses (82%, 27 TOF fetuses and 31 controls) in the LV. 9/70 fetuses (12%, 6 TOF fetuses and 3 controls) showed one vortex, and 4/70 fetuses (6%, 2 TOF fetuses and 2 controls) exhibited three vortices. The additional vortices of LV were often found below the mitral lobe, and usually presented a smaller size than the main vortex. Compared with the controls, the length, width, and SI of the LV vortex in fetuses with mild to moderate TOF had no significant difference. LV vortex in fetuses with severe TOF was larger than normal fetuses (P < 0.05).

Linear regression analysis demonstrated that the main vortex area of RV was positively related to RV-SI (r = 0.325, P < 0.001) as well as IRT (r = 0.326, P < 0.001) and MPI (r = 0.377, P < 0.001) of RV in the fetuses with TOF respectively. Vortex area of LV in fetuses with severe TOF was slightly positively correlated with LV-IRT (r = 0.153, P = 0.038), but not with LV-SI or LV-MPI (all P > 0.05) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

To date, there are few studies investigating the mechanical characteristics of cardiac vortex flow patterns in fetuses with TOF. This work indicates that it is feasible to proceed quantitative assessment of cardiac vortex patterns in fetuses with TOF by BST echocardiography. We noted that the fetuses with TOF exhibited significantly larger and longer duration of RV vortex than that of normal fetuses, and the increased vortex area of RV in fetuses with TOF was closely related to RV anatomical and functional abnormalities.

The generation and motion of the cardiac vortex are dependent on the filling pattern of blood flow [17]. The study of Wiputra H et al. reported that fetuses with TOF had higher tricuspid inflow compared with normal fetuses because left intraventricular diastolic blood flow was partly from right atrium (RA) inflow via RV and ventricular septal defect (VSD) [18]. On the other hand, we supposed that the VSD of fetuses with TOF in diastolic period may have bidirectional shunt or left-to-right shunt, resulting in the RV needing to accommodate shunt blood from the LV in addition to receiving blood flow from the RA, which will also lead to increased volume load of the RV in diastolic period. This process complicates the right ventricular blood flow disorder and hemodynamics in fetuses with TOF, resulting in increased vortex area and prolonged duration. In addition, severe pulmonary artery stenosis in fetuses with severe TOF leads to right ventricular exhaust obstruction, reduced right ventricular compliance and limited diastolic filling, and abnormal blood flow path in the RV, which further aggravates the degree of vortex. Understanding the hemodynamics of the fetal RV may be more important than understanding the LV. In fetal circulation, the RV provides most of the energy pumped by blood and supplies blood to a larger part of the body than the LV, which is different from postnatal circulation.

The present study demonstrated that in fetuses with mild to moderate TOF, the diastolic vortex in the LV was primarily localized to the mid-portion adjacent to the ventricular septum, whereas the vortex in the RV was predominantly situated along the free wall near the tricuspid valve. Furthermore, the spatial localization of the vortex in both ventricles among fetuses with mild to moderate TOF was largely consistent with observations in normal fetuses. In normal fetal hearts, the endocardial surface of RV exhibits a characteristically coarse architecture due to prominent trabeculations, in contrast to the relatively smooth endocardial surface of LV. We conclude that the differential position of vortex between the fetal left and right ventricles is principally attributable to disparities in transvalvular flow volumes through the mitral and tricuspid orifices during diastole, combined with differences in the degree of endocardial surface roughness between the two ventricles. This supports the interpretation that intracardiac geometric architecture serves as a critical determinant in the initiation and maturation of vortical flow patterns [19].The endocardial surface of the fetal LV is flat, and the incoming diastolic blood flow to the left ventricular outflow tract requires a U-turn [20]. However, RV differs from LV in terms of anatomy, muscle fiber arrangement, and systolic and diastolic properties. RV is a chamber with a funnel-shaped geometry that contracts in a peristaltic fashion from the inlet to the outlet area to form a spiral blood flow [21]. Our study also found that the diastolic right ventricular vortex in fetuses with severe TOF shifted slightly toward the cardiac apex. This may also be explained by obstructed right ventricular blood flow in fetuses with severe TOF, resulting in more diastolic blood flow to the RV near the apex of the heart, creating a more significant, prolonged vortex.

Cardiac geometry in this work showed that fetuses with severe TOF exhibited smaller RVIDEDD and larger RVSI size compared with controls. As to the reason for the change of right ventricular geometry in fetuses with severe TOF, we hypothesized that the larger vortex of RV has more contact with the ventricular wall and this process leads to enhanced vorticity dynamics and increased WSS in the ventricular wall. WSS directly acts on a variety of mechanical receptors on cardiovascular endothelial cells to express mechanobiological responses, thus affecting the development and remodeling of cardiovascular tissue [22, 23]. This process may lead to hyperplasia of the right ventricular wall myocardium and shrinkage of the right ventricular cavity in fetuses with severe TOF during the second and late trimesters.

Moreover, our data suggested that there was no obvious difference in the location, length, width, area, and vortex duration of the vortex incidence in the LV of fetuses with mild to moderate TOF compared to that of normal fetuses. This may be due to the presence of a VSD which equalizes the pressure between the bilateral ventricles in fetuses with TOF, and the bidirectional blood shunt is mostly a low-velocity laminar flow. However, we found that the left ventricular vortex area of fetuses with severe TOF was larger than that of normal fetuses. This may be due to the obvious obstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract in the fetuses with severe TOF, resulting in more blood shunt to LV via the VSD, disrupting the normal laminar flow pattern in the LV. The LV needs to process both the filling flow from the mitral valve orifice and the shunt blood from the RV, which may lead to diastolic flow disturbance and increased vortex area.

Linear regression analysis in this study demonstrated that vortex area of RV was positively related to IRT and MPI of RV in the fetuses with TOF respectively. This means that the enlarged vortex of RV in fetuses with TOF is closely associated with impaired right ventricular diastolic function. Increased WSS caused by enlarged vortex of RV may lead to chamber stiffness, and this process can lead to limited diastolic filling of the RV and affect ventricular diastolic function gradually. This finding is comparable to that in a previous study by Marquez-Gonzalez H et al. in which children with TOF presented RV dysfunction [24]. Our study suggests that impaired right ventricular diastolic function in patients with TOF is likely to be present during fetal life. Several fetuses with severe TOF in our study exhibited a single larger vortex, which indicated that the inflow waveform was monophasic and this process was often a sign of diastolic dysfunction. The left ventricular vortex area of fetuses with severe TOF was also slightly positively correlated with LV-IRT, indicating a close correlation between ventricular vortex area and ventricular diastolic function.

This study has several limitations. The enrolled sample size of the fetuses with TOF is relatively small. Moreover, the blood flow pattern in the cardiovascular system is three-dimensional, but the acquisition and analysis of cardiac vortex using BST is limited to a two-dimensional imaging techniques. BST may result in a less detailed estimate of the actual characteristics of cardiovascular vortex flow.

Conclusions

The fetuses with TOF exhibited significantly larger and longer duration of RV vortex than that of normal fetuses, and the increased vortex area of RV in fetuses with TOF was closely related to anatomical abnormalities and impaired right ventricular function. These findings will provide a preliminary understanding of the unique intracardial blood flow patterns and early myocardial remodeling in fetuses with TOF.
