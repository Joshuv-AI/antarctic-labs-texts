Subarachnoid hemorrhage from a basilar artery perforator pseudoaneurysm: a rare challenging lesion in the posterior circulation. Illustrative case
Authors: Tenzing Lhamo, Muhaison Hanifa Ibrahim, Benjamin Lee, Laura Stone McGuire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDBasilar artery perforator pseudoaneurysms are rare vascular lesions that present significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Their management remains unstandardized, with limited evidence guiding treatment decisions.OBSERVATIONSThe authors report the case of a 74-year-old man with multiple comorbidities who presented with subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). Initial CT/CT angiography revealed diffuse SAH with intraventricular extension. Digital subtraction angiography demonstrated a pseudoaneurysm arising from a basilar perforator. The absence of an accessible inflow combined with the prohibitive procedural and surgical risks, neither endovascular embolization nor open surgery was attempted. Thus, conservative management was pursued, and repeat angiography on postoperative day 2 revealed spontaneous thrombosis of the pseudoaneurysm. Despite radiographic resolution, the patient’s course was complicated by vasospasm, infarction, and persistent coma, resulting in the patient’s death on day 21.LESSONSBasilar perforator pseudoaneurysms are rare lesions that may be occult on initial imaging; repeat angiography is essential when posterior-circulation SAH lacks an identified source. Endovascular treatment risks perforator occlusion or require antiplatelets in an acute SAH setting. Open surgery also has its limitations with deep exposure, lack of proximal/distal control, and intraoperative rupture. Since neither route is safely feasible, conservative management with intensive neurocritical care and serial imaging is often the management approach. Traumatic aneurysms remain a rare entity and represent only about 1% of intracranial aneurysms with resulting high mortality,1 most of which turn out to be false aneurysms or pseudoaneurysms. The mechanism of pseudoaneurysm formation is mostly attributed to closed head injury but can arise from trauma such as gunshot wounds and penetration injuries or iatrogenic following skull base surgeries or infections.2,3 Lesser described causes of intracranial pseudoaneurysms include severe coughing and other extreme sources of Valsalva maneuvers.4,5,6 Given the rarity and varied causes, management of pseudoaneurysms remains unstandardized, with case-dependent variation in treatment strategy. Zheng et al.7 reviewed several reports describing successful management of basilar pseudoaneurysms with various strategies. Surgical trapping,8 endovascular coiling with stent placement,9 and primary coil embolization have been described with some cases requiring retreatment or flow diversion due to recurrence.10 Alhough uncommon, conservative management has also been reported, with most evidence limited to isolated case reports.3,11 This approach may be considered in carefully selected patients, particularly those demonstrating interval reduction in aneurysm size or flow on serial imaging.3,11 While most extracranial pseudoaneurysms can be managed with medical therapy, intracranial pseudoaneurysms pose a higher risk and are managed more with endovascular interventions or surgical repair, when feasible. In this report, we highlight a rare presentation of intracranial pseudoaneurysm and its natural history over the course of several follow-up diagnostic cerebral angiograms.

Illustrative Case

A 74-year-old man with a complex medical history of hypertension, congestive heart failure, diabetes mellitus, and multivessel coronary artery disease, on dual antiplatelet therapy, presented with sudden-onset headache, nausea, vomiting, and left hemiparesis with facial droop after a fall. He was initially seen at an outside hospital where initial CT angiography revealed extensive subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) with areas of subdural hemorrhage along with enlargement of lateral ventricles and the third ventricle (Fig. 1). Initial noncontrast head CT revealed diffuse SAH with intraventricular extension, Hunt and Hess grade IV, Fisher grade 4 (Fig. 1). The patient was taken urgently to the operating room for placement of an external ventricular drain and diagnostic cerebral angiography with possible intervention. Imaging revealed a pseudoaneurysm arising from a perforator of the basilar artery (Fig. 2). Given the lack of accessible inflow for intervention, as well as the elevated risk of treatment in this location, endovascular embolization was not attempted, and conservative management was pursued. Postoperatively, the patient was admitted to the neurocritical care unit for strict blood pressure control and other standard management for aneurysmal SAH (aSAH), including antiepileptic and nimodipine administration. Prompt multidisciplinary discussion noted the complexity of treatment and determined the pseudoaneurysm was not amenable to open or endovascular options and agreed in conservative management with repeat diagnostic cerebral angiograms.

On hospital day 2, a repeat diagnostic cerebral angiogram was performed due to the severity of the hemorrhage, the presence of an unsecured aneurysm, and need to clarify the management strategy for potential vasospasm. The study demonstrated no residual filling of the previously visualized pseudoaneurysm, suggestive of interval thrombosis, so a conservative management strategy was maintained (Fig. 3). MRI and MR angiography of the head with and without contrast was also done and showed stable multifocal hemorrhages with no new or enlarging hemorrhage but did demonstrate the presence of a brainstem infarct (Fig. 4). The patient remained comatose with persistent intracranial hypertension and subclinical seizures. Follow-up cerebral angiography on hospital day 5 revealed mild vasospasm in the left and right internal carotid arteries and posterior circulation, which was treated with intra-arterial verapamil. The remainder of his hospitalization was complicated by the development of deep venous thrombosis in his left subclavian and axillary veins; however, anticoagulation was held due to a concern for rebleeding from an unsecured pseudoaneurysm. Subsequent follow-up CT of the head revealed a right cerebellar infarct, and repeat cerebral angiogram showed progression to moderate to severe vasospasm in bilateral ICA territories, again managed with intra-arterial verapamil.

Despite aggressive supportive care, the patient showed no neurological improvement. After multidisciplinary discussions and extensive counseling, the patient’s family opted to transition to comfort-based care, and he died on postbleed day 21.

Discussion

Observations

The key observation in this case is a ruptured basilar artery perforator pseudoaneurysm that spontaneously thrombosed on early follow-up angiography; however, the patient’s clinical course remained poor due to secondary complications. This is noteworthy because basilar perforator pseudoaneurysms are rare and often small and technically difficult to treat, and, as such, reporting real-world clinical courses helps anchor expectations about imaging, surveillance, and outcomes.11 Previous series and reviews have confirmed that basilar perforator aneurysms are an underrecognized cause of aSAH and pose major diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, with management individualized to anatomy and risk.12,13

A second important observation is the dynamic behavior of perforator lesions. Intermittent filling and early thrombosis can occur, stressing the importance of repeat angiography even when initial or early studies are unrevealing or equivocal. Prior case reports have shown wide variation in basilar artery pseudoaneurysm management and outcomes; some reports have shown complete resolution with conservative care, whereas others have noted transient appearance and spontaneous disappearance.3,11,14 Further studies such as that by Chau and colleagues opted for selective coiling with good results, and Cho et al. noted complete obliteration with no recurrence on follow-up with stent-assisted coiling15,16 (Table 1). There are several important considerations in conservatively managed pseudoaneurysms; however, care must be taken in initiating treatment for vasospasm or prophylaxis for deep venous thrombosis in the setting of an unsecured ruptured aneurysm. In our case, conservative management prevailed initially due to the limited and high-risk treatment options; however, had the aneurysm morphology changed on follow-up angiography, such as interval growth, options for intervention would have been reconsidered.

Lastly, our patient’s course underscores that angiographic success (thrombosis in this case) does not guarantee neurological recovery. Although a few case reports and small series have also documented spontaneous disappearances of basilar artery perforator aneurysms, the outcomes were hinged on the initial hemorrhage severity and secondary ischemic events such as vasospasm and infarction.11,17

Lessons

This case of a basilar perforator pseudoaneurysm highlights several limitations in the current treatment landscape for intracranial pseudoaneurysms. These lesions remain rare and notoriously challenging to manage particularly when located in the posterior circulation. Furthermore, no standardized management guidelines exist given the rarity of basilar artery pseudoaneurysms. The available evidence is largely limited to case reports and small series, in which endovascular approaches have emerged as the preferred treatment modality in most cases.

Theoretical endovascular strategies are frequently unsafe or infeasible for perforator-origin pseudoaneurysms.18 Super selective catheterization for coiling or liquid embolic agents is rarely achievable in a tiny basilar perforator; even if reached, coil mass or embolic reflux can sacrifice the perforator and precipitate brainstem infarction.19 Stent-assisted techniques and flow diversion risk “jailing” critical perforators and obligate dual antiplatelet therapy in the acute SAH setting, which is often an unfavorable balance when the lesion lacks a definable neck or a safe working length like in this case.20

Similarly, open surgery faces formidable barriers. Surgical corridors to the mid-/distal basilar artery are deep and narrow, making proximal and distal control of a perforator-based pseudoaneurysm limited. Also, the friable wall raises the risk of intraoperative rupture without a reliable rescue. In such anatomy, conservative management including intensive neurocritical care with scheduled repeat angiography, can be a defensible course, recognizing that spontaneous thrombosis may occur but does not ensure good outcomes. Multidisciplinary deliberation, vigilant surveillance, and clear family counseling remain the pillars of care for these exceptionally high-risk lesions.

In the case of our patient, had he survived, our follow-up plan would have mirrored recommendations described in the literature for spinal artery pseudoaneurysms.6 Short-interval imaging within 6 weeks would have been performed, and if angiography remained negative, delayed follow-up angiography at approximately 6 months would have been pursued. When feasible, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) would have been the preferred modality, given its superior image quality and temporal resolution.

Treatment of basilar perforator pseudoaneurysms is challenging due to microanatomy and rupture biology that precludes safe endovascular and open approaches. Further research is needed to formulate a comprehensive approach to this difficult pathology. Currently management relies on early neurocritical management, repeat vascular imaging, and truly multidisciplinary decision-making.
