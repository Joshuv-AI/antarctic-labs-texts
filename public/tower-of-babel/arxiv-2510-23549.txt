Cosmic Vine: High abundance of massive galaxies and dark matter halos in a forming cluster at z=3.44
Nikolaj B. Sillassen, Shuowen Jin, Georgios E. Magdis, Francesco Valentino, Emanuele Daddi, Raphael Gobat, Malte Brinch, Kei Ito, Tao Wang, Hanwen Sun, Gabriel Brammer, Sune Toft, Thomas Greve

Abstract
The Cosmic Vine is a massive protocluster at z = 3.44 z=3.44 in the JWST CEERS field, offering an ideal laboratory for studying the early phases of cluster formation. Using the data from the DAWN JWST Archive, we conduct a comprehensive study on the large-scale structure, stellar mass function (SMF), quiescent members, and dark matter halos in the Cosmic Vine. First, we spectroscopically confirm 136 galaxies in the Vine at z ≈ 3.44 z\approx 3.44 , and an additional 47 galaxies belonging to a diffuse foreground structure at z ≈ 3.34 z\approx 3.34 which we dub the Leaf. We identify four subgroups comprising the Cosmic Vine and two subgroups within the Leaf. Second, we identified 11 quiescent members with log ⁡ ( M ∗ / M ⊙ ) = 9.5 − 11.0 \log(M_{*}/{M_{\odot}})=9.5-11.0 , the largest sample of quiescent galaxies in overdense environments at z > 3 z>3 , which gives an enhanced quiescent galaxy number density ∼ 2 × 10 − 4 \sim 2\times 10^{-4} cMpc -3 that is three times above the field level at log ⁡ ( M ∗ / M ⊙ ) > 10 \log(M_{*}/{M_{\odot}})>10 . Notably, these quiescent members form a tight red sequence on the color-magnitude diagram, making it one of the earliest red sequences known to date. Third, by constructing the SMFs for both star-forming and quiescent members, we find that both SMFs are top-heavy, with a significantly enhanced quiescent fraction at log ⁡ ( M ∗ / M ⊙ ) > 10.5 \log(M_{*}/{M_{\odot}})>10.5 compared to field counterparts. The stellar mass–size analysis reveals that star-forming members are more compact at higher masses than their field counterparts. Finally, we estimate a halo mass of log ⁡ ( M h / M ⊙ ) = 13.2 ± 0.3 \log(M_{\rm h}/{M_{\odot}})=13.2\pm 0.3 for the protocluster core, and log ⁡ ( M h / M ⊙ ) = 11.9 − 12.4 \log(M_{\rm h}/{M_{\odot}})=11.9-12.4 for satellite subgroups. The phase-space analysis indicates that three subgroups are likely infalling to the core. This work reveals a high abundance of massive galaxies and dark matter halos in this forming cluster, demonstrating the accelerated assembly of massive galaxies in massive halos when the Universe was less than 2 billion years old.



1
Introduction

Clusters of galaxies are the most massive structures in the Universe bound by the immense gravity of a virialized dark matter halo.
The galaxy population in local galaxy clusters is dominated by old gigantic ellipticals that have little ongoing star formation, which tightly assemble in a ’red-sequence’ in colour-magnitude space
(
Kodama et al. 1998
;
Valentinuzzi et al. 2011
;
Wetzel et al. 2012
)
.
How these massive cluster galaxies formed and ceased their star formation remain open questions, and probing the progenitors of clusters at early cosmic time is key to the answers.
Protoclusters, representing the densest structures in the early Universe with accelerated galaxy formation and evolution, are promising progenitors of local galaxy clusters
(
Chiang et al. 2013
;
Overzier 2016
)
.
Massive galaxies in protoclusters hold critical clues for the formation of brightest cluster galaxies (BCGs) and massive ellipticals in local clusters, and massive quiescent members are particularly important for studying the emergence of the red sequence and testing the models of galaxy quenching and environmental effects
(
Strazzullo et al. 2016
;
Kubo et al. 2021
;
Ito et al. 2023
;
Ito et al. 2025
;
Afanasiev et al. 2023
;
Sun et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Xu et al. 2025
)
.
Cosmological simulations predict that protoclusters form within dense nodes of the filamentary cosmic web at early cosmic time and grow through accretion of cold gas and mergers of galaxies and dark matter halos
(
Dekel et al. 2009
;
Mandelker et al. 2020
;
Schaye et al. 2023
;
Montenegro-Taborda et al. 2023
;
Nelson et al. 2024
)
.
Observations have revealed the large-scale cosmic web and extreme overdensities of galaxies at high redshift, showing a coherent general picture as seen in simulations. Although, the models have not yet been thoroughly tested, and simulations have been seen with clear shortcomings compared with observations
(
Shen et al. 2022
;
Kimmig et al. 2023
;
Jin et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. For example, current simulations often fail to realistically reproduce protoclusters and massive galaxies within them, preventing us from investigating detailed physics of cluster galaxy formation and the environmental effects that shape their evolution
(
Granato et al. 2015
;
Bassini et al. 2020
;
Jin et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. Dark matter halos play a fundamental role in structure formation, but assessing their masses and properties is extremely challenging in observations.
Therefore, deep observations in high-
z
protocluster fields and improved halo mass estimates are crucial for unveiling both the bright and dark sides of early cluster formation.

To date, massive protoclusters have been discovered at the epoch of cosmic noon (
z\sim 2
) up to cosmic dawn (
z\sim 6
) using various methods (e.g.,
Pentericci et al. 2000
;
Gobat et al. 2011
;
Capak et al. 2011
;
Walter et al. 2012
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Oteo et al. 2018
;
Miller et al. 2018
;
Pavesi et al. 2018
;
McConachie et al. 2022
;
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Jin et al. 2024
;
Sillassen et al. 2024
).
However, due to the limited sensitivity and wavelength coverage of the Hubble space telescope (HST) and ground-based facilities, our view of these protoclusters is based on only a small number of galaxies that could be confirmed with spectroscopy, such as Ly
\alpha
emitters (LAEs), H
\alpha
emitters (HAEs)
(
Shimakawa et al. 2014
;
Brinch et al. 2024
;
Pérez-Martínez et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, and dusty star-forming galaxies (DSFGs)
(
Daddi et al. 2009
;
Dannerbauer et al. 2014
;
Oteo et al. 2018
;
Miller et al. 2018
;
Jin et al. 2021
;
Sillassen et al. 2022
)
.
Without highly complete memberships and deep multi-wavelength observations, it is infeasible to robustly constrain the global properties (e.g., total stellar masses), the subtle environmental effects, and the dark matter halos.
Recently, JWST has provided a great chance to discover protoclusters at high redshift. Especially, massive structures have been discovered in JWST deep fields with highly complete memberships
(
Sun et al. 2024
;
Jin et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Umehata et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.
These discoveries have revealed large-scale structures that span tens of Mpc
(
Jin et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
)
. Massive quiescent galaxies have been discovered in dense environments at
z>3

(
Kubo et al. 2021
;
McConachie et al. 2022
;
Jin et al. 2024
;
Tanaka et al. 2024
;
Kakimoto et al. 2024
;
Ito et al. 2025
;
de Graaff et al. 2025b
)
, and the red sequence has been revealed at
z>2

(
Willis et al. 2020
;
Ito et al. 2023
;
Tanaka et al. 2024
)
, which has allowed the study of potential environmental effects. Recently, top-heavy stellar mass functions (SMFs) have been found in protoclusters at
z\gtrsim 2.5

(
Sun et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2025
)
, indicating a top-down scenario of cluster galaxy formation.
With an increasing number of JWST images and spectra, we are at a great time to further push these frontiers and unveil the detailed processes that govern the formation and growth of galaxy clusters.

On the one hand, simulations predict that protoclusters consist of a series of dark matter halos of subgroups assembled along the cosmic web.
Nevertheless, characterizing dark matter halos with observations remains a challenging task, because traditional tracers such as diffuse X-ray emission, Sunyaev-Zel’dovich effect, and gravitational lensing are observationally expensive at high redshift
(
Clowe et al. 2006
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Gobat et al. 2019
;
Di Mascolo et al. 2023
;
Cha et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Travascio et al. 2025
)
, leaving only stellar to halo mass relations (SHMR) as a practical tool to estimate dark matter masses for high-
z
samples, although carrying a large uncertainty. Recently, the exploration of SHMR has been pushed towards higher redshift
(
Shuntov et al. 2022
;
Paquereau et al. 2025
)
, and stellar mass measurements have been significantly improved with deep multi-wavelength surveys
(
Weaver et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
, allowing for better constraints of the halo mass of
z\gtrsim 3
structures.
For example,
Sillassen et al. 2024
extensively tested several methods of estimating halo masses in a sample of massive groups at
1.5<z<4
using data from the Northern Extended Millimeter Array (NOEMA) and the COSMOS2020 catalog
(
Weaver et al. 2022
)
, finding that the SHMR-based methods can constrain the halo mass uncertainty down to approximately
0.3\,{\rm dex}
at
z\sim 3
. With these improved methods and new JWST data, protoclusters in JWST deep fields provide us ideal targets to characterize the assembly of massive dark matter halos.

In this paper, we use JWST data to investigate the galaxy population, SMFs, and dark matter halos in the
z=3.44
protocluster Cosmic Vine found by
Jin et al. 2024
in the JWST CEERS field.
In
Fig.

1
we present the observations, the sample selection, and the methodology.
Section

3
describes the main results and discusses the implications. We summarise our results and conclude in
Section

4
.
We adopt a flat cosmology with
\Omega_{m}=0.27
,
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.73
, and
H_{0}=70\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}
, and use an initial mass function (IMF) from
Chabrier 2003
. All reported magnitudes are in the AB system
(
Oke 1974
)
.

2.1
DJA photometry and spec-z catalogs

For the photometric data throughout the paper, we use JWST and HST data from the publicly available DAWN JWST Archive
(
Valentino et al. 2023a
;
Brammer & Valentino 2025
;
de Graaff et al. 2025a
;
Heintz et al. 2025
, DJA; photometry v7.4; spec-z v4.4;)
. For spectroscopic data, we make use of the DJA spectra compilation v4.4 in addition to the
Jin et al. 2024
sample, which includes new NIRSpec data from the following JWST programs; AEGIS (PID #1213, PI: Luetzgendorf), CEERS (PID #1345, PI: Finkelstein; PID #2565, PI: Glazebrook; PID #2750, PI: Arrabal-Haro; PID #4106, PI: Nelson; PID #4287, PI: Mason), RUBIES (PID #4233, PI: de Graaff), CAPERS (PID #6368, PI: Dickinson), and DeepDive (PID #3567, PI: Valentino).

To determine the completeness of the DJA CEERS catalog, we use the F444W photometry and fit a power law to the observed magnitudes, and determine where the observed magnitudes deviate from the power law by 20 percent, as shown in
Fig.

6
.
We estimate the 80% completeness of the F444W observations in CEERS to be 28.8 AB mag, while the 80% completeness in F444W is 27.1 mag for the Cosmic Vine members.

The accuracy of the photometric redshifts are determined by calculating the normalized median absolute deviation (
\sigma_{\rm NMAD}
, Eq. 3 in
Weaver et al. 2022
).
The fraction of outliers,
\eta
, is defined as
\eta=|\Delta z|>0.15(1+z_{\rm spec})
, and the bias is defined as
b={\rm median}(\Delta z)
. We show the
z_{\rm phot}-z_{\rm spec}
comparison in
Fig.

8
, where we find
\sigma_{\rm NMAD}=0.017
,
\eta=5.32\%
, and
b=0.001
.

2.2
Membership and completeness

Using the CEERS catalogue from the DJA, we identify photometric candidate members by selecting galaxies with the 16th and 84th percentile
z_{\rm phot}
within
z=3.44\pm 0.25
, this selection is chosen to cover both peaks in the spec-
z
distribution of CEERS (
Fig.

7
), while accounting for photo-
z
uncertainty (
Sections

2.1
and
8
).
Spectroscopic members are selected as galaxies with
3.3<z_{\rm spec}<3.5
, encompassing the two identified peaks in the spec-
z
distribution of CEERS (
Fig.

7
). The redshift distribution of spectroscopically confirmed members of the Vine and the Leaf can be seen in
Fig.

7
.

By enforcing the 16th and 84th percentile of the photometric redshift probability density function (PDF(z)) of each galaxy to be in a small threshold, we are limiting the number of expected interlopers, however, we are also introducing a bias toward brighter galaxies. This bias can be seen in
Fig.

6
, where the 80% completeness level of members is 1.7 mag brighter than the field. To estimate the mass completeness, we follow the method from
Pozzetti et al. 2010
. In short, we select the 30% faintest galaxies above the completeness limit, and rescale their masses as if they were detected at the completeness limit following
Eq.

1
.

\log M_{m_{\rm lim}}=\log M_{\ast}+0.4(m_{\rm F444W}-m_{\rm lim})

(1)

Then we define the mass completeness limit as the 95th percentile of the
M_{m_{\rm lim}}
distribution. This yields stellar mass completeness limits of
M_{\ast}>10^{8.32}\,{\rm M_{\odot}}
for the field, and
M_{\ast}>10^{8.61}\,{\rm M_{\odot}}
for the Vine members.

We find 183 spec-
z
confirmed galaxies and 314 photo-
z
candidate members.
Based on these newly identified members, we update the galaxy overdensity using the mapping code from
Brinch et al. 2023
, from which the updated overdensity contours are shown in Fig.
1
.
We note that given the uncertainty of photometric redshifts (
Fig.

8
), it is impossible to distinguish if photo-
z
candidate members belong to the Vine or the Leaf, so we treat all candidate members as members of the whole structure.

2.3
Bagpipes SED fitting

Using a similar setup as described in
Jin et al. 2024
, we fit the DJA JWST+HST photometry using Bagpipes
(
Carnall et al. 2018
)
. For spectroscopically confirmed members, we fix the redshift of the fit, while for candidate members, we let the redshift vary between
3<z_{\rm phot}<4
. In detail, we use a double-power-law star formation history with log priors allowing for stellar metallicities in the range
-2.3<\log(Z/{\rm Z_{\odot}})<0.7
and ages in the range
0.1\,{\rm Gyr}<\tau<15\,{\rm Gyr}
, a dust attenuation law from
Salim & Narayanan 2020
in the range
0<A_{V}<4
, and nebular emission with radiation fields in the range
-4<\log(U)<-2
and gas-phase metallicities in the range
-2.3<\log(Z_{\rm neb}/{\rm Z_{\odot}})<0.7
.

2.4
Quiescent galaxy selection

We classify quiescent galaxies as galaxies that either fulfil the rest-frame UVJ colour criteria from
Williams et al. 2009
(
Fig.

2
-left), or have a specific star formation rate (
{\rm sSFR}={\rm SFR}/M_{\ast}
) of
\log({\rm sSFR_{\rm 84th}}/{\rm yr^{-1}})<-10.3
(
\sim 1.8\,{\rm dex}
below the star-forming main sequence at
z=3.44
,
Schreiber et al. 2015
). The adopted sSFR limit is conservative compared to the criterion of
Carnall et al. 2023
, which would be
\log({\rm sSFR_{50th}}/{\rm yr^{-1}})<-10.0
at the redshift of the Cosmic Vine. We present the results in
Section

3.2
. We note that while the completeness limit of quiescent galaxies is likely fainter than the SFG completeness limit
(
Weaver et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
, the faintest QG member is 2 mag brighter than the SFG completeness limit in F444W. Further, the least massive QG is 1 dex more massive than the SFG stellar mass completeness limit, where the difference in COSMOS2020 is at most 0.5 dex
(
Weaver et al. 2022
)
.

2.5
Dark Matter halo mass estimates

To estimate the dark matter halo masses, we follow methods presented in
Sillassen et al. 2024
. In short, using the 2D density contours (
Fig.

1
) as initial halo centres, assuming spherical shape, we get an initial halo mass by scaling the most massive galaxy within a 15” circle with the stellar to halo mass relation (SHMR) from
Behroozi et al. 2013
(
M_{\rm h}(1)
1
1

1

We use
M_{\rm h}(n)
as short to refer to the n-th halo mass method presented in
Sillassen et al. 2024
). Using the recovered initial halo mass, we infer a virial radius using the relation from
Goerdt et al. 2010
, and find a new halo centre by calculating the stellar mass weighted centre of mass of the galaxies within the new virial radius. With the updated centres, we calculate the total stellar mass, accounting for background contamination and stellar mass completeness
(
Daddi et al. 2021
;
Daddi et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. We then scale the total stellar mass with SHMRs from
Shuntov et al. 2022
(
M_{\rm h}(2a)
) and
van der Burg et al. 2014
(
M_{\rm h}(2b)
). Other than using stellar masses, we estimate the overdensity of each halo and use this overdensity to estimate halo mass (
M_{\rm h}(3)
). We do this iteratively, until the halo mass and halo members converge. The halo masses of the four methods are overall consistent, we thus adopt an average of the four as the best estimate of halo mass.

Further, we estimate halo mass of the core using the velocity dispersion of member galaxies in two ways, for both methods we define the core members as galaxies which within the expected virial radius and
3\sigma_{v}
of a
\log(M_{h}/{\rm M_{\odot}})\sim 13
halo at
z=3.438
, (1) we measure the standard deviation of redshift by fitting the redshift distribution of the core galaxies with a Gaussian, which we convert to a velocity dispersion with
\sigma_{v}=c\sigma_{z}/(1+z)
. (2) following
Bayliss et al. 2014
we calculate the square root of the biweight variance of the galaxy proper velocities (
v_{i}=c(z_{i}-z)/(1+z)
) using jackknife sampling.

3
Results and discussion

3.1
Large-scale structure and subgroups

With a total of 183 spectroscopic redshifts and 314 photo-
z
members, we confirm the large-scale structure reported by
Jin et al. 2024
. The new spec-
z
s significantly improve the membership completeness, enabling us to identify several sub-structures in the Vine. As shown in the spec-
z
histogram in
Fig.

7
, we find two major peaks at
z=3.44
and
z=3.34
, of which the
z=3.44
peak traces the main structure of the Vine. The
z=3.34
peak reveals a foreground structure that is obviously more diffuse than the Vine, which we dub the Leaf. The new data confirm the massive and overdense nature of the Vine, while the Leaf is dominated by low-mass galaxies and consists of 2 substructures. The spec-
z
confirmed members of the Vine span an area of
8.2\,{\rm pMpc}\times 3.6\,{\rm pMpc}
.

As traced by the galaxy overdensities in
Fig.

1
, we identify four subgroups in the Vine. In the core, four more galaxies are spectroscopically confirmed in addition to the sample from
Jin et al. 2024
.
The other subgroups also host massive galaxies (
\log(M_{\ast}/{\rm M_{\odot}})>10
), with two to ten spectroscopically confirmed members in each subgroup.
In addition to spec-
z
members, we selected 314 photo-
z
members with
3.19<z_{\rm phot}<3.69
. Together, we have 497 confirmed and candidate members that extend over the entire CEERS field, corresponding to
13.1\,{\rm pMpc}\times 4.1\,{\rm pMpc}
.
We note that the true size of the large-scale structure is likely extending further than the FoV of the CEERS field, additional data are needed to verify if this is the case.

This large number of quiescent galaxies in a

8.2\times 3.6
Mpc
2
in physical scale (as reported in
Section

3.1
) and a redshift range of
3.4<z<3.5
, which correspond to a co-moving volume of 4.86 cMpc
3
. There are 9 quiescent members with
\log(M_{*}/M_{\odot})>10

1

2.1\times 10^{-4}
cMpc
-3

3.1

6.57\pm 1.03\times 10^{-5}
cMpc
-3
reported by
Baker et al. 2025
at
3.0<z<3.5
and

With a sample of 11 quiescent galaxies, we investigate if the ubiquitous red sequence of local galaxy clusters is already present in this early large-scale structure. To do this, we create a colour-magnitude diagram using NIRCam F150W/F200W colour, where the two bands respectively cover blue- and red-wards of the Balmer and
D_{n}{\rm 4000}
break at
z=3.44
. In
Fig.

2

M_{\rm F200W}=21
, resulting in a negative slope
-0.07\pm 0.06
and an intercept of
1.5\pm 0.1
. This red sequence is in excellent agreement with the observed red sequence intercept (
1.41
) and slope (
-0.013
) of the
z=1.98
cluster XLSSC122
(
Willis et al. 2020
)
.
This notable red sequence is one of the two earliest known to date: the other one is at
z\sim 4
reported by
Tanaka et al. 2024
, which exhibits a red sequence in
H-K
vs
K
diagram but is more scattered than the Vine’s. We note that the structure reported by
Tanaka et al. 2024
is more diffuse and less complete in terms of spectroscopically confirmed members than the Vine.

The early emergence of a red sequence and the high abundance of quiescent galaxies suggest that the Cosmic Vine is in an early stage of forming typical cluster galaxies like the ones in the local Universe.
As is evident from the multiple subgroups, it demonstrates that the red sequence arises before the subgroups coalesce and the structure virializes.
Intriguingly, only two massive quiescent members are in the core, and the majority of them are located outside of the densest regions. As high pressure hot gas is unlikely to be present in such a large elongated structure, it disfavours the dominance of environmental quenching mechanisms that require hot gas and virialization, e.g., ram pressure stripping (RPS). Instead, these massive galaxies are likely dominated by mass quenching mechanisms, such as AGN and compact starbursts
(
Jin et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
, indicating that they share similar quenching pathways with field quiescent galaxies
(
Carnall et al. 2023
;
Ito et al. 2025
;
Valentino et al. 2025
)
.

3.3
Stellar mass function and quiescent fraction

To further characterise the assembly of the galaxy population in the Vine, we establish the SMFs of star-forming galaxies (SFGs) and QGs separately and fit them with the Schechter function (
Schechter 1976
,
Eq.

2
). We use a Monte-Carlo Markov-Chain (MCMC) fitting procedure for both members and the rest of the CEERS field. We select a sample of 2863 field galaxies in CEERS as a control sample, which are defined as galaxies with
3<z_{\rm phot}<4
that are not selected as candidate members of the Vine.
The SMFs are constructed as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi d\log M=&\ln(10)\exp\left(-10^{\log M-\log M^{\ast}}\right)\\
&\times\left(\Phi^{\ast}10^{\log M-\log M^{\ast}}\right)^{\alpha+1}d(\log M)\end{split}

(2)

We calculate the observed
\Phi d(\log M)
, following the
1/V_{\rm max}
method described in
Schmidt 1968
and
Weigel et al. 2016
. In short, for each galaxy in each mass-bin we estimate the maximum volume in which a galaxy at the given mass and redshift could be detected;
V_{\rm max,i}
. The
V_{\rm max,i}
for each galaxy is calculated with
Eq.

3

V_{\rm max,i}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\frac{\Omega_{\rm survey}}{\Omega_{\rm sky}}\left(d_{c}(z_{\rm max})^{3}-d_{c}(z_{\rm min})^{3}\right)

(3)

Where
\Omega_{\rm survey}
is the sky coverage of the survey (
105.2\,{\rm arcmin^{2}}
), and
\Omega_{\rm sky}=41253\,{\rm deg^{2}}
is the total area of the sky,
d_{c}(z)
is the co-moving distance to redshift
z
,
z_{\rm min}
is the lower edge of the redshift bin, and
z_{\rm max,i}
is the minimum value of the upper edge of the redshift bin and the maximum redshift at which a galaxy at this mass could be detected;
z_{\rm max,i}=\min(z_{\rm max,bin},z_{\rm max,galaxy})
. The number density in each mass bin is then weighted by the JWST/F444W completeness of each galaxy and
{V_{\rm max,i}}

\Phi d\log M=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{bin}}\frac{w_{\rm complete,i}}{V_{\rm\max,i}}

(4)

The SMF of the Vine is normalized to the field SMF, based on the number of galaxies in each sample.

The large number of spec-z confirmed members across the entire structure allow us to create a phase-space diagram and assess whether the members and subgroups are gravitationally bound. To construct the phase-space diagram, we firstly measure the projected radii from the centre of the most massive core to the spec-z confirmed galaxies in physical kpc. Then we measure the velocity difference between to the redshift of central galaxies (
Table

3
), and the galaxies with
\Delta v_{i}=c(z_{i}-z_{\rm halo})/(1+z_{\rm halo})
. The velocity differences are multiplied by
\sqrt{3}
to correct the measured projected velocities to 3D velocities
(
Rhee et al. 2017
)
assuming velocity isotropy. Finally, we calculate the escape velocity as a function of distance from the central halo, using a Navarro-Frenk-White
(
Navarro et al. 1996
)
density profile as described in
Rhee et al. 2017
, shown in
Eq.

6
, where
c_{\rm halo}
is the concentration parameter of the dark matter halo. We estimate the

using the relation from
Ludlow et al. 2016
.

v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM_{\rm vir}}{R_{\rm vir}}\frac{\frac{\ln\left(1+c_{\rm halo}\frac{R}{R_{\rm vir}}\right)}{\frac{R}{R_{\rm vir}}}}{\ln(1+c_{\rm halo})-\frac{c_{\rm halo}}{1+c_{\rm halo}}}}

(6)

Fig.

5
-left, the members of the core are gravitationally bound to the most massive dark matter halo. Interestingly, we also find several massive galaxies with the same redshift as the core out to a projected radius of
R_{\rm proj}\sim 3\,{\rm pMpc}
. This projected radius corresponds to a radius of
\sim 15\,{\rm cMpc}
, aligning with the expected effective radius of a Coma-like cluster progenitor at
z\sim 3.5

(
Chiang et al. 2013
)
. The estimated most massive halo mass of
\log(M_{h}/{\rm M_{\odot}})=13.2\pm 0.3
is also consistent with the expectation of a Coma-like progenitor at
z\sim 3.5
. Together with the size and mass of the Cosmic Vine, all evidence suggests it will evolve to a massive galaxy cluster with
\log(M_{h}/{\rm M_{\odot}})>15
at redshift
z\sim 0
.
Furthermore, we show the centres of each of the identified subgroups in
Fig.

5
-right. Two subgroups are enveloped within the escape velocity curves, and the third subgroup (Halo 4) is just above the velocity curve. This could indicate that the three subgroups are infalling clumps, which will eventually merge with the core.

We note that massive galaxies not bound to the core are a part of the filamentary large structure that connects the core and subgroups of the Cosmic Vine.
We also note that the 3D separation between the core of the Cosmic Vine and the Leaf is
\sim 87.4\,{\rm cMpc}
. While this distance is larger than the expected extent of a single forming galaxy cluster at
z\sim 3.5

(
Muldrew et al. 2015
;
Chiang et al. 2017
)
, it is well within the expected size of a filamentary structure of galaxy protoclusters similar to the Hyperion super-protocluster
(
Cucciati et al. 2018
;
Ata et al. 2022
)
.

4
Conclusions

The Cosmic Vine is one of the richest structures discovered at
z>3
to date. In this work, we further revealed the large structure, and studied the member galaxy populations and the dark matter halos. We draw the following conclusions:

1.

Using JWST archival data, we spectroscopically confirmed 136 members in the Cosmic Vine at
z\approx 3.44
, and 47 members in a newly discovered foreground structure at
z\approx 3.34
which we dub the Leaf. In total, we identified 314 candidate, and 183 confirmed members belonging to the whole structure, covering an area of
8.2\times 3.6\,{\rm Mpc^{2}}
.
We revealed six subgroups with halo mass
\log(M_{h}/{\rm M_{\odot}})\gtrsim 12
, in which four of them are within the Vine and the remaining two subgroups belongs to the Leaf.

2.

Using both UVJ and sSFR, we identified 11 quiescent members with
\log(M_{\ast}/{\rm M_{\odot}})=9.5-11.0
in the whole structure, constitutes the largest sample of quiescent galaxies in overdense environments at
z>3
to date. This large number of quiescent galaxies in a dense structure infers a high number density of quiescent galaxies
\sim 2\times 10^{-4}
cMpc
-3
that is three times above the field level at
\log(M_{*}/{\rm M_{\odot}})>10

(
Baker et al. 2025
)
, demonstrating accelerated galaxy evolution in dense environment.

3.

Remarkably, the quiescent galaxies form a tight red sequence on the color-magnitude diagram, significantly elevated from the star-forming members and the field galaxies—this is one of the earliest red sequences known to date.

4.

We find robust top-heavy SMFs for both star-forming and quiescent members of the Vine compared to the field counterparts, and the quiescent fraction is significantly elevated at high stellar mass
\log(M_{\ast}/{\rm M_{\odot}})>10.5
.

5.

Our stellar mass-size analysis shows that star-forming members are more compact than the field counterparts at higher stellar masses, which is consistent with the predictions from simulations
(
Sommer-Larsen & Toft 2010
, e.g,)
.

6.

The halo mass of the most massive subgroup, identified as the core of the structure in
Jin et al. 2024
, is estimated to be
\log(M_{h}/{\rm M_{\odot}})=13.2\pm 0.3
, and for the other five subgroups we estimate halo masses in the range
\log(M_{h}/{\rm M_{\odot}})=11.9-12.4
.

7.

The phase-space analysis of the structure reveals that several massive galaxies are gravitationally bound to the core, and members out to a distance of
R\sim 3\,{\rm pMpc}
have the same redshift as the core, consistent with the effective radius of coma-like cluster progenitors in simulations. Further, the three identified subgroups of the Vine are possibly infalling clumps, supporting the picture that these halos are merging and on the way to forming a massive cluster.

Coupling the evolved galactic population with the estimated mass and extent of the structure, the Cosmic Vine is likely evolving to a massive galaxy cluster with
\log(M_{h}/{\rm M_{\odot}})>15
at
z\sim 0
, and already seems to be in an early stage of maturing, before coalescing into a single dark-matter halo. With the wealth of data already available in CEERS, and more incoming, the Cosmic Vine will be an ideal laboratory to study different aspects of cluster formation and galaxy evolution in the future.
