Outcomes of radiotherapy of hepatocellular carcinoma: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Volker Rudat, Jing Li, Ruping Zhao, Yanyan Shi, Liang Huang, Maixuan Qiu, Jun Li, Andrew X Zhu, Yiqun Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTechnical advances and a better understanding of liver tolerance have changed the role of radiotherapy in the treatment of liver cancer. The goal of this retrospective study was to evaluate the outcomes of radiotherapy with curative intent in patients ineligible for surgery and palliative intent in patients with advanced disease.MethodsPatients were treated with stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), hypofractionated radiotherapy, or conventionally fractionated radiotherapy. Patients with a solitary liver tumor, no vascular invasion, Child-Pugh class A, and no extrahepatic tumors at the time of radiotherapy were considered for radiotherapy with curative intent, and the remaining patients for radiotherapy with palliative intent. Kaplan-Meier statistics and Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were used to evaluate overall survival.ResultsBetween 04/2019 and 08/2022, 93 consecutive patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) were treated external beam radiotherapy in our department. A total of 94.6% of the patients had received prior treatment. The 1-, 2-, and 3-year overall survival rates of patients treated with curative intent (n = 33) were 100%, 76%, and 65%, respectively, and those treated with palliative intent (n = 60) 63%, 31%, and 11%, respectively. No acute radiation reactions of grade ≥ 3 (CTCAE v5.0) were observed.ConclusionsOur data confirm that radiotherapy is an effective treatment option with curative intent for selected patients ineligible for surgery, and with palliative intent for those with advanced disease.Keywords: Hepatocellular cancer, Radiotherapy, SBRT, Hypofractionation, Overall survival

Background

Liver cancer is the third leading cause of cancer death after lung and colorectal cancer, and the sixth most frequently diagnosed cancer in 2022 [1]. Primary liver cancer mainly comprises hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (75% to 85% of cases) and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) (10% to 15% of cases), with chronic HBV or HCV infection responsible for 21% to 55% of HCC cases worldwide. Other risk factors for HCC include aflatoxin exposure, heavy alcohol consumption, excess body weight, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and smoking. The predominant cause of HCC is shifting worldwide from viral to nonviral, in parallel with the high global prevalence of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease and increasing alcohol consumption in many countries [2]. The age-standardized incidence rate for men and women is 22.4 and 7.2 in East Asia and 8.5 and 2.8 in Western Europe [2]. While HCC- and gallbladder cancer-related mortality has steadily decreased in the United States in recent years, the intrahepatic CCA- and ampulla of Vater cancer-related mortality has increased, and the extrahepatic CCA remained stable [3].

Technological advancements in image guidance (e.g., kilovoltage cone-beam computed tomography, kV-CBCT; optical surface monitoring system, OSMS), treatment planning and radiotherapy delivery (e.g., volumetric modulated arc therapy, VMAT; stereotactic body radiotherapy, SBRT) have made it possible to apply highly conformal dose distributions with improved target volume coverage and sparing of normal tissues. The application of higher radiation doses and a more thorough understanding of liver and organ at risk (OAR) tolerance have changed the role of radiotherapy in curative and palliative liver cancer therapy.

Surgical excision and liver transplantation are the most effective treatment options for eligible patients with HCC. Locoregional therapies, such as radiofrequency ablation (RFA), microwave ablation (MWA), transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE), transarterial radioembolisation (TARE), and stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), are the mainstay of treatment for unresectable HCC, metastatic HCC, or may serve as bridging or downstaging for liver transplantation. Meta-analyses have shown that SBRT is a viable alternative to other ablative locoregional therapies such as RFA [4] or TACE [5]. In early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma, SBRT has been utilized for patients with unresectable tumors that are unsuitable for ablative techniques such as MWA and RFA. SBRT has demonstrated remarkable efficacy as a standalone treatment or in combination with other locoregional therapies or systemic treatments in advanced-stage hepatocellular carcinoma [6]. A recent study demonstrated enhanced overall survival by incorporating SBRT with immune checkpoint inhibitors and anti-angiogenic treatment [7]. Further studies are required to explore the possible synergy between radiation therapy and immunotherapy [8].

In light of the recent promising findings, several guidelines have incorporated external beam radiotherapy into their recommendations for liver cancer therapy [9–14]. Owing to the still low number of prospective studies, others have not yet done so [15, 16].

This study was conducted at a newly established international cancer center in China, reflecting a dedicated effort to explore and standardize liver-directed radiation therapy. The aim of our retrospective analysis was to evaluate the outcomes of patients with unresectable HCC who received radiotherapy with curative intent, as well as those with advanced disease who underwent palliative radiotherapy. Additionally, we sought to compare our findings with those reported in existing literature.

Methods

Patients

Patients who received external beam radiotherapy for HCC in our department were retrospectively analyzed. The treatment planning involved the selection of patients into the treatment groups “radiotherapy with curative intent” and “radiotherapy with palliative intent”. The selection criteria for the treatment group radiotherapy with curative intent were presentation for radiotherapy with a solitary tumor, no other intra- or extrahepatic tumors, no macrovascular invasion, and Child-Pugh class A. The preferred treatment technique was SBRT. All other patients were included in the treatment group radiotherapy with palliative intent. The preferred treatment technique for these patients was moderate hypofractionation or SBRT.

Simulation

Patients were simulated in the supine position with arms above the head using a knee fix, feet fix, and wing board as positioning devices (CIVCO Medical Instruments Co., Inc., Orange, IA, USA). A multi-phase contrast-enhanced 4D computed tomography (4DCT) scan was performed on all patients with a slice thickness of 3 mm using a Brilliance Big Bore CT (Philips, Amsterdam, the Netherlands). In addition, a reference image was acquired using the optical surface monitoring system (OSMS) AlignRT (Vision RT Ltd., London, UK, Version 5.1.2). The patients were instructed to breathe shallowly during imaging. No vacuum cushions, abdominal pressure devices, or fiducial markers were used for patient positioning. Skin marks were applied using conventional 3-point localization.

Treatment planning and radiotherapy

Where possible, diagnostic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or positron emission tomography (PET/CT) images were co-registered with the treatment planning CT. Gross tumor volumes (GTVs) and clinical target volumes (CTVs) were manually contoured on all acquired phases of the 4DCT scan. GTV-CTV margins varied depending on tumor size and location, liver function and image quality. Typical margins were 5 mm for SBRT and 10 mm for hypofractionated radiotherapy. The internal target volume (ITV) was defined as the combined volume of the CTVs in multiple 4DCT phases. Based on a previous investigation of our study group using daily kV-CBCT and OSMS (AlignRT) [17], a setup margin of 3 mm in all directions was added to the ITV to obtain the planning target volume (PTV). Automatic contouring of the organs at risk (OAR) was performed using AccuContour Version 3.1 (Manteia Technology LTD, Xiamen, China). The dose to the PTV was prescribed according to the recommendations of the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurement (ICRU) Reports 50 and 62. The PTV was surrounded by the 95% isodose. The treatment planning system was Monaco Version 5.40.03 (Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden), and the treatment technique was Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (VMAT). Radiotherapy was delivered using VersaHD (Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden) with Agility MLC (5 mm leaves) and a Hexapod 6D treatment couch. The patients were instructed to breathe shallowly during beam application. During radiotherapy, daily online verification was performed using kV-CBCT and OSMS (AlignRT), and translational and rotational errors were corrected by adjusting the Hexapod 6D treatment couch.

Fractionation regimen

The selection criteria for SBRT included a PTV containing a liver tumor with a maximal tumor diameter of ≤5 cm, regardless of the presence or absence of other hepatic or extrahepatic tumors, and compliance with the dose constraints of the OARs. All other patients qualified for hypofractionation (preferred) or conventional fractionation. SBRT consisted of 5 or 6 radiotherapy fractions with a fraction dose of 6.0 Gy, 7.0 Gy, or 8.0 Gy. Hypofractionation consisted of 15 fractions, with a fraction dose of 2.67 Gy or 3.0 Gy. A fraction dose of 1.8 Gy was used for conventional fractionation. The prescribed total dose was dependent on the dose for the organs at risk (OAR). The dose constraints of OARs are listed in Table 1.

Data collection

Patient-, disease-, and treatment-related data were entered immediately after treatment planning into a custom-made database (Access, Microsoft, Redmond, USA). Dosimetric parameters were extracted from the dose-volume histograms (DVH). Acute adverse events were assessed weekly using the Common Toxicity Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5 (CTCAE v5.0). The maximum acute adverse event grade observed was used in the analysis. The “lymph node status” represented the locoregional lymph node metastasis status. The “extrahepatic tumor status” represented the extrahepatic distant metastasis status or status of the primary tumor of the patients treated for liver metastasis. The “tumor diameter” represented the maximal tumor diameter of the largest tumor within the PTV. All patient and disease characteristics reflect their status at the start of radiotherapy. To enable a comparison of the total radiation dose prescribed to the PTV of the different fractionation regimens, the biologically effective dose (BED10) using an α/β ratio of 10 was calculated. Follow-up data was collected from patient files. Many patients came from remote areas and received follow-up care at hospitals close to home. To obtain data from patients unable to visit our hospitals for follow-up, we contacted remote hospitals, patients, or their families.

Statistical analysis

Overall survival was defined as the time from the date of the first radiotherapy treatment to the date of death from any cause or the last follow-up. Univariable and multivariable analysis of the possible impact factors on the overall survival were performed using the Cox proportional hazards model. Factors with a p-value < 0.2 on univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable model. Model selection was performed using a backward stepwise strategy with the likelihood ratio test. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated, and the log-rank test was used to test for differences between the groups. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess correlations between tumor diameter, GTV, and PTV. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software package (version 26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago). Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using the statistical software MedCalc (version 23.0.2, MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2024).

Results

Patients

From 04/2019 to 08/2022, our department treated 93 patients with external beam radiotherapy for HCC. The patient, disease, and treatment characteristics are presented in Table 2. Thirty-three patients (35.5%) were treated with curative intend, and 60 (65.5%) with palliative intend. Two of the 93 patients did not receive the full total dose as planned because one patient developed tumor progression and another acute appendicitis. Of the 36 patients treated with SBRT, three had a tumor diameter greater than 5 cm. The dose constraints on the OARs could be respected, and the decision for SBRT was made owing to strong patient preference. At the time of statistical analysis, 56 of the 93 patients had died (56%).

Prior therapy

Of the 93 patients, 88 received single or multiple therapies prior to radiotherapy. 78 patients received local therapies (liver surgery, n = 41; TACE, n = 70; RFA, n = 26), and 64 patients received systemic therapy (targeted therapy, n = 56; immunotherapy, n = 44; chemotherapy, n = 6). 24 patients received local therapy alone, and 10 patients received systemic therapy alone. Common agents for targeted therapy were Lenvatinib and Sorafenib; for immunotherapy, the PD-1 inhibitors Pembrolizumab and Nivolumab; and for chemotherapy, 5FU, Capecitabine, Cisplatin, and Gemcitabine. The median time between the diagnosis of HCC and liver radiotherapy was 19 months.

Overall survival

The 1-, 2-, and 3-year overall survival rates of patients with HCC receiving radiotherapy with curative intent (n = 33) were 100%, 76%, and 65%, respectively (Table 3, Fig. 1A). Patients with HCC receiving radiotherapy with palliative intent (n = 50) showed significantly lower overall survival rates of 63, 31, and 11%, respectively.

Univariable analysis revealed significant associations for disease extent factors (BCLC stage, lymph node status, PVTT), dose parameters (BED10, fractionation regimen), and tumor volume measures (tumor diameter, GTV, PTV) (Table 4).

Multivariable analysis identified BCLC stage, PVTT status, and tumor diameter as the sole significant independent predictors.

All factors were tested dichotomously. To further evaluate tumor diameter’s role in HCC patients’ survival, multivariable analysis was repeated with continuous tumor diameter and BED10. It reconfirmed BCLC stage, PVTT status, and tumor diameter as independent predictors (HR 1.09 (95% CI: 1.02–1.16), p = 0.014), indicating a 9% increased mortality risk per 1-cm diameter increase (BCLC/PVTT held constant).

Spearman rank correlations were highly significant: r = 0.850 (tumor diameter and GTV), r = 0.819 (tumor diameter and PTV), and r = 0.959 (GTV and PTV). However, neither the GTV nor the PTV emerged as independent predictors in the multivariable model, even when the tumor diameter was excluded. Since tumor diameter only crudely approximates true tumor volume, its prognostic value in HCC must be interpreted with caution.

Acute adverse events

Radiotherapy was well tolerated. In total, two acute adverse events ≥ grade 3 (abdominal pain, n = 1; fever, n = 1) were observed in two of the 93 patients (Table 5). These adverse events were probably not caused by radiotherapy. One of the two patients developed a local tumor progress, and the second acute appendicitis. The most common adverse event was fatigue grade 1 (n = 12, 12.9%).

Many patients came for treatment from remote areas and underwent follow-up examinations close to home. Although we could collect overall survival data for all patients, gathering reliable data on local control and late toxicity proved challenging. Owing to missing data, we decided not to analyze local tumor control and late toxicity in this analysis. However, we observed only one late adverse event ≥ grade 3, which was likely related to radiotherapy (duodenal bleeding).

Discussion

Patients with HCC receiving radiotherapy with curative intent (n = 33) achieved 1-, 2-, and 3-year overall survival rates of 100, 76 and 65%, respectively.

These results are well within the range reported by other study groups using SBRT for early-stage HCC (Table 6). The median BED10 (min; max) was 60 Gy (38 Gy; 72 Gy). However, several studies have applied higher radiation doses and observed higher overall survival rates (Table 6).

In a recent expert consensus paper, the minimum treatment dose of ablative radiation therapy in patients with HCC was defined as BED10 ≥ 80 Gy [40]. However, in the first round of the modified Delphi survey of the expert panel, most participants agreed that the minimum ablative BED10 should be either 60 Gy (e.g., 35 Gy in 5 fractions, as applied in our study) or 80 Gy (e.g., 43 Gy in 5 fractions).

Zheng et al. performed a retrospective analysis of 150 patients with centrally located early-stage HCC treated with definitive SBRT as an alternative to surgery [39]. A median BED10 of 97 (72–132) Gy was applied to this highly selected patient group. The 1-, 2-, and 3-year overall survival rates were 98, 94, and 87%, respectively. No grade 3 or higher adverse events were observed according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE v4.03). Eighteen patients developed classic or non-classic radiation-induced liver disease (RILD). On multivariable analysis, BED10 > 100 Gy yielded significantly better overall survival than BED10 ≤ 100 Gy. Better overall survival rates with BED10 > 100 Gy have been reported by several study groups [41–47]. In contrast, other study groups did not find a significant association between higher SBRT dose and outcome [48–52]. In our analysis, BED10 was not significantly associated with overall survival in the multivariable model. This finding may reflect the comparatively lower BED10 values administered to patients treated with curative intent in our cohort, relative to those reported in studies employing higher radiation dose regimens (Table 6). Multiple factors impact the outcome of SBRT, and the total radiation dose must be carefully selected to avoid severe toxicity. Further randomized studies are required to determine the optimal radiation dose for specific patient populations.

Palliative radiotherapy was administered to 56 patients with BCLC stage C HCC in our study. These patients achieved overall survival rates of 61,30, and 12% at 1, 2, and 3 years, respectively. The median survival was 16 months (Table 3). Our findings align closely with those reported by other study groups investigating radiotherapy for HCC BCLC stage C (Table 7). A multinational, multi-institutional, retrospective study involving 1035 patients with PVTT compared the clinical outcomes of liver-directed combined radiotherapy with sorafenib alone [55]. The liver-directed combined radiotherapy group demonstrated a significantly improved median overall survival. A multicenter phase 3 randomized clinical trial comparing sorafenib treatment with SBRT followed by sorafenib in patients with locally advanced HCC demonstrated a clinically meaningful, though not statistically significant, improvement in overall survival with SBRT compared to sorafenib alone [56]. These findings underscore the potential clinical efficacy of palliative radiotherapy in patients with advanced HCC. However, while in our study the overall survival of patients treated with palliative radiotherapy aligns with outcomes reported in the literature, the precise impact of SBRT remains challenging to interpret due to the heterogeneous systemic treatments and tumor characteristics among our patient cohort.

In our study, the maximal tumor diameter was significantly associated with overall survival in the multivariable analysis. Our data indicate that, among patients with HCC, the risk of death increases by approximately 9% for each 1‑cm increase in tumor diameter. However, as neither GTV nor PTV reached statistical significance in the multivariable model, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Consistent with our results, tumor size has been identified as an independent prognostic factor for overall survival following SBRT in several previous studies [7, 20, 28, 31, 37, 39, 47, 50, 57]. The tumor size is most probably related to the number of clonogen tumor cells, and the number of clonogen tumor cells to the total radiation dose required for effective sterilization of the tumor cells. An analysis using radiobiological models of data from 4323 patients with early-stage HCC from 41 published papers revealed a steep dose response in the BED ranges of 50–80 Gy with a plateau at around 120 Gy [58]. The authors concluded that most existing hypofractionated protocols [59] are suboptimal for achieving maximal long-term tumor control.

Other prognostic factors that were statistically significant on multivariable analysis in our study were BCLC and PVTT status (Table 4). BCLC stage and PVTT are well-known poor prognostic factors in patients with HCC. In some studies, Child-Pugh class has been reported to be a significant independent prognostic factor for overall survival [28, 48, 52, 55]. In our study, 92.4% of HCC patients had Child-Pugh class A. Therefore, its impact could not be evaluated.

In agreement with the literature, radiotherapy was well-tolerated. No clear radiotherapy-attributable acute adverse events of grade 3 or higher were observed. A meta-analysis of 1889 HCC patients treated with SBRT reported no acute adverse events CTCAE ≥ grade 3 in five studies; 1%-7% in five studies, and 16%-30% in three studies [50].

The limitations of our study are related to its retrospective study design and heterogeneous patient cohort. The strengths of this retrospective study are the consistent treatment according to predefined radiotherapy concepts, the documentation of patient- and treatment-related data before and during radiotherapy, and the treatment of 93 HCC patients over a relatively short 40-month time period.

Conclusions

Our data confirm that radiotherapy represents an effective treatment modality with curative intent for selected patients ineligible for surgery, and with palliative intent for those with advanced disease.
