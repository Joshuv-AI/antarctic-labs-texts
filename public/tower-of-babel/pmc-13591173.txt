Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Assessing the Effects of Focal and Radial Extracorporeal Shockwave Therapy on Pain and Function in Patellar Tendinopathy
Authors: Adam Friedman, Seth Spicer, Jared Garfinkle, Hassan Tahir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness of focal or radial extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT) in improving pain and function in individuals with patellar tendinopathy (PT) through a systematic review and meta-analysis.Data SourcesA comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science up to May 27, 2025. Search terms were related to ESWT and PT. Additional gray literature was assessed using Google Scholar.Study SelectionStudies were included if they used focused or radial ESWT to treat PT and reported pre- and posttreatment outcomes using the visual analog scale (VAS) and/or the Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment (VISA). Randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, and retrospective chart reviews were eligible. Thirteen studies (n=530 for VAS; n=369 for VISA) met the inclusion criteria.Data ExtractionTwo authors independently extracted VAS, VISA, SDs, and sample sizes. Data from 12- to 16-week follow-up were prioritized when multiple follow-up points were reported.Data SynthesisA random-effects meta-analysis was performed across 13 studies totaling 899 participants (530 for VAS, 369 for VISA). ESWT demonstrated a significant VAS reduction of 2.53 (from 5.24 to 2.71) and VISA increase of 20.22 (from 54.65 to 74.86), both exceeding minimal clinically important difference thresholds (VAS: 1.2; VISA: 7.3). Effect sizes were large (VAS d=−1.54; VISA d=1.76). Randomized trials generally had a low risk of bias; nonrandomized studies had higher bias but minimal influence on pooled effects. Certainty of evidence was rated using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation: 4 high, 6 moderate, and 3 low-quality studies. Heterogeneity was high, but most ESWT arms showed consistent clinical benefit.ConclusionsESWT significantly reduces pain and improves function in patients with PT, with effect sizes exceeding clinically meaningful thresholds. It represents a viable noninvasive treatment option for individuals not responding to conservative care. Future research should focus on standardizing ESWT protocols and follow-up intervals to enhance reproducibility and clinical translation.KEYWORDS: Extracorporeal shockwave therapy, Meta-analysis, Noninvasive therapy, Patellar tendinopathy, Rehabilitation, Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment, Visual analog scale

Patellar tendinopathy (PT), also known as jumper’s knee, is a chronic overuse injury characterized by localized anterior knee pain and dysfunction, particularly during jumping or running activities. It typically affects athletes in sports involving repetitive knee loading. Pathology is most commonly located at the proximal insertion of the patellar tendon on the inferior pole of the patella.1 Although the condition has a multifactorial etiology, the most widely accepted pathophysiological mechanism is attributed to chronic repetitive overload, leading to failed tendon healing and degenerative collagen disorganization.2

PT is highly prevalent in athletic populations, with reported rates of ≤40% among professional jumping athletes and approximately 5.6% among adolescent athletes.2,3 Prevalence estimates in the general adult population vary considerably by detection method. A registry-based study of general practice in Denmark reported substantially lower incidence and prevalence rates than those drawn from imaging-based or sport-specific cohorts, underscoring that population context and diagnostic criteria significantly influence reported figures.4 Despite its burden on performance and quality of life, a consensus on optimal treatment remains unclear, reflecting the need for evidence-based and standardized management protocols.

Because of the condition’s chronic degenerative nature, PT remains a significant clinical challenge. First-line therapies are typically conservative, including rest, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories, local cryotherapy, stretching, eccentric exercise regimens, and patellar tendon straps.5 However, these interventions often yield limited success, with many patients experiencing persistent symptoms and functional limitations, highlighting the necessity of exploring alternative or adjunctive noninvasive, yet biologically active, treatments.

One such modality gaining increased clinical attention is extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT). Originally developed in the 1980s for urologic lithotripsy to break up renal calculi, ESWT now serves as a promising regenerative treatment for various musculoskeletal disorders.6,7 ESWT involves the transmission of acoustic shockwaves into tissues, delivering high-pressure mechanical pulses generated by electromagnetic, electrohydraulic, or piezoelectric mechanisms. These shockwaves propagate as longitudinal pressure waves, accompanied by velocity shear forces, and result in abrupt changes in tissue pressure, triggering the release of cytokines and biological responses.8,9

From a biophysical perspective, ESWT delivers mechanical energy to tendons and surrounding tissues through mechanotransduction, the process by which cells convert external mechanical stimuli into intracellular biochemical signals that regulate gene expression and cellular behavior, rather than acting solely as a purely mechanical disruptor.10, 11, 12 Preclinical and translational studies suggest that this mechanotransducive stimulus can enhance cellular proliferation, upregulate angiogenic growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor, and stimulate collagen synthesis, although these effects are incompletely characterized in human tendinopathy.10, 11, 12, 13 Localized thermal and cavitation phenomena accompanying shockwave propagation may further induce transient microtrauma, potentially amplifying downstream tissue remodeling responses.10, 11, 12,14

Prior systematic reviews of ESWT for lower-limb tendinopathies, such as Mani-Babu et al,15 Liao et al,17 and Charles et al,18 have pooled anatomic sites and reported mixed or modest findings for PT specifically.16 The condition-specific effectiveness of ESWT for PT, assessed with patellar-validated outcome measures, therefore remains incompletely characterized and warrants a dedicated synthesis.

Given its regenerative potential, minimal invasiveness, and growing use in tendinopathy protocols, ESWT presents as a plausible treatment option for managing refractory PT. However, clinical practice is still limited by variability in dosing parameters, treatment protocols, and outcome measures. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize current evidence regarding the effectiveness of ESWT in improving pain and functional outcomes in patients with PT, with a specific focus on validated tools such as the visual analog scale (VAS) and the Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment (VISA).

Methods

A systematic review and meta-analysis were performed following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines.19

Search procedure

Three databases (PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science) were searched using the following search strings:

PubMed: (“extracorporeal shockwave therapy”[MeSH Terms] OR “shockwave therapy”[Title/Abstract] OR “shock wave therapy”[Title/Abstract] OR “extracorporeal therapy”[Title/Abstract]) AND (“patellar tendinopathy”[Title/Abstract] OR “patellar tendinosis”[Title/Abstract] OR “patellar tendinitis”[Title/Abstract] OR “jumpers knee”[Title/Abstract])

Embase: (‘shock wave therapy’/exp OR ‘shock wave therapy’ OR ‘shockwave therapy’ OR ‘extracorporeal therapy’/exp OR ‘extracorporeal therapy’) AND (‘patellar tendinopathy’/exp OR ‘patellar tendinopathy’ OR ‘patellar tendinitis’/exp OR ‘patellar tendinitis’ OR ‘patellar tendinosis’/exp OR ‘patellar tendinosis’ OR ‘jumpers knee’/exp OR ‘jumpers knee’)

Web of Science: (((TS=(shockwave therapy)) OR TS=(shock wave therapy)) OR TS=(extracorporeal therapy)) AND (((TS=(patellar tendinopathy)) OR TS=(patellar tendinitis)) OR TS=(patellar tendinosis) OR TS=(jumper’s knee))

The searches were conducted on any timeframe and language up to May 27, 2025. In addition, Google Scholar was manually skimmed for relevant gray literature, of which none meeting the inclusion criteria was found. A total of 372 results were found and exported to Rayyan.ai for duplicate detection with automatic deletion set for abstracts with >95% similarity, followed by manual sorting of the remainder. Two hundred thirty-five remaining articles were screened by title and abstract following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines (fig 1) for inclusion and exclusion criteria by 2 authors. After this, 24 papers remained, which were sought for full-text review, of which 4 could not be found. Studies meeting inclusion criteria were subsequently imported into a shared spreadsheet for structured data extraction, with each reviewer recording outcome values, SDs, sample sizes, and study characteristics independently before cross-verification and reconciliation.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram. Abbreviation: PRP, platelet-rich plasma.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Included were randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective cohort studies, retrospective chart reviews, and observational studies of any publication time or language that used focused ESWT (f-ESWT) or radial ESWT (r-ESWT) to treat PT. Studies were included if they measured the pre-ESWT and post-ESWT pain level and function, measured on VAS and the VISA, respectively. Excluded were studies with data issues in which the authors could not be contacted (n=4), which were studies that did not report mean VAS or VISA values with SDs in a reasonable manner for data extraction and statistical analysis. The use of platelet-rich plasma (n=1) as an adjuvant therapy to ESWT was also excluded, as well as commentary design (n=1) and secondary analyses (n=1) on the results of an already included study. Studies with reliable data extraction, reporting the mean VAS and/or VISA values with SD, were exclusively used. Two authors agreed on including 13 studies in the analysis. All 13 studies reported VAS and 9 reported VISA, consisting of 530 and 369 participants, respectively.

Data collection and analysis

VAS, VISA, SD, and sample size were collected from the included studies, among other qualitative data, for quality analysis. When VAS was reported at rest and during exercise, the value during the exercise was used for data extraction. When multiple measurements were recorded throughout the study period, timepoints between 3 and 4 months (or 12-16wk) were used for data extraction. Once data collection was complete, a random-effects meta-analysis was performed on the data using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.20,a Because study designs and controls inherently vary, a random-effect meta-analysis was employed. Statistical significance and measures of effect size were reported as (P<.05, effect size (Cohen's d=mean; 95% CI [lower limit - uppler limit]). Heterogeneity was assessed using the between-study variance (τ²), the ratio of total variation to within-study variation (H²), and the proportion of total variability attributable to true between-study heterogeneity (I²), which served as the primary heterogeneity metric. I² values were interpreted using conventional thresholds: <25% indicating low, 25%-50% indicating moderate, and >50% indicating high heterogeneity.21

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence assessment

After conducting a comprehensive quantitative analysis, a dual approach was employed for the qualitative assessment of evidence quality and risk of bias. The risk of bias in the included studies was systematically evaluated using the RoB2b (Cochrane Risk of Bias in randomized trials) and ROBINS-Ic (Cochrane Risk of Bias in nonrandomized trials) tools by 2 research members.22 The results were visualized using stoplight plots and summary plots generated with the ROBVIS toold (Cochrane visualization tool for risk of bias assessments in a systematic review) presented in figures 2 and 3, enhancing the clarity of bias assessments.23

In parallel, the quality of evidence was appraised using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation method.24 This involved a detailed examination of factors such as study design, consistency of results, directness, precision, and potential publication bias. Results

The primary outcomes of interest were pre-post-ESWT application pain reduction and functional improvement measured by VAS and VISA, respectively. Effect of intervention

A meta-analysis was performed to compare the effects of ESWT on pain and function with control. In figures 4 and 5, group 1 refers to the control arm (placebo, sham, or non-ESWT comparator) and group 2 refers to the ESWT treatment arm. Average follow-up was 12.5±8.1 weeks. Thirteen studies were included in the VAS analysis, resulting in 530 included participants (ESWT n=372; control n=158). Significant differences were found between baseline and follow-up in the control group (P<.01; Cohen's d=−0.48 [−0.76 to −0.2]) and in the ESWT group (P<.01; Cohen's d=−1.54 [−2.06 to −1.03]) (fig 4). Significant differences were also found between groups as shown by the subgroup analysis of homogeneity (P<.01; Q=12.67) (fig 4). Pooled analysis of VAS scores revealed a mean decrease of 1.09 in the control group (4.89 → 3.80), whereas the ESWT group saw a decrease of 2.53 between baseline and follow-up (5.24 → 2.71) (fig 4). Every study in the ESWT group showed significant decreases in pain at follow-up, whereas only 2 of the 7 included control groups displayed significant decreases in pain. Heterogeneity was low in the control group (Tau2=0.44; H2=1.47; I2=32%) and high in the ESWT group (Tau2=0.899; H2=9.35; I2=89%) (fig 4).

Nine studies were included in the VISA analysis, resulting in 369 included participants (ESWT n=246; control n=123) with an average follow-up of 14.53±7.13 weeks. Significant differences were found between baseline and follow-up in the control group (P=.04; Cohen’s d=0.56 [0.02-1.11]) and in the ESWT group (P<.01; Cohen’s d=1.76 [0.90-2.62]). Significant differences were also found between groups as shown by the subgroup analysis of homogeneity (P=.02; Q=5.3) (fig 5). Pooled analysis of VISA scores revealed a mean increase of 8.266 in the control group (54.90 → 63.16), whereas the ESWT group saw an increase of 20.218 between baseline and follow-up (54.65 → 74.86) (fig 5). Ten of 11 studies in the ESWT group showed significant improvements in function at follow-up, whereas only 2 of the 5 included control groups displayed significant improvements in function. Heterogeneity was high in both the control group (Tau²=0.289; H²=4.289; I²=77%) and the ESWT group (Tau²=1.945; H²=16.811; I²=94%) (fig 5).

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence assessment

A thorough examination of the risk of bias and certainty of evidence was performed on the selected studies by 2 members of the research team. The completed plots displaying the RoB2b results for randomized studies showed that the majority demonstrated low risk across key domains (Fig 4, Fig 5). Although a few studies showed high risk or some concerns related to missing outcome data and reporting bias, these biases are unlikely to severely compromise the overall reliability of the estimated effects of ESWT. Based on the ROBINS-I risk of bias assessment for nonrandomized studies included in this review, most studies demonstrated serious or moderate risk of bias, particularly because of confounding, classification of interventions, and outcome measurement (figs 6 and 7). The overall Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation analysis yielded a detailed breakdown: 4 studies with high-quality evidence, 6 with moderate quality evidence, and 3 studies with low-quality evidence (table 1).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis assessed the efficacy of ESWT for PT, focusing on pain and functional outcomes measured by VAS and VISA, respectively. Across 13 included studies, ESWT was associated with statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvements in both pain and function compared with control interventions. In the overall pooled analysis, the ESWT group demonstrated a mean VAS reduction of 2.53 points (from 5.24 to 2.71) and a mean VISA increase of 20.218 points (from 54.65 to 74.86), whereas corresponding changes in control groups were smaller. These findings, together with large, standardized effect sizes for pain (Cohen’s d=−1.54) and function (Cohen’s d=1.76), support a robust treatment effect of ESWT for PT beyond statistical significance alone.

The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for VAS in PT has been estimated at 1.2 points on a 10-point scale, although an MCID of 7.3 points on VISA is cited for symptomatic improvement in PT.25 The magnitude of change observed in our pooled ESWT group exceeded these thresholds for both pain and function, indicating that the benefits of ESWT are likely to be meaningful to patients in everyday activities and sport. In contrast, changes in the control groups, although sometimes statistically significant, were generally closer to or only modestly above MCID values. This divergence reinforces the clinical relevance of ESWT and suggests that improvements exceed those expected from usual care, placebo, or non-ESWT conservative interventions alone.

Heterogeneity in the ESWT arms was high for both VAS (I2=89%) and VISA (I2=94%), reflecting substantial methodological variability across studies. Potential sources of heterogeneity include differences in ESWT modality (focused vs radial), energy flux density and number of impulses, number and spacing of treatment sessions, use of adjunct eccentric loading or other exercise programs, and study design (randomized vs nonrandomized). Despite this variability, nearly all ESWT arms demonstrated improvements in VAS and VISA that exceeded MCID thresholds, whereas improvements in control arms were less consistent. Moreover, the randomized trials within this evidence base generally showed similar or greater improvements than nonrandomized studies, supporting the interpretation that our conclusions are not solely driven by lower-quality observational data.

Our findings extended and refined conclusions from previous systematic reviews of ESWT in lower-limb tendinopathy. Earlier reviews by Mani-Babu et al15 and Korakakis et al16 suggested that ESWT is a promising option for lower-limb tendinopathies overall but included relatively few patellar-specific trials and did not provide a dedicated meta-analysis for PT alone. Liao et al17 likewise reported positive pooled effects of ESWT in lower-limb tendinopathy, with subgroup analyses hinting that dosage and waveform may influence outcomes. More recently, Charles et al18 examined ESWT for PT, Achilles tendinopathy, and plantar fasciitis collectively and concluded that there is low-to-moderate quality evidence of negligible benefit of ESWT for PT, in contrast to the large effects observed for plantar fasciitis. Several methodological differences may explain the discrepancy between their conclusions for PT and ours. First, we restricted our analysis to PT and synthesized only patellar-specific outcomes, avoiding dilution of effects across multiple tendinopathy sites. Second, we focused on pre- to posttreatment change scores on validated patellar measures (VAS and VISA) and standardized follow-up to 12-16 weeks where possible, which may better capture medium term response to ESWT. Third, our search extended beyond the time frame of prior reviews and incorporated more recent randomized and nonrandomized studies, increasing the number of patellar-specific data points. Taken together, our results suggest that when examined within a PT-specific framework, ESWT confers clinically important benefits in pain and function.

Focused versus radial extracorporeal shockwave therapy

A direct meta-analysis comparing focused and radial ESWT was not feasible within this review, as only 1 included study (van der Worp et al26) directly randomized participants to both waveforms within the same trial. The remaining studies employed either focused or radial ESWT exclusively, precluding between-waveform pooling. Qualitatively, both modalities produced clinically meaningful improvements: in the van der Worp et al26 trial, no significant difference in VAS or VISA outcomes was observed between focused and radial ESWT when each was combined with eccentric training, suggesting comparable effectiveness in that context. Randomized and prospective studies employing focused ESWT, including Thijs et al,27 similarly reported improvements exceeding MCID thresholds, whereas observational cohorts using radial ESWT documented substantial reductions in pain and functional gains. A single-application low-energy radial ESWT protocol studied by Furia et al28 further demonstrated significant symptomatic improvement relative to nonoperative treatment, although its retrospective design and unique single-session approach limit direct comparability with the multisession protocols comprising most included studies. Across the available evidence, no consistent pattern of superiority emerged for either waveform. Until high-quality head-to-head RCTs with adequate statistical power are available, modality selection may reasonably be guided by device availability, clinician experience, and patient preference.

Beyond waveform, variation in energy flux density, number of impulses, number of treatment sessions, and the integration of ESWT with structured eccentric loading programs likely influences clinical outcomes. The meta-analysis by Liao et al17 suggests that higher-dose focused and radial ESWT protocols may yield larger effects than lower-dose regimens across lower-limb tendinopathies, a pattern that is compatible with the generally larger improvements seen in higher-energy PT protocols included in our review. However, dose-response relationships remain insufficiently defined in patellar-specific trials to support firm recommendations regarding optimal energy or session number. Standardization of treatment parameters in future RCTs will be critical for determining the minimal effective dose and clarifying whether combining ESWT with eccentric exercise confers additive benefits beyond either modality alone.

Clinically, the magnitude of pain relief and functional improvement observed in this review suggests that ESWT is a valuable noninvasive option for patients with PT who have not responded adequately to first-line conservative measures. The changes in VAS and VISA observed with ESWT are likely to translate into improved tolerance for jumping and running, reduced symptom burden during sport, and enhanced capacity to meet training demands. Given its favorable safety profile and the durability of response reported in several included studies at intermediate follow-up, ESWT may help reduce the need for more invasive interventions such as injections or surgery in selected patients. At the same time, the heterogeneity of study designs, devices, and protocols, and the presence of residual risk of bias in some nonrandomized studies, underline the importance of individualized decision-making and shared discussion of expected benefits and uncertainties with patients.

Study limitations

Most RCTs included in this review were judged to have a low risk of bias according to the RoB 2 tool.b These trials used appropriate randomization methods, validated outcome measures, and maintained acceptable follow-up. A few RCTs presented some concerns because of missing outcome data or lack of blinding of outcome assessors, but these limitations did not appear to systematically favor ESWT over control. Nonrandomized studies assessed using ROBINS-Ic exhibited moderate to serious risk of bias, primarily because of potential confounding, absence of concurrent comparators, and nonblinded assessments. These observational studies contributed a smaller proportion of the overall evidence and were interpreted as supportive but less definitive than the randomized data. Accordingly, our primary conclusions regarding the effectiveness of ESWT for PT are based largely on randomized evidence, with nonrandomized findings reinforcing but not determining the direction of effect.

The most consequential limitation of this meta-analysis is the high between-study heterogeneity observed in both the VAS (I2=89%) and VISA (I2=94%) pooled estimates. This level of statistical heterogeneity substantially limits the precision and generalizability of the pooled effect sizes and warrants cautious interpretation. The likely sources include variation in ESWT modality (focused vs radial), energy flux density, total impulses delivered, number of treatment sessions, use of concurrent eccentric training, and differences in participant chronicity and baseline severity. Although nearly all ESWT arms demonstrated improvements exceeding MCID thresholds, suggesting a consistent direction of benefit, the magnitude of effect likely varies according to protocol parameters that the available data do not allow us to model formally. Future analyses using individual patient data or standardized protocols will be necessary to quantify these sources of variability and define the conditions under which ESWT yields the largest benefit.

Future directions

Future research should prioritize the development of standardized ESWT protocols, including optimal settings for energy flux density, treatment frequency, and number of sessions. A key gap in the literature is the lack of standardized follow-up intervals. Future studies should prioritize uniform time points for both short term and long-term follow-up to more accurately observe treatment response.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that ESWT produces significant and clinically meaningful improvements in both pain and function among individuals with PT. The observed benefits exceed established MCIDs and are supported by large effect sizes. Despite heterogeneity across study designs and protocols, the consistent direction of benefit across nearly all ESWT arms reinforces its therapeutic potential. Given its noninvasive nature and favorable risk profile, ESWT represents a viable treatment option for patients who do not respond to conservative management. Future research should aim to standardize treatment parameters and follow-up durations to enhance reproducibility and guide clinical practice.
