Exploring the use of COVID-19 antigen rapid diagnostic tests among displaced populations in Iraq: findings from a pilot project in four IDP camps
Authors: Lara Abou Ammar, Caitlin M Wolfe, Lamiaa Nagib, Mohammed Slebei, Sezan Shawkat, Dilman Amo, Raveen Abdullah, Rawshan Abdulmalik, Hiwa Muhammed Amin, Huda Shafiq, Shivan Hafthalah, Aso Qahraman, Jirjees Mohammed, Hassan Ghawji, Srinath Satyanarayana, Nevin Wilson, Sanjay Sarin, Kekeletso Kao, Nellie Ghusayni
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIOM piloted the use of Ag RDTs for COVID-19 in Iraq, in collaboration with FIND, the Global Alliance for Diagnostics, to facilitate access to testing and understand barriers and opportunities for testing in a displacement context. The purpose was to (i) evaluate the performance metrics of the Sure Status COVID-19 Antigen Card Test in this population; (ii) compare Ag RDT results across high- and low-probability cases, vaccination status, and symptom severity; and (iii) report participant perspectives on Ag RDT use for COVID-19 and other diseases.MethodsSecondary analysis was conducted using de-identified cross-sectional data collected from November 2022-March 2023 in four IDP camps in the Kurdistan region of Iraq. Data was collected during the recording, reporting, and monitoring processes of the pilot project using Ag RDTs for COVID-19 among displaced populations. Descriptive statistics and bivariate analyses were conducted in Stata (version 17).Results9,346 Sure Status Ag RDTs were performed from November 2022-March 2023. Observed performance metrics were as follows: sensitivity = 72.73% (95% CI: 69.56–75.80%); specificity = 99.74% (95% CI: 99.39–100%), PPV = 92.31% (95% CI: 92.31–94.15%), and NPV = 98.85% (95% CI: 98.11–99.58%). Ag RDT uptake was higher among women and symptomatic individuals, and preference for Ag RDTs was especially strong among illiterate respondents. The overall positivity of Ag RDTs and PCR results in this population remained lower than national averages. Concerningly, 23.39% of respondents declined an Ag RDT because they did not believe in COVID-19.ConclusionsThis pilot program was among the first to explore Ag RDT use in Iraqi Kurdistan. Over 99% of respondents reported satisfaction with their experience, and over 90% wanted to see Ag RDTs available for other diseases. Findings can inform implementation of RDTs and screening protocols for other infectious diseases, and patient perspectives on Ag RDTs for testing and screening of COVID-19 among displaced populations can inform health programming within Iraq and globally.Keywords: Antigen rapid diagnostic test, COVID-19, Outbreak response, Displaced populations, Operational research

Background

Iraq, along with much of the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, faced a high morbidity and mortality burden due to COVID-19 [1], with more than 2.4 million cases and over 25,000 deaths [2]. Some of this burden can be attributed to a high burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and a diverse range of vulnerable and at-risk populations due to poverty, inequality, and humanitarian crises. COVID-19 has caused a significant burden on MENA health systems through direct and indirect effects on care-seeking for other essential health services [3]. Due to low testing in many of the MENA countries, including Iraq, it is likely that the morbidity and mortality are higher than reported. The number of PCR tests conducted for COVID-19 in Iraq has steadily declined since early 2022 [4]. In addition, delays in detection and diagnosis hindered the national and international efforts for containing the pandemic [5].

Displaced populations pose one of the most significant vulnerable groups, particularly in terms of service delivery [6, 7]. In addition, displaced population face several challenges to access testing services for COVID-19, including poor awareness about symptoms and limited geographical access [7]. Other barriers identified in previous research in developing countries include stigma [7, 8], delays in reporting results [8], and emotional barriers to testing such as fear of pain and fear of infection during testing [9]. As recommended by the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) in early 2020, when community transmission is established, testing for healthcare workers and symptomatic individuals needs to be prioritized [10]. Facilitating access to testing therefore represents an important aspect for health promotion among disadvantaged populations.

Barriers to testing among displaced populations include financial burden of the test and fear of stigma from the surrounding community [1, 7]. Furthermore, among displaced Syrians, the pandemic was largely viewed as “propaganda” [11], and many held conspiracy beliefs [7], thereby negating the perceived usefulness or importance of PCR testing for COVID-19. Nevertheless, barriers to testing are still not fully understood in refugee or displaced contexts [13]. Antigen rapid diagnostic tests (Ag RDTs) can both fill gaps in access to testing and reduce pressure on laboratories performing nucleic acid amplification tests, thereby reducing delays in diagnosis and optimizing use of available tools. There is currently little documented programmatic experience of using Ag RDTs for SARS-CoV-2 detection in the context of displaced population settings [1, 6].

In response to the COVID-19 pandemic, primary health care centers (PHCCs) within camps for internally displaced people (IDP) established a symptom-based triage system to screen patients for possible COVID-19 to minimize potential transmission within the health facility [12]. The screeners at each facility used a questionnaire to ask patients presenting to the PHCC about their symptoms and known exposures to COVID-19. The questionnaire used in the COVID-19 symptom screening process, built on KoBo Toolbox (Harvard Humanitarian Initiative, Cambridge, MA, USA) [13] and updated as common symptoms changed with new variants, included instructions on where to direct the patients: those with symptoms consistent with possible COVID-19 or those with known COVID-19 exposures were immediately directed to the isolation area.

The International Organization of Migration (IOM) launched a pilot project assessing the use of Ag RDTs for COVID-19 in Iraq, in collaboration with the Foundation for Innovative New Diagnostics (FIND) [14], the Global Alliance for Diagnostics, to facilitate access to testing and to understand the barriers and opportunities for testing in a displacement context. The project involved the implementation of testing and screening for COVID-19 using 9,346 Ag-RDTs (Sure Status COVID-19 Antigen Card Test [15, 16] in two Iraqi governorates in the Kurdistan region (Dohuk and Erbil) in camps for IDPs.

Here, operational research was performed through secondary analysis of data collected during this project. The purpose of this secondary data analysis was to (i) evaluate the performance metrics (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value) of the Sure Status COVID-19 Antigen Card Test among internally displaced populations compared to published product specifications; (ii) compare Ag RDT results across high- and low-probability cases, vaccination status, and severity of symptoms; and (iii) document and report participant perspectives on the use of Ag RDTs for COVID-19 and other diseases.

Methods

Study design

Secondary data analysis was conducted using de-identified cross-sectional data collected from November 2022 through March 2023 at four IDP camps in the Kurdistan region of Iraq. Quantitative and qualitative data was collected during the recording, reporting, and monitoring processes of the joint IOM and FIND activities to pilot the use of Ag RDTs for COVID-19 among displaced populations.

Study setting

The data used in this secondary analysis were collected from three IDP camps in Dohuk governorate and one IDP camp in Erbil governorate. Dohuk and Erbil governorates are in the Kurdistan region of Iraq, where the remaining IDP camps following the defeat of the Islamic State (Da’esh) insurgency [17, 18] are located. The population size of the IDP camps included in this study ranged from 3,183 (Darkar) to 14,291 (Khanke). There is one PHCC in each camp, with each PHCC routinely seeing 200 or more patients per day. Additional descriptions of the study locations are provided in Table 1. The Sure Status COVID-19 Antigen Card Test® (professional version) [16], designated by the World Health Organization (WHO) for emergency use, was used in this pilot. This Ag RDT was manufactured by Premier Medical Corporation (PMC) India, with a manufacturer-reported sensitivity of 94.16% and specificity of 100% [16].

PHCC staff involved with project implementation received training on the eligibility criteria, data collection tools, sample collection, and the use and interpretation of the Sure Status Ag RDT. Supervisory visits were conducted at the beginning and as needed through the duration of the project.

Participant selection and eligibility criteria

The data used in this secondary analysis was collected from participants 18 years of age or older presenting to IOM-supported PHCCs in one of four IDP camps in Kurdistan region of Iraq (Khanke, Darkar, and Sheikhan in Dohuk, and Debaga in Erbil) or healthcare workers at the participating PHCCs who consented to participate in the Ag RDT for COVID-19 program piloted at IOM-supported PHCCs from November 2022 through March 2023.

Patient eligibility and interpretation of test results are summarized in Fig. 1, which demonstrates the context-specific algorithm used in this pilot consistent with WHO recommendations for low-prevalence settings. Patients or individuals who met the eligibility criteria were enrolled for Ag-RDT testing and tests were only conducted after consent was received. Patients who tested positive for COVID-19 by Ag RDT received clinical care at the PHCC as required.

Data collection

Data collection occurred from November 2022-March 2023 across all four locations. Screeners or laboratory technicians employed at the PHCCs (i) introduced the study to eligible patients, (ii) provided informed consent documents, (iii) reviewed the contents of the informed consent process with those who were illiterate, (iv) documented informed consent in the facility logbook. Laboratory technicians collected samples from those who consented to participate.

Quantitative data: the PHCC logbook for administered ag RDTs

Data collected by screeners were entered in paper logbooks present at the PHCC and included patient age (in years), gender, number of COVID-19 vaccine doses received (zero, one, two, or two or more), and reason for testing (including having symptoms, history of contact, and being a healthcare worker). This information was used to create a new variable (case type) classifying high probable and low probable cases. Those reporting symptoms or history of contact with COVID19 case or being a healthcare worker were classified as high probable cases, while the remaining participants were classified as low probable cases. If symptoms were reported, symptom duration (in days), and symptom severity (mild or severe) were collected. Severe symptoms were defined as difficulty in breathing, shortness of breath, chest pain, lack of consciousness, inability to speak or move, or any blue coloring. Consent documentation was also collected. If consent was granted, patient name, address, and phone number were registered in addition to Ag RDT test date, type of facility where the test was administered (health clinic or mobile health clinic), Ag RDT test result, and presence of comorbidities. Where available, confirmatory PCR testing was also performed, though systematic PCR testing was not conducted due to limited availability. In these patients, informed consent was collected for both Ag RDT testing and PCR testing, and the laboratory technicians at the PHCCs collected samples for both tests. The samples collected for PCR were sent to the nearest laboratory for testing while Ag RDTs were conducted at the PHCC. Test results from the laboratory were shared with the PHCC and linked with patient logbook records. At the end of each day, data collected on the paper PHCC logbooks were entered into the data collection tool built with KoBo Toolbox® [13].

Qualitative data: the semi-structured questionnaire on Ag RDT patient experience

Convenience sampling was used, and the semi-structured questionnaire was administered by the screeners to patients presenting to the PHCC for evaluation (both those who opted for using Ag RDTs and those who opted against using Ag RDTs). The screeners administering the semi-structured questionnaire entered participant responses directly into the data collection tool built using KoBo Toolbox® [13] for those who verbally consented to participate. The semi-structured questionnaire was administered during the last month of project implementation (March 2023).

Data management

The quantitative and qualitative data was reviewed and cleaned weekly by IOM MHD’s Information Management Team. Additionally, the data collected from both the quantitative and qualitative tools was fully de-identified by IOM’s information management team before the secondary analysis was conducted. Logbooks and consent forms were stored in locked offices of the PHCCs and IOM’s password-protected KOBO server housed all digital data, in accordance with IOM’s data protection principles.

Statistical analyses

For the data on Ag RDT testing, participant demographics were reported through frequencies and percent. Demographic variables included age, sex, reason for testing, duration of symptoms, severity of symptoms, vaccination status, presence of other comorbidities, and governorate. We further studied the profile of consenting participants in comparison to those who did not consent to participate across the different demographic variables; odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were reported. Performance metrics for the Sure Status COVID-19 Antigen Card Test used in this specific population were calculated using the Ag RDT result and the confirmatory PCR test result. The performance metrics included sensitivity (# positive by Ag RDT / total # positive by PCR), specificity (# negative by Ag RDT / total # negative by PCR), positive predictive value (# positive by both Ag RDT and PCR / total # positive by Ag RDT), and negative predictive value (# negative by both Ag RDT and PCR / total # negative by Ag RDT, calculated using Stata’s diagtest command. Bivariate analyses were used to identify any differences in RDT outcome by facility, gender, vaccination status, presence of co-morbidities, case type (high or low probable case), reason for testing, duration of symptoms, and severity of symptoms. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals calculated using logistic regression were reported.

For the patient perceptions and experiences survey, participant demographics were reported through frequencies and percent, and included age, sex, education level, and governorate. Descriptive statistics for perspectives on Ag RDT use were reported as frequencies and percent and included decision to take Ag RDT, reasons for taking Ag RDT, reasons for not taking Ag RDT, satisfaction with Ag RDT experience, preference for Ag RDT or COVID-19 symptomatic screening process, and preference to see RDTs used for other diseases. A binary variable to reflect preference for Ag RDT vs. symptomatic screening was created (coded as 0 indicating preference for symptomatic screening and 1 indicating preference of Ag RDT) and bivariate logistic regression was conducted to examine the difference between the two preference groups (Ag RDT vs. symptomatic screening) across multiple variables including age, gender, education level and testing site. Common reasons behind preference of Ag RDTs vs. symptomatic screening for COVID-19 were summarized by main ideas.

All statistical analyses were performed in Stata (version 17) [19].

Results

Over the course of the pilot program, 18,883 eligible individuals were approached about participation and offered an Ag RDT. Eligible individuals included those presenting to the PHCC for the first time during the study period and those presenting for additional visits, as the risk of having COVID-19 remained the same. Most of these potential participants were in Dohuk (71.70%, n = 13,539) and female (73.60%, n = 13,898). Of these individuals, 49.50% (n = 9,346) consented to participate.

A total of 9,346 Sure Status Ag RDTs were performed from November 2022 through March 2023. The majority of Ag RDT testing (85.11%, n = 7,955) was conducted in Dohuk governorate, and the largest percentage of Ag RDT testing activities occurred in Khanke PHCC (Table 2). More females (76.12%, n = 7,114) than males consented to participate in Ag RDT testing, and over 75% of participants (n = 7,069) received two or more doses of vaccinations against COVID-19. Only 8.53% of participants (n = 797) reported co-morbidities at the time of their participation (Table 2).

Participants in Darkar (Dohuk governorate), were more likely to test positive by Ag RDT (odds ratio [OR] = 6.88, 95% CI: 1.94–24.41) and by PCR (OR = 9.32, 95% CI: 2.39–36.35) compared to Sheikhan camp, as shown in Table 6. Conversely, participants in Debaga (Erbil governorate) were less likely to test positive by PCR (OR = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.03–0.73). Female participants were more likely to test positive by Ag RDT than males (OR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.19–5.05). Participants who received more than 2 doses of vaccination against COVID-19 were less likely to test positive by PCR than those who received less or no doses (OR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.07–0.69). High probable cases were more likely to test positive by both Ag RDT (OR = 32.53, 95% CI: 4.43-285.85) and PCR (OR = 11.91, 95% CI: 1.62–87.75) compared to low probable cases. Those with COVID-19 symptoms at the time of testing and participants who were healthcare workers were more likely to test positive by both Ag RDT and PCR compared to those who were not, as were those who experienced severe symptoms.

Among the 1,939 respondents who completed the patient perspectives questionnaire, 44.61% (n = 865) also underwent the previous symptom-based screening process for COVID-19 employed at the PHCC. Of these 865 respondents, 87.17% (n = 754) preferred the Ag RDT to the screening process (Table 8). Among all respondents, 94.33% (n = 1,829) indicated they would like to see Ag RDTs available for other diseases. Compared to Sheikhan camp, Respondents in Khanke were more likely to prefer the Ag RDT (OR = 8.21, 95% CI: 3.92–17.19) while respondents in Debaga were more likely to prefer the screening process (OR = 0.26, 95% CI 0.08–0.78), as shown in Table 9. Males were less likely than females to prefer the Ag RDT (OR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.31–0.76). Respondents who were not literate preferred the Ag RDT to the screening process (OR = 6.58, 95% CI: 2.87–15.08).

Discussion

This pilot program was one of the first to explore the use of COVID-19 Ag RDTs in the Kurdistan region of Iraq. At the time of implementation, Ag RDTs had not yet been approved for use in the Kurdistan region. The project also experienced delays in receiving the shipment of Ag RDTs to deploy for use in this project, however once the project was formally started, implementation progressed smoothly. All four PHCC locations across Dohuk and Erbil governorates successfully implemented and ran the Ag RDT for COVID-19 testing pilot program.

Of note, the uptake of these Ag RDTs was higher among women and among symptomatic individuals, and the preference for Ag RDTs was especially strong among illiterate populations. This may be due to the perceptions that Ag RDTs were easier and faster than PCR tests and were more accessible, as they were administered within the camp PHCC for free as part of the pilot program. In this study, individuals over the age of 18 who were younger, male, and fully vaccinated were significantly less likely to consent to participate, similar to COVID-19 Ag RDT uptake reported among displaced populations in north-west Syria [1]. Community engagement efforts are needed to encourage younger, male, and fully vaccinated individuals on the importance of COVID-19 testing given the risk of breakthrough infections as new variants continue to emerge [20]. Additionally, individuals classified as high probable cases (those reporting symptoms or history of contact and more severe symptoms) were more likely to participate in this study, also in agreement with findings from the study conducted in north-west Syria [1].

The overall positivity rate of Ag RDTs and PCR results remained very low in this pilot program (0.30% and 0.41%, respectively), lower than the national average of 1.31% reported in Iraq over the course of the pilot program [4]. However, the national averages in Iraq could have been impacted by the lack of available PCR tests. In terms of the performance metrics for this Ag RDT, the observed sensitivity in this pilot project (72.73%) was lower than the manufacturer-reported value (94.16%), while the observed specificity (99.74%) was close to the manufacturer reported value (100%) [16]. Quality of the sample collection or difficulties processing the collected samples and using the reagents in the test kits could have contributed to this discrepancy [1, 21–25].

Participants in this study who received more than 2 doses of vaccination against COVID-19 were less likely to test positive by PCR than those who received less or no doses. The majority of participants in this study received at least two doses of vaccination against COVID-19, though the estimated percent of the whole Iraq population fully vaccinated was below 30% in January 2023 [26] when health authorities stopped reporting COVID-19 data [4]. Further, those with COVID-19 symptoms at the time of testing and participants who were healthcare workers were more likely to test positive by both Ag RDT and PCR compared to those who were not. Participants who experienced severe symptoms were also more likely to test positive by PCR compared to those with mild or no symptoms. These findings are supported by a recent systematic review that highlighted the relationship between viral load, the presence of symptoms, and symptom severity [27].

Among the respondents to patient perspective questionnaire who declined the Ag-RDT, over 60% were not symptomatic. Given that the Ag RDTs are most accurate within 7 days of developing symptoms [28, 29], detecting asymptomatic transmission remains a challenge. Concerningly, 23.39% of respondents opted not to take an Ag RDT because they did not believe in COVID-19.

Benefits of Ag RDT use

As was demonstrated repeatedly during the COVID-19 outbreak, early detection of infectious diseases in a community allows for earlier treatment, isolation, and/or quarantine of infected individuals, thereby interrupting the chain of transmission and decreasing disease incidence in the community. This was very challenging when PCR testing was the only diagnostic option, as there were notable delays in the time from sample collection to test results (10–14 days in some locations), and the availability of PCR has declined notably.

Initial participation in this study allowed participants to determine if they were likely infected with SARS-CoV-2 more quickly than waiting for traditional PCR results, as the availability of PCR testing has greatly decreased in Iraq. More than 99% of respondents in this study reported satisfaction with their experiencing using the Ag RDT, and more than 90% reported wanting to see Ag RDTs available for other diseases. The findings from this evaluation may help IOM and other health partner organizations and agencies to implement rapid diagnostic testing and screening protocols for other infectious diseases. Additionally, patient perspectives on the use of Ag RDTs for testing and screening of COVID-19 among displaced populations can inform health programming within Iraq and globally.

Limitations

This project was implemented in areas with low prevalence of COVID-19. Notably, there were repeated delays in receiving the shipment of Ag RDTs into Iraq which prevented the implementation of this project before November 2022, when cases had already notably declined from the July peak [4]. It is possible that increased interest in Ag RDTs may have been found earlier in the course of the pandemic. However, the questionnaire on patient perspectives was administered to both individuals who consented to Ag RDT use and those who opted not to take the Ag RDT, providing insight on current reasons for accepting or declining the Ag RDT. Further, though 15 healthcare workers consented to participate in weekly screening using the Ag RDTs, the subsample of healthcare workers was too small for meaningful analysis. Additionally, information on prior known infection among patients was not collected. Finally, the cross-sectional questionnaire on patient perspectives was not able to capture how opinions on COVID-19 have evolved over time.

Conclusions

Ag RDTs are easy to perform, can yield a result in 15–20 min, and are less costly than nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT) like PCR that are relatively more accurate and are considered as gold standard for the detection of SARS-CoV-2 infection [30]. However, performing high volumes of NAATs is technically challenging, labor intensive, reliant on efficient specimen transport and reporting systems, and expensive [31], all of which contribute to inequitable access to testing. Hence, SARS-CoV-2 Ag-RDTs are increasingly being integrated in testing strategies around the world.

A combination of different test types is needed to expand testing capacity to meet demand for COVID-19 in all settings. SARS-CoV-2 Ag-RDTs can be incorporated into testing strategies where nucleic acid amplification test methods (such as rt-PCR) are not available, or turnaround time is too long to inform clinical decision-making or for undertaking public health measures such as contact tracing. In Iraq, the lag time between sample collection and PCR results exceeded two weeks, largely hindering their purpose [5]. Prolonged turnaround times may be secondary to multiple factors, including limited human or financial resources, sample transport requirements, and reagent shortages. Employing the use of point-of-care testing, such as Ag RDTs, offers a remedy to these challenges.

During outbreaks of infectious diseases, such as COVID-19, it is imperative to quickly detect and isolate suspect cases, investigate confirmed cases, and trace and monitor the contacts of confirmed cases. This need is even greater in densely populated areas, such as IDP camps, where respiratory viruses have been known to rapidly spread [32, 33]. The accuracy of Ag-RDTs depends on testing time from onset of infection, the concentration of virus in the specimen, the quality and processing of the specimen collected from a person, and the precise formulation of the reagents in the test kits. A 2021 systematic review found that Ag-RDTs detect most SARS-CoV-2 infected persons within the first week of symptom onset and those with high viral load [29]. Due to this, Ag RDTs can increase the accuracy of diagnostics in the early phase of disease, making them a valuable tool to fight the spread of SARS-CoV-2 and other infectious diseases [29].
