Associations between inflammation and depressive symptoms in diabetes: A scoping review
Authors: Nathaniel J. Smail, Jefferson C. Frisbee, Stephanie J. Frisbee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Depression is a clinically important comorbidity in people living with diabetes, yet biological pathways linking these conditions remain unclear. Chronic inflammation has been proposed as one mechanism connecting metabolic dysfunction with depressive symptoms. This scoping review examined knowledge on inflammation and depression/depressive symptoms in adults with diabetes, with attention to differences by diabetes type and sex. Thirty-seven studies met inclusion criteria, predominantly observational and focused on type 2 diabetes. Inflammatory markers were more frequently associated with depressive symptoms in type 2 diabetes than in type 1. CRP/hs-CRP was the most commonly studied biomarker and was often positively associated with depressive symptoms, while composite immune-metabolic indices reflected depressive symptom burden across studies. In contrast, findings for individual cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α, were heterogenous and appeared dependent on adiposity, metabolic health, or vascular comorbidity. Evidence in type 1 diabetes was less uniform, with studies suggesting involvement of vascular, lipid-associated, or non-classical inflammatory pathways. Sex appeared to modify inflammation and depression associations, although patterns varied across biomarkers and study populations. Overall, the literature suggests that inflammation is relevant to depression in diabetes, particularly in type 2, but that this relationship is heterogeneous and shaped by diabetes type, sex, adiposity, and vascular burden.

Introduction

Depression is a common mental health comorbidity in people living with diabetes; both type 1 (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) are associated with elevated risk of depressive symptoms and major depressive disorder.1–5 This burden is significant as comorbid depression is linked to poor self-care and treatment adherence, worse glycemic control, higher vascular complication rates, increased healthcare utilization, reduced quality of life, and elevated mortality. 6 Despite these important concerns, biological pathways connecting diabetes to depression remain unclear.

Chronic inflammation has been proposed as a mechanistic link between diabetic complications and depression. Meta-analyses and large cohort studies report elevated inflammatory biomarkers, including C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), in individuals with major depressive disorder and in those subsequently developing depression. 7 Additionally, T1D and T2D are recognized as chronic inflammatory conditions characterized by immune-metabolic dysregulation and elevated oxidative and nitrosative stress. 8 These findings support hypothesized connections between immuno-metabolic dysfunction and depression, where chronic inflammation and metabolic disruption contribute to depression onset and maintenance. 9 Emerging evidence also suggests that elevated inflammatory markers are associated with reduced response to antidepressant therapy, suggesting a potential role for inflammation in treatment-resistant depression. 10

People with diabetes are particularly vulnerable to immuno-metabolic mechanisms because both T1D and T2D promote inflammatory signaling and endothelial dysfunction, which may affect cerebral perfusion, stress responsivity and neurotransmitter systems relevant to mood regulation. 8 However, diabetes encompasses distinct pathophysiological states that can differ in immune involvement, comorbidity patterns, and vascular disease risk which can influence depression risk. 11 Further, women have a consistently higher risk of major depression in the general population, with evidence suggesting that diabetes may be a stronger risk factor for major depressive disorder than in men.12,13 However, which inflammatory biomarkers are better associated with depression in diabetes, and whether these associations differ by diabetes type and sex remains unknown.

The primary goal of this PRISMA-guided scoping review was to summarize existing knowledge on associations between inflammation and depression/depressive symptoms in individuals with diabetes, including the impact of biomarker class, diabetes type, and sex.

Materials and methods

This scoping review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews and was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). 14

Information sources and search strategy

PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase were searched using Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms and keywords for diabetes, inflammation, and depression/depressive symptoms with database-specific filters for humans and publication type. Search strategies and filters are outlined in Supplementary Table S1. The final search was completed on February 11th, 2025.

Eligibility criteria

The search identified primary human studies examining relationships between inflammatory markers and depressive symptoms in populations with diabetes. Eligible articles were published within 20 years of the final search, included participants with diabetes, measured both inflammatory markers and depression/depressive symptoms, and reported data on their relationship. Eligible study designs included observational and non-observational studies reporting relevant primary data. Reviews, meta-analyses, commentaries, editorials, case reports/series, animal, in vitro, pediatric, non-English, and abstract/conference-only studies were excluded.

Study selection process

Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of all records identified in the search to determine eligibility for inclusion. Subsequently, the full text of potentially relevant articles was assessed to confirm eligibility and extract relevant data.

Data items and data collective process

Data extracted included study design, country, sample size, participant age/sex/diabetes type, inflammatory markers and assay medium, depression assessment method, and analytic measures. 15

Synthesis of results

Extracted data were systematically tabulated and synthesized. To facilitate synthesis across heterogeneous literature, inflammatory markers were grouped into biologically and clinically relevant categories: cytokines and immune mediators; endothelial/adhesion and coagulation markers; adipokines and non-classic inflammatory proteins; cell count-based and composite inflammatory indices; and oxidative stress or omics-based markers. These categories were intended to reflect the principal physiological pathways represented by the measured biomarkers and to support comparison across studies.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 2220 records were retrieved from database searches, as outlined in the PRISMA flow diagram (Supplementary Figure S1). Following title and abstract screening, 85 full-text articles were assessed; 37 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final review. Most studies included adults with T2D (23 studies), with fewer mixed T1D/T2D (9 studies) or T1D-only cohorts (5 studies). Studies spanned Europe, Asia, and North America, mostly enrolling middle-aged or older adults. All Supplemental Data and Files for this study can be accessed at: 10.6084/m9. figshare.32351487.

Most included studies were observational; 30 of 37 were cross-sectional or case-control studies in outpatient, inpatient, or community-based diabetes populations (Supplementary Table S2; Figure 1(a)). Four prospective cohort studies assessed whether baseline inflammation predicted later depressive symptoms or depression changes over 1-7.5 years. Synthesis of results

Cytokines and immune mediators

As summarized in Supplementary Table S3, CRP/hs-CRP was the most frequently examined biomarker, with several studies reporting positive associations with depressive symptoms in adults with T2D. Ten of fourteen T2D studies examining CRP reported higher CRP or hs-CRP levels among participants with greater depressive symptom severity or comorbid major depressive disorder.16,17,20,28,30,35,38,40,50,51 Similar associations were observed in population-based and older adult cohorts, particularly among participants with both T2D and elevated depressive symptoms,17,38,40 and were more apparent in subgroups with obesity or poorer metabolic health.17,27,52 In contrast, significant positive associations were less prevalent for IL-6 (2/7 studies)21,38 and TNF-α (2/6 studies)21,25 with some associations attenuated after adjustment for adiposity or metabolic covariates. 30 IL-1–related markers were positively associated with depressive symptoms in three of four studies,28,30,33 whereas MCP-1/CCL2 was associated in one of two studies, 30 particularly in newly diagnosed T2D and cohorts with comorbid depressive symptoms.

In recent-onset T1D, higher IL-1RA and hs-CRP were associated with poorer well-being or greater depressive symptoms, whereas IL-6, IL-18, CCL2, and adiponectin had weaker or null adjusted associations. 28 In long-duration T1D, hs-CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α were not associated with depression or quality of life. 31

Endothelial, adhesion, and coagulation markers

As presented in Supplementary Table S4, five cross-sectional studies examined endothelial activation,18,19,22,48,49 adhesion molecules, and pro-thrombotic factors as potential links between inflammation, depression, and vascular risk in T2D, two of which focused on elderly adults.48,49 Overall, four reported higher inflammatory, endothelial, or platelet-related markers among participants with depressive symptoms.19,22,48,49 One study found higher thrombomodulin, plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, and fibrinogen, all of which correlated with depression severity. 49 Another reported elevated hs-CRP, soluble ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and E-selectin, with higher sICAM-1 independently associated with depressive syndrome. 48 Two studies reported greater platelet activation or thrombo-inflammatory activity in T2D patients with comorbid depression,19,22 whereas one found no significant association between hs-CRP and depression-related platelet markers. 18

For T1D, evidence was limited to one mixed diabetes study of recent-onset T1D and T2D. 47 In that study, sICAM-1 was weakly positively associated with depressive symptoms in T1D, whereas other inflammatory markers were not robustly associated after correction for multiple testing. 47

Adipokines and non-classic inflammatory proteins

Adiponectin and other non-classical inflammatory proteins were examined mainly in elderly T2D and adult T1D cohorts (Supplementary Table S5). In T2D, one of two studies reported a significant association with depression.30,50 Among very elderly individuals with T2D, those with combined mild cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms had lower adiponectin and higher hs-CRP and TNF-α; adiponectin was inversely associated with depression scores, inflammation, and HbA1c, and positively associated with cognition. 50 By contrast, the other T2D study found that the adiponectin and depression association was no longer significant after BMI adjustment. 30

In T1D, one of four studies reported a significant association between non-classical inflammatory proteins and depression, whereas three found no independent association.42–45 The significant study found that low soluble TWEAK (Tumor Necrosis Factor Like Weak Inducer of Apoptosis) and high galectin-3 were associated with depression. 44 By contrast, high sCD163 was associated with inflammatory burden and diabetic retinopathy but not depression, 43 galectin-3 binding protein was unrelated to depression despite reflecting inflammatory and metabolic stress, 42 and hs-CRP was not independently associated with depression. 45 However, lower HDL-cholesterol was associated with depressive symptoms, suggesting potential lipid-inflammation interactions in T1D. 45

Cell-counts based indices and composite inflammatory scores

As summarized in Supplementary Table S6, recent studies increasingly examined composite immune-metabolic indices derived from routine blood counts and metabolic markers. All three of these cross-sectional studies reported positive associations between these indices and depressive symptoms in people with diabetes.29,34,46 In adults with diabetes from NHANES, higher systemic immune-inflammation index (SII, derived from neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte counts), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, and hs-CRP were associated with greater odds of depression after extensive adjustment and matching. 34 In Indonesian T2D cohorts, insufficient physical activity and high SII were independently and synergistically associated with stress, anxiety, and depression, with the highest distress observed among participants with both low activity and high SII. 46 Another large cross-sectional study found that a composite C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index was positively associated with PHQ-9 scores, with participants in the highest quartile having approximately twice the odds of depressive symptoms versus the lowest quartile. 29 Overall, composite immune-metabolic indices were associated with depressive symptoms in cross-sectional studies.

Oxidative stress and omics-based markers

As summarized in Supplementary Table S7, a smaller body of work examined oxidative stress markers, lipid mediators, and multianalyte inflammatory panels. Both studies in this subgroup reported significant associations between inflammation-related biomarkers and depressive symptoms in people with diabetes.36,39 In T2D, higher plasma DPP4 activity, lower brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and an elevated DPP4/BDNF ratio were associated with moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms, with oxidative stress partially mediating these relationships. 36 Large-scale proteomic profiling in combined T1D and T2D cohorts indicated that multiple inflammatory proteins were positively associated with depression scores, with stronger effects for somatic and anhedonia symptom clusters and evidence of diabetes type–specific interactions. 39

Longitudinal associations between depression and inflammation

Longitudinal studies were fewer (7/37) and reported more varied findings than cross-sectional studies (Supplementary Table S8). One cohort reported separate T1D and T2D analyses; thus, seven studies contributed eight diabetes type-specific comparisons. Overall, five of eight studies reported significant inflammation-depression associations, whereas three did not. Three of four longitudinal cohort studies reported significant associations, all in T2D; the single T1D cohort was null.23,32,40,41 In T2D, higher baseline CRP predicted higher depressive symptom scores and greater odds of incident depression during follow-up. 40 A larger IL-6 response to acute mental stress also predicted higher depressive symptoms and poorer mental health–related quality of life 7.5 years later. 41 Herder et al. saw that reductions in depressive symptoms over 12 months paralleled decreases in hs-CRP, IL-18, and IL-1RA, whereas higher baseline CCL2/MCP-1 predicted less symptom improvement. 23 Similarly, Moulton et al. reported that higher inflammatory burden predicted worse subsequent depressive symptoms among women. 32 In T1D, baseline hs-CRP, IL-6, IL-18, CCL2/MCP-1, IL-1RA, and adiponectin did not predict one-year changes in depressive symptoms, nor did biomarker changes correlate with depression changes over time. 23

Among randomized controlled trials, findings varied: one of three reported a significant association between depressive symptom and biomarker changes, whereas two were null.24,26,37 A diabetes-specific stepped-care intervention improved well-being over 12 months but did not alter inflammatory biomarkers, and baseline inflammation did not moderate treatment response. 26 In another trial, psychoeducation, exercise, and enhanced usual care each reduced depressive symptoms over 6 months, with concurrent changes in several oxidative and inflammatory markers, although hs-CRP and adiponectin were unchanged. 37 Bright light therapy did not outperform placebo in reducing depressive symptoms or inflammatory markers in adults with T2D and depression, and symptom changes were not correlated with CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, or IFN-γ changes over 4 weeks. 24

Sex-based differences in inflammatory and depressive associations

Across studies examining sex differences, six of seven reported at least one significant sex-specific association, whereas one was null, suggesting that sex often modified inflammation and depression associations in diabetes, although effects varied by biomarker and diabetes type (Supplementary Table S9).32,34,35,42,43,45,51

In T2D, all four studies reported significant sex-related differences.32,34,35,51 Some cohorts showed stronger inflammation-depression associations in women. In one prospective study, women with high inflammation had higher 2-year PHQ-9 scores than men, whereas no sex difference was observed in the low-inflammation group. 32 Another cross-sectional study reported that higher hs-CRP was associated with greater depressive and anxiety symptom severity in women but not men. 35 Webb et al. also reported sex-specific inflammatory correlates, with depressed women showing higher TNF-α and depressed men higher CRP than non-depressed counterparts. 51 By contrast, an NHANES analysis found that the association between systemic immune-inflammation index and depressive symptoms was significant only in men. 34

In T1D, two of three studies reported sex-specific findings, whereas one was null.42,43,45 Sex differences were more apparent in inflammatory and metabolic profiles related to depression than in direct inflammation-depression associations. Women were more likely to have high galectin-3 binding protein, which clustered with higher sCD163, higher total cholesterol, lower HDL-cholesterol, and higher HbA1c. 42 In another analysis, lower HDL-cholesterol, reflecting a more adverse and potentially pro-inflammatory lipid profile, was independently associated with depression, especially in women, who also showed an association between antidepressant use and abdominal obesity. 45 By contrast, one T1D study found no direct association, although elevated sCD163 had sex-specific clinical correlates, being more closely linked to antidepressant use in women and to retinopathy and higher blood pressure in men. 43

Discussion

Among adults with T2D, CRP/hs-CRP was most frequently associated with depressive symptoms, including greater symptom severity, comorbid major depressive disorder, or incident depression.16,17,20,27,30,35,38,40 Biologically, CRP is an integrated downstream marker of chronic innate immune activation and may better capture the cumulative inflammatory effects of adiposity, insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, and metabolic stress than single cytokines measured at one time point.53–56 Recent studies of composite indices also found positive associations between depressive symptoms and SII, NLR/PLR, and CTI, suggesting that combined immune-metabolic measures may capture relevant pathophysiology more effectively than single biomarkers.29,34,46 In contrast, evidence for IL-6, TNF-α, and other cytokines was more variable21,25 with limited association after adjustment for BMI, adiposity, or other metabolic covariates.18,30 This variability may reflect the inherent dynamics of cytokine expression, including effects of circadian rhythms, acute stress, infection, medications, assay variability, and limited detectability. 57

Several studies suggested more frequent inflammation and depression associations in the presence of higher adiposity, reduced physical activity, or poorer metabolic health. For example, hs-CRP was associated with depression primarily in obese adults with T2D, 27 and mediated associations linking obesity and physical activity to depression risk in older T2D cohorts. 17 Similarly, low physical activity and high SII were independently and synergistically associated with psychological distress in T2D. 46

A second source of heterogeneity is vascular and endothelial dysfunction, particularly in older and more comorbid T2D samples. Depressive symptoms in elderly adults with T2D clustered with thrombomodulin, PAI-1, fibrinogen, sICAM-1, VCAM-1, and E-selectin, often alongside stroke, neuropathy, mild cognitive impairment, or greater overall comorbidity.48–50 Similarly, platelet activation markers in T2D were associated with comorbid major depression and related to hs-CRP and insulin resistance indices.19,22 These studies suggest that, for some patients, depressive symptoms in diabetes may reflect a vascular inflammatory phenotype involving endothelial activation, pro-thrombotic signaling, and microvascular or macrovascular disease burden, rather than generalized cytokine elevation alone. However, associations between inflammation and depression should be interpreted cautiously, as they may be partly confounded or mediated by comorbidity burden.

In patients with recent-onset T1D, some associations were observed for hs-CRP, IL-1RA, and sICAM-1, but classical cytokines often demonstrated weak associations.28,47 In long duration T1D, hs-CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α were not associated with depression or quality of life. 31 , 23 Alternatively, several T1D studies implicated macrophage related proteins, galectins, sTWEAK, and lipid associated inflammatory profiles, including lower HDL-cholesterol.42–45 While this evidence remains preliminary, it suggests that the inflammatory correlates of depression may differ by diabetes type. In T2D, depression appears more consistently associated with low grade systemic inflammation embedded in obesity and insulin resistance. In T1D, where the disease background is autoimmune and treatment exposures and complication profiles differ, depressive symptoms may instead relate more to macrophage activation, vascular injury, lipid dysregulation, or chronic complication burden.

Several prospective studies suggest that inflammatory burden may serve as a risk marker for later depressive symptoms in T2D. Baseline CRP predicted future depressive symptoms and incident depression in adults with diabetes, 40 and heightened IL-6 responses to acute stress predicted higher depressive symptoms and poorer mental health-related quality of life over 7.5 years. 41 In addition, higher baseline MCP-1 predicted smaller improvement in depressive symptoms over one year, while reductions in depressive symptoms correlated with decreases in hs-CRP, IL-18, and IL-1RA in T2D. 23 However, the intervention based studies also demonstrated that clinically meaningful improvement in depressive symptoms does not necessarily coincide with comparable changes in common systemic inflammatory biomarkers.24,26

Sex also emerged as an important but incompletely understood modifier. Across available studies, women with T2D more often showed associations between hs-CRP or inflammatory reactivity and depressive symptoms, whereas one study found a stronger SII-depression association in men.32,34,35 In T1D, sex differences were more apparent in the clustering of inflammatory and metabolic risk features than in direct biomarker and depression associations.42,43,45 These findings suggest that sex effects are unlikely to be uniform across markers and may depend on body fat distribution, hormonal conditions, vascular risk, and depression phenotype.

Clinically, current evidence does not support inflammatory biomarkers as stand-alone diagnostic tools for depression in diabetes. However, repeated associations for hs-CRP and composite immune-metabolic indices suggest potential value for identifying biologically distinct subgroups in precision-treatment approaches. The more immediate implication is that depression in diabetes should not be viewed solely as a psychological reaction to chronic illness, but in at least some patients may reflect part of a broader immunometabolic and vascular syndrome. This perspective may help explain why depressive symptoms cluster with obesity, poor physical functioning, vascular complications, and reduced treatment response in some individuals.

The reader should be aware of some study limitations, including that the preponderance of studies were cross-sectional (limiting causal inference), that the evidence base was methodologically heterogeneous (e.g., variation in biomarker selection, covariate adjustment, and study populations), and that depression measures varied across studies, with some using symptom scales and others using diagnostic interviews. Future work should prioritize longitudinal studies with repeated biomarker measurements, with greater attention paid to sex, vascular complications, and behavioral pathways such as physical activity.
