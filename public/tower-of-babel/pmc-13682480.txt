Sustained type I interferon activity and N-antigenemia form a vicious cycle during severe COVID-19

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAlthough antigenemia is associated with poor outcomes in respiratory viral infections, the underlying mechanisms driving viral bloodstream dissemination remain not fully understood. Herein, we aimed to characterise SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N)-antigenemia kinetics in patients with severe COVID-19. We also sought to identify host and viral determinants associated with viral dissemination through a multiparametric integrated analysis and assess the associated risk of death.MethodsThis observational study included hospitalised patients with severe COVID-19 across three independent cohorts (discovery, n = 66; confirmation, n = 137; validation, n = 27), used respectively for identification, replication, and validation of findings, along with patients with mild COVID-19 and with severe influenza infection, as a disease severity and non-COVID-19 control group. From blood and nasal samples, N-antigenemia levels were analysed in relation to 45 host/viral parameters. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to assess the association with in-hospital mortality. Single-cell RNA sequencing was used to identify host determinants, and in vitro primary human airway epithelial models were used to assess epithelial barrier integrity and viral translocation.FindingsAcross cohorts, we observed a significantly higher detection rate and concentration of N-antigenemia in patients with severe COVID-19 at admission compared to those with mild COVID-19 or controls (fold change [FC] >54.7, p < 0.001 [Kruskal–Wallis, Dunn post-hoc]). Type-I interferon (IFN–I) activity was the strongest correlate of N-antigenemia (ρ = 0.72, p.adj < 0.001 [Spearman, Benjamini-Hochberg]), and longitudinal analyses revealed a strong positive sigmoidal relationship (p < 0.001 [approximate Wald test]). Patients with severe COVID-19 with neutralising autoantibodies against IFN-I lacked detectable N-antigenemia, further supporting a link between systemic viral dissemination and IFN-I activity. Single-cell RNA sequencing showed higher expression of interferon-stimulated genes in monocytes and dendritic cells in patients with a high N-antigenemia level. Furthermore, high N-antigenemia at hospital admission was independently associated with an increased risk of death (aOR 2.6, 95% CI 1.0–6.7; p = 0.042 [Wald test]). Finally, we demonstrated that prolonged IFN-I exposure in vitro appears to impair epithelial barrier integrity.InterpretationThese findings suggest that sustained IFN-I activity is associated with systemic viral dissemination and epithelial barrier dysfunction in patient with severe COVID-19. The concordance between clinical observations and in vitro data supports a potential link between prolonged IFN-I responses and these processes, although causality remains to be established. Research in context

Evidence before this study

A recent review reported a pooled viraemia prevalence of 34% in severe SARS-CoV-2 disease, with substantial heterogeneity largely driven by assay variability and inconsistent methodologies. Notably, antigenemia appeared more sensitive than RNAemia to assess systemic viral dissemination. We subsequently searched PubMed, Medline and bioRxiv/medRxiv for articles published between 2020 and 2026, without language restrictions, using the search terms “SARS-CoV-2” AND “Antigenemia.” We identified 37 publications reporting mainly associations between antigenemia and clinical severity. Although these studies proposed multiple mechanisms of viral dissemination into the bloodstream, including inflammatory responses and endothelial dysfunction, the mechanisms allowing viral entry into blood and their consequences remain poorly understood, since findings are fragmented and, at times, discrepant.

Added value of this study

This study integrates quantitative antigenemia profiling and broad biomarker screening across three independent cohorts of severe COVID-19. We identified type I interferon (IFN–I) activity as the strongest correlate of N-antigenemia and found a patient subgroup with sustained IFN-α2 signalling and persistent antigenemia. Single-cell analysis revealed enhanced interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) expression in circulating monocytes and dendritic cells during antigenemia, while in vitro airway epithelial models demonstrated that prolonged IFN-I exposure impairs epithelial barrier integrity and may facilitate viral passage. High N-antigenemia levels at hospital admission independently predicted mortality, confirming its potential value as a prognostic marker.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings refine current understanding of systemic viral dissemination by linking sustained IFN-I activity to epithelial barrier dysfunction and enhanced viral translocation. They confirm that N-antigenemia is associated with severe disease but might also predict mortality at hospital admission. Antigenemia may therefore represent a robust, clinically actionable biomarker for risk stratification in severe COVID-19, and the mechanistic insights provided herein may extend to other respiratory viral infections in which interferon-driven barrier disruption may contribute to systemic spread and poor outcomes.

Introduction

Increasing evidence supports an association between viraemia and severe Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), as well as other respiratory viruses, underscoring its potential utility as a biomarker for disease monitoring and risk stratification.1 In this review, Hagman et al.1 reported a pooled viraemia prevalence of 34% in Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection, with estimates ranging from 6% to 65% for other respiratory viruses among patients with severe disease. Notably, substantial inter-study heterogeneity was observed, driven partly by differences in study populations but predominantly by methodological variability, including assay sensitivity (RT-qPCR versus droplet digital PCR) and the viral target measured in the blood (RNA versus antigen). When available, antigenemia appears to provide a more stable and sensitive assessment of systemic viral dissemination than RNAemia.2 Consistently, antigenemia has also been reported in bacterial pneumonia and proposed as a marker of disease severity across both bacterial and viral respiratory infections.3, 4, 5

Although antigenemia has been consistently associated with poor outcomes in respiratory viral infections, the mechanisms underlying viral entry into the bloodstream remain incompletely understood.1 Several hypotheses have been proposed, including endothelial injury and increased vascular permeability, insufficient specific humoural immunity, sustained viral replication that increases local viral burden, upregulated expression of entry factors that promote systemic spread, and dysregulated inflammatory responses with several different altered pathways and contradictory results.1,2,6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 However, an integrated, comprehensive analytical approach has not yet been undertaken.

In the present study, we aimed to elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying COVID-19 severity, with a focus on systemic viral dissemination. Specifically, we (i) characterised the prevalence and longitudinal kinetics of nucleocapsid (N)-antigenemia in severe COVID-19 using a validated assay; (ii) identified host and viral determinants of N-antigenemia through comprehensive biomarker profiling; (iii) examined its relationship with type I interferon (IFN–I) responses across cohorts, including longitudinal analyses; and (iv) assessed its association with clinical outcomes, including in-hospital mortality, and complemented by single-cell transcriptomic and in vitro analyses to explore underlying mechanisms.

Methods

Study design and participants

Samples from hospitalised patients with severe COVID-19 were obtained from three independent clinical studies and assembled into discovery, confirmation, and validation cohorts. Severe disease was defined as a score ≥6 on the World Health Organisation (WHO) Clinical Progression Scale.12 The discovery cohort included 66 intensive care unit (ICU) patients from a single-centre observational study conducted at the Hospices Civils de Lyon (HCL) between December 2021 and February 2022 (MIR-COVID study), with blood and nasopharyngeal swab (NPS) samples collected at ICU admission. The confirmation cohort included 137 patients enrolled in a European multicentre clinical trial conducted between June 2021 and March 2022 (Bari-SolidAct study, NCT04891133), with longitudinal blood and NPS sampling on days 1, 3, 8, 15, and 22 after hospital admission.13 The validation cohort included 27 ICU patients from an ongoing prospective single-centre observational study initiated at the HCL in November 2022 (INTERFERICUS study, NCT05536219), with blood and NPS samples collected on days 1, 3, and 7 after ICU admission. Within the same study, an additional and independent group of 21 ICU patients with laboratory-confirmed influenza infection was included and analysed as a non-COVID-19 severe respiratory infection control group to confirm the specificity of the N-antigenemia assay. We also included 30 healthy controls (HCs) recruited among blood donors from the Lyon blood bank (Etablissement Français du Sang, EFS) as negative controls.

Additional control groups included 100 healthcare workers (HCWs) with mild COVID-19 from a prospective longitudinal cohort study conducted at the HCL between August 2021 and February 2022 (COVID-IVAC study, NCT05060939),14 in which samples were collected at diagnosis, these individuals were used to assess the impact of disease severity on N-antigenemia level. Patient groups, cohorts, and analytical procedures are detailed in the Supplementary Material and Figure S1.

Ethics

All human samples were collected and used in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable French and European regulations. In compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (EU 2016/679), Directive 95/46/EC, and French data protection law (Law n°78-17 of 06/01/1978 and Décret n°2019-536 of 29/05/2019), written informed consent or non-opposition consent was obtained from all participants according to the study design. All samples were fully anonymised prior to analysis.

HCs were recruited through the Lyon blood bank (Etablissement Français du Sang, EFS) in accordance with the French Public Health Code (Article R.1243–49). The mild COVID-19 cohort was approved by a regional ethics committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes Sud Méditerranée I; ID-RCB 2021-A01877-34). The severe COVID-19 cohorts included three studies: the discovery cohort was approved by the Commission nationale de l'informatique et des libertés (CNIL, 20–097) and the Comité de Protection des Personnes HCL (CPP, N°20–41); the confirmation cohort (Bari-SolidAct, NCT04891133) was approved by ethics committees in each participating country; and the validation cohort (INTERFERICUS, NCT05536219) was approved by the CPP Sud-Ouest et Outre-Mer II (2022-A01824-39). Detailed descriptions of each cohort, including study design, approvals, and consent procedures are provided in the Supplementary Material.

The data were obtained from three independent studies, each originally designed to address distinct primary objectives. In all cases, N-antigenemia analysis was included as a secondary objective; therefore, no formal sample size calculation was performed for this specific outcome.

45 parameters integrated analysis

Virological investigation

The normalised nasal viral load was determined for each sample as previously described (Supplementary Material).15 SARS-CoV-2 N-antigenemia was quantified from serum samples using the Lumipulse G SARS-CoV-2 Ag assay (Fujirebio Holdings Inc., Tokyo, Japan, 260,340) performed on the Lumipulse G600II automated immunoassay analyser (Fujirebio; RRID:SCR_018018, Supplementary Material). A positivity cut-off of 2.97 pg/mL, previously reported on serum,16 was used.

Protein quantification and serological investigations

Serum IFN-α2 concentrations were quantified using a commercial single molecule array (SIMOA®) assay (Quanterix™, Lexington, MA, USA; kit 100,860) on an HD-X analyser. Serum IL-6 concentrations were measured using an automated VIDAS® enzyme-linked fluorescent assay (bioMérieux; kit 423,305). Markers of endothelial dysfunction, inflammation and coagulation, including angiopoietin-2 (ANG-2), E-selectin, TNFR-1, VEGFR-2, ICAM-1, PTX-3, MMP-9, CRP, D-dimer, ferritin and PAI-1, were quantified using customised assays on the ELLA Automated Immunoassay System with Simple Plex Technology (ProteinSimple, San Jose, CA, USA; RRID:SCR_027757). Detailed assay procedures and analytical characteristics are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Anti-receptor binding domain (RBD) IgG antibodies were quantified using the VIDAS® SARS-CoV-2 IgG assay (bioMérieux; kit 423,834) and expressed as binding antibody units (BAU)/mL according to the manufacturer's recommendations. Anti-IFN-α2 autoantibodies were measured by ELISA (Thermo Fisher Scientific; kit BMS217) using a previously validated positivity threshold of 34 ng/mL. Their capacity to neutralise IFN-α2 at 100 pg/mL and 10 ng/mL was determined in vitro using a previously described cell-based functional assay.17 Additional methodological details are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Pulmonary parameters

For patients included in the discovery cohort, computed tomography (CT) acquired at 72 h of ICU admission were analysed using a machine learning-based algorithm (Supplementary Material, Figure S2).

Host-response transcriptomic panels

As previously described, host response was investigated at the transcriptomic level using two FilmArray®panel pouch prototypes (BioFire Diagnostics, Salt Lake City, UT, USA): Immune profiling panel (IPP) and IFN-I panel encompassing four IFN-stimulated genes (ISG, Supplementary Material).18, 19, 20

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using the Rhapsody Single-Cell Analysis System (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA, RRID:SCR_027096). Cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed, viability-sorted, and processed according to the manufacturer instructions. Libraries were prepared using the Rhapsody Whole Transcriptome Analysis workflow (BD Biosciences, 666,262, 633,773, and 633,801) and sequenced on NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA, RRID:SCR_016387). Raw data processing, viral transcript detection, and downstream analyses—including normalisation, integration, clustering, and cell-type annotation—were performed using Rhapsody workflow (Becton Dickinson) and Seurat (v5.0; Satija Lab, Supplementary Material).

Reconstituted human airway epithelial (HAE) model

Reconstituted human airway epithelia (HAE) were obtained from human primary cells obtained from nasal, bronchial or alveolar biopsies (Epithelix SARL, Geneva, Switzerland, EP02MP, EP01MD, AlveolAir™) were maintained in an air-liquid interface. The basal medium of HAE was treated with 10 ng/mL or 1 μg/mL IFN-α2 for four or five consecutive days; in parallel a series of control (untreated) HAEs were exposed with corresponding solvent under identical experimental conditions (confocal immunofluorescence microscopy, trans-epithelial permeability assay and transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurement protocols detailed in Supplementary Material).

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8) and R (v4.4.1). Quantitative variables are presented as median (IQR) and categorical variables as n (%). Group comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis, or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. Correlations were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficients. Multiple-testing correction was performed using Dunn's test or the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Longitudinal kinetics of N-antigenemia and IFN-α2 were modelled using generalised additive models, with days post-symptom onset (DPSO) modelled as a smoothing term and patient identifier included as a random effect. Distinct N-antigenemia kinetic profiles were identified using k-means clustering and validated in an independent cohort using a random forest classifier. Association between in-hospital mortality and N-antigenemia adjusted for age, sex, and obesity was assessed using Firth's bias-reduced penalised-likelihood multivariate logistic regression. No formal sample size calculation was performed for the present analyses, as all samples included came from the participating cohorts meeting predefined eligibility criteria. Randomisation and blinding were not applicable given the observational nature of the study and the use of samples collected within previously approved clinical cohorts. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined in the respective parent studies and are described in the cohort descriptions in Supplementary Material. For individual analyses, samples with missing values for variables of interest were excluded and additional analysis-specific eligibility criteria were applied as described in the Supplementary Material. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05. Additional methodological details are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Role of funders

The funders had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, manuscript preparation, or decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

First, we assessed the specificity of the Lumipulse assay for detecting SARS-CoV-2 N-antigenemia. All 30 uninfected HCs and the 21 patients with severe influenza infection (Table S1) had N-antigenemia concentrations below the cut-off value, confirming the assay's specificity (Fig. 1A). Next, we compared N-antigenemia between the 66 patients with severe COVID-19 in the discovery cohort and the 100 HCWs with mild symptoms, and found that both the detection rate and the N-antigenemia levels were significantly higher in patients with severe COVID-19 at ICU admission (51/66, 77.3%; 27.8[3.2–97.2] pg/mL) than in patients with mild COVID-19 at diagnosis (1/100, 1.0%; 0.5[0.4–0.8] pg/mL, p < 0.0001, [Kruskal–Wallis, Dunn post-hoc]; Fig. 1A). In parallel, RNAemia was detected in 47.7% (31/65) of patients with severe COVID-19, and all RNAemia positive patients were also positive for N-antigenemia. Moreover, N-antigenemia concentrations were significantly higher in patients with RNAemia than in patients without (65.6[28.3–138.3] vs 12.4[2–32.9] pg/mL, FC 5.3; p < 0.0001, [Wilcoxon]; Figure S3A), which could indicate that plasma-antigen detection is somewhat more sensitive than RNAemia for identifying viraemia, as defined by Hagman et al.1 as the presence of circulating viral material and/or viral components in blood rather than strictly infectious virions. These findings confirm the specificity of the Lumipulse assay for N-antigenemia detection. We then sought to explore the factors that might contribute to this higher N-antigenemia level in patients with severe COVID-19 and to investigate the underlying mechanisms, by analysing N-antigenemia levels in relation to 45 parameters through a comprehensive analysis (Fig. 1B, Table S2). Although markers of endothelial activation or damage (e.g. VEGFR-2, ANG-2, and ICAM-1) and inflammatory markers were increased in patients with severe COVID-19 (data not shown), we did not observe any significant positive correlation with N-antigenemia levels. However, N-antigenemia appeared to be significantly and inversely correlated with RBD-specific antibody response (ρ = −0.60, p.adj < 0.001, [Spearman, Benjamini-Hochberg]; Fig. 1C and D), confirming previously published findings.23 More interestingly, the strongest correlation was observed for the IFN-I response. This was observed at the protein level, with circulating IFN-α2 concentrations (ρ = 0.72, p.adj < 0.001, [Spearman, Benjamini-Hochberg]), and at the transcriptomic level, through OAS2 (ρ = 0.66, p.adj < 0.001, [Spearman, Benjamini-Hochberg]) and CXCL10 (ρ = 0.62, p.adj < 0.001, [Spearman, Benjamini-Hochberg], Fig. 1C and D), expression, two canonical ISGs involved, respectively, in antiviral RNA degradation and immune cell recruitment. Importantly, we observed no significant effect of the SARS-CoV-2 strain on N-antigenemia (Figure S3B).

To further investigate this preferential correlation between N-antigenemia and IFN-I activity, we characterised both kinetics using longitudinal samples from patients with severe COVID-19 of the confirmation cohort. We confirmed the high frequency of patients positive for N-antigenemia at hospital admission, with similar median concentration compared to the discovery cohort (Figure S4; 72.4%, 92/127; 20.0[2.3–105.8] pg/mL). By analysing the temporal kinetics of N-antigenemia (Fig. 2A) and IFN-α2 concentrations (Fig. 2B) according to days post-symptom onset (DPSO) in the 116 out of 137 patients with longitudinal samples available for both parameters, we observed a strong positive sigmoidal relationship between them over the course of disease (p < 0.001, [approximate Wald test], Fig. 2C). Despite inter-individual variability, the highest values for both N-antigenemia and IFN-α2 concentrations were observed at admission and during the first 10 days after symptom onset. This was followed by a progressive decline of N-antigenemia (−0.57 log2 pg/mL per day, 95% CI -0.66 to −0.48) and IFN-α2 (−0.41 log2 pg/mL per day, 95% CI -0.48 to −0.34) concentrations between DPSO 10 and 20, i.e. a stabilisation phase in which levels reached a lower, near-basal steady state (Fig. 2A and B). Interestingly, unsupervised clustering found three distinct clusters corresponding to different N-antigenemia trajectories throughout the hospitalisation period (Fig. 2D). In contrast, the two other clusters showed markedly distinct temporal patterns. Cluster 2 (35.3%, 41/116) exhibited persistently low/undetectable N-antigenemia throughout hospitalisation. In summary, this longitudinal analysis revealed heterogeneous N-antigenemia trajectories among patients with severe COVID-19, ranging from negligible to persistently elevated levels, suggesting that monitoring N-antigenemia kinetics better captures patient-specific disease course than single time-point measurements.

We next estimated the association between N-antigenemia levels at hospital or ICU admission and in-hospital mortality, adjusting for age, sex, and obesity. To increase statistical power, analyses were performed on the pooled cohort combining the three study phases (discovery, confirmation, and validation), including 201 samples collected at hospital admission from patients with available N-antigenemia measurements and complete clinical data (overall mortality: 24.9% 50/201). Overall, compared to the reference category (no N-antigenemia), high N-antigenemia (defined as >Q3 i.e. 7log pg/mL) was significantly associated with increased odds of death (aOR 2.6, 95% CI 1–6.7; p = 0.042, [Wald test]). Increasing age was also independently associated with in-hospital mortality, although with smaller effect size (aOR 1.4 per 10-year increase, 95% CI 1.1–1.9; p = 0.007, [Wald test]). By contrast, obesity (p > 0.086, [Wald test]) and sex (p > 0.312, [Wald test]) were not associated with in-hospital mortality despite a trend towards higher risk in males and obese patients (Fig. 4). Taken together, these findings suggest that, despite similar disease severity and impaired IFN-I activity due to AAN-IFN-I, patients were more likely to lack detectable N-antigenemia, suggesting that systemic N-antigenemia may not simply reflect uncontrolled viral replication. Although limited by the small number of patients with AAN-IFN-I, precluding definitive conclusions, these observations nevertheless support a potential role for sustained IFN-I responses in promoting viral bloodstream dissemination, suggesting an additional mechanism that may contribute to severe disease and mortality beyond impaired IFN-I immunity.

We next investigated the IFN-I pathway activation through a score encompassing nine ISGs (Supplementary Material) in the cell populations described in Fig. 6A. Higher signal intensity was observed within the myeloid compartment, particularly among CD14+/CD16+ monocytes and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs; Fig. 6B). Comparison by N-antigenemia status revealed increased IFN-I transcriptional activity in CD14+ monocytes and, to a lesser extent, in DCs in patients with high versus undetectable N-antigenemia, while other immune populations showed limited differences (Fig. 6C). To further characterise monocyte functional states, we additionally assessed pathway-level signatures related to NF-κB (Fig. 6D and Figure S5B) and monocyte inflammation (Fig. 6E and Figure S5C) signalling pathways within the same dataset. In contrast to the IFN-I signature, both pathways displayed lower activity in patients with high N-antigenemia within CD14+/CD16+ monocytes and conventional/plasmacytoid dendritic cells (Fig. 6D and E). These data suggest that high N-antigenemia is associated with enhanced IFN-I transcriptional activity in innate myeloid cells alongside a relative dampening of inflammatory and NF-κB-related pathways, highlighting distinct and potentially divergent monocyte activation programs. Collectively, these observations suggest a model in which prolonged IFN-I activation, reflected by increased circulating IFN-α2 levels and sustained IFN-I pathway activation in innate myeloid cells, may contribute to the pathophysiology of severe COVID-19 associated with high N-antigenemia.

We therefore postulate that this prolonged IFN-I signalling may compromise respiratory epithelium integrity, thereby promoting barrier dysfunction, facilitating viral dissemination into the circulation, and perpetuating a vicious host–virus cycle. We previously reported, using a reconstituted human airway epithelial (HAE) model of SARS-CoV-2 infection, that treatment by IFN-α2 on HAE completely blocks viral replication and that AAN-IFN-α2 compromise this antiviral activity. We notably reported that IFN-α2 treatment, during a short period, had no negative effect on the epithelium.15 However, under different conditions, previous studies have demonstrated the deleterious effect of IFN on the epithelial barrier,28,29 which may contribute to the wider dissemination of the virus.

We therefore treated reconstituted HAEs from nasal, alveolar, or bronchial origins (Fig. 7A–F) with high doses of IFN-α2 and measured TEER over a 120-h period to assess epithelial integrity. The TEER of the treated epithelium was significantly lower at 96- or 120-h post-treatment, depending on the model, compared to untreated epithelia (Fig. 7A, C, and E). This reduction in epithelial integrity was accompanied by an increased expression of IFN-induced genes (ISG) mRNA associated with the IFN-I response (Fig. 7B, D, and F). We also demonstrated, in the bronchial HAE model, that the detrimental effect of IFN-α2 treatment on TEER was dose-dependent, with more pronounced and significant effects at higher concentrations (Fig. 7E). To complement these observations, we investigated trans-epithelial permeability using FITC-Dextran, which has a molecular weight comparable to that of a viral antigen (Fig. 7G). These measurements revealed a significant increase in permeability from 120 h (p < 0.01, [Student's t test]) in HAEs treated with 1 μg/mL of IFN-α2. Taken together, these observations suggest that prolonged exposure to IFN-α2 may compromise the epithelial barrier, potentially facilitating N-antigenemia. These IFN-induced epithelial lesions were observed in a non-infectious context, given the difficulty in distinguishing the effects of IFN-I during SARS-CoV-2 infection. However, they likely reflect the conditions prevailing at infection sites, where uninfected epithelial cells are exposed to high local levels of IFN with deleterious effects.

Discussion

Using three independent cohorts of patients with severe COVID-19, we confirmed the high prevalence of N-antigenemia and its strong association with disease severity, confirming its potential value as a prognostic marker.1 To further explore the mechanisms underlying systemic viral dissemination, we applied a multiparametric approach in a discovery cohort to identify host and viral determinants of bloodstream viral entry. Although respiratory viral load was associated with circulating N-antigenemia levels in patients with severe COVID-19, this relationship was modest. In contrast, N-antigenemia showed stronger associations with several host-related parameters, suggesting that systemic viral dissemination may reflect host responses and pathophysiological processes beyond viral replication in the respiratory tract alone.

We evaluated the contribution of pulmonary damage and endothelial injury. Herein, circulating N-antigen levels were not associated with CT-based markers of lung involvement, contrasting with the findings reported by Nagaoka et al.30 Regarding markers of endothelial activation or damage we observed only modest correlations with N-antigenemia, consistent with the absence of association between N-antigen concentrations and endothelial/vascular injury markers reported by Wick et al..31 Herein, our results appeared to indicate that endothelial damage is unlikely to represent a dominant mechanism of systemic viral dissemination in SARS-CoV-2 infection. Interestingly, among the 45 parameters investigated, the strongest associations with N-antigenemia levels were observed for the IFN-I response, assessed at transcriptomic and protein levels. This close relationship between systemic viral dissemination and IFN-I activity was further confirmed in an independent cohort. Longitudinal analyses revealed two atypical patient profiles. The first atypical profile comprised patients with absent or persistently low N-antigenemia throughout hospitalisation, reflecting a profound impairment of the IFN-I response, as indicated by extremely low circulating IFN-α2 levels. IFN-I impairment in COVID-19—driven by AAN-IFN-I or inborn genetic errors of immunity—is a well-established risk factor for severe disease.32 We detected AAN-IFN-I in a subset of patients with severe COVID-19 in our cohorts, confirming previous reports that these autoantibodies are generally pre-existing rather than infection-induced and are strongly associated with severe COVID-19 due to impaired antiviral IFN-I activity.33 We showed that patients with severe COVID-19 with AAN-IFN-α or AAN-IFN-β exhibit absent or very low N-antigenemia despite prolonged high respiratory viral loads, reinforcing the notion that systemic viral dissemination is not merely a direct consequence of mucosal viral burden and suggesting that IFN-I responses may contribute, at least partially, to the dissemination of viral particles or viral components into the bloodstream.

The second atypical profile consisted of patients with a sustained IFN-I response who exhibited a progressive increase in circulating N-antigen levels over time. However, the mechanisms and consequences of prolonged IFN-I responses observed in a subset of COVID-19 patients remain poorly understood. The strong association between prolonged IFN-I responses and N-antigenemia led us to hypothesise a bidirectional relationship, without establishing a definitive causality between both phenomena. On the one hand, circulating viral particles may act as a trigger for systemic IFN-I production, providing a potential explanation for the sustained IFN-I responses observed in some patients with severe COVID-19, as previously suggested at the single-cell level in viral RNA–containing cells.34 On the other hand, the association between bloodstream viral dissemination and persistent IFN-I activity raises the possibility that prolonged IFN-I secretion may facilitate viral entry into the circulation.

Then, to investigate the detrimental impact of prolonged exposure to IFN-α2 on epithelial integrity, we used a reconstituted HAE model. Of note, our objective was not to reproduce the exact in vivo kinetics or physiological concentrations of IFN-I, but rather to assess whether sustained IFN-α2 exposure could mechanistically alter epithelial barrier integrity and thereby contribute to viral antigenemia. We found a reduction in TEER and tight junction integrity following prolonged IFN-α2 treatment. In line with these findings, previous studies have reported connections between IFN-I and vascular leakage35 and, more importantly, between this innate immune pathway and impairment of epithelium regeneration.28 Furthermore, an increase in IFN production during infection combined with greater induction of antiproliferative pathways compromised the endothelium repair, as reported by Braggazzi-Cunha et al.36 who found that persistent IFN-I signalling induces antiproliferative effects contributing to deficient alveolar regeneration. These findings are consistent with those of Major et al.28 who reported that IFN-induced p53 is directly responsible for reducing epithelial proliferation and differentiation. Collectively, these results, along with those reported herein, support the biological plausibility that sustained IFN-I responses may contribute to respiratory epithelial disruption and thereby facilitate bloodstream viral dissemination. This is in line with previous reports suggesting that sustained ISG responses can impair lung epithelial barrier integrity, including those induced by type III IFNs (IFN-λ).37 However, these findings do not exclude the alternative possibility that persistent viral replication contributes to both N-antigenemia and prolonged IFN-I activation.

Altogether, these findings support a model of three distinct temporal patterns of IFN-I activity during SARS-CoV-2 infection. An early and transient IFN-I response, detected at both the mucosal and systemic levels, is associated with efficient viral clearance and a mild disease course.15 In contrast, an impaired IFN-I response—particularly in individuals harbouring neutralising anti–IFN-I autoantibodies—predisposes to life-threatening COVID-19.33 This interpretation is further supported by evidence that low IFN-α2 levels and reduced IFN-I activity may precede progression to critical illness, even in the absence of systemic viral dissemination.38 Our findings further suggest the existence of a third pattern characterised by sustained IFN-I activity. In this context, persistently elevated IFN-α2 levels—used here as a proxy for broader IFN-I activity—may reflect a dysregulated and prolonged interferon response that could contribute to persistent antigenemia rather than effective viral control. This hypothesis is consistent with previous reports suggesting that sustained or maladaptive IFN-I responses may occur in critically ill patients, in whom strong interferon signatures can coexist with exaggerated inflammation without achieving efficient viral clearance.39

This study has several limitations. First, the number of patients with longitudinal samples was limited, reducing the statistical power to detect correlations between clinical or laboratory parameters and N-antigenemia kinetics. Second, technical constraints prevented us from assessing RNAemia in samples obtained from patients with mild COVID-19, limiting our ability to compare viral RNA dynamics across the full disease severity spectrum. In addition, although RT-PCR allowed detection of RNAemia, the lack of formal validation for quantitative use in whole blood led us to adopt a qualitative approach. Future studies using more sensitive and quantitative methods, such as digital droplet PCR (ddPCR), could provide a more accurate characterisation of RNAemia dynamics. Third, comparing the expression of ISGs in viral RNA-positive versus -negative cells would have strengthened the conclusions; however, no viral reads were detected despite high sequencing depth. Incorporating single-cell analyses from cells obtained from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from patients with severe COVID-19 with or without N-antigenemia could provide additional mechanistic insights.34 The present study was not designed to determine the precise mechanisms or cellular sources driving prolonged IFN-I responses in severe COVID-19. Addressing these questions would require dedicated mechanistic and cell-specific approaches, including experimental models and longitudinal tissue-level analyses. Finally, this study was not specifically designed to assess sex-related differences, and sex-disaggregated analyses were therefore not conducted beyond the inclusion of sex as a covariate in selected models.

To conclude, beyond the well-established risk of severe disease associated with anti-IFN-I autoantibodies, we suggest that viral bloodstream dissemination could be an additional mechanism contributing to COVID-19 severity in patients with an IFN-I response. This process may participate in a self-reinforcing interplay between viral dissemination, sustained IFN-I activation, and heightened pro-inflammatory responses, although the directionality of these relationships remains to be fully established. Notably, in patients without detectable anti-IFN autoantibodies, viraemia itself could emerge as an independent risk factor associated with disease severity. These data reinforce the importance of the timing and duration of IFN-I responses, with significant implications for the use of IFN-based biotherapies in patients with life-threatening COVID-19, particularly in those with high N-antigenemia levels.
