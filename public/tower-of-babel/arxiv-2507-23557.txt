Tree-indexed sums of Catalan numbers
Alin Bostan, Valentin Féray, Paul Thévenin

Abstract
. We consider a family of infinite sums of products of Catalan numbers, indexed by trees. We show that these sums are polynomials in 1 / π 1/\pi with rational coefficients; the proof is effective and provides an algorithm to explicitly compute these sums. Along the way we introduce parametric liftings of our sums, and show that they are polynomials in the complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kind. Moreover, the degrees of these polynomials are at most half of the number of vertices of the tree. The computation of these tree-indexed sums is motivated by the study of large meandric systems, which are non-crossing configurations of loops in the plane.

1.2.
Motivation: shape probabilities in the infinite noodle

Our initial interest in the sums
S(T)
comes from discrete probability theory,

.
It was introduced in
[

]
, and further studied in

]

Informally, we consider the bi-infinite discrete line

All directions are chosen independently of each other, and uniformly among

Then we connect arrows pointing to the right with arrows pointing to the left, in the unique non-crossing way
(independently above and below the line).
An example is shown in
Fig.

2

We are interested in the component of

The sums

Being able to compute these sums does not solve the above conjectures,

Informally, the shape of the component of

We refer to

Writing

1.3.
What computer algebra can (and cannot) do

For a fixed tree
T
, one can obtain different types of information about the sum
S(T)(t)
in (
1
), using various computer algebra tools. Ideally, a user equipped with a computer algebra system such as
Maple
,
Mathematica
,
SageMath
, etc.
might hope to get directly a “formula” for the power series
S(T)(t)
and for the value
S(T)(1/4)
, by simply appealing to a one-line command.

While relatively simple sums, such as those in
Section

3
, can be computed this way, the situation is far less simple in general. We now briefly explain what computer algebra can (and cannot) do, with respect to sums such as
S(T)(t)
and
S(T)(1/4)
, relying on a number of advanced functionalities for symbolic summation and integration. More detailed accounts dedicated to this topic are
[
Sch07
,
Sal19
,
KW21
]
.

The sum
S(T)(t)
in (
1
) belongs to the class of
definite multiple sums with parameters
; the parameter here is
t
. Such sums can be tackled algorithmically using the so-called “holonomic systems approach” initiated by Zeilberger in the 1990s
[
Zei90
]
. Its central algorithmic paradigm is called “creative telescoping”; it is an important and very active research area in computer algebra. The central concept is that of
D-finite functions
(power series satisfying linear differential equations with polynomial coefficients) and of
P-recursive sequences
(sequences satisfying linear recurrence equations with polynomial coefficients, or equivalently, coefficient sequences of D-finite functions), to which the recent book
[
Kau23
]
is dedicated.

In fact, the coefficient
u_{n}=u_{n}(T)
of
t^{n}
in
S(T)(t)
in (
1
) is of a very particular form: it is a
multiple binomial sum
in the sense of
[
BLS17
]
(see Footnote
3
). As such,
S(T)(t)=\sumop\displaylimits_{n\geq 0}u_{n}t^{n}
is a generating function of a very special type: it is the diagonal of a multivariate rational function
[
BLS17
, §3]
. It can be written as a multiple definite integral, and also as an algebraic residue, of some explicit multivariate rational functions. Due to these general theories, we know beforehand that the sequence
(u_{n}(T))_{n\geq 0}

is P-recursive and that the power series
S(T)(t)
is D-finite. Moreover, there are (several generations of)
creative telescoping algorithms
for computing recurrence relations satisfied by
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and linear differential equations for
S(T)(t)
, starting either from the expression (
1
), or from a diagonal or residue expression of it
[
Chy00
,
Kou10
,
BLS13
,
Lai16
]
. Creative telescoping algorithms do not always deliver the minimal-order differential equation satisfied by the integral/residue/diagonal; however, there exist efficient algorithms for performing this
minimization
step
[
BRS24
]
.

Now, due to our structural result (
Theorem

1
), we moreover know that the minimal-order linear differential
equation satisfied by
S(T)
decomposes as the least common left multiple (LCLM) of smaller-order
equations, each of them being homomorphic with symmetric powers of Gauss hypergeometric equations; the
corresponding computations (LCLM factorizations, symmetric powers, homomorphisms) can be performed
using algorithms due to van Hoeij
[
vH96
]
(and implemented for instance in the DEtools package in Maple).

All in all, the closed form expression predicted by
Theorem

1
can be computed from a basis of solutions of the minimal-order differential equation:
S(T)
is written as a polynomial in

H_{1}(t)={}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2};1;16t^{2}\big)

and

H_{2}(t)={}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2};2;16t^{2}\big)
.
Finally
S(T)(1/4)
is deduced as an explicit polynomial in
1/\pi
using the classical identities (
4
),

H_{1}(1/4)=4/\pi

and

H_{2}(1/4)=8/(3\pi)
.
Below, we illustrate this algorithmic chain on a non-trivial example. A similar type of
hypergeometric expressions for generating functions appeared in a different combinatorial context
(for counting lattice walks in the quarter plane) in
[
BCvH+17
]
, where tools similar to
the ones described here were applied.

Note that our
Theorem

1
was crucially used in the algorithmic chain sketched above. Without it, it would be by no means clear how to solve in explicit terms the minimal linear differential equation for
S(T)
.
Finally, we insist on the fact that the algorithm sketched above only works for a fixed tree
T
(it does not prove
Theorem

1
), and that its (time and space) complexity depends on the size of the tree
T

Remark 5

A variant of the algorithm described above, which is more efficient in practice, is based on our structural result (
Theorem

1
) and finds the representation of
S(T)
as an element in the algebra
\mathcal{A}
by using the degree bounds and Hermite-Padé approximation algorithms
[
BL94
]
, before specializing the result at
t=1/4
.

Another route, more symbolic-numeric, to find an expression of
S(T)(1/4)
would be the following.
There exist algorithms able to evaluate efficiently a D-finite power series (here,
S(T)
) at an
algebraic point (here,
t=1/4
); the result is however not given by a formula, but rather by an
approximation of the value (here,
S(T)(1/4)
) whose computed digits are guaranteed to be correct
up to the required precision
[
MS10
,
Mez10
]
. This approach enables approximating the
value
S(T)(1/4)
with high precision, typically hundreds or thousands of decimal digits. In many
cases, this is sufficient for algorithms performing constant recognition (based on integer
relation algorithms such as PSLQ
[
FBA99
,
BB01
]
and LLL
[
LLL82
,
NSV11
]
) to identify
the value.
But here the user should be aware of two important aspects regarding this powerful symbolic-numeric
approach: first, even with our
Theorem

1
at hand, constant recognition algorithms need
quite a high numerical precision as input; second, even when they are successful, their output
(here, the expression of
S(T)(1/4)
as a polynomial in
1/\pi
) is only
guessed
, and

not proved
.

1.4.
Notation and terminology

In the whole paper, we will always consider rooted trees, although our sums
S(T)
are a priori defined for unrooted trees, and only depend on isomorphism classes of such unrooted trees.
The root of the tree will always be denoted by
\rho
. The height of a vertex
v\in T
is defined as
h(v)\coloneqq d_{T}(\rho,v)
, where
d_{T}
denotes the usual graph distance on
T
(all edges have length
1
). For
v,w\in T
, denote by
\llbracket v,w\rrbracket
the unique geodesic in
T
between the vertices
v
and
w
. We say that
v
is an ancestor of
w
if
v\in\llbracket\rho,w\rrbracket
. If this holds, we also say that
w
is a descendant of
v
. We define the
fringe subtree
rooted at a vertex
v
as the tree formed by
v
and all its descendants, keeping all edges existing in
T
between these vertices.

2.
Shape probabilities in the infinite noodle

2.1.
The formula from
[
FT23
]

By definition, a meander
M
of size
k
is a simple curve in the plane intersecting the real line
exactly at points
0,\ldots,2k-1
, with simple intersection points.
Meanders are always considered up to continuous deformation fixing the horizontal axis.
Combinatorially, a meander can be represented
as a pair of non-crossing pair-partitions, being drawn respectively above and below the real line
and chosen such that the resulting graph consists of a single loop, see
Fig.

3
.

Given a meander
M
, we consider its union with the real line;
this divides the plane in regions, two unbounded ones and several bounded ones.
We call the bounded regions
faces
of the meander,
and denote their set by
\mathcal{F}(M)
.
Some faces are
interior faces
, in the sense that they are contained in the interior
of the simple curve defining the meander, the others are called
exterior faces
.
A face
F
in
\mathcal{F}(M)
is incident to a number of segments
[i;i+1]
;
we let
I(F)
be the set of indices
i
such that
F
is incident to
[i;i+1]
.
It is easy to see that
if
F
is an interior face (resp. exterior face) of the meander, then
I(F)

consists only of even (resp. odd) integers.
A meander with ten faces and some of the corresponding index sets
are shown on
Fig.

3
.

Figure 3.
A meander of size
k=5
and its faces.
Faces
F_{1}
to
F_{6}
are interior faces, while faces
F_{7}
to
F_{10}
are exterior faces.
In this example, we have

I(F_{3})=\{2,8\}
,
I(F_{4})=\{4,6,8\}
,
I(F_{8})=\{3,5,7\}
.

With this notation, we have the following statement (see
[
FT23
, Proposition 10]
), where
S_{0}
denotes the shape of the component of
0
in the infinite noodle.

Proposition 7
.

For any meander
M
of size
k
, we have

(8)

\mathbb{P}(S_{0}=M)=2^{-4k+1}k\,\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell_{0},\dots,\ell_{2k-2}\geq 0}\left(\prodop\displaylimits_{F\!\in\ \mathcal{F}(M)}\Cat_{\ell_{I(F)}}4^{-\ell_{I(F)}}\right),

where, for a set
I
of indices, we use the notation
\ell_{I}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i\in I}\ell_{i}
.

2.2.
Meanders and trees

Let
M
be a meander. We will associate a forest
G_{M}
with
M
.
To this end, put a vertex
v_{F}
in each face
F
of
M
. Then we connect vertices corresponding to faces sharing
a segment
[i;i+1]
as boundary, i.e.
v_{F}
is connected to
v_{F^{\prime}}
if
F
and
F^{\prime}
are connected.
Additionally, we add a half-edge to vertices sharing a segment
[i;i+1]
with an unbounded region.
We call
G_{M}
the resulting graph,
V(G_{M})
and
E(G_{M})
being respectively its vertex- and edge-sets.
Since a meander is made of a single curve,
G_{M}
does not have cycles,
i.e. is a forest.
More precisely, the vertices associated with interior faces form a single tree without half-edge,
while vertices associated with exterior faces might form one or several trees, each with exactly one half-edge.
An example is shown on
Fig.

4
.

Figure 4.
The forest associated with the meander of
Fig.

3
.
On the left, we show the forest with dashed edges superimposed on the meander,
on the right, the same forest is drawn as an abstract graph.
Vertices corresponding to interior faces are red full squares, while those corresponding
to exterior faces are blue empty squares.

The sum in (
8
) is naturally written in terms of the forest
G_{M}
.
Indeed edges of the forest are in one-to-one correspondence with the segments
[i;i+1]
(for
0\leq i\leq 2k-2
),
so that we have one variable
x_{e}
for each edge in the tree.
Also, vertices of the forest correspond to faces of the meander, so we have one (renormalized) Catalan factor for
each vertex of the forest.
Each of these Catalan factors is indexed by the sum of variables corresponding to edges incident to that vertex.
In formula,

(9)

\mathbb{P}(S_{0}=M)=2^{-4k+1}k\,\sumop\displaylimits_{(x_{e})\in\mathbb R_{+}^{E(G_{M})}}\left(\prodop\displaylimits_{v\!\in\ V(G_{M})}\Cat_{X_{v}}4^{-X_{v}}\right),

where, for each
v
in
V(G_{M})
, we set
X_{v}=\sumop\displaylimits_{e:\,v\in e}x_{e}
.
We note that the sum factorizes over connected components.
Namely, if
G_{M}=\biguplusop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{d}T_{i}
, we have

(10)

\mathbb{P}(S_{0}=M)=2^{-4k+1}k\,\prodop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{d}\left[\sumop\displaylimits_{(x_{e})\in\mathbb R_{+}^{E(T_{i})}}\left(\prodop\displaylimits_{v\!\in\ V(T_{i})}\Cat_{X_{v}}4^{-X_{v}}\right)\right]=2^{-4k+1}k\,\prodop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{d}S(T_{i})\big(\tfrac{1}{4}\big).

Thus the computation of
\mathbb{P}(S_{0}=M)
can be reduced to that of
S(T)\big(\tfrac{1}{4}\big)
,
as claimed in the introduction.

3.
Some first examples

In this section, we explain how to compute the sum
S(T)
in (
1
) for small trees
T
.
This serves as an introduction of some basic manipulations used in the proof of
Theorem

1
.

3.1.
The path with two vertices

As in the introduction, we denote by
P_{2}
the tree with two vertices connected by an edge.
The associated sum is

\displaystyle S(P_{2})=\sumop\displaylimits_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\left(\Cat_{x}t^{x}\right)^{2}.

Setting
u_{n}=4t^{2n}\Cat_{n-1}^{2}
for
n\geq 1
and
u_{0}=1
, it holds that, for all
n\geq 0
,

16t^{2}(n-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}\,u_{n}=(n+1)^{2}u_{n+1}.

This recursion implies that

\sumop\displaylimits_{n\geq 0}u_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{n\geq 0}(16t^{2})^{n}\frac{\big((-\tfrac{1}{2})^{\frac{n}{\,}}\big)^{2}}{(n!)^{2}}={}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2};1;16t^{2}\big),

where we recall that
{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}

is the hypergeometric function defined in (
2
).
We thus get that

\displaystyle S(P_{2})=\frac{1}{4t^{2}}\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{n\geq 1}u_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{4t^{2}}\left(-1+{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2};1;16t^{2}\big)\right).

The evaluation at
t=1/4
is given by the Gauss summation identity (
4
), which yields

{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2};1;1\big)=\frac{\Gamma(1)\,\Gamma(2)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{3}{2})^{2}}=\frac{4}{\pi},

and, therefore,

S(P_{2})(1/4)=\frac{1}{4\cdot(1/4)^{2}}\left(-1+{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2};1;1\big)\right)=4\left(\frac{4}{\pi}-1\right)=\frac{16}{\pi}-4.

This computation, or to be more precise, a generalization of it which can be treated
with the same method (see
Section

5.2
),
will serve as base case in the proof of our main theorem.

3.2.
The path with two vertices, and an extra half-edge

We now consider a tree with two vertices connected by an edge, and an extra half-edge on one of them.
As in the introduction, we denote this tree by
P^{h}_{2}
. The associated sum is

\displaystyle S(P^{h}_{2})=\sumop\displaylimits_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\Cat_{x_{1}}t^{x_{1}}\Cat_{x_{1}+x_{2}}t^{x_{1}+x_{2}}.

We perform a change of variable and set
m=x_{1}
and
\ell=x_{1}+x_{2}
.
Note that
m
and
\ell
are nonnegative integers,
and that we necessarily have
\ell\geq m
.
We get

S(P^{h}_{2})=\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell,m\geq 0}\Cat_{\ell}\Cat_{m}t^{\ell+m}\bm{1}[\ell\geq m].

The idea is now to use that

(11)

\bm{1}[\ell\geq m]+\bm{1}[m\geq\ell]=1+\bm{1}[m=\ell].

We multiply by
\Cat_{\ell}\Cat_{m}t^{\ell+m}
and sum over
\ell,m\geq 0
.
By symmetry, both
\bm{1}[\ell\geq m]
and
\bm{1}[m\geq\ell]
yield
S(P^{h}_{2})
. We get

\displaystyle 2S(P^{h}_{2})=\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell}\Cat_{\ell}t^{\ell}\right)^{2}+\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell\geq 0}\Cat_{\ell}^{2}t^{2\ell}=\frac{1}{4t^{2}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-4t}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4t^{2}}\left(-1+{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2};1;16t^{2}\big)\right).

Specializing to
t=1/4
, we get

\displaystyle 2S(P^{h}_{2})(1/4)=4+\big(\tfrac{16}{\pi}-4\big)=\tfrac{16}{\pi}.

We conclude that
S(P^{h}_{2})(1/4)=\frac{8}{\pi}
.

Some ideas from this computation will be useful in the general case.
In particular,
we will always start by a change of variables to get a sum of products of Catalan numbers
indexed by single summation variables subject to some inequalities.
We will also make use of relations like (
11
) to
“manipulate”
inequalities in a convenient way.

3.3.
The path with three vertices

Let us now consider the path with three vertices (without half-edge),
which we denote by
P_{3}
.
The associated sum is

\displaystyle S(P_{3})=\sumop\displaylimits_{x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\Cat_{x_{1}}t^{x_{1}}\Cat_{x_{1}+x_{2}}t^{x_{1}+x_{2}}\Cat_{x_{2}}t^{x_{2}}.

We split the sum according to the value of
X=x_{1}+x_{2}
:

S(P_{3})=\sumop\displaylimits_{X\geq 0}\Cat_{X}t^{2X}\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{x_{1},x_{2}\geq 0\atop x_{1}+x_{2}=X}\Cat_{x_{1}}\Cat_{x_{2}}\right).

Using the standard quadratic recurrence relation for Catalan numbers, we recognize the inner sum to be equal to
\Cat_{X+1}
, so that

S(P_{3})=\sumop\displaylimits_{X\geq 0}\Cat_{X}\Cat_{X+1}t^{2X}.

This is a variant of the sum
S(P_{2})
, which can be handled in a similar way.
Defining
v_{n}
by
v_{n}=-2\Cat_{n-1}\Cat_{n}t^{2n}
for
n\geq 1
and
v_{0}=1
, we have the recursion

16t^{2}\big(n-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\,\big(n+\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\,v_{n}=(n+1)(n+2)\,v_{n+1},\text{ for $n\geq 0$.}

Using the definition of hypergeometric functions, we obtain

\sumop\displaylimits_{n\geq 0}v_{n}={}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2};2;16t^{2}\big),

so that

\displaystyle S(P_{3})=\frac{1}{2t^{2}}\left(1-{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2};2;16t^{2}\big)\right).

Again, the value at
t=1/4
can be determined through the Gauss summation identity (
4
):

{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2};2;1\big)=\frac{8}{3\pi},

and, consequently,

S(P_{3})(1/4)=\frac{1}{2\cdot(1/4)^{2}}\left(1-\frac{8}{3\pi}\right)=8-\frac{64}{3\pi}.

This example illustrates the use of the quadratic Catalan recurrence to reduce the number of summation indices.
This technique will also be used in the general case.
This however induces a shift in the summation indices, which prompts us
to consider more general sums than
S(T)
to be able to prove
Theorem

1

by induction. This generalization is introduced in
Section

4.2
below.

3.4.
The path with four vertices

We consider a last example, namely the path
P_{4}
with four vertices,
showcasing that, in general, we need to combine the changes of variables and inequality manipulations from
Section

3.2

with the reduction of number of variables via the quadratic Catalan recurrence from
Section

3.3
.
By definition, we have

S(P_{4})=\sumop\displaylimits_{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\Cat_{x_{1}}t^{x_{1}}\Cat_{x_{1}+x_{2}}t^{x_{1}+x_{2}}\Cat_{x_{2}+x_{3}}t^{x_{2}+x_{3}}\Cat_{x_{3}}t^{x_{3}}.

We set
\ell_{1}=x_{1}
,
m_{1}=x_{1}+x_{2}
,
\ell_{2}=x_{2}+x_{3}
and
m_{2}=x_{3}
. This defines a bijection from
\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{3}
to the set of quadruples
(\ell_{1},m_{1},\ell_{2},m_{2})
satisfying the relations

\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=m_{1}+m_{2}\text{ and }\ell_{1}\leq m_{1}.

Using
\bm{1}[\ell_{1}\geq m_{1}]+\bm{1}[m_{1}\geq\ell_{1}]=1+\bm{1}[m_{1}=\ell_{1}]
and the symmetry between
\ell
’s and
m
’s,
we get

2S(P_{4})=\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell_{1},m_{1},\ell_{2},m_{2}\atop\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=m_{1}+m_{2}}\Cat_{\ell_{1}}\Cat_{m_{1}}\Cat_{\ell_{2}}\Cat_{m_{2}}t^{\ell_{1}+m_{1}+\ell_{2}+m_{2}}\\
+\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell_{1},m_{1},\ell_{2},m_{2}\atop\ell_{1}=m_{1},\ell_{2}=m_{2}}\Cat_{\ell_{1}}\Cat_{m_{1}}\Cat_{\ell_{2}}\Cat_{m_{2}}t^{\ell_{1}+m_{1}+\ell_{2}+m_{2}}.

The second sum can be factored out and computed as follows:

\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell_{1},m_{1},\ell_{2},m_{2}\atop\ell_{1}=m_{1},\ell_{2}=m_{2}}\Cat_{\ell_{1}}\Cat_{m_{1}}\Cat_{\ell_{2}}\Cat_{m_{2}}t^{\ell_{1}+m_{1}+\ell_{2}+m_{2}}=\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell}\Cat_{\ell}^{2}t^{2\ell}\right)^{2}=\left(S(P_{2})\right)^{2}.

To compute the first sum, we split the sum according to the value
L
of

\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
and use the quadratic recurrence:

\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell_{1},m_{1},\ell_{2},m_{2}\atop\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=m_{1}+m_{2}}\Cat_{\ell_{1}}\Cat_{m_{1}}\Cat_{\ell_{2}}\Cat_{m_{2}}t^{\ell_{1}+m_{1}+\ell_{2}+m_{2}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{L\geq 0}t^{2L}\Cat_{L+1}^{2}\\
=t^{-2}\sumop\displaylimits_{L^{\prime}\geq 1}t^{2L^{\prime}}\Cat_{L^{\prime}}^{2}=t^{-2}\cdot S(P_{2})-t^{-2}.

Altogether, we get

\displaystyle S(P_{4})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(S(P_{2})\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2t^{2}}\left(S(P_{2})\right)-\frac{1}{2t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{32t^{4}}\left(-3-16t^{2}+2{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2};1;16t^{2}\big)+{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2};1;16t^{2}\big)^{2}\right).

Specializing to
t=1/4
, we get

S(P_{4})(1/4)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{16}{\pi}-4\right)^{2}+8\left(\frac{16}{\pi}-4\right)-8=-32+\frac{64}{\pi}+\frac{128}{\pi^{2}}.

4.
Changing variables and generalizing

4.1.
Vertex variables

We will rewrite the sum
S(T)
using one variable per vertex.
For this, it is convenient to work with rooted trees, i.e. trees with a distinguished vertex
\rho
,
called the root.
If
T
has a half-edge, we will always consider the extremity of the half-edge as the root
of the tree.
Otherwise, we choose arbitrarily a root in
T
.
Then, we color vertices at even (resp. odd) distance from the root
in white (resp. black). Let
V_{\circ}(T)
and
V_{\bullet}(T)
be the sets
of white and black vertices of
T
respectively.
Also, we use the notation
v^{\prime}\leq_{T}v
(or sometimes
v\geq_{T}v^{\prime}
) to say that
v^{\prime}
is a descendant of
v
,
i.e. that
v
lies on the path from
\rho
to
v^{\prime}
.
Finally, we recall that if
(x_{e})_{e\in E(T)}
are edge-weights,
we let
X_{v}=\sumop\displaylimits_{e\ni v}x_{e}
be the sum of the weights of the edges incident to
v
.

Lemma 8
.

If
T
is a tree without half-edge,
the map from
\mathbb Z_{+}^{E(T)}
to
\mathbb Z_{+}^{V(T)}

which maps
(x_{e})_{e\in E(T)}
to
(X_{v})_{v\in V(T)}
is injective and its range
is the set of
(X_{v})_{v\in V(T)}
in
\mathbb Z_{+}^{V(T)}
satisfying the following conditions

(12)

\begin{cases}\quad\bullet\quad\sumop\displaylimits_{w\in V_{\circ}(T)}X_{w}=\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T)}X_{b},\\
\quad\bullet\quad\text{for all $v\in V_{\circ}(T)$, }\sumop\displaylimits_{w\in V_{\circ}(T),w\leq_{T}v}X_{w}\geq\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T),b\leq_{T}v}X_{b},\\
\quad\bullet\quad\text{for all $v\in V_{\bullet}(T)$, }\sumop\displaylimits_{w\in V_{\circ}(T),w\leq_{T}v}X_{w}\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T),b\leq_{T}v}X_{b}.\end{cases}

The same holds for a tree with a half-edge replacing the first condition
with

\textstyle\sumop\displaylimits_{w\in V_{\circ}(T)}X_{w}\geq\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T)}X_{b}.

Proof.

We consider first the case of a tree
T
without half-edge.
Let us consider a vector
(x_{e},e\in E(T))
mapped to
(X_{v},v\in V(T))
,
i.e., for any
v
we have
X_{v}=\sumop\displaylimits_{e\ni v}x_{e}
.
Since each edge has exactly one white and one black endpoint, the first condition of (
12
) is fulfilled. Furthermore, we have that

\displaystyle\quad\bullet\quad\text{for all }v\in V_{\circ}(T),\sumop\displaylimits_{w\in V_{\circ}(T),w\leq_{T}v}X_{w}=\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T),b\leq_{T}v}X_{b}+x_{e(v)},

(13)

\displaystyle\quad\bullet\quad\text{for all }v\in V_{\bullet}(T),\sumop\displaylimits_{w\in V_{\circ}(T),w\leq_{T}v}X_{w}=\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T),b\leq_{T}v}X_{b}-x_{e(v)},

where
e(v)
is the edge between
v
and its parent in
T
(and
x_{e(\rho)}=0
for convenience).
Since all
x_{e}
’s are nonnegative, this proves the inequalities in (
12
).

Conversely, being given a family of vertex-weights
(X_{v},v\in V(T))
satisfying (
12
), it is clear that (
13
) characterize the corresponding family of edge-weights
(x_{e},e\in E(T))
.
Hence the map
(x_{e},e\in E(T))\mapsto(X_{v},v\in V(T))
is indeed injective.
Moreover, the signs in (
13
), together with the inequalities (
12
),
ensure that all edge-weights defined this way are indeed nonnegative.
This proves the lemma for trees without half-edges.

We now consider the case where
T
is a tree with a half-edge
\overline{e}
. Then, since each other edge connects a black vertex to a white vertex, we get that

\displaystyle x_{\overline{e}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{w\in V_{\circ}(T)}X_{w}-\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T)}X_{b}.

Using this identity together with the same strategy as in the case without half-edge proves the result.
∎

Since variables indexed by white and black vertices play different roles,
from now on, we denote the former by
(\ell_{w})_{w\in V_{\circ}(T)}
and the latter by

(m_{b})_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T)}
.
Then using Lemma
8
,
S(T)
can be rewritten as

(14)

S(T)=\sumop\displaylimits_{(\ell_{w}),(m_{b})\geq 0}\left(\prodop\displaylimits_{w\in V_{\circ}(T)}\Cat_{\ell_{w}}t^{\ell_{w}}\right)\left(\prodop\displaylimits_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T)}\Cat_{m_{b}}t^{m_{b}}\right)\left(\prodop\displaylimits_{v\in V(T)}\bm{1}[C_{v}]\right),

where
C_{v}
is the following condition

\begin{cases}\quad\bullet\quad\sumop\displaylimits_{w\in V_{\circ}(T)}\ell_{w}=\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T)}m_{b}&\text{ if $v=\rho$,}\\
\quad\bullet\quad\sumop\displaylimits_{w\in V_{\circ}(T),w\leq_{T}v}\ell_{w}\geq\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T),b\leq_{T}v}m_{b}&\text{ if $v\in V_{\circ}(T)$,}\\
\quad\bullet\quad\sumop\displaylimits_{w\in V_{\circ}(T),w\leq_{T}v}\ell_{w}\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{b\in V_{\bullet}(T),b\leq_{T}v}m_{b}&\text{ if $v\in V_{\bullet}(T)$.}\end{cases}

Again, if the tree
T
has a half-edge with extremity
\rho
,
then the equality in the case
v=\rho
should be replaced by an inequality.

4.2.
A generalization

We now consider a general framework,
which (strictly) contains sums associated with trees with exactly one or without half-edge,
and in addition, will be sufficiently flexible to allow one to compute the sums recursively.

Let us consider a rooted tree
T
,
where each vertex is decorated with a triple
(\varepsilon_{v},\bowtie_{v},K_{v})

living in the product space
\{-1,0,1\}\times\{=,\leq,\geq,\varnothing\}\times\mathbb Z
.
Vertices
v
with
\varepsilon_{v}=-1
(resp.
\varepsilon_{v}=0
,
\varepsilon_{v}=1
)
will be referred to as black (resp. gray, white).
We do not impose that the coloring is proper,
i.e. black vertices can be connected to black
vertices and similarly for white and gray vertices.

To define
S(T)
in this general setting, we associate with each black vertex
b
(resp. white vertex
w
) a variable
m_{b}
(resp.
\ell_{w}
). No variable is associated with a gray vertex. Furthermore, with each vertex, we associate a condition (inequality or equality)

(
C^{T}_{v}
)

\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{w\leq_{T}v\atop\varepsilon_{w}=1}\ell_{w}-\sumop\displaylimits_{b\leq_{T}v\atop\varepsilon_{b}=-1}m_{b}\right)\bowtie_{v}\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{v^{\prime}\leq_{T}v}K_{v^{\prime}}\right),

where the condition is void (and thus always satisfied) if
\bowtie_{v}=\varnothing
.
When there is no ambiguity regarding the tree under consideration,
we shall denote this condition simply by
(C_{v})
.

With this in hand, we define

(15)

S(T)=\sumop\displaylimits_{(\ell_{w}),(m_{b})}\left(\prodop\displaylimits_{w:\,\varepsilon_{w}=1}\Cat_{\ell_{w}}t^{\ell_{w}}\right)\left(\prodop\displaylimits_{b:\,\varepsilon_{b}=-1}\Cat_{m_{b}}t^{m_{b}}\right)\left(\prodop\displaylimits_{v}\bm{1}[C_{v}]\right),

where the sum is taken over tuples
(\ell_{w})
and
(m_{b})
of nonnegative integers indexed by white and black vertices of
T
, respectively.
Also, in the inner products,
w
runs over white vertices (indicated by the condition
\varepsilon_{w}=1
),

b
over black ones (indicated by the condition
\varepsilon_{b}=-1
), and
v
over all vertices.
In later discussions, the condition
\varepsilon_{w}=1
(resp.
\varepsilon_{b}=-1
) will be implicit when using the variable
w
, resp.
b
.

We make here a small abuse of notation compared to the definition of (
1
). Indeed, letting
T
be a non-decorated tree with possibly a half-edge.
We associate to
T
a rooted decorated version
T^{\prime}
of
T
as follows:

•

If
T
contains a half-edge with extremity
\rho
, then
\rho
is chosen as the root of
T^{\prime}
. Otherwise, choose an arbitrary vertex
\rho
as the root of
T^{\prime}
;

•

The root of
T^{\prime}
is white, decorated with
(=,0)
if
T
has no half-edge,
or with
(\geq,0)
in case of a tree
T
with a half-edge;

•

T^{\prime}
has only black and white vertices and they alternate;

•

all the black vertices are decorated with
(\leq,0)
and,
finally, all the white vertices are decorated with
(\geq,0)
.

With this construction, the sum
S(T)
in the sense of (
1
) corresponds to
S(T^{\prime})
in the sense of (
15
); see
Eq.

14
.
We will sometimes refer to
T^{\prime}
as the canonical coloration and decoration of
T
.

We will prove the following theorem,
containing
Theorem

1
as a special case.

Theorem 9
.

For any decorated tree
T
as above, one has
S(T)\in\mathcal{B}
.
Furthermore, if the root of
T
is decorated with an equality symbol,
then
S(T)\in\mathcal{A}
.

In both cases, denoting by
\tilde{V}_{T}
the number of vertices that are not gray (i.e. either black or white vertices) in
T
, we have
d_{S(T)}\leq\tilde{V}_{T}
.
As a consequence, for any decorated tree
T
, the evaluation

S(T)(\frac{1}{4})
is a polynomial in
1/\pi

with rational coefficients and degree at most
\lfloor\frac{\tilde{V}_{T}}{2}\rfloor
.

5.
The base case: trees with two vertices

5.1.
Preliminary: contiguous relations between hypergeometric functions

We prove here a result that will be useful in
Section

5.2
.
For any
K\geq 0
, consider the hypergeometric function

\displaystyle H^{(K)}:z\mapsto{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},K-\tfrac{1}{2};K+1;z\big).

Proposition 10
.

For all
K\geq 0
, the series
H^{(K)}(16t^{2})
is an element of
\mathcal{A}
of degree
2
.

Proof.

By definition,
t\mapsto H^{(0)}(16t^{2})
and
t\mapsto H^{(1)}(16t^{2})
are elements of
\mathcal{A}
, and their degree is 2. Hence, it suffices to show that, for all
K\geq 2
,
H^{(K)}(16t^{2})
is a linear combination of
H^{(0)}(16t^{2})
and
H^{(1)}(16t^{2})
. In fact, we will prove the following identity: for all
K\geq 0
all
z
such that
|z|<1
, we have

(16)

\frac{(K+1/2)(K+5/2)}{K+2}z\,H^{(K+2)}(z)=(K+1)(1+z)H^{(K+1)}(z)-(K+1)H^{(K)}(z).

In order to prove (
16
), we rely on the so-called contiguous relations between hypergeometric functions (see e.g.
[
AAR99
, Section
2.5
]
). We use in particular the following three relations, for all
a,b,c
:

(i)

c(1-z)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\left(a,b;c;z\right)-c\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\left(a,b-1;c;z\right)+(c-a)z\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\left(a,b;c+1;z\right)=0,

(ii)

(c-b)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\left(a,b-1;c;z\right)+\left(2b-c+(a-b)z\right)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\left(a,b;c;z\right)+b(z-1)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\left(a,b+1;c;z\right)=0,

(iii)

(c-b-1)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\left(a,b;c;z\right)+b\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\left(a,b+1;c;z\right)-(c-1)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\left(a,b;c-1;z\right)=0
.

Applying (i) to
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})=(a,b+2,c+1)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle(c+1-a)z\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+2;c+2;z)=(c+1)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+1;c+1;z)+(c+1)(z-1)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+2;c+1;z).

Applying now (ii) to
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c)=(a,b+1,c+1)
, we get

(b+1)(z-1)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+2;c+1;z)=-(c-b)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b;c+1;z)\\
-(2b+1-c+(a-b-1)z)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+1;c+1;z).

Finally, applying (iii) to
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})=(a,b,c+1)
, we get:

\displaystyle(c-b)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b;c+1;z)=b\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+1;c+1;z)-c\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b;c;z).

Using these three identities, we get

\displaystyle(c+1-a)z

\displaystyle\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+2;c+2;z)=(c+1)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+1;c+1;z)+(c+1)(z-1)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+2;c+1;z)

\displaystyle=(c+1)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+1;c+1;z)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{c+1}{b+1}\big[(c-b)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b;c+1;z)+(2b+1-c+(a-b-1)z)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+1;c+1;z)\big]

\displaystyle=(c+1)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+1;c+1;z)-\frac{c+1}{b+1}(2b+1-c+(a-b-1)z)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+1;c+1;z)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{c+1}{b+1}\big[b\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+1;c+1;z)-c\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b;c;z)\big].

This implies:

\displaystyle\frac{b+1}{c+1}(c+1-a)z\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+2;c+2;z)

\displaystyle=\left(b+1-(2b+1-c+(a-b-1)z+b\right)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+1;c+1;z)

\displaystyle\qquad-c\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b;c;z)

\displaystyle=\left(c+(b+1-a)z\right)\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b+1;c+1;z)-c\,{}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}(a,b;c;z).

In particular, setting
a=-1/2,b=K-1/2,c=K+1
, we get (
16
).
∎

5.2.
Decorated trees with two vertices

We consider here simple decorated trees
T
with two vertices, one black, one white, and where the nonroot
vertex is decorated with
(\varnothing,0)
(we will see in the next section
that this is a harmless hypothesis). The sum
S(T)
depends only on the decoration
(\bowtie,K)

of the root vertex and is given by

S(T)=S_{\bowtie,K}=\sumop\displaylimits_{a,b\geq 0}\Cat_{a}\Cat_{b}t^{a+b}\bm{1}[a\bowtie b+K]

The following proposition
will serve as the initialization case in the proof of
Theorem

1
.

Proposition 11
.

For any decoration
(\bowtie,K)
, the sum

S_{\bowtie,K}
is in
\mathcal{B}
. Moreover, if
\bowtie
is the equality symbol, then

S_{\bowtie,K}
is in
\mathcal{A}
. In all cases,
S_{\bowtie,K}
has degree at most 2.

Proof.

If
\bowtie\,=\varnothing
, the condition
a\bowtie b+K
is void (always satisfied) and

S_{\varnothing,K}=\sumop\displaylimits_{a,b\geq 0}\Cat_{a}\Cat_{b}t^{a+b}=\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{a\geq 0}\Cat_{a}t^{a}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{4t^{2}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-4t}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{4t^{2}}\left(2-4t-2\sqrt{1-4t}\right).

In particular, it lies in
\mathcal{B}
and has degree
1
.
We consider now the case where
\bowtie
is the equality symbol.
Since
S_{=,K}=S_{=,-K}
, we focus on the case
K\geq 0
,
for which we have

S_{=,K}=\sumop\displaylimits_{X\geq 0}\Cat_{X}\Cat_{X+K}t^{2X+K}.

The following argument is a slight generalization of that of
Sections

3.1
and
3.3
.
For
n\geq 1
, define
v_{n}
by
v_{n}=\alpha_{K}\Cat_{n-1}\Cat_{n+K-1}t^{2n+K-2}
, where

\alpha_{K}
will be specified later.
Then, for
n\geq 1
, we have the recursion

16t^{2}\big(n-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\,\big(n+K-\tfrac{1}{2}\big)\,v_{n}=(n+1)(n+K+1)\,v_{n+1}.

Setting
v_{0}=1
and choosing
\alpha_{K}=\frac{-4\,(2K-1)t^{2-K}}{(K+1)\Cat_{K}}
, this recursion also holds for
n=0
.
Using the definition of hypergeometric functions, we obtain

\sumop\displaylimits_{n\geq 0}v_{n}={}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},K-\tfrac{1}{2};K+1;16t^{2}\big),

so that

S_{=,K}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{K}}\sumop\displaylimits_{n\geq 1}v_{n}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{K}}\,\Big({}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},K-\tfrac{1}{2};K+1;16t^{2}\big)-1\Big).

In particular, by
Proposition

10
,
S_{=,K}
is in
\mathcal{A}
and has degree
2
. It remains to consider the sums
S_{\geq,K}
and
S_{\leq,K}
. Since one has
S_{\leq,K}=S_{\geq,-K}
, we only consider the former. For
K=0
, the sum
S_{\geq,0}
coincides with the quantity
S(P_{2}^{h})
computed in Section
3.2
. The formula obtained there shows that, indeed,

S_{\geq,0}
is in
\mathcal{B}
and has degree
2
.
Furthermore, for
K\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle S_{\geq,K}=S_{\geq,0}-\sumop\displaylimits_{r=0}^{K-1}S_{=,r}\ \text{and}\ S_{\geq,-K}=S_{\geq,0}+\sumop\displaylimits_{r=1}^{K}S_{=,-r}.

Hence for all
K
in
\mathbb{Z}
, the sum
S_{\geq,K}
is in
\mathcal{B}
and has degree at most
2
, concluding the proof.
∎

7.
Further examples

We present here further examples,
using now the tools from
Section

6
.
In the first two examples (Sections

and

we are able to express the sum associated to the tree of interest in terms

The example of

we will need to consider a whole family of trees simultaneously,
and compute the associated sums as solutions of a linear system.
This is a special case of more general relations which will be presented in Section
8

In all these examples, we do not aim at
computing explicitly the sum
S(T)
, but at proving that
S(T)
belongs to
\mathcal{A}

(we only consider here trees decorated by an equality symbol at the root).

7.1.
A decorated path with four vertices

We consider the tree of
Fig.

5
, left.
This is a decorated variant of the path with four vertices that we denote
P_{4}^{+1}
.
We will see in the next section that this term appears in the recursive decomposition of
S(P_{5})

(
P_{5}
being the path with five vertices), and thus deserves our attention.
By definition, we have

S(P_{4}^{+1})=\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m_{1},m_{2}\\
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=m_{1}+m_{2}+1\end{subarray}}\Cat_{\ell_{1}}\Cat_{\ell_{2}}\Cat_{m_{1}}\Cat_{m_{2}}t^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+m_{1}+m_{2}}\bm{1}[\ell_{1}\leq m_{1}].

We could, as in
Section

3.4
,
express

in terms of similar expressions with
\ell_{1}\leq m_{1}

replaced by no condition/an equality/the reverse inequality
\ell_{1}\geq m_{1}
.
However, the shift by

Figure 5.
Examples of trees considered in
Section

7

Right: a

The idea is to introduce artificially comparisons between

Locally in this section, for
\bowtie_{1},\bowtie_{2}

Combining the identities

\bm{1}[\ell_{i}\geq m_{i}]=1+\bm{1}[\ell_{i}=m_{i}]-\bm{1}[\ell_{i}\leq m_{i}]

\mathcal{A}

All terms involving an equality symbol factorize trivially (recall Lemma

can be proved to be in

. The quantity

is equal to
0
,
since there is no tuples

with

\ell_{i}\leq m_{i}
for

in
\{1,2\}
(empty sum).
For

\displaystyle A[\varnothing,\varnothing]

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},m_{1},m_{2}\atop\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=m_{1}+m_{2}+1}\Cat_{\ell_{1}}\Cat_{\ell_{2}}\Cat_{m_{1}}\Cat_{m_{2}}t^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+m_{1}+m_{2}}

with the notation

\mathcal{A}
(

To sum up, we have proved that all terms in

\mathcal{A}

9

An interesting feature in this example is that we did not simply start form

and apply
reduction operations from the previous section, but

appears as

7.2.
The path with five vertices

We consider here the path

with five vertices. Rooting it in its central vertex,

by no condition,
\ell_{1}=m_{1}
and

successively
(the last one appearing with a minus sign).

and

7.3.
A tree with six vertices

We consider here the tree
T_{6}
from
Fig.

5
, right.
Such trees will be referred to as long stars, as they have a central vertex, to which branches of length at most 2 are attached, and will be considered more in detail in

We have

S(T_{6})=\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3},m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}\\

the above sum, where the condition

is the number of inequalities in the direction

, and

.

and

B_{2,0}+B_{1,1}\cong B_{1,0},\quad B_{1,1}+B_{0,2}\cong B_{0,1}.

Let

with a special non-decorated leaf, which we call blossom.
We say that

is trivial if it is reduced to that single blossom.
Given a decorated tree

, we write

for the tree
obtained by grafting

on

,
i.e. replacing the blossom of

by

.
The following families of trees will be of particular interest in this section: let

U^{\Delta}_{i,j,k}=\hskip-8.53581pt\begin{array}[]{c}\includegraphics[]{Long_Stars_White}\end{array},\quad\overline{U}^{\Delta}_{i,j,k}=\hskip-8.53581pt\begin{array}[]{c}\includegraphics[]{Long_Stars_Black}\end{array},\quad V^{\Delta}_{i,j,k}=\hskip-8.53581pt\begin{array}[]{c}\includegraphics[]{Long_Stars_Gray}\end{array}.

\varnothing_{0}
. We call all these trees

.
As above,
\Delta
represents a generic pair in
\{=,\leq,\geq,\varnothing\}\times\mathbb Z
.
We note that these trees have many equivalent forms (equivalent in the sense that
the associated sums are the same).
In particular, switching the color of a black leaf and of its white parent
does not change the sum (as it does not change the set of summation variables,
nor the conditions).
Observe that, for any
k\geq 1
, in a tree

The

along with Lemma

S\big(R|U^{\Delta}_{i,j,k}\big)=t^{-1}S\big(R|V^{\Delta+1}_{i,j,k}\big)-t^{-1}S\big(R|\overline{U}^{\Delta+1}_{i,j,k-1}\big).

Appendix A
Algebraic independence of the generators

We prove in an elementary way the algebraic independence of
H_{1}(t)
and
H_{2}(t)
, or, equivalently, of the complete elliptic integrals of first and second kinds,
K(t)
and
E(t)
.
It may well be that this result is classical; however we could not locate it in the extremely rich literature on special functions.
Our proof is based on asymptotic arguments and on the transcendence of
\pi
.
(In the footnote
2
we sketched another proof relying on a much harder result in transcendence theory.)

We consider the functions

f_{1}(t)\coloneqq\frac{2K(\sqrt{t})}{\pi}={}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2};1;t\big),\qquad f_{2}(t)\coloneqq\frac{2E(\sqrt{t})}{\pi}={}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2};1;t\big).

We first study the asymptotic behavior of the coefficients of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
,
and the behavior of
f_{1}(t)
and
f_{2}(t)
near
t=1
.
We observe via Stirling’s formula that
f_{1}(t)
expands as

f_{1}(t)=\sumop\displaylimits_{n\geq 0}c_{n,1}t^{n}
with
c_{n,1}=\binom{2n}{n}\cdot 16^{-n}\sim 1/(\pi n)
.
This yields the estimate
f_{1}(t)\sim|\log(1-t)|/\pi
as
t
tends to
1
from below.
On the other hand, the Gauss summation formula (
4
) gives

f_{2}(1)=\frac{\Gamma(1)^{2}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2})\Gamma(\tfrac{3}{2})}=\frac{2}{\pi}.

Now, for the sake of contradiction, we assume that there exists a nonzero polynomial with rational coefficients such that
P(t,f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t))=0
.
We expand
P(t,x,y)
in the form
\sumop\displaylimits_{i,j\geq 0}(t-1)^{i}x^{j}P_{i,j}(y)
,
and consider the nonzero term
(t-1)^{i_{0}}x^{j_{0}}P_{i_{0},j_{0}}(y)

which is asymptotically the largest around
t=1
after substituting
x=f_{1}(t)
and
y=f_{2}(t)
.
Concretely,
(-i_{0},j_{0})
is the maximum of
\{(-i,j):P_{i,j}\neq 0\}

in lexicographic order.
Looking at the asymptotic behavior of
P(t,f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t))
as
t
tends to
1

from below, we have

P(t,f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t))\,=\,(P_{i_{0},j_{0}}(\tfrac{2}{\pi})+o(1))\cdot(t-1)^{i_{0}}\cdot(|\log(1-t)|^{j_{0}}/\pi^{j_{0}}).

Since this should be equal to
0
, it forces
P_{i_{0},j_{0}}(\tfrac{2}{\pi})=0
.
But
\pi
is transcendental, so that we must have
P_{i_{0},j_{0}}=0
.
We have reached a contradiction, proving the algebraic independence of
f_{1}(t)
and
f_{2}(t)
over
\mathbb Q(t)
.
By
[
ABD19
, Lemma 7.2]
(whose proof can be found in Section 88, pages 133-134, of Mahler’s book
[
Mah76
]
), they are also algebraically independent over
\mathbb C(t)
, since their coefficients lie in
\mathbb{Q}
.
Thus
E(t)
and
K(t)
are
algebraically independent over
\mathbb C(t)
,
and, using (
3
), it is also the case for
H_{1}(t)
and
H_{2}(t)
.

\square

Remark 33
.

Yet a different proof, of a more arithmetic flavor, could be based on the results of
[
ABD19
]
.
This proof still uses asymptotics, but does not rely on the transcendence of
\pi
; it uses instead properties (“à la Lucas”) modulo primes of the coefficients of

f_{1}(t)=1+4t+36t^{2}+\cdots
and of

f_{2}(t)=1-4t-12t^{2}-80t^{3}-\cdots
.
Note that while the coefficient sequence
\binom{2n}{n}^{2}
of
f_{1}(t)
is
p
-Lucas for all primes
p\in\mathcal{P}
, this property does not hold for the coefficient sequence
\binom{2n}{n}^{2}/(1-2n)
of
f_{2}(t)
, i.e.
f_{2}\notin{\mathfrak{L}(\mathcal{P})}
. Hence, one cannot conclude as in
[
ABD19
, Theorem 7.7]
. However, one can show that
f_{2}(t)
belongs to the larger set
{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})}
(that is,
f_{2}(t)/f_{2}(t^{p})\bmod p
is a rational function in
\mathbb{F}_{p}(t)
for all

), so that
[
ABD19
, Proposition 7.6]
can be applied.

.

•

Third, we observe that
S\left(V_{0,1,1}^{(=,0)}\right)=S(P_{4})
which was computed in Section
3.4
.

•

Finally, by (
22
), we get

\displaystyle S\left(V_{0,1,2}^{(=,0)}\right)=t^{-2}S\left(V_{0,1,1}^{(=,0)}\right)+t^{-2}S\left(V_{0,1,0}^{(=,0)}\right)-t^{-2}S\left(U_{0,1,0}^{(=,0)}\right)-t^{-2}S\left(\overline{U}_{0,1,0}^{(=,0)}\right).

But we have
S\left(U_{0,1,0}^{(=,0)}\right)=S(P_{3})
,
which has been computed in
Section

3.3
.
Moreover,
S\left(\overline{U}_{0,1,0}^{(=,0)}\right)=S_{=,0}
(the conditions force here the variable indexed by the root vertex to be equal to
0
), which gives us

\displaystyle S\left(V_{0,1,2}^{(=,0)}\right)=t^{-2}S\left(P_{4}\right)-t^{-2}S(P_{3}).

We can finally write:

\displaystyle S(T)

\displaystyle=S\left(V_{2,0,1}^{(=,0)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left[S\left(V_{0,1,2}^{(=,0)}\right)+2S\left(V_{0,1,1}^{(=,0)}\right)\cdot S_{=,0}+S\left(V_{0,1,0}^{(=,0)}\right)\cdot(S_{=,0})^{2}+2S\left(V_{0,3,0}^{(=,0)}\right)\right]-S\left(V_{2,0,0}^{(=,0)}\right)\cdot S_{=,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\left[t^{-2}S\left(P_{4}\right)-t^{-2}S(P_{3})+2S\left(P_{4}\right)S_{=,0}+3(S_{=,0})^{3}\right]-(S_{=,0})^{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}S\left(P_{4}\right)(t^{-2}+2\,S_{=,0})-\frac{t^{-2}}{3}S\left(P_{3}\right).

Using the expression for
S_{=,0}
,
S(P_{3})
and
S(P_{4})
given in
Sections

3.1
,
3.3
and
3.4

respectively, we get

S(T)=\frac{H_{1}^{3}+3H_{1}^{2}-\left(16t^{2}+1\right)H_{1}+32t^{2}H_{2}-(48t^{2}+3)}{192t^{6}},

where

H_{1}={}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2};1;16t^{2}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad H_{2}={}_{2}{{}{{}{F_{1}}}}\big(-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2};2;16t^{2}\big).

Using
H_{1}(1/4)=\frac{4}{\pi}
and
H_{2}(1/4)=\frac{8}{3\pi}
, this specializes to

\displaystyle S(T)(1/4)=-128-\frac{512}{9\pi}+\frac{1024}{\pi^{2}}+\frac{4096}{3\pi^{3}}.
