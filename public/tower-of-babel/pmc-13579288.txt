Benchmarking AI: comparing a fully automated spinal motion solution to a validated analyst-driven technology
Authors: Seth C. Coomer, John A. Hipp, Christopher D. Chaput, William J. Owens, Patrick Newman, Trevor F. Grieco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundManual line drawings in spinal assessments are associated with poor accuracy and reproducibility, while many AI models lack validation. A semi-automated image analysis technology, Quantitative Motion Analysis (QMA), has been used to produce radiographic measures in over 300 clinical trials of spinal treatment and shown to have sub-degree and sub-millimeter accuracy (0.47° and 0.54 mm on average). Recently, a fully automated analysis pipeline of AI models has been developed to provide equivalent accuracy and reliability of QMA in clinical workflow. The objective of this study is to present a direct comparison between motion measurements obtained by a fully automated image analysis tool, SpineCAMP, to those obtained by experienced operators using a legacy semi-automated method, QMA.MethodsAbout 120 lateral radiographs, evenly distributed across the cervical and lumbar spine regions with 30 flexion-extension exams per anatomical region, were collected from 49 different clinical sites and include preoperative and postoperative imaging. After excluding images with discrepant labeling (4/120, 3.3%), 66 cervical and 59 lumbar levels were analyzed. Each exam had segmental rotation and translation measured by 5 trained QMA analysts and SpineCAMP’s fully automated AI pipeline.ResultsSpineCAMP had consistent anatomy and image view with QMA in 96.7% of images. QMA and SpineCAMP demonstrated excellent agreement with vertebral landmark placement, (r2 = 0.9999, 0.9999 for cervical landmarks X and Y positions; r2 = 0.9996, 0.9997 for lumbar landmarks X and Y positions). Mean absolute errors between QMA and SpineCAMP for segmental rotation and translation were 0.16° and 0.09 mm in the cervical spine and 0.18° and 0.14 mm in the lumbar spine.ConclusionSpinal motion measures are commonly utilized to support clinical decisions. A fully automated, AI-driven technology can deliver strong agreement to validated analyst-driven technology, thereby offering clinicians a convenient solution for measuring spinal motion.

Background

The spine is a complex. biomechanical structure that provides balance for upright posture and enables controlled motion. Degenerative diseases or trauma can disrupt this biomechanical stability, which may compromise spinal functions and result in symptoms that need to be managed. Appropriate management requires a reliable and accurate assessment of functional impairment. Spinal segmental motion and alignment are commonly measured from lateral radiographs with standard DICOM viewer line drawing tools. Unfortunately, manual measurements have shown poor accuracy and reproducibility (inter- and intra-observer differences) associated with (1) inconsistent anatomical landmark/feature specification [1], (2) dissimilar magnification between images [2], and (3) unreliable DICOM scale factors (if reporting data in real world units) [3,4] (see Appendix A for a summary of publications that detail the performance of manual line measurements from spinal X-rays). While alternatives, like AI models, have shown promise, many AI models for the diagnostic analysis of medical imaging lack sufficient validation and assessment [5].

Quantitative Motion Analysis (QMA) (Medical Metrics, Inc.) is an established semi-automated image analysis technology that has been used to accurately and reliably obtain radiographic measures of interest in over 300 clinical trials of spinal treatments since 2005. The QMA software can be operated by highly-trained analysts to measure intervertebral motion from flexion-extension X-rays through a series of computer-assisted steps, forming a landmark pattern that defines a fixed shape and size for that vertebra. The analyst then digitally positions this landmark pattern onto the same vertebra in the other image and utilizes image registration to spatially align both images. This registration process, known as QMA Stabilization, involves alternately displaying 2 images and performing iterative operations to rotate, translate, and adjust magnification of 1 image in small increments until the vertebra remains in a constant, stationary position on the display. Using mathematical matrix transformations, the QMA software calculates the coordinates of the landmarks in the second image. The analyst repeats this process for each vertebra. The software automatically calculates and reports lengths and angles between the landmarks as well as linear and angular displacements between images (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Two X-rays (flexion and extension) are superimposed based on the L4 vertebral pattern shown. The QMA method bypasses common reproducibility errors associated with attempting to select the same anatomic landmarks in different images. The rigid-body landmark pattern is enforced by the software such that the relative distances and angles between the landmarks of a vertebra are maintained across both images. The image registration process is aided with visualization filters that help the operator to detect differences in the size, shape, and radiographic density of the anatomic features being matched. QMA allows the analyst to compensate for differences in parallax (ie, X-ray beam angle poorly aligned with anatomy of interest) by focusing on the spinous process, which is less sensitive to out-of-plane effects due to its relatively thin mediolateral dimension. Additionally, the magnification adjustments between images during image registration ensure that calibration is applied consistently across both images to obtain distance measurements in mm despite potential variability in the distances between the X-ray source, target anatomy, and detector arising during image acquisition.

QMA software has been utilized to support over 200 spine related publications and has been used as a reference measurement method in previous studies of spinal motion [6]. The performance of QMA is well documented (Table 1) and has been independently shown to have sub-degree and sub-millimeter accuracy (0.47° and 0.54 mm on average) [7], including in the presence of parallax [8]. QMA has also been shown to have substantially better observer agreement in measures of intervertebral translation and rotation than manual line drawings (ICC: 0.959–0.997 for QMA, 0.557–0.870 for manual measurements) [9]. When these measures and respective thresholds are utilized to detect clinically relevant motion abnormalities, QMA exhibited improved observer agreement compared to diagnosing abnormalities via traditional manual assessment (Kappa values of 0.17 vs. 0.77 after QMA use) [10]. However, QMA has been used exclusively for research and is not clinically viable due to rigorous analyst training, requiring over 6 months to reach expert proficiency and total analysis time that is incompatible with clinical workflows. This was commented on by Rhee et al. in a 2015 review of spinal fusion assessment methods in which they stated “Although there is evidence that using QMA in conjunction with dynamic radiographs may be superior (to the interspinous method), the need for specialized additional technology and currently lack of widespread availability make QMA less useful in clinical practice” [11].Table 1QMA accuracy and reproducibility summary.Table 1: dummy alt textStudySpine regionSample size and typeMeasurement methodsReliability metrics (rotation/translation)Key contributions/findingsZhao et al. QMA accuracy and reproducibility summary.

Recently, SpineCAMP (Medical Metrics, Inc.), a fully automated analysis pipeline of AI models and coded logic, was developed to provide clinicians with the equivalent accuracy and reliability of QMA at the point of care and in real time. SpineCAMP analyzes lateral X-rays of the cervical and lumbar spine and provides clinicians customizable reports that can characterize spinal motion in relation to published normative data. SpineCAMP was trained on a large, proprietary collection of over 120,000 lateral lumbar and over 100,000 lateral cervical images acquired from hundreds of clinics and hospitals from all over the world, spanning a wide range of patient demographics, X-ray scanners, and pathology, including many types of spinal hardware.

SpineCAMP automates the QMA methodology by using convolutional neural networks to identify the anatomy and view of each input image (eg, Cervical Flexion), standardize their appearance, localize and label the vertebral bodies, specify landmarks on the vertebral corners, spatially register each vertebra to ensure consistent positioning of rigid landmark patterns, calibrate the images to report distances in mm, calculate intervertebral metrics from the anatomical landmarks, and generate reports and stabilized visualizations to aid in clinical interpretation. This fully automated pipeline incorporates multiple quality control (QC) algorithms designed to catch and suppress erroneous inference results, and when appropriate to warn clinician users of elevated uncertainty. The purpose of this study is to benchmark a fully automated AI-driven spinal measurement system through direct comparison to a well-established reference standard. Building on the substantial equivalence testing that supported SpineCAMP's FDA 510(k) clearance against QMA as the predicate device, the authors hypothesize that the 2 systems will demonstrate strong agreement in their measurements.

Methods

Curation of imaging dataset

Pearl IRB (www.pearlirb.com) concluded this research project to be exempt according to 45 CFR 46.104(d)(4) Secondary Research Uses of Data or Specimens on January 5, 2024 IRB ID: 2024-0008. A dataset of lumbar and cervical flexion-extension exams, intentionally curated for the purpose of certifying QMA operators to perform analysis in regulated clinical trials, was utilized for this retrospective method comparison study. Best estimate of truth (BET) spinal motion measurements for the test dataset were established from the average of 5 analysts who received rigorous training and oversight by MMI biomedical engineers on the operation of QMA software for a period ranging from 6 months to 9 years of continuous, full-time employment experience. Each analyst was blinded to test data collected from other analysts. Vertebral numbering discrepancies between analysts (eg, due to ambiguous lumbosacral anatomy) were corrected as a QC step to ensure valid comparison of intervertebral measurements to establish the QMA BET reference data. The imaging in this test dataset was strictly kept separate from the AI and software development teams to prevent any potential bias during training, development, and internal testing/refinement of SpineCAMP.

The dataset comprises 120 lateral radiographs, evenly distributed across the cervical and lumbar spine regions with 30 flexion-extension exams per anatomical region. The images in this dataset were collected from 49 different clinical sites and include preoperative and postoperative imaging of male and female patients of at least 18 years of age. The images include a variety of implants (eg, fusion constructs, artificial discs, etc.). Imaging was intentionally selected to evenly span the range from low- to high-quality (eg, poor resolution, poor contrast due to under/overexposure, parallax, etc.).

Vertebral labeling and landmarking

Due to the time intensive nature associated with the QMA method and image quality challenges, not all levels were analyzed. All 5 analysts evaluated between 1 and 3 of the same intervertebral levels for each flexion-extension exam. Although SpineCAMP analysis was performed for all analyzable levels, only those in which data existed for both methods could be compared. SpineCAMP’s numbering of vertebral bodies was evaluated for consistency with QMA operator labeling (eg, potential differences due to ambiguous lumbosacral anatomy). To avoid invalid comparisons of landmark error and intervertebral measurements performed from different vertebrae between methods, labeling discrepancies were excluded from the statistical analysis, and their prevalence/nature was separately described. The analyzable sample with both QMA and SpineCAMP results was 66 cervical levels and 59 lumbar levels.

All QMA and SpineCAMP calculations are derived from specified landmarks that characterize the mid-sagittal plane of the vertebral body as described by Hipp et al. [12]. Anterior-superior, posterior-superior, anterior-inferior, and posterior-inferior vertebral body landmark coordinates specified by QMA and SpineCAMP were evaluated for correlation using linear regression analysis.

Measurement definitions

Angular motion between flexion and extension radiographs and translational motion between flexion and extension radiographs were evaluated. Angular motion is defined as the change in the angle between the superior endplate of inferior vertebra and the inferior endplate of the superior vertebra between flexion and extension. Translational motion is defined as the change in sagittal plane offset of the posterior inferior corner of the superior vertebra with respect to the posterior superior corner of the inferior vertebra (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Diagrams for intervertebral motion measurements.Fig 3: dummy alt text

Statistical analyses

The mean difference, mean absolute difference, standard deviation absolute difference, and maximum absolute difference between methods were reported. Linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate correlation between methods. Bland-Altman analyses were performed to demonstrate differences between methods. Bland-Altman limits of agreement were calculated from the mean and standard deviation of the SpineCAMP minus QMA differences. To verify that the limits were not biased by the fact that some subjects contributed multiple intervertebral levels, a sensitivity analysis was performed using a mixed-effects Bland-Altman model with a subject random intercept, with a parallel test for proportional bias by regressing the method difference on the method mean. The mixed-effects limits agreed with the primary analysis to within ±0.01° and ±0.03 mm, and any detected proportional bias was small in magnitude, indicating that within-exam clustering did not affect the agreement conclusion.

The average of each intervertebral metric across all QMA operators was utilized to represent the BET for the QMA method. Agreement between SpineCAMP and the QMA BET was determined for each measurement with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICCs were calculated using a 2-way mixed effects model for absolute agreement (ICC(3,1) absolute agreement form per Shrout and Fleiss [13]), where the “goodness” of agreement was interpreted per the guidelines of Koo et al.: poor, ICC < 0.50; fair, ICC ≥ 0.50 and < 0.75; good, ICC ≥ 0.75 and < 0.90; excellent, ICC ≥ 0.90 [14]. The ICC was reported for all intervertebral motion measurements. All statistical analyses were done using Stata (version 15.1, StatCorp LLC).

Results

Image labeling

SpineCAMP v1.1 had consistent anatomy and image view with QMA in 116/120 images (96.66%). In 2 of the images with inconsistent image views, improper flexion/extension markers were placed in the field of view at acquisition by the X-ray technologist (Fig. 4). Four lumbar spine exams were identified to have inconsistent vertebral numbering between SpineCAMP and QMA. One such case is presented in Fig. 5. Since full body imaging was not available for these cases, it was not possible to determine with certainty if QMA or SpineCAMP vertebral body numbering was “correct,” as transitional anatomies (lumbarized sacral vertebra/sacralized lumbar vertebra) are not always labeled by clinicians in the same way [15]. In each of these cases, SpineCAMP provided the following warning: “The anatomy appears to exhibit ambiguous morphology and/or poor image quality. Please review vertebral labeling.” To avoid invalid statistical comparisons, these samples were excluded from subsequent analyses. Landmark placement

Linear regression analyses document almost perfect agreement between QMA and SpineCAMP landmarks. A very strong linear correlation (slope near 1 and y-intercept near zero) was observed for both the cervical landmark X coordinates (y = 0.999x + 0.0206, r2 = 0.9999) and Y coordinates (y = 1.0002x - 0.0001, r2 = 0.9999). A strong linear correlation was also observed for both the lumbar landmark X coordinates (y = 0.9956x + 0.195, r2 = 0.9996) and Y coordinates (y = 0.9956x + 0.1364, r2 = 0.9997) (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Scatter plot with linear regression illustrates the correlation between SpineCAMP and QMA for cervical spine vertebral body landmark X (top left) and Y (top right) coordinates and lumbar spine vertebral body landmark X (bottom left) and Y (bottom right) coordinates.Fig 6: dummy alt text

Intervertebral motion

Intervertebral rotation and translation were evaluated across 66 cervical levels and 59 lumbar levels from lateral flexion-extension radiographs. Mean absolute errors for the cervical spine remained quite small, with mean absolute error of 0.16° for rotation and 0.09 mm for translation (Table 2). Mean absolute errors were small for the lumbar spine as well, with mean absolute error of 0.18° for rotation and 0.14 mm for translation (Table 3). For both the lumbar and cervical regions the ICC score of 1.00 was achieved for rotation, reflecting perfect agreement. The ICC for translation was also excellent at 0.99 for cervical and 0.99 for lumbar. The variance of the errors associated with rotation and translation between the 2 measurement techniques are illustrated using Bland-Altman plots (Fig. 7, Fig. 8). In the Bland-Altman plots, the limits of agreement (LoA) for cervical angular motion measurements were −0.40° to 0.49°, while LoA for cervical translational motion measurements were −0.29 to 0.29 mm. The LoA for lumbar rotational motion measurements were −0.42° to 0.49°, while LoA for lumbar translational motion measurements were −0.37 to 0.30 mm. Discussion

Millions of radiographs are acquired annually in the United States to assist in the diagnosis and assessment of spine related symptoms, but the manner in which these radiographs are utilized and interpreted differs by clinician and geographical region [16]. The metrics utilized to measure spinal motion is a common divergence between diagnostic radiologists and referring physicians [17]. Harmonizing interpretation of spine radiographs across disciplines and eliminating physician bias may be enabled in part by fully-automated image analysis technologies that are standardized, objective, accurate, and validated. Historically, clinicians have not had access to such measurement tools. As seen in Table 1, QMA has been independently validated across multiple studies and extensively published in peer-reviewed journals as the method for assessing dynamic intervertebral motion from flexion-extension radiographs. Its well-established reliability and reproducibility make it an ideal benchmark for evaluating the performance of emerging AI-based radiographic analysis tools for measuring intervertebral motion. This study demonstrates very strong and highly consistent agreement between SpineCAMP and QMA.

Spine care has evolved significantly over the past 20 years. The introduction and advancement of enabling technologies into both nonoperative and operative workflow span a spectrum of sensors, instruments, implants, wearables, and software. Despite these advancements, the assessment of radiographs, especially when used for functional assessment, remains largely unchanged, and manual measurement by clinicians with digital calipers remains the standard. As presented in Appendix A, the measurement tolerance of conventional line drawing methods is significant, with most sources reporting above 2° mean absolute error for intervertebral rotation and some reporting measurement error as high as 7° [9,[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Although there are no universally defined clinical thresholds for acceptable radiographic measurement accuracy, measurement error should be small relative to the clinical effect of interest. Consequently, acceptable accuracy varies by application. Nonetheless, emerging measurement systems such as SpineCAMP are generally expected to match or exceed the performance of established clinical or research standards, such as conventional line drawing methods. Measurement inaccuracy can drive false positives and false negatives when diagnoses depend on or involve comparing radiographic measurements to specific thresholds. These incorrect diagnoses may influence clinical decisions and can negatively impact patient care. For example, the diagnosis of pseudarthrosis is often determined by the presence of intervertebral motion above a certain threshold at the fused level [[22], [23], [24]]. Another use for this data is in the assessment of spinal instability. Although the criteria used to diagnose instability varies by stakeholder and use case, it is most commonly defined by some degree of angular and/or translational motion that is abnormal with respect to reference data [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. This suggests inaccuracies in intervertebral motion measurements could lead to improper diagnoses of spinal instability. False positives and false negatives may lead to unnecessary surgeries or improperly influence the decisions of referring clinicians with respect to the need for assessment by a spine specialist [29]. When compared to QMA for intervertebral motion measurements obtained from X-rays, SpineCAMP produces accurate and consistent measurements with as little as 1/20th the error, on average, of conventional methods.

Despite the importance of these measurements, accurate measurements between dynamic images have often proven challenging for clinicians due to numerous ways error can be introduced. Parallax effects and magnification errors are commonly pointed to by skeptics of the use of flexion-extension exams to assess for spinal instability, as they are difficult to account for when making manual measurements [2,29]. However, computerized measurement systems like SpineCAMP allow for “image stabilization,” the registration of vertebral bodies between images. This display technique positions a vertebra in the same location across registered images to enhance clinician interpretation by visualizing motion of adjacent structures relative to the stabilized vertebra (see supplemental material). SpineCAMP’s image registration methodology also helps to overcome parallax and variable magnification, which are common sources of measurement error in X-rays [6,30]. This allows the system to perform in the presence of poor image quality and a variety of implants, as shown in this study. However, as with any image processing system, the quality of outputs depends on the quality of inputs. Suboptimal imaging characterized by limited field of view, contrast, occluded anatomy, gross out of plane, etc. can limit the accuracy of reported measurements. To this extent, high quality image acquisition protocol is necessary to maximize the diagnostic utility of flexion-extension radiographs, regardless of measurement technique.

Although this study was carefully controlled, several limitations should be acknowledged. Clinical validation of SpineCAMP's impact on workflow, clinical decision-making, patient outcomes, and healthcare economics is beyond the scope of this study and will be addressed in future research. The sample size of the dataset included only 60 flexion-extension exams, and QMA analysis was not performed at every level in the exam, thereby limiting the ability to stratify performance by level. While QMA has demonstrated strong accuracy and reliability, SpineCAMP was not evaluated against an independent physical reference standard or against conventional measurements made by radiologists for this dataset. Although the imaging was sourced from 49 different clinical sites and included a variety of spinal hardware, specific patient demographic information for the imaging used in this study was blinded, so it is not known whether this dataset is representative of the global population. The imaging dataset was also curated, rather than randomly sampled from clinical practices, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additional studies with larger sample sizes would be useful to confirm the results of this investigation and to address some of the concerns about generalizability of these results across a more diverse global population. While the most commonly used motion metrics were assessed, there are many spinal measurements utilized by clinicians that were not analyzed in this study. Agreement among other measurements may not demonstrate the same level of consistency. Finally, the current study only addresses motion measurements made from C2–C7 and L1–S1 in lateral X-rays. While this aligns with the FDA clearance of SpineCAMP, many clinicians utilize measurements outside of this scope.

Conclusion

SpineCAMP measurements demonstrated strong agreement with QMA, a validated analyst-driven technology for spinal motion assessment. These findings provide evidence supporting the measurement performance of a clinically available, automated spinal motion assessment tool compared against an established reference standard. Future studies will evaluate device performance in clinical workflows across diverse patient populations and therapeutic indications.
