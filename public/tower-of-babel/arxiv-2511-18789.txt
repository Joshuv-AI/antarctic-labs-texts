Doubly Wild Refitting: Model-Free Evaluation of High Dimensional Black-Box Predictions under Convex Losses
Haichen Hu, David Simchi-Levi

Abstract
We study the problem of excess risk evaluation for empirical risk minimization (ERM) under general convex loss functions. Our contribution is an efficient refitting procedure that computes the excess risk and provides high-probability upper bounds under the fixed-design setting. Assuming only black-box access to the training algorithm and a single dataset, we begin by generating two sets of artificially modified pseudo-outcomes—termed wild responses—created by stochastically perturbing the gradient vectors with carefully chosen scaling. Using these two pseudo-labeled datasets, we then refit the black-box procedure twice to obtain two corresponding wild predictors. Finally, leveraging the original predictor, the two wild predictors, and the constructed wild responses, we derive an efficient excess-risk upper bound. A key feature of our analysis is that it requires no prior knowledge of the complexity of the underlying function class. As a result, the method is essentially model-free and holds significant promise for theoretically evaluating modern opaque machine learning systems—such as deep neural networks and generative models—where traditional capacity-based learning theory becomes infeasible due to the extreme complexity of the hypothesis class. Key words: Statistical Machine Learning, Artificial Intelligence, Wild Refitting

Related Works

Our work is closely connected to two streams of research: statistical machine learning and the emerging paradigm of wild refitting.

Statistical learning

(
Vapnik 2013
)
has long served as a building block in the theoretical analysis of machine learning algorithms, and the most popular form of machine learning algorithms is Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)
(
Vapnik 1991
)
. A central and active line in statistical learning focuses on understanding the
excess risk
or generalization error of learning algorithms. Classical approaches rely on empirical process theory, with complexity measures such as the VC dimension
(
Vapnik and Chervonenkis 2015
;
Floyd and Warmuth 1995
)
, covering numbers
(
van de Geer 2000
)
, Rademacher complexity
(
Massart 2007
;
Bartlett et al. 2005
)
, and eigendecay rate
(
Goel and Klivans 2017
;
Hu et al. 2025
)
. Despite these advances, bounding these metrics fundamentally depends on the structure of the underlying model class. When the hypothesis space is extremely rich, these methods break down and fail to yield meaningful excess risk guarantees. In contrast, our approach is model-free and bypasses these structural limitations.

Wild refitting
is a recently proposed framework for bounding the excess risk of black-box machine learning algorithms. The central idea is to take the trained model and the original dataset, construct pseudo-responses by perturbing the model’s predicted values, and treat the resulting pseudo-labeled outcomes as new valid data points. A second predictor is then retrained on this artificial dataset. Finally, it uses information extracted from the pair of predictors to upper bound the excess risk.

Wainwright 2025
introduced this approach in the setting of scalar-valued regression with symmetric noise. Subsequently,
Hu and Simchi-Levi 2025
extended wild refitting to scalar-valued empirical risk minimization (ERM) under general Bregman losses, still assuming symmetric noise.
In this paper, we investigate wild refitting for ERM with high-dimensional predictors under general convex loss functions and non-symmetric noise. Under asymmetry, a single retraining step is no longer sufficient; instead, we construct two pseudo-labeled datasets and perform two separate refitting steps to obtain a valid excess risk bound.

3
Model: ERM with Convex Losses

In this section, we formally introduce the
Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)
problem that serves as the foundational model of our study.
We assume basic familiarity with functional analysis and variational analysis; a detailed mathematical background is provided in Appendix
A
.
Specifically, we consider a general ERM setting with an input space
\mathcal{X}
and an output (or prediction) space
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
A predictor is defined as a mapping
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, and the dataset is denoted by
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

In the
fixed-design
setting, the covariates
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are treated as fixed, and
y_{i}
is sampled independently from some unknown conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}(\cdot|x_{i})
.
whereas in the
random-design
setting, both
(x_{i},y_{i})
are random.
In this paper,
we focus on fixed-design exclusively
.
The population-level objective in fixed-design is given by

f^{*}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{y_{i}}[\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i})\mid x_{i}]\right\}.

The empirical counterpart, corresponding to the ERM estimator, is defined as

\widehat{f}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i})\right\},

where
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,+\infty)
denotes the loss function,
and
\mathcal{F}
represents the function class over which optimization is performed.
Throughout, we assume that
\mathcal{F}
is a convex Banach function space. To facilitate our theoretical analysis, we impose the following assumption on the loss function.

Assumption 3.1
.

The loss function
\ell
has the following properties:

•

Given any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, the function
\ell(z,y)
is
\beta
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex with respect to
z
.

•

\forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
-\nabla_{1}\ell(z,y)
is continuous and coercive with respect to
y
,
\lim_{\|y\|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\nabla_{1}\ell(z,y)^{T}y}{\|y\|_{2}}=-\infty
.

•

\forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
-\nabla_{1}\ell(z,y)
is monotone with respect to
y
, i.e.,
\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(z,y_{1})-\nabla_{1}\ell(z,y_{2}),y_{1}-y_{2}\right\rangle\leq 0
.

This assumption is satisfied by a broad class of commonly used loss functions, including the squared loss, the cross entropy loss with clipping, and most
L_{2}
regularized convex losses.

Example 3.2
.

The squared loss
\ell(u,y)=\|u-y\|_{2}^{2}
satisfies Assumption
3.1
.

Example 3.3
.

For exponential family with parameter
\theta
,
p(y|\theta)=h(y)\exp\left\{\left\langle\theta,T(y)\right\rangle-A(\theta)\right\}
, where
T(y)
is sufficient statistics and
A(\theta)=\log\int h(y)\exp\left\{\left\langle\theta,T(y)\right\rangle\right\}dy
is the normalization factor. Therefore,
-\log(p(y|\theta))=-\log h(y)-\left\langle\theta,T(y)\right\rangle+A(\theta)
. The regularized negative log-likelohood loss function as
\ell(z,y)=A(z)-z^{T}y+\frac{\mu}{2}\|z\|_{2}^{2}
satisfies Assumption
3.1
.

Example 3.4
.

In
L_{p}
regression with bounded region, the loss function
\ell(z,y)=\frac{1}{p}\|z-y\|_{p}^{p}
,
p\geq 2
satisfies Assumption
3.1
.

Now we provide the properties of the predictors
\widehat{f}
and
f^{*}
. First, by the first order optimality condition in variational analysis
(
Rockafellar and Wets 1998
)
, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 3.5
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
f^{*}(x)
minimizes the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{y}[\ell(f(x),y)|x]
. Then, the directional Gâteaux derivative at any measurable function is zero, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y}[\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x),y),h(x)\right\rangle|x]=0\Rightarrow\mathbb{E}_{y}[\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x),y)|x]=\mathbf{0}.

(3.1)

Therefore, given any
x
,
\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x),y)
is a centered random variable. Naturally, in this paper, we assume the following sub-Gaussian tail property about it.

Assumption 3.6
.

Let
g(x):=\nabla_{1}\ell\big(f^{\ast}(x),y\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
consider the case when
\{\|g(x)\|>0\}
.
Fix
x
,
we assume that
g(x)
is
conditionally zero-mean and
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian along directions
, i.e., for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, and any unit vector
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

\mathbb{E}[g(x)|\left\langle g(x),e\right\rangle=\pm||g(x)||]=\mathbf{0},

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\Big\{\lambda\big(\left\langle g(x),e\right\rangle-m_{e}(x)\big)\Big\}\,\Big|\,x,\ |\left\langle g(x),e\right\rangle|=\|g(x)\|\right]\ \leq\ \exp\!\Big(\tfrac{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}\Big),

where
m_{e}(x):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\langle g(x),e\right\rangle\,\middle|\,x,e\right]
. On the event
\{\|g(x)\|=0\}
, the condition is vacuous. We use
\varphi_{x,e}
to denote the corresponding conditional probability distribution.

Intuitively, Assumption
3.6
states that if we take the random vector
\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x),y)

and “slice” its probability density along any given direction, the resulting one-dimensional conditional distribution is centered and sub-Gaussian. In other words, every directional projection of the noise behaves like a zero-mean sub-Gaussian variable. This condition is mild and is satisfied by many classical distributions, including the multivariate normal distribution and multivariate

t
-distributions.

At the same time, since
\widehat{f}
minimizes the empirical risk, again by computing the first order condition about the Gâteaux derivative, we have the following proposition similar to Proposition
3.5
.

Proposition 3.7
.

For the empirical risk minimization procedure, given
\mathcal{F}
is convex, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}),f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle=0,\ \forall f\in\mathcal{F}.

(3.2)

We are now ready to elaborate on the excess risk, which serves as the performance metric of any algorithm in statistical learning. In the fixed design setting, the
excess risk
is defined as

\mathcal{E}_{fix}(\widehat{f}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}_{i}}[\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y^{\prime}_{i})-\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y^{\prime}_{i})],

where
\left\{y_{i}^{\prime}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
are new test data points that are independent of
\widehat{f}
. Under the ERM procedure, we can also define the
empirical excess risk
as

\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{f}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i}).

Now, to compare the difference between
\mathcal{E}_{fix}(\widehat{f})
and
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{f})
, we first use the Bregman representation of any convex function to get that

\displaystyle\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})=\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})+\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i}),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})\right\rangle+D_{\ell(\cdot,y_{i})}(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),f^{*}(x_{i})).

Given this equation, Proposition
3.5
, and Proposition
3.7
, we can upper bound the the true excess risk
\mathcal{E}_{fix}(\widehat{f})
by empirical excess risk
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{f})
and some other stochastic term in the following lemma, whose proof is deferred to Appendix
B
.

Proposition 3.8
.

\mathcal{E}_{fix}(\widehat{f})\leq\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{f})+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i}),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})\right\rangle.

By the definition of
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{f})
, we can upper bound it by the empirical training error
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})
. Consequently, combining this with Proposition
3.8
, the task of controlling the excess risk
\mathcal{E}_{fix}(\widehat{f})
reduces to bounding the term

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big\langle\nabla_{1}\ell\big(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i}\big),\,\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})\Big\rangle.

We refer to this quantity as
true optimism
, denoted by
\text{Opt}^{*}(\widehat{f})
. This term represents the central analytical object in our study and is the primary quantity we aim to bound throughout the paper.

4
Doubly Wild Refitting via Perturbing the Gradients

In this section, we introduce our algorithm,
Doubly Wild Refitting
, which provides a function-class-free approach to bounding the excess risk under non-symmetric noise.
The central idea of wild refitting is to leverage the trained model
\widehat{f}
to artificially construct new datasets by applying carefully designed perturbations to the predicted values.
The model is then retrained on these perturbed datasets, and the resulting refitted models are used to extract statistical information that enables a bound on the excess risk.

Previous works on wild refitting
(
Wainwright 2025
;
Hu and Simchi-Levi 2025
)
addressed single-dimensional prediction problems with symmetric noise distributions.
This simpler setting required only a single perturbed dataset and one model refit.
In contrast, the high-dimensional, non-symmetric distribution setting we consider necessitates constructing two perturbed datasets and refitting the model on each separately, which is also the reason our algorithm is named “doubly wild refitting”.
This double refitting approach is essential for our randomized symmetrization technique, as it is required to bound the risk estimation error in both directions.

We now describe our doubly wild-refitting concretely. It is divided into two sub-procedures: perturbation construction, and wild refitting.

Perturbation Construction:

After training
\widehat{f}
, we compute the gradients
\left\{\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
After that, we construct a sequence of i.i.d. Rademacher random variables
\left\{\varepsilon_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
Then, we construct the wild responses
\left\{y_{i}^{\diamond}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{y_{i}^{\sharp}\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that

\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\diamond})=\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})-2\rho_{1}\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},

\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})=\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})+2\rho_{2}\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},

Unlike the wild refitting method of
Wainwright 2025
, which perturbs the predicted responses directly, our approach perturbs the gradient vectors of the loss function. This design is intentional: by injecting noise into the gradient rather than the prediction, we perturb the local geometry of the loss landscape while preserving its global structure. In particular, gradient perturbations allow us to control how the loss grows around
\widehat{f}
, which is crucial for deriving non-asymptotic bounds on the empirical processes. Intuitively, this “doubly wild” perturbation simulates stochastic variations in the first-order conditions of the optimization problem, providing a direct handle on curvature and stability rather than on output residuals alone.

To show that our perturbation scheme is well defined, we establish the following proposition, which guarantees that the doubly wild refitting procedure is both well defined and computationally tractable. The theoretical justification relies on the Browder–Minty theorem
(
Browder 1967
;
Minty 1962
)
from functional analysis, to which we refer the reader in Appendix
A
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, the doubly wild refitting is well-defined. That means, for any
1\leq i\leq n
, any scaling numbers
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
, and any realization of
\varepsilon_{i}
,

\exists

y_{i}^{\diamond}
and
y_{i}^{\sharp}
such that

\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\diamond})=\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})-2\rho_{1}\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},\ \nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})=\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})+2\rho_{2}\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i}.

Moreover, if
\ell(u,y)
is strictly monotone with respect to
y
, then
y_{i}^{\diamond}
and
y_{i}^{\sharp}
are also unique.

Wild Refitting:

After the perturbation operation, we can construct two artificially perturbed datasets
\mathcal{D}^{\diamond}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}
.
Specifically, we define
\mathcal{D}^{\diamond}=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{\diamond})\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{\sharp})\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
Then, we retrain the model based on
\mathcal{D}^{\diamond}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\sharp}
to get wild refitting predictors
f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}
and
f_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}
.

Combining the perturbation procedure and the refitting procedure together, we summarize the doubly wild refitting algorithm as pseudo-code in Algorithm

.

5
Statistical Guarantees

In this section, we present our main statistical guarantees. We first establish excess risk bounds in the fixed-design setting and then extend the results to the random-design case. While the theoretical guarantees in both settings are rigorous, we conclude with a practical discussion on how these results can be effectively carried out in real applications.

5.1
Evaluating the Excess Risk in Fixed Design

We now bound the excess risk in the fixed design. Before we dive into the concrete results, we first provide several important definitions and mathematical notations that are useful in this section. Recall that we need to bound the
true optimism
term
\text{Opt}^{*}(\widehat{f})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i}),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})\right\rangle
.

When there is model misspecification, as the sample size
n\rightarrow\infty
, our trained predictor
\widehat{f}
will not converge to
f^{*}
, instead, it will converge to the best approximator within the model class
\mathcal{F}
, which is denoted by
f^{\dagger}
, i.e.,

f^{\dagger}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{y_{i}}[\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i})|x_{i}]\right\}.

We denote the empirical
L_{2}
distance between
\widehat{f}
and
f^{\dagger}
as
\widehat{r}_{n}
, i.e.,
\widehat{r}_{n}=||\widehat{f}-f^{\dagger}||_{n}
. Correspondingly, we name the following term as
oracle optimism
.

\text{Opt}^{\dagger}(\widehat{f}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i}),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\right\rangle.

Moreover, we shall define the following empirical processes. Denoting
\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})
as
\widetilde{g}_{i}
, we define

W_{n}(r):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\},

T_{n}(r):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}.

To induce the randomized symmetrization, for any index
i
, we use
w_{i}
to denote the random variable
\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})
, and
e_{i}
to denote its directional vector. Conditioned on its direction vector, we denote
w_{i}^{\prime}
as a random vector sampled from the following probability distribution,

w_{i}^{\prime}\sim\varphi_{x_{i},e_{i}},\ w_{i}^{\prime}\perp w_{i}.

With this conditional independent copy, we construct a symmetric random variable
\widetilde{w}_{i}:=\frac{w_{i}-w_{i}^{\prime}}{2}
, and define the following empirical process:

Z_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(f^{\dagger})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{w}_{i},f(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}.

Traditionally, bounding empirical processes such as
W_{n}(r)
and
T_{n}(r)
relies on detailed knowledge of the underlying function class—through measures like the Rademacher complexity, or the VC dimension. In contrast, our doubly wild refitting procedure demonstrates that these quantities can be effectively controlled without explicit access to such structural information. Instead, we establish that the empirical processes can be bounded at certain radii directly using the outputs generated by Algorithm
1
. To formalize this idea, we introduce the following quantities, which we refer to as wild optimisms.

\text{Opt}^{\diamond}(f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}):=\frac{\beta}{4\rho_{1}n}||f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}-\widehat{f}||_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\rho_{1}n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\diamond})-\frac{1}{2\rho_{1}n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\diamond}),

\text{Opt}^{\diamond}(f_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}):=\frac{\beta}{4\rho_{2}n}||f_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}-\widehat{f}||_{n}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\rho_{2}n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})-\frac{1}{2\rho_{2}n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp}),

Then, we have the following key Lemma
5.1
bounding the empirical processes
W_{n}
and
T_{n}
by the wild optimism.

Lemma 5.1
.

We have that

W_{n}(\|f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}-\widehat{f}\|_{n})\leq\text{Opt}^{\diamond}(f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}),\ T_{n}(\|f_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}-\widehat{f}\|_{n})\leq\text{Opt}^{\sharp}(f_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}).

The proof is deferred to Appendix
D.1
. Lemma
5.1
establishes that, at certain radii, the empirical process can be upper bounded by the quantities
\text{Opt}^{\diamond}(f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond})
and
\text{Opt}^{\sharp}(f_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp})
, both of which can be computed efficiently from the outputs of
Algorithm

1
. This lemma serves as a key theoretical component underpinning the wild-refitting procedure, because it enables us to upper bound the empirical processes without knowledge of the underlying function class.

With Lemma
5.1
, we have the following theorem regarding the evaluation of the excess risk
\mathcal{E}_{fix}(\widehat{f})
. The proof of this theorem is deferred to Appendix
D
.

Theorem 5.2
.

For any radius
r\geq\widehat{r}_{n}
, let
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
be the noise scales for which
\|f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}-\widehat{f}\|
and
\|f_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}-\widehat{f}\|
are equal to
2r
. Then, for any
t>0
, with probability at least
1-6e^{-t^{2}}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{fix}(\widehat{f})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{f})+\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left(\text{Opt}^{\diamond}(f^{\diamond}_{\rho_{1}})+\text{Opt}^{\sharp}(f^{\sharp}_{\rho_{2}})+B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})+B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f})\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle[(5\sqrt{d}+3\sqrt{\log n}+4)r+\|f^{\dagger}-f^{*}\|_{n}]\frac{2\sqrt{2}\beta\sigma t}{\alpha\sqrt{n}}.

In this bound,

B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{2r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\left(\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\right),f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\},

and

B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{2r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\left(\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\right),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\},

are called
pilot error
terms.

We provide a brief explanation of Theorem
5.2
. Intuitively, there are three parts in the bound. The first part is
\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{f})
, which is the empirical excess risk. The second part consists of the wild optimism terms
\text{Opt}^{\diamond}(f^{\diamond}_{\rho_{1}})
and
\text{Opt}^{\sharp}(f^{\sharp}_{\rho_{2}})
, which are the main components related to bounding the true optimism
\text{Opt}^{*}(\widehat{f})
. The third part consists of higher order terms, which include the probabilistic fluctuation term
[(5\sqrt{d}+3\sqrt{\log n}+4)r+\|f^{\dagger}-f^{*}\|_{n}]\frac{2\sqrt{2}\beta\sigma t}{\alpha\sqrt{n}}
and the pilot error terms
B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})
and
B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f})
. For the probabilistic term, by
(
Rakhlin 2022
)
, the rate of this deviation term coincides with the standard results derived from Talagrand’s inequality and Dudley’s entropy integral. Thus, it will be dominated by the wild optimism in terms of order in Theorem
5.2
.

For the pilot error terms, we provide the following intuitive explanation. In the term
B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})
, intuitively, the quantity
\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})
captures the discrepancy between
f^{*}
and
\widehat{f}
, and therefore contains meaningful information about the model estimation. Recall that:

\displaystyle T_{n}(2r)=

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{2r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{2r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})+\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i}),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{2r}(\widehat{f})}\bigg\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}),f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\varepsilon_{i}\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i}),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\bigg\},

where
w_{i}=\nabla_{1}(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})
s a zero-mean random variable that can be interpreted as an additional ”noise term.” Comparing
B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})
with
T_{n}(2r)
, we see that the only difference is precisely the presence of this extra noise term
w_{i}
in
T_{n}(2r)
. Hence,
T_{n}(2r)
may be viewed as a noisier empirical process, and we should intuitively expect it to have larger fluctuations than
B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})
. By Lemma
5.1
, this observation suggests that
B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})
is upper bounded by the corresponding wild optimism term
\text{Opt}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f})
. An entirely analogous argument shows that
B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f})
is expected to be controlled by the wild optimism
\text{Opt}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})
. Therefore, briefly speaking, the doubly wild refitting algorithm enables us to bound the excess risk by terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\bar{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{f})+\text{Opt}^{\diamond}(f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond})+\text{Opt}^{\sharp}(f^{\sharp}_{\rho_{2}}))
.

5.2
Bounding the Distance between
\widehat{f}
and
f^{\dagger}

Theorem
5.2
is useful only when
\widehat{r}_{n}=\|\widehat{f}-f^{\dagger}\|_{n}
is known. In this subsection, we provide an effective method for bounding this quantity. For the simplicity of discussion, we consider a well-specified scenario in this subsection, i.e.,
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
and therefore
f^{\dagger}=f^{*}
. Then, we have the following theorem about bounding
\widehat{r}_{n}
.

Theorem 5.3
.

For any
t>0
, with probability at least
1-2e^{-t^{2}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\widehat{r}_{n}\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}\left(W_{n}(2\widehat{r}_{n}))+T_{n}(2\widehat{r}_{n})+B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})+B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f})\right)}+\left(\frac{10\sqrt{d}+6\sqrt{\log n}+4}{\alpha}+1\right)\frac{2\sqrt{2}\sigma t}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}\left(W_{n}(2\widehat{r}_{n}))+T_{n}(2\widehat{r}_{n}))\right)}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}(B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})+B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f}))}+\left(\frac{10\sqrt{d}+6\sqrt{\log n}+4}{\alpha}+1\right)\frac{2\sqrt{2}\sigma t}{\sqrt{n}}

We highlight one improvement of our theorem compared with
Wainwright 2025
. In
Wainwright 2025
, one needs the non-expansiveness condition, which assumes that the difference between the predictor trained on a noisy dataset and that trained on a noiseless dataset is upper bounded by the noise scale. In this paper, we successfully remove this assumption and provide a valid method for bounding
\widehat{r}_{n}
under the well-specified condition.

To illustrate how to apply Theorem
5.3
to bound
\widehat{r}_{n}
, observe that the right-hand side of the theorem contains three components. The first part is
\sqrt{W_{n}(2\widehat{r}_{n})+T_{n}(2\widehat{r}_{n})}
, which is closely connected with the
wild optimism
term. The second part is the pilot error
B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})+B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f})
. The last part is the probability deviation term
\left(\frac{10\sqrt{d}+6\sqrt{\log n}+4}{\alpha}+1\right)\frac{2\sqrt{2}\sigma t}{\sqrt{n}}
. Among these, whenever the function class
\mathcal{F}
is more complex than a simple parametric family, the dominant term will be
\sqrt{\,W_{n}(2\widehat{r}_{n})+T_{n}(2\widehat{r}_{n})\,}
.

Ignoring the lower order terms, we achieve the following practical approach: we tune the radius parameter
r
such that it satisfies

\displaystyle r\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}\Bigl(W_{n}(2r)+T_{n}(2r)\Bigr)}.

(5.1)

Any such
r
then provides a valid upper bound for
\widehat{r}_{n}
. Importantly, this procedure is fully data-driven, since given any
s
, by definition, both
W_{n}(s)
and
T_{n}(s)
are computable solely from the trained predictor
\widehat{f}
and the quantities
\{\widetilde{g}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

In fact, we also have the following corollary with a more interpretable bound.

Corollary 5.4
.

For any noise scale
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
, for any
t>0
, with probability at least
1-2e^{-t^{2}}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{r}_{n}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\max\left\{(r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond})^{2},(r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp})^{2},\left(\frac{4W_{n}(2r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond})}{\alpha r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}}+\frac{4T_{n}(2r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp})}{\alpha r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}}\right)\cdot\widehat{r}_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{4(B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})+B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f}))}{\alpha}+\left(\frac{10\sqrt{d}+6\sqrt{\log n}+4}{\alpha}+1\right)^{2}\frac{16\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{n}.

The bound in Corollary
5.4
is slightly weaker compared to Theorem
5.3
but more interpretable because we no longer need to tune the radius
r
to solve Inequality
5.1
. Instead, we just need to tune the noise scale. If we disregard the probability deviation and pilot error terms, we can roughly summarize Corollary
5.4
in the following inequality:

\widehat{r}_{n}\leq\max\left\{r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond},r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp},\frac{4W_{n}(2r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond})}{\alpha r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}}+\frac{4T_{n}(2r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp})}{\alpha r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}}\right\}.

This inequality allows us to tune
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
to find a valid and tight upper bound of
\widehat{r}_{n}
more easily.

Finally, putting Theorem
5.2
and Theorem
5.2
together, we achieve an efficient algorithm for bounding the excess risk of any black-box machine learning or deep learning algorithm with rigorous statistical guarantees under the fixed design setting.

6
Discussion

In this paper, we investigate a doubly wild refitting procedure for upper bounding the excess risk of arbitrary black-box ERM algorithms in machine learning. We show that the core mechanism underlying wild refitting is the perturbation of gradient vectors, through which our framework subsumes both
Wainwright 2025
and
Hu and Simchi-Levi 2025
as special cases.

Unlike traditional approaches that rely on explicit complexity measures of the underlying hypothesis class, our method bypasses such requirements entirely. Instead, we leverage the Rademacher symmetrization of the gradient vectors and control the resulting empirical processes using perturbed pseudo-outcomes, the two refitted wild predictors, and the original trained predictor.

Several promising directions for future research remain open. First, our analysis currently focuses on the fixed-design setting; extending wild refitting to the random-design regime is important and technically challenging. Second, while we provide a useful explanation for the pilot error terms, developing a rigorous characterization of them is highly valuable. Moreover, existing methods of wild refitting require full data memorization for gradient perturbation at every data point, and designing a data-efficient variant would be very appealing. Wild refitting for ERM problems endowed with non-Euclidean geometric structures also suggests an intriguing opportunity for integrating information geometry with statistical learning. We leave these directions for future work.

Appendix A
Useful Mathematical Tools

In this section, we introduce some background knowledge about variational analysis, operator theory, and probability theory.

Definition A.1

(Gâteaux Derivative)
.

Let
X
be a real Banach space,
U\subset X
open, and
F:U\to\mathbb{R}
.
We say
F
is Gâteaux differentiable at
x\in U
if for every
h\in X
the
directional limit

F^{\prime}(x)[h]:=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{F(x+th)-F(x)}{t}

exists and the map
h\mapsto F^{\prime}(x)[h]
is
positively homogeneous
and
additive on rays
.
If, in addition,
h\mapsto F^{\prime}(x)[h]
is continuous and linear, we identify it with an element

F^{\prime}_{G}(x)\in X^{\ast}
(the Gâteaux gradient) via
F^{\prime}(x)[h]=\langle F^{\prime}_{G}(x),h\rangle
.

Definition A.2

(Monotone and strictly monotone mapping)
.

Let
A:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a mapping.

•

A
is
monotone
if
\langle A(x)-A(y),\,x-y\rangle\geq 0,\ \forall\,x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

•

A
is
strictly monotone
if

\langle A(x)-A(y),\,x-y\rangle>0,\ \forall\,x\neq y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Definition A.3

(Resolvent)
.

For
\lambda>0
, the
resolvent
of
A
(when it exists globally) is the mapping

J_{\lambda}^{A}:=(I+\lambda A)^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m},\ \text{i.e.}\ x=J_{\lambda}^{A}(b)\ \text{iff}\ x+\lambda A(x)=b.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be independent random vectors satisfying

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=0,
and for all unit vectors
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
\langle u,X_{i}\rangle
is
\sigma^{2}
sub-Gaussian. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|X_{i}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\sigma\,\sqrt{\,8\big(d\log 5+\log\tfrac{2n}{\delta}\big)}.

Lemma A.5
.

(
Rockafellar and Wets 1998
)

Let
X
be a real Banach space,
U\subset X
open, and
F:U\to\mathbb{R}
be Gâteaux differentiable at
x^{\ast}\in U
.
If
x^{\ast}
is a local minimizer of
F
on
U
, then

F^{\prime}(x^{\ast})[h]\geq 0\ \text{and}\ F^{\prime}(x^{\ast})[-h]\geq 0\qquad(\forall\,h\in X),

hence
F^{\prime}(x^{\ast})[h]=0\quad(\forall\,h\in X)
.
In particular, if
X
is a Hilbert space, when
F^{\prime}(x^{\ast})[\cdot]
is represented by a continuous linear functional via the Riesz representation theorem
(
Riesz 1909
)
,

F^{\prime}_{G}(x^{\ast})\in X^{\ast}
, the condition is equivalent to
F^{\prime}_{G}(x^{\ast})=\mathbf{0}\in X^{\ast}
.

Lemma A.6

(Browder-Minty theorem)
.

(
Ciarlet 2025
, Theorem 9.14-1)

A bounded, continuous, coercive and monotone function
T
from a real, separable reflexive Banach space
X
into its continuous dual space
X^{*}
is automatically surjective, i.e., for any
g\in X^{*},\ \exists u\in X
s.t.
T(u)=g
. Moreover, if
T
is strictly monotone, then
T
is also injective and thus bijective.

Lemma A.7
.

(
Vershynin 2018
)

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be independent, mean-zero random vectors satisfying the

\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian moment generating function bound:

\mathbb{E}\exp\!\big(t\,\langle u,X_{i}\rangle\big)\;\leq\;\exp\!\left(\tfrac{\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2}\right)\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R},\ u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\ i=1,\ldots,n.

Let
f:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be
L
-Lipschitz with respect to the product Euclidean norm:

|f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})-f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})|\;\leq\;L\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}-y_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Then, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\mathbb{E}\exp\!\Big(\lambda\big(f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\mathbb{E}f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\big)\Big)\;\leq\;\exp\!\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}L^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}\right).

Consequently, for all
t\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big|f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\mathbb{E}f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\big|\geq t\right)\;\leq\;2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}L^{2}}\right).

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})-\frac{2\rho_{1}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle+\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}||f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})||_{2}^{2}.

Rearranging both inequalities, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\diamond})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\diamond})\leq\frac{2\rho_{1}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle+\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}||f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})||_{2}^{2},

(D.1)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})\leq\frac{2\rho_{2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle+\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}||f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})||_{2}^{2}.

(D.2)

Notice that

\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\diamond})\right\}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\diamond})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\diamond})\right\}

and

\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})\right\}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})\right\}.

Taking the minimization on both sides about inequality
D.1
and inequality
D.2
, and notice that
f^{\diamond}_{\rho_{1}}
and
f^{\sharp}_{\rho_{2}}
are corresponding minimizers, we have that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\diamond})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\diamond})\leq\frac{\beta}{2n}||f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}-\widehat{f}||_{n}^{2}-2\rho_{1}W_{n}(||f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}-\widehat{f}||_{n}).

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i}^{\sharp})\leq\frac{\beta}{2n}||f_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}-\widehat{f}||_{n}^{2}-2\rho_{2}T_{n}(||f_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}-\widehat{f}||_{n}),

where
W_{n}
and
T_{n}
are empirical processes:

W_{n}(r)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\},

T_{n}(r)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}.

Therefore, we conclude that

W_{n}(\|f^{\diamond}_{\rho_{1}}-\widehat{f}\|_{n})\leq\text{Opt}^{\diamond}(f^{\diamond}_{\rho_{1}}),\ T_{n}(\|f^{\sharp}_{\rho_{2}}-\widehat{f}\|_{n})\leq\text{Opt}^{\sharp}(f^{\sharp}_{\rho_{2}}),

and finish the proof.
∎

Proof of Corollary
5.4
.

We first show that
W_{n}
and
T_{n}
are concave functions. We prove the case for
W_{n}
. For any
s,t
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
, denote
r:=\alpha s+(1-\alpha)t
. Let
f_{s}
and
f_{t}
be functions achieving the suprema that define
W_{n}(s)
and
W_{n}(t)
. Define
f_{r}=\alpha f_{s}+(1-\alpha)f_{t}\in\mathcal{F}
. By the triangle inequality,

\|f_{r}-\widehat{f}\|_{n}\leq\alpha\|f_{s}-\widehat{f}\|_{n}+(1-\alpha)\|f_{t}-\widehat{f}\|_{n}\leq\alpha s+(1-\alpha)t=r.

Thus, the function
f_{r}
is feasible for the supremum defining
W_{n}(r)
, so that we have

\alpha W_{n}(s)+(1-\alpha)W_{n}(t)\leq W_{n}(r).

Similarly,
r\mapsto T_{n}(r)
is concave. By the fact that
W_{n}(0)=T_{n}(0)=0
, we have that

\frac{W_{n}(s)}{s}\leq\frac{W_{n}(t)}{t},\ \frac{T_{n}(s)}{s}\leq\frac{T_{n}(t)}{t},\ \text{for any}\ s\geq t>0.

Now we prove the corollary. We either have 1)
\widehat{r}_{n}\leq\max\left\{r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond},r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}\right\}
or 2)
\widehat{r}_{n}>\max\left\{r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond},r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}\right\}
. In the latter circumstance, we have

W_{n}(2\widehat{r}_{n})=2\widehat{r}_{n}\frac{W_{n}(2\widehat{r}_{n})}{2\widehat{r}_{n}}\leq 2\widehat{r}_{n}\frac{W_{n}(2r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond})}{2r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}}=\widehat{r}_{n}\frac{W_{n}(2r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond})}{r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}}.

Similarly, we have

T_{n}(2\widehat{r}_{n})\leq\widehat{r}_{n}\frac{T_{n}(2r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp})}{r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}}.

Combining these inequalities with Theorem
5.3
, we get a quadratic inequality with respect to
\widehat{r}_{n}
.

\widehat{r}_{n}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{4W_{n}(2r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond})}{\alpha r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}}+\frac{4T_{n}(2r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp})}{\alpha r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}}\right)\cdot\widehat{r}_{n}+\frac{4(B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})+B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f}))}{\alpha}+\left(\frac{10\sqrt{d}+6\sqrt{\log n}+4}{\alpha}+1\right)^{2}\frac{16\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{n}.

We thus get our result. Moreover, we could solve this inequality to get

\widehat{r}_{n}\leq\left(\frac{4W_{n}(2r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond})}{\alpha r_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}}+\frac{4T_{n}(2r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp})}{\alpha r_{\rho_{2}}^{\sharp}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{8(B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})+B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f}))}{\alpha}}+\left(\frac{10\sqrt{d}+6\sqrt{\log n}+4}{\alpha}+1\right)\frac{4\sqrt{2}\sigma t}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle Y(\varepsilon)-Y(\varepsilon^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{w}_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}-\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}\widetilde{w}_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime})\widetilde{w}_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(\varepsilon_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime})\widetilde{w}_{i}\|\cdot\|f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\|\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\max_{i=1,\cdots,n}\|\widetilde{w}_{i}\|_{2})r}{\sqrt{n}}\|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Similarly,
Y(\varepsilon^{\prime})-Y(\varepsilon)
has the same upper bound, so we conclude that
Y(\varepsilon)
is Lipschitz-continuous with constant
\frac{(\max_{i=1,\cdots,n}\|\widetilde{w}_{i}\|_{2})rt}{\sqrt{n}}
. Therefore, applying Lemma
A.7
, we have that with probability at least
1-e^{-t^{2}}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{1:n}}[U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)]\leq U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)+\frac{\sqrt{2}(\max_{i=1,\cdots,n}\|\widetilde{w}_{i}\|_{2})rt}{\sqrt{n}}.

Taking expectation on
\widetilde{w}_{1:n}|e_{1:n}
, we have that

\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{1:n},\widetilde{w}_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{w}_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)]+\frac{\sqrt{2}\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{w}_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[\max_{i=1,\cdots,n}\|\widetilde{w}_{i}\|_{2}]rt}{\sqrt{n}}.

We focus on the right hand side of this inequality. For the term
\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{w}_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[\max_{i\in\left\{1,\cdots,n\right\}}\|\widetilde{w}_{i}\|_{2}]
, we apply Lemma
A.4
and get

\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{w}_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[\max_{i\in\left\{1,\cdots,n\right\}}\|\widetilde{w}_{i}\|_{2}]\leq\sigma\sqrt{8(d\log 5+\log 2n)}.

For the term
\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{w}_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)]
, we define the function

Q(\widetilde{w}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{w}_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}.

Then, we have that

\displaystyle Q(\widetilde{w})-Q(\widetilde{w}^{\prime})\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\widetilde{w}-\widetilde{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}\|f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\|_{2}\right\}\leq\frac{r}{\sqrt{n}}\|\widetilde{w}-\widetilde{w}^{\prime}\|_{F}.

We then apply Lemma
A.7
, to get that with probability at least
1-e^{-t^{2}}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{w}_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)]\leq U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)+\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma rt}{\sqrt{n}}.

Combining all these parts together, we have that with probability at least
1-2e^{-t^{2}}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon_{1:n},\widetilde{w}_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)]\leq U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)+\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma(5\sqrt{d}+3\sqrt{\log n}+1)rt}{\sqrt{n}}.

So we finish the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
D.5
.

Recall that
w_{i}^{\prime}
is a conditionally independent copy of
w_{i}=\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})
; then we have

\displaystyle U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)=

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\frac{w_{i}-w_{i}^{\prime}}{2},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}}_{\text{term I}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}^{\prime},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}}_{\text{term II}}.

For the first term, we could directly use the property of
f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond}
to get:

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}\leq\text{Opt}^{\diamond}_{\rho_{1}}(f_{\rho_{1}}^{\diamond})+B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f}).

For term II, it is trickier because
w_{i}^{\prime}
is not the original noise, but rather a conditionally independent copy whose realization is not contained in the dataset. We first apply Lemma
A.7
to obtain that, with probability at least
1-e^{-t^{2}}
,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}^{\prime},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,w_{i}^{\prime}}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}^{\prime},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right]+\frac{\sqrt{2}r\sigma t}{\sqrt{n}}.

By the definition of independent copy, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,w^{\prime}}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}^{\prime},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right]+\frac{\sqrt{2}r\sigma t}{\sqrt{n}}=\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,w}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right]+\frac{\sqrt{2}r\sigma t}{\sqrt{n}}

Again, by applying Lemma
A.7
, we have that with probability at least
1-e^{-t^{2}}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,w}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right]\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle+\frac{\sqrt{2}r\sigma t}{\sqrt{n}}.

Thus, we have that with probability at least
1-2e^{-t^{2}}
, for any
r>0
,

U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}\right)+\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma rt}{\sqrt{n}}.

By the doubly wild refitting procedure, we have

\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})=\varepsilon_{i}\left(\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})\right)+\varepsilon_{i}\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i}).

This is equivalent to

\varepsilon_{i}w_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}\left(\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\right).

Plugging this in, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}w_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\left(\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\right),f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\left(\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\right),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}

This holds for all radius
r
. Finally, by the condition that
\|f^{\diamond}_{\rho_{1}}-\widehat{f}\|_{n}=\|f^{\sharp}_{\rho_{2}}-\widehat{f}\|_{n}=2r
, we apply Lemma
5.1
, and change
r\leftarrow 2r
, to get:

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{2r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}=W_{n}(\|f^{\diamond}_{\rho_{1}}-\widehat{f}\|_{n})\leq\text{Opt}^{\diamond}(f^{\diamond}_{\rho_{1}}),

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{2r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\widetilde{g}_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}=T_{n}(\|f^{\sharp}_{\rho_{2}}-\widehat{f}\|_{n})\leq\text{Opt}^{\sharp}(f^{\sharp}_{\rho_{2}}),

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{2r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\left(\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\right),f(x_{i})-\widehat{f}(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}=B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f}),

and

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{2r}(\widehat{f})}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\varepsilon_{i}\left(\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{*}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\nabla_{1}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})\right),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f(x_{i})\right\rangle\right\}=B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f}).

Adding them together, we prove that when

, and the noise scales

satisfy

\displaystyle U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(2r)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(W_{n}(2r)+T_{n}(2r)+B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})+B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f})\right)+\frac{2\sqrt{2}\sigma rt}{\sqrt{n}}

(E.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\text{Opt}^{\diamond}(f^{\diamond}_{\rho_{1}})+\text{Opt}^{\sharp}(f^{\sharp}_{\rho_{2}})+B_{n}^{\diamond}(\widehat{f})+B_{n}^{\sharp}(\widehat{f})\right)+\frac{2\sqrt{2}\sigma rt}{\sqrt{n}},

∎

Proof of Lemma
D.6
.

Recall that our oracle predictor is

f^{\dagger}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{y_{i}}[\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i})]\right\}
. Then, we compute the Gâteaux derivative and apply the first order optimality condition to get that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{y_{i}}[\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{\dagger}(x_{i}),y_{i})],f(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\right\rangle=0.

By Assumption
3.1
, and the well-specified condition

, we have that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{f}-f^{\dagger}\|_{n}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{\ell(\cdot,y_{i})}(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),f^{\dagger}(x_{i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\widehat{f}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\ell(f^{\dagger}(x_{i}),y_{i})-\left\langle\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{\dagger}(x_{i}),y_{i}),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle-\nabla_{1}\ell(f^{\dagger}(x_{i}),y_{i}),\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle-w_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\right\rangle.

In the last inequality, we use the property that ERM
\widehat{f}
minimizes the empirical risk. In the last equality, we apply the well-specification condition that
f^{\dagger}=f^{*}
.

Now we analyze this term. Define the empirical process
M(w):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}_{\widehat{r}_{n}}(f^{\dagger})}\left\langle-w_{i},\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{\dagger}(x_{i})\right\rangle
.
Then, we apply the Lemma
A.7
to get that for any
t>0
, with probability at least
1-e^{-t^{2}}
,

M(w)\leq\mathbb{E}_{w_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[M(w)]+\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma\widehat{r}_{n}t}{\sqrt{n}}.

Similar to the proof of Lemma
D.2
, we have that

\mathbb{E}_{w_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[M(w)]\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{w_{1:n},\widetilde{w}_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[Z_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)].

By Lemma
D.3
and Lemma

, we have that with probability at least

\mathbb{E}_{w_{1:n},\widetilde{w}_{1:n}|e_{1:n}}[Z_{n}^{\varepsilon}(r)]\leq U_{n}^{\varepsilon}(2\widehat{r}_{n})+\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma(5\sqrt{d}+3\sqrt{\log n}+1)\widehat{r}_{n}t}{\sqrt{n}}.

Appendix F
Computation

In this section, we provide an efficient method for computing
y_{i}^{\diamond}
and
y_{i}^{\sharp}
in our doubly wild refitting Algorithm
1
. The method is called the proximal point algorithm (PPA).

In general, the proximal point algorithm (PPA) aims to solve the monotone inclusion problem
0\in T(x)
for maximal monotone operator
T
. By the result of
Minty 1962
, the Moreau–Yosida resolvent
P_{\lambda}=(I+\lambda T)^{-1}
will be firmly non-expansive. This suggests that the inclusion problem
0\in T(x)
can be iteratively approximated via the following recursion
z_{k+1}\approx P_{c_{k}}(z_{k})
, where
c_{k}
is the step size or learning rate. We write ”
\approx
” because computing ”
z_{k+1}=P_{c_{k}}(z_{k})
” can be computationally intractable. The following theorem of
Rockafellar 1976
provides the convergence guarantees for this inexact proximal point algorithm.

Theorem F.1
.

Define the operator
S_{k}(z)=T_{k}(z)+\frac{1}{c_{k}}(z-z_{k})
. Assuming that computationally, we have the following approximation procedure
z_{k+1}\approx P_{k}(z_{k})=(I+c_{k}T)^{-1}(z_{k})
that satisfies

\text{dist}(0,S_{k}(z_{k+1})=\|S_{k}(z_{k+1})\|_{2}\leq\frac{\delta_{k}}{c_{k}}\|z_{k+1}-z_{k}\|_{2},

for some convergent series
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\delta_{k}<\infty
, then, we have that

\|z_{k+1}-P_{c_{k}}(z_{k})\|_{2}\leq\delta_{k}\|z_{k+1}-z_{k}\|_{2}.

The limit point
z_{\infty}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}z_{k}
satisfies that
T(z_{\infty})=0
. Moreover, if
T^{-1}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
a
in a neighborhood around
0
with a radius
\tau>0
. Then, for
\left\{c_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
nondecreasing, define
\mu_{k}=\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^{2}+c_{k}^{2}}}<1
. There
\exists

\bar{k}
such that for all
k>\bar{k}
,

\|z_{k+1}-z_{\infty}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\mu_{k}+\delta_{k}}{1-\delta_{k}}\|z_{k}-z_{\infty}\|_{2},\ \frac{\mu_{k}+\delta_{k}}{1-\delta_{k}}<1,\ \forall k\geq\bar{k}.

Therefore, it suffices to focus on computing the iterative update
z_{k+1}\approx P_{k}(z_{k})
. A variety of numerical methods have been developed for evaluating such proximal-type operators, including Douglas–Rachford splitting
(
Eckstein and Bertsekas 1992
)
, Peaceman–Rachford splitting
(
Lions and Mercier 1979
)
, and the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)
(
Boţ and Csetnek 2019
)
concerning monotone operators. We do not elaborate on these algorithms here; instead, we refer the reader to the cited works for detailed discussions.
