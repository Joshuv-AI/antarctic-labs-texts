Chromatin Remodelling Complexes in Plant Adaptation to Abiotic Stress: Mechanisms and Perspectives
Authors: Xiuxiu Zhang, Yueran Zhang, Jiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs sessile organisms, plants have evolved elaborate epigenetic regulatory networks to adapt to a wide range of abiotic stress. Chromatin remodelling is a core epigenetic regulatory mechanism that refers to the process by which chromatin remodelling complexes mediate dynamic changes in chromatin spatial conformation and DNA accessibility through nucleosome sliding, histone variant exchange, nucleosome eviction, or spacing adjustment. The flexible and reversible modifications of chromatin structure enable plants to rapidly respond to external stimuli, thereby safeguarding their survival and promoting adaptive plasticity. This review summarises the research progress on chromatin remodelling complexes and their regulatory mechanisms in plant environmental adaptation. Furthermore, we discuss the current challenges and future research directions in this field, including the need to decipher the recruitment specificity of chromatin remodellers, investigate their coordination networks under combined abiotic stresses, and translate mechanistic insights into epigenetic engineering for crop improvement. Collectively, this review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of chromatin remodelling‐mediated stress adaptation and lay a foundation for developing stress‐tolerant crop varieties via epigenetic engineering.

Introduction

Global climate change has drastically increased the frequency and severity of abiotic stresses, such as drought, salinity, and extreme temperatures (Barnes and Korber 2021). These adverse environmental conditions severely perturb plant physiological processes, impair growth and development, and ultimately lead to substantial reductions in global crop yields, thereby posing a serious threat to worldwide food security (Barnes and Korber 2021). As sessile organisms, plants are unable to actively escape unfavourable environmental conditions; instead, they have evolved sophisticated regulatory networks to perceive stress signals and trigger rapid, precise adaptive responses (Chinnusamy and Zhu 2009). Among these diverse regulatory mechanisms, chromatin remodelling emerges as a pivotal epigenetic strategy that enables plants to dynamically modulate chromatin accessibility without altering the chemical composition of chromatin, distinguishing it from DNA methylation and histone modifications (Huang et al. 2025).

Chromatin is the fundamental structural unit of the genome, consisting of approximately 147 base pairs of DNA wrapped around a core histone octamer, which consists of one H3‐H4 tetramer and two H2A‐H2B dimers (Cutter and Hayes 2015). Notably, chromatin structure is not static but rather dynamically regulated. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that chromatin remodelling coordinates with other epigenetic marks to fine‐tune the expression of stress‐responsive genes in response to abiotic stresses (Brzezinka et al. 2016; Guo et al. 2019; Hong et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2024; Miao et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025). Plšková et al. (2024) reviewed how cellular redox balance impacts chromatin remodellers, histone modifiers, and DNA methyltransferases through oxidative post‐translational modifications. Miryeganeh (2025) further synthesised epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non‐coding RNAs, in plants exposed to various abiotic stresses, highlighting how dynamic epigenetic regulation enables rapid phenotypic adjustments to environmental challenges. However, despite these important contributions, a comprehensive overview specifically dedicated to the classification, core mechanisms, and regulatory networks of chromatin remodelling complexes, as distinct from other epigenetic layers, in plant abiotic stress adaptation remains lacking.

In this context, we focus on the functions of major chromatin remodelling complexes in plant abiotic stress adaptation, highlighting their underlying molecular mechanisms and regulatory networks. We first summarise the subunit composition of plant chromatin remodelling complexes and then discuss how these complexes mediate stress adaptation through diverse regulatory pathways. Finally, we outline the current challenges in this field and propose future research directions, aiming to provide a comprehensive overview of chromatin remodelling‐mediated stress adaptation and lay a theoretical foundation for the development of stress‐tolerant crop varieties via epigenetic engineering.

Chromatin Remodeller Complexes: Identification and Subunit Composition

Chromatin remodelling complexes are the major functional machineries regulating chromatin structure and gene expression in eukaryotes, and their core catalytic activity is conferred by ATP‐dependent remodellers. These remodellers are categorised into four canonical subfamilies in yeast, including SWI/SNF (Switch/Sucrose non‐fermenting), ISWI (IMITATION SWI), CHD (Chromodomain, Helicase, and DNA binding), and INO80 (INOSITOL REQUIRING 80). The classification is based on sequence divergence within the ATPase insertion domain and their associated accessory subunits (Clapier et al. 2017). The ATPase remodellers carry multiple conserved functional domains that govern complex assembly and catalytic efficiency. Specifically, conserved HSA domains facilitate assembly of actin‐related protein modules, while AutoN and NegC act as intrinsic cis‐regulators tuning ATPase activity.

SWI/SNF ATPases share an N‐terminal QLQ motif that mediates protein‐protein interactions (Figure 1a; Shang and He 2022). Conserved SnAC, AT‐hook and bromodomain features define this subfamily across eukaryotes (Clapier et al. 2017). Arabidopsis encodes four SWI/SNF‐type ATPases, namely BRM, SYD, MINU1 and MINU2, among which only BRM harbours a C‐terminal bromodomain capable of recognising acetylated histone marks (Shang and He 2022).

ISWI remodellers rely on a C‐terminal HSS module for linker DNA and histone H4 tail recognition (Figure 1b; Boyer et al. 2004), with CHR11 and CHR17 serving as the core catalytic ATPases in Arabidopsis. All eukaryotic CHD proteins contain tandem chromodomains and a C‐terminal SANT‐SLIDE DNA‐binding unit. Plant CHD paralogs additionally bear PHD fingers for modified histone readout (Figure 1c; Shang and He 2022). The INO80 clade is defined by an unusually large insertion in the ATPase domain that scaffolds recruitment of Rvb1/Rvb2 AAA+ ATPases. Arabidopsis INO80 and PIE1 form two distinct multi‐subunit assemblies of this family (Figure 1d; Jha and Dutta 2009).

Conserved core subunits of plant chromatin‐remodelling complexes were initially predicted by sequence homology with yeast (Figure 2, Upper panels; Table 1) and further validated through genetic isolation. With advances in mass spectrometry, numerous additional components have been identified via immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (IP‐MS) (Shang and He 2022). Arabidopsis SWI/SNF chromatin remodellers are categorised into three distinct assemblies: BAS‐A/B, SAS and MAS. Each complex incorporates one of the four canonical SWI/SNF ATPases, alongside deeply conserved core auxiliary subunits including SWI3 paralogs, BSH and SWP73A/B (Figure 2a lower panel; Table 1; Jégu et al. 2014; Sarnowski et al. 2005). ISWI complexes rely on ATPases and associate with DDT‐domain proteins plus plant‐specific ARID5 and FHA2 (Gu et al. 2020; Li et al. 2012). In Arabidopsis, the DDT‐domain protein family consists of 12 members, including RLT1‐3, DDW1, DDP1‐3, and DDR1‐5. IP‐MS data indicate that all DDT proteins except RLT3 and DDR2 interact with CHR11/17 to form functionally redundant ISWI assemblies, and distinct DDT proteins do not interact with one another (Figure 2b, lower panel; Table 1; Tan et al. 2020). FHA2 is exclusively present in CRAF complexes and participates in stamen development and plant fertility, suggesting its potential role in balancing reproductive growth and stress responses (Gu et al. 2020). Biochemical and structural results demonstrate that ARID5 carries linked ARID‐PHD dual domain cassette to concurrently recognise H3K4me3 and AT‐rich DNA to guide remodellers to transcriptionally active loci and regulate floral transition (Tan et al. 2020). CDM complexes target chromatin via the PHD fingers of DDP subunits that bind H3K4me3, whereas the recruitment mechanism of CDD complexes remains uncharacterised due to the absence of known histone‐ or DNA‐binding motifs in their auxiliary subunits (Shang and He 2022; Tan et al. 2020).

Arabidopsis INO80 and SWR1 complexes retain the conserved RVB1/RVB2 (RIN1/RIN2) and Arp subunits but integrate plant‐specific components, such as the class III COMPASS module in INO80 and MBD9/CHR11/17 in SWR1 (Figure 2c, lower panels; Table 1) (Luo et al. 2020; Shang et al. 2021). The COMPASS module is involved in histone H3K4 methylation, linking chromatin remodelling to histone modification in plant‐specific stress regulatory networks (Shang et al. 2021). The Arabidopsis CHD family includes CHR5 and CHD3 proteins (Figure 2d, middle and lower panels; Table 1; Huang et al. 2025).

Collectively, the four chromatin remodeller subfamilies show major evolutionary differences in their ATPase domains and accessory subunits. SWI/SNF has histone acetylation‐binding bromodomains, ISWI uses HSS modules to sense linker DNA length, plant CHD proteins carry chromodomains plus PHD fingers for methyl histone recognition, and plant INO80 uniquely associates with a COMPASS methyltransferase module. Such structural variations lead to distinct nucleosome remodelling activities, which are detailed in the following section.

Core Mechanisms of ATP‐Dependent Chromatin Remodelling

Nucleosome Sliding

Chromatin dynamics are largely governed by ATP‑dependent chromatin remodellers. A core mechanism is nucleosome sliding, which repositions intact histone octamers along DNA strands without disrupting octamer integrity, thereby exposing or occluding specific regulatory DNA elements (Clapier et al. 2017). SWI/SNF complexes bind nucleosomes at superhelical location 2 (SHL2) via the ATPase motor and use ATP hydrolysis to translocate DNA. This process facilitates nucleosome sliding even in the presence of DNA‐binding proteins or neighbouring nucleosomes, which may further induce nucleosome eviction or collision‐dependent chromatin remodelling. (Figure 3a, Engeholm et al. 2009). ISWI complexes rely on a conserved “ruler” mechanism, in which the SLIDE domain senses a linker DNA length of 20‐60 bp, while the HAND/SANT domain interacts with histone H4 tails to direct nucleosome sliding toward the centre of DNA fragments, equalising linker DNA distances between adjacent nucleosomes (Figure 3a, Kagalwala et al. 2004; Yamada et al. 2011). In yeast, ISW2 specifically contributes to precise nucleosome positioning across genomic regions (Figure 3a, Donovan et al. 2021). The INO80 family regulates nucleosome sliding in a linker DNA‑dependent manner through the Arp8 module, acting at SHL6 and supporting sliding of both canonical nucleosomes and hexasomic intermediates (Figure 3a, Hsieh et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2018). At gene promoters, INO80 activity is restrained by general regulatory factors (GRFs) to stabilise the +1 nucleosome at a defined distance from transcription start sites (TSSs) (Oberbeckmann et al. 2021). CHD complexes bind nucleosomes at SHL2 via their chromodomain and motor ATPase domain, and drive sequence‑dependent nucleosome sliding. Chd1 preferentially acts on poly(dA:dT)‑rich sequences to support nucleosome‑free regions (NFRs) formation and maintains genome‑wide nucleosome order through bidirectional sliding (Figure 3a, Ocampo et al. 2016; Winger and Bowman 2017).

Nucleosome Ejection

Another major function of chromatin remodellers is nucleosome ejection, which removes histone octamers or displaces H2A‐H2B dimers from nucleosomes to generate NFRs or subnucleosomal particles (Bruno et al. 2003). SWI/SNF family members, particularly the RSC complex, act as major mediators of ATP‐dependent nucleosome ejection at promoters enriched in poly(dA:dT) sequences, playing a critical role in defining the size of NFRs (Barnes and Korber 2021; Lorch et al. 2014). And they also act cooperatively with histone chaperones such as Nap1 to complete octamer eviction (Figure 3b, Lorch et al. 2014). ISWI complexes contribute to nucleosome ejection in a context‑dependent manner. ISW1 complexes cooperate with RSC and INO80 to refine NFR borders, ensuring precise nucleosome ejection at gene promoters (Barnes and Korber 2021; Hsieh et al. 2022), whereas ISW2 antagonises ejection to stabilise nucleosomes at poly(dA:dT) tracts maintaining nucleosome stability in distinct genomic regions (Figure 3b, Barnes and Korber 2021). INO80 mediates partial ejection by displacing H2A‑H2B dimers to form hexasome intermediates, with full octamer ejection requiring flanking DNA longer than 60 bp. In contrast, hexasome ejection is insensitive to DNA length, enabling INO80 function in genomic regions with short linker DNA (Figure 3b, Hsieh et al. 2022). CHD remodellers indirectly modulate ejection by regulating hexasome dynamics and preventing excessive nucleosome loss to preserve chromatin integrity (Figure 3b, Hsieh et al. 2022).

Nucleosome Exchange

Chromatin remodellers also control histone variant exchange, a process that replaces canonical histones with specialised histone variants to alter chromatin properties. Two major pathways are conserved across eukaryotes: H2A.Z‐H2B dimer exchange catalysed by INO80 family complexes and H3.3‐H4 tetramer deposition mediated by CHD and SWI/SNF subfamilies (Figure 3C, Konev et al. 2007; Mizuguchi et al. 2004). SWI/SNF and RSC promote H2A‐H2B dimer exchange between chromatin fragments by sliding nucleosomes toward DNA ends (Bruno et al. 2003), with RSC selectively disassembling H2A.Z‐containing nucleosomes at promoters to facilitate NFR formation (Figure 3C, Cakiroglu et al. 2019). ISWI complexes contribute histone variant exchange indirectly by stabilising nucleosome arrays, supporting H2A.Z retention and SWR1C‑dependent H2A.Z deposition (Clapier et al. 2017; Kagalwala et al. 2004). In yeast, the ISW1b complex not only drives low‐level H2A‐H2B dimer exchange via nucleosome sliding toward DNA ends (Figure 3C, Bruno et al. 2003) but also modulates H2A.Z turnover by adjusting nucleosome density throughout the genome (Smolle et al. 2012). The SWR1 complex catalysers unidirectional H2A.Z incorporation, a process that is strongly preferred at nucleosomes harbouring poly(dA:dT) tracts (Figure 3C, Mizuguchi et al. 2004; Sun et al. 2020). While INO80 mediates H2A.Z removal to restore canonical H2A (Figure 3C, Brahma et al. 2017). While CHD1 directly deposits H3.3 at active genes and enhancers in higher eukaryotes (Figure 3C, Konev et al. 2007), its yeast ortholog Chd1 contributes to residual nucleosome order in the absence of ISW1b, thereby indirectly sustaining H2A.Z‐dependent chromatin organisation and function (Smolle et al. 2012).

Nucleosome Spacing

Finally, chromatin remodellers govern nucleosome spacing and array phasing, which define the regular organisation of nucleosomes relative to TSSs and other genomic landmarks (Krietenstein et al. 2016; Eriksson and Clark 2021). In yeast, RSC promotes nucleosome eviction at poly(dA:dT) sequences to establish phasing reference points while counteracting ISWI‐mediated transcriptional repression at TSSs (Figure 3d, Parnell et al. 2015; Krietenstein et al. 2016). ISWI and CHD1‐type remodellers maintain uniform nucleosome spacing through a clamping activity that both establishes and maintains regular, constant nucleosome spacing across arrays (Lieleg et al. 2015). ISWI uses a “ruler” mechanism to set consistent linker lengths (Lieleg et al. 2015), with ISW1b organising global nucleosome arrays and resolving dinucleosomes (Eriksson and Clark 2021). ISW1a, by contrast, plays a minor role in global organisation but contributes residual order in the absence of ISW1b and Chd1 (Eriksson and Clark 2021). ISW2 interacts with sequence‐specific factors via its accessory subunit Itc1 to position single nucleosomes and define array boundaries (Figure 3d, Donovan et al. 2021). Chd1 determines nucleosome repeat length via its N‐terminal region, accounting for interspecies variation in nucleosome repeat length (NRL) (Hughes and Rando 2015). And Chd1 acts with ISW1b to resolve dinucleosomes maintaining regular nucleosome spacing (Figure 3d, Eriksson and Clark 2021; Hughes and Rando 2015). INO80 contributes to +1 nucleosome positioning and initial array phasing (Udugama et al. 2011), with subsequent refinement by ISW1a to establish the uniform spacing required for proper chromatin function and transcriptional regulation (Figure 3d, Krietenstein et al. 2016).

Chromatin Remodelling Mediates Plants Response to Drought Stress

Under normal conditions, the Arabidopsis BRM‐SWI3C complex binds to ABREs (ABA‐responsive elements) across the ABI5 promoter and TSS‐flanking regions. This binding stabilises the +1 nucleosome at the ABI5 locus, maintaining high nucleosome occupancy, establishing a repressive chromatin state, and dampening basal transcription of ABI5. Drought stress triggers abscisic acid (ABA) accumulation and subsequent activation of SnRK2 kinases. These activated kinases phosphorylate the C‐terminal bromodomain of BRM, impairing the chromatin remodelling capacity of BRM. This impairment leads to reduced nucleosome occupancy at the ABI5 locus, switching from a repressive to an open chromatin state, and ultimately activating the downstream drought‐responsive gene networks (Han et al. 2012; Peirats‐Llobet et al. 2016).

In addition, BRM also mediates an ABA‐independent drought signalling cascade (Liu et al. 2024). In unstressed Arabidopsis plants, BRM interacts with the transcription factor Di19, recruiting histone‐modifying enzymes CLF/SWN to deposit repressive H3K27me3 epigenetic marks at the loci of pathogenesis‐related (PR) genes. The H3K27me3 mark compacts local chromatin, limits Di19 access to target gene promoters, and constitutively represses PR genes transcription. Upon drought stress, upregulated BAPID (BRM‐associated protein involved in drought response) competitively binds to BRM and Di19, disrupting the endogenous BRM‐Di19 complex, and relieving H3K27me3‐mediated silencing of PR genes. Then Di19 directly binds the promoters of PR genes to trigger their transcription. The elevated PR proteins modulate stomatal dynamics to reduce water loss, thereby reinforcing plant drought tolerance (Figure 4a; Liu et al. 2013, 2024).

Besides BRM, AtCHR12 (also known as MINU1) coordinates reversible stress‐triggered growth arrest under drought and heat (Mlynárová et al. 2007). Phenotypic analysis shows that loss‐of‐function atchr12 mutants show weaker stress‐induced growth inhibition, while AtCHR12 overexpression lines exhibit prolonged growth suppression and slower post‐stress recovery. Microarray profiling reveals that dormancy‐associated DRM genes are transcriptionally upregulated in AtCHR12 overexpression lines, suggesting a potential regulatory link between AtCHR12 and DRM genes expression. However, direct chromatin‐binding or biochemical evidence proving AtCHR12 directly targets DRM promoters to remodel chromatin is still absent. Thus, further ChIP and protein interaction assays are required to validate this hypothetical regulatory cascade.

OsSWI3C, the ortholog of Arabidopsis AtSWI3C, also undergoes stress‐dependent redistribution upon drought exposure, yet via a distinct regulatory mechanism linked to the nuclear periphery. In rice, drought stress drives a physical interaction between the OsSWI3C and nuclear periphery protein OsNMCP1 (Nuclear Matrix Constituent Protein). This interaction releases OsSWI3C from repressive SWI/SNF assemblies, enabling it to regulate target gene expression. The released OsSWI3C increases chromatin accessibility at the promoters of drought‐responsive and root development genes such as NAC10, ERF48, SNAC1 and bZIP23. This chromatin remodelling event reshapes root architecture and boosts drought survival (Figure 4C; Yang et al. 2020). In maize, ZmCHB101, the ortholog of Arabidopsis SWI3D, plays a separate mode of chromatin regulation in drought stress response. ZmCHB101 targets nucleosomes surrounding stress gene TSSs to reduce local occupancy and open chromatin. Additionally, ZmCHB101 recruits RNA polymerase II to the promoters of target genes, activating ABA, calcium and ROS homoeostasis pathways, conferring broad osmotic stress tolerance (Figure 4C; Yu et al. 2018).

Apart from SWI/SNF complexes, CHD3‐type chromatin remodellers represent another key epigenetic regulator of plant drought responses. During seed germination, loss‐of‐function pkl mutants display ABA hypersensitivity, a phenotype driven by the persistent upregulation of ABI3 and ABI5 (Perruc et al. 2007). Although PKL is required for repressing ABI5 during germination, the factor that recruits PKL to the ABI5 promoter was characterised in leaf tissues. The transcription factor BBX21 recruits PKL to the ABI5 promoter to reshape local chromatin and restrain ABI5 transcription in leaf stomatal responses to drought (Kang et al. 2018). PKL can also cooperate with other epigenetic complexes to orchestrate large‐scale chromatin repression. PKL cooperates with PRC2 (Polycomb Repressive Complex 2) to spread repressive H3K27me3 across target chromatin regions (Liang et al. 2024). Upon drought stress, reduced PKL abundance is proposed to disrupt H3K27me3 spreading at stress gene loci, relieving transcriptional repression and activating drought tolerance programs. Jing et al. (2023) uncovered the upstream post‐translational pathway controlling PKL protein abundance. Under non‐stress conditions, SUMO E3 ligase MMS21 stabilises PKL via SUMO1 modification. PKL directly occupies the promoter and gene body of AFL1 to deposit H3K27me3 and silence this positive drought regulator. Drought or ABA treatment reduces MMS21 and PKL protein abundance, diminishing H3K27me3 at the AFL1 locus and de‐repressing AFL1 expression to strengthen drought resistance (Figure 4b; Jing et al. 2023).

OsCHR4, a rice CHD3 chromatin remodeller, shares partially conserved epigenetic activities governing histone modifications (Guo et al. 2019). Disrupted nucleosome distribution in oschr4 mutants elevates activating H3K4me3 and reduces repressive H3K27me3 at target promoters. Such epigenetic reprogramming upregulates auxin and cuticular wax biosynthetic genes, boosting leaf wax accumulation to limit transpiration and balancing auxin homoeostasis for coordinated root and shoot drought responses. Collectively, OsCHR4 links chromatin remodelling to hormone and metabolic signalling to fine‐tune crop drought acclimation (Guo et al. 2019).

In summary, core regulatory modules mediated by chromatin remodellers, including nucleosome occupancy modulation and regulation of histone modifications, are conserved across Arabidopsis, rice, and maize. However, the specific target genes, interacting partners, and downstream physiological pathways vary among species.

Chromatin Remodelling Mediates Plants Response to Salt Stress

Saez et al. (2008) characterised the role of SWI3B in ABA signalling. SWI3B physically interacts with the PP2C phosphatase HAB1, a negative regulator of ABA signalling. The interaction happens close to the ABREs and TATA boxes of ABA‐responsive genes under non‐stress conditions to repress their transcription. Exogenous ABA treatment releases HAB1 from these chromatin loci and abolishes its inhibitory effect on SWI3B. SWI3B is proposed to mediate nucleosome rearrangement, enhancing the chromatin accessibility of promoter regions. This promotes the transcriptional activation of ABA‐responsive genes and might initiate plant salt and drouht stress adaptation (Figure 4d; Saez et al. 2008). Apart from SWI3B, Nguyen et al. (2019) performed RT‐qPCR assays and found that the brm‐3 mutants accumulate markedly higher transcript levels of ABI1, ABI2, and HAI1 under salt treatment relative to wild‐type. Consistently, genome‐wide ChIP‐seq data from Li et al. (2016) confirmed that BRM directly occupies the promoter regions of these PP2C genes under normal growth conditions. However, Li's work did not provide ChIP‐qPCR evidence of salt‐induced dynamic shifts in BRM occupancy at PP2C promoters. Instead, Nguyen et al. (2019) combined anti‐GFP ChIP and RT‐qPCR assays to demonstrate that salt stress induces the dissociation of AtMYB44 from PP2C promoters, which relieves transcriptional repression of PP2C genes.

Beyond canonical SWI/SNF complexes, actin‐related protein ARP6 represents a distinct chromatin remodelling factor contributing to plant salt resistance. Arabidopsis ARP6 was initially characterised as a nuclear actin‐related protein required for plant development, flowering repression, and organ growth control (Deal et al. 2005). Phylogenetic analysis confirms that pineapple AcARP6 is homologous to Arabidopsis ARP6. AcARP6 is induced by salinity and osmotic stress. Its ectopic expression in Arabidopsis alters plant growth, delays flowering and improves both drought and salt resistance (Jakada et al. 2022). At present, however, only phenotypic evidence is available, and no direct chromatin‐level data explain how ARP6 mediates salt stress signalling.

Chromatin Remodelling Mediates Plants Response to Temperature Stress

In Arabidopsis, HD2C directly interacts with SWI3B to form a repressive HD2C‐SWI3B‐BRM complex. Under normal conditions, this complex targets heat‐responsive loci, where HD2C erases the activating H4K16ac mark and BRM remodels nucleosomes to maintain basal repression of heat‐responsive genes. Heat stress upregulates HD2C and strengthens its interaction with SWI3B. Identical thermotolerance phenotypes and elevated expression of heat‐responsive genes are observed in hd2c, brm single mutants, as well as brm hd2c double mutants, confirming HD2C functions in the same genetic pathway as BRM (Buszewicz et al. 2016). The SWI3 paralog SWI3C contributes to cold acclimation by dynamically targeting cold regulatory genes ICE1, MYB15, and CBF1‐3 in a temperature‐dependent manner. At 22°C, SWI3C binds downstream of TSSs, yet shifts to upstream promoter regions at 14°C. Loss of SWI3C disturbs nucleosome occupancy at ICE1, MYB15, and CBF1 promoters, elevating chromatin accessibility and triggering cold‐responsive gene overexpression (Gratkowska‐Zmuda et al. 2020).

Rice ISWI chromatin remodeller OsCHR11 positively governs chilling tolerance, as loss‐of‐function chr11 mutants showed aggravated cold sensitivity. Transcriptome sequencing shows weakened induction of core cold regulatory genes in mutants under low temperature. ChIP‐seq confirms that OsCHR11 preferentially occupies promoters and TSSs, with trehalose biosynthetic genes enriched among its binding targets. ATAC‐seq (Assay for Transposase‐Accessible Chromatin using sequencing) uncovers that chilling triggers chromatin opening at promoters and TSS downstream regions in wild‐type rice, yet cold‐induced promoter chromatin accessibility is largely abolished in chr11 mutants. Integrative multi‐omics data verify a positive correlation between OsCHR11‐mediated chromatin relaxation and cold gene transcription (Li et al. 2025).

Apart from cold regulatory pathways, the Arabidopsis INO80 complex (INO80C) governs thermomorphogenesis. Under cool conditions, PIF4 (PHYTOCHROME ‐INTERACTING FACTOR 4) exhibits low activity, as high H2A.Z occupancy at its target genes restricts transcription. When temperatures rise, INO80C directly interacts with PIF4 to mediate H2A.Z eviction from its target loci. It also recruits COMPASS methyltransferases to deposit permissive H3K4me3 and cooperates with SPT4/SPT5 to facilitate RNA polymerase II elongation. Collectively, these epigenetic modifications activate temperature‐ and auxin‐responsive genes and drive morphological adaptation to high ambient temperatures (Figure 4a; Xue et al. 2021).

Synthesising the above findings, chromatin remodellers are targeted to stress‐associated genomic regions through four core recruitment strategies, which include recognition by transcription factors (Figure 4a), binding via histone modification readers (Figure 4b), anchoring to nuclear scaffold proteins (Figure 4c), and interaction with ABA signalling components (Figure 4d). These targeting modes allow remodellers to accurately regulate stress‐adaptive genes under abiotic stress.

Chromatin Remodelling in Plant Stress Memory

Plants can store signals from past stress to mount stronger responses to subsequent challenges, a trait known as stress memory (Lämke and Bäurle 2017). Emerging evidence implicates chromatin remodelling in establishing and maintaining stress memory. A key breakthrough came with the identification of FGT1 (FORGETTER1) in Arabidopsis, a protein specific to heat stress memory with no effect on initial heat responses (Brzezinka et al. 2016). FGT1 physically interacts with two major classes of chromatin remodellers, BRM, as well as CHR11 and CHR17. Both brm and chr11 chr17 mutants display impaired heat stress memory and phenocopy fgt1 mutants. FGT1 and its partner remodellers modulate nucleosome occupancy around the TSSs of heat memory‐associated genes, including HSA32, HSP18.2, and HSP22.0. In wild‐type plants, heat stress triggers a rapid and sustained reduction in nucleosome occupancy at these loci that lasts for days throughout the memory phase. In fgt1 mutants, however, nucleosome occupancy recovers prematurely, leading to accelerated transcriptional shut‐off and loss of memory (Brzezinka et al. 2016).

Beyond the above module, multiple chromatin remodelling factors participate in diverse stress memory pathways. During cold acclimation, PKL facilitates the removal of repressive H3K27me3 marks from COR genes, and this depletion is maintained even after temperatures normalise, contributing to cold memory (Kwon et al. 2009; Yang et al. 2019). For vernalisation, H3K27me3‐mediated silencing of FLC (FLOWERING LOCUS C) is maintained for weeks, requiring SWI/SNF chromatin remodelling complexes for gene loop formation (Jégu et al. 2014).

Interestingly, remodelling‐dependent stress memories can last across a plant's lifespan and, under certain conditions, may be transmitted to subsequent generations. For instance, the chromatin remodeller DDM1 (DECREASE IN DNA METHYLATION 1), together with MOM1 (MORPHEUS MOLECULE 1), acts redundantly to actively reset stress‐induced epigenetic states, thereby erasing epigenetic stress memories and preventing their transgenerational transmission. In the ddm1 mom1 double mutant, stress‐induced transcriptional signatures persist and can be detected in subsequent generations (Iwasaki and Paszkowski 2014).

Concluding Remarks and Future Perspectives

As a core epigenetic mechanism, chromatin remodelling acts as a regulatory hub by interacting with other epigenetic regulators and histone modifications (Liu et al. 2024). Its reversibility enables plants to balance rapid stress responses with subsequent growth restoration once stress subsides (Brzezinka et al. 2016). This dynamic regulation depends on the accurate recruitment of chromatin remodellers to specific genomic loci. Although multiple recruitment pathways for distinct chromatin remodellers have been characterised (Figure 4), it remains unclear whether a unified regulatory principle governs the targeted localisation of remodellers to stress‐responsive loci under diverse abiotic stress conditions. Plskova et al. (2024) proposed that cellular redox status may influence remodeller recruitment through redox‐sensitive residues, but whether this mechanism contributes to target specificity awaits further investigation.

Beyond the unclear regulatory rules governing remodeller targeting specificity, two key gaps still limit our full understanding of chromatin remodelling in plant stress responses. First, how do distinct remodeller families coordinate their functions under abiotic stress? Plants trigger numerous seemingly independent chromatin remodelling events upon abiotic stress, and a conceptual model of crosstalk among epigenetic regulators has been proposed (Halder et al. 2022). Second, how do remodellers from the same family switch between activating and repressive roles according to stress context to balance stress tolerance and growth fitness? Activating remodellers increase chromatin accessibility to drive target gene expression, while repressive remodellers silence stress‐responsive genes under normal growth environments and thereby prevent abnormal growth defects (Kim 2021).

In addition, natural field conditions subject plants to multiple simultaneous stresses, pointing to another critical research limitation. Many chromatin remodellers respond to multiple distinct stresses, suggesting that they may act as hubs for integrating diverse environmental signals (Buszewicz et al. 2016; Jing et al. 2023; Nguyen et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2019). Accordingly, how chromatin remodellers integrate environmental signals to coordinate physiological responses under combined stress remains a puzzle unresolved by single‐stress experiments (Barnes and Korber 2021).

Given numerous unresolved questions about chromatin remodelling in plant stress responses, several promising research directions are worthy of future exploration. Most existing research relies on bulk sequencing of whole seedlings or organs, masking cell‐type‐specific epigenetic signals. Single‐cell ATAC‐seq and cell‐type‐specific ChIP‐seq can capture chromatin accessibility at single‐cell resolution and identify rare cell populations governing systemic stress responses. For instance, guard cell nuclei purification uncovered ABA‐induced unique chromatin shifts in guard cells absent from roots and mesophyll cells (Seller and Schroeder 2023). Consistently, shoot apical meristem (SAM) stem cells show weaker heat shock responses than intact seedlings, emphasising cell‐specific stress adaptation (Nguyen et al. 2025). Chromatin Immunoprecipitation‑exonuclease sequencing (ChIP‐exo) achieves single‐base resolution mapping of remodeller binding sites, outperforming conventional ChIP‐seq. It helps clarify remodeller binding preferences for nucleosome‐free regions, linker DNA or sequence motifs, as well as the cis‐elements recruiting remodellers to stress‐related loci. Cryo‐EM resolves 3D structures of remodelling complexes bound to nucleosomes, histones and stress transcription factors (Clapier et al. 2017), illustrating how a single complex shifts between activating and repressive functional states. CRISPR/Cas9 mutagenesis and epigenome editing validate whether specific chromatin remodelling events drive plant stress tolerance. Combining multi‐dimensional chromatin profiling, high‐resolution binding mapping, structural biology and genetic editing enables construction of causal mechanistic models rather than simple correlative observations (Figure 5). Deploying these tools under controlled combined stress is critical to decipher how remodelling networks convert diverse environmental signals into stress responses.

Even with these technical advances, chromatin remodelling research still faces bottlenecks limiting its application in crop improvement. Current knowledge of chromatin remodelling is overwhelmingly derived from Arabidopsis, yet cereal crops exhibit substantial genome divergence in their regulatory architecture at multiple levels (Figure 5; Zhang et al. 2025). Such divergence limits the broad utility of epigenetic transgenic tools initially characterised in Arabidopsis when applied to staple cereal crops. Therefore, parallel characterisation of chromatin remodelling regulatory networks in cereal systems is essential to distinguish universally conserved core mechanisms from lineage‐specific regulatory modules. The second bottleneck lies in experimental constraints. Most studies employ extreme, short‐term laboratory stress treatments that fail to faithfully replicate field environments. Accordingly, the second key research priority is to establish physiologically relevant multi‐stress systems that mimic field conditions and validate core results under controlled setups. The third bottleneck concerns unclarified causal links. Though abundant chromatin remodelling events associated with stress tolerance have been uncovered, there remains no conclusive evidence that manipulation of a single remodeller improves crop yield under severe field stress. Accordingly, the third research priority is to shift from correlative observations to causality‐focused functional manipulation. The fourth and most subtle bottleneck lies in agronomic trade‐offs. Though stress‐induced chromatin memory enables fast stress defence (Siddique et al. 2024), it may trap plants in a sustained defensive state that blocks full growth recovery and reduces grain yield under favourable growing conditions. Future strategies should develop precise, stress‐inducible and reversible chromatin remodelling modulation, which maximally decouples stress resistance from yield penalties.
