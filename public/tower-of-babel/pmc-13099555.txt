Molten iron at extreme conditions reveals compositional inhomogeneity in Earth’s lower outer core
Authors: Bo Gan, Jun Li, Heli Ma, Long Hao, Zhipeng Gao, Xiang Wu, Qiang Wu, Youjun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceEarth’s magnetic field is sustained by convection in the liquid iron outer core, yet the thermoelastic behavior of molten iron under core conditions remains poorly constrained. By extending high-precision temperature measurements of shock-generated molten iron, we establish pressure–temperature–density–sound velocity relations for liquid iron under core conditions. These measurements reveal systematic, depth-dependent deviations in density and compressional-wave velocity between the outer core and pure molten iron, implying that the basal outer core is compositionally stratified, with a partially crystallized F-layer and an overlying light-element gradient. This result challenges the long-standing assumption of a chemically homogeneous outer core and provides a robust experimental basis for understanding the geodynamo’s persistence and the long-term thermochemical evolution of Earth’s outer core.

Earth’s magnetic field originates from vigorous convection in the liquid outer core (1), where the motion of electrically conducting fluid drives the geodynamo (2, 3). Prior to inner-core nucleation, secular cooling might provide sufficient thermal buoyancy to maintain convection and the geomagnetic field for billions of years (2, 4, 5). The onset of inner-core crystallization introduced a new source of compositional buoyancy through the expulsion of incompatible light elements (1, 2, 6–8), a process widely regarded as key to sustaining the geodynamo since ~0.6 to 1.0 Ga (9–11). Given the ultralow molecular diffusivities predicted for the liquid outer core (10−9–10−7 m2·s− 1) (12, 13), vigorous turbulent convection is expected to occur, possibly redistributing the released light elements and maintaining a nearly homogeneous bulk composition (2, 14). However, recent experimental and theoretical investigations suggest that liquid immiscibility may develop under outer core conditions, potentially generating chemical heterogeneity even within a convecting fluid (15, 16). Complementary seismic observations further reveal the presence of a distinct F-layer at the base of the outer core, characterized by a 100 to 400 km-thick gradient in compressional sound velocity (VP, equivalent to bulk sound velocity, VB, in liquids) (17–20). These findings challenge the long-held assumption of efficient convective mixing and raise fundamental questions regarding the transport of light elements and the long-term stability of Earth’s geodynamo (3, 21).

Iron is the dominant constituent of Earth’s core (22). Comparing the density (ρ) and VP of molten iron under extreme pressure–temperature (P–T) conditions (~136 to 364 GPa and ~4,000 to 7,000 K) with seismic models provides key constraints on the structure, composition, and dynamics of the liquid outer core. However, direct experimental determination of these properties under such conditions remains technically challenging. Large volume press experiments are limited to a few GPa at high temperatures (23), and although laser-heated diamond-anvil cell (LH-DAC) techniques have extended the VP and ρ measurements to ~45 GPa at 2,700 K and ~116 GPa at 4,350 K for molten iron (24), these static approaches still fall far short of outer-core pressures. Furthermore, strong thermal gradients and poorly constrained thermal pressure in LH-DAC measurements hinder reliable extrapolation to outer-core conditions (25, 26). Dynamic compression techniques enable the ρ and VP measurements of molten iron to several hundred GPa (27–33), but historically suffered from poorly constrained shock temperatures (34–37). Recent advances in pyrometry diagnostics now enable accurate measurements (38, 39), yielding melting data consistent with static experiments and theoretical predictions (40–42), and allowing robust determination of the P–T-ρ-VP relations of molten iron under Earth’s outer core conditions.

Theoretical approaches, including ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD), first-principles molecular dynamics (FPMD), and thermodynamic modeling, provide additional constraints (43–50), but discrepancies persist due to electron correlation effects and limited experimental benchmarks. Extensive experimental and computational studies have further explored the ρ and VP of iron alloys at high P–T to examine the effects of light elements and their distribution within the outer core (14, 23, 45, 46, 50–56), but interpretations are limited by inconsistent reference values for pure iron.

Here, we report accurate shock temperature measurements of molten iron up to 364 GPa. Combining these data with existing ρ and VP measurements (27–31), we establish a comprehensive P–T-ρ-VP-γ (Grüneisen parameter) relation for shock-compressed molten iron. Using thermodynamic corrections (28, 57), we determine the ρ and VP of molten iron along the core adiabat and compare them with the seismic models [PREM (17) and AK135 (18)] to quantify the density deficit and velocity excess of the outer core relative to molten iron at the outer core conditions. The observed depth variations in density deficit and velocity excess provide direct experimental evidence for chemical heterogeneity in the lower outer core, with implications for the long-term dynamics of outer-core convection and geomagnetic field sustainability.

Results

Shock Temperature of Molten Iron.

We determined the shock temperature of bulk polycrystalline iron along the Hugoniot via a two-stage light-gas gun equipped with a 16-channel quasi-spectral pyrometer. A single-crystal lithium fluoride (LiF) window was placed behind the sample to maintain the compressed state and enable direct thermal emission measurements (see Methods and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Five shock experiments were performed with impact velocities ranging from 5.95 to 6.82 km/s, corresponding to Hugoniot pressures (PH) of 262.3 to 364.3 GPa (SI Appendix, Table S1) (27, 30, 31, 58), under which iron is completely molten (29, 59–61). Time-resolved spectral radiance data from the Fe–LiF interface show stable plateaus after an initial transient decrease, with all channels converging to quasi-steady levels whose variations are typically within ~2% (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The interfacial temperature (TI) and emissivity (ε) were derived by fitting the spectral intensities to Planck’s equation for gray-body radiation (SI Appendix, Table S2), with a representative fit for shot Fe-L1 shown in Fig. 1B (others in SI Appendix, Fig. S3). All spectra are well represented by a single gray-body Planck function, with no systematic long-wavelength excess or evidence for secondary cooler radiative sources, indicating that the measured emissions are dominated by the Fe–LiF interface.

Simultaneous photon Doppler velocimetry (PDV) measurements of particle velocity (uPi) show stable plateaus lasting 260 to 320 ns, characteristic of a plastic single-wave structure (Fig. 1C). The high temporal resolution of the pyrometer (~3 ns) enabled time-resolved TI profiles, which were compared to the uPi profiles (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The TI profiles show a brief initial overshoot, followed by a gradual relaxation and a stable plateau that persists until release. Both the magnitude and duration of the preplateau temperature overshoot increase systematically with shock pressure, from ~200 K and ~50 ns at 262 GPa to ~700 K and ~135 ns at 364 GPa. This behavior is likely associated with the shock-induced melting of LiF (62, 63), which may promote limited interfacial mass mixing. Importantly, all uPi profiles show no evidence of wave dispersion or sloped velocity histories throughout the shocked duration, indicating the presence of a sharp impedance boundary at the Fe–LiF interface. The shock duration, limited to several hundred nanoseconds, is orders of magnitude shorter than the characteristic timescales required for diffusive mixing over micrometer scales (12, 13). Consequently, significant interfacial broadening is unlikely even if both materials at the interface are shock-molten. Instead, the initial temperature overshoot most likely arises from nanometer-scale nonideal contact at the Fe–LiF interface (64, 65). Accordingly, the interfacial temperatures are determined from the subsequent stable plateaus, which persist for more than half of the shocked duration and indicate a well-established thermal equilibrium at the interface. Owing to the large thermal mass of both the iron sample and LiF window, the transient excess energy associated with this overshoot has a negligible effect on the plateau temperature.

As the shock wave transmitted from iron into the lower-impedance LiF, both pressure and temperature decreased from the Hugoniot state (PH, TH) to a partially released state (PR, TR). TR was derived from the measured TI via a one-dimensional thermal conduction model, and TH was subsequently retrieved using the Mie-Grüneisen thermal equation (see Methods and SI Appendix, Table S2) (38). Our measured TH of 5,600 K at 262.3 GPa agrees well with a previous value of 5,500 K at 256 GPa (Fig. 2) (38). More broadly, our determined TH between 262.3 and 364.3 GPa is consistent with ab initio and density-functional theory-molecular dynamics calculations (40, 59), but is 400 to 2,000 K lower than those reported in early shock-temperature measurements using thin iron films/foils (~1 to 25 μm) and pyrometers with limited spectral channels, temporal resolution, and calibration (35–37). Those earlier configurations promoted nonideal sample–window contact and incorporation of short-lived radiative overshoots into fitted temperatures, leading to systematic overestimation (38, 66). In contrast, our bulk-sample configuration with near-ideal Fe–LiF contact and quasi-spectral pyrometry determines interfacial temperatures exclusively from long-lived equilibrium plateaus, thereby minimizing such artifacts. Uncertainties introduced by updated shock data and high-P–T thermodynamic parameters of iron and LiF contribute only ∼50 to 200 K to TH (44, 62, 63, 67, 68), far smaller than the discrepancy with prior studies. We therefore attribute the lower temperatures primarily to improved direct temperature diagnostics and sample preparation, yielding more reliable Hugoniot temperatures for molten iron.

By integrating the measured temperature with the Hugoniot P–T relation and the high-pressure melting temperatures of solid iron (38, 69, 70), we determined the complete melting point of iron along the Hugoniot to be 260(5) GPa and 5,530(300) K. This result is consistent with independent determinations of iron melting under dynamic compression, including sound velocity measurements in gas-gun experiments (29) and in-situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) and X-ray absorption spectroscopy measurements under laser-driven shocks (59–61). The associated latent heat release leads to a temperature change of ~1,100 K, corresponding to an entropy change of 0.62R (R: gas constant). This is slightly lower than the 0.65R measured for an Fe-8Ni-10Si alloy (71), and the 0.70 to 0.75R calculated for Fe-light element alloys (72), suggesting that light elements elevate the entropy of melting by lowering the melting point, enhancing atomic disorder, or altering electronic structure (73).

The determined Hugoniot P–T relation of molten iron shows that the TH increases quasi-linearly with shock pressure, from 5,600 K at 262.3 GPa to 8,105 K at 364.3 GPa, enabling precise TH assignment for existing P-ρ and P-VP shock datasets (SI Appendix, Tables S3 and S4) (27–31). The Hugoniot data on shock wave velocity (US, km/s) and particle velocity (uP, km/s) of shock-molten iron can be fitted linearly as US = 4.11(±0.13) + 1.53(±0.03)up (27, 30, 31), which accordingly yields the P-ρ Hugoniot relation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Combined with existing VP data (28, 29), we further determined the P-VP and P-γ relations (see Methods and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). These together define a self-consistent P–T-ρ-VP-γ relation for shock-molten iron along the Hugoniot (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), providing a fundamental reference for comparison with the thermoelastic properties of Earth’s outer core.

Density and Sound Velocity of Molten Iron under Earth’s Outer Core Conditions.

Extensive experimental and theoretical studies have constrained the melting temperature of iron at the inner-core boundary (ICB) to 6,000 to 6,200 K (38, 40–42, 60, 70). Accounting for the melting point depression induced by light elements (73), a temperature of ~5,400 K at the ICB (TICB) is commonly adopted to derive the outer-core geotherm (Methods) (24, 48). Since the outer core lies on a cooler adiabat than the Hugoniot, thermal corrections to the ρ and VP of molten iron are required for comparison with seismological models.

To address this, we developed a thermal equation of state (EoS) for molten iron using the Mie-Grüneisen formalism constrained by Hugoniot and LH-DAC data (Methods, Fig. 3A) (44). Our EoS model reproduces the discrete Hugoniot densities with relative deviations within ±0.8% (Fig. 3B), and deviations from the Hugoniot relation are further reduced to ±0.08% over the investigated P–T range (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). These results indicate that the present model accurately reproduces density variations along the Hugoniot and provides a robust basis for thermal extrapolation toward the outer core conditions. Our model is also broadly consistent with LH-DAC measurements at lower pressures, yielding density deviations comparable to the EoS model fitted exclusively to static data (SI Appendix, Fig. S8) (24). In contrast, the low-pressure-based model systematically underestimates Hugoniot densities at high P–T (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), highlighting the increased uncertainties associated with extrapolating static datasets to extreme conditions. These discrepancies likely reflect the influence of strong thermal gradients and poorly constrained thermal pressure contributions in static experiments. Comparisons with AIMD and FPMD simulations reveal substantial discrepancies in pressure derivatives (Fig. 3C) (44, 47, 50). Our EoS model yields lower densities than simulations at low pressures but exceeds them above a crossover pressure, with the 6,000 K isotherm intersecting simulation results between ~150 and 350 GPa (44, 47, 50). These deviations likely originate from systematic biases in generalized-gradient-approximation functionals and the neglect of many-body and finite-temperature electronic effects (50), highlighting the need for improved theoretical descriptions of molten iron under extreme conditions.

Thermal corrections to VP, also derived from the Mie-Grüneisen framework (Methods) (28, 57), yield ρ and VP of molten iron along the outer-core geotherm (TICB = 5,400 K) between 262 and 330 GPa (~4,200 to 5,150 km depth, Fig. 4). Both properties increase with depth, reaching 13.33 g/cm3 and 10.15 km/s, respectively, at the ICB. These values are systematically higher than most previous experimental and theoretical estimates (24, 43–49), but consistent with recent FPMD simulations incorporating pressure corrections and finite-temperature electronic excitations (SI Appendix, Fig. S9) (50). The higher density of molten iron implies greater light-element enrichment in the outer core than previously inferred, offering a critical high P–T benchmark for quantifying their effects on the thermoelastic behavior of liquid iron and refining constraints on the outer-core composition.

AIMD and FPMD simulations indicate that candidate light elements (Si, C, S, O, H) generally lower ρ and increase VP of liquid iron under the outer-core conditions, with minor nonideal mixing effects (14, 50, 51). However, neither single-element additions nor multicomponent mixtures can match the seismic observations: Light elements efficiently reduce ρ to observed values, but the corresponding VP remains systematically overestimated. Extrapolations from LH-DAC measurements (52, 54, 55) further indicate even stronger VP enhancements for C, S, and Si than predicted by simulations (14, 50, 51), highlighting unresolved complexities in the elastic behavior of Fe-light-element liquids. In contrast, thermodynamic and molecular-dynamics studies suggest that oxygen may lower VP by softening the liquid structure through the formation of isolated atoms or low-coordination clusters (12, 45, 76, 77). This softening effect partially counteracts the VP increase from other elements, enabling oxygen-bearing melts to simultaneously reproduce the seismically constrained ρ and VP of the outer core.

At the ICB, our corrected values for molten iron are ~0.15 g/cm3 less dense and ~1.07 km/s slower than solid hexagonal-close-packed (hcp) iron (74, 75). The density difference is smaller than the seismically observed jump across the ICB by ~0.42 g/cm3 (~3.5% of the outer core density) (18), indicating that the outer core must contain at least one incompatible light element that partitions strongly into the melt. Oxygen is the most likely candidate, with a solid–liquid partition coefficient significantly lower than that of other light elements (78). Experiments and simulations consistently show that crystallized solid iron alloys are nearly devoid of oxygen (78–80), requiring at least ~3.4 wt% oxygen in the outer core to explain the observed density discontinuity (50). Furthermore, the VP contrast between solid and liquid iron exceeds the seismic jump by ~0.32 km/s (3.1% of the outer core VP) (18), imposing an additional constraint on the composition and elastic behavior of the core.

Radial Inhomogeneity in the Lower Outer Core and Its Influence on the Core Dynamics.

Compared with pure molten iron, Earth’s lower outer core exhibits a significant ρ deficit and a systematic VP excess over the investigated depth range (Fig. 4). In both the PREM and AK135 reference models, the ρ deficit increases quasi-linearly with depth from ~4,200 to 4,870 km before leveling off, reaching 8.74 to 8.96% at the ICB. In contrast, the VP excess generally decreases with depth, attaining 1.34 to 2.00% at the ICB. If the outer core were compositionally homogeneous, both ρ deficit and VP excess would be expected to increase concurrently, since a less compressible mixture would enhance both contrasts. The observed opposite trends therefore suggest a depth-dependent compositional effect, most plausibly a progressive enrichment in oxygen with increasing depth, which would simultaneously reduce density and lower VP in molten iron under outer-core conditions (12, 45, 76, 77). We note that the absolute amplitudes of the ρ deficit and VP excess may remain subject to refinement, given present experimental and seismic uncertainties (SI Appendix, Text S1). Nevertheless, their internally consistent and opposite depth-dependent trends provide a robust qualitative indicator of radial compositional variation.

Furthermore, our results reveal subtle inflections in both the ρ deficit and VP excess near ~4,870 km depth (Fig. 4), coinciding with seismological evidence for a distinct F-layer at the lowermost ~280 km of the outer core (18–20). A simple oxygen compositional gradient alone cannot fully account for the simultaneous flattening of the ρ deficit and steepening of the VP excess in the lowermost outer core. These features are more consistent with a two-phase slurry consisting of Fe-rich solid particles suspended in an oxygen-enriched melt (19, 20). The settling of dense solids would increase the bulk density, thereby moderating the ρ deficit, while scattering at solid–liquid interfaces and disruption of liquid connectivity would reduce the VP relative to that of a homogeneous melt (81, 82).

High P–T experiments and thermodynamic modeling demonstrate that Fe-light-element liquids can demix into two immiscible liquids under the outer-core conditions: a denser, light-element-poor melt and a lighter, light-element-rich (e.g., O-bearing) melt (15, 16). Such immiscibility provides a physical basis for the coexistence of a basal F-layer and an overlying compositional gradient in the lower outer core (Fig. 5). During inner-core crystallization, light elements such as O accumulate in the residual liquid; once the local concentration exceeds the solvus limit, liquid–liquid separation is triggered. The buoyant, light-element-rich melt ascends and progressively enriches the overlying liquid, while the Fe-rich, light-element-depleted phase remains near the ICB, where its higher Fe chemical potential and elevated melting point favor preferential crystallization (19, 73), producing a two-phase slurry. Continued cooling further enhances liquid immiscibility, driving sustained upward migration of the lighter melt and reinforcing both the basal F-layer and the radial compositional gradient within the overlying liquid (Fig. 5) (19–21).

Given a present-day inner-core growth rate of ~1 mm/y (6, 84), the release of light elements such as oxygen (JO = ~1.3 × 10−8 kg·m−2·s−1) can sustain composition gradients within limits consistent with the dynamic stability of the outer core. The gradient [∂CO/∂x = −JO/(ρK)] is governed by the effective eddy diffusivity (K), which scales with the root-mean-squared velocity (urms) and mixing scale (ℓ) of turbulence (K ~ urms·ℓ) (85). Geomagnetic observations and numerical simulations indicate lateral urms and ℓ on the order of 10−4 m/s and 10 to 100 km, respectively, within the outer core (86–89). Such vigorous lateral mixing leads to an almost homogeneous composition, with variations <10−4 wt.% over 1,000 km. In contrast, rotational and magnetic forces strongly suppress radial motions, with urms and ℓ likely 1 to 3 orders of magnitude smaller than their lateral counterparts (88, 90–92). Consequently, the radial transport efficiency is several orders of magnitude lower, permitting the development of quasi-steady radial light-element gradients of ~0.1 to 1.0 wt.% per 1,000 km. Our observations are compatible with the presence of such subtle but persistent compositional gradients in the lower outer core, although the exact magnitude remains uncertain. Further experimental and theoretical constraints on the thermoelastic effects of light elements (e.g., oxygen) on molten iron along the geotherm are required to refine these estimates.

Prior to inner-core nucleation, secular cooling likely sustained vigorous thermal convection, ensuring a well-mixed core (1, 2, 4). The onset of crystallization introduced a new source of compositional buoyancy that enhanced convective power (1, 93) and likely intensified the geomagnetic field between 0.6 and 1.0 Ga (9–11). Continued crystallization enriches the liquid outer core in light elements, promoting thickening of the F-layer and the development of an overlying radial compositional gradient above it (Fig. 5). This evolving gradient augments the available compositional buoyancy, partially compensating for the decline in thermal convection as the core cools, and now represents the dominant energy source sustaining the geodynamo (7, 83). However, as the F-layer continues to thicken, increasing chemical stratification may eventually inhibit efficient upward transport of buoyant fluid. Once compositional convection can no longer offset the declining thermal power, the outer core’s convective vigor will wane, potentially compromising the long-term stability of Earth’s magnetic field.

Methods

Shock Temperature Measurements.

Shock experiments were conducted using a 30-mm-bore two-stage light-gas gun at the Institute of Fluid Physics, China Academy of Engineering Physics. The experimental setup for shock temperature measurements is shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1. Tantalum (Ta) and platinum (Pt) flyer plates, with a diameter of 25 mm and a thickness of 1.5 to 2 mm, were accelerated to several km/s by the gun. The hypervelocity impact generated shock waves that compressed the flyer and sample to high pressure–temperature (P–T) states. The impact velocity was measured using the optic beam break-out method with an uncertainty of ∼1% (SI Appendix, Table S1). High purity (>99.98%) fine-grained polycrystalline bulk iron (grain size 10 to 20 µm) was used to manufacture samples with a diameter of 36 mm and a thickness of 2 to 2.5 mm (38). A transparent single-crystal LiF with the (100) orientation was used as a window with a diameter and thickness of 30 mm and 8 mm, respectively. The window was pressed against the sample to maintain the compression state and provide continuous optical observations of the thermal radiance emitted from the sample surface. The single-crystal LiF has excellent optical properties with minimal shock-induced extinction and remains transparent under shock compression up to ~200 GPa (94), even when shock melting occurs at ~135 to 150 GPa (62, 63). Both the iron sample and LiF window were polished to surface roughness <15 nm and flatness <100 nm (38). The sample and window were carefully assembled into an aluminum bracket, and no glue was used between the sample and window interfaces. Newton’s interference fringes at the sample/window interface were visible to ensure a smooth interface boundary (38). To eliminate possible strong scattering of the emitted light, the edge and peripheral surface of the LiF window were coated with black epoxy resin, and the bracket interior was evacuated to a vacuum of ~1 Pa prior to each shot.

A time-resolved quasi-spectral optical pyrometer with 16 channels in the wavelength range of 400 to 800 nm was used to measure the shock temperature at the iron/LiF interface. A 16-core silica optical fiber collected thermal radiation from the interface and transmitted it to the pyrometer. The photomultiplier tubes coupled to 10 nm (FWHM) narrow-bandwidth filters converted emission intensities into voltage signals with a high temporal resolution of ~3 ns, recorded by an oscilloscope. Before each shot, each pyrometer channel was calibrated using the spectral radiance from a tungsten-halogen standard lamp. Based on these calibrations, the measured voltages were converted to spectral radiance, which was then fitted to obtain the temperature (TI) and emissivity (ε) at the interface using Planck’s equation for gray-body radiation:[1]Imea.λ,ε,TI=εC1λ-5expC2/λTI-1-1,

where Imea.(λ, ε, T) is the measured spectral radiance, λ is the wavelength, and C1 (1.191 × 10−16 W m2/Sr) and C2 (1.439 × 10−2 m K) are the first and second radiation constants, respectively. The LiF window remains optically transparent with negligible intrinsic emission over the present P–T range, and its reflectivity is sufficiently low that interfacial reflection contributes negligibly to the detected signal (95, 96). Accordingly, the detected radiance is modeled as emission from a single effective radiating surface at the Fe–LiF interface using a single gray-body Planck function.

To evaluate the stability of the Fe–LiF interface during shock melting and assess the potential influence of interfacial mixing on the inferred interfacial temperature, the particle velocity at the Fe–LiF interface was simultaneously recorded using PDV. The PDV system employed a 1,550-nm laser delivered via a single-mode optical fiber.

Hugoniot Temperature of Shock-Compressed Iron.

The Hugoniot pressure (PH) in the shock-compressed iron was calculated using the impedance matching method, based on the measured impact velocities and Hugoniot relations of iron (27, 30, 31) and the flyer materials (SI Appendix, Table S1) (58). Since the shock impedance of LiF is lower than that of iron, the shock state is partially released to the interface pressure (PR) when the shock wave propagates from the sample to the window. PR was determined using the measured particle velocities and the Hugoniot relation for LiF (62). Uncertainties in PH and PR were estimated using standard error propagation methods (58).

The partial release at the interface was treated as a quasi-isentropic process, and TH was derived using the Mie-Grüneisen thermal equation (38):[2]TH=TRexp-∫VRVHγ/V dV,

where VH and VR are the volumes of the shock-compressed iron at PH and PR, respectively, calculated from the Rankine–Hugoniot conservation equations and impedance matching method; γ is the Grüneisen parameter, constrained from the PH-VB Hugoniot relation of iron; TR is the release temperature of the sample at PR, derived from the measured interfacial temperature (TI) using a one-dimensional thermal conduction model (38):[3]TR=TI+TI-TW/α,

where TW is the shock temperature of LiF at PR, and α is the proportionality coefficient between the sample and window, calculated as (38):[4]α=ρCVκSρCVκW1/2,

where ρ, CV, and κ represent density, specific heat capacity, and thermal conductivity, respectively, with subscripts S and W denoting the iron sample and LiF window. Parameters for LiF were obtained from previous experiments and calculations (62, 63, 67), while the CV and κ of iron were taken from theoretical calculations (SI Appendix, Table S2) (44, 68). Uncertainties in TH and TR were propagated from pyrometric measurements, Planck law fitting, and parameter uncertainties (38). Although the thermal conductivity of iron lacks verification by experimental measurements, this uncertainty has a limited effect on determining TH (e.g., doubling the thermal conductivity would only introduce an additional uncertainty of 50 to 70 K).

Hugoniot Relations of Molten Iron under Shock Compression.

Converging evidence from sound-velocity measurements, in situ XRD and X-ray absorption spectroscopy diagnostics, and density-functional theory-molecular dynamics simulations indicates that iron completely melts along the Hugoniot at ~260 GPa (29, 40, 59–61). We reviewed Hugoniot data from the literature on shock wave velocity (US) and particle velocity (uP) for shock-molten iron (27, 30, 31) and performed a linear fit using the following equation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5):[5]US=C0+λuP,

where λ is a constant, and Us, up, and C0 are in km/s. From the fitting results, the pressure-density (PH-ρH) Hugoniot relation of shock-molten iron was represented as (SI Appendix, Fig. S5):[6]PH=ρ0C02η/(1-λη)2,

where subscripts H and 0 denote Hugoniot and ambient conditions, respectively, and η = 1 – ρ0/ρH. This relation was used to calculate the bulk sound velocity (VB) of shock-molten iron along the Hugoniot (SI Appendix, Fig. S5) (57):[7]VB2=γ2ρHPH-P0+1-12γρH1ρ0-1ρHdPdρ.

The Grüneisen parameter (γ) was modeled using a common functional form:[8]γ=γ0ρ0/ρn.

By matching the calculated sound velocity with previous measurements above 260 GPa (SI Appendix, Table S3) (28, 29), the PH-VB-γ relations for molten iron along the Hugoniot were established (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

Due to the latent heat of melting, TH in the molten state can be determined by subtracting a temperature change (ΔT) from the extrapolation of TH in the solid state (71). We determined the PH-TH relation of molten iron by adjusting the ΔT to match the TH of solid iron (38, 40, 69) with our experimental data (Fig. 2). Notably, the P-ρ-T-VP-γ relations for molten iron along the Hugoniot were subsequently derived.

The entropy change (ΔS) of iron across melting was further determined as (71):[9]ΔS=CVΔT/Tom,

where Tom is the melting temperature of iron at its melting onset pressure (38).

Thermal Equation of State of Molten Fe.

We developed a thermal EoS of liquid Fe using a single EoS model based on the Mie-Grüneisen equation (44):[10]Pρ,T=PT0ρ+ΔPthρ,T,

where P(ρ,T) is the total pressure, which is the sum of the reference pressure PT0ρ at the reference temperature T0 and the thermal pressure correction ΔPth(ρ,T). T0 was set to the melting temperature of iron at atmospheric pressure (1,811 K). PT0ρ was calculated using the Vinet (Morse-Rydberg) equation (44):[11]PT0ρ=3KT0ρT0ρ-2/31-ρT0ρ1/3exp32KT0′-11-ρT0ρ1/3,

where KT0 and K’T0 are the isothermal bulk modulus and its pressure derivative at zero pressure and T0, respectively, and ρT0 is the density of liquid iron at T0. ΔPth(ρ,T) was expressed as (44):[12]ΔPthρ,T=ργΔEthρ,T=ργEthρ,T-Ethρ,T0,

where Eth(ρ,T) is the internal thermal energy, which was modeled as a second-order polynomial of temperature with a volume-dependent coefficient (44):[13]Ethρ,T=3NRT+e0ρ0ρgT2,

where N is the number of atoms in the formula unit, R is the gas constant, and e0 and g are constants. Based on the P–T-ρ data of molten iron from dynamic compression (SI Appendix, Table S4) and static LH-DAC measurements (24), the eight EoS parameters were determined by minimizing the error-weighted sum of the squared residuals between modeled and measured values. The uncertainty of the EoS parameter fit is ~1.0% at the 2σ level.

Sensitivity analyses indicate that the thermal reference energy parameters (e0 and g) have only a limited influence on the pressure dependence of density at high P–T, whereas the Grüneisen parameter, the isothermal bulk modulus, and its pressure derivative exert the dominant control. The Grüneisen parameter was independently constrained by combining the Hugoniot equation of state with sound velocities of molten iron, rather than being treated as a free fitting parameter. This strategy minimizes covariance among thermal pressure coefficients and avoids artificial trade-offs in EoS parameterization. Uncertainty propagation further shows that plausible variations in the Grüneisen parameter produce density deviations of less than ~0.1% over the investigated pressure range, an effect fully incorporated into the reported EoS uncertainty.

The Hugoniot dataset was primarily used to constrain parameters governing high-pressure density evolution, while the LH-DAC data provide additional leverage on e0 and g. Although the static data are obtained at lower P–T conditions (24), the associated uncertainties in e0 and g introduce negligible effects on density over the investigated P–T range. This combined strategy effectively reduces parameter trade-offs and yields a self-consistent thermal EoS that enables reliable thermal correction of molten iron densities from Hugoniot conditions to Earth’s outer-core conditions.

Thermal Corrections to Density and Sound Velocity of Liquid Iron along the Core Adiabat.

The adiabatic temperature of the outer core (TOC) was derived from the temperature at the ICB (TICB) using an adiabatic gradient model (71):[14]TOC=TICBρOC/ρICBγOC,

where ρOC is the density of the outer core along the adiabat and ρICB is the outer core density at the ICB (17); γOC is the Grüneisen parameter of 1.5 for the outer core (71). Using our thermal EoS, the density of liquid iron along the Hugoniot was thermally corrected to the adiabatic core geotherm. The uncertainty in the corrected density (σρ,total), without taking into account the uncertainty in the adiabatic temperature, is given by the following equation:[15]σρ,total=σρ,Hugoniot2+σEoS2+∂ρ∂T·σT2,

where σρ,Hugoniot, σEoS, and σT represent the uncertainties in the Hugoniot P-ρ, EoS parameters, and Hugoniot P–T, respectively. Thermal correction to VP of liquid iron along the adiabatic core geotherm was calculated through the Mie-Grüneisen theory (28, 53, 57):[16]ΔP=POR-PH=ργ3RTOR-TH+βe2ρ0ργTOR2-TH2, [17]VOR2=VB2+ργΔPρ2,

where VOR is the VP of liquid iron along the geotherm; βe is proportional to the density of electrons at the Fermi level; ΔP is the thermal pressure offset, representing the pressure difference between the Hugoniot and geotherm at a given volume. ΔP was obtained from our modeled thermal EoS, enabling the determination of the electronic contribution parameter and VOR. Similarly, without taking into account the uncertainty in the adiabatic temperature, the uncertainties in the corrected VP result from the uncertainties in the Hugoniot P-VP, EoS, and Hugoniot P–T:[18]σVP,total=σVP,Hugoniot2+σEoS2+∂VP∂T·σT2.

Since the temperature has only a small effect on the VP (44), the uncertainty in Hugoniot temperature would introduce an additional error of ~0.2% in the pressure range of 260 to 330 GPa.
