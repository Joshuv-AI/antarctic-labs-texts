Kaolin particle film repellent effect against the wild cochineal Dactylopius opuntiae and its impact on cactus pear health
Authors: Chaimae Ramdani, Karim El Fakhouri, Asma Tika, Mohamed Amine Sadeq, Oumaima Moustaid, Noamane Taarji, Mustapha El Bouhssini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOpuntia ficus-indica (L.), a cactus, a critical crop in Morocco, has been severely damaged by Dactylopius opuntiae since its introduction in 2014. This study evaluated the insecticidal and preventive effects of kaolin clay against D. opuntiae females and nymphs under laboratory and field conditions and assessed its impact on the physiological parameters of health and wettability of cactus cladodes. Laboratory cage experiments revealed that Kaolin-treated cladodes (30 g/L) had significantly fewer colonies (3.67) than water-treated controls (7.33) after 42 days, with stable evolution up to 60 days. Choice tests showed more nymphs on untreated cladodes (7) than on treated ones (3) after one day. No-choice tests revealed significantly higher nymph mortality on kaolin-treated cladodes (75 dead nymphs) compared to controls (21) by day 44. Field trials supported these findings, with treated cladodes showing only 16 colonies after 40 days compared to 35 on untreated ones. Kaolin also induced direct insecticidal activity, causing 62% and 74% nymph mortality three days after application at 30 g/L and 60 g/L, respectively. Female mortality reached 32% after five days at the double dose. In addition, Kaolin preserved greener cladodes with darker tissues, and higher chlorophyll levels, while infested cladodes showed chlorophyll loss and lighter color. Kaolin also transformed cladode surfaces from hydrophobic (contact angle: 111.11°) to hydrophilic (contact angle: 67°) after one day, with a decrease to 57° after 21 days. Introduction

The prickly pear, Opuntia ficus-indica (L.) Mill. (Caryophyllales: Cactaceae), is a type of cactus belonging to the Cactaceae family, which comprises 130 genera and around 2,000 species1. It is widely cultivated in semi-arid and arid regions2. It grows in areas with sandy soil, variable, low, and irregular rainfall, and dry, arid conditions unsuitable for conventional crops3–5. Introduced to Morocco in 1770, the cactus crop is now widely distributed across the national landscape. Moreover, due to prolonged drought, the area dedicated to its cultivation has significantly increased over the past two decades, growing from around 50,000 hectares in 1998 to over 150,000 hectares in 20176.

Cactus pear is highly valued as both a food and feed source, owing to its nutritional benefits. Furthermore, it has numerous applications in the pharmaceutical industry, enhancing its overall significance7. However, since 2014, the prickly pear crop in Morocco has been under attack by a sap-sucking insect pest, the wild cochineal Dactylopius opuntiae (Hemiptera: Dactylopiidae). This pest’s rapid and aggressive spread across various regions has led to significant socioeconomic impacts8. Various management strategies have been implemented to control cochineal9–14. However, the insect’s body is covered with a waxy surface that protects it against environmental factors, predatory insects, and even pesticides15. The best option to initiate control of cochineal is during the young stage (i.e., nymph I) before they develop the protective cottony layer. Both nymphs and adult females of D. opuntiae feed on the sap of plant cladodes, causing them to dry out, weaken, and ultimately die16.

The agricultural sector in Morocco is characterized by semi-arid to arid conditions, where crops are exposed to high temperatures and potentially irregular rainfall. These factors lead to regular periods of heat and water stress, which significantly reduce the growth and productivity of plants. These severe environmental constraints intensify evaporative demand, decrease photosynthetic efficiency, and promote rapid desiccation of plants17,18. In these conditions, kaolin-based particle film technology is a highly appropriate strategy, as its high reflectivity reduces the thermal stress on plant surfaces, decreases the temperature of plant cover and minimizes transpiration losses, thereby increasing plant resilience to drought and heat stress19.

In addition, Kaolin particle film technology has been proposed as an effective and eco-friendly alternative to synthetic insecticides for controlling arthropod pests in semi-arid to arid conditions, including the olive fly, Bactrocera oleae (Diptera: Tephritidae), on olive trees in Morocco20,21. This method is particularly useful for reducing chemical pesticide use, which can harm the environment and human health. Kaolin is an inert aluminosilicate mineral characterized by its white color, non-abrasive nature, non-toxic properties, and water-dispersibility, which makes it highly suitable for agricultural applications. Additionally, Kaolin is widely utilized in industries such as paints, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals due to its versatility and safety22. Previous studies by Glenn et al.21 and Puterka et al.23 have highlighted the efficacy of Kaolin as a physical barrier or repellent against various insect pests. These findings have led to the development of Kaolin-based particle film technology, an innovative, environmentally friendly, and effective approach to pest control. This method offers an effective tool for managing insect populations, reduces heat stress, and enhances the quality and yield of fruits and vegetables.

Kaolin forms a physical barrier on plant surfaces that impedes insect movement24,25, effectively repelling them and preventing them from laying eggs26,27. Thus, Kaolin has been found effective against a wide range of insect pests, including aphids28,29, olive fruit flies27, and pear psyllids21,23,30, fruit flies26,27, Lepidoptera22,31,32, and Coleoptera33,34. Beyond pest control, Kaolin has also positively influenced plants’ physiological and morphological characteristics. According to Gharaghani et al.35, Kaolin increases the quality and yield of crops, facilitates photosynthesis in the plant, and improves the overall physiological health of plants. Recently, Terán et al.36 also demonstrated that the Kaolin application confers several physiological advantages, including the reduction of foliar proline levels, increasing chlorophyll content, prevention of carotenoid degradation, improving physiological parameters, and reducing leaf damage and abscission.

Although kaolin has been the subject of numerous studies to assess its effectiveness on various species of insect pests, its mode of action on D. opuntiae remains unknown. In Morocco, no study has yet examined the biological mechanisms by which this film of particles acts on this pest, which is characterized by heavy waxy secretion, and strong surface adhesion mechanisms. There are major gaps in our understanding of how kaolin acts on the surface of cactus cladodes, whether it interferes with insect adhesion. This study fills these gaps and provides the first experimental data in Morocco on the properties of kaolin and its behavior with D. opuntiae, offering a fundamental approach that can contribute to the development of more effective and resilient cactus-specific management strategies.As such, this study aimed to assess the efficacy of Kaolin clay application as a preventive control strategy on D. opuntiae populations, focusing on its effectiveness in reducing infestation levels in both laboratory and field settings. In addition, the study aimed to assess the effects of Kaolin clay on cactus quality and physiological parameters, including changes in chlorophyll content and variations in color intensity that serve as indicators of health and vigor.

Materials and methods

Laboratory bioassays

Insect rearing

Non-infested young cladodes of a susceptible variety of O. ficus indica were cultivated in plastic pots (27 cm Ø × 24 cm H) using a substrate mixture composed of 1/3 soil, 1/3 sand, and 1/3 peat. The cladodes were initially placed in a greenhouse maintained at 30 °C, then exposed to strongly infested cladodes with D. opuntiae, collected at the UM6P experimental farm, BenGuerir (32° 13.08″ N. 7° 53.230 W). Each infested cladode previously infested with D. opuntiae was placed between two pots of healthy cladodes. After one month of infestation, the cladodes with established colonies of adult females were selected for kaolin treatment.

Repellent effect

The repellent effect of Kaolin was evaluated under controlled conditions maintained at a temperature of 25 ± 1 °C, a relative humidity (RH) of 75 ± 5%, and a photoperiod of 14:10 (light: dark). For the experiment, three cladodes of Opuntia ficus-indica from a susceptible cactus variety, each measuring 30 cm × 16 cm, were placed inside a glass cage (90 cm × 90 cm × 90 cm), including one cladode moderately infested with D. opuntiae based on the infestation scale of Silva37, as the infestation source and one cladode treated with Kaolin (ARGICAL PRO, Kaolin 99%, CMGP-CAS, Morocco) at the recommended dose of 30 g/L and one cladode sprayed with water as control. The cladodes were fixed inside the cage at a height of 30 cm from the top, with the infested cladode positioned centrally between the two treated cladodes (Water and kaolin treated). Colony development and establishment were then monitored until colonies became visible on the treated and untreated cladodes. The treatments were replicated three times, and each cage served as a repetition.

Choice test

Two pieces of cladodes of approximately the same size (6 cm × 6 cm) were placed inside a Petri dish (150 mm Ø) under controlled conditions maintained at a temperature of 25 ± 1 °C, an RH of 75 ± 5%, and a photoperiod of 14:10 (light: dark). The first piece was treated with water (control) or Kaolin (30 g/L), while the second piece was treated with water as a control. After two hours of treatment, ten first-instar nymphs of D. opuntiae of the same age (21 h) were introduced into the middle of the Petri dish, between the two pieces of cladodes, allowing the nymphs to choose between the Kaolin-treated or water-treated cladodes. The number of nymphs on each piece of cladode was then counted at 1, 2, 3, 6 h, and 1, 2, and 3 days post-treatment. A completely randomized design was adopted using nine replicates for each treatment.

No choice test

Freshly cut pieces of cladodes (6 cm × 6 cm) were treated with water (control) or Kaolin (30 g/L) and placed in Petri dishes (90 mm Ø). Ten first-instar nymphs of D. opuntiae of the same age (21 h) were then deposited on each treated cladode, using a completely randomized design with twenty replicates. The numbers of dead nymphs and those that have developed were recorded after 42 days of release to assess the effectiveness of the Kaolin treatment.

Insecticidal effect on females of D. opuntiae

The insecticidal activity of Kaolin was evaluated through contact application at 25 ± 2 °C temperature, 75% RH, and a 14:10 (light: dark) photoperiod. Briefly, ten mature females of D. opuntiae of the same age were individually transferred, using an entomological brush, onto cladodes of uniform size and placed inside a Petri dish (90 mm Ø). The females were then sprayed with different concentrations of Kaolin (7.5, 15, 30, and 60 g/L) or water (control), following a completely randomized design with five replicates. The mortality of adult females was recorded every 24 h for 8 days using a binocular microscope (Motic DM-143). Dead females exhibited a dark brown coloration, and their bodies appeared desiccated12.

Insecticidal effect on nymphs of D. opuntiae

Ten first-instar nymphs of D. opuntiae, all of the same age (21 h), were placed on cladodes cuts (6 cm × 6 cm) and placed inside a Petri dish (90 mm Ø). The nymphs were then sprayed directly with different concentrations of Kaolin or distilled water (control), following a completely randomized design with five replicates. Mortality was monitored every 24 h over an 8-day period. Dead nymphs did not move and showed changes in coloration, as described by Ramdani et al.12.

Field bioassays

Experimental site

The assessment of Kaolin’s repellent effect, with or without black soap treatment as an integrated pest management (IPM) approach against D. opuntiae, was conducted in various trials in an open cactus field during October–November 2020. The field trials were conducted in the Marchouch region (33° 56′ 10″ N, 6°69′ 21″ W). The cactus field was first infested in the mid-2017 season, with infestation levels predominantly varying from low to medium across the 2 hectares. Normal agronomic practices (i.e., rainfed) were followed for growing the cactus crop, with rows typically spaced about 3 m apart.

Repellent effect

The first experiment included a single treatment of the infested cladodes with water (control) or Kaolin (ARGICAL PRO from CMGP. CAS: Kaolin 99%) applied at (30 g/L), using a 2-L hand sprayer. A randomized complete block design was adopted with three replicates, where five uninfested cladodes were picked from each plot to be treated. The appearance of colonies developed on the cladodes treated with Kaolin or water was monitored daily after one month of treatment.

Combination of Kaolin and black soap

The second experiment consisted of an initial treatment with water (control) or a Kaolin (30 g/L), followed by treatment with black soap (60 g/L) upon the emergence of the first colonies of D. opuntiae. The black soap was applied on cladodes with a low infestation level (i.e., 1–25%) using a rating scale modified by Silva37. The non-infested cladodes treated only with water were considered as a control. A randomized complete block design was considered with four replicates. The average number of colonies was monitored for 40 days in the different treatments.

Phytotoxicity of Kaolin treatment

Effect on color intensity

The cladodes were initially cleaned to remove the developed colonies and/or kaolin, then cut into 8 conform pieces. The color measurements were performed using the KONICA MINOLTA CM-5 spectrophotometer (Japan), obtaining independent dimensions according to the CIELAB color space38. Here, L* (lightness) defines a range of colors that goes from white (0%) to black (100%). a* (+ red/-green) ranges from red to green, and b* (+ yellow/− blue) varies from blue to yellow. a* and b* both move in the two axes, forming a plane orthogonal to L*. They do not have specific numerical limits, and both are between negative and positive values. C*ab is the relative strength of color, chroma, or saturation of color: 39. Hue*ab refers to the dominant wavelength, which ranges from 0° to 360°.

Effect on chlorophyll content

Twenty mg of air-dried cladodes were mixed with 5 mL of acetone 80% (v/v), followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then recovered, and its optical density (Abs.) was measured at 663 nm and 646 nm for chlorophyll a and b, respectively. The absorbance readings were realized using a Shimadzu spectrophotometer (model UV-1900i, Japan) in the red absorption maximum, and all measurements were performed in the absorbance range of 0.3–1.0 to obtain correct Chl a, Chl b, and total chlorophylls values. The obtained optical density values were used to calculate the concentrations of photosynthetic pigments using Eqs. (1), (2), and (3)40.

Contact angle measurements

The contact angle measurements were performed using the sessile drop method (OCA 15EC, Filderstadt, Germany) over 28 days of repeated Kaolin application. The first application was performed at the beginning of the experiment (day 0), followed by a second and third treatment on day 7 and day 14, respectively. Briefly, small droplets of water (4 to 6 µL) were deposited on the surface of cladodes and recorded until reaching the apparent equilibrium of contact angles.

SEM observations

The dispersion of Kaolin particles on the surfaces of cladodes and insects was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Observations were performed with a Thermo Fisher Quanta FEG450 SEM equipped with an Everhart–Thornley Detector (ETD) for secondary electron imaging. Micrographs were captured at various magnifications: X400, X1000, X1500, and X2000 targeting the dorsal, legs, antennae, and abdomen part of the treated nymphs with Kaolin, and X120, X130, and X260 to check the cactus surface treated with Kaolin. The samples were mounted on stubs using carbon tape and coated with a thin carbon layer to ensure conductivity during SEM imaging. To assess Kaolin’s insecticidal and repellent properties on D. opuntiae nymphs, the distribution of Kaolin particles in their bodies was studied using SEM. This technique enabled the analysis and evaluation of Kaolin particle deposition, particularly on the legs, to better understand its effects on the nymphs’ mobility and behavior.

Data analysis

The statistical analyses of color coordinates and photosynthetic pigment content variation were performed using R software programs (version 4.1.2). The statistical tests performed were considered significant at p < 0.05 and highly significant at p < 0.001. The variance analysis (Two-way ANOVA) was studied using R Studio. For laboratory repellency tests under cages, the number of live and dead nymphs and colonies of females in different experiments were subjected to a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), where exposure days were used as a fixed factor. The means were compared by Tukey test at p < 0.05 using Genstat (22nd Edition, VSN Inter-national, UK). In addition, One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s Test were used to compare the effects of different doses of Kaolin on the percentage of mortality at each development stage of cochineal D. opuntiae and at the same time after treatment, which were considered as fixed factors.

Results

Laboratory repellency tests under cages

As shown in (Fig. 1), the number of nymphs on the check cladodes increased significantly (p < 0.05) on the 14th day of post-treatment, compared to the Kaolin-treated cladodes. By day 35, the control group reached an average of 10 nymphs/cladode, whereas the group treated with the Kaolin had approximately 5.3 nymphs/cladode. No significant variation was observed between the two groups after the 42nd post-treatment day.

Effect of Kaolin on nymphs’ choice test

On the first day after treatment, the control cladodes contained an average of 7 nymphs/cladode, whereas the Kaolin-treated cladodes contained only 3 nymphs/cladode (Fig. 3). This number remained constant (p < 0.05) until the end of the experiment, indicating a sustained deterrent effect of Kaolin treatment.

Effect of Kaolin and water (control) on the evolution of the number of nymphs up to 72 h after treatment. Different letters indicate significant differences based on the Tukey test (p < 0.05) at each time after treatment. N = 9.

Effect of Kaolin on nymphs’ no-choice test

A total of 60 dead nymphs were recorded on the Kaolin-treated cladodes after 21 days of post-treatment, compared to only 13.5 on the control cladodes. On day 44, the mortality increased to 75 dead nymphs on the treated cladodes and 21 dead nymphs on the control cladodes (Fig. 4). The number of emerged adult females was also significantly (p < 0.001) higher on the control cladodes (51 adults) compared to the Kaolin-treated cladodes (7 adults) (Fig. 5). This suggests that Kaolin treatment reduced the emergence of adult females on treated cladodes, compared to the control (water).

Field conditions

Experiment 1: repellent effect of Kaolin

In the field experiment, the number of colonies containing cochineal females was significantly higher on the control cladodes compared to the Kaolin-treated cladodes (Fig. 6). The first colonies appeared on water-treated (control) cladodes after 17 days post-treatment, starting with 3 colonies and increasing to 35 colonies by day 40. The colonies on Kaolin-treated cladodes did not appear until the 34th day of post-treatment, with an initial count of 2 colonies/cladodes on day 35 and 16 colonies/cladodes by day 40.

Evolution of number (means ± SE) of colonies of the wild cochineal on treated cladodes by Kaolin (30 g/L) and control (water) up to 40 days after treatment under field conditions. N = 5.

Experiment 2: Comparative effect of Kaolin in combination with 60 g/L black soap

The preventive application of Kaolin and the curative use of black soap led to significantly different infestation levels, demonstrating the effectiveness of each approach to pest control (Fig. 7). Colonies of D. opuntiae began to appear on cladodes treated with Kaolin (30 g/L) only 33 days after treatment. In contrast, colonies on cladodes treated with water appeared much earlier, 15 days after treatment. When the colonies began to form under conditions of low infestation on the cladodes treated with Kaolin, a curative treatment with black soap (60 g/L) was applied. This intervention eliminated newly established colonies containing females of D. opuntiae, preventing the infestation from spreading.

Evolution of number (means ± SE) of colonies of the wild cochineal on treated cladodes by Kaolin (30 g/L), Kaolin + black soap (60 g/L), and in the control (water) up to 50 days after treatment under field conditions. N = 5.

Effect of Kaolin application on cladodes color coordinates

The lightness (L*) ranged from 40.35 to 45.19% for the control cladodes (water), 38.97 to 45.93% for the Kaolin-treated cladodes, and 47.45 to 52.26% for cochineal-infested cladodes. All values were negative for the* parameter (+ red/-green), indicating a tendency toward green. The values ranged from − 7.650 to − 8.985 for the control, − 5.53 to − 8.92 for Kaolin-treated cladodes, and − 6.73 to − 7.41% for infested cladodes. The b* parameter indicated an increase in yellowing in water-treated cladodes and cochineal-infested cladodes for up to 30 days of the experiment. It decreased the b* values of cladodes treated with Kaolin. Regarding C*ab and Hue angle, the values ranged from 18.24 to 23.61 and from 107.18° to 113.38° for the infested cladodes, respectively. The chroma values ranged from 12.43 to 21.96 for the Kaolin-treated cladodes, and the Hue angles ranged from 113.28° to 116.45° (Supplementary Table S1).

Effect of Kaolin application on cladodes chlorophyll content

To better understand the mechanisms and physiological responses underlying chlorophyll degradation caused by cochineal infestation, a detailed quantitative assessment of photosynthetic pigment concentration was conducted in infested cladodes and those treated with Kaolin or water (control). The results revealed a decrease in chlorophyll pigment concentration in infested cladodes compared to both Kaolin-treated and control cladodes, aligning with their reduced green pigmentation (Fig. 8).

Correlations among CIELAB color parameters and photosynthetic pigment content

This analysis aimed to investigate the correlations among the studied parameters measured on cactus cladodes, including CIELAB color parameters (L*, a*, b*, ΔE, C*ab, and Hue°) and photosynthetic pigments (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total chlorophyll content) under different treatments (control, Kaolin-treated, and infested cladodes) (Fig. 9). The results showed no significant correlation between the CIELAB parameters and photosynthetic pigment concentrations (ns, p ≥ 0.05). However, significant (p < 0.05) correlations were observed among the CIELAB parameters and the photosynthetic pigment concentrations. Moreover, for the CIELAB parameters, Pearson’s test revealed highly negative correlations (p < 0.001) between b* and ΔE, L* and ΔE, and b* and a*, with correlation coefficients ranging from -0.74 to -0.94. In contrast, L* and b* exhibited a moderate positive correlation (p < 0.01) with a coefficient of 0.49. In photosynthetic pigments, highly significant (p < 0.001) correlations were observed between chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b, chlorophyll a and total chlorophyll, and chlorophyll b and total chlorophyll.

Effect of Kaolin on cladodes wetting

As shown in Fig. 10a, applying a single treatment of Kaolin (30 g/L) significantly decreased the contact angle, dropping from about 111° for a healthy, untreated cladode to 67° on the same day after application (day 0). The contact angle reached approximately 78° on day 1 and remained stable until the end of the 28-day period of measurement. After a second application of the same dose on day 7, the contact angle dropped to approximately 55° and then gradually increased to 73° by the end of the experiment. Lastly, the third application, performed on day 14, caused another significant (p < 0.05) decrease in the contact angle, reaching 53°, then gradually increasing until reaching a final value of 72°.

Analyse of SEM observation

Parameters such as particle size (0.5 to 1 µm), shape, arrangement, and adhesion to the insect surface were examined using SEM. As shown in (Fig. 10), Kaolin particles adhered uniformly to the nymphs’ entire body, with a notable concentration on the locomotor appendages (Fig. 11A,B,C,D). The small particles were evenly distributed, forming a homogenous layer. Additionally, a thin Kaolin film covered extensive areas of the cactus plant surface (Fig. 12A,B,C) without obstructing the plant’s stomata. These findings provide valuable insights into Kaolin’s dual role as a physical barrier and its impact on the interaction dynamics between D. opuntiae and its cactus host.

Discussion

Applying Kaolin particle films is a highly effective and environmentally sustainable alternative to conventional insecticides, making it an ideal component for integrated pest management (IPM) strategies41. Consequently, it has been applied previously in managing pest populations across various insect orders, including Coleoptera, Diptera, Hemiptera, and Lepidoptera. In this study, we investigated the effect of Kaolin treatment on reducing wild cochineal (D. opuntiae) populations under both laboratory and field conditions. The results demonstrated that Kaolin treatments significantly reduced populations and exhibited dual functionality, acting both as a physical repellent and an insecticide.

Regarding the repellent effect, the field trials showed that Kaolin protected cactus pear from cochineal infestation for 34 days after treatment. This suggests that kaolin coating acts as a barrier against the pest D. opuntiae, preventing its establishment and proliferation on the cactus pear. Our study on D. opuntiae provides new insights, as it is one of the few reports on the effectiveness of kaolin against this pest. These findings are consistent with previous studies, such as those by Saour and Makee27, which demonstrated the efficacy of Kaolin particle film in suppressing pest populations. Specifically, they found that applying Kaolin at a concentration of 5 kg per 100 L of water significantly reduced the populations of Bactrocera oleae (Diptera, Tephritidae), maintaining control for over 14 weeks. The repellent effect of Kaolin particles was also reported previously by Tacoli et al.42 who found that a 2% (w/v) concentration of Kaolin, used commercially with the trade name Surround® at a rate of 1000 L per hectare, effectively reduced the field populations of Empoasca vitis (Göthe) (Hemiptera: Cicadellidae) and Zygina rhamni Ferrari (Hemiptera: Cicadellidae) nymphs, under both preventive and curative treatments in vineyards. The preventive application of Kaolin provided long-lasting protection, with effects persisting for up to 4 weeks. Several studies have demonstrated the broad-spectrum efficacy of kaolin particle films against various insect pests. Mazor and Erez26 reported an almost total reduction in infestation by the Mediterranean fruit fly (Ceratitis capitata, Diptera: Tephritidae) on fruit treated with kaolin compared to untreated controls. Similar protective effects have also been observed against pear psyllids, various species of aphids, mites, leafhoppers, and root weevils21,33,34,43. In addition, it has been shown that the application of kaolin deters several harmful insects of the Lepidoptera family from laying their eggs22,31.In addition to its repellent effect, Kaolin demonstrated a good mortality rate of 62 up to 71% against nymphs of D. opuntiae using recommended (30 g/L) and double concentrations (60 g/L), respectively (Tables 1 and 2). This insecticidal effect of Kaolin has been reported in several studies, notably against leafminer (Liriomyza huidobrensis), green peach aphid (Myzus persicae) (Hemiptera: Aphididae)44, boll weevil (Anthonomus grandis) (Coleoptera: Curculionidae)45, and cotton bollworm (Helicoverpa armigera) (Lepidoptera; Noctuidae)46. The mechanism by which kaolin protects cactus pear in our study likely involves a combination of physical and behavioral effects. The white kaolin film forms a thin layer of particles on the cladodes, which is known to deter pests by altering the surface properties of the cactus plant and interfering with insect attachment, movement, and feeding. In this context, Glenn et al.21 proposed several mechanisms by which Kaolin may affect insect populations. Kaolin may act as an insect deterrent by discouraging oviposition, directly damaging insects’ different life stages, or interfering with their feeding behavior. In addition, Kaolin can form a protective physical barrier when applied to plants, disrupting the ability of insects to locate and interact with their host plants. This disruption occurs in several ways, such as altering the plant’s visual and tactile characteristics or impeding the insects’ movement and feeding as the particles adhere to their bodies, as shown in the current study (See Video in the supplementary materials).

The control cladodes presented more nymphs in the choice tests than the Kaolin-treated cladodes (Fig. 4). In a previous study conducted by Barker et al.47, the second-instar M. persicae nymphs also showed a strong preference for untreated (water-treated) calabrese (Brassica oleracea) leaf discs, compared to Kaolin-treated discs. Of the 200 nymphs observed, 104 favored the untreated side, while only 60 preferred the Kaolin-treated side. In the no-choice test, the results showed that the mortality of nymphs was significantly higher on Kaolin-treated cladodes, with 75 dead nymphs, compared to 21 on untreated controls after 44 days of post-treatment (Fig. 4).

Similarly, in a non-choice test, Larentzaki et al.48 reported that kaolin delayed the larval development of Thrips tabaci (Thysanoptera: Thripidae) and increased mortality to 51.6% compared to 6.6% in the control. The same study reported additional insecticidal effects, with direct kaolin application (60 g/L) causing 5% larval and 14% adult mortality, while no mortality occurred in the water-treated controls.

Interestingly, applying Kaolin impacted insect populations and induced physiological changes in plant tissues, as depicted by color intensity and chlorophyll content measurements. Comprehensively, the Kaolin-treated cladodes had a darker green color with decreased luminosity (L*) and increased hue angle (h°) compared to the untreated cladodes (water). Moreover, they presented significantly higher chlorophylls a, b, and total chlorophyll content than the infested cladodes and those treated with water (control). This could be attributed to an impaired chlorophyll synthesis upon cochineal infestation due to an inhibition of various biosynthesis enzymes and/or accelerated chlorophyll degradation49. Previous studies have indicated that the use of Kaolin has beneficial physiological effects on crops such as citrus50,51, grapevine52, and olive53. Particularly, the Kaolin application could prevent chlorophyll degradation, as reported by54, Nanos55, and Segura-Monroy et al.56. The kaolin particles formed a continuous film on the cactus surface without obstructing the stomata, which typically range from 43 to 57 µm in Opuntia spp.57, suggesting that the particle size of the kaolin formulation was sufficiently small to provide coverage while preserving gas exchange. Kaolinite particles typically range in size from 0.1 to 10 µm, forming hexagonal, platy crystals visible under electron microscopy58,59.

Applying Kaolin to the surfaces of the cladodes reduced water contact angle, as depicted in Fig. 10. Kaolin exhibits inherently hydrophilic properties, attributed to the presence of hydroxyl groups (–OH) on its surface60. These groups can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, promoting wetting and increasing the hydrophilicity of surfaces treated with kaolin. These results indicate that the repeated application of kaolin helped to maintain a hydrophilic surface on the cladodes; the limited increase of contact angle in the following days after Kaolin treatment could be explained by the partial detachment of Kaolin particles. This persistent coverage likely contributed to prolonging the effectiveness as a physical barrier against pest infestation, as confirmed by the entomological tests.

Gharaghani et al.35 reported that the Kaolin application is an ideal and cheap solution for protecting walnut trees against the damage caused by light and heat stresses. Hence, its application in cacti is highly recommended as a preventive treatment to mitigate abiotic and biotic stresses, especially against cochineal damage at different terms: (1) it enhances the greenness of the cladode’s chlorophyll color and protects them from insect damage, and (2) enhanced chlorophyll content under cochineal stress. Moreover, (3) it reduced the surface hydrophobicity of the cladodes, which could be beneficial for the application of aqueous pesticide formulations, and (4) it adsorbed efficiently on the surface of the cladodes, with slightly limited detachment until one month of post-application.

However, the requirement for persistent coverage of cladode surfaces with kaolin particle film necessitates multiple applications and improved coverage using specialized sprayer equipment with pressure operating under field conditions to safeguard both the entire cladode surface and the newly emerging cladodes. Moreover, its application may be limited in regions where frequent rainfall could wash off the coating.

Conclusion

In the present study, we demonstrated the effectiveness of Kaolin mineral powder as a sustainable and preventive approach to managing D. opuntiae infestations on cactus pear. The application of Kaolin involved several mechanisms, including its ability to alter the plant’s physical appearance, making it unrecognizable to the cochineal insects. In addition, the film acts as a physical barrier that disrupts the movement of the insects, preventing them from settling, feeding, and reproducing effectively. Furthermore, the direct application of Kaolin exhibited a significant mortality rate against nymphs of D. opuntiae, which is crucial to disrupting the cochineal lifecycle at an early stage.

These combined mechanisms highlight the potential of Kaolin-based treatments as an environmentally friendly and innovative strategy for controlling D. opuntia populations in cactus pear crops. They offer a practical alternative to chemical pesticides while minimizing environmental impact.
