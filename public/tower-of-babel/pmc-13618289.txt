Defect‐Rich CoNi Prussian Blue Analogues Enable Highly Selective Electrochemical Hydrogen Peroxide Production
Authors: Kai Sun, Yu Mao, Yongfang Zhou, Ziyun Wang, Geoffrey I. N. Waterhouse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDecentralized electrosynthesis of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) via the two‐electron oxygen reduction reaction (2e− ORR) offers a promising alternative to the traditional anthraquinone process, though developing non‐precious metal electrocatalysts with high activity, selectivity, and industrial durability remains challenging. Herein, we report a defect‐engineering strategy to synthesize CoNi Prussian blue analogues (PBAs) with precisely tunable [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy concentrations via kinetic trapping. Advanced synchrotron X‐ray diffraction and absorption spectroscopy (EXAFS) reveal that these vacancies transform the local coordination of adjacent nickel atoms from saturated octahedral geometries to unsaturated square‐planar Ni–N4 motifs. This structural modulation triggers a fundamental shift in the ORR pathway, delivering an H2O2 selectivity exceeding 97% and a remarkable production rate of 6.2 molgcat.−1h−1 in a flow‐cell device. Crucially, our defect‐rich catalyst demonstrates exceptional durability under a rigorous 120‐h variable‐current stability test. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations identify the coordinatively unsaturated Ni–N4 sites as the intrinsic active centers, which optimize the binding energy of the *OOH intermediate and suppress the four‐electron ORR pathway. This work identifies a robust H2O2 synthesis electrocatalyst and establishes a validated protocol for defect engineering in coordination frameworks.

Introduction

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is a green and versatile oxidant that finds widespread use in industrial bleaching, environmental remediation, chemical synthesis, and healthcare applications [1]. Its appeal lies in its high oxidative potential and environmentally benign decomposition pathway, yielding only water as the final product [2]. Currently, the global supply of H2O2 is dominated by the Riedl–Pfleiderer process (also known as the anthraquinone process), a multi‐step reaction pathway that requires centralized, capital‐intensive infrastructure, complex solvent recycling procedures, and significant energy input [3, 4]. Moreover, the obtained concentrated H2O2 solutions pose serious risks during storage and transportation due to the instability of H2O2 and its potential to decompose explosively under improper conditions [5]. These limitations have stimulated interest in alternative, decentralized methods of H2O2 production. Among these emerging strategies, the electrochemical two‐electron oxygen reduction reaction (2e− ORR) has gained considerable attention as a promising route to generate H2O2 from molecular oxygen and water under ambient conditions [6]. This approach offers a number of advantages, including low energy requirements, easy system integration, and the possibility of point‐of‐use production, thereby eliminating the need for long‐distance transport and storage [7]. However, the performance of 2e− ORR systems is currently hindered by the lack of efficient, selective, and durable catalysts. Noble metal‐based catalysts, such as PtHg4 [7] or Pd‐based alloys [8], often demonstrate good selectivity but suffer from high cost and poor durability in alkaline media. This motivates the continuing search for non‐precious metal, earth‐abundant catalysts with high selectivity, activity, and stability for the 2e− ORR.

Prussian blue analogues (PBAs) are a family of open‐framework transition metal coordination polymers constructed from cyanide‐bridged metal centers [9, 10]. PBAs have the approximate general formula AM2+[M3+(CN)6] [11], where A represents an alkali metal such as Li+, Na+, or K+; M2+ represents a divalent transition metal cation such as Fe2+, Co2+, Ni2+, Mn2+, or Cu2+; and M3+ represents a trivalent transition metal cation such as Fe3+, Mn3+, or Co3+ [12, 13]. Their highly ordered cubic structures, tunable compositions, and rich redox chemistry make PBAs attractive for various energy‐related applications [14], such as batteries [15, 16], electrochromic devices [17], and electrocatalysis [18, 19, 20, 21]. In particular, PBAs have been widely studied as cathode materials in sodium‐ion batteries [22, 23] and zinc‐ion batteries [24, 25] due to their large interstitial voids and robust frameworks that facilitate fast and reversible ion transport. To achieve optimal ion diffusion and cycling stability in battery applications, considerable effort has been devoted to synthesizing PBAs with high crystallinity, uniform morphologies, and minimal structural defects [26]. In these systems, a well‐ordered lattice is generally believed to be essential for maintaining efficient ionic channels and stable electrochemical performance [27]. As a result, defective PBAs have often been regarded as undesirable and have received limited attention. However, from a catalytic perspective, structural defects, such as cation/anion vacancies [28], distortions [29], or coordination asymmetries [30], can profoundly alter the electronic structure, surface reactivity, and adsorption properties for improved performance. The potential of defect‐engineered PBAs in the context of 2e− ORR has received minimal attention to date, motivating a detailed investigation.

In this work, we synthesized a series of CoNi Prussian blue analogues (CoNi‐PBAs) with different defect densities via a wet‐chemical co‐precipitation method. The materials were constructed using [Co(CN)6]3− as the primary coordination unit and Ni2+ as the guest transition metal ion, forming a typical cyanide‐bridged framework (with Co3+ coordinated by C atoms of cyano groups, and Ni2+ atoms coordinated by N atoms of cyano groups). By modulating the synthetic conditions, we were able to introduce controlled levels of [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies into the PBA crystal lattice, thereby generating a series of CoNi‐PBA samples with distinct defect concentration and structural characteristics. The morphology and crystallinity of the PBAs were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and selected area electron diffraction (SAED). Crucially, to move beyond qualitative descriptions and accurately quantify the defect density and probe the local coordination environment, we utilized high‐resolution synchrotron powder diffraction coupled with Rietveld refinement and X‐ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), including detailed extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) fitting. The electronic states were further probed by X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Finally, the electrochemical evaluations were conducted using rotating ring‐disk electrode (RRDE) techniques to determine intrinsic selectivity, alongside flow‐cell electrolyzer tests to assess the H2O2 production rate and device‐level stability under practical, high‐current operating conditions. Our results reveal a strong correlation between the abundance of [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies and 2e− ORR selectivity, with our most defective catalyst, denoted herein as CoNi‐PBA‐high, exhibiting an outstanding H2O2 selectivity exceeding 97% across a wide range of potentials. Furthermore, flow‐cell measurements confirmed that CoNi‐PBA‐high delivered the highest H2O2 production rate of 6.2 molgcat.−1h−1 among all tested samples, outperforming most recently reported 2e− ORR catalysts. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to gain deep mechanistic insights into the origin of the enhanced catalytic activity. The theoretical results suggest that the introduction of [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies creates coordinatively unsaturated Ni sites with a four‐fold square‐planar coordination (as opposed to the normal octahedral coordination), which serve as the primary active centers for selective 2e− ORR to H2O2. These defect‐induced electronic and geometric modulations facilitate the adsorption and activation of O2 and derived oxygen intermediates, thereby enhancing both the activity and selectivity toward H2O2 formation. By establishing a rigorous correlation between the [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy concentration and catalytic performance, this work not only identifies the square‐planar Ni–N4 moieties as the intrinsic active center for 2e− ORR but also provides a validated protocol for defect engineering in Prussian blue analogue frameworks.

Results and Discussion

To investigate the role of structural defects on electrocatalytic ORR performance, we first synthesized a series of CoNi‐PBAs with systematically varied [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy densities. Crucially, to ensure reproducibility and isolate the effect of defects, all samples were prepared using identical co‐precipitation conditions, with the defect density regulated by controlling the duration of the post‐synthetic aging process in the mother liquor [11]. The defect‐rich CoNi‐PBA‐high sample was isolated soon after precipitation, capturing a kinetically trapped framework with abundant [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies formed during rapid nucleation. Extending the aging time in the mother liquor allowed for thermodynamic structural reorganization: CoNi‐PBA‐med was obtained after aging at room temperature for 24 h, while CoNi‐PBA‐low was subjected to hydrothermal aging to drive the system toward a thermodynamically stable, defect‐minimized state. Consequently, the [Co(CN)6]3− defect concentration follows the order CoNi‐PBA‐high > CoNi‐PBA‐med > CoNi‐PBA‐low, governed by the balance between reaction kinetics and thermodynamic equilibration.

The morphological evolution of the CoNi‐PBA samples during the aging process was tracked using electron microscopy. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and elemental mapping (Figures S1 and S2) revealed all samples to possess a uniform cubic morphology (∼200 nm) and a homogeneous elemental distribution. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) exposed critical structural differences between the samples. As shown in Figure 1a, the kinetically trapped CoNi‐PBA‐high sample exhibits poorly defined crystal boundaries with rounded corners. Its corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern showed diffuse diffraction rings (Inset of Figure 1a), indicative of a low degree of crystallinity and a defect‐rich lattice. In stark contrast, as the aging period increased, the cubes became highly crystalline (i.e., approached thermodynamic equilibrium). The CoNi‐PBA‐med and CoNi‐PBA‐low samples (Figure 1b and 1c, respectively) display progressively sharper edges and well‐defined facets. Crucially, their SAED patterns evolve into clear, discrete diffraction spots, confirming highly ordered, long‐range crystalline frameworks possessing few defects.

To provide rigorous crystallographic verification of the defect structures in the three samples, we applied both powder X‐ray diffraction (PXRD) and high‐resolution synchrotron powder diffraction. As shown in Figure S3a and the enlarged view in Figure S3b, all samples displayed XRD patterns characteristic of face‐centered cubic PBA (PDF#22‐0215). However, peaks for CoNi‐PBA‐high showed a distinct shift toward lower 2θ angles. This lattice expansion suggests a framework relaxation induced by the high concentration of [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies in the sample (evidence for which is provided below) [31], consistent with the kinetically trapped, less crystalline nature of the sample revealed by TEM.

To quantify the [Co(CN)6]3− defects in the samples, we first performed inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) and elemental analysis (EA). Table S1 shows that the Co/Ni atomic ratio decreased significantly from 0.98 for CoNi‐PBA‐low to 0.81 for CoNi‐PBA‐high. Crucially, the decrease in the Co content in the samples was accompanied by a proportional decrease in carbon and nitrogen contents determined by EA, confirming the presence of [Co(CN)6]3− defects. This conclusion was further corroborated by spectroscopic analyses: both FTIR (Figure 1d) and Raman spectroscopy (Figure S4) showed a marked reduction in the intensity of cyano (C≡N stretching) vibrations for the defect‐rich CoNi‐PBA‐high sample, consistent with the loss of [Co(CN)6]3− units [32]. The absence of a pronounced C≡N stretching‐frequency shift further suggests that the remaining cyanide bridges largely preserve their original Co─C≡N─Ni bonding configuration. Thus, the vibrational spectra mainly evidence the reduced population of cyanide bridges and retention of the PBA framework, while the local metal coordination environments are examined below by X‐ray absorption spectroscopy.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Figure 1e) further revealed that the coordinated water content [14, 32, 33] in the samples increased significantly from 21.19% (CoNi‐PBA‐low) to 27.51% (CoNi‐PBA‐high). This excess water presumably fills the voids generated by the [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies, serving a critical dual role: it physically supports the framework to prevent structural collapse and, in the form of protonated clusters, chemically balances the lattice charge [34]. Integrating the TGA data with the elemental analysis results discussed above, we derived the precise quantitative chemical formula for CoNi‐PBA‐high as K0.28Ni[Co(CN)6]0.81·5.1 H2O (Table S2).

Finally, as the most critical structural evidence to verify our defect model, we performed high‐resolution synchrotron powder diffraction with Rietveld refinement on all three samples (Figure 1f). The refinement results (summarized in Table S3) confirm the trend observed in the XRD data: the cubic lattice constant increases systematically from 10.213(5) Å (CoNi‐PBA‐low) to 10.312(4) Å (CoNi‐PBA‐high). This lattice expansion provides crystallographic proof of the framework relaxation caused by the presence of [Co(CN)6]3− defects [31].

Crucially, we focused our refinement on the occupancy of Co sites to directly quantify the concentration of [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies. For the CoNi‐PBA‐low, the Co occupancy refined to 0.98(2), confirming a nearly defect‐free, perfect crystal lattice. In sharp contrast, for the CoNi‐PBA‐high sample, the Co occupancy dropped to 0.82(3). This value directly translates to a vacancy concentration of ∼18%, providing indisputable crystallographic evidence for the existence of abundant [Co(CN)6]3− defects. Notably, this structural quantification (∼18%) aligns perfectly with the composition derived from our chemical analysis (∼19%), firmly establishing the accuracy and reliability of our defect engineering strategy.

It should be noted that the vacancy concentration derived from Rietveld refinement represents a bulk‐averaged Co‐site occupancy and should not be interpreted as an exact surface active‐site density. Under the kinetically trapped growth conditions used for CoNi‐PBA‐high, surface or near‐surface enrichment of [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies is chemically plausible, because incomplete incorporation of the hexacyanocobaltate building units may occur preferentially at the crystal growth front before subsequent defect healing. Such possible surface enrichment does not contradict the bulk defect model; rather, it indicates that the catalytically accessible vacancy‐associated Ni sites may differ from the bulk‐averaged vacancy concentration.

Next, the valence states of Co and Ni in the various CoNi‐PBA catalysts were probed using X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X‐ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). The XPS survey spectra for all the catalysts showed the presence of Co, Ni, C, N, O and K (Figure S5), as expected for the family of PBAs studied in this work. The Co 2p XPS spectra for the CoNi‐PBAs showed peaks at 781.3 eV (Co 2p3/2) and 796.3 eV (Co 2p1/2) in a 2:1 area ratio (Figure 2a), corresponding to Co3+ in [Co(CN)6]3− units. For all samples, the binding energies for both the Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2 peaks were identical (as expected since all Co3+ sites in the CoNi‐PBAs should remain coordinatively saturated despite the varying [Co(CN)6]3− defect concentrations). The Co 2p peak areas were decreased by ∼20% for CoNi‐PBA‐high compared to CoNi‐PBA‐low, consistent with the abundant [Co(CN)6]3− defects in the former. To further verify the local coordination environment of the cobalt centers, we conducted Co K‐edge X‐ray absorption near‐edge structure (XANES) and extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses. As shown in Figure 2b, the Co K‐edge XANES spectra for all three samples are virtually superimposable, with the absorption edge positions being identical. This confirms that the cobalt atoms in the three samples maintain a stable low‐spin Co3+ oxidation state regardless of the [Co(CN)6]3− defect density. Consistent with this, the Fourier‐transformed (FT) k3‐weighted EXAFS spectra in R‐space (Figure 2c) exhibit two main peaks for all catalysts in the low‐R region (below 3 Å). The first peak, located between 1.0 and 2.0 Å corresponds to the single scattering path of the first coordination shell (Co─C). The second feature, located between 2.0 and 3.0 Å, represents a superposition of a Co─N single scattering path and linear Co─C─N / Co─C─N─C multiple scattering paths. The perfect linearity of the Co─C─N─Ni linkages in the CoNi‐PBA samples resulted in the Co─C─N and Co─C─N─C multiple scattering contributions having unusually high intensities. Furthermore, all three samples displayed an additional broad feature at higher R space (around 4.0–5.0 Å), which is tentatively assigned to the long‐range third‐shell Co–Ni metal–metal scattering in highly crystalline PBA frameworks, even though it falls outside the primary EXAFS fitting window (below 3 Å). The EXAFS fitting results (Table S4) further reveal that the Co atoms in all samples possess an average first‐shell coordination number of approximately 5.8, close to the theoretical value of 6 for a perfect octahedral coordination. These combined XPS and XAS results conclusively demonstrate that the Co centers in the three CoNi‐PBA samples remain electronically and geometrically unperturbed by the introduction of [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies.

In stark contrast to the similarity of the Co3+ centers, the Ni sites showed a pronounced sensitivity to the [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy concentration. The Ni 2p XPS spectra (Figure 2d) were deconvoluted into two primary chemical environments, together with some Ni2+ “shake‐up” satellites. The dominant component with Ni 2p3/2 ∼855.9 eV in all samples was assigned to Ni2+ in saturated Ni–N6 sites, characteristic of the perfect PBA lattice [35]. The second species at higher binding energies is associated with defect‐adjacent Ni centers. As the [Co(CN)6]3− defect density increased, the corresponding Ni 2p3/2 peak for the defect‐adjacent Ni species exhibited a progressive negative shift in binding energy, moving from 858.0 eV in CoNi‐PBA‐low to 857.7 eV in CoNi‐PBA‐med, and finally to 857.3 eV in the defect‐rich CoNi‐PBA‐high. This progressive reduction in binding energy signals an increase in local electron density at the Ni centers [36]. This electronic modulation is chemically intuitive: the removal of strong electron‐withdrawing cyanide (CN−) groups with [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy formation lowers the effective valence state of the adjacent Ni atoms, analogous to the reduction of metal cations adjacent to oxygen vacancies in oxide materials. The XPS findings strongly imply the emergence of coordinatively unsaturated Ni sites possessing distinct electronic properties with [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy formation. To move from this qualitative electronic assignment to a precise geometric quantification of the Ni coordination sphere, we subsequently performed detailed EXAFS fitting analyses.

The Ni K‐edge XANES spectrum (Figure 2e) of CoNi‐PBA‐high exhibited distinctly different features compared to the CoNi‐PBA‐med and CoNi‐PBA‐low samples. The pronounced white‐line broadening indicates symmetry breaking in the local Ni coordination environment, resulting from the transformation of octahedral Ni coordination to a square‐planar geometry due to the abundant [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies [37]. The absorption edge position for CoNi‐PBA‐high was at lower energy compared to the other two samples (Figure S6b), implying the presence of Ni in a slightly lower average oxidation state, thus matching the XPS findings. To further probe the Ni oxidation states, we analyzed the first‐derivative XANES spectra (Figure S7). Again, a lower average Ni oxidation state was seen for the CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst relative to its less‐defective counterparts, consistent with our preceding analyses.

To explicitly determine the local coordination geometry of the nickel centers, we fitted the Ni K‐edge EXAFS data in R‐space (Figure 2f and Table S5). The Fourier‐transformed spectra exhibit two dominant features in the 1.0–3.0 Å range. The first intense peak located between 1.0 and 2.0 Å corresponds to the single scattering path of the first coordination shell (Ni–N). The second feature, located between 2.0 and 3.0 Å, represents a superposition of the Ni–C single scattering path and the linear Ni–N–C/Ni–N–C–N multiple scattering paths. For the CoNi‐PBA‐med and CoNi‐PBA‐low samples, a long‐range Ni–Co metal–metal scattering peak was observed around ∼ 5.2 Å (tentatively assigned to a Ni–Co scattering path), which was almost absent for the CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst. The absence of this feature for CoNi‐PBA‐high reflects an absence of long‐range order, consistent with the high concentration of [Co(CN)6]3− defects in the sample.

A closer inspection of the Ni K‐edge spectra revealed a systematic shift of these peaks toward higher R‐values as the [Co(CN)6]3− defect density increased. Quantitative fitting confirms this lattice relaxation: the Ni─N bond length elongates from 2.05 Å in the highly crystalline CoNi‐PBA‐low catalyst to 2.11 Å in the defect‐rich CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst. Similarly, the Ni–C distance expanded from 3.19 to 3.25 Å. This bond lengthening is consistent with the macroscopic lattice expansion observed in XRD and synchrotron powder diffraction data, and is attributed to the structural relaxation induced by the incorporation of additional water molecules and protons into the [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy sites. Concomitant with this bond elongation is a marked reduction in peak intensity, indicating a decrease in coordination number (CN). The fitting results reveal that the average CN of the first Ni‐N shell decreased systematically from 5.54 (CoNi‐PBA‐low) to 5.35 (CoNi‐PBA‐med), and finally to 4.82 (CoNi‐PBA‐high). This quantitative reduction confirms that the introduction of [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies effectively strips the axial ligands from the adjacent Ni atoms, transforming the normal octahedral Ni2+ coordination into unsaturated, four‐coordinate square‐planar geometries.

In summary, our comprehensive structural analyses verified the successful construction of a series of CoNi‐PBA model catalysts with different [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy concentrations. By manipulating the competition between kinetic nucleation and thermodynamic aging, we modulated the bulk [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy concentration from negligible levels in the CoNi‐PBA‐low catalyst to ∼19% in the defect‐rich CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst. Crucially, these vacancies are not merely structural voids; their presence effectively transforms the local coordination geometry of adjacent nickel atoms from saturated octahedra to unsaturated square‐planar Ni–N4 motifs. This unique structural platform allows us to rigorously isolate the contribution of these under‐coordinated Ni sites to the electrocatalytic performance of the different catalysts.

Electrochemical H2O2 Production Tests

Rotating ring‐disk electrode (RRDE) measurements were conducted for all three samples in an O2‐saturated 0.1 M KOH electrolyte. Prior to testing, the collection efficiency (N) was calibrated to be 0.3759 (Figure S8). As shown in Figure 3a, the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) profiles reveal a distinct structure‐performance correlation. The CoNi‐PBA‐low catalyst exhibited the highest limiting current (∼0.95 mA) but the poorest H2O2 selectivity (∼40%, Figure 3b), indicating a preference for the four‐electron pathway typical of defect‐free PBA lattices. In sharp contrast, the defect‐rich CoNi‐PBA‐high containing abundant [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies and exposed square‐planar Ni sites demonstrated an outstanding H2O2 selectivity of approximately 97% across a wide potential range (0.2–0.6 V). This striking performance shift confirms that the transformation of the Ni coordination environment effectively steers the reaction pathway from a 4e− process toward a highly selective 2e− route. Notably, all three CoNi‐PBA catalysts exhibited similar onset potentials (∼0.77 V vs. RHE), suggesting that while the active site geometry controls selectivity, it preserves the intrinsic thermodynamic onset of the ORR process. For benchmarking purposes, commercial Pt/C tested under identical conditions showed negligible H2O2 selectivity (<10%), consistent with its established four‐electron reduction mechanism.

The electron transfer number (n), which serves as a crucial descriptor for determining the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) pathway, was calculated to further validate the selectivity trends observed in RRDE measurements. As presented in Figure S9, the CoNi‐PBA‐high sample exhibited an electron transfer number close to 2 over a wide potential range, providing strong evidence that the ORR process on the catalyst followed a two‐electron pathway leading to the selective production of H2O2. To assess the reaction kinetics, we further conducted a Tafel analysis for all three catalysts (Figure S10). CoNi‐PBA‐high exhibited the smallest Tafel slope of 68.61 mV dec−1, much lower than the values for CoNi‐PBA‐med (71.94 mV dec−1) and CoNi‐PBA‐low (80.63 mV dec−1). This result confirms that the defect‐rich CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst not only promotes a favorable 2e− reaction pathway but also facilitates faster charge transfer kinetics, underscoring its superiority as a two‐electron ORR electrocatalyst. Additionally, electrochemically active surface areas (ECSA) were estimated via double‐layer capacitance (Cdl) measurements (Figure S11). All three catalysts exhibited comparable capacitive behaviors, with the defect‐rich CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst showing the highest value of 9.1 mF cm−2, consistent with the increased exposure of surface sites in the defective framework.

To examine whether the different initial K+ contents could dominate the observed ORR trend, we conducted additional alkali‐cation exchange experiments by operating the catalysts in NaOH electrolyte followed by ICP‐MS analysis. Partial K+/Na+ exchange was observed for all samples, confirming that alkali cations can exchange under electrochemical ORR conditions. However, the original activity and H2O2 selectivity order was retained after this treatment, with CoNi‐PBA‐high remaining the most selective catalyst (Figure S12). These results indicate that the enhanced 2e− ORR performance is not primarily governed by a K+‐specific effect, but correlates more directly with the [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy concentration and the associated Ni coordination environment.

A cross‐correlation of catalyst structure and electrochemical performance offers critical mechanistic insights. As evidenced by the spectroscopic data, the cobalt centers in all the CoNi‐PBA catalysts retain a rigorous octahedral coordination and a Co3+ valence state. Consequently, the dramatic shift in ORR pathway—from a 4‐electron process on CoNi‐PBA‐low to a highly selective 2‐electron process on CoNi‐PBA‐high—cannot be attributed to the Co sites. Instead, this performance flip closely tracks the population of unsaturated square‐planar Ni centers.

To experimentally probe the role of vacancy‐associated under‐coordinated sites, we performed active‐site poisoning experiments using 1‐methylimidazole as a mild monodentate N‐donor ligand. After poisoning, CoNi‐PBA‐high showed the largest decrease in H2O2 selectivity and H2O2 partial current, whereas CoNi‐PBA‐low was only weakly affected (Figure S13). The inhibition trend followed the order CoNi‐PBA‐high > CoNi‐PBA‐med > CoNi‐PBA‐low, consistent with the increasing [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy concentration and the corresponding formation of under‐coordinated Ni–N4 motifs. Together, these results identify the vacancy‐induced Ni‐N4 motif as the key structural feature associated with the enhanced 2e− ORR selectivity.

To further assess the practical applicability and scalability of the CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst, we fabricated a gas diffusion electrode (GDE) and integrated it into a custom‐built flow‐cell electrolyzer. In this configuration (Figure S14), the catalyst‐loaded GDE serves as the cathode, separated from the anode by an anion exchange membrane, with the electrolyte (1.0 M KOH) continuously circulated to minimize diffusion limitations. Compared with traditional RRDE systems, this flow setup offers superior mass transport and high reaction interface areas, closely simulating industrial operating conditions. To bridge the RRDE screening results with the electrolyte condition used in the flow cell, we further performed RRDE measurements in O2‐saturated 1.0 M KOH (Figure S15). Although the absolute current response changed in the concentrated electrolyte, the H2O2 selectivity order remained unchanged, with CoNi‐PBA‐high > CoNi‐PBA‐med > CoNi‐PBA‐low. This confirms that the defect‐dependent 2e− ORR trend observed in 0.1 M KOH is retained under the alkaline concentration used for flow‐cell H2O2 production. Crucially, to ensure the rigorous quantification of the bulk H2O2 product during continuous electrolysis, we employed a cerium(IV) sulfate titration method coupled with UV–vis spectroscopy (Figure S16). This independent chemical analytical strategy (detailed in the Supporting Information) allows for the precise determination of accumulated H2O2 concentration, providing a robust cross‐validation of the electrochemical performance.

Figure 3c presents the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) profiles of the catalysts in the flow‐cell configuration. To rigorously validate the reproducibility of the performance metrics, three independent measurements were conducted on three separately fabricated electrodes, with the standard deviations represented by the shaded error bands. The CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst exhibits a much earlier onset potential and a steeper current rise compared to its counterparts, maintaining the highest current density throughout the potential window. At a practical potential of 0.15 V versus RHE, it delivers a robust current density of approximately 90 mA cm−2, far exceeding that of CoNi‐PBA‐med (∼68 mA cm−2) and CoNi‐PBA‐low (∼45 mA cm−2). This performance enhancement underscores the importance of abundant Ni‐N4 active sites in realizing high reaction rates.

The catalytic efficiency was further quantified by determining the H2O2 yield rate and Faradaic efficiency (FE) at various potentials. As shown in Figure S17, the CoNi‐PBA‐high electrode maintained a steady‐state current density of approximately 85 mA cm−2 at 0.15 V, showing no significant decay over time. In contrast, the CoNi‐PBA‐low electrode only sustained a current density of around 40 mA cm−2 under the same conditions, indicating inferior catalytic performance and possibly faster deactivation. We further analyzed the electrolyte at 15‐min intervals via the independent Ce(SO4)2 titration method to quantify the cumulative H2O2 production and determine the Faradaic efficiency (FE) over time (The corresponding raw UV–vis spectra are provided in Figures S18–S20). The results are summarized in Figure S21. All samples demonstrated stable Faradaic efficiencies throughout the 60‐min electrolysis period, confirming the consistent two‐electron ORR pathway. Impressively, CoNi‐PBA‐high maintained an average H2O2 selectivity of ∼97% over the full electrolysis test period. The cumulative H2O2 concentration in the electrolyte reached as high as 18 mM at 0.15 V, which is among the highest reported for Prussian blue analogues under comparable conditions.

The results of the 60‐min electrolysis experiments were further summarized and presented in Figure 3d, where the H2O2 production rates were also calculated to provide a clear quantitative comparison of the catalytic performance among the three CoNi‐PBA catalysts. As shown, the H2O2 selectivity of CoNi‐PBA‐high at various applied potentials remained largely consistent with the RRDE measurements, indicating that the two‐electron ORR pathway is reliably maintained even under continuous operation in the flow‐cell system. However, a remarkable enhancement in the H2O2 production rate was observed for all catalysts due to the improved mass transport and larger electrode area inherent to the flow‐cell configuration. Notably, the CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst delivered the highest production rate, reaching 6.2 molgcat.−1h−1 at 0.15 V versus RHE. This represents a substantial advancement compared to the less defective counterparts, further confirming that the introduction of [Co(CN)6]3− vacancies significantly boosts the availability of active Ni–N4 sites favorable for selective H2O2 generation.

While standard constant‐current stability tests are routinely reported, they fail to capture the dynamic fluctuations inherent to real‐world renewable energy inputs (e.g., solar or wind power). To rigorously evaluate the industrial viability of the CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst under harsh and non‐steady‐state conditions, we subjected the CoNi‐PBA‐high electrode to a demanding 120‐hour variable‐current stability protocol (Figure 3e). In this stress test, the current density was sequentially stepped up from 60 to 80 and then to 100 mA cm−2, holding for 24 h at each stage, before returning to the baseline. To mitigate the risk of excessive H2O2 accumulation and potential decomposition during this prolonged operation, the standard reservoir was replaced with a large‐capacity 1.0 L recirculation tank. The electrolyte was sampled and replenished every 12 h to quantify the H2O2 product concentration, with the corresponding raw UV–vis absorption spectra presented in Figure S22.

Remarkably, the CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst exhibited exceptional voltage stability at each tier, rapidly reaching equilibrium without any obvious polarization increases. Even at the peak current of 100 mA cm−2, the Faradaic efficiency remained consistently high (>92%), demonstrating that the selective 2e− pathway is robust even under high‐rate turnover conditions. Most critically, upon returning the current density to 60 mA cm− 2 after 120 h of intense operation, the operating potential recovered almost exactly to its initial value. This indicates that the catalyst suffered no irreversible degradation or mass transport blockage during the high‐current excursions. This macroscopic robustness is rooted in the structural integrity of the material. Post‐catalysis characterization (Figures S23–S25) reveals that the cubic morphology (SEM), crystalline phase (XRD), and surface metal electronic states (XPS) of the electrode were well preserved after 120 h of operation. In particular, the Ni 2p and Co 2p spectra of CoNi‐PBA‐high after the stability test show no obvious peak shifts or emergence of NiOx/NiOOH‐like or CoOx/CoOOH‐like features, supporting the absence of pronounced surface reconstruction under the tested ORR conditions.

Finally, we benchmarked CoNi‐PBA‐high against state‐of‐the‐art alternatives (Figure 3f and Table S6). As illustrated in the performance scatter plot, our catalyst occupies the favorable upper‐right quadrant, achieving a rare synergy of high H2O2 selectivity (>96%) and industrial‐level productivity (6.2 mol gcat.−1h−1, corresponding to 1.84 mmol cm−2 h−1; Table S7). Furthermore, the stability comparison draws attention to our rigorous 120‐hour variable‐current protocol. This test confirms that CoNi‐PBA‐high possesses the essential structural resilience to withstand dynamic operating fluctuations, proving its exceptional adaptability for future renewable energy‐driven H2O2 electrosynthesis.

To decipher the reaction mechanism, we mapped the full free energy profiles for both 2e− and 4e− ORR pathways on all potential active sites (Figure S26). The analysis of the initial O2 chemisorption step immediately distinguishes the intrinsic activity of the sites. Specifically, the Perfect_Ni_site exhibits an endothermic O2 adsorption process, rendering it thermodynamically inert for initiating the ORR [38]. Consequently, the reaction is dominated by the remaining active sites, where the selectivity is dictated by the competitive pathways at the *OOH bifurcation point. To rigorously quantify this competition, we defined a thermodynamic descriptor, |ΔG| = G(H2O2)—G(*O), representing the thermodynamic driving force for O─O bond scission relative to product desorption [38, 39]. A larger |ΔG| implies a stronger tendency to break the O─O bond (favoring H2O), while a smaller value suggests the preservation of the peroxide species. As shown in Figure S26, both perfect and defective Co sites exhibit extremely large |ΔG| values (1.44 and 1.39 eV), indicating that they act as thermodynamic sinks for the 4e− pathway. In contrast, the Defective_Ni_site displays the smallest |ΔG| = 0.44 eV among all active centers. This minimized thermodynamic driving force for bond cleavage effectively suppresses the deep reduction to *O, thereby prioritizing the desorption of H2O2. This theoretical finding perfectly rationalizes the experimental trend where a higher concentration of defect‐engineered Ni–N4 sites correlates directly with superior H2O2 selectivity.

To quantify how this initial interaction dictates the catalytic thermodynamics, we analyzed the Gibbs free energy diagrams at the equilibrium potential (U = 0.695 V vs. RHE, Figure 4c). At this potential, the ΔG of O2 → *OOH should be close to zero for the optimal 2e ORR catalysts, which is equivalent to the adsorption energy of *OOH being ∼4.23 eV [40]. The weak O2 adsorption on the Perfect_Ni_site translates directly into a prohibitively unstable *OOH intermediate (ΔG = 0.48 eV), confirming that the reaction is thermodynamically limited by the formation of the hydroperoxyl species. Conversely, the Co sites exhibit the opposite extreme: they bind the intermediate too strongly (deep potential well), which hinders product desorption and surface regeneration. Most notably, the introduction of vacancies in the CoNi‐PBA‐high sample effectively eliminates the initial adsorption barrier observed in Figure S26b. This structural engineering optimizes the *OOH binding energy to a near‐ideal value of ΔG = 0.15 eV. This “just‐right” binding strength, governed by the Sabatier principle [41, 42], perfectly balances the facilitation of intermediate formation with the ease of product desorption, identifying the defect‐engineered square‐planar Ni–N4 site as the intrinsic active center.

The electronic origin of this boosted activity was further corroborated by structural and electronic population analyses. Upon adsorption, the central Ni atom in the defective site undergoes a structural relaxation, being slightly pulled out of the N4 plane due to the absence of axial coordination. This geometrical flexibility significantly facilitates charge redistribution. As quantified by differential charge density analysis, the Defective_Ni_site transfers a substantial amount of electrons (0.23 e, Figure 4e) to the *OOH intermediate, whereas the charge transfer on the Perfect_Ni_site is negligible (0.04 e, Figure 4d). This enhanced electron donation is further confirmed by the Crystal Orbital Hamilton Population (COHP) analysis (Figure 4f), which quantifies the relative contribution of bonding and antibonding states to the metal–oxygen interaction. The Ni─O bond on the defective site exhibits a more negative integrated COHP (ICOHP) value of −1.55 eV compared to −1.06 eV for the perfect site, indicating a higher fraction of occupied bonding orbitals and thus a stronger Ni─O bond. These results provide robust evidence that the vacancy‐induced electronic modulation strengthens the covalency of the Ni–O interaction, thereby stabilizing the key intermediate and driving the highly efficient 2e− ORR.

Conclusions

In summary, we have successfully constructed a series of CoNi‐PBA electrocatalysts with different [Co(CN)6]3− vacancy concentrations by modulating the interplay between nucleation kinetics and thermodynamics during catalyst synthesis. By combining high‐resolution synchrotron powder diffraction, X‐ray absorption spectroscopy, and quantitative chemical analysis, we established a direct correlation between bulk lattice vacancies ([Co(CN)6]3− defects) and the emergence of specific local coordination environments (square‐planar Ni–N4 sites). Electrochemical evaluations together with theoretical calculations identified the exposed Ni–N4 sites as the intrinsic active center for the 2e− ORR, which optimizes the electronic structure to lower the adsorption barrier of the *OOH intermediate, thereby thermodynamically prohibiting the 4e− pathway. Consequently, the defect‐rich CoNi‐PBA‐high catalyst delivered an exceptional H2O2 yield (6.2 mol gcat.−1h−1) in a flow‐cell electrolyzer and remarkable robustness at industrial‐level current densities (no deterioration over a 120‐h test period). This work not only underscores the immense potential of defect engineering in enhancing the electrocatalytic performance of PBAs, but also provides mechanistic insights and synthetic guidelines for the molecular‐level design of high‐performance electrocatalysts for green H2O2 synthesis.
