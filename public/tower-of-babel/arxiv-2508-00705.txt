An Online Data Analysis Framework for Accelerator-Based Physics Experiments Journal: Nuclear Instruments and Methods A
H. Ramm, P. Simon, P. Alexaki, C. Arran, R. Bingham, A. Goillot, J. T. Gudmundsson, J. W. D. Halliday, B. Lloyd, E. Los, V. Stergiou, S. Zhang, G. Gregori a and N. Charitonidis d

Abstract
A robust and flexible architecture capable of providing real-time analysis on diagnostic data is of crucial importance to physics experiments. In this paper, we present such an online framework, used in June 2025 as part of the HRMT-68 experiment, performed at the HiRadMat facility at CERN, using the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS) beam line. HRMT-68 was a fixed-target laboratory astrophysics experiment aiming to identify plasma instabilities generated by a relativistic electron-positron beam during traversal of an argon plasma. This framework was essential for experimental data acquisition and analysis, and can be adapted for a broad range of experiments with a variety of experimental diagnostics. The framework’s modular and customizable design enabled us to rapidly observe and extract emergent features from a diverse range of diagnostic data. Simultaneously, it allowed for both the introduction of new diagnostic devices and the modification of our analysis as features of interest were identified. As a result, we were able to effectively diagnose equipment malfunction, and infer the beam’s response to varying bunch duration, beam intensity, and the plasma state without resorting to offline analysis, at which time adjustment or improvement would have been impossible. We present the features of this agile framework, whose codebase we have made publicly available, which can be adapted for future experiments with minimal modification.

Introduction and Motivation

Rapid data acquisition is imperative for modern experimental physics. With the advent of the High-Luminosity upgrade to the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) (HL-LHC) at CERN, it is estimated that ATLAS’s Trigger and Data Acquisition (TDAQ) systems will be capable of processing data at an input rate of
4~\text{TB/s}

[
1
]
. Such extensive upgrades to data collection at CERN have already been introduced during the run-up to Run 3 of the LHC
[
2
,
3
]
, which began in 2022. Furthermore, experiments at CERN often rely on observing events of exceptional rarity, with the creation of the Higgs boson occurring only approximately once in every
10^{9}
proton-proton collisions
[
4
]
. The data extracted from these events require extensive analysis, and the volume of data collection has often necessitated the use of sophisticated architectures, such as neural networks
[
5
,
6
]
.

Even in smaller experiments, this scope of data collection, and the often-small fraction of shots occupied by events of interest, motivates the construction of software frameworks not only capable of discerning events of interest among a large quantity of data, but also of performing analysis and extracting features of interest during experimental runtime. This ability to perform data analysis in real-time is of immense value in cases where offline analysis would fail to identify faults such equipment failure or beam misalignment until long after the experiment’s conclusion.

In this paper, we present such a robust, flexible, and reusable framework for online data analysis of experimental diagnostics, first employed during the HRMT-68 experiment performed at the HiRadMat facility
[
7
]
at CERN, part of the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS) accelerator
[
8
]
. This was a laboratory astrophysics (see the discussion of recent advances in laboratory astrophysics by Savin
et al.

[
9
]
) experiment, which aimed to study plasma instabilities
[
10
,
11
]
produced by an electron-positron pair beam
[
12
]
traversing an argon plasma
[
13
]
. This framework places an emphasis on readability and modularity, making it easily-recyclable for a use in a diverse range of future experiments. Authored in Python, with the option to incorporate a JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) configuration file (see Section
4
), it conforms rigidly to the object-oriented programming (OOP) paradigm for maximized encapsulation and abstraction
[
14
,
15
]
, producing a software package that can be easily utilized by those with minimal programming expertise. The resulting framework executes most of its complex data extraction and analysis pipeline without directly interfacing with the user; this “under-the-hood” operation is the framework’s primary strength.

The paper is outlined as follows: the motivation and experimental layout of the HRMT-68 experiment is discussed in Section
2
. We then present our methods of continuous data acquisition in Section
3

4

5

2.1
B-dot Probe Oscilloscopes

Magnetic field diagnostics for the plasma were provided by three B-dot probes, named for the overdot denoting differentiation of the magnetic field with respect to time:
\dot{\mathbf{B}}

[
19
]
. These probes measure the magnetic field strength via a practical application of Faraday’s Law
[
20
]
,

{\cal E}=-\frac{\text{d}\phi}{\text{d}t}=-N\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int_{S}\vec{dS}\cdot\vec{B}=-N\int_{S}\vec{dS}\cdot\dot{\vec{B}}

(1)

where
{\cal E}

is the e.m.f. induced in the coil,
N
is the number of turns in the B-dot probe coil,
\vec{B}
is the observed magnetic field vector, and
\vec{dS}
represents an infinitesimal vector area element of a single turn of the coil. Magnetic field strength can thus be inferred via time-integration of the induced voltage in the coil (for a more detailed derivation, we point the reader to the work of Bose
et al.

[
19
]
).

The e.m.f. induced in the B-dot coils was measured using two four-channel oscilloscopes (Tektronix 6 Series MSO
[
21
]
). Of the eight oscilloscope channels, seven were used to collect data from the B-dot probes, with the eighth channel left unused.

Two oscilloscope channels were allocated to each B-dot probe in order to distinguish between inductive and capacitive signals; a relative
180^{\circ}
coil rotation with respect to the magnetic field lines is introduced between the two channels, resulting in induced voltages of opposite sign. Therefore, subtraction between each probe’s two channels is expected to produce a nonzero signal for inductive voltages, and zero for capacitive voltages, and formed a key part of the analysis.

Essential also for analysis was the extraction of both time and voltage amplitude data (“samples”) from the oscilloscope outputs. Time data included the sample number (
N
) and time step (
\delta t
) between samples, in addition to the timestamp of each sample. This was used to calculate the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) of each channel’s voltage data in order to image the magnetic field in both the time and frequency domains.

2.2
Chromox and Radiation-Hard Cameras

Visual diagnostics were provided by four screens coupled with optical cameras. Additionally, one extra radiation-hard camera provided a live feed of the plasma. This device produced no quantitative data, but was instead used to identify any plasma instabilities during plasma generation and therefore was not included in the online data analysis framework. Due to its role as a largely heuristic diagnostic, the radiation-hard camera’s images were not collected or saved, and no analysis was performed on this device’s output.

Four screens made of chromox, a chromium-doped alumina ceramic (

2

3

\vec{\mu}=[\bar{x},~\bar{y}]=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}~I(p_{i})\times[x_{i},~y_{i}]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}~I(p_{i})}

Plots of intensity as a function of the Cartesian

\vec{\sigma^{2}}=[\sigma_{x}^{2},~\sigma_{y}^{2}]=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}~([x_{i},y_{i}]-\vec{\mu})\circ([x_{i},y_{i}]-\vec{\mu})~I(p_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}~I(p_{i})}

(3)

where

is the total number of pixels,
I(p_{i})
is intensity of the
i\text{th}
pixel, and
[x_{i},~y_{i}]
is the pixel coordinate. In Equation (

denotes the element-wise product between two matrices.

Two additional sums were performed over the polar radial (
r

\phi

r_{i}=\sqrt{x_{i}^{2}+y_{i}^{2}}
and
\theta=\arg(z_{i})

Two further chromox screens were introduced outside of the cell, beyond the dipole magnet: these screens were used to infer the electron and positron energy spectra of the beam after exiting the plasma cell. Thus, these two cameras were referred to as the “spectrometer cameras”. For these cameras, the code was designed to perform background-subtraction, ensemble averaging, and integrals over a specified axis.

2.3
Streak Unit Camera

For any initial velocity, charged particles emit optical transition radiation (OTR) when passing between two media of differing dielectric constant; the variation in electric and magnetic fields across this boundary results in this radiative emission
[
24
,
25
]
. The collection of photons produced via OTR is often used in beam diagnostics to characterize the longitudinal density profile of accelerator beams
[
26
,
27
]
. OTR was collected by a digital camera mounted to a Hamamatsu streak unit
[
28
]
with an adjustable slit width, producing data which can be encoded in a 2D image containing information about the pulse duration of incident radiation. In addition to background-subtraction and ensemble averaging, the code was designed to allow integrals over a single axis of the image. These were produced by summing across the axis of the image perpendicular to the temporal axis, as per Equation (
4
).

\vec{I}_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{x}}~M_{ij}

(4)

where
N_{x}
is the number of pixels corresponding to the image’s
x
-dimension (column number), and
M
is a matrix of dimensions image pixels, with each element of the matrix corresponding to pixel intensity. This produces
\vec{I}_{t}
, a
1\times N_{y}
matrix, with
N_{y}
denoting the image’s
y
-dimension and hence the number of time steps captured in the streak image, and where each entry in
\vec{I}_{t}
is therefore an approximate radiation intensity at each time step.
\vec{I}_{t}
is then used to produce an intensity versus time plot. The code was therefore constructed to both produce these plots, and a visual plot of the 2D image captured by the camera attached to the streak unit.

Synchrotron radiation is produced by the deflection of charged particles in magnetic fields

Data Collection

Collection and logging of device data was potentially the most challenging aspect of the online framework. Data from each of the experiment’s diagnostics (enumerated in Section
2
) was collected into a centralized repository, with the online analysis framework constructed to accommodate this and read each device’s data from a specified location (see Section
4
).

3.1
Construction of a Centralized Data Repository

CERN’s sophisticated Accelerator Data Logging Service (NXCALS)
[
33
]
, which necessitates the existence of a special front-end, designated ‘‘Front-End Software
Architecture’’ (FESA)
2
2

2

See
https://confluence.cern.ch/spaces/viewspace.action?key=FESA3
.
, was developed for LHC and its injectors, and is extremely versatile and adaptable. However, the versatile hardware used by this service, designed to accommodate a wide berth of accelerator-based experiments, was not sufficiently specialized for implementation in HRMT-68. This necessitated a system of logging and analysis external to the FESA/NXCALS framework.

This motivated the construction of an easily-accessible, experiment-specific central repository of data collected during the HRMT-68 campaign. In our case, we profited from CERN’s online Jupyter environment, Service for Web-based ANalysis (SWAN, described in the CERN SWAN documentation
[
34
]
), which allows users to run code from any location, with direct access to CERN’s EOS Open Storage (EOS)
3
3

3

EOS acronym is recursive.
-CERNbox online file storage configuration; EOS is CERN’s disk storage system, with a storage capacity of
\sim 780~\text{PB}

[
35
]
, and CERNbox is CERN’s cloud-based file-sharing and storage service
[
36
]
. Data from each diagnostic was assigned a unique directory in this central data repository, which was pointed to by the code’s configuration (as described in Section
4
). However, this framework would work equally well with any cloud-based or local file storage service.

In order to sync device data to the experiment’s CERNbox repository, each device was configured to either save data directly into CERNbox via a mounted network drive, or to a local directory synced continuously with the appropriate device directory on CERNbox, depending on device. The speed of the synchronization process varied greatly between devices, from
\lesssim 5
seconds for devices with access to a mounted network drive, to
\sim 30

4
Code Structure and Design

The goal of this project was to construct a set of analysis codes which could perform analysis on the output data for all devices on the experiment as soon as the data was available on a centralized repository. This demanded both flexibility (the ability to introduce and remove diagnostics at will) and specificity (with each device requiring its own unique analysis and processing). The codebase was written in Python 3.11.9.

Figure 3:
Schematic tree of the framework’s hierarchical structure, with modules shown in square boxes. Care is taken to distinguish device-agnostic modules (responsible for collecting file names from directories) from device-specific ones, where derivative classes for different device species were implemented to account for differing file formats and extensions, number of channels of data, and different plotting/analysis requirements for each device.

The code’s hierarchical structure, shown schematically in Figure
3
, was chosen to allow a high degree of modularity, addressing both the issue of flexibility and the need to specify analysis individually for each device.

The code’s high degree of modularity and class-based structure also allowed members of the collaboration not directly involved in the code’s development to sample code piecewise from the analysis pipeline to perform their own specialized analysis in parallel with the online data collection.

4.1
Configurability

The code was designed to read commands from an input configuration (either via JSON file or a manually-edited “input configuration” dictionary in the Python environment) to maximize readability and customization. The JSON format is simple, has minimal syntax, and closely resembles plain English. The configuration file was its ability to reduce the proliferation of hard-coded values in the framework which would need to be changed between runs, a process prone to human error. In the input configuration, users could specify shot numbers of interest and/or request statistical averaging across these shots. As depicted in Figure
4
, the user is able to specify “background” data to be subtracted from data of interest. The configuration allowed for displaying images without performing any analysis to expedite execution speed; quantitative assessment of how this contributed to the code’s execution speed can be found in table
2
.

Figure 4:
Schematic showing the code’s image averaging pipeline, including how multiple background shots could be combined into a single “composite background”. Multiple foreground shots could also be combined into a “composite foreground” shot. Subsequent analysis is performed on the output of the subtraction between the composite images.

4.2
Modularity

The code’s modular structure, depicted in Figure
3
, was key to its adaptability. As experimental goals were modified, or regions of interest identified, quick refactors could be made to analysis methods without disrupting the data collection and extraction processes. This allowed for a “plug-in-and-play” model: collaborators external to the project could implement their own analysis code, done independently and without any knowledge of the HRMT-68 online analysis framework, by simply inserting their extraction and analysis methods into the relevant modules in the framework.

The
file manager
module is tasked with creating an index of file
names
and their corresponding upload time, and this index is converted into a time-ordered index of file
paths
by the
path manager
. The
run
and
load manager
modules have two subclasses, one each for handling time-domain waveform data from oscilloscopes, and for handling bitmap image data from cameras. All device-specific analysis during runtime is controlled by the
run manager
, including statistical averaging and error propagation. The
load manager
subclass that the code calls is device-specific to account for the different file types and data logging structure of each device. All analysis and data visualization after the relevant data is extracted and preprocessed is handled by the
operations manager
, after which the code terminates.

Performance and Results

In this section, we quantify the code’s performance when producing analysis and/or visualization of diagnostic device data. For these tests, we tested the code with data from: the OTR streak unit camera, synchrotron spectrometer camera, a single chromox camera (the plasma cell camera monitoring the upstream chromox screen, see Section
2
), and oscilloscope 1 (see Section
1
).

In Section
5.1
, we present a summary of the code’s performance in Tables
2
and
3

[

[
40

\sim 200~\text{MB/s}

All tests were performed with the background subtraction routine - this was done by averaging over 5 shots designated as background to create a composite background image, which was then subtracted from the foreground image. This is in order to demonstrate the speed of execution even when a larger computational load is demanded of the code. For shot averaging, background removal was also performed, with the same 5 background shots selected to produce the background composite. The foreground image was produced by averaging over five shots, meaning that the code was required to process 10 images during runs with foreground averaging. Each entry of execution time in Table
2
is an average over 10 trials.

As mentioned previously in section

From the tests of code speed displayed in Table
2
, we conclude that the foreground-averaging in the absence of analysis methods introduces negligible additional overhead for the chromox camera and B-dot probe data. In the former case, execution time for foreground-averaging only introduces a
\sim(24\pm 12)~\%
increase in execution time, whereas effects to execution time for displays on the B-dot probe oscilloscope execution time are statistically negligible, and indeed execution time actually decreased, changing by
\sim(-3\pm 30)~\%
when averaging was introduced. We conclude that the code’s statistical averaging methods are not the limiting factor, and that instead variations in connection/download speed between clusters accessed via SWAN and the CERNbox service, affected by network load and user demand, are far more likely to be responsible. However, due to user restrictions on the SWAN service, network round-trip time (RTT) between SWAN and CERNbox could not be tested via pinging the SWAN domain.

Inclusion of analysis methods, however, did introduce significant overhead in the execution process- in the case of the chromox cameras: this resulted in percentage increases of
\sim(540\pm 40)~\%
and
\sim(480\pm 60)~\%
in execution time for the “single shot” and “average shot” cases, respectively. The methods used to calculate first- and second-order moments are computationally-costly (see Section
2.2
), iterating over all pixels in the image and therefore scaling as
\sim N^{2}
for an image of pixel dimension
N
. Additionally, in order to display radial/azimuthal marginals, methods tasked with transforming the images’ pixels from Cartesian coordinates,
(x,~y)
, to planar polar coordinates,
(r,~\theta)
, were implemented. As shown in Table
4
, the execution times for these two functions were investigated individually. Cumulatively, they accounted for
(58\pm 2)\%
of the entire runtime for calling analysis on the chromox images.

For the B-dot probe oscilloscope analysis, percentage increases for execution time with the introduction of analysis was
\sim(8\pm 30)~\%
in the “single shot” case, and
\sim(43\pm 30)~\%
in the “average shot” case. The large confidence intervals on these results is a reflection that run-to-run fluctuations in execution speed were a significant fraction of the mean execution speed.

Operations with the streak unit camera were clear outliers in both execution time (Table
2
) and memory consumption (Table
3
). This is likely due to the larger pixel dimensions of the images from the synchrotron spectrometer and OTR streak unit cameras (
1024\times 1026
and
2049\times 2049
pixels, respectively) compared to the dimensions of the single chromox camera which was used during code testing (
349\times 605

We also highlight that, for all devices, the analysis methods incur only a small amount of memory cost; indeed, memory consumption during tests across all devices actually decreased between the “singleshot” cases when analysis was introduced; owing to the fact that there was an increase in memory consumption after the addition of analysis for the “averageshot” cases, and the fact that the drop in peak memory consumption occurred for all devices, we suspect that this may be due to changes in the Jupyter kernel state between tests, though the exact mechanism explaining this occurrence remains unknown. This may also explain the decrease in execution time when analysis was introduced in the “singleshot” cases for the OTR streak unit camera and spectrometer cameras, which are difficult to analyze due to the increased number of operations performed in the analysis case.

6
Conclusions and Future Work

In this work, we have presented an online framework which is flexible to varying demands from a user, is moderately fast even under comparatively adversarial circumstances (connection to a remote server over a wireless connection with modest download speed), and is highly modular, allowing easy implementation of data extraction and analysis code for additional diagnostic devices. Though the code’s performance during experimental runtime was largely successful, we conclude by highlighting the framework’s weaknesses, and how the framework could be improved and modified.

6.1
Introducing Caching

Improvements could be made to execution speed by locally caching data from shots of interest. Often, it was desirable to plot data from the same shot multiple times in order to, for example, crop to regions of interest or adjust image contrast for visibility. This required multiple code runs, with each run limited by the speed at which data could be acquired over a network connection. In future, it would be useful to introduce an optional “caching” feature, allowing users to store data from a desired shot locally over a short period, with the option of “clearing” the cache when this data was no longer required. Caching was performed in only one case, and this was for combining data from the two different oscilloscopes used during runtime in order to allow subtraction between the two channels of the downstream longitudinal B-dot probe (see Section
2.1
).

6.2
Tagging by Cyclestamp

Experimental collaborations using the framework should aim to have some universally-recognized system of logging data by acquisition time. At CERN, as discussed in Section
3
, a strong candidate would be the UNIX timestamp corresponding to each accelerator cycle, and data filenames can be “stamped” by unique UNIX number. The framework codebase is easily adjusted to allow indexing by UNIX timestamp so long as it knows the position of the timestamp in the filename string; however, due to problems highlighted in Section
3
, this was not implemented.

6.3
Avoiding Intermediate Cloud-Based Services

Though the CERN SWAN service allowed direct user access to the CERNbox service, it introduced several obstacles. SWAN has no native functionality for editing
.json
file types, and thus configuration had to be stored manually in a front-facing Jupyter notebook file. The Jupyter kernel introduces further overhead, and online code refactors in the framework modules are not recognized unless the kernel is restarted. Additionally, SWAN requires a stable connection to the CERN network, which is in-demand during working hours and this introduces unpredictable latency.

Future experiments should aim to save data to a local computer on which the framework can be used, or at least maintain a frequently-updated clone of the data repository for ease of access which does not require an internet connection during code execution.
