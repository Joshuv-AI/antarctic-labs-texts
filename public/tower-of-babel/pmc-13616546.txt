TTR Variant p.Ala65Val Causing Early-Onset Hereditary Transthyretin Amyloidosis With Mixed Cardiac and Neuropathic Phenotype
Authors: Javier Torres-Valencia, Sebastian Reyes-Villanes, Elder Quispe, Nelson Purizaca-Rosillo, Luisser Saavedra-Cordova, Mary M. Araujo-Chumacero, Ricardo Román, Franklin Espinoza, Julio F. Mamani, Miguel Reyes-Rocha

History of Presentation

A 48-year-old man with a recent diagnosis of atrial fibrillation and receiving rivaroxaban and bisoprolol presented to the emergency department with a 6-month history of recurrent syncope and exertional dyspnea on mild effort, as well as a 1-year history of unintentional weight loss and lower extremity paresthesia. On admission, vital signs were notable for hypotension with blood pressure 90/60 mm Hg, heart rate 64 beats/min, respiratory rate 14 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. Cardiac auscultation revealed irregular heart sounds without murmurs; pulmonary examination was unremarkable.

Past Medical History

The patient was a native resident of Yanama, Yungay, Ancash, a high-altitude community in the Peruvian Andes (Supplemental Figure 1). He reported no family history of structural heart disease; however, his mother had died at an early age from an undetermined cause.

Differential Diagnosis

Common causes of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), including hypertensive heart disease, aortic stenosis, and athlete's heart, were considered unlikely based on the clinical presentation and initial investigations. Conventional hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), cardiac amyloidosis, and genetic systemic disorders were therefore entertained. Among the latter, Fabry disease warranted particular consideration given the patient's age: This X-linked lysosomal storage disorder is well recognized to mimic HCM in adults, typically manifesting in the third to fourth decade with progressive LVH, small-fiber neuropathy, renal involvement, and angiokeratomas. However, no features specific to Fabry disease or other systemic storage disorders were identified. HCM and cardiac amyloidosis remained the leading etiologies, with the presence of sensory neurological symptoms favoring amyloidosis.

Investigations

Laboratory analyses demonstrated hemoglobin 13 g/dL, creatinine 0.8 mg/dL, albumin 4.1 g/dL, 24-hour urinary protein 0.04 g/d, high-sensitivity troponin T 47 ng/L (0-14 pg/mL), and N-terminal pro B–type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) 147 pg/mL (0-125 pg/mL). Chest radiography showed an increased pulmonary vascular pattern with blunting of the costophrenic angles, without parenchymal consolidation (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 61%, severe concentric LVH (interventricular septum 16 mm, posterior wall 17 mm), global longitudinal strain of −9.7% with apical sparing, right ventricular wall thickening, mild biatrial dilatation, and a small pericardial effusion (Figures 2B and 2C). Cardiac magnetic resonance confirmed the echocardiographic findings and revealed diffuse subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement (Figures 3A to 3C). Serum and urine immunofixation electrophoresis and free light chain assays excluded monoclonal gammopathy. Given the presence of sensory symptoms, a comprehensive neurological evaluation was performed. Examination revealed global areflexia and bilateral distal predominant hypoesthesia in 4 limbs. Quantitative sensory testing demonstrated significant hyposensitivity to cold thermal stimuli and heat-nociceptive stimuli in both upper and lower extremities. Additionally, paradoxical heat sensation was elicited in the lower limbs, a phenomenon reflecting aberrant transduction of cold stimuli perceived as warmth, indicative of small fiber dysfunction. Autonomic function testing confirmed neurogenic orthostatic hypotension. The Small Fiber Neuropathy Symptoms Inventory Questionnaire score was 15/39, and the Composite Autonomic Symptom Score was 29, consistent with moderate autonomic dysfunction. Electromyography confirmed a length-dependent sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with mild right carpal tunnel syndrome. Nerve ultrasound revealed enlargement of the cross-sectional area of the fibular nerve (25.5 mm2; normal <7.1 mm2) and tibial nerve (3 2.7 mm2; normal <8.43 mm2), consistent with peripheral nerve hypertrophy.

Genetic testing identified a heterozygous pathogenic p.Ala65Val variant in the TTR gene, confirmatory for variant transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTRv). Cascade genetic screening identified the same pathogenic variant in the proband's son, 2 brothers, 1 sister, and 1 maternal uncle; no other younger relatives exhibited cardiac or neurologic involvement at the time of evaluation. The maternal uncle, however, demonstrated evidence of cardiac compromise (Supplemental Figure 2).

Management

Following confirmation of ATTRv cardiomyopathy, the patient was evaluated for disease-modifying therapy. No TTR-stabilizing or gene-silencing agents are currently approved or accessible in Peru. A formal request for compassionate access was submitted to the hospital's administrative authorities; no response had been received at the time of this report. Management was therefore limited to guideline-directed symptomatic therapy, including spironolactone 25 mg daily, as-needed furosemide for peripheral edema, rivaroxaban 20 mg daily, and dapagliflozin 10 mg daily.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At 3 years following diagnosis, and in the absence of disease-modifying therapy, the patient continues regular outpatient follow-up without additional heart failure hospitalizations. However, he has experienced progressive worsening of autonomic symptoms and skeletal muscle weakness, alongside a parallel rise in cardiac biomarkers—high-sensitivity troponin T 47.9 ng/L (reference: 0-14 pg/mL) and NT-proBNP 9,560 pg/mL (reference: 0-125 pg/mL)—consistent with ongoing disease progression. Discussion

ATTRv remains undercharacterized in Latin America, with limited epidemiologic data and inadequate awareness of its regional mutational diversity. Reporting novel or rare variants from underrepresented populations is essential to expand the known genetic and phenotypic spectrum of this disease. We report a case of early-onset ATTRv amyloidosis caused by the p.Ala65Val variant, manifesting with a mixed cardiac and neuropathic phenotype, thereby extending the recognized spectrum of amyloidogenic TTR mutations.

ATTRv is an autosomal dominant disease with variable age of onset, ethnic clustering, and a broad clinical spectrum encompassing cardiomyopathy, peripheral neuropathy, central nervous system involvement, soft tissue infiltration, nephropathy, and ocular disease.1,2 Phenotypic expression is largely determined by genotype, alongside other factors influencing disease penetrance. The p.Ala65Val TTR variant identified in our patient was not catalogued as amyloidogenic in the Mutations in Hereditary Amyloidosis database3; however, ClinVar classifies it as pathogenic or likely pathogenic based on a limited number of submitted interpretations.4

To our knowledge, only 1 prior case harboring this variant has been reported—a European patient with late-onset ATTRv with mixed cardiac and neuropathic phenotype. In that report, cardiac amyloidosis was incidentally diagnosed on echocardiography performed during preoperative evaluation for lumbar canal stenosis, and tafamidis was initiated. The subsequent emergence of neuropathic symptoms and rapid disease progression despite treatment prompted genetic testing, ultimately identifying the p.Ala65Val mutation.5 In contrast, our patient had an early-onset presentation, broadening the known clinical expression of this variant.

Three disease-modifying therapies are currently approved for ATTRv cardiomyopathy: tafamidis, acoramidis, and vutrisiran. However, patients with ATTRv represented only 24%, 10%, and 12% of enrollees in the respective pivotal trials, of whom approximately two-thirds carried the Val142Ile variant.2 This narrow representation limits the generalizability of efficacy data to other TTR variants, and differential effectiveness across specific mutations cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, because these trials were placebo-controlled rather than head-to-head comparative studies, no direct evidence exists to establish the superiority of 1 agent over another.2 In patients with ATTRv cardiomyopathy and concomitant amyloid polyneuropathy, vutrisiran may be the preferred option, given its demonstrated benefit in ATTRv-related polyneuropathy.6 Patisiran and inotersen have also shown efficacy in halting—and potentially reversing—neurologic progression in hereditary polyneuropathy; notably, patisiran demonstrated improvement in cardiac indexes and heart failure biomarkers in patients with hereditary polyneuropathy and concomitant cardiomyopathy.7

Latin American representation in pivotal clinical trials remains critically insufficient—for example, comprising only 7% of participants in the HELIOS-B trial8—reflecting a systemic gap that undermines the applicability of current evidence to this population and perpetuates diagnostic and therapeutic inequities. Beyond underrepresentation in research, access to approved therapies across Latin America is severely constrained by regulatory, economic, and policy barriers. In Peru, for instance, inotersen is the only agent with national regulatory approval, yet it remains unavailable in practice: patients are unable to sustain the financial burden of long-term treatment, and existing health policy frameworks have not yet enabled its evaluation for coverage by the Ministry of Health.9

This case expands the known phenotypic spectrum of the rare p.Ala65Val TTR variant, demonstrating that early-onset multisystem ATTRv amyloidosis can occur beyond previously reported populations.
