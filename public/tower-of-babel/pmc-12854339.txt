Knowledge of undergraduate nursing students about palliative care
Authors: Aruna Jothishanmugam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Palliative care is an essential component of holistic health care that aims to improve the quality of life for patients with life-threatening illnesses. Nurses play a crucial role in delivering palliative care, yet knowledge gaps persist, particularly among nursing students. This study assesses nursing students’ knowledge of palliative care and identifies areas for improvement to enhance nursing education.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 137 third- and fourth-year nursing students and interns from the Department of Nursing. A purposive sampling technique was used, and data were collected from November 2023 to January 2024. The Palliative Care Quiz for Nursing (PCQN) was used to assess knowledge, and descriptive and inferential statistical analyses, including one-way ANOVA and independent t-tests, were applied to evaluate knowledge scores based on year of study, prior educational exposure, and attendance in palliative care courses.RESULTS:The overall mean knowledge score was 9.94 of 15. Students showed strong knowledge in pain and symptom management (mean: 7.78); however, substantial gaps were evident in psychosocial and spiritual care (mean: 0.21). Most students (80.3%) scored within the 70–100% range, indicating generally satisfactory knowledge, but misconceptions regarding family presence at the bedside and opioid use were prevalent. Students who attended university-based or non-university palliative care courses had significantly higher knowledge scores (P < 0.05). Year of study was not a significant predictor of overall knowledge, although clinical exposure improved pain and symptom management understanding.CONCLUSION:While nursing students exhibited satisfactory palliative care knowledge, critical deficiencies in psychosocial and spiritual care remain. Strengthening palliative care education through curriculum enhancements, interactive learning, clinical simulations, and interdisciplinary collaboration is essential. Increased exposure to clinical practice, structured mentorship, and continuous professional development programs are recommended to bridge existing knowledge gaps. These improvements will better equip future nurses to provide holistic, patient-centered care, thereby enhancing the quality of life for patients and their families.

Introduction

Palliative care is a specialized approach to health care that anticipates, alleviates, and prevents suffering, enhancing the quality of life for patients with life-threatening illnesses and their families. This comprehensive care addresses physical, emotional, psychological, and spiritual needs, making it an essential component of holistic health care.[1] Delivering effective palliative care requires a multidisciplinary team, including doctors, nurses, social workers, spiritual advisors, pharmacists, and counselors, who collaborate to meet complex patient needs.[234] Care can be provided in diverse settings, such as tertiary care facilities, community health centers, and patients’ homes, ensuring accessibility across care contexts.

Although palliative care is inherently interdisciplinary, nurses play a pivotal role in patient care. Their responsibilities include managing pain, ensuring patient comfort, providing emotional and spiritual support, educating patients and families, and facilitating communication among care providers.[5] Given their close and continuous contact with patients, nurses often develop deep bonds of trust and understanding, positioning them as key advocates in end-of-life care.[67] Palliative care extends beyond addressing patients’ physical and psychological well-being to encompass the quality of life for their families, promoting holistic health outcomes and easing the burden of caregiving.[8]

Despite the growing global need for palliative care, educational gaps persist, particularly among nursing students who represent the future healthcare workforce. The insufficient integration of palliative care education in nursing curricula leaves students unprepared for end-of-life care, compromising patient outcomes and highlighting a critical gap in nursing education. The effectiveness of palliative care delivery is influenced by health care providers’ knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and clinical experiences, which shape their practices and interactions with patients. Studies indicate that many nursing students lack sufficient education and training in this area, impairing their ability to deliver comprehensive, high-quality care.[91011] Addressing this gap is essential to adequately prepare students for real-world clinical practice and to improve patient care outcomes.

Globally, an estimated 40 million people require palliative care annually, with the majority living in low- and middle-income countries where access to services remains limited.[12] In Saudi Arabia, palliative care is still emerging, with a pressing need for enhanced education and training to meet patients’ and families’ needs.[1113] This growing demand for palliative services, coupled with the limited availability of trained professionals, highlights the urgent need to strengthen nursing education in this domain. Nursing students’ knowledge of palliative care is a key determinant of care quality, influencing pain management, emotional support, and communication with patients and families. Without targeted educational interventions, future nurses may lack the competencies needed to deliver holistic palliative care, exacerbating patient suffering and increasing healthcare burdens.

Although palliative care is gaining recognition in Saudi Arabia, substantial gaps remain, particularly regarding healthcare staff education and service implementation.[14] Research highlights a lack of confidence among medical students and significant knowledge and practice deficits among home-based palliative care nurses, emphasizing the urgent need for curriculum enhancements and targeted educational interventions.[1516] The limited integration of palliative care education within nursing curricula exacerbates this issue, leaving many students unprepared for end-of-life care scenarios.[1117] A well-trained nursing workforce is crucial to ensure patients receive holistic care addressing their physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs. Strengthening nursing curricula to better address palliative care principles is not only a necessity but a moral imperative to ensure patients receive dignified and compassionate care.

Bridging this educational gap is vital to equip future nurses with the competencies necessary to provide high-quality care to terminally ill patients. Researching nursing students’ knowledge of palliative care can inform curriculum enhancements and guide targeted educational interventions. The findings of this study will guide curriculum development, inform faculty training, and support policy changes to enhance palliative care education in nursing programs. Assessing students’ understanding of palliative care principles will provide valuable insights for strengthening nursing curricula, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes and a more competent, compassionate nursing workforce.[1819]

This study aims to evaluate nursing students’ knowledge of palliative care, identify educational gaps, and contribute to developing more effective teaching strategies. The findings will support the creation of effective educational programs, ensuring future nurses are well-prepared to meet the growing demand for palliative care services and deliver comprehensive, patient-centered care.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study design was adopted to assess nursing students’ knowledge of palliative care. The study was conducted in the Department of Nursing, University College at Aldair, Jazan University, Saudi Arabia.

Study participants and sampling

The study sample included 137 students in their third and fourth years of study, as well as those in the internship phase. Participants were selected through purposive sampling, and data collection took place between November 2023 and January 2024. Students who were available during the data collection period and willing to participate after being briefed on the study’s purpose were selected during the initial stage of data collection. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection, which was conducted in person. The collected data were assessed for consistency and entered into an Excel sheet for analysis.

Data collection tools and technique

Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire designed to gather demographic information and assess participants’ knowledge of palliative care. The data collection occurred between November 2023 and January 2024. The students’ level of study and whether they had attended a palliative care course were assessed using a demographic data questionnaire.

The second part of the questionnaire was the Palliative Care Quiz for Nursing (PCQN), developed by Ross et al. (1996).[20] This questionnaire was used to assess the knowledge of nursing students about palliative care. The PCQN consists of 20 items with three response options: true, false, or “I don’t know.” Scores were obtained by summing the correct answers, with a total possible score ranging from 0 to 20. Higher scores corresponded to higher levels of palliative care knowledge. The PCQN is subdivided into three theoretical dimensions: pain and symptom management (items 2-4, 6-8, 10, 13-16, 18, and 20), philosophy and principles of palliative care (items 1, 9, 12, and 17), and psychosocial and spiritual care (items 5, 11, and 19).[21] The tool’s validity and reliability were previously confirmed, demonstrating an internal consistency of 0.78.[21] In this study, Cronbach alpha coefficient was 0.72.

Out of the 20 items, questions 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12, 13, 15, 17, and 20 are negatively worded statements intended to asses common misconceptions about palliative care. These items were reverse-scored, such that a correct response was awarded one point and incorrect or “I don’t know” responses were scored as zero, consistent with standard PCQN scoring procedures.[22]

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Standing Committee for Scientific Research (Reference No.: REC-44/08/579) at the Deanship of Research Department. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study and the data collection process. Participation was voluntary, and participants were free to withdraw at any time. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study, and informed consent was secured before data collection commenced.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide valuable insights into nursing students’ knowledge of palliative care, highlighting both strengths and areas needing improvement.[11] A notable 98.5% of participants were from public universities, with 48.9% in the internship phase, followed by fourth-year students (26.3%) and third-year students (24.8%). Given that the majority of students were in their final years, it would be expected that their knowledge of palliative care would increase with progression through their studies. However, the absence of significant differences in overall knowledge scores across different years of study suggests [Table 6] that advancement in nursing education does not necessarily lead to better palliative care knowledge.[23]

Regarding palliative care education, 62.8% of students reported attending a palliative care course in their nursing college, while 36.5% had received additional non-university education on the topic. While formal education plays a crucial role, the low percentage of students receiving external education [Table 7] underscores the limited exposure to palliative care training outside the academic curriculum.[24]

The overall mean knowledge score indicates a generally satisfactory level of understanding of understanding of palliative care principle among nursing students. Notably, the highest scores were observed in pain and symptom management (mean: 7.78), indicating that students have a strong grasp of physical symptom control. However, the lowest scores were recorded in psychosocial and spiritual care (mean: 0.21), reflecting a critical gap in students’ awareness of emotional, psychological, and spiritual dimensions of palliative care.[252627]

Further supporting these findings, the distribution of knowledge scores [Table 4] showed that 80.3% of students scored within the 70-100% range, indicating a generally satisfactory level of knowledge. Several misconceptions persist, including the belief that family members must remain at the bedside until death and concerns about morphine use leading to addiction [Table 5].[282930]

Year-wise comparisons [Table 6] revealed that knowledge in pain and symptom management significantly improved as students progressed through their studies. This finding suggests that clinical exposure and hands-on experience contribute to increased understanding of physical symptom management.[31] Similarly, the impact of non-university educational sessions and formal college-based palliative care courses was statistically significant [Tables 7 and 8]. Students who had attended nonuniversity training or a dedicated palliative care course achieved higher knowledge scores. This finding is consistent with research by Sadhu et al.[28]

Limitation and recommendation

The study findings cannot be generalized due to the Purposive sampling method and the inclusion of participants from single University College.

Conclusion

The following conclusions are presented to help prepare nursing students and future nurses for working in palliative care settings.

Increase Exposure to Palliative Care Education: The relatively low percentage of students who had received nonuniversity education on palliative care indicates a gap that should be addressed. Nursing programs should prioritize incorporating external training opportunities.[3132]

Strengthen Psychosocial and Spiritual Care: Given the low knowledge scores in psychosocial and spiritual care, there is a need to enhance focus on these aspects of palliative care through diverse educational strategies. This may include incorporating communication skills, ethical awareness, and psychological support for patients and families into ongoing learning experiences.[27]

Enhance Clinical Practice Integration: Strengthening opportunities for practical learning can support the application of knowledge in pain and symptom management. Research

Future research directions could focus on several key areas to enhance the curriculum and preparation of nursing students in palliative care. Investigating the factors contributing to gaps in psychosocial and spiritual care knowledge can inform targeted educational interventions. Longitudinal studies could assess knowledge retention and the long-term impact of palliative care education on clinical practice. Exploring the influence of clinical placements, simulation-based learning, and inter professional education on student competency may provide insights into effective teaching strategies. These research efforts can collectively strengthen nursing education and ultimately improve patient care outcomes.
