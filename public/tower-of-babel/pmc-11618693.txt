Enhanced dielectric and ferroelectric properties near the morphotropic phase boundary in BNBT-BSN ceramics prepared by the solid-state combustion technique
Authors: Anupong Luangpangai, Thanwarat Jirattitikarn, Widchaya Somsri, Aurawan Rittidech, Sasipohn Prasertpalichat, Theerachai Bongkarn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effect of firing temperatures on the phase structure, microstructure, and electrical properties of 0.99Bi0.47Na0.47Ba0.06TiO3-0.01BaSn0.70Nb0.24O3 (abbreviated as BNBT-BSN) lead-free ceramics fabricated by the solid-state combustion technique, with glycine used as the fuel, were investigated. All BNBT-BSN samples were calcined at temperatures ranging from 750 to 850 °C for 1 to 4 h and sintered at temperatures from 1100 to 1200 °C for 2 h. A pure perovskite structure was obtained after calcination at 800 °C for 2 h. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns showed the coexistence of rhombohedral and tetragonal phases in all ceramics. The average grain size tended to increase with rising sintering temperatures. The optimal condition for fabricating BNBT-BSN ceramics with a morphotropic phase boundary (MPB) was observed at a sintering temperature of 1175 °C for 2 h, where the material exhibited the highest measured density (5.50 g/cm3), maximum dielectric constant at Tm (ɛm = 6513), maximum polarization (Pmax = 42.21 µC/cm2), the largest bulk resistivity (ρ = 1.70 × 104) and the highest activation energy of conduction (Ea = 1.52 eV). These features suggest that this ceramic is a suitable material for applications in actuators, transducers, and high-performance capacitors.Keywords: BNBT-BSN, Phase structure, Dielectric, Ferroelectric, ResistivitySubject terms: Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Lead-based ceramics like Pb(Zr,Ti)O3 (PZT) are now the most commonly produced electrical materials due to their excellent properties and extensive use across various electrical industries. Nevertheless, the use of lead in these ceramics poses significant threats to the environment and human health. As a result, researchers are developing environmentally friendly, lead-free ceramics to replace lead-based ceramics1–4. Several lead-free ceramics, including Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3 (BNT), BaTiO3 (BT), and K0.5Na0.5NbO3 (KNN), have been researched for their potential in dielectric, ferroelectric, piezoelectric and energy storage applications5–9.

Bismuth sodium titanate (BNT) ceramics are good alternatives among lead-free ceramics because of their good ferroelectric properties, with a maximum polarization (Pmax) of 40 μC/cm2 and remnant polarization (Pr) of 38 μC/cm2. However, their high leakage current and coercive field (Ec of 73 kV/cm) limit their effectiveness10–12. Adding solid solutions of other perovskite materials, such as BaTiO3 (BT), Bi0.5K0.5TiO3 (BKT), and SrTiO3 (ST) can improve BNT ceramics, a strategy many researchers have explored13–15.

For example, Hussain et al.16 investigated Bi0.475Na0.475Ba0.05Ti0.95Zr0.05O3 (BNBTZ) ceramics using the conventional mixed oxide technique with a calcination temperature of 850 °C for 2 h and a sintering temperature of 1150 °C for 2 h. The BNBTZ ceramics exhibited a high maximum dielectric constant (ɛm ~ 3000) and good ferroelectric properties (Pmax ~ 35 µC/cm2, Pr ~ 20 µC/cm2 and Ec ~ 20 kV/cm). Similarly, Thatawong et al.17 successfully synthesized Bi0.47Na0.47Ba0.06TiO3 (BNBT) ceramics via the solid-state combustion technique with a low firing temperature and short dwell time (calcination temperature of 750 °C for 2 h and a sintering temperature of 1150 °C for 2 h). A morphotropic phase boundary (MPB) between rhombohedral (R) and tetragonal (T) phases was observed, resulting in good electrical properties (ɛm = 8405, Pr = 28.2 µC/cm2, Ec = 22.1 kV/cm and d33 = 161 pC/N).

The morphotropic phase boundary (MPB) in BNT-based ceramics is well known to significantly impact their electrical performance, especially dielectric and ferroelectric properties. The MPB is a region in a solid solution where the ratio of rhombohedral to tetragonal is nearly equal. This coexistence creates a high degree of structural instability, leading to enhanced polarization, which results in stronger ferroelectric behavior and improved electromechanical coupling, including lower dielectric losses that improved the overall dielectric performance18,19.

As reported in the literature, Fernandez-Benavides et al.20, successfully fabricated 0.875Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3-0.10Bi0.5K0.5TiO3-0.025BaTiO3 ceramics at the MPB. This ceramic showed enhanced electrical properties (ɛ of 1490, tanδ of 0.05, Pr of 34.4 µC/cm2 and d33 of 109 pC/N). Likewise, Chandrasekhar et al.21 synthesized a 0.934Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3-0.07K0.5Na0.5NbO3 ceramic using the conventional solid-state reaction method. The ceramic exhibited an MPB condition, resulting in an excellent strain of 0.62%, ɛr of 945, Pmax of 31.7 µC/cm2, and Pr of 21.2 µC/cm2. Similarly, the MPB was induced in 0.90Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3-0.08KNbO3-0.02SrTiO3 ceramics, as presented by Bai et al.22. The ceramic exhibited a high electrostrictive coefficient (Q11) of 0.0236 m4C-2, a strain of 0.148%, and a Pr of ~ 25 µC/cm2.

These findings highlight the importance of examining MPB conditions in BNT-based ceramics, as the MPB location offers significant potential for enhancing performance through modifications.

To improve the phase structure and electrical properties of BNBT ceramics’, a solid solution can be introduced into the BNBT system23–25. Li et al.23 fabricated (1-x)Bi0.47Na0.47Ba0.06TiO3-xBaSnO3 (BNBT-BS) ceramics with x ranging from 0 to 0.20 using the solid-state method, calcinating at 850 °C for 4 h and sintering at 1170 °C for 4 h. BNBT ceramics modified with BaSnO3 doping showed improved temperature-stable dielectric properties. In addition, the 0.85BNBT-0.15BS ceramics exhibited a high ɛm of ~ 3000 and good ferroelectric and energy storage properties (Pmax of ~ 18 µC/cm2, Pr of 0.4 µC/cm2, Wrec of 1.2 J/cm3 and ƞ of 86.7%). Jia et al.26 also improved Bi0.47Na0.47Ba0.06TiO3 (BNBT) ceramics by doping with Ba(Sn0.70Nb0.24)O3 (BSN) using the traditional solid-state technique, with calcination and sintering temperatures of 850 °C for 4 h and 1120–1200 °C for 2.5 h, respectively. The 0.99BNBT-0.01BSN ceramic showed a pseudo-cubic perovskite structure with an ɛm of ~ 4000. BSN modification reduced dielectric loss and increased the relaxation properties of BNBT ceramics. Furthermore, excellent strain and energy storage properties were observed (Smax of 0.43%, d33* of 633 pm/V, Pmax of ~ 40 µC/cm2, Wrec of ~ 0.75 J/cm3 and ƞ of ~ 55%). Doping with BSN reduces the randomly distributed ferroelectric phase and introduces polar nano regions (PNRs), increasing the strain and energy storage properties of the BNBT system.

According to recent studies, the synthesis technique is important for improving particle size distribution, densification, and the electrical properties of ceramics. The solid-state combustion technique has emerged as an appealing preparation technique for ceramics. This technique utilizes energy from the combustion of fuel, which efficiently boosts the chemical reaction, resulting in ultrafine powders, high ceramic densities, and improved electrical properties. Compared to conventional solid-state techniques, it offers lower firing temperatures and shorter dwell times27,28. Kornphom et al.29 used the solid-state combustion technique to fabricate (Ba0.85Ca0.15)(Ti0.90Zr0.10)O3 (BCTZ) ceramics with a low firing temperature (calcination temperature of 1050 °C) and a short dwell time (2 h). This resulted in powders with fine particle size and high purity, contributing to dense BCTZ ceramics, with a high ɛm of 4485 and Pr of 18 µC/cm2, values that are higher than ceramics produced with conventional solid-state techniques30.

To date, no studies on 0.99Bi0.47Na0.47Ba0.06TiO3-0.01Ba(Sn0.70Nb0.24)O3 (BNBT-BSN) ceramics generated via the solid-state combustion technique have been published. Thus, this research aims to study the phase structure, microstructure, dielectric and ferroelectric properties, including the impedance and band gap energy of BNBT-BSN ceramics synthesized via the solid-state combustion technique. According to the literature, impedance and band gap measurements of ceramics produced with different sintering temperatures have been rarely studied or given little attention. Therefore, studying the effect of sintering temperature on impedance and band gap properties is interesting. It is also expected that phase structure changes toward MPB conditions could improve the electrical properties.

Experiments

In this study, 0.99Bi0.47Na0.47Ba0.06TiO3-0.01Ba(Sn0.70Nb0.24)O3 (BNBT-BSN) ceramics were synthesized using the solid-state combustion technique. Bi(NO3)3.5H2O (98.50%, KEMAUS, AU), NaNO3 (99.00%, Ajex Finechem, AU), Ba(NO3)2 (99.00%, HiMedia, USA), TiO2 (99.00%, KEMAUS, AU), SnO2 (99.00%, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and Nb2O5 (99.00%, Eleps, RUS) were used as the initial materials. These materials were combined according to the stoichiometric ratio and ball milled in an ethanol solvent with zirconium balls for 24 h. The resulting mixture was dried and ground in an agate mortar with glycine (C2H5NO2: 99.00%, KemAus, AUS) at a propellant ratio of 1.1111 wt.%. The powder was then calcined between 750 and 850 °C for 1 to 4 h, with a heating rate of 5 °C/min. The pure calcined powder (calcined at 800 °C for 2 h) was then mixed with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) in ethanol for 12 h. After drying, the powder was pressed into green pellets with a diameter of ~ 15 mm and a thickness of ~ 1.3 mm at a pressure of 80 MPa. Lastly, the green pellets were sintered at 1100 to 1200 °C for 2 h.

The BNBT-BSN ceramics’ phase structure, microstructure, dielectric and ferroelectric properties, as well as impedance and band gap energy were examined in detail. To analyze the phase structure of all ceramics, X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker, D8 Discover) with Cu-Kα1,2 radiation over a scanning range of 2θ = 10–70° at room temperature, followed by Rietveld refinement. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Apreo S) was used to examine the microstructure of the sintered ceramics after thermal etching. The mean linear intercept approach was used to calculate the average grain size, measuring over 300 grains for accuracy. The density of the ceramics was determined using Archimedes technique.

The ceramics were polished and coated with silver paste (IRMS sp5574, CN) and then heated to 500 °C for 30 min to form electrical contacts. The dielectric properties of the ceramics were analyzed at different frequencies (1, 10, and 100 kHz) from room temperature to 400 °C using an LCR meter (HP 4284A). A computer-controlled, modified Sawyer–Tower circuit (Radiant, PLC2for P-E loop measurements) operating at 1 Hz was used to measure the ferroelectric properties of the ceramics, with samples 0.5 mm thick. Complex impedance was measured using an impedance analyzer (Solatron, 1260A) over a temperature range of 480 to 560 °C at a frequency range of 10 Hz to 10 MHz. Band gap energy measurement was analyzed with a UV–VIS-NIR scanning spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-3101PC) at room temperature.

Results and discussion

Phase structure

Figure 1a displays the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of BNBT-BSN powders at room temperature, which were calcined at temperatures between 750 °C and 850 °C for 4 h, for scans performed over the 2θ range of 10–70°. All samples displayed diffraction peaks typical for a perovskite structure, matching the JCPDS file no. 36–0339 and 36–0340. However, an impurity phase of Nb2O5 was detected in powders calcined at 750 °C suggesting incomplete chemical reactions during calcination at this temperature.

The BNBT-BSN powders calcined at temperatures of 800 °C or higher for 4 h had no observable impurity phases, and 100% purity, as seen in Fig. 1a.

In addition, a pure BNBT-BSN powder calcined at 800 °C was selected for further testing at different dwell times. Figure 1b shows the XRD patterns of BNBT-BSN powders produced with a calcination temperature of 800 °C with dwell times between 1 and 4 h, measured at room temperature. It was found that the powder calcined at 800 °C for a short dwell time of 1 h had an impurity phase of Nb2O5; however, this impurity phase was eliminated at longer dwell times (≥ 2 h). The purity percentage for the powder calcined at 800 °C for 1 h was 96.3%, which increased to 100% when the dwell time was extended to at least 2 h, as observed in Fig. 1b. Thus, the optimal condition to synthesize BNBT-BSN powders using the solid-state combustion technique was determined to be a calcination temperature of 800 °C for 2 h. This technique used a temperature that was 50 °C lower and a dwell time that was 2 h shorter compared to the solid-state reaction technique26.

The XRD patterns of BNBT-BSN ceramics produced with sintering temperatures between 1100 and 1200 °C for 2 h, measured at room temperature, are depicted in Fig. 2a. A pure perovskite structure with no impurity phase, was observed in all sintered ceramics. To identify the phase structure of the XRD patterns, Fig. 2b shows the magnified patterns in the 2θ ranging from 10 to 70°. The rhombohedral phase is normally characterized by dual peaks of (003)R/(021)R at a 2θ of 40° and a single peak of (202)R at a 2θ of 46°, according to JCPDS files No. 36–034033,34. While the tetragonal phase is usually defined by a single peak of (111)T at a 2θ of 40° and dual peaks of (002)T/(200)T at a 2θ of 46°, according to JCPDS files No. 36–033935,36. The XRD patterns of all ceramics exhibited non-symmetric and split peaks at 2θ of 40° and 46°, suggesting the coexistence of both rhombohedral and tetragonal phases, consistent with other BNBT-based studies27,37,38. Moreover, the peaks at 2θ of 40 and 46° shifted towards lower angles, as shown by the dashed arrow in Fig. 2b, suggesting an expansion of the lattice parameters.

XRD patterns of BNBT-BSN ceramics synthesized at sintering temperatures between 1100 and 1200 °C for 2 h, measured at room temperature. (a) The patterns in the 2θ range of 10–70° and (b) magnified patterns in the 2θ range of 35–50°.

In particular, the change in phase percentage of phase is influenced by the sintering temperature, which has been shown to have a significant effect on the electrical properties18–22. To further investigate the impact of sintering temperatures on the phase structure and lattice parameters, the XRD patterns of all BNBT-BSN ceramics with various sintering temperatures were examined using the Rietveld refinement technique implemented in the Fullprof program. The Crystallography Open Database (COD) provided the data sources for setting the lattice parameters, such as lengths (a, b, c), angles (α, β, γ), atomic data, and occupancy rate. The model used to fit the XRD data of the BNBT-BSN ceramics consisted of a structural approach based on BNT with a rhombohedral phase (R3c space group) and a tetragonal phase (P4bm space group)39,40. However, there is no consensus on the meaning of the R3c and P4bm phases. Two conceptual approaches have been proposed: 1) R3c is a ferroelectric while P4bm is antiferroelectric; 2) R3c is a polar phase, and P4bm is either “weakly polar” or "almost non-polar"41.

The background was modeled using a Chebyshev function with six polynomial coefficients, and the XRD peaks were fit with a pseudo-Voigt function. Atomic occupancies were assumed to be the same as the chemical formula for each composition. Figure 3a–e shows the fitted profiles based on the Rietveld refinement results for the BNBT-BSN ceramics. The refinement results showed an acceptable alignment between the calculated and observed patterns in all samples. Table 1 shows the quality of fit, lattice parameters, and percentage of each phase for all ceramics. The refinement models accurately characterized the samples, as indicated by the high reliability factors (χ2 < 4) and a good fit between calculated and observed patterns. The coexistence of the rhombohedral (R) and tetragonal (T) phases was confirmed by the refinement results. The phase structure of the BNBT-BSN ceramics was influenced by the sintering temperature, with the R phase increasing from 17 to 60% with the T phase decreasing from 83 to 40%, as the sintering temperature increased from 1100 to 1200 °C. Notably, the ceramic sintered at 1175 °C exhibited a nearly equal phase ratio of R to T at 45:55. The significant variations in the R3c and P4bm phases indicate that the sintering temperature has an important effect on phase structure. Furthermore, the unit cell volume (V) for the R phase increased from 365.117 to 376.842 Å3, while for the T phase, it increased from 121.142 to 123.586 Å3 as the sintering temperatures increased from 1100 °C to 1200 °C. These results could be explained by thermal expansion during the sintering process28,42.

Microstructure and density

Figure 4a–e displays the surface morphology of BNBT-BSN ceramics sintered between 1100 °C and 1200 °C for 2 h, using a scanning electron microscope (SEM). The images show polygonal grain shapes and anisotropic grain growth in all samples. At the lowest sintering temperature of 1100 °C, the ceramics exhibited unclear grain boundaries with visible pores located along the grain boundaries. When the sintering temperature increased to 1125 °C and 1150 °C, clearer grain boundaries were observed, although pores remained. The ceramic sintered at 1175 °C had the best morphology, with well-packed grains and fewer pores. However, further increasing the sintering temperature to 1200 °C led to recurring porosity, caused by the evaporation of Bi3+ and Na+ ions during the sintering process43,44.

Surface morphology of BNBT-BSN ceramics sintered at (a) 1100 °C, (b) 1125 °C, (c) 1150 °C, (d) 1175 °C and (e) 1200 °C.

The grain size distribution of BNBT-BSN ceramics with different sintering temperatures is plotted in Fig. 5a–e. The grain size distribution became broader with increasing sintering temperatures, suggesting a more non-uniform grain size at higher sintering temperatures. The average grain size of the BNBT-BSN ceramics increased from 128 to 154 nm as the sintering temperature increased from 1100 °C to 1200 °C, as listed in Table 2. The average grain size of BNBT-BSN ceramics synthesized using the solid-state combustion technique was smaller than that of BNBT-BSN ceramics synthesized using the traditional solid-state technique, which is in the range of 1160 to 1360 nm26. Furthermore, the measured density of the BNBT-BSN ceramics increased from 5.36 to a maximum of 5.59 g/cm3 as the sintering temperature increased from 1100 to 1175 °C, and subsequently dropped when the sintering temperature increased up to 1200 °C, which corresponded with the SEM results, as seen in Table 2.

Dielectric properties

Figure 6a–e shows the temperature-dependent relative dielectric constant (ε) and loss (tanδ) of BNBT-BSN ceramics sintered at different temperatures, measured at temperatures ranging from room temperature to 400 °C at frequencies of 1, 10 and 100 kHz. All samples displayed two abnormal dielectric peaks. The first peak occurs at the depolarization temperature (Td), which is associated with the thermal evolution of polar nano-regions (PNRs) from a rhombohedral structure with R3c symmetry to a tetragonal structure with P4bm symmetry45,46. The second peak, at Tm, is the temperature where the maximum dielectric constant occurs47,48. As shown in Table 2, Td at 1 kHz increased slightly from 140 °C to 143 °C as the sintering temperature increased from 1100 °C to 1200 °C. Meanwhile, Tm, measured at 1 kHz, was in the range of 282–288 °C and was not significantly affected by the sintering temperature. It is well known that Td is correlated with the stability of the ferroelectric order49,50. The increase in Td could be explained by a change in phase structure when the sintering temperature increased, causing a shift to the rhombohedral structure. Muramatsu et al.51 reported that an increased rhombohedral phase in BNT-based ceramics led to a higher Td. Similarly, an increase in Td in Bi0.5Na0.46Li0.04TiO3 ceramics was due to an increased rhombohedral phase, as reported by Yoneda et al.52.

The temperature-dependent relative dielectric constant (ε) and loss (tanδ) of BNBT-BSN ceramics sintered at (a) 1100 °C, (b) 1125 °C, (c) 1150 °C, (d) 1175 °C and (e) 1200 °C, measured at temperatures ranging from room temperature to 400 °C and the frequencies of 1, 10 and 100 kHz.

The dielectric curves of all the ceramics showed a broad peak, with a substantial dispersion of frequencies in the low temperature range and a small dispersion of frequencies in the higher temperature range, which was also reported in BNT-based ceramics53,54. As the frequency increased from 1 to 100 kHz, the Td and tanδ peaks at Td shifted to a higher temperature, as shown by the dashed arrow in Fig. 6a–e, while the Tm peak shifted to a lower temperature, as shown by the arrow in Fig. 6a–e. From these results, it was found that the BNBT-BSN ceramics showed relaxor ferroelectric behavior55,56.

The dielectric constant at room temperature (ɛr), at Tm (ɛm), and dielectric loss (tanδ) at Tm of BNBT-BSN ceramics produced from various sintering temperatures, measured at 1 kHz, are presented in Table 2. It was observed that the ɛr and ɛm values gradually increased from 1787 to 2015 and from 5269 to 6513, respectively, when the sintering temperatures increased from 1100 °C to 1175 °C and subsequently decreased as sintering temperatures rose to 1200 °C. The largest ɛr and ɛm were in the BNBT-BSN ceramic sintered at 1175 °C, which could be related to the impact of the MPB, the well-packed microstructure, and the highest density (5.59 g/cm3). When compared to the ɛm value of BNBT-BSN ceramics prepared via the traditional solid-state technique (ɛm ~ 4100)26, the BNBT-BSN ceramic synthesized via the solid-state combustion technique, sintered at 1175 °C for 2 h, showed a higher ɛm value (6513). Furthermore, the tanδ at Tm, measured at 1 kHz, were within the 0.01–0.02 range, indicating that sintering temperatures had no impact on the tanδ values.

Ferroelectric properties

Figure 7 presents the hysteresis (P-E) loops of the BNBT-BSN ceramics synthesized with various sintering temperatures, measured under an electric field of 75 kV/cm, at room temperature. All samples displayed saturated and pinched loops, demonstrating that these ceramics had relaxor ferroelectric characteristics and were in agreement with the dielectric properties results57,58. Non-ergodic relaxor ferroelectric (NER) behavior is well known for exhibiting ferroelectric-like behavior that irreversibly transforms into normal ferroelectric behavior when the electric field is removed. It is commonly known that the nanometer-sized polar region (PNRs) in the relaxor freezes in an NER state, leading to regular square-shaped ferroelectric P-E loops with a high maximum polarization (Pmax) and remnant polarization (Pr). While the ergodic relaxor ferroelectric (ER) behavior demonstrates a field-induced ferroelectric order that turns back to relaxor behavior when the electric field is removed59,60. All ceramics showed a nearly square P-E loop with a large maximum polarization (Pmax) and remnant polarization (Pr), indicating NER behavior, which corresponds with the results of Bi0.465Na0.465Ba0.07TiO3 ceramics61. According to Table 2, it was found that the Pmax values of BNBT-BSN ceramics continuously increased from 37.59 to 42.21 µC/cm2 when the sintering temperatures increased from 1125 °C to 1175 °C, and then slightly decreased to 41.98 µC/cm2 at a sintering temperature of 1200 °C. Meanwhile, the Pr gradually increased from 23.11 to 30.15 µC/cm2 when the sintering temperature increased from 1125 to 1200 °C, whereas the coercive field (Ec) varied between 13.46 and 15.08 kV/cm, as listed in Table 2.

The hysteresis (P-E) loops of BNBT-BSN ceramics produced with various sintering temperatures, measured under an electric field of 75 kV/cm at room temperature.

The P-E loops became wider and the Pmax and Pr values tended to increase, indicating the ceramics developed more NER behavior with increasing sintering temperatures. These results may be caused by long-range ferroelectric order and reduced PNRs when increasing sintering temperatures. An increase in Pr could be associated with the creation of long-range ferroelectric order as sintering temperatures increased, resulting from more of the ceramics having a rhombohedral structure, as reported by Xu et al. and Schmitt et al.62,63. In comparison to BNBT-BSN ceramics synthesized using the traditional solid-state technique (Pmax of ~ 38 µC/cm2 and Pr of ~ 5 µC/cm2), BNBT-BSN ceramics fabricated using the solid-state combustion technique improved Pmax and Pr values more efficiently (Pmax of 42.21 µC/cm2 and Pr of 28.98 µC/cm2). Significantly different Pr values were observed between ceramics produced with solid-state combustion and traditional solid-state methods. This may be due to the increased rhombohedral structure at higher sintering temperatures for BNBT-BSN ceramics synthesized by the solid-state combustion technique, which is an important factor in electrical properties, especially ferroelectric properties. Whereas, the BNBT-BSN ceramics prepared by the traditional solid-state technique showed a typical pseudo-cubic structure, as reported by Jia26. The rhombohedral phase has a crystal structure that is more deformed from the cubic structure than the pseudocubic phase, resulting in dipole moments with a clearer and more consistent magnitude and direction, resulting in higher Pr values64. The P-E loops of the BNBT-BSN ceramics sintered at the lowest sintering temperature of 1100 °C, for 2 h, could not be measured in an electric field of 75 kV/cm due to low density and poor microstructure.

Impedance properties

Impedance spectroscopy was used to examine the high-temperature conductivity of the BNBT-BSN ceramics. Figure 8a–e shows the impedance complex plane (Z*) plots of BNBT-BSN ceramics made with various sintering temperatures, in the temperature range of 480 °C to 560 °C in 20 °C steps. All ceramics had a single semicircle across the entire temperature range, which is likely due to a bulk response, indicating that only one mechanism impacts the conduction26,61. As the measurement temperatures increased, the radius of the semicircles in all samples continuously decreased, implying a decrease in resistance. This phenomenon corresponded to the negative temperature coefficient of resistance (NTCR) effect, which typically occurs in BNT-based ceramics65,66.

Band gap energy

The UV–VIS absorption spectra were used to determine the band gap of BNBT-BSN ceramics. Figure 10a depicts the UV–VIS absorption spectra of BNBT-BSN ceramics with varying sintering temperatures over a wavelength range of 200 to 700 nm. As sintering temperatures increased, the band gap broadened and shifted to a higher wavelength, indicating that electron transition from the valence band to the conduction band became more challenging, resulting in a higher intrinsic breakdown strength72. The Eg value represents the energy necessary for electrons to transition from the valence band to the conduction band. The increasing sintering temperature increased atomic diffusion, resulting in a denser structure with reduced interatomic spacing. The difference in phase structure obtained in this study by solid-state combustion led to enhanced electrical properties when compared to the traditional solid-state technique. The BNBT-BSN ceramic synthesized using solid-state combustion and sintered at 1175 °C for 2 h revealed excellent dielectric properties (ɛr of 2015 and ɛm of 6513), as well as favorable ferroelectric properties (Pmax of 42.21 µC/cm2 and Pr of 28.98 µC/cm2). This ceramic surpassed those of BNBT-BSN ceramics prepared using the traditional solid-state technique, with better dielectric and ferroelectric properties (ɛr of ~ 1800, ɛm of ~ 4100, Pmax of ~ 38 µC/cm2 and Pr of ~ 5 µC/cm216). This suggests that this ceramic, which is nearly an MPB composition, with an R-to-T phase ratio of 45:55, as indicated by the Rietveld refinement data, had good morphology and maximum density. Previous studies have discussed that MPB conditions in BNT-based ceramics can enhance electrical properties20–22. Likewise, the MPB condition obtained from the BNBT-BSN ceramic sintered at 1175 °C in this study also contributed to improved electric properties.

Conclusion

In summary, 0.99Bi0.47Na0.47Ba0.06TiO3-0.01Ba(Sn0.70Nb0.24)O3 (BNBT-BSN) ceramics were prepared via the solid-state combustion technique using glycine as fuel. The BNBT-BSN powders were calcined at temperatures between 750 °C and 850 °C for 1 to 4 h and sintered between 1100 °C and 1200 °C for 2 h. A pure perovskite structure was obtained at a calcination temperature of 800 °C for 2 h. All sintered BNBT-BSN ceramics exhibited a perovskite structure with coexisting rhombohedral and tetragonal phases. When the sintering temperatures increased from 1100 °C to 1200 °C, the rhombohedral (R) phase increased from 17 to 60%, and the tetragonal (T) phase decreased from 83 to 40%. The average grain size tended to increase with increased sintering temperatures. The maximum dielectric constant at room temperature (ɛr = 2015) and at Tm (ɛm = 6513), along with the maximum polarization (Pmax = 42.21 µC/cm2), were observed in the BNBT-BSN ceramic sintered at 1175 °C. The existence of an MPB with an R:T ratio of 45:55, a well-packed microstructure, the highest density (5.59 g/cm3), and the largest resistivity (ρ = 1.70 × 104), resulted from the optimal sintering temperature of 1175 °C, making it ideal for synthesizing BNBT-BSN ceramics. This study successfully developed BNBT-BSN ceramics with improved dielectric and ferroelectric properties, utilizing low firing temperatures and short dwell times, which could be beneficial for future dielectric and ferroelectric applications.
