GLP-1 receptor agonists and their implications for female reproductive health
Authors: Eli Eagles, Ben Simpson, Julija Makajeva, Paul T-Y Ayuk
Published: 2026

Introduction

The last five years have seen a dramatic shift in the use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA), from specialized diabetes treatments to mainstream options for obesity management and increasing use as ‘lifestyle’ medications. One in four 25–64-year-olds from 99 countries studied were eligible for GLP-1RA on the basis of obesity or type 2 diabetes mellitus [1] and the World Health Organization (WHO) has added GLP-1RA to its list of essential drugs [2]. Women account for over 75% of GLP-1RA prescriptions, with women aged 30–49 years being twice as likely to use these drugs as men. There has been a recent spike in use in women aged 18–25 years. Given these demographics, and the known metabolic effects of these drugs, there is a need for greater understanding of the implications for female reproductive health.

The cloning of the proglucagon gene in the 1980s led to the identification of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and the subsequent development of the drug class ‘incretin mimetics’, including GLP-1RAs. These agents function by activating G-protein coupled GLP-1 receptors on the surface of pancreatic beta-cells, as well as cells in the brain, lungs, stomach, intestine, and kidneys, initiating a cascade of physiological responses that include increased insulin secretion, suppression of glucagon release, and a reduction in the rate of gastric emptying [3]. Collectively, these effects contribute to lower blood glucose levels and also promote a sense of satiety. The therapeutic potential of endogenous GLP-1 is limited by its rapid inactivation in the bloodstream by dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4). The discovery of DPP-4 resistant exendin-4, isolated from the saliva of the Gila monster lizard, was the key to overcoming this limitation. Exenatide, a synthetic analogue of exendin-4, was the first GLP-1RA to be approved for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus [3]. Initially, GLP-1RA formulations were delivered subcutaneously but oral formulations are now being approved or are in development [4]. Side-effect profiles are broadly similar – with gastrointestinal upset being the most common side-effect reported. More serious side-effects include pancreatitis and anaphylaxis, but these are rare [5].

The most recent guidance from the UK College of Sexual and Reproductive Healthcare (CoSRH) recommends that women of reproductive age using GLP-1RAs should be on an effective form of contraception as these medications should not be used in pregnancy [6]. This recommendation is echoed by the UK MHRA (Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency), the European Medicines Agency and the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA). The FDA has not yet assigned a risk category for use in pregnancy. Tirzepatide (Mounjaro®) and semaglutide (Ozempic®/Wegovy®) delay gastric emptying, potentially impacting the absorption of other oral medications, including contraceptives. With tirzepatide, the CoSRH and the FDA recommend that women using oral contraceptives should switch to non-oral methods or use an additional barrier contraception initially for four weeks, and for a further four weeks after any dose increase. Side-effects such as diarrhoea and vomiting may also reduce the effectiveness of oral contraceptives. The efficacy of parenteral methods of contraception is unlikely to be impacted by GLP-1RAs, and no additional precautions are required. Semaglutide is not considered to affect the efficacy of oral contraceptives and additional precautions are not required.

The UK CoSRH recommends that GLP-1RAs should be avoided prior to a planned pregnancy, with a a one-month washout period for tirzepatide and two months for semaglutide. Weight loss caused by GLP-1RAs may induce ovulation in women with obesity and anovulation, but these agents have not been evaluated for ovulation induction because of potential foetal risks and the need for a wash-out period. Further guidance on this is likely to appear as new oral GLP-1RAs become more available.

GLP-1 RAs are increasingly popular for weight loss in women of reproductive age in the absence of type 2 diabetes. Given the rate of unplanned pregnancy, some women are likely to have conceived whilst taking these medications and may be concerned about potential adverse effects. Fortunately, the emerging evidence has been reassuring [7]. Cognisant of the potential effects of untreated type 2 diabetes on pregnancy outcomes, the FDA proposes that GLP-1RAs should be used in pregnancy only if the potential benefits justify the potential risks to the foetus. Data on periconceptual GLP-1 RA usage are dominated by cases where the usage has been discontinued upon the discovery of an unplanned pregnancy. Earlier case reviews suggested a possible increased risk of miscarriage, but more recent data suggest that the risks of miscarriage, congenital anomaly or pre-term birth are not increased [7]. Cohorts which include cases of GLP-1RA use throughout pregnancy are starting to appear in the literature without any obvious signal of harm [8].

When it comes to using GLP-1RAs prior to pregnancy for weight loss, a recent systematic review with a total of 4268 women suggests that for women who have used GLP-1RAs in the 2 years before conception, the risk of gestational diabetes is reduced (although less than that observed for pre-pregnancy bariatric surgery), and the risks of hypertensive disease of pregnancy, pre-term birth and caesarean delivery are also lower than they are for matched controls [9]. GLP-1RAs should not be used during breastfeeding because of the lack of data. However, empirically, the peptide molecules in the injectable formulations do not cross the gut epithelium and will not be expected to cross the mammary gland epithelium. Any traces of drug that entered breast milk would be digested in the gastrointestinal tract of the baby and any that escaped digestion would not be absorbed. Emerging data are consistent with this empirical chemistry [10], and this is important as women may use GLP-1RAs to shed pregnancy weight gain and this may reduce breastfeeding rates.

With respect to late reproductive health, it is important to consider the role of additional comorbidities (such as obesity and type 2 diabetes) and evolving drug therapies and risk factors including the use of hormone replacement therapy (HRT). As such, the use of GLP-1RAs to help support weight loss (and modify these risk factors) is especially important. When combining GLP-1RAs and HRT, use of parenteral progestogens and oestrogens should be considered instead of the enteral route. Oral oestrogen or progestogen formulations face similar theoretical absorption difficulties as in the use of oral contraceptives. Additionally, obesity and diabetes are significant risk factors for venous thromboembolism (VTE), and transdermal oestrogen preparations, which carry a lower VTE risk, are often preferred in this cohort. With obesity being a risk factor for the development of endometrial cancer, there is potential for GLP-1RAs to help in cancer risk-reduction. Recent developments in the management of endometrial hyperplasia and early-stage endometrial cancer suggest that women who lost 10% of their total body weight during intrauterine progestogen-based treatment had improvements in their oncological outcomes [11]. This study utilised weight loss through bariatric surgery or low-calorie diet. GLP-1RAs may offer equivalent weight-loss-related risk reduction. Overall, data on GLP-1RAs and cancer risk reduction are mixed.

Oral formulations or alternatives to the sub-cutaneous GLP-1RA injections are being introduced. Oral semaglutide (Rybelsus®) differs from the injectable drug because of the addition of an absorption enhancer, sodium N-(8-[2-hydroxybenzoyl] amino) caprylate (SNAC) but the bioavailability remains low, at 1–2%. Orforglipron is a non-peptide oral GLP-1 RA in phase 3 development [3]. These and other oral agents in development should have a shorter wash-out period but may also be more likely to cross the placenta or the mammary epithelium. The safety profile of GLP-1 RAs for women of reproductive age is likely to continue to evolve.

Conclusion

Given the prevalence of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus, GLP1-RAs are understandably recognised as essential medications. Their increasing use as ‘lifestyle’ medication among women in general, and women of reproductive age in particular, provides additional therapeutic and preventative opportunities but also comes with risks. Women and their clinicians need to be up to date with these evolving opportunities and risks, as does the wider public, given the increasing over-the-counter use of these medications.
