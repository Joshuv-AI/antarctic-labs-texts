Timing Isn’t Everything: Rethinking Mortality Risk in Type A Aortic Dissection Surgery
Authors: Anna H Xue, Joseph S Coselli
Published: 2026

Despite improved early recognition and advances in operative technique, acute Type A aortic dissection (TAAD) continues to be associated with high in-hospital mortality rates even after surgical treatment.1 There has been ongoing debate in the literature about the “weekend effect,” that is, the notion that patients admitted on weekends might have worse outcomes than those admitted on weekdays.

In the current issue of Interdisciplinary CardioVascular and Thoracic Surgery, Van Hove and colleagues2 examine this “weekend effect” by analysing data from the Nationwide Readmissions Database (NRD). Weekday and weekend admissions were identified from October 2016 to December 2021. In total, the authors examined data on 14 260 TAAD hospitalizations, of which 10 582 (74.2%) were weekday admissions, and 3678 (25.8%) were weekend admissions. Patients admitted on weekends had a higher in-hospital mortality rate (17.3%) than patients admitted on weekdays (15.4%) (P = .027). On multivariable analysis, weekend admission was independently associated with greater mortality (P = .04). The authors noted that weekend-admitted patients were more likely to have pre-existing liver disease and evidence of malperfusion, features consistent with higher presenting acuity. However, because these variables were independently associated with mortality, and the weekend effect persisted after multivariable adjustment, differences in baseline clinical severity alone are unlikely to fully explain the observed mortality disparity. In contrast, treated at large-volume or teaching hospitals had lower overall mortality, an advantage that persisted even for weekend admissions, effectively mitigating the “weekend effect” at these institutions.

The literature on this topic is mixed, such that the true impact of the “weekend effect” on TAAD outcomes remains unclear. Because surgical repair of acute TAAD is an emergency procedure, patients undergoing this operation could be particularly vulnerable to any potential deficiencies in weekend care. Additionally, the time before patient recognition of symptoms and presentation at a medical facility may also affect outcomes, although whether these factors differ systematically between weekday and weekend admissions is uncertain.

Several registry-based and contemporary analyses have found similar mortality rates regardless of whether surgery was performed on a weekend or a weekday. For example, an analysis of 1159 patients from 8 Nordic centers (Nordic Consortium for Acute Type A Aortic Dissection database) reported mortality rates of 17% for weekday operations versus 20% for weekend operations.3 The difference was not statistically significant (P = .45), and even after adjustment for other factors, there was no independent weekend effect on mortality. Similarly, a National Inpatient Sample study (2015-2020) compared 1007 patients who underwent TAAD repair on weekends to 3275 who underwent repair on weekdays.4 After risk adjustment, in-hospital mortality was nearly identical for weekend vs weekday admissions (15.99% vs 14.84%), and hospital length of stay and total hospital charges were also comparable between the 2 groups.

However, other large studies have arrived at the opposite conclusion, suggesting that outcomes are slightly worse for TAAD patients admitted on weekends. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 9 studies (with 16 501 patients) found that undergoing TAAD surgery on a weekend was associated with a 41% higher in-hospital mortality risk than undergoing TAAD surgery on a weekday.1 The authors suggest that several factors might explain this disparity, including systems-level limitations in weekend hospital operations, such as fewer experienced surgeons and nursing staff available, and less robust on-call arrangements and multidisciplinary coordination. They suggested that establishing organized rapid-response teams for aortic emergencies could help reduce the time from diagnosis to surgical intervention.1 Additionally, a 10-year study using the US Nationwide Emergency Department Sample (NEDS) evaluated data from 984 hospitals (2006-2017) and found that patients who presented with aortic dissection on weekends (n = 11 318) had higher in-hospital mortality than those who presented on weekdays (n = 26 759) (11.8% vs 10.4%, P < .001).5 Weekend presentation independently predicted mortality (P = .003).

Thus, the overall evidence to date remains indeterminate regarding the influence of weekend versus weekday admission on outcomes of surgical treatment for acute TAADs. Nevertheless, the findings of Van Hove et al.,2 together with prior research, suggest several plausible reasons why weekend admissions might have poorer results. These include reduced staffing levels at many hospitals, limited access to diagnostic and ancillary services, fewer senior surgeons immediately available, and, in some cases, restricted operating room availability on weekends. Notably, the current study found that weekend-admitted patients had a higher prevalence of systemic malperfusion, a well-known predictor of mortality. It is also possible that weekend presentations are associated with delays in diagnosis and/or transfer to specialized centres. In many hospitals, dedicated aortic teams are in place on weekdays, but a different on-call team covers the weekends.

Although survival after surgical management of acute Type A aortic dissection has improved in recent years, achieving further improvements will require addressing the issues outlined above, with particular emphasis on providing equally rapid, high-quality care on weekends. Patients and their families rightly expect that the care received on a weekend is equivalent to that delivered during the week. This is not just a reasonable goal but also, most certainly, an achievable one.
