Association between intraocular pressure and climate parameters
Authors: Ryo Terauchi, Kota Fukai, Kyoko Ito, Shumpei Ogawa, Takahiko Noro, Tomohiro Kato, Kiminori Kato, Masayuki Tatemichi, Yoshiaki Kabata, Tadashi Nakano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the association between climate parameters and intraocular pressure (IOP). We used two large, multicenter cross-sectional datasets: the Japan Ningen Dock Study (JNDS), with 1,477,066 participants (52.3 ± 10.8 years), and the Jikei dataset, with 10,361 participants (50.3 ± 11.0 years). In both, IOP was measured via noncontact tonometry. We obtained daily average air temperature, station-level air pressure, and relative humidity from the Japan Meteorological Agency. We used multiple linear regression, adjusting for known IOP-related factors, to evaluate the relationship between climate parameters and IOP; the same analysis was also performed by prefecture as a sensitivity analysis. The mean right-eye IOP was 13.4 ± 3.0 mmHg (JNDS) and 12.7 ± 2.8 mmHg (Jikei). Air temperature showed a significant negative correlation with IOP (JNDS: β = − 0.032, 95% CI: − 0.033 to − 0.032, P < 0.001; Jikei: β = − 0.023, 95% CI: − 0.031 to − 0.016, P < 0.001), whereas neither air pressure nor humidity was associated with IOP. Sensitivity analysis indicated a stronger, though not significant, relationship between IOP and temperature in colder regions (correlation coefficient R = − 0.21, P = 0.174). These findings underscore the importance of considering climate, particularly temperature, in IOP fluctuations.

Introduction

Glaucoma is the second most common cause of blindness worldwide1, and the global prevalence of glaucoma is estimated to increase to 111.8 million by 20402. Intraocular pressure (IOP) plays a pivotal role in glaucoma development. Elevated IOP is considered the most important and clinically modifiable risk factor for glaucoma development and progression3. At present, IOP reduction is the only proven treatment for glaucoma. Therefore, it is important to fully understand the characteristics of IOP, including its influencing factors.

IOP is known to exhibit seasonal fluctuations, with higher levels observed in winter and lower levels in summer4. Seasonal IOP variations occur in both non-glaucomatous and glaucoma patients, including those with normal tension glaucoma5–7. This seasonal variation in IOP has been suggested to be associated with air temperature8. Moreover, a recent cohort study conducted in Mainz, Germany, investigated the association between IOP and climate parameters, reporting that among air temperature, pressure, and humidity, both temperature and air pressure were significantly associated with IOP9. Several studies have also reported associations between IOP and climatic parameters10,11. However, these associations have been investigated only in limited geographic regions and ethnic groups, and thus remain insufficiently elucidated. Further detailed investigations across different climates and populations are warranted to elucidate these associations.

In this study, as part of the Japan Ningen Dock Study (JNDS)12,13, we conducted a large-scale investigation of IOP across the geographically extensive Japanese archipelago, which spans a diverse range of climates from southwest to northeast. Our aim was to examine the association between climatic parameters and IOP across this broad climatic gradient.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study used Japan Ningen Dock Study (JNDS) and the Jikei University datasets. Data were retrospectively collected and anonymized by decoding identifying information from each institute. We obtained demographic, systemic, and anthropometric data including IOP measurements. The study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Japan Society of Ningen Dock and the Ethics Committee of Jikei University School of Medicine. Prior written informed consent was obtained from all participants in the Jikei University Hospital, and informed consent was obtained using the opt-out method in JNDS.

A total of 291 institutes participated in the JNDS, which was conducted from April 1, 2018 to March 31, 2019. We enrolled participants with complete data for IOP in both eyes; body mass index (BMI); systolic blood pressure (SBP); hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c); and a self-administered questionnaire that included details on smoking, drinking, and exercise habits. Among the 1,949,561 participants in the JNDS dataset who were initially evaluated, 1,477,066 eligible participants from 235 institutions, covering 43 of the 47 prefectures in Japan, were included in the final analysis.

The Jikei dataset enrolled examinees who underwent a series of medical examinations at the Center for Preventive Medicine of Jikei University Hospital between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2018. The comprehensive health checkup included physical measurement; blood pressure; electrocardiogram; heart rate measurement; audiometry; respiratory function test; blood, urine, and fecal tests; chest radiography and upper gastrointestinal series; abdominal ultrasound; physical examination by physicians (chest auscultation and neck and abdominal palpation); and interviews for medical history and lifestyle habits. The ophthalmic examinations include visual acuity test, tonometry, and fundus photography. The Jikei dataset involved 37,064 individuals. Duplicate individuals and those with ocular diseases including glaucoma were excluded. Ocular diseases were identified based on the medical interview at the health checkup. Finally, we enrolled participants who had complete data for IOP in both eyes; BMI; waist circumference (WC); SBP; diastolic blood pressure (DBP); heart rate; fasting plasma glucose (FPG); HbA1c; and a self-administered questionnaire that included details on smoking, drinking, and exercise habits. This resulted in the inclusion of 10,361 participants.

Measurements

IOP was measured using a non-contact tonometer. TX-10 (Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was used as the IOP-measuring device at Jikei University Hospital, while the devices varied among facilities in the JNDS, as shown in the previous study12. The timing of IOP measurement was not strictly standardized and varied among participants; however, in most cases, the measurements were performed in the morning during the health checkup. In the Jikei dataset, IOP was measured between 9:00 and 12:00 p.m.

Data included SBP; DBP; and anthropometric parameters such as weight, height, and WC. BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m)2. FPG was measured using auto-analyzers, and HbA1c was determined using a latex immunoturbidimetric method or high-performance liquid chromatography. Smoking and drinking status and exercise habits were determined based on the patients’ answers to the questionnaire, which was in accordance with the “specific medical checkup” prescribed by the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare in Japan.

The meteorological data was obtained from the open data provided by the Japan Meteorological Agency. Based on the prefecture and date where participants underwent tonometry, we assigned the corresponding daily average air temperature, daily average air pressure at station level, and daily average relative humidity to each participant. For the climate parameters of each prefecture, we adopted the information observed in the prefectural capital.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The 95% confidence interval (CI) was included as appropriate. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with the mean IOP value of the right and left eye as the dependent variable. We selected factors associated with IOP from previously published data14–19. Age, BMI, WC, SBP, DBP, heart rate, FPG, and HbA1c were treated as continuous variables, whereas sex, smoking, drinking status, and exercise were treated as categorical variables and converted to dummy variables. Subsequently, univariate linear regression analysis was performed to identify potential individual factors. Finally, significant variables in the univariate analysis (i.e., those with P < 0.05) were included in the multivariable analysis to identify independent factors. Considering multicollinearity, variables that were significantly correlated were not enrolled simultaneously. To further examine whether the effects of climatic parameters on IOP differed between warmer and colder regions in Japan, we conducted additional multivariable regression analyses including interaction terms between longitude and each climatic parameter. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1; http://r-project.org).

Results

The characteristics of the 1,477,066 study participants in the JNDS dataset are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 52.3 ± 10.8 years (range, 20–98 years), and 612,182 (41.4%) of the participants were women. The mean IOP in the right eye was 13.4 ± 3.0 mmHg. After adjusting for covariates, JNDS dataset demonstrated that IOP decreased as the temperature increased (β=−0.032, 95%CI: −0.033 to −0.032, P < 0.001) (Table 2). The other two metrics, air pressure and humidity, showed little to no correlation with IOP (β = 0.003, 95%CI: 0.002 to 0.003, P < 0.001; β=−0.001, 95%CI: −0.001 to −0.000, P = 0.002).

As a sensitivity analysis, we performed the same multiple regression analysis for each prefecture separately. The β values obtained from these analyses were plotted in a scatterplot (Fig. 1). Although there was some variation in the β values, the association between temperature and IOP generally showed a median β value of −0.027 (Fig. 1A). Although not statistically significant, there was a tendency for the impact of temperature on IOP to be more pronounced in prefectures located on the eastern side of Japan (correlation coefficient R = −0.21, P = 0.174). On the other hand, the β coefficient for the association between IOP and air pressure was close to 0 in many prefectures (Fig. 1B). Similarly, the β coefficient for the association between IOP and humidity also hovered around 0 (Fig. 1C). Based on the results of Fig. 1A, which suggested regional differences in the β values for the association between temperature and IOP across Japan, we further examined the interaction between longitude and temperature using a multivariable regression analysis. The interaction term was statistically significant (β = −0.0005, 95% CI: −0.0007 to −0.0003, P < 0.001). For instance, when temperature decreases by 10 °C, the predicted increase in IOP is approximately 0.07 mmHg greater in Hokkaido (longitude 141.3°E) than in Okinawa (longitude 127.7°E). These findings are consistent with the trend illustrated in Fig. 1A.

Discussion

Using nationwide IOP data from the Japanese population, we analyzed the association between IOP and three climatic parameters: air temperature, air pressure, and humidity. The results indicated that air temperature had the strongest association with IOP; specifically, a 10 °C decrease in temperature was associated with a 0.32 mmHg increase in IOP. In contrast, air pressure and humidity had minimal effects on IOP. Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results, further supported by analyses using an independent dataset. In Japan, the annual difference between the minimum and maximum daily temperatures exceeds 30 °C, suggesting that IOP may fluctuate by approximately 0.96 mmHg or more due to temperature changes. In our previous study, we reported that seasonal IOP fluctuations with an average amplitude of 1.13 mmHg in patients with primary open-angle glaucoma were associated with retinal nerve fiber layer thinning20. Taken together, these findings indicate that the influence of temperature on IOP should not be considered negligible in clinical practice.

A study from Mainz, Germany, similarly investigated these three climatic parameters in relation to IOP and reported that air temperature had the strongest influence9. Asaoka et al. conducted a similar analysis in the Chubu region of Japan, showing that multiple meteorological factors, including temperature, were associated with IOP10. Taken together with our findings, these studies provide consistent evidence of a temperature–IOP relationship. The β coefficient for this association was − 0.013 in Hartmann et al.’s report from Germany and − 0.009 in Asaoka et al.’s study in Japan, whereas our study demonstrated a stronger association at − 0.032. The Mainz study also indicated that air pressure and humidity minimally affected IOP, which aligns with our results. In contrast, Ikushima et al., analyzing data from 2,431 participants in the population-based Tajimi Study, found no statistically significant correlation between temperature and IOP, and reported a positive correlation between atmospheric pressure and IOP11. The reason for this discrepancy remains unclear but may involve differences in sample size or in the confounding factors adjusted for in each study. It should also be noted that Ikushima et al. measured IOP using a Goldmann applanation tonometer, which may contribute to variations in results.

Although IOP measurements are conducted indoors, the meteorological data are observed outdoors. In colder regions and seasons, IOP tests are performed in heated facilities, while in hotter regions and seasons, they are conducted in air-conditioned environments. Therefore, it is important to note that this study may not fully capture the true association between air temperature and IOP. The results obtained in this study might underestimate the true association.

The sensitivity analysis conducted for each prefecture showed a slight tendency for the association between temperature and IOP to become stronger as longitude increases. Since the Japanese archipelago extends from the southwest to the northeast, regions with higher longitudes are located further north and thus experience colder climates. This suggests that the impact of temperature on IOP may be more pronounced in colder regions. In other words, the association between temperature and IOP might not be linear, but rather non-linear. It should be noted that the finding was not statistically significant and only showed a slight trend. In any case, by encompassing diverse climate zones across the entire Japanese archipelago, our study demonstrated a consistently negative correlation between IOP and air temperature in every region.

One of the strengths of big data analysis is its ability to identify subtle factors related to IOP that may have been undetectable with smaller sample sizes13. We analyzed two large, independent datasets, each with distinct strengths and limitations, as previously reported21. The Jikei dataset, though smaller, excluded patients with glaucoma and thus avoided the influence of IOP-lowering medications while utilizing a uniform IOP-measurement device. Contrastingly, the JNDS dataset employed multiple devices across participating facilities and included individuals taking IOP-lowering agents but offered a much larger population for more detailed stratification analyses. By examining both datasets with these complementary characteristics, we strengthened the robustness of our findings. The impact of air temperature, which is weaker than that of known IOP-related factors, such as blood pressure and BMI, may not have been discernible without validation in a large sample size.

This study had some limitations. First, IOP was measured using noncontact tonometry, which is considered less accurate than applanation tonometry. And, in JNDS dataset, the IOP measuring device and number of measurements varied among participating institutions. Second, in the Jikei dataset, the presence of ocular diseases including glaucoma was assessed by medical interview; therefore, undiagnosed ocular diseases could not be identified. Third, the comprehensive health checkup “Ningen Dock” is often conducted for employers to manage and ensure the health of their employees or wealthy individuals who are health conscious. Therefore, our study participants were likely to be healthier and unrepresentative of the general population of Japan. Fourth, it should be noted that while our analysis considered outdoor air temperature, room temperature at the time of IOP measurement could not be assessed in this study. Finally, our study was unable to account for several potential confounding factors. In particular, seasonal variations in daylight duration, sleep patterns, physical activity, hormonal changes, and autonomic nervous system activity could not be assessed in this analysis.

In conclusion, after adjusting for known factors influencing IOP, we found that among air temperature, air pressure, and humidity, only air temperature was significantly associated with IOP. Specifically, a 10 °C decrease in temperature was associated with a 0.2–0.3 mmHg increase in IOP. While there appeared to be a trend suggesting that the association between temperature and IOP strengthens as temperatures decrease, this was not clearly demonstrated in our study. Air pressure and humidity had minimal impact on IOP. Further research is needed to determine whether these associations hold true across different climates and ethnicities. We believe that this study represents an important step toward a more detailed understanding of the characteristics of IOP variation.
