Association Between Resident Involvement in Cesarean Delivery, Complications, and Operative Times
Authors: Joseph R. Mims, Amanda A. Allshouse, Torri D. Metz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Resident involvement in cesarean delivery was not associated with higher rates of major maternal operative morbidity such as organ injury, reoperation, or death.

Cesarean delivery is the most common major abdominal surgical procedure performed yearly in the United States, making up 32.4% of all deliveries in 2024.1,2 In an era of increasing pressure on hospitals to continue improving quality metrics, reduce maternal morbidity, and obtain increasingly better control over resource utilization and health care costs, studies evaluating modifiable preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative factors are imperative. Of these factors, resident involvement in cesarean deliveries has been relatively understudied. Studies in other surgical specialties, as well as gynecology-specific subspecialties, have suggested longer operative times but overall similar safety outcomes during procedures with resident involvement.3–7 However, obstetrics-specific data, particularly with respect to cesarean delivery, are limited.

For many obstetrics and gynecology trainees, cesarean delivery is the first major surgical procedure in which they participate. It also tends to be the most ubiquitous, with many residents graduating with several hundred cases performed.8 Because of the sheer number of cesarean deliveries performed annually, this procedure offers an opportunity to further explore how resident involvement affects patient quality outcomes (ie, maternal and neonatal morbidity) and operating room resource utilization (ie, time).

To address this gap in the literature, we examined whether there is an association between resident involvement in cesarean deliveries and adverse clinical outcomes and operative times in a large multicenter cohort including both academic and community hospitals. We hypothesized that resident involvement would not be associated with major maternal morbidity but would be associated with longer operative times.

METHODS

This was a secondary analysis of the Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development Maternal-Fetal Medicine Units Network APEX (The Assessment of Perinatal Excellence) study, an observational study aimed at assisting in the development of quality measures for intrapartum obstetrics care.9 In the APEX study, data were abstracted from the medical records of eligible participants who delivered on randomly selected days between March 2008 and February 2011. Eligible participants were at least at 23 weeks of gestation and delivered at 1 of the 25 included hospitals with a live fetus. Approval from the IRB was obtained at all sites at the time of the parent study. This secondary analysis was exempt from additional IRB approval given the use of deidentified data.

The present analysis was limited to patients undergoing cesarean delivery with singleton, nonanomalous, term (more than 37 weeks) pregnancies. Patients diagnosed with multifetal gestation, antepartum stillbirth, fetal anomalies, or placenta accreta spectrum were excluded. In addition, patients who had missing data on key exposure or outcome variables were excluded.

The primary exposure was any resident involvement in the cesarean delivery recorded as a dichotomous variable based on abstraction from the operative note. In exploratory analyses, resident involvement was evaluated on the basis of the number of residents involved in the cesarean delivery, which was originally abstracted from the operative note as zero, one, or two or more residents involved. Resident level was also examined in exploratory analyses as first year (PGY-1) or other (PGY-2+).

The primary outcome was major maternal operative morbidity, defined as bowel or genitourinary injury, peripartum hysterectomy for uterine trauma, reoperation for bleeding or uterine trauma, wound dehiscence requiring reoperation, or maternal death. Secondary outcomes were minor maternal operative morbidity (postpartum hemorrhage requiring blood transfusion, wound reopening, or wound cellulitis), a composite of neonatal morbidity (Apgar score below 7 at 5 minutes, chest compressions, intubation for ventilation, skin laceration, arterial cord pH below 7.0, neonatal intensive care unit admission, neonatal seizures, intrapartum stillbirth, or neonatal death), and operative time (time from skin incision to delivery and total operative time from skin incision to completion of skin closure). Participants with implausibly short or long surgery times, defined as total surgery time less than 4 minutes or greater than 6.5 hours, or incision to delivery time greater than 2 hours were excluded from analyses of operative times.

We summarized demographic characteristics, clinical history, labor characteristics, and neonatal characteristics with frequency and percentages for categorical measures. We compared characteristics between patients whose care team included a resident and those whose care team did not, using a χ2 test for categorical or a t test for continuous measures. For the primary outcome and each of its components, we summarized the results by reporting frequencies and percentages, and we compared the proportions with the outcome using χ2 or exact χ2 in presence of cell count(s) less than 5. A similar approach was taken for the secondary maternal and neonatal morbidity outcomes. For secondary time duration outcomes, outcomes were summarized with geometric mean and 95% CIs and compared between patients whose care team included a resident and those whose care team did not with a two-sample t test.

Propensity score analysis was used to balance exposure groups on the following characteristics, which were defined a priori based on clinical importance: body mass index (BMI; continuous or dichotomized at 30), number of prior cesarean deliveries (categorical: 0, 1–2, 3 or more), timing of cesarean delivery (intrapartum or scheduled), diagnosis of suspected chorioamnionitis, maternal age, insurance status (private, Medicaid, or other), medical comorbidities (any diabetes mellitus or chronic hypertension), substance use, birth weight for gestational age category (high, average, low), and gestational age in weeks (integer 37–42). Using this set of variables, we estimated a logistic regression model with the outcome “received care from resident.” Balance between groups was determined by evaluation of the standardized mean difference (SMD) for each attribute used in the estimation of propensity scores, with an SMD less than 0.25 deemed acceptable in achieving balance. From the estimated propensity score of resident involvement, an average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) weight was calculated for each individual equal to 1 for patients whose care team included a resident and weighted by odds [pi/(1−pi)] where pi is the propensity score (probability care team included a resident) for the ith subject for patients whose care did not include a resident. Three sets of propensity scores were estimated: binary exposure (primary and secondary analyses), three-category number of residents involved (exploratory analysis 1: none, one, two to three), and three-category years of resident experience (exploratory analysis 2: none, year 1, year 2–4, or other resident[s]).

We reported risk ratios from ATT-weighted robust Poisson regression for binary outcomes and ATT-weighted average percent longer duration of surgery between groups for duration outcomes from log linear regression, with the outcome, duration, log transformed, yielding estimates of percentage change (difference) from one group to the other. For comparisons in which exposure was in three categories, generalized propensity scores were estimated from the same covariate list as previously stated, followed by modified Poisson regression to estimate relative risks (RRs).10 All analyses were performed in SAS 9.4, with propensity scores created with PSMATCH. We followed STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for reporting of observational studies. Adjustment was not made for multiple comparisons in the analysis.

RESULTS

Of the 118,422 deliveries in the parent study, 28,087 were included for this analysis (Fig. 1). A total of 20,728 deliveries (73.8%) involved at least one resident during the cesarean, with 13,359 involving only one resident and 7,369 with two or three residents. In addition, 1,708 deliveries (6%) were excluded from operative time analysis because of implausible length of procedure, leaving 26,379 deliveries with valid surgical time data.

Delivery involving resident care was associated with younger age, lower BMI, delivering person race, history of substance use (cocaine or methamphetamine), publicly funded health insurance, medical comorbidities (hypertension or diabetes), diagnosis of chorioamnionitis during labor, and primary cesarean. Resident involvement was inversely associated with scheduled cesarean deliveries. Neonatal characteristics also varied between the two groups (Table 1), with resident care being associated with later gestational age at delivery.

In unadjusted analysis, the rate of the primary outcome, a composite of major maternal operative morbidity, was 0.6% (n=119) and 0.4% (n=27) in the resident involvement and no resident involvement groups, respectively (P=.034) (Table 2). Among the individual components of the composite outcome, resident involvement was associated with a higher rate of any operative injury in unadjusted analyses (0.2% vs 0.1%, P=.027) (Table 2). No other significant between-group differences were detected in unadjusted comparisons. Groups were balanced after propensity score weighting (Appendix 1, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E696), with the largest observed absolute SMD below 0.05 (substantially below our threshold of 0.25). In models using ATT-weighted Poisson regression, there was no difference in the primary major maternal operative composite morbidity outcome between the two study groups (ATT-weighted RR [ATT RR] 1.24, 95% CI, 0.72–2.14) (Table 3).

In exploratory analyses, the ATT results for the primary outcome remained nonsignificant regardless of the number of residents involved (one versus none: ATT RR 1.07, 95% CI, 0.50–2.33; two to three versus none: ATT RR 1.22, 95% CI, 0.57–2.59) (Appendix 2, http://links.lww.com/AOG/E696) or the resident's level of training (any first-year resident vs none: ATT RR 0.87, 95% CI, 0.40–1.93; higher-level resident vs none: ATT RR 1.37, 95% CI, 0.65–2.89) (Appendix 3, http://links.lww.com/AOG/E696).

In unadjusted analyses, the rate of the secondary outcome, a composite of minor maternal operative morbidity, was 5.4% (n=1,125) in cases with resident involvement and 2.2% (n=159) in those without (P<.001) (Table 2). With respect to the individual components of the secondary outcome, postpartum hemorrhage requiring blood transfusion occurred more frequently with resident involvement, which drove the observed association (5.0% vs 1.8%, P<.001) (Table 2). There were no other significant differences between groups. Resident involvement remained associated with a higher rate of the secondary composite minor maternal operative morbidity outcome in weighted modeling (ATT RR 1.88, 95% CI, 1.52–2.32) (Table 3).

In exploratory analyses, an association remained between resident involvement and the secondary outcome, by both number of residents (one vs none: ATT RR 1.42, 95% CI, 1.03–1.96; two to three vs none: ATT RR 2.44, 95% CI, 1.78–3.34) (Appendix 2, http://links.lww.com/AOG/E696) and level of training (any first-year resident vs none: ATT RR 1.79, 95% CI, 1.30–2.46; higher-level resident vs none: ATT RR 2.06, 95% CI, 1.51–2.83) (Appendix 3, http://links.lww.com/AOG/E696).

Neonatal morbidity occurred in 11.0% of deliveries with resident involvement (n=2,283) and 10.0% of those without (n=734, P=.013) (Table 2). In ATT-weighted modeling, resident involvement was associated with a lower rate of neonatal morbidity (ATT RR 0.87, 95% CI, 0.79–0.96) (Table 3). Compared with cases without resident involvement, neonatal morbidity was lower in deliveries with one resident (ATT RR 0.81, 95% CI, 0.70–0.94) and two to three residents (ATT RR 0.80, 95% CI, 0.69–0.92) (Appendix 2, http://links.lww.com/AOG/E696). This pattern was consistent across resident levels, including involvement of first-year residents (ATT RR 0.79, 95% CI, 0.68–0.91) and higher-level residents (ATT RR 0.81, 95% CI, 0.70–0.93) (Appendix 3, http://links.lww.com/AOG/E696).

Operative time among cases with resident involvement was significantly longer than in cases without a resident. In unadjusted comparisons, geometric mean incision-to-delivery time was 8.5 minutes (95% CI, 8.4–8.6) with resident involvement and 6.9 minutes (95% CI, 6.8–6.9) without (P<.001). Mean total operative time was 47.5 minutes (95% CI, 47.2–47.7) and 36.9 minutes (95% CI, 26.6–37.2) with and without residents, respectively (P<.001) (Table 2). In ATT-weighted modeling, these differences in operative time persisted. Resident involvement was associated with a 22.7% longer incision-to-delivery operative time (95% CI, 20.4–25.0) and 24.6% longer total operative time (95% CI, 23.3–26.0) (Table 3).

In exploratory analyses, both a higher number of residents and involvement of a first-year resident were associated with longer operative time. The geometric mean incision-to-delivery time was 8.4 minutes when one resident was involved (ATT 15.4% longer duration compared with no resident involvement, 95% CI, 11.8–19.0%), 8.7 minutes when two to three residents were involved (ATT 22.5% longer duration compared with no resident involvement, 95% CI, 18.9–26.1%), and 6.9 minutes with no resident involvement. Total operative times were geometric mean 45.0 minutes (ATT 16.4% longer duration, 95% CI, 14.3–18.5%) and geometric mean 52.1 minutes (ATT 32.6% longer duration [95% CI, 30.5–34.6%]) for one resident involved and two to three residents involved, respectively (Appendix 2, http://links.lww.com/AOG/E696). When we examined the association between resident level and operative times, first-year involvement was associated with a mean incision-to-delivery time of geometric mean 9.0 minutes (ATT 25.7% longer duration, 95% CI, 22.1–29.2%) compared with geometric mean 8.1 minutes (ATT 14.4% longer duration, 95% CI, 10.8–18.0%) for higher-level residents. Total operative time was geometric mean 48.5 minutes (ATT 26.6% longer duration, 95% CI, 24.4–28.7%) and geometric mean 46.8 minutes (ATT 23.4% longer duration, 95% CI, 21.3–25.5%), respectively (Appendix 3, http://links.lww.com/AOG/E696).

DISCUSSION

In our study of more than 28,000 term, nonanomalous, singleton cesarean deliveries, resident participation was not associated with a significant increase in our primary outcome of major maternal operative morbidity such as organ injury, hysterectomy, or death among those exposed to resident involvement in their deliveries. Resident involvement was associated with higher rates of the secondary outcome of minor operative morbidity, driven primarily by postpartum hemorrhage requiring a blood transfusion, and longer surgical duration. Notably, resident participation was also associated with lower neonatal morbidity; however, this association was small in magnitude.

Prior studies on surgical outcomes stratified by resident involvement within obstetrics and gynecology report similar findings. Coates et al3 evaluated surgical outcomes and complications in a cohort of patients undergoing vaginal pelvic reconstructive surgery for pelvic organ prolapse. The cohorts were defined by the surgical team, either attending alone or a resident under the supervision of a chief resident or attending. Over 5 years, 310 patients underwent surgical prolapse repair. Between the two groups, there was a statistically significant longer operative duration in the resident-involved cases compared with the attending-only cases. There were, however, no differences in surgical outcomes, including blood loss, urologic complications, length of hospital admission, or durability, as evidenced by pelvic prolapse recurrence. Akingba et al4 compared surgical outcomes of patients undergoing emergent or elective hysterectomy for benign conditions when performed by supervised residents with those performed by attendings without resident involvement. Of 424 total cases, there was a statistically significant longer operative duration in cases with resident involvement. However, this was not felt to be clinically significant, being only 13 minutes longer. There were no significant differences in surgical outcomes between the two groups. Igwe et al5 performed a retrospective review of National Surgical Quality Improvement Project data evaluating the effect of resident involvement on surgical times and operative outcomes in patients undergoing laparoscopic hysterectomy for benign indications. Although resident involvement was not associated with infection, wound, neurorenal, thromboembolic, septic, or cardiopulmonary complications, composite morbidity, or 30-day mortality, there was a statistically significant increase in operative duration, rates of postoperative packed red blood cell transfusion, reoperation, and 30-day readmission.

These findings have been replicated in surgical specialties other than obstetrics and gynecology. Papandria et al6 and Hernández-Irizarry et al7 assessed the effect of resident involvement on patient outcomes and surgical duration in general surgery procedures, consisting mostly of laparoscopic appendectomies, laparoscopic cholecystectomies, and open and laparoscopic inguinal hernia repairs. Again, they found no significant difference in surgical outcomes; however, they observed increased operative times. In addition, Papandria et al6 found that there was no difference in operative duration by resident level. Using American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Project data, Raval et al11 found slightly higher morbidity and no difference in mortality with resident involvement in care for general surgery procedures. We similarly saw no association between resident involvement and serious maternal morbidity or mortality. Although there was an association with minor maternal morbidity in our study, neonatal outcomes were improved.

Mourad et al12 conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients undergoing cesarean delivery in a single obstetric practice performed by either two attending physicians or an attending physician supervising a resident physician. However, their study was limited to patients undergoing high-order cesarean delivery, defined as a third- or higher-order cesarean. They observed slightly increased operative times in resident-involved cases but no significant differences in measured outcomes, including wound complications, blood loss, blood transfusion, major maternal morbidity, postoperative endometritis, or postoperative days in the hospital. There were also no differences in outcomes between cases performed by junior residents and cases performed by senior residents.

We also noted an association between resident involvement in the cesarean delivery and improved neonatal outcomes in adjusted analyses of this cohort. The slightly lower ATT-weighted risk of neonatal morbidity could be explained by closer supervision and vigilance on the part of the surgical team with the involvement of residents during labor management who are assigned strictly to coverage of labor and delivery without competing obligations. There may also be unknown differences in delivery characteristics that were not adequately accounted for with propensity score analysis. Notably, the absolute proportions of the neonatal composite outcome in the groups were 11% and 10%. Thus, the differences are unlikely to be clinically significant.

The finding of an association between resident involvement and higher minor operative morbidity rates warrants further consideration. Although it is true that the patients with resident involvement in their delivery were more likely to have been delivered unscheduled or diagnosed with suspected chorioamnionitis and other comorbid conditions such as hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and diabetes, this finding persisted even after adjustment for these potential confounders. Given the fact that minor operative morbidity was driven predominantly by the increased rate of postpartum hemorrhage requiring blood transfusion, we hypothesize that this may be directly related to the increased operative duration. More specifically, a longer interval between delivery and surgical completion could explain a higher estimated surgical blood loss.

This study has several strengths, including the large, multicenter cohort including both academic and community hospitals and the analytic approach that incorporates propensity score weighting to balance characteristics between the comparison groups. All data were abstracted from the medical record by trained research personnel. We were also able to perform exploratory analyses examining multiple levels of resident participation. This allowed us to perform a more nuanced assessment of how trainee involvement could influence outcomes. The study also reflects a diverse patient population with respect to clinical characteristics. However, differences in care settings and clinician-level variation between the groups, as well as the inability to adjust for hospital site, may limit the generalizability of these findings.

The largest limitation is the observational nature of the study. Residual confounding remains a possibility despite rigorous adjustment, the most significant of which may be the level of resident involvement in the procedure (eg, it is unknown how often the resident was functioning as the primary surgeon or as a surgical assistant). In addition, operative time, although a meaningful efficiency measure, is influenced by teaching activities that are essential for surgical education and may not necessarily reflect inefficiency on the part of the surgical resident. In addition, we could not adjust for hospital site in our models because it was not available in the deidentified data release. Lastly, there were statistically and plausibly clinically significant differences in several demographic and clinical characteristics between the two groups, including race, insurance status, and comorbid conditions. Although most of these variables were incorporated into the propensity score model and balance was achieved after weighting, residual confounding remains possible.

In addition, the data for this analysis were collected between 2008 and 2011 for the APEX study. Changes in obstetric practice and health system factors, including those related to the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, may limit the applicability of these findings today. However, many fundamental aspects of cesarean delivery and resident surgical training have remained consistent, which likely supports the contemporary relevance of these results. Furthermore, participating institutions in the Maternal-Fetal Medicine Units are often large, academic, or high-resource centers with specific clinical protocols and supervision standards. These factors may contribute to improved outcomes independently of resident involvement. Hence, our findings may be most generalizable to similar practice environments and may not fully reflect outcomes in low-resource or nonacademic settings.

In conclusion, resident involvement in cesarean delivery was not associated with an increase in major maternal or neonatal morbidity among those exposed to resident involvement in their deliveries. However, it was associated with longer operative times and minor maternal morbidity, especially postpartum hemorrhage requiring blood transfusion. Overall, these findings support that resident participation in cesarean delivery is not associated with major adverse outcomes, providing unique evidence to guide discussion about surgical education and operating room efficiency.
