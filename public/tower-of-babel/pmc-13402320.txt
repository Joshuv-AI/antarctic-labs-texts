Liver fibrosis burden and risk of incident arrhythmia in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease
Authors: Keungmo Yang, Seung Hyun Kim, Jaejun Lee, Young Chang, Jeong-Ju Yoo, Sae Hwan Lee, Soung Won Jeong, Sang Gyune Kim, Jae Young Jang, Young Seok Kim, Hong Soo Kim, Yongeun Cho, Tom Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a highly prevalent condition associated with increased cardiometabolic risk. While hepatic fibrosis is a determinant of prognosis in MASLD, its relationship with incident arrhythmic outcomes, particularly atrial fibrillation (AF), remains incompletely defined. We investigated whether liver fibrosis burden assessed by noninvasive fibrosis scores is associated with incident electrocardiogram (ECG)-detected arrhythmia in a population-based cohort. We analyzed data from the Lifelines prospective cohort study in the Netherlands. MASLD was defined using the hepatic steatosis index, cardiometabolic risk factors, and exclusion of excess alcohol intake. Liver fibrosis was assessed using the fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index and the aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI). Incident arrhythmia and AF were identified exclusively by follow-up ECG recordings. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate associations, with sequential adjustment for cardiometabolic confounders and age-stratified analyses. Among 10,263 participants with MASLD, elevated fibrosis burden defined by FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 was consistently associated with a higher risk of incident ECG-detected arrhythmia and AF. This association remained significant after adjustment for major metabolic risk factors and was observed within the MASLD cohort. Notably, the excess arrhythmic risk was age dependent, with associations limited to participants aged ≥ 50 years, whereas no incident events occurred among younger individuals with elevated FIB-4. Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has been focused as the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide. Recent population-based data from the United States demonstrate that the age-adjusted prevalence of MASLD reaches approximately 32% among adults, exceeding that of other steatotic liver disease etiologies1. Beyond steatosis, hepatic fibrosis represents the important determinant of prognosis in MASLD. In nationally representative cohorts, nearly 30% of individuals with diabetes and MASLD exhibit significant fibrosis, while advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis are present in approximately 13% and 10%, respectively2. These findings present that a substantial proportion of MASLD patients in the general population already has clinically significant liver injury, even outside tertiary care settings. Such epidemiologic patterns reinforce the concept that MASLD-related fibrosis reflects a high-risk systemic phenotype rather than an isolated hepatic condition.

Given the close overlap between metabolic dysfunction and cardiovascular remodeling, increasing attention has focused on the potential extrahepatic consequences of MASLD-related fibrosis, particularly its role in cardiovascular and arrhythmic outcomes3.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and other clinically meaningful arrhythmias represent a major and growing public health burden4. Their prevalence rises with age, and AF is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality through thromboembolism, heart failure, and impaired quality of life5. Contemporary epidemiologic data also highlight that arrhythmia risk is shaped by a mixture of fixed susceptibility (age, gender) and modifiable cardiometabolic exposures (hypertension, diabetes, adiposity, alcohol intake), many of which are enriched in MASLD6. Importantly, arrhythmias are often unrecognized when ascertained solely through routine clinical encounters or self-report, because paroxysmal events and subclinical AF frequently go undetected without structured rhythm evaluation7. Recent global burden of disease analyses indicate that while the burden of AF remains concentrated in older adults, mortality- and disability-related burden is increasingly rising in younger and middle-aged populations, driven by modifiable cardiometabolic risk factors8.

Evidences suggests that liver disease and hepatic fibrosis might develop arrhythmia through several mechanisms. Chronic hepatic inflammation and systemic cytokine activation may promote atrial structural remodeling and electrical instability9. Liver stiffness might affect broader systemic hemodynamic stability, potentially related to subclinical venous congestion observed in individuals with AF10. In addition, MASLD-related cardiometabolic dysfunction, sleep-disordered breathing, and changes in gut-liver signaling might aggravate atrial stretch11. However, the existing literature remains heterogeneous with respect to liver disease definitions, arrhythmia ascertainment methods, and the degree of confounding control, making it difficult to determine whether hepatic fibrosis itself, or the cardiometabolic environment it represents, is the dominant driver of arrhythmic risk.

Noninvasive fibrosis scores such as the fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index and the aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI) are widely used in clinical practice because they are readily available and scalable for epidemiologic studies12. FIB-4 incorporates age, aminotransferases, and platelet count, and has been used to enrich for clinically relevant fibrosis in MASLD13,14. APRI emphasizes aminotransferase and platelet changes and is frequently applied across diverse liver disease settings15,16. Yet, whether these scoring system identify individuals at higher risk for objectively detected incident arrhythmias remains insufficiently clarified.

To address these gaps, we evaluated the association between liver fibrosis burden using noninvasive fibrosis scores with incident electrocardiogram (ECG)–detected arrhythmia in a large population cohort. By focusing on ECG-detected outcomes during follow-up, we aimed to reduce misclassification related to symptom-driven diagnoses and to clearly capture incident events over time. We examined associations in the overall cohort and within the MASLD subgroup and further assessed age-stratified effects to analyze heterogeneity across clinically relevant age groups. We hypothesized that higher fibrosis burden would be associated with an increased risk of ECG-detected incident arrhythmia, particularly AF, and that this relationship would be more prominent in older individuals with MASLD.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted using data from the Lifelines cohort, a large, prospective, population-based cohort study in the Netherlands designed to investigate determinants of chronic diseases and healthy aging. Lifelines includes extensive baseline assessments and repeated follow-up examinations, comprising questionnaires, physical measurements, laboratory tests, and ECG recordings. Lifelines study received ethical approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of the University Medical Center Groningen (METC UMCG, METc 2007/152). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Among 151,113 participants initially enrolled in the Lifelines cohort, 6628 individuals with a cancer diagnosis at baseline were excluded. Of the remaining 144,485 participants, 1436 individuals with any form of hepatitis diagnosed at baseline were further excluded. Among the 143,049 participants who remained, individuals who did not meet the criteria for MASLD or had missing data required for the assessment of fibrosis indices were excluded (n = 132,786). The final cohort therefore comprised 10,263 participants with MASLD, including 9,257 participants with FIB-4 < 1.45 and 1006 participants with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 (Fig. 1).

Definition of MASLD

MASLD was operationally defined at baseline using the hepatic steatosis index (HSI), alcohol intake thresholds, and cardiometabolic risk factors (CMRFs). HSI was calculated as follows:

HSI = 8 × [alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L)/aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L)] + body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) + 2 (if diabetes mellitus) + 2 (if female)17.

Hepatic steatosis was defined as HSI ≥ 36. Average daily alcohol intake (g/day) was derived from baseline questionnaire data on drinking frequency and the number of standard drinks (assuming 10 g ethanol per drink). Participants were considered eligible for MASLD only if alcohol intake was below gender-specific thresholds (≤ 30 g/day for men and ≤ 20 g/day for women).

CMRFs were defined using baseline measurements and/or self-reported physician diagnoses. A participant was considered to have metabolic dysfunction if any of the following were present: overweight (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), central obesity (waist circumference ≥ 94 cm in men or ≥ 80 cm in women), prediabetes/diabetes (HbA1c ≥ 5.7% or self-reported diabetes), elevated systolic blood pressure (SBP, ≥ 130 mmHg or self-reported hypertension), or dyslipidemia defined by triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (≤ 40 mg/dL in men or ≤ 50 mg/dL in women), or a self-reported history of dyslipidemia.

MASLD was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis (HSI ≥ 36) in combination with at least one CMRF and alcohol intake within the predefined thresholds18.

Assessment of liver fibrosis with noninvasive scores

Liver fibrosis burden was assessed at baseline using two noninvasive fibrosis indices: FIB-4 and APRI.

The FIB-4 score was calculated using the following formula:

FIB-4 = Age (years) × AST (U/L)/[Platelet count (109/L) × ALT(U/L)1/2] 13.

Participants were categorized into two groups using a predefined cutoff of FIB-4 ≥ 1.45, indicating elevated fibrosis burden, and FIB-4 < 1.4513.

The APRI score was calculated as:

APRI = [AST (U/L)/upper normal limit for AST (U/L)/platelet counts (109/L)] × 10015.

An APRI cutoff value of ≥ 0.46 was applied in secondary analyses19.

Arrhythmia outcomes and follow-up

At baseline, arrhythmia status was comprehensively assessed using both questionnaire-based self-reported information and ECG recordings. Questionnaire data captured a history of physician-diagnosed arrhythmia and arrhythmia-related symptoms. In addition, a resting 12-lead ECG was performed at the baseline visit, and the presence of AF, atrial flutter, or other supraventricular arrhythmias was recorded. Baseline arrhythmia was defined as the presence of any self-reported arrhythmia diagnosis or symptoms, or any arrhythmia detected on baseline ECG.

For incident analyses, participants with any arrhythmia detected on baseline ECG were excluded, irrespective of questionnaire-reported history or symptoms, to ensure that all analyzed outcomes represent incident ECG-detected events.

During follow-up, arrhythmic outcomes were identified exclusively based on ECG findings, without use of questionnaire-based symptom reports. Follow-up ECG assessments consisted of standardized resting 12-lead ECG recordings performed at scheduled study visits, including baseline (1 A) and follow-up assessments (e.g., 2 A). Incident arrhythmia was defined as the first occurrence of any ECG-detected arrhythmia at follow-up, including AF, atrial flutter, or other supraventricular arrhythmias, among participants who were free of arrhythmia on baseline ECG. AF identified on follow-up ECG was evaluated as a specified secondary outcome.

Time-to-event definition and censoring

Time-to-event was calculated in months using defined examination intervals derived from the Lifelines global summary data. For participants who developed incident arrhythmia, follow-up time was defined as the interval from the baseline ECG assessment to the first follow-up ECG at which arrhythmia was detected. For participants without incident arrhythmia, follow-up time was defined as the interval from baseline to the most recent ECG assessment available.

To ensure consistency across participants, follow-up time was capped at 120 months (10 years). Participants who reached 120 months of follow-up without an incident arrhythmic event were censored at that time point. Individuals with missing follow-up interval data or invalid follow-up times were excluded from time-to-event analyses.

Statistical analysis

Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. The cumulative incidence of ECG-detected arrhythmia and AF was estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods and compared between fibrosis categories using the log-rank test. Associations between liver fibrosis scoring and incident arrhythmic outcomes were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariable models were constructed sequentially: Model 1 was adjusted for age and gender; Model 2 additionally included alcohol consumption; Model 3 further adjusted for waist circumference; and Model 4 included diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.

Age-stratified analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modification, with analyses performed separately in participants aged < 50 years and ≥ 50 years, reflecting prior evidence that cardiovascular and arrhythmic risks rise disproportionately beyond midlife and that individuals < 50 years constitute a relatively low-risk reference group20. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.2. (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the MASLD cohort was stratified by FIB-4 category. Among 10,263 participants with MASLD, 1006 (9.8%) had elevated fibrosis burden defined by FIB-4 ≥ 1.45. Participants with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 were more likely to be male (82.7% vs. 48.9%, P < 0.001) and exhibited a less favorable cardiometabolic profiles compared with those with FIB-4 < 1.45. Although median age did not differ between groups, the high FIB-4 group had a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus and a greater burden of central obesity, as represented by significantly larger waist circumference. Blood pressure levels were significantly higher in the FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 group, with higher median systolic and diastolic blood pressure.

Liver-related biochemical parameters differed between fibrosis categories. Participants with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 demonstrated markedly higher AST and ALT levels and lower platelet counts. Serum albumin levels were also significantly lower in the high FIB-4 group. Lipid profiles differed between groups, with higher triglyceride levels and lower HDL-cholesterol concentrations observed among participants with higher FIB-4 index. Median alcohol consumption was higher in the FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 group, although all participants met defined alcohol intake thresholds for MASLD.

Baseline arrhythmic characteristics were largely comparable between fibrosis groups. The prevalence of any arrhythmia, arrhythmia diagnosis, and arrhythmia-related symptoms did not differ significantly according to FIB-4 category (Table 1).

Baseline characteristics stratified by APRI category were also presented. Compared with participants with APRI < 0.46, those with APRI ≥ 0.46 were older and had a higher prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus and hypertension. The APRI ≥ 0.46 group also demonstrated more prominent liver-related abnormalities, including lower platelet counts and a higher prevalence of cirrhosis. In contrast to FIB-4 stratification, baseline arrhythmic conditions were more prevalent among participants with elevated APRI (Supplementary Table 1).

Association between liver fibrosis and baseline arrhythmia

At baseline, the association between liver fibrosis and prevalent arrhythmia varied according to the fibrosis index used. In the MASLD population, liver fibrosis defined by FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 was significantly associated with higher odds of baseline arrhythmia in crude analysis [odds ratio (OR) 1.24, 95% CI 1.07–1.44; P = 0.004]. This association remained significant after adjustment for age and gender (Model 1: OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.18–1.52; P < 0.001) and after additional adjustment for alcohol consumption and waist circumference (Models 2–3; Model 3: OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.09–1.48; P = 0.002). However, the association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant after further adjustment for cardiometabolic comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia (Model 4: OR 1.07, 95% CI 0.92–1.25; P = 0.355).

In age-stratified analyses, participants aged < 50 years showed a similar trend, with higher odds of baseline arrhythmia in crude analysis (OR 1.40, 95% CI 0.88–2.17; P = 0.038). Nevertheless, this association did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for age and gender (Model 1: P = 0.073) or in fully adjusted models (Model 4: OR 1.34, 95% CI 0.83–2.09; P = 0.211). In contrast, no significant association between FIB-4 category and baseline arrhythmia was observed in participants aged ≥ 50 years across all models (Model 4: OR 1.04, 95% CI 0.87–1.24; P = 0.640) (Supplementary Table 2).

In contrast to FIB-4, liver fibrosis defined by APRI ≥ 0.46 was not significantly associated with baseline arrhythmia in the overall MASLD population (Model 4: OR 1.13, 95% CI 0.93–1.37; P = 0.218) or in age-stratified analyses (age < 5 0 years: Model 4 P = 0.278; age ≥ 50 years: Model 4 P = 0.551) (Supplementary Table 3).

Association between liver fibrosis by FIB-4 index and incident arrhythmia during follow up

During follow-up, the incidence rates of ECG-detected arrhythmia were consistently higher among participants with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 compared with those with FIB-4 < 1.45. In the overall population, the incidence rate was 24.8 per 1000 person-years in the FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 group versus 3.1 per 1000 person-years in the FIB-4 < 1.45 group. Similarly, among participants with MASLD, higher incidence rates were observed in those with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 compared with those with FIB-4 < 1.45 (3.34 vs. 0.62 per 1000 person-years). In age-stratified analyses, no incident arrhythmia events occurred among MASLD participants aged < 50 years with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45, whereas higher incidence rates were evident among those aged ≥ 50 years with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 (3.75 vs. 1.7 per 1 000 person-years) (Supplementary Table 4).

Kaplan–Meier analyses demonstrated higher cumulative incidence of ECG-detected incident arrhythmia among participants with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 compared with those with FIB-4 < 1.45. Significant separation of the cumulative incidence curves was observed in the overall population and in the MASLD subgroup (P < 0.001 for both) (Fig. 2A,B). In age-stratified analyses among MASLD participants, no difference in cumulative incidence was observed in individuals aged < 50 years (P = 0.793) (Fig. 2C), whereas a significant difference according to FIB-4 category was prominent in those aged ≥ 50 years (P = 0.019) (Fig. 2D).

Consistent with the Kaplan–Meier findings, Cox proportional hazards analyses showed a strong association between FIB-4-defined fibrosis and incident arrhythmia. In the overall population, FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 was associated with significantly increased risk of incident arrhythmia across all models, including after full multivariable adjustment (Model 4: HR 7.06, 95% CI 4.43–11.23; P < 0.001) (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Similar results were observed in the MASLD subgroup (Model 4: HR 5.10, 95% CI 2.46–10.57; P < 0.001) (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 1B). In age-stratified analyses, no incident arrhythmia events occurred among MASLD participants aged < 50 years with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45, which did not allow HR estimation (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 1C). In contrast, among those aged ≥ 50 years, FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 was associated with a significantly increased risk of incident arrhythmia in crude analysis (HR 2.34, 95% CI 1.31–4.86; P = 0.023), with an attenuated association after multivariable adjustment (Model 4: HR 1.99, 95% CI 0.94–4.20; P = 0.071) (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 1D).

Association between liver fibrosis by APRI and incident arrhythmia during follow up

The incidence rates of ECG-detected arrhythmia according to APRI category were low and comparable across all strata (Supplementary Table 5). In the overall population, the incidence rate was 0.94 per 1000 person-years in participants with APRI ≥ 0.46 and 0.54 per 1000 person-years in those with APRI < 0.46, with similarly low rates observed in the MASLD subgroup and age-stratified analyses.

Consistent with the incidence rate findings, Kaplan–Meier analyses showed no significant differences in cumulative incidence according to APRI category in the overall population, the MASLD subgroup, or across age strata (Supplementary Fig. 2).

In Cox proportional hazards analyses, APRI ≥ 0.46 was not associated with an increased risk of incident arrhythmia in the overall population or in participants with MASLD across all models. No incident arrhythmia events occurred among MASLD participants aged < 50 years with APRI ≥ 0.46, which did not allow HR estimation in this subgroup (Supplementary Table 6, Supplementary Fig. 3).

Association between liver fibrosis by FIB-4 index and incident AF during follow up

With restriction of the outcome to ECG-detected incident AF, incidence rates were higher among participants with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 compared with those with FIB-4 < 1.45. In the overall population, the incidence rate was 2.16 per 1000 person-years in the FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 group versus 0.27 per 1000 person-years in the FIB-4 < 1.45 group. Similarly, among participants with MASLD, higher incidence rates were observed in those with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 (3.33 vs. 0.45 per 1000 person-years). No AF events occurred among MASLD participants aged ≤ 50 years with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45, whereas among those aged > 50 years, the incidence rate was higher in the FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 group compared with the FIB-4 < 1.45 group (3.74 vs. 1.23 per 1000 person-years) (Supplementary Table 7).

Consistent with these findings, Kaplan–Meier analyses demonstrated a significantly higher cumulative incidence of AF among participants with FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 in the overall population and in the MASLD subgroup (P < 0.001 for both) (Supplementary Fig. 4A,B). In age-stratified analyses, no difference was observed in participants aged ≤ 50 years (P = 0.826), whereas a significant separation of cumulative incidence curves according to FIB-4 category was evident among those aged > 50 years (P = 0.002) (Supplementary Fig. 4C,D).

In Cox proportional hazards analyses, FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 was significantly associated with an increased risk of incident AF across all models. In the fully adjusted model, the risk of atrial fibrillation remained markedly elevated in the overall population (HR 7.05, 95% CI 4.23–11.76; P < 0.001) and among participants with MASLD (HR 6.82, 95% CI 3.14–14.78; P < 0.001). No hazard ratio could be estimated in MASLD participants aged ≤ 50 years due to the absence of events, whereas among those aged > 50 years, FIB-4 ≥ 1.45 remained independently associated with incident AF after full adjustment (HR 2.68, 95% CI 1.20–5.97; P = 0.016) (Table 3; Fig. 3).

When modeled as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines, FIB-4 demonstrated a monotonic increase in the risk of incident AF in both the overall population and the MASLD cohort, with no strong evidence of nonlinearity (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Association between liver fibrosis by APRI index and incident AF during follow up

The incidence rates of ECG-detected incident AF according to APRI category were low and comparable across all strata. In the overall population, the incidence rate was 0.42 per 1000 person-years in participants with APRI ≥ 0.46 and 0.49 per 1000 person-years in those with APRI < 0.46. Similarly, low incidence rates were observed in the MASLD subgroup and in age-stratified analyses. No AF events occurred among MASLD participants aged ≤ 50 years with APRI ≥ 0.46 (Supplementary Table 8).

Consistent with these findings, Kaplan–Meier analyses showed no significant differences in the cumulative incidence of AF according to APRI category in the overall population, the MASLD subgroup, or across age strata (Supplementary Fig. 6).

In Cox proportional hazards analyses, APRI ≥ 0.46 was not associated with incident AF in the overall population or among participants with MASLD across all models. No significant associations were observed in age-stratified analyses, including among MASLD participants aged ≥ 50 years (Supplementary Table 9, Supplementary Fig. 7).

Discussion

In this large, population-based cohort with structured ECG follow-up, we found that elevated liver fibrosis burden assessed by the FIB-4 index was significantly associated with an increased risk of incident ECG-detected arrhythmia, particularly atrial fibrillation, among individuals with MASLD. This association was most prominent for incident events during follow-up and remained significant after adjustment for major CMRFs. In contrast, fibrosis defined by APRI was not consistently associated with either prevalent or incident arrhythmic outcomes. Importantly, the observed associations were age dependent, with excess risk confined to participants aged ≥ 50 years, whereas no incident arrhythmic events were observed among younger MASLD participants with elevated FIB-4.

Although MASLD is increasingly recognized as a multisystem disease, the relative contribution of hepatic fibrosis, as opposed to steatosis alone, to cardiovascular and arrhythmic outcomes has remained uncertain. Prior studies have reported heterogeneous associations between fatty liver disease and AF, partly attributable to differences in liver disease definitions and arrhythmia identification. Such investigations have relied on ultrasonography-based definitions of hepatic steatosis, administrative or clinical records, or self-reported arrhythmia history and these approach might contribute to variability in exposure and outcome classification across studies21,22. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating that fibrosis burden captured by FIB-4, rather than MASLD itself, identifies individuals at significantly increased risk of objectively detected incident arrhythmia.

Notably, the lack of association between APRI and incident arrhythmic outcomes presents that not all noninvasive fibrosis scores perform equivalently in this context. While both FIB-4 and APRI incorporate aminotransferases and platelet count, FIB-4 additionally integrates age, which would reflect cumulative exposure metabolic stress. Prior epidemiologic studies have shown that FIB-4 more consistently predicts long-term outcomes, including all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in the patients with liver diseases23. Our results suggest that FIB-4 similarly capture a fibrosis-related arrhythmic phenotype that is not adequately identified by APRI. This result was prominent in in population-based settings with relatively low prevalence of advanced liver disease.

An important observation in this study is the discrepancy between baseline and incident associations. Although FIB-4 was modestly associated with prevalent arrhythmia in crude and partially adjusted models, this relationship was attenuated after accounting for cardiometabolic comorbidities. In contrast, the association with incident ECG-detected arrhythmia during follow up was significant and persisted after multivariable adjustment. These data suggest that fibrosis-related arrhythmic risk would not be fully expressed by cross-sectional analyses and becomes more evident when longitudinal analyses are applied.

From a mechanistic perspective, this temporal pattern is biologically explained. Hepatic fibrosis reflects chronic inflammatory signaling, altered platelet biology, and systemic metabolic dysregulation24,25. These rationales might contribute to progressive atrial remodeling rather than immediate arrhythmia onset. Structural atrial changes, including fibrosis, chamber dilation, and electrical heterogeneity, develop over years and manifest clinically after a latency period26,27. The absence of excess baseline arrhythmia in fully adjusted models therefore does not exclude a potential contribution of fibrosis, but instead emphasizes the value of longitudinal assessment.

The age-stratified findings provide further insight into the clinical context in which fibrosis-related arrhythmic risk becomes relevant. No incident arrhythmia or AF events were observed among MASLD participants aged < 50 years with elevated FIB-4, which did not allow HR estimation in this subgroup. In contrast, among participants aged ≥ 50 years, elevated FIB-4 was consistently associated with higher cumulative incidence and increased hazard of incident arrhythmia and AF, even after adjustment for cardiometabolic factors. These results are consistent to broader epidemiologic data showing that arrhythmic risk accelerates at higher rate beyond midlife, presenting age-related atrial vulnerability and cumulative exposure to metabolic insult28–30. Importantly, FIB-4 category in our MASLD cohort exhibited similar age distributions, minimizing concerns that the observed associations were driven solely by chronological age. Instead, our findings support the concept that fibrosis burden confers arrhythmic risk beyond age itself, particularly once a susceptible atrial substrate is established. Importantly, when FIB-4 was modeled as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines, the association with incident AF remained consistent and showed no strong evidence of nonlinearity, further supporting that the observed relationship is not solely driven by the categorical cutoff or the age component of FIB-4.

Several methodological features strengthen the validity of our findings. First, arrhythmic outcomes were identified exclusively through standardized ECG recordings during follow-up, minimizing reliance on symptom-driven diagnoses or self-report. Second, the use of a prospective cohort with clear follow-up intervals allowed for precise time-to-event analysis. Third, sequential multivariable modeling enabled evaluation of whether fibrosis-related risk was independent of shared cardiometabolic pathways.

From a clinical perspective, our results suggest that noninvasive fibrosis assessment might help identify MASLD patients at increased risk for future arrhythmia, particularly AF. While routine rhythm screening is not currently recommended solely on the basis of liver disease, individuals with elevated FIB-4 and MASLD, especially those aged ≥ 50 years, might represent a subgroup in whom closer monitoring for arrhythmia is required.

A major strength of this study is rigorous case definition of MASLD and the strict control of alcohol exposure. MASLD was defined using a combination of hepatic steatosis, CMRFs, and strict exclusion of individuals exceeding gender-specific alcohol intake thresholds, thereby minimizing misclassification with alcohol-related or mixed-etiology liver disease. This is particularly important given the well-recognized association between alcohol consumption and arrhythmic risk, as well as the frequent overlap between metabolic and alcohol-related liver disease in population-based studies31,32.

Several limitations are presented in this analysis. First, FIB-4 incorporates age as a component, which may partially contribute to observed associations despite age-stratified analyses. However, the persistence of associations within age strata and the absence of age imbalance between fibrosis categories argue against age alone explaining our findings. Second, although ECG-based ascertainment improves specificity, ECG recordings were obtained at discrete study visits, and intermittent or paroxysmal arrhythmias may have been captured between assessments. Third, we lacked direct measures of liver stiffness or histologic fibrosis, although FIB-4 is widely accepted for population-level risk stratification. Finally, the relatively small number of events in younger participants limited inference in this subgroup.

Conclusions

In conclusion, elevated liver fibrosis burden assessed by FIB-4 is significantly associated with an increased risk of incident ECG-detected arrhythmia, particularly AF, among individuals with MASLD. This risk emerges longitudinally, is most evident in participants aged ≥ 50 years, and is not captured by APRI. Our findings suggest that fibrosis in MASLD represents a broader systemic risk profile associated with arrhythmia and highlight the potential role of noninvasive fibrosis markers in improving arrhythmic risk assessment in the general population.
