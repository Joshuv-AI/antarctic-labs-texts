Limits of Kernelization and Parametrization for Phylogenetic Diversity with Dependencies This paper was accepted for an oral presentation at the 17th Latin American Theoretical Informatics Symposium (LATIN 2026) in Florianópolis, Brazil, April 2026.
Niels Holtgrefe, Jannik Schestag, Norbert Zeh

Abstract
In the Maximize Phylogenetic Diversity problem, we are given a phylogenetic tree that represents the genetic proximity of species, and we are asked to select a subset of species of maximum phylogenetic diversity to be preserved through conservation efforts, subject to budgetary constraints that allow only k k species to be saved. This neglects that it is futile to preserve a predatory species if we do not also preserve at least a subset of the prey it feeds on. Thus, in the Optimizing PD with Dependencies ( ε \varepsilon -PDD ) problem, we are additionally given a food web that represents the predator-prey relationships between species. The goal is to save a set of k k species of maximum phylogenetic diversity such that for every saved species, at least one of its prey is also saved. This problem is NP-hard even when the phylogenetic tree is a star. The α \alpha -PDD problem alters ε \varepsilon -PDD by requiring that at least some fraction α \alpha of the prey of every saved species are also saved. In this paper, we study the parameterized complexity of α \alpha -PDD . We prove that the problem is W[1]-hard and in XP when parameterized by the solution size k k , the diversity threshold D D , or their complements. When parameterized by the vertex cover number of the food web, α \alpha -PDD is fixed-parameter tractable (FPT). A key measure of the computational difficulty of a problem that is FPT is the size of the smallest kernel that can be obtained. We prove that, when parameterized by the distance to clique, 1 1 -PDD admits a linear kernel. Our main contribution is to prove that α \alpha -PDD does not admit a polynomial kernel when parameterized by the vertex cover number plus the diversity threshold D D , even if the phylogenetic tree is a star. This implies the non-existence of a polynomial kernel for α \alpha -PDD also when parameterized by a range of structural parameters of the food web, such as its distance to cluster, treewidth, pathwidth, feedback vertex set, and others.

Introduction

Humanity is living through what biologists increasingly call the
sixth
mass extinction

[
1
,
6
]
, with species
disappearing at an unprecedented pace. Beyond the tragic immediate loss of
biodiversity, the decline of entire lineages undermines ecological stability
and the evolutionary potential of life itself
[
5
]
.
Given that an insufficient amount of resources is given to preserving
all species, this forces conservationists to prioritize: Which
subset of species should be preserved to maximize genetic and ecological value?
A key framework for such prioritization is
phylogenetic diversity

(PD)
[
10
]
has become a central concept in biodiversity assessment,
as it is for example the basis of the “EDGE of existence”
programme of the Zoological Society of London
[
12
]
, and has influenced
policy discussions on evolutionary distinctiveness and ecosystem
resilience
[
34
]
.

Given a phylogenetic “tree of life” of all species, the phylogenetic
diversity of any subset of species is defined as the total length of
all edges on paths from the root of the tree to species in this subset. A
high-PD subset of species is expected to preserve a wide range of functional
and genetic traits
[
11
,
18
]
.

Given a phylogenetic tree, a set of
k
species that maximize PD can be selected using a
simple greedy strategy
[
10
,
23
,
31
]
. This combination
of algorithmic tractability and biological relevance spurred
generalizations that attempt to model real-world ecological and temporal
constraints more faithfully
[
14
,
16
,
20
,
24
]
. These variants
address, among others, heterogeneous conservation costs
[
20
,
24
]
,
spatially restricted habitats
[
4
]
, or the presence of
reticulate
events
[
3
,
15
,
32
,
33
,
35
]
.

Species do not exist in isolation though: Their survival often depends on prey,
hosts, or mutualistic partners. Conserving a predator without maintaining
sufficient prey populations is therefore futile
[
25
]
. This
insight led to the introduction of the
Optimizing PD with Dependencies
(
\varepsilon
-PDD
) problem
[
22
]
,
which augments the classical model with a (directed) ecological network
encoding, for example, food-web interactions and which stipulates that a viable
selection of saved species contains at least one prey for each predator that is saved.

In a solution of
\varepsilon
-PDD
that preserves only one prey per predator, the extinction
of a single species can trigger an entire extinction cascade of species that
transitively depend on its survival.
Moreover, not all prey contribute equally
to a predator’s survival. Weighted food webs were introduced in

[
21
,
28
,
36
]
as models that support more robust
solutions that preserve more than one prey per predator. This leads to weighted
versions of
\varepsilon
-PDD

[
27
]
, which stipulate that at least some fraction
of the prey of each preserved predator are also preserved.

\varepsilon
-PDD
is NP-hard
[
13
,
30
]
. Thus, it is interesting to study
\varepsilon
-PDD
and its weighted variants through the lens of parameterized complexity theory,
which provides a framework for analyzing which aspects of a problem drive its
computational hardness
[
7
,
9
]
and for developing efficient
algorithms for many problems in spite of their classical intractability.

Our contribution.

We extend the work of
[
27
]
by analyzing the limits of
parameterized algorithms and kernelizations for maximization of PD with food
webs. Specifically, we introduce
\alpha
-PDD
, which requires that at least an

\alpha
-fraction of the prey of any preserved predator must also be preserved.
This generalizes the previously studied
1
-PDD
and
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
-PDD
problems
[
17
,
27
]
(for
\alpha=1
and
for
\alpha=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
), as well as
\varepsilon
-PDD

[
22
]
(for
\alpha
approaching
zero). In
Section

4
, we prove that
1
-PDD
is W[1]-hard with respect
to both the diversity threshold
D
and its complement parameter
{\overline{D}}
, even when
the food web is directed bipartite. In our main result, in
Section

5
,
we prove that
1
-PDD
admits no polynomial kernel when parameterized by the vertex cover number
plus
D
, unless NP
\not\subseteq
coNP/poly. This implies the non-existence of
polynomial kernels for other structural parameters, also, including treewidth,
distance to (co-)cluster, and more. In
section

3
, we provide
reductions from
\varepsilon
-PDD
and
1
-PDD
to
\alpha
-PDD
, for any fixed
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
allowing hardness results of
sections

4
and
5
to extend to
\alpha
-PDD
,
for any constant
\alpha\in(0,1]
. We complement these non-existence results
by showing that
1
-PDD
admits a linear kernel parameterized by the distance of
the food web to a clique.

2.1
Definitions

Graphs.

(1)

For any edge

A

Parameterized complexity.

We study
\varepsilon
-PDD
and
\alpha
-PDD
from a parametrized perspective.
There
exists a natural bijection between decision problems and formal languages over
some alphabet
\Sigma
. A
parameterized language
is subset
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
. For a pair
(\sigma,k)\in L
,
k
is called the

parameter
of this pair. A parameterized language is

fixed-parameter tractable
(FPT) if there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that
decides for any input
(\sigma,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
whether
(\sigma,k)\in L
, and does so in time
f(k)\cdot p(|\sigma|)
, where

f(\cdot)
is some computable function and
p(\cdot)
is some polynomial. A
parameterized language is
slicewise polynomial
(XP) if there exists an
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that decides for any input
(\sigma,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}

whether
(\sigma,k)\in L
, and does so in time at most
|\sigma|^{p(k)}
,
where
p(\cdot)
is some polynomial. Every problem that is FPT is also in XP.

{W[h]}
-hard problems, for
h\geq 1
, are unlikely to be FPT.
See
[
7
,
9
]
for more detailed background on parameterized
complexity.

A
compression
from a parameterized language
P\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
to a parameterized language
Q\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
is an
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
which, given a pair
(\sigma,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
,
constructs another pair
(\tau,\ell)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
such that
\ell\leq k
,
|\tau|\leq f(k)
, for some computable function
f(\cdot)
,
and
(\sigma,k)\in P
if and only if
(\tau,\ell)\in Q
. Moreover,
\mathcal{A}
must
accomplish this construction in time polynomial in
|\sigma|
. If
P=Q
,
then we call
\mathcal{A}
a
kernelization
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is a

polynomial
compression or kernel if
f(\cdot)
is a polynomial.

The main parameters we consider are the
solution size

k
, the

diversity threshold

D
, their complement parameters—the

species loss

{\overline{k}}:=|X|-k
and the
acceptable loss of
diversity

{\overline{D}}:={PD_{{\mathcal{T}}}}(X)-D
—, the
vertex cover number

\vc
of

{\mathcal{F}}
(i.e., the size of a smallest subset of vertices that contains at least
one endpoint of every edge), and the
distance to clique

d
of
{\mathcal{F}}

(i.e., the minimum number of vertices to be deleted to make
{\mathcal{F}}
a clique).

OR-cross compositions.

Cross compositions
[
7
, Chapter 15]
are the standard framework for
proving that a parameterized problem
\Pi
does not admit a polynomial kernel.
A
polynomial equivalence relation
on some alphabet
\Sigma^{*}
is an
equivalence relation
\sim
such that, for polynomials
p(\cdot)
and
q(\cdot)
,

(i)

\sim
partitions
\Sigma^{\leq n}
into
p(n)
equivalence classes, for
each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, and

(ii)

It takes
q(|\sigma_{1}|+|\sigma_{2}|)

time to decide whether
\sigma_{1}\sim\sigma_{2}
, for any
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma^{*}
.

A decision problem
\Pi_{1}

(OR-)cross-composes
into a parameterized decision problem

\Pi_{2}
if
a polynomial equivalence relation
\sim
on
\Sigma^{*}
and an
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
exist that, given
t
equivalent (under
\sim
) instances

{\mathcal{I}}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{I}}_{t}
of
\Pi_{1}
, constructs, in
(\sum_{i=1}^{t}|\sigma_{i}|)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
time, an input
({\mathcal{I}},k)
of
\Pi_{2}
such that

(i)

k\leq p(\max\{|\sigma_{1}|,\ldots,|\sigma_{t}|\}+\log t)
, for some polynomial
p(\cdot)
, and

(ii)

({\mathcal{I}},k)
is a
yes
-instance of
\Pi_{2}
if and only if
{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
is a
yes
-instance of
\Pi_{1}
, for some
i\in[t]
.

Theorem 1

(
[
7
, Theorem 15.9]
)
.

If an
NP
-hard problem
\Pi_{1}
cross-composes into a parameterized problem

,
then
\Pi_{2}
does not admit a kernelization of polynomial size, assuming
NP
\not\subseteq

.

6
Discussion

In this paper, we considered the problem of saving
k
species of maximum
phylogenetic diversity subject to the constraint that at least an

\alpha
-fraction of the prey of every saved species is also saved. This
problem had been studied before for
\alpha\in\{1/2,1\}
, while we allow any
value
\alpha\in(0,1]
in this paper.

Our main contributions are W[1]-hardness results for
\alpha
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
parameterized by the solution size
k
, the diversity threshold
D
, and their
complements, as well as a proof that
\alpha
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
does not admit
a polynomial kernel with respect to
D
plus the vertex cover number of the
food web, which implies that
\alpha
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
does not admit a polynomial kernel with
respect to a range of structural parameters of the food web visualized in

fig.

1
. Together, these results paint a fairly comprehensive
picture of the parameterized complexity landscape of
\alpha
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
.

The most interesting open question is whether
\alpha
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
admits a polynomial kernel
with respect to the food web’s scanwidth, or at least its directed acyclic
cutwidth. These questions are marked open in
fig.

1
.

Our hardness results for
\alpha
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
were obtained via reductions from
1
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
and
\varepsilon
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
.
A natural question is for which structural parameters of the food web

\alpha
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
is harder to solve than
1
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
or
\varepsilon
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
.
For example,
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}
is W[1]-hard with respect to the treewidth of the food
web
[
26
]
, while for the same parameter,
\varepsilon
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}

[
19
]

and
1
-PDD
{}_{\text{s}}

[
27
]
are FPT.
