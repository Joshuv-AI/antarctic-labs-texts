Background

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a common spinal deformity often requiring surgical intervention. Posterior spinal fusion is the standard approach, with advancement significantly improving safety [1]. However, rare but severe complications, such as major vascular injury leading to hemothorax can occur [2, 3]. Intercostal artery injury is a high-risk complication due to the vessels’ proximity to the surgical field and the potential for retractor or instrument penetration, coupled with difficulty in early detection as bleeding may initially be concealed within the pleural cavity. These events are life-threatening if not managed effectively [4]. A delayed recognition of a developing tension hemothorax can rapidly lead to cardiopulmonary compromise, making immediate intervention critical to prevent a fatal outcome. Despite the prevalence of AIS surgeries, the incidence of severe complications such as intercostal artery injury leading to tension hemothorax remains poorly understood [5].

Previous studies have reported non-neurological complications of up to 15.4% in AIS surgeries [2], highlighting the need for vigilance and effective management strategies. This case report details the management of a massive tension hemothorax and emphasizes critical lessons for prevention and intervention. This manuscript adheres to the CARE guidelines for case report.

Clinical presentation

Methods

This is a case report of a rare surgical complication. The patient and her parents provided informed consent for the procedure and publication of this report. The reporting follows the Consensus-based Clinical Case Reporting (CARE) guideline.

Clinical description

A 14-year-old girl with adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), thoracic curve measured 82° and lumbar curve 46°, classified as Lenke 1 C (Fig. 1), with no significant past medical history. She underwent selective thoracic posterior correction surgery from T3 to L1. The surgery lasted 3 h with an estimated blood loss of 800 ml. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring and wake-up test were normal. Following rod placement and correction, the patient experienced a sudden drop in blood pressure to 64/39 mmHg, which was stabilized with fluid resuscitation, vasopressors, and blood transfusion. Postoperatively, mean arterial pressure remained 60–70 mmHg with hemoglobin 6.0–6.5 g/dL.

A Appearance photo. B Preoperative X-rays of the patient

Upon extubation, the patient complained of shortness of breath and became hypotensive again (69/45 mmHg). Physical examination revealed absent breath sounds on the left side, mediastinal shift, and signs of cardiac compression. Chest X-ray confirmed left-sided tension hemothorax (Fig. 2A).

A 2 h postoperative chest radiographs. B Thoracentesis and drainage

An emergent thoracentesis was performed on the 6th intercostal space. Draining 5000 ml of blood with signs of ongoing hemorrhage (Fig. 2B). Despite continuous blood transfusion, the patient remained unstable. A digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was performed but failed to identify the bleeding site (Fig. 3A). Due to persistent hemodynamic instability, an urgent surgical exploration was decided.

A Radiograph image of DSA procedure. B Endoscopic image of the intercostal artery cauterization

An left-sided thoracotomy was performed through the 5th intercostal space. After evacuating a larger volume of blood clots, the active bleeding site was identified deep within the 3rd left intercostal space. An endoscopic approach was performed to achieve precise cauterization of the vessel, which immediately controlled the bleeding and stabilized the patient’s vitals (Fig. 3B). the total blood product transfusion from the onset of the event was 10,370 ml. Postoperative radiographs showed satisfactory correction (Fig. 4A). The patient recovered fully, and at the one-year follow up, the correction was maintained with no complications (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

This case illustrates a life-threatening complication following AIS surgery. Non-neurological complications occur up to 15.4% of AIS surgeries, massive hemothorax remains exceptionally rare [9]. Factors for surgical complications include low BMI, prominent thoracic hump, surgical approach, pulmonary function, and Lenke curve type. The pointed tips of gelpi retractors and screw probing are commonly implicated in such complications [1, 4, 5]. In our patient the bleeding occurred on the concave side of the major curve, suggesting a potential mechanism of vascular stretch or tear during correction maneuver, in addition to possible direct retractor injury. We believe bleeding resulted from anterior wall breach during pin placement, direct vessel injury, or overly deep positioning of the distal spreader. Another possible reason we suspected was excessive force that was applied to the curved side of the chest, which may have caused traction on the intercostal blood vessel, which resulted in bleeding. Surgeons should be more cautious when using gelpi retractors with pointed tips. Patients with anatomical variations may have less soft tissue padding, having vascular structures more prone to injury [6, 7].

Reviewing literature reports of intraoperative hemothorax in AIS surgery, most cases also occurred on the concave side of thoracic curves. Therefore, during concave-side correction in patients with large thoracic curves, gentle manipulation is essential. Blood pressure must be closely monitored. If a sharp drop in blood pressure occurs that is disproportionate to the observed surgical field bleeding, hemothorax should be suspected. The key management lesson is the imperative for rapid diagnosis and escalation of care (Table 1). The primary steps are clinical examination, chest X-ray, and immediate tube thoracostomy. If significant bleeding continues (>1500 ml initially or >200 ml/hr) further intervention is mandatory [7]. Alternative approach should be used during surgery to avoid complications such as, blunt-tipped retractors, use of intraoperative ultrasound to locate the structures, and Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) to detect early signs of injury [8, 9]. In an unstable patient, timely surgical exploration should not be delayed. Thoracotomy provides direct visualization and definitive control of the bleeding source and is a critical life-saving step when other measures fail.

Summary of complications associated with posterior correction surgery in AIS

Our literature review encountered several case studies highlighting similar complications associated with the use of Gelpi retractors in thoracic spine surgeries. Hicks et al. [5] in a systemic review reported that vascular injury is rare but can be severe which requires immediate management. Carreon et al. [2] also reported that non-neurological complications, like hemothorax, occurs in up to 15.4% of AIS surgeries. Emphasizing the need for vigilance throughout the procedure. To minimize the risk of complications, we recommend preoperative planning, use of alternative instruments, interdisciplinary collaboration with interventional radiology, and intraoperative monitoring. Further research studies should include prospective studies to understand the true incidence of intercostal artery injury during posterior correction surgery, comparative analysis of different retraction methods, and risk stratification to identify high-risk patients.

Conclusion

Tension hemothorax is a rare but devastating complication of AIS surgery. Surgeons must maintain a high index of suspicion in the face of unexplained hypotension. A structural management protocol, prompt diagnosis, chest drainage, and readiness for surgical exploration is essential for patient survival. This case highlights that when interventional radiology fails, urgent thoracotomy is definitive and life-saving solution.
