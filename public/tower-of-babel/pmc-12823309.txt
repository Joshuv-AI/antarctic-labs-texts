Generating Control Groups for Organ Impairment Studies: A Case‐Study Comparing Statistical and Population Pharmacokinetic‐Based Matching Approaches
Authors: Jessica Barry, Sumit Bhatnagar, Wei Liu, Mohamed‐Eslam F. Mohamed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA common challenge in conducting phase 1 studies that assess the impact of organ impairment on the pharmacokinetics of a drug is the recruitment of a demographically matched control group. The work presented here evaluated alternative approaches for generating control groups in these studies. Available phase 1 data from the upadacitinib and elagolix clinical programs were leveraged as case studies. A statistical matching approach and a population pharmacokinetic model‐based approach were evaluated retrospectively for these programs' hepatic and renal impairment clinical studies. Geometric mean ratios of logarithmically transformed Cmax and AUCinf were used to compare exposure in organ impairment groups to respective matched or virtual control groups. In the statistical matching approach, the genetic matching algorithm using Mahalanobis distance showed that external control groups were adequately demographically balanced across all impairment groups of the study except for age. A 3:1 k‐match approach minimized the prediction error between matched and reference in‐study results for both case studies, resulting in differences in geometric mean ratios ranging from −19% to 3% and −27% to 40% for upadacitinib and elagolix, respectively, compared to in‐study controls. Similarly, the population pharmacokinetic approach used models developed from phase 1 data in healthy participants and found that the results were generally comparable to the in‐study results, with differences in geometric mean ratios ranging from −30% to 17% and −24% to 41% for upadacitinib and elagolix, respectively. These analyses demonstrate that both approaches may be viable alternatives to assess the impact of organ impairment on pharmacokinetics.

Introduction

One key objective of clinical pharmacology in drug development is to characterize the effect of hepatic and renal impairment on the pharmacokinetics of a drug. Hepatic and/or renal impairment can often lead to an increase in drug exposures, potentially impacting the safety profile of the drug and, thus, the benefit–risk of drug use in patients with hepatic and/or renal impairment [1, 2]. Adequate assessments of the impact of organ impairment on drug exposures are required to provide healthcare professionals with valuable guidance on the need for dose adjustments in these special patient populations. This ensures that the efficacy and safety of the drug remain favorable and consistent with the overall population [3].

A phase 1 clinical study that assesses the effect of organ impairment on the pharmacokinetics of a drug typically recruits participants with different degrees of organ impairment (i.e., mild, moderate, or severe) and a control group with no organ impairment. The control group is selected such that the demographics of the group are similar in age, weight, sex, and gender compared to the organ impairment groups [4, 5]. This is done to minimize the potential confounding effect of these demographics when evaluating the pharmacokinetics of a drug, such that the difference in pharmacokinetics can be directly attributed to organ impairment.

However, it can be challenging to recruit healthy participants to closely match the desired demographics across all groups with organ impairment; for example, patients with organ impairment may be older than the age range typically enrolled in phase 1 healthy participant studies (18–55 years) due to study inclusion criteria differences. In addition, patients with varying degrees of organ impairment may differ in demographics, which makes it improbable for one control group with a relatively small sample size (typically 6–8 participants) to have similar demographics to three or four different impairment groups with varying degrees of organ impairment (18–24 participants total). This potential mismatch in demographics can confound the assessment of the change in pharmacokinetics due to organ impairment. Additionally, enrollment of healthy participants with normal organ function who match the key demographic characteristics of organ‐impaired participants can be a rate‐limiting factor for the completion of the organ impairment study. Therefore, an earlier assessment of organ impairment effects on the pharmacokinetics of a drug in development through leveraging in‐house healthy participant data can enable a closer control group match to the organ impairment groups, potentially providing a better estimate of the impact of organ impairment on drug exposures.

Organ impairment studies are typically conducted at a later stage of drug development (e.g., in parallel to phase 3 studies). At this stage, there is usually a significant number of healthy participants who have been evaluated in phase 1 studies. The work presented here aimed at assessing two alternative approaches for the generation of control groups in organ impairment studies by leveraging available phase 1 data: (1) a statistical matching approach to generate a control group based on observed data, and (2) a population pharmacokinetic modeling approach to generate a virtual control group. These approaches were applied retrospectively to two case studies utilzing data from upadacitinib and elagolix clinical studies [6, 7] to evaluate their utility within this context. The results from the assessment of organ impairment effects using the generated control groups of the two approaches to using in‐study control groups were compared and are presented.

Data

Data for upadacitinib pharmacokinetics in participants with organ impairment included mild, moderate, and severe renal impairment groups from a phase 1 renal impairment study, as well as mild and moderate hepatic impairment groups from a phase 1 hepatic impairment study [6, 8]. Data from the healthy participants in the organ impairment studies were considered the in‐study control group and used for comparison across methods. Upadacitinib was not evaluated in participants with severe hepatic impairment. Data for upadacitinib in participants with normal organ function from five phase 1 studies (relative bioavailability studies and an Asian pharmacokinetic study) were included in the analyses.

Similarly, for elagolix, pharmacokinetic data in participants with organ impairment included moderate/severe and end‐stage renal disease groups from a phase 1 renal impairment study, as well as mild, moderate, and severe hepatic impairment groups from a phase 1 hepatic impairment study, in addition to healthy participant (in‐study control) data [7]. Healthy participant data from three phase 1 relative bioavailability studies of elagolix were included in the analyses, representing data in participants with normal renal and hepatic function.

Datasets for the statistical matching approach included demographic data and pharmacokinetic parameters. A summary of the participants' demographics included in these analyses is provided in Table 1. Demographic data included age, sex, weight, and race for each participant. Pharmacokinetic data consisted of the dosage, maximal observed plasma concentration (Cmax), area under the curve during the dosing interval (AUCt), and AUC from time 0 to infinite time (AUCinf) based on non‐compartmental analyses conducted using Phoenix version 8.0 (Certara USA Inc., Princeton, NJ). The population pharmacokinetic datasets were created in a NONMEM‐compliant format, which included dosing times, dosage, observed plasma concentration data with the corresponding sampling times, and demographic data associated with each participant [9].

Statistical Matching

Statistical matching is a form of subsetting where a larger general dataset is trimmed to produce a new sample where a treatment effect, in this case the organ impairment effect, is less confounded by covariates. Matching methods are nonparametric and strive to rely less on model specification and assumptions to support causal inference [10]. Three matching algorithms were implemented and assessed using the MatchIt (version 4.5.3) and Cobalt (version 4.5.1) packages used in the R Statistical Software (version 4.0.1): (1) nearest neighbor matching using propensity score, (2) nearest neighbor matching using Mahalanobis distance, and (3) genetic matching algorithm using Mahalanobis distance. These algorithms differ in that they offer different distance measures to assess the ideal or closest match per patient with organ impairment. These case studies used age, sex, weight, and race for matching purposes, but additional covariates that are known to impact the pharmacokinetics of a drug (e.g., metabolic enzyme polymorphisms) should be considered for inclusion in the matching process. Details about these methods are provided in the Supporting Information.

In addition to matching each organ‐impaired participant with one control participant, a k:1 matching approach was evaluated, which consisted of finding multiple control matches, denoted by k, for each organ‐impaired participant. In each of these k‐matched scenarios, the effects of organ impairment on pharmacokinetic exposures were calculated and compared to the published in‐study results [6, 7].

Evaluation of Statistical Matching

The statistical matching methods were evaluated by comparing the achieved balance in covariates to that achieved with the in‐study control group based on absolute standardized mean differences (ASMD) for continuous covariates (Equation (1)) using Love plots, available within the MatchIt package [11]. Categorical covariates were treated as binary, and they were assessed via raw differences in proportion. (1) ASMD=μ1−μ2σ12+σ22/2 where μi is the mean of a covariate in the healthy or organ‐impaired sample and σi2 is the variance of the same sample. In addition, a visual inspection of density plots was conducted to compare the distribution of demographics that were adequately matched in the impaired and control groups.

The optimal number of participants with normal organ function to match each participant with organ impairment was evaluated based on the cumulative prediction error derived from the difference in the matched results and the reference in‐study results. The cumulative prediction error was defined as the sum of the squares of the difference between the reference in‐study and matched ratios of the AUCinf and Cmax across each impairment group, stratified by organ and drug of interest. This error quantification was used to assess the relative accuracy across the range of match counts. Participants were matched without replacement within each organ impairment group but were replaced across groups (i.e., the same healthy control participant cannot be used more than once within a group but may be used more than once in different groups).

Population Pharmacokinetic Modeling

Two population pharmacokinetic models were built independently for upadacitinib and elagolix using NONMEM (ICON, version 7.5.0). The models were developed by testing different structural models (e.g., one‐compartment, two‐compartment, or three‐compartment), including inter‐individual variability key parameters (e.g., apparent oral clearance), applying residual error models, and evaluating covariates on key parameters. Additive, proportional, and combined residual error models were tested and compared for the best description of the residual unexplained variability.

Multiple covariates were tested for significance, including weight, age, sex, race, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), creatinine clearance, and various clinical laboratory parameters such as aspartate and alanine aminotransferase, bilirubin, and albumin. Covariates were included based on a stepwise covariate approach using forward inclusion and backward elimination with significant levels of 0.01 and 0.001, respectively. The final model for each drug was selected based on the objective function values, ability of the model to converge adequately, goodness‐of‐fit plots, visual predictive checks, and numerical predictive checks [12, 13, 14].

Generation of Virtual Control Group Using Population Pharmacokinetic Models

The final population pharmacokinetic models developed in NONMEM for upadacitinib and elagolix were replicated in mrgSolve (version 1.0.9) and used to simulate pharmacokinetic profiles for the virtual control groups by matching the statistically significant demographic characteristics to those of the respective organ impairment groups. Summarized pharmacokinetic parameters (e.g., AUCinf, Cmax) were generated for each virtual individual using PKNCA [15] (version 0.10.2).

Assessment of the Effect of Hepatic or Renal Impairment on Upadacitinib and Elagolix Pharmacokinetics

The results from the published in‐study hepatic and renal impairment analyses for both case studies served as a reference for the statistical matching and population pharmacokinetic modeling approaches introduced in this work [6, 7, 8]. The geometric mean ratios were calculated on a group level in a stepwise manner by first summarizing the exposure metrics for the organ‐impaired and control groups. After the group exposure metrics (e.g., Cmax) were calculated, the ratio of each summarized metric was calculated by comparing the organ‐impaired group to the control group. Evaluating the matched hepatic impairment analysis against the reference in‐study results, the geometric mean ratios of logarithmically transformed Cmax and AUCinf for each drug in hepatic impairment participants relative to the respective control groups were calculated using ANCOVA. For the matched renal impairment analysis, a regression analysis against eGFR was performed on logarithmically transformed Cmax and AUCinf for each drug in renal impaired patients relative to the respective matched control groups. In both cases, covariates (i.e., weight, race, sex, age) were tested for inclusion at a significance level of 0.10.

For the population pharmacokinetic approach, the individual parameters of logarithmically transformed Cmax and AUCinf were used to calculate the geometric mean ratios based on ANOVA analysis. Covariates were not considered, as virtual participants were designed to be an exact covariate match to their impaired counterparts.

Results

Demographic Data

Data were available from three to six organ‐impaired participants across the different renal and hepatic impairment groups for upadacitinib and elagolix (Table 1). The healthy participant data for upadacitinib and elagolix consisted of 298 and 252 participants, respectively. In general, the distribution of the different key demographic characteristics (age, body weight, race, and sex) was comparable between healthy and the organ‐impaired participants, except for the lower mean age for the healthy participants (38 years) in the upadacitinib dataset compared to the organ‐impaired participants (51 years–68 years).

Statistical Matching

The statistical matching approach was used to select the closest healthy participant match to an organ‐impaired participant using distance measures on demographic covariates in lieu of using healthy participant controls that were enrolled in the organ impairment study. Three algorithms consisting of a nearest neighbor matching using propensity score, nearest neighbor matching using Mahalanobis distance, and a genetic matching algorithm using Mahalanobis distance were used to generate external control groups for each impairment group for both case studies. The evaluations of all algorithms based on Love plots showed that the genetic algorithm provided the most balanced control groups with respect to all the demographics (Figure S1). Visual inspection of the density plots was also conducted to ensure adequately matched demographics across the groups (example shown in Figure S2). A comparison of the in‐study control group and the statistically matched external control group based on the genetic algorithm (k = 3) showed that external control groups were adequately balanced across all the impairment groups, with the exception of age, mainly due to limitations in inclusion criteria for healthy participant studies (Figures 2 and S1).

The optimal number of matched control participants for each organ‐impaired participant in a group was assessed using the genetic algorithm to find 1:1 (matched control: organ‐impaired participants), 2:1, 3:1, and 4:1 matches. The differences in the prediction error of the estimates for each impairment group were summarized into a cumulative prediction error term and plotted for each drug (Figure 3). The 3:1 match scenario was selected for the remaining analyses in each case study, as this scenario had generally lower prediction error overall for both renal and hepatic impairment analyses while maintaining adequate covariate balance.

Population Pharmacokinetic‐Based Matching

The population pharmacokinetic approach was used to generate healthy virtual controls of the organ‐impaired participants using statistically significant covariates that were identified during the development of the population pharmacokinetic model. Upadacitinib and elagolix pharmacokinetics from healthy participants were best described by two separate two‐compartment models with first‐order absorption, lag time, and first‐order elimination and were developed as the structural models for each of the two drugs, with IIV included on apparent clearance, apparent volume of the central compartment, apparent volume of the peripheral compartment, and the absorption rate constants. The final model for upadacitinib included a combined error model and the following covariates: weight on apparent oral clearance, apparent volume of the central compartment, and intercompartmental flow rate, as well as sex on apparent oral clearance. The final model for elagolix included a combined error model and weight on the apparent volume of the central compartment. Neither model included eGFR as a significant covariate.

The final population pharmacokinetic model for each drug adequately described the pharmacokinetics of the respective drugs (Figure S3). The goodness‐of‐fit plots showed no bias or model misspecifications for either of the final models (Figure S4).

Effect of Hepatic or Renal Impairment on Upadacitinib and Elagolix Pharmacokinetics Using Virtual Controls

A comparison between the in‐study results and results from each alternative approach found that the impact of organ impairment on pharmacokinetics was generally comparable across methods (Tables 2 and 3). The statistical matching method with a 3:1 match for healthy participants to organ‐impaired participants resulted in differences in Cmax and AUCinf ratios ranging from −19% to 3% and −27% to 40% for upadacitinib and elagolix, respectively, compared to in‐study controls (Table 2). Similarly, the assessment based on virtual healthy participants from population pharmacokinetic modeling showed that the results were generally comparable to the in‐study results, with differences in ratios ranging from −30% to 17% and from −24% to 41% for upadacitinib and elagolix, respectively (Table 3).

Discussion

In this work, we report the application of a statistical matching approach and a population pharmacokinetics‐based approach to leverage data from healthy participants in lieu of in‐study controls or to support the results using in‐study controls. In the two case studies, the two approaches had similar performance. They were generally consistent with results using in‐study controls for the effect of renal or hepatic impairment on the exposures of the two drugs. Based on the lack of steep exposure‐response relationships for either drug within the therapeutic dose range [16, 17], dosing recommendations from the analyses leveraging the generated controls using either approach would likely have resulted in the same dosing recommendations as those currently followed using in‐study controls.

The statistical matching methodology used in these analyses is applied in fields such as epidemiology and economics to match individuals to reduce bias in estimating a treatment effect by comparing groups that are as similar as possible [18]. A commonly used application within drug development is propensity score matching for the generation of external placebo data for open‐label studies in order to better understand the efficacy of the investigational drug. To our knowledge, this represents the first report of applying a statistical matching algorithm in the context of matching controls for organ impairment studies. There are several advantages to using matching methods, notably that they can be used in combination with regression to characterize covariate effects while limiting confounding variables. Additionally, matching methods can be assessed by relatively simple and interpretable diagnostics.

The statistical matching approach generally provided a closer match of key demographic characteristics for participants with organ impairment compared to in‐study controls, as the methodology provides flexibility in finding a match for organ‐impaired participants from a more extensive database compared to the in‐study controls, which rely on a group of six participants. In addition, statistical matching also provides an opportunity to match more than one control for each participant with organ impairment to potentially improve the robustness of the analyses via increased resilience to outliers. One inherent limitation of this approach is that the phase 1 database is limited by the inclusion criteria for phase 1 studies. For example, the age of the participants did not result in ideal matches in this approach, because phase 1 studies for both case study drugs were limited to participants aged 18–55. In contrast, organ impairment participants may be older than 55 years. Organ impairment participants also had a wider eligible range for body mass index (BMI) values compared to the healthy participant studies, thus potentially reducing the strength of matching participants on body weight.

The population pharmacokinetic approach provides an alternative way to generate a control group. It provides the flexibility to identically match the covariates of the organ‐impaired participants, so long as the characteristic has a statistically significant impact on the drug's pharmacokinetics and is included in the model. This approach relies heavily on a good understanding of the drug's overall pharmacokinetics, including the impact of key covariates. Both approaches have certain limitations in terms of generating matches for organ‐impaired participants, but can be used together to provide a robust assessment of the impact of organ impairment in lieu of in‐study controls.

Depending on the available data, matching methodologies can also come with some statistical concerns, highlighting the need for a robust database with a wide range of demographic characteristics. The presented case studies were highly reproducible, and the stochastic nature of the statistical matching algorithms did not influence the results. The application of this methodology on smaller datasets may provide errant conclusions, and the success of k:1 matching may depend highly on the size of the dataset. While a higher k can drive down sampling uncertainty, bias can increase if the healthy participant dataset is too small or has fewer good matching opportunities. Dataset size and covariate distribution overlap must be considered when choosing whether to pair multiple matches, as each additional match for k > 1 is typically worse. While k:1 matching may help maintain precision in some cases, it typically has diminishing returns for k > 4, and 1:1 or 2:1 matches are best in many cases [19, 20]. Overall, the total number of participants required to adequately inform this exercise will likely be drug‐dependent based on the impact that these demographic characteristics have on the drug's pharmacokinetics.

Implementing matching methodologies can come with some tradeoffs. Inter‐study variability is introduced into the analysis when data are pooled across studies to generate these healthy participant datasets. If this variability is relatively high, it adds a confounding factor to the data assessment that could potentially influence the outcomes of the analyses. Dose linearity (if the phase 1 studies evaluated a wide range of doses), bioanalytical assay variation, and relative bioavailability between the formulations evaluated in the studies must be factored in when generating the healthy participant datasets. Additionally, sampling schedule differences across studies may affect the calculation and comparison of PK parameters and should be considered.

The case studies presented in this manuscript had abundant phase 1 data available. In scenarios where this approach is utilized for drugs with limited healthy participant data generated in phase 1 studies, it will be important to assess if the healthy participant data available are adequate to provide close matches to the organ‐impaired participants. It is feasible to assess this through the statistical matching approach using the Love plots (Figure 2). Scenarios limited in healthy participant data (e.g., in therapeutic areas like oncology) may provide a good opportunity for the utilization of the population pharmacokinetic‐based approach, assuming the population pharmacokinetic dataset enables adequate characterization of the effects of different covariates. In silico methods and, more specifically, population pharmacokinetic analyses have routinely been used to explore situations of altered pharmacokinetics, including the assessment of organ impairment [4, 21, 22, 23].

In conclusion, statistical matching and population pharmacokinetic‐based modeling to provide healthy participant controls offer avenues to aid in the robust assessment of the effect of organ impairment on a drug's pharmacokinetics. As both approaches yield comparable results, the implementation of either or both methods can be made on a case‐by‐case basis. Factors such as the amount of data and observed inter‐study variability in pharmacokinetics may lead to the choice of one method over the other.
