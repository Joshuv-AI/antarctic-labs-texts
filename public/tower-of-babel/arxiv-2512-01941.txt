Weak coupling for Schrödinger operators with complex potentials
Jussi Behrndt, Markus Holzmann, Petr Siegl, Nicolas Weber

Abstract
. We study the discrete eigenvalues emerging from the threshold of the essential spectrum of one or two-dimensional Schrödinger operators with complex-valued L p L^{p} -potentials in a weak coupling regime. We derive necessary and sufficient conditions on the potential for the existence or absence of discrete eigenvalues in this regime and also analyze their uniqueness and algebraic multiplicity. Our results can be viewed as natural non-self-adjoint extensions of the well-known classical weak coupling phenomenon for self-adjoint Schrödinger operators with real-valued potentials going back half a century to Simon’s famous paper [ 63 ] .

1.1.
Strategy of the proof

Our proof originates in the ideas in
[
63
]
, which do not rely on variational arguments,
but instead exploit the criticality of
-\Delta
in
d=1,2
and the related singularity of the Green’s function (see (
2.3
)). The strategy of the proof is also inspired by
[
15
, Thm. 2.2]
, where a one-dimensional Dirac operator with non-self-adjoint potentials
in a weak coupling setting is studied.
In particular, starting with the Birman-Schwinger principle (see Section
2
), the analysis of weakly coupled eigenvalues of
H_{\beta}
in a certain subset
\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
of
\mathbb{C}

is reduced to the study of zeros of an analytic function
\Lambda_{\beta}
(including the multiplicities), which can be asymptotically expanded as
\beta\to 0
, see Proposition
2.4
. In this step, only the basic integrability conditions (
1.7
) are used. The additional condition (
1.8
) is employed in Section
3
to characterize
all
weakly coupled eigenvalues of
H_{\beta}
as the zeros of
\Lambda_{\beta}
and also to expand
\Lambda_{\beta}
further, see Proposition
3.2
. In Section
4
, where the main results are proved, we use Lemma
A.6
, which is
based on Rouché’s theorem, to show the existence of a simple zero and hence a simple eigenvalue of
H_{\beta}
. Moreover, the complete characterization of all weakly coupled eigenvalues of
H_{\beta}
in Proposition
3.2
makes it possible
to exclude further eigenvalues and to establish the non-existence of weakly coupled eigenvalues under a complementary condition (
1.14
).

2.1.

Recall first that for
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
the resolvent
{(-\Delta+z^{2})^{-1}}
of the free Laplacian in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

is an integral operator with the kernel

(2.3)

\displaystyle\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(d)}}(x,y;z)=\begin{cases}(2z)^{-1}e^{-z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}&\text{ if }d=1,\\
(2\pi)^{-1}K_{0}(z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert)&\text{ if }d=2,\end{cases}\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
K_{0}:\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
denotes the modified Bessel function of
second kind of order zero (for details
see Section
A.1
).
The eigenvalues
-z^{2}
of
H_{\beta}
outside
[0,\infty)
can be characterized via the Birman-Schwinger operator

Q(z)
,
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, which is a compact integral operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with kernel

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{(d)}(x,y;z)=|V(x)|^{\frac{1}{2}}\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(d)}}(x,y;z)V(y)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where we use the convention

\displaystyle V(x)^{\frac{1}{2}}:=\begin{cases}|V(x)|^{-\frac{1}{2}}V(x)&\text{ if }V(x)\neq 0,\\
0&\text{ if }V(x)=0.\end{cases}

The Birman-Schwinger principle now reads as follows (for the definition of eigenvalues of operator families and eigenvalues of finite type, see Section
A.2
). For a proof we
refer to
[
31
, Prop. 4.1]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
H_{\beta}=-\Delta-\beta V
be as in (
2.1
) for
V
satisfying
(
2.2
). Let
Q(z)
,
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, be the compact integral operator
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with kernel (
2.4
).
Then, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, there holds

(2.5)

\displaystyle\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}(H_{\beta}+z^{2})=\operatorname{dim}\operatorname{ker}(1-\beta Q(z)).

Moreover, any eigenvalue of the family
{1-\beta Q}
is an eigenvalue of finite type, and
the algebraic multiplicity of
{1-\beta Q}
at
z
coincides with the algebraic multiplicity of
-z^{2}
as an eigenvalue
of
H_{\beta}
.

2.2.
Reduction to zeros of a holomorphic function

The main idea to characterize the eigenvalues of
H_{\beta}
in the weak coupling limit goes back
[
63
]
, where the Birman-Schwinger operator
Q(z)
is decomposed in the form

(2.6)

\displaystyle Q(z)=L(z)+M(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}_{+};

with integral operators
L(z)
and
M(z)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respective kernels

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(d)}(x,y;z)

\displaystyle:=|V(x)|^{\frac{1}{2}}g_{1}(z)V(y)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(d)}(x,y;z)

\displaystyle:=|V(x)|^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(d)}}(x,y;z)-g_{1}(z)\big)V(y)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and the function
g_{1}:\mathbb{C}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is given by

(2.8)

\displaystyle g_{1}(z)=\begin{cases}(2z)^{-1}&\text{if }d=1,\\
-(2\pi)^{-1}\log(z)&\text{if }d=2.\end{cases}

Note that
g_{1}
is injective and holomorphic on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
. Observe that
L(z)
,
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, is
a rank one operator and that
\|L(\cdot)\|
has a singularity of the same order as
g_{1}
at
z=0
.
The usefulness of the decomposition (
2.6
) is demonstrated in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
H_{\beta}=-\Delta-\beta V
be as in (
2.1
) for
V
satisfying
(
2.2
), let

U=\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

be as in (
1.10
),
and let
g_{1}:\mathbb{C}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be as in (
2.8
).
Then for all sufficiently small
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
the set

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\beta}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}:\|\beta M(z)\|<\frac{1}{2}}}\right\}

is non-empty and open, the function
\Lambda_{\beta}:\mathcal{N}_{\beta}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
given by

(2.10)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\beta}(z)=1-g_{1}(z)\big[U\beta+\beta\big([1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}\beta M(z)|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},\smash{\overline{V}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big)\big],\quad z\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta},

is holomorphic on
\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
, and for all
z\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
one has

(2.11)

\displaystyle-z^{2}\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(H_{\beta})\iff\Lambda_{\beta}(z)=0.

Moreover, if
\Lambda_{\beta}(z)=0
, then the algebraic multiplicity of
-z^{2}
as an eigenvalue of
H_{\beta}
coincides with the multiplicity of
z
as
a root of
\Lambda_{\beta}
.

Proof.

Since
M
is an analytic family of bounded operators on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
, the set
\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
is open and it is also non-empty for all
sufficiently small
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
. Moreover,
\Lambda_{\beta}
is well-defined for all
z\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
as
1-\beta M(z)
is boundedly invertible;
the holomorphicity of
\Lambda_{\beta}
follows from the analyticity of
M
and the holomorphicity of
g_{1}
on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
.

Let us continue by proving the equivalence (
2.11
).
By the Birman-Schwinger principle from Lemma
2.1
,
it suffices to show that for all
z\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
the equivalence

(2.12)

\displaystyle 0\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(1-\beta Q(z))\iff\Lambda_{\beta}(z)=0

is valid.
Since
1-\beta M(z)
is boundedly invertible for
z\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
, the decomposition (
2.6
) can be rewritten as

(2.13)

\displaystyle 1-\beta Q(z)

\displaystyle=1-\beta(L(z)+M(z))

\displaystyle=(1-\beta M(z))(1-[1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}\beta L(z))

and this yields the equivalence

(2.14)

\displaystyle 0\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(1-\beta Q(z))\iff 0\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(1-[1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}\beta L(z)).

Next, let us set

\displaystyle B:\begin{cases}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\\
f\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(y)^{\frac{1}{2}}f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y,\end{cases}A_{\beta}(z):\begin{cases}\mathbb{C}\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\\
\phi\mapsto[1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi.\end{cases}

A short computation shows the operator identity

(2.15)

\displaystyle[1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}\beta L(z)=g_{1}(z)\beta A_{\beta}(z)B;

indeed, for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
one clearly has
g_{1}(z)|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}Bf=L(z)f
and hence

\displaystyle g_{1}(z)\beta A_{\beta}(z)Bf=g_{1}(z)\beta[1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}Bf=[1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}\beta L(z)f.

By combining (
2.15
) with (
2.14
),
it is clear that

(2.16)

\displaystyle 0\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(1-\beta Q(z))

\displaystyle\iff 0\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(1-[1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}\beta L(z))

\displaystyle\iff 0\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(1-g_{1}(z)\beta A_{\beta}(z)B)

\displaystyle\iff 0\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(1-g_{1}(z)\beta BA_{\beta}(z)),

where we used for the last equivalence that

0\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(1-CD)\iff 0\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(1-DC)

for everywhere defined and bounded operators
C
and
D
between Hilbert spaces.
Finally, using the definition of
A_{\beta}(z)
,
B
, and
U
we obtain

\displaystyle\beta BA_{\beta}(z)

\displaystyle=\beta\big([1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},\smash{\overline{V}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big)

\displaystyle=\beta\big([1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}(1-\beta M(z)+\beta M(z))|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},\smash{\overline{V}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big)

\displaystyle=U\beta+\beta\big([1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}\beta M(z)|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},\smash{\overline{V}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big).

Hence

1-g_{1}(z)\beta BA_{\beta}(z)=1-g_{1}(z)\big[U\beta+\beta\big([1-\beta M(z)]^{-1}\beta M(z)|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},\smash{\overline{V}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big)\big]=\Lambda_{\beta}(z),

which together with (
2.16
) shows
(
2.12
) and thus (
2.11
).

It remains to prove the claim about the algebraic multiplicities.
By the Birman-Schwinger principle from Lemma
2.1
, the algebraic multiplicity of

-z^{2}\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(H_{\beta})
with
z\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
coincides with the algebraic multiplicity of the family
{1-\beta Q}

at
z
, so the claim follows if we verify

(2.17)

\displaystyle m_{a}(z;1-\beta Q)=m_{a}(z;\Lambda_{\beta}).

To justify (
2.17
) recall that

1-\beta M(z)
is boundedly invertible for all
z\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
.
Hence, we use the factorization
(
2.13
)
and the fact that eigenvalues of operator families are invariant under multiplications by boundedly invertible
operators (see Lemma
A.3
) and conclude

\displaystyle m_{a}(z;1-\beta Q)

\displaystyle=m_{a}(z;1-[1-\beta M]^{-1}\beta L).

Finally, (
2.15
) implies

\displaystyle m_{a}(z;1-[1-\beta M]^{-1}\beta L)

\displaystyle=m_{a}(z;1-g_{1}\beta A_{\beta}B)

\displaystyle=m_{a}(z;1-g_{1}\beta BA_{\beta})

\displaystyle=m_{a}(z;\Lambda_{\beta})

where we used Lemma
A.4
to obtain the second equality.
The last quantity is the multiplicity of
z
as a root of
\Lambda_{\beta}
, see Remark
A.2
.
∎

2.3.
Transformation of
\Lambda_{\beta}

We will now transform the argument of the function
\Lambda_{\beta}
in (
2.10
), which is useful
in our further analysis.
For this consider a restriction of the function
g_{1}
in (
2.8
) given by

g_{1}:D\Big(0;\>\frac{1}{2}\Big)\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}\rightarrow g_{1}\Big(D\Big(0;\>\frac{1}{2}\Big)\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}\Big),\quad z\mapsto\begin{cases}(2z)^{-1}&\text{if }d=1,\\
-(2\pi)^{-1}\log(z)&\text{if }d=2,\end{cases}

and observe that
g_{1}
is holomorphic on
D(0;\frac{1}{2})\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}
and bijective in the present context.
The corresponding inverse has the form

g_{1}^{-1}:g_{1}\Big(D\Big(0;\>\frac{1}{2}\Big)\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}\Big)\rightarrow D\Big(0;\>\frac{1}{2}\Big)\cap\mathbb{C}_{+},\quad w\mapsto\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2w}&\text{ if }d=1,\\
\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-2\pi w}}\right)&\text{ if }d=2.\end{cases}

Next, we define the set

(2.18)

\displaystyle\Omega:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}:\frac{1}{w}\in g_{1}\Big(D\Big(0;\>\frac{1}{2}\Big)\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}\Big)}}\right\},

and we note that
\Omega
is non-empty since
g_{1}(D(0;\frac{1}{2})\cap\mathbb{C}_{+})\subset\mathbb{C}_{+}
.
Furthermore, we make use of the mapping

\phi:\Omega\rightarrow g_{1}\Big(D\Big(0;\>\frac{1}{2}\Big)\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}\Big),\quad w\mapsto\frac{1}{w},

and we define

(2.19)

\Phi:=g_{1}^{-1}\circ\phi:\Omega\rightarrow D\Big(0;\>\frac{1}{2}\Big)\cap\mathbb{C}_{+},\quad w\mapsto\begin{cases}\frac{w}{2}&\text{ if }d=1,\\
\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{2\pi}{w}}}\right)&\text{ if }d=2.\end{cases}

From the construction it is clear that the function
\Phi:\Omega\rightarrow D(0;\frac{1}{2})\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}
is
well-defined, holomorphic, bijective, and one has
g_{1}(\Phi(w))=\frac{1}{w}
for
w\in\Omega
.
In the next lemma we determine the set
\Omega
explicitly.

Lemma 2.3
.

The set
\Omega
in (
2.18
) is given by

(2.20)

\Omega=D(0;1)\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}\quad\text{if}\,\,d=1

and

(2.21)

\Omega=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}:\>\operatorname{Re}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{w}}}\right)>\frac{\log(2)}{2\pi},\>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\operatorname{Im}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{w}}}\right)}}\right\rvert<\frac{1}{4}}}\right\}\quad\text{if}\,\,d=2,

and, in particular,
\Omega
is bounded.

Figure 4
.
An illustration of the set
\Omega
defined in (
2.18
).

(a)

\Omega
in

; cf. (
2.20
).

(b)

\Omega
in
d=2
; cf. (

) and (

).

Proof.

For
d=1
, a direct computation shows

(2.22)

\displaystyle g_{1}\Big(D\Big(0;\>\frac{1}{2}\Big)\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}\Big)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}:|z|>1}}\right\}

and hence we conclude (
2.20
). For

, we obtain

(2.23)

\displaystyle g_{1}\Big(D\Big(0;\>\frac{1}{2}\Big)\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}\Big)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}:\operatorname{Re}z>\frac{\log(2)}{2\pi},\>|{\operatorname{Im}z}|<\frac{1}{4}}}\right\},

which leads to (

). To see that

is bounded in the case

\frac{\operatorname{Re}w}{|w|^{2}}=\operatorname{Re}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{w}}}\right)>\frac{\log(2)}{2\pi},\quad w\in\Omega,

.
∎

We also remark that for

one has

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{w}}}\right)>\frac{\log(2)}{2\pi}\iff\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{Re}w-\frac{\pi}{\log(2)}}}\right)^{2}+(\operatorname{Im}w)^{2}<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\pi}{\log(2)}}}\right)^{2},

thus leading to the alternative form (see Figure

)

(2.24)

\Omega=D\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\pi}{\log(2)};\frac{\pi}{\log(2)}}}\right)\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}:\>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\operatorname{Im}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{w}}}\right)}}\right\rvert<\frac{1}{4}}}\right\}.

Now the map
\Phi
will be used to transform the function

in (

)
to the form (

Proposition 2.4
.

Let

) for

satisfying
(
2.2

) and (

Then for all sufficiently small

, the following items hold.

(i)

For all

\hskip 28.45274pt-\Phi(w)^{2}\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(H_{\beta})\iff\Lambda_{\beta}(\Phi(w))=0.

\hskip 28.45274pt\Lambda_{\beta}(\Phi(w))=1-\frac{U\beta}{w}-\frac{\beta}{w}\big([1-\beta M(\Phi(w))]^{-1}\beta M(\Phi(w))|V|^{\frac{1}{2}},\smash{\overline{V}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big).

3.
Characterization of all eigenvalues for Rollnik-type potentials

In this section we continue investigating the discrete eigenvalues of the operators

H_{\beta}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
, in (
2.1
). From
now on, for the rest of this paper, we assume, in addition to the condition (
2.2
), that the potential
V:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfies the Rollnik-type integrability condition

(3.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert V(x)}}\right\rvert\big|\mu^{(d)}(x-y)\big|^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert V(y)}}\right\rvert\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)<\infty;

cf. (
1.8
).
For such potentials, it turns out in the next lemma that the operator family
M
(which
are integral operators with kernels given by (
2.7
)) is uniformly bounded,
which then leads to a characterization of

all
discrete eigenvalues of
H_{\beta}
as zeros of the function
\Lambda_{\beta}\circ\Phi
(for all sufficiently small
\beta
), see Proposition
3.2
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
M(z)
,
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, be the integral operator with kernel (
2.7
).
Suppose that
V
satisfies (
2.2
) and (
3.1
). Then there exists a constant
C_{M}>0
such that

(3.2)

\|M(z)\|\leq C_{M}

holds for all
z\in D(0;\frac{1}{2})\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}
. In particular,

(3.3)

D\Big(0;\>\frac{1}{2}\Big)\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\beta}

for all sufficiently small
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

By the definition of
M(z)
we have

\displaystyle\|M(z)\|^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V(x)||\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(d)}}(x,y;z)-g_{1}(z)|^{2}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y).

Using Lemma
A.1
(i)(a) and Lemma
A.1
(ii)(a)
we obtain (recall the we use the convention that
C
is a positive constant that may
change in between estimates)

\displaystyle|\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}(x,y;z)-g_{1}(z)|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}|x-y|^{2}=|\mu^{(1)}(x-y)|^{2},

\displaystyle|\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(2)}}(x,y;z)-g_{1}(z))|^{2}\leq C(1+\big|{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}\big|^{2})\leq C(1+|\mu^{(2)}(x-y)|^{2}),

for
d=1
and
d=2
, respectively. Hence, in both cases

\displaystyle\|M(z)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|V\|_{L^{1}}^{2}+C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V(x)||\mu^{(d)}(x-y)|^{2}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)<\infty;

cf. (
3.1
). Finally, (
3.3
) is an immediate consequence of (
3.2
), see (
2.9
).
∎

Recall the definitions of the complex numbers

(3.4)

U:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{and}\quad U_{1}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}V(x)\mu^{(d)}(x-y)V(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)

from (
1.10
). Note that (
3.1
) ensures that
U_{1}
is well-defined since
the inequality
|{\mu^{(d)}(\cdot)}|\leq 1+|{\mu^{(d)}(\cdot)}|^{2}
implies

(3.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}

\displaystyle|V(x)||\mu^{(d)}(x-y)||V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\|V\|_{L^{1}}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|V(x)||\mu^{(d)}(x-y)|^{2}|V(y)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(x,y)<\infty.

Next, for
U
and
U_{1}
as above let

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\beta)=\begin{cases}U\beta-U_{1}\beta^{2}&\text{ if }d=1,\\
U\beta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{1}-(2\pi)^{-1}(\log(2)-\gamma)U^{2}}}\right]\beta^{2}&\text{ if }d=2,\end{cases}

where
\gamma
denotes the Euler-Masceroni constant; cf. (
1.11
).
It is helpful to notice that in the case
U\neq 0
there exists a constant
m_{\mathcal{U}}\geq 1
such that

(3.7)

\frac{1}{m_{\mathcal{U}}}|\mathcal{U}(\beta)|\leq|\beta|\leq m_{\mathcal{U}}|\mathcal{U}(\beta)|

for all sufficiently small
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
.

The main result of this section is the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
H_{\beta}=-\Delta-\beta V
be as in (
2.1
) for
V
satisfying
(
2.2
) and (
3.1
).
Let
\Lambda_{\beta}:\mathcal{N}_{\beta}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and
{\Phi:\Omega\rightarrow D(0;\frac{1}{2})\cap\mathbb{C}_{+}}

be given by (
2.10
) and (
2.19
), respectively, and let
\mathcal{U}(\beta)
be as in (
3.6
).
Then for all sufficiently small
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
, the following items are true.

(i)

There holds

(3.8)

\sigma_{\rm{p}}(H_{\beta})\setminus[0,\infty)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\Phi(w)^{2}:w\in\Omega\text{ and }\Lambda_{\beta}(\Phi(w))=0}}\right\}.

If
w
is a root of
\Lambda_{\beta}\circ\Phi
, then its multiplicity as a root of
\Lambda_{\beta}\circ\Phi
coincides with
the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue
-\Phi(w)^{2}\in\sigma_{\rm{p}}(H_{\beta})
.

(ii)

The function
\Lambda_{\beta}\circ\Phi
admits the expansion

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\beta}(\Phi(w))=1-\frac{1}{w}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{U}(\beta)+r_{\beta}(w)}}\right),\quad w\in\Omega,

where
r_{\beta}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a holomorphic

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{U}(\beta)=U\operatorname{Re}\beta\bigl(1+\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{Re}\beta)\bigr),\quad\beta\to 0.

In particular, with (

) and (
4.33
)

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{U}(\beta)}{2|{\operatorname{Im}\mathcal{U}(\beta)}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{U^{\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{Re}\beta}{2|{\operatorname{Im}\beta}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{Re}\beta)\big)+\mathcal{O}(|{\operatorname{Im}\beta|}^{\frac{3}{2}})

\displaystyle>\big(1+R^{\prime}|{\operatorname{Re}\beta}|\big)\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\operatorname{Re}\beta)\big)+\mathcal{O}([\operatorname{Re}\beta]^{3})

\displaystyle=1+|{\operatorname{Re}\beta}|(R^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}(1))

Proof of (ii) for

We prove that

Appendix A

A.1.
Inequalities related to the Green’s function

Recall that for
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
the integral kernel of the resolvent
(-\Delta+z^{2})^{-1}

of the free Laplacian in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is given by

(A.1)

\displaystyle\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(d)}}(x,y;z)=\begin{cases}(2z)^{-1}e^{-z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}&\text{ if }d=1,\\
(2\pi)^{-1}K_{0}(z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert)&\text{ if }d=2,\end{cases}\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
K_{0}:\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

denotes the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order zero.

For the convenience of the reader, let us briefly recall some properties of
K_{0}
(for an extensive treatment, we refer to

[
2
, Chap. 9.6]
).
First,
K_{0}:\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

is a holomorphic function, that by
[
2
, Eq. (9.6.13)]
admits the expansion

(A.2)

\displaystyle K_{0}(w)=-\log(w)+\log(2)-\gamma+\mathcal{O}(w^{2}\log(w)),\quad w\to 0,\>w\in\mathbb{C}_{+},

where
\log:\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is the principal branch of the complex logarithm, and

\gamma
is the Euler-Masceroni constant. In particular,
K_{0}
has a logarithmic singularity at
w=0
.
For large
w
with non-negative real part, it follows from
[
2
, Eq. (9.7.2)]
that
K_{0}
has
the asymptotic behavior

(A.3)

\displaystyle K_{0}(w)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\pi}{2w}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-w}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\mathcal{O}(w^{-1})}}\right),\quad w\to\infty,\>\operatorname{Re}w\geq 0,

so
K_{0}(w)
tends to zero uniformly if
w\rightarrow\infty

with
\operatorname{Re}w\geq 0
.

Since we have to deal with the complex logarithm, let us briefly summarize
some properties of its principal branch. First, there holds

(A.4)

\displaystyle\big|{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w}}\right\rvert}\big|\leq|{\log(w)}|<\big|{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w}}\right\rvert}\big|+\pi,\quad w\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0],

and as a consequence (using
\pi<C|{\log(1/2)}|<C|{\log|w|}|
for
|w|<1/2
)

(A.5)

\displaystyle\big|{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w}}\right\rvert}\big|\leq|{\log(w)}|\leq C\big|{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w}}\right\rvert}\big|,\quad w\in D\Big(0;\>\frac{1}{2}\Big)\setminus(-\infty,0].

Furthermore, it is not difficult to verify the identity

(A.6)

\displaystyle\log(w_{1}w_{2})=\log(w_{1})+\log(w_{2})\phantom{\quad}\text{if}\phantom{\quad}|{\arg(w_{1})+\arg(w_{2})}|<\pi

for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
,
where
\arg(w)\in(-\pi,\pi)
denotes the argument of a complex number

w\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
.

In the following, recall that for
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
we have

(A.7)

\displaystyle g_{1}(z)=\begin{cases}(2z)^{-1}&\text{if }d=1,\\
-(2\pi)^{-1}\log(z)&\text{if }d=2,\end{cases}

and, in addition, define for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with
x\neq y
if
d=2
),

(A.8)

\displaystyle g_{2}(x,y;z)=\begin{cases}(2z)^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}}\right)&\text{ if }d=1,\\
-(2\pi)^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\log(z|x-y|)-\log(2)+\gamma}}\right)&\text{ if }d=2.\end{cases}

In the next lemma, we collect useful inequalities for certain integral kernels that appear in the analysis
of the Schrödinger operator
H_{\beta}
.

Lemma A.1
.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(d)}}(x,y;z)
be as in (
A.1
). Let
g_{1}(z)
and
g_{2}(x,y;z)
be given by (
A.7
) and (
A.8
), respectively. Then, the following inequalities hold.

(i)

If
d=1
, then

(a)

|\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}(x,y;z)-g_{1}(z)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert
,

(b)

|\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}(x,y;z)-g_{2}(x,y;z)|\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert^{2}

(ii)

If

there exists a constant

(a)

|\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(2)}}(x,y;z)-g_{1}(z)|^{2}\leq C(1+\big|{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}\big|^{2})
,

(b)

|\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(2)}}(x,y;z)-g_{2}(x,y;z)|\leq C(1+\big|{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}\big|)
,

(c)

|\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(2)}}(x,y;z)-g_{2}(x,y;z)|\leq C|z||{\log(z)}|
if
|x-y|<|z|^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.

Proof.

(i)
For
d=1
, there holds

\displaystyle\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}(x,y;z)-g_{1}(z)=\frac{e^{-z|x-y|}-1}{2z},

\displaystyle\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(1)}}(x,y;z)-g_{2}(x,y;z)=\frac{e^{-z|x-y|}-1+z|x-y|}{2z}.

In order to see (a), notice that for any
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
one has

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{e^{-z|x-y|}-1}{2z}}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{1}{2z}\int_{0}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}e^{-zt}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}}\right\rvert=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\int_{0}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}e^{-zt}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2}|x-y|,

i.e., the claimed inequality.
Estimate (b) follows from integration by parts,

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}-1+z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}{2z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2z}\int_{0}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert-t)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{2}}e^{-zt}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{z}{2}\int_{0}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert-t)e^{-zt}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

and hence

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{e^{-z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}-1+z\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}{2z}}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{|z|}{2}\int_{0}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert-t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{4}|z||x-y|^{2}.

(ii) Consider
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
x\neq y
and set

(A.9)

\displaystyle w:=z|x-y|

for
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
with
|z|<1/2
; it follows that
w\neq 0
and
w\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
.
With
w
in (
A.9
), the kernels we have to estimate read as

(A.10)

\displaystyle\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(2)}}(x,y;z)-g_{1}(z)=\frac{K_{0}(w)+\log(z)}{2\pi},

(A.11)

\displaystyle\vphantom{R^{2}}\smash{\mathcal{G}^{(2)}}(x,y;z)-g_{2}(x,y;z)=\frac{K_{0}(w)+\log(w)-\log(2)+\gamma}{2\pi}.

For the inequalities below recall that we use the convention that

C
denotes a positive constant that may change in
between the estimates.
Let us start by proving the claim (a).
If
|w|<1
, then (
A.2
) implies

\displaystyle|K_{0}(w)+\log(w)|^{2}\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+|w|^{2}|{\log(w)}|}}\right)^{2}\leq C.

The last inequality together with (see (
A.6
))

(A.12)

\displaystyle\log(z)=\log(w)-\log|x-y|

and the elementary inequality
(|a|+|b|)^{2}\leq 2|a|^{2}+2|b|^{2}
yield

\displaystyle|K_{0}(w)+\log(z)|^{2}=|K_{0}(w)+\log(w)-\log|x-y||^{2}\leq C(1+\big|{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x-y}}\right\rvert}\big|^{2}).

If
|w|\geq 1
, then (
A.3
) and (
A.5
) yield (recall that
|z|<1/2
)

\begin{split}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert K_{0}(w)+\log(z)}}\right\rvert\leq|K_{0}(w)|+|{\log(z)}|\leq C+|{\log(z)}|\leq C(1+\log{(|z|^{-1})}).\end{split}

Since
|z|<1/2
and
2<|z|^{-1}\leq|x-y|
if
|w|\geq 1
by (
A.9
), we arrive at

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert K_{0}(w)+\log(z)}}\right\rvert^{2}\leq C(1+|{\log|x-y|}|^{2}).

In summary, the inequality (a) holds.

It is not difficult to see that (b) follows from (a);
indeed, taking the square root in (a) and applying
the elementary inequality
(|a|^{2}+|b|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq|a|+|b|
gives us

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert K_{0}(w)+\log(z)}}\right\rvert\leq C(1+|{\log|x-y|}|),

so with (
A.12
) and the triangle inequality

\displaystyle|{K_{0}(w)+\log(w)-\log(2)+\gamma}|

\displaystyle\leq|{K_{0}(w)+\log(z)}|+|{\log|x-y|}|+C

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|{\log|x-y|}|)+|{\log|x-y|}|+C

\displaystyle=C(1+|{\log|x-y|}|),

which is the claim of (b).

Finally, to show (c), suppose that
|x-y|\leq|z|^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, which is equivalent to

(A.13)

\displaystyle|w|\leq|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By rearranging (
A.2
) and using (
A.6
) (we have
|{\arg{w}}|<\pi/2
) one verifies

\displaystyle|{K_{0}(w)+\log(w)-\log(2)+\gamma}|\leq C|{w^{2}\log(w)}|=C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{w^{2}}{2}\log(w^{2})}}\right\rvert\leq C|w|^{2}|{\log(w^{2})}|

and hence with (
A.5
) (note that
|w|^{2}\leq|z|<1/2
by (
A.13
))

\displaystyle|K_{0}(w)+\log(w)-\log(2)+\gamma|\leq C|w|^{2}|{\log|w|^{2}}|.

Finally, a combination of (
A.13
) and the fact that
(0,1)\ni x\mapsto x^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|{\log(x^{2})}|+1}}\right)
is monotonously increasing yields

\displaystyle|K_{0}(w)+\log(w)-\log(2)+\gamma|

\displaystyle\leq C|w|^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|{\log|w|^{2}}|+1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C|z|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|{\log|z|}|+1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C|z||{\log|z|}|

\displaystyle\leq C|z||{\log(z)}|,

where we again used (
A.5
) for the last inequality;
(c) is shown. ∎

A.2.
Eigenvalues of operator functions

This section follows the presentation of
[
37
, Chap. XI.9]
.
We assume that
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{G}
are Hilbert spaces and that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
is a non-empty, open, and connected set.

For an analytic operator function
W:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})

we call
z_{0}\in\Omega
an
eigenvalue
of
W
if

\ker{W(z_{0})}\neq\{0\}.

For an eigenvalue
z_{0}\in\Omega
the quantity

\operatorname{dim}\ker W(z_{0})
is called the
geometric multiplicity
of
W
at
z_{0}
.
If
z_{0}\in\Omega
is an eigenvalue of
W
and, in addition,

W(z_{0})
is Fredholm (that is,
\operatorname{ran}{W(z_{0})}
is closed and both

\operatorname{dim}\ker{W(z_{0})}
and
\operatorname{codim}\operatorname{ran}W(z_{0})

are finite) and
W(z)
is boundedly invertible for all

0<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z-z_{0}}}\right\rvert<\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
, then

z_{0}
is said to be an
eigenvalue of finite type
of
W
.
If
z_{0}
is an eigenvalue of finite type of
W
, then by
[
37
, Thm. XI.8.1]

W(z)
admits a factorization

(A.14)

\displaystyle W(z)=E(z)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(P_{0}+(z-z_{0})^{\kappa_{1}}P_{1}+\ldots+(z-z_{0})^{\kappa_{r}}P_{r}}}\right)F(z)

in a small neighborhood
D
of
z_{0}
, where
E:D\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and
F:D\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
are analytic operator functions,
boundedly invertible on
D
,

1\leq\kappa_{1}\leq\kappa_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\kappa_{r},

are positive integers,
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}
are mutually disjoint projections of rank one, and

P_{0}=I_{\mathcal{H}}-P_{1}-\ldots-P_{r};

here
I_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the identity operator in
\mathcal{H}
.
The sum
\kappa_{1}+\ldots+\kappa_{r}

is called the
algebraic multiplicity
of

W
at
z_{0}
, and will be denoted by

m_{a}(z_{0};W)
.

Remark A.2
.

Observe that in the scalar case, that is,
W:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a complex-valued
holomorphic function, the notions above simplify accordingly. More precisely, eigenvalues are zeros of
W
and each eigenvalue
is an eigenvalue of finite type, its geometric multiplicity is one, and its algebraic multiplicity
coincides with the
multiplicity of the zero of
W
.

It follows from the factorization (
A.14
) that the eigenvalues of finite type of an operator function
and their respective algebraic multiplicities are invariant under multiplications by boundedly invertible operator functions.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
W:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and
R:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})

be analytic operator functions, and suppose that
R(z)
is boundedly invertible for all

z\in\Omega
.
Then
z_{0}\in\Omega
is an eigenvalue of finite type of
W
if and only if
it is an eigenvalue of finite type of
RW
and
WR
, in which case

\displaystyle m_{a}(z_{0};W)=m_{a}(z_{0};RW)=m_{a}(z_{0};WR).

In the following lemma
I_{\mathcal{H}}
and
I_{\mathcal{G}}
denote the identity operators in the Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}

and
\mathcal{G}
, respectively. It turns out that operator functions of the special form

I_{\mathcal{G}}+AB
and
I_{\mathcal{H}}+BA
have the same eigenvalues of finite type.
For the proof we make use of Fredholm determinants; for a definition and basic properties of the
latter we refer to
[
65
]
and
for further details to
[
36
, App. C]
.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
A:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{G})
and
B:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H})
be
analytic families of Hilbert-Schmidt operators.
Then
z_{0}\in\Omega
is an eigenvalue of finite of
I_{\mathcal{G}}+AB
if and only
if it is an eigenvalue of finite type of
I_{\mathcal{H}}+BA
, in which case

\displaystyle m_{a}(z_{0};I_{\mathcal{G}}+AB)=m_{a}(z_{0};I_{\mathcal{H}}+BA).

Proof.

Recall that for an analytic family
W:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
of trace class operators
the Fredholm determinant

(A.15)

\displaystyle\Omega\ni z\mapsto\det(I_{\mathcal{H}}+W(z))

is an analytic function. Moreover, the zeros of (
A.15
) are
exactly the eigenvalues of finite type of
I_{\mathcal{H}}+W
and their respective algebraic
multiplicities coincide with their multiplicity as a root of
{\det(I_{\mathcal{H}}+W)}
. Hence,
the claim follows from the well-known identity (see, e.g.,
[
36
, Thm. C.4.4(iv)]

\displaystyle\det(I_{\mathcal{G}}+A(z)B(z))=\det(I_{\mathcal{H}}+B(z)A(z)),\quad z\in\Omega,

which is satisfied since by assumption
A(z)B(z)
and
B(z)A(z)
are trace class for every
z\in\Omega
;
cf.
[
71
, Satz 3.23]
.
∎

A.3.
Useful tools from complex analysis

We collect useful results from complex analysis. The first lemma concerns the
multiplicity of roots under substitutions.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\subset\mathbb{C}
be open, let
h:\Omega_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be holomorphic, let

{\Psi:\Omega_{2}\rightarrow\Omega_{1}}
be holomorphic and bijective, and assume that

h(\Psi(w_{0}))=0
for some
w_{0}\in\Omega_{2}
.
Then the multiplicity of
\Psi(w_{0})
as a root of the function
h
coincides with the multiplicity of
w_{0}
as a root of the function
h\circ\Psi
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|w-w_{0}|=r\}
, where
r>0
is chosen such that

\Gamma
and its interior lie in
\Omega_{2}
, and suppose that
\gamma
is a simple, positively oriented parametrization of
\Gamma
.
The argument principle for holomorphic functions then implies

\displaystyle m_{a}(w_{0};h\circ\Psi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{(h\circ\Psi)^{\prime}(w)}{(h\circ\Psi)(w)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{h^{\prime}(\Phi(w))\Psi^{\prime}(w)}{h(\Psi(w))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Psi(\gamma)}\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{h(z)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=m_{a}(\Psi(w_{0});h),

where in the last equality we used that
\Psi(\gamma)
is a simple, smooth and positively oriented curve around
\Psi(w_{0})
;
this, in turn, holds as

\Psi
is assumed to be holomorphic and bijective.
∎

The following lemma, based on Rouche’s theorem, is an important ingredient
to find zeros of the function
\Lambda_{\beta}\circ\Phi
in Proposition
3.2
.

Lemma A.6
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, let
\mathcal{O}_{a}
be an open neighborhood of
a
such that

0\not\in\mathcal{O}_{a}
, let
b:\mathcal{O}_{a}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, and consider the function

(A.16)

\displaystyle h(w)=1-\frac{1}{w}(a+b(w)),\quad w\in\mathcal{O}_{a}.

If for some
\delta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{12})
the following conditions hold,

(i)

D(a;4|a|\delta)\subset\mathcal{O}_{a}
,

(ii)

b
is holomorphic on
D(a;4|a|\delta)
,

(iii)

|b(w)|<\delta|a|
for all
w\in D(a;4|a|\delta)
,

then the function
h
has exactly one root in the disc
D(a;|a|\delta)
and this root is simple.

Proof.

We start by proving the existence of a root, which is essentially a consequence of Rouche’s theorem.
Consider the mapping

\displaystyle\Psi:D(0;3\delta)\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\quad\alpha\mapsto\Psi(\alpha)=\frac{a}{1-\alpha};

since
|\alpha|<1/4
,
\Psi
is well-defined, injective, and holomorphic.
Moreover,
|\alpha|<1/4
also implies

\displaystyle|a-\Psi(\alpha)|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert a-\frac{a}{1-\alpha}}}\right\rvert\leq|a|\frac{|\alpha|}{1-|\alpha|}<|a|\frac{3\delta}{1-\frac{1}{4}}=4|a|\delta,

which shows that
\Psi
maps
D(0;3\delta)
into
D(a;4|a|\delta)
.
In particular, the function
f=h\circ\Psi
is holomorphic on
D(0;3\delta)
due to assumption (i) and (ii).
A direct computation shows

\displaystyle f(\alpha)=h(\Psi(\alpha))=1-\frac{1}{\Psi(\alpha)}(a+b(\Psi(\alpha)))=\alpha-(1-\alpha)\frac{b(\Psi(\alpha))}{a},\quad\alpha\in D(0;3\delta),

and setting
g(\alpha)=-\alpha
we obtain for
\alpha\in\partial D(0;2\delta)
the estimate

\displaystyle|f(\alpha)+g(\alpha)|\leq|1-\alpha|\frac{|b(\Psi(\alpha))|}{|a|}<(1+2\delta)\delta<2\delta=|\alpha|\leq|f(\alpha)|+|g(\alpha)|,

where we used assumption (iii) for the second inequality and
2\delta<1/6
for the third inequality.
Therefore, we can apply
Rouche’s theorem (see, e.g.,
[
10
, Chap. V, Thm. 3.8]
) to the functions
f=h\circ\Psi

and
g
on the disc

D(0;2\delta)
, and conclude that
f=h\circ\Psi
has exactly one zero
\alpha_{\delta}
in
D(0;2\delta)
,
which, in addition, is simple. Therefore, it follows that
w_{\delta}:=\Psi(\alpha_{\delta})
is a zero
of
h
in
D(a;4|a|\delta)
. Since the function

\Psi:D(0;3\delta)\rightarrow D(a;4|a|\delta)
is holomorphic and injective,
thus bijective onto its range,
w_{\delta}
is a simple zero of
h
by Lemma
A.5
.

It remains to prove that
w_{\delta}
belongs to the smaller disc
D(a;|a|\delta)
and that it
is the only zero of
h
inside this disc.
To see the first claim, note that
h(w_{\delta})=0
and assumption (iii) imply

\displaystyle|a-w_{\delta}|=|b(w_{\delta})|<\delta|a|.

To show that
w_{\delta}
is the only zero of
h
in
D(a;|a|\delta)
consider

\widetilde{w}\in D(a;|a|\delta)

such that
h(\widetilde{w})=0
and set

\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}:=\frac{\widetilde{w}-a}{\widetilde{w}}.

Then we have
\Psi(\widetilde{\alpha})=\widetilde{w}
and

|\widetilde{\alpha}|=\frac{|\widetilde{w}-a|}{|\widetilde{w}|}\leq\frac{|a|\delta}{|a|-|a|\delta}=\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}<2\delta,

that is,
\tilde{\alpha}\in D(0;2\delta)
.
Moreover, since
\widetilde{w}
is a root of
h
, it follows that
\widetilde{\alpha}
is a root of
h\circ\Psi
.
However, since
\alpha_{\delta}
is the only zero of
h\circ\Psi
in
D(0;2\delta)
, this implies

\widetilde{\alpha}=\alpha_{\delta}
and thus
\widetilde{w}=w_{\delta}
.
∎
