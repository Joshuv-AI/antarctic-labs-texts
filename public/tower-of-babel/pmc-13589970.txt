Clinical Characteristics and Long-Term Follow-Up of Leukoencephalopathy in Pediatric Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia After Chemotherapy
Authors: Jun Feng, Xiao Xiao, Jihong Tang, Bingbing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the clinical characteristics and long-term outcomes of leukoencephalopathy (LE) in pediatric patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) after chemotherapy, with the goal of evaluating diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 48 pediatric ALL patients diagnosed with LE after chemotherapy at the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (February 2010–December 2024). Data included general information, Clinical manifestations and diagnostic imaging results. Compared with a control group of 27 healthy children, the patients’ cognitive function was assessed by using the Behavioural Rating Inventory of Executive Function-2 (BRIEF-2) and the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC).ResultsNeurological symptoms were observed in 47.92% (23/48) of LE patients, including seizures (27.08%), paralysis (12.50%), limb numbness (10.42%), and speech impairment (10.42%). Brain MRI revealed white matter abnormalities, predominantly in the periventricular regions (47.92%), parietal lobes (37.50%), and frontal lobes (22.92%). Long-term follow-up demonstrated that 73.91% of symptomatic patients experienced rapid neurological improvement, and 83.33% of cases showed a significant reduction in MRI lesions within 12 months. Compared to controls, patients with LE exhibited higher BRIEF-2 scores (indicating executive dysfunction) for self-monitoring, emotional control, working memory, and planning/organization (all P < 0.05). The WISC scores were significantly lower for the total IQ, perceptual reasoning, working memory, and processing speed (P < 0.05).ConclusionPost-chemotherapy LE in pediatric ALL presents with diverse neurological symptoms and neuroimaging abnormalities accompanied by mild-to-moderate cognitive deficits. However, the prognosis appears favorable, with most patients showing symptom resolution and improved imaging findings.

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is one of the most prevalent pediatric hematologic malignancies. With advances in modern medical technology, both the survival rates and survival times of children with ALL have improved significantly. As the survival times of children with ALL have increased, greater attention had be paid to neurological complications. Children with ALL following chemotherapy is frequently complicated by leukoencephalopathy (LE), a neurological disorder characterized by compromised myelin sheath integrity and reduced cerebral white and gray matter volumes. LE exerts profound impacts on neurological development and cognitive function in children.1,2

LE is primarily diagnosed through neuroimaging, and the pathogenesis is significantly associated with chemotherapy regimens. A retrospective study conducted a quantitative analysis of standardized MRI scan acquired at the end of treatment in 171 children with lymphoblastic leukaemia/lymphoma who had undergone chemotherapy, and demonstrated evidence for the occurrence of LE during treatment in approximately 52% of cases, with younger aged patients being at increased risk. Furthermore, increased intravenous methotrexate (MTX) intensity could potentially increase the risk of such lesions.3 Our published literature demonstrated that the main symptoms of children with ALL who developed LE following chemotherapy include seizure, nausea, vomiting, paralysis, and numbness, and neuroradiological lesions were mostly distributed in the periventricular area, parietal lobe, and basal ganglia. Children with early diagnosis and treatment have better prognoses.4,5

The growth and development of white and grey matter are closely linked to the maturation of cognitive and behavioural functions. Consequently, the impact of LE in childhood is clear and profound. Jacoba et al6 conducted a comparative analysis of 1560 ALL patients and their siblings, revealing significantly higher incidences of neurobehavioral impairments in chemotherapy-treated cohorts, including headstrong behavior (P = 0.010), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (P < 0.0001), social withdrawal (P = 0.002), and learning difficulties (P < 0.0001). Pryweller JR et al7 conducted multiple MRI scans of the brain in 377 children with ALL who had not received radiotherapy, and carried out neurocognitive assessments at the end of treatment and two years later. The study revealed that nearly a quarter of patients experience asymptomatic LE during treatment, which is associated with cognitive decline, behavioural problems and learning difficulties. De Ville de Goyet et al investigated the cumulative incidence of neurological sequelae among 890 paediatric ALL survivors who had undergone treatment. It demonstrated that cranial radiotherapy increases the incidence of leukoencephalopathy and cognitive impairment. The high single dose of MTX may have also played a same role.8 Specifically, ALL-associated LE predominantly affects executive function, working memory, intellectual capacity, and attentional control. The protracted maturation of the prefrontal cortex renders executive function, a core cognitive domain regulated by this region, particularly susceptible to disruption, explaining its predominance among reported cognitive deficits in pediatric LE cases.9,10

There were very few published studies on LE following chemotherapy for childhood ALL. The previous studies primarily emphasize long-term neuroimaging biomarkers, neurocognitive evaluations and case report. Against this background, the present study reviews 14 years of clinical data from a major pediatric center systematically, the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University. By integrating comprehensive clinical assessments with standardized neuropsychological measures, this study elucidates the Clinical characteristics, cognitive function and long-term outcomes of LE in pediatric ALL after chemotherapy.

Methods

Study Participants

This study enrolled 48 pediatric patients diagnosed with chemotherapy-induced leukoencephalopathy following ALL treatment at the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University between February 2010 and December 2024 recruited from the hematology, intensive care, and neurology departments. Diagnostic criteria were established based on published literature,4 with the following inclusion criteria: (1) definitive ALL diagnosis confirmed through comprehensive clinical evaluation, hematological parameters, and bone marrow examination; (2) development of leukoencephalopathy manifestations after ALL diagnosis, corroborated by characteristic white matter abnormalities on cranial MRI; (3) presence of acute neurological dysfunction symptoms, including seizures, motor/sensory deficits, aphasia, ataxia, altered consciousness, or intracranial hypertension (headache, nausea, vomiting); (4) MRI evidence of white matter hyperintensities on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences that potentially extend to gray matter structures; and (5) exclusive chemotherapy exposure prior to leukoencephalopathy onset, excluding radiotherapy, chimeric antigen receptor T-cell immunotherapy (CAR-T), or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) concurrent central nervous system pathologies (leukemia, tumors, infections, hemorrhage, infarction, trauma, or genetic metabolic disorders) potentially contributing to white matter damage and (2) incomplete medical records despite meeting the diagnostic criteria. All participants were required to satisfy both the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

A control group of 27 healthy children (17 males, 10 females) aged 6–16 years who had undergone routine health checks at the paediatric health clinic was recruited. These children were matched with the case group in terms of age, gender and years of schooling. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) a history of central nervous system disorders, (2) abnormal cranial MRI findings, and (3) noncompliance during cognitive assessments.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (Approval Number: 2021CS093). Parents provided written informed consent. All relevant information was stored in specific equipment, and only authorized personnel had access to the data.

Chemotherapy Regimen and Definition of White Matter Change

For the central nervous system, the risks associated with intrathecal chemotherapy are higher than intravenous administration. Intrathecal chemotherapy regimens for acute lymphoblastic leukemia were typically administered: For children under 1 year of age, those aged 1–3 years, and those over 3 years of age, IT triple therapy consisting of 6, 9, 12.5mg MTX, 15, 25,35 mg cytarabine, and 2.5, 2.5, 5mg Dexamethasone, respectively. MRIs were reviewed by an independent neuroradiologist, having knowledge of diagnosis, age, and sex of the enrolled patients, but lacking information on other clinical details. The MRI criteria for LE were irregularly distributed patchy lesions that characteristically involved the bilateral centrum semiovales around the ventricles, parietal lobe, frontal lobe, basal ganglia, etc. These lesions showed low signal intensity on T1-weighted images, and as high signal intensity on T2-weighted and FLAIR (fluid attenuated image recovery) images. White matter change was defined according to the grading proposed by Monaco et al11 The scale for evaluating MRI in clinical practice that reflected the centrifugal progression of chemotherapy induced white matter change from the periventricular white matter. A grading scale was devised to assess imaging changes using T2 or FLAIR sequences: grade 1=little or no white matter hyperintensity; grade 2=limited periventricular hyperintensity; and grade 3=diffuse white matter hyperintensity.11

Clinical Data

During the study period, 48 pediatric cases that met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria were systematically evaluated. The initial analysis focused on comprehensive inpatient medical records during the leukoencephalopathy diagnostic phase, encompassing general information, clinical manifestations, cranial MRI findings. Subsequent longitudinal follow-up data were collected during outpatient visits or hospital admissions at 1-, 3-, 6-, and 12-month intervals, documenting neurological symptom progression, prognostic outcomes, and cranial MRI findings. Prognosis were assessed using multimodal evaluation strategies combining outpatient clinical examinations, structured telephone interviews, and questionnaire administration.

Therapeutic Interventions

For patients with significant neurological manifestations, chemotherapy was temporarily discontinued upon leukoencephalopathy diagnosis and reintroduced following clinical improvement. The therapeutic protocol consisted of intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy (15–20 mg·kg−1·d−1; maximum 1000 mg/d) for three consecutive days, followed by dose reduction to 50% and conversion to oral prednisone (1–2 mg·kg−1·d−1), with gradual tapering over 1–2 months. Concurrent intravenous immunoglobulin therapy was administered at a total dose of 2 g·kg−1, fractionated over 3–5 days. The neuroprotective strategies include olanzapine, B-complex vitamins, and monosialotetrahexosylganglioside sodium. Symptom-targeted therapies, particularly antiepileptic regimens for seizure management, are implemented on the basis of clinical indications.

Cognitive Function Assessment

Among the 48 ALL patients who developed leukoencephalopathy after chemotherapy, 27 were willing to cooperate in completing cognitive function assessments. The assessments used to analyze and evaluate the cognitive function of the children included the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Functional-2 (BRIEF2), the Parent Version (applicable for ages 5–18), and the Chinese Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children Version 4 (applicable for ages 6–16).

Parents and legal guardians completed the BRIEF2 questionnaire.12 Comprising 63 items, the BRIEF2 questionnaire rated each item on a 3 points scale: 0 = never, 1 = sometimes, and 2 = frequently in the past six months. The BRIEF2 has three indices: the Behavioral Regulation Index (BRI), Emotional Regulation Index (ERI), and Cognitive Regulation Index (CRI), which jointly comprise the Global Executive Composite Score (GEC). The BRI includes two subscales, inhibition and self-monitoring. The ERI includes two subscales, transfer and emotional control. The CRI includes five subscales: priming, working memory, planning and organization, task monitoring, and material organization. The raw score was converted into a standardized T-score, where higher scores suggested more severe impairment of executive function. All children with ALL who participated in the Wechsler Intelligence Scale test and the healthy children in the control group were examined in a quiet environment by physicians qualified to administer the Wechsler Intelligence Scale.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or as median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent two-sample t-tests, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

Clinical Manifestations

Neurological manifestations were identified in 47.92% (23/48) of the pediatric ALL patients with leukoencephalopathy. The predominant clinical features were epileptic episodes (27.08%, 13/48), motor deficits (12.50%, 6/48), limb paresthesia (10.42%, 5/48), and dysarthria (10.42%, 5/48). Additional neurological abnormalities included somnolence (8.33%, 4/48), ataxic gait (8.33%, 4/48), visual disturbances (6.25%, 3/48), impaired consciousness (4.17%, 2/48), sensory ataxia (6.25%, 3/48), and cephalalgia with vertigo (6.25%, 3/48). Seizure semiology analysis revealed that generalized tonic-clonic seizures (14.58%, 7/48) were the most frequent subtype, followed by focal seizures (12.50%, 6/48) with 6.25% (3/48) progressing to bilateral tonic-clonic activity. Motor impairments manifested as unilateral central facial palsy (4.17%, 2/48), hemiparesis (6.25%, 3/48), and paraparesis (2.08%, 1/48) and were frequently accompanied by ipsilateral or bilateral somatosensory deficits. The remaining 52.08% (25/48) of asymptomatic cases were identified through routine neuroimaging surveillance and were characterized by distinctive white matter lesions consistent with the leukoencephalopathy criteria.

73.91% (17/23) of symptomatic patients demonstrated rapid neurological improvement, with the majority achieving significant recovery within one month. Patients with symptomatic epilepsy have all achieved effective control following treatment with anti-seizure medication. Gait disturbances, frequently associated with motor weakness, gradually improved over several months through combined neuroprotective therapy and physical rehabilitation. The overall mortality rate was 6.25% (3/48), with causes including sepsis (n=1), post-liver transplantation graft-versus-host disease (n=1), and multi-organ failure secondary to respiratory compromise (n=1). Importantly, all fatalities were no significantly associated with leukoencephalopathy.

Neuroimaging Features

Cranial MRI examinations were performed in all 48 pediatric cases, demonstrating characteristic white matter abnormalities with occasional gray matter involvement. The spatial distribution of the signal abnormalities predominantly affected the periventricular and semi-oval central white matter regions (47.92%, 23/48), followed by the parietal (37.50%, 18/48), frontal (22.92%, 11/48), basal ganglia (12.50%, 6/48), temporal (12.50%, 6/48), and occipital lobes (10.42%, 5/48). Additional neuroanatomical involvement included the hippocampus (4.16%, 2/48), corpus callosum (4.16%, 2/48), pons (4.16%, 2/48), thalamus (6.25%, 3/48), brainstem (2.08%, 1/48), and cerebellar structures (2.08%, 1/48).

MRI signal characteristics revealed isointense to mildly hypointense lesions on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), with corresponding hyperintense signals on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences. Discussion

Advances in medical therapeutics have significantly improved survival rates and longevity in children with ALL while concurrently increasing clinical concerns regarding neurological complications associated with prolonged survival. LE, the most common neurological complication following chemotherapy for ALL, primarily affects the cerebral white matter integrity. Given that white matter maturation is intrinsically linked to cognitive behavioral development and continuous structural reorganization until adulthood, ALL-associated leukoencephalopathy poses a substantial risk to pediatric neurodevelopment. This study retrospectively analyzed 48 pediatric cases of leukoencephalopathy post-chemotherapy, demonstrating a male predominance, with a mean ALL diagnosis age of 7.13 years. Leukoencephalopathy diagnosis occurred at a median 0.74 years post-chemotherapy initiation, with B-cell lymphoblastic leukemia constituting most of cases.

The clinical presentation of leukoencephalopathy in pediatric ALL patients exhibits significant heterogeneity, ranging from overt neurological manifestations to subclinical forms. In this cohort, 52.08% (25/48) of patients demonstrated asymptomatic cerebral white matter lesions detected solely through routine neuroimaging surveillance. This phenomenon may be attributed to both the limited sensitivity of clinical symptom recognition in young children and relatively mild neuropathological alterations observed radiologically. Conversely, 47.92% (23/48) of the patients presented with definitive neurological deficits at diagnosis, predominantly manifesting as epileptic episodes (n=13), motor impairment (n=6), sensory abnormalities (n=5), and dysarthria (n=5). These findings align with longitudinal observations by Rijmenams et al,3 whose multicenter study of 129 pediatric hematologic malignancy patients identified leukoencephalopathy in 52.71% (68/129) post-treatment, yet only 19.12% (13/68) exhibited treatment-related neurological symptoms. This substantial discrepancy between neuroimaging abnormalities and clinical symptomatology underscores the insidious nature of chemotherapy-induced neurotoxicity in pediatric populations, emphasizing the critical role of routine neuroimaging monitoring in this vulnerable cohort.

Notable discrepancies emerged when comparing our current findings with prior investigations4 on ALL-associated leukoencephalopathy. While our earlier cohort (n=28) demonstrated neurological symptoms in 85.71% of cases, the present study’s lower symptomatic rate (47.92%) reflects methodological variations in case ascertainment. This discrepancy stems in part from the systematic inclusion of subclinical cases identified by routine cranial MRI surveillance in the current protocol. Additional factors include: advances in MRI technology have led to the identification of more patients who present only with abnormal head MRI findings and no neurological symptoms; the lack of sufficient clinical follow-up time may have resulted in the omission of some children who developed clinical symptoms later on; and while the sample size of this study is larger than previous ones and may be more representative, it also suggests the need for further studies with even larger sample sizes. Such comparative evidence underscores the diagnostic necessity of implementing standardized neuroimaging protocols, particularly serial MRI evaluations, to enhance early detection of white matter pathology in asymptomatic patients.

We also conducted a preliminary observation of asymptomatic patients with MRI abnormalities. Upon reviewing their status one year after the initial detection of MRI abnormalities, it was found that a very small number of children developed neurological symptoms, while the majority showed a reduction in lesion size on follow-up cranial MRI scans. Furthermore, assessment using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale and the Behavioural Assessment of Executive Functioning revealed that some children exhibited mild cognitive deficits. However, as the sample size was small and not representative, further research with a larger sample size is required.

Neuroimaging confirmation remains the diagnostic cornerstone, with characteristic MRI findings in this cohort including periventricular and subcortical white matter hyperintensities on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences, typically demonstrating patchy, asymmetric lesion distribution across semioval centers, frontoparietal regions, and basal ganglia, while progressive cerebral atrophy was consistently observed in advanced cases,3 collectively establishing a distinct neuroimaging profile for chemotherapy-induced LE.

Advanced quantitative techniques provide deeper pathophysiological insights, as demonstrated by DTI analysis documenting significant fractional anisotropy (FA) reductions (P<0.05) in critical white matter tracts, including the cingulum, uncinate fasciculus, and internal capsule post-chemotherapy,14 while functional MRI studies revealing altered regional homogeneity (ReHo) and functional connectivity density (FCD) patterns that are significantly correlated with domain-specific neurocognitive deficits,15 collectively advancing our understanding of microstructural and functional alterations underlying chemotherapy-induced neurotoxicity. These multimodal imaging biomarkers not only facilitate early diagnosis but also enable longitudinal monitoring of white matter integrity and positioning neuroimaging as an indispensable tool for the comprehensive management of chemotherapy-induced leukoencephalopathy.

Therapeutic management of chemotherapy-induced leukoencephalopathy remains empirically guided, though emerging mechanistic insights provide biological plausibility for current regimens, as clinical observations demonstrate symptomatic amelioration and neuroradiological improvement in the cases receiving corticosteroid therapy combined with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) through potential blood-brain barrier (BBB) stabilization mechanisms, supported by murine models of cisplatin-induced neurotoxicity showing glucocorticoid-mediated upregulation of ZO-1 tight junction proteins (2.3-fold increase vs controls, P<0.01)16 concurrent with attenuated HMGB1-mediated neuroinflammation,17 while comparative analysis by Partap et al13 further validates these findings through demonstrated superior neurocognitive outcomes in triple-therapy cohorts (methylprednisolone + methotrexate + cytarabine) versus monotherapy, collectively suggesting synergistic neuroprotection mechanisms. These multilayered findings suggest that corticosteroids modulate BBB permeability and neuroinflammatory cascades, providing a mechanistic rationale for their therapeutic efficacy in white matter preservation.

The long-term prognosis of ALL-associated leukoencephalopathy in this cohort appeared favorable. Longitudinal follow-up revealed neurological manifestations are transient in the majority of cases, with sequelae such as persistent symptomatic epilepsy observed in a small number of patients. Notably, clinical symptom resolution typically preceded neuroradiological normalization, demonstrating a paradoxical temporal dissociation between functional recovery and structural repair. Among the 48 patients, 62.50% (30/48) completed serial MRI surveillance, with 83.33% (25/30) exhibiting significant white matter lesion regression at the 12-month follow-up.

Neurocognitive assessments revealed distinct disease-specific impairments, with significant executive dysfunction observed across multiple domains including self-monitoring (p=0.048), emotional regulation (p=0.045), working memory (p=0.024), and organizational planning (p=0.047) compared to controls (Table 4), while global cognitive evaluation demonstrated reduction in Wechsler Full-Scale IQ (p=0.030) with particularly pronounced deficits in perceptual reasoning (p=0.037), processing speed (p=0.016), and working memory (p=0.027) domains (Table 5), collectively indicating substantial neurocognitive sequelae associated with ALL-related leukoencephalopathy. These quantitative findings substantiate the neurodevelopmental risks associated with chemotherapy-induced white matter injury, emphasizing the need for longitudinal neuropsychological surveillance of survivors.

While chemotherapeutic neurotoxicity (particularly methotrexate-induced leukoencephalopathy18) is a principal etiological factor, current research gaps persist regarding neurotoxic mechanisms, such as dose-response relationships across different chemotherapeutic agents; vulnerability factors, such as genetic polymorphisms in blood-brain barrier transporters and recovery trajectories, including neuroplasticity-mediated compensation patterns. The Krull cohort study19 (n=218) revealed preserved global cognition with domain-specific deficits in executive functioning, processing speed, and declarative memory. Notably, an elevated MTX AUC (Area under the curve) was correlated with frontostriatal pathway damage and dorsolateral prefrontal white matter tract degeneration, suggesting threshold-dependent neurotoxicity. Emerging evidence suggests that supratherapeutic methotrexate concentrations impair neural network efficiency and predispose to neurobehavioral sequelae, particularly attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) phenotypes.6 Beyond methotrexate, additional chemotherapeutic agents, including vincristine, L-asparaginase, and nelarabine, demonstrate distinct neurotoxic profiles;20 however, their specific contributions to white matter pathogenesis require systematic investigation.

Therapeutic context analysis revealed that 77.08% (37/48) of leukoencephalopathy cases occurred following intensive intrathecal chemotherapy (mean: 9.62 triple intrathecal injections), predominantly during the induction or consolidation phases in low-to-intermediate risk patients. This temporal clustering aligns with previous reports of heightened neurotoxicity associated with intrathecal methotrexate administration (vs intravenous route),13 suggesting route-dependent vulnerability mechanisms.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provided a comprehensive analysis of clinical characteristics and outcomes in pediatric ALL-associated leukoencephalopathy, revealing distinct neuroimaging signatures and heterogeneous clinical presentations accompanied by mild-to-moderate cognitive deficits. Systematic imaging surveillance proves crucial for early detection and therapeutic optimization. The prognosis appears favorable, with most patients showing symptom resolution and improved imaging findings.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations inherent in present study should be stated. First of all, the present study is a single-center retrospective study with a small sample size and limited representativeness. Further research with a larger sample size is needed. Thus, we had not conducted systematic studies on asymptomatic patients and were therefore unable to accurately determine their disease progression and prognosis. Future research should focus on asymptomatic patients. Third, we did not investigate the effects of the type, dose, and administration route of chemotherapy drugs on the incidence and prognosis of leukoencephalopathy, nor did we include the cumulative dose of methotrexate, a known causative agent, as a study variable. Future research should focus on the effects of the type, dose, and administration method of chemotherapy drugs on the disease.
