On the wake and flapping dynamics of different aspect ratio flags
Gaétan Raynaud, Karen Mulleners

Abstract
The flapping of flags is a classical problem involving fast and large amplitude deformations of a thin flexible plate and unsteady flow phenomena. We perform systematic time and space-resolved measurements of the deformation and drag acting on flapping flags for various aspect ratios and mass ratios. Bending waves travel from the root to the tip at a speed close to the incoming flow and the typical wavelength of the waves scales with the length of the flag. With smaller aspect ratio, the local dynamic pressure exerted by the fluid on the flag is reduced, lowering the wave propagation speed, and reducing the tip frequency. The effect of aspect ratio on the vortex formation in the near wake is analysed using flow field measurements. We identified two characteristic length scales, the ratio of the flag area over its perimeter L ∗ L^{*} and the square root of area H ​ L \sqrt{HL} that scale the circulation shed during a cycle. Changes in aspect ratio and mass ratio generate a wide scattering of the mean drag coefficient, ranging from 0 0 to 0.55 0.55 . We discuss a kinematic-based model for the mean drag coefficient. This model uses the mass ratio and the typical tip speed, which depends linearly on the wave speeds, to predict the mean drag coefficient without any fitting parameter.

1
Introduction

Flags are pieces of soft material used to display symbols, signals, or insignia and their typical waving in the wind makes them recognisable (Encyclopaedia Britannica,
Various 1910
).
The flapping flag problem and the various applications of flutter, the dynamic instability causing the motion of flags, have attracted research interest over many years.
Comprehensive reviews of the key contributions in this field are provided by
Shelley & Zhang 2011
and
Yu et al. 2019
.
Flutter designates a dynamic fluid-structure instability where small deformations of an object in a fluid flow grow in amplitude to a limit cycle oscillation with constant frequency
(
Paidoussis 2014
)
.
The onset of flutter occurs when the flow speed exceeds a critical flow velocity such that the restoring effects of bending stiffness and flow-induced tension can no longer balance the inertial response of the flag, and the flag extracts energy from the flow
(
Connell & Yue 2007
;
Shelley & Zhang 2011
)
.

The stability and the dynamics of flapping flags are governed by the interplay between inertia, bending rigidity, and fluid dynamic forces.
The relative strengths of these contributing factors are described by several non-dimensional parameters.
The main dimensionless parameters that are relevant here are the reduced velocity, the mass ratio, the aspect ratio, and the Reynolds number.
Following the notations of
Eloy et al. 2007
, these parameters are defined as:

U^{*}={\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}L\sqrt{\frac{{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}e}{D}},~M^{*}=\frac{{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{f}}L}{{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}e},~\frac{H}{L},\text{ and }Re=\frac{{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}L}{\nu},

(1)

for a flag of length
L
, height
H
, thickness
e
, density
{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}
, and bending rigidity
D
in an incoming fluid flow of velocity
{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
, fluid density
{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{f}}
, and kinematic viscosity
\nu
.

The reduced velocity
U^{*}
compares the characteristic period of free vibrations of a beam in vacuum
\sim L^{2}\sqrt{{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}e/D}
to the convective time of the surrounding flow
L/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}

(
Virot et al. 2013
)
.
The mass ratio
M^{*}
compares the magnitude of the fluid mass displaced during a flapping cycle of the flag
\sim{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{f}}HL^{2}
to the mass of the flag itself
{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}eLH
.
The mass ratio can also be seen as the scaling factor between the normalised pressure forces and the normalised acceleration of the flag in the spanwise-averaged momentum balance
(
Eloy et al. 2007
)
.
Large mass ratios correspond to flags in a dense fluid, or light flags.
The aspect ratio
H/L
compares the height to the length of a flag.
Flags in the limit of low aspect ratio
H/L\rightarrow 0
behave like ribbons
(
Lemaitre et al. 2005
)
.
Two-dimensional conditions are approximated by large aspect ratio flags
H/L\gg 1
and have been a classical simplification in the study of flag stability
(
Alben 2022
;
Connell & Yue 2007
;
Michelin et al. 2008
)
.

The stability boundary of the flag is marked by the critical reduced velocity.
Analytical models of flags have focused on predicting the critical reduced velocity as a function of mass ratio and aspect ratio.
The critical velocity is most sensitive to the mass ratio and the value of the mass ratio governs which flapping mode becomes unstable at the flapping onset
(
Yu et al. 2019
)
.
At low mass ratio (
M^{*}<\mathcal{O}(1)
), the critical reduced velocity
U^{*}
is a decreasing function of
M^{*}
.
For higher mass ratios (
M^{*}>1
), the critical reduced velocity settles around
U^{*}\sim$10$
and different branches described the evolution of
U^{*}
with
M^{*}
depending on the unstable flapping mode that is excited
(
Michelin et al. 2008
;
Eloy et al. 2008
)
.
The minimum values of the critical velocity for different branches are found for infinite aspect ratio flags.
Finite aspect ratio flags are subject to three-dimensional flow effects that reduce the pressure forces and delay the onset of flapping to higher reduced velocities.
The critical reduced velocity tends to increase with decreasing aspect ratio
H/L

(
Eloy et al. 2007
;
Kumar et al. 2024
)
.
Overall, analytical models tend to underestimate the critical velocity compared to experimental results
(
Yu et al. 2019
)
, and critical velocities obtained from experiments show some inherent fluctuations, some of which are due to planeity defects
(
Eloy et al. 2012
)
.

Flags maintain large amplitude flapping for reduced velocities beyond the onset velocity, which is referred to as the post-critical regime
(
Tang & Païdoussis 2007
)
.
The spatial characteristics of post-critical flapping are extracted from the flapping amplitude envelope.
The first person to experimentally study the amplitude envelope through stroboscopic imaging and to identify a travelling deformation wave propagating towards the tip
Taneda 1968
.
Depending on mass ratio, different flapping envelopes are observed for different flapping modes
(
Taneda 1968
)
.
Necks are used to describe the spatial evolution of the flapping envelope.
Necks are regions of the flag where the envelope has locally a smaller amplitude.
The number of necks along the flag increases with higher order flapping modes and change the bending distribution along the flag.
An accurate localisation of the high bending regions along the flag is important to design energy harvesters based on piezzo-electric patches, where the typical milli-Watt output power is relevant to supply wireless sensor networks
(
Doaré & Michelin 2011
;
Yu & Liu 2016
)
and for battery-less flow sensors
(
Liu et al. 2012
)
.

The main temporal characteristic of post-critical flapping is the flapping frequency.
The tip frequency
f
increases linearly with the inverse of the convective time
{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}/L

(
Yu et al. 2019
)
.
The ratio
fL/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
corresponds to the Strouhal number and typically ranges between
0.11
with several groups of values for each flapping mode
(
Taneda 1968
)
.
The tip velocity decreases, with lower dimensionless frequency
fL/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
, for decreasing aspect ratios at constant mass ratios
(
Huang & Sung 2010
;
Kumar et al. 2024
)
.
An explanation for the slowdown of the tip velocity is that pressure difference is reduced with lower aspect ratio flags due to flow structures at the edges, reducing the driving force of the flapping
(
Huang & Sung 2010
)
.
More details on the origin of the flapping time scales is relevant for precise diagnostics of respiratory wheezes
(
Gavriely et al. 1989
)
, orinasal snoring and sleep apnoea syndromes
(
Huang 1995
;
Aurégan & Depollier 1995
)
where the frequency of pressure waves is perceived as an audible sound.
Understanding the effect of aspect ratio and mass ratio on the flapping timescales also matters because human tissues come in a variety of shapes and thicknesses.

During the large amplitude flapping, vorticity is accumulated along the surface on both side of the flags and released alternatingly during the upstroke and downstroke motion of the tip
(
Kumar et al. 2021
)
.
Three-dimensional flow structures are characterised by an alternating release of horse-shoe shaped vortex tubes from the trailing edge, and the wake structures weaken with decreasing aspect ratio
(
Huang & Sung 2010
;
Kumar et al. 2024
)
.
The time-varying wake produced during flutter serves practical applications such as micro-scale mixers on lab-on-chip devices
(
Rips & Mittal 2019
)
, or low-Reynolds number heat dissipation systems
(
Shoele & Mittal 2014
)
.
The wake past a fluttering leaflet of an aortic valves prosthesis generates higher levels of shear stress compared to a non-fluttering leaflet, contributing to the formation of thrombus
(
Bornemann & Obrist 2025
)
.
Functional relationships between the shape properties and wake quantities would help design biomedical implants and mixing devices matching the desired flow properties.

A sudden increase in drag force is observed as the flag starts flapping
(
Taneda 1968
)
.
The origin of the drag increase has been associated with the dynamically-induced tension of the oscillating flag
(
Moretti 2003
)
, and with flow reorientation in the alternating wake
(
Müller 2003
;
Rips & Mittal 2019
)
.
Strong variations are observed in the mean and fluctuating drag coefficients and the efforts to model the drag have focused on quantities derived from the tip amplitude and frequency
(
Virot et al. 2013
)
.
Increased forces compared to a non-flapping flag is responsible for material damage, and can deteriorate the production quality in fast-operating industrial printing press
(
Watanabe et al. 2002
)
.
Overall, a better understanding of the interaction between flag deformation, wake parameters, and force would be desirable to better characterise the post-critical regime and its dependency with the flag shape.

In the present work, we study the effect of aspect ratio on the post-critical flapping of rectangular flags.
Variations in flapping timescales, tip speed, and wave propagation speeds are investigated through systematic measurements of deformation at and after the onset of flapping.
Flow measurements of the close wake show how these changes in timescales and speeds affect the formation of the vortex wake.
Mean drag data is measured and presented for various reduced velocities.
We discuss and test a predictive model for the mean drag coefficients based on the tip velocity.

Experimental methods

2.1
Rectangular flags

Rectangular flags were made out of white paper (PAPETERIA A4
80\text{\,}\mathrm{g}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-2}
).
Flags were designed with alignment holes to be clamped on a flag pole and held with the long paper side along the stream-wise direction (
fig.

1
a).
The rigidity of the A4 paper sheets in the long direction was measured using an elasto-gravity bending procedure, resulting in
D=Ee^{3}/12(1-{\nu}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}^{2})=$3.4(0.4)e-4\text{\,}\mathrm{N}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
where
E
and
{\nu}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}
are the Young modulus and the Poisson ratio.
A total of
48
shapes were cut using a desktop laser cutter with various heights
H
ranging from
4\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}19.6\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
and lengths
L
ranging from
10\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}20\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
(
fig.

1
b).
In this study, we consider the aspect ratios
$0.22$<H/L<$1.92$
, mass ratios
1.4<M^{*}={\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{f}}L/\left({\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}e\right)<2.8
, and the reduced velocities
7<U^{*}={\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}L\sqrt{{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}e/D}<31
.
For all the experiments, the Reynolds number
Re={\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}L/\nu
falls between
3\text{\times}{10}^{4}1.5\text{\times}{10}^{5}
.

2.2
Experimental set-up

The experiments are conducted in a wind tunnel with an open section of
45\text{\times}45\text{\,}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}
.
Flags are held vertically at the centre of the flow stream (
fig.

1
a).
For each flag, a measurement series consists of gradual steps of flow velocity
\Delta{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
.
The flow velocity is first increased from rest to the onset of flapping with
\Delta{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}=$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
.
Once flapping is reached, the flow velocity is decreased to capture the hysteresis with smaller steps of
\Delta{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}=-$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
.
The velocity at which the flag restabilises defines the offset velocity.
Then, the velocity is increased again to above the onset velocity to gather additional data in the post-critical regime with velocity increase of
\Delta{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}=$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
. At every flow velocity, the spatiotemporal deformation of the centreline is measured (
fig.

1
b).
A continuous laser pointer and a Powell lens generate a horizontal light sheet at the flag mid-height.
The reflection of the centreline of the flag is recorded with an event-based camera (Century Arks SilkyEvCam VGA) for a duration of
6\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
.
The planar deformations are reconstructed from the raw stream of events using an iterative spatial-clustering algorithm
(
Raynaud & Mulleners 2025
)
.
The reconstruction error for this methodology is of the order or below one percent of the flag length.
As output of the deformation reconstructions we obtain series of
x
and
y
coordinates sampled at
50
points uniformly distributed along the flag centreline (
fig.

1
c).
The reconstruction update rate of the output deformation is set during post-processing with a range of values between
1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}2\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
depending on the flapping frequency. A load cell and an analogue to digital converter (National Instruments NI-9215) gather drag data for a duration of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
, at a sampling rate of
{F}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}=$2\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}$
.
The load cell is manually calibrated over a range of
\pm$2\text{\,}\mathrm{N}$
.
The discretisation error from the
16
bit digital converter is lower than
0.7\text{\,}\mathrm{mN}
and the mean residual error is
10\text{\,}\mathrm{mN}
after calibration.
The total uncertainty for the mean drag force and mean drag coefficient is the sum of the discretisation error, the calibration uncertainty, and the repeatability of the mean drag value computed on independent segments of the recording, and has an average value of
16\text{\,}\mathrm{mN}
.

Particle image velocimetry

Flow data are gathered for a selection of flags of length

3
Results and discussion

The focus of this paper is the post-critical flapping regime which corresponds to the range of flow velocities beyond the critical velocity. First, we revise how the aspect ratio affects the critical velocity (
section

3.1
).
Then, we focus on the spatial properties of the flapping kinematics (
section

3.2
) before investigating the temporal properties and their variations with aspect ratio and mass ratio (
section

3.3
).
We further report variations of wake quantities and discuss scaling length to incorporate three-dimensional effects (
section

3.4
).
Finally, we focus on force data, and we combine the results from the flapping time scales to predict the mean drag coefficient during flapping (
section

3.5
).

Experimental values of critical reduced velocities are typically larger than the theoretical predictions and show inherent fluctuations

The reduced critical velocities from our experiment and from the three-dimensional model are driven by the two neck flutter regime and only weakly affected by aspect ratio and mass ratio.

(3)

The main spatial characteristics of the post-critical flag flapping are the amplitude envelope, the tip amplitude, and the wavelength of the travelling deformation wave.
All flags tested in this study show the same double-neck flapping mode in the post-critical regime.
The spatial characteristics non-dimensionalised by the length of the flag are independent of the aspect ratio, and are predominantly governed by the mass ratio.
An increase in the mass ratio leads to a shift in the necks toward the tip and a decrease in the nondimensional tip amplitude.
The dimensionless wavelength remains approximately constant and only slightly decreases for the lowest mass ratios tested.
Next, we will focus on how the aspect ratio and mass ratio affect the temporal characteristics of the deformation speed, including the wave speed, oscillation frequency, and Strouhal numbers.

3.3
Temporal characteristics of the deformation waves

The results of the extracted wave speeds from the spatial-correlation delay plots (
fig.

4
c) are presented in
fig.

5
a for flags of different aspect ratio, at different mass ratios, and reduced velocities in the post-critical regime (
18<U^{*}<22
).
The dimensionless wave speed is of the order of
1
, with a minimum value of
c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}=$0.61$
and a maximum value of
c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}=$1.07$
for our dataset.
For individual flags, the standard deviation of
c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
for measurements at different flow velocities is lower than
5\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
.
The constant ratio
c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
per flag shows that the dimensional wave speed
c
scales linearly with the external flow velocity
{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
and the external flow drives the propagation of the wave along the flag.
Dimensionless wave speeds
c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}>1
are observed because the wave speed is measured along the curvilinear abscissa of the flag and the motion travels faster in curvilinear coordinates than in the streamwise direction.
Dimensionless wave speed increases both with aspect ratio and mass ratio.
Waves travel faster for taller flags when we fix the mass ratio.
The dimensionless wave speed
c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
starts from
0.61
at
H/L=0.5
and increases to
c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}=$1.04$
when the aspect ratio reaches
H/L=1.92
for the example case of
M^{*}=$1.4$
.
The wave speed also increases with mass ratio when we fix the aspect ratio.
Higher mass ratio flapping flags has less inertia, and resist less to the driving force of the flow.
Analytical models often assume that deformations and the associated flow structures are convected at the same speed as the external flow
(
Argentina & Mahadevan 2005
)
.

Shelley et al. 2005
also observed a linear increase of the wave speed with flow velocity.
They found
c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}\approx$0.66$
, which is on the lower side of our data range.
The value
c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}\approx$0.66$
was obtained for a flag of aspect ratio
H/L=$1.46$
and mass ratio
M^{*}=$19.8$
, which lies outside our data range (
1.4<M^{*}<2.8
) and suggests a different flutter regime with a smaller dimensionless wavelength (
\lambda/L\sim 0.5
).

For our dataset, the hypothesis
c={\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
is valid for light or tall flags, but not for heavy or short flags.
A deflection of the flag starting near the root will be pushed towards the tip by the local dynamic pressure exerted by the air flow on the flag.
As the air will try to follow the path of the least resistance, the dynamic pressure will be reduced for flags with shorter heights, as more of the flow can pass around the top or bottom edges when the flag is deflected.
A lower dynamic pressure results in a lower wave speed.

Here, we explore further this hypothesis that decreasing the aspect ratio leads to a reduction in the dynamic pressure experienced by the flag and that the decrease in dynamic pressure is responsible for slowing down the wave propagation.
From the measured kinematics of the flag, we can directly calculate an estimate of the spanwise averaged pressure difference across the flag,
{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}
, according to the model proposed by
Eloy et al. 2007
:

{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}(\tilde{s},\tilde{t})=\frac{1}{M^{*}}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{y}(\tilde{s},\tilde{t})}{\partial\tilde{t}^{2}}+\frac{1}{U^{*2}}\frac{\partial^{4}\tilde{y}(\tilde{s},\tilde{t})}{\partial\tilde{x}^{4}}\right]\quad,

(4)

where
{<.>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}
stands for spanwise averaged quantities, and
\tilde{.}
stands for dimensionless quantities:

\tilde{y}=\frac{y}{L},~\tilde{s}=\frac{s}{L},~\tilde{x}=\frac{x}{L},~\tilde{t}=\frac{t{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}}{L},~\tilde{p}=\frac{p}{{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{f}}{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}^{2}}\quad.

(5)

Equation

4
is the result of the linearised conservation of transverse momentum equation (more details on the derivation are included in appendix
appendix

B
).
To obtain a single representative value of the pressure difference per flapping flag at a given
U^{*}
, we compute the overall euclidean norm of the calculated
{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}(\tilde{s},\tilde{t})
over both
\tilde{s}
and
\tilde{t}
.
We denote this overall dynamic pressure difference by
\left|{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}\right|_{s,t}
and show the results for our entire dataset in
fig.

5
b.
The estimated overall dynamic pressure difference increases with increasing aspect ratio and decreasing mass ratio.
It is more sensitive to variations in aspect ratio than in mass ratio.

Our data is compared in
fig.

5
b to predictions by
Eloy et al. 2007
and
Argentina & Mahadevan 2005
.
The predictions by
Eloy et al. 2007
stem from a linear stability analysis of the three-dimensional potential flow around a flag of finite height in the Fourier space.
This approach yields a single spanwise-averaged pressure difference as a function of the normalised streamwise wave number
{\bar{k}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}=\pi H/\lambda
, which they fitted by

{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}\left({\bar{k}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}\right)~\approx~1-\frac{1}{\displaystyle 2\pi\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{H}{L}+\exp{\left(\left(\pi/4-2 \right)\pi\frac{L}{\lambda}\frac{H}{L} \right)}}\quad.

(6)

The fit is shown in
fig.

5
b by the dashed line.

Argentina & Mahadevan 2005
followed a different approach to determine three-dimensional or aspect ratio effect on pressure.
They numerically calculate a correction factor for the pressure
A
, which is the ratio of the spanwise-averaged non-circulatory potential of a plate of finite height and the non-circulatory potential of an infinite-height plate facing a constant flow.
We extract the values of
A(H/L)
from figure 5b in
Argentina & Mahadevan 2005
and include it in
fig.

5
b for further comparison.
Our experimental data is in good agreement with the theoretical prediction by
Eloy et al. 2007
and the numerically obtained correction factor by
Argentina & Mahadevan 2005
.
Our pressure estimates lie between the values predicted by both models for low and high aspect ratios and is smaller in the intermediate region
0.5<H/L<1.5
.
The agreement between the two pressure models and our data support the argument that edge effects induce a reduction in the overall pressure difference during flapping.

Now, we want to link the pressure to the wave speed.
Based on the comparison of the order of magnitude of the terms in the momentum equation, which we discuss in
appendix

B
, we obtain the following scaling for the dimensionless wave speed:

c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}\propto\sqrt{M^{*}{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}}\quad.

(7)

This scaling indicates that there is a contribution of the mass ratio and the dynamic pressure difference on the wave speed.
This scaling confirms our experimental observation of increasing wave speed with increasing mass ratio (
fig.

5

fig.

5
b).

The wave propagation speed is directly linked to the flapping frequency
c=\lambda f
.
The flapping frequency is typically non-dimensionalised using a characteristic length scale and the flow velocity to form a Strouhal number.
In literature, both the tip to tip amplitude
2A
and the flag length

are used to define a Strouhal number.
In

.
We compare our data in

Huang & Sung 2010
, who also observed an increase of

with aspect ratio.
The values of
{St}_{\scalebox{0.65}{A}}
from the simulations are lower in magnitude due to a different flapping regime (

and

corresponding to the single neck flutter).

In

against aspect ratio for all our data.
The dimensionless tip frequency

increases with aspect ratio, and to a lesser extent with mass ratio for our tested flags, which are all in the double-neck flutter regime.
On the lower side,

On the higher side,

Experimental values of

Overall, the magnitude of the dimensionless tip-frequency depends on the flutter mode, and increases with aspect ratio within a flutter regime.

The relationship between tip frequency and wave speed can be rewritten as

\frac{c}{{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}}=\frac{\lambda}{L}\times\frac{fL}{{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}}.

(8)

In

For our dataset,

indeed increases approximately linearly with

, with a slope equal to

.
The range of length-based Strouhal number within a given flutter regime is explained by variations in the wave propagation speed.
Larger variations in the length-based Strouhal number are associated with variations in the flutter regime.
For a flutter regime with more necks and a lower wavelength, we expect higher length-based Strouhal numbers.

We highlight the topology of the vortex street in the wake using the negative finite time Lyapunov exponent (nFTLE)

As taller flags flap faster, the wake wavelength

The area-to-perimeter ratio

The square root of area

The square root of area is also more robust in the case of fractal objects in steady motions where the perimeter to area ratio can become extremely large without having a measurable influence on the flow properties

3.5
Link between mean drag coefficient and flapping dynamics

The mean drag force
{\bar{F}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}
at the root of the flag is measured systematically for different flow velocities.
We take the example of three flags of equal area to analyse how the drag varies with flow velocity (
fig.

8
a).
The mean drag is close to zero before the flapping onset as the only contribution to drag comes from the friction drag.
A jump to a significant positive mean drag value occurs after the flapping onset when the pressure drag increase due to the flapping.
With increasing flow velocity, the mean drag increases following a quadratic increase with flow velocity as we expect for bluff bodies (
fig.

8
a).
The mean drag significantly differs between the three flags of same area when compared at the same flow velocity.
The three flags have different lengths, so their flapping onset occurs at a different flow velocity.
The short flag starts flapping before the tall flag such that the drag of the short flag is higher around
{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}=$7\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
.
When the tallest flag finally starts flapping, the mean drag increases faster with

than for the other flags and overtakes the values of the other flags for

The mean drag coefficient

(9)

of the three example flags undergoes a transition from

for

to

for

(

The individual values of mean drag coefficient for the

with no clear relationship with the reduced velocity

(

The absence of clear trend suggests that the choice of typical area or velocity are not the relevant scales to predict the mean force acting on the flag.
The projected area

At fixed reduced velocity
U^{*}
, the mean drag coefficient
{\bar{C}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}
tends to increase with aspect ratio.
The scattering of mean drag coefficient suggests that the typical aerodynamic force normalisation with the surface area and the square of the flow velocity fails to scale the mean drag of flapping flags.

To explain the distribution of mean drag coefficient, we revisit the work of
Thoma 1939
and derive a semi-empirical prediction using the measured flag tip velocity.

Thoma 1939
presented a model for a fully flexible rope oscillating under the action of normal forces and derived a two-dimensional estimation of the mean tension at the root of the rope.
The mean drag per unit height was derived as

{\bar{F}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}=\frac{1}{2}m{\overline{v^{2}}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{tip}}\quad,

(10)

where
\overline{.}
stands for a time-averaged quantity, and
m={\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}eH
is the mass per unit length of the rope.
For flags,
{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}eHL
is the total mass.
The right-hand side of
eq.

10
represents the tension induced by the in-plane kinetic energy of the flag.
In dimensionless quantities,
eq.

10
can be formulated as

{\bar{C}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}=\displaystyle\frac{\frac{1}{2}{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}eH{\overline{v^{2}}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{tip}}}{\frac{1}{2}{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{f}}HL{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}^{2}}=\frac{{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{s}}e}{{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{f}}L}\times\frac{{\overline{v^{2}}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{tip}}}{{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}^{2}}\quad,

(11)

which can be rewritten as

{\bar{C}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}=\frac{1}{M^{*}}\frac{{\overline{v^{2}}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{tip}}}{{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}^{2}}\quad.

(12)

The mean drag coefficient is proportional to the ratio of the mean square tip velocity and the incoming flow velocity squared with the mass ratio as the proportionality factor.
In
fig.

8
d, we combine the deformation measurement
{v}_{\scalebox{0.65}{tip}}
and the load cell data
{\bar{C}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}
.
The two-dimensional kinematic model of
eq.

12
describes our data well.
The influence of the aspect ratio on the mean drag coefficient is indirectly included in the form of the reduced tip speed.

Following
Moretti 2003
, we can simplify the tip speed by assuming that the tip displacement is described by
{y}_{\scalebox{0.65}{tip}}=-A\cos{2\pi f t}
.
The simplified tip velocity takes the form
\overline{{v}_{\scalebox{0.65}{tip}}^{2}}=2\pi^{2}f^{2}A^{2}
.
The mean drag coefficient estimate can be rewritten as

{\bar{C}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\times\frac{{St}_{\scalebox{0.65}{A}}^{2}}{M^{*}}\quad,

(13)

where the amplitude-based Strouhal number
{St}_{\scalebox{0.65}{A}}=2fA/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
, shown in
fig.

5
c, accounts for the increasing tip speed with increasing aspect ratio.
In
fig.

8
e, we show the measured mean drag coefficient against the simplified estimate based on
{St}_{\scalebox{0.65}{A}}
from
eq.

13
.
The average discrepancies between the model and the measured data
\left|{\bar{C}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}-(\pi^{2}/2)\times{St}_{\scalebox{0.65}{A}}^{2}/M^{*}\right|
is
0.023
.
The simplified model is more accurate than the model using the direct tip velocity (
eq.

12

\left|{\bar{C}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}-{v}_{\scalebox{0.65}{tip}}^{2}/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}^{2}M^{*}\right|
of
0.039
.
Overall, an increase in aspect ratio induces an increase in tip speed (
fig.

4
Conclusion

We report systematic experimental measurements of rectangular flags in the post-critical flapping regime.
Finite height flags, with lower aspect ratios, display different kinematics than quasi two-dimensional flags of large aspect ratio. The dimensionless flapping frequency, wave speed, and mean drag coefficient decrease with decreasing aspect ratio.
Edge effects reduce the dynamic pressure difference across the flag leading to a lower wave speed.
The constant dimensionless wave length for a given flapping regime induces a reduction of flapping frequency and tip speed.
With lower tip speed and stronger edge effects, less vorticity is released in the wake and vortices do not roll up any more.
The typical length scale to predict the reduction in released circulation during half a stroke should include the height to account for the three-dimensional nature of the wake.
The ratio of the area over the perimeter
L^{*}
, and the square root of area
\sqrt{LH}
include both the information of the height and the length and are suitable scale parameters for the circulation of different aspect ratio flags.
We measure a spread in the mean drag coefficient for flags of different aspect ratios and mass ratios.
We provide a systematic verification of a kinematic model based on the tip velocity squared and the inverse of the mass ratio and show a good agreement for
{\bar{C}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{x}}
ranging from
00.55
.

This paper provides a better connection between the deformation, flow, and force characteristics of flags flapping of different aspect ratio in the post-critical regime.
Systematic high temporally and spatially resolved deformation data, unlocked by event-based imaging, give access to precise extraction of wave propagation speeds, wave lengths, and tip speed motions for a variety of flag aspect ratios, mass ratios, and reduced velocities.
Simultaneous deformation and force measurements for
48
different flags at various reduced velocities allowed us to validate analytical drag estimations based on the tip velocity.
Future work could investigate the relationship between time-varying forces, three-dimensional flow fields, and deformations of more complex flag shapes.

Appendix B
Pressure estimates

In this section, we describe the computation of the pressure estimate and we derive a relationship between pressure and wave speed.
We follow the model from
Eloy et al. 2007
.
This model is linear and two-dimensional in terms of deformations, it assumes that the motion of the centreline
y
is small (
y\ll L
) and that it does not vary with
z
.
The model also assumes that the motion of the flag is only driven by normal pressure forces.
With spanwise invariance of the motion, the local momentum equation is averaged over the span
-H/2<z<H/2
.
The spanwise-averaged momentum balance of the linear model writes as

\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{y}}{\partial\tilde{t}^{2}}+\frac{1}{U^{*2}}\frac{\partial^{4}\tilde{y}}{\partial\tilde{x}^{4}}=M^{*}{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}},

(15)

where the
\tilde{~}
stands for dimensionless quantities normalised by
L
,
{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
and
{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{f}}
:

\tilde{y}=\frac{y}{L},~\tilde{x}=\frac{x}{L},~\tilde{t}=\frac{t{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}}{L},~\tilde{p}=\frac{p}{{\rho}_{\scalebox{0.65}{f}}{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}^{2}}.

(16)

We numerically differentiate our time and space resolved data
\tilde{y}(\tilde{s},\tilde{t})
and use the small deformation approximation
\tilde{s}\approx\tilde{x}
to replace
\tilde{y}(\tilde{x},\tilde{t})
by
\tilde{y}(\tilde{s},\tilde{t})
.
The second derivative in time and fourth derivative in space provide the left hand side of
eq.

15
, which is equal to the spanwise-averaged pressure estimate
{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}
multiplied by the mass ratio
M^{*}
on the right hand side.
The spanwise-averaged pressure estimate
{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}
is a space-time field of the
\tilde{s}
and
\tilde{t}
variables.
To compare different measurement time series, we take the euclidean norm in space and time over the recorded duration
{T}_{\scalebox{0.65}{r}}
:

\|<\tilde{p}>_{z}\|_{s,t}=\left(\frac{L}{{T}_{\scalebox{0.65}{r}}{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}}\int_{\tilde{t}=0}^{\tilde{t}={T}_{\scalebox{0.65}{r}}{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}/L}\int_{\tilde{s}=0}^{\tilde{s}=1}<\tilde{p}>^{2}_{z}d\tilde{s}d\tilde{t}\right)^{1/2}.

(17)

Figure 9:
Details on the phase-averaged mode coefficients corresponding to the three first cantilevered beam mode shapes.

We simplify
eq.

15
to highlight the link between dynamic pressure and wave speed.
We follow the approach of
Moretti 2003
that consists of (1) using an analytical approximation for the deformation and (2) neglecting the bending term of the momentum equation.
The analytical approximation for the deformation
\tilde{y}
is a sinusoidal travelling wave modulated by a space-varying amplitude

\tilde{y}(\tilde{s},\tilde{t})=\frac{A\tilde{x}}{L}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tilde{\lambda}}(\tilde{c}\tilde{t}-\tilde{x})\right),

(18)

where
A
is the tip amplitude,
\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda/L
is the dimensionless wavelength and
\tilde{c}=c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}
is the dimensionless wave speed.
The simplification of a linear amplitude growth in
eq.

18
is justified by
Moretti 2003
citing the envelope data from
Watanabe et al. 2002
.
The linear approximation does not reproduce the two dips around the necks compared to the amplitude growth data presented in
fig.

3
c and e, but this simplification captures the overall behavior of the increasing envelope from root to tip.
The expression for the deformation of
eq.

18
is injected in the left hand side of momentumm balance of
eq.

15
.
The bending stiffness is neglected, which corresponds to high
U^{*}
so that the term
\frac{1}{U^{*2}}\frac{\partial^{4}\tilde{y}}{\partial\tilde{x}^{4}}
is neglected compared to the acceleration
\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{y}}{\partial\tilde{t}^{2}}
in
eq.

15
.
The momentum balance writes as

\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{y}}{\partial\tilde{t}^{2}}=-\frac{A\tilde{x}}{L}\left(\frac{2\pi\tilde{c}}{\tilde{\lambda}}\right)^{2}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tilde{\lambda}}(\tilde{c}\tilde{t}-\tilde{x})\right)=M^{*}{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}

(19)

for which we take the euclidean norm over space and the recorded duration

\frac{A}{L}\left(\frac{2\pi\tilde{c}}{\tilde{\lambda}}\right)^{2}\norm{ \tilde{x} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tilde{\lambda}}(\tilde{c}\tilde{t}-\tilde{x})\right) }_{s,t}=M^{*}\norm{ \kindex{<\tilde{p}>}{z} }_{s,t}.

(20)

The right hand side is our pressure estimate introduced in
eq.

17
and the left hand side contains the properties of the wave approximation.
The norm of the
\norm{ \tilde{x} \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tilde{\lambda}}(\tilde{c}\tilde{t}-\tilde{x})\right) }_{s,t}
does depend of
\tilde{\lambda}
but is overall of the order of unity.
We rewrite
eq.

20
using the dimensional values and taking the norm of the envelope times the sine as a constant factor:

\frac{1}{M^{*}}\left(\frac{c}{{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}}\right)^{2}\frac{A}{L}\propto\left(\frac{\lambda}{L}\right)^{2}{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}.

(21)

The constant dimensionless wavelength
\lambda/L=$1.87$
and neglecting the tip amplitude variations (assuming
A/L\approx 0.3
) simplifies this relationship into:

c/{\textrm{U}}_{\scalebox{0.65}{$\infty$}}\propto\sqrt{M^{*}{<\tilde{p}>}_{\scalebox{0.65}{z}}}.

(22)

Overall, the dimensionless wave speed varies as the square root of the mass ratio times the square root of the dynamic pressure and the dynamic pressure is weakened by lower aspect ratio flags as predicted in analytical models.
