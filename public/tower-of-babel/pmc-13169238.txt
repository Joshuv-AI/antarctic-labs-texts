A Facile Strategy for 3′‐Terminal Functionalization of DNA and RNA via On‐Column Conjugation with Integration into Tandem Oligonucleotide Synthesis
Authors: Jagandeep S. Saraya, Derek K. O'Flaherty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmino‐functionalized oligonucleotides are valuable tools in therapeutics, biosensing, and nanotechnology. Although 5′‐terminal modifications are readily accessible, incorporation at the 3′‐terminus remains more challenging and often requires pre‐functionalized solid supports or specialized reagents. To address these limitations, we developed a bis(hydroxymethyl)malonyl CPG (CPR II CPG) solid support that can be activated with N,N′‐disuccinimidyl carbonate (DSC) to generate a carbamate‐forming platform. Using this support, we established an on‐support bis‐carbamate construction strategy that enables efficient incorporation of amino modifiers in high yields and crude purities. Furthermore, by leveraging a trifunctional linker (1‐amino‐3‐(((4‐methoxyphenyl)diphenylmethyl)amino)propan‐2‐ol), we developed a modular approach in which one amine is tethered to the solid support while the second amine can be selectively detritylated and conjugated to diverse ligands. The free hydroxyl group can then be used to extend an oligonucleotide through automated solid‐phase synthesis. This strategy accommodates a broad range of ligands through multiple conjugation chemistries, including NHS esters/carbonates and EDC‐mediated amide bond formation, with good conversions. Finally, we describe a tandem oligonucleotide synthesis (TOS) protocol for generating strands of DNA bearing ligands at defined terminal positions, enabling rapid access to highly modified oligonucleotides. All oligonucleotide conjugates were purified using standard workflows, including strong anion exchange high‐performance liquid chromatography (SAX‐HPLC), and characterized by mass spectrometry (MS). © 2026 The Author(s). Introduction

Chemical modification is a central strategy for tailoring oligonucleotide function for therapeutics, biosensing, and nanotechnology (Egli & Manoharan, 2023; Jung et al., 2016; Lönnberg, 2009; Madsen & Gothelf, 2019). Among available functionalities, amino groups are particularly versatile, enabling site‐specific conjugation of a wide range of ligands and facilitating chemistries relevant to nonenzymatic genome replication (Chai et al., 2021; Franzini et al., 2015; Izgu et al., 2016; Li & Huang, 2018; O'Flaherty et al., 2019; Saraya, Horton, Sammons, et al., 2025; Shah et al., 1996; Shah et al., 2018; Willems et al., 2024; Yuen et al., 2020; Zhang, Blain, et al., 2013; Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2019, 2020b, 2020a). The recent approval of several small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapeutics bearing N‐acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) conjugates has demonstrated the clinical importance of oligonucleotide conjugates (Egli & Manoharan, 2023).

Currently, amino functionalities can be introduced internally or at the 5′‐ and 3′‐termini of oligonucleotides (Aigner et al., 2011; Guzaev & Pon, 2013; Kundu et al., 2022; Redman et al., 2023; Salo et al., 1999; Zatsepin et al., 2005). Whereas 5′‐modification is straightforward under conventional 3′→5′ solid‐phase synthesis, installation at the 3′‐terminus is more demanding and typically requires pre‐functionalized solid supports. Reverse (5′→3′) synthesis offers an alternative approach but often depends on specialized phosphoramidites and costly reagents, making it less accessible to low resource laboratories (Claeboe et al., 2003; Lelyveld et al., 2019; Zielinska et al., 2006). Although numerous amino‐bearing supports have been developed, many involve laborious multistep preparation, thereby limiting versatility and on‐column conjugation (Asseline & Thuong, 1990; Aviñó et al., 1996; Eisenhuth & Richert, 2009; McMinn & Greenberg, 1996).

In hopes of addressing these limitations, we previously reported a commercially available sulfone‐containing solid support that enables the rapid conjugation of diverse amino modifiers (Fig. 1A; Saraya, Horton, Sammons, et al., 2025). Notably, this system accommodates both primary and secondary amines under aqueous or organic conditions. However, the sulfone linker is susceptible to base‐mediated cleavage, requiring short coupling times with aliphatic amines to minimize immolation via β‐elimination. In particular, on‐solid‐support construction of certain bis‐amino modifiers (such as putrescine) proved challenging and required individual optimization of reaction time, concentration, and washing conditions due to linker degradation. Specifically, we observed that even at a reduced concentration of 50 mM putrescine (compared to the standard 100 mM), 10 min of coupling led to significant byproduct formation and concomitant sulfonyl immolation.

(A) Previously developed sulfonyl‐based solid support strategy for the incorporation of amino modifiers ( S ‐CPG). In some implementations, S ‐CPG can also be conjugated to the solid support through a carbamate linkage. (B) This work focuses on the use of CPR II CPG to incorporate amino modifiers via initial activation with N,N′‐disuccinimidyl carbonate (DSC), generating R ‐CPG. A trifunctional ligand (protected 1,3‐diamino‐2‐propanol) enables anchoring to the solid support, followed by selective conjugation at the amine and subsequent strand elongation from the hydroxyl group.

We explored whether bis(hydroxymethyl)malonyl‐based controlled‐pore glass (a so‐called chemical phosphorylating reagent II, or CPR II CPG) could serve as a more stable platform for the incorporation of amino modifiers via carbamate linkages, while remaining compatible with standard deprotection and cleavage conditions (Fig. 1B). CPR II CPG offers a key advantage over sulfonyl linkers in that its proposed immolation mechanism involves saponification of the ester and then a retro‐aldol reaction followed by β‐elimination, resulting in decarboxylative release of the amine; this mechanism may improve stability under amino‐modifier coupling conditions (Guzaev et al., 1995; Horn & Urdea, 1986). Another advantage of this approach is the ability to perform orthogonal on‐column functionalization. Importantly, CPR II CPG is compatible with diethylamine treatment, which may be useful in removing electrophilic species capable of reacting with strongly nucleophilic amines (Glen Research, 2009). Such side reactions, including transamidation, have been persistent challenges during the synthesis of amino‐modified oligonucleotides in our hands.

Here, we describe a bis‐carbamate strategy for incorporation of amino modifiers on a base‐labile solid support (Basic Protocol 1). Using this robust platform, we outline a trimodular approach based on tritylated 1,3‐diamino‐2‐propanol to anchor one amine to the support, selectively deprotect the other amino group, and conjugate it with ligands through various conjugation chemistries directly on a solid support (Basic Protocol 2; Alternate Protocols 1 and 2). The resulting free hydroxyl group is subsequently subjected to automated oligonucleotide solid‐phase synthesis (SPS) to assemble DNA and RNA conjugates (that are amenable further post‐synthetic conjugation). Finally, we describe a tandem oligonucleotide synthesis strategy that enables preparation of two (complementary) strands, each bearing a ligand of choice (at each terminal ends), to generate highly modified duplexes (Basic Protocol 3).

SYNTHESIS OF R‐CPG AND INCORPORATION OF BIS‐AMINO MODIFIERS SUCH AS PUTRESCINE)

The synthesis of amino modifiers on a solid support can be achieved using standard N,N′‐disuccinimidyl carbonate (DSC) chemistry. Our process begins with the reaction of 5′‐O‐(4,4′‐dimethoxytrityl)‐thymidine (DMTr‐T) with an excess of DSC dissolved in acetonitrile, in the presence of triethylamine. Upon completion, a standard aqueous workup affords the crude mixed‐carbonate intermediate (1), which can be used directly in the next step without further purification (Fig. 2A). Analogously, CPR II CPG is detritylated on an automated nucleic acid synthesizer and then coupled with a saturated solution of DSC in 1:1 (v/v) ACN/pyridine for 2 hr to generate the activated R ‐CPG (Fig. 2B). R ‐CPG is subsequently conjugated with the bis‐amino modifier (in this case, putrescine), washed, and coupled with DMTr‐thymidine succinimide 1. The resulting solid support is thoroughly washed and capped on the synthesizer, and is then ready for strand elongation (Fig. 2C).

(C) On‐solid‐support construction of a putrescine carbamate‐linked nucleoside, which can be used for strand elongation subsequently. SYNTHESIS OF MMTr‐1,3‐DIAMINO‐2‐PROPANOL (2), R‐CPG FUNCTIONALIZATION, DERIVATIZATION, AND DNA STRAND EXTENSION

The previously activated R ‐CPG can be used to conjugate a variety of amino handles, which can themselves be further derivatized. 1,3‐Diamino‐2‐propanol serves as a versatile scaffold: one amine may be tethered to R ‐CPG, the second may be conjugated using diverse chemistries, and the free hydroxyl may serve as a starting point for conventional SPS. To begin, 1,3‐diamino‐2‐propanol is protected with a 4‐methoxytrityl (MMTr) group by dissolution in acetonitrile with excess DBU, followed by the addition of MMTr‐Cl. The resulting product is concentrated, subjected to standard aqueous workup, and purified by standard flash column chromatography (Fig. 5A). The purified compound is then conjugated to R ‐CPG, detritylated, and reacted with an NHS‐ester ligand (e.g., propargyl, Fig. 5B). After extensive washing, the DNA strand is elongated, purified by SAX‐HPLC, and confirmed by LR‐ESI‐MS. Notably, these steps follow the conventional workflow, tolerate moisture and require no specialized instrumentation, making this strategy broadly accessible for oligonucleotide derivatization.

CONJUGATIONAL TANDEM OLIGONUCLEOTIDE SYNTHESIS (cTOS) OF HIGHLY MODIFIED DNA DUPLEXES

The previously developed strategy for synthesizing high‐purity, high‐yield oligonucleotide conjugates can be extended to tandem oligonucleotide synthesis (TOS). In this approach, the two strands are separated by a base‐labile sulfonyl linker, which, upon standard deprotection, produces two separate oligonucleotides with terminal phosphates. Before cleavage, the terminal hydroxyl of the second strand can be derivatized via DSC‐mediated carbamate chemistry with a desired moiety. In this example, an azide‐containing amino compound was used as the ligand to generate a highly modified DNA duplex (Fig. 14A). The resulting mixture of strands can be analyzed by SAX‐HPLC, purified, and characterized using LR‐ESI‐MS.

The previously derivatized solid support can be employed in tandem oligonucleotide synthesis of multiple conjugates (cTOS). In this application, a duplex strand was selected for synthesis with a C12 tag conjugated to the solid support, as described above. The terminal hydroxyl was then conjugated to amine‐PEG3‐azide under aqueous‐compatible conditions to generate a highly modified duplex (A). In this approach, R ‐CPG was first functionalized with lauric acid‐OSu, and this was followed by extension of the first duplex strand. A base‐labile sulfonyl amidite ( X ‐amidite) was then incorporated before extension of the second strand. COMMENTARY

Background Information

Solid‐support functionalization reactions are of particular interest to our research group and others because of their well‐documented advantages, including high yields and ease of reagent, byproduct, and impurity removal. The solid support acts as a stable scaffold that enables a wide range of chemistries to be performed sequentially, with intermediate washing steps between each reaction, provided that the support remains inert under the reaction conditions. Importantly, it is unnecessary to isolate or purify each intermediate, saving time, reagents (e.g., those used for purification), and overall cost.

In this context, we have developed multiple methods to tether amino modifiers to solid support, initially using a base‐labile support and subsequently a reduction‐labile support (Saraya, Horton, Capperauld, et al., 2025; Saraya, Horton, Sammons, et al., 2025). However, incorporation of certain bis‐amino linkers onto the base‐labile support frequently resulted in significant impurities and degradation. Although using a disulfide‐containing solid support can circumvent this, deprotection issues with redox‐active ligands such as azides made this approach limiting for some applications. We therefore explored a more robust, yet still base‐labile, malonyl‐based support (CPR II CPG, Fig. 1). Using this support, putrescine incorporation was achieved efficiently without major issues in yield or crude purity, a feature not shared when using S ‐CPG (Fig. 1).

We next explored whether 1,3‐diamino‐2‐propanol could serve as a versatile 3′‐conjugator. Initial conjugation trials with the unprotected molecule resulted in undesired crosslinking. To address this, the compound was first monotritylated, conjugated to R ‐CPG, and subsequently subjected to diverse chemistries at the free amine after detritylation on instrument. This approach enables the synthesis of libraries of 3′‐conjugates that are not commercially available. Although derivatization of solid supports has been reported for a variety of ligands, these procedures are often laborious and time consuming, limiting systematic studies of ligand diversity (Eisenhuth & Richert, 2009; Kiviniemi et al., 2012; Müggenburg et al., 2021). In this work, our on‐column approach provides a fast and cost‐effective means of accessing oligonucleotides functionalized with various handles, including propargyl groups, aliphatic handles, PEGylated groups, azides, amino acids such as Lys, Val, and Gly, aromatic handles such as 4‐bromobenzoic acid, as well as acetyl groups and protecting groups including Boc, Cbz, and Alloc (Saraya & O'Flaherty, 2026). Our conjugation strategy provides a rapid and efficient route to access a wide variety of 3′‐conjugates. One potential application is the systematic investigation of fatty acid chain length on coacervate stability or localization onto vesicular systems, a project that would particularly benefit from on‐support diversification.

Building on this strategy, we investigated whether it could be applied to the synthesis of highly modified duplexes. Recent successes using the sulfonyl‐containing amidite ( X ‐amidite) as a cleavable linker have enabled various TOS applications, such as amino‐TOS and modular‐TOS (Saraya, Horton, Capperauld, et al., 2025; Saraya, Horton, Sammons, et al., 2025; Saraya & O'Flaherty, 2024). To the best of our knowledge, published reports on the tandem synthesis of highly modified complementary or non‐complementary strands remain limited. We hypothesized that our methodology, which enables the synthesis of diverse 3′‐conjugates, could be extended by incorporating the X ‐amidite and subsequently conjugated at the 5′‐position of the upstream strand. To improve accessibility and broader applicability, the conjugation chemistry at the 5′‐position is performed on the free hydroxyl, as canonical DNA phosphoramidites are widely available and relatively inexpensive. We hypothesized that conjugation of the terminal hydroxyl via DSC‐mediated carbamate chemistry would produce duplexes bearing two points of functionalization, one at the 3′‐end and the other at the 5′‐end. Furthermore, the free amine on the downstream strand could be derivatized in solution to introduce an additional layer of structural complexity. Given the growing importance of highly modified oligonucleotides in clinical and biotechnological applications, this cTOS approach became a central focus of our investigation (Egli & Manoharan, 2023). As an example, we focused on the synthesis of a duplex containing a hydrophobic tail on one strand and an azide modifier on the other. This architecture enables orthogonal click reactions on one strand, while the hydrophobic tail on the complementary strand may facilitate localization to membranes in vivo.

Critical Parameters

Thorough washing of the solid support is essential at every stage of the procedure. For instance, complete removal of residual water before solid‐phase synthesis (SPS) is essential, as trace moisture can reduce coupling efficiency and promote side reactions. Washing protocols were optimized to ensure effective removal of water and removal of residual reagents, solvents, and impurities. Inadequate washing may lead to increased failed syntheses.

Ligand solubility is crucial for successful amine conjugation. Ligands, including carboxylates, NHS esters, and NHS carbonates, must be fully dissolved prior to on‐column transfer to ensure homogeneous reaction conditions and reproducible coupling. Poor solubility may result in incomplete reactions or irreproducible results. Depending on ligand properties, conjugations may be performed under organic or aqueous‐compatible conditions, with solvent selection guided by both solubility and reaction type.

The TOS workflow requires particular care. During amino modifier conjugation to the terminal DSC‐activated mixed carbonate, the base‐cleavable sulfonyl amidite is susceptible to premature immolation upon prolonged exposure to the amine, even under buffered conditions. Conjugation in HEPES buffer (50 mM, pH 8) must therefore be limited to at most 5 min as described. Extended reaction times can increase linker degradation and reduce the relative yield of the duplex strands. We opted to utilize amines for derivatization of the terminal strand, as this provides access to a wide array of commercially available amino modifiers containing desirable handles (e.g., PEG tags, azides, alkynes). Although certain phosphoramidites can achieve similar results, the use of amines may be more cost effective and allows the derivatization of a single 1‐µmol synthesis into multiple different conjugates, rather than requiring separate syntheses for each modification.

Understanding the Results

Mass spectrometry (MS) is essential for characterizing amino‐modified oligonucleotides to support successful conjugation. Adducts frequently form during deprotection, presumably from ethyl cyanoethyl and nucleobase protecting groups, making MS critical for supporting the desired product identity. Given the multiple reaction steps performed on the solid support, a single conjugation (e.g., DSC activation of R ‐CPG) may fail or be incomplete. In such cases, SAX‐HPLC may be misleading, as it may show a major peak with seemingly high yield. MS provides the definitive means to distinguish diamino incorporation, ligand conjugation, and strand extension, ensuring accurate assessment of the final product.

Time Considerations

It is critical to follow each step carefully and in the correct order, as all procedures have been optimized. The DSC‐mediated activation of R ‐CPG is typically complete within 2 hr and is followed by amino modifier incorporation for ∼10 min. The support is then thoroughly washed and detritylated on the automated synthesizer, which may take up to 10 min. NHS‐mediated conjugations, whether via carbonates or esters, generally require 2 hr, whereas the EDC‐mediated amide coupling route requires 16 hr (two 8‐hr couplings). A final wash on the synthesizer to remove residual water takes ∼10 min. Overall, the total workflow takes ∼4.5 hr for NHS ester/carbonate chemistry and ∼18.5 hr for the EDC route (the latter usually performed overnight).
