Determining and comparing the level of motivation for exercise according to the sociodemographic characteristics of university students
Authors: Mohammad Ahsan, Mohammed Feroz Ali, Mumtaz Alam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMotivation levels for exercise vary widely among individuals and are influenced by various factors. This study aimed to compare exercise motivation levels and explore influencing factors among university students.MethodsA cross-sectional research design targeted university students aged range 18 to 32 years. Participants (n = 148) were selected via cluster random sampling. Motivation for exercise was determine by the Exercise Motivation Inventory (EMI-2). EMI-2 comprises fourteen different subscales of motivation, with each subscale containing three to four items. All items were rated on a scale of 0 to 5, with 0 indicating “not at all true for me” and 5 indicating “very true for me.” Statistical analyses included ANOVA and Pearson correlation coefficient to assess differences among demographic variables (year of study, age, gender, marital status, location, and family size) and relationships between motivational aspects.ResultsThe average exercise motivation level among all students was 166.94 ± 32.20. Fifth-year students exhibited the highest motivation 178.33 ± 30.37. No significant gender differences were found (p = 0.149). However, age (p = 0.024), location (p = 0.015), marital status (p = 0.050), and family size (p = 0.030) significantly influenced exercise motivation.ConclusionUniversity students demonstrate inherent motivation for physical activity, with significant variations observed across demographic factors. These findings underscore the importance of tailored interventions to promote exercise and enhance student well-being. Further research, including longitudinal studies, is warranted to comprehensively understand exercise motivation dynamics in this population.Keywords: Exercise motivation, University students, Demographic factors, Cross-sectional study and tailored interventions

Introduction

Physical activity plays a crucial role in maintaining both physical and mental well-being. Numerous studies have highlighted its importance in reducing the risk of chronic diseases such as obesity, cardiovascular diseases, and diabetes, as well as in enhancing cognitive function and reducing symptoms of anxiety and depression [3, 14, 28]. For university students, who often face high levels of stress and sedentary behaviours due to academic demands and lifestyle factors, engaging in regular physical activity is particularly essential. It not only helps manage stress and improve mood but also fosters better concentration and academic performance [13]. Motivating university students for physical activity is paramount for cultivating lifelong healthy habits. The college years represent a critical period for establishing healthy behaviours that can extend into adulthood [1]. By instilling a sense of motivation and enthusiasm for physical activity during this time, universities can empower students to prioritize their health and well-being amidst academic pressures and busy schedules. Additionally, promoting campus physical activity creates a supportive environment that encourages social interaction, teamwork, and overall campus engagement, contributing to a holistic college experience [8].

To effectively motivate university students to engage in physical activity, interventions should be tailored to address this demographic group’s unique needs and preferences. Incorporating diverse and engaging activities, providing accessible facilities and resources, and offering incentives and rewards can enhance motivation and participation rates among students [20]. Furthermore, leveraging technology and social media platforms can facilitate communication and community-building efforts, fostering a sense of belonging and accountability among students striving to maintain an active lifestyle [10]. By prioritizing physical activity promotion initiatives, universities can play a pivotal role in nurturing a healthier, more vibrant campus community [4]. Activities related to exercise are complex in nature, and motivation for exercise is a concern. Numerous theories and models have been developed to motivate people in a routine exercise program [32]. Traditional conceptual theories of motivation are known as dichotomous constructs in which individuals are internally or externally motivated [7]. Self-determination theory is quite important in the literature on exercise and sports psychology [21]. However, models or theories have made much difference in encouraging and motivating regular exercise in everyday life for people [2]. Self-determination theory helps to identify why students are participating in exercise and determine whether they will continue exercising long-term [29]. An individual’s motivation toward an exercise can be distinguished by the level at which it represents autonomous or controlled reasons for behavioural engagement [16, 27]. Everyday involvement in physical exercise is directly related to improvements in psychological, biological, societal, and spiritual benefits. Exercise reduces health risks, such as heart disease, diabetes, heart stroke, several forms of cancer, and depression [19]. Cho & Beck [9], reported that students may suffer from health risks if they do not participate in regular exercise programs and become obese. The low level of prolonged participation in exercise has driven researchers to concentrate on the motivation for people to stay involved in daily exercise programs.

A few studies have been conducted to determine motivation’s effect on students’ exercise. Annesi et al., [5] investigated the implications of an exercise program by examining the health status of students. He found a significant positive relationship between exercise programs and muscular strength, endurance, and cardiorespiratory endurance improvements. The motivation to maintain an exercise routine is a crucial factor impacting students’ consistent participation in physical exercise [39]. Several kinds of motivation affect regular performance in exercise routines [12]. Ryan & Deci [31], confirmed that exercise can intrinsically or extrinsically motivate people. Intrinsically motivated students participate in exercise because they consider it enjoyable, interesting, and challenging. However, extrinsically motivated students participate in an exercise program to avoid feeling negative or to achieve an extrinsic reward. There is evidence that students are innately moved to exercise due to improved appearance or weight loss [23], improved personal health or skills [35], stress release from physical activity [33], and the avoidance of punishment or reward [31]. Motivation has been consistently linked to exercise adherence. The evidence revealed that motivation is reflected for many reasons. Irwin [15], investigated gender differences in exercise performance and reported that males are more active than females. The results showed that in performing regular exercise, gender factors motivate people differently. Azmi et al., [6] revealed that those who take part in regular exercise differed during several age phases and at different stages of achievement of goals and tasks, values, and state of health over time.

Since sedentary behaviour remains a prominent health problem, it is imperative to determine whether variances between university students affect exercise motivation. Therefore, the aim of this research was to study whether exercise motivation differs among students across categorical variables (age, gender, residence, family size, marital status, and level of study) and to identify the differences and relationships among the subscales of exercise motivation.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and sample size

Cross-sectional research was conducted online. The calculated sample size was 148. The Krejcie & Morgan [17], sample table calculated the sample size. The calculation was based on the estimated motivation to exercise among students and a target population size of 240 (the number of students studying in the Department of Physical Education); the significance level was 0.05, with a confidence interval of 0.95.

Ethical approval

This study received approval from the University Human Research Ethics Committee (FNU-HREC-22-40). All participants provided written informed consent before participating voluntarily. They were also informed that they could withdraw from the study without any consequences.

Demographic information

Students were required to state their biological details, such as their age, gender, and location, in the questionnaire. Students were asked about their marital status, family size, region (rural or urban), and level of education (years).

Exercise motivation inventory-2

The Exercise Motivation Inventory (EMI-2) developed by Markland & Hardy [22], was used to determine motivational level. The EMI-2 comprises fourteen different subscales of motivation, with each subscale containing three to four items. These subscales include affiliation (items No. 10, 24, 38, & 49), appearance (items No. 2, 18, 32, & 44), challenge (items No. 14, 28, 42, & 51), competition (items No. 12, 26, 40, & 50), enjoyment (items No. 9, 23, 37, & 48), health pressures (items No. 11, 25, & 39), ill-health avoidance (items No. 2, 16, & 30), nimbleness (items No. 13, 27, & 41), positive health (items No. 7, 21, & 35), revitalization (items No. 3, 17, & 31), social recognition (items No. 5, 19, 33, & 45), strength and endurance (items No. 8, 22, 36, & 47), stress management (items No. 6, 20, 34, & 46), and weight management (items No. 1, 15, 29, & 43). All items were rated on a scale of 0 to 5, with 0 indicating “not at all true for me” and 5 indicating “very true for me.” The EMI-2 has demonstrated good validity and reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.92 to 0.96 for all fourteen subscales [23]. The EMI was chosen to assess the motives of the participants to examine motives and the way motives might impact the activities undertaken that were chosen. It also helps assess the way affective responses of participants to exercise may be impacted by reasons for exercising. The motive for this research was to determine how involvement in physical activity might have a reciprocal impact on participants’ motives.

Data collection

Before data collection, prior permission was obtained from the head of the department for approaching students. The data collection was performed in the online survey setting. The questionnaires were developed in Google form, and the link was provided to the students via their email and WhatsApp numbers. Before providing their responses to the questionnaires, the objective of the study and all instructions regarding how to provide the most related responses were described. Students also provided their consent to participate in this study. The students responded to each question according to how they were motivated towards exercise. All respondents’ data were stored on Google Forms and downloaded in the form of an MS Excel worksheet. The data were extracted and processed for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed with IBM SPSS v-26 software. Parametric procedures were used for the statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics such as frequencies, means, and standard deviations (SDs) were derived from the analysis. ANOVA was also used to analyse these data inferentially. ANOVA was used to compare the scores across different years of study, gender, location, marital status, and family size to identify possible significant differences. Correlations between different exercise motivation parameters were also determined by using Pearson correlation coefficient test to determine the relationships among them.

Discussion

This study investigated whether differences in exercise motivation exist in different categorical variables (age, gender, location, family size, marital status, and level of study) among students. The results of the present study showed that significant differences existed between various categorical variables such as age, marital status, location, and family size. However, nonsignificant differences were detected in terms of gender and level of study. These findings were partially supported by earlier findings [6, 26, 34] and our research hypothesis, determining categorical differences in students’ exercise motivation. This study partially agrees with [36], who reported that males were motivated by ego orientation and performance factors such as affiliation, social recognition, competence, challenges, and strength and resistance. There was a high level of motivation among females for appearance, weight control, positive health, agility, and stress management [30]. A study conducted in Taiwan indicated that motivation to exercise was to improve their looks and body image and develop strength, and males competed in sports participation. In contrast, females are highly motivated to exercise when athletes do well at international athletic competitions because of the influence of celebrities [34]. Another study in Malaysia suggested that exercise motivation for males was strongly linked to intrinsic factors, such as facing challenges, competition, and gaining strength. On the other hand, females’ exercise motivation is strongly linked to extrinsic factors, such as attaining an attractive body image and looking and weight management [26].

Gender analysis revealed that males had a lower level of intrinsic motivation than females. However, female exercise behaviour was lower for extrinsic motivation [12, 25]. Irwin investigated gender differences in exercise performance and reported that males are more active than females [15]. The results showed that gender factors motivate people differently when performing regular exercise. Azmi et al., [6] revealed that people who perform regular exercise differ during several age phases, which can be attributed to their goals and tasks, values, and health status over time. Married students demonstrated a significantly greater rate of exercise motivation [18]. This might be attributed to married individuals promoting exercise to family members. This study revealed that married students have greater exercise motivation than single students, which contradicts the findings of other studies [37].

The findings of this study help advance our understanding of exercise motivation in terms of several categorical variables. With respect to exercise motivation for various years of study, the students in their final year were highly motivated to exercise. Despite being final-year students, first-year students were also motivated to participate in the different exercise programs. This might be due to their willingness to acquire benefits from available facilities as much as they are in the college. However, other students found themselves engaged in academic commitments and load. A similar study was conducted to investigate exercise motivation from middle school to high school. Measurement equivalence has not been established across age or time for males and females. The findings showed that females were less motivated towards exercise than males in the 6th grade, but the motivation towards exercise was similar for males and females in the 7th to 9th grade [11].

In addition, the present findings provide evidence of fourteen subscales, and the scores indicate that most participants were motivated to exercise due to positive health, strength endurance, poor health, and nimbleness. The least motivated participants were from health pressure and social recognition. Among the fourteen subscales, most students were more intrinsically motivated (positive health, strength endurance, poor health, and nimbleness) towards exercise. However, few students are motivated by health pressure and social recognition. These results support the self-determination theory of motivation, which comprises intrinsic and extrinsic motivation components that lead to volitional and long-lasting behaviour among different perspectives, including exercise motivation subscales [22].

Welk et al., [38] reported that the most common motivations among male and female high school students were strengthening the relationship and health domains. Students’ self-determination theory purports that the continuum should include self-satisfaction from autonomy, competence, and relatedness [24]. revealed that students with a high level of motivation towards exercise reported a lower level of laziness, anxiety, and lack of time than those with moderate motivation. These findings showed that students do not have high extrinsic motivation. More studies are needed to determine the best motivational structure to lead students to participate in the daily routine of moderate to vigorous exercise recommended by the World Health Organization.

Overall, exercise motivation varies according to individuals’ interest in or participation in exercise. In the present research, females and males differed in their motivation to exercise. These differences will likely increase or decrease for females and males in different age groups, marital statuses, locations, family sizes, or years of study. Although the overall motivation toward exercise remains constant, it could be more realistic to investigate the motivational profile of different sociodemographic variables [18].

This study has several limitations. First, the data were collected just before the end of the academic session. Students might be busy completing and submitting their academic assignments and not manage spare time to indulge in daily exercise programs. Second, the data were collected from only one department. Therefore, the results cannot be generalized to all students nationwide. Students from different streams might be engaged in various sports and physical activities. Third, the factors in the analysis were taken from the online data collection. Only those students who had access to internet-based facilities could participate. Fourth, it was not easy to separate all the students into different possible categories according to their sociodemographic status. Religious and cultural differences can impact the level of physical activity. Moreover, the level of exercise was not determined. It is assumed that all the students were aware of different types of exercise and their purposes. This means that they could provide the correct response for exercise motivation.

Conclusion

The exercise motivation in several categorical variables and in subscales seems equal, whereas the difference in all exercise motivation means that scores showed significant differences between variables. All the exercise motivation scores indicated that our students were more intrinsically motivated. Moreover, this study extends the current literature by demonstrating how different students who belong to categorical variables are motivated to exercise. The findings of this study could be utilized to design exercise motivation interventions to help students lead active and healthy lifestyles. Customized intervention programs should be developed based on the results of this study. The programs should focus on enhancing exercise motivation and target category variables found in the research. These programs can be tailored to target the distinct motivating elements that impact exercise habits across various student groups. Furthermore, the integration of intrinsic motivation techniques, which is indicated in the research, had a greater preference for intrinsic motivation among students, and intervention methods should prioritize the cultivation and augmentation of intrinsic motivational elements, such as pleasure, contentment, and individual gratification, in physical activities. This may involve integrating activities corresponding to individual interests and preferences, fostering autonomy and self-initiated goal establishment, and highlighting the inherent advantages of physical activity beyond external incentives.

The utilization of subscale analysis in this study offers valuable insights into the elements of exercise motivation that contribute to overall motivation levels. Future intervention programs can utilize this information to focus on motivations that may need to be addressed or improved among various student demographics. Incorporation of Behavioral Change Techniques: By integrating evidence-based behavioural change approaches, such as setting goals, measuring progress, receiving social support, and modifying the environment, exercise motivation interventions can effectively promote long-term behavioural change and encourage students to adopt active and healthy lives. Longitudinal follow-up studies are recommended to evaluate exercise motivation programs’ efficacy and enduring effects. These studies involve observing participants’ exercise behaviours and motivation levels over an extended period. This will yield significant data regarding the long-term viability of behaviour modification and contribute to the continuous improvement and advancement of the program.
