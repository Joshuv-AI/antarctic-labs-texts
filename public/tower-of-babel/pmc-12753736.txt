Leveraging similarity and community-based features for link prediction in temporal social networks using attention-enabled LSTM
Authors: Rohit Ahuja, Simranjit Kaur, Harish Kumar Shakya, Shashank Sheshar Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLink prediction refers to predicting future or missing links within the social network. Static social networks, with their fixed topology, require less data analysis, making predicting future or missing links relatively easier than in dynamic social networks. Dynamic social networks change over time, which makes link prediction challenging. Modern similarity-based features look at the topological structure of the network but only use a single snapshot to calculate, so they miss important patterns and changes in the network over time. Community features, which divide the whole network into communities, help to improve the prediction by focusing on links between the intra-communities. Our proposed framework, SimCom-AN-LSTM, shows that combining structural and community features can leverage the strengths of both approaches. The sequence-based Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model processes the snapshot sequences to capture the network’s temporal dependencies. These features help the model learn temporal patterns and predict future links by capturing local interaction between the nodes, the overall network topology, and community structure. The attention mechanism enhances the performance of the LSTM model by giving more weight to the feature that yields the best results. Results indicate that the proposed framework surpasses the individual state-of-the-art algorithms.Keywords: Link prediction, Community detection, Temporal social networks, LSTM, AttentionSubject terms: Computer science, Information technology

Introduction

Nowadays, social networks have become an integral part of our daily lives because they allow us to create connections, communicate, and share information with people around the world1. Nearly 66% of the global population has internet access, enabling them to communicate and share information instantly, facilitating social connections. Social networks are dynamic and constantly changing, continuously forming and dissolving new links, and the interactions between the nodes change continuously. Dynamic networks are gaining importance due to their application in several fields, such as transportation systems, biology, and social networks2. These networks change over time by dissolving former links and forming newer ones. The major concern that hinders its application lies in the prediction of the evolution of networks over time. Several researchers have focused on confronting the major challenge of understanding and predicting these changes within dynamic social networks3.

Social networks are complex systems because they show how different nodes are connected to each other in complex ways. Network science research used to focus on static networks, which are networks with fixed topologies that do not allow for any dynamic interaction. In contrast, many real-world systems exhibit dynamic behaviour, characterised by evolving relationships and temporal dependencies. These networks can be very accurate, efficient in terms of processing, and flexible when it comes to different types of inputs by changing their structure or parameters. Dynamic networks offer a more accurate representation of the basic relationships and processes in several domains, such as biological, social, and technological systems4,5. Dynamic networks can replicate the complexity and depth of most real-world networks, which are essentially dynamic. This is a key difference between dynamic and static networks. So, temporal characteristics are what make dynamic networks look dynamic because of how their links and information flow change over time. To understand how networks work and develop over time, it is important to be able to predict how the connections between them will change, expand, and dissolve. Additionally, because social networks are always changing, it is possible to look at how long-term network events like diffusion, contagion, and information cascades effect link production. Link prediction in dynamic networks facilitates the discovery of important nodes and connections, as well as the mechanisms influencing network dynamics, by analysing temporal dependencies6.

Similarity-based features are used by traditional link prediction algorithms7. Features based on similarity look at how the network is structured topologically. There are local features, global features, and quasi-local features among these. Local features look at the node’s direct connections to other nodes and only look at its immediate neighbourhood. These characteristics, encompassing Common Neighbours (CN)8, Adamic Adar (AA)9, Jaccard’s Coefficient (JC)10, and Preferential Attachment (PA)11, consider the node’s local topology. Local features only look at a limited area of the social network, which makes them easy to compute. But these traits are short-sighted and might miss the long-term information that needs a complete understanding of the network. Global features look at the whole network topology and take into account how the network is set up as a whole. These features can be more effective in sparse networks where local features fail to predict links accurately. Global features include Shortest Path (SP)12, Cosine Similarity (COSP), Matrix Forest Index (MFI)13, and Average Commute Time (ACT). Global features are computationally expensive because they require the analysis of the entire social network.

Quasi-local features help in maintaining a balance between local and global features14. They look beyond the immediate neighbourhood, but they don’t consider the entire network structure. Various quasi-local features are Local Path (LP)15, Path of Length 3 (LP), Clustering Coefficient-Based Link Prediction (CCLP)16, and Node and Link Clustering Coefficient (NLC). These features offer limited global insights and fail to predict the links between distant nodes. Community features divide the entire social network into clusters or communities. Various community detection algorithms are Diffusion Entropy Reduction (DER), Eigen Vector, Common Pott’s Matter (CPM)17, Surprise Communities (SUPR), Stochastic Block Model (SBM)18, and Leiden19.

Recently, some advanced methods for link prediction have been introduced,20. For instance, in20, authors presented a fusion-based graph convolutional network model, while another study,21, introduced novel hypergraph motifs and a network embedding-based method for link prediction. The authors of22 presented a graph neural network-based link prediction method in microservice architectures. In23, the authors presented a -tree-based community-enhanced link prediction technique for dynamic networks. In24, a fuzzy-based information diffusion enabled a link prediction method in multiplex social networks. Similarly, authors of25 discussed another approach for multiplex link prediction using merged node and edge relevance. The authors of26 introduced a quantum-enabled method based on a variational quantum classifier for predicting missing links on social networks.

Despite the significant progress in link prediction for temporal social networks, existing methods often struggle to effectively capture dynamic patterns of evolving relationships, leading to suboptimal performance in real-world scenarios. Traditional methods mostly look at static features or homogeneous graph structures. They do not take into account the temporal relationships and community-based impacts that are important for understanding how networks change over time. Additionally, limited research has utilised similarity measures alongside community data to improve predictive accuracy5. To fill these gaps, we propose SimCom-AN-LSTM, a new framework that combines community and similarity-based features with an attention-enabled Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model. This method tries to find both local and global trends in how networks change over time, which makes it easier to predict future links. The attention mechanism makes the model even better at focusing on important time-based information, which makes link prediction in temporal social networks more precise and reliable. Related work

Link prediction

Link prediction has attracted several researchers across the globe to catalyze network analysis to identify possible links between the nodes within the social network. Several approaches ranging from machine learning to conventional graph-based algorithms have been employed for link prediction. Earlier link prediction methods relied on the structural properties of the network for predicting the future or missing links27. Graph-based approaches retrieve the similarity scores between pairs of nodes as the Adamic/Adar Index, Jaccard Coefficient, Preferential Attachment and Common Neighbors, considering the topology of the network or familiar neighbors of the node pairs. These features only capture the local view of the network, merely taking into consideration the immediate neighboring nodes, ignoring the global structure of the network. Moreover, these features generally assume homophily, i.e., similar nodes are more likely to be connected. This assumption fails in heterophilous networks where dissimilar nodes are also likely to be connected.

Furthermore, community-enhanced link prediction28 assumes the associated information of node communities for better prediction accuracy and further realises that community structure indicates essential knowledge regarding network dynamics. These techniques harness the strong community structure in communities by mining interactions within as well as between communities for predicting possible links28. Deep learning techniques play a vital role in identification at every step of the link prediction process, from feature extraction to link prediction. The emergence of machine learning algorithms has accelerated the development of machine learning, which takes full advantage of the predictive variables derived from the traits of the nodes, the structures of the networks, and external data. Supervised learning techniques help to forecast the addition of new links to the network or find existing links within the network but not present in the available data. Nowadays, graph neural networks have been widely used for link prediction due to their ability to learn better representations of nodes and edges within networks29. These models might potentially mitigate the inaccuracy of link prediction by capturing complex connections and relationships in network topology.

Several link prediction models have benefited from the R-Models (RMLs) neural network because it provides a deep learning solution to the link weight prediction problem by Yuchen Hou and Lawrence B. Holder30, illustrating the usage of state-of-the-art neural network models in link prediction. Deep learning techniques, such as deep neural networks, are quite useful in link prediction tasks to leverage complex neural architecture’s capacity to recognise complex patterns in network data. Link prediction relies majorly on feature engineering, where supervised machine learning models are trained using topological and aggregated data. Raeini et al.31 suggested a supervised-learning link prediction using single-layer and multiplex networks and evaluated the effectiveness of aggregated and topological features in link prediction using various supervised machine-learning methods, emphasising the importance of feature selection in link prediction, which indeed increases the ability of the model to predict future links correctly. In another study5, authors have presented machine learning models for link prediction using similarity-based and community features. Recently, few studies have focused on prediction of performance based on the question-answer interactions in temporal settings32 and urban commuting flow prediction33.

Community detection

Community-based features are employed to enhance the predictive accuracy of future or missing links in complex social networks. Numerous real-world networks have community structure, which offers important insights into the underlying connections between nodes. Community-based features are incorporated with an aim to enhance the link prediction algorithm because it captures the complex interactions between nodes within communities. Communities are cohesive collections of nodes within a network, identified using community detection methods such as the frequently used Greedy Modularity algorithm34. It divides nodes into communities in such a way that it maximizes the modularity of the final partition. Modularity is a quantitative measure of the quality of community structure based on the density of links inside the communities compared to random networks. The greedy modularity algorithm maximizes the overall modularity score by iteratively merging pairs of communities. It suggests changes in modularity due to the merger of two communities and chooses the merger that maximizes the rise in modularity at each stage. This process iterates until further merging no longer leads to increased modularity. The method is ideal for extensive network analysis due to its greedy nature, which ensures computational efficiency35.

Clauset-Newman-Moore (CPM)36 detects cohesive clusters of nodes in a network such that the maximum modularity score is pulled up. Modularity measures the density of links within communities compared to a random network, serving as a key indicator of the community structure. CPM is a two-step process; firstly, it assigns all the nodes to a community and then merges them again and again. This process aims to find a merger that maximises the increase in modularity at each level. The gain in modularity is determined by assessing how much merging two communities enhances the overall modularity score. The process continues until further merging does not increase modularity. The statistical community technique is widely used to extract communities in networks, which groups nodes based on their statistical significance. Significance Communities37 considers the statistical significance of the observed community structure concerning random null models, in opposition to classic community identification methods that just take into account the topology of the network.

Leiden19 is a community detection technique that identifies the communities in the networks by maximizing the modularity quality function. The quality of a community partition is measured by the modularity , where higher values suggest stronger connections inside communities as opposed to across communities. Leiden, which was created as an expansion of the Louvain algorithm38, provides better performance and scalability without sacrificing the quality of the community detection outcomes. The goal of these attributes is to capture the idea of “homophily”, which is when nodes with a lot of similar neighbours are likely to become connected in the future. Global feature

Global features provide a full understanding of how the network is structure. Global features can show patterns and characteristics of the network’s structure and behavior that local features may overlook40. Following metrics are involved in global features4,5:

Shortest Path Length (SP): It counts the lowest number of edges needed to connect two nodes41. In link prediction, the shortest path is used to find out how likely it is that two nodes are linked based on how close they are to one other in the network. If two nodes have shorter paths, they are more likely to be connected. Members of social networks who have shorter paths between them are more likely to be friends or have comparable interests. This metric is crucial in understanding the efficiency of information flow and connectivity within the network.

Quasi-local feature

In dynamic networks, quasi-local features provide us a better look at how nodes interact and how the network is structured. Quasi-local features take into account more contextual information, such as the structure and attributes of the network’s larger neighborhood. Quasi-local characteristics demonstrate the rationale for the clustering of nodes and links inside the network, capturing its intermediate-scale patterns and dependencies. These link prediction methods include the node clustering coefficient, the local path, the path of length, and the clustering coefficient4.

Local Path: Examining pathways of a particular length between nodes in order to estimate the likelihood of a link is known as the local path feature in link prediction algorithms44. Local path feature delivers a more nuanced view of possible links between nodes by taking into account pathways of different lengths and reveals patterns of connectivity within the network. A local path between two nodes u and v in a network can be defined as a sequence of edges or nodes that connect them, with a limited length or depth. The length of the path varies depending on the specific context and the properties of the network.

Proposed work

This paper proposes the SimCom-AN-LSTM framework for link prediction in temporal social networks. The proposed work aims to predict the link accurately in dynamic temporal social networks. Our approach to the problem of predicting links in dynamic networks uses a combination of community features, similarity-based features, and an enhanced Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network with an attention mechanism, as seen in Fig. 3. The goal is to design a multifaceted approach that can capture the complex and evolving dynamics of social networks. Firstly, we load the social network dataset for data processing and snapshot generation, retrieving information about source nodes, target nodes, and timestamps. These elements represent the interactions or connections between nodes over time. Given the dynamic nature of social networks, we partition the dataset into five snapshots, each reflecting the network at specific time intervals. These snapshots enable us to analyse the evolution of networks, which plays a vital role in predicting future links followed by future generations.

Feature generation & training dynamic snapshots

The LSTM-enabled model then undergoes training using four types of features. We use four types of features: local, global, quasi-local, and community-based. By considering the network’s topological structure, we extract the first three types of similarity-based features. Local features include Common Neighbors (CN), Jaccard Coefficient (JC), Adamic/Adar Index (AA), and Preferential Attachment (PA). Global features provide a broader perspective on network connectivity, which consists of Shortest Path, Average Commute Time (ACT), Matrix Forest Index (MFI), and COSP. Local Path Index (LP), Path of Length 3 (L3), Clustering Coefficient-based Link Prediction (CCLP), and Node and Link Clustering Coefficient (NLC) are all examples of quasi-local features. We use different community detection algorithms, such as Diffusion Entropy Reducer (DER), Surprise Communities (SURP), Stochastic Block Model (SBM), LEIDEN, Eigenvector (EIGEN), and Constant Potts Model (CPM), to get information about communities. We combine these features to generate a feature set. The core of the proposed methodology is the LSTM model enhanced with attention mechanisms, as shown in Fig. 4.

We combined similarity-based features (local, quasi-local, and global) with community-based features when we generated the feature vector. This selection is inspired by the theoretical understanding of social network formation, wherein both local and global structural similarity (e.g., common neighbors, shortest paths) and meso-level cohesion (e.g., modular communities) collaboratively contribute link formation. We employ the attention mechanism in the LSTM to automatically give more weight to more informative features across temporal snapshots instead of manually selecting or weighting information, which may lead us to miss useful information.

Testing & evaluation

The LSTM network is particularly suited for handling sequential data, making it ideal for modeling dynamic networks. The attention layer utilizes the common-neighbor feature. The attention mechanism improves the model’s ability to focus on the most relevant parts of the input sequence, enhancing prediction accuracy by highlighting important temporal dependencies and patterns. We use an attention mechanism model to enhance the accuracy of the LSTM. We calculate the optimal values of several hyperparameters, including batch size, number of dense units, number of epochs, number of LSTM units, and sequence length. Algorithm

Algorithm 1 predicts future links or missing links in a temporal social network using similarity and community features using an improved LSTM model with an attention mechanism. It expresses the rationale behind link generation over time in social networks by designing a snapshot construction method and a feature extraction strategy and then using an attention-enriched LSTM model to predict forward links. LSTMs compress all sequence information into a single hidden state, which often makes it difficult to capture long-term dependencies. By introducing attention, the model learns to assign importance weights to each timestamp using Algorithm 2, ensuring that the most relevant parts of the sequence influence the prediction more strongly. Thus, not only the performance is improved but also directly enhances interpretability.

Hyperparameter tuning in the attention-mechanism enhanced LSTM model is described in Algorithm 3. The algorithm first sets a sequence of the hyperparameter space (batch_size, dense_units, epochs, lstm_units, and sequence length) consecutively. Then, Algorithm 3 describes the suggested grid search method that is used in the process of fine-tuning the hyperparameters for the attention-mechanism enhanced LSTM model. Firstly, hyperparameter space, consisting of batch size, dense units, epochs, LSTM units, and the sequence length is discussed. Then, the loop iterates over all the possible combinations of hyperparameters and trains one LSTM for each combination by building the LSTM using the first four snapshots. Next, we evaluate each model using three matrices: AUC, AUPR, and average precision, comparing it to the fifth snapshot. Thus, Algorithm 3 records and updates the best-performing hyperparameters based on these metrics. After all the possible values, it outputs the optimal hyperparameter values.

Performance metrics

The Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC)59 is a useful measure for evaluating how well binary classification models, like those employed in link prediction work. The AUC shows how the true positive rate (sensitivity) and the false positive rate (specificity) change with different threshold values. Its value lies in the range (0,1). A value near 1 represents the models that give good performances. It measures the total discriminative capacity of a classifier over all conceivable threshold settings. It provides a comprehensive evaluation of the discrimination capacity of the link prediction model to discriminate positive instances (existing connections) from negative ones (non-existing links) in the network; hence, it gives a critical statistic on the problems concerning the link prediction. where is the -th point on the ROC curve, where is the false positive rate and the true positive rate at the -th threshold.

Significantly, the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPR) has been widely used in capturing the potential performance on imbalanced datasets, which is often employed in the scenario of link prediction, as the number of non-links is greater than the number of practical links. At various threshold levels, the AUPR measures the area under the curve when plotting precision (the ratio of true positives to the sum of true positives and false positives) against recall (the ratio of true positives to the sum of true positives and false negatives). AUPR values provide insight into the trade-off of precision versus recall curves for different thresholds. A higher AUPR value indicates better performance because the model maintains more precision while achieving a higher recall. AUPR is of high relevance within the context of our work, as it allows for quantifying the model performance in scenarios where the classes are imbalanced or where the absence of a link is much more common than the presence of a link. Average precision provides a way to summarize a precision-recall curve into a single number, usually representing the area under the curve. The weighted average of all possible precisions found at each threshold where recall changes determines the average precision, with the increase in recall from the previous threshold serving as the weight. It provides a single-figure measurement of quality across all levels of recall. For most tasks where false positives and false negatives are costly, a higher average precision value indicates the model’s precision across high recall levels.

Statistical significance test

In this section, we present the significance testing over different parameters based paired (dependent) t-test on AUC score. We compare AUC scores of each algorithm with proposed algorithm SIMCOM-AN-LSTM (control algorithm), for each dataset. Each pair of AUC values consist of one from SIMCOM-AN-LSTM and one from compared algorithms, both measured on the same dataset and split ratio. The paired nature controls for random variability and differences between algorithm performances are not affected by split-to-split variance in the data, since both algorithms see the same train/test sets or folds. This design is more sensitive than treating the results as independent, because the paired samples remove the noise of between split variation. Conclusion

In summary, this study presents a novel approach to link prediction in temporal social networks by proposing the SimCom-AN-LSTM framework. By integrating similarity-based features including local, global, and quasi-local metrics with community detection and an attention-enhanced LSTM model, we capture the complex, evolving dynamics that characterize real-world networks. The inclusion of an attention mechanism allows the model to focus on the most relevant temporal patterns, improving predictive accuracy and adaptability. Experimental results across five diverse social network datasets demonstrate the superior performance of our approach over traditional algorithms in terms of AUC, AUPR, and average precision. These findings highlight not only the potential of hybrid deep learning methods for dynamic network analysis, but also the importance of combining structural and contextual features to enhance link prediction problem. In future work, the framework can be extended to directed and weighted temporal graphs considering real-world networks. Moreover, incorporating multi-relational temporal dynamics will allow the proposed model to address heterogeneous relationships across domains such as citation, biological, and knowledge networks. These extensions would further broaden the applicability and robustness of SimCom-AN-LSTM.
