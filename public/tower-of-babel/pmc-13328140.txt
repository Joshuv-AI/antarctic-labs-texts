Addressing the silent epidemic: a narrative review and evidence synthesis of digital health and telehealth interventions for loneliness in older adults
Authors: Motti Haimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLoneliness and social isolation represent major threats to the health and well-being of older adults, conferring elevated risks for depression, cognitive decline, cardiovascular disease, and premature mortality. The rapid proliferation of digital health technologies and telehealth services—accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic—has opened new pathways for addressing these challenges at scale.ObjectiveThis narrative review synthesizes the current evidence on the effectiveness of digital health and telehealth interventions in reducing loneliness and social isolation among older adults, examines the diverse modalities employed, identifies barriers to equitable adoption, and proposes directions for future research.MethodsA narrative synthesis was conducted drawing on systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, and observational studies identified through PubMed, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, primarily spanning 2020–2025. Interventions examined include telehealth video visits, empathy-focused telephone programs, group videoconferencing, digital mental health platforms, mobile health applications, social robots, and AI-enabled companions.ResultsTelehealth video visits, empathy-focused telephone programs, and group-based videoconferencing demonstrate meaningful reductions in loneliness, depression, and anxiety in several RCTs and pilot studies. Digital mental health platforms incorporating cognitive behavioral therapy and mindfulness show promise, as do AI-enabled social robots in institutional and community settings. However, meta-analytic evidence reveals considerable heterogeneity: some pooled analyses report modest to null overall effects, while others find medium effect sizes (d = − 0.47). Intervention effectiveness appears contingent on design features, population characteristics, training support, and integration with existing social networks. The digital divide—limited digital literacy, technology access, usability challenges, and preference for in-person care—remains a challenging barrier to equitable implementation.ConclusionsDigital health and telehealth interventions hold considerable promise for mitigating loneliness among older adults. However, their benefits cannot be realized without systematically addressing the digital divide. Large-scale, well-powered RCTs with standardized outcome measures and longer follow-up periods are urgently needed, alongside implementation science research to translate evidence into scalable practice.

Introduction

Loneliness, the subjective perception that one’s social relationships are deficient in quantity or quality—has emerged as a critical public health concern worldwide. Conceptually distinct from social isolation, which refers to the objective absence or paucity of social contacts and networks [1], loneliness can affect individuals regardless of the size of their social network. Both phenomena disproportionately affect older adults, with recent estimates placing the prevalence of chronic loneliness in this population at approximately 20.8% [2] and social isolation affecting a comparable proportion of community-dwelling older persons.

The health consequences are well established. Epidemiological evidence links loneliness and social isolation to elevated risks for depression, anxiety, cognitive decline, cardiovascular disease, impaired immune function, and all-cause mortality, with effect sizes comparable to those of smoking and obesity [3]. A comprehensive meta-analysis by Duffner et al. [4] characterized loneliness as the next “geriatric giant,” warranting the same clinical attention as falls, incontinence, and delirium. Hajek et al. [2] confirmed through a meta-analysis of 17 studies that chronic loneliness affects roughly one in five older adults, with higher rates among women (21.7%) than men (17.7%), and that it is associated with worse mental health, cognitive, and physical outcomes relative to transient loneliness.

The COVID-19 pandemic substantially exacerbated the problem. Lockdowns, visitor restrictions, and the cessation of community programs disrupted the already fragile social networks of older adults [5]. A systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the pooled prevalence of loneliness among older adults during the pandemic at 28.6% and social isolation at 31.2%, with estimates rising significantly after the first three months [6], and the resulting surge in loneliness has been described as a parallel epidemic [7]. In response, digital health technologies and telehealth services emerged as essential tools for maintaining both healthcare access and social connection [8].

The term “digital health” encompasses mobile health (mHealth) applications, wearable devices, telehealth and telemedicine services, social media platforms, online peer-support communities, and, more recently, artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled conversational agents and social robots [5]. The adoption of these technologies by older adults increased markedly during the pandemic, though disparities persist along lines of age, income, education, race, and geography [9]. Against this backdrop, a growing body of literature has examined whether digital interventions can meaningfully reduce loneliness and social isolation in older populations.

Previous systematic reviews have produced mixed findings. Some meta-analyses report modest or null pooled effects of technology-based interventions [10, 11]. Historically, an earlier systematic review concluded that technology interventions exert generally positive effects contingent on design, training, and relational context [12], providing foundational evidence that subsequent research has sought to update and extend. An umbrella review of loneliness interventions noted that reviews themselves are of variable quality and often offer conflicting conclusions [13]. Several important developments since these earlier reviews—including new RCTs, the emergence of AI-enabled social robots, and an expanding literature on the digital divide—justify a contemporary narrative synthesis.

The purpose of this narrative review is to provide a comprehensive, critical appraisal of the current evidence on digital health and telehealth interventions for loneliness and social isolation among older adults. We examine the diverse modalities studied, evaluate their effectiveness by drawing on systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and key RCTs, explore barriers to adoption, and identify priorities for future research and practice.

Methods

Search strategy

This study adopted a non-systematic narrative review methodology. While narrative reviews do not follow the full PRISMA protocol and are not typically pre-registered in databases such as PROSPERO, we employed a structured search strategy to enhance transparency and reproducibility. A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, PsycINFO, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, covering publications from database inception through December 2025. Search terms included controlled vocabulary (MeSH/Emtree) and free-text combinations of the following keywords: (“loneliness” OR “social isolation” OR “social disconnectedness”) AND (“older adults” OR “elderly” OR “aged” OR “geriatric”) AND (“telehealth” OR “telemedicine” OR “digital health” OR “mHealth” OR “mobile health” OR “video conferencing” OR “social robot” OR “digital intervention” OR “online intervention”). Reference lists of included reviews were hand-searched for additional relevant publications. We acknowledge that the narrative approach may introduce selection and interpretation biases, as the study identification process was not conducted independently by multiple reviewers and was not pre-registered.

Eligibility criteria

The search prioritized literature published between January 2020 and December 2025, to capture the post-pandemic surge in evidence, although seminal earlier works were retained. We included systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental studies, and key observational and qualitative studies. Eligible studies examined interventions delivered via any form of digital technology or telecommunications to adults aged 50 years or older, with loneliness, social isolation, or social connectedness as a primary or secondary outcome measured by a validated instrument (e.g., UCLA Loneliness Scale, De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale, PROMIS Social Isolation Scale). Studies restricted to populations under 50, those not available in English, or those examining purely in-person interventions without a digital component were excluded. The initial database search yielded 1,247 records. After removal of 404 duplicates, 843 unique records were screened by title and abstract, of which 731 were excluded as not meeting the inclusion criteria. The remaining 112 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility; 78 were excluded (45 did not report loneliness or social isolation as a measured outcome, 18 did not include older adults as the target population, and 15 were conference abstracts or commentaries without original data). A total of 34 publications were included in the final narrative synthesis. Figure 1 illustrates the literature search flow diagram.

Study selection and data synthesis

Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, and full texts of potentially eligible articles were retrieved and assessed. Given the narrative nature of this review, a formal systematic quality appraisal (e.g., AMSTAR-2 or Cochrane Risk of Bias) was not applied; however, the methodological rigor and limitations of included studies are discussed throughout the results. Data were synthesized thematically across the following domains: (a) telehealth video visits and remote consultations; (b) telephone-based interventions; (c) group-based videoconferencing programs; (d) digital mental health platforms and mobile applications; (e) AI-enabled social robots; and (f) online peer-support and virtual communities. Where available, pooled effect sizes from meta-analyses are reported. To facilitate assessment of the strength of the evidence, each study included in the summary table (Table 1) was classified according to the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (CEBM) 2011 Levels of Evidence hierarchy.

Results

The search yielded a total of 34 key publications included in this narrative synthesis: 8 systematic reviews or meta-analyses, 5 umbrella or overview reviews, 7 RCTs, 4 quasi-experimental or pilot studies, 6 qualitative or mixed-methods studies, and 4 review protocols or commentaries with original data. The results are organized thematically by intervention modality, followed by a cross-cutting synthesis of effectiveness and an examination of moderating factors.

Telehealth video visits and remote consultations

Telehealth video visits represent the most direct digital analogue to in-person clinical encounters. Fisher [14], in a Doctor of Nursing Practice project, delivered telehealth video visits to homebound older adults over 90 days and observed significant reductions in UCLA Loneliness Scale scores, depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), primary care visits, and hospital admissions/readmissions. While the uncontrolled, quality-improvement design limits causal inference, the findings suggested that regular video-mediated clinician contact may help alleviate loneliness.

Stronger evidence comes from a recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Pinero de Plaza et al. [15], which evaluated telehealth for homebound populations across 10 studies, 8 of which were included in pooled analysis. The meta-analysis found a significant reduction in well-being-related symptomatology—including depression, loneliness, and isolation—with a pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) of − 0.31 (95% CI: − 0.47 to − 0.15; p < .01). The magnitude of effect increased over time, reaching an SMD of − 0.55 at the second follow-up, suggesting that sustained engagement amplifies benefits. However, heterogeneity was high (I² = 79%), and the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate.

Telephone-based empathy-focused programs

Among the most methodologically rigorous evidence in this field is the RCT by Kahlon et al. [16], published in JAMA Psychiatry. A total of 240 homebound adults receiving Meals on Wheels services were randomized to a 4-week, empathy-oriented telephone call program delivered by briefly trained young laypersons (n = 16 callers; 2 h of training), or usual care. Callers contacted participants daily for the first five days, after which participants could reduce call frequency (minimum two per week). The intervention group showed significant improvements relative to controls across all primary outcomes: loneliness (UCLA Loneliness Scale improvement = 1.1; 95% CI: 0.5–1.7; Cohen’s d = 0.48; p < .001), depression (PHQ-9 improvement = 1.5; 95% CI: 0.22–2.7; d = 0.31; p < .001), anxiety (GAD-7 improvement = 1.8; 95% CI: 0.44–3.2; d = 0.35; p < .001), and general mental health (SF-12 improvement = 2.6; d = 0.46; p = .003). Physical health outcomes did not differ between groups.

The Kahlon trial is notable for several reasons: it showed that a simple, low-cost, and highly scalable intervention achieved clinically meaningful effects within four weeks; it utilized lay callers rather than clinicians, greatly reducing workforce and cost barriers; and it identified empathetic listening as the core therapeutic ingredient. Building on these findings, Jiang et al. [17] replicated and extended this work in Hong Kong, conducting a three-arm RCT among 287 community-dwelling lonely older adults comparing a telephone-based wisdom-enhancement narrative therapy (Tele-NT), an empathy-focused call program adapted from Kahlon (Tele-EP), and an active control group receiving regular calls. At 4-week follow-up, the Tele-NT group was associated with significantly reduced loneliness on the De Jong Gierveld Scale compared to the active control group. Notably, however, the empathy-focused arm did not show a significant difference versus the control, suggesting that cultural context may moderate intervention effects and that narrative-therapeutic techniques may offer additional benefit beyond empathic listening alone.

Group-based videoconferencing interventions

Group telehealth programs delivered via videoconferencing platforms represent a growing category. The CONNECT Program [18], designed for adults aged 55 and older, utilizes structured group videoconferencing and draws on prior pilot RCTs showing significant reductions in loneliness, depression, and anxiety through digital group interventions. Group formats offer the advantage of fostering peer connections that may persist beyond the intervention period.

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Shekelle et al. [19], encompassing 60 studies (36 RCTs), provided moderate-certainty evidence that group-based treatments were associated with reduced loneliness (pooled SMD for 11 RCTs = − 0.27; 95% CI: − 0.48 to − 0.08). Internet training interventions—which equipped older adults with the digital skills to participate in online social activities—also showed a significant pooled effect (SMD = − 0.22; 95% CI: − 0.30 to − 0.14; 5 RCTs), suggesting that both the social content of interventions and the acquisition of digital competence contribute to loneliness reduction. Evidence was insufficient, however, to draw firm conclusions about telephone-delivered or individual internet-based interventions.

Digital mental health platforms and mobile applications

Multicomponent digital mental health programs integrating psychoeducation, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), and mindfulness-based techniques have shown particular promise. Gould et al. [20] evaluated the Meru Health Program, a 12-week therapist-supported smartphone intervention for middle-aged and older adults with depression. Participants showed improvements in mental health–related quality of life and significant decreases in loneliness, with mindfulness components identified as potentially the most important therapeutic element. Because the study lacked a randomized control group, it should be interpreted cautiously as providing preliminary evidence.

In a larger observational study, Magid et al. [21] analyzed data from 919 adults engaging with a digital mental health platform and found that participants reporting loneliness at baseline who sustained engagement over time exhibited the greatest reductions in loneliness and improvements in mental and social health outcomes. This apparent dose-response relationship suggests that ongoing, active engagement is critical for meaningful change, though self-selection bias cannot be excluded.

A recent Japanese RCT [22] tested a mobile nutrition management application used alone versus with family involvement among 101 participants aged 40 and above. The family-supported arm showed significantly greater improvement in social behavior, leisure activities, and total frailty scores (p = .037), though social isolation per se did not improve. This finding highlights that mobile tools may be most effective when they facilitate human connection rather than substituting for it.

AI-enabled social robots

Social robots—physically embodied, AI-enabled devices designed to provide companionship—represent an emerging modality. A meta-analysis by Mehrabi and Ghezelbash [23], published in “The Gerontologist”, synthesized 19 studies with 1,083 participants and found that social robot interventions were associated with meaningful reductions in loneliness among older adults. Moderator analyses indicated that pet-like robots, interventions of longer duration, and use among individuals living alone or with cognitive impairment were associated with greater effectiveness.

Yen et al. [24] conducted a meta-analysis of 8 RCTs focused on social robots in long-term care facilities and reported large positive effect sizes for both depression and loneliness. Group-based robot activities outperformed individual activities for depression outcomes, and longer intervention durations were associated with greater improvement. Among commercially deployed robots, ElliQ—an AI-driven proactive companion with conversational, music, and health-coaching capabilities—was piloted in a New York State program. Preliminary data from 107 users showed a reported 95% reduction in loneliness and improved well-being after 30 days of use, with high daily engagement rates [25]. However, the absence of a control group and reliance on a novel self-report instrument limits the interpretability of these findings.

A Japanese RCT [26] tested the BOCCO emo social communication robot among 73 community-dwelling older adults who lived alone. Over a 4-week intervention period, the robot facilitated conversations with human operators and family members while providing daily task reminders. Qualitative findings revealed that participants experienced increased laughter, reduced isolation feelings, and a sense of being cared for, suggesting that social robots can function as mediators that strengthen rather than replace human relationships.

Online peer-support platforms and virtual communities

In dementia care, where social isolation is particularly severe, telehealth-delivered group programs have shown promise. Chen et al. [27], in an overview of reviews published in JMIR Mental Health, found that videoconferencing, online peer-support platforms, and virtual cognitive stimulation groups reduced social isolation and loneliness among people with dementia and their caregivers, while enhancing social engagement. Vaidyam et al. [28] piloted a computer-generated “digital human” delivering an internet-based psychological intervention during COVID-19, suggesting feasibility and acceptability among both younger and older adults, though the pilot was not powered to detect efficacy.

More broadly, Sen et al. [29] conducted a systematic review of 25 studies and concluded that digital technology use was linked to reduced social isolation in older adults by enhancing social connectivity and digital self-efficacy. The review emphasized that mobile technology applications helped families stay connected and linked older adults to healthcare resources, though standardized definitions and outcome measures were lacking across studies.

Cross-cutting synthesis of effectiveness

Synthesizing across modalities, the evidence reveals both promising signals and important limitations. Table 1 summarizes key meta-analytic findings. At the individual study level, several well-designed interventions [14, 16, 20] suggest clinically meaningful reductions in loneliness. At the pooled level, however, the picture is more complex.

Shah et al. [10] meta-analyzed 5 trials of digital technology interventions and found no statistically significant overall effect versus usual care at 3–6 months. Li et al. [11] similarly found little or no overall effect across 6 RCTs of computer training and videoconferencing. In an earlier foundational review, Chen and Schulz [12] concluded that technology interventions exert an overall positive influence, with effectiveness depending on design, training intensity, and whether technology supports existing relationships. The most comprehensive recent meta-analysis [4], pooling 27 studies with 1,756 participants, found a medium overall effect (d = − 0.47; 95% CI: − 0.62 to − 0.32) for loneliness interventions, though between-study heterogeneity was substantial and non-technology-based interventions showed larger effects on average (Δd = − 0.35; p = .029).

A network meta-analysis by Sun et al. [30] compared multiple intervention types and found that hybrid and psychological interventions appeared advantageous, with group-based delivery modes adding extra benefits.

Taken together, the evidence suggests that the most effective digital interventions incorporate psychological therapeutic techniques (CBT, mindfulness, empathic engagement) within a group or interpersonal format, rather than relying on technology provision alone.

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

This narrative review identified a rapidly expanding evidence base on digital health and telehealth interventions targeting loneliness and social isolation in older adults. Several well-designed individual studies suggest that telehealth video visits, empathy-focused telephone programs, group videoconferencing, therapist-supported mobile platforms, and AI-enabled social robots can meaningfully reduce loneliness, depression, and anxiety. The Kahlon et al. [16] RCT stands as a landmark study, showing medium-sized effects on loneliness (d = 0.48) through a remarkably simple, scalable, layperson-delivered intervention. Similarly, meta-analytic evidence from Duffner et al. [4] and Shekelle et al. [19] supports the overall effectiveness of structured loneliness interventions, with group-based and psychologically informed approaches showing the strongest signals.

However, the pooled evidence is far from unequivocal. Some meta-analyses [10, 11] found no significant overall effects of technology-based interventions, and even the more optimistic reviews acknowledge substantial between-study heterogeneity. These discrepancies likely reflect the extraordinary diversity of interventions grouped under the “digital health” umbrella—from simple telephone calls to AI-driven robots—as well as variability in study populations, outcome measures, follow-up durations, and methodological quality.

Interpretation in context of prior literature

Our findings extend earlier foundational reviews [12] and more recent umbrella reviews [13] by incorporating post-2023 evidence, including new data on social robots and the sustained post-pandemic trajectory of digital health adoption. The observation that non-technology interventions tend to show larger effect sizes than technology-based ones [4] warrants careful interpretation. This does not necessarily indicate that technology is less effective per se, but may reflect the additional barriers (digital literacy, technical difficulties, impersonal interfaces) that technology introduces, which can attenuate engagement and, consequently, outcomes.

A recurrent theme across the literature is that technology is most effective when it serves as a conduit for meaningful human connection rather than as a standalone solution. The Kahlon trial succeeded not because of the telephone technology, but because of the empathetic human engagement it facilitated. The Japanese BOCCO emo robot functioned as a mediator connecting isolated older adults to family members and operators. Gould et al.’s [20] digital platform succeeded in part because it included therapist support. This pattern aligns with the broader social science understanding that loneliness is fundamentally a relational deficit that requires relational solutions [31].

Importantly, the relevance of addressing loneliness through digital interventions is supported by international evidence beyond the predominantly North American and East Asian studies included in this review. A cross-sectional study of 525 older adults in Portugal found that depression prevalence remained high in the post-pandemic period, with loneliness and lower quality of life strongly associated with depressive symptoms regardless of institutionalization status [32]. A systematic review of depression among older adults during the COVID-19 pandemic confirmed that remote interventions were associated with reductions in depressive symptoms [33], consistent with the telehealth findings of the present review. Furthermore, a meta-analysis of psychotherapeutic interventions for community-dwelling older adults found that both group and individual psychotherapeutic approaches were associated with significant decreases in depressive symptoms [34], supporting our conclusion that psychologically informed digital interventions offer the greatest promise for addressing loneliness and its mental health consequences.

Barriers to equitable adoption

The digital divide remains a critical obstacle to realizing the potential of digital health interventions for loneliness. Our review identified barriers operating at multiple levels. At the individual level, limited digital literacy and low technology self-efficacy are the most frequently cited obstacles [8, 9]. Data from U.S. Medicare beneficiaries indicate that approximately 41% lack the high-speed internet or smartphones required for eHealth, and 38% are unprepared for video telehealth [35]. Among homebound older adults, these disparities are even more pronounced.

Physical and cognitive barriers compound these challenges. Age-related declines in vision, hearing, fine motor dexterity, and processing speed can render small screens, complex interfaces, and multi-step workflows inaccessible without assistance. Attitudinal barriers also play a role: some older adults do not perceive video or telephone consultations as legitimate medical encounters, and many express strong preferences for in-person interaction. Privacy concerns and distrust of digital platforms further contribute to resistance. These barriers are inequitably distributed, disproportionately affecting racial and ethnic minorities, individuals with low incomes, those with limited education, and rural populations.

Addressing the digital divide requires coordinated, multi-level action. At the policy level, ensuring broadband access and affordability is a prerequisite. At the healthcare system level, screening tools such as the Telehealth Literacy Screening Tool [36] can help identify patients needing support. At the community level, integrated programs that combine social services with digital training—such as the Talking Tech program, which delivers digital literacy instruction alongside meal delivery to homebound older adults [35]—represent promising models. At the technology design level, age-friendly interfaces with large fonts, intuitive navigation, accessible audio and visual features, and available technical support are essential.

Strengths and limitations

This review offers a timely, comprehensive synthesis that integrates the most recent evidence on an evolving and clinically important topic. By covering a wide range of modalities—from telephone calls to social robots—it provides a broad overview that complements more narrowly focused systematic reviews. The inclusion of meta-analytic effect sizes enables comparison across intervention types.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a narrative review, this work did not employ a systematic protocol with pre-registered inclusion criteria, formal quality assessment, or PRISMA-compliant reporting. While we searched multiple databases and prioritized high-quality evidence, some relevant studies may have been missed, and the synthesis may be subject to selection and interpretation biases inherent to the narrative approach. Second, the underlying evidence base is itself limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, heterogeneous outcome measures, high attrition rates, and frequent methodological weaknesses (e.g., lack of blinding, inadequate allocation concealment). Third, publication bias—the tendency for studies with positive findings to be more readily published—may inflate the apparent effectiveness of interventions. Fourth, the rapid evolution of technology means that some interventions described here may already be superseded by newer tools or platforms not yet studied. Fifth, the vast majority of included studies were conducted in the USA, Japan, and Hong Kong, which limits the generalizability of findings to European, Latin American, African, and middle-income country contexts, where healthcare infrastructure, cultural attitudes toward aging, and technology access may differ substantially. Sixth, the exclusion of non-English-language publications represents a methodological limitation that may have excluded relevant evidence from non-Anglophone countries, further compounding the geographical bias and potentially underrepresenting promising interventions developed in diverse cultural contexts. Seventh, as a narrative review, this work was not prospectively registered (e.g., in PROSPERO), which is a further limitation inherent to the review design.

Clinical and policy implications

Despite the limitations, several practical implications emerge. Healthcare systems should consider integrating routine screening for loneliness and social isolation into geriatric assessments, using validated instruments such as the UCLA Loneliness Scale or the De Jong Gierveld Scale. For patients identified as lonely, digital interventions should be considered as part of a multimodal approach, with the choice of modality guided by patient preferences, digital literacy, and available support.

The success of simple, low-cost telephone interventions [16] suggests that high-technology solutions are not always necessary. Where they are employed, interventions should be theoretically grounded—targeting mechanisms such as maladaptive social cognitions, limited social skills, or restricted opportunities for interaction—rather than simply providing access to technology. Group-based formats that foster peer connection and mutual support appear particularly promising. Social robots, though still emerging, offer a viable option for older adults with severe mobility limitations or cognitive impairment, where human-delivered interventions may be logistically challenging.

Policymakers should recognize the digital divide as a social determinant of health and invest in broadband infrastructure, digital literacy programs, and subsidized technology access for vulnerable populations. Technology developers should adopt user-centered, co-design approaches that involve older adults in the design process, ensuring that platforms are intuitive, accessible, and responsive to the needs of users with sensory, motor, or cognitive limitations.

Future research directions

Several priorities for future research emerge from this review. First, there is an urgent need for large-scale, well-powered RCTs with adequate follow-up durations (at minimum 6–12 months). Studies should employ standardized loneliness measures and pre-specify the distinction between loneliness and social isolation as separate outcomes. Second, research should attend to moderators and mediators of effectiveness, including baseline severity, living arrangement, cognitive status, cultural context, and digital literacy [37], to enable more targeted intervention matching.

Third, the role of AI and emerging technologies warrants dedicated investigation. The integration of large language models (LLMs) into social robots and conversational agents offers the potential for more naturalistic interactions, but raises ethical questions about consent, dependency, and the boundary between genuine and simulated social relationships. Longitudinal studies of human-robot interaction effects are particularly needed. Fourth, implementation science frameworks should be applied to study the scalability, cost-effectiveness, and sustainability of effective interventions in real-world clinical and community settings [38]. Fifth, equity-focused research should ensure that intervention development and evaluation actively include the populations most marginalized by the digital divide.

Conclusion

Loneliness among older adults is a pervasive, consequential public health challenge demanding innovative, scalable, and accessible solutions. Digital health and telehealth interventions—from empathy-focused telephone calls and group videoconferencing to therapist-supported mobile platforms and AI-driven social robots—offer a diverse and rapidly expanding toolkit. The evidence reviewed here indicates that these interventions can meaningfully reduce loneliness, depression, and anxiety, particularly when they incorporate psychologically informed content, utilize group or interpersonal formats, and are supported by adequate training and technical assistance.

The overall evidence base, however, remains limited by methodological heterogeneity, small studies, and inconsistent meta-analytic results. Moreover, the promise of digital health can only be realized if the digital divide is systematically addressed. Without deliberate efforts to ensure equitable access, build digital literacy, and design age-friendly technologies, digital interventions risk disproportionately benefiting those already least isolated while further marginalizing the most vulnerable. The path forward requires a collaborative, multi-sectoral effort—spanning healthcare, technology, policy, and community sectors—that places older adults at the center of innovation and implementation. Rigorous research, equitable design, and political will are all needed to transform the potential of digital health into meaningful reductions in the loneliness epidemic among aging populations.
