Biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles using Phoma herbarum and evaluation of their fungicidal effects against onion basal root rot disease
Authors: Idris Bektas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a new alternative biofungicidal product in controlling the fungal disease agent, Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cepae, which causes considerable losses during onion storage. In the first stage of this study, silver nanoparticles derived from the microorganism (Phoma herbarum) were biosynthesized using the green synthesis method in nanotechnology. Characterisation tests of the nano product, which was synthesized through the biosynthesis process, confirmed that it is a silver-based nano product with size dimensions ranging from 4 to 40 nm. In the second phase of the study, the fungicidal efficacy of the synthesized silver nanoparticle product, at concentrations ranging from 2.5 to 500 ppm, was investigated against pathogenic fungi. Its effectiveness was evaluated under both in vitro and in vivo conditions. In the in vitro study conducted in petri dishes, the highest mycelial growth, 87.66 ± 0.57 mm, was observed in the control group. Following the application of 2.5 and 5 ppm silver nanoparticles, the mycelium diameter measured 86.66 ± 1.15 mm. However, as the concentration of silver nanoparticles increased, a significant reduction in mycelial growth was observed. At 200 ppm, the mycelial diameter decreased to 10.66 ± 1.15 mm; at 500 ppm, no mycelial growth was observed. In the in vivo study conducted on onion bulbs, the fungicidal activity of silver nanoparticles was evaluated by measuring the disease inhibition rates after the application of silver nanoparticles on the bulbs. When disease inhibition rates are compared in onion bulbs, the dose-dependent rot diameters are as follows: positive control(50.20 ± 0.20 mm); 10 ppm (34.60 ± 0.50 mm); 25 ppm (27.60 ± 0.30 mm); 50 ppm (19.00 ± 0.38); 100 ppm (12.00 ± 0.40); 200 ppm (6.80 ± 0.31 mm) and 500 ppm (1.20 ± 0.17 mm). In addition to these results, different concentrations of silver nanoparticles demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in disease incidence, ranging from 28.89% to 94.42%. When evaluated collectively, the results indicated that silver nanoparticles synthesized through the green synthesis method exhibited varying degrees of fungicidal activity under both in vitro and in vivo conditions, with effectiveness dependent on the concentration applied. Consequently, the synthesized silver nanoparticles product has demonstrated considerable potential as an alternative solution for effectively managing Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cepae a significant threat to stored onion bulbs.

Introduction

Bulb onion (Allium cepa L.) is one of the most marketable and highly demanded vegetable crops in Türkiye and worldwide. It plays a significant role in agricultural economies and food security due to its nutritional value and versatility in culinary applications [1]. According to the latest data from the FAO, global onion production in 2024 reached 78.4 million tons, cultivated over an area of 6.8 million hectares. This resulted in an average yield of 23.9 tons per hectare. According to the 2024 Turkish Statistical Institute data, a significant area of onion was cultivated in Turkey, totaling 604.871 decares (da). This resulted in the production of 2.6 million tonnes of dry onions. However, the cultivation of onions is challenged by various pathogens, particularly Fusarium species (F. oxysporum, F. proliferatum, F. culmorum, F. solani F. redolens, F. falciforme, F. brachigibbosum, F. anthophilium and F. acutatum) which cause root rot and can lead to substantial post-harvest losses [2]. Among these, Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cepae (FOC) has been reported to cause crop losses ranging from 3% to 35%, depending on environmental conditions, variety, and pathogen load. However, the most significant crop losses, reaching up to 40%, are observed during the storage of commercially mature onions [3]. This significant loss underscores the importance of effective management practices to control this disease and protect both the yield and the quality of produce during storage and distribution. It is essential to explore innovative solutions, such as biogenic control agents, to address this persistent challenge and minimize economic impacts on the agricultural sector. Currently, no sources of resistance to rot in onions have been identified; however, effective control of the causal agents may be achieved through the use of chemical pesticides as seed treatments [4]. These chemicals can lead to harmful residues in food products, human health and may have adverse effects on ecosystems [5]. Given the challenges posed by synthetic agrochemicals and the increasing concern over their impact on human health and the environment, there is a pressing need for alternative methods to manage Fusarium root rot in onion cultivation. Recently, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have been studied as antimicrobial agents for their effectiveness in controlling various plant pathogens.These nanoparticles exhibit unique properties that enhance their effectiveness against pathogens, thereby making them a promising alternative to traditional chemical treatments [6]. The AgNPs help prevent pathogens’ development of resistance by enhancing their antimicrobial activity [7]. The antimicrobial properties of AgNPs refer to their ability to inhibit or eradicate the growth of plant pathogens while ensuring the safety of surrounding plant tissues [8]. AgNPs have been shown to inhibit the growth of various plant pathogenic fungi, including Fragaria ananassa [9], Aspergillus templicola [10], Aspergillus niger [11], Botrytis cinerea [12], Fusarium sp [13] and Trichoderma virens [14]. Nanoparticles of different metals can be produced using chemical, physical, and biological methods, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. Chemical methods often involve hazardous substances that pose environmental and health risks and can generate toxic by-products. Physical methods are energy-intensive and require advanced equipment, leading to potential inconsistencies in nanoparticle size. In contrast, biological methods, using plant extracts or microorganisms, offer an eco-friendly and sustainable alternative. They minimize the use of toxic chemicals and tend to produce nanoparticles of uniform size and shape, enhancing their effectiveness across various applications [15]. Mycogenic nanoparticles are recognized for their safety and environmental friendliness. They possess a wide range of applications across various sectors, including agriculture, textiles, medicine, drug delivery, biochemical sensing, and related fields [16].

This study was conducted to synthesize AgNPs using the fast-growing fungus P. herbarum. The resulting nanoparticles were thoroughly characterized through a variety of techniques, including UV–Vis spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and FT-IR analysis. The objective of this study is to evaluate the fungicidal effects of synthesized AgNPs against basal root rot in onions.

Materials and methods

Biomass preparation and synthesis of AgNPs

The fungus P. herbarum (NCBI Acc No. PV390840), stored in Potato Dextrose Agar(PDA) medium, was obtained from the culture collection of the Microbiology Laboratory at Suluova Vocational School, Amasya University, Amasya, Turkiye. Five fungal discs, each measuring 10 mm in diameter, were obtained from the PDA medium using a fungal punch and subsequently inoculated into 250 ml of Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB)(20 g/L). The culture was incubated for 5 days at 28 ± 2 °C and 150 rpm in an orbital shaker (Stuart SSL 1) under dark conditions. Then, the mycelium of the fungi was separated by filtration (Whatman filter paper No. 1) and subsequently washed three times with deionized water to remove any residual media. A 25 g biomass (wet weight) was placed in individual flasks containing 100 mL Milli-Q water and incubated in a shaker at 200 rpm under dark conditions at 28 ± 2 °C 24 h. The biomass was thoroughly filtered, yielding crude cell filtrate [17]. AgNPs were synthesized by mixing 50 mL of cell filtrate with 10 mL of a 1 mM AgNO3 solution in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask. The mixture was then incubated at 28 ± 2 °C in the dark for 24 h. A control flask was maintained without the addition of silver ions. To concentrate the AgNPs, the reaction mixture was centrifuged twice for 10 min each at 10.000 rpm using a fixed-angle centrifuge (Hermle ZZ06A). The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitated AgNPs were collected. They were then rinsed with double-distilled water (ddH2O), thoroughly dried, and stored for further use in the experiments [18].

Analysis of AgNPs synthesized from P. herbarum

The AgNPs were synthesized using an extract derived from the fungus P. herbarum. To gain a deeper understanding of their characteristics, the nanoparticles were subjected to a range of analytical techniques.

UV–spectrophotometry analysis

UV-Vis spectroscopy (Shimadzu, UV-1800, Japan) was used to measure the absorption characteristics in the ultraviolet and visible regions of the electromagnetic spectrum. To achieve optimal uniformity, the purified AgNPs were meticulously dissolved in 1 mL of deionized distilled water using a vortex mixer. Following this, a UV–visible analysis was conducted to evaluate the optical properties of the nanoparticles. The absorption spectrum was recorded from 300 to 800 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer [19].

XRD analysis

The XRD analysis was conducted using a Shimadzu Maxima-X-7000 system to assess the oxidation state and crystal structure of the AgNPs. The analysis was performed at 40 kV and 30 mA using Cu-Kα radiation [20]. The samples were analyzed using X-ray diffraction with a step size of 0.04 and a step time of 0.5 s, covering a 2θ range from 10° to 90°. To calculate the average particle size, the Debye–Scherrer formula was employed:

SEM analysis

In order to visualize the morphology of the synthesized AgNPs at the submicron scale and obtain detailed elemental information at the micron scale. SEM was utilized (JEOL JSM-7001 F-SEM, Japan). For the SEM analysis, samples were cast onto glass slides and dried at 25–30 °C. Once dry, the slides were affixed to copper supports. To enhance conductivity for imaging, a thin gold layer was applied by sputtering, ensuring a coating thickness of 5–20 nm. The surfaces were then examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) at an accelerating voltage of 20–30 kV. This technique enabled precise imaging, revealing the size and shape of the AgNPs and confirming their composition [21].

TEM analysis

The AgNPs were prepared by a reduction method and then diluted 10-fold before being drip-deposited onto carbon-coated copper slides for imaging. The carbon-coated copper slides used for TEM analysis have a grid mesh size of 200. Transmission electron microscopy (JEOL JSM-7001 F, Japan) was used to assess the shape, size, and size distribution of the nanoparticles [22].

EDS analyses

The metallic fraction in the samples was confirmed by energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), which revealed detailed elemental compositions. To further analyze the AgNPs, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) linked with EDS was utilized, employing the advanced JEOL JSM-7001 F microscope known for its precision in nanomaterial characterisation [23].

FT-IR analyses

Fourier- transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was used to investigate the organic functional groups associated with the surfaces of the synthesized AgNPs. The Potassium bromide (KBr) support method was used for FT-IR analysis of the dried fungal extract and AgNPs. In both FT-IR determinations, the KBr pellet was applied at a ratio of 1:100 and the spectrum was recorded using a Jasco 430 model Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (JASCO, Tokyo, Japan) in transmittance mode operating at 4 cm− 1 resolution and the FT-IR spectrum graph was obtained [24].

Pathogen fungal strain

In this research, the FOC isolate used in this study was obtained from the microbiology laboratory of Amasya University Suluova Vocational School [25]. The strain was maintained on PDA.

In vitro effects of AgNPs against FOC

The in vitro effect of AgNPs on FOC colony growth was investigated by solving the AgNPs in PDA medium [26]. To prepare AgNPs concentrations for the experiment, dried and powdered AgNPs were weighed and mixed into 50 ml of sterile PDA medium to create concentrations of 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200 and 500 ppm. The medium was added to Petri dishes to solidification. A 5-mm-diameter agar disc, taken from a 4-day-old FOC colony using a fungal punch, was placed in the centre of the culture medium in the Petri dish. The FOC strain was also grown on PDA without the addition of nanoparticles to serve as a control in the experiment. All Petri dishes were incubated at 28 ± 2 °C in an incubator under dark conditions. After 7 days, colony growth was measured to assess the efficacy of the synthesized AgNPs at inhibiting fungal development. The measurement of the growth diameter was recorded to compare the treatment with the control [27]. Each treatment was performed in triplicate, and the experiment was repeated twice to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the results. Onion bulbs

After the in vitro evaluation of the synthesized AgNPs was completed (against FOC), their potential for controlling post-harvest root rot in onion bulbs was investigated. In this study, Kantartopu onion bulbs, known for their susceptibility to disease, were used [1]. Commercial onion bulbs were obtained from the Department of Plant and Animal Production, Suluova Vocational School, Amasya University. The bulbs were selected for uniform size and health to ensure the reliability of the experiments in the study.

The effectiveness of AgNPs against FOC

The effectiveness of AgNPs in suppressing basal rot symptoms caused by FOC was tested on uniform onion bulbs. Five myclium discs with a diameter of 10 mm were growth in potato dextrose broth (PDB)(40 g L− 1) for ten days in a incubator at 28 ± 2 °C, with shaking on the orbital shaker at 125 rpm. After the incubation period, the cultures were filtered with the Whatman filter paper no. 1 to collect the fungal biomass. The collected fungal biomass was rinsed once with sterile water to remove any residual broth. The concentration of the spore suspension was adjusted to 1 × 106 spores mL− 1 in pure water using a hemocytometer [29]. The onion bulbs were first disinfected by immersing them in a 1% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution for 10 min to eliminate potential contaminants. After the disinfection process was completed, the bulbs were thoroughly rinsed three times with distilled and sterilized water to remove any remaining sodium hypochlorite. Finally, the bulbs were allowed to dry before inoculation. Then, a sterile 5 mm cork borer was used to create a wound (5 × 5 mm) on the each onion basal region [30]. Then, onion bulbs were inoculated with 100 µL 1.0 × 106 spores mL − 1 suspension of FOC. To evaluate the effectiveness of AgNPs at varying concentrations (10, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 500 ppm) 100 µL of AgNPs suspension was inoculated into the wounds infected with FOC. The positive controls in the experiment consisted of onions that had been infected with 100 µL FOC without any AgNPs treatment, while the negative controls were onions without FOC inoculation but with the 100 µL AgNPs at the 500 ppm concentration. In the solvent-control application, only 100 µL of water was inoculated into the onions.

After being air-dried, all treated onions were placed in a plastic box maintained at 28 ± 2 °C with high relative humidity (approximately 80%) for 7 days. Each treatment had five replicates. At the end of 7 days, the rot diameter of lesions around the inoculated wound regions was measured separately with a caliper. These results were then compared with those from the other treatments and the control to assess the efficacy of the AgNPs synthesized from P. herbarum. Statistical analysis

Experiments used a completely randomized block design, and statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics (SPSS 2017, Version 25). Results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The one-way ANOVA with Duncan’s multiple range test (p < 0.05) was employed to assess significant differences [32].

Results

The synthesis of AgNPs was confirmed by the observation of a distinct color change during the reaction. Initially, the reaction mixture was yellow and then turned dark brown, signaling the successful formation of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs). After this observation was completed, the nanoparticles were purified to remove non-reactive substances and by products.

Analysis of AgNPs

The synthesized AgNPs were analyzed using UV–Vis spectrophotometry for property characterization and quality control. The formation of the AgNPs was monitored by measuring the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) over the range of 300–800 nm. A clear and sharp characteristic peak with an absorption ratio of 1.75 was obtained around 429 nm (Fig. 1-A). The “absorption rate of 1.75” refers to the absorbance value measured by UV–visible spectrophotometric analysis of the synthesized AgNPs. This value quantifies the amount of light absorbed by the AgNPs at a specific wavelength, thereby indicating the particle concentration in the solution. Hence, a value of 1.75 suggests a relatively high concentration of nanoparticles, which correlates with their effective biosynthesis.

The XRD analysis verifies the crystalline structure of the AgNPs, as evidenced by five distinct sharp peaks corresponding to specific diffraction planes. Five sharp peaks were obtained in the XRD pattern, and these peaks correspond to the following diffraction planes for the structure of the synthesized silver nanoparticles: (122) at 33.658°, (111) at 38.230°, (200) at 44.400°, (220) at 64.440°, and (311) at 77.246°. The maximum intensity peak found at the (122) plane indicates the ideal orientation of the silver nanoparticles’ structure at 2θ (Fig. 1-B). Additional peaks observed in the graph can be attributed to the biological components present in the extract. The peaks obtained from the analysis correspond closely to the standard Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) data file No. 04-0783, shows strong agreement with the expected crystallographic patterns.

In vitro antifungal effects of AgNPs against FOC

AgNPs have demonstrated notable antifungal activity. Figure 5 illustrates the in vitro antifungal activity of AgNPs at varying concentrations against the phytopathogenic fungus FOC. AgNPs at high concentrations significantly inhibited fungal growth compared to low concentrations. Consequently, inhibition showed a dose-dependent variability. The results of in vitro inhibition assays showed that AgNPs exhibited a dose-dependent antifungal effect against the FOC strain.

The dose-dependent antifungal effects observed at different AgNP concentrations against the FOC strain indicate that AgNPs inhibit mycelial growth to varying degrees (Table 1). The highest fungal growth was recorded in the control treatment, measuring 87.66 ± 0.57 mm.This was followed by the 2.5 ppm and 5 ppm applications; both showed 86.66 ± 1.52 mm fungal growth, and there was no statistically significant difference between them. At an application of 10 ppm, the mycelial growth of the fungi was measured at 65 ± 2.00 mm, resulting in a 25.84% reduction compared to the control group (p < 0.05). When the concentration was increased to 25 ppm, the mycelial diameter decreased to 51.33 ± 1.52 mm, demonstrating a more significant inhibition effect of 41.44% (p < 0.05). At 50 ppm, the growth was further suppressed, with a mycelial diameter of 47 ± 2.00 mm, which reflected a 46.38% reduction compared to the control (p < 0.05). A dramatic effect was observed at 100 ppm, where the mycelial growth declined to 22.66 ± 2.51 mm, yielding a substantial 74.15% inhibition (p < 0.05). However, at 200 ppm, the fungi exhibited minimal growth, only measuring 10.66 ± 1.15 mm, which corresponded to an 87.83% suppression of mycelial growth relative to the control (p < 0.05). Finally, at the highest concentration of 500 ppm, no mycelial growth was observed, resulting in 100% inhibition compared with the control (p < 0.05).

In vivo efficacy of AgNPs against basal rot root of onion

The efficacy of AgNPs against onion fungal diseases, basal root rot, was evaluated through bulb experiments focusing on disease reduction (Fig. 6). AgNPs at different concentrations (10, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 500 ppm) showed a dose-dependent reduction in disease incidence ranging from 28.89% to 94.42% compared to the positive control.

When analyzing the effects of different AgNP concentrations (10, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 500 ppm) on onion rot caused by fungi after 7 days, the rot diameters were measured as follows: 34.60 ± 0.50 mm for 10 ppm, 27.60 ± 0.30 mm for 25 ppm, 19.00 ± 0.38 mm for 50 ppm, 12.00 ± 0.40 mm for 100 ppm, 6.80 ± 0.31 mm for 200 ppm, and 1.20 ± 0.17 mm for 500 ppm. A correlation between the concentration of AgNPs applied and the level of disease reduction observed. The applied concentrations of AgNPs at 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, and 500 ppm resulted in disease reductions applied concentrations 4%, 67.85%, 78.97%, and 94.42%, respectively, compared to the positive control.

Discussion

Previous studies reported that AgNPs can be synthesized by fungi such as Arthroderma fulvum [11], Fusarium concolor [33], Cladosporium cladosporioides [34], Aspergillus sydowii [35], Trichoderma sp [36]. The appearance of color upon addition of AgNO3 solution to the cell-free extract of P. herbarum is attributed to surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of the synthesized AgNPs. This phenomenon, as previously noted by Abdel-Aziz et al. [37], confirms the successful synthesis and presence of AgNPs.

In agreement with the results of this study, previous reports have shown that the UV absorption peaks of AgNPs typically range from 400 to 450 nm [38]. Specific studies have observed peaks at 405 nm [39], 410 nm [40], 419 nm [41], 426 nm [42] and 429 nm indicating a consistent pattern in the synthesis and characterization of AgNPs across different research efforts. In the present study, the absorbance peak was centered at 429 nm, which falls within the crucial 400–450 nm wavelength range associated with green-synthesized silver nanoparticles (AgNPs). This finding aligns with previously reported literature and confirms the successful synthesis and characterization of AgNPs in this study.

The XRD pattern obtained for the synthesized AgNPs confirmed their crystalline nature and indicated a face-centered cubic (FCC) phase. This finding is consistent with many earlier studies reporting similar results for AgNPs synthesized using various fungal extracts [43–45]. In our study, the maximum-intensity peak was observed at 33.658° (122). This observation suggests that the peak intensities of synthesized silver nanoparticles can vary with the synthesis source. Such variations highlight the influence of the synthesis method and the biological material used, which can affect the properties and characteristics of the resulting nanoparticles.

The size of the AgNPs was estimated to be 17.98 ± 0.82 nm, calculated using the Debye–Scherrer formula [46]. In a similar study conducted by Amna et al. [47], the nanoparticles synthesized using Fusarium oxysporum extract were found to have a size of 49.06 nm, as calculated using the Debye-Scherrer equation based on XRD analysis. The size of AgNPs synthesized using C. cladosporioides CBS 174.62 extract ranged between 22.6 and 89.45 nm [48], which is higher than the sizes observed in our study, where the nanoparticles were generally smaller. The average diameter of the AgNPs in that study was 67.87 nm. In this study, observed that the synthesized AgNPs exhibited a size range of 4–40 nm, which differs from findings in other studies that typically diffrent nanoparticle sizes. This variation may arise from differences in the synthesis methods(such as temperature, pH and concentration of reagents) or biological materials used. The smaller size of the AgNPs in our research could enhance their reactivity and efficacy in applications, particularly for controlling pathogens.

These SEM results align well with previous findings. Isaq et al. [49] explored the surface topography of silver nanoparticles synthesized using the fungal endophyte Cladosporium oxysporum through SEM analysis. The uniform dispersion of nanoparticles observed in their study is consistent with our results, reinforcing the conclusion of successful nanoparticle synthesis.

Consistent with earlier research, a pronounced peak at 3 keV was observed in the EDS spectrum, signifying the presence of silver ions [50, 51]. However, there was a slight difference in the chemical composition of AgNPs observed in this study compared to other studies. This could be attributed to variations in the synthesis methods, the biological materials used, or environmental factors during the nanoparticle formation process. For instance, Ibrahim et al. [52] revealed that the biogenic AgNPs contain silver composition of 91.80%.

Alsharif et al. [53] identified the presence of functional groups, including O–H, N–H, and C–N, within the FT-IR spectrum of the biological AgNPs. In a study by Salem et al. [54], the FT-IR spectrum of biological AgNPs was analyzed, revealing the presence of functional groups, including O–H, N–H, C–N, and C–H. Similar reduction states obtained from the obtained FT-IR graph were also observed in many similar fungal AgNPs synthesis studies and recorded in FT-IR graphs [55, 56]. The the small differences in peak size observed in the FT-IR analysis of our study are likely attributable to several factors. First, variations in sample concentration can change peak intensities because a higher concentration may result in more pronounced absorption features. Additionally, slight variations in the synthesis methods, such as in temperature or reaction time, can cause disparities in the structural properties of the synthesized nanoparticles, thereby affecting the functional groups present and their corresponding vibrational modes. Moreover, the homogeneity of the samples is crucial; any aggregation or inconsistency in particle size could influence the spectral response. Finally, the interaction between nanoparticles and the matrix or solvent used to prepare the samples for FT-IR analysis might also contribute to these differences, as it can alter infrared light transmission through the samples. Collectively, these factors can result in small variations observed in peak sizes during FT-IR analysis.

Numerous studies have highlighted the potent antifungal properties of AgNPs, demonstrating their effectiveness in inhibiting fungal spore formation and colony growth. AgNPs inhibit pathogenic fungi by disrupting protein synthesis and degrading fungal hyphal walls. This process results in the release of cellular contents and, ultimately, cell death [57].

Soleimani et al. [58] investigated the synthesis of AgNPs from B. bassiana and M. anisopliae isolates, producing nanoparticles with sizes ranging from 23 to 101 nm. They found that for both isolates, a concentration of 15 µL/mL resulted in complete inhibition (100%) of mycelial growth of Rhizoctonia solani. In a study, the antifungal activity of AgNPs was evaluated against several plant pathogens, including A. alternata, A. brassicae, (A) brassicicola, (B) oryzae, B. cinerea, P. digitatum, (C) higginsianum, C. orbiculare, F. oxysporum, and P. oryzae. The findings revealed that AgNPs significantly inhibited the spore germination of nearly all tested pathogens, highlighting their potential effectiveness as a biocontrol agent in managing fungal diseases in plants [59]. Investigations were conducted to evaluate the growth-inhibitory activity of AgNPs against eighteen plant-pathogenic fungi cultured in vitro on various substrates. The findings indicated that AgNPs can inhibit the growth of these pathogens; however, effectiveness varied with the concentration and type of AgNPs used. Higher concentrations of AgNPs generally resulted in a greater reduction in fungal growth, indicating a potential dose-dependent relationship [60]. Additionally, the efficacy of AgNPs seemed to differ among the fungal species tested, suggesting that specific structural or biological characteristics of the pathogens may influence their susceptibility to AgNPs. In a study, different doses of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) at 1000, 1500, and 2000 ppm inhibited the mycelial growth of Fusarium oxysporum by 64%, 70%, and 74%, respectively [61]. In comparison, the doses employed in our research demonstrated even greater effectiveness, achieving higher percent inhibition across a range of concentrations. For instance, at the highest concentration, 500 ppm, we observed complete inhibition of fungal growth, resulting in a 100% reduction relative to the control. This suggests that the AgNPs synthesized from P. herbarum in our study exhibit superior antifungal activity against FOC compared with that achieved at the previously reported doses in the literature. In a study similar to ours Macías Sánchez et al. [62], investigated the effects of five different concentrations of 38.5 ± 18.5 nm diameter AgNPs on the mycelial growth of Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici (Fol) in vitro. They found that a concentration of 150 mg/L resulted in 100% inhibition of mycelial growth. In our study, a 500 ppm dose of mycelial AgNPs completely inhibited the growth of FOC mycelium. Although the size of the nanoparticles obtained in our study was smaller (17.95 nm), the reason for the observed efficacy at higher doses could be attributed to the variation in the specific strains of Fusarium tested (Fol vs. FOC) could influence the sensitivity to AgNPs. Different fungal species may exhibit varying levels of resistance and susceptibility to antimicrobial agents, including nanoparticles.

As in the present study, morphological alterations in hyphae and conidia were observed in fungal isolates treated with nanoparticles [63, 64]. These changes included variations in hyphal structure and conidial shape, indicating that the antifungal agents had a significant impact on fungal morphology. In particular, The treatment with nanoparticle-based compounds led to noticeable disruptions to the integrity of the fungal cell walls, contributing to the observed inhibition of growth and spore formation.

In a study, Mahdy and Aboelmagd [65], reported a disease index of 22.2 when using 500 ppm AgNPs with diameters ranging from 20 to 500 nm, synthesized by Trichoderma reesei, to control onion white rot disease under greenhouse conditions. However, in our investigation we observed a disease index of zero at the same concentration of 500 ppm AgNPs. This suggests that our synthesized AgNPs may have exhibited a more potent antifungal activity compared to those used in study, leading to a complete suppression of the disease at this concentration. The difference in the disease index results between the two studies may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, the synthesis method and the precise characteristics of the AgNPs, such as size, shape, and surface charge, can significantly impact their antifungal efficacy. In our study, the AgNPs may have had enhanced properties or higher bioavailability, leading to a more effective inhibition of the onion rot root diseases. In a similar study examining the effects of silver nanoparticles on purple onion disease, Das et al. [66] reported inhibition of the in vitro mycelial growth of the pathogen Alternaria porri, with significant reductions at concentrations of 150 ppm (91.71%), 125 ppm (91.24%), and 100 ppm (87.56%). Corresponding in vivo trials further supported these findings, with disease inhibition recorded at 80.93% for 100 ppm and 77.17% for 75 ppm, slightly lower than the in vitro results. In our investigation, the 100-ppm treatment with AgNPs inhibited onion basal rot by 67.85%. The observed difference in antifungal efficacy is likely attributable to the specific pathogens targeted in each study. Ibrahim et al. [67], in a study investigating the control of AgNPs against pea root rot agents M. phaseolina, P. graminicola, R. solani and S.rolfsii, observed that AgNP application alone did not induce any cytotoxic effects on the plants, similar to our findings. This highlights the potential of AgNPs as a safe and effective treatment option for managing fungal diseases without harming host plants. AgNPs exhibit antifungal activity by disrupting the pathogen’s membrane integrity, as demonstrated by Elgorban et al. [68]. This mechanism not only disrupts the cellular structure of the fungi but also prevents its growth and spread, making AgNPs a promising agent in the management of fungal diseases like those affecting onion crops. Their ability to target and damage the membranes of pathogens contributes to their effectiveness as an alternative to traditional fungicides, which may have limited efficacy or pose environmental concerns. Recent research has demonstrated that the application of AgNPs to plants can lead to a range of beneficial and harmful effects [69–71]. These responses depend primarily on several factors, including the concentration of AgNPs, the specific plant species treated, the exposure time to AgNPs, and their shape, size, and other physicochemical properties [72]. Our study showed that AgNPs exert differential effects on pathogenic fungal cells with increasing doses. The proposed mechanism of action suggests that AgNPs interact with the fungal cell membrane, causing membrane disruption that leads to cell death. At lower concentrations, AgNPs may induce oxidative stress in cells, thereby inhibiting growth and reproduction. However, as the concentration increases, the nanoparticles can penetrate the cell wall more effectively and cause more severe damage to cellular components. This connection between the results and the mechanisms of action highlights the potential of AgNPs as antifungal agents while underscoring the importance of dosage in achieving desired therapeutic effects.

Conclusions

In the present study, AgNPs were synthesized using the fungal strain P. herbarum. The findings suggest that AgNPs could be effectively used to develop new antifungal agents to control the fungal disease onion basal rot. Their ability to significantly inhibit the mycelial growth of FOC onion pathogen indicates that AgNPs have strong a biocontrol potential. Further exploration into optimizing AgNPs formulations could lead to innovative solutions for managing fungal infections in agriculture. In future studies, it would be valuable to investigate the effects of various doses of AgNPs on human blood cells to assess their biocompatibility and potential cytotoxicity. However, silver accumulation in plant tissues can be measured. Therefore, further studies will be needed to fully understand the agro-nanotechnological potential of AgNPs derived from P. herbarum.
