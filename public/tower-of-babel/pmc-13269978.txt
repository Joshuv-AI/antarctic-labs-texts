School-Based Educational Interventions for Improving Knowledge of Back Health, Ergonomics and Postural Behaviour of School Children Aged 4-18 Years: A Best Evidence Systematic Review
Authors: Josette Bettany-Saltikov, Robert Mcsherry, Roongtip Duangkaew, Gokulakannan Kandasamy, Garikoitz Aristegui Racero, Paul van Schaik, Julie Hogg, Mona Salman, Fiona McPherson

AbstractBackgroundMusculoskeletal and back health of school aged children is a global health problem, with evidence that the prevalence of these problems is increasing. With the incidence of musculoskeletal pain reported not only in adult populations but increasingly in young people, an understanding of spinal health and posture may be essential for students.ObjectivesThe main objective for conducting this review was to conduct a best evidence systematic review. This means we only included the highest quality papers on the effectiveness of school-based education programs on back health for improving knowledge of back health, ergonomics and postural behaviour in school children aged 4-18 years.Search MethodsWe searched the following 21 electronic databases from inception till the 20th January 2023: AMED (EBSCOhost), APA PsycINFO (EBSCOhost), Best Evidence Medical Education (BEME) (EBSCOhost), British Education Index (EBSCOhost), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Wiley), EMBASE (Ovid), ERIC (EBSCOhost), EThOS (British Library), Europe PMC (Europepmc.org), GoogleScholar. Health Technology Assessment (HTA) (INAHTA), Health Management Information Consortium (HMIC) (Ovid), MEDLINE (EBSCOhost), PEDro (pedro.org.au), ProQuest Dissertations & Theses (Proquest), ProQuest Nursing and Allied Health Database (ProQuest), SCOPUS (Elsevier), SportDISCUS (EBSCOhost), Web of Science (Arts and Humanities Citation Index, Science Citation Index Expanded, Social Sciences Citation Index, Emerging Sources Citation Index, Conference Proceedings Citation Index – Science, Conference Proceedings Citation Index – Social Sciences and Humanities; Clarivate), ZEToC (British Library). Selection Criteria PopulationThe population eligible for the review included all children and young people between 4 and 18 years of age, attending school. Exclusion criteria: Children under 4 years of age and adults over 18 years of age; chronic disease or conditions or co-morbidities.InterventionStudies needed to meet the following criteria to be included: any formal educational school-based programme that included back health, ergonomics and postural behaviour that was designed to support the educational performance of students’ knowledge of posture and ergonomics within an educational establishment. We excluded physical activity or exercise only interventions.ComparisonIncluded studies were required to have a control group that represented either business-as-usual (no intervention), or a wait-list design.OutcomesWe included studies that examined back care knowledge, knowledge of back care ergonomics, back care behaviours and knowledge of back posture.Types of Research DesignThis was a Best Evidence Systematic Review and only research papers scoring a “good” or “excellent “on the Downs and Black (1998) risk-of-bias form were included. The following research designs were included: randomised controlled trials (RCTs), cluster randomised controlled trials (CRCT) and quasi-randomised controlled trials (QRCTs). We included prospective non-randomised studies (NRSs) with a control group because it was anticipated that very few RCTs would be found. Controlled before-after studies (CBAs), interrupted time series (ITS) studies as well as controlled studies that were non-randomised and included a pre- and post-test were also eligible for inclusion.Data Collection and AnalysisTitles and abstracts were screened in duplicate. Full texts of studies with seemingly relevant abstracts were retrieved and assessed for eligibility using the pre-specified inclusion criteria. Full texts were also screened in duplicate. Studies were classified as either included or excluded. Two authors independently extracted data from relevant studies.Main Results1,327 potentially relevant papers were found through the searches of the academic databases, while no studies were identified through searches of grey literature and reference lists. After removing 1,256 articles which did not meet the inclusion criteria, 71 studies remained for screening. 37 articles were excluded based on screening the titles and abstracts. This left 34 articles for further examination of full texts. After the full texts had been read, these 34 studies continued to the quality assessment/risk of bias stage using the Downs and Black ROB form. Only two papers scored 20 or over (i.e. good or excellent) and were included in this systematic review. As the outcomes of the two studies differed, it was not possible to conduct a meta-analysis. No studies achieved an “excellent” score, that provided “excellent” evidence, on the effectiveness of school-based education programs on back health for improving knowledge of back health, ergonomics and postural behaviour in school children aged 4-18 years. This highlights the urgent need for high-quality studies to be conducted to assess the effects of these programmes due to the importance of ergonomics and posture in the activities of daily living of school children.Keywords: back health, educational intervention, school based, knowledge of back health, knowledge of ergonomics, knowledge of postural behaviour, best evidence, systematic review

Background

Description of the Problem, Condition, or Issue

Musculoskeletal and back Health of school aged children is a global health problem with evidence that the prevalence of these problems is increasing (Pavithra & Anand, 2013; Weiguang et al., 2011). As Dugan (2018) states that “many modern school systems require children to assume sedentary positions for extended periods of time, and the increasing use of classroom-based technology adds to the number of hours seated. With the incidence of musculoskeletal pain reported not only in adult populations but increasingly in young people, an understanding of spinal health and posture may be essential for students. Posture is the attitude assumed by the body either when it is stationary or when it is moving. It is attained because of the coordinated action of various muscles working to maintain stability (Dena, 1957). Poor posture is the result of musculoskeletal distortion in the neck and lower and upper back. Most people think of poor posture as simply slumping over, but this is not necessarily the case. Further due to the variety of body types, incorrect posture differs from person to person. One person’s proper posture can be an incorrect posture for someone else and vice versa. Posture holds the body upright against gravity while standing, sitting or lying down. The ideal “normal” erect posture is one in which the line of gravity (the vertical line drawn through the body’s centre of gravity) runs when viewed from each side. In layman`s terms this means that good posture (Carolyn et al., 2017) is the position which is attained when the joints are not bent or twisted, and the spine is aligned. Maintaining good posture involves training one`s body to move and function where the least strain is placed on the bones, joints and soft tissues. Poor posture can result in numerous health problems such as tight neck muscles, muscle soreness, pain (shoulder, neck and back and arms), headaches, poor circulation, physical and mental stress as well as poor sleep.

To prevent the health problems mentioned above, it is important to “fit” any work or school environment to the user’s needs (correct ergonomics) (Pavithra & Anand, 2013). Ergonomics aims to increase efficiency and productivity by reducing discomfort. A sound understanding of ergonomics by students can help prevent school place injuries by adjusting the tools (for example desk, chair, computer screen) to the user, putting an emphasis on proper posture to reduce the impact of repetitive movements or the potential for straining muscles and joints. The use of computers together with the rapidly changing technology in modern schools has greatly increased the need for ergonomics. Desks, chairs, monitors, keyboards and lighting all need to be assessed when creating a work or school space, whether it is at the office, at home or at school. It is important to study ergonomics because faulty ergonomics has been known to cause musculoskeletal aches and pains (Sellschop et al., 2018). Ergonomic assessment, especially in schoolchildren, is gaining ground as the activities and the postures used to perform those activities could be one of the reasons for the high prevalence of pain in this young population, which may lead to chronic pain in adulthood (Pavithra & Anand, 2013; Weiguang et al., 2011).

The International Association for the Study of Pain defines pain as “an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with real or potential tissue injury or described as caused by this injury or whose presence is revealed by visible and/or audible behaviour manifestations”. Recent population-based studies conducted in a range of countries have reported point, one-year, and lifetime prevalence rates of back pain in adult populations of up to 52%, 84%, and 91%, respectively (Steele et al., 2006). However, spinal pain is an issue across the lifespan, not just in adulthood. Cross-sectional surveys conducted in different countries with children and adolescents between the ages of 8 and 16 years of age variably report one-month prevalence rates of up to 39%, and lifetime prevalence rates of back pain up to 69.3% (Kovacs Francisco et al., 2003; Niels et al., 2001; Watson Kath et al., 2002). Although posture as well as movement may not appear to be directly relevant in the context of schooling, in western Europe most courses are either dedicated to improving the brain or the body (Dugan, 2018). Within school settings, the latter are usually considered to be secondary to the former. As Dugan (2018) states “There is, evidence to suggest that the body and brain are inextricably linked, and that physical health is valuable if not essential for educational pursuits; this can be demonstrated by links between body and memory retrieval (e.g. Katinka et al., 2005), logical concept acquisition (Fischer & Brugger, 2011) as well as emotional and personality factors (e.g. Hallee & Stephen, 2004), all of which come into play in the classroom”.

The causes of back pain in youth are challenging and difficult to diagnose and as briefly mentioned above have been theorised to include; mechanical back shape, incorrect posture while using technology (game consoles, computers, Gameboys, iPad, iPhone) and carrying heavy back packs, improper lifting techniques, and incorrect posture during prolonged sitting and standing, together with a lack of back care knowledge and the lack of opportunity to move around frequently (in standing, sitting). These issues may be exacerbated by the educational environment because of inadequate or unavailable school lockers, ill-fitting school furniture and the changing structure of the school day which provides fewer opportunities for movement (Bettany-Saltikov et al., 2008; Cardon et al., 2004; Feingold & Jacobs, 2002; Legg & Cruz, 2004; Sheldon, 1994). Dugan (2018) further suggests that because of the significance of the human body in learning, studies have looked at all the above causes that could potentially impact how students’ bodies function in educational settings. Interventions have also been conducted helping students discriminate between healthy and poor postures, reminding them to sit properly at their desks and computer workstations and modifying their physical habits and routines. Historically, educating children and youth about the importance of back health and posture was an important element of the physical education national curriculum in many countries but was overlooked during the 1980’s when heart health became a greater priority (Tinning, 2001).

The high prevalence rates of back pain in children and adolescents, as well as the predictive value of adolescent spinal pain for spinal pain in adults, have caused several authors to advocate for, and implement, spinal health interventions in the school setting. Cardon et al. (2004) further suggest that guidelines to support teachers made a difference to the back health of school children and strongly recommend that guidelines are formulated. Back care knowledge among children, parents and teachers has also been reported to be very poor (Arghavani et al., 2014). This is further compounded by the lack of media coverage and governmental structured programs to help to inform youth (Bettany-Saltikov et al., 2008; Cardon et al., 2004). This has subsequently resulted in not only children but also parents and teachers being unaware of the prevalence and risk factors of musculoskeletal pain and disorders (Hashemi et al., 2012), resulting in the increase of poor back health behaviours routinely being undertaken in schools (Salminen Jouko et al., 1995).

Numerous authors have also suggested that poor posture, ergonomics and body use in childhood may cause a further increase in these issues in future years (Harreby et al., 1996). It is generally assumed that as “young people are more flexible and adaptive learners than adults this may make childhood the best time to effect postural change and undo the physical harm modern lifestyles can inflict while at the same time setting up young people for healthy body use as adults. If this is true to any degree, elementary schools may be the best opportunity to introduce important concepts about healthy posture to learners” (Dugan, 2018, p. 643). To summarise, the patterns, disciplines and habits, whether correct or incorrect, that are learnt during children`s school years have an influence on the possible development of back pathology in the future. Many authors consider that the appearance and development of back pain among teenagers is a risk factor for back pain in adults (Harreby et al., 1996) with the risk increasing the more pain one has suffered during adolescence (Lise, Charlotte, & Kyvik Kirsten, 2006; Lise, Charlotte, Ohm, et al., 2006).

The Intervention

School environment interventions are strongly supported by the World Health Organisation (WHO) framework for Health Promoting Schools (Vivian et al., 2006). Indeed school-based education interventions for improving knowledge of back health and postural behaviour have been used in numerous countries worldwide to improve children’s and adolescents’ knowledge of back health, postural awareness and behaviour (Dugan, 2018). Back health educational programs aim to decrease the risk of spinal, back and other musculoskeletal problems which may lead to disability and musculoskeletal pain both in the present and in the future (Arghavani et al., 2014; Hashemi et al., 2012). Whilst numerous school-based education programmes have been implemented to reduce smoking, decrease alcohol consumption and teenage pregnancies, increase physical activity and healthy eating, and prevent obesity in children and youth, educational programmes teaching children and youth about the importance of back health, posture and ergonomics, together with ways of preventing back pain, have not received as much attention. There is currently no standard school-based educational program for improving knowledge of back health, ergonomics and postural behaviour of school children. These programs are designed to support students’ academic success in educational establishments. Educational establishments, or schools are broadly defined as institutions dedicated to education. These interventions generally engage school children in some form of active learning that cognitively and physically engages them in learning to improve knowledge, ergonomics and postural behaviour (Dugan, 2018). The programs described in the literature vary from country to country as well as within countries. The contents of the education back health programs have included lectures or lectures with actual demonstrations, practical sessions, workshops, individual lessons, class group lessons, curriculum lessons, posters, hands-on learning as well as educational modules. These studies have varied in their aims, the teachers teaching the intervention, the duration and intensity of the interventions as well as the content and strategies of the programs. These are discussed in turn below.

Aims of the Educational Programs

The aims of the programs are varied and have included all the following: to decrease the prevalence of back pain, to improve body mechanics and improve posture while performing various tasks, to improve the safety as well as methods of wearing a backpack, to teach acceptable sitting postures, safe lifting techniques and sports injury prevention procedures, to train students to make appropriate and safe decisions regarding the use of their bodies in order to prevent the onset of back pain as well as to improve students’ knowledge of back care principles (Steele et al., 2006).

Teachers of Back Health Programs

Steele et al.'s (2006) systematic review on school-based interventions for spinal pain reported that the teachers of these back-health education programs are diverse and have included all of the following: the classroom teacher, physical therapists, occupational therapists, physical education teachers as well as physical therapy students.

Duration and Intensity of These Program

The duration and intensity of these programs have varied widely. They range from one 30 min session, one 60 min session, 6 one-hour sessions over 6 weeks, one session with an unspecified duration, 11 sessions over 8 weeks (total 19 hours), 3 sessions of unspecified duration, and 3 years duration with no intensity specified (Steele et al., 2006).

Content of the Back-Health Programs

The reported content of these programs (Steele et al., 2006) have included the following: principles of Swedish back school, anatomy, physiology, spinal care principles and exercises, how to choose, lift and wear backpacks safely, how to recognise when a backpack is too heavy, exercises combined with behavioural intention and self-monitoring, biomechanics and risk factors for injury as well as how to incorporate this knowledge into everyday life using lifting techniques.

Strategies for the Delivery of These Programs

The strategies for the delivery of these programs have included a variety of approaches. Cardon et al.'s (2004) back health program has included teacher and parent involvement, and posters in the classroom or have been included within the curriculum where guided self-discovery and active hands-on methods were used. Goodgold and Nielsen (2003) used a whole-school approach with teacher and parent involvement, posters in the classroom and inclusion in the curriculum of the following: lecture, worksheets, demonstration, hands-on activity. In the Goodgold and Nielsen (2003) study, the strategies for the delivery of these programs were altered slightly to cater to a younger age group. Mendez Francisco and Antonia (2001) used teacher and parent involvement whilst also including posters in the classroom. The program was included in the curriculum as lectures, demonstrations, and practice. Robertson and Lee (1990) included the information in the Curriculum only and comprised of lectures, worksheets, games, demonstrations and practice whilst Schwartz & Jacobs (1992) strategy was included in the curriculum only: and comprised a lecture, demonstration and practice. Sheldon’s (1994) strategy was also only included in the curriculum and comprised a lecture and demonstration practice. Lastly, Spence et al.’s (1984) strategy was also only included within the curriculum and comprised a lecture, demonstration OR guided self-discovery.

How the Intervention Might Work

Many schools have traditionally held a “transmissionist” or “instructionist” model in which a teacher or lecturer ‘transmits’ information to pupils, for instance giving a lecture or presentation. In contrast, Vygotsky’s theory promotes learning contexts in which pupils play an active role in learning. The roles of the teacher and pupil are therefore shifted, as a teacher collaborates with his or her pupils to help facilitate meaning construction in their pupils. Further when an educational intervention is planned for children, for optimal learning to occur, children taking part need to have a greater reliance on so-called ‘hands-on’ learning, or learning by doing (Hartman Beth et al., 2000). Hartman Beth et al. (2000) focused on the effect of learning by doing versus learning by demonstration. These authors stated that children who had participated in a ‘hands-on’ project had a significantly greater amount of recall of the task than children who only had a demonstration. The children who were taught in the ‘hands-on’ condition also had a greater memory recall of the process. The results of this study appear to support the idea that ‘hands-on’ learning is more effective than verbal instruction for fostering retention and recall of the steps or the procedures of a skill in children.

Furthermore, a contemporary theoretical framework called the dual-process model Wendy et al. (2016) was recently developed for interventions that improve children’s physical health at school. The model draws on various theories and other models, including the elaboration likelihood model (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) and social cognitive theory (Bonell et al., 2013). Behaviour change is promoted through an automatic pathway to target habits and a reflective pathway to target knowledge, attitude and self-efficacy. Methods include the provision of rewards and positive reinforcement for habits, active learning and advance organisers for knowledge, and mere exposure and positive reinforcement for attitude and goal setting, monitoring and feedback for self-efficacy.

Why it is Important to do This Review

Research in this area has to date not received much attention. As (Dugan, 2018) states and as already stated above “most modern school systems require children to assume sedentary positions for extended periods of time, the increasing use of classroom-based technology adds to the number of hours seated” (Dugan, 2018). With the incidence of musculoskeletal pain reported not only in adult populations but increasingly in young people, knowing whether back health educational programs are effective on spinal health, ergonomics and posture is a very important consideration for all stakeholders: pupils, teachers, parents, researchers as well as clinicians. Dugan’s (2018) recent systematic review on the diverse range of posture interventions used within primary schools suggests that “although approaches to promoting postural health in primary schools vary, studies could be compared in terms of their impetus (e.g. low back pain in students, increasing classroom technology as well as aims and methodologies). Trends in the literature include delivery by posture experts (as opposed to homeroom teachers), examination of both sitting and moving postures in children and the impact of computer use on musculoskeletal heath. Much of the literature however relied largely on self-report data and assessment instruments were wide-ranging”.

Steele et al. (2006) in another systematic review evaluating the effectiveness of school-based interventions on spinal pain was published 20 years ago. Twelve papers were included in that review with all papers receiving a “weak” quality rating. The result of the systematic review indicated that educational school-based back health interventions may be effective in increasing spinal care knowledge and decreasing the prevalence of spinal pain. However, overall, as the evidence was weak the results were inconclusive regarding spinal care behaviours. A more recent systematic review in a related area by Bonell et al. (2013) looked at other school-based educational interventions but did not include educational interventions to improve back health knowledge and posture. Bonell et al. (2013), concluded, however, that whilst there is definitely the potential for school environment interventions to promote young people’s health, the evidence base is far from definitive. Completing such an educational programme may help children improve their knowledge and develop an understanding of the importance of postural, ergonomic, and spinal back-health education for the prevention of back pain at a young age as well as later in adulthood. As stated previously the causes of LBP in youth are challenging and difficult to diagnose and have been theorised to include a lack of back care knowledge together with lack of knowledge regarding the best way of dealing with associated back problems. All the following: poor mechanical back shape, incorrect posture while using technology (game consoles, computers, Gameboys, iPhone), carrying heavy back packs, improper lifting techniques, incorrect posture during prolonged sitting and standing, lack of back care knowledge and the lack of opportunity to move around frequently (in standing, sitting) which may interfere with the educational environment. Inadequate or unavailable school lockers and ill-fitting school furniture have also been implicated (Bettany-Saltikov et al., 2008; Cardon et al., 2004; Feingold & Jacobs, 2002; Legg & Cruz, 2004; Sheldon, 1994). Therefore, as research in this area is still controversial, a systematic review conducted with state-of-the-art, high-quality Campbell Collaboration methodology is urgently needed to provide all stakeholders (“parents, children, teachers, researchers and clinicians) with the latest evidence to help inform policy and practice in this area of public health.

Objectives

The main objective for conducting this review was to conduct a Best Evidence Systematic Review. Only the highest-quality papers or best evidence on the effectiveness of school-based education programs on back health for improving knowledge of back health, ergonomics and postural behaviour in school children aged 4-18 years were included. The review seeks to gather, summarise and integrate the best empirical research on the review question so as to help people understand the evidence: for school children and adolescents aged 4-18 how effective are back care educational programs as compared with no back-care education for improving knowledge regarding ergonomics and posture as well as postural behavior?

Criteria for Considering Studies for This Review

Types of Study

In this study we included the following research designs; randomised controlled trials (RCTs), cluster randomised controlled trials and quasi-randomised controlled trials (QRCTs). If a study was a well-controlled RCT, then the experimental groups were assumed to be equal at the start of the study. Prospective non-randomised studies (NRSs) with a control group were also included because it was anticipated that very few RCTs would be found. These included controlled before-after studies (CBAs) and interrupted time series (ITS) studies. Controlled studies that were nonrandomised were required to have a pre and post-test. The studies needed to include, at least, a treatment and a control group. Only studies of the highest quality that scored “good” or “excellent quality” quality as assessed by the Downs and Black (1998), risk-of-bias form were included. Retrospective studies as well as qualitative studies were excluded. We also excluded narrative and other types of non-systematic reviews (e.g. critical reviews, overviews, state-of-the-art reviews), clinical practice guidelines, evidence summaries, critically appraised topics, clinical paths, consumer information sheets, best practice information sheets, technical reports, and other evidence-based pieces.

Type of Participant

We included all children and young people between 4 and 18 years of age, attending school. Exclusion criteria were children under 4 years of age and adults over 18 years of age, chronic disease or conditions or co-morbidities. We excluded studies in which all subjects in the sample presented with pain, spinal diseases or surgical vertebral treatments.

Types of Intervention

The intervention of interest in this systematic review was any formal educational school-based program that included back health, ergonomics and postural behavior that was designed to support the academic success of students’ knowledge of posture and ergonomics within an educational establishment. Educational establishments, or schools are broadly defined as institutions dedicated to education. To be eligible, the interventions must have engaged school children in some form of active learning that cognitively and physically engages them in learning to improve their knowledge of ergonomics and postural behavior. The contents (lectures or lectures with actual demonstrations and practice, workshops, individual lessons, class group lessons, curriculum lessons, educational modules), length (hours, days, weeks, months, years) and manner of delivery (face to face, face to face with complementary materials, group and individual practical participation, observations) of the program may have varied in each of the studies to be included, as there is no standard school-based educational program for improving knowledge of back health, ergonomics and postural behaviour of school children. We planned to exclude physical activity or exercise only interventions. The control condition needed to include “usual” health and physical education programs provided by schools or no educational school program on back health and posture interventions. Studies comparing the effects of back health education programs to another type of back health intervention, were excluded.

Types of Outcome Measure

Primary Outcomes

To the researchers’ knowledge there is no consensus regarding indicators for outcome measurement in the evaluation of educational and health promotion programs. While a change in a health outcome is the overall goal of most programs, often such changes may not occur within the evaluation timeframe, and intermediate endpoints must also be measured to gauge effectiveness. Intermediate endpoints often evaluated after the implementation of a health promotion program are the level of knowledge regarding the health issue, and the frequency in which relevant health behaviors are undertaken. The construction and administration of the outcome measures are typically developed by primary study authors. There may, however, be the occasional paper where these measures are both standardized and validated. Most measures of self-report appear to be undertaken by the youth themselves.

We included studies that examined at least one of the following outcome measures.

1. Back care knowledge

1. Back care knowledge

2. Knowledge of back care ergonomics

2. Knowledge of back care ergonomics

3. Back care behaviors

3. Back care behaviors

4. Knowledge of back postural outcomes (primary and secondary)

4. Knowledge of back postural outcomes (primary and secondary)

Any outcomes not mentioned above that were related to postural behavioral change were included. We intended to include studies that used validated outcome measures that related to the knowledge and/or understanding of all the above using the results of surveys, actual measurements, and other validated specific questionnaires.

Secondary Outcomes

Secondary outcomes included any adverse effects, for example pain or stiffness or other adverse effects reported in the included studies. If adverse effects were reported that were not listed here, we would have still reported them in our review.

Duration of Follow-Up

We evaluated any educational programs where pupils were followed up for 6 months, one year and 2 years.

Types of Setting

Settings included any educational setting and excluded any studies undertaken at a university (greater than 18 years of age) level or any kindergarten educational setting that taught children aged under 4 years.

All searches included non-English language literature. Government publications or those of international organisations were not specifically targeted during searches, so that could be acknowledged as a limitation. However, the HMIC (His Majesty’s Inspectorate of Constabulary and Fire & Rescue Service) database was searched, and this incorporates Department of Health official publications. The search strategy aimed to find both published and unpublished studies. A three-step search strategy was utilized in this review. An initial limited search of MEDLINE and CINAHL was undertaken, followed by the analysis of the text words contained in the title and abstract, and of the index terms used to describe the article. A second search using all identified keywords and index terms was then undertaken across all included databases. Thirdly, the reference list of all identified reports and articles was searched for additional studies. There was no restriction by date of publication. Specific conferences were not targeted during the searches, but conference proceedings were identified through database searches that indexed this type of publication, such as Web of Science and SCOPUS.

Data Collection and Analysis

Selection of Studies

Two authors independently screened the search outputs and abstracts for relevant studies. Full texts of studies with seemingly relevant abstracts were retrieved and assessed for eligibility using the pre-specified inclusion criteria. Studies were classified as either included, excluded, awaiting assessment, or on-going. Two authors independently extracted data from relevant studies. In the case of differences in the extracted data, the authors discussed these to reach a consensus. If unresolved, these were discussed with a third author. In the case of missing data, we contacted the original study author for clarification.

A data selection form was first developed on the basis of the inclusion criteria and then piloted and tested for both intra-observer and inter-observer reliability by two review authors, who then independently screened the search results by reading titles and abstracts. Potentially relevant studies were obtained in full text and once again they were independently assessed for inclusion by two review authors, who resolved disagreement through discussion. A third review author was contacted if disagreements persisted. If a review author was also the author of a paper, another review author who has not authored any of the papers undertook the selection.

Data Extraction and Management

Two authors (JBS and RMcs) independently extracted the data from the results section of the two included study. Any disagreements were referred to a third author (GK). For the Gallardo study which was written in Spanish one of the authors GAR who is Spanish, translated, collected and extracted the data from the Gallardo paper.

Assessment of Risk of Bias in Included Studies

The quality assessment or risk of bias for both randomised studies and NRSs was assessed using the criteria from the Downs and Black checklist, as outlined in Appendix 2. Originally, we had stated that we would use both the Downs and Black form as well as the Furlan critical appraisal form, but we found it too complicated to combine the two. We therefore only used the Downs and Black criteria.

The Downs and Black Checklist (D) is a tool used to critically appraise the quality of quantitative (randomised and non-randomised) research studies. It assesses five key areas: study quality, external validity, study bias, confounding and selection bias, and the power of the study.

Results

Results of the Search

The PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1) below shows the number of papers identified in the search for literature, included and excluded in the study selection process. 1,327 possibly relevant papers were found through the searches of the 21 academic databases, as well as searches through the grey literature and reference lists. After removing 1,256 duplicates, 71 remained for screening. 37 articles were excluded based on screening the titles and abstracts. This left 34 articles for further examination of full texts. After reading the full texts, all the 34 studies continued to the quality assessment stage. We conducted a Best Evidence Synthesis using the Downs and Black risk of bias form which quantifies the quality of a paper by using an overall mark based on the individual grade of each question as well as for each section. If a paper was marked as having 14 points or under it was classed as a poor paper; 15-19 points was considered fair, good was considered to be between 20-25 points and excellent is 26-28 points. For the purposes of this study only the best evidence was included i.e. only 2 papers that achieved a score of 20 or over (good and excellent) were retained and included within this review, whilst papers that received a score of 19 and under (n = 32) were exluded.

Discussion of the Outcomes of Each Study

Candotti’s Outcomes (2012)

The questionnaire in this study measured the theoretical knowledge of the spine, with a top score of 18. The study reported control versus intervention group comparisons. Control group scores for knowledge and behaviour showed no significant change over time. Experimental groups improved significantly after the intervention, and while scores dropped slightly at follow-up, they remained above baseline levels.

Gallardo’s Outcomes (2013)

Theory Underlying Intervention

The intervention is based on the premise that heavy backpacks can cause back pain in children. Numerous research papers show, that carrying more than 10–15% of a child’s body weight can affect spinal health and cause pain. The study aimed to reduce backpack weight through an educational session. In the intervention group, a researcher measured students’ backpack and body weight at three points: before, immediately after, and three months after the session. During the session, students learned to carry only necessary school items, and their backpacks were reweighed afterward. In the control group, measurements were taken only at the start and after three months, with no educational session. All measurements were done consistently to ensure accuracy.

Collective Summary of What the Two Intervention Studies Showed

In summary this systematic review comprised of the following two studies:

The Candotti et al. (2011) study focused on a postural education program and demonstrated significant improvements in knowledge and behaviour related to back health.

The Gallardo et al. (2013) assessed backpack weight management and reported that students who received the intervention were significantly less likely to carry excessive backpack weight

Both studies showed that educational interventions improved students’ knowledge about ergonomics and posture, but the effects faded over time. This review highlights the need for high-quality research and standardized educational programs to address back health and ergonomics in schools effectively. In summary, both studies demonstrated short-term improvement.

Overall Discussion

Summary of Main Results

This systematic review investigated the effectiveness of educational interventions developed to improve knowledge regarding ergonomics and posture, as well as postural behaviour in children aged 4-18 years including primary, secondary and sixth form students (ages 16-18 years). The comprehensive search strategy retrieved 1327 articles that could potentially answer the research question of this review. After sifting the titles and abstracts of the identified papers based on the inclusion criteria (PICOT), the authors identified 71 potentially relevant articles. A total of 37 papers were excluded as detailed in Table 1 and the PRISMA flow chart Figure 1. The remaining 34 papers were then assessed for risk of bias (ROB) using the Downs and Black, (1998) checklist. This checklist was designed to evaluate the methodological quality of both randomised and non-randomised studies (a copy of this form can be found in Table 5). No studies achieved excellent (scores 26-28) but the two included studies were found to have ‘good’ scores (20-25). The results from the two included studies indicated that at three months after the interventions, both interventions were effective compared to the control group.

Discussion of the Risk of Bias in the Two Included Papers

The risk of bias table can be found above in Table 5 above.

Reporting of Papers

Both studies scored 22/28 on the Downs and Black form, indicating a lower risk of bias and classification as good papers. Although both studies received the same final score, they achieved different scores for various sections of the assessment. In the initial part of the assessment, the reporting of the papers was evaluated. The Candotti paper scored 9/12 points (75%), while the Gallardo paper scored 10/12 points (83%). Overall, the Gallardo paper had the highest score with the lowest bias in the reporting section.

External Validity

Both included studies achieved a perfect score of 3/3 (100%) for external validity. This finding suggests that the results of both studies could be generalized to the population from which the study subjects were drawn.

Internal Validity - Confounding Bias

Candotti et al. (2011) scored 4/5 (80%) points in this section, whereas Gallardo et al. (2013) scored 5/5 points (100%). This indicates high internal validity for both authors, though the Candotti et al. (2011) paper was not as well designed as the one by Gallardo et al. (2013) To adequately control confounding bias, data collection must include all factors related to both treatment choice and the outcome of interest.

Internal Validity (Selection Bias)

Both papers scored 3.7 points (43%) in this section. None of the papers scored highly here. To adequately control for selection bias, data collection must include all factors related to why certain students participated in a study and others did not.

Internal Validity (Confounding Bias)

The next item, also internal validity, refers to how well the study was executed. Both papers scored 3/7 points (42%) for confounding bias, suggesting that improvements are necessary in this area.

The authors believe that there are potentially confounding variables present which are interfering with the study’s ability to truly separate out the effect of the intervention and thus the reported impact of the intervention may be over/underestimated.

Power analysis

Both studies conducted a power analysis and scored 22 points (79%) each.

Power Calculations

“Power” is the probability of avoiding a type II error in a comparative study. A type II error occurs when the false null hypothesis is not rejected, indicating no effect or difference despite its existence. Small sample sizes result in lower power and increase type II error risk, potentially suggesting ineffective interventions when they are effective, thus biasing results. The 2 included studies scored high on power analysis. However, other studies may not have been published and therefore not reviewed here, because of their low statistical power, non-significant results and small effect sizes. Therefore, the effect size of the reviewed studies could be an overestimate of the true effect. Overall, whilst both studies scored well and were classed as good-quality studies on the overall Downs and Black risk of bias forms, the Candotti (75%) paper lost points in the reporting section which means this could be improved. The Gallardo’s paper scored 83% in this section. Furthermore, all papers scored very highly 3/3 for external validity which means that the results of the papers have very good generalisability. The internal validity for the overall bias for both papers scored highly with Candotti scoring 4/5 whilst the other paper scored 5/5. We found the Downs and Black RoB form relatively easy to understand and fill in. The large number of questions assessing each aspect of the quality appraisal and risk of bias makes it easier than other forms (which may have only half the number of questions) to identify where and for what reason each paper lost points in the evaluation.

Limitations of the Review Processes

The main limitation of our review in our opinion were the number of authors involved. When conducting the selection as well as the evaluation of the quality of studies, our group was split into 3 groups of 2 assessors to make sure the assessments were as rigorous and valid as possible. However, problems arose for a couple of the papers when the overall value for the ROB assessment between the pairs differed by several points. We believe this was due to the fact that one pair of assessors had much more experience in critiquing papers than the other pair. Another way to view this is that the assessors were not sufficiently well trained in making the assessments prior to doing so.

Another limitation is the fact that Government publications or those of international organisations were not specifically targeted during searches. However, the HMIC database was searched, and this database incorporates Department of Health official publications. Additionally, Government information was not identified because the likelihood of finding references that would use the included study designs was limited. It is also important to remember that the latest searches were conducted in 2023 and there may well be published papers since that time that might be eligible for inclusion. Furthermore, although comprehensive, sensitive and robust, it is possible that our search strategy failed to identify additional references.

Agreements and Disagreements With Other Studies or Reviews

The primary distinction between this systematic review and others in the field lies in its best-evidence synthesis approach, which included only the highest quality papers, not just in terms of the level of evidence but also as to how well each RCT was operationalised. In contrast, other systematic reviews considered all papers meeting the inclusion criteria, including those of poor and fair quality. The systematic reviews by Minana-Signes et al. (2021) and Anyachukwu et al. (2024) were the most comparable to this one, as they only included randomized control trials (RCTs). While RCTs are generally regarded as providing the highest quality evidence, these trials may suffer from flaws in their operationalization and execution.

Futhermore, whilst in our review we searched through 21 databases for relevant papers from inception till 2023, the AnyaChukwu et al. (2024) systematic review included only 5 databases, and the search was conducted between the years 2000-2022. The PEDRO scale was used to assess the quality of papers in this review. AnyaChukwu et al. (2024) found 8 studies that met their criteria whilst the Minana-Signes et al. (2021) review included 7 databases and reported finding 6 RCT’s. AnyaChukwu et al.’s (2024) review only included papers in English whereas Minana-Signes’s et al. (2021) review reported studies in both English and Spanish. Our own review included papers in five languages. With regards to the outcomes, those reported in the other two reviews were similar to the ones reported in our review, but both also included pain as an outcome, which was not investigated in our own review. Furthermore, Minana-Signes’s et al.'s (2021) review had a focus on student-centred approaches, which neither our own review nor Anyachukwu et al.’s (2024) included.

Our systematic review included many of the same research papers as the two other reviews, but we excluded a number of these papers, at the risk-of-bias stage, if they had not scored 20 points or higher. We used the Black and Downs risk of Bias form with 28 questions (see Appendix 2), whilst Minana-Signes et al. (2021) and Anyachukwu et al. (2024) used the PEDro scale with 10 questions. We found the Black and Downs assessment was more discriminating than the PEDro scale. For instance, the Habybabady et al. (2012) paper scored 6/10 (60%) in the other two reviews but only 13/28 (46%) in ours. So, in our review this was classified as a “poor study” and excluded. This suggests the need to compare the two different critical appraisal forms in this research area to understand where the score differences occurred. Having said this, the association between the overall PEDro and Downs and Black critical appraisal form was evaluated by L’Aubut et al. (2013), who found moderately high correlation scores but in a study on acquired brain injury (r = 0.71, p < 0.01); this indicates a significant relationship between these two quality assessment tools for the assessment of acquired brain injury. Despite the positive correlation, however, B&D may produce relatively low scores and PEDro relatively high scores. Finally, similar to our own review, both Anyachukwu et al. (2024) as well as Minana-Signes et al. (2021) concluded that Back Health Educational interventions were effective and needed to be implemented for school children.

It is acknowledged that completing risk-of-bias forms is subjective, as scores depend not only on assessors’ interpretations but also on their knowledge and experience in research methodology. In our review, each paper was independently evaluated by two pairs of scorers who then reached a consensus. Despite this method, final scores varied significantly in a couple of papers. For example, one pair scored an RCT at 22 points, while another scored it at 14 points, showing an 8-point difference. A strength of our review was using two independent sets of assessors for risk of bias, ensuring a comprehensive and rigorous evaluation. However, comparing different studies remains challenging due to the various quality appraisal/risk of bias forms. To our knowledge little is known as to how the PEDro scale and the Downs and Black compare in the evaluation of back health education papers as both were developed to assess clinical papers and not education research.

Authors’ Conclusions

Implications for Practice and Policy

This systematic review aimed to answer the following question: “For school children and adolescents aged 4-18 how effective are back care educational programs as compared to no back-care education for improving knowledge regarding ergonomics and posture as well as postural behavior?” This review found “good” evidence from 2 RCT’s of what the educational program impacts are, of implementing this approach, such as improved knowledge regarding ergonomics and posture as well as postural behavior in school children. The Gallardo study also provided evidence that the children carried bags that were of much less weight after their intervention. Whilst many authors and programs have provided evidence of the effectiveness in terms of knowledge regarding ergonomics, and postural behavior, these studies were excluded from this best evidence synthesis review due to being of poor or fair quality. The inclusion of the 2 studies by Candotti and Gallardo demonstrate that in the short term (3-8 months) postural education programs appear to be effective in improving knowledge of back health posture, ergonomics and behaviour; however, because we could only include 2 good papers, both of which suffered from problems of selection bias and confounding bias, and no excellent papers, we are unable to make firm conclusions and recommendations for policy and practice. The results suggest that implementing postural and ergonomic educational programs in primary/secondary schools significantly and positively impacts children’s knowledge of back health. Further studies are needed to evaluate different approaches to educating children about back health, including the latest technology and computer applications, such as those available on smartphones and tablets.

Implications for Research

As stated above, future studies need to rigorously investigate the impact of back health educational interventions on knowledge related to back health, ergonomics, and posture, utilizing high-quality research designs. It is imperative that these studies encompass children across the complete age spectrum from 4-18 years, including those in kindergarten, primary and secondary schools, as well as sixth-form colleges. The studies we included only comprised children aged 10-13. Systematic reviews should incorporate well-conducted randomised controlled trials (RCTs), which are regarded as the gold standard for evaluating interventions. However, it is essential that these RCTs are conducted and operationalised at the highest level. This is because we found that several of the RCT papers did not score highly on the Downs and Black risk-of-bias (ROB) form. Furthermore, authors need to possess a comprehensive understanding of research designs and methodology to accurately assess research papers and exclude non-randomized studies due to their lower evidence quality. Implementing blinded research to mitigate performance bias is recommended, though challenging within school settings. Furthermore, considering the limitations of ROB forms, only one form designed for assessing the quality of educational interventions was found. Therefore, most systematic reviews in this domain rely heavily on critical appraisal or risk of bias forms intended for clinical health research papers, potentially leading to errors.
