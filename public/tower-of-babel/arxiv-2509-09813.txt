Nearly optimal algorithms to learn sparse quantum Hamiltonians in physically motivated distances
Amira Abbas, Nunzia Cerrato, Francisco Escudero Gutiérrez, Dmitry Grinko, Francesco Anna Mele, Pulkit Sinha

Abstract
We study the problem of learning Hamiltonians H H that are s s -sparse in the Pauli basis, given access to their time-evolution operators. Although Hamiltonian learning has been extensively investigated, two issues recur in much of the existing literature: the absence of lower bounds establishing optimality and the use of mathematically convenient but physically opaque error measures. We address both challenges by introducing two physically motivated notions of distance between Hamiltonians and designing a nearly optimal algorithm with respect to one of these metrics. The first, the time-constrained distance , quantifies distinguishability through dynamical evolution up to a bounded time. The second, the temperature-constrained distance , captures distinguishability through thermal states at bounded inverse temperatures. We show that s s -sparse Hamiltonians with bounded operator norm can be learned under both distances using only O ~ ​ ( s ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) ) \widetilde{O}(s\log(1/\varepsilon)) experiments and O ~ ​ ( s 2 / ε ) \widetilde{O}(s^{2}/\varepsilon) total evolution time. For the time-constrained distance, we further establish lower bounds of Ω ⁡ ( ( s / n ) ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) + s ) \Omega((s/n)\log(1/\varepsilon)+s) experiments and Ω ⁡ ( s / ε ) \Omega(\sqrt{s}/\varepsilon) total evolution time, demonstrating near-optimality in the number of experiments. As an intermediate result, we obtain an algorithm that learns every Pauli coefficient of s s -sparse Hamiltonians up to error ε \varepsilon in O ~ ​ ( s ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) ) \widetilde{O}(s\log(1/\varepsilon)) experiments and O ~ ​ ( s / ε ) \widetilde{O}(s/\varepsilon) total evolution time, improving upon several recent results. The source of this improvement is a new isolation technique , inspired by the Valiant-Vazirani theorem (STOC’85), which shows that NP is as easy as detecting unique solutions . This isolation technique allows us to query the time evolution of a single Pauli coefficient of a sparse Hamiltonian—even when the Pauli support of the Hamiltonian is unknown—ultimately enabling us to recover the Pauli support itself.

1.1
Problem statement

Given a quantum system of
n
qubits, its dynamics and thermal behaviour are governed by a Hamiltonian
H
, which formally is a self-adjoint matrix of
(\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2})^{\otimes n}
. As such,
H
can be expanded in the Pauli basis,

H=\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}h_{P}P,

where
h_{P}=\Tr[HP]/2^{n}
are the Pauli coefficients. The set
\mathcal{H}=\{P:h_{P}\neq 0\}
is the Pauli support of the Hamiltonian, and the set
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}=\{P:\ |h_{P}|\geq\varepsilon\}
is the
\varepsilon
-effective support of the Hamiltonian.

By the Schrödinger equation, the dynamics of a system governed by a Hamiltonian
H
is determined by the time-evolution operator
U(t)=e^{-iHt}
, meaning that if the state of the system at time
0
is
\rho,
then at time
t
it will evolve to
U(t)\rho U^{\dagger}(t)
. Thus, a natural access model for Hamiltonians, introduced in
[
HTFS23
]

experiments
of the following kind: prepare a state
\rho,
apply
U(t_{1})
(this involves making a query to
U(t_{1})
, which in a lab can be implemented by letting the system evolve for time
t_{1}
), apply a unitary operator
V_{1}
independent of
H
, query
U(t_{2})
, apply a unitary operator
V_{2}
independent of
H
, query
U(t_{2})\dots
, and so on, then measure. See
Fig.

1
for a pictorial representation of such an experiment. In this access model, there are different figures of merit that one may want to minimise. The two usually considered as the most important are the
number of experiments
, and the
total evolution time
, which is the sum of all times
t_{i}
at which the algorithm queries
U(t_{i})
.

Hamiltonian Learning:

Let

1.2
Main result

Motivated by the role of Hamiltonians in quantum system dynamics, we introduce the time-constrained distance (with budget time
T
) between two Hamiltonians as

d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})=\max_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{1}{2}\norm{\mathcal U_{H_1,t}-\mathcal U_{H_2,t}}_{\diamond},

where
\mathcal{U}_{H,t}(\cdot)=e^{-itH}(\cdot)e^{itH}
and
\norm{\cdot}_{\diamond}
is the diamond norm. Due to the operational meaning of the diamond norm, the time-constrained distance has a clear physical interpretation: it quantifies the best probability of successfully discriminating the Hamiltonians by optimising over all protocols of the form “prepare, evolve for a time
t\leq T
, and finally measure”.

We also introduce an analogue temperature-constrained distance, motivated by the fact that the thermal equilibrium state of a system governed by
H
at inverse temperature
\beta
is the Gibbs state
\rho_{H}(\beta)=e^{-\beta H}/\Tr[e^{-\beta H}]
(we refer to
[
Alh23
]
for background on quantum thermal equilibrium). Thus, we can naturally define the temperature-constrained distance (with inverse temperature budget of
B
) between two Hamiltonians as

d_{B}(H_{1},H_{2})=\max_{\beta\in[0,B]}\frac{1}{2}\norm{\rho_{H_1}(\beta)-\rho_{H_2}(\beta)}_{\mathrm{tr}}.

(1)

Due to the operational meaning of the trace norm, the temperature-constrained distance has a clear physical interpretation: it quantifies the best probability of successfully discriminating the Hamiltonians by optimising over all protocols of the form “prepare a thermal state at inverse temperature
\beta\leq B
, and measure”.

Result 1

(Nearly-optimal Hamiltonian learning in physically motivated distances)
.

Let
H

n
-qubit

1.3
Techniques

1.3.1
Relating the physically meaningful distances to the operator norm

We start by relating the time-constrained and temperature-constrained distance to the operator norm, which is easier to understand from a mathematical point of view. In particular, we prove the following (for a formal statement, see
Lemmas

5.2
and
5.4
and
5.13
).

Result 2

(Relation of the operator norm and the physically motivated distainces)
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be two
n
-qubit Hamiltonians with operator norm at most 1. Then,

1.

For constant time budget
T>0
,
d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})=\Theta(\norm{H_1-H_2}_{\mathrm{op}})
.

2.

For constant inverse temperature budget
B>0
,
d_{B}(H_{1},H_{2})=O(\norm{H_1-H_2}_{\mathrm{op}})
.

The upper bound
d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})\leq T\norm{H_1-H_2}_{\mathrm{op}}
was already known (see for instance,
[
MFPT24
, Section 7.1.]
). Our contribution here is to show that the time-constrained distance is not only upper bounded by the operator norm, but also equivalent, i.e., to show
\Omega(\norm{H_1-H_2}_{\mathrm{op}})
. An upper bound of
d_{B}(H_{1},H_{2})=e^{B}\norm{H_1-H_2}_{\mathrm{op}}
was known
[
BKL+17
, Lemma 16]
, and here we exponentially improve the dependence on
B.
One may wonder if the temperature-constrained distance is also equivalent to the operator norm, however, we show that that is not the case (see
Section

5.2.2
).

In terms of learning, Result
2
means that learning in the time-constrained distance is equivalent to learning in the operator norm, and that upper bounds in the operator norm imply upper bounds in the temperature-constrained distance. Thus, from now on we focus on learning in the operator norm.

1.3.2
Upper bounds for learning

We first note that proper learning in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm of the Pauli coefficients implies learning in the operator norm (with a different error parameter). Indeed, a proper
(\varepsilon/2s)
-learner in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm outputs an
s
-sparse Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
such that
|h_{P}-h_{P}^{\prime}|\leq\varepsilon/2s.
Thus, by the triangle inequality and the fact that
\norm{P}_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
, it follows that
\norm{H-H'}_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\varepsilon.
We also note that an improper learner in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm can easily be made proper, by rounding the coefficients
h^{\prime}_{P}\leq\varepsilon/2s
to
0.
Thus, from now on, we focus on showing upper bounds for learning in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm.

We divide our learning algorithm into two stages. In the first stage, we learn the effective Pauli support of the Hamiltonian. To be precise, we will find a set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
satisfying, with high probability, that
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
and
|\mathcal{P}|=\widetilde{O}(s).

Learning the effective Pauli support.

The key novelty that allows us to learn the effective Pauli support of a
s
-sparse Hamiltonian
H
is the isolation technique. This a randomized protocol that for every
P\in\mathcal{H}
prepares
e^{-ith_{P}P}
with probability
\Omega(1/s)
. Once we have access to the isolated time evolution of
e^{-ith_{P}P}=\cos(h_Pt)I^{\otimes n}+i\sin(h_Pt)P
for
P\in\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
, if we pick a uniformly random
t\in[\pi/4,1/\varepsilon]
and perform Pauli sampling of
e^{-ith_{P}P}
(i.e., preparing the Choi-Jamiolkowski state of
e^{-ith_{P}P}
and measure in the Bell basis, which outputs
P
with probability
|\sin(h_Pt)|^{2}
), then we will obtain
P
as the outcome with constant probability. Hence, repeating this process a constant number of times allows us to detect
P\in\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}.
Thus, if we repeat this iteration
\widetilde{O}(s)
times and store the outcomes in a set
\mathcal{P}
, then we will have that
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
with high probability. This way, it remains to explain how to access the isolated time evolution. To do that, we introduce some notation. For
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
, we define

H_{Q_{1}}=\frac{H+Q_{1}HQ_{1}}{2}\text{ and }H_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{i}}=\frac{H_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{i-1}}+Q_{i}H_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{i-1}}Q_{i}}{2},\text{ for }2\leq i\leq r.

Now, we note three things. First, we can prepare
e^{-itH_{Q_{1}}}
by making queries to the time evolution of
H
with a total evolution time
t
. Indeed, this is true by Trotterization (see
Theorem

2.1
), that allows one to approximate
e^{-it(A+B)}
by making queries to
e^{-itA}
and
e^{-itB}
, and the fact that
e^{-itQ_{1}HQ_{1}/2}=Q_{1}e^{-itH/2}Q_{1}
. The same is true for
e^{-itH_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{i}}}
, so we may assume that we have access to
e^{-itH_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}}}
without any overhead in the total evolution time.

Second, notice that
H_{Q}=\sum_{P:[P,Q]=0}h_{P}P
, i.e., the action of
Q
on
H

kills
all the Paulis that do not commute with
P
. Similarly,
H_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}}=\sum_{P:[P,Q_{1}]=0\land\dots\land[P,Q_{r}]=0}h_{P}P
.

Third, getting inspiration from
[
Val84
]
, we prove that, given
P\in\mathcal{H}
, if we choose
r=\lceil\log(s)\rceil+2
and pick
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}
uniformly at random from
\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
, then we have that with probability
\Omega(1/s)

P
is the only element of
\mathcal{H}
that commutes with all
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}
(see Lemma
3.2
below).

Putting these three observations together, it follows that we can prepare
e^{-ith_{P}P}
with probability
\Omega(1/s).
Now, we may state our result for learning the effective support of a
s
-sparse Hamiltonian (see
Theorem

3.4
for a formal statement).

Learning a single Pauli coefficient.

Our strategy for learning a single Pauli coefficient is also based on the isolation technique. Now, given a known
P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
and
s
-sparse Hamiltonian, we pick uniformly random

Result 5

(Learning a single Pauli coefficient of a sparse Hamiltonian)
.

Let

s
-sparse Hamiltonian with
|h_{P}|\leq 1
for every

. Let
P_{0}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}

Other comments about our upper bounds for learning.

For the sake of simplicity, we have deferred to the main text the analysis of figures of merit other than number of experiments and total evolution time. However, we have performed an exhaustive complexity analysis of our algorithms, which are efficient (polynomial dependence with respect to all parameters) in other figures of merit such as classical post-processing or query complexity. Additionally, our algorithms are proper learners. Moreover, when they succeed, they output a Hamiltonian whose Pauli support is contained in the Pauli support of the Hamiltonian to be learned.

Lower bounds for number of experiments.

Result 6

(Lower bound on the number of experiments)
.

An algorithm with
O(n)
ancilla qubits that, with probability
\geq 0.9,

\varepsilon
-learns in operator norm
s
-sparse Hamiltonians with
\norm{H}_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
, must make
\Omega((s/n)\log(1/\eps))

To sketch the proof of

6

O(n)

O(n)

If
\varepsilon<1/s
and

\{\sum_{P\in\mathcal{H}}\varepsilon P:\,|\mathcal{H}|=s\}

\Omega(s).

We fix a subset
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}-\{I^{\otimes n}\}

\mathcal{H}
such that every pair of the set is

A volume argument shows that the size of such a set is at least
\Omega((1/\varepsilon)^{s}),

\Omega((s/n)\cdot\log(1/\eps))

1.4
Relation to previous work

Since the seminal work of Anshu, Arunachalam, Kuwahara, and Soleimanifar
[
AAKS21
]
, there have been numerous papers about quantum Hamiltonian learning. Many of them assume prior knowledge of the Pauli support of the Hamiltonian, that the Hamiltonian is local (meaning that every Pauli in the support acts on a bounded number of qubits) and some geometrical structure (such as every qubit being acted on by a constant number of Pauli operators)
[
AAKS21
,
DOS23
,
HTFS23
]
. The first algorithms for learning without prior knowledge of the support of the Hamiltonian were proposed recently
[
ADE25
,
BLMT24
,
MFPT24
]
, and the locality assumption was also dropped in a recent series of work that only assumes sparsity
[
ADE25
,
Zha25
,
HMG+25
,
ST25
]
.

Most of the aforementioned works learn in the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm or the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the Pauli coefficients. The
\ell_{\infty}
-norm lacks a direct physical interpretation, but is a coarse distance, easy to work with mathematically. The
\ell_{2}
-norm is less coarse and has an average-case physical interpretation: for a short time, two Hamiltonians that are close in
\ell_{2}
-norm produce time evolutions that are close on Haar-random inputs (see
[
MFPT24
, Section 7.2]
). Only
[
MFPT24
]
learns in the
\ell_{1}
-norm of the Pauli coefficients, which has a worst-case physical interpretation due to
Result

2
and the fact that
\norm{\cdot}_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\norm{\cdot}_{\ell_{1}}
.

To the best of our knowledge, the only prior optimal result for learning Hamiltonians is the one by Huang, Tong, Fang, and Su
[
HTFS23
]
, who showed that for low interacting Hamiltonians (those that are local and where every qubit is acted on by a bounded number of Pauli operators)
\Theta(\varepsilon^{-1})
evolution time suffices to learn in the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm of the Pauli coefficients.

Thus,
Result

1
gives the first learning algorithm for Hamiltonians that is optimal in one of the main figures of merit, considers a physically meaningful distance, does not require locality and does not assume prior knowledge of the Pauli support.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

Given two bit strings
a,b\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we define their dot product as
a\cdot b=\sum_{i\in[n]}a_{i}b_{i}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)
. Given two bit strings
x=(a,b),\,y=(c,d)\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
, we define their symplectic product as
[x,y]=a\cdot c+b\cdot d\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)
. We use
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
to refer to the
n
-dimensional canonical basis. We use
I,X,Y,Z
to refer to the
2\crossproduct 2
Pauli matrices. We use
\mathrm{Id}
to refer to the identity matrix of a dimension that will be clear from the context. Given a matrix
A
of
(\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2})^{\otimes n}
, we consider its expansion in the Pauli basis

A=\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}a_{P}P,

where
a_{P}=\tr[PA]/2^{n}
are its Pauli coefficients. Given such a matrix, we use
\norm{A}_{\mathrm{op}}
to denote its operator norm when regarded as linear map from
\ell_{2}
to
\ell_{2},
which coincides with its largest singular value. We use
\norm{A}_{\ell_{p}}=(\sum_{P}|a_{P}|^{p})^{1/p}
to refer to the
\ell_{p}
-norm of its Pauli coefficients.

We will identify
n
-qubit Pauli strings with 2
n
-bit strings. For every Pauli string
P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
, there are a unique bit string
(a,b)\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
and a unique number
\alpha_{P}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

P=\alpha_{P}X^{a_{1}}Z^{b_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes X^{a_{n}}Z^{b_{n}}.

Note that these bit strings capture the commutation relations if the Pauli strings. Namely, if
P=\alpha_{P}X^{a_{1}}Z^{b_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes X^{a_{n}}Z^{b_{n}}
and
P^{\prime}=\alpha_{P^{\prime}}X^{a^{\prime}_{1}}Z^{b^{\prime}_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes X^{a^{\prime}_{n}}Z^{b^{\prime}_{n}}
, then
P
and
P^{\prime}
commute if
[(a,b),(c,d)]=0
, and anticommute otherwise.

2.2
Hamiltonians

An
n
-qubit Hamiltonian
H
is a self-adjoint matrix of dimension
(\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2})^{\otimes n}
. Its support is given by
\mathcal{H}\coloneqq\{P:|h_{P}|>0\}
and its
\varepsilon
-effective support is given by
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\{P:|h_{P}|\geq\varepsilon\}
. We assume that
|h_{P}|\leq 1
for every
P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
. We note that a matrix
A
is a Hamiltonian if and only if its Pauli coefficients are real numbers.

We will use the Taylor expansion of the exponential, that ensures that

e^{-itH}=\mathrm{Id}-itH+R_{1}(t),

(2)

for
t\leq 1/\norm{H}_{\mathrm{op}},
where
R_{1}(t)
is the first-order remainder and satisfies
\norm{R_1(t)}_{\mathrm{op}}\leq Ct^{2}\norm{H}_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}
for some constant
C\geq 1.

2.3
Access model

Hamiltonians govern the dynamics of quantum systems due to the Schrödinger equation. In particular, if a quantum system governed by a time-independent Hamiltonian
H
and the state describing the system at time
0
is
\rho,

at time
t
the state will have evolved to
U(t)\rho U^{\dagger}(t)
, where
U(t)=\exp(-itH)
is the time evolution operator of
H
at time
t
.

Thus, a natural access model for Hamiltonians is to perform
experiments
of the following kind: prepare a state
\rho,
apply
U(t_{1})
(this is making a query to
U(t_{1})
, which in a lab can be implemented by letting the system evolve for time
t_{1}
), apply a unitary operator
V_{1}
independent of
H
, query
U(t_{2})
, apply apply a unitary operator
V_{2}
independent of
H
, query
U(t_{2})
,
\dots
and finally measure.

In this access model, there are different figures of merit that one may care about. The two usually considered as the most important are the
total evolution time
, which is the sum of all times at which the algorithm queries
U(t),
and the
number of experiments
. Other figures of merit that we will also keep track of are the
number of queries,
the
time resolution
(the minimum time at which the algorithm queries the time evolution operator), the
classical post-processing time
, and the number of
ancilla qubits
.

Finally, our algorithms will also be robust to
state-preparation and measurement (SPAM) error
. Following
[
MFPT24
, Definition 4]
, an experiment suffers from an
\varepsilon
-amount of SPAM error if the channels applied before the first query to prepare the initial state and the channels applied after the last query to measure have in total
\varepsilon
error in diamond norm. We will say that an algorithm is
robust
to an
\varepsilon
amount of SPAM error (or any other error) if the performance guarantees of the algorithm do not change in the presence of that error, maybe after increasing the complexities by constant factors.

Trotterization.

Given access to
e^{-itA}
and
e^{-itB}
for two Hamiltonians
A
and
B
and arbitrary times
t
, Trotterization allows us to implement
e^{-it(A+B)}
up to arbitrary error while also preserving the total time evolution and without using extra qubits. Thus, to analyze the number of experiments and the total time evolution required by our algorithms, if we have access to
e^{-itA}
and
e^{-itB}
, we may assume access to
e^{-it(A+B)}
. However, the number of queries and the time resolution change. To be more precise, we will use the following result.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
CST+21
, Corollary 2]

Let
t>0
, let
\varepsilon>0
, let
H_{1},\dots,H_{R}
Hamiltonians acting on
n
-qubits and let
c=\max\{\norm{H_1}_{\mathrm{op}},\dots,\norm{H_R}_{\mathrm{op}}\}
. Let
l=\left\lceil O\left(\sqrt{(Rct)^{3}/\varepsilon}\right)\right\rceil
and
V=(e^{-itH_{R}/2l}\cdots e^{-itH_{1}/2l}e^{-itH_{1}/2l}\cdots e^{-itH_{R}/2l})^{l}
. Then,

\norm{e^{-it(H_1+\dots+H_R)}\cdot e^{it(H_1+\dots+H_R)}-V\cdot V^{\dagger}}_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon.

2.4.2
Valiant-Vazirani lemma

Here, we state the lemma by Valiant and Vazirani to show that NP is as easy as detecting unique solutions. We will use this lemma to learn the effective support of a sparse Hamiltonian

, Theorem 2.3]

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}-\{0^{n}\}
. Let

be iid uniformly random elements of

Let

\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{S}|]=2^{-r}|\mathcal{X}|\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Var}[|\mathcal{S}|]=2^{-r}(1-2^{-r})|\mathcal{X}|.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}-\{0^{n}\}
. Let

be iid uniformly random elements of

By Lemma

\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{S}|]=\frac{1}{4}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Var}[|\mathcal{S}|]<\frac{1}{4}.

Then, by Chebychev’s inequality (Lemma

\Pr[|\mathcal{S}|\geq\underbrace{\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}_{\mu+\sqrt{2}\sigma}\bigg]\leq\frac{1}{2}.

As

and

only takes integer values, we have that
\Pr[|\mathcal{S}|=0]\geq 1/2.

∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}-\{0^{n}\}

. Let

2.4.3
Pauli sampling.

Suppose
U
is a unitary and we write out its Pauli decomposition as
U=\sum_{P}u_{P}P
, then by Parseval’s identity
\sum|u_{P}|^{2}=\Tr[U^{\dagger}U]/2^{n}=1
, i.e.,
\{|u_{P}|^{2}\}_{P}
is a
probability distribution
. We will be using the fact below extensively.

Fact 2.7
.

Given access to a unitary
U
, one can sample from the distribution
\{|u_{P}|^{2}\}_{P}
.

Proof.

The proof simply follows by applying
U\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{2^{n}}
to
n
EPR pairs (i.e., preparing the Choi-Jamiolkowski state of
U
) and measuring in the Bell basis, because

U\otimes\mathrm{Id}_{2^{n}}\ket{\mathrm{EPR}_n}=\sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}}u_{P}\mathop{\bigotimes}_{i\in[n]}(P_{i}\otimes I\ket{\mathrm{EPR}}),

and the Bell states can be written as
P\otimes I\ket{\mathrm{EPR}}
for
P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}
, where
\ket{\mathrm{EPR}}=(\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt{2}
.
∎

2.4.4
The Clifford group

We recall that the
n
-qubit Clifford group is the subgroup of the
n
-qubit unitaries that, via conjugation, map
n
qubit Pauli operators to
n
-qubit Pauli operators, up to a phase. It can alternatively be described as the group of unitary operators that are generated by
H,S
and
CX
gates acting upon any of the
n
qubits. Note that tensor products of Clifford Unitaries are also Clifford Unitaries.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
P_{1},\ P_{2}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}-\{I^{\otimes n}\}
. Then, there is a Clifford unitary
C
and a complex number
c
such that
CP_{1}C^{\dagger}=cP_{2}
. Furthermore, up to a phase,
P\to C^{\dagger}PC
is a bijection of
\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
.

Proof.

Any two Pauli operators
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
for which there exists Clifford unitary
C
such that
CP_{1}C^{\dagger}=cP_{2}
are said to be Clifford equivalent, and it can be verified that this is indeed an equivalence relation.

For the first part, it suffices to show that each non identity Pauli is Clifford equivalent to
X\otimes I^{\otimes(n-1)}
up to a phase.
Now, note that all non identity single Qubit Pauli’s are equivalent to each other, because

HXH\propto Z\text{ and }SXS^{\dagger}\propto Y.

Thus, we have that any
n
-qubit Pauli operator is Clifford equivalent to a tensor product of
X
s and
I
s. We continue by noting that
CX(X\otimes X)CX=I\otimes X
, so every tensor product of
X
s and
I
s is Clifford equivalent to a tensor product of just one
X
and
I
s. Finally, by conjugating with swap gates (which also lies in the Clifford group), we can move that
X
to the first qubit. This shows that all Pauli strings are Clifford equivalent to
X\otimes I^{\otimes(n-1)}
, as claimed.

For the second part, it suffices to note that, by definition, Clifford circuits map, by conjugation, Pauli strings to Pauli strings (up to phases) and that this conjugation operatoion is invertible.
∎

3.1
Learning the effective support

In this section, we show that the effective support of an
s
-sparse Hamiltonian can be learned efficiently. The key idea in the proof of this result (
Theorem

3.4
below) is a random procedure that, for every
P\in\mathcal{H}
allows us to prepare the
isolated Hamiltonian evolution

e^{-ith_{P}P}
with some non-negligible probability. Once we have access to
e^{-ith_{P}P}
, it is easy to detect
P
if
|h_{P}|\geq\varepsilon.
To sketch the procedure to prepare the isolated Hamiltonian evolution, we should introduce some notation.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
and let
H
be an
n
-qubit Hamiltonian. We define

H_{Q_{1}}=\frac{H+Q_{1}HQ_{1}}{2}\text{ and }H_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{i}}=\frac{H_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{i-1}}+Q_{i}H_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{i-1}}Q_{i}}{2},\text{ for }2\leq i\leq r.

First, note that we can prepare
e^{-itH_{Q_{1}}}
by making queries to the time evolution of
H
with a total evolution time
t
and without using extra qubits. Indeed, this is true because of
Theorem

2.1
and the fact that
e^{-itQ_{1}HQ_{1}/2}=Q_{1}e^{-itH/2}Q_{1}
. The same is true for
e^{-itH_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{i}}}
, so we may assume that we have access to
e^{-itH_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}}}
without any overhead in the total evolution time nor in the number of extra qubits.

Second, notice that
H_{Q}=\sum_{P:[P,Q]=0}h_{P}P
, i.e., the action of
Q
on
H

kills
all the Paulis that do not commute with
P
. Similarly,
H_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}}=\sum_{P:[P,Q_{1}]=0\land\dots\land[P,Q_{r}]=0}h_{P}P
.

Third, we prove (see Lemma
3.2
below) that, given
P\in\mathcal{H}
, if we choose
r=\lceil\log(s)\rceil+2
and pick
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}
uniformly at random from
\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
, then we have that with probability
\Omega(1/s)

P
is the only element of
\mathcal{H}
that commutes with all
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}.
Putting these three observations together, it follows that we can prepare
e^{-ith_{P}P}
with probability
\Omega(1/s).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
r=\lceil\log(s)\rceil+2
. Let
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}
iid uniformly distributed elements of
\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
. Let
H
be an
s
-sparse Hamiltonian and let
P\in\mathcal{H}
. Then, the probability that
P
is the only element of
\mathcal{H}
that commutes with all
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}
is
\Omega(1/s)
.

Proof.

For the second part, without loss of generality, we can assume
P=Z\otimes I^{\otimes{(n-1)}}
. Indeed, for any other non-identity
P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
, by Lemma
2.8
, there is a Clifford operation
C
such that
CPC^{\dagger}=Z\otimes I^{\otimes(n-1)}
, so instead of working with
H=\sum a_{i}P_{i}
, we can assume that we are working with
CHC^{\dagger}=\sum a_{i}CPC^{\dagger}
. This is does not affect the commutation relations nor the randomness of
Q
, because
Q
and
P
commute if and only if
CPC^{\dagger}
and
CQC^{\dagger}
commute, and
Q
is uniformly random if and only if
C^{\dagger}QC
is uniformly random.

Now, we associate every Pauli string with a
2n
-bit string as in
Section

2
. We then write
H
as

H=\sum_{i\in[s]}h_{i}\alpha_{i}X^{a^{i}_{1}}Z^{b^{i}_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes X^{a^{i}_{n}}Z^{b^{i}_{n}},

for some
x^{1}=(a^{1},b^{1}),\dots,x^{s}=(a^{s},b^{s})\in\{0,1\}^{2n}-\{0^{2n}\}
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s}\in\mathbb{C}.
We may assume without loss of generality that
P=Z\otimes I^{\otimes n}
corresponds to
x^{1}=e_{n+1}.
Also, we write
Q_{l}
as

\beta_{l}X^{c^{l}_{1}}Z^{d^{l}_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes X^{c^{l}_{n}}Z^{d^{l}_{n}},

for some
y^{1}=(c^{1},d^{1}),\dots,y^{r}=(c^{r},d^{r})\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
and
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r}\in\mathbb{C}.

With this notation, we can write

\displaystyle\Pr[P\text{ is the only element of }\mathcal{H}\text{ that commutes with }Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\{[e_{n+1},y^{j}]=0\ \forall j\in[r]\}\land\{\forall\ 2\leq i\leq s,\ \exists\ j\ [x^{i},y^{j}]=1\}]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\{y^{j}_{1}=0\ \forall j\in[r]\}\land\{\forall\ 2\leq i\leq s,\ \exists\ j\ [x^{i},y^{j}]=1\}].

Now, we write

\displaystyle\Pr[H_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}}=h_{P}P]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Pr[y^{j}_{1}=0\ \forall j\in[r]]}_{(*)}\cdot\underbrace{\Pr[\{\forall\ 2\leq i\leq s,\ \exists\ j\ [x^{i},y^{j}]=1\}|\{y^{j}_{1}=0\ \forall j\in[r]\}]}_{(**)}.

To finish, it suffices to show that
(*)=\Omega(1/s)
and that
(**)=\Omega(1).
We prove both things separately.

First, to show that
(*)=\Omega(1/s)
, note that for a uniformly random
y\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
satisfies
y_{1}=0
with probability
1/2
, so

(*)=\frac{1}{2^{r}}=\frac{1}{4s}.

Second, to show that
(**)=\Omega(1)
we notice that

\displaystyle(**)=\Pr[\forall\ 2\leq i\leq s,\ \exists\ j\ \sum_{l\neq n+1}x^{i}_{l}y^{j}_{l}=1]

where
y^{j}_{l}
are iid uniformly random elements of
\{0,1\}.

Finally, we have that

(x^{i}_{1},\dots,x^{i}_{n},x^{i}_{n+2},\dots,x^{i}_{2n})\neq 0^{2n-1}

because
x^{i}\neq 0^{2n}
and
x^{i}\neq x^{1}=e_{n+1},
so we can apply Corollary
2.5
to conclude that
(**)\geq 1/2=\Omega(1)
.

To control the query overhead required to apply
e^{itH_{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}}}
we will use the following consequence of

.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

5
Physically motivated distances between Hamiltonians

In this section, we first introduce notions of distance between Hamiltonians that carry a strong physical meaning. Subsequently, we show that these notions are closely related to the operator norm between Hamiltonians, thereby endowing the latter with a clear operational interpretation. Before proceeding, it is useful to recall the most relevant notions of distance for quantum states and quantum channels.

For quantum states, the most natural distance between two density operators
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
is the
trace distance
, defined as

\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\,.

The operational meaning of the trace distance is captured by the well-known
Holevo-Helstrom theorem

[
Hol73
,
Hel69
]
. This result establishes that if one is given one copy of an unknown quantum state, promised to be either
\rho_{1}
or
\rho_{2}
with equal prior probability
1/2
, then the maximum probability of correctly identifying the state is

P_{\text{succ}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\Bigr)\,.

Moreover, the trace distance plays a crucial role because it directly bounds the distinguishability of expectation values of bounded observables. Specifically, for any observable
O
, Holder’s inequality implies that
[
Wil11
]

\big|\Tr[\rho_{1}O]-\Tr[\rho_{2}O]\big|\leq\|O\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\,,

which shows that if two states are close in trace distance, then the expectation values of any bounded observable with respect to those states must also be close.

For quantum channels, the most meaningful notion of distance between two quantum channels
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
is the
diamond distance

[
Wil11
]
, defined as

\frac{1}{2}\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{\diamond}\coloneqq\sup_{\rho_{AA^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{2}\|\mathrm{Id}_{A}\otimes\Phi_{1}(\rho_{AA^{\prime}})-\mathrm{Id}_{A}\otimes\Phi_{2}(\rho_{AA^{\prime}})\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\,,

where the supremum is taken over all the input states
\rho_{AA^{\prime}}
on the input system
A^{\prime}
and an arbitrary ancilla
A
. The operational meaning of the diamond distance again follows from the Holevo-Helstrom theorem: if one is given a single use of an unknown channel, promised to be either
\Phi_{1}
or
\Phi_{2}
with equal prior probability
1/2
, then the maximum probability of correctly identifying the channel is

P_{\text{succ}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{2}\|\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}\|_{\diamond}\Bigr)\,.

(12)

Moreover, if two channels are close in diamond distance, the expectation values of any observable on the output states
\Phi_{1}(\rho)
and
\Phi_{2}(\rho)
are also close, for all input states
\rho
.
In the special case of unitary channels, Aharonov et al.
[
AKN98
]
proved that the diamond distance can be evaluated without the need of an ancillary system, and the optimization can be always restricted to pure input states. More precisely, given two unitaries
V
and
W
, it holds that

\frac{1}{2}\|V(\cdot)V^{\dagger}-W(\cdot)W^{\dagger}\|_{\diamond}=\sup_{\psi}\frac{1}{2}\bigl\|V\ket{\psi}\!\bra{\psi}V^{\dagger}-W\ket{\psi}\!\bra{\psi}W^{\dagger}\bigr\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\,,

(13)

and, by using the well-known formula for the trace distance between pure states, we can write

\frac{1}{2}\|V(\cdot)V^{\dagger}-W(\cdot)W^{\dagger}\|_{\diamond}=\sqrt{1-\inf_{\psi}|\bra{\psi}V^{\dagger}W\ket{\psi}|^{2}}\,.

(14)

Motivated by these meaningful distances for states and channels, we now turn to Hamiltonians. One may then ask: in which physical scenarios do Hamiltonians arise? In practice, there are two natural settings:

•

Time-evolution setting
, where the Hamiltonian governs the unitary dynamics of the system;

•

Thermodynamics setting
, where the Hamiltonian determines the Gibbs state at the thermal equilibrium.

These two perspectives motivate the introduction of two notions of distance that capture Hamiltonian distinguishability in an operationally meaningful way: the
time-constrained diamond distance
and the
temperature-constrained trace distance
, which will be presented in the following subsections.

5.1
Time-constrained diamond distance

We define the
time-constrained diamond distance
between Hamiltonians at time
T
as

d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\mathcal{U}_{H_{1},t}-\mathcal{U}_{H_{2},t}\|_{\diamond},

(15)

where
\mathcal{U}_{H_{j},t}
, for
j=1,2
, denotes the channel associated with the time evolution operator
e^{-iH_{j}t}
, namely
\mathcal{U}_{H_{j},t}(\,\cdot\,)\coloneqq e^{-iH_{j}t}(\,\cdot\,)e^{iH_{j}t}.
By the exposition above the time-constrained distance quantifies the best probability of successfully discriminating the Hamiltonians by optimising over all protocols of the form “prepare, evolve for a time
t\leq T
, and finally measure”.

Here, the parameter
T
can be understood as an experimental time budget. Moreover, if this time budget was unconstrained, then any pair of Hamiltonians would be maximally distinguishable.

Remark 5.1

(Time-unconstrained diamond distance and motivation for
d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})
)
.

Taking the limit
T\to\infty
in Eq. (
15
) yields a
time-unconstrained
diamond distance which is overly sensitive to small perturbations of the Hamiltonians.
Indeed, there exist pairs of Hamiltonians that are arbitrarily close in operator norm yet maximally distant with respect to this measure.

To illustrate this, consider commuting Hamiltonians, i.e.
[H_{1},H_{2}]=0
. By using Eq. (
14
) we obtain

d_{\infty}(H_{1},H_{2})=\sup_{t\geq 0}\sqrt{1-\inf_{\ket{\psi}}\bigl|\bra{\psi}e^{-i(H_{1}-H_{2})t}\ket{\psi}\bigr|^{2}}.

If
H_{1}-H_{2}
is proportional to the identity, the two evolutions differ only by a global phase, and therefore
d_{\infty}(H_{1},H_{2})=0
.

If instead
H_{1}-H_{2}
is not proportional to the identity, then
d_{\infty}(H_{1},H_{2})=1
.
Indeed, in this case
H_{1}-H_{2}
has at least two distinct eigenvalues, say
\varepsilon_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{1}
, with eigenvectors
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
.
Restricting the optimization to the subspace
\mathrm{Span}\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
gives

\begin{split}\inf_{\ket{\psi}}\bigl|\bra{\psi}e^{-i(H_{1}-H_{2})t}\ket{\psi}\bigr|^{2}&\leq\inf_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathrm{Span}\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}}\bigl|\bra{\psi}e^{-i(H_{1}-H_{2})t}\ket{\psi}\bigr|^{2}\\
&=\inf_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathrm{Span}\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}}\bigl|\bra{\psi}e^{-i(\varepsilon_{0}-\varepsilon_{1})\tfrac{\sigma_{z}}{2}t}\ket{\psi}\bigr|^{2}.\end{split}

(16)

Choosing, for example,
\ket{\psi}=(\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}
, the overlap in Eq. (
16
) reduces to

\bigl|\cos\bigl((\varepsilon_{0}-\varepsilon_{1})t/2\bigr)\bigr|,

which vanishes whenever

\frac{(\varepsilon_{0}-\varepsilon_{1})t}{2}=\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2},\dots,

corresponding to evolution into an orthogonal state within this two-dimensional subspace.
Hence the minimum overlap is zero, and we conclude that
d_{\infty}(H_{1},H_{2})=1
.

In summary, for commuting Hamiltonians
H_{1},H_{2}
one has

d_{\infty}(H_{1},H_{2})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }H_{1}-H_{2}\propto\mathrm{Id},\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus, in the infinite-time limit any nontrivial difference between Hamiltonians implies maximal distinguishability under the time-unconstrained diamond distance.
This makes the latter both mathematically unsatisfactory and physically unrealistic, since in practice experiments can only probe finite evolution times.
These observations motivate the introduction of the
time-constrained diamond distance
in Eq. (
15
), which is operationally meaningful and, as shown in Subsections
5.1.1
and
5.1.2
, closely related to the operator norm between Hamiltonians—in contrast to its unconstrained counterpart.

We will now show that the time-constrained diamond distance
d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})
is equivalent to the operator norm
\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
up to multiplicative factors depending on
T
. To begin, we establish an upper bound on
d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})
.

5.1.1
Upper bounding the time-constrained distance

This subsection is devoted to establish an upper bound on the time-constrained diamond distance in terms of the operator norm of the difference between the Hamiltonians. Specifically, the following bound holds.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be Hamiltonians. Then

d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})\leq\sin\Big( \min\Big( \frac{\pi}{2}, T\Vert H_{1} - H_{2}\Vert_{\opnorm} \Big)\Big),

(17)

and, in particular, the following weaker upper bound holds:

d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})\leq T\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,.

(18)

To begin with, we show how the bound in Eq. (
18
) can be obtained from standard tools. Consider the definition of
d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})
in terms of the diamond norm:

d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})\coloneqq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{1}{2}\|e^{-iH_{1}t}(\,\cdot\,)e^{iH_{1}t}-e^{-iH_{2}t}(\,\cdot\,)e^{iH_{2}t}\|_{\diamond}\,.

We can bound the above expression using standard tools as follows:

\frac{1}{2}\|e^{-iH_{1}t}(\,\cdot\,)e^{iH_{1}t}-e^{-iH_{2}t}(\,\cdot\,)e^{iH_{2}t}\|_{\diamond}\leq\|e^{iH_{1}t}-e^{iH_{2}t}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq t\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,,

where the first inequality follows from
[
HKOT23
, Proposition 1.6]
and the second is a consequence of
[
HKOT23
, Lemma 3.2 (c)]
. This directly implies the bound in Eq. (
18
). Our novel contribution lies in the stronger inequality

\frac{1}{2}\|e^{-iH_{1}t}(\,\cdot\,)e^{iH_{1}t}-e^{-iH_{2}t}(\,\cdot\,)e^{iH_{2}t}\|_{\diamond}\leq\sin\Big( \min\Big( \frac{\pi}{2}, t\Vert H_{1} - H_{2}\Vert_{\opnorm} \Big)\Big)\,,

(19)

which directly implies Eq. (
17
) and, to the best of our knowledge, does not appear in the existing literature. In the following, we provide a proof of this improved bound. Before proceeding, it is useful to recall the following result, given by Pfeifer
[
Pfe93
]
.

Theorem 5.3

(
[
Pfe93
]
)
.

Let
H(t^{\prime})
be a time-dependent Hamiltonian, and let
\ket{\psi_t}
denote the solution of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation
i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi_t}=H(t)\ket{\psi_t}
, with initial state
\ket{\psi_{t=0}}\coloneqq\ket{\psi_0}.

Then, the overlap between the evolved state
\ket{\psi_t}
and the initial state
\ket{\psi_0}
satisfies the lower bound

|\langle\psi_{0}|\psi_{t}\rangle|\geq\cos\Biggl(\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\bra{\psi_0}H(t^{\prime})^{2}\ket{\psi_0}-\bra{\psi_0}H(t^{\prime})\ket{\psi_0}^{2}}\,dt^{\prime}\Biggr).

(20)

We are now ready to prove Lemma
5.2
.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

As recalled in Eq. (
13
), the diamond norm between two unitary channels can be expressed as

\frac{1}{2}\|e^{-iH_{1}t}(\,\cdot\,)e^{iH_{1}t}-e^{-iH_{2}t}(\,\cdot\,)e^{iH_{2}t}\|_{\diamond}=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\ket{\psi}}\|e^{-iH_{1}t}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}e^{iH_{1}t}-e^{-iH_{2}t}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}e^{iH_{2}t}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}.

Hence, by using Eq. (
14
), we have

\frac{1}{2}\|e^{-iH_{1}t}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}e^{iH_{1}t}-e^{-iH_{2}t}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}e^{iH_{2}t}\|_{S_{1}}=\sqrt{1-|\bra{\psi}e^{-iH_{1}t}e^{iH_{2}t}\ket{\psi}|^{2}}\,.

(21)

To bound the overlap inside the square root, if we define
\ket{\psi(t)}\coloneqq e^{-iH_{1}t}e^{iH_{2}t}\ket{\psi}
, we can recognize the time-dependent Schrödinger equation

\frac{d\ket{\psi(t)}}{dt}=iH(t)\ket{\psi(t)}\,,

where
H(t)\coloneqq e^{-iH_{1}t}(H_{2}-H_{1})e^{iH_{1}t}
. In this way, we can bound the scalar product in Eq. (
21
) by using the result from Pfeifer
[
Pfe93
]
in Eq. (
20
)

|\langle\psi|\psi(t)\rangle|\geq\cos\Big(\min\Big( \frac{\pi}{2} , \int_{0}^{t}dt' \sqrt{\bra{\psi}H^{2}(t')\ket{\psi} - |\bra{\psi}H(t')\ket{\psi}|^2} \Big)\Big).

Hence, this gives

\begin{split}\sup_{\ket{\psi}}\sqrt{1-|\langle\psi|\psi(t)\rangle|^{2}}&\leq\sup_{\ket{\psi}}\sin\Bigg( \min\Bigg( \frac{\pi}{2}, \int_{0}^{t} dt'\,\sqrt{\bra{\psi}H^{2}(t')\ket{\psi}} \Bigg) \Bigg)\\
&\leq\sin\Bigg( \min\Bigg( \frac{\pi}{2}, \int_{0}^{t} dt'\,\|H(t')\|_{\opnorm} \Bigg) \Bigg),\end{split}

where we have used
\|\Theta\|_{\mathrm{op}}\coloneqq\sup_{\ket{\psi}}\sqrt{\bra{\psi}\Theta^{\dagger}\Theta\ket{\psi}}
. Finally, we use the unitary invariance of the operator norm to simplify the expression inside the integral:

\|H(t^{\prime})\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\|H_{2}-H_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Therefore,

\sin\Bigg( \min\Bigg( \frac{\pi}{2}, \int_{0}^{t} dt'\,\|H(t')\|_{\opnorm} \Bigg) \Bigg)=\sin\Big( \min\Big( \frac{\pi}{2}, t\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\opnorm} \Big)\Big),

which, by putting everything together, proves the Lemma.
∎

5.1.2
Lower bounding the time-constrained distance

This subsection is devoted to derive a general lower bound on the time-constrained diamond distance between Hamiltonians in terms of the operator norm. The main result of this subsection is the following lemma, which will be proved at the end.

Lemma 5.4

(Diamond distance lower bound on Hamiltonians)
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be Hamiltonians such that
\|H_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
and
\Tr H_{1}=\Tr H_{2}
. Let
T>0
. Then,

d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})\;\geq\;\frac{1}{4\pi}\min\!\left(T,\frac{1}{4\pi}\right)\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

To start, we introduce the following notation:

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq x\!\!\!\!\!\mod 2\pi\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R},

(22)

\displaystyle q(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(x+\pi)\!\!\!\!\!\mod 2\pi-\pi\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}.

(23)

Intuitively,
p(x)
denotes the projection of
x
onto the interval
[0,2\pi]
, while
q(x)
denotes the projection of
x
onto
[-\pi,\pi]
. We now begin with the following auxiliary lemma, whose proof can be found in
Appendix

A
.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, it holds that

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\max\bigl(|q(a-x)|,|q(b-x)|\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\min\!\left(|q(a)-q(b)|,\,|p(a)-p(b)|\right).

We now present the following lemma, which provides a lower bound on the diamond distance between a unitary Hamiltonian evolution and the identity channel.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
H
be a
d
-dimensional Hamiltonian with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
. Then

\frac{1}{2}\left\|e^{-iH}(\cdot)e^{iH}-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\diamond}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\max_{j,k\in[d]}\min\!\bigl(|p(\lambda_{j})-p(\lambda_{k})|,\,|q(\lambda_{j})-q(\lambda_{k})|\bigr),

where
p
and
q
are defined in Eq. (
22
).

Proof.

Fix
j,k\in[d]
. By Lemma
2.9
, we have

\|e^{-iH}(\cdot)e^{iH}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\diamond}\;\geq\;\min_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}}\|e^{i(H-\phi\mathrm{Id})}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Since the operator norm is at least as large as the modulus of any eigenvalue, it follows that

\min_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}}\|e^{i(H-\phi\mathrm{Id})}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\;\geq\;\min_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}}\max\!\left(|e^{i(\lambda_{j}-\phi)}-1|,\,|e^{i(\lambda_{k}-\phi)}-1|\right).

For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,

|e^{i\theta}-1|=|e^{iq(\theta)}-1|=2\Bigl|\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac{q(\theta)}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr|\;\geq\;\tfrac{2}{\pi}|q(\theta)|,

where the last inequality uses
|\sin(x/2)|\geq|x|/\pi
for all
x\in[-\pi,\pi]
.
Combining the above gives

\tfrac{1}{2}\|e^{-iH}(\cdot)e^{iH}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\diamond}\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{\pi}\min_{\phi\in\mathbb{R}}\!\left(|q(\lambda_{j}-\phi)|,|q(\lambda_{k}-\phi)|\right).

Finally, applying Lemma
5.5
yields

\tfrac{1}{2}\|e^{-iH}(\cdot)e^{iH}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\diamond}\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\min\!\left(|q(\lambda_{j})-q(\lambda_{k})|,\,|p(\lambda_{j})-p(\lambda_{k})|\right).

To finish, it suffices to take the supremum of the above equaition over all
j,k\in[d].

∎

As a consequence of
Lemma

5.6
, we obtain the following. Given a Hamiltonian
H,
we define
R(H)
as
R(H):=\max|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|
, where the maximum ranges over all eigenvalues
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
of
H.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian with
\|H\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}
. Then,

\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|e^{-iH}(\cdot)e^{iH}-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\diamond}\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2\pi}R(H).

Proof.

By Lemma
5.6
,

\tfrac{1}{2}\|e^{-iH}(\cdot)e^{iH}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\diamond}\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\max_{j,k}\min\!\bigl(|p(\lambda_{j})-p(\lambda_{k})|,\,|q(\lambda_{j})-q(\lambda_{k})|\bigr).

Since
\|H\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}
, all eigenvalues of
H
lie in
[-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}]
. Thus,
q(\lambda)=\lambda
for each eigenvalue
\lambda
, and we obtain

\tfrac{1}{2}\|e^{-iH}(\cdot)e^{iH}-\mathrm{Id}\|_{\diamond}\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\max_{j,k}|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}|=\tfrac{1}{2\pi}R(H).

∎

Lemma

5.7
yields the following result, which is almost the result we would like to prove, but with the caveat of the second Hamiltonian being equal to
0
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian with
\|H\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\tfrac{\pi}{2}
and
\Tr H=0
. Then,

\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|e^{-iH}(\cdot)e^{iH}-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\diamond}\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\|H\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Proof.

Since
\Tr H=0
,
H
must have both positive and negative eigenvalues, hence
R(H)\geq\|H\|_{\mathrm{op}}
. Applying Lemma
5.7
completes the proof.
∎

We now address the case of non-commuting Hamiltonians.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be Hamiltonians such that
\|H_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\tfrac{1}{4\pi}
and
\Tr H_{1}=\Tr H_{2}
. Then

\frac{1}{2}\left\|e^{-iH_{1}}(\cdot)e^{iH_{1}}-e^{-iH_{2}}(\cdot)e^{iH_{2}}\right\|_{\diamond}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{4\pi}\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Proof.

We proceed as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|e^{-iH_{1}}(\cdot)e^{iH_{1}}-e^{-iH_{2}}(\cdot)e^{iH_{2}}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\mathrm{(i)}}{=}\;\frac{1}{2}\left\|e^{-iH_{1}}e^{iH_{2}}(\cdot)e^{iH_{1}}e^{-iH_{2}}-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\mathrm{(ii)}}{\geq}\;\frac{1}{2}\left\|e^{-i(H_{1}-H_{2})}(\cdot)e^{i(H_{1}-H_{2})}-\mathrm{Id}\right\|_{\diamond}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|e^{-i(H_{1}-H_{2})}(\cdot)e^{i(H_{1}-H_{2})}-e^{-iH_{1}}e^{iH_{2}}(\cdot)e^{iH_{1}}e^{-iH_{2}}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\mathrm{(iii)}}{\geq}\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|e^{-i(H_{1}-H_{2})}(\cdot)e^{i(H_{1}-H_{2})}-e^{-iH_{1}}e^{iH_{2}}(\cdot)e^{iH_{1}}e^{-iH_{2}}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\mathrm{(iv)}}{\geq}\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}-\left\|e^{-i(H_{1}-H_{2})}-e^{-iH_{1}}e^{iH_{2}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\mathrm{(v)}}{\geq}\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\|[H_{1},H_{2}]\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\quad\overset{\mathrm{(vi)}}{\geq}\;\frac{1}{2\pi}\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}-\tfrac{1}{4\pi}\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{4\pi}\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,.

The justifications are as follows:
(i) invariance of the diamond norm under conjugation by unitaries;
(ii) triangle inequality;
(iii) application of Lemma
5.8
and the bounds on
\|H_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
;
(iv) Lemma
2.9
, relating the diamond and operator norms;
(v) Lemma
2.10
;
(vi) the estimate

\|[H_{1},H_{2}]\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\|H_{1}(H_{2}-H_{1})+(H_{1}-H_{2})H_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2\|H_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}},

together with
\|H_{1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\tfrac{1}{4\pi}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We can now prove the main result of this section.

Proof of
Lemma

5.4
.

By definition of the time-contrained distance and
Lemma

5.9
,

\displaystyle d_{T}(H_{1},H_{2})

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\,\frac{1}{2}\left\|e^{-itH_{1}}(\cdot)e^{itH_{1}}-e^{-itH_{2}}(\cdot)e^{itH_{2}}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{t\in[0,\min(T,\tfrac{1}{4\pi})]}\,\frac{1}{2}\left\|e^{-itH_{1}}(\cdot)e^{itH_{1}}-e^{-itH_{2}}(\cdot)e^{itH_{2}}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{t\in[0,\min(T,\tfrac{1}{4\pi})]}\,\frac{1}{4\pi}\|tH_{1}-tH_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\min\!\left(T,\frac{1}{4\pi}\right)\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

∎

Remark 5.10
.

The constants in Lemma
5.4
can be slightly improved by refining the inequality
|\sin(x/2)|\geq\tfrac{1}{\pi}|x|
(valid for
x\in[-\pi,\pi]
) used in Lemma
5.6
. In particular, if the Hamiltonians satisfy
\|H\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq c
for some
c<\pi
, as in Lemma
5.9
, sharper constants can be obtained.

5.2
Temperature-constrained trace distance

In statistical mechanics and quantum thermodynamics, it is often of interest to compare the equilibrium properties of systems governed by different Hamiltonians. A natural way to do this is to compare their Gibbs states,
\rho_{i}(\beta)=e^{-\beta H_{i}}/\mathrm{Tr}[e^{-\beta H_{i}}]
, which describe the state of the system governed by the Hamiltonians
H_{i}
at inverse temperature
\beta
. Such a comparison can be used, for example, to quantify how a perturbation to the Hamiltonian affects the thermal properties of the system, or to assess the accuracy of a simulation of a thermal state.

From a physical standpoint, it is reasonable to restrict this comparison to a finite range of temperatures, corresponding to a bounded interval
\beta\in[0,B]
of accessible inverse temperatures. In realistic experiments, one cannot prepare systems at arbitrarily low temperatures (large
\beta
) due to practical limitations or constraints on preparation times. Focusing on a finite range of
\beta
therefore reflects actual laboratory capabilities.

From a mathematical perspective, thanks to the Holevo-Helstrom theorem, employing the trace distance to compare Gibbs states provides an operational meaning:

\tfrac{1}{2}\|\rho_{1}(\beta)-\rho_{2}(\beta)\|_{\mathrm{tr}}
quantifies the optimal distinguishability of the two thermal states at inverse temperature
\beta
. Taking the supremum over
\beta\in[0,B]
then corresponds to the maximum distinguishability achievable within the permitted temperature range.

Motivated by these considerations, we introduce the
temperature-constrained trace distance
between Hamiltonians:

d_{B}(H_{1},H_{2})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\beta\in[0,B]}\|\rho_{1}(\beta)-\rho_{2}(\beta)\|_{\mathrm{tr}},

(24)

where
\rho_{i}(\beta)\coloneqq e^{-\beta H_{i}}/\Tr[e^{-\beta H_{i}}]
.

Remark 5.11
.

It is instructive to contrast the definition of the temperature-constrained trace distance in Eq. (
24
) with the unconstrained setting, i.e. when
B=\infty
. In this regime one has

d_{\infty}(H_{1},H_{2})\;\geq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,\|\rho_{1}(\infty)-\rho_{2}(\infty)\|_{\mathrm{tr}}.

If a Hamiltonian
H
has a unique ground state, then the corresponding Gibbs state at infinite inverse temperature reduces to this ground state.
For istance, for
H_{1}=\varepsilon Z
and
H_{2}=-\varepsilon Z
, we obtain
\rho_{1}(\infty)=\ket{0}\!\bra{0}
and
\rho_{2}(\infty)=\ket{1}\!\bra{1}
, which gives

d_{\infty}(H_{1},H_{2})=1,

even though
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are arbitrarily close (e.g. in operator norm).
This highlights that, just as in the time-unconstrained setting, allowing arbitrarily low temperatures leads to maximal distinguishability, even between nearly identical Hamiltonians.

Similarly to what we have done for the time-constrained diamond distance, we can upper bound
d_{B}
in terms of a operator norm between the Hamiltonian.

5.2.1
Upper bounding the temperature-constrained distance

This subsection is devoted to derive an upper bound on the temperature-constrained trace distance in terms of the operator norm of the Hamiltonian difference. We start with the following lemma, which provides un upper bound on the trace distance between Gibbs states. The strategy is to relate the trace distance between Gibbs states to the symmetrized Kullback–Leibler divergence (SKL) via Pinsker’s inequality, and then obtain a bound on the SKL in terms of
\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
.

Lemma 5.12
.

For all Hamiltonians
H_{1},H_{2}
it holds that:

\left\|\frac{e^{H_{1}}}{\Tr e^{H_{1}}}-\frac{e^{H_{2}}}{\Tr e^{H_{2}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\;\leq\;\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

(25)

Lemma

5.12
is strictly tighter than the known bound of
[
BS17
, Lemma 16]
, which states that

\left\|\frac{e^{H_{1}}}{\Tr e^{H_{1}}}-\frac{e^{H_{2}}}{\Tr e^{H_{2}}}\right\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\leq 2\left(e^{\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}-1\right)\,.

Proof of
Lemma

5.12
.

Let
\rho_{1}\coloneqq\frac{e^{H_{1}}}{\Tr e^{H_{1}}}
and
\rho_{2}\coloneqq\frac{e^{H_{2}}}{\Tr e^{H_{2}}}
. The symmetrized Kullback–Leibler divergence (SKL)
[
KL51
]
is defined as

d_{\mathrm{SKL}}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\coloneqq D(\rho_{1}\|\rho_{2})+D(\rho_{2}\|\rho_{1}),

where
D(\rho_{1}\|\rho_{2})
is the relative entropy between the states
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
, defined as

D(\rho_{1}\|\rho_{2})\coloneqq\Tr\left[\rho_{1}\,(\ln\rho_{1}-\ln\rho_{2})\right]\,.

Our first claim is that the following inequality holds:

d_{\mathrm{SKL}}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\;\leq\,\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}.

(26)

To prove
Eq.

26
, we start by computing
d_{\mathrm{SKL}}
explicitly for Gibbs states. Using the definition of relative entropy, we have

d_{\mathrm{SKL}}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\Tr[\rho_{1}(\ln\rho_{1}-\ln\rho_{2})]+\Tr[\rho_{2}(\ln\rho_{2}-\ln\rho_{1})].

(27)

Hence, by substituting in Eq. (
27
) the explicit expression of the Gibbs states, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{SKL}}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})

\displaystyle=-\Tr[\rho_{1}H_{1}]-\ln Z_{1}+\Tr[\rho_{1}H_{2}]+\ln Z_{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\Tr[\rho_{2}H_{2}]-\ln Z_{2}+\Tr[\rho_{2}H_{1}]+\ln Z_{1}

\displaystyle=\Tr[(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})(H_{2}-H_{1})\big].

By exploiting Hölder’s inequality, this expression immediately implies
Eq.

26
. Given this, we now want to prove Eq. (
25
). To this end, we recall Pinsker’s inequality
[
Pin64
]
, which relates relative entropy to trace distance:

\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}\leq D(\rho_{1}\|\rho_{2})\,.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}\leq D(\rho_{1}\|\rho_{2})+D(\rho_{2}\|\rho_{1})=d_{\mathrm{SKL}}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}).

Combining this with Eq. (
26
), we arrive at

\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}\;\leq\,\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\|_{\mathrm{tr}}\,,

(28)

which proves the lemma.
∎

Note that if
H_{1}\mapsto-\beta H_{1}
and
H_{2}\mapsto-\beta H_{2}
, by taking the supremum over
\beta\in[0,B]
we can prove the following result

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be Hamiltonians and let
d_{B}(H_{1},H_{2})
be the temperature-constrained trace distance between
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
. Then

d_{B}(H_{1},H_{2})\;\leq\;\frac{B}{2}\,\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,.

5.2.2
No lower bound for the temperature-constrained distance

We now investigate whether the distance
d_{B}(H_{1},H_{2})
admits a nontrivial lower bound in terms of the operator norm of the difference of Hamiltonians. In particular, one might ask whether inequalities of the form

d_{B}(H_{1},H_{2})\geq\mathrm{poly}\bigl(B,\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\bigr)

can hold in general. The following example shows that this is not the case: even when the operator norm of the difference between
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
is constant, the corresponding Gibbs states can become arbitrarily close in trace distance as the system size increases. To see this, consider the
n
-qubit Hamiltonians

H_{1}=\ket{0^n}\bra{0^n}-\ket{1^n}\bra{1^n},\qquad H_{2}=-\ket{0^n}\bra{0^n}+\ket{1^n}\bra{1^n}.

In the computational basis their spectra are
\mathrm{spec}(H_{1})=\{+1,-1,0,\dots,0\}
and
\mathrm{spec}(H_{2})=\{-1,+1,0,\dots,0\}
, where
0
appears
2^{n}-2
times. Consequently,
e^{-\beta H_{1}}
and
e^{-\beta H_{2}}
are diagonal and the partition functions coincide, i.e.
Z_{1}(\beta)=Z_{2}(\beta)=\Tr(e^{-\beta H_1})=\Tr(e^{-\beta H_2})=2^{n}-2+e^{\beta}+e^{-\beta}.
Thus, the corresponding Gibbs states at inverse temperature
\beta
are

\rho_{1}(\beta)=\frac{1}{Z_{1}(\beta)}\begin{pmatrix}e^{-\beta}&&&\\
&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&e^{\beta}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\rho_{2}(\beta)=\frac{1}{Z_{2}(\beta)}\begin{pmatrix}e^{\beta}&&&\\
&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&e^{-\beta}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, the trace norm of their difference is

\|\rho_{1}(\beta)-\rho_{2}(\beta)\|_{\mathrm{tr}}=\frac{2(e^{\beta}-e^{-\beta})}{2^{n}-2+e^{\beta}+e^{-\beta}}.

(29)

Fixing
B
as a constant, we have that for any
\beta\in[0,B]
the numerator of Eq. (
29
) remains of order
O(1)
while the denominator grows as
2^{n}
. Hence,

\sup_{\beta\in[0,B]}\|\rho_{1}(\beta)-\rho_{2}(\beta)\|_{\mathrm{op}}=O\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
5.5

In this appendix, we prove Lemma
5.5
, which we restate for reader’s convenience.

Proof.

We first show that the minimum of

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\max\bigl(|q(a-x)|,|q(b-x)|\bigr)

(30)

is attained at solutions of

|q(a-x)|=|q(b-x)|\,.

(31)

To begin, consider the following general observation. Given continuous functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the minimizers of

x\mapsto\max(f(x),g(x))

(32)

must lie in one of the following sets:

(i)

Points where
f
has a local minimum and
f(x)>g(x)
,

(ii)

Points where
g
has a local minimum and
g(x)>f(x)
,

(iii)

Points where
f(x)=g(x)
.

In our case, with

f(x)\coloneqq|q(a-x)|,\qquad g(x)\coloneqq|q(b-x)|,

(33)

each local minimum of
f
or
g
equals
0
and
f
and
g
are nonnegative. Thus, (i) and (ii) do not occur, and minimizers must belong to case (iii), i.e. satisfy (
31
).

We now analyse the solutions of Eq. (
31
). Such solutions must satisfy one of the following two conditions:

1.

q(b-x)=q(a-x)
, which is true only if
q(a)=q(b)
, in which case both sides of

equal 0, so the statement is true.

2.

q(b-x)=-q(a-x)
, which gives
q(2x)=q(a+b)
, i.e.

x=\tfrac{a+b}{2}+n\pi,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Hence, since the function
x\mapsto\max(|q(a-x)|,|q(b-x)|)

2\pi

x=\tfrac{a+b}{2}
or
x=\tfrac{a+b}{2}+\pi
. Hence, we obtain that

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\max(|q(a-x)|,|q(b-x)|)

\displaystyle=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\max(|q(q(a)-x)|,|q(q(b)-x)|)

(34)

\displaystyle=\min\!\left(\biggl|q\Bigl(\tfrac{q(a)-q(b)}{2}\Bigr)\biggr|,\,\biggl|q\Bigl(\tfrac{q(a)-q(b)}{2}-\pi\Bigr)\biggr|\right)

(35)

(36)

q(a)\geq q(b)

Case 1:
\tfrac{q(a)-q(b)}{2}\in[0,\pi/2]

Then,
\bigl|q(\tfrac{q(b)+2\pi-q(a)}{2})\bigr|\in[\pi/2,\pi]

36

(37)

Since
|q(a)-q(b)|\in[0,\pi]

|q(a)-q(b)|=\min(|q(a)-q(b)|,|p(a)-p(b)|).

(38)

Combining
Eqs.

37
and
38

Case 2:
\tfrac{q(a)-q(b)}{2}\in[\pi/2,\pi]

Then,
q(a)\in[0,\pi]

q(b)\in[-\pi,0]

q(a)=p(a),\qquad q(b)+2\pi=p(b).

(39)

Thus, as
p(a),\,p(b)\in[0,2\pi]

(p(a)-p(b))/2\in[-\pi,\pi]
