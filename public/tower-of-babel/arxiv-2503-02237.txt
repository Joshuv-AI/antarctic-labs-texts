Women’s Status and Fertility: A Novel Perspective on Low Fertility Issue
Ruiwu Liu

Abstract
This research delves into the impact of women’s social status on familial fertility choices through a concise microeconomic model. This model offers a compelling explanation for the observed decline in fertility rates in developed countries, correlating it with the rising economic status of women, and thereby providing valuable insights for policy-making. In other words, through a game theory model, we identify the rising economic status of women as the underlying cause of low fertility rates in modern developed countries.

1
Introduction

In recent decades, fertility rates in developed countries, especially in East Asia, have consistently remained at low levels. Meanwhile, some developing countries, like China, are also encountering this problem. Economists have been working on explaining the causes of this phenomenon from various perspectives. However, these theories remain contentious, and a universal mechanism to explain the low-fertility issue has yet to be established.

Becker and Barro’s foundational model (1988) introduced the trade-off between the quantity and quality of children. This framework is crucial to understanding the incentives families have to limit fertility as income rises, especially in developing countries, where studies have confirmed this trade-off more strongly than in developed economies (Rosenzweig and Zhang, 2009). On the other hand, Rindfuss and Brewster (1996) argue that: ”insofar as labor force participation acts as a constraint on fertility, we would expect fertility to rise in response to any easing of the worker-mother conflict”. By extension, they furthermore argue that ”We would expect, other things being equal, that improvements in childcare availability acceptability, and quality, and decreases in its cost would have a positive impact on fertility”. At the core of these hypotheses is the assumption that childbearing is a rational decision, and that parents weigh the costs and benefits of having children against their income, career expectations, own standards concerning the quality of care for children.

In addition, according to the neoclassic economic theory of fertility, the decision to have a child is subject to an economically rational decision (a utility
maximization process), and is a function of the economic cost and benefits of children, subject to an income constraint and to individuals’ preferences for children (as opposed to other goods). According to this model, any reduction in the cost of children (as a result of public subsidy) or any increase in income is therefore expected to increase the demand for children (Beck 1981; Cigno 1991). Many other studies also suggest an overall positive preference regarding having children when examining the issue of low fertility. As argued in the gender theory proposed by McDonald (2000), gender inequalities are responsible for countries’ low levels of fertility. However, there is little empirical evidence for McDonald’s gender theory.

In contrast, a large body of literature (Adserà & Ferrer (2012), Gauthier(2007), Matysiak & Vignoli (2007)) suggests an aversion to having children among females in developed countries. Behrman & Gonalons-Pons (2020) also empirically suggest a positive relationship between women’s salaries and their aversion to childbirth.

A prevailing consensus has emerged: as women’s educational attainment and economic status rise, the traditional family fertility patterns are undergoing significant shifts, leading to a substantial decrease in women’s average desire to bear children. This chapter aims to construct a compact and straightforward game theory model to elucidate the impact of women’s economic status on fertility rates, providing theoretical support for policymakers.
In summary, under our foundational assumptions, the husband makes transfer payments to facilitate more consumption for the wife in exchange for having offspring. Through implications from this chapter, we further validate the intrinsic link between women’s economic status and fertility rates. In the following section, we first construct a non-game model as a benchmark.

2
A Benchmark Model

Consider a representative family where the husband (agent
i=m
) always benefits from having children, while the wife (agent
i=w
) is averse to childbearing. Specifically, we assume that the wife’s total utility from raising a child is always negative due the physical and time (economic) costs.

The utility functions for the husband and the wife in this representative family are given by:

u^{m}=\ln(c^{m})+\alpha\ln(n),

[formula]

and

u^{w}=\gamma\ln(c^{w})-\delta\ln(n),

[formula]

where
\gamma
indicates that males and females have different preferences for consumption.

To begin, assume that if they do not form a family or a marriage, both males and females face a same structured budget constraint:

c_{i}+\beta_{i}n\leq a_{i}

[formula]

where
i\in\{w,m\}
,
a_{i}
represents the income of the husband or wife, and
\beta_{i}
denotes the rearing cost per child.

In this benchmark model, although the wife exhibits a negative preference for childbirth, we assume that the representative family operates under a shared constraint due to the wife’s relatively low status. This implies that the wife’s decisions aim to maximize the overall benefits of the representative family while adhering to a unified budget constraint (where
\beta=\beta_{w}+\beta_{m}
, is the total cost of childrearing):

c_{m}+c_{w}+\beta n\leq a_{m}+a_{w}.

[formula]

Another important assumption is that the husband’s preference for childbirth always outweighs the wife’s aversion to childbirth,
\alpha>\delta
. Referring to the utility functions for the husband and wife, we can deduce that the husband benefits from having a family (i.e., having children), whereas the wife is always worse off compared to remaining childless.

Therefore, the representative family’s utility function is

u^{f}=\gamma\ln(c^{w})+\ln(c^{m})+(\alpha-\delta)\ln(n),

[formula]

where
f
represents the family.

Solving the above utility optimization problem under the family constraint
(4.5)
leads to the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1

Under the assumptions of this benchmark model, a family’s fertility is positively influenced by the husband’s preference for childbirth and negatively influenced by the wife’s aversion to childbirth, her preference for consumption, and the costs of childbearing.

We have now constructed the benchmark model for this chapter, which assumes that the representative females hold a relatively low status within the household and have little to no bargaining power. In the next section, we will develop a game theory model to explore another scenario that better reflects the realities of a low-fertility world.

3
A Game between the Wife and Husband

With the awakening of women’s self-awareness (or the improvement of their social and economic status), they begin to prioritize their own benefits in marriage rather than solely focusing on family utility. In this context, we introduce two new utility functions for different genders.

The wife’s utility function is as follows:

u^{w}=\gamma\ln(c^{w})-\delta n.

[formula]

In contrast, the husband’s utility function is

u^{m}=\ln(c^{m})+\alpha n.

[formula]

Intuitively, the wife will not bear children unless she receives additional benefits (or promises) from her husband. In other words, the wife will choose to bear children only if the utility under the option of childbirth exceeds the utility under no-birth strategy, or

\gamma\ln(a^{w})<\gamma\ln(c^{w})-\delta n.

[formula]

To ensure a feasible condition for childbirth, we assume that the husband transfers a portion of his income to his wife. Thus, the budget constraint for this representative wife is given by:

c^{w}\leq a^{w}+\rho n,

[formula]

where
\rho
is the husband’s transfer payment per child or his willingness to have children.

As discussed, the representative husband in this game will choose to transfer an uncertain portion of his income to encourage his wife to give birth only if the benefits of having children outweigh the sacrifice in his consumption utility, equivalently we have

\ln(a^{m})\leq\ln(c^{m})+\alpha n.

[formula]

Thus, the husband’s constraint can be expressed as:

c^{m}\leq a^{m}-\rho n,

[formula]

where childrearing cost is incorporated into the transfer.

Although, compared to the benchmark model, we implicitly assume an improvement in women’s status through transfers and a game structure, it is evident that in most countries today, the average social status of women remains lower than that of men. Therefore, in the following models, we allow the husband, through transfers, to determine the final number of children.

Consider the wife’s reaction to the husband’s transfer by solving her utility maximization problem,

\max_{n}u^{w}(c^{w},n)=\gamma\ln(c^{w})-\delta n,

[formula]

subject to the above wife’s budget constraint
(4.9)
, we derive the wife’s reaction function:

n^{*}(\rho)=\frac{\gamma}{\delta}-\frac{a^{w}}{\rho},

[formula]

where
\frac{\gamma}{\delta}
is defined as the (wife’s) preference effect, and
(-\frac{a^{w}}{\rho})
is defined as the transfer effect.

Substituting the wife’s reaction function
(4.13)
into the husband’s utility function and solving his utility maximization problem:

\max_{\rho}u^{m}(c^{m},\rho),

[formula]

subject to the husband’s budget constraint, we derive the equilibrium level of transfer:

\rho^{*}=-\frac{\alpha a_{w}}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\alpha^{2}a_{w}^{2}}{4}+\frac{\alpha\delta}{\gamma}[a_{w}(a_{w}+a_{m})]}.

[formula]

3.0.1
the Husband’s Transfer Strategy

Now, we can derive the following results for the optimal level of transfer:

\frac{\partial\rho^{*}}{\partial\alpha}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial n^{*}}{\partial\alpha}>0;

[formula]

\frac{\partial\rho^{*}}{\partial\delta}>0,

[formula]

when the negative marginal preference effect (with respect to the wife’s aversion) is smaller than the marginal positive transfer effect, or
\frac{a_{w}}{\rho^{*2}}\frac{\partial\rho^{*}}{\partial\delta}>\frac{\gamma}{\delta^{2}}
, we have
\frac{\partial n^{*}}{\partial\delta}>0
; when the negative marginal preference effect (with respect to the wife’s aversion) is greater than the marginal positive transfer effect, or
\frac{a_{w}}{\rho^{*2}}\frac{\partial\rho^{*}}{\partial\delta}\leq\frac{\gamma}{\delta^{2}}
, we have
\frac{\partial n^{*}}{\partial\delta}\leq 0
.

\frac{\partial\rho^{*}}{\partial\gamma}<0,

[formula]

when the negative marginal transfer effect (with respect to the wife’s consumption preference) is smaller than the marginal positive preference effect, or
\frac{1}{\delta}>-\frac{a_{w}}{\rho^{*2}}\frac{\partial\rho^{*}}{\partial\gamma}
, we have
\frac{\partial n^{*}}{\partial\gamma}>0
; when the negative marginal transfer effect (with respect to the wife’s consumption preference) is greater than the marginal positive preference effect, or
\frac{1}{\delta}\leq-\frac{a_{w}}{\rho^{*2}}\frac{\partial\rho^{*}}{\partial\gamma}
, we have
\frac{\partial n^{*}}{\partial\gamma}\leq 0
.

\frac{\partial\rho^{*}}{\partial a_{w}}>\frac{\partial\rho^{*}}{\partial a_{m}}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial n^{*}}{\partial\frac{a_{w}}{a_{m}}}<0.

[formula]

\frac{\partial n^{*}}{\partial(a_{w}+a_{m})}\gtrless 0.

[formula]

Proposition 4.1

The representative family’s fertility is positively associated with the husband’s preference for childbirth and negatively associated with the wife’s relative income; there exists a certain level of the wife’s income beyond which she will no longer have children.

Proposition 4.2

The relationship between fertility and family income can be either negative or positive. Similarly, the relationship between fertility and the wife’s consumption preference (or her aversion to childbirth) can also be either negative or positive, depending on the value of relative income.

3.1
Implications

This model sheds light on the low fertility problem in developed countries, where gender equality is better. When the wife’s income is sufficiently high (compared to her husband’s), there is no childbirth. More importantly, it highlights that relative income (or social status) plays a decisive role in fertility choices, rather than national per capita or household income. This is why some developing countries (with better gender equality) like China are also suffering from low-level fertility.

This section provides a valid explanation for the low fertility issue in developed countries under a concise game theory framework, complementing the basic quantity and quality interpretation. It innovatively interprets the issue of low fertility rates by incorporating the assumption of the wife’s negative preference for childbirth. An overall negative preference for childbirth among women aligns with the current consensus. The reasons behind this phenomenon are complex, but women’s empowerment is clearly an essential factor. According to this model, low-level fertility is an unavoidable outcome of social development in the absence of policy intervention.

4
An Extended Model

In the previous model, the child-rearing cost was incorporated into the husband’s transfer payment
\rho
. For a more general case, we rewrite the husband’s constraint by explicitly including a child-rearing cost term:

c^{m}\leq a^{m}-\beta n-\rho n,

[formula]

where the other settings and assumptions remain consistent with the model in the previous section.

Reprocessing the husband’s utility maximization problem subject to this new budget constraint (the wife’s reaction strategy remains the same as before), we derive a cubic equation in terms of the husband transfer
\rho
:

\frac{\Gamma}{a_{w}}\rho^{3}+\alpha\Gamma\rho^{2}+[\beta+\alpha\beta\Gamma-\alpha(a_{m}+a_{w})]\rho-\alpha\beta a_{w}=0,

[formula]

where
\Gamma=\frac{\gamma}{\delta}
.

It is provable that there exists a certain interval of parameters that guarantee two solutions to this equation, where we define
\rho_{1}^{*}<\rho_{2}^{*}
.

If there are no specific restrictions on the value of the transfer
\rho
, the outcomes and implications of this model become trivial and ultimately align with those of the previous one. In other words, in a purely mathematical problem, the agent will undoubtedly choose the transfer amount that provides him with higher utility. However, when taking cultural differences across countries into account, reality appears to align with this special result. Next, we will interpret this result through a real-life perspective.

Proposition 4.3

The permissible domain of transfer payments is constrained, with different intervals of transfer values representing different cultures.

4.0.1
Exclusive Implication of the Extended Model

First of all, it is trivial that most of the implications of this model align with those presented in the previous section. That is, the improvement of women’s social status remains a key driving force behind the low fertility issue under this extended model, so we will not elaborate further to avoid redundancy. Instead, we will focus on interpreting the practical significance of the two-solution result.

Let us first consider the example of China, where high bride prices are culturally common (Bao & Zhang, 2015; Chakrabarti, 2022; Tang, 2023). Most males tend to opt for higher transfer payments, regardless of whether this high bride price strategy yields higher utility compared to another solution to the equation
(4.27)
. A stark contrast can be observed in India, where high dowries are a prevalent cultural phenomenon (Mithun, 2018; Raizada, 2018; Chiplunkar & Weaver, 2023). This implies that husbands in India are often able to opt for lower transfer payments.

Directly comparing China and India may be contentious, as, despite both being developing countries, their current levels of economic development differ significantly. A more compelling example is Singapore, a developed Asian country historically dominated by a Chinese immigrant population, where Indian immigrants have increasingly settled in recent years and now comprise about 9.1% of the population. As a young immigrant nation, it is reasonable to assume that ethnic groups largely retain their original cultures, allowing us to observe a significant fertility gap (Saw, 1990; Jones, 2012; Yeung, 2019) between Chinese and Indian populations within the same national context.

That is, different feasible intervals of the husbands’ transfer payments
\rho
represent different cultural or social contexts. Technically, rather than an ambiguous interpretation of ‘cultural difference’, the mathematical interval of
\rho^{*}
, or the ‘culture’, is actually represented by the relationship between the wife’s relative income and other parameters. This section offers a more tractable and interpretable approach to the discussion.

4.1
Conclusion and Further Discussion

The real world is far more complex than the economic models presented in this section, but our models still provide valuable insights into modern low-fertility phenomena. Clearly, the rise in women’s social status enables them to choose the number of giving birth that maximizes their own benefits within a family structure. It is worth noting, however, that we still acknowledge the husband can determine the final number of children through transfer payments, which aligns with the reality that women’s overall social and economic status remains lower than men’s according to a substantial body of statistical evidence. Nevertheless, the rise in women’s relative income has granted them greater bargaining power, which helps us to explain the low fertility rates observed across all developed economies.

From a policy perspective, as we conclude that low fertility will become inevitable for all countries in the future with the development of human civilization, governments aiming to stimulate childbirth could enhance transfers by providing childbirth subsidies to married women. On the other hand, the costs of child-rearing are understated in this chapter. Whether based on our models or the classic quantity-quality tradeoff theory, the relatively high costs of child-rearing in developed countries have significantly impacted fertility rates. In practice, the rising cost of education is one of the primary drivers of declining birth rates (Colleran & Snopkowski, 2018; Smith & Doe, 2022; DeCicca & Krashinsky, 2022). Governments should also influence family birth decisions by adjusting the supply in the education market.

In the extreme case of this model, which often aligns with reality, wives may choose not to have children. This decision stems from an assumed aversion to childbirth—an aversion that is not only physiological or economic but also psychological or rooted in societal perceptions. The reality appears to be far more challenging than what current economic theories imply. Among many young women, the inclination toward childlessness has seemingly moved beyond purely economic considerations, taking on the characteristics of a broader, almost ideological collective identity (Buss, 2016; Price, 2024). Obviously, governments would not want this trend to crystallize into a widely accepted belief, as the challenges it presents extend well beyond the scope of fiscal policy solutions. In this case, the government could perhaps take a more active role in safeguarding women’s rights, reshaping societal fertiltiy attitudes, and even fostering better gender relations in society.

Moreover, since we are working with a compact economic model, the simplified assumption that the husband will make transfer payments is obviously not entirely realistic. This setting, like other economics models, is not universally applicable. However, as a concise representation of general cases, our model is capable of deriving many intriguing conclusions and serviceable policy implications.

In summary, our models suggest that when women have a higher socio-economic status, that is, greater bargaining power within the family, families tend to nurture fewer offspring.

We consider the cubic equation

where all parameters

\Gamma,\alpha,\beta,a_{m},a_{w}

has
two

.

5.0.4
Basic Observations

(a) Positivity of parameters.

We have:

\Gamma>0,\quad\alpha>0,\quad\beta>0,\quad a_{m}>0,\quad a_{w}>0.

(b) Value at
\rho=0

f(0)\;=\;-\,\alpha\,\beta\,a_{w}\;<\;0.

\lim_{\rho\to+\infty}f(\rho)\;=\;+\infty.

By the Intermediate Value Theorem, there is at least one positive root.
To prove there are

crosses zero at two different positive values of
\rho

We write

If

\displaystyle\text{increasing on }(0,\rho_{1}),

\displaystyle\text{decreasing on }(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}),

A common strategy:

1.

2.
