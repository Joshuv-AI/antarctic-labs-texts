Proportional Response Dynamics in Gross Substitutes Markets
Yun Kuen Cheung, Richard Cole, Yixin Tao

Abstract
Proportional response is a well-established distributed algorithm which has been shown to converge to competitive equilibria in both Fisher and Arrow-Debreu markets, for various sub-families of homogeneous utilities, including linear and constant elasticity of substitution utilities. We propose a natural generalization of proportional response for gross substitutes utilities, and prove that it converges to competitive equilibria in Fisher markets. This is the first convergence result of a proportional response style dynamics in Fisher markets for utilities beyond the homogeneous utilities covered by the Eisenberg-Gale convex program. We show an empirical convergence rate of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / T ) \mathcal{O}(1/T) for the prices. Furthermore, we show that the allocations of a lazy version of the generalized proportional response dynamics converge to competitive equilibria in Arrow-Debreu markets.

Introduction

Competitive (aka market) equilibrium is a fundamental concept in the study of markets, describing stable outcomes that can emerge from agents’ trading. Originally proposed by Léon Walras more than 150 years ago
Walras 1874
, its existence under mild conditions was formally proved by Nobel laureates Kenneth Arrow and Gérald Debreu and Guggenheim fellow Lionel McKenzie in the 1950’s
Arrow and Debreu 1954
;
McKenzie 1954
;
McKenzie 1959
. The early 2000’s saw a surge of interest at the intersection of computer science and economics, leading researchers to adopt an algorithmic perspective on equilibrium. The relevance of algorithms to economics is neatly captured by Kamal Jain’s remark: “If your laptop cannot find it, neither can the market.”
Jain 2004
Nevertheless, real-world economic systems are often more akin to decentralized computing environments, where information is dispersed among agents who trade in a distributed manner, rather than relying on a central “computer” (the
invisible hand
) with perfect information. Understanding how equilibrium can be achieved in such settings remains a challenge at the frontier of economic theory and algorithmic game theory.

Among various natural candidates of distributed market algorithms, proportional response (PR) has attracted significant attention, partly due to its simple implementation in networked markets
Levin et al. 2008
. The PR protocol is particularly intuitive in Fisher markets, where agents are divided into buyers with money and sellers with goods. It operates as an iterated process: in each round, buyers update their bids – reflecting the amount of money they intend to spend on various goods – based on their utility functions and the goods received in the previous round, while sellers allocate goods according to a Shapley-Shubik-style mechanism, distributing them in proportion to buyers’ bids
Brânzei 2021
.
Wu and Zhang 2007
were the first to formally identify the power of PR in driving Arrow-Debreu markets toward competitive equilibrium. Since then, a growing body of research has extended PR’s convergence guarantees to Arrow-Debreu markets, Fisher markets,
production economies and attention markets, demonstrating its robustness across a range of settings.
Zhang 2011
;
Birnbaum et al. 2011
;
Cheung et al. 2018
;
Brânzei et al. 2018
;
Cheung et al. 2019
;
Gao and Kroer 2020
;
Brânzei et al. 2021
;
Cheung et al. 2021
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Kolumbus et al. 2023
;
Li and Tang 2024
;
Cheng et al. 2024
.

For PR in Fisher markets, all known convergence results have been established under the assumption that buyers have linear or constant elasticity of substitution (CES) utility functions, i.e.,
u(\mathbf{x})=\left(\sum_{j}a_{j}x_{j}^{\rho}\right)^{1/\rho}
. The competitive equilibria in such Fisher markets are characterized by the Eisenberg-Gale convex program
Eisenberg and Gale 1959
;
Eisenberg 1961
, which has provided valuable insights through connections to optimization algorithms. In fact, this convex program extends beyond CES utilities to capture equilibria for the broader class of homogeneous utility functions, i.e.,
u(c\mathbf{x})=c\cdot u(\mathbf{x})
for any
c>0
and good bundle
\mathbf{x}
.
However, homogeneous utilities remain a relatively restrictive subset compared to the diverse preferences that economists have considered.
For instance, even the intuitive separable utilities of the form
u(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j}u_{j}(x_{j})
are generally not homogeneous.
This raises the open question: to what extent can PR be applied to markets with non-homogeneous utility functions?

The prior algorithmic success in competitive equilibrium computation suggests that gross substitute (GS) utilities are a promising target for addressing the open question. GS utilities emerged as a natural sufficient condition for the convergence of
tâtonnement

Arrow and Hurwicz 1958
;
Arrow et al. 1959
,
a natural market dynamics introduced by
Walras 1874
alongside the concept of competitive equilibrium.
While the GS assumption enables an intuitive proof of tâtonnement convergence, it is not clear what the PR update rule for GS utilities should be, and whether it is sufficient to guarantee the convergence of PR.

Our contributions.

We propose a new PR rule that extends to any differentiable utilities in Fisher markets.
This generalizes the PR rule for substitute CES utilities.
In each round, a buyer with utility
u
allocates monetary bids across each good
j
in proportion to
x_{j}\nabla_{j}u(\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{x}
is the good bundle she received in the previous round. Precisely, if the buyer’s budget is
e
, her bid for good
j
is

\displaystyle b_{j}~=~e\frac{x_{j}\nabla_{j}u(\mathbf{x})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{j^{\prime}}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u(\mathbf{x})}~.

(1)

Based on the bids from all buyers, each good is allocated in proportion to the bids placed on that good. Here are a few remarks to our new PR rule:

1.

The PR rule (
1

2.

When
u
is a CES utility function of the form
u(\mathbf{x})=\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}}a_{j^{\prime}}x_{j^{\prime}}^{\rho}\right)^{1/\rho}
where
0<\rho\leq 1
, the PR update (
1
) simplifies to
b_{j}=e\frac{a_{j}(x_{j})^{\rho}}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}a_{j}x_{j^{\prime}}^{\rho}}
, which aligns with the PR rule analyzed in
Zhang 2011
and
Cheung et al. 2018
.

3.

When
u
is a homogeneous utility function, i.e.,
u(c\mathbf{x})=c\cdot u(\mathbf{x})
for any
c>0
and good bundle
\mathbf{x}
, by Euler’s homogeneous function theorem
u(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{j^{\prime}}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u(\mathbf{x})
, so the PR update (
1
) simplifies to
b_{j}=e\frac{x_{j}\nabla_{j}u(\mathbf{x})}{u(\mathbf{x})}
.

4.

For general utility function
u

\mathbf{q}=\left\{e\frac{\nabla_{j}u(\mathbf{x})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{j^{\prime}}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u(\mathbf{x})}\right\}_{j}
can be interpreted as a price vector at which
\mathbf{x}
is the optimal demand bundle. Precisely,

\mathbf{x}
is the optimal solution to
\arg\max_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}u(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
subject to the budget constraint
\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\leq e
.
Thus, the PR update rule can be interpreted as follows: given the allocation from the previous round, the agent assumes the current price to be the one at which this allocation is the best response. In the next round, the agent adjusts spending accordingly to optimize demand under this assumed price.

We show that if all buyers have GS utilities satisfying a few standard conditions, the new PR guarantees an empirical convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/T)
toward competitive equilibrium prices; that is, the distance between the average of the prices over the first
T
iterations and the equilibrium prices is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1/T)
. Our analysis also shows that the new PR converges point-wise to competitive equilibrium, but it does not provide a convergence rate.

To extend our analysis to Arrow-Debreu markets, we consider a
lazy
version of the new PR update.
The idea of lazy PR was first proposed by
Brânzei et al. 2021
: each agent maintains a bank account where she accumulates all revenues from selling her endowment of goods. Instead of spending her entire balance in each round, she allocates only a fixed fraction (strictly between 0 and 1) of her savings.
We show the allocations of this lazy PR update converge to the allocation of a competitive equilibrium.

Our proof strategy involves transforming the update rule into a price-demand system for each agent, as outlined in Remark (4).

\mathbf{q}_{i}

based on this price vector. If the total demand bundle remains feasible, i.e.,
\sum_{i}x_{ij}=1

Further Related Work.

The study of market dynamics has long been a fundamental topic in both economics and algorithmic game theory.

One of the foundational dynamics in this area is the tâtonnement process, introduced by
Walras 1874
, which describes a natural market adjustment driven by supply and demand. In this process, agents always obtain their preferred bundles of goods while prices adjust dynamically—rising for overdemanded goods and falling for underdemanded ones. The development of the tâtonnement procedure progressed with the advancement of general equilibrium theory.
Samuelson 1946
modeled tâtonnement as a continuous dynamical system, laying the foundation for subsequent theoretical studies. In seminal works by
Arrow and Hurwicz 1958
and
Arrow et al. 1959
, the gross substitutes property was introduced as a crucial condition to ensure the convergence of the tâtonnement process. In the early 2000s, increasing interest at the intersection of computer science and economics led researchers to adopt an algorithmic perspective on market equilibrium. This shift prompted the exploration of discrete variants of the tâtonnement algorithms. See e.g.,
Codenotti et al. 2005a
;
Avigdor-Elgrabli et al. 2014
;
Goktas et al. 2023
;
Cole and Fleischer 2008
;
Cheung et al. 2012
;
Cheung et al. 2013
;
Cole and Tao 2019
;
Goktas et al. 2021
;
Dvijotham et al. 2022
;
Gao and Kroer 2020
;
Fleischer et al. 2008
;
Cheung and Cole 2018
.

Many researchers have explored an even simpler market dynamic: auction algorithms. While tâtonnement adjusts prices bidirectionally in response to supply and demand, auction algorithms follow an ascending price trajectory. The first auction algorithm was introduced by
Garg and Kapoor 2006a
for linear utility functions and was later extended to broader settings
Garg and Kapoor 2006b
;
Garg and Kapoor 2007
;
Garg et al. 2004
, including separable concave gross substitutes utility functions and uniformly separable gross substitutes utility functions. For general weak gross substitutes (WGS) utilities, ascending-price algorithms have been studied by
Bei et al. 2019a
and
Garg et al. 2023
.

Most of these results fall within the domain of gross substitutes utility functions
Fisher 1972
. However, outside this domain, certain classes of non-gross substitutes utility functions have been shown to pose significant computational challenges for finding market equilibria in either Fisher markets or Arrow-Debreu markets
Chen and Teng 2009
;
Chen et al. 2009
;
Chen et al. 2017
;
Codenotti et al. 2006
;
Garg et al. 2017
;
Vazirani and Yannakakis 2011
;
Bei et al. 2019b
. Despite these difficulties, substantial research effort has been dedicated to developing algorithms for computing market equilibria under various settings
Brainard and Scarf 2005
;
Codenotti et al. 2005b
;
Codenotti et al. 2004
;
Devanur et al. 2008
;
Duan and Mehlhorn 2015
;
Garg and Végh 2023
;
Jain and Varadarajan 2006
;
Ye 2008
.

Corresponding Price

Since the utility function is concave, given any allocation

is the demand of buyer

Lemma 2

For any allocation

Proof.

Consider any subgradient

Fisher market equilibrium

In a Fisher market, each good
j
has a price
p_{j}
and each buyer
i
receives a bundle
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, where
x_{ij}
denotes the allocation of good
j

i
. The concept of Fisher market equilibrium characterizes the equilibrium allocation and prices in the market. WLOG, we assume that each good has one unit of supply.

Definition 6

(Fisher market equilibrium)
.

An allocation-price pair
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
is a Fisher market equilibrium if

1.

each buyer
i
receives the optimal bundle given the price,
\mathbf{x}^{*}_{i}\in\mathbf{x}^{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(\mathbf{p}^{*},e_{i})
;

2.

no good is oversold,
\sum_{i}x^{*}_{ij}\leq 1
for all
j
;

3.

every good with a positive price is fully allocated,
\sum_{i}x^{*}_{ij}<1

.

Since utility functions are strictly increasing, the following observation holds.

Observation 7
.

At equilibrium,
\mathbf{p}^{*}>0
and for each good

\sum_{i}x^{*}_{ij}=1
.

3
Proportional Response Dynamics in Fisher markets

Proportional Response Dynamics is an iterative procedure. At each iteration
t
, the dynamics are characterized by the following steps:

1.

Budget Spending: Each buyer
i
allocates their budget
e_{i}
among the goods. Let
b^{t}_{ij}
denotes the spending of buyer
i
on good
j
at round
t
. It follows that
\sum_{j}b_{ij}^{t}=e_{i}
for each buyer
i
.

2.

Goods Allocation: Based on the spending
\{b^{t}_{ij}\}_{\{i,j\}}
, the amount of good
j
allocated buyer
i
is proportional to their spending on good
j
:
x_{ij}^{t}=b^{t}_{ij}/(\sum_{i}b_{ij}^{t})
.

3.

Spending Update: For the next round, the spending
b_{ij}^{t+1}
is updated to be proportional to
x_{ij}^{t}\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})
.

The process begins with an initial spending allocation
b_{ij}^{0}>0
for all buyer
i
and good
j
.

Mathematically, the entire procedure can be expressed as:

\displaystyle b^{t+1}_{ij}=e_{i}\frac{x_{ij}^{t}\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{ij^{\prime}}^{t}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}\hskip 7.22743pt\text{ for all agent $i$ and good $j$,}

where
x_{ij}^{t}=\frac{b_{ij}^{t}}{p_{j}^{t}}
and
p_{j}^{t}=\sum_{i}b_{ij}^{t}
.

(3)

The update rule can be rewritten as follows:

b_{ij}^{t+1}=x_{ij}^{t}q_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})\hskip 7.22743pt\text{ for all agent $i$ and good $j$},

where
q_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})
is the corresponding price of allocation
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t}
(see (
2
)). This implies that when buyer
i
receives bundle
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t}
in round
t
, in next round, buyer
i
will make the optimal spending assuming the price is the corresponding price of
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t}
.

Example 8
.

When
u_{i}
is a homogeneous utility function, i.e.,
u_{i}(c\mathbf{x}_{i})=c\cdot u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
for any
c>0
and bundle
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, by Euler’s homogeneous function theorem,
u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{ij^{\prime}}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
, the update rule simplifies to
b_{ij}^{t+1}=e_{i}\frac{x_{ij}^{t}\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}{u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}
. Moreover, for CES utility functions,
u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=(\sum_{j}a_{ij}x_{ij}^{\rho_{i}})^{1/\rho_{i}}
, the update rule can further be simplified to:
b^{t+1}_{ij}=e_{i}\frac{a_{ij}(x_{ij}^{t})^{\rho_{i}}}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}a_{ij^{\prime}}(x_{ij^{\prime}}^{t})^{\rho_{i}}}.

\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{e_{i}\frac{\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{ij^{\prime}}^{t}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}}
is non-positive, for this implies that the KL divergence for the spending decreases monotonically.

In order to apply Lemma
12
, we use the corresponding price vector
\mathbf{q}_{i}(\cdot)
for buyer
i
:

\displaystyle q_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})=e_{i}\frac{\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{ij^{\prime}}^{t}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}.

By Observation
5
, and given that
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t}>0
, we have
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t}=\mathbf{x}^{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(\mathbf{q}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t}),e_{i})
, and this implies
\sum_{i}x^{\mathcal{D}}_{ij}(\mathbf{q}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t}),e_{i})=\sum_{i}x_{ij}^{t}=1
for all goods
j
.
Therefore, by Lemma
12
,

\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{e_{i}\frac{\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{ij^{\prime}}^{t}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}}=\sum_{ij}x_{ij}^{*}p_{j}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{q_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}\leq 0.

Thus, the KL divergence decreases monotonically,

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{b_{ij}^{*}}{b_{ij}^{t+1}}\leq\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{b_{ij}^{*}}{b_{ij}^{t}}-\sum_{j}p_{j}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{p_{j}^{t}}.

(5)

This implies the sequence
\mathbf{p}^{t}
converges to
\mathbf{p}^{*}
. Furthermore, by the convexity of the KL divergence:

\displaystyle\sum_{j}p_{j}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}p_{j}^{t}}\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{ij}p_{j}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{p_{j}^{t}}\overset{(a)}{\leq}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{b_{ij}^{*}}{b_{ij}^{0}},

(a) follows by summing (
5
) over
t
.
This establishes the convergence rate of
O(1/T)
for the average price vector.

∎

(6)

Proof of Theorem

In this section, we aim to demonstrate that the allocation

Our first lemma shows that, given an allocation, there exists a threshold such that for any price vector resulting in this allocation, the price vector must be no less than this threshold.

Lemma 18

Given an allocation
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}^{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(\mathbf{p},e_{i})
and
\mathbf{p}>0
, there exists a threshold

such that

We construct a sequence of allocations

for all
k
. These allocations take the form:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{i}=\alpha\mathbf{a}_{k}+(1-\beta)\mathbf{x}_{i},

\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
and
\mathbf{a}_{k}
is the allocation vector that assigns a unit of the

\alpha_{k}

\beta_{k}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{i}

\displaystyle=\alpha(p^{\prime}_{k})+(1-\beta)(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}

Setting of Exchange markets

Consider an exchange market with
n
agents, where each agent
i
is initially endowed with goods,
\mathcal{G}_{i}
. Each agent has a utility function defined over all goods, and these utility functions satisfy Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
, as in Fisher markets.

In the exchange market, agents begin by selling their endowed goods to obtain money. They then use the money to purchase goods that maximize their utilities. Thus, given a price vector
\mathbf{p}
over goods, the demand of agent
i
is defined as:

\mathbf{x}^{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(\mathbf{p},e_{i})=\arg\max_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}\text{~s.t.~}\mathbf{p}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}\leq e_{i}\text{~and~}\mathbf{x}_{i}\geq 0,

where
e_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}p_{j}
.

Exchange market equilibrium

An exchange market equilibrium characterizes the equilibrium allocation and price in the market.

Definition 21

(Exchange market equilibrium)
.

An allocation-price pair
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{p}^{*})
is an exchange market equilibrium if the following conditions hold:

1.

each agent
i
receives the optimal bundle given the price,
\mathbf{x}^{*}_{i}\in\mathbf{x}^{\mathcal{D}}_{i}(\mathbf{p}^{*},e^{*}_{i})
, where
e^{*}_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}p^{*}_{j}
;

2.

no good is oversold,
\sum_{i}x^{*}_{ij}\leq 1
for all
j
;

3.

every good with a positive price is fully allocated,
\sum_{i}x^{*}_{ij}<1
implies
p^{*}_{j}=0
.

Since utility functions are strictly increasing, the following observation still holds.

Observation 22
.

At equilibrium,
\mathbf{p}^{*}>0
and for each good
j
,
\sum_{i}x^{*}_{ij}=1
.

4.1
Lazy Proportional Response Dynamics

Lazy Proportional Response Dynamics is an iterative procedure introduced by
Brânzei et al. 2021
in exchange markets. Each agent has a parameter
0<\alpha_{i}<1
, which specifies the proportion of her current budget agent
i
wants to spend in the market. At each iteration
t
, the dynamics are characterized by the following steps:

1.

Budget Spending: Each agent
i
allocates a fraction,
\alpha_{i}
, of the total budget
B_{i}^{t}
among the goods. Let
b^{t}_{ij}
denotes the spending of agent
i
on good
j
at round
t
. It follows that
\sum_{j}b_{ij}^{t}=\alpha_{i}B_{i}^{t}
for each agent
i
.

2.

Goods Allocation: Based on the spending
\{b^{t}_{ij}\}_{\{i,j\}}
, the amount of good
j
allocated to agent
i
is proportional to her spending:
x_{ij}^{t}=b^{t}_{ij}/(\sum_{i}b_{ij}^{t})
.

3.

Budget update: For the next round, each agent
i
collects all agents’ spending on goods in
i
and adds it to her budget: the budget of agent
i
,
B_{i}^{t+1}
, is updated to be
(1-\alpha_{j})B_{i}^{t}+\sum_{i^{\prime}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}b_{i^{\prime}j}^{t}
.

4.

Spending Update: the spending
b_{ij}^{t+1}
is updated to be proportional to
x_{ij}^{t}\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})
.

The process begins with an initial budget
B_{i}^{0}>0
and initial spending
b_{ij}^{0}>0
for all agents
i
and goods
j
.

Mathematically, letting
e_{i}^{t}\triangleq\alpha_{i}B_{i}^{t}
, which is the total spending of agent
i
at round
t
, the entire procedure can be expressed as:

\displaystyle b^{t+1}_{ij}=e_{i}^{t+1}\frac{x_{ij}^{t}\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{ij^{\prime}}^{t}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}~~~\text{for all $i$ and $j$}

where
x_{ij}^{t}=\frac{b_{ij}^{t}}{p_{j}^{t}}
,
p_{j}^{t}=\sum_{i}b_{ij}^{t}
, and
e_{i}^{t+1}=(1-\alpha_{i})e_{i}^{t}+\alpha_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{G}_{i}}p_{j}^{t}
.

(13)

WLOG, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 3
.

The initial total budgets equals
1
:
\sum_{i}B_{i}^{0}=1
.

Since the money will not be consumed, we have the following straightforward claim.

Claim 23
.

At any round
t
,
\sum_{i}B_{i}^{t}=1
.

4.2
Convergence Result

The following theorem shows the sequence of allocations will converge to the exchange market equilibrium.

Theorem 24
.

If a market equilibrium exists, then the sequence of allocations
\mathbf{x}^{t}
converges to an equilibrium allocation
\mathbf{x}^{*}

Proof.

Consider a market equilibrium
(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{*},\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{*})

\tilde{B}_{j}^{*}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}\tilde{p}^{*}_{j}

\tilde{e}_{i}^{*}=\tilde{p}^{*}_{i}
, and

1

\mathbf{p}^{*}=\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{*}/(\sum_{i}\tilde{B}_{i}^{*})

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{b_{ij}^{*}}{b_{ij}^{t+1}}+\sum_{i}\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)e_{i}^{*}\log\frac{e_{i}^{*}}{e_{i}^{t+1}}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~\hskip 14.45377pt=\sum_{ij}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{e_{i}^{*}}{e_{i}^{t+1}}+\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{b_{ij}^{*}}{b_{ij}^{t}}-\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{p_{j}^{t}}+\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{e_{i}^{*}\frac{\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{ij^{\prime}}^{t}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~\hskip 14.45377pt=\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{b_{ij}^{*}}{b_{ij}^{t}}+\sum_{i}\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)e_{i}^{*}\log\frac{e_{i}^{*}}{e_{i}^{t}}+\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{e_{i}^{*}\frac{\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{ij^{\prime}}^{t}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\hskip 14.45377pt\hskip 14.45377pt+\sum_{ij}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{e_{i}^{*}}{e_{i}^{t+1}}-\sum_{i}\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)e_{i}^{*}\log\frac{e_{i}^{*}}{e_{i}^{t}}-\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{p_{j}^{t}}.

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{b_{ij}^{*}}{b_{ij}^{t+1}}+\sum_{i}\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)e_{i}^{*}\log\frac{e_{i}^{*}}{e_{i}^{t+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{b_{ij}^{*}}{b_{ij}^{t+1}}+\sum_{i}\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)e_{i}^{*}\log\frac{\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)e_{i}^{*}}{\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)e_{i}^{t+1}}

can be viewed as the money kept by agent

Thus, letting

\displaystyle\mathbf{KL}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{KL}(t)+\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{e_{i}^{*}\frac{\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{ij^{\prime}}^{t}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~\hskip 14.45377pt\hskip 14.45377pt+\sum_{ij}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{e_{i}^{*}}{e_{i}^{t+1}}-\sum_{i}\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)e_{i}^{*}\log\frac{e_{i}^{*}}{e_{i}^{t}}-\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{p_{j}^{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{KL}(t)+\sum_{ij}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{e_{i}^{*}\frac{\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{ij^{\prime}}^{t}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~\hskip 14.45377pt\hskip 14.45377pt+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}e_{i}^{*}\log\frac{e_{i}^{*}}{e_{i}^{t+1}}-\sum_{i}\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}\right)e_{i}^{*}\log\frac{e_{i}^{*}}{e_{i}^{t}}-\sum_{j}p_{j}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{p_{j}^{t}}.

Note that
\sum_{ij}^{*}b_{ij}^{*}\log\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{e_{i}^{*}\frac{\nabla_{j}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}}x_{ij^{\prime}}^{t}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{t})}}\leq 0

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce a new PR update rule. We demonstrate that in Fisher markets, when all buyers have GS utility functions, the new PR rule converges point-wise to the competitive equilibrium. Moreover, we establish that the PR process guarantees an empirical convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/T)
towards the equilibrium price. In Arrow-Debreu markets, we focus on a lazy version of the PR update and show that it also converges point-wise to the competitive equilibrium.

One interesting direction for future research is to explore the cases where linear convergence rate can be established for PR dynamics. Previous work
Zhang 2011
;
Cheung et al. 2018
analyzed the PR process with CES utility functions, showing that the convergence rate is linear when the CES parameter
\rho
is bounded away from 1, with
\rho=1
corresponding to the linear utility case. The question remains whether this linear convergence result can be extended to broader classes of utility functions beyond CES utilities.

Convergence results for natural market dynamics have largely been established for subfamilies of GS utilities, with only a few exceptions
Cheung et al. 2018
;
Cheung et al. 2013
;
Cheung et al. 2012
.
Our findings contribute to this line of research, moving toward the ultimate goal of delineating the boundaries of markets in which PR and tâtonnement remain effective.
