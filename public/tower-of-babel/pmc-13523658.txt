Impact of an Interaction-Oriented, Information and Communication Technology-Based Community Care Service on Nutritional Status and Dietary Behavior of Community-Dwelling Older Adults: A Quasi-Experimental Study
Authors: Seojin Yun, Seulgi Lee, Eun Young Lee, Kirang Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of an information and communication technology-based, interaction-oriented community care service (Touch Care service) on the nutritional status and dietary behaviors of community-dwelling older adults. It integrated internet of things-based daily life monitoring, wearable devices, and AI voice interaction, providing personalized health feedback and notifications over an approximately 5-month intervention period. A total of 102 residents aged ≥65 years from Bugang-myeon, Sejong, were recruited, and 72 participants－33 and 39 in the case and control groups, respectively－who completed both pre- and post-assessments were finally analyzed. The case group received Touch Care; nutritional status was ascertained based on the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA), and dietary behaviors were evaluated via a simplified questionnaire on food group and daily meal frequencies, and water intake. At baseline, there were no significant between-group differences in any outcome measure. Following the intervention, the MNA score (P=0.020) and daily water intake (P<0.001) increased significantly from baseline in the intervention group, whereas no significant within-group changes were observed in food group intake frequency or meal frequency. In the control group, no significant changes were observed in any outcome measure. When the magnitude of change was compared between groups, the differences were not statistically significant for either MNA score (P=0.448) or daily water intake (P=0.849). These findings suggest that Touch Care service was feasible to deliver and acceptable to community-dwelling older adults, suggesting that conversational digital interventions may be worth evaluating as a complement to existing community care models.Keywords: aged, community health services, dietary behavior, nutritional status

INTRODUCTION

South Korea became a super-aged society in 2025, with a percentage of the population ≥65 years reaching 20.3% (Statistics Korea, 2025). Additionally, a rapid increase in the population aged ≥75 years, as well as in single-person households among older adults, is projected. Consequently, the current proportion of older adults living alone is expected to more than double by 2052 (Statistics Korea, 2024).

Older adults are highly susceptible to malnutrition due to a complex interplay of physiological changes－such as frailty, chronic diseases, and decline in masticatory function and digestive absorption capacity－as well as psychosocial factors, including living alone and social isolation (Lim and Lee, 2004). In particular, 86% of older adults aged ≥65 years in Korea suffer from chronic diseases (MOHW, 2021), underscoring the increasing importance of tailored nutritional management strategies based on the characteristics of specific diseases (Choi et al., 2023). However, community-based nutritional services have failed to meet actual demands due to a shortage of professional personnel, poor accessibility, and a lack of systematic management infrastructure (Cho et al., 2021). To address these challenges, significant attention is being paid to community care services based on information and communication technology (ICT) (Lee et al., 2021; Kim, 2024). Health management services based on these technologies can enhance the self-management capabilities of users by providing functions such as monitoring, feedback, and counseling, even in non-face-to-face situations, and can promote the physical and emotional health of older adults (Kim et al., 2023a, 2023b; Tian et al., 2024).

Recently, non-face-to-face care services using artificial intelligence (AI) care robots and AI/internet of things (IoT)-based healthcare pilot projects for older adults led by the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW) have provided for emotional bonding and lifestyle data-based monitoring through voice-based interfaces (MOHW, 2023; Woo et al., 2024). These initiatives have positively affected older adults, including alleviation of depression, improved compliance with daily management, and enhanced well-being (MOHW, 2023; Woo et al., 2024). These domestic efforts demonstrate that a structured approach－encompassing emotional bonding, lifestyle data-based management, and interactive feedback－enables more complex and communication-oriented interventions than traditional, simple notification-type ICT services. However, most have been designed primarily for emotional care and safety management, leaving research on the development and efficacy of systematic nutrition-based intervention services－such as dietary habits and fluid intake－extremely limited (No and Kang, 2022; Oh et al., 2022).

Considering the outcomes of these projects, studies identifying whether conversational or interactive services can contribute to improving the nutritional status of older adults are lacking. Such a gap is particularly concerning given that malnutrition and inadequate dietary intake are highly prevalent among community-dwelling elders and are closely associated with the elevated risks of frailty, functional decline, and cardiometabolic disorders. Nutritional status is therefore not a peripheral concern but a central component of healthy aging that warrants dedicated attention within community care frameworks. Addressing this gap requires moving beyond emotion- and safety-focused ICT models toward more comprehensive approaches. Therefore, ICT-based community care services need to expand single-function models into integrated support ones that encompass nutrition, dietary habits, and overall health.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the influence of a communication-based community care (Touch Care) service－an IoT-based platform recently developed by the Electronics and Telecommunications Research Institute (ETRI) and DNX Co., Ltd.－on the nutritional status and dietary behaviors of older community-living adults. Specifically, this study intends to verify the impact of interactive interventions－grounded in regular communication and emotional exchange－on long-term nutritional management systems for elders and to provide strategic directions for nutrition management models within futuristic community-based integrated care.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

This study was conducted with adults aged 65 and above residing in Bugang-myeon, Sejong-si, South Korea. In total, 102 participants were recruited who completed the baseline assessment, of whom 44 consented to the installation of Touch Care and were assigned to the case group; the remaining 58 who declined formed the control group. At post-intervention assessment, 33/44 case group (75.0%) and 39/58 control group (67.2%) participants completed the study, resulting in an overall completion rate of 70.6% (72/102). The attrition rates were 25.0% (11/44) and 32.8% (19/58), respectively. In both groups, the most frequently reported reason for non-completion was scheduling conflicts on the day of the post-intervention survey, including hospital visits and extended stays at family members’ homes. These reasons were largely considered unrelated to the intervention itself or to participants’ health status during the study period. The study was performed in compliance with the requirements of Good Clinical Practice/International Council for Harmonisation Guidelines for clinical trials. The study gained full board review from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) on Aug 11, 2022, by the Catholic Kkottongnae University IRB (No. 2022-002-001-02), Cheongju, South Korea. The procedures were explained in detail to all participants before the study began, and signed informed consent was obtained from them.

Intervention components of the interaction-based service

This study used “AI Soon-i,” a Touch Care service jointly developed by ETRI and DNX Co., Ltd., which was provided to older adults residing in Bugang-myeon. The overall study period spanned approximately 9 months, from the baseline survey in October 2022 to the follow-up survey in June 2023, during which the active intervention was delivered for approximately 5 months, from January to May 2023. AI Soon-i was designed to collect lifestyle data through object-contact sensors and digital wristwatch-based behavior recognition, provide tailored care for older adults through AI voice dialogues and interactions based on the data, and improve nutritional status while promoting healthy living through behavioral changes. The case group received the Touch Care service, which integrated IoT-based daily life monitoring, wearable devices, and AI voice interaction, providing personalized health feedback and notifications for approximately 5 months. The overall configuration is shown in Fig. 1.

Process flow-chart of the Touch Care service and data-driven health management process.

Before commencement, all participants in both the intervention and control groups completed a simplified food group frequency questionnaire and a fluid intake assessment. Based on the data collected, individualized nutritional reports were provided to each participant, followed by one-on-one counseling sessions. During these sessions, participants were informed of their dietary inadequacy relative to the recommended number of servings for each food group and received targeted nutritional education on recommended foods for groups in which their intake was identified as insufficient. This baseline nutritional assessment, individualized report, and counseling session constituted the standard intervention, which was provided identically to both groups. In addition to this standard intervention, the intervention group received the Touch Care service for approximately 5 months, whereas the control group received the standard intervention only.

For service intervention, an IoT environment was established in the participants’ homes, where the Touch Care device and software operated in tandem. To collect lifestyle pattern data－such as meal status, medication adherence, and TV viewing－six or more tags were attached to objects and locations within frequently used living spaces and items, including refrigerators, TV remote controls, and doors. Subsequently, participants were provided with a wearable device, a Touch Watch, to collect data on walking step counts, distance traveled, travel time, and physical activity levels. After applications were installed and registered on smartphones for the participants and their guardians, real-time activity information and behavior-promoting notifications－including hydration and medication reminders, meal status verification, and exercise prompts－were delivered at appropriate times based on the data collected from the Touch Watch. Additionally, participants were encouraged to engage in various group activities through a communication platform (Fig. 2). The Touch Care app promoted interactions among users via live audio or video broadcasts, covering activities such as shopping, eating together, solving proverbs, IT classes, and sharing relevant information. This allowed for communication among participants beyond one-on-one dialogues with AI Soon-i, thereby fostering a greater sense of social interconnectedness. During the intervention period, the service included at least nine periodic home visits for device and application inspections and education. Considering the limited digital literacy of older adults, instructions on device usage, app updates, and charging habits were repeated.

Program evaluation

To evaluate the effects of “AI Soon-i” on the nutritional condition of older adults, structured nutrition-assessment items, including the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA), simplified food group frequency questionnaire, fluid intake, and daily meal frequency, were utilized. Considering that the participants were aged 65 and older, both the pre- and post-surveys used identical items through one-on-one interviews. Intervention effects were evaluated by examining within-group changes from pre- to post-assessment and between-group differences at each time point.

MNA: The MNA is a tool developed to identify the risk of malnutrition in older adults at an early stage, comprehensively reflecting not only anthropometric measurements but also dietary habits, health, and functional status (Guigoz et al., 1996; Vellas et al., 1997). It consists of 18 items across four domains: general assessment, dietary intake, self-assessment, and anthropometric measurements. Based on the total MNA score (maximum 30 points), nutritional status was classified as normal (24－30 points), at risk of malnutrition (17－23.5 points), or malnourished (<17 points). This study applied the tool in the same manner before and after the use of Touch Care.

Simplified food group frequency questionnaire: The simplified food group frequency questionnaire is designed to rapidly assess dietary patterns and food type intake (Vuckovic et al., 2000; Cade et al., 2002; Huang et al., 2011). In this study, to reduce the response burden of older adults, food group intake frequency rather than individual nutrient intake was assessed. Through repeated surveys before and after the intervention, changes in intake frequency and adherence to dietary recommendations were evaluated. The questionnaire was constructed for this study based on a food group frequency questionnaire of the Seoul Food Survey and consisted of six items covering five food groups: whole grains; meat, fish, eggs, and legumes; vegetables (total and excluding kimchi); fresh fruits; and dairy products. For each item, participants reported their usual consumption frequency on a 9-point scale: (1) less than once a month or never, (2) once a month, (3) 2－3 times a month, (4) 1－2 times a week, (5) 3－4 times a week, (6) 5－6 times a week, (7) once a day, (8) twice a day, and (9) three or more times a day. Each response was converted to a daily intake frequency by dividing the midpoint of the category by the corresponding number of days, yielding 0, 0.03, 0.08, 0.21, 0.50, 0.79, 1.0, 2.0, and 3.0 times/d, respectively.

Water intake: The total daily water intake was assessed based on a standard measure of 1 cup－200 mL－of water, including tea. This study identified usual fluid intake habits and evaluated the effects of the Touch Care intervention on improving daily fluid intake.

Meal intake frequency: The daily average meal intake frequency of older adults was established based on their dietary habits over the past week. By identifying meal type－home-cooked or dining out－and with or without companions, the study comprehensively evaluated dietary regularity and social participation. Identical items were repeatedly surveyed before and after the intervention to observe for any changes.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were calculated to establish the general characteristics of older adults. Continuous variables－such as MNA scores, water intake, and intake frequency by food group were presented as the mean±standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Baseline comparability of continuous variables between the case and control groups was examined using the independent t-test. Within-group changes from pre- to post-intervention were assessed using the paired t-test, and the change scores (post－pre) were compared between the two groups using the independent t-test. The data collected were analyzed using SPSS 28.0 for Windows (IBM Corp.). Statistical significance levels were determined based on P<0.05.

DISCUSSION

Given the accelerating demographic shift toward a super-aged society and the recognized limitations of conventional community care models, this study evaluated the effectiveness of an interactive Touch Care service on the nutritional status and dietary behavior of community-dwelling elders. Specifically, it sought to verify how interactive interventions－grounded in regular communication and emotional exchange－influence long-term nutritional management systems and inform directions for future strategic integrated care models. After the AI Soon-i intervention, the intervention group showed statistically significant within-group increases in both MNA score and daily water intake, whereas no corresponding changes were observed in the control group. However, the between-group differences in change scores for MNA and water intake did not reach statistical significance, which limits the strength of causal inference regarding intervention efficacy.

The increase in MNA scores for the case group may be interpreted as a potential reflection of the complementary effects of the service’s multidimensional intervention. The MNA tool used covers not only simple intake but also self-assessment, mobility, and emotional status; it appears that “confirming changes in meals” and “nutritional status self-assessment” notifications led to cognitive improvements among participants. This finding is consistent with studies showing that even if direct alterations in food intake are not substantial, an integration of scores from non-intake domains, such as emotional stability and self-management routines, improves the overall nutritional status (Guigoz et al., 1996; Vellas et al., 1999). Specifically, reports have demonstrated that ICT-based remote interventions help restructure the daily lives of older adults and can improve the total score through non-intake domains (Johnson et al., 2021; Dong et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023a).

The prominent increase in water intake within the case group (P<0.001) represents the most specific behavioral outcome of this intervention. Low-intake-associated dehydration, which occurs due to the blunting of thirst perception by aging, is directly linked to sarcopenia and cognitive decline in older adults (Oh et al., 2006; Popkin et al., 2010; Yoo et al., 2018; Li et al., 2023). This service directly changed the water intake routine of the participants through voice-based interactions and “reminder-prompt intervention.” This effect is noteworthy in that it provides empirical evidence, through a community-based model, of studies showing that even simple alarm devices or apps linked to water intake can significantly enhance fluid consumption in older adults (Simmons et al., 2001; Luo et al., 2022; Reeves et al., 2023).

In contrast, the fact that no marked changes were observed in food group intake or meal intake frequencies suggests several structural limitations. First, as this intervention consisted of reminders and encouragement rather than specific dietary education, its intensity may have been too low to impact deep-rooted eating habits (Choi et al., 2016; Jang et al., 2021; Morishita-Suzuki and Watanabe, 2024). Second, due to the unique nature of rural areas, structural barriers, such as reduced geographical and economic accessibility to fresh food, existed (Kim and Kim, 2019; Park et al., 2020; Jo et al., 2022). Third, the range of changes was limited due to the ceiling effect, as participants already maintained a relatively regular eating habit of 2.7 meals per day at baseline (Khil, 2021; Kwon et al., 2023).

In this study, the absence of significant between-group differences in change scores could be explained by several points. Both groups underwent an identical baseline nutritional assessment followed by an individualized nutritional report and a one-on-one counseling session. The control group therefore served as an active rather than an untreated comparator, and any incremental benefit of the Touch Care service would have been superimposed on the effect of this shared standard intervention. Another explanation could be that the sample size was determined by the number of eligible residents in the study area rather than by an a priori power calculation, and the study may therefore have been underpowered to detect a modest incremental effect.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively short intervention period was insufficient to confirm long-term dietary behavior changes (Nigg and Long, 2012; Grimmett et al., 2019; Buyl et al., 2020). Second, the use of a convenience sample recruited from a geographically and demographically homogeneous rural community constrains the generalizability of the findings, warranting their replication in more diverse community settings. Third, the inability to fully consider the impact of differences in digital literacy among older adults on the intervention effect remains a drawback (Bertolazzi et al., 2024; Hwang et al., 2024; Lee, 2024; Kim et al., 2025). Fourth, the attrition rate was somewhat higher in the control (32.8%) than in the case group (25.0%), which may reflect a lower level of study engagement among participants who declined the service at the outset. The most frequently reported reasons for dropout in both groups included scheduling conflicts on the day of the post-intervention survey, such as hospital visits and extended stays with family members, which were largely considered unrelated to the intervention itself or to participants’ health status during the study period. Nevertheless, the baseline characteristics between completers and non-completers were not formally compared; therefore, the potential influence of attrition on the findings cannot be excluded. Finally, the quasi-experimental design employed introduces a considerable risk of selection bias. Participants were allocated to the case or control group based on their willingness to receive the Touch Care service, rather than randomly. As a result, those who voluntarily accepted the intervention may have had elevated levels of digital literacy, greater health awareness, or stronger motivation for healthy behavioral changes than those who declined. These pre-existing variations, rather than the intervention itself, may have contributed in part to the improvements in nutritional status and healthy behaviors observed in the case group. Despite these limitations, the present findings may serve as preliminary data to inform the development of community-based digital care models and related policies for older adults.

In conclusion, within-group improvements in MNA score and daily water intake were observed in the intervention group, although these did not differ significantly from the changes observed in the control group. These findings provide preliminary evidence that a conversational ICT-based care service can be delivered feasibly to older adults in a non-face-to-face community setting. Rrandomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and longer intervention periods are needed to determine whether such services confer additional benefit beyond standard nutritional counseling.
