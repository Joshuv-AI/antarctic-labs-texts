C-ZUPT: Stationarity-Aided Aerial Hovering
Daniel Engelsman, and Itzik Klein

Abstract
Autonomous systems across diverse domains have underscored the need for drift-resilient state estimation. Although satellite-based positioning and cameras are widely used, they often suffer from limited availability in many environments. As a result, positioning must rely solely on inertial sensors, leading to rapid accuracy degradation over time due to sensor biases and noise. To counteract this, alternative update sources—referred to as information aiding—serve as anchors of certainty. Among these, the zero-velocity update (ZUPT) is particularly effective in providing accurate corrections during stationary intervals, though it is restricted to surface-bound platforms. This work introduces a controlled ZUPT (C-ZUPT) approach for aerial navigation and control, independent of surface contact. By defining an uncertainty threshold, C-ZUPT identifies quasi-static equilibria to deliver precise velocity updates to the estimation filter. Extensive validation confirms that these opportunistic, high-quality updates significantly reduce inertial drift and control effort. As a result, C-ZUPT mitigates filter divergence and enhances navigation stability, enabling more energy-efficient hovering and substantially extending sustained flight—key advantages for resource-constrained aerial systems.



I
Introduction

Despite substantial progress in navigation technologies, maintaining accurate state estimation over extended durations remains a core challenge—particularly in global navigation satellite systems (GNSS)-denied environments and on highly dynamic platforms. The Kalman filter (KF) is central to most INS/GNSS integrated systems, where it propagates state estimates based on high-frequency inertial measurements, with corrections applied whenever external updates are available. However, disruptions due to signal blockage or communication failures can sever these correction loops, allowing estimation errors to accumulate unchecked due to inertial sensor biases and noise
[
1
]
.
In such situations, any piece of information that enhances situational awareness acts as an island of stability—especially when derived without additional sensors. Aiding mechanisms that exploit motion constraints and environmental interactions, known as information aiding, act as synthetic measurements, providing timely corrections once activation criteria are met
[
2
,
3
]
.

Among these, Zero-Velocity Updates (ZUPT) have garnered significant attention due to their simplicity, making them one of the most widely adopted techniques in practical navigation systems. Examples include vehicle stops at red lights or in traffic jams
[
4
]
, in-field vehicle calibration
[
5
]
, pedestrian gait cycles
[
6
]
, emergency descent in quadrotor failsafe mode
[
7
]
, surface anchoring
[
8
]
, and underwater docking
[
9
]
.

Commercial inertial measurement units (IMUs) powered by micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) technology have become the primary enabler for a wide range of ZUPT-aided applications, extending their use to wearable health devices, pedestrian navigation, autonomous vehicles, indoor robotics, and underwater systems
[
10
,
11
]
.
Up until a decade ago, most ZUPT implementations relied on particle filtering
[
12
]
, analytical methods
[
13
]
, or statistical tests
[
14
]
, with a cost of some precision in favor of improved state estimation. Known for their explainability, simplicity, and ability to model dynamic motion, these methods often used fuzzy logic to handle uncertainty. However, recent machine learning advances have shifted the paradigm, offering greater precision through superior generalization across diverse motion scenarios
[
15
]
.

Recent literature highlights a broader range of mechanisms beyond ZUPT, including zero-angular rate update (ZARU), zero-down velocity (ZDV), zero-yaw rate (ZYR), constant altitude (CA), and constant position (CP), among others
[
16
]
.
Except in airborne docking contexts
[
17
]
, however, these methods have yet to be validated on non-stationary platforms and thus remain limited to terrestrial systems, where ground contact offers a stable reference. Nonetheless, recent advancements in control strategies have enabled aerial platforms to maintain nearly fixed positions while hovering, even in the presence of significant disturbances
[
18
,
19
]
.
This raises a key question:

Can actively controlled equilibrium be leveraged to enable ZUPT during aerial hovering ?

To answer this, we offer a sensor-free controlled ZUPT (C-ZUPT) state correction approach for aerial navigation without surface contact constraint. Through extensive validation and analysis, the framework centers on three core aspects:

(i)

High-Fidelity Motion Modeling
: An aerial dynamics model supports LQG control under sensor-limited conditions, enabling in-flight disturbance compensation.

(ii)

Drift-Resilient Navigation Framework
: A quadrotor-specific velocity correction method that suppresses drift during stationary phases; publicly available @
GitHub
.

(iii)

Battery-Aware Estimation
: An integrated circuit model enables real-time energy monitoring and enforces power constraints, aiding evaluation of the control strategy.

We show that these elements improve estimator–controller interaction, enhance disturbance rejection by 30%, and reduce long-term energy use—yielding nearly a 6% efficiency gain. Such enhanced performance can be critical in time-sensitive hovering missions, including urban warfare, policing, rescue, persistent surveillance, infrastructure inspection, and delivery.

Theoretical Background

Given the variety of parameters and reference frames in this work, the symbolic framework adopts the following convention: non-bold symbols (
a
) for scalars, bold lowercase (
\bm{a}
) for vectors, and bold uppercase (
\mathbf{A}

The system is modeled as a continuous-time dynamical system, defined by the following differential equation

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=\bm{f}(\bm{x}(t),\bm{u}(t))\ ,

(1)

where
\bm{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state vector,
\bm{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the control input vector, and
\bm{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a smooth function that defines the system’s dynamics. The solution to this system describes the trajectory of the state
\bm{x}(t)
, starting from an initial condition
\bm{x}(0)
and evolving under the influence of the input
\bm{u}(t)
. The system is said to be at an equilibrium point, denoted by subscript
e
, when the dynamics no longer change and the state remains constant, as defined by
[
20
]

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{x}}(t)=\bm{f}(\bm{x}_{e},\bm{u}_{e})=\bm{0}\ .

(2)

The behavior of the equilibrium is assessed using the concept of local linearity, which approximates the nonlinear dynamics
\bm{f}
by a linear model near the equilibrium point
(\bm{x}_{e},\bm{u}_{e})
. The system’s response to small perturbations is then studied to classify the equilibrium as stable if trajectories return to it, or unstable if they diverge.

II-A

Information-aiding

In navigation systems, dynamic equilibria can be leveraged to improve state estimation accuracy by providing additional constraints. This is achieved through pseudo-measurements (PMs), which are sensor-free observations derived from the platform’s assumed physics. By exploiting predictable system behaviors at equilibrium, these equilibria facilitate the indirect estimation of unobservable state variables through the system’s dynamics, thereby enhancing both the accuracy and robustness of the state estimation process
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

As shown in the rightmost column, each constraint is applicable only within a characteristic time scale, determined by how long the system remains sufficiently stationary

For instance, gait cycles occur frequently but are brief in duration, whereas vehicle stops or platform alignments tend to last longer but occur less frequently.

Being largely unschedulable, these traditional constraints are ineffective in inherently unstable aerial environments, where near-stationary equilibria can only be achieved through active control.

II-B

Stable Operating Point

Fixed-wing aircraft typically reach trim conditions during straight and level flight, coordinated turns, and steady climbs or descents. These equilibrium points are linearized to develop an appropriate control scheme, but none of these phases involve zero-velocity timeframes, either translational or angular. In contrast, rotorcraft—especially in hover mode—achieve aerial stationarity by continuously correcting deviations from a fixed position or orientation
[
23
]
.

Recent developments in military, law enforcement, and search and rescue operations have renewed attention on hover mode. While relatively simple, it provides a stable vertical perspective, making it especially effective for tasks requiring precise positioning over extended durations
[

Based on linear systems theory, flight conditions where forces and moments are balanced correspond to steady-state dynamics, allowing the nonlinear system (
1
) to be linearized around the equilibrium point
(\bm{x}_{e},\bm{u}_{e})
through a first-order Taylor expansion
[
28
]
. Such approximation results in

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{x}}(t)\approx\mathbf{A}(\bm{x}_{e},\bm{u}_{e})(\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}_{e})+\mathbf{B}(\bm{x}_{e},\bm{u}_{e})(\bm{u}(t)-\bm{u}_{e}),

(3)

where
\bm{x}(t)
denotes the state vector,
\bm{u}(t)
represents the control inputs, and the deviations from equilibrium are described by

\displaystyle\delta\bm{x}(t)=\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}_{e}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \delta\bm{u}(t)=\bm{u}(t)-\bm{u}_{e}\ .

(4)

Under the assumption of linear time-invariant (LTI) dynamics near the equilibrium points, the Jacobian matrices of the system with respect to the state and input are expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\bm{x}_{e},\bm{u}_{e})=\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{x}}\bigg|_{(\bm{x}_{e},\bm{u}_{e})}\,\text{and}\ \,\mathbf{B}(\bm{x}_{e},\bm{u}_{e})=\frac{\partial\bm{f}}{\partial\bm{u}}\bigg|_{(\bm{x}_{e},\bm{u}_{e})}.

(5)

Since
\bm{x}_{e}
and
\bm{u}_{e}
are constants, they vanish upon differentiation. Therefore, the following identities hold

\displaystyle\bm{x}(t)=\delta\bm{x}(t)+\bm{x}_{e}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \bm{u}(t)=\delta\bm{u}(t)+\bm{u}_{e}\ ,

(6)

simplifying the error dynamics to the standard LTI form

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\,\bm{x}(t)+\mathbf{B}\,\bm{u}(t)\ ,

(7)

\displaystyle\bm{y}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}\,\bm{x}(t)\ ,

(8)

where
\mathbf{C}
selects the measurable components.

To incorporate model discrepancies, we introduce zero-mean white Gaussian noise terms, drawn from their respective process (
\mathbf{W}
) and measurement (
\mathbf{V}
) noise covariance matrices

\displaystyle\bm{w}(t)

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{W})\ ,\quad\mathbb{E}[\bm{ww}^{\top}]=\mathbf{W}\succ 0\ ,

(9)

\displaystyle\bm{v}(t)

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{V})\ \ ,\quad\mathbb{E}[\bm{vv}^{\top}]=\mathbf{V}\succeq 0\ .

(10)

These drive the open-loop uncertainty, with covariance matrix
{\mathbf{P}}(t)
evolve via the differential Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{P}}(t)=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}(t)+\mathbf{P}(t)\mathbf{A}^{\top}+\mathbf{W}.

(11)

Consequently, at each fixed time
t
, the joint distribution of the states and outputs can be approximated as Gaussian

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}(t)\\
\bm{y}(t)\end{bmatrix}\hskip-1.42262pt\sim\mathcal{N}\hskip-1.42262pt\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mu}(t)\\
\mathbf{C}\bm{\mu}(t)\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{P}(t)&\mathbf{P}(t)\mathbf{C}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{P}(t)&\mathbf{C}\mathbf{P}(t)\mathbf{C}^{\top}+\mathbf{V}\end{bmatrix}\right).

(12)

where
\bm{\mu}(t)
denotes the nominal noise-free state trajectory, initialized as
\bm{\mu}(t_{0})=\mathbb{E}[\bm{x}(t_{0})]
, and evolving according to

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\mu}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\bm{\mu}(t)+\mathbf{B}\bm{u}(t)\ .

(13)

II-C

Linear–Quadratic–Gaussian Controller

While static feedback assumes full-state access, practical systems typically measure only
m<n
states. To reconstruct the full state vector from noisy measurements, a linear-quadratic estimator (LQE) is employed
[
29
,
30
]
.

For LTI systems, these estimates are then fed into a linear quadratic regulator (LQR), which computes control inputs minimizing a quadratic cost over a finite time horizon
T
, typically defined as

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{u}(t)}J\triangleq\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\bigg(\|\bm{x}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathbf{Q}}+\|\bm{u}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathbf{R}}\bigg)\mathrm{d}t\,\right],

(14)

subject to (
13
), with
\mathbf{Q}\succeq 0
and
\mathbf{R}\succ 0
encoding penalties on state error and input energy, respectively.

Provided that the pairs
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
and
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{W}^{1/2})
are controllable, and
(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{A})
and
(\mathbf{Q}^{1/2},\mathbf{A})
are observable, the optimal linear quadratic Gaussian (LQG) controller is obtained by combining the LQR with a Kalman filter, which serves as the LQE.
Denoting the estimated state by
\hat{\bm{x}}(t)
, the resulting LQG closed-loop dynamics follow

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\bm{x}}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\,\hat{\bm{x}}(t)+\mathbf{B}\,\bm{u}(t)+\mathbf{L}\left(\bm{y}(t)-\mathbf{C}\hat{\bm{x}}(t)\right)\ ,

(15)

\displaystyle\bm{u}(t)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{K}\,\left(\hat{\bm{x}}(t)-\bm{x}_{\text{ref}}\right)=-\mathbf{K}\,\hat{\bm{x}}(t)\ .

(16)

The optimal control gain matrix
\mathbf{K}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{S}\ ,

(17)

where
\mathbf{S}
is the unique positive semi-definite solution to the algebraic Riccati equation (ARE) given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{SA}-\mathbf{SBR}^{-1}\mathbf{B^{\top}S+Q}=\bm{0}\ .

(18)

Similarly, the KF optimal gain
\mathbf{L}
is then given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{L=PC^{\top}\left(CPC^{\top}+V\right)}^{-1}\ ,

(19)

where
\mathbf{P}
is the steady-state estimation error covariance matrix, obtained by solving the following ARE

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}^{\top}-\mathbf{P}\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{0}\ .

(20)

With
\bm{0}
and
\mathbf{I}
representing the zero and identity matrices of the appropriate dimensions, the augmented state space, combining the dynamics from both (
7
) and (
15

(21)

Airframe Dynamics

Building on the Newton-Euler formalism
[
35
]
, the coupled translational and rotational dynamics can be expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{f}^{\mathcal{B}}\\
\bm{\tau}^{\mathcal{B}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m\bm{I}_{3}&\bm{0}_{3}\\
\bm{0}_{3}&\bm{J}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\bm{v}}^{\mathcal{B}}\\

(28)

where
m
represents the rigid body mass, and
\bm{J}
,
\bm{I}_{3}
, and
\bm{0}_{3}
denote the
3\times 3
inertia tensor, identity matrix, and zero matrix, respectively. Both the forces
\bm{f}
and moments
\bm{\tau}
are expressed in the body frame, governing the time derivatives of both the linear velocity
\bm{v}^{\mathcal{B}}
and the angular velocity
\bm{\omega}^{\mathcal{B}}

During fixed-point hovering, near-zero body velocities render drag forces negligible due to their quadratic nature. As a result, the net force in the body frame consists solely of the gravitational force
\textbf{{g}}^{\mathcal{B}}=\textit{g}\,\textbf{e}^{\mathcal{B}}_{z}
, and the vertical rotor thrusts
\bm{f}_{\textit{z},i}^{\mathcal{B}}
, resulting in the balance

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{\mathcal{B}}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\bm{f}_{\textit{z},i}^{\mathcal{B}}-m\,\textbf{{g}}^{\mathcal{B}}\ .

(29)

Assuming no blade flapping or variable pitch, each rotor’s spin axis is directed along the body-frame
z
-axis, allowing the thrust generated by the
i
-th propeller to be modeled as

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{\mathcal{B}}_{z,i}={f}_{z,i}\,\textbf{e}^{\mathcal{B}}_{z}=\left(k_{T}\Omega_{i}^{2}\right)\textbf{e}^{\mathcal{B}}_{z}\,,

(30)

where

is a lumped coefficient addressed in Sec.

.

Attitude stabilization relies on differential thrust between two opposing rotors located at a distance
\ell
from the center of mass. The rolling moment is generated by adjusting the lateral thrust difference between motors #4 (left) and #2 (right), given by

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}_{\phi}^{\mathcal{B}}=\ell\left(\bm{f}^{\mathcal{B}}_{z,4}-\bm{f}^{\mathcal{B}}_{z,2}\right)\textbf{e}^{\mathcal{B}}_{x}\,,

(31)

while the pitch moment is controlled by the longitudinal thrust difference between motors #1 (front) and #3 (rear), as

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}_{\theta}^{\mathcal{B}}=\ell\left(\bm{f}^{\mathcal{B}}_{z,3}-\bm{f}^{\mathcal{B}}_{z,1}\right)\textbf{e}^{\mathcal{B}}_{y}\,.

(32)

Lastly, yawing occurs when the individual rotor drag torques

sum to a nonzero value, reflecting an asymmetry. With

denoting the torque coefficient and positive yaw defined as counter-clockwise about
\mathbf{e}_{z}^{\mathcal{B}}
, the resulting yaw torque is

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}_{\psi}^{\mathcal{B}}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\tau_{M,i}=(-1)^{i}\left(k_{M}\Omega_{i}^{2}\right)\textbf{e}^{\mathcal{B}}_{z}\,.

(33)

Accounting for these propulsive effects, the six-degree-of-freedom (6-DoF) motion model in (

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\bm{v}}^{\mathcal{B}}\\
\dot{\bm{\omega}}^{\mathcal{B}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m^{-1}\bm{f}^{\mathcal{B}}-{\bm{\omega}}^{\mathcal{B}}\times{\bm{v}}^{\mathcal{B}}-\textbf{{g}}^{\mathcal{B}}\\
\bm{J}^{-1}\left(\bm{\tau}^{\mathcal{B}}-\bm{\omega}^{\mathcal{B}}\times\bm{J}\bm{\omega}^{\mathcal{B}}\right)\end{pmatrix},

(34)

where the thrust and torques are generated by the rotor speeds according to the control allocation (mixer) matrix

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{f}_{z}\\

0&-\ell k_{T}&0&\ell k_{T}\\
-\ell k_{T}&0&\ell k_{T}&0\\
-k_{M}&k_{M}&-k_{M}&k_{M}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Omega_{1}^{2}\\
\Omega_{2}^{2}\\
\Omega_{3}^{2}\\
\Omega_{4}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

(35)

or, in a more compact form using Hadamard power

\displaystyle\bm{u}=\mathbf{M}\,\bm{\Omega}^{\circ 2}\,.

(36)

Kinematics

The platform’s trajectory is derived by time-integrating its dynamics and applying a coordinate transformation to an inertial reference frame, letting the position vector be
[
36
]

\displaystyle{\bm{\xi}}^{\mathcal{I}}=\left(x,y,z\right)^{\top}\,,

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\xi}}^{\mathcal{I}}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{B}}^{\mathcal{I}}{\bm{v}}^{\mathcal{B}}\,.

Proposed Approach

We begin by analyzing the near-stationarity dynamics at a local hovering equilibrium, followed by an observability analysis that motivates the proposed aiding mechanism.

IV-A

Motivation

Since hovering occurs near equilibrium with small state variables, local behavior can be approximated using small-angle assumptions. This simplifies (
40

26

\dot{\bm{\eta}}^{\mathcal{I}}\approx\bm{\omega}^{\mathcal{B}}
and

with the corresponding sub-Jacobian of

IV-C

Control Signal Flow

While the idealized dynamics assume direct application of the LQR control input, real-world systems introduce delays and nonlinearities that alter the actual output
\bm{u}_{\text{out}}
. To reflect these effects in the process model, we identify three key transitional stages that significantly influence the signal flow.

IV-C
1
Command Processing

The LQR control law onboard the flight controller (
16
) computes the net forces and moments to minimize tracking error
[
30
]
.
However, due to the structure of the mixer matrix (
36
), directly inverting it can yield negative squared rotor speeds, which are not physically meaningful. To ensure feasibility, the desired rotor speeds
\bm{\Omega}_{\text{des}}
are obtained via a non-negative least squares (NNLS) problem

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\arg\min_{\bm{x}\geq 0}\left\|\bm{u}_{\text{LQR}}-\mathbf{M}\,\bm{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(56)

followed by
\bm{\Omega}_{\text{des}}=\sqrt{\bm{x}}
. To ensure proper actuation, the
i
-th rotor speed is constrained within a lower limit
\underline{\Omega}
, set by phase resistance and starting torque, and an upper limit
\overline{\Omega}
, beyond which additional input no longer increases thrust, as given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{in},i}=\min\left(\,\overline{\Omega},\,\max\left(\Omega_{\text{des},i},\underline{\Omega}\right)\right)\,.

(57)

Next, the microcontroller unit (MCU) translates the rotational speed commands into electrical signals by generating pulse-width modulation (PWM) signals to drive the motors. The low-level motor control pathway is modeled compactly as

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{\text{in}}=\mathcal{G}_{1}(\bm{u}_{\text{LQR}})\,.

(58)

IV-C
2
Power System

Battery-powered aerial platforms, particularly those using Lithium-Polymer (LiPo) cells, are subject to high-current transients due to aggressive propulsion demands. These bursts give rise to nonlinear phenomena such as voltage sag, capacitive loading, and relaxation effects. To capture these time-varying behavior, the Thevenin-based equivalent circuit model is widely adopted
[
25
,
38
,
39
]
.

Given that
\Omega_{\text{in},i}
is bounded by (
57
), and assuming a non-ideal rotor efficiency
\eta_{\text{rot}}<1
, the electrical power drawn by the
i
-th motor scales cubically with speed as

\displaystyle P_{\text{in},i}=\frac{\tau_{M,i}\,\Omega_{\text{in},i}}{\eta_{\text{rot}}}=\frac{k_{M}\,\Omega_{\text{in},i}^{3}}{\eta_{\text{rot}}}\,.

(59)

Accordingly, the total current drawn from the battery to supply the combined motor power
P_{\text{out}}(t)
is then

\displaystyle I(t)={\sum_{i=1}^{4}P_{\text{in},i}(t)}\,/\,{V(t)}\,,

(60)

In this framework, the terminal voltage
V(t)
is expressed as

\displaystyle V(t)=V_{\text{oc}}(t)-R_{0}I(t)-V_{1}(t)\,,

(61)

where
R_{0}
is the internal ohmic resistance, and
V_{\text{oc}}(t)
is the open-circuit voltage (OCV), given by the quadratic fit

\displaystyle V_{\text{oc}}(t)=\nu_{0}+\nu_{1}\,\text{SoC}(t)+\nu_{2}\,\text{SoC}(t)^{2}\,,

(62)

with
\nu_{i}
’s being battery-specific constants listed in Appendix
B
.
The normalized state-of-charge (SoC
\in[0,1]
), representing the remaining usable energy, evolves according to

\displaystyle\dot{\text{SoC}}(t)=-\frac{I(t)}{C_{\text{bat}}}\,,

(63)

where
C_{\text{bat}}
is the nominal capacity. The voltage drop due to first-order electrochemical polarization dynamics follows

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{1}(t)=-\frac{1}{\underbrace{R_{1}C_{1}}_{\tau_{RC}}}V_{1}(t)+\frac{1}{C_{1}}I(t)\,,

(64)

where
R_{1}
and
C_{1}
characterizes the transient response time.

Since the five variables involved in (
60
)–(
64
) are implicitly interdependent, the system constitutes a differential-algebraic equation (DAE), which is solved numerically at each time step to propagate the battery’s internal dynamics as

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{\text{cmd}}=\mathcal{G}_{2}(\bm{\Omega}_{\text{in}})\,.

(65)

IV-C
3
Actuator Dynamics

The final stage models the airframe’s dynamic response and its influence on the generated thrust and torques. While the control signal
\bm{\Omega}_{\text{cmd}}
represents the idealized instantaneous command, rotor inertia and aerodynamic effects introduce a time lag in the actual output speeds
\bm{\Omega}_{\text{out}}
.

To capture this behavior, the rotor dynamics are modeled as a linear first-order system
[
28
]

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\Omega}}_{\text{out}}=\frac{1}{\tau_{\text{rot}}}({\bm{\Omega}}_{\text{cmd}}-{\bm{\Omega}}_{\text{out}})\,,

(66)

where
\tau_{\text{rot}}
denotes the dominant time constant.
The output rotor speeds, which ultimately determine the generated forces and torques, are then governed by the dynamic mapping

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{\text{out}}=\mathcal{G}_{3}(\bm{\Omega}_{\text{cmd}})\,.

(67)

To conclude, this chapter analyzes the linear hover characteristics, examines the real-time contribution of C-ZUPT—along with its heuristic basis and application—and ends with a model of the effective actuation output, encapsulated as

\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}_{\text{out}}=\mathcal{G}_{3}\big(\mathcal{G}_{2}\big(\mathcal{G}_{1}(\bm{u}_{\text{LQR}})\big)\big)\,.

(68)

VI
Discussion and Conclusion

This work presents a sensor-free C-ZUPT state correction method for aerial navigation and control, independent of surface contact constraints. Based on a Newton–Euler framework, the C-ZUPT mechanism—triggered under near-stationary conditions—enables accurate state estimation, improved navigation, and control. Validation under varying disturbances shows consistent gains in hover stability, with notable reductions in estimation drift, control effort, and power consumption, alongside improved disturbance rejection.
The results show that the proposed C-ZUPT mechanism reduces control effort, extends flight time, and delays battery depletion to its safety threshold—thereby improving efficiency by lowering the power-to-weight ratio required to counteract gravity. These findings also underscore the method’s robust disturbance rejection, making it particularly well-suited for resource-constrained aerial platforms operating sensor-free at flexible update rates.
Nonetheless, several limitations warrant consideration:

i)
Point of no return
: C-ZUPT works best under mild dynamics as velocity errors remain bounded; however, once velocity estimates exceed a critical threshold, stable recovery without position fixes becomes unlikely, and triggering may fail.

ii)
Trade-off heuristics
: Tuning the window size and threshold values involves balancing sensitivity and robustness—values that are too small can lead to noisy false detections, while overly large values risk missing true events.

iii)
Context sensitivity
: Effective LQG control relies on carefully tuned gains and weights specific to each scenario. Thus, observed improvements apply to the tested platform, trajectories, and noise profiles, and may vary otherwise.

Despite these limitations, the proposed approach offers a practical means of leveraging aerial stationarity intervals—a key asset in modern aviation. This enhanced performance can be decisive in time-critical hovering operations such as urban warfare, policing, rescue missions, surveillance, infrastructure inspection, environmental monitoring, and delivery services.
