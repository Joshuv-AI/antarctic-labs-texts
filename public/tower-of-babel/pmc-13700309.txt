Expert guidance to inform coordinated suicide postvention responses: A Delphi study
Authors: Lennart Reifels, Trisnasari Fraser, Alison Asche, Mark Bekerman, Melody Sutton, Louise Flynn, Sonja Bottern, Robyn Humphries, Merryl Whyte, Karl Andriessen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of suicide is profound for affected individuals, families and communities. Suicide postvention responses, inclusive of bereavement support, have been recognised as having made a valuable contribution to suicide prevention efforts. Postvention Protocol Response Groups (PPRGs) have emerged as a central mechanism to facilitate a coordinated service system response following a suspected suicide, mobilising a range of local services to provide early intervention support to bereaved communities across a defined geographical area. There is an important role to be played by these key agencies, support services, and community groups in responding to these impacts. However, little is known about the effective implementation of PPRGs.MethodsWe used the Delphi method to obtain expert consensus regarding best practice implementation of PPRGs. The two-round online Delphi survey involved a panel of 30 Australian experts (comprising past or present PPRG members, postvention experts, and response beneficiaries) who rated statements in terms of their importance for PPRG implementation.ResultsThe 126 statements endorsed by 80% or more of panel members as “essential” or “important” regarding PPRG implementation covered key aspects of stakeholder engagement, governance, practice principles, operational aspects, data surveillance, preventative capacity, sustainability, and special considerations in rural areas. Expert recommendations will serve as the foundation for the development of a PPRG implementation guide.ConclusionsThis study provides valuable expert guidance and practice-based evidence to inform the establishment of PPRGs in areas of need, enhance the effectiveness and sustainability of existing PPRGs, and strengthen the implementation of localised postvention responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28686-2.Keywords: Suicide, Postvention, Implementation, Delphi method, Expert consensus

Background

Suicide is a major public health problem with wide ranging human and societal impacts. Globally, more than 720,000 lives are lost to suicide every year, at a rate of approximately 9 suicides per 100,000 population [1], whereas Australia counted 3,264 suicides in 2023, representing a rate of almost 12 suicides per 100,000 population [2]. Each death by suicide has wide reaching impacts for affected individuals, families and social networks, workplaces and communities.

Each suicide death has been estimated to affect between 6 family members and 135 community members [3, 4], with potential to cause significant distress among those affected. This varies in intensity and duration related to their social, psychological, and geographical proximity to the deceased. People impacted by a suicide may themselves have an elevated risk of developing mental health concerns, as well as future suicidal distress [5]. People bereaved by suicide have approximately a three-fold risk of dying by suicide compared to those non-bereaved [6].

Mainstream and social media attention following a suicide can raise the risk of further contagion of suicidal behaviour [7] through mechanisms such as social influence, affiliation, imitation, or exposure to suicide related information. This, in turn, can drive the emergence of suicide clusters, involving a greater than expected number of suicides in a specific location or linked suicides that are spread across a geographic area over a short period [8]. Suicide clusters have been estimated to account for 5.6% of youth and 2.3% of adult suicides in Australia [9] and their impact can be overwhelming for local communities, stakeholders and services [10]. Taken together, these wide-ranging suicide impacts highlight the importance of a well-coordinated postvention response to identify and provide support to those bereaved, reduce community distress, avoid duplication of response efforts, and prevent further suicide [8, 11].

Suicide postvention has been recognised as an important component of national suicide prevention strategies [12]. However, little is known about the effective implementation of coordinated community responses [13], underscoring the need for evidence-based guidance to address varied support needs across settings. While postvention guidelines have been developed for specific settings such as schools and universities [14], there is little guidance to coordinate effective multi-sectoral and multi-agency postvention responses across an entire geographical area.

Postvention as an intervention has historically been about the provision of therapeutic and psychosocial bereavement support to individuals and families impacted by a suicide. Bereavement support is acknowledged as being preventative, with the understanding that individuals bereaved by suicide are themselves at disproportionate risk [11]. The original approach to suicide postvention was based on a crisis intervention model and mainly delivered post-hoc by individuals/organisations in response to concerns about the risk of contagion and clusters. More recently, however, Postvention Protocol Response Groups (or PPRGs as they are known in Victoria), have increasingly been developed across Australia. PPRGs facilitate a coordinated service system response following a suspected suicide, mobilising local services to provide early intervention support to bereaved communities, within an agreed framework that authorises and guides collective actions. PPRGs shifted away from the crisis intervention model to a public health approach, which integrates seamlessly with contemporary suicide prevention strategies. The earliest iteration of a PPRG was established in 2012 in response to a Victorian youth suicide cluster [15]. A subsequent coronial inquiry highlighted the critical role that local organisations play in both the identification of and response to suicide, commending the coordinated ensuing response, and recommending the development of a suicide prevention and postvention response framework [16]. These early service system responses have formed the foundation of PPRGs which have matured to be key enablers of a broader and more comprehensive postvention response to people impacted by suicide. While the height of PPRG activity was originally located in Victoria during the first decade, increasing national policy and funding momentum over recent years resulted in the establishment of PPRGs in other Australian states and territories.

With several PPRGs now at varying stages of operation and a growing interest in PPRGs nationally, it is essential that key insights from their establishment and implementation be harnessed, and a solid evidence base be developed to enable and guide their future implementation. To this end, we conducted a Delphi expert consensus study with the aim of developing the evidence base for best practice establishment and implementation of PPRGs and to inform the development of an implementation guide.

Methods

We used the Delphi method as it applies to mental health research [17], involving survey construction, expert panel selection, and survey administration over two rounds. Ethics approval for the study was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee from The University of Melbourne (#2024-31419-60748-2). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Survey construction

The Delphi survey developed for this study included survey statements that were informed through a review of the scientific and grey literature on the topic. Given the relative recency of postvention protocol response groups, literature was sourced by LR, MB, and AA, using a purposive approach, drawing on the authors’ professional networks and experience in establishing PPRGs. Literature included six peer-reviewed studies [18–23], four existing Australian postvention protocols [24–27], three protocol pilot evaluations [15, 28, 29] and a postvention discussion paper [30]. Summaries of feedback from a workshop with suicide postvention protocol and bereavement experts held at the 2024 National Suicide Prevention Conference in Adelaide, Australia, were also used to inform survey items [31].

The first author (LR) established a high-level structure for the Delphi survey based on a review of the literature, which included sections relating to key aspects of protocol establishment, stakeholder engagement, governance, practice principles, operational aspects, data surveillance, preventative capacity, sustainability, and special considerations in rural areas. The second author (TF) reviewed the literature and workshop summaries and extracted statements that aligned with those sections and could inform best practice implementation of PPRGs. For example, the statement “Key elements needed in a postvention response activation include: Provision of emotional first aid for affected first responders” was extracted from peer-reviewed literature [20]; the statement “Factors used to identify if postvention response activation is appropriate include: The deceased was aged 10–25 years” was extracted from an existing postvention protocol [27]; and the statement “Sustainability of regional postvention protocol response groups is enhanced by: Longer term funding models” was extracted from a postvention protocol pilot evaluation [28].

Two researchers (LR & KA) reviewed all statements to ensure applicability to the topic and that each captured a distinct idea. A revised version of the survey was reviewed by AA, MB, MS, SB, LF, RH and MW, all of whom have substantive experience in establishing PPRGs. Following final revision, 129 statements were organised into four superordinate sections: (1) Establishing PPRGs (with subsections Understanding needs and context, Stakeholder engagement, Governance); (2) PPRGs in Action (with subsections Practice principles, Operational aspects, External communication, Data surveillance); (3) Preventative capacity and sustainability; and (4) Contextual considerations in rural areas. The final Delphi survey is provided in supplementary material.

Expert panel selection

An expert panel was formed comprising (1) PPRG members (past or present), (2) postvention experts with substantive experience in postvention protocol or bereavement responses, and (3) people with experience of suicide postvention services coordinated by PPRGs, either as service providers (e.g., first responders, health practitioners), community leaders, or people impacted by a loss to suicide more than six months ago (e.g., family, friends, colleagues, community members). The six months exclusion criterion, which has been used successfully before, was applied to avoid recruiting participants who may still be in crisis due to the bereavement [32]. Due to the particular nature of the expertise required for the study (i.e., PPRG involvement), we used purposive recruitment, with AA, MB, MS, SB, LF, RH and MW inviting members in their extensive professional networks to participate in the study via email, followed by up to two email reminders.

Survey administration

Online data collection occurred in March and April 2025 (Round 1 survey) and May and June 2025 (Round 2 survey) via Qualtrics (Provo, UT). Panellists were offered a $AUD25 gift voucher for their participation in each survey round. The Round 1 survey comprised (1) demographic questions including gender, location, nature of expertise, and whether the panellist identified as a person with a lived experience of suicide or suicide bereavement; (2) 129 statements to be rated in importance as key recommendations for the implementation of PPRGs; and (3) opportunity to provide feedback about existing statements or offer suggestions for additional statements. Panellists were required to rate each statement on a 5-point Likert scale as “essential”, “important”, “don’t know/ depends”, “unimportant” or “should not be included”. All panellists who completed Round 1 were invited to participate in Round 2. Preceding the Round 2 survey invitation, panellists were provided with anonymous feedback outlining how their Round 1 responses compared with the rest of the panel.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using Microsoft Excel, including descriptive statistics for demographic characteristics and calculation of endorsement for statements. Based on consensus thresholds used successfully in prior Delphi studies [33] and aligned with Delphi guidance [34], statements rated as “important” or “essential” by 80% or more of panellists were indicative of endorsement by the panel for inclusion in key recommendations for PPRG implementation. Statements rated by less than 70% of panellists as “important” or “essential” were excluded. Statements rated by 70-79.9% of panellists as “important” or “essential” were included in Round 2 for re-rating (or excluded after Round 2).

Overall statement endorsement ratings were high across all survey sections, ranging between 40% and 100% in Round 1 and 76%-100% in Round 2. Thirteen Round 1 statements and one Round 2 statement received the maximum 100% endorsement rating.

Discussion

The occurrence of a death by suicide can have wide-ranging ramifications, impacting people of varying social, psychological, and geographical proximity to the deceased across multiple settings [35]. Timely and well-coordinated postvention responses are therefore essential to provide bereavement support to affected individuals and communities, and ameliorate the risk of further adverse health outcomes, including suicide contagion and future suicidal behaviour [36, 37].

In view of the limited postvention implementation research [13] and designated evaluations of postvention protocol responses, this Delphi expert consensus study provides valuable guidance to inform the establishment and implementation of PPRGs, as a key coordinating mechanism for postvention responses across defined geographical areas. The resulting PPRG guidance makes an important contribution to the wider postvention literature as it extends the scope of setting-specific postvention guidelines [14] to the coordination of multi-sectoral and multi-agency responses across entire regions. PPRG recommendations do not necessarily prescribe what type of support should be delivered, as guidelines would (e.g., information, assistance, counselling, or psychotherapy [38]), but rather outline how postvention system responses should be effectively integrated and organised in a timely, proportionate and well-coordinated fashion. As such, PPRG recommendations can also serve to operationalise postvention guidelines across the spectrum of locally available support services.

PPRG guidance is applicable in instances of both single and multiple suicides. It concords well with core components of suicide cluster response frameworks, which emphasise the importance of (1) Preparation; (2) Routine monitoring and detection; (3) Coordination with the media and monitoring social media; (4) Identifying and supporting individuals at risk; (5) Promoting help-seeking and building community resilience; and (6) Long-term follow-up and evaluation [39]. Inherent challenges in evaluating complex responses to relatively rare suicide clusters, and limited resulting evaluations [39], further underpin the merit of practice-based evidence and expert guidance in this field.

Key stakeholders can utilise recommendations to establish PPRGs in areas of need, reinvigorate and enhance the effectiveness and sustainability of existing PPRGs, and strengthen the implementation of postvention responses. Most guidance sections will be valuable when first establishing PPRGs and setting up broad parameters around their purpose and functioning (i.e., understanding needs and context, stakeholder engagement, governance, practice principles, response activation and deactivation, external communication, data surveillance), but will also be worthy of review at regular intervals and following response activations. Other guidance sections highlight the potential to bolster the preventative capacity and sustainability of existing PPRGs and special considerations for rural areas. Notably, beyond their role in enabling effective postvention system responses, PPRGs can also have significant preventative potential by actively monitoring community vulnerabilities, identifying those most at risk and affected, facilitating support service access, preventing further adverse health outcomes, and leveraging and building system capacity [40].

While the population groups affected by suicide and the composition of locally available services will inevitably vary across areas, many of the broader issues addressed by the recommendations will resonate widely across settings and need to be considered and addressed in devising well-coordinated and appropriate postvention responses to meet the needs of impacted communities.

Strengths and limitations

Delphi recommendations were informed by an Australian expert panel, which may limit their generalisability to other countries and contexts. While the recommendations are intended to provide overarching guidance that is applicable across a variety of settings, these may not necessarily capture every detail relevant to unique local circumstances. As such, the guidance may benefit from further adaptation or special considerations when establishing PPRGs for specific population groups or settings. The expert panel size in each round exceeded the recommended threshold of 20 panel members which makes for robust findings [17]. The panel composition primarily reflected PPRG members and postvention experts with expertise in establishing PPRGs and coordinating postvention responses, and to a lesser extent beneficiaries of these responses, whose perspectives could be captured more explicitly in future studies. Nonetheless, most panel members also brought their own lived experience insights to the task. Expert panel membership required substantive experience in postvention protocol or bereavement responses, with limited information on credentials. While Delphi recommendations reflect expert consensus and are informed by practice, not all presently have evidence for their effectiveness. The dearth of evaluation evidence on comprehensive postvention responses in terms of individual, community, and service level outcomes thus highlights the critical need for adopting evidence-informed postvention components [14, 36] aligned with current best-practice postvention guidance [38] when establishing PPRGs as well as the ongoing importance of evaluating PPRG responses in terms of implementation and effectiveness outcomes [41]. Further research regarding the implementation of PPRGs and effectiveness of postvention responses remains important, which will be aided by a designated evaluation framework to capture PPRG outcomes at multiple levels. As a next step, the Delphi expert recommendations will serve as the foundation for the development of a PPRG implementation guide [42].

Conclusions

Postvention Protocol Response Groups (PPRGs) have emerged as a central mechanism to coordinate the implementation of postvention responses, providing bereavement support services for suicide-affected people and communities across entire regions. In view of inherent challenges to evaluating complex system responses to impactful but rare events, this Delphi consensus study provides valuable expert guidance and practice-based evidence to inform the establishment of PPRGs and strengthen the implementation of postvention responses.
