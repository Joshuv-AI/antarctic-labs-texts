Real and complex spherical designs and their Gramian
Shayne Waldron

Abstract
If a (weighted) spherical design is defined as an integration (cubature) rule for a unitarily invariant space P P of polynomials (on the sphere), then any unitary image of it is also such a spherical design. It therefore follows that such spherical designs are determined by their Gramian (Gram matrix). We outline a general method to obtain such a characterisation as the minima of a function of the Gramian, which we call a potential. This characterisation can be used for the numerical and analytic construction of spherical designs. When the space P P of polynomials is not irreducible under the action of the unitary group, then the potential is not unique. In several cases of interest, e.g., spherical t t -designs and half-designs, we use this flexibility to provide potentials with a very simple form. We then use our results to develop certain aspects of the theory of real and complex spherical designs for unitarily invariant polynomial spaces.

Introduction

Let

in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathbb{C}^{d}

A (

)

This is essentially the first of the four definitions given in
[
41

We say that a polynomial

The existence of spherical designs for constant weights, i.e.,

In applications, it is often required that

In the real case, the unitary (inner product preserving) maps are the orthogonal transformations.

We say that sequences of vectors

[
46
]

Combining this with our previous observation gives:

•

The real or complex spherical designs
(v_{j})
for a unitarily invariant polynomial space

(v_{j})
.

It should therefore be possible to express the condition of

design in terms of
\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle
,
1\leq j,k\leq n
, and the weights
(w_{j})

The primary objective of this paper is to give such a characterisation
for being a spherical design, which is as simple as possible.
Some key features of our approach are:

•

Because the reproducing kernel

is a function of

, we are able to find a

•

The potential

can be given by a univariate polynomial

,
which is not unique when

).

•

The polynomial space

can be described by a finite set of indices

or

,
respectively, which index the irreducible subspaces of

.

Motivated by a careful analysis of the real
spherical designs

]
,
we obtain a unified theory of the most general real and complex
spherical designs, which includes:

A general variational characterisation for all types of designs (Theorem

).

•

New characterisations for real spherical designs, including

-designs
(Theorem
4.1
) and half-designs (Theorem
4.2
).

•

New characterisations for complex spherical designs (Theorem

,
Theorem

).

•

A natural description for real and complex projective spherical designs
(§
6
).

•

A description of the Gegenbauer polynomials that appear naturally

spherical designs as orthogonal polynomials (of two variables),

7
, §
8
).

•

A unified approach to bounds on the numbers of points in a spherical design

9
).

•

Unified results about the

-angular sets (§

As much as possible, we treat the real and complex cases simultaneously,
with

Basic definitions

Sometimes it is convenient to describe a weighted spherical design for
P
as
a sequence of (possibly not unit) vectors
(v_{j})
,
where the weights are inferred from the
\|v_{j}\|
by

w_{j}=w_{j}^{(m)}:={\|v_{j}\|^{m}\over\sum_{\ell}\|v_{\ell}\|^{m}},\quad\hbox{which we call the {\bf$m$-weights}}.

(
2.2
)

Designs with constant weights are called

,
classical

or simply
designs

It is a subtle but important point, which follows from (
1.1
),
that a spherical design depends only the restriction of

to the sphere,
i.e. the space of harmonic polynomials

\mathop{\rm harm}\nolimits(P):=P|_{\mathbb{S}},

which we will call the

Choices for the (unitarily invariant) polynomial space

of interest include:

\displaystyle\Pi_{t}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\hbox{polynomials of degree $\leq t$ on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$}\quad\hbox{({\bf spherical $t$-designs})},

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\hbox{homogeneous polynomials of degree $m$ on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$}\quad\hbox{({\bf spherical half-designs})},

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\hbox{harmonic polynomials in $\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$}\quad\hbox{({\bf spherical designs of harmonic index $m$})},

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{t,t}(\mathbb{C}^{d})=\mathop{\rm span}\nolimits\{|\langle\cdot,v\rangle|^{2t}:v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\}\quad\hbox{({\bf spherical $(t,t)$-designs}, {\bf projective $t$-designs})}.

The half-designs for

P=\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{2t}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are also called
(
real
)
spherical
(t,t)
-designs
.

Example 2.1

From the observation

\int_{\mathbb{S}}\|x\|^{2k}p(x)\,d\sigma(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}p(x)\,d\sigma(x)=\sum_{j}w_{j}p\Bigl({v_{j}\over\|v_{j}\|}\Bigr)=\sum_{j}w_{j}\bigl(\|\cdot\|^{2k}p\bigr)\Bigl({v_{j}\over\|v_{j}\|}\Bigr),

it follows that a spherical design for
P
also integrates the spaces

P^{-}=\bigl\{q:\|\cdot\|^{2j}q\in P,\exists j\geq 0\bigr\},\qquad P^{+}=\bigl\{\|\cdot\|^{2k}p:p\in P,k\geq 0\bigr\},

with
\mathop{\rm harm}\nolimits(P^{-})=\mathop{\rm harm}\nolimits(P^{+})=\mathop{\rm harm}\nolimits(P)
.
In particular, for
P=\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

P^{-}=\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\oplus\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{m-2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\oplus\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{m-4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\oplus\cdots,\quad P^{+}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\|\cdot\|^{2k}\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Because of this,
[
29
]
refer to the spherical designs for
P=\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

as the spherical half-designs (of order
m
).

Every homogeneous polynomial
p\in\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of degree
k

can be written uniquely

p(x)=\sum_{0\leq j\leq{k\over 2}}\|x\|^{2j}p_{k-2j}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{[{k\over 2}]}\|x\|^{2j}p_{k-2j}(x),

(
2.3
)

where
p_{k-2j}\in\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k-2j}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and the restriction map

\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L_{2}(\mathbb{S}):f\mapsto f|_{\mathbb{S}}

is injective, with image denoted
\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{S})
. We will freely identify spaces of
harmonic functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{S}
(
solid
and
surface
spherical harmonics).
It follows from the irreducibility of the summands above
[
1
]
,
that every unitarily invariant (invariant under orthogonal transformations) polynomial space on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

can be written uniquely as a direct sum

P=\bigoplus_{(j,k)\in{\cal J}}\|\cdot\|^{2j}\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k-2j}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

for a subset
{\cal J}
of the indices
\{(j,k):0\leq j\leq{k\over 2}\}
.
For the purpose of integration on
\mathbb{S}
, it suffices to
consider the (possibly lower dimensional) space of harmonic polynomials

\mathop{\rm harm}\nolimits(P)=P|_{\mathbb{S}}=\bigoplus_{\ell\in L}\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{\ell}(\mathbb{S})\approx\bigoplus_{\ell\in L}\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad L:=\{k-2j:(j,k)\in{\cal J}\}\subset\mathbb{N}.

(
2.4
)

We note, in particular

\mathop{\rm harm}\nolimits(\Pi_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{m}\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad\mathop{\rm harm}\nolimits(\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))=\bigoplus_{0\leq j\leq{m\over 2}}\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{m-2j}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

A necessary condition for
(v_{j})
to integrate
\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

with the
m
-weights (
2.2
) is that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\langle x,y\rangle^{m}d\sigma(x)\,d\sigma(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\|v_{j}\|^{m}\over\sum_{\ell}\|v_{\ell}\|^{m}}\langle{v_{j}\over\|v_{j}\|},y\rangle^{m}d\sigma(y)

(
2.5
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\|v_{j}\|^{m}\|v_{k}\|^{m}\over(\sum_{\ell}\|v_{\ell}\|^{m})^{2}}\langle{v_{j}\over\|v_{j}\|},{v_{k}\over\|v_{k}\|}\rangle^{m}

(
2.6
)

\displaystyle={1\over(\sum_{\ell}\|v_{\ell}\|^{m})^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle^{m}.

(
2.7
)

To also integrate
\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with these
m
-weights
a necessary condition is

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\langle x,y\rangle^{m-1}d\sigma(x)\,d\sigma(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\|v_{j}\|^{m}\over\sum_{\ell}\|v_{\ell}\|^{m}}\langle{v_{j}\over\|v_{j}\|},y\rangle^{m-1}d\sigma(y)

(
2.8
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\|v_{j}\|^{m}\|v_{k}\|^{m}\over(\sum_{\ell}\|v_{\ell}\|^{m})^{2}}\langle{v_{j}\over\|v_{j}\|},{v_{k}\over\|v_{k}\|}\rangle^{m-1}

(
2.9
)

\displaystyle={1\over(\sum_{\ell}\|v_{\ell}\|^{m})^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|v_{j}\|\|v_{k}\|\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle^{m-1}.

(
2.10
)

We will show that these conditions (for a weighted spherical

-design)
are also sufficient (Theorem

).
It is most natural to view this result as a special case of a
very general variational characterisation of spherical designs,

3
The variational characterisation of designs

We generalise the surface area measure
\sigma
on
\mathbb{S}
to a measure

\mu
on a set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
. We say that a sequence of points
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})

in
\Omega
and weights

w=(w_{j})
,
w_{1}+\cdots+w_{n}=1
,
w_{j}\geq 0
,
is a
weighted
(
spherical
)
design

for a space
P
of functions
\Omega\to\mathbb{F}

(or simply a
P
-design
) if

\int_{\Omega}p(x)\,d\mu(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}p(v_{j}),\qquad\forall p\in P.

If point evaluation on
P
is a continuous linear functional with respect
to the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mu}:=\int_{\Omega}f\overline{g}\,d\mu

given by
\mu

(as is the case for
P
finite dimensional),
then it can be represented by the
reproducing kernel

K=K_{P}:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
,
which is given by

f(x)=\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)f(y)\,d\mu(y),\qquad\forall f\in P,

where
K(x,y)=\sum_{s}Y_{s}(x)\overline{Y_{s}(y)}

for
(Y_{s})
an orthonormal basis of
P
.

Theorem 3.1

(Variational characterisation)
Let
\mu
be a measure on
\Omega
with
\mu(\Omega)=1
,
P
be a finite dimensional
space of
integrable functions on
\Omega
, and
H

be the subspace of functions which are
orthogonal to the constants, i.e.,

H=P\ominus\mathop{\rm span}\nolimits\{1\}=\{p\in P:\int_{\Omega}p\,d\mu=0\}.

Let
\Phi=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
,
v_{j}\in\Omega
, and
w=(w_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be weights
with
w_{1}+\cdots+w_{n}=1
. Write
H
as a direct sum
H=\oplus_{\ell}H^{(\ell)}
, with

K_{\ell}
the reproducing kernel of
H^{(\ell)}
and
c_{\ell}>0
.
Then

A_{w,c}(\Phi)=A_{P,\mu,w,c}(\Phi):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{j}w_{k}\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}K_{\ell}(v_{j},v_{k})\geq 0,

(
3.11
)

with equality if and only if
(v_{j}),(w_{j})
is a weighted spherical
design for
P
.

Proof:

We first recall that the reproducing kernel
K_{\ell}
for
H^{(\ell)}

represents the point evaluations, i.e.,

f(x)=\int_{\Omega}K_{\ell}(x,y)f(y)\,d\mu(y),\qquad\forall f\in H^{(\ell)},

and that

K_{\ell}(x,y)=\sum_{s}Y_{s}^{(\ell)}(x)\overline{Y_{s}^{(\ell)}(y)},

where
(Y_{s}^{(\ell)})
is an orthonormal basis for
H^{(\ell)}
.
We therefore compute

\displaystyle A_{w,c}(\Phi)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{j}w_{k}\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}K_{\ell}(v_{j},v_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{j}w_{k}\sum_{s}Y_{s}^{(\ell)}(v_{j})\overline{Y_{s}^{(\ell)}(v_{k})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}\sum_{s}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}Y_{s}^{(\ell)}(v_{j})\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\overline{Y_{s}^{(\ell)}(v_{k})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}c_{\ell}\sum_{s}\Bigl|\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}Y_{s}^{(\ell)}(v_{j})\Bigr|^{2}\geq 0,

with equality if and only if

\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}Y_{s}^{(\ell)}(v_{j})=0=\int_{\Omega}Y_{s}^{(\ell)}(x)\,d\mu(x),\qquad\forall s,\ \forall\ell.

which is equivalent
to
(v_{j}),(w_{j})
being a
P
-design
(by linearity and the fact
\sum_{j}w_{j}=1
ensures
that the constants are integrated).

This result is essentially an abstract version of
[
44
]
(Theorem 3),
which was for spherical designs on the real sphere, with
P=\Pi_{t}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
For examples of such potentials, we will use a variety of subscripts for
A
indicating
parameters that it depends on, and which we choose to emphasize in a particular context.

We call the
A_{w,c}(\Phi)
of (
3.1
)
a
potential
for the
P
-designs (with weights
w
), given by

K:=\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}K_{\ell}.

The potential for
P
when there is a single summand and
c_{1}=1
is
called the
canonical potential
, and is denoted by
A_{P}
.
This can be obtained by taking
\oplus_{\ell}H^{(\ell)}
to be an orthogonal
direct sum and
c_{\ell}=1
,
\forall\ell
.
It is also convenient at times
to add a (positive) constant
b_{0}
to a potential, to obtain
a
potential with constant

B(\Phi)=A_{w,c}(\Phi)+b_{0}
, with (
3.11
)
then replaced by
B(\Phi)\geq b_{0}
.

There are
many
possible potentials for
P
-designs,
and so
P
-designs are “universally optimal distributions of points” (cf.
[
13
]
)
for the above class of such potentials.
Given a tractable formula for a potential,

P
-designs
can be constructed
numerically
(for sufficiently many points)
by minimising it (to zero)

[
6
]
,
[
19
]
. We now investigate
the case of real and complex spherical designs in detail,
where
K(x,y)
is a univariate function

of
\langle x,y\rangle
,
which we also refer to as (giving) a

, and hence

is
indeed function of the Gramian of

4
Real spherical designs

We have observed in (
2.4
) that every unitarily invariant polynomial
space
P
restricted to the real sphere has the form

P|_{\mathbb{S}}=P_{L}:=\bigoplus_{\ell\in L}\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{\ell}(\mathbb{S})\qquad\hbox{(orthogonal direct sum)},

(
4.12
)

with
L\subset\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
an index set
(which is finite for
P|_{\mathbb{S}}
finite-dimensional). For the irreducible subspace
\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the reproducing kernel is

K_{\ell}^{(d)}(x,y):=\|x\|^{\ell}\|y\|^{\ell}Q_{\ell}^{(d)}\Bigl({\langle x,y\rangle\over\|x\|\|y\|}\Bigl),

(
4.13
)

where
Q_{k}=Q_{k}^{(d)}
are the orthogonal polynomials for the
Gegenbauer weight for
\lambda={d-2\over 2}
, i.e.,
(1-x^{2})^{d-3\over 2}
on
[-1,1]
, with the
normalisation
Q_{k}^{(d)}(1)=\dim(\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

[
16
]
.
These satisfy

\displaystyle Q_{\ell}^{(d)}(x)

\displaystyle=(2\ell+d-2)\sum_{j=0}^{[\ell/2]}(-1)^{j}{d(d+2)\cdots(d+2\ell-2j-4)\over 2^{j}j!(\ell-2j)!}x^{\ell-2j}

\displaystyle=C_{\ell}^{({d\over 2})}(x)-C_{\ell-2}^{({d\over 2})}(x)={2\ell+d-2\over d-2}C_{\ell}^{({d-2\over 2})}(x),

(
4.14
)

where
C_{\ell}^{(\lambda)}
are the Gegenbauer (ultraspherical) polynomials,
with
C_{\ell}^{(\lambda)}:=0
,
\ell<0
.
They are given by
Q_{0}(x)=1
,
Q_{1}(x)=dx
, and the three-term recurrence

\lambda_{k+1}Q_{k+1}(x)=xQ_{k}(x)-(1-\lambda_{k-1})Q_{k-1}(x),\qquad\lambda_{k}:={k\over 2k+d-2}.

Since surface area measure on the sphere is unitarily invariant,
for
U
unitary,
we have

(f\circ U)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}K(Ux,y)f(y)\,d\sigma(y)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}K(Ux,Uy)(f\circ U)(y)\,d\sigma(y).

Hence
the reproducing kernel
K(x,y)
of
a unitarily invariant space

is a function of
\langle x,y\rangle
,
and hence the potential is a function of the Gramian
[\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle]
.
The direct calculation of the formula (
4.13
)
is called the
addition formula
(see
[
10
]
). We also observe, that for the

\langle x,y\rangle={1\over 2}\bigl(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}-\|x-y\|^{2}\bigr)=1-{1\over 2}\|x-y\|^{2},

(
4.15
)

so that the reproducing kernel on
\mathbb{S}
can also be thought

[

]
).

Since
\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{0}(\mathbb{S})=\mathop{\rm span}\nolimits\{1\}
,
a potential for
P
of the form (
4.12
),
with weights

, is given by

A_{w,c}(\Phi)=A_{F,w}(\Phi):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{j}w_{k}F(\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle),

(

)

where

is the

polynomial given by

F(x):=\sum_{\ell\in L\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell}Q_{\ell}(x)=\sum_{\ell\in L\setminus\{0\}}c_{\ell}\bigl\{C_{\ell}^{({d\over 2})}(x)-C_{\ell-2}^{({d\over 2})}(x)\bigr\}.

(

)

Indeed, every univariate polynomial
F=\sum_{k}c_{k}Q_{k}
with

gives
a potential for the unitarily invariant polynomial
space
P=P_{L}
of (
4.12
), where
L:=\{k>0:c_{k}>0\}
.
We will say that such a polynomial

is a
potential

for

. The function

of (

) is an example of what Schoenberg
[
40
]
calls

positive definite function on the sphere
, i.e., a continuous function

F:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
for which the

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{j}w_{k}F(\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle)=w^{T}[F(\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle)]_{j,k=1}^{n}w

is nonnegative for all choices of points

and

]

In this terminology, we can paraphrase our observation:

•

For spherical designs of harmonic index

5
Complex spherical designs

The complex unitary matrices for
\mathbb{C}^{d}
are a subgroup of the unitary matrices for
\mathbb{R}^{2d}

(the orthogonal group), and so irreducible subspaces under the action of the orthogonal group
may not be irreducible under the action of the complex unitary group.

For the complex sphere
\mathbb{S}
,
the harmonic functions
\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{C}^{d})\approx\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
can be further decomposed
into orthogonal (complex) unitarily invariant irreducible subspaces

\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{C}^{d})=H(k,0)\oplus H(k-1,1)\oplus\cdots\oplus H(0,k),

where
H(p,q)
consists of all harmonic homogeneous polynomials on
\mathbb{C}^{d}

that have degree
p
in the variables
z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}
and
degree
q
in the variables
\overline{z_{1}},\ldots,\overline{z_{d}}

(see
[
39
]
).
Thus the unitarily invariant subspaces of polynomials restricted
to the complex sphere have the form

P|_{\mathbb{S}}=P_{\tau}:=\bigoplus_{(p,q)\in\tau}H(p,q),\qquad\hbox{(orthogonal direct sum)}

for
\tau
a finite subset of indices from
\{(j,k):j,k\geq 0\}
.
Thus, the most general complex spherical design is one which
integrates
P_{\tau}
, which we call a (
spherical
)
\tau
-design
.
Aspects of these
\tau
-designs
have been studied by
[
32
]
,
[
38
]
and
[
34
]
.

There is a subtlety in defining classes of complex spherical designs, as the
\tau
defining
a class is not unique (as is the
L
in the real case), as we now see.

The reproducing kernel
K_{pq}=K_{pq}^{(d)}
for
H(p,q)
(and hence for any unitarily
invariant space of polynomials)
has been calculated explicitly by
[
21
]
for
d>1
as
a function of
\langle z,w\rangle

K_{d}^{(p,q)}(z,w)=Q_{pq}^{(d)}(\langle z,w\rangle),\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{d},

where
Q_{pq}=Q_{pq}^{(d)}

is the univariate polynomial

\displaystyle Q_{pq}^{(d)}(z)

\displaystyle:=c_{pq}^{(d)}\,z^{p-\min\{p,q\}}\overline{z}^{q-\min\{p,q\}}{P^{(d-2,|p-q|)}_{\min\{p,q\}}(2|z|^{2}-1)\over P^{(d-2,|p-q|)}_{\min\{p,q\}}(1)}

\displaystyle={p+q+d-1\over(d-1)!}\sum_{j=0}^{\min\{p,q\}}(-1)^{j}{(d+p+q-j-2)!\over j!(p-j)!(q-j)!}z^{p-j}\overline{z}^{q-j},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

(
5.27
)

and

c_{pq}^{(d)}:=\dim(H(p,q))={(p+q+d-1)(p+d-2)!(q+d-2)!\over p!q!(d-1)!(d-2)!}=Q_{pq}^{(d)}(1).

(
5.28
)

The second of these formulas also holds for
d=1
, where

H(p,q)=0
, unless
p=0
or
q=0
, in which
case
H(p,0)=\mathop{\rm span}\nolimits\{z^{p}\}
,
H(0,q)=\mathop{\rm span}\nolimits\{\overline{z}^{q}\}
.
We also have

\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits(p,q)=\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits_{p,q}(\mathbb{C}^{d})|_{\mathbb{S}}=H(p,q)\oplus H(p-1,q-1)\oplus\cdots.

We observe that the expansion for
Q_{pq}(z)
in terms of the
monomials
z^{j}\overline{z}^{k}
has real coefficients, so that

\overline{Q_{pq}(z)}=Q_{pq}(\overline{z})=Q_{qp}(z),

and so the canonical potentials for
H(p,q)
and
H(q,p)
are equal,
by the calculation

\displaystyle A_{w,H(p,q)}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\overline{A_{w,H(p,q)}(\Phi)}=\sum_{j}\sum_{k}w_{j}w_{k}\overline{Q_{pq}(\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle)}

(
5.29
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{k}w_{j}w_{k}Q_{qp}^{(d)}(\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle)=A_{w,H(q,p)}(\Phi),

(
5.30
)

as are those for
\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits(p,q)
and
\mathop{\rm Hom}\nolimits(q,p)
.
Since the canonical potentials for
H(p,q)
and
H(q,p)
are equal,
the class of spherical designs for some unitarily invariant polynomial spaces
are equal.
For a set of indices
\tau
, we define

\tau^{\rm rev}:=\{(q,p):(p,q)\in\tau\}.

By the
class
of a spherical
\tau
-design we mean the maximal unitarily invariant
subspace that it integrates, or the indices
\tau^{*}
of that subspace.

Proposition 5.1

The class of complex spherical designs for

P_{L}
and
P_{K}
are equal if and only if

L\cup L^{\rm rev}\cup\{0\}=K\cup K^{\rm rev}\cup\{0\}.

In particular, the class (of indices) for any
\tau
-design is

\tau^{*}=\tau\cup\tau^{\rm rev}\cup\{0\}.

Proof:
Since every complex spherical design integrates the constants,
we can add
0=(0,0)
to the set of indices
L
without changing the class of
the spherical designs it gives. Similarly,
since
H(p,q)
and
H(q,p)
have the same canonical potential, by (
5.29
),
we may add
(q,p)
for
(p,q)\in L
. Thus if
L\cup L^{\rm rev}\cup\{0\}\subset K\cup K^{\rm rev}\cup\{0\}
, then a spherical design for
P_{K}
is spherical design for
P_{L}
.
This gives the forward implication.

The converse follows from the fact that the canonical potentials for different classes
differ by at least one term, and then a linear algebra argument.

We will not labour the point, but the classes of complex spherical designs are
given by the possible choices for
\tau^{*}
, and for a
\tau
-design we will refer
to
\tau^{*}
as the
canonical indices
for the design, and use

\tau^{*}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
to calculate the
canonical potential
.

We will carry over terminology from the real case,
e.g., we say that a univariate polynomial

F=\sum_{(p,q)}f_{pq}Q_{pq},\qquad f_{pq}\geq 0,

(
5.31
)

gives a potential
A_{F,w}(\Phi)
for the unitarily invariant polynomial space

P=P_{\tau}=\bigoplus_{(p,q)\in\tau}H(p,q),\qquad\tau:=\{(p,q):f_{pq}>0,(p,q)\neq(0,0)\}.

Orthogonality of the Gegenbauer polynomials

The polynomials
Q_{k}^{(d)}
of (
4.14
) that give the reproducing kernel for

\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are orthogonal with respect to the Gegenbauer
weight
(1-x^{2})^{{d-2\over 2}-{1\over 2}}
on
[-1,1]
.
Indeed

\langle Q_{j},Q_{k}\rangle_{\mathop{\rm geg}\nolimits}=\begin{cases}Q_{j}(1)=\dim(\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),&j=k;\cr 0,&j\neq k,\end{cases}

(
7.40
)

where

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathop{\rm geg}\nolimits}:={\Gamma({1\over 2}d)\over\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma({1\over 2}d-{1\over 2})}\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)g(x)\,(1-x^{2})^{d-3\over 2}\,dx.

We will refer to these, and the polynomials
Q_{pq}^{(d)}
of (
5.27
)
giving the reproducing kernel for
H(p,q)
as (real or complex)
Gegenbauer polynomials
.
The terms “Jacobi” and “disk” polynomial are sometimes used for the
latter
[
38
]
,
[
32
]
.

We now show the polynomials
Q_{pq}^{(d)}
are orthogonal with respect to
the Gegenbauer weight
(1-|z|^{2})^{d-2}
on the unit disc

\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1\}
in
\mathbb{C}
(cf.
[
32
]
).

Proposition 7.1

For
d>1
, the (complex) Gegenbauer polynomials

Q_{pq}^{(d)}
of
(
5.27
)
are orthogonal
with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathbb{D},d}

\displaystyle:={d-1\over\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}f(z)\overline{g(z)}(1-|z|^{2})^{d-2}\,dA(z)

\displaystyle={d-1\over\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(re^{i\theta})\overline{g(re^{i\theta})}\,(1-r^{2})^{d-2}r\,dr\,d\theta,

where
A
is the area measure on
\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and

\langle Q_{pq},Q_{k\ell}\rangle_{\mathbb{D},d}=\begin{cases}Q_{pq}(1)=\dim(H(p,q)),&(p,q)=(k,\ell);\cr 0,&(p,q)\neq(k,\ell).\end{cases}

(
7.41
)

Proof:
We write
z=re^{i\theta}
, so that

Q_{pq}^{(d)}(z)=c_{pq}^{(d)}\,(re^{i\theta})^{p-m}(r\overline{e^{i\theta}})^{q-m}{P^{(d-2,|p-q|)}_{m}(2r^{2}-1)\over P^{(d-2,|p-q|)}_{m}(1)},\qquad m:=\min\{p,q\}.

By integrating in
\theta
first, we see that

\langle Q_{pq}^{(d)},Q_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}^{(d)}\rangle_{\mathbb{D},d}=0
, except when
p-q=p^{\prime}-q^{\prime}
.
In this case, we may suppose, without loss of generality, that

p-q=p^{\prime}-q^{\prime}=k\geq 0
, i.e.,
m=q
,
m^{\prime}=q^{\prime}
,
so that

(re^{i\theta})^{p-m}(r\overline{e^{i\theta}})^{q-m}(r\overline{e^{i\theta}})^{p^{\prime}-m^{\prime}}(re^{i\theta})^{q^{\prime}-m^{\prime}}=r^{2k}
,
and

\langle Q_{pq}^{(d)},Q_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}^{(d)}\rangle_{\mathbb{D},d}=2c_{pq}^{(d)}c_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}^{(d)}\int_{0}^{1}r^{2k}P_{m}^{(d-2,k)}(2r^{2}-1)P_{m^{\prime}}^{(d-2,k)}(2r^{2}-1)\,(1-r^{2})^{d-2}\,r\,dr.

By making the change of variables

x=2r^{2}-1
, the integral in
r
above becomes

\int_{-1}^{1}\left({1+x\over 2}\right)^{k}P_{m}^{(d-2,k)}(x)P_{m^{\prime}}^{(d-2,k)}(x)\,\left({1-x\over 2}\right)^{d-2}\,{1\over 4}dx,

which is zero, unless
m=m^{\prime}
, in which case
(p,q)=(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
.
The calculation of the constant for the nonzero inner product
is a straight forward calculation.

This orthogonality of the polynomials
Q_{pq}^{(d)}
can also be proved
from the reproducing kernel property,
by using the orthogonality of the
H(p,q)
, and the
result (see §1.4.5 of
[
39
]
) that for
\mathbb{S}
the unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{d}

and
f
a univariate function

\int_{\mathbb{S}}f(\langle z,w\rangle)\,d\sigma(w)={d-1\over\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}f(\zeta)(1-|\zeta|^{2})^{d-2}\,dA(\zeta),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{d},\ \|z\|=1.

In particular, we can calculate the orthogonality constant

\displaystyle Q_{pq}^{(d)}(1)=\overline{Q_{pq}^{(d)}(\langle z,z\rangle)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}Q_{pq}^{(d)}(\langle z,w\rangle)\overline{Q_{pq}^{(d)}(\langle z,w\rangle)}\,d\sigma(w)

\displaystyle={d-1\over\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}|Q_{pq}^{(d)}(\zeta)|^{2}(1-|\zeta|^{2})^{d-2}\,dA(\zeta)=\langle Q_{pq}^{(d)},Q_{pq}^{(d)}\rangle_{\mathbb{D},d}.

Since
\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{C}^{d})=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}H(p,q)
, we have

Q_{k}^{(2d)}\bigl((x,y)\bigr)=\sum_{p+q=k}Q_{pq}^{(d)}(z),\qquad z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

For
d=1
, the polynomials
Q_{pq}^{(1)}(z)
can be viewed
as being orthogonal with respect to the “singular Gegenbauer weight”

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathbb{D},d}={1\over 2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(e^{i\theta})\overline{g(e^{i\theta})}\,d\theta.

8
Products of the Gegenbauer polynomials

To construct positive functions
F
giving a potential for
a spherical design, it is useful to know the Gegenbauer
expansion for a product of Gegenbauer polynomials.
In the real case, there is the following celebrated formula
dating back to Rogers (1895)

Q_{k}^{(d)}Q_{l}^{(d)}=\sum_{j=0}^{\min\{k,l\}}{(k+\nu)(l+\nu)(k+l-2j)!(\nu)_{j}(\nu)_{k-j}(\nu)_{l-j}(2\nu)_{k+l-j}\over\nu(k+l+\nu-j)j!(k-j)!(l-j)!(\nu)_{k+l-j}(2\nu)_{k+l-2j}}Q_{k+l-2j}^{(d)},

(
8.42
)

where
\nu:={d-2\over 2}
,
(\nu)_{j}
is the Pochhammer symbol,
and the coefficients are clearly positive.
We will denote by
k\cdot l
the degrees of the Gegenbauer polynomials
occuring in (
8.42
), i.e.,

k\cdot l:=\{k+l-2j:0\leq j\leq m\},\qquad m:=\min\{k,l\},

(
8.43
)

which we extend to subsets in the natural way

K\cdot L:=\bigcup_{k\in K\atop l\in L}k\cdot l.

(
8.44
)

As an example, for
L=\{1,4\}
, we have

L\cdot L=\{0,2,3,4,5,6,8\}
.

Lemma 8.1

If
F
and
G
give potentials for
P_{L}
and
P_{K}
(on the real sphere),
then
FG
gives a potential for
P_{L\cdot K}
.

Proof:
Multiply out the Gegenbauer expansions for
F
and
G

and use (
8.42
).

We now present the analogue of Lemma
8.1

for the product of complex Gegenbauer polynomials.
It can be shown (see
[
39
]
) that

H(p,q)H(r,s)\subset\sum_{j=0}^{\mu}H(p+r-j,q+s-j),\quad\mu:=\min\{p,s\}+\min\{q,r\},

(
8.45
)

where there is equality for
d\geq 3
.
Motivated by this,
for indices
(p,q)
and
(r,s)
, we define the operation

(p,q)\cdot(r,s):=\bigcup_{j=0}^{\mu}\{(p+r-j,q+s-j)\},\quad\mu:=\min\{p,s\}+\min\{q,r\},

(
8.46
)

which we extend to subsets of indices as in (
8.44
).
As examples, we have

\displaystyle(p,q)\cdot(q,p)

\displaystyle=\{(0,0),(1,1),\ldots,(k,k)\},\quad k=p+q,

so that
(0,0)\in{\cal U}\cdot{\cal U}^{\rm rev}
, when
{\cal U}
is nonempty,
and

\displaystyle(p,q)\cdot(p,q)

\displaystyle=\{(2p,2q),(2p-1,2q-1),\ldots,(2p-2m,2q-2m)\},\quad m=\min\{p,q\}.

In view of (
8.45
) and (
8.46
),
it follows that

P_{L}P_{K}\subset\sum_{(p,q)\in L\cdot K}H(p,q).

The corresponding analogue of the Roger’s formula (
8.42
) is

Q_{pq}^{(d)}Q_{rs}^{(d)}=\sum_{j=0}^{\mu}c_{p+r-j,q+s-j}^{(d)}Q_{p+r-j,q+s-j}^{(d)},\quad\mu:=\min\{p,s\}+\min\{q,r\},

with nonnegative coefficients (see
[
14
]
).
The nonnegativity of the coefficients follows from the
Schur product theorem.
This gives the complex analogue of Lemma
8.1
.

Theorem 8.1

The product of functions giving a potential
for the real or complex sphere
gives
a potential. Indeed, if
F
and
G
are give potentials for

P_{\cal U}
and
P_{\cal V}

gives a potential for
a subspace of

P_{{\cal U}\cdot{\cal V}}

for the real sphere and the complex sphere when

The following particular case will be useful.

Corollary 8.1

If
F=\sum_{(p,q)}f_{pq}Q_{pq}

the complex sphere, then

G=\overline{Q_{ab}}F/Q_{ab}(1)

Proof:

9
Bounds for real and complex spherical designs

Here we consider the relationship between our results on potentials
and the seminal paper
[
16
]
on codes and spherical designs.
In particular, we seek to understand the given bounds
on the number of vectors in real spherical designs,
and then extend them in a natural way to complex spherical designs.

It is now convenient to allow the univariate polynomial
F
giving a potential
for real or complex spherical designs
\Phi=(v_{j})_{j=1}^{n}

to have a (possibly nonzero) constant term
f_{0}
in its Gegenbauer expansion,
so that the potential (with constant) satisfies

n^{2}A_{F}(\Phi)=\sum_{j}\sum_{k}F(\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle)\geq n^{2}f_{0},

with equality if and only if
(v_{j})
is a design.
A very specific way equality can be achieved is by having each nondiagonal term in the sum
be constant, i.e.,

F(\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle)=c,\quad\forall j\neq k.

(
9.47
)

The value of the constant
c
depends strongly on the choice of
F
, indeed

n^{2}A_{F}(\Phi)=nF(1)+(n^{2}-n)c=n^{2}f_{0}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c={nf_{0}-F(1)\over n-1}.

In actuality, the number of equations in (
9.47
) depends on
the number of
angles
of
\Phi
, i.e., size of the set of inner products

\mathop{\rm Ang}\nolimits(\Phi):=\{\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle:v_{j}\neq v_{k}\}\subset[-1,1),

and the value of the potential depends on these angles and their multiplicities. i.e.,

n^{2}A_{F}(\Phi)=nF(1)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathop{\rm Ang}\nolimits(\Phi)}m_{\alpha}F(\alpha)\geq n^{2}f_{0},

(
9.48
)

where
m_{\alpha}>0
is the multiplicity of the angle
\alpha
as an entry
of the Gramian
[\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle]
.

We are now in a position to give a transparent statement and proof of
Theorem 4.3 of
[
16
]
. This is the key result which gives upper bounds
for the number of points in codes and designs, given a suitable choice of potential
F
.

Let
A\subset[-1,1)
. A finite set
X=(v_{j})
of
n
unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an
A
-code

if its angles
\mathop{\rm Ang}\nolimits(X)
are contained in
A
.

Theorem 9.1

(Upper bound)
Let
F=\sum_{k}f_{k}Q_{k}
be a polynomial with

f_{k}\geq 0
,
f_{0}>0
, i.e., a potential with a positive constant
f_{0}
,
for which

F(\alpha)\leq 0,\qquad\alpha\in A\subset[-1,1).

(
9.49
)

Then the size
n
of any
A
-code
X
satisfies

n\leq{F(1)\over f_{0}},

(
9.50
)

with equality if and only if
the angles of
X
are roots of
F
, and
X
is a spherical
P
-design for

P=P_{L}=\bigoplus_{k\in L}\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{S}),\quad L=\{k:f_{k}>0\}.

Proof:
Suppose that
X
is an
A
-code. Then applying (
9.49
) to
(
9.48
) gives

n^{2}f_{0}\leq n^{2}A_{F}(\Phi)=nF(1)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathop{\rm Ang}\nolimits(\Phi)}m_{\alpha}F(\alpha)\leq nF(1),

which gives the inequality (
9.50
). There is equality above when
F(\alpha)=0
,
\alpha\in\mathop{\rm Ang}\nolimits(X)
,
and
A_{F}(\Phi)=f_{0}
, i.e.,
X
is a spherical design for the potential
F
,
i.e., for
P=P_{L}
.

The weighted version of (
9.48
) is

A_{F}(\Phi)=F(1)\Bigl(\sum_{j}w_{j}^{2}\Bigr)+\sum_{\alpha\in A}F(\alpha)\Bigl(\sum_{j,k\atop\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle=\alpha}w_{j}w_{k}\Bigr)\geq f_{0},

which allows Theorem
9.1
to be generalised, with (
9.50
) becoming

n\leq{F(0)\over f_{0}}\bigl(n\sum_{j}w_{j}^{2}\bigr).

Before giving examples of Theorem
9.1
,
we consider the corresponding lower bound on
n
given by Theorem 5.10 of
[
16
]
,
which can be established using a similar method, where
F
is a difference of potentials.
For
F=\sum_{k}f_{k}Q_{k}
a univariate polynomial,
with Gegenbauer coefficients
f_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
, we call

F=f_{0}+F^{+}-F^{-},\qquad F^{+}:=\sum_{k\neq 0\atop f_{k}>0}f_{k}Q_{k},\quad F^{-}:=-\sum_{k\neq 0\atop f_{k}<0}f_{k}Q_{k},

its decomposition into potentials. We have the following version of (
9.48
)

\sum_{j}\sum_{k}F(\langle v_{j},v_{k}\rangle)=n^{2}f_{0}+n^{2}A_{F^{+}}(\Phi)-n^{2}A_{F^{-}}(\Phi)=nF(1)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathop{\rm Ang}\nolimits(\Phi)}m_{\alpha}F(\alpha).

(
9.51
)

Theorem 9.2

(Lower bound)
Let
P=P_{L}
be a unitarily invariant space of polynomials,
and
F=\sum_{k}f_{k}Q_{k}
a be a univariate polynomial with

\{k:f_{k}>0\}=L\cup\{0\}.\qquad F(\alpha)\geq 0,\quad\forall\alpha\in[-1,1].

Then the size
n
of any
P
-design
\Phi
satisfies

n\geq{F(1)\over f_{0}},

(
9.52
)

with equality if and only if
the angles of
\Phi
are roots of
F
, and
\Phi
is a spherical

P_{K}
-design for

K=\{k:f_{k}<0\}
.

Proof:
By assumption,
F^{+}
is a potential for
P=P_{L}
, and so
A_{F^{+}}(\Phi)=0
.
Thus (
9.51
) reduces to

n^{2}f_{0}-n^{2}A_{F^{-}}(\Phi)-nF(1)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathop{\rm Ang}\nolimits(\Phi)}m_{\alpha}F(\alpha).

Since
F(\alpha)\geq 0
, we obtain the inequality

n^{2}f_{0}-nF(1)\geq n^{2}A_{F^{-}}(\Phi),

with equality when
F(\alpha)=0
,
\alpha\in\mathop{\rm Ang}\nolimits(\Phi)
.
Since
F^{-}
is a potential for
P_{K}
and
f_{0}>0
, we have

n^{2}f_{0}-nF(1)\geq n^{2}A_{F^{-}}(\Phi)\geq 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n\geq{F(1)\over f_{0}},

which gives (
9.52
). Moreover, there is equality above when

A_{F^{-}}(\Phi)=0
, i.e., when
\Phi
is a spherical
P_{K}
-design.

The original statement of Theorem
9.2
in
[
16
]
(Theorem 5.10) was
for spherical
t
-designs, i.e.,
L=\{0,1,2,\ldots,t\}

(see Example
9.1
).
The weighted version of this result can be obtained, in the obvious way,
giving the lower bound

n\geq{F(0)\over f_{0}}\bigl(n\sum_{j}w_{j}^{2}\bigr).

The easiest way to find an
F
satisfying
F(x)\geq 0
on
[-1,1]
, which
we will refer to as a “nonnegative potential”, is to take the
square of an appropriate univariate polynomial.

Corollary 9.1

Let
E\subset\mathbb{N}
be a nonempty finite set of indices, and
L=E\cdot E
.
Then

F:=\Bigl(\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}\Bigr)^{2},

(
9.53
)

gives a nonnegative potential for
P_{L}
, and the number of points
n
in a

P_{L}
-design satisfies

n\geq{F(1)\over f_{0}}=\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}(1)=\sum_{k\in E}\dim(\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

(
9.54
)

Proof:
Clearly,
F
is nonnegative, and by Lemma
8.1
, it is
a potential for
L=E\cdot E
. Thus, we may apply Theorem
9.2
.
By the orthogonality relations (
7.40
), we have

f_{0}=\langle F,1\rangle_{\mathop{\rm geg}\nolimits}=\langle\Bigl(\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}\Bigr)^{2},1\rangle_{\mathop{\rm geg}\nolimits}=\langle\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k},\sum_{\ell\in E}Q_{\ell}\rangle_{\mathop{\rm geg}\nolimits}=\sum_{k\in E}\langle Q_{k},Q_{k}\rangle_{\mathop{\rm geg}\nolimits}=\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}(1),

so that

{F(1)\over f_{0}}=\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}(1)=\sum_{k\in E}\dim(\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),

and we obtain the desired estimate.

Corollary 9.2

Let
E\subset\mathbb{N}
be a finite set of even indices or of odd indices, and

F:=\Bigl({Q_{1}\over d}+1\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}\Bigr)^{2},\qquad L=\{0,1\}\cdot(E\cdot E).

(
9.55
)

Then
F
gives a nonnegative potential for
P_{L}
, and the number of points
n
in a

P_{L}
-design satisfies

n\geq{F(1)\over f_{0}}=2\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}(1)=2\sum_{k\in E}\dim(\mathop{\rm Harm}\nolimits_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

(
9.56
)

Proof:
The proof is similar to that of Corollary
9.1
.
The first factor of
F
satisfies

{Q_{1}(x)\over d}+1=x+1\geq 0,\qquad x\in[-1,1],

and so
F
gives a nonnegative potential for
L
.
The polynomial
Q_{1}(\sum_{k}Q_{k})^{2}
is odd, so has zero integral with
respect to the Gegenbauer weight, and we have

f_{0}=\langle F,1\rangle_{\mathop{\rm geg}\nolimits}=\langle\Bigl({Q_{1}\over d}+1\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}\Bigr)^{2},1\rangle_{\mathop{\rm geg}\nolimits}=\langle\Bigl(\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}\Bigr)^{2},1\rangle_{\mathop{\rm geg}\nolimits}=\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}(1)>0.

Further,

F(1)=2\Bigl(\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}(1)\Bigr)^{2},

and so we obtain the desired estimate from Theorem
9.2
.

We observe that the choice of the polynomial
\sum_{k\in E}Q_{k}
in
Corollaries
9.1
and
9.2
is optimal.
Indeed, if a different convex combination

\sum_{k}c_{k}Q_{k}
is taken in the potential
F
,
then term

\sum_{k}Q_{k}(1)
in the lower estimate
becomes

M={\Bigr(\sum_{k}c_{k}Q_{k}(1)\Bigr)^{2}\over\sum_{k}c_{k}^{2}Q_{k}(1)}.

By Cauchy-Schwarz, we have

\Bigl(\sum_{k}c_{k}Q_{k}(1)\Bigr)^{2}=\Bigl(\sum_{k}c_{k}\sqrt{Q_{k}(1)}\sqrt{Q_{k}(1)}\Bigr)^{2}\leq\Bigl(\sum_{k}c_{k}^{2}Q_{k}(1)\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_{k}Q_{k}(1)\Bigr),

so that

M\leq\sum_{k}Q_{k}(1),

with equality if and only if
c_{k}=1
,
\forall k
.

We now consider bounds for complex designs.
As before, we will take polynomials
of the form

F=\sum_{(p,q)}f_{pq}Q_{pq},\qquad f_{pq}\in\mathbb{R}.

These are polynomials of a complex variable with real coefficients,
and so take complex values in general (unless
f_{pq}=f_{qp}
,
\forall(p,q)
). Nevertheless, they do have
F(1),f_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
and, most importantly,

\sum_{\alpha\in A}F(\alpha)m_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha\in A}\Re(F(\alpha))m_{\alpha}.

(
9.57
)

The last equation follows since
\overline{F(z)}=F(\overline{z})
,
and an angle
\alpha
and
\overline{\alpha}
appear with the same multiplicity
m_{\alpha}=m_{\overline{\alpha}}

in (

so the sum of the corresponding pair of
terms is

\displaystyle m_{\alpha}F(\alpha)+m_{\overline{\alpha}}F(\overline{\alpha})

\displaystyle=m_{\alpha}F(\alpha)+m_{\alpha}\overline{F(\alpha)}=2m_{\alpha}\Re(F(\alpha))

\displaystyle=m_{\alpha}\Re(F(\alpha))+m_{\overline{\alpha}}\Re(\overline{F(\alpha)})=m_{\alpha}\Re(F(\alpha))+m_{\overline{\alpha}}\Re(F(\overline{\alpha})).

In view of (

and

(Upper and Lower bounds)
Let
F=\sum_{(p,q)}f_{pq}Q_{pq}

f_{pq}\in\mathbb{R},\qquad f_{0}=f_{00}>0,\qquad\tau=\tau^{+}:=\{(p,q):f_{pq}>0\},\quad\tau^{-}:=\{(p,q):f_{pq}<0\},

and

(i)

If
F
is a potential, i.e.,
\tau^{-}=\{\}
, and

,

of any

, and

\tau

(ii)

,

For a finite set of indices

Corollary 9.3

Let
{\cal E}\subset\mathbb{N}^{2}
be a nonempty finite set of indices. Then

(

is a potential for

(

)

with equality if and only if the angles of the design are roots of the polynomial

Proof:

Since
\overline{Q_{pq}}=Q_{qp}
, we have the equality in (

),
and the multiplication rule of Theorem

implies that

F=Q_{\cal E}\overline{Q_{\cal E}}

{\cal E}\cdot{\cal E}^{\rm rev}

Since
F=|Q_{\cal E}|^{2}\geq 0
, by construction, Theorem
9.3
gives the estimate

n\geq{F(1)\over f_{00}}={|Q_{\cal E}(1)|^{2}\over Q_{\cal E}(1)}=Q_{\cal E}(1)=\sum_{(p,q)\in{\cal E}}Q_{pq}(1)=\sum_{(p,q)\in{\cal E}}\dim\bigl(H(p,q)\bigr)=\dim(P_{\cal E}),

Q_{\cal E}
.

The inequality (
9.60
) is given in
[
38
]
(Theorem 4.2) for
{\cal U}
a
lower set, via a different argument. We now explain, and give the argument, which is classical
and neat. A “convolution” product

on indices (and sets of indices) is given in

{\cal E}*{\cal E}:={\cal E}+{\cal E}^{\rm rev}\subset{\cal E}\cdot{\cal E}^{\rm rev}.

For

a lower set, one has

Let

be a spherical

-design, and

(f_{j})
be an orthonormal basis for

It follows from (

f_{j}\overline{f_{k}}\in\Bigl(\bigoplus_{(p,q)\in{\cal E}}H(p,q)\Bigr)\Bigl(\bigoplus_{(r,s)\in{\cal E}^{\rm rev}}H(r,s)\Bigr)\subset\bigoplus_{(p,q)\in{\cal E}\cdot{\cal E}^{\rm rev}}H(p,q),

and so we have, by the cubature rule, that

{1\over|X|}\sum_{x\in X}f_{j}(x)\overline{f_{k}(x)}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}f_{j}\overline{f_{k}}\,d\sigma=\delta_{jk},

We observe that the sets

Example 9.4

(Projective indices) For any

(Spherical
