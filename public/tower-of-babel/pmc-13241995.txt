Construction of a chimeric multi-antigen fusion vaccine, EimeriaBig, and evaluation of immune response and protective effect in Eimeria necatrix
Authors: Xiangjie Chen, Haiming Cai, Shenquan Liao, Nanshan Qi, Juan Li, Minna Lv, Xuhui Lin, Yongle Song, Yibin Zhu, Jianfei Zhang, Xiaohui Zhang, Mingfei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAvian coccidiosis, especially infection caused by the highly pathogenic Eimeria necatrix, causes major economic loss in the poultry production. With the increasing limitations of anticoccidial drugs and live vaccines, there is an urgent need for novel, broad-spectrum control strategies. In this study, a reverse vaccinology workflow was used to screen the proteomes of five Eimeria species. Six highly conserved membrane protein families were selected: carbonic anhydrase (CA), mechanosensitive ion channel (MSC), cell division control protein 50 (CDC50), major facilitator superfamily transporter (MFS), ATP-binding cassette transporter (ABC) and lipid transfer protein (LTP). Antigenic fragments from these families were linked with EAAAK linkers to generate a chimeric multi-antigen fusion construct, EimeriaBig. Five prime-boost regimens were tested in chickens challenged with E. necatrix. The regimen of one protein prime followed by two DNA boosts showed the highest protection among the tested regimens, with moderate protective efficacy. Our results demonstrated that a heterologous strategy, comprising a recombinant protein prime (rEimeriaBig) followed by two DNA vaccine boosts (pcDNA3.1-EimeriaBig), conferred the best protection among the regimens tested, with moderate efficacy. In this group, cecal lesion score was reduced by 47.64 % and oocyst output was reduced by 49.75 %, and the anticoccidial index was 168.72. The same regimen produced higher antigen-specific IgY and increased transcription of IL-2, IFN-γ, and IL-10. These results support further optimization of this vaccine strategy for control of E. necatrix.Keywords: Avian coccidiosis, Eimeria necatrix, Reverse vaccinology, Chimeric multi-antigen fusion vaccine, Heterologous prime-boost

Introduction

Avian coccidiosis, a devastating enteric disease caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Eimeria, imposes a staggering economic burden on the global poultry industry, with annual losses estimated at over £10 billion (Blake et al., 2020). Current control strategies primarily rely on anticoccidial drugs and live-attenuated vaccines; however, these approaches face significant limitations. The extensive use of anticoccidial drugs has led to the emergence of widespread drug resistance across nearly all available compound classes. Meanwhile, live vaccines, though effective, are hampered by high production costs, strict cold-chain requirements, and the risk of vaccine-induced pathogenicity (Blake et al., 2020; Soutter et al., 2020). Furthermore, commercial vaccines often provide insufficient cross-protection against Eimeria necatrix, one of the most pathogenic species characterized by high mortality and severe intestinal lesions. These challenges necessitate the urgent development of novel, safe, and broad-spectrum control strategies.

Over the past two decades, reverse vaccinology has revolutionized antigen discovery (Khalid and Poh, 2023). Unlike traditional empirical methods that rely on pathogen cultivation, this in silico approach screens entire pathogen genomes to rapidly identify potential vaccine candidates based on properties such as surface exposure, antigenicity, and evolutionary conservation. This strategy has successfully accelerated vaccine development for various pathogens, including Neisseria meningitidis and Plasmodium spp. Building on this, multi-epitope vaccines represent a promising advancement. By genetically fusing selected B-cell and T-cell epitopes from different antigens, multi-epitope vaccines can induce broad immune responses, minimize allergic reactions by excluding non-essential regions, and prevent immune evasion caused by antigenic variation.

For obligate intracellular parasites like Eimeria, membrane proteins are crucial for host-parasite interactions, including invasion, nutrient acquisition, and evasion of host immunity (Blume and Seeber, 2018; Dean et al., 2014). Consequently, functional membrane proteins represent ideal vaccine targets. In this study, we focused on six key membrane protein families: Carbonic Anhydrases (CA), which catalyze the reversible hydration of carbon dioxide and are essential for pH regulation and anion transport (Capasso and Supuran, 2013); Cell Division Control proteins (CDC50), which act as non-catalytic subunits for P4-ATPases and are vital for the biogenesis of secretory organelles (rhoptries/micronemes) required for host invasion (Günay-Esiyok et al., 2019); Mechanosensitive Ion Channels (MSC), which regulate osmotic stress responses (Ma et al., 2018); Major Facilitator Superfamily (MFS) transporters, which mediate the uptake of essential nutrients (Hammoudi et al., 2018); ATP-binding Cassette (ABC) transporters, which are involved in substrate translocation and often associated with multidrug resistance (Schneider and Hunke, 1998); and Lipid Transfer Proteins (LTP), which are critical for membrane biogenesis during parasite replication (Helle et al., 2013). Despite their physiological importance, the immunoprotective potential of these conserved membrane proteins in Eimeria spp. remains largely underexplored.

In the present study, we employed a comprehensive reverse vaccinology pipeline to screen and identify conserved antigenic targets from these six membrane protein families across five major Eimeria species. We constructed a novel chimeric multi-antigen fusion vaccine, termed "EimeriaBig" by linking selected antigenic fragments with rigid linkers. We then evaluated its immunogenicity and protective efficacy against E. necatrix challenge in chickens. Crucially, to optimize the immune response, we investigated the efficacy of a heterologous prime-boost immunization strategy, comparing it with homologous protein or DNA vaccination regimens. Our findings provide new insights into the rational design of multi-component vaccines for coccidiosis control.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All procedures involving the chicken care and management in this study were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Health, Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences (approval no. YC-PT2023032).

Parasites and animals

The sporulated oocysts within three months of E. necatrix HZ strain were stored in 2.5 % potassium dichromate at 4 °C and provided by the parasitology laboratory of the Institute of Animal Health, Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences. One-day-old male Mahuang broilers were purchased from Guangdong Zhiwei Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd. All broilers were provided ad libitum access to food and water without any coccidiostats.

Sequence retrieval and dataset preparation

Proteome sequences of five Eimeria species (E. necatrix UP000018050, E. tenella UP000030747, E. maxima UP000030750, E. brunetti UP000030754, and E. acervulina UP000030763) and the chicken host (Gallus gallus UP000000539) were retrieved from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). These five Eimeria species represent the most economically important coccidian parasites affecting poultry production worldwide.

Host homology filtering

To eliminate potential autoimmune responses, proteins showing significant homology to the chicken host were filtered out using BLASTp (BLAST+ v2.16.0) (Camacho et al., 2009). A local BLAST database was constructed from the chicken proteome using makeblastdb. Each Eimeria proteome was queried against the chicken database with the following parameters: E-value threshold ≤0.01, maximum target sequences = 1, and 100 threads for parallel processing. Proteins were retained as non-host candidates if they met any of the following criteria: (1) no BLAST hit against the host, (2) sequence identity <35 %, or (3) E-value >0.005. This conservative approach ensures the removal of proteins with high similarity to host proteins while preserving parasite-specific antigens.

Subcellular localization prediction

Complete protein sequences (starting with methionine and containing no ambiguous residues) were subjected to subcellular localization prediction using a multi-tool consensus approach to maximize prediction accuracy and coverage. SignalP 5.0 (Almagro Armenteros et al., 2019) was employed to predict signal peptides with the short output format, identifying proteins destined for secretion or membrane insertion through the classical secretory pathway. Transmembrane helices (TMHs) were predicted using TMHMM 2.0 (Krogh et al., 2001), and proteins with one or more predicted TMHs were classified as integral membrane proteins. To complement these structure-based predictions, DeepLoc 1.0 (Krogh et al., 2001) was used to provide comprehensive subcellular localization predictions based on deep learning algorithms trained on experimentally validated localization data. The tool was run in batch mode with 20 sequences per batch and 100 parallel threads to optimize computational efficiency while maintaining prediction accuracy. Additionally, PredGPI 1.0 (Pierleoni et al., 2008) was employed to identify proteins with glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors, which are commonly found in parasite surface proteins and represent important vaccine targets due to their surface exposure.

Membrane proteins were identified using relaxed criteria to maximize candidate coverage while maintaining biological relevance. Proteins were classified as membrane-associated if they possessed at least one transmembrane helix, a predicted GPI anchor, or both a signal peptide and cell membrane localization prediction from DeepLoc. This multi-criteria approach ensures comprehensive identification of surface-exposed and membrane-associated proteins that are accessible to the host immune system during parasite invasion and development, making them suitable targets for antibody-mediated immunity.

Orthology analysis and conservation assessment

To identify conserved antigens across all five Eimeria species, orthology analysis was performed using OrthoFinder v2.5.5 (Emms and Kelly, 2019) with the MSA (Multiple Sequence Alignment) method. Membrane proteins from each species were used as input, and the analysis was conducted with 100 threads for BLAST searches and 16 threads for alignment. Orthogroups (clusters of orthologous proteins) present in all five species were considered conserved and retained for further analysis. This stringent conservation criterion ensures that selected antigens are broadly applicable across different Eimeria species, potentially providing cross-protective immunity.

Antigenicity prediction

Conserved proteins were evaluated for antigenicity using VaxiJen v2.0 (Doytchinova and Flower, 2007) (http://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html) with the parasite model. VaxiJen employs an alignment-independent approach based on auto-cross-covariance transformation of protein sequences. Proteins with VaxiJen scores ≥0.4 were classified as probable antigens, as this threshold has been validated for parasite antigens in previous studies (Aslam et al., 2020; Doytchinova and Flower, 2007; Rashid et al., 2017; Ud-din et al., 2022). Only orthogroups where all member proteins from the five species showed antigenic potential were selected for vaccine design.

Functional annotation

To understand the biological functions of selected antigens and validate their relevance as vaccine targets, functional annotation was performed using eggNOG-mapper v2.1.12 (Cantalapiedra et al., 2021) with the Diamond alignment mode against the eggNOG 5.0 database. This analysis assigned Gene Ontology (GO) terms, KEGG pathways, COG categories, and functional descriptions based on orthology relationships, providing insights into the evolutionary conservation and functional roles of the selected proteins across diverse organisms. The annotations confirmed that the selected proteins are involved in critical biological processes such as host cell invasion, nutrient acquisition, and membrane biogenesis, validating their suitability as vaccine targets that are essential for parasite survival and pathogenesis.

Chimeric multi-antigen fusion vaccine design

For vaccine construction, antigenic fragments from six selected membrane protein families were identified using the Protean tool of the Lasergene v7.0 (Burland, 2000) with the Jameson-Wolf antigenic index method to predict linear B-cell epitopes. Multiple sequence alignments were performed using Jalview v2.11.5.0 (Waterhouse et al., 2009) with the Clustal algorithm to assess conservation across all five Eimeria species. Fragments were selected based on high local antigenicity, sequence conservation (>80 % identity), surface exposure, and appropriate length (30–130 amino acids).

The chimeric multi-antigen fusion vaccine "EimeriaBig" was constructed by linking six antigenic fragments with EAAAK pentapeptide linkers. This linker was chosen to maintain structural independence of each fragment while preventing junctional neoepitopes. Three-dimensional structure prediction was performed using AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024) to evaluate the overall conformation and spatial organization of the vaccine construct.

Expression and purification of rEimeriaBig

The plasmids pET-30a(+)-EimeriaBig and pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig were synthesized by Changzhou Jiyu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and insert sequences were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Guangzhou IGE Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). For recombinant protein production, pET-30a(+)-EimeriaBig was first amplified in TOP10 cells and then transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) for expression. rEimeriaBig was purified with a His-tag purification kit (cat. no. P2229S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The purified protein was refolded by stepwise dialysis in buffers containing 6, 4, 1, and 0 mol/L urea. The final yield of purified rEimeriaBig was approximately 10 mg/L bacterial culture.

Transfection and western blot analysis of the pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig in vitro

The plasmid pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig was transfected into DF-1 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (cat. no. L3000015, Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer protocol. Whole-cell protein was extracted with cell lysis buffer (cat. no. P0013, Beyotime, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5 % non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 hour, and then incubated with His-tag mouse monoclonal antibody (cat. no. 66005, Proteintech, USA; 1:3000). After three washes with PBST (PBS with 0.05 % Tween 20), the membrane was incubated with goat anti-mouse IgG HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (cat. no. CW0102S, Cowin Biotech, China; 1:6000) for 1 hour at room temperature. Signal was developed with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; cat. no. 32209, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and imaged with an Amersham Imager 600 (GE HealthCare, USA).

Preparation of chitosan-calcium phosphate-plasmid DNA nanoparticles

The preparation technology of Chitosan-calcium phosphate-pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig nanoparticles has been modified in the process with reference (Pedraza et al., 2008; Qiu et al., 2022; Rabiee et al., 2022; Sokolova et al., 2006). Briefly, 15.6 mM sodium citrate, 12.5 mmol/L sodium hydrogen phosphate and 12.5 mmol/L calcium chloride were stirred at 25 °C for two days, followed by ultrasonication at 200 W for 30 mins. Subsequently, the pH value was adjusted greater than the isoelectric point (pI) of the plasmid. The complex was centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 1 min and collected the precipitate and combined with plasmid at 75 rpm in 4 °C. In the next step, a mixed solution was prepared by adding 10 mg of chitosan powder into 10 mL 1 % acetic acid and stirred at 4 °C for half a day. The tripolyphosphate was added into the mixed solution and then added the plasmid mixture to prepare the c-CapNps-pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig.

Immunization and challenge

The chickens were separated into nine groups with 33 chickens in each group, including five vaccination groups (G1-G5) and four control groups (G6-G9). All groups were immunized at 3, 10, and 17 days of age, with one injection per time point and only one substance per injection. The recombinant protein rEimeriaBig was adjuvanted with oil adjuvant (chalina biotech co., Ltd, Guangzhou, China) and pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig with chitosan-calcium phosphate. The immunization schedule is illustrated in Fig. 3. Briefly, G1 (3× DNA) received 50 μg pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig at each of three immunizations; G2 (3× protein) received 50 μg rEimeriaBig at each of three immunizations; G3 (DNA-2× protein) received the 1st immunization with 50 μg pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig and the 2nd and 3rd immunizations each with 50 μg rEimeriaBig; G4 (2× DNA-protein) received the 1st and 2nd immunizations each with 50 μg pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig and the 3rd immunization with 50 μg rEimeriaBig; G5 (protein-2× DNA) received the 1st immunization with 50 μg rEimeriaBig and the 2nd and 3rd immunizations each with 50 μg pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig; G6 (3× pcDNA3.1(+)) received 50 μg pcDNA3.1(+) at each immunization; G7 (3× oil adjuvant) received 50 μg oil adjuvant at each immunization; G8 (challenged control) and G9 (unchallenged control) received 100 μL PBS at each immunization. In particular, rEimeriaBig was administered via cervical subcutaneous injection and pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig was administered by an intramuscular injection into the leg. At 1 week of each post-vaccination, 3 chickens per group were sacrificed and spleen were harvested for cytokines assays. The remaining 24 chickens in all groups except the unchallenged group were challenged by oral gavage of 5,000 sporulated E.necatrix oocysts on day 7 post-third immunization.

Determination of cytokine transcription by quantitative real-time PCR

Spleens of three chickens in each group were collected after 7 days post-immunization. Total RNA extracted from the spleen samples were reversed transcript into cDNA PrimeScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (cat. no. 6210A, Takara, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was employed to determine interleukin 2 (IL-2), IL-10 and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) (Gaghan et al., 2022; Kaiser et al., 2003) mRNA levels. The PCR reaction mixture contained 5 μL Takara Premix Taq (cat. no. R004, Takara, Japan), 1 μL of each primer, 1 μL template cDNA (200 μg/μL), and 2 μL deionized water. The PCR reaction was performed under the following condition: 95 °C for 3 min; followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 seconds, 60 °C for 30 seconds and followed by a melting curve program at 95 °C for 10 seconds, increment of 0.5°C from 65 °C to 95 °C for 5 seconds using real-time PCR detection system (CFX96, Bio-Rad, California, USA). The chicken actin gene was used as an internal control. The qPCR primers are listed in Table 1. Each reaction was measured by triplicate technical replicate. The relative quantification of cytokine gene mRNA was determined by the 2−△△Ct method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Determination of IgY serum antibody level by indirect ELISA

Blood samples of three chickens from each group were collected by cardiac puncture after 7 days post-immunization. Serum was collected for determining antigen specific IgY antibody levels by indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using multimode microplate reader (Varioskan™ LUX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). In brief, 100 μL rEimeriaBig (2 μg/ml) was coated in flat-bottomed 96-well plates with 0.05 mol/L carbonate buffer per well overnight at 4 °C. After five times washes with PBS-Tween 0.05 %, the plates were blocked with 300 μL 1 % BSA (Bovine serum albumin) for 1 h at 37 °C. After another five washes with PBST, 100 μL serum samples diluted 1:1000 were added to the plate to incubate at 37 °C for 1 h. Serum from unchallenged chickens was set as the negative control. The plate was incubated with 100 μL/well of HRP-conjugated anti-chicken IgY antibody (cat.no, SAB3700189, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany, dilutions 1:2000) at 37 °C for 1 h after five times washes. Colour development was conducted with 100 μL tetramethylbenzidine, TMB (cat. no. 002023, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) per well in the dark at 37 °C for 10 min and observed by a spectrophotometric method at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Evaluation of protective efficacy

The protective efficacy of rEimeriaBig and pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig was evaluated based on survival rate, reduction in lesion scores (RLS), relative oocyst production (ROP), and the anticoccidial index (ACI). The survival rate was determined as the percentage of surviving chickens relative to the initial number in each group. Lesion scores were assessed using the method established by Johnson and Reid (Johnson and Reid, 1970). The RLS was calculated as the percentage reduction in the mean lesion score of the immunized group compared to the challenged control group. Oocyst output, expressed as oocysts per gram (OPG) of feces, was quantified using the McMaster technique (Daş et al., 2020; Scare et al., 2017). Subsequently, ROP was determined by calculating the ratio of the mean oocyst output in the immunized group to that of the challenged control group. Finally, the ACI was computed as a composite index integrating survival rate, relative body weight gain, lesion scores, and oocyst value, following the standard formula described previously (Mcmanus et al., 1968).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD for protective efficacy endpoints and mean ± SEM for qPCR and ELISA endpoints. Normality and homogeneity were checked before parametric testing. One-way ANOVA followed by LSD post hoc test was used for the primary analysis. For immune endpoints with n = 3 per group, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn post hoc correction was run as sensitivity analysis. Analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Proteome dataset and host homology filtering

As shown in Figure S1, candidate antigens were selected through sequential filtering steps before experimental validation. The initial dataset comprised 38,838 proteins from five Eimeria species: E. necatrix (UP000018050, 6,867 proteins), E. tenella (UP000030747, 8,595 proteins), E. maxima (UP000030750, 8,711 proteins), E. brunetti (UP000030754, 8,608 proteins), and E. acervulina (UP000030763, 6,057 proteins). To eliminate potential autoimmune responses, BLASTp analysis was performed against the chicken host proteome. Host homology filtering removed 7,168 proteins showing significant similarity to chicken proteins (identity ≥ 35 % and E-value ≤ 0.005), resulting in 31,670 non-host proteins (Table 2). The filtering rates varied among species: E. necatrix (20.6 % removed), E. tenella (19.4 % removed), E. maxima (14.5 % removed), E. brunetti (19.2 % removed), and E. acervulina (19.3 % removed), with an overall filtering rate of 18.5 %. This conservative filtering ensures that selected antigens are parasite-specific and unlikely to trigger autoimmune reactions.

Subcellular localization and membrane protein identification

Complete protein sequences (starting with methionine, no ambiguous residues) from the non-host dataset were subjected to subcellular localization prediction. After filtering for sequence completeness, 36,299 proteins were analyzed using a multi-tool consensus approach combining SignalP 5.0, TMHMM 2.0, DeepLoc 1.0, and PredGPI 1.0. Membrane protein identification revealed 7,420 proteins (20.4 % of complete sequences) meeting the relaxed criteria (Table 3). The distribution of membrane proteins across species was: E. necatrix (1,185 proteins, 18.8 % of complete sequences), E. tenella (1,416 proteins, 18.4 %), E. maxima (1,885 proteins, 22.1 %), E. brunetti (1,923 proteins, 23.9 %), and E. acervulina (1,011 proteins, 17.6 %). The variation in membrane protein proportions across species may reflect differences in genome annotation quality and biological characteristics of each species. This diverse set of membrane proteins, identified through a multi-tool consensus approach, represents potential vaccine targets that are accessible to the host immune system during parasite invasion and intracellular development.

Conservation and antigenicity analysis

OrthoFinder analysis of the 7,420 membrane proteins identified orthogroups across the five species, of which 278 orthogroups were present in all five Eimeria species, representing highly conserved membrane proteins. These conserved orthogroups contained 1,736 individual proteins (average 6.2 proteins per orthogroup) (Table S1), indicating that most orthogroups have single-copy orthologs in each species, though some showed species-specific duplications. The relatively small number of conserved orthogroups (278 out of total orthogroups) reflects the stringent criterion requiring 100 % presence across all five species, ensuring that selected antigens are truly pan-Eimeria targets suitable for broad-spectrum vaccine development.

All 1,736 conserved membrane proteins were subsequently evaluated for antigenicity using VaxiJen 2.0 with the parasite model. VaxiJen scores ranged from 0.271 to 1.089 (Table S1), with a mean score of 0.612 and a median of 0.604 (Fig. 1A). Using the threshold of ≥0.4 for antigenicity classification, 1,691 proteins (97.4 %) were classified as antigenic, while only 45 proteins (2.6 %) scored below the threshold (Fig. 1B). Applying the stringent criterion that all members of an orthogroup must be antigenic (VaxiJen ≥0.4), 254 orthogroups (91.4 % of conserved orthogroups) were retained as antigenic candidates, while the remaining 24 orthogroups containing at least one non-antigenic protein were excluded. This high proportion of antigenic orthogroups indicates that conserved membrane proteins in Eimeria species possess strong inherent antigenicity, likely due to their surface exposure and critical roles in host-parasite interactions. The high mean VaxiJen score (0.612) and the fact that 97.4 % of individual proteins exceeded the antigenicity threshold further support the suitability of these proteins as vaccine targets.

Functional annotation and vaccine antigen selection

Functional annotation using eggNOG-mapper was performed on all 1,691 antigenic proteins from the 254 antigenic orthogroups. Of these, 1,021 proteins (60.4 %) obtained functional annotations through homology matching in the eggNOG database, while 670 proteins (39.6 %) showed no significant matches and remained unannotated (Table S2). Among the annotated proteins, functional classification based on COG (Clusters of Orthologous Groups) categories and functional descriptions revealed several major functional groups. Membrane transporters represented the largest annotated category (151 proteins, 14.8 % of annotated proteins), including major facilitator superfamily (MFS) transporters, ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, and various ion channels involved in nutrient acquisition and drug resistance. Metabolic enzymes comprised 116 proteins (11.4 %), including carbonic anhydrases, kinases, phosphatases, and oxidoreductases essential for parasite metabolism and energy production. Protein trafficking components (20 proteins, 2.0 %) included CDC50 family proteins, vesicle trafficking machinery, and secretory pathway proteins critical for invasion and egress. Lipid metabolism-related proteins (17 proteins, 1.7 %) included lipid transfer proteins, phospholipases, and enzymes involved in membrane biogenesis. Signal transduction proteins (11 proteins, 1.1 %) such as protein kinases and calcium-binding proteins regulate parasite responses to environmental stimuli. Notably, 706 annotated proteins (69.1 % of annotated proteins) were classified as functionally uncharacterized despite having database matches. Combined with the 670 unannotated proteins, a total of 1,376 proteins (81.4 % of all antigenic proteins) lack clear functional characterization, representing a substantial reservoir of novel potential targets that warrant further investigation.

Based on the eggNOG functional annotations, six membrane protein families were identified and selected for vaccine construction: carbonic anhydrase (CA, OG0000215), mechanosensitive ion channel (MSC, OG0000083), cell division control protein 50 (CDC50, OG0000224), major facilitator superfamily transporter (MFS, OG0000176), lipid transfer protein (LTP, OG0000099) and ATP-binding cassette transporter (ABC, OG0000124). These families were selected based on: (1) clear functional annotation from eggNOG database, (2) high conservation across all five species, (3) strong antigenicity scores (VaxiJen > 0.6), (4) critical biological functions essential for parasite survival, and (5) surface exposure or membrane association making them accessible to host immune responses. The selected protein families represent diverse functional categories involved in pH regulation and pyrimidine biosynthesis (CA), osmoregulation (MSC), host cell invasion and egress (CDC50), nutrient transport (MFS), substrate translocation and drug resistance (ABC), and membrane biogenesis (LTP), providing comprehensive coverage of critical parasite processes and maximizing the potential for cross-protective immunity.

Chimeric multi-antigen fusion vaccine construction

The EimeriaBig chimeric multi-antigen fusion vaccine was constructed by linking six antigenic fragments with EAAAK pentapeptide linkers (Fig. 2A). The selected fragments and their positions were: CA (U6L2×1, residues 151-241 in original protein, 91 aa in vaccine construct at positions 1-91), MSC (U6N7Q7, residues 1195-1317, 123 aa at positions 96-219), CDC50 (U6MU08, residues 148-181, 34 aa at positions 224-257), MFS (U6MRG1, residues 68-101, 34 aa at positions 262-295), ABC (U6MY19, residues 1066-1116, 51 aa at positions 300-350), and LTP (U6N1V4, residues 716-746, 31 aa at positions 355-385). The final vaccine construct comprised 391 amino acids with a predicted molecular weight of 43.86 kDa. The linear sequence organization of EimeriaBig shows clear demarcation of the six antigen fragments connected by five EAAAK linkers (Figure S2). Each fragment maintains its predicted antigenic properties while being integrated into a single polypeptide chain. The positional mapping demonstrates that fragments were strategically selected from surface-exposed or extracellular regions of their respective parent proteins, avoiding extensive transmembrane domains that could interfere with expression or folding (Figure S3).

Construction and expression analysis of recombinant EimeriaBig

To investigate the function of EimeriaBig, eukaryotic and prokaryotic expression vectors were constructed. The coding sequence was cloned into pcDNA3.1(+) and pET30a(+) (codon-optimized), respectively. The schematic maps of the resulting recombinant plasmids, pET30a(+)-EimeriaBig and pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig, are shown in Figure S4. The expression of the recombinant protein was analyzed in both systems. In the prokaryotic system, SDS-PAGE analysis indicated that rEimeriaBig was expressed primarily in the form of inclusion bodies (Figure S5A, red arrows). Western blot analysis using an anti-His tag antibody confirmed the presence of a specific band at approximately 44.8 kDa (Figure S5B). The recombinant protein was subsequently purified and renatured, showing high purity on SDS-PAGE (Figure S5C). In the eukaryotic system, DF-1 cells were transfected with the pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig plasmid. Western blot analysis detected a specific protein band at approximately 40 kDa in the transfected cell lysates (Figure S5D, Lane 3). In contrast, no specific signal was observed in cells transfected with the empty vector or in the blank control cells (Figure S5D, Lanes 1–2), confirming the successful expression and specificity of the EimeriaBig protein in vitro.

Protective efficacy of pcDNA3.1(+)-EimeriaBig and rEimeriaBig against E. necatrix

The protective efficacy of different immunization strategies was evaluated following the schedule outlined in Fig. 3. Following the challenge with sporulated E. necatrix oocysts on Day 24, chickens in the challenged groups displayed clinical symptoms such as reduced feed intake and lethargy. Eight days post-challenge, varying degrees of bloody feces were observed in the challenged groups. No mortality occurred during the experiment, resulting in a 100 % survival rate across all groups.

Among the tested regimens, G5 (protein-2×DNA) showed the highest protection. Relative body weight gain in G5 reached 93.12 %, although differences in body weight gain among groups were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). G5 had lower lesion score and oocyst output than G8 (P < 0.05), with a reduction in lesion score of 47.64 %, a reduction in oocyst output of 49.75 %, and an anticoccidial index of 168.72 (Table 4). Other immunized groups did not show significant differences from G8 for lesion score or oocyst output.

Discussion

Eimeria spp. remain a primary cause of intestinal pathology and economic loss in poultry production (Qu et al., 2022). While traditional live vaccines have been successful, the development of recombinant subunit and DNA vaccines offers a safer, more stable, and scalable alternative. In this study, we utilized immunoinformatics to construct "EimeriaBig" a chimeric multi-antigen fusion vaccine targeting six conserved membrane protein families. Our results showed that a specific heterologous prime-boost regimen (Protein-DNA-DNA) incorporating EimeriaBig provided moderate but statistically significant protection against E. necatrix, characterized by reduced lesion scores and oocyst shedding. These findings provide a strong proof-of-concept for rational antigen selection plus regimen optimization in next-generation vaccine development.

The selection of appropriate antigens is the cornerstone of vaccine efficacy. Unlike cytoplasmic proteins, membrane proteins are directly exposed to the host immune system and are often functionally essential for parasite survival. The six protein families selected in this study (CA, MSC, CDC50, MFS, ABC, and LTP) were prioritized using a combined workflow of conservation, membrane association/predicted accessibility, antigenicity, and functional annotation, and play key roles in parasite physiology. For instance, CDC50 and MFS transporters are integral to the apical organelles, governing the secretion of invasion factors and gliding motility (Patel et al., 2022; Qu et al., 2022). ABC transporters regulate xenobiotic efflux and stress responses (Sauvage et al., 2006), while CA is critical for maintaining intracellular pH homeostasis (Krungkrai and Supuran, 2008). By targeting these functionally essential regions, the EimeriaBig vaccine was designed to enhance immune recognition and may partially interfere with transporter-associated functions. Antibodies induced by vaccination may sterically hinder the function of these proteins—blocking nutrient uptake or preventing the conformational changes required for invasion—a mechanism akin to "functional blockade" observed in effective malaria vaccines (Kisalu et al., 2018; Tan et al., 2018). Beyond target selection, the effective presentation of these antigens is equally critical. Therefore, the structural integrity of the chimeric fusion construct was maintained by the use of rigid EAAAK linkers. These linkers effectively separate the functional domains, which may reduce inter-domain interference and support optimal antigen presentation (Meza et al., 2017; Shams et al., 2023). However, direct family-by-family immunogenicity and protective validation are still required in the future.

A pivotal finding of our study is the superior efficacy of the G5 (protein-2× DNA) immunization strategy. This heterologous regimen showed the highest protection among tested regimens, suggesting a synergistic interplay between the two vaccine platforms. We propose that the initial Protein priming (rEimeriaBig) provided a high concentration of exogenous antigen, which was efficiently captured by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) to stimulate a robust B-cell memory and high-titer IgY production early in the immunization schedule. This established a "humoral barrier" capable of neutralizing extracellular sporozoites (Ding et al., 2012; Kang et al., 2000). However, antibodies alone are often insufficient against established intracellular stages of Eimeria. The subsequent DNA boosters may have played a dual role in enhancing protection. First, DNA vaccines facilitate endogenous antigen synthesis, favoring cytosolic processing and presentation via the MHC-I pathway, which is essential for activating CD8+ Cytotoxic T Lymphocytes (CTLs) to clear infected cells. Second, the plasmid DNA backbone, containing unmethylated CpG motifs, likely acted as an innate immune stimulant through CpG-sensing pathways (Cornelie et al., 2004). This intrinsic adjuvant effect may reshape the immune microenvironment, steering the pre-existing immunity toward a Th1-biased phenotype. Thus, the G5 (protein-2× DNA) strategy likely exploited the strengths of both platforms: the protein prime maximized antibody magnitude, while the DNA boosts broadened the effector function towards cellular immunity. Nevertheless, at present, this mechanism should be considered a plausible interpretation rather than definitive proof.

The immune mechanisms underlying protection against coccidiosis involve a complex balance between humoral and cellular responses. In our study, specific IgY levels were significantly elevated in all rEimeriaBig-immunized groups. While IgY antibodies can inhibit the attachment of sporozoites to host cells and limit parasite dissemination (Fachado et al., 2003; Pagheh et al., 2019), our data showed that high antibody titers alone (as seen in G2, Protein-only group) did not correlate with significant protection. This aligns with the consensus that Cell-Mediated Immunity (CMI) is the primary effector arm against intracellular Eimeria (Lillehoj and Trout, 1996). Consequently, we analyzed the cytokine profiles in the spleen. The significant upregulation of IL-2 and IFN-γ in the G5 (protein-2× DNA) group indicates the successful activation of a Th1-dominant response. IL-2 is crucial for T-cell proliferation, while IFN-γ acts as a potent macrophage activator, inducing the production of nitric oxide (NO) to inhibit intracellular parasite replication (Heriveau et al., 2000; Lillehoj et al., 2004). Therefore, our data support the view that cellular immune activation is a major contributor to the observed protection.

Interestingly, alongside Th1 cytokines, we observed a significant upregulation of IL-10 in the G5 (protein-2× DNA) group after the third immunization. IL-10 is a pleiotropic regulatory cytokine that dampens excessive inflammation (Couper et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2024). In the context of coccidiosis, severe intestinal pathology is often driven by an uncontrolled inflammatory response rather than the parasite itself. Therefore, the concurrent induction of pro-inflammatory (IFN-γ) and anti-inflammatory (IL-10) cytokines in the G5 (protein-2× DNA) may reflect a "balanced immune response." The vaccine induced sufficient IFN-γ to suppress parasite replication while simultaneously triggering IL-10 to limit collateral damage to the gut mucosa (Bremner et al., 2021; Couper et al., 2008). This interpretation is consistent with the lower lesion scores observed in challenged G5 chickens.

The protection conferred by EimeriaBig was moderate (ACI ∼168) compared to the near-complete protection often seen with live vaccines. This may be attributed to the lack of strong mucosal immunity (IgA) stimulation via the intramuscular/subcutaneous routes used in this study. The gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) is the first line of defense against enteric pathogens. Future studies should explore mucosal delivery systems, such as Lactobacillus vectors or chitosan-coated oral nanoparticles, to effectively stimulate local IgA responses and intercept sporozoites at the site of entry. Additionally, further investigation into the specific T-cell subsets (e.g., CD4+ vs. CD8+ memory cells) would provide deeper insights into the longevity of the induced protection. While the current three-dose injectable regimen successfully provided a proof-of-concept for the efficacy of EimeriaBig, translating these promising results into field applications will require schedule simplification or adaptation for mass-administration methods suitable for the poultry industry.

In summary, we successfully constructed a novel chimeric multi-antigen fusion vaccine, EimeriaBig, targeting six conserved membrane transporters identified through reverse vaccinology. Our study provides evidence supporting that a heterologous prime-boost strategy (Protein prime/DNA boost) elicits a combined humoral and cellular immune profile, characterized by high IgY titers and the co-expression of IFN-γ and IL-10. This regimen significantly alleviated clinical symptoms and reduced parasite shedding in chickens challenged with E. necatrix. These findings validate conserved membrane proteins as viable vaccine targets and underscore the importance of optimizing immunization regimens to achieve synergistic protective immunity against avian coccidiosis. While the induced protection was partial, these results establish a strong foundation. Future studies involving cross-species challenge models and broader independent cohorts will be essential to fully evaluate the cross-protective potential of this promising vaccine candidate.
