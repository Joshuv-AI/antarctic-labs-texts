Comparison of academic performance of medical students previously and non-previously graduated from other college courses in a PBL hybrid curriculum
Authors: Lucélio Bernardes Couto, Marina Toledo de Durand, Cassiano Merussi Neiva, Ana Cláudia Silva Reis, Tufik José Magalhães Geleilete, Gustavo Salata Romão, Marcelo Engracia Garcia, Helen Figueiredo Fumagalli, Renata Dellalibera-Joviliano, Milton Faria Jr., Reinaldo Bulgarelli Bestetti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe admission of graduate students (GS) into medical courses is seen as a mechanism that allows students to have better academic performance during the medical course and to become more competent professionals. For these reasons, specific programs for this purpose agree that GS may have better academic performance in medical courses than nongraduate students (NGS). This study aimed to assess whether graduate-entry students (GS) outperformed admission test (AT) and transfer (Tr) students in both the pre-clinical and clinical phases of an undergraduate medical program that adopts a hybrid Problem-Based Learning (PBL) methodology.MethodsA retrospective longitudinal cohort study was carried out, collecting and analysing non-academic data upon entry into the course, as well as academic and non-academic data during the course, from medical students enrolled at the time of the study (2016–2023). We analysed the data relating to the students who, at the beginning of the course, were previously graduates (GS) or nongraduates (NGS), which were composed of students submitted to the admission test (AT) and transferred (Tr). Continuous variables were presented as the median, the interquartile ranges (IR) was used to calculate the measure of dispersion of the data and Cohen’s d was used to estimate effect size. Differences were evaluated using the Kruskal‒Wallis test and post hoc multiple comparisons were carried out with Dunn’s test. The Bonferroni correction was used to control for Type I error across the three pairwise comparisons, in most of the data presented, setting a revised significance threshold of α = 0.0167 for each test (0.05 / 3). Thus, only p-values below 0.0167 were deemed statistically significant.ResultsGS students performed better than AT and Tr students in terms of overall grades, in the Tutorial sessions, in special in attitudinal performance, and on areas of Clinical skills, Primary care and Clerkship, better than Tr. In Clinical skills and Clerkship, AT performed better than Tr. In the progress test (PT), GS performed better than all the other groups and AT performed better than Tr also in the OSCE. In the GS group, male and female students performed similarly, and both groups performed better than male and female students in all the other groups (except for the both gender of the GS group that performed similarly to both gender of the AT group). GS students had a greater median age than did all the other students. On the other hand, Tr students presented the lowest scores for all analysed parameters and presented, except for the AT in the Primary care and Attitudinal, for GS in the OSCE. Tr presented a greater median age than did the AT group.ConclusionThis study suggests that there are advantages of adapting programs to integrate GS students into medical courses that adopt the hybrid PBL model before the clerkship.

Background

Recognizing the qualities that facilitate the development of proficient medical practitioners is fundamental to shaping medical education curricula. To achieve this aim, educational Institutions worldwide have been striving to ensure that candidates with greater technical and personal qualifications enter different medical course curricular programmes [1, 2].

Individuals with higher education degrees seem to meet this demand. By leveraging the GS students’ prior knowledge in basic sciences, the duration of the medical program for these students is usually shorter (4 years versus 5–6 years). This creates the perception that medical schools train professionals more quickly and help address the shortage of medical personnel in many countries [3]. However, this reasoning overlooks the years of prior education these students completed in their previous undergraduate programs. Furthermore, the entry of GS is seen as a mechanism to increase the variety of the students’ personal and professional background in terms of life experience and socioeconomic status [4, 5].

Although literature data show that GS students perform better than NGS [3, 6–10], it has been shown that GS perform equally or even worse than NGS at some point in the medical course [11–17]. These divergences lead to a lack of agreement on the real advantages of GS inclusion programs in medical courses.

Adult learning theory assumes that students who have completed a previous undergraduate course potentially have greater motivation and self-direction, providing, especially, positive results for learning skills in subsequent medical courses [18, 19]. These characteristics can even increase the motivation of professors in clinical areas to teach as a result of contact with graduates with these qualifications [20–22]. In this respect, support and enthusiasm from teachers have previously been identified as a major influence on students’ acquisition of clinical skills [23].

These characteristics are confirmed by McCRORIE [24], who reports that GS are “highly motivated and committed”, in addition to being “much more self-directed, challenging, demanding and questioning”. Furthermore, WILKINSON et al. [25] suggested that maturity is related to a high degree of certainty and motivation in relation to career choice, which is not observed, in the same way, for other students. Thus, for the set of reasons presented, it is assumed that GS may have better academic performance in medical courses than NGS.

There is inconsistency regarding the real value of programs for inserting GS into medical courses [11, 26]. It should be attributed, in part, to the large variation between studies in relation to performance measures to compare GS and NGS performed in the evaluated medical courses [3]. In this respect, several works use aggregated performance measures without differentiating them between basic sciences and clinical skills [12, 27, 28].

Furthermore, the difference between medical programmes at the different universities where the studies were carried out must also be considered, especially the differences between the phases of the levelling programmes offered to graduate students [8, 9].

The discrepancy in sample sizes may also be a factor that leads to variability in the results observed, given that, due to the relatively new insertion programs for graduate students in medical courses, most studies comparing performance between graduates and nongraduates involve a greater number of nongraduate students [3, 13, 14].

The medicine course at the University of Ribeirão Preto adopted the hybrid PBL methodology. This active methodology proposes that teaching be centred on the student, and to this end, it is considered desirable that the student has a level of personal maturity capable of providing greater motivation for individual study. Furthermore, it is also desirable for the student to have easy relationships with their peers and promote better time management to meet the demands of the course. These characteristics were already noted when the methodology was created in the medicine course at McMaster University, Canada [29].

We believe that the PBL methodology aligns well with the characteristics commonly observed in graduate-entry students (GS). For instance, GS are generally considered to possess more advanced learning skills than non-graduate students (NGS) [12]. In this regard, McCrorie [24] highlights that GS tend to demonstrate greater personal maturity, which enables them to be more self-motivated and effective in independent study. Furthermore, they often exhibit better interpersonal skills, which is particularly advantageous in medical education contexts where early clinical exposure — as promoted by the PBL approach — is emphasized.

The course also includes a program that reserves places for students who have already graduated from health-related fields, as well as for students transferring from other medical schools.

Data regarding the characteristics of GS in relation to NGS in medical courses in Brazil are limited. The analysis of these characteristics seems to be restricted to the motivations, difficulties and expectations that such students demonstrate during their medical course [30].

Given that most studies comparing students with and without a science background do not distinguish between basic science knowledge and clinical skills, the present study sought to specifically examine whether GS demonstrated superior academic performance compared to AT and Tr students in the pre-clinical and clinical components of the medical program at University of Ribeirão Preto (Unaerp).

Methods

The medical course

The Unaerp medicine course is developed in the city of Ribeirão Preto, Brazil, and adopts a hybrid teaching methodology using the Problem-Based Learning (PBL) in the pre-clerkship period (1st to 8th semester). This stage comprises the different areas of knowledge: Tutorial sessions, Medical Skills, Primary Care and Extension Practice.

The Tutorial discipline runs learning modules, in which the study topics are covered in an integrated and contextualised manner. Each module is developed with problems analysed by students in two tutorial sessions (first analysis phase and, subsequently, report phase), following the model proposed by Schmidt [31]. In each studied problem, students are assessed on their level of knowledge (before and after the reporting phases) and attitudes (during the analysis and reporting phases). The final grade for this area is made up of these assessments in association with others, formed by a final written test made up of multiple-choices questions and open-ended questions. In this way, the final grade is made up of assessments that evaluate the student’s attitudes and knowledge in this curricular unit. The details of our PBL curriculum have been described elsewhere [32].

The area of Clinical skills comprises a set of disciplines with content associated with the curricular area of Tutorial sessions. In this area, the student develops medical and communication skills in a protected environment, using specialised laboratories and in contact with patients under the direct supervision of specialist teachers.

In the Primary Care curricular unit, small groups of students carry out home visits supervised by teachers to families served by the Family Health Program, developed by the Brazilian Ministry of Health. Positioned within the first four years of the medical program, this unit stands out from the traditional clerkship by being integrated with other concurrently taught curricular components and by adopting an innovative theoretical–practical pedagogical approach. By adopting a variation of the PBL model, cases of medical interest found during visits are recorded and used as study objectives by students. Details of this process are presented elsewhere [33].

Clerkship takes place in the last two years of the course and is characterised by supervised in-service training carried out in caring for patients in wards hospital, outpatient clinics and specialised. Teaching is enriched with bedside discussions, seminars and classes.

The Extension Practice inserts the student into an educational and social practice and it is carried out through supervised academic activity through programs, projects, courses, events and provision of services to the community, linked to the student’s training and included in the academic curriculum. Details of this curriculum have been published elsewhere [34].

The assessment process is as comprehensive as possible and covers knowledge, skills and attitudes. Formative assessment is present in all areas of knowledge of the course through the assessment of attitudes (applied by teachers according to previously standardized criteria) and knowledge (multiple choice questions in the key-feature characteristic format) and open-ended question. The summative assessment is carried out at the end of each curricular area and is also composed of questions in the key characteristics format, in addition to questions in the open format. The OSCE and the PT are administered every semester to students of the course. Attitudinal assessment is carried out in all areas of the course.

The medicine course at the University of Ribeirão Preto offers, every six months, the possibility of entry into its regular program for candidates who are graduating from other national medical education Institutions (Tr - transferred) and graduates from courses in areas related to medicine (GS – graduate students). These two groups of students were admitted to the course to fill vacant places in two different ways during the study period. From the first semester of 2016 to the first semester of 2019, the selection process consisted of two stages. First, all candidates took a multiple-choice test, with 100 tests, covering basic and clinical knowledge relevant to the course. This test was used for ranking purposes, ordering candidates based on their performance. The number of candidates advancing to the second stage was determined by the availability of places for the semester, with three candidates being called per place, according to their ranking in the first stage. The second stage consisted of a simulated tutorial session (‘analysing phase’), where candidates were evaluated by a course tutor experienced in the PBL methodology. After being presented to a problem, candidates were assessed (with grades from zero to ten) on their ability to generate scientifically grounded hypotheses to solve it, presenting them in a concise and clear manner, as well as on their demonstration of positive group interaction skills, such as the ability to give and accept respectful feedback from peers. Candidates were ranked based on the weighted mean obtained in both evaluation stages.

In the second part of the study period (from the second semester of 2019 to the first semester of 2023), the admission of students to fill vacant places, when applicable, did not involve any selection process.

Finally, the candidate’s year of admission into the course was determined after evaluating their prior academic completion at their original institution (if transferring) or the completion of coursework from their previous degree (if they already graduated).

Samples

A longitudinal retrospective cohort study using grades from 1,708 students (53 GS, 1,567 AT and 88 Tr) was conducted using academic data from medical students regularly enrolled in the Unaerp medical course between the first semester of 2016 and the first semester of 2023. Students who dropped out or failed during the study period were included in the analysis. Students admitted before or after these dates were excluded. In the first semester of 2020 no assessments were carried out on the course due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, due to the same reason, the PT and attitudinal assessment were not carried out in the first and second semester of 2020 and the first semester of 2021 and the OSCE was not carried out in the first and second semester of 2020 and 2021. Student grades were provided by the Institutional Academic Records Division, whose members were not involved in the investigation. The data used here were obtained with all student identifiable information removed.

Statistical analysis

Firstly, we investigated the assumption of normal data distribution, using Anderson-Darling, D’Agostino & Pearson, Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality tests, and found that they were violated. So, the statistical analysis involved nonparametric tests. Continuous variables are given as the median (Interquartile Range - IR). To calculate the effect size for comparisons was used Cohen’s effect sizes (d), where a larger value would denote a greater effect size. The size of the effect was classified as small (0.20 to 0.49), medium (0.50 to 0.79) or large (0.8) according to Cohen’s method [35]. Statistical differences were evaluated using the Kruskal‒Wallis test and post hoc multiple comparisons were carried out with Dunn’s test. The Bonferroni correction was used to control for Type I error across the three pairwise comparisons, setting a revised significance threshold of α = 0.0167 for each test (0.05/3). Thus, only p-values below 0.0167 were deemed statistically significant. p -values from the one pairwise comparison shown in Table 6 were adjusted using the Bonferroni method, setting a family-wise significance threshold of α = 0.05 (0.05/1). p -values from the eight pairwise comparisons shown in Table 8. were adjusted using the Bonferroni method, setting a family-wise significance threshold of α = 0.00625 (0.05/8). Only adjusted p-values below these thresholds were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism™ 8.0.1 software.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The students’ marks were supplied by the Division of Academic Registers, whose members did not take part in the investigation. This retrospective study was conducted complying with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki; the marks were provided in an aggregated and fully anonymized format, ensuring the privacy and confidentiality of all individuals. The need for ethical approval and informed consent was waived by the University of Ribeirão Preto (UNAERP) Institutional Review Board (IRB)/Comitê de Ética em Pesquisa (CEP) da Universidade de Ribeirão Preto. This waiver is in accordance with Brazilian National Health Council Resolution No. 510/2016, which exempts retrospective studies using anonymized public domain or institutional administrative data from requiring ethical approval and individual consent.

Results

The results showed that GS students obtained a median of final grades (composed of the set of all grades that make up the subject and that define approval or failure) that was significantly greater [7.7 (6.8–8.5); n = 1,975 grades from 53 students; average of 37.26 grades/student] than the median of the AT [7.5 (6.7–8.4); n = 51,633 grades from 1,567 students; average of 32.95 grades/student; p ≤ 0.0001; d = 0.12] and Tr groups [7.3 (6.5–8.3); n = 3,125 grades from 88 students; average of 35.51 grades/student; p ≤ 0.0001; d = 0.23]. On the other hand, transferred students obtained the lowest medians compared to the grades of students from all the other groups (p ≤ 0.0001; d = 0.22 with GS and p ≤ 0.0001; d = 0.11 with AT). Discussion

The results obtained in the present study showed that GS had better final grades than did AT or Tr. The grades analysis showed that this group presented the best performance, in relation to the other groups, in disciplines from area of the Tutorial sessions. On the Clinical skills, Primary care and Clerkship, GS students performed better than Tr students. GS showed better performance in relation to other groups of students in attitudes (relating grades for participation in tutorial sessions) and knowledge (better performance in PT). The performance in the final grades was similar between the genders in the GS group; however, both of genders in this group performed better than both of genders of Tr group. The students in the GS group had the highest median age at which they entered the course in relation to students in all the other groups.

Several factors may be related to the differences observed in the obtained results. Factors such as prior undergraduate education appear to be related to student performance during the medical course, and some studies have shown varying results. Although studies conducted in medical schools have demonstrated that students previously trained in the humanities performed similarly or even better than those with a health sciences background [36–39], other studies have shown superior outcomes for health sciences graduates [27, 40]. This better performance was observed not only during basic [41] or clinical activities [42] but also throughout the entire medical course [43].

According to the rules from our Institution, only candidates who have completed a higher education degree in the health field are accepted for vacant places in the medical course at our Institution. This requirement ensures the selection of individuals with combined theoretical and clinical experience before undergoing the selection process.

The analysis of the groups’ grades in the different disciplines from the different areas of the course showed a better performance of the GS group in relation to all the other students in the Tutorial sessions and in relation to Tr students in the Clinical skills and Primary care (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Problem-Based Learning (PBL) fosters an environment where students contribute their individual perceptual backgrounds and prior learning experiences to a dynamic and interactive setting [44]. Successful learning within this framework involves an internal shift in students’ consciousness or beliefs, aligned with the adoption of specific activities and strategies necessary to achieve learning objectives. This process corresponds to the principle of self-directed learning (SDL), as described by Newman (2004) [45].

Although not conclusive, there is evidence that supports the proposal that PBL encourages SDL as students’ progress in their studies [46]. Students who use the PBL methodology exercise SDL skills more intensely and contribute more actively to group learning, in addition to using more resources than students in traditional teaching programs [47, 48]. Consequently, students are encouraged to take responsibility for their own learning [49]. In this sense, the results of the present study corroborate previous data showing that GS from medical courses that use the PBL methodology efficiently use SDL strategies [24, 50–52].

The characteristic of the Tutorial sessions, Clinical skills and Primary care running in the first two-thirds of the course suggested that the students in the GS group performed better in preclinical phase of the course (Table 3). Although some studies have shown that GS performed worse [14, 53] or similar [6] to NGS in the initial and intermediate phases of the medical course, other studies have shown that GS performs significantly better than NGS in these stage of medical course [3, 14, 15].

Several studies have shown that GS performed better than NGS in the clinical phase of the medical course [3, 8, 9, 15], which was not confirmed in the present study, in which the results between the GS and AT groups were similar, but different from the Tr group, which had the lowest performance among the three groups evaluated. These results support previous studies [6, 13, 14, 53], which also did not identify an improvement in the performance of the GS group in relation to the NGS.

A possible explanation for these results would be that some characteristics present in these students, such as professional experience carried out in periods prior to entering the medical course, maturity and SDL, could favor them for a better performance in the first phases of the course. In the final phases, these characteristics would also be achieved by the other students, which would mean that this differentiation would be absent and the performance among the students would become similar. Thus, according to Shehmar [6], the advantages obtained in longer courses by GS may diminish over time, becoming irrelevant in their final phase. Furthermore, the results obtained suggest that the understanding and mastery of fundamental sciences are not sufficiently comprehensive to facilitate the application of basic science to clinical scenarios and that any advantage obtained by GS is limited to the preclinical phase of the course.

However, the data from the present study would not allow us to disregard the better clinical performance of students in the GS group since this group showed better performance in the PT (Table 5; Fig. 4), applied to students in the preclinical and clinical stages of the course. However, despite the higher scores achieved by GS students in the PT throughout the program, including during the clerkship, it is assumed that their overall superior performance—both in these assessments and in final course grades—may have been influenced by results obtained during the pre-clerkship period.

The better performance of GS students in the Tutorial sessions identified in the present study raises interesting considerations. In PBL, groups of students gather in small numbers and discuss aspects related to a problem, guided by a tutor who directs the session, exploring only the students’ prior knowledge and formulating questions that will require further elaboration [54]. After an individual study, the group meets again to discuss the topic. Thus, in this methodology, the aim is for the student to have the ability to structure and relate information and critically evaluate knowledge by adopting a deep learning approach [55]. In our course, the student is evaluated by the tutor in relation to their attitude during the tutorial sessions, with criteria being the ability to generate scientifically based hypotheses and present them clearly to their peers, in addition to demonstrating study and developing a good relationship with other group members. Such characteristics are expected in students with greater academic experience, prior knowledge and a greater ability to communicate with peers. In this respect, students with greater academic experience reported a greater capacity for SDL than did beginning students in medical courses that used the PBL methodology [50]. Perhaps, in our reality, our students from the GS group performed better than the others in this assessment by bringing these specific characteristics (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Clinical skills comprises a set of disciplines conducted in parallel with the Tutorial sessions, aimed at progressively developing students’ clinical competencies throughout the pre-clerkship period and preparing them for the final stages of the medical program. Our data indicate that GS students outperformed than Tr groups in this cluster of courses (Table 2; Fig. 2). Despite previous studies showing the absence of difference between GS and NGS students in clinical skills [13], the better performance of the GS students on this area find support in previous studies [3, 6, 8]. The theoretical knowledge previously acquired in their previous course associated with clinical experience in their profession in the health area allowed these students to present a superior performance in relation to their NGS colleagues during the medical course. These characteristics appear to be related to the finding that GS demonstrate greater confidence, less anxiety and feel less intimidated by activities in the clinical work environment [19]. This set of characteristics, associated with the finding that GS students consider that the use of SDL is an effective opportunity to practice clinical skills [51], improving their performance, especially in the initial phases of the medical course.

Furthermore, the PBL methodology adopts the use of prior knowledge to assimilate and understand new information [31]. It is expected that GS students have a richer framework of knowledge regarding topics related to medical education than their NGS peers, favoring better learning. In addition, the PBL methodology advocates teaching contextualized to medical practice from the initial periods of the course. GS students are health professionals in areas related to the medical universe and with clinical practice before entering the undergraduate medical course. These characteristics enable them to adapt more effectively to the activities and to feel more confident and comfortable in this environment during this phase of the medical course.

On this base, these characteristics also could justify the better performance of students in the GS group in the area of Primary care in relation to Tr students, as observed in the present study. In this aspect, a recent study carried out in the United Kingdom [56] showed a positive correlation between GS students and professional performance in primary care, as a GP doctors, after concluding the medicine course.

A key instrument for evaluating theoretical knowledge in our program is the Progress Test (PT). This comprehensive exam assesses the breadth of knowledge expected of a medical graduate upon completion of the course. The PT is administered simultaneously across all students, regardless of their stage in the curriculum, and is applied longitudinally throughout their medical education to monitor knowledge development over time [57].

This feature means that the PT can, in addition to measuring the extent of the student’s knowledge at a given point in the course, also monitor their progress in the different areas of knowledge that make up the study program. Studies demonstrate that the test reliably identifies an increase in students’ knowledge over time [58, 59]. As it addresses a large amount of information, it discourages students from preparing by studying in a targeted way to obtain good results on the test [60]. Several studies have shown that PT is well accepted by students [61, 62] and that providing detailed feedback to students promotes learning [63, 64]. As it is aimed at completing the course, the construction of new items that, in our course, is made by a multidisciplinary team seeks to explore knowledge rather than memorise the information obtained.

Previous studies have shown the high degree of PT reliability, with a positive correlation between PT performance and formative and OSCE assessments during medical course [65] and during medical residency [66]. This positive correlation between the two assessments supports partially the results obtained in the present study, which showed significantly greater performance of students in the GS group in the PT (Table 5; Fig. 4).

Aiming to assess students’ clinical competence, the OSCE consists of objective tests in which student’s performance is directly analysed in simulation scenarios [67]. The exam has the ability to provide, with great breadth, formative and summative assessment of the student’s level of learning in different specialties and disciplines [68]. Despite the existence of several questions regarding the efficiency of the OSCE as an assessment instrument [69–71], the exam continues to be reliable and valid [72]. Although bibliographical data show that the OSCE explore competence in skills, processes and clinical management in the psychomotor domain, attitudes and behaviour [73], the results obtained in this study did not show better performance of the GS group compared to the other groups. In this regard, students in the AT group performed better than students in the Tr group (Table 6; Fig. 5) in this assessment during the studied period.

The students’ gender also appears to be a factor involved in the academic achievement of medical students with or without previous higher education courses. The results obtained in the present study showed that men performed similarly to women in GS group but better than men in the AT group and both of genders of the Tr group did (Tables 7 and 8; Fig. 6). These results differ from others studies that show that, in general aspects, have shown that women perform better than men in medical course [8, 13, 74]. In addition, men obtained lower scores on final clinical and written exams [75], greater academic dropout and need for exams, additional fees, greater failure in medical course subjects and a lower probability of obtaining honour in a medical degree [76, 77]. However, other studies have shown that men and women have similar results [2] or that men perform better than women do [78] during the medical course.

The fact that men in the GS group did not perform worse than women in the GS group may be related to some factors, such as better adaptation to the conditions of the medical course. In this respect, Atkinson [79] showed that GS women presented higher levels of stress than their NGS male colleagues, which could negatively impact their performance during the medical course. In addition, Sandover et al. [80] showed, in relation to general data from medical students, a significant difference between genders in strategies for coping with stress related to the medical course, with men adopting self-control and women seeking social support and planned problem-solving. Associated with the characteristics of greater age and previous academic and professional experience, the strategy adopted by male students could provide greater efficiency for stress management in these students than in their NGS peers. On the other hand, greater maturity, associated with greater ease of interpersonal relationships, could positively impact this coping, potentially providing support for better learning performance.

In relation to representation, the results obtained in the present study (Tables 7 and 8; Fig. 6) confirm the findings of previous studies showing that women constitute the group with the highest proportion of graduate students [2, 3, 8, 13].

Students in the GS group were also older than were the other groups when entering the course (Table 9; Fig. 7). Considering that this group of students had already completed at least one higher education course in the health area and, in almost all cases, worked as professionals in their respective professions, the increase in age was an expected factor. Furthermore, these results confirm previous data showing that older students with higher education degrees perform better in medical courses [78].

We consider these findings particularly relevant within our context. The relatively low average age of medical students in Brazil may adversely affect their psychological preparedness, potentially hindering their adaptation to the curriculum, as well as their motivation and capacity to interpret integrated and contextualized knowledge—factors that can influence academic performance throughout the course [34]. Nonetheless, these conclusions remain inconclusive, given the existence of divergent perspectives concerning the relationships among age, prior higher education, and academic achievement in medical programs.

In this sense, the influence of age-related emotional factors on GS and NGS students presents conflicting results. Atkinson (2020) [79] showed similarities in the levels of psychological stress among GS students older than NGS students during medical courses in Australia. However, some studies have shown that older students experience significant stress during medical training and do not perform well in a learning environment with a high load of activities [12, 81].

In fact, anxiety and stress can have a positive influence, especially on evaluation, as an excessively relaxed attitude can lead to complacency and lack of effort [82]. However, excessive stress and anxiety can lead to poor performance in the assessment and acquisition of clinical skills, which can lead to deficiencies in clinical performance and possibly in patient care and need to be avoided whenever possible [83].

On the other hand, James et al. (2009) [84] showed that graduate medical students at the University of Nottingham (England) were more conscientious, adopted behaviours with a community vision and appeared more confident and less anxious than their nongraduate colleagues were. The psychological characteristics presented by such students are commonly found in older individuals [85]. In fact, since the beginning of this century, the mean age of university graduates entering English medical schools has been 23 to 24 years, while the mean age of nongraduate students has been 17 to 18 years [86]. Furthermore, despite a previous studies showing no difference between older students in relation to young people with higher education [28, 81], it has been reported that medical students over the age of 21 performed better than those younger than that [78, 87]. Baeten et al. [88] identified student factors related to age, such as previous academic and professional experience, coping strategies, self-direction and habits in the learning process, motivation and pleasure for learning. All these factors are important for a better learning process and may be related to the better performance presented by students in the GS group.

Wilkinson et al. (2004) [25] propose that the better performance of GS students in a medical course is due exclusively to the age at which the students enter the medical course, and not to the fact that they have a previous higher education course. Our data do not seem to corroborate this proposal, since, despite the difference in age at entry between students in the GS and NGS groups, composed of AT and Tr, the results obtained in the present study suggest that age, by itself, is not a factor related to the better performance of GS students. This is because Tr students, who had a higher median age than AT students, scored lower than these in most of the parameters analyzed. These results suggest that age should be associated with the various factors mentioned above, such as previous training, maturity, SDL, in order to provide greater knowledge gain during the medical course. Therefore, the existence of prior academic training on the part of the GS seems to be the definitive factor for this better performance. Likewise, the results obtained confirm the importance of administering admission tests when selecting students with the best performance in the course.

The GS group in this study is composed of health professionals who, in addition to having completed higher education, also, in most cases, worked professionally before enrolling in the medical course at our institution. This is due to the fact that only students who graduated in health-related courses were allowed to participate in the selection process for admission to our course. This characteristic differs from other institutions in different parts of the world, which accept students graduated in other areas of knowledge.

Another strength of the current study is the extensive dataset, encompassing thousands of grades collected and analysed over a seven-year period. Another aspect is that the present study evaluated the performance of GS and NGS students at different moments of the medical course and in different scenarios present in the basic and clinical areas. This study has some limitations. Discrepancies in sample sizes can lead to increased variability in the results. This factor is due to the smaller number of places offered to GS and transferred students at our Institution in relation to other students. This limitation is shared in several other studies previously presented in the literature [3, 13, 14]. Furthermore, due to the volume of data analysed, some academic situations could not be monitored in detail, such as interruption of the study throughout the course or a consecutive sequence of absences from assessments due to absences for various reasons [53], which may increase bias in the results obtained. Likewise, similar limitations can also be observed in previous studies [3, 42].

Finally, during the period of this study, the COVID-19 pandemic occurred, which prevented face-to-face exams, such as PT and OSCE, from being carried out in some semesters. Furthermore, even considering the profound changes in the teaching-learning process around the world resulting from the coronavirus pandemic caused by COVID-19 in recent years, the present study did not seek to identify possible changes in the results before and after the pandemic. However, such a model was also applied in works described in the literature [56, 89, 90].

Although literature data show that GS students perform better than NGS [3, 6–10], it has been shown that GS perform equally or even worse than NGS at some point in the medical course [12–17, 26]. These divergences show a lack of agreement on the real advantages of GS insertion programs in medical courses. Although, the data obtained in the present study allow us to consider the adoption of GS insertion programs in medical courses as advantageous.

Finally, an important limitation of this study involves the interpretation of the significance in the statistical analyses. This study showed that, along with the statistically significant differences between the performance of the different groups, the observed effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were generally modest. This suggests that, although these differences are present and reliable and not due to chance and while prior graduation can confer a measurable advantage, the practical significance of these results within the educational context may be limited. Consequently, while supporting the integration of GS students, these findings should be interpreted with nuance, acknowledging that the absolute benefit, though consistent, may require a more complex interpretation. Future research incorporating qualitative methods or assessments of clinical competence in practice may determine whether these statistical differences translate into meaningful differences in professional performance or long-term career outcomes for students.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that graduate students (GS) outperform both admission test (AT) and transfer students (Tr) in several areas of the medical program. These findings suggest the advantage of integrating students with prior degrees into medical programs that employ a hybrid PBL model. We propose that selection processes incorporating tools and assessments similar to those used in the curriculum, combined with prior training in health-related fields, may contribute to improved performance throughout the medical course. Transfer students—who neither took the entrance exam nor possess previous higher education—performed worse than their peers in most areas. This underscores the importance for medical schools to consider the academic backgrounds of transfer students and to monitor their progress longitudinally. Furthermore, qualitative studies are warranted to more comprehensively explore the experiences of GS in medical curricula adopting PBL methodologies over time.
