Epigenetic aging in anxiety disorders: diagnostic subtype differences and associations with social functioning
Authors: Kazutaka Ohi, Daisuke Fujikane, Yasuhisa Oida, Kentaro Takai, Tatsuhide Ukisu, Maho Nakahara, Hiroki Murakami, Koji Fujita, Ayumi Kuramitsu, Yukimasa Muto, Shunsuke Sugiyama, Kiyoto Kasai, Shinsuke Koike, Kiyoyuki Kitaichi, Toshiki Shioiri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety disorders, including subtypes such as social anxiety disorder (SAD), panic disorder (PD), and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), are among the most common psychiatric disorders. They are characterized by excessive fear, anxiety, and behavioral disturbances, leading to significant impairments in social and occupational functioning. While both accelerated and suppressed epigenetic aging have been reported in various psychiatric disorders, findings in anxiety disorders remain limited. We investigated diagnostic subtype-specific patterns of epigenetic age acceleration in anxiety disorders and examined the influence of social functioning.MethodsDNA methylation (DNAm) was quantified in 266 healthy individuals and 30 individuals with anxiety disorders (11 SAD, 11 PD, and 8 GAD) using Illumina MethylationEPIC arrays. Epigenetic age acceleration was estimated using PCHannumG2013 as the primary clock, with sensitivity analyses conducted using four additional clocks. Social functioning was assessed with the Social Functioning Scale. Meta-analyses incorporating data from previous DNAm-based studies (423 healthy individuals and 278 individuals with anxiety disorders) were performed.ResultsCompared with healthy individuals, individuals with anxiety disorders had suppressed epigenetic age acceleration, particularly in SAD, in our cohort. Lower social functioning, especially impaired independence-competence and reduced prosocial activities, was associated with suppressed epigenetic age acceleration. Meta-analyses revealed suppressed epigenetic aging in patients with SAD and a marginal increase in patients with PD. Sensitivity analyses revealed that the suppression in SAD was consistently detected across clock types, except for DunedinPACE.ConclusionsOur findings suggest possible diagnostic subtype-specific differences in epigenetic age acceleration in anxiety disorders, which may indicate a potential protective role of social avoidance in patients with SAD and the influence of inflammatory processes in patients with PD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04480-6.

Background

Anxiety disorders are among the most prevalent psychiatric disorders, with lifetime prevalence rates of approximately 30% in the general population [1]. Anxiety disorders encompass a range of diagnoses, including social anxiety disorder (SAD), panic disorder (PD), and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), each characterized by excessive fear or anxiety and associated behavioral disturbances. SAD involves intense fear of social situations and judgment by others, leading to considerable avoidance of social interactions. PD is characterized by recurrent unexpected panic attacks—sudden surges of intense fear or discomfort that are often accompanied by pronounced physical symptoms such as palpitations, sweating, trembling, chest pain, or shortness of breath—and persistent concern about future attacks. GAD is characterized by excessive, uncontrollable worry about various aspects of daily life and is often accompanied by milder but chronic physical symptoms such as restlessness, muscle tension, and fatigue. Taken together, these disorders can lead to substantial impairments in social, occupational, and other important areas of functioning [2].

Both genetic and environmental factors contribute to the development of anxiety disorders. Twin and family studies have demonstrated moderate heritability estimates, ranging from 30 to 60% [3, 4]. Recently, a large-scale genome-wide association study of anxiety disorders successfully identified 58 genome-wide significant loci [5]. Environmental exposures, such as childhood adversity, chronic stress, and social isolation, also play crucial roles in shaping vulnerability to anxiety disorders [6, 7]. Recent advances in psychiatric genetics and epigenetics have highlighted the importance of epigenetic mechanisms—particularly DNA methylation (DNAm)—as potential mediators linking genetic predispositions and environmental influences with psychiatric disorders, including their onset, severity, and overall outcomes [8].

Epigenetic clocks, which estimate biological age from DNAm profiles, have emerged as robust biomarkers of aging and age-related health outcomes [9]. Among these, the Hannum clock [10] was specifically developed using blood samples and is widely used in studies investigating links between DNAm age acceleration and physical or mental health. Accelerated DNAm aging has been reported to be associated with various psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia and major depressive disorder [11, 12]. In contrast, some studies have reported suppressed or decelerated DNAm aging in certain conditions, such as schizophrenia, in specific cohorts [13] and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), in relation to symptom severity [14]. These findings highlight that epigenetic aging patterns may differ across psychiatric disorders and clinical contexts. However, studies on anxiety disorders have been limited, and no significant difference in SAD has been reported [15], including in a study based on the GSE164056 dataset [16].

Given that profound social impairments, such as social avoidance, are characteristic of anxiety disorders [17–19], it is plausible that social functioning may influence biological aging processes. In the general population, social adversity and perceived isolation have been associated with markers of accelerated biological aging, including shorter telomere length and faster epigenetic aging [20, 21]. However, direct evidence linking social dysfunction in anxiety disorders to biological aging is limited, and the potential heterogeneity in biological aging across anxiety disorder subtypes remains poorly understood. We hypothesized that epigenetic age acceleration would differ between healthy individuals and those with anxiety disorders, reflecting variations in social impairments, and that both the patterns (increased or suppressed) and degree of epigenetic age acceleration would vary across anxiety disorder subtypes.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the association between anxiety disorders and epigenetic age acceleration in a Japanese cohort, with a focus on SAD, PD, and GAD. We also examined the influence of social functioning on the acceleration of epigenetic aging. Furthermore, we conducted meta-analyses incorporating data from previous DNAm-based studies on anxiety disorders [16, 22] to clarify diagnostic subtype-specific effects. Through these efforts, we sought to enhance the understanding of the biological aging processes underlying anxiety disorders and their functional correlates.

Methods

Cohort description

Our cohort consisted of 30 individuals with anxiety disorders whose primary diagnoses included SAD (n = 11), PD (n = 11), and GAD (n = 8) (mean age ± SD, 39.6 ± 20.9 years; 7 men and 23 women; 76.7% women) and 266 healthy individuals (34.4 ± 12.9 years; 165 men and 101 women; 38.0% women). The demographic characteristics of these individuals are summarized in Table 1. A small subset of healthy individuals, all of whom were included in the current analysis, had participated in our previous studies [23–25], and their DNA methylation (DNAm) and Social Functioning Scale (SFS) data were partially reused in the current analysis. These participants were recruited from the Social Anxiety, Genetics, and Environmental Associations (SaGa) project, the Schizophrenia Non-Affected Relative Project (SNARP), and the Bipolar & Schizophrenia Network on Intermediate Phenotypes-JAPAN (B-SNIP-J) [26–34]. All individuals were of Japanese descent and had no biological first- or second-degree relatives among participants. Outpatients were recruited from Gifu University Hospital and affiliated hospitals. Each patient with an anxiety disorder was diagnosed with the Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-5 (SCID) and medical records according to the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). None of the individuals with anxiety disorders had comorbid psychiatric disorders except for other anxiety disorders. Healthy individuals were recruited through local advertisements as well as from health care students and hospital staff. They were evaluated using the Structured Clinical Interviews for DSM-IV or DSM-5 Nonpatient Edition (SCID-NP) to exclude individuals who had current or past psychiatric contact, had received psychiatric medications, or had a family history of neuropsychiatric disorders in first- or second-degree relatives. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after a full explanation of the procedures was provided. This study was conducted in accordance with the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Research Ethics Committees of Gifu University (2019–233) and Kanazawa Medical University (G113 and G174). Assessment of social functioning

Social functioning was assessed in a large subset of our cohort (Table 1) with the Japanese version of the SFS, a self-report questionnaire [35]. The SFS comprises seven domains of social functioning [24, 25, 36]: (i) Social engagement/withdrawal—time spent alone, initiation of conversation, and social avoidance (score range, 0–15); (ii) Interpersonal communication—number of friends, presence of a romantic partner, and quality of communication (0–12); (iii) Independence-performance—actual performance of skills necessary for independent living (0–39); (iv) Independence-competence—perceived ability to perform skills necessary for independent living (0–39); (v) Recreation—engagement in common hobbies, interests, and pastimes (0–45); (vi) Prosocial activities—participation in typical social activities (e.g., sports, club activities) (0–66); and (vii) Employment/Occupation—involvement in productive employment or a structured daily program (0–10). The total score is calculated as the sum of the seven subscale scores, yielding a range of 0226, with higher scores indicating better social functioning. In the present study, age-adjusted residuals of the total SFS score and each subscale score were used in the analyses to control for age effects.

Methylation quantification and quality control

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral whole-blood samples, and bisulfite conversion was performed to quantify DNAm using the Illumina Infinium® Human MethylationEPIC v1.0 or v2.0 BeadChip arrays (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). To minimize potential bias due to slide or positional effects, the DNA samples were randomly assigned to positions on the BeadChips.

Signal intensity processing, background correction, and normalization of the methylation β values at individual CpG sites were conducted using the R package “meffil” [37] (https://github.com/perishky/meffil/), which provides efficient algorithms for analyzing DNAm data. The raw IDAT files from the Illumina methylation arrays were loaded into R software (version 4.2.3). Quality control (QC) procedures were applied to exclude poor-quality probes and samples with insufficient bead numbers or low detection rates, as described in previous studies [23, 38]. The QC thresholds were set as follows: (i) beadnum.samples.threshold = 0.1 (maximum fraction of probes with too few detected beads per sample); (ii) detectionp.samples.threshold = 0.1 (maximum fraction of undetected probes per sample); (iii) beadnum.cpgs.threshold = 0.1 (maximum fraction of samples with too few detected beads per probe); (iv) detectionp.cpgs.threshold = 0.1 (maximum fraction of samples in which the probe is undetected); and (v) sex.outlier.sd = 10 (number of standard deviations used to identify sex outliers).

These QC procedures were conducted using a larger cohort than the final analysis sample to increase the robustness and accuracy of normalization and outlier detection, particularly because QC performance improves with increasing sample size. On the basis of these criteria, no samples were excluded prior to quantile normalization. In terms of probes, exclusions were applied if (i) > 10% of the samples had detection p values > 0.01 (n = 388) and/or (ii) > 10% of the samples had fewer than three beads at the CpG sites (n = 344). Next, to adjust for technical variance in the methylation measurements, we determined the number of principal components (PCs) derived from control probes and applied functional normalization using the first 15 PCs and the slide variable as covariates. After these QC and normalization steps, a total of 720,989 CpG sites were retained for downstream analyses.

The proportions of six blood cell types—B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, monocytes, neutrophils, and natural killer cells—were estimated from the methylation data using meffil’s “blood gse35069 complete” reference profile.

Assessment of epigenetic age acceleration

Epigenetic age was estimated from the Illumina Human Methylation microarray using the PCHannumG2013 DNAm clock [10], which was originally developed on the basis of blood-derived methylation data and is widely used for its compatibility with whole-blood samples. In the present study, we employed a principal component-based (PC-based) version of the HannumG2013 clock (PCHannumG2013), which derives PCs from the methylation levels at the specific CpG sites included in the clock. This approach effectively reduces technical variation [39], including batch effects arising from the use of both EPIC v1.0 and v2.0 BeadChip arrays, thereby enhancing the robustness and reliability of DNAm age estimation in blood samples. DNAm age was calculated from quantile-normalized β values obtained from the Illumina Human MethylationEPIC BeadChip arrays processed through the meffil R package [37]. The epigenetic age acceleration value was computed as the residual from a linear regression of DNAm age on chronological age, fitted across all study participants. This approach accounts for age-related trends and yields an age-independent measure of biological aging. Positive residuals indicate accelerated epigenetic aging, whereas negative residuals reflect decelerated or suppressed epigenetic aging. All the statistical analyses assessing diagnostic group differences and associations with social functioning were performed using this age-adjusted epigenetic age acceleration measure, ensuring that the observed effects were independent of chronological age. Sensitivity analyses using alternative epigenetic clocks—ZhangQ2019 [40], PCHorvathS2018 [41], PCPhenoAge [42], and DunedinPACE [43] (Additional file 1: Table S1)—were conducted to confirm our findings obtained using PCHannumG2013 as the primary clock.

Previous DNAm-based studies on anxiety disorders for meta-analysis

Two publicly available DNAm datasets in IDAT format were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). One dataset included peripheral blood samples from healthy individuals and individuals with SAD (GSE164056_RAW) [16], and the other included samples from healthy individuals and individuals with PD (GSE201016_RAW) [22]. The detailed recruitment and diagnostic procedures have been previously described [16, 22]. Briefly, all participants were of Caucasian descent. Individuals with SAD were recruited at Eberhard Karls University of Tübingen, Germany, and those with PD were recruited at the Max Planck Institute of Psychiatry in Munich, Germany. Diagnoses were made according to the DSM-IV criteria with the SCID. Healthy controls for SAD were screened with the SCID to exclude individuals with SAD, whereas controls with PD were matched for age and sex, screened for Axis I disorders, and confirmed to be free of psychiatric medication. Since age and sex information were not included in the PD dataset [22], we contacted the corresponding author and obtained these data. In addition, because information on current smoking status was unavailable in the PD dataset, we estimated smoking status using a DNAm-based algorithm (EpiSmokEr; Epigenetic Smoking status Estimator) [44].

After applying the identical QC procedures as described above [38], 77 healthy individuals and 66 individuals with SAD from GSE164056 and 80 healthy individuals and 182 individuals with PD from GSE201016 were retained for analysis.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0 software (IBM Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and R software (version 4.2.3). Continuous variables, such as age, cell type proportions, and SFS scores, were compared between healthy individuals and those with anxiety disorders using the Mann‒Whitney U test, whereas categorical variables, such as sex and smoking status, were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test. Linear regression analyses were conducted to assess the difference in epigenetic age acceleration between diagnostic groups and the effects of social functioning on epigenetic age acceleration, with epigenetic age acceleration as a dependent variable; diagnostic status or SFS scales as independent variables; and sex, current smoking status, and slide effects as covariates.

To evaluate the diagnostic effects of anxiety disorders on epigenetic age acceleration across the current and previous studies, meta-analyses were performed using the metafor package in R. Effect sizes were defined as unstandardized regression coefficients (B) and their standard errors (SEs), which were derived from multiple linear regression models. A fixed-effects model was used to estimate pooled effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The choice was based on the limited number of studies (< 3 per diagnostic category), for which random-effects models are generally discouraged owing to the unstable estimation of between-study variance (τ2). Each study contributed a B coefficient and SE from analyses adjusting for potential covariates, ensuring methodological consistency across datasets. A threshold of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all the statistical tests.

Results

Demographic characteristics of healthy individuals and individuals with anxiety disorders in our cohort

Compared with healthy individuals (n = 266), individuals with anxiety disorders (n = 30), whose primary diagnoses included SAD (n = 11), PD (n = 11), and GAD (n = 8), had a greater female ratio (76.7%, χ2 = 16.6, p = 4.70 × 10−5), lower estimated B-cell proportion (z = − 2.8, p = 5.02 × 10−3), and higher estimated monocyte proportion (z = 3.0, p = 2.35 × 10−3) on the basis of the estimated blood cell types (Table 1). However, the diagnostic differences in B-cell and monocyte proportions were no longer significant after adjusting for age, sex, current smoking status, and plate effects as covariates in the linear regression analysis (p > 0.05).

We investigated diagnostic differences in epigenetic age acceleration between healthy individuals and those with anxiety disorders (Fig. 1). Compared with healthy individuals, individuals with anxiety disorders presented significantly suppressed epigenetic age acceleration (B ± SE = − 1.77 ± 0.82, p = 0.032). When anxiety disorders were divided into their primary diagnostic subtypes—SAD, PD, and GAD—individuals with SAD presented significantly suppressed epigenetic age acceleration compared with healthy individuals (B = − 3.46 ± 1.16, p = 3.14 × 10−3). Influences of social functioning on epigenetic age acceleration

Given that SAD is characterized by marked social avoidance and interpersonal fear, we further examined whether reduced social functioning assessed using the SFS might be associated with suppressed epigenetic age acceleration in our cohort (Fig. 2). A lower total SFS score was significantly associated with suppressed epigenetic age acceleration (B ± SE = 0.02 ± 0.01, p = 0.035). We then explored the individual subscales of the SFS. Among the seven subscales, lower scores in independence-competence and prosocial activities were significantly associated with suppressed epigenetic age acceleration (Independence-competence: B ± SE = 0.09 ± 0.05, p = 0.049; prosocial activities: B ± SE = 0.05 ± 0.03, p = 0.039). Meta-analysis of the diagnostic effects of anxiety disorders on epigenetic age acceleration

Two raw IDAT files from previous DNA methylation-based studies on anxiety disorders are publicly available from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). One dataset involves patients with SAD (GSE164056_RAW), including 77 healthy individuals and 66 individuals with SAD [16], and the other involves patients with PD (GSE201016_RAW), including 80 healthy individuals and 182 individuals with PD [22]. We first performed a meta-analysis of the diagnostic effects of anxiety disorders (k = 3) on epigenetic age acceleration in 423 healthy individuals and 278 individuals with anxiety disorders (Fig. 3). The meta-analysis revealed no significant overall difference in epigenetic age acceleration between healthy individuals and those with anxiety disorders (B ± 95% CI = − 0.08 ± 0.68, p = 0.81). However, the current study and the study of Czamara et al. (2022) revealed significant diagnostic effects in opposite directions (current study, B ± 95% CI = − 1.77 ± 1.61, p = 0.032; Czamara et al., B ± 95% CI = 1.05 ± 1.01, p = 0.043). To clarify the inconsistency, we performed separate meta-analyses of the diagnostic effects of SAD (k = 2, 343 healthy individuals and 77 individuals with SAD) and PD (k = 2, 346 healthy individuals and 193 individuals with PD) on epigenetic age acceleration (Fig. 3). The meta-analysis revealed that SAD was significantly associated with suppressed epigenetic age acceleration (B ± 95% CI = − 1.19 ± 1.00, p = 0.020), whereas PD was marginally associated with increased epigenetic age acceleration (B ± 95% CI = 0.80 ± 0.93, p = 0.092). Discussion

This study is the first to report differences in epigenetic age acceleration between healthy individuals and those with anxiety disorders. We found that individuals with anxiety disorders, particularly those with SAD, tended to exhibit suppressed epigenetic age acceleration compared with healthy individuals in our cohort. Moreover, lower social functioning, which mainly reflects impaired independence-competence and reduced prosocial activities, was associated with suppressed epigenetic age acceleration. Meta-analyses suggested that the pattern of epigenetic age acceleration might differ across specific anxiety disorder subtypes, with suppressed epigenetic age acceleration observed in patients with SAD and marginally increased epigenetic age acceleration in patients with PD. These findings raise the possibility that epigenetic age acceleration in individuals with SAD may be associated with socially avoidant behaviors, potentially reflecting reduced exposure to cumulative stressful life events. Similarly, individuals with PD may exhibit accelerated epigenetic aging that could be related to environmental factors such as stressful life events and inflammation, as observed in other psychiatric disorders. However, both interpretations remain speculative, and future longitudinal and biological mechanism studies are required to clarify whether social avoidance or stress-related processes exert direct influences on biological aging trajectories in anxiety disorders.

Consistent across clock types, except for DunedinPACE, individuals with SAD exhibited suppressed epigenetic age acceleration. The DunedinPACE clock estimates the pace of biological aging and has been shown to correlate with cumulative exposure to harmful environmental factors, including stressful life events that promote inflammation [45]. If individuals with SAD experienced stressful life events that triggered inflammation, as observed in other psychiatric disorders, we would expect accelerated rather than suppressed epigenetic aging according to this clock. However, as demonstrated by our methylation risk score (MRS) analysis [38], individuals with SAD presented a lower MRS associated with stressful life events—particularly those linked to PD—than healthy individuals did. This finding suggests that the characteristic social avoidance of individuals with SAD may reduce their exposure to cumulative stressful life events, thereby contributing to the observed suppression of epigenetic age acceleration.

The association between increased epigenetic age acceleration and PD was clock type-specific and was most evident with the PCPhenoAge clock, with PCHannumG2013 showing only a non-significant trend in the same direction. No associations were observed with the ZhangQ2019, PCHorvathS2018, or DunedinPACE clocks. Both PCHannumG2013 and PCPhenoAge were developed using blood-based DNA methylation data and are known to be sensitive to age-related immune and inflammatory changes reflected in blood biomarkers [10, 42]. In contrast, the ZhangQ2019 and PCHorvathS2018 clocks were designed for broader multi-tissue applicability, whereas the DunedinPACE clock was developed to measure the current pace of biological aging rather than the cumulative biological age. These differences may explain why only the blood-based clocks detected the change in aging of patients with PD, where immune or inflammatory signatures may play a central role. Interestingly, in patients with SAD, suppressed epigenetic age acceleration was consistently observed across all clocks except for DunedinPACE, suggesting that SAD-related alterations during biological aging may be relatively consistent across multiple clocks. The inability of most clocks—other than PCHannumG2013 and PCPhenoAge—to capture PD-related acceleration may reflect more heterogeneous or subtle aging processes in patients with PD or indicate that such effects are more closely linked to blood-specific inflammatory pathways.

Although our cohort revealed suppressed epigenetic age acceleration in individuals with anxiety disorders, the meta-analysis of overall anxiety disorders did not detect significant differences in epigenetic age acceleration between healthy individuals and those with anxiety disorders. This discrepancy likely reflects heterogeneity across studies in terms of sample characteristics, anxiety disorder subtypes, and environmental exposures. The meta-analysis combined data from cohorts that differed in diagnostic composition (e.g., varying proportions of SAD and PD), demographic factors, and potential confounding variables such as stress exposure and inflammation, which may differentially influence epigenetic aging. These sources of heterogeneity could have diluted subtype-specific effects, such as the suppression observed in patients with SAD or the acceleration potentially seen in patients with PD. Importantly, in each cohort, batch effects were modeled as covariates (slide effects) prior to meta-analysis, and the resulting summary statistics were used for integration. Thus, potential technical confounding was minimized and the credibility of the meta-analytic findings was strengthened [37]. This highlights the importance of considering diagnostic subtypes and the environmental context when examining biological aging in anxiety disorders.

Suppressed epigenetic age acceleration, as observed in patients with SAD, has also been reported in other psychiatric conditions [13, 14, 46]. Boks et al. [14] reported that greater PTSD symptom severity was associated with decelerated epigenetic aging in a cohort of military personnel, suggesting complex interactions between stress exposure, resilience, and biological aging. Similarly, Segura et al. [46] reported epigenetic age deacceleration in youth at familial high risk for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, highlighting that delayed biological aging may emerge even before disorder onset and could be linked to neurodevelopmental or protective mechanisms during adolescence. Okazaki et al. [13] also reported significantly decelerated epigenetic aging in Japanese patients with schizophrenia using Horvath’s pan-tissue clock, suggesting that some subtypes or stages of schizophrenia may be associated with slower biological aging processes. These findings indicate that suppressed epigenetic age acceleration may not be unique to patients with SAD but could reflect broader patterns associated with specific psychopathological processes, such as social withdrawal, reduced exposure to external stressors, neurodevelopmental delay, or compensatory biological mechanisms. Further comparative studies are warranted to clarify whether these patterns represent common pathways across disorders or distinct biological aging signatures.

A recent study by Song et al. [47] reported accelerated epigenetic aging in young individuals with SAD [47], based on a subset of the GSE164056 dataset [16] restricted to 37 patients with SAD and 41 controls under the age of 25. Their findings were not significant without adjustment for covariates (p > 0.05), but became only marginally significant with the Horvath clock after adjusting for sex, smoking status, cellular composition, and population structure (p = 0.046), while remaining non-significant with the GrimAge clock (p = 0.54). In contrast, our analyses employed the PCHannumG2013 and additional clocks, included broader age ranges. The covariates included in the models varied between studies; Song et al. adjusted for sex, smoking status, cellular composition, and population structure, while our covariates focused on sex, smoking status, and batch effects. Importantly, when we applied our covariate structure to the same GSE164056 dataset restricted to younger participants, no significant acceleration was observed (p > 0.05). These analytic differences, rather than whether or not the sample was restricted to younger SAD participants, may strongly influence the detection of subtle effects. Taken together, while Song et al. reported a weak and model-dependent acceleration, our meta-analyses consistently provided more robust evidence for suppressed epigenetic aging in SAD.

There are several limitations to consider when interpreting our findings. First, although we detected statistically significant differences in epigenetic age acceleration in individuals with anxiety disorders, the effect sizes were modest, suggesting that the biological aging alterations associated with anxiety disorders may be subtle. Second, the sample size of individuals with anxiety disorders in our cohort was relatively small (n = 30 in total, subdivided into three diagnostic groups), which may reduce statistical power and limit the reliability of subtype-specific conclusions. Moreover, there was an imbalance between the number of patients and controls, which could potentially bias effect estimates or inflate the apparent robustness of case–control comparisons. To mitigate these limitations, we conducted meta-analyses incorporating data from previous studies, which supported our main findings and strengthened the robustness of our conclusions. Nevertheless, our subtype-specific results should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger and more balanced cohorts are needed to confirm these patterns. Third, while there were no significant age differences between the diagnostic groups in our cohort, the anxiety disorder group included a greater proportion of female participants, likely reflecting the known epidemiology of anxiety disorders. Importantly, we statistically controlled for both age and sex in all analyses to minimize potential confounding effects. Finally, because our study employed a cross-sectional design, causal relationships between social functioning and epigenetic age dynamics cannot be established. Longitudinal studies will be necessary to clarify whether impaired social functioning contributes to changes in epigenetic aging or whether altered epigenetic aging influences social functioning.

Conclusions

Our study suggests that epigenetic age acceleration may differ between healthy individuals and those with anxiety disorders, with a potential suppressed epigenetic aging observed particularly in patients with SAD and marginally increased aging in patients with PD. These exploratory patterns were influenced by diagnostic subtype, social functioning, and clock type, highlighting the complexity of biological aging processes in anxiety disorders.
