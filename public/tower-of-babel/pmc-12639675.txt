Social media positive feedback and its impact on adolescents’ health risk behaviors: the chain mediation role of cognitive bias and emotions
Authors: Li Yang, Zhao Ziting, Zhang Min, Peng LongYan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescence is a critical period for development, and health risk behaviors at this stage can have lasting physical and mental health consequences. While previous studies have examined social media’s role in adolescent behavior, they often overlook the specific impact of positive feedback and the underlying psychological mechanisms. This study addresses this gap by exploring the influence of social media positive feedback on adolescents’ health risk behaviors and analyzing the mediating roles of cognitive bias and negative emotions.MethodsA cross-sectional survey design was adopted for this study, and data were collected from 6210 adolescents. The Health Risk Behavior Scale, Social Media Positive Feedback Scale, Negative Cognitive Processing Bias Questionnaire, and DASS-21 Scale were used for assessment. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was employed to examine both the direct and indirect effects of social media positive feedback on adolescents’ health risk behaviors and to explore the mediating roles of cognitive bias and emotions.ResultsPositive feedback on social media, adolescents’ health risk behaviors, cognitive bias, and negative emotions were all found to be significantly positively correlated. The analysis indicated that social media positive feedback was associated with adolescents’ health risk behaviors both directly and indirectly through cognitive bias and negative emotions. Furthermore, cognitive bias and negative emotions acted as chain mediators in the relationship between social media positive feedback and health risk behaviors.ConclusionsThis study found that social media positive feedback was significantly associated with adolescents’ health risk behaviors, and cognitive bias and emotional states play a mediating role in this influence. These findings highlight the potential role of social media in influencing adolescents’ risk behaviors and suggest that public health interventions should focus on the impact of the social media environment on adolescents’ behaviors. Interventions that improve emotional regulation and reduce cognitive biases may help decrease the occurrence of health risk behaviors.Keywords: Adolescents, Health risk behaviors, Social media positive feedback, Cognitive bias, Emotions

Background

Adolescence is a critical stage of both physiological and psychological development, marked by significant physical changes and shifts in social roles [1]. During this period, adolescents face numerous internal and external pressures and are highly susceptible to influences from peers and their social environment. This often leads to the engagement in a variety of health risk behaviors, including smoking, drinking, substance abuse, and unsafe sexual practices [2–4]. These behaviors not only pose immediate threats to adolescents’ physical and mental health but can also have long-term consequences for their overall quality of life [5]. Therefore, understanding the psychological mechanisms underlying adolescents’ health risk behaviors has become a key focus in global public health research.

With the proliferation of the internet and social media, adolescents’ interactions in the virtual world have markedly increased. Online social platforms have become important avenues for them to access information, express emotions, and form interpersonal relationships [6, 7]. Social media positive feedback refers to the prompt, positive responses that individuals receive when they post updates, upload pictures, or share information on social networks, including likes, comments, and shares [8]. Research has shown that positive feedback on social media (such as likes, comments, and shares) has significant effects on adolescents’ self-perception, emotional state, and behavioral decisions [9–11]. Such feedback not only facilitates social interaction and identity formation but may also influence adolescents’ health-related decision-making. The growing body of research on this topic suggests that social media feedback can shape behavior through reward sensitivity and social comparison mechanisms [12, 13]. However, findings remain mixed: while some studies have emphasized the supportive role of online feedback in fostering social connection [14, 15], others have highlighted its potential to encourage risky or extreme behaviors aimed at gaining visibility and approval [16].

Adolescents exhibit heightened behavioral sensitivity to social feedback, with stronger reward learning effects compared to adults [9]. These findings reinforce the view that adolescents may engage in behaviors—both online and offline—that are strategically oriented toward maximizing social approval. A parallel meta-analysis by Purba et al. identified significant correlations between high-frequency social media use and various health risk behaviors across diverse adolescent populations, including increased likelihood of alcohol consumption, smoking, and unsafe sexual behaviors [17]. Nevertheless, many of these studies stop short of identifying the internal mechanisms through which social media feedback influences such behaviors.

According to Social Identity Theory, social feedback are often internalized as signals of social acceptance and self-worth rather than serving as mere indicators of popularity. For many adolescents, whose sense of identity is still under formation, online approval becomes deeply tied to how they evaluate themselves and their place within peer groups. Over time, this dynamic may foster biased patterns of information processing, where greater cognitive weight is given to socially rewarding feedback and negative or absent feedback is overlooked, dismissed, or excessively magnified. Cognitive bias refers to the selective processing of information that individuals exhibit during cognitive tasks, such as attention bias, memory bias, and interpretation bias [18]. These biases can influence emotional responses and behavioral decisions. Social media positive feedback may reinforce specific cognitive patterns in adolescents, influencing their behavioral choices [10, 19]. Adolescents with cognitive biases may be more likely to focus on the positive feedback they receive on social media, enhancing their attention to behaviors that trigger such feedback. Through memory and interpretation biases, these adolescents might be more inclined to engage in risk behaviors in an attempt to gain more positive feedback from social media, thereby maintaining their image and status within their social circles [20]. Thus, in the context of social media, adolescents with heightened cognitive biases may selectively focus on positive feedback, reinforcing behaviors that elicit such approval. These biased perceptions can promote the internalization of social reward cues, shaping adolescents’ motivation to repeat or escalate attention-seeking behaviors.

Importantly, these biased patterns of information processing are not emotionally neutral. Rather, they directly affect how adolescents experience and regulate their emotions. Adolescence is characterized by heightened emotional reactivity and unstable affective states, making individuals particularly sensitive to perceived social validation or exclusion [21]. Emotions play a crucial role in decision-making during this period, especially when adolescents are confronted with stress, anxiety, and depression. In such emotional states, adolescents are more likely to make impulsive and irrational decisions [22]. When under stress or emotional distress, social media positive feedback may provide short-term emotional gratification. However, this satisfaction typically does not alleviate underlying emotional issues and may even exacerbate emotional fluctuations. Adolescents may become overly dependent on social media feedback to cope with emotions, making them more likely to engage in risk behaviors during emotional lows [23].

Studies have shown that cognitive biases, especially attention bias and interpretation bias, play a significant role in predicting emotional problems such as depression and anxiety in adolescents. Adolescents often ignore negative feedback and overemphasize positive responses, linking them closely to their sense of self-worth [24, 25]. However, this biased attention and interpretation may lead them to misinterpret information on social media when they do not receive sufficient positive feedback or encounter negative feedback, thereby increasing emotional instability. This emotional turmoil can contribute to feelings of anxiety and depression. When emotions are at their lowest, adolescents may be more prone to making impulsive and risky decisions, such as smoking, binge drinking, or engaging in unsafe sexual behaviors [26].

The Cognitive Appraisal Theory (CAT) posits that emotions are elicited through individuals’ subjective evaluations—or appraisals—of external events in terms of relevance, meaning, and control. Within this framework, cognitive bias serves as the initial appraisal mechanism, shaping how adolescents interpret social media feedback. These appraisals then generate corresponding emotional responses, which ultimately influence behavior [27]. Among adolescents, such biased appraisals may evoke emotional highs or lows, driving risk-taking behaviors aimed at regaining perceived social standing. Thus, social media positive feedback may indirectly foster health risk behaviors in adolescents through the chain mediating roles of cognitive biases and emotional states.

Although some prior studies have explored either cognitive or emotional mediators individually, few have tested their sequential interaction as a mechanism linking social media use to adolescent risk behavior. To further investigate the relationships among social media positive feedback, cognitive bias, emotions, and health risk behaviors in adolescents, this study proposes the following hypotheses: ① Social media positive feedback significantly associated with adolescents’ health risk behaviors. ② Cognitive bias and emotions play a chain mediating role in the relationship between social media positive feedback and health risk behaviors. This study aims to elucidate the sequential cognitive–emotional mechanisms linking adolescents’ online social experiences to offline health risk behaviors, and in doing so, offers a more integrated theoretical perspective on how digital environments shape adolescent development.

This study employed a cluster sampling method based on school classes to recruit participants from middle and high schools in Daqing City, Heilongjiang Province, China. A total of 6,897 adolescents aged between 12 and 18 years were included. Schools of different educational levels and geographical locations were considered to improve the representativeness of the sample.

Instruments

Social media positive feedback scale

This scale was developed by Liu and Brown to measure the positive feedback individuals receive after posting updates on social media [28]. The feedback includes likes, supportive comments, shares, etc. The scale consists of five items, scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “frequently.” A higher score indicates a higher frequency of receiving positive feedback online. The scale has been widely used by domestic researchers and has demonstrated good reliability and validity. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.816. The results of the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated that χ²/df = 5.001, RMSEA = 0.018, CFI = 0.995, GFI = 0.994, IFI = 0.995, and TLI = 0.983, all of which met the acceptable thresholds for model fit. The composite reliability (CR) was 0.886 and the average variance extracted (AVE) was 0.611, demonstrating good convergent validity of the scale. In addition, the correlation coefficients among the items ranged from 0.537 to 0.755, all lower than the square root of the AVE, indicating satisfactory discriminant validity.

Negative Cognitive Processing Bias Questionnaire (NCPBQ)

The Negative Cognitive Processing Bias Questionnaire, developed by Yan et al. in 2017, measures four types of negative cognitive biases: negative attention bias, negative memory bias, negative interpretation bias, and negative rumination bias [29]. Excluding attitude and lie detection questions, the scale contains 20 items, with a 4-point scoring system, where 1 represents “completely disagree” and 4 represents “completely agree.” Higher scores indicate a higher degree of negative cognitive processing bias. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.935, with the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the negative attention bias, negative memory bias, negative interpretation bias, and negative rumination bias dimensions being 0.823、0.837、0.816 and 0.836, respectively. The results of the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) showed that χ²/df = 4.993, RMSEA = 0.025, CFI = 0.988, GFI = 0.987, IFI = 0.988, and TLI = 0.980, all of which reached acceptable levels of model fit. The composite reliability (CR) values for the four dimensions were respectively, while the average variance extracted (AVE) values were 0.512, 0.503, 0.509, and 0.501, with the standardized factor loadings of the observed variables being, respectively. These results indicate that the scale demonstrates good convergent validity. Furthermore, the correlation coefficients among the dimensions ranged from 0.612 to 0.637, all of which were lower than the square root of the AVE, providing evidence of satisfactory discriminant validity.

Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21)

The DASS-21, revised by Gong et al., was used to assess depression, anxiety, and stress [30]. The scale contains 21 items, with 7 items each for depression, anxiety, and stress. It uses a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (does not apply) to 3 (applies most or all of the time). Higher scores indicate more severe symptoms. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.952, with the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the anxiety, depression, and stress dimensions being 0.896、0.859, and 0.860, respectively. The results of the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated that χ²/df = 4.774, RMSEA = 0.025, CFI = 0.923, GFI = 0.899, IFI = 0.923, and TLI = 0.908, all of which met the acceptable thresholds for model fit. The composite reliability (CR) values for the three dimensions were 0.878, 0.892, and 0.881, respectively, while the average variance extracted (AVE) values were 0.508, 0.545, and 0.518. The standardized factor loadings of the observed variables were 0.746, 0.767, and 0.743, respectively, suggesting that the scale exhibits good convergent validity. In addition, the correlation coefficients among the dimensions ranged from 0.612 to 0.637, all of which were lower than the square root of the corresponding AVE values, thereby providing evidence of satisfactory discriminant validity.

Chinese adolescent health risk behavior survey

This study used the Chinese Adolescent Health Risk Behavior Survey revised by Ji et al., which measures adolescents’ risky health behaviors in seven areas: dietary behaviors, weight loss behaviors, unintentional injury behaviors, intentional injury behaviors, substance addiction behaviors, Sedentary Behavior, and sexual behaviors [31]. The scale has been widely used in adolescent populations and has demonstrated good reliability and validity. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.746, with the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the dimensions being 0.629、0.650、0.649、0.754、0.724、0.644 and 0.663, respectively. The results of the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) showed that χ²/df = 4.888, RMSEA = 0.025, CFI = 0.950, GFI = 0.917, IFI = 0.950, and TLI = 0.940, all of which reached acceptable levels of model fit. The composite reliability (CR) values for the seven dimensions were 0.854, 0.871, 0.848, 0.766, 0.817, 0.858, and 0.817, respectively, while the average variance extracted (AVE) values were 0.495, 0.529, 0.509, 0.503, 0.506, 0.530, and 0.513. The standardized factor loadings of the observed variables were 0.744, 0.750, 0.650, 0.714, 0.698, 0.769, and 0.614, respectively, indicating good convergent validity of the scale. Moreover, the correlation coefficients among the dimensions ranged from 0.445 to 0.597, all of which were lower than the square root of the corresponding AVE values, thereby providing evidence of satisfactory discriminant validity.

In addition, the convergent validity and discriminant validity of each instruments were tested, seen in Tables 1, 2 and 3. The combined reliability coefficients CR of the scale were 0.886, 0.868, 0.796 and 0.875 respectively, and the AVE were 0.611, 0.621, 0.566 and 0.501 respectively, indicating that the scale has good convergent validity. If the value of the average variance variance (AVE) is greater than 0.5 and the value of the combined reliability (CR) is greater than 0.7, it indicates that the variable has good convergent validity [32]. Furthermore, by calculating the correlation coefficients among the variables, it was found that their values ranged from 0.20 to 0.31, all of which were lower than the square root of the mean variance extract (AVE), demonstrating good discriminative validity.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Harbin Medical University- Daqing Campus.

Data collection

After communication and obtaining consent from the relevant school administrators, the study officially began. Within each selected school, entire classes were randomly sampled from each grade level. Prior to participant recruitment, school administrators assisted in identifying and excluding students with known and documented mental disorders from the eligible participant pool, in accordance with ethical guidelines and to ensure the appropriateness of participation. These exclusions were made before the survey distribution phase. All students in the eligible and selected classes were then invited to participate in the survey under the supervision of trained research assistants and school teachers. The research team distributed the questionnaires on-site to the participants. Before the distribution, all participants received detailed training to ensure they understood the questionnaire content and completion requirements. During this session, the research team addressed any participant questions to ensure accuracy in responses. Both the distribution and completion of the questionnaires were conducted on-site by the research team, allowing for immediate support and ensuring data completeness. A total of 6,210 questionnaires were collected, resulting in a valid response rate of 90.04%.

Data analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 28.0. Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage, were calculated to summarize participant characteristics and core variables. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to examine associations among social media positive feedback, cognitive bias, emotions, and health risk behaviors. Before conducting structural equation modeling (SEM), multicollinearity diagnostics were performed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Tolerance statistics; results indicated no significant multicollinearity, as all VIF values were below 5 and Tolerance values exceeded 0.2, supporting the stability and interpretability of the regression estimates. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and SEM were conducted using AMOS with the maximum likelihood estimation method to test the hypothesized chain mediation model. The indirect effects of cognitive bias and emotions were assessed using a bias-corrected bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples and 95% confidence intervals (CI); mediation was considered significant if the CI did not include zero. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: CMIN/DF < 3 (good fit) or < 5 (acceptable fit), CFI ≥ 0.90, TLI ≥ 0.90, GFI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, and SRMR ≤ 0.06. A two-tailed p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Common bias

To assess the potential impact of common method bias (CMB), Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. All items from the main study variables were entered into an exploratory factor analysis. The results showed that the first unrotated factor accounted for 32.49% of the total variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%, indicating that common method bias is unlikely to be a major concern in this study.

Demographic characteristics of participants

Among the 6,210 participants, the average age was 15.30 years (SD = 2.30). There were 3,512 females (56.6%) and 2,698 males (43.4%). Regarding participants’ place of origin, 1,660 (26.73%) were from rural areas, and 4,550 (73.27%) were from urban areas. In terms of family structure, 3,862 (62.19%) participants came from nuclear families, and 2,348 (37.81%) came from non-nuclear families. The distribution of household monthly income was as follows: 2,091 (33.67%) had a household income of < 3000 RMB, 1,695 (27.29%) had an income of 3001–5000 RMB, and 2,424 (39.04%) had an income of > 5000 RMB.

Discussion

This study found that social media positive feedback, cognitive bias, emotions, and adolescent health risk behaviors were significantly correlated. Adolescents reporting higher levels of positive feedback on social media also reported greater engagement in risky behaviors, and this association may be influenced by cognitive distortions and emotional responses. The results support a chain mediation model, in which social media positive feedback affects behavior indirectly through cognition and emotion, jointly accounting for 45.67% of the total effect.

Social media use and risky health behaviors in adolescents

Positive feedback on social media was found to be significantly associated with adolescents’ health risk behaviors, accounting for 54.33% of the total explained variance in the model. This finding is consistent with prior research suggesting that feedback-driven platforms amplify adolescents’ sensitivity to peer evaluation and may be linked to an increased likelihood of impulsive behaviors, such as substance use and participation in risky online challenges [33]. The immediacy of likes and comments creates a reinforcement loop, where behaviors that garner visibility are more likely to be repeated.

Moreover, our findings extend existing literature by focusing not just on general social media exposure, but on positive feedback as a distinct behavioral driver. Unlike studies that emphasize the social benefits of social media use—such as enhanced peer connection or identity exploration [34], our results highlight its potential to trigger problematic behaviors through reward-seeking and performance pressure. This contrast underscores the dual nature of social media, where the same feedback mechanisms that support social development can also reinforce maladaptive behavior patterns.

Importantly, we contribute to ongoing debates regarding the social identity function of online feedback. Our findings support the view that adolescents internalize public approval as a proxy for social belonging, and this internalization may foster performative, and at times risky, behavior intended to maintain or elevate perceived status. The WHO has likewise recognized the increase in problematic social media use among youth, with behavioral consequences tied to compulsive validation-seeking and reduced self-regulation [35].

The chain mediation role of cognitive bias and negative emotions

The indirect effects through cognitive bias (31.89%) and emotion (13.78%) support a two-stage psychological process. The relatively stronger cognitive path suggests that information processing distortions—such as selective attention to peer-approved behavior—are primary drivers of behavioral imitation. This is consistent with research indicating that adolescents prioritize immediate social rewards and are more prone to reinforcing short-term gain over long-term consequences [9, 36]. While the emotional path was statistically significant, its effect was comparatively weaker. This may be partly due to limitations in measurement sensitivity—emotions were assessed via the DASS-21, a generalized tool that may not capture context-specific emotional fluctuations triggered by social media. Moreover, adolescents may underreport negative emotional states due to social desirability, contributing to an underestimation of emotional influence. Nonetheless, emotions remain an important catalytic factor, particularly in amplifying the effects of cognitive bias and reducing behavioral inhibition under stress [37]. From a theoretical perspective, the findings are supported by Cognitive Appraisal Theory (CAT), which posits that emotions are not direct reactions to events but are generated through subjective appraisals. In this study, cognitive biases function as distorted appraisal filters, shaping how adolescents interpret feedback and driving subsequent emotional responses. The activation of emotion—especially negative affect like anxiety—then increases the likelihood of impulsive or risk-oriented actions. Thus, cognition and emotion form a dynamic, interdependent mechanism, with cognitive bias initiating and emotion intensifying behavior responses.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships among social media positive feedback, cognitive bias, emotion, and health risk behaviors. Second, reliance on self-reported measures may introduce biases such as social desirability and recall inaccuracy. In particular, the relatively weak indirect effect of emotion observed in the mediation model may reflect limitations in the sensitivity of the DASS-21 scale and the tendency of adolescents to underreport emotional fluctuations, potentially leading to an underestimation of emotion’s role. Additionally, the sample was limited to adolescents in Daqing, China, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Future research should consider using longitudinal designs, more nuanced emotion assessments, and diverse samples to enhance the robustness and applicability of the results.

Conclusion

This study reveals the complex associations between social media positive feedback and adolescents’ health risk behaviors. The results show that social media positive feedback not only demonstrates a direct association with health risk behaviors but also shows indirect associations through cognitive biases, emotional states, and their sequential interaction. These findings are consistent with a potential pathway whereby the social media environment may be associated with adolescent behavior through the dynamic sequence of “cognitive processing—emotional responses—behavior occurrence.” However, given the cross-sectional nature of this study, these relationships should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Future research should employ longitudinal tracking designs to establish the temporal sequence and potential causal relationships within this proposed pathway.

In light of these findings, prevention and intervention strategies should consider addressing both cognitive and emotional processes among adolescents who are highly engaged in social media. Programs targeting early and middle adolescents, particularly within school-based or digital education settings, may incorporate techniques such as attention bias modification, cognitive reframing, and emotion regulation training (e.g., mindfulness or stress management workshops). These approaches may help adolescents critically evaluate online feedback, regulate their emotional responses, and reduce reliance on social approval for behavioral validation. Such targeted interventions could serve as a foundation for mitigating the health risks linked to adolescents’ increasing immersion in digital social environments.
