Health technology assessment of robotic surgery for rectal cancer in China: protocol for a nationally multicentre prospective cohort study
Authors: Beini Lyu, Zerong Cai, Qingyang Feng, Sanjun Cai, Yan-Bing Zhou, Qian Liu, Guiyu Wang, Meng Wang, Yingjiang Ye, Tao Li, Sheng Dai, Weitang Yuan, Ying Huang, Ligang Xia, Yan Shi, Baoqing Jia, Taiyuan Li, Zhiyun He, Ziting Wu, Haoran Zhan, Xuefeng Hu, Jianping Wang, Jianmin Xu, Xiaojian Wu, Gordon G Liu, SMART study group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundRobot-assisted surgery (RAS) has been increasingly adopted for rectal cancer because of its technical advantages in the confined pelvic space, but its clinical and economic value relative to laparoscopic and open surgery remains uncertain. Existing health technology assessments have relied largely on small single-centre studies or modelling approaches, with limited evidence from low- and middle-income countries such as China, which has the world’s largest population of patients with rectal cancer and rapid diffusion of RAS.Methods and analysisWe will conduct a prospective, multicentre, three-arm cohort study comparing RAS, laparoscopic and open low anterior resection for mid- and low-rectal adenocarcinoma. A total of 1200 patients (RAS 540, laparoscopy 540, open 120) will be recruited from 17 tertiary hospitals across geographically and socioeconomically diverse regions of China and followed for 36 months. Outcomes include surgical complications, oncologic efficacy (disease-free survival, overall survival, local recurrence), health-related quality of life (EQ-5D-5L), preoperative anxiety (PAS-7), surgeon comfort, number of personnel required for the surgery and comprehensive costs from a societal perspective (direct medical, direct non-medical and indirect). Cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analyses will be performed, with quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) derived from EQ-5D-5L. Analyses will use propensity score methods to control for confounding, supplemented by sensitivity and scenario analyses including a 36-month decision-analytic model. Subgroup analyses will explore heterogeneity by clinical, socioeconomic and institutional factors.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has been reviewed and approved by Peking University Health Science Center’s Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-24054). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants. Findings will be reported according to best-practice guidelines for HTA and disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, international conferences and policy briefs. This study is designed to evaluate the clinical and economic outcomes of RAS compared with laparoscopic surgery, with additional descriptive analysis of open surgery in real-world practice.Keywords: HEALTH ECONOMICS, Colorectal surgery, Prospective studies, Surveys and Questionnaires

Background

Colorectal cancer is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer death worldwide; in China, it ranks second in incidence and fourth in mortality.1 2 Rectal cancer accounts for approximately 50–60% of cases, with most tumours arising in the technically demanding mid and low rectum.3 Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative therapy, yet the narrow bony pelvis limits visualisation and instrument manoeuvrability, complicating oncologic dissection and sphincter preservation.4 5

Laparoscopic surgery is widely adopted for rectal cancer because it reduces surgical trauma and accelerates recovery while achieving long-term survival comparable to open surgery.6–9 Nevertheless, oncologic outcomes after laparoscopy remain debated, particularly in challenging pelvic anatomy. Robot-assisted surgery (RAS) offers three-dimensional high-definition visualisation and articulating instruments that enhance precision in confined spaces, potentially improving both surgical and oncologic endpoints.10 Current evidence suggests shorter hospital stays, fewer conversions to open surgery, faster return of bowel function and fewer postoperative complications with RAS versus laparoscopy.11 Observational registry analyses have further associated RAS with lower 30- and 90-day mortality and improved 5-year overall survival.12 A recent multicentre randomised trial in China reported lower locoregional recurrence and higher 3-year disease-free survival with RAS compared with laparoscopy.10

These clinical advantages must be weighed against substantially higher costs, including capital acquisition, disposable instruments and maintenance. Systematic reviews generally find greater perioperative and total costs for RAS, with incremental expenditures on the order of several thousand US dollars relative to laparoscopy.13–15 Cost-effectiveness evidence is mixed and appears sensitive to healthcare system context: a prospective Spanish study suggested RAS may be cost-effective at willingness-to-pay thresholds of €20 000–€30 000 per QALY,16 whereas the UK ROLARR trial estimated an incremental cost of £980 for a QALY gain of 0.014 over 6 months (ICER £69 837/QALY), exceeding commonly used thresholds.17

Important gaps remain in the health technology assessment (HTA) of RAS versus laparoscopic and open surgery. First, most prior work prioritised clinical endpoints, with relatively few studies incorporating quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). Second, QALY-based evaluations often relied on small samples with limited statistical power. Third, single-centre designs predominate, constraining generalisability in the face of centre-level variation (learning curves, case volume) that can materially influence cost-effectiveness.18–20 Fourth, few analyses adopt a societal perspective, omitting indirect costs such as return to work, caregiver burden and productivity—factors that may offset higher direct medical costs. Finally, robust HTA evidence from low- and middle-income settings is scarce, despite China’s large rectal cancer burden and rapid nationwide adoption of RAS.1 21 22 High-quality, context-specific evidence is essential to inform reimbursement and technology diffusion policies.

To address these gaps, we will conduct a prospective, nationwide, multicentre cohort study to evaluate the clinical and economic outcomes of low anterior resection (LAR) for mid- and low-rectal adenocarcinoma across RAS, laparoscopic and open approaches. The study will assess oncologic outcomes, health-related quality of life and costs and will conduct a cost-effectiveness analysis from a societal perspective. The primary comparison will focus on robot-assisted versus laparoscopic surgery, with open surgery described to reflect real-world practice.

Methods

Overall study design

This is a prospective, multicentre, three-arm, real-world cohort study conducted at 17 high-volume tertiary hospitals across geographically and socioeconomically diverse regions of China (figure 1). The study is aligned within the IDEAL framework (Idea, Development, Exploration, Assessment, Long-term study) and aims to evaluate clinical effectiveness and safety in real-world practice. In addition, the study integrates a health technology assessment perspective and includes the economic outcomes.

Geographical distribution of 17 participating centres in China.

Eligible patients who elect RAS, laparoscopic surgery or open surgery for LAR will be enrolled after written informed consent is obtained from the patient or an authorised delegate. The target sample size is 1200 (RAS 540; laparoscopy 540; open 120). Surgical, oncologic, health-related quality of life, cost and surgeon comfort outcomes will be collected. Follow-up visits are scheduled at baseline and at 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, 30 and 36 months post-operatively (participant flow in figure 2).

Participant flow for the cohort study. LAS, laparoscopic-assisted surgery; OS, open surgery; RAS, robotic-assisted surgery.

The study commenced in November 2024 and is expected to be completed in 2029. The study was prospectively registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400086310).

The main institutional review board approval was obtained in July 2024. Local ethics approvals and site contracts were completed in a staggered manner between July 2024 and July 2025. The electronic case report form (eCRF) system was developed and pilot tested in October 2024, and the first patient was enrolled in November 2024. Patient recruitment is currently ongoing and is expected to be completed by September 2026. Data quality control and source-data verification will be conducted throughout the study, with interim data reviews performed every 3 months. After completion of enrolment, perioperative data cleaning and database lock are planned for 2026. Participants will be followed for 36 months after surgery, with final follow-up expected by 2029 and final database lock planned by the end of 2029. Subsequent analyses will include long-term oncologic, quality-of-life and economic outcomes, as well as decision-analytic modelling and sensitivity analyses. Recruitment and exposure classification

Eligible patients are screened by site investigators. Treatment selection is determined by clinical assessment, surgeon preference and institutional resources; the study does not randomise or otherwise intervene. Patients initially planned for LAR but intraoperatively converted to abdominoperineal resection (APR) will be classified as APR. These cases will be recorded with reasons for conversion and excluded from the primary analysis focused on LAR procedures. Sensitivity analyses may be conducted if appropriate.

Based on the surgery received, patients are categorised into the RAS, laparoscopic or open surgery group. Classification of the surgical approach is based on the technique used during the primary dissection phase. Definitions for each group are as follows:

RAS group: Dissection performed with a surgical robot using a laparoscopic camera and robotic arms. Specimen extraction and anastomosis may be robotic, laparoscopic or open. If conversion to laparoscopy or open occurs during dissection, patients remain in the RAS group; conversions are recorded.23

Laparoscopic group: Dissection performed using conventional laparoscopic instruments. Hybrid procedures, such as laparoscopic mobilisation followed by open anastomosis, will be classified as laparoscopic if the dissection is performed laparoscopically. Hand-assisted laparoscopic procedures will also be classified as laparoscopic when the dissection is primarily laparoscopic. Procedures in which only diagnostic laparoscopy is performed, followed by open resection, will be classified as open surgery. In cases where conversion to open surgery occurs during the dissection phase, patients will remain classified in the laparoscopic group, and conversions will be recorded.24

Open group: Dissection performed through a laparotomy with standard open techniques.

Study centres and surgical experience

Participating centres are high-volume tertiary hospitals located across geographically and socioeconomically diverse regions of China. To preserve the real-world nature of the study, we did not restrict participating centres to those with established robotic programmes or require surgeons to have completed a minimum number of robotic procedures. Instead, surgeon and institutional experience will be explicitly measured, including surgeon cumulative experience with RAS, laparoscopic and open LAR procedures, and annual centre LAR volume.

Outcomes

The study will collect outcomes across multiple domains, including surgical outcomes, oncologic efficacy, health-related quality of life, economic outcomes, surgeon comfort and surgical team composition. In addition, surgeon experience and centre-level surgical volume will be recorded.

1) Surgical outcomes include perioperative surgical complications, intraoperative blood loss, conversion to open surgery, time to first postoperative flatus, unplanned readmission within 30 days after surgery, unplanned reoperation within 30 days and death within 30 days after surgery. Intraoperative blood loss will be extracted from the operative record in the hospital medical record system, reflecting routinely documented intraoperative estimates. The number, location and length of surgical incisions will be recorded.

Perioperative complications are defined as surgery-related adverse events occurring from the intraoperative period to 30 days postoperatively, including but not limited to pulmonary infection, urinary tract infection, wound infection, anastomotic leakage, urinary dysfunction, intra-abdominal haemorrhage, postoperative anastomotic bleeding and deep vein thrombosis.

2) Oncologic efficacy outcomes include disease-free survival, overall survival, R0 resection rate and local recurrence rate. Within 30 days after surgery, data will be collected on the patient’s postoperative pathological Tumour, Node, Metastasis (TNM) staging and pathological circumferential resection margin status.

3) Quality of life will be measured using the European Five-Dimensional Quality of Life Scale (EQ-5D-5L), a preference-based measure that enables estimation of health utility and QALYs and is widely used in health technology assessment in China.25 Given the policy-oriented objective of this study, its use facilitates comparability with existing economic evaluations. Preoperative anxiety is assessed using the Perioperative Anxiety Scale (PAS-7), validated for Chinese populations.26

4) Economic outcomes: the study will include costs from a societal perspective, including direct medical costs, direct non-medical costs and indirect costs over the study period.

Direct medical costs cover inpatient and outpatient expenses related to surgery, medication, laboratory tests, nursing and rehabilitation. Costs for index hospitalisation will be obtained from hospital administrative and billing systems and categorised into standardised components (eg, diagnostic, laboratory, surgery-related, material, medication, nursing and bed charges). Surgery-related costs represent bundled charges including operative procedures, anaesthesia and associated intraoperative services, while material costs include medical consumables. Nursing costs are calculated based on a fixed daily rate multiplied by length of stay, and bed charges reflect standardised per diem hospitalisation costs.

Follow-up direct medical costs will be obtained through patients’ medical insurance accounts (UEBMI or URRBMI). Patients can access detailed records of medically reimbursed expenses by logging into their insurance accounts, where they can obtain a breakdown of their medical expenditures, including outpatient treatments, medications and hospital visits.

Direct non-medical costs include transportation, food and accommodation costs related to the treatment for patients and their caregivers. Indirect costs include lost wages incurred by patients and their caregivers due to the disease. These two categories of costs will be collected using standardised patient questionnaires. All patient-reported costs will be defined as costs directly attributable to rectal cancer and its treatment.

5) Surgeon comfort: surgeon comfort level using Local Experienced Discomfort scale will be collected after each surgery.27

6) Number of personnel for the surgery: the number of personnel involved in the surgery will be recorded for each case, including surgeons and nurses. Surgeons will include the primary surgeon (chief surgeon) and all assistants (first, second and third assistants), which may include trainees directly involved in the procedure. Nurses will include the scrub nurse, circulating nurse and any other nursing staff involved in the surgery, such as those assisting with anaesthesia. Personnel not directly involved in the procedure, such as those covering breaks, will not be included.

Measurement of covariates

Patient demographic, socioeconomic status and clinical characteristics, and surgeon characteristics will be collected.

Demographic characteristics including age at enrolment, date of birth, sex, height, weight, place of residence and family size will be collected.

Socioeconomic status including marriage status, education, health insurance, occupation, working hours, total income, consumption, disposable household income and debt will be collected.

Clinical characteristics: laboratory, radiological and surgical operation data will be collected. Laboratory data include data such as complete blood count, liver and kidney function, coagulation function and preoperative tumour markers. Radiological data include data such as chest CT, contrast-enhanced thoracoabdominal-pelvic CT, colonoscopy, clinical TNM staging and any use of preoperative radiotherapy. Within 30 days after surgery, we will collect data on surgical approach, duration of surgery, intraoperative bleeding and postoperative complications. Postoperative chemotherapy regimens will also be documented, including frequency, drug types and dosages.

Surgeon characteristics include age, sex, education, time of medical licensure, professional ranking and the number of RAS, laparoscopic and open LAR surgeries performed.

Sample size calculation

The sample size is based on non-inferiority of 3-year disease-free survival (DFS) for RAS versus laparoscopic using log-rank calculation in PASS software.28 Assuming 3-year DFS=0.85 in both groups, a non-inferiority margin of 6.5%, one-sided α=0.025 and 80% power, 418 participants per group are required. Given low expected use of open surgery, a 45:45:10 allocation (RAS:laparoscopy:open) was adopted, requiring ≥92 open cases. To accommodate attrition and adjust for confounding, target enrolment was increased to 540:540:120, for a total of 1200 patients.

Exit criteria

Participants may voluntarily withdraw from the study or exit the study due to safety concerns raised by clinical investigators or an inability to complete the follow-up visits. Data from patients who exit the study will be included in the analysis.

Data collection, management and quality control

Patient data will be collected by trained study personnel at each participating centre and entered into a centralised eCRF system. Baseline data will be collected during in-person visits. Follow-up data will be collected primarily during scheduled in-person visits and coordinated with routine clinical care where possible. If patients are unable to attend in-person visits, follow-up data will be collected via telephone interviews conducted by study personnel. All patient-reported information will be obtained through structured interviews to ensure consistency and data quality across sites.

Data will be entered into the eCRF system on a secure platform with real-time validation. Electronic data reside on institutional servers protected by firewalls, with hard copies stored in locked cabinets. Access is role-based and limited to study personnel. De-identified analytic datasets will be maintained with a separately stored linkage file.

Before study initiation, all sites will receive protocol training and readiness assessment. During the study, investigators follow institutional standard operating procedures and the study protocol to ensure timely and accurate data entry. Dedicated quality-control staff perform routine monitoring and source-data verification; at study close-out, documents will be reviewed and archived. Institutional quality assurance may audit sites and any non-compliance triggers corrective actions.

To minimise missing data, the eCRF system will include mandatory fields for key variables, range checks, logic checks and automated queries for incomplete or inconsistent entries. Regular data quality reports will be generated and sent to sites, with follow-up on unresolved issues by the coordinating centre. Site investigators and research coordinators will receive standardised training and refresher training when needed.

Timeliness and quality of data entry will be ensured through the eCRF system, which enables real-time monitoring, automated logic checks and query generation for missing or inconsistent data. A contracted research organisation will oversee patient enrolment and data entry progress, using real-time tracking, automated reminders and regular manual follow-ups to ensure timely completion.

Ongoing data quality control will include routine monitoring and interim data reviews every 3 months. Data queries will be issued and resolved in a timely manner to maintain data completeness and accuracy. To further promote timely data entry and coordination, regular investigator meetings will be held to review enrolment progress, data quality and site performance.

Statistical analysis plan

Baseline characteristics and outcomes will be summarised overall and by group. Continuous variables will be reported as mean (SD) or median (IQR) and compared using t-tests or non-parametric tests, as appropriate; categorical variables will be compared using Χ2 tests.

Given the limited and highly selected use of open surgery in current clinical practice, the primary comparative analysis will focus on RAS versus laparoscopic surgery. Patients undergoing open surgery will be described separately to characterise real-world use, patient profiles, surgical outcomes and costs. No formal statistical comparison between open surgery and either RAS or laparoscopic surgery will be conducted.

Time-to-event outcomes (DFS, OS) will be analysed using Kaplan-Meier estimates and Cox proportional hazards models; proportional hazards assumptions will be assessed. Binary outcomes (eg, 30-day complications) will use logistic regression; continuous outcomes (eg, blood loss, time to flatus) will use generalised linear models with appropriate distributions/links. Models will adjust for pre-specified confounders (demographics, socioeconomic status, disease factors, surgeon characteristics and centre). Clustering by centre will be addressed via mixed effects or cluster-robust inference.

Cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analyses will be conducted from the societal perspective. Utilities derived from EQ-5D-5L will use the Chinese value set.29 Both costs and QALYs will be discounted at 5% annually.28 30 Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios will be estimated for RAS versus laparoscopic surgery.

Given the observational design, we will use propensity score methods (matching, weighting or subclassification) to adjust for measured confounding, incorporating patient demographics, socioeconomic indicators, clinical severity and operator/centre characteristics. Balance will be assessed using standardised mean differences.

To assess robustness, we will embed empirical estimates into a 36-month decision tree for each surgical arm, incorporating perioperative complications, conversion to open surgery, 30-day readmission, 30-day reoperation and 30-day mortality as explicit branches, and accumulating arm-specific costs, oncologic outcomes, QALYs and surgeon comfort along terminal nodes; expected values will be computed via roll-back analysis.27 31 One-way sensitivity analysis will vary each parameter within an evidence-based range; if external bounds are unavailable, ±10% around base-case value will be applied.32 Probabilistic sensitivity analysis will run 10 000 Monte Carlo simulations using Gamma distributions for costs/resource use, Beta distribution for probabilities and utilities and log-normal for relative risks and times.33 34

Heterogeneity will be explored by the following subgroups, including age, sex, tumour height (mid vs low rectum), narrow-pelvis surrogates, receipt of neoadjuvant therapy, clinical stage, ASA class, baseline EQ-5D-5L, insurance scheme, socioeconomic strata, centre annual LAR volume and surgeon cumulative RAS experience.

The proportion of missing data will be reported for key variables. Patterns of missingness will be examined by treatment group, centre and follow-up time point. If missingness is limited, complete-case analysis may be used; otherwise, multiple imputation will be performed under the missing-at-random assumption. The imputation model will include treatment group, baseline covariates, centre, surgeon characteristics and relevant outcomes. Sensitivity analyses, including complete-case analysis and alternative missing-data assumptions where appropriate, will be conducted to assess robustness.

Discussion

This prospective multicentre cohort study is designed to evaluate the clinical and economic outcomes of RAS compared with laparoscopic surgery for mid- and low-rectal cancer, with additional descriptive analysis of open surgery in real-world practice.

Variation in surgeon and institutional experience is an important consideration when evaluating surgical technologies. As observed in prior surgical trials such as ROLARR,17 differences in the maturity of competing technologies and learning-curve effects may influence comparative effectiveness. In this study, participating centres include tertiary hospitals with varying levels of robotic surgery experience, reflecting real-world adoption in China. While this may introduce heterogeneity, it enhances the generalisability and policy relevance of the findings. We will address this through adjustment for surgeon- and centre-level experience and through subgroup analyses examining learning-curve effects.

The choice of EQ-5D-5L reflects the study’s health technology assessment objective, as it enables estimation of utility values and QALYs using a Chinese value set. However, EQ-5D-5L may not fully capture rectal cancer-specific functional outcomes. To complement this generic preference-based measure, postoperative functional outcomes, including urinary function, will also be collected.

The study has several strengths. First, it will collect prospective real-world data across 17 tertiary hospitals in geographically diverse regions of China and is the largest HTA study of RAS in rectal cancer to date and offers strong generalisability. Second, it integrates clinical outcomes, patient-reported measures, surgical team composition, surgeon comfort and cost data, allowing assessment from both clinical and health technology assessment perspectives. Third, standardised data collection through a centralised eCRF system, regular data quality monitoring and periodic data reviews will support data completeness and consistency across centres.

Limitations should be acknowledged. As an observational study, residual confounding may persist despite propensity score methods and multivariable adjustment. The predominance of high-volume tertiary centres may limit generalisability to smaller or lower-resource hospitals. In addition, the 3-year follow-up horizon may not capture lifetime clinical and economic outcomes. Recruitment into the open surgery group may be limited because open resection is increasingly reserved for selected or complex cases. As a result, open surgery patients may differ systematically from patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery, and residual confounding is likely even after statistical adjustment. Therefore, open surgery will be presented descriptively rather than included in formal comparative effectiveness analyses. In addition, intraoperative blood loss will be based on routinely recorded operative documentation and may be subject to estimation error; however, it is consistently captured across centres and reflects real-world clinical practice.

In summary, this study is designed to evaluate the effectiveness, costs and cost-effectiveness of RAS compared with laparoscopic surgery, with additional descriptive analyses of open surgery in real-world practice.
