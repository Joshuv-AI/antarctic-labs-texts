Risk Factors for a Systemic Inflammatory Response After Major Oral Cancer Surgery with Microvascular Free Flap Reconstruction: An Exploratory Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Noah-Benedikt Kukulenz, Tobias Schuerholz, Michael Dau, Bernhard Frerich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMicrovascular free flap reconstruction represents the standard approach for restoring bone and soft tissue defects following oral cancer resection. Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) is known to influence surgical outcomes, however the determinants and risk factors contributing to its development remain incompletely understood. This retrospective study aimed to identify risk factors for SIRS and their association with postoperative complications in-patients undergoing oral cancer surgery with simultaneous microvascular reconstruction.Material and MethodsA total of 123 consecutive patients (37 females and 86 males) who underwent oral cancer surgery with curative intent and primary defect reconstruction using microvascular free flaps at the university medical center of Rostock between January 2011 and December 2016 were included and analyzed. Data were retrospectively extracted from the institutional hospital information systems Copra, Medlinq and SAP.ResultsSIRS was observed in 51 cases (41.5%). Univariate analyses identified multiple significant predictors for the occurrence of SIRS (p < 0.05), including number of additional needed operations, anastomosis revisions, intraoperative blood loss, hemoglobin drop, postoperative blood transfusions, inflammatory markers, preoperative leukocyte count, postoperative ibuprofen and dexamethasone use, ICU stay and Clavien–Dindo score. In multivariate analysis, preoperative leukocyte count (adj. OR = 1.3, 95% CI: 1.16–1.79), postoperative transfusions (adj. OR = 1.003, 95% CI: 1.002–1.006), intraoperative blood loss (adj. OR = 1.002, 95% CI: 1.001–1.003), postoperative dexamethasone (adj. OR = 0.032, 95% CI: 0.002–0.62) and ibuprofen administration (adj. OR = 14.6, 95% CI: 1.3–163.70) remained independent predictors.ConclusionClinicians should be aware of risk factors associated with systemic inflammatory response after major oral cancer surgery. Preoperative leukocyte count and intraoperative blood loss should be given particular attention, as they emerged as critical independent predictors. The observed associations may suggest a protective association for dexamethasone and a risk-increasing association for ibuprofen administration. Both findings, however, should be regarded as exploratory signals rather than definitive clinical guidance. Given the limited number of exposed patients, potential causal relationships require further investigation.Keywords: systemic inflammatory response syndrome, oral cancer, risk factor, microvascular flaps, retrospective study, observational study

Introduction

The concept of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) was originally introduced in the early 1990s to describe a systemic reaction of the body to various insults such as major surgery, trauma, ischemia, or extensive burns.1,2 It is characterized by an excessive inflammatory host response, including massive cytokine release and activation of endothelial cells and platelets, leading to inflammatory cascades that may cause organ dysfunction or death. Although the SIRS criteria no longer form the basis of the current Sepsis-3 definition, they remain a pragmatic, rather sensitive and widely used tool for characterizing early systemic inflammatory responses in surgical and critical care research.1,3,4 The underlying pathophysiology is complex, and the exact mechanisms that trigger and modulate the severity of SIRS remain incompletely understood.1,2,5

In the context of major oral cancer surgery with microvascular free flap reconstruction, postoperative systemic inflammation may occur before an organ dysfunction becomes apparent and may reflect the combined effects of extensive surgical trauma, tissue ischemia of the flap, infection, and perioperative physiological stress.5–7 Patients undergoing major oral cancer surgery often present with a relevant comorbidity burden, including advanced age, frailty, malnutrition, tobacco- and alcohol-associated disease, and cardiovascular or pulmonary comorbidities, which may reduce physiological reserve and increase vulnerability to postoperative systemic inflammatory responses.8–10 Transfusion and blood loss may further contribute to systemic inflammatory activation, as shown in trauma and surgical cohorts.11,12 In turn, systemic inflammation may be associated with endothelial dysfunction and a procoagulant condition, which could potentially contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle of ischemia, inflammation, and physiological stress.5,13 Only a few studies have investigated SIRS-related factors in patients with oral cancer undergoing microvascular flap reconstruction.7 However, evidence from other surgical specialties, such as cardiac surgery, suggests that multiple factors are likely to contribute to the development of postoperative SIRS.14–19 In particular, prolonged flap ischemia time, microvascular compromise, surgical re-exploration, and anastomotic revision and subsequent reperfusion or revascularization may contribute to an inflammatory response through ischemia-reperfusion mechanisms.5,13

Perioperative pharmacological modulation of inflammation may further influence this response. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), including ibuprofen, are commonly used as part of multimodal postoperative analgesia and may reduce pain, opioid requirements, and inflammatory activity.20 However, their use in microvascular reconstructive surgery remains clinically relevant because inhibition of cyclooxygenase pathways may affect platelet function and bleeding risk, and postoperative hematoma may compromise flap perfusion or necessitate surgical re-exploration. Glucocorticoids such as dexamethasone are administered in major head and neck surgery to reduce postoperative edema, nausea, and inflammatory symptoms. Despite these potential benefits, glucocorticoids may also influence immune regulation, leukocyte dynamics, glucose metabolism, wound healing, susceptibility to infection, and coagulation pathways.21–23 Both NSAIDs and glucocorticoids represent plausible perioperative factors that may modulate early systemic inflammatory responses after microvascular free flap reconstruction.

For retrospective analyses, SIRS criteria offer the additional advantage that they are based on routinely documented clinical and laboratory parameters, allowing standardized assessment across larger patient cohorts.1,3 Accordingly, in the present study, SIRS was not used as a surrogate definition of sepsis, but as a sensitive operational marker of early postoperative systemic inflammatory response after major oral cancer surgery with microvascular free flap reconstruction. This approach enables the identification of perioperative and flap-related risk factors associated with early inflammatory deterioration, bearing in mind that SIRS is not specific to infections and does not capture organ dysfunction, as highlighted in Sepsis-3.4,24

The aim of this exploratory study was to identify risk factors for SIRS in patients undergoing oral cancer surgery with microvascular flap reconstruction using multivariate analysis. A better understanding of these factors and further subsequent investigation based on the study may allow earlier recognition of patient-specific risk for developing systemic inflammatory response syndrome, even prior to surgery. This is particular important, as SIRS adversely affects the outcome in often multimorbid patients and increases the risk of severe complications.

Materials and Methods

In this retrospective anonymized cohort study, 123 patients (86 males and 37 females) treated at the Department of Oral, Maxillofacial and Facial Plastic Surgery, University Medical Centre Rostock (Rostock, Germany) were included. All patients underwent oral cancer surgery with simultaneous defect reconstruction using a microvascular flap. Ethical approval (A2018-0148) was obtained from the institutional ethics committee of the University Medical Center Rostock in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.25 Surgical procedures were performed between January 2011 and December 2016, with a minimum follow-up of two years after the primary operation. Inclusion criteria were (1) age ≥ 18 years and (2) simultaneous resection of an oral tumor and reconstruction with a microvascular flap. Patients undergoing repeat microvascular flap reconstruction after a previous operation within the same treatment course were excluded, as the preceding intervention may have already increased systemic inflammatory parameters and thereby biased the assessment of postoperative SIRS.

The diagnosis of SIRS was based on the definition proposed by Bone et al and was considered fulfilled, if at least two of the following criteria were present for a minimum of 24 hours: 1. body temperature above 38°C or below 36°C, 2. heart rate above 90 beats/minute, 3. respiratory rate above 20/minute or the PaCO2 less than 32 mmHg and 4. leukocyte count below 4000/mm3 or greater than 12000/mm3 or more than 10% immature leukocytes. For further analyses, patients were divided into two subgroups – a SIRS and a non-SIRS group – according to these predefined parameters.1

Clinical variables were categorized into three categories. Preoperative patient-related parameters included sex, age, tobacco and alcohol consumption, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular and kidney comorbidities, body mass index and preoperative leukocyte levels. Surgical procedure parameters comprised the type of microvascular flap, neck dissection, tracheotomy and bone resection. Perioperative (intra- and postoperative) course variables included duration of surgery and anesthesia, blood loss, hemoglobin decrease, transfusion requirements, intraoperative sodium level, postoperative C-reactive protein and procalcitonin levels, mean hemoglobin level and their course, Clavien-Dindo-classification of postoperative complications, number of surgical interventions and anastomosis revisions, duration of the postoperative intensive care unit stay, as well as the administration of analgesics, catecholamines, and tranquillizers. In addition, the 2-year mortality was assessed to evaluate the potential impact of SIRS on patient survival.

Anesthesiologic and perioperative data were extracted from the institutional Medlinq and COPRA information systems, while surgical data were obtained from the SAP-based clinical documentation system. After pseudonymization, data from the different systems were merged using the institutional patient identification number. Due to the retrospective design, some variables, particularly inflammatory laboratory markers such as CRP and PCT, were not available for all patients. Missing laboratory values were not imputed; analyses involving these variables were performed as available-case analyses, with the number of available observations reported for each parameter. Patients with incomplete data for individual variables were retained in the overall cohort to avoid unnecessary loss of sample size and potential selection bias. The study was reported in accordance with the RECORD recommendations for routinely collected observational health data.

Statistics

Data collection and statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel 16.78 (Microsoft Inc., Redmond, WA, USA) and SPSS Statistics 27.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Figure 1 was created with Novak´s TriNetX Forrest Plot Generator v1.57.0 (Streamlit, CA, USA). Figure 2 was created with AI Flowchart Generator (Eraser Labs Inc., CA, USA). Associations between variables and the risk of developing SIRS were assessed using fisher exact test and Chi-squared test for categorial parameters. The metric parameters were first tested for their normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. After that the t-test used for independent samples for normally distributed parameters and the Mann–Whitney-U-test for non-normally distributed ordinal variables. The univariate significant parameters were integrated into a binary logistic regression model following multivariate analyses and the adjusted odds ratios (adj. OR) were calculated. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (Figure 2).

Results

Overall, SIRS was observed in 51 cases (41.5%). Analysis of preoperative variables, such as sex, regular tobacco and alcohol consumption (anamnestic), and preexisting conditions including diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular diseases, showed no significant association with SIRS. Among preoperative factors, only preoperative leukocyte count was significant higher in the SIRS group (p = 0.001). Surgical procedure parameters, including the type of flap, primary vs secondary reconstruction or other examined operative procedures (tracheotomy, neck dissection or the bone resection) also showed no correlation with SIRS. However, univariate analyses of perioperative course variables revealed a significantly higher total number of surgical procedures during hospitalization in the SIRS group (1.9 ± 1.3) compared with the non-SIRS group (1.4 ± 0.8; p = 0.025). A greater number of procedures was associated with increased revision surgery of microvascular anastomosis. Furthermore, patients in the SIRS group experienced greater intraoperative blood loss (728 ± 786 mL vs 299 ± 439 mL; p = 0.001) and hemoglobin decrease (1.7 ± 1.02 vs 1.1 ± 0.86; p = 0.002), which corresponded to higher postoperative (793 ± 1108 mL versus 146 ± 306 mL; p = 0.001) and cumulative blood transfusion requirements (1150 ± 1353 mL to 404 ± 655 mL; p = 0.001). Laboratory parameters such as C-reactive protein and procalcitonin levels were also significantly elevated in the SIRS group (each p = 0.001). In addition, a higher Clavien-Dindo classification score and more frequent postoperative revisions of microvascular anastomoses were significantly associated with SIRS development (p = 0.001). Regarding drug-related factors, univariate analysis revealed a significant association between the occurrence of SIRS and exposure to dexamethasone (p = 0.007), as well the ibuprofen (p = 0.008) (Table 1).

Discussion

Although the SIRS criteria have been widely used in surgical and critical care research, their limited specificity has been questioned, and current sepsis definitions (Sepsis-3) place greater emphasis on organ dysfunction rather than systemic inflammation alone.4 However, both approaches have distinct strengths and limitations. SIRS-based criteria are simple to assess and highly sensitive, and may therefore identify early postoperative inflammatory deterioration before overt organ dysfunction becomes apparent. This potential advantage comes at the expense of specificity, particularly after major surgery, where non-infectious inflammatory responses are common. In contrast, SOFA-based assessment provides a more organ dysfunction-oriented and clinically specific framework, but its greater complexity and dependence on laboratory parameters may limit its applicability outside the intensive care unit or in resource-limited settings. Future studies should therefore evaluate combined or refined approaches that preserve the early warning value of SIRS while incorporating organ dysfunction parameters to improve diagnostic accuracy and guide timely clinical management.3

To date, no studies have specifically investigated the risk factors for SIRS in oral cancer undergoing microvascular flap reconstruction. However, recent studies in other major surgical procedures suggest the existence of potential risk factors, with reported SIRS rates ranging from 23.8% to 91% in cardiac, vascular, and general surgery.13,15,16,26 In the present study, SIRS occurred in 41.5% of cases, placing these results in the lower mid-range compared with these reports. Unlike many of the aforementioned studies, which relied solely on univariate analyses, our study also employed multivariate analysis to identify independent risk factors. Although the investigated surgical procedures differ substantially, these findings are broadly comparable and provide a useful basis for interpreting our results.

Findings from comparable oral cancer cohorts further support the relevance of intraoperative blood loss as a marker of postoperative risk. The study by Hasegawa et al is particularly relevant because of the comparable patient population, underlying disease, and extent of surgery.10 They identified high intraoperative blood loss as a significant risk factor for postoperative delirium. Similarly, in our cohort, intraoperative blood loss emerged as an independent factor associated with SIRS. Together with further reports linking blood loss to postoperative complications after oral cancer surgery,27,28 these findings suggest that blood loss may represent a common marker of surgical burden and postoperative vulnerability, affecting both inflammatory and neurological outcomes.

Similar findings were reported by Kasahara et al, who identified a significant correlation between blood loss and SIRS in patients undergoing orthognathic surgery.29 Furthermore, postoperative blood transfusions, which are closely related to intraoperative blood loss, were significantly associated with SIRS in our binary logistic regression model. This relationship between transfusion requirement and the development of SIRS has been described in multiple studies.6,10–12,30

Preoperatively elevated leukocyte levels emerged as an independent risk factor for SIRS, highlighting their prognostic significance. This finding is supported by Hensel et al who reported similar associations in cardiac surgery.31 Unlike most previous studies, which focused on postoperative leukocyte measurements,32 our study included preoperative values, emphasizing the importance of performing surgery during inflammation-free intervals an addressing any existing inflammation preoperatively or perioperatively to minimize the risk of SIRS. Moreover, our univariate analysis corroborates previous report linking elevated preoperative PCT and CRP levels with an increased risk of SIRS.32 However, given the smaller number of available cases for these inflammatory markers, these findings require confirmation in further studies.

With regard to the investigated medications, both univariate and multivariate analyses showed significant associations between the occurrence of SIRS and postoperative administration of ibuprofen and dexamethasone. Drug exposure was analyzed only as postoperative administration yes/no. Dose, timing, duration, route of administration, and clinical indication were not consistently available. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than causal, and require confirmation in future prospective studies with standardized documentation of medication exposure. Dexamethasone was associated with lower odds of SIRS (OR = 0.032), whereas ibuprofen was associated with higher odds of SIRS (OR = 14.6) (Table 2 and Figure 1). The restraint use of postoperative corticosteroids in out cohort reflected institutional concerns that glucocorticoids might adversely affect perfusion in compromised microvascular flaps and could potentially interfere with immune-mediated tumor surveillance. Accordingly, only a limited number of patients received Dexamethasone perioperatively. The role of glucocorticoids in this setting remains controversial. Kilger et al reported findings consistent with a potential protective effect of perioperative glucocorticoid administration,22 whereas other studies have described conflicting results, including increased inflammatory responses,5 or no significant effect on SIRS development.21 Therefore, the observed association between dexamethasone and lower odds of SIRS in the present study should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of a causal protective effect.

Similarly, evidence regarding perioperative ibuprofen administration remains inconsistent. Rossaint et al5 also observed an association between perioperative ibuprofen use and an increased risk of SIRS, in line with our findings. In contrast, other researchers have described an anti-inflammatory effect of ibuprofen, suggesting a reduced risk of developing SIRS. Although our data only indicated a statistical trend towards an association between SIRS and longer duration of the operation (p = 0.06), several studies have demonstrated a significant link between extended operative times and postoperative inflammatory complications. This relationship is likely mediated by the strong connection between longer surgical procedures and increased intraoperative blood loss. The absence of significance in our cohort may be attributed to relatively standardized operative times resulting from uniform surgical protocols.6–8,27,29,30 However, similar to dexamethasone, this can only be considered a hypothesis and further studies should be conducted.

When interpreting the study results, some limitations must be taken into account. Due to the retrospective design, SOFA and qSOFA scores could not be reliably reconstructed. Several score components, including standardized neurological assessment, vasopressor dosage and timing, arterial oxygenation parameters, and complete time-matched laboratory values, were not consistently available across the cohort. Therefore, the study cannot determine whether the observed SIRS-associated factors reflect clinically meaningful organ dysfunction as defined by Sepsis-3 or rather transient postoperative physiological responses after major surgery. Further prospective studies with standardized SOFA/qSOFA assessment might better distinguish early systemic inflammation from sepsis-associated organ dysfunction.

Second, the retrospective design resulted in incomplete availability of some laboratory parameters, particularly PCT and CRP. This was partly related to paper-based documentation of earlier cases and incomplete retrievability of laboratory records. Moreover, selective measurement, especially of PCT in patients with suspected infection or clinical deterioration, cannot be excluded. Thus, selection bias may have influenced the interpretation of these inflammatory markers.

Third, although multicollinearity diagnostics did not indicate relevant collinearity among the included predictors, the limited number of SIRS events and the sparse exposure distributions for some variables, particularly postoperative ibuprofen and dexamethasone administration, may have contributed to sparse-data bias, unstable coefficient estimates, and wide confidence intervals. These findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory or causal.

The drug-related associations require particular caution. Postoperative medication exposure was analyzed only as administration yes/no, without standardized information on indication, dose, timing, route of administration, or duration. Accordingly, confounding by indication and reverse causation cannot be excluded. Ibuprofen may have been administered in response to early postoperative fever, pain, or inflammatory symptoms and may therefore reflect early manifestations of an evolving systemic inflammatory response rather than a causal risk factor. Similarly, the apparent association between dexamethasone administration and lower odds of SIRS may reflect residual confounding, institutional prescribing preferences, selection bias, or sparse exposure data rather than a true protective pharmacological effect. Prospective studies with standardized documentation of medication indication, dose, timing, and duration are needed to clarify these associations.

Clavien–Dindo grading was not included in the final multivariable regression model because it reflects postoperative complications that may occur concurrently with or after the onset of SIRS. Its inclusion as a predictor could therefore introduce temporal ambiguity and potential overadjustment. Clavien–Dindo classification was instead considered descriptively as an indicator of postoperative morbidity.

Conclusion

The presented study identified several factors associated with the development of SIRS in oral cancer surgery with simultaneous defection reconstruction using microvascular flaps. Elevated preoperative leukocyte count, increased intraoperative blood loss, greater postoperative blood transfusion volumes, and postoperative ibuprofen administration were found to promote the development of systemic inflammatory response syndrome to various degrees. Patients with these risk factors should therefore be monitored more closely during the peri- and postoperative period to enable early detection of SIRS. In particular, the preoperatively determined leukocyte count is of great benefit, as it allows potential risk patients to be identified preoperatively in order to prevent the development of SIRS. Moreover, perioperative administration of dexamethasone appeared to have a protective effect against SIRS development. The findings regarding postoperative dexamethasone and ibuprofen administration should be regarded as exploratory, particularly due to the limited number of exposed patients and the resulting statistical uncertainty. These associations require further validation in large-scale prospective studies and, where appropriate, randomized controlled trials, and should not currently be interpreted as causal effects or definitive clinical guidance.
