Spectral Density and Eigenvector Nonorthogonality in Complex Symmetric Random Matrices
Gernot Akemann, Yan V. Fyodorov, Dmitry V. Savin

Abstract
Non-Hermitian random matrices with statistical spectral characteristics beyond the standard Ginibre ensembles have recently emerged in the description of dissipative quantum many-body systems as well as in non-ergodic wave transport in complex media. We investigate the class AI † of complex symmetric random matrices, for which available analytic results remain scarce. Using a recently proposed framework by one of the authors, we analyze this class for Gaussian entries and derive an explicit, closed-form expression for the joint distribution of a complex eigenvalue and its right eigenvector for arbitrary matrix size N ≥ 2 N\geq 2 in the entire complex plane. From this, we obtain the distribution of the eigenvector non-orthogonality overlap and the mean eigenvalue density, both for finite N N and in the large- N N limit. Notably, at the spectral edge both the eigenvalue density and eigenvector statistics exhibits a limiting behavior that differs from the Ginibre universtality class. This behavior is expected to be universal, as further supported by numerical evidence for Bernoulli random matrices.

To analyze these systems, it is essential to recall a key algebraic property: any generic non-Hermitian random matrix
J

J\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=z_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{\dagger}J=z_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{\dagger}\,,

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}

\boldsymbol{u}^{{\dagger}}_{k}\boldsymbol{v}_{l}=0

Main results
.—In this Letter we derive exact results for the joint probability density function (JPDF)
\mathcal{P}(z,\boldsymbol{v})
of a complex eigenvalue
z
and the associated right eigenvector
\boldsymbol{v}
of a random complex symmetric matrix
J=X+iY
, where
X
and
Y
are two independent copies of the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (GOE) of size
N
. The latter is characterized by real Gaussian-distributed entries with mean zero and the covariances
\left\langle X_{nm}X_{kl}\right\rangle=N^{-1}(\delta_{nk}\delta_{ml}+\delta_{nl}\delta_{mk})
, and similarly for
Y
, where
\left\langle\cdots\right\rangle
denotes the ensemble average.

Working with complex symmetric matrices we found it more natural to normalize the left and right eigenvectors as
\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{u}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{u}=1
rather than
\boldsymbol{u}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}=1
. It is important to note that due to the symmetry
J^{T}=J
the corresponding left eigenvectors are related to the right ones as
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}=\boldsymbol{v}
, where the star denotes complex conjugation. This enables us to express the associated diagonal entry of the overlap matrix solely in terms of
\boldsymbol{v}
, which in our normalization becomes

\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{diag}}=\frac{(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{u}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{u})}{|\boldsymbol{u}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}}=\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}}\equiv 1+t,

(2)

where the parameter
t\geq 0
quantifies the degree of eigenvector nonorthogonality. In particular, this implies that for complex symmetric matrices the knowledge of
\mathcal{P}(z,\boldsymbol{v})
provides the full description of both the spectral density and the nonorthogonality statistics in the entire complex plane.

To evaluate such an object we follow the general framework for non-Hermitian random matrices recently developed by one of us
Fyodorov 2025
, which extends the Kac-Rice approach to complex eigenvalue problems. We begin with the following representation
Sommers and Iida 1994
;
Fyodorov 2025
:

\mathcal{P}(z,\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{\delta(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}-1)}{\pi N}\left\langle\delta^{(2)}[(J-z)\boldsymbol{v}]\left|\frac{d}{dz}\det(J-z)\right|^{2}\right\rangle\,.

(3)

Here
\delta^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{v})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\delta(\mathrm{Re\,}v_{i})\delta(\mathrm{Im\,}v_{i})
is the delta-function of a complex-valued vector argument,
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{N})^{T}
. The statistical averaging over the ensemble can be performed exactly using a variant of the supersymmetry technique tailored to this problem, as detailed in the Supplemental Material (SM)
SM
. In this way one arrives at an exact integral representation for the JPDF valid for all
N\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(z,\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}-1)N^{2N+1}}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dq\,q^{3}e^{-\frac{N}{2}q^{2}}\int\!\frac{d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{2N}}

\displaystyle\times e^{-\frac{N}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(z,\boldsymbol{v})}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{z}\,\partial{z}^{*}}(q^{2}+|z|^{2})^{N-4}D_{q}(z,\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{k})\,,

(4)

with
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(z,\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k}+|\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}+z(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})-z^{*}(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})^{*}
acting as an effective Lagrangian governing the integration over the complex
N
-dimensional vector field
\boldsymbol{k}
and
D_{q}(z,\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{k})
being

D_{q}(z,\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{k})=\det\left[(q^{2}+|z|^{2})\mathbb{I}_{4}-\begin{pmatrix}zA&qB\\
qB^{*}&-z^{*}A^{*}\end{pmatrix}\right]\,.

(5)

Here, the
2\times 2
matrices
A
and
B
are given by

A=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}&\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{k}&\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{k}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}&1\\
\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k}&\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k}\end{pmatrix}\,.

The advantage of the obtained representation lies in the quadratic dependence of the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(z,\boldsymbol{v})
on the complex vector
\boldsymbol{k}
. This structure enables an exact evaluation of the
\boldsymbol{k}
-integration in Eq. (

) via Wick’s theorem, as the integrand is a polynomial in
\boldsymbol{k}
in the pre-exponential factor. While algebraically intensive, the computation can be efficiently handled using the method developed in the SM (see Sec.
S-II
of
SM
for details). The final result takes the following form:

\mathcal{P}(z,\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{\delta(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}-1)}{8\pi^{N+1}}|\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}g\left(\frac{N|z|^{2}}{2},|\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\right)\,,

(6)

where the function
g(x,y)
is defined by

\displaystyle g(x,y)=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{xy/2}}{N(N-1)}\left\{x^{N}e^{-x}(N-1-x+xy)+\Gamma(N,x)\right.

\displaystyle\times\left.\left[(N-1)(N-2x+xy)+(1-y)x^{2}\right]\right\},

(7)

with
x=\frac{N}{2}|z|^{2}
,
y=|\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}
, and the upper incomplete gamma function
\Gamma(a,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{a-1}e^{-t}dt
.

Spectral density
.—
As a first application of the above result, we evaluate the eigenvalue density at finite
N
. This density has rotational symmetry and depends only on the radial coordinate
r=|z|
. Hence, it is natural to introduce the radial density
\rho(r)=\pi r\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{v}\mathcal{P}(z,\boldsymbol{v})
, normalized as
\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\rho(r)dr=1
. Integrating the joint distribution (
6
) over
\boldsymbol{v}
can be performed using the following identity, valid for any
N\geq 2
and any well-behaved function
f

Fyodorov 2025
:

\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{v}\delta(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}-1)f(|\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dp\,p^{N-2}\pi^{N}}{\Gamma(N-1)}f(1-p^{2})\,.

(8)

Applying this to the radial density leads to the final result

\displaystyle\rho(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\Gamma(N+2)}\left\{\sqrt{\frac{x}{2}}\left[k^{2}(x)+\left(N-\frac{x}{2}\right)\Gamma(N,x)\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+2^{\frac{N}{2}}\gamma\left(\frac{N+3}{2},\frac{x}{2}\right)\left[k(x)+\frac{(N-1-x)\Gamma(N,x)}{2k(x)}\right]\right\},

(9)

where we have introduced
k(x)=x^{N/2}e^{-x/2}
,
x=\frac{N}{2}r^{2}
, and
\gamma(a,x)=\Gamma(a)-\Gamma(a,x)
for the lower incomplete gamma function. The density behaves as
\rho(r)=\frac{N}{N+1}r+O(r^{2})
near the origin, reaches its maximum around
r\approx\sqrt{2}
and becomes exponentially suppressed when
r\gg 1
, see Fig.

Universal large-
N
results
.— The obtained expressions are well suited for the asymptotic analysis in the limit
N\gg 1
, when spectral characteristics are expected to become universal. To this end, it is convenient to introduce a scaled variable
s=\sqrt{N}(\frac{x}{N}-1)=\sqrt{N}(\frac{r^{2}}{2}-1)

Discussion
.—In summary, we have derived and analyzed the eigenvalue density and the distribution of the diagonal non-orthogonality factor
t=\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{diag}}-1
for complex symmetric Gaussian matrices of arbitrary size
N\geq 2
. To address this challenging problem rigorously, we developed a powerful approach that provides simultaneously direct access to eigenvalues and eigenvectors of this class of non-Hermitian random matrices. We expect this framework to be broadly applicable to other non-Hermitian settings. In the large-
N
limit, when the results serve as universal representatives of the class AI
†
of quantum chaotic dissipative systems, the global properties in the spectral bulk resemble those of the standard Ginibre matrices. In contrast, the spectral edge statistics exhibit clear and distinctive features specific to this symmetry class, highlighting a fundamental departure from Ginibre universality. The different bulk and edge scaling forms of the non-orthogonality distribution established here should prove important for future applications, e.g., in quantifying universal relaxation behavior in Markovian open quantum systems
Bao 2025
.

We further note that, as argued already by Chalker and Mehlig
Mehlig and Chalker 2000
, large values of the non-orthogonality factor are controlled by rare events where an eigenvalue
z_{k}
comes anomalously close to another eigenvalue. This mechanism was made rigorous for the Ginibre case by the representation of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{diag}}
in Theorem 2.2 of
Bourgade and Dubach 2020
. Hence the fact that the
t\gg 1
tail of the non-orthogonality factor distribution for complex symmetric random matrices retains the same cubic form as in complex Ginibre
Bourgade and Dubach 2020
;
Fyodorov 2018
for any
N\geq 2
strongly suggests that eigenvalue repulsion in the bulk is governed primarily by the same factor
|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{2}
(possibly with logarithmic corrections known to occur for
N=2

Jaiswal et al. 2019
;
Hamazaki et al. 2020
).
Reconciling this observation with the excellent numerical fit of
eigenvalue spacing distributions with
\beta=1.4
Coulomb gas statistics
Akemann et al. 2022
;
Akemann et al. 2025b
remains an open challenge for theory.

To establish the large-
N
behavior of the spectral density, we require the leading-order asymptotic forms of the function
k(x)=x^{N/2}e^{-x/2}
and of the incomplete lower and upper gamma-functions appearing in Eq. (
Spectral Density and Eigenvector Nonorthogonality in Complex Symmetric Random Matrices
) at
N\to\infty
. These forms depend on the spectral parameter
r
. Accordingly, two different regimes arise: the global scaling limit in the bulk, where
x=Np
and
p\in[0,1)
is fixed, and the local scaling limit at the edge, where
p=1+\frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}
with
s
fixed. Using standard asymptotic analysis
NIS
, we obtain the required form

\frac{k(Np)}{N^{N/2}}\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}p^{\frac{N}{2}}e^{-Np/2},&\quad\mbox{bulk}\\
e^{-N/2}e^{-s^{2}/4},&\quad\mbox{edge}\end{array}\right..

(A1)

The corresponding asymptotic forms for the incomplete lower and upper gamma-functions are respectively given by

\frac{\gamma\left(\frac{N+3}{2},\frac{Np}{2}\right)}{(N/2)^{\frac{N+1}{2}}}\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}p^{\frac{N+3}{2}}e^{-Np/2}\frac{1}{1-p},&\quad\mbox{bulk}\\
e^{-N/2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\mbox{erfc}{\left(-\frac{s}{2}\right)},&\quad\mbox{edge}\end{array}\right.

(A2)

and

\frac{\Gamma\left(N,Np\right)}{\Gamma(N)}\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1,&\quad\mbox{bulk}\\
\frac{1}{2}\mbox{erfc}{\left(\frac{s}{\sqrt{2}}\right)},&\quad\mbox{edge}\end{array}\right..

(A3)

For the distribution
P_{r}(t)
given by Eq. (

N

from Eq. (

P_{0}(t)\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{1}{4N\tau^{3}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\tau}},&\quad\mbox{bulk}\\
\frac{\sqrt{N}}{4\sigma^{3}}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2\sigma}+\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}},&\quad\mbox{edge}\end{array}\right..

(A4)

Finally, for the function

(A5)

e^{xy/2}=\exp\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma}\left(\sqrt{N}+\frac{s-\frac{1}{\sigma}}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\sigma}}\right)\right]\,,

“Spectral Density and Eigenvector Nonorthogonality in Complex Symmetric Random Matrices”

by Gernot Akemann, Yan V. Fyodorov, and Dmitry V. Savin

This Supplemental Material provides additional details of the calculations outlined in the main text. The first section describes the approach used to derive the integral representation (
Spectral Density and Eigenvector Nonorthogonality in Complex Symmetric Random Matrices
). The following section develops an efficient integration method based on Wick’s theorem, leading to the main final result (
6
).

S-I
Supersymmetry method and integral representation

S-I.1
Ensemble averages

We will use the simplified notations
\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}=(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b})
and
\boldsymbol{a}\boldsymbol{b}^{T}=\boldsymbol{a}\otimes\boldsymbol{b}
to denote the inner and outer products, respectively.
It is convenient to start with the form
J=X+iY
and represent the eigenvalue problem for a complex eigenvalue
z=\lambda_{1}+i\lambda_{2}
with the right eigenvector
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{s}+i\boldsymbol{t}
in terms of the relevant real (
X,\lambda_{1},\boldsymbol{s}
) and imaginary (
Y,\lambda_{2},\boldsymbol{t}
) parts. The eigenvector is normalized according to
\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{s}^{T}\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{t}^{T}\boldsymbol{t}=1
. Our starting point is the formula (
3
) which can then be expressed as

\mathcal{P}(z,\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{\delta(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}-1)}{\pi N}\left\langle\delta\left[(X-\lambda_{1})\boldsymbol{s}-(Y-\lambda_{2})\boldsymbol{t}\right]\delta\left[(Y-\lambda_{2})\boldsymbol{s}+(X-\lambda_{1})\boldsymbol{t}\right]\left|\frac{d}{dz}\det(J-z)\right|^{2}\right\rangle,

(S1)

involving only the delta-functions of a real-valued vector argument. We can now employ the Fourier representation

\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{\sigma})\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{N}\delta(x_{\sigma,i})=\int\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{\sigma}}{(2\pi)^{N}}\exp(i\boldsymbol{k}_{\sigma}^{T}\boldsymbol{a}_{\sigma})\,,\qquad d\boldsymbol{k}_{\sigma}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}dk_{\sigma,i}\,,\quad\sigma=1,2\,,

(S2)

for each of the two vector delta-functions in the product
\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
above and cast the JPDF as follows:

\mathcal{P}(z,\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{\delta(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}-1)}{\pi N}\iint\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{1}d\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2N}}e^{-i\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{t})+i\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{s})}\left\langle e^{i\mathrm{Tr}[X(\boldsymbol{s}\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}+\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T})]+i\mathrm{Tr}[Y(\boldsymbol{s}\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}-\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T})]}\left|\frac{d}{dz}\det(J-z)\right|^{2}\right\rangle.

(S3)

Here, we have used the identity
f(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{b})=f(\mathrm{Tr}[\boldsymbol{b}\boldsymbol{a}^{T}])
which follows from the invariance of a trace under cyclic permutations.

Next, we can use a Hermitization trick

\left|\frac{d}{dz}\det(J-z)\right|^{2}=(-1)^{N}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{z}\,\partial{z^{*}}}\det\begin{pmatrix}0&J-z\\
J^{\dagger}-z^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}

by doubling the matrix dimension, and then represent the determinant as the Gaussian
integral of Berezin type

\det\begin{pmatrix}0&J-z\\
J^{\dagger}-z^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}=(-1)^{N}\int D(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\phi})\exp\left[-(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T})\begin{pmatrix}0&J-z\\
J^{\dagger}-z^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right],

(S4)

where the vectors
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\sigma}=(\psi_{\sigma,1},\ldots,\psi_{\sigma,N})^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\sigma}=(\phi_{\sigma,1},\ldots,\phi_{\sigma,N})^{T}
, with
\sigma=1,2
, contain anticommuning (Grassmann) variables only, and the integration measure
D(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\prod_{\sigma=1,2}\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\psi_{\sigma,i}d\phi_{\sigma,i}
. All these steps combined, the result is

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(z,\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{\delta(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}-1)}{\pi N}\!\!\iint\!\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{1}d\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2N}}e^{-i\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{t})+i\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{s})}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{z}\,\partial{z^{*}}}\!\!\int\!D(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\phi})e^{z(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})+z^{*}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})}\left\langle e^{i\mathrm{Tr}(XT_{x})+i\mathrm{Tr}(YT_{y})}\right\rangle,

(S5)

where we have introduced the following two matrices:

T_{x}=(\boldsymbol{s}\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}+\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T})-i(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}+\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T})\quad\mbox{and}\quad T_{y}=(\boldsymbol{s}\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}-\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T})+(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T})\,.

(S6)

The obtained expression (
S5
) is well suited for statistical averaging. Since
X
and
Y
are statistically independent, the last term
\left\langle\cdots\right\rangle
in Eq. (
S5
) factorizes into the product
\left\langle e^{i\mathrm{Tr}(XT_{x})}\right\rangle_{X}\left\langle e^{i\mathrm{Tr}(YT_{y})}\right\rangle_{Y}
, representing two separate averages over GOE matrices. Due to the symmetry
X^{T}=X
, the GOE averaging requires symmetrization and can be carried over as follows:

\left\langle e^{i\mathrm{Tr}(XT_{x})}\right\rangle_{X}=\left\langle e^{\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[X(T_{x}+T_{x}^{T})]}\right\rangle_{X}=e^{-\frac{1}{4N}\mathrm{Tr}(T_{x}+T_{x}^{T})^{2}}

(S7)

and similarly for
Y
. To this end, it is convenient to specify the symmetrized versions of the matrices
T_{x,y}
and introduce

\begin{array}[]{l}T_{x}+T_{x}^{T}=(\boldsymbol{s}\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}+\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\boldsymbol{s}^{T}+\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2}\boldsymbol{t}^{T})-i(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{T}+\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}^{T})\equiv B_{+}-iF_{+}\\[4.30554pt]
T_{y}+T_{y}^{T}=(\boldsymbol{s}\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2}\boldsymbol{s}^{T}-\boldsymbol{t}\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}-\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\boldsymbol{t}^{T})+(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{T}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}+\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}^{T})\equiv B_{-}+F_{-}\end{array}\,.

(S8)

Note that these matrices contain both ordinary (“bosonic”,
B_{\pm}
) and nilpotent (“fermionic”,
F_{\pm}
) parts arising from anticommuting variables. In particular, this leads to the useful identity
\mathrm{Tr}(F_{\pm})^{2}=\mp 4[(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})-(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1})]
.
Substituting these forms into (
S7
), we arrive after straightforward but lengthy algebra at the average factor expressed as

\displaystyle\left\langle e^{i\mathrm{Tr}(XT_{x})+i\mathrm{Tr}(YT_{y})}\right\rangle=I_{BB}I_{BF}I_{FF}\,,

(S9)

which in turn leads to the following integral representation of the JPDF:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(z,\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{\delta(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}-1)}{\pi N}\!\!\iint\!\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}_{1}d\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2N}}e^{-i\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{t})+i\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{s})}I_{BB}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{z}\,\partial{z^{*}}}\!\!\int\!D(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\phi})e^{z(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})+z^{*}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})}I_{BF}I_{FF},

(S10)

where

\displaystyle I_{BB}=e^{-\frac{1}{2N}[\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{k}_{2}+(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{s})^{2}+(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{t})^{2}+(\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{s})^{2}+(\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{t})^{2}+2(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{t})(\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{s})^{2}-2(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{s})(\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{t})^{2}]}\,,

(S11a)

\displaystyle I_{BF}=e^{-\frac{1}{2N}[\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}(iB_{+}-B_{-})\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{T}(-iB_{+}+B_{-})\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}(iB_{+}+B_{-})\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}^{T}(iB_{+}+B_{-})\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}]}\,,

(S11b)

\displaystyle I_{FF}=e^{-\frac{2}{N}[(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})-(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1})]}\,.

(S11c)

As is typical for the supersymmetry approach, the resulting expression is bi-quadratic in the integration vectors. Conventional techniques such as the Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation or bosonization are usually employed to perform the integration by reducing it to one over a certain supermatrix. However, we found those methods to be less efficient here due to the algebraic complexity of the expressions involved. To overcome this difficulty, we adopt a different strategy: we first carry out the exact integration over the anticommuting variables. This reduces the problem to an integration involving a Pfaffian, as described next.

S-I.2
Reduction to a Pfaffian problem

To integrate over the Grassmann variables, we first need to decouple the bi-quadratic terms appearing in the factor
I_{FF}
of Eq. (
S9
). This can be accomplished using the following identities, valid for any real
a,b
:

e^{\frac{1}{N}ab}=\frac{N}{\pi}\int d^{2}qe^{-N|q|^{2}-qa-q^{*}b}\quad\mbox{and}\quad e^{-\frac{1}{N}ab}=\frac{N}{\pi}\int d^{2}qe^{-N|q|^{2}-iqa-iq^{*}b}\,,\qquad d^{2}q=d(\textrm{Re}{q})\,d(\textrm{Im}{q})\,.

(S12)

Introducing auxiliary integrations over two complex variables
q_{1,2}
, we obtain

I_{FF}=\left(\frac{N}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\iint d^{2}q_{1}d^{2}q_{2}\,e^{-\frac{N}{2}(|q_{1}|^{2}+|q_{2}|^{2})-q_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})-q_{1}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1})-iq_{2}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})-iq_{2}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})}.

(S13)

Collecting all anticommuting variables into one
4N
-dimensional vector

\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}=(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{T},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}^{T})
, we can further represent

I_{FF}=\left(\frac{N}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{q}\,e^{-\frac{N}{2}|\boldsymbol{q}|^{2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}0&-Q^{T}\\
Q&0\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Psi}\right]\,,\qquad Q\equiv\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}^{*}&iq_{2}^{*}\\
-iq_{2}&-q_{1}\end{pmatrix}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N}\,,

(S14)

where we have also combined
q_{1,2}
into the complex 2-vector
\boldsymbol{q}=(q_{1},q_{2})
, with
d^{2}\boldsymbol{q}=d^{2}q_{1}d^{2}q_{2}
. Similarly, one can write

e^{z(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})+z^{*}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})}I_{BF}=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}P_{-}\otimes\tau_{2}&0\\
0&P_{+}\otimes\tau_{2}\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Psi}\right]\,,\qquad\begin{array}[]{l}P_{+}\equiv z^{*}\mathbb{I}_{N}-\frac{1}{N}(B_{-}+iB_{+})\\[4.30554pt]
P_{-}\equiv z\mathbb{I}_{N}-\frac{1}{N}(B_{-}-iB_{+})\end{array}\,,

(S15)

where the Pauli matrix
\tau_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
. As a result, the action associated with the anticommuting integration variables in (
S10
) become quadratic,
e^{-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{T}S\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
, where the
4N\times 4N
skew-symmetric matrix
S
is defined by

S=\begin{pmatrix}P_{-}\otimes\tau_{2}&-Q^{T}\\
Q&P_{+}\otimes\tau_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(S16)

The resulting Gaussian integral over
\boldsymbol{\Psi}
can then be evaluated explicitly, yielding the Pfaffian of
S
and leading to

\int\!D(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\phi})e^{z(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})+z^{*}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})}I_{BF}I_{FF}=\left(\frac{N}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{q}\,e^{-\frac{N}{2}|\boldsymbol{q}|^{2}}\mathrm{Pf}(S)\,.

(S17)

The Pfaffian of a block matrix can be computed using the identity
\mathrm{Pf}\begin{pmatrix}A&-Q^{T}\\
Q&D\end{pmatrix}=\sqrt{\det(DA+DQ^{T}D^{-1}Q)}
. In our case, we find
DA=-(P_{+}P_{-})\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}
and
DQ^{T}D^{-1}Q=-(|q_{1}|^{2}+|q_{2}|^{2})\mathbb{I}_{N}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}
, which together yield a block-diagonal structure for the
2N\times 2N
matrix
DA+DQ^{T}D^{-1}Q=-(|\boldsymbol{q}|^{2}\mathbb{I}_{N}+P_{+}P_{-})\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}
. This structure eliminates the square root and leads to a compact expression for the Pfaffian as the following determinant:

\mathrm{Pf}(S)=\det(q^{2}\mathbb{I}_{N}+P_{+}P_{-}),\qquad q\equiv|\boldsymbol{q}|=\sqrt{|q_{1}|^{2}+|q_{2}|^{2}}\,.

(S18)

This representation significantly simplifies the problem. However, computing the resulting determinant directly making use of algebraic methods proves challenging due to its structure. To address this, we adopt an alternative approach and compute it by introducing an auxiliary Grassmann integration, as detailed below.

S-I.3
Computation of the determinant

We first observe that the algebraic structure of the determinant in (
S18
) allows it to be represented as a Gaussian integral

D_{N}\equiv\det(q^{2}\mathbb{I}_{N}+P_{+}P_{-})=\det\begin{pmatrix}q\mathbb{I}_{N}&P_{+}\\
-P_{-}&q\mathbb{I}_{N}\end{pmatrix}=\int d[\boldsymbol{\chi}]\exp\left[-(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1}^{\dagger},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{2}^{\dagger},)\begin{pmatrix}q\mathbb{I}_{N}&P_{+}\\
-P_{-}&q\mathbb{I}_{N}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right]

(S19)

over
2N
-vector
\boldsymbol{\chi}^{T}=(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1}^{T},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{2}^{T})
, with complex Grassmann entries
\{\chi_{\sigma,i}\}
, where
d[\boldsymbol{\chi}]=\prod_{\sigma=1,2}\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\chi_{\sigma,i}^{*}d\chi_{\sigma,i}
.
It is convenient at this stage to introduce back the complex vector
\boldsymbol{s}+i\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{v}
and define a new complex vector
\boldsymbol{k}_{2}+i\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\equiv N\boldsymbol{k}
(note the scaling with
N
). In terms of these variables, the matrices
B_{-}\pm iB_{+}
from Eq. (
S15
) take the following compact form:

B_{-}+iB_{+}=N(\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{k}^{T}+\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{v}^{T})\quad\mbox{and}\quad B_{-}-iB_{+}=(B_{-}+iB_{+})^{*}=N(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}+\boldsymbol{k}^{*}\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger})\,.

(S20)

Expanding the quadratic form in Eq. (
S19
), we obtain

D_{N}=\int d[\boldsymbol{\chi}]e^{-q(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\chi}_{2}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{2})-z^{*}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{2})+z(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{2}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1})}e^{(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{2})+(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k})(\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{2})+(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{2}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1})+(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{2}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k}^{*})(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1})}.

(S21)

Integrating over
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{1,2}
expresses the determinant in terms of all the invariant combinations of the vectors
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{k}
and their complex conjugates. To perform this, we need to decouple the terms appearing in the last factor above. This can be achieved by introducing an additional Grassmann integration using the following identity, valid for any anticommuting variables
\alpha,\beta
:

\int d\rho^{*}d\rho\,e^{-\rho^{*}\rho-\rho^{*}\alpha-\rho\beta}=e^{\alpha\beta}=e^{-\beta\alpha}\,.

(S22)

This can be viewed as an anticommuting extension of the standard decoupling identity for commuting variables, Eq. (
S12
).

Introducing four additional Grassmann variables, organised as 4-vector
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3},\rho_{4})^{T}
, we can cast Eq. (
S21
) as

D_{N}=\int d[\boldsymbol{\rho}]\,e^{-\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\rho}}\int d[\boldsymbol{\chi}]\,e^{-\boldsymbol{\chi}^{\dagger}F\boldsymbol{\chi}+\boldsymbol{\chi}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\boldsymbol{\chi}},

(S23)

where

F=\begin{pmatrix}q&z^{*}\\
-z&q\end{pmatrix}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N}\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{v}+\rho_{2}^{*}\boldsymbol{k}\\
\rho_{3}^{*}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}+\rho_{4}^{*}\boldsymbol{k}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{3}\boldsymbol{k}^{*}+\rho_{4}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\\
\rho_{1}\boldsymbol{k}+\rho_{2}\boldsymbol{v}\end{pmatrix}.

(S24)

The integration over
\boldsymbol{\chi}
can now be performed using the following identity, valid for any invertable matrix
F
:

\int d[\boldsymbol{\chi}]\,e^{-\boldsymbol{\chi}^{\dagger}F\boldsymbol{\chi}+\boldsymbol{\chi}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\boldsymbol{\chi}}=\det(F)\,e^{-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}F^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}}.

(S25)

This formula provides a vector generalization of the decoupling rule (
S22
).

Using the explicit forms given in Eq. (
S24
), we first find
\det{F}=(q^{2}+|z|^{2})^{N}
and

F^{-1}=\frac{1}{q^{2}+|z|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}q&-z^{*}\\
z&q\end{pmatrix}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{N}
. Next, evaluating
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}F^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and performing some algebra, we obtain for
D_{N}
from Eq. (
S23
) the following result:

D_{N}=(q^{2}+|z|^{2})^{N}\int d[\boldsymbol{\rho}]\,e^{-\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\dagger}M\boldsymbol{\rho}}=(q^{2}+|z|^{2})^{N}\det(\mathbb{I}_{4}-M)\,,

(S26)

where the
4\times 4
matrix
M
is defined as follows

M=\frac{1}{q^{2}+|z|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}zA&qB\\
qB^{*}&-z^{*}A^{*}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mbox{with}\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}&\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{k}&\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{k}\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}&\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}\\
\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k}&\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(S27)

Since
\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}=1
due to the normalization, we readily find
D_{N}=(q^{2}+|z|^{2})^{N-4}D_{q}(z,\boldsymbol{v,\boldsymbol{k}})
, as stated in Eq. (
5
) of the main text.

Finally, we observe that the remaining two factors in the JPDF from Eq. (
S10
) can be expressed as

e^{-i\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{t})+i\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{k}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{s})}=e^{-\frac{N}{2}[z(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})-z^{*}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})]}\quad\mbox{and}\quad I_{BB}=e^{-\frac{N}{2}[\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k}+(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})]}

(S28)

in terms of the complex vectors
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{k}
. Note that the scaling with
N
introduced for
\boldsymbol{k}
contributes an additional
N
-dependent factor to the integration measure
d\boldsymbol{k}_{1}d\boldsymbol{k}_{2}=(N)^{2N}d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}
. Moreover, since the integration over
\boldsymbol{q}
turns out to depend only on its magnitude
q=|\boldsymbol{q}|
, the measure becomes
d^{2}\boldsymbol{q}=V_{4}q^{3}dq
, where
V_{4}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}
is the volume of the unit ball in the four-dimensional Cartesian space
(\textrm{Re}{q}_{1},\textrm{Im}{q}_{1},\textrm{Re}{q}_{2},\textrm{Im}{q}_{2})
. Collecting all factors together, we arrive at the final integral representation for the JPDF given by Eq. (
6
) of the main text. In the next section, we develop the method for performing the
\boldsymbol{k}
-integration exactly.

S-II
Integration over
\boldsymbol{k}
and Wick’s theorem

It is convenient to represent the joint distribution in Eq. (
6
) in the equivalent form

\mathcal{P}(z,\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{N^{2N+1}}{8\pi}\delta(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dq\,q^{3}e^{-\frac{N}{2}q^{2}}\lim_{|w-z|\to 0}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{z}\,\partial{z}^{*}}(q^{2}+|z|^{2})^{N-4}\mathcal{Z}(w,z,\boldsymbol{v})\,,

(S29)

where we have introduced an auxiliary complex variable
w
to allow swapping the order of the
z
-derivatives and the
\boldsymbol{k}
-integration (with the limit
w\to{z}
to be taken at the end). The generating function
\mathcal{Z}(w,z,\boldsymbol{v})
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(w,z,\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\equiv\int\frac{d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{2N}}e^{-\frac{N}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(w,\boldsymbol{v})}D_{q}(z,\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{k})

(S30)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{2N}}e^{-\frac{N}{2}[\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k}+(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})+w(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})-w^{*}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})]}\det\left[(q^{2}+|z|^{2})\mathbb{I}_{4}-\begin{pmatrix}zA&qB\\
qB^{*}&-z^{*}A^{*}\end{pmatrix}\right]\,.

This representation offers two key advantages. First, performing the
\boldsymbol{k}
-integration upfront will drastically reduce the complexity of the expression to be differentiated, as the only remaining invariants involving the vector
\boldsymbol{v}
are
\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}=1
(fixed by normalization) and
\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}
. The latter is related to the non-orthogonality overlap. Second, and more importantly, this integration can be carried out exactly because the action depends quadratically on
\boldsymbol{k}
, enabling the use of Wick’s theorem, which we discuss next.

S-II.1
Normal coordinates and diagrammatic rules

The most efficient way to apply Wick’s theorem in our setting is to first transform the quadratic form
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(w,\boldsymbol{v})
into normal coordinates and then establish the diagrammatic rules for the second-order correlators in these variables. To achieve this, we first begin by linearizing the mixed term
(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})
in the exponent of Eq. (
S30
). This is done making use of the decoupling identity (
S12
) again and introducing an auxiliary integration over a complex variable
\xi
,

e^{-\frac{N}{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})}=\frac{N}{2\pi}\int d^{2}\xi\,e^{-\frac{N}{2}[\xi\xi^{*}++i\xi(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})+i\xi^{*}(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})]}\,,

(S31)

which leads to

e^{-\frac{N}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(w,\boldsymbol{v})}=\frac{N}{2\pi}\int d^{2}\xi\,e^{-\frac{N}{2}[\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k}+(w\boldsymbol{v}^{T}+i\xi^{*}\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}(w^{*}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-i\xi\boldsymbol{v})+\xi\xi^{*}]}\,.

(S32)

Centralizing the quadratic form that appears above proceeds in two steps. First, we apply a complex vector shift

\boldsymbol{k}\to\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{k}-(w^{*}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-i\xi\boldsymbol{v})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\to\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}+(w\boldsymbol{v}^{T}+i\xi^{*}\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger})

(S33)

to render the quadratic dependence normal in the new coordinates
(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger})
. Next, we centralize the remaining quadratic function in the variables
(\xi,\xi^{*})
by applying a complex scalar shift

\textstyle\xi\to\zeta=\xi+\frac{i}{2}w^{*}(\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})^{*}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\xi^{*}\to\zeta^{*}=\xi^{*}-\frac{i}{2}w(\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})\,.

(S34)

Combined, these steps yield the following transformation to normal coordinates:

\textstyle\boldsymbol{k}=\boldsymbol{r}-i\zeta\boldsymbol{v}+w^{*}[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\frac{(\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})^{*}}{2}\boldsymbol{v}]\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}-i\zeta^{*}\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}-w[\boldsymbol{v}^{T}-\frac{(\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})}{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}]\,.

(S35)

As a result, the generating function (
S30
) takes the following attractive representation:

\mathcal{Z}(w,z,\boldsymbol{v})=e^{-\frac{N}{2}|w|^{2}(1-\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2})}\frac{N}{2\pi}\iint\frac{d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\,d^{2}\zeta}{(2\pi)^{2N}}e^{-\frac{N}{2}\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{r}}e^{-N|\zeta|^{2}}D_{q}[z,\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{r},\zeta)]\,.

(S36)

This representation simplifies the task of performing the
\boldsymbol{k}
-integration substantially and reduces it to averaging over two independent – one vector (
\boldsymbol{r}
) and one scalar (
\zeta
) – uncorrelated Gaussian fields.

The pre-exponential factor
D_{q}
is a forth-order polynomial in these variables. Therefore, we can apply Wick’s theorem directly, once the second-order correlators are specified. To this end, it is convenient to represent expression (
S36
) as

\mathcal{Z}(w,z,\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{1}{2(2\pi N)^{N}}e^{-\frac{N}{2}|w|^{2}(1-\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2})}\left\langle\left\langle D_{q}\right\rangle\right\rangle\,,

(S37)

where we have introduced the notation
\left\langle\left\langle\cdots\right\rangle\right\rangle
to denote avereging over the Gaussian fields

\left\langle\left\langle\cdots\right\rangle\right\rangle=\frac{N^{N+1}}{\pi(2\pi)^{N}}\iint d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}\,d^{2}\zeta\,e^{-\frac{N}{2}\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{r}}e^{-N|\zeta|^{2}}(\cdots),

(S38)

with the normalization constant chosen to satisfy
\left\langle\left\langle 1\right\rangle\right\rangle=1
. This implies zero mean for the components

\left\langle\left\langle r_{i}\right\rangle\right\rangle=\left\langle\left\langle\zeta\right\rangle\right\rangle=0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\left\langle\left\langle r_{i}\zeta\right\rangle\right\rangle=\left\langle\left\langle r_{i}\right\rangle\right\rangle\left\langle\left\langle\zeta\right\rangle\right\rangle=0\,.

(S39)

The only nonzero contributions to (
S38
) arise from the correlators of the second and fourth order, which are given by

\left\langle\left\langle|\zeta|^{2}\right\rangle\right\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\,,\qquad\left\langle\left\langle r^{*}_{i}r_{j}\right\rangle\right\rangle=\frac{2}{N}\delta_{ij}

(S40)

and

\left\langle\left\langle|\zeta|^{4}\right\rangle\right\rangle=\frac{2}{N^{2}}\,,\qquad\left\langle\left\langle r^{*}_{i}r^{*}_{j}r_{k}r_{l}\right\rangle\right\rangle=\frac{4}{N^{2}}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})\,.

(S41)

These relations allow us to formulate a set of substitution rules to efficiently implement the integration in Eq. (
S36
).

S-II.2
Substitution rules for
\boldsymbol{k}
-integration

To evaluate
\mathcal{Z}(w,z,\boldsymbol{v})
, we first require an explicit form for the determinant
D_{q}
. This determinant is obtained through a straightforward yet lengthy calculation and can be expressed in the following polynomial form:

D_{q}=R^{4}+C_{3}R^{3}+C_{2}R^{2}+C_{1}R+C_{0},\qquad\mbox{with}\quad R=q^{2}+|z|^{2}.

(S42)

The four coefficients
C_{n}
,
n=0,\ldots,3
are, in turn, given by (
4{-}n
)-th order polynomials in
\boldsymbol{k}
as follows

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=-2z(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})+2z^{*}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}),

(S43a)

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=-2q^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k}+|\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2})-4|z|^{2}|\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}+z^{2}\bigl[(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})^{2}-(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{k})(\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})\bigr]+\mbox{c.c.},

(S43b)

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=2q^{2}\Bigl(z\bigl[(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k})(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})-(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{k})(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})+|\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})-(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k})(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})(\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})\bigr]-\mbox{c.c.}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+2|z|^{2}\Bigl(z\bigl[(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})|\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}-(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{k})(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})(\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})\bigr]-\mbox{c.c.}\Bigr),

(S43c)

\displaystyle C_{0}

\displaystyle=q^{4}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k}-|\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\Bigr)^{2}+q^{2}|z|^{2}\Bigl(|\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{k}|^{2}+2(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k})|\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}+2|\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}|\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}-2(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{k})(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\quad+\bigl[(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{k})(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})^{2}-2(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{k})(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})\bigr](\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})^{*}+\mbox{c.c.}\Bigr)+|z|^{4}\Bigl|(\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})^{2}-(\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{k})(\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})\Bigr|^{2}.

(S43d)

The next step is to represent these coefficients in terms of the normal coordinates
(\boldsymbol{r},\zeta)
, using the transformation (
S35
). Applying the diagrammatic rules derived above then yields expressions for the average coefficients
\left\langle\left\langle C_{n}\right\rangle\right\rangle
and, ultimately, for the average determinant
\left\langle\left\langle D_{q}\right\rangle\right\rangle
. For instance, one readily finds:

\textstyle\left\langle\!\left\langle\boldsymbol{k}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=w^{*}[\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\frac{(\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})^{*}}{2}\boldsymbol{v}]\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\left\langle\!\left\langle\boldsymbol{k}^{\dagger}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=-w[\boldsymbol{v}^{T}-\frac{(\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v})}{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}]\,.

(S44)

Noting that
\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{v}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{v})^{*}=1
, the first (linear in
\boldsymbol{k}
) average coefficient
\left\langle\left\langle C_{3}\right\rangle\right\rangle
is obtained exactly as

\textstyle\left\langle\!\left\langle C_{3}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=-2(zw^{*}+z^{*}w)\nu,\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\nu\equiv 1-\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{v}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}.

(S45)

For
\left\langle\!\left\langle C_{2}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle
and the remaining coefficients, which involve higher-order terms in
\boldsymbol{k}
, it is convenient to average over
\zeta
first, reducing the algebraic complexity before averaging over
\boldsymbol{r}
. To this end, we note that the vector
\boldsymbol{r}
enters as a fourth-order polynomial involving scalar products
(\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{a})
, where
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{*},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\}
. It is therefore convenient, for practical purposes, to reformulate the diagrammatic rules in an equivalent form involving these scalar products. All nonzero second-order averages are then given by

\textstyle\left\langle\!\left\langle\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{r}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=2\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\left\langle\!\left\langle(\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{a})(\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=\frac{2}{N}(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{b}).

(S46)

The nonzero averages of the fourth order are given by

\left\langle\!\left\langle(\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{r})^{2}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=N(N+1)\bigl({\textstyle\frac{2}{N}}\bigr)^{2}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\left\langle\!\left\langle|\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}|^{2}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=2N\bigl({\textstyle\frac{2}{N}}\bigr)^{2}

(S47)

for the ‘diagonal’ terms, and by

\displaystyle\left\langle\!\left\langle(\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{r})(\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{a})^{2}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=2(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{a})\bigl({\textstyle\frac{2}{N}}\bigr)^{2}\,,

(S48a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\!\left\langle(\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{r})(\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{a})(\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{b})\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=2(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{b})\bigl({\textstyle\frac{2}{N}}\bigr)^{2}\,,

(S48b)

\displaystyle\left\langle\!\left\langle(\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{r})(\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{a})(\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{b})\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=(N+1)(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{b})\bigl({\textstyle\frac{2}{N}}\bigr)^{2}\,,

(S48c)

\displaystyle\left\langle\!\left\langle(\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{a})^{2}(\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{b})^{2}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=2(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{b})^{2}\bigl({\textstyle\frac{2}{N}}\bigr)^{2}\,,

(S48d)

\displaystyle\left\langle\!\left\langle(\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{a})(\boldsymbol{r}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{b})(\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{a})(\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{b})\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=[(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{b})(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{b})+(\boldsymbol{a}^{T}\boldsymbol{a})(\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{b})]\bigl({\textstyle\frac{2}{N}}\bigr)^{2}\,,

(S48e)

for ‘cross-diagonal’ terms, where now
\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in\{\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\}
. In this formulation, the
\boldsymbol{k}
-integration is reduced to averaging the Gaussian vector field
\boldsymbol{r}
, which can be performed efficiently and transparently. For example, the next (quadratic) coefficient
\left\langle\!\left\langle C_{2}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle
is

\textstyle\left\langle\!\left\langle C_{2}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle=\frac{2}{N}\left[\frac{N}{2}\Bigl(2q^{2}(|w|^{2}\nu-2)+[(zw^{*})^{2}+(z^{*}w)^{2}](2\nu-1)\Bigr)-4|z|^{2}\nu\bigl(1-\frac{N}{2}|w|^{2}\nu\bigr)\right].

(S49)

The computation of the last two terms
\left\langle\!\left\langle C_{1}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle
and
\left\langle\!\left\langle C_{0}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle
is more involved but can be carried out efficiently making use of computer algebra systems like
Mathematica
to implement the substitution rules specified above. This procedure yields the final expression for
\left\langle\!\left\langle D_{q}\right\rangle\!\right\rangle
and, consequently, for the generating function
\mathcal{Z}(w,z,\boldsymbol{v})
from (
S38
). Substituting this result into (
S29
), performing the integration over
q
, and taking the
2D
-Laplacian gives our final result (
6
) presented in the main text.
