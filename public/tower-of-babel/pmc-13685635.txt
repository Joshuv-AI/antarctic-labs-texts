Ocular Manifestations and Management of Graft-versus-Host Disease: A Comprehensive Review
Authors: Sumana Chatterjee, Jaya Gupta, Shweta Agarwal, Jayanta Kuila, Vishal Jhanji, Namrata Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOcular graft-versus-host disease (oGVHD) is a critical complication following allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, characterised by a T-cell-mediated immune response that results in inflammation and damage to various ocular structures.SummaryWhile the ocular surface is primarily affected, oGVHD can also impact other eye components, leading to debilitating symptoms. The diagnosis of oGVHD poses significant challenges due to its relapsing and remitting nature, complicating effective treatment strategies. Prompt identification of ocular manifestations is essential, as early intervention can prevent vision-threatening complications and improve patient outcomes. As research continues to evolve, understanding the underlying mechanisms and exploring innovative therapeutic options will be crucial in managing this complex condition effectively.Key MessageThis review highlights the importance of heightened awareness among healthcare providers regarding the ocular signs of GVHD, advocating for timely diagnosis and personalised treatment approaches.

Introduction

Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) is most commonly observed after allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). It can also be seen in patients receiving other therapies such as blood transfusions, solid organ transplantation, and immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies [1–4]. Ocular GVHD (oGVHD) affects more than 50% of allo-HSCT patients [5]. It is often underdiagnosed, as a relatively small number of patients undergo allo-HSCT, diagnostic criteria are evolving over the years, and there is limited access for pre-HSCT and post-HSCT patients to ophthalmologists [6]. Ocular involvement occurs in all types of GVHD, including acute GVHD (aGVHD), chronic GVHD (cGVHD), and overlap syndrome [7, 8]. cGVHD occurs in 30%–70% of patients who underwent allo-HSCT [9, 10], usually with the clinical features of autoimmune disease, and the most common organs affected are skin, mouth, gastrointestinal tract, liver, and eye [10, 11]. Chronic oGVHD (coGVHD), which usually occurs between 5 and 24 months or earlier post-HSCT [12] is the most frequent form of ocular involvement. The events that activate the immune system in other organ GVHD can trigger oGVHD also, and thus it can occur simultaneously or shortly after other organ affection [8, 13].

Risk Factors for oGVHD

Risk factors for the development of oGVHD include previous aGVHD [14], use of peripheral blood stem cells [14, 15], transplantation from a female donor to a male recipient [16, 17], absence of anti-thymocyte globulin prophylaxis [14], a larger number of organs and tissues involved with GVHD [14, 18], and non-Caucasian and Epstein-Barr Virus seropositive donors [13]. Other risk factors include a mismatch of HLA antigens, higher donor or recipient ages, and diabetes mellitus [14, 17]. Increased occurrence of oGVHD has been found in patients with involvement of the skin [19, 20], oral mucosa [19, 20], liver [13], or gastrointestinal tract, and pulmonary involvement in cGVHD [14]. Multi-organ GVHD involvement, especially skin, may be the hallmark of severe oGVHD patients and indicates a poor prognosis [21].

Diagnosis and Classification

In the past, GVHD was classified as either acute or chronic based on an arbitrary time frame of 100 days post-transplantation. The 2014 National Institutes of Health (NIH) consensus on criteria for clinical trials in cGVHD (2014 NIH consensus criteria) further divides aGVHD [22] into “classic acute GVHD” and “persistent, recurrent, or late-onset acute GVHD” (>3 months), and cGVHD into “classic chronic GVHD” or “chronic GVHD with an overlap syndrome” (no time limit) based on the clinical presentations of patients [23, 24].

There are currently two diagnostic criteria of oGVHD. The NIH CC Diagnostic Criteria (Table 1) and ICCGVHD (International Chronic Ocular GVHD Consensus Group) Diagnostic Criteria (Table 2) [25].

ICCGVHD consensus group diagnostic criteria

The 2014 National Institutes of Health (NIH) criteria define a symptom-based diagnosis of oGVHD [23, 26] where it is a new ocular sicca starting post-HSCT (Schirmer’s test ≤5 mm/5 min or 6–10 mm/5 min with new onset of keratoconjunctivitis sicca [KCS] [post-HSCT] by slit lamp not due to other causes) [27]. The updated 2015 NIH classification removed the Schirmer’s test after reports of poor correlation with physician- and patient-reported changes: numerical changes in Schirmer scores did not match how patients felt or how physicians rated their conditions, and it had low reproducibility. So NIH shifted to grading based on the patient’s chief eye complaint and the frequency of artificial tears [24].

In 2013, the ICCGVHD gave new diagnostic metrics to increase objectivity in the diagnosis and follow-up of cGVHD [28]. In patients after HSCT the criteria describes Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) score as subjective variable and Schirmer’s score without anaesthesia, corneal fluorescein staining, and conjunctival injection as objective variables with each scored 0–2 or 0–3 making the maximum composite score to 11. There can be three diagnostic categories taking into account the presence or absence of systemic GVHD – no, probable, or definite oGVHD. A score ≥6 showed good adherence in diagnosing oGVHD in patients with or without systemic GVHD [29]. But this criteria cannot fully describe the condition as it does not take into account the effects of fibrosis in eyelids, meibomian glands and lacrimal ducts [5]. It also lacks indicators of immune-related and quantitative objective inflammatory factors to diagnose and assess the disease severity of oGVHD [30]. An extension of the Tear Film and Ocular Surface Society Dry Eye Workshop II (TFOS DEWS II) and the Japanese Dry Eye Society criteria for diagnosing dry eye [31] gives more emphasis on unstable tear film (tear film breakup time [TFBUT <5 s] and subjective symptoms [32].

The German/Austrian/Swiss Consensus Conference (GAS CC) [33] participants agreed that they might include the overall extent of cGVHD concerning the following: “(a) tissue involvement-extent of lacrimal gland dysfunction, involvement of the lids (Blepharitis, MGD), conjunctival involvement (e.g., cicatricial conjunctivitis), and corneal involvement (keratitis, epithelial defects, corneal ulceration), (b) inflammatory activity (e.g., chronic hyperemia of bulbar conjunctiva in cGVHD that is not well-controlled, and (c) complications and functional impairment (e.g., reduced visual acuity, corneal perforation, or secondary glaucoma).” [33] Routine visits to an ophthalmologist are often missed, leading to late diagnosis and delayed treatment of coGVHD [34] as patients remain more preoccupied with life-threatening complications.

Pathophysiology

After allogeneic transplantation, host antigen-presenting cells (APCs) are activated by tissue damage and innate immune activation. They then migrate to draining lymph nodes and present recipient alloantigens to donor T cells. Donor-derived APCs present antigens to T cells through Major Histocompatibility Complex molecules. This triggers a T-cell response which can be beneficial, like graft-versus-leukaemia effect, or detrimental, like GVHD. Host APCs play a key role in aGVHD, but in cGVHD, both host and donor APCs are responsible for inflammation [35].

The interplay of thymic injury, B-cell autoantibody synthesis, and profibrotic lesion formation may result in the inflammatory insult in cGVHD [36, 37]. It was thought that cGVHD is a predominantly Th2-mediated disease, but involvement of Th1 and Th17 pathways is also implicated in recent studies [38].

In post-HSCT eyes with oGVHD, interferon γ, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-17A, matrix metalloproteinase −9, and VEGF were found to be elevated as compared to non-oGVHD eyes and controls, amongst which interferon γ is elevated in the initial stages, and IL-6 in later stages, indicating severity [38]. Gene transcription via the STAT pathway is activated when inflammatory cytokines bind to receptors using Janus Kinase (JAK) signalling [39]. This cascade is intricately involved in the pathogenesis of cGVHD and oGVHD. An et al. [40] have shown that “neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and NET-associated proteins have a role in the pathogenesis of oGVHD.” Confocal microscopy reveals that in patients with oGVHD, the globular immune cells and dendritic cells infiltrate more around the basal nerve in the central cornea and limbus area [41]. Ocular GVHD has three important biological processes: lacrimal gland dysfunction, meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD), and corneal and conjunctival inflammatory infiltration [42].

Lacrimal Gland Dysfunction

T cells and other inflammatory cells usually target the medium-sized ducts of the lacrimal gland in the initial stage [43]. Donor fibroblasts play a role in the development of lacrimal gland fibrosis [27] and blockage of the ducts of the lacrimal and meibomian glands and the nasolacrimal duct. Patchy acinar cell loss and mild lymphocytic infiltration [27] of the lacrimal gland [44] lead to apoptosis and ongoing acinar cell loss in coGVHD [27]. Moreover, T cells and fibroblasts may co-stimulate each other to produce an ongoing inflammatory environment in coGVHD, leading to severe aqueous-deficient dry eyes (ADDEs).

Meibomian Gland Dysfunction

MGD is the second most frequent complication, with a prevalence of 47.8% in coGVHD [45]. The coGVHD-related inflammatory cascade causes massive meibomian gland cell apoptosis, which may lead to a reduction in lipid synthesis and triglyceride content [46]. Prominent inflammation and fibrosis of the meibomian gland microstructure, including acini, ductules, main ducts, and orifices, have been detected by in vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) in patients with coGVHD [45]. Thus, obstructive MGD is the most common phenomenon [47]. Meibomian gland loss evident in noncontact meibography can occur before HSCT, due to conjunctival mucosal inflammation, meibum stasis [48], infiltration of the glands by tumour cells, chemotherapy, or irradiation. Also, MGD is directly associated with the disease severity of oGVHD [49].

Conjunctival Inflammatory Infiltration

Conjunctival involvement is generally more common in aGVHD than in cGVHD and indicates systemic disease severity [31]. Findings in the conjunctiva in aoGVHD resemble those of skin aGVHD, like mononuclear inflammatory infiltrate of the basal epithelium and dyskeratosis [27, 31]. It is the early and primary manifestation of aoGVHD, which is only 1% of ocular involvement in post-HSCT patients [14]. Conjunctival findings unique to aoGVHD include chemosis [27] and pseudomembrane formation [27, 31].

Conjunctival involvement has also been examined in coGVHD and involves both inflammatory and fibrotic components [27]. Decreased conjunctival goblet cell density, increased conjunctival squamous metaplasia, and infiltration of tissues with inflammatory cells and a lymphocytic infiltration with epithelial cell necrosis and lymphocytic exocytosis are usual histology findings [42]. Conjunctival keratinisation can also be a part of GVHD. The conjunctival disease staging system in oGVHD is given as: “stage I, hyperemia; stage II, hyperemia with serosanguineous chemosis; stage III, pseudomembranous conjunctivitis; and stage IV, pseudomembranous conjunctivitis with corneal epithelial sloughing [31]. While stage III oGVHD may be seen beyond day 100 (acute pattern in the cGVHD period), stage IV oGVHD typically occurs only in the acute or hyperacute phase post-transplantation.” [22].

Chronic cicatrising conjunctivitis (CCC) occurs in 6–50% of patients with coGVHD [50]. The risk of CCC increases with time, with 29% of individuals with coGVHD having an incidence of CCC after 5 years and 49% of individuals having an incidence of CCC after 10 years of HSCT [27]. Also, younger individuals (≤50 years) may have a higher risk of developing CCC [27]. It is proposed that the fibrosis in cases of CCC is contributed to by the host epithelial cells undergoing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and also could be caused by donor fibroblasts [51]. Robinson et al. [52] developed a grading criterion (Table 3) for coGVHD based on clinically noted conjunctival cicatrisation.

Dry Eye Disease

The occurrence of DED may be multifactorial as a combination of the immune effect of HSTC, MGD, immunosuppressive therapy, total body irradiation, and ocular toxicity due to chemotherapy [10]. The pathogenesis of dry eye is different from Sjögren Syndrome (SS) related dry eye, as in SS, B cells are the dominating infiltrating cells in the acinar areas of the lacrimal gland [53]. The TLR2-mediated NF-κB signalling pathway was found to be responsible for dry eye associated with cGVHD [38].

Limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD) may be related to alloreactivity to recipient tissues or repetitive frictional microtrauma to the limbal stem cells in an already inflamed environment is an uncommon complication of GVHD [54]. Corneal changes in oGVHD can affect all the corneal layers. Recently, the reduction in corneal endothelial cell density in oGVHD has been speculated to involve the substance P/neurokinin-1 receptor (NK1R) pathway [37]. The risk factors for developing corneal complications are diabetes, LSCD, filamentary keratitis, subconjunctival fibrosis, a high NIH score and pretreatment regimens that involve radiation or chemotherapy [55]. Corneal ulcers, both due to inflammation and infection, can be seen in coGVHD, with perforation as a potential visually threatening complication [27, 31]. Risk factors for corneal ulceration included black race, previous ocular surgery, lid margin abnormalities, and topical corticosteroid use [56]. T cells and macrophages, apoptotic epithelial cells and keratocytes, and intense expression of matrix metalloproteinase-9 have been found in perforated corneal specimens from patients with cGVHD [25, 57]. Bourdin et al. [58] have shown in their study that male patients and HLA-mismatched acute HSCT patients were independently associated with an increased risk of corneal ulceration or perforation. The rapid progression of infectious ulcers is likely due to a combination of poor wound healing from severe dryness, chronic steroid usage, and an altered immune response from systemic immune suppression and GVHD itself [59]. The localisation of corneal perforations in the central or para-central area, similar to neurotrophic ulcers and persistent epithelial defects in LSCD [60], may signal a common pathway of chronic inflammation that could reveal promising molecular therapeutic targets [61].

In cGVHD, IVCM shows reduced nerve density and increased tortuosity of corneal nerves. IVCM and tear cytokine analysis may serve as valuable tools for early diagnosis of coGVHD, monitoring progression, and scope for future targeted therapies [62].

Clinical Manifestations

All the layers of the eye, including the lid, lacrimal gland, conjunctiva, cornea, and the vitreous and choroids, can be affected in oGVHD, but the posterior involvement of oGVHD is rare [63].

Symptoms

Individuals with aoGVHD report discharge, redness (Fig. 1), excessive tearing, and a sensation of dryness. On average, symptoms begin 8.0 ± 5.0 months after transplantation [27]. In those with coGVHD, symptoms included light sensitivity (Fig. 1) and lack of tearing. Only a minority of individuals reported “mucus” eye secretions, but the majority described “fibrous and stringy” secretions, highlighting different symptom profiles between acute and coGVHD. On average, symptoms in those with coGVHD began 10.7 ± 9.1 months after transplantation [27].

Signs

aoGVHD is less frequent and less well-studied than coGVHD. Signs between the two conditions are also different. Both acute and coGVHD can affect various ocular structures, most commonly the conjunctiva and cornea [27].

Eyelid findings reported in cases of coGVHD include eyebrow and eyelash loss (madarosis) and depigmentation (poliosis) [27] along with hypopigmentation, hyperpigmentation, xerosis, and ichthyosis [64]. Trichiasis, entropion, ectropion, and lagophthalmos can occur due to chronic tarsal conjunctival inflammation [27]. Skin involvement may lead to mechanical shortening of the anterior lamella of the eyelid, causing cicatricial ectropion in oGVHD. Eyelid laxity is also noted in patients with oGVHD [65]. Spontaneous punctal occlusion in oGVHD patients is associated with the severity of dry eye disease and the severity of the systemic disease [66]. Bilateral nasolacrimal duct obstruction has also been reported [67].

One of the earliest signs of conjunctival involvement in coGVHD is clinical conjunctival subepithelial fibrosis [68]. Kheirkhah et al. [50] have shown subtarsal fibrosis (more common in the upper eyelids) seen in 50% of patients with coGVHD, to be a significant cause of ocular surface epitheliopathy [51]. Conjunctival cicatrisation found in coGVHD can lead to lagophthalmos, entropion/ectropion, and symblepharon [69]. Superior limbic keratoconjunctivitis (SLKC) and episcleritis are also seen [70]. Sivaraman et al. [71] opined that SLKC-like inflammation may lead to frank LSCD in GVHD. Corneal pannus formation may be aggravated in SLKC which can correlate with the degree of upper eyelid laxity [65].

Clinically, meibomian gland orifice obstruction (by hyperkeratinization), displacement of the mucocutaneous junction, vascular tortuosity, and altered quality and quantity of sebum are seen in oGVHD [44, 72]. Studies have shown ongoing meibomian gland loss despite proper management [73].

DED occurs in 69%–77% of cases, with the typical onset being within 6–9 months [10, 17]. It is generally associated with cGVHD, although lacrimal gland dysfunction is also common with conjunctival inflammation in aGVHD [22]. Poor OSDI scores and worse TFBUT and ocular surface staining, along with poor gland morphology, were found in the coGVHD group compared with the SS and MGD groups [49]. Severe ADDE with Schirmer’s <5 mm was noted in 50–76% of patients with coGVHD in a study [74]. The severity of ADDE is correlated with the degree of fibrotic changes, as opposed to the degree of lymphocytic infiltration [75]. Fibrosis is a distinctive feature of coGVHD that is not as commonly seen in DED [27]. Secondary to severe ADDE, these eyes may have congestion (Fig. 1), superficial punctate keratitis, filamentary keratitis, corneal epithelial defect, and corneal perforation (Fig. 2).

Sterile corneal ulcers are more common and can progress to corneal perforation (Fig. 2) [76]. Other corneal findings in aoGVHD (but not unique to aoGVHD) include epithelial erosions (with epithelial sloughing, a more severe form) and filamentary keratitis [27, 31]. Singh et al. [77] noted in their study that neurotrophic keratopathy (NK) was present in 14% of patients with coGVHD. Persistent epithelial defect (PED) or corneal ulceration can occur as a sequelae of NK in oGVHD patients with a higher NIH severity score and previous history of cataract surgery [77]. LSCD seen in patients with coGVHD can be related to eyes showing signs of NK [71].

Ocular hypertension was noted in 15% of patients with coGVHD, of which one-third of patients needed anti-glaucoma treatment [78]. It is mainly due to the use of topical and systemic corticosteroid therapy, which is often transient and with resolution seen by decreasing the steroid dose [78].

Toxicity of antimitotic chemotherapeutic agents, total body irradiation during the pre-bone marrow transplant conditioning process, and prolonged use of high-dose systemic corticosteroids in the treatment of GVHD can cause cataract, which results in poor vision post-HSCT [79]. Allan et al. [80] have found cataracts in 39% of patients with coGVHD, and they were associated with a 4-fold risk of visual acuity 20/50 or worse.

In oGVHD, sometimes vitreous cells may be the only finding [81]. Other features like retinal haemorrhages, cotton wool spots, etc., are typically secondary to the patient’s treatment and their haematological profiles with anaemia or thrombocytopenia, but not due to oGVHD [80]. In acute or hyperacute GVHD, posterior scleritis, choroidal thickening, and serous detachments that may represent oGVHD are rarely seen and respond to high-dose steroids [82].

Screening and Prevention

The First ICCGVHD Group, the German-Austrian Swiss Consensus Conference, and the 2015 updated NIH Consensus Conference have recommended a pre-HSCT ophthalmological assessment to classify the onset of ocular symptoms and signs post-HSCT as incident cases [28, 33, 83]. On the contrary, if DED is already present before HSCT, the current criteria to diagnose oGVHD may not be fulfilled [83]. As it is seen that the reflex tearing starts to decrease by 3 months post-HSCT and Schirmer test value around 6 months [31], it is best to start systematic GVHD screening, which is essential for early recognition of oGVHD at 6 months post-HSCT [84]. Another protocol is a routine ophthalmologic screening at 3 months and 6 months post-HSCT and at the time of diagnosis of cGVHD of any site [85].

Treatment

Management of coGVHD cases needs a multidisciplinary approach involving a haematologist and an ophthalmologist [86]. Both local and systemic treatment along with systemic prophylactic therapy are employed for treating GVHD and ocular GVHD. The ideal GVHD prophylaxis should preserve graft versus malignancy effect but prevent acute and/or cGVHD [27]. After HSCT Methotrexate and a calcineurin inhibitor like tacrolimus are continued for at least 100 days before tapering [27]. Systemic corticosteroids with or without calcineurin inhibitors are the first-line treatment if there is atleast an overall grade of moderate cGVHD [27]. In oGVHD, topical therapies are preferred and systemic therapies are avoided to prevent immunosuppression [27]. The treatment of cGVHD aims at decreasing the symptoms, sustained control of disease activity, and prevention of disability without toxicity [86].

Indications for Systemic Treatment for oGVHD

As defined by the NIH criteria, after initial systemic treatment, mostly with corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors, the rate of complete or partial response of oGVHD was 23% at 6 months [87]. The average response rates of oGVHD are 43% with extracorporeal photopheresis, which can increase the number and activity of regulatory T cells promoting immune tolerance [87], 31% with rituximab [88], 60% with sirolimus [89], and 33% with mycophenolate mofetil [90] in a corticosteroid-refractory setting. But small sample sizes, variable response criteria, and differing assessment timing across studies do affect response rates [17]. Local Treatment

The NIH cGVHD consensus workshop has outlined four main supportive goals for the treatment of oGVHD: lubrication, decrease in ocular inflammation, control of drainage, and control of evaporation [91].

Lubrication

Preservative-free artificial tears along with viscous ointments help decrease ocular discomfort by decreasing friction between lid and ocular surface; protect against epithelial damage, and improve visual function [91]. Oral secretagogues like Pilocarpine may be used, with attention to potential systemic side effects and drug interactions.

In oGVHD, conjunctival goblet cells are affected [27]. So, Diquafosol ophthalmic solution, which activates P2Y2 receptors on the conjunctiva, can be used to increase mucin production [27]. Diquafosol and Rebamipide improved symptoms and led to better TBUT scores and ocular surface staining patterns over 6 months in a study by Yamane et al. [92].

Anti-Inflammatory Treatment

Artificial tears may dilute inflammatory cytokines in tear fluid and may thus protect epithelial health [27]. Short courses of topical corticosteroids and long-term treatment with various concentrations of cyclosporine are usually used to treat various DED subtypes [27]. Increased disease severity can be indicated by use of corticosteroids as they are used to promote quiten in severe ocular surface inflammation [93], but chronic use can lead to cataract and glaucoma development [27].

Cyclosporine and tacrolimus are commonly used as they reduce T cell activation and increase goblet cell density [27]. In KCS, including oGVHD, randomised studies have shown that cyclosporine improves the Schirmer test results and tear film breakup time and reduces punctate keratopathy [27, 94].

Topical cyclosporine has proven beneficial in the treatment of oGVHD-associated DED, and cyclosporine is currently being investigated as a prophylactic treatment for the prevention of the same [95]. A limitation of these therapies is that many individuals cannot tolerate them due to instillation site burning and a possible increased risk of ocular herpes keratitis [27]. Topical lifitegrast, an integrin antagonist that inhibits LFA-1/ICAM-1 interaction, downregulates T-cell-mediated inflammatory reactions and shows promising clinical utility as a steroid-sparing agent in the management of symptomatic oGVHD [96].

Autologous serum tears (ASTs) have been shown to improve symptoms and corneal surface health in oGVHD, exerting their effect via growth factors in the serum [27]. AST can act on all three mechanisms of severe dry eye by lubricating the ocular surface, reducing inflammation, and promoting wound healing. Superimposed infection and the effects of systemically applied immunosuppressive drugs in serum are possible risk factors of long-term use of AST [97]. Orally administered cyclosporine and mycophenolic acid for the treatment of systemic GVHD, but not everolimus and tacrolimus, are distinctly detectable in AST in relevant concentrations [81]. Alternative options for patients who cannot provide AST are allogeneic serum [98], umbilical cord serum, and platelet-derived eye drops [99]. Allogeneic serum use carries the risk of blood-borne diseases and allergic reactions [97]. Data are needed on the impact of recombinant growth factors, such as nerve growth factor, on coGVHD symptoms and signs [27].

Reducing Tear Evaporation – Treatment of MGD

MGD can be first treated by maintaining lid hygiene, warm compresses, and lid massage [100]. Topical ointment like azithromycin with oral antibiotics (doxycycline) helps reduce the bacterial colony on the lid margins and thus reduces the meibomian gland inflammation [101]. Moist chamber goggles [102], and gas-permeable scleral lenses, which have a fluid-filled reservoir, help in reducing tear evaporation, improving photophobia, and visual acuity in patients with coGVHD [103]. Intense pulsed light, along with meibomian gland expression, may also improve meibomian gland secretion [104].

Reducing tear drainage and surface protection can be achieved with collagen or silicone plugs which maintain lubrication of the ocular surface for symptomatic patients [105]. The tear meniscus height is improved, but it can also increase the concentration of the inflammatory cytokines in the tear film. Sabti et al. [105] have shown that the use of punctal plugs did not increase ocular inflammation in patients with coGVHD. But many patients lose the punctal plugs within days of insertion [106]. In such cases and in severe ADDE, permanent punctal occlusion by thermal cauterisation or surgical occlusion can be performed in some patients. [94]. In a study by Yaguchi et al. [107] subjective improvement in symptoms, with improvement in Schirmer’s test values, ocular surface staining scores, and TBUT score, significantly improved 3 months after the procedure. The recanalisation rate is 21% over a median follow-up period of 1 year [108].

Scleral contact lenses provide symptomatic relief in patients with severe oGVHD [109]. Bandage soft contact lenses (BSCL) have also been used in oGVHD to improve symptoms and surface health [27]. Scleral lenses and prosthetic replacement of the ocular surface ecosystem devices (SL/PDs) (PROSE, Boston Sight, Needham, MA, USA) are a standardised, FDA-approved treatment choice for a variety of ocular surface diseases, including oGVHD [110].

In oGVHD, amniotic membrane can be used [27]. 1% progesterone gel applied on forehead was found to provide symptomatic relief in coGVHD after 6 weeks [27] in comparison to placebo, which may be due to a novel neuroaxis drug delivery mechanism [111].

Handling Corneal Ulcers

Culture of scraped material from corneal ulcers associated with oGVHD is a must to rule out infection. The size of the perforation and the presence or absence of anterior-chamber leakage determine whether surgery is required or not. Bandage contact lens, cyanoacrylate tissue adhesive, Tenon’s patch graft or multilayer AM transplantation can be used to seal small perforations; but penetrating keratoplasty is generally deferred until the eye is quiet, as the increased risk of graft failure may offset the benefits [112, 113]. Though considered high risk because of intense surface inflammation [93], a large perforation requires full-thickness tectonic corneal grafting. Tectonic Descemet Stripping Endothelial Keratoplasty can be a useful alternative in this setting to avoid suture-related complications and delayed healing in a compromised epithelial surface [114].

Surgical Therapy

AMT is required in PEDs and descemetocele [115, 116]. Corneal patch grafts or tectonic keratoplasty are done in corneal perforation, but severe ocular surface inflammation and associated dry eye give rise to poor outcomes [117]. Conjunctival pedicle flaps can be tried in the imminent risk of corneal perforation [118]. For LSCD, in allogeneic limbal stem cell transplantation, the limbus has been harvested from the same HLA-identical living-related donor from whom the bone marrow transplantation was performed to avoid systemic immunosuppression, and a stable surface was noted at 31 months [118]. Conjunctival tissue for providing more goblet cells, in addition to limbal tissue, has also been harvested from the same bone marrow donor [119]. Tarsorrhaphy and Symblepharon release with AMT can be tried in the end stages. In cases with end-stage severely dry GVHD eyes, osteo-odonto keratoprosthesis and Boston Type II keratoprosthesis can be performed [120]. But the Boston Type I keratoprosthesis is avoided in such cases.

Cataract surgery, in eyes with coGVHD, significantly improves vision [121]. But filamentary keratitis and corneal epithelial defects can occur post-operatively in these eyes due to severe dry eyes, so close post-operative monitoring is very important [122]. Starting intensive ocular surface lubrication and topical anti-inflammatory therapy before cataract surgery and continuing in the post-operative period helps in improving the surgical outcome.

Future Therapies

The main aim of future therapies was to avoid the toxicity of generalised immunosuppression and utilise cellular therapies. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are a source of cells that promote tissue repair and are thus effective in oGVHD-associated DED. Wang and colleagues showed that intravenously injected BM-MSCs could attenuate T-cell-mediated ocular inflammation and improve DED in 12 out of 22 BM-MSC-treated oGVHD patients [123]. Experimental and pilot clinical trials showed that MSC-Exos could be used in the treatment of oGVHD [124].

JAK inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, and proteasome inhibitors also have promising potential. United States Food and Drug Administration has approved Ruxolitinib, a JAK1/2 inhibitor for the treatment of steroid-refractory systemic GVHD [39, 125]. Systemic baricitinib also helped patients without pre-existing conjunctival fibrosis [39]. Transilast, a TGF-β inhibitor, is assumed to cause less lacrimal gland fibrosis as it preserved Schirmer scores after 3 months of use [27, 126]. NK1R antagonist Fosaprepitant was studied in a mouse model and showed a significant reduction in corneal epithelial damage and immune cell infiltration [37]. Targeting C3 with a cobra venom-derived drug provided corneal protection [37]. Topical insulin has recently been shown to be superior to autologous serum in the treatment of persistent epithelial defects [37]. Heparin eye drops and recombinant DNase eye drops, which are NET-dismantling biologics, can reduce the severity of signs and symptoms in oGHVD [40, 127]. The early experience with adjunct human IVIG and platelet lysate eye drops in oGHVD patients is also promising [128]. Topical steroid and lubricant dependency can be reduced by topical immunoglobulin IgG 0.4% as adjunct therapy in conditions like Sjogren’s disease, oGVHD, and chronic Stevens-Johnson’s disease [129]. Belumosudil, a selective ROCK2 (Rho-associated coiled-coil kinase 2) inhibitor, has emerged as a promising treatment option for refractory cGVHD (Fig. 4). It acts by suppressing Th17-driven inflammation and antifibrotic activity by blocking tumour growth factor-beta (TGF-beta) signalling [130]. It was approved by the FDA for the third-line treatment of cGvHD after ruxolitinib failure in 2021 [130]. The potential of modulating the microbiome in improving immune function is also being investigated.

Conclusion

With the rise in HSCT and advancements in managing life-threatening GVHD complications, eye care professionals will likely see more patients with oGVHD. oGVHD presents a significant challenge affecting a substantial proportion of patients and leading to considerable ocular morbidity. As our understanding of the pathophysiology of oGVHD evolves, it is crucial to recognise the multifaceted nature of this condition and thus the need for a holistic multidisciplinary approach. Continued collaboration among specialists and further investigation into innovative treatment strategies will be essential in improving outcomes for those affected by ocular manifestations of graft-versus-host disease.

Methods of Literature Search

The literature search was performed on PubMed and Google Scholar with the major terms being “Graft-Versus-Host Disease” and “Ocular GVHD.” Original studies, case reports, and editorials about GVHD and oGVHD published within the last 30 years were reviewed. Non-English articles were excluded.
