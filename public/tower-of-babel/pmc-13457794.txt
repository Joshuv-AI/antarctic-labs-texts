Global Burden of Intracerebral Hemorrhage, 1990–2021, and Projections to 2050: A Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Na Yun Kim, Yeona Jo, Dongjin Yeo, Sooji Lee, Jeongseon Oh, Tae Hyeon Kim, Jinyoung Jeong, Yuseon Kang, Hanseul Cho, Jiseung Kang, Masoud Rahmati, Ho Geol Woo, Dong Keon Yon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite resulting in significant mortality and long-term disability, research on the burden of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) remains insufficient. Therefore, this study estimated the global burden of ICH and projected changes in the disease burden through 2050 under scenarios of improved risk factor profiles.MethodsThe burden of ICH was estimated using data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, based on incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and was stratified by region, age, year, sex, and Socio-demographic Index (SDI) level. In addition, we conducted an attribution analysis of the ICH burden, focusing on behavioral (including dietary risks and tobacco) and metabolic risk factors, and developed scenario-based projections through 2050 to estimate the potential impact of improved risk management.ResultsIn 2021, a total of 3,444.34 thousand incident cases (95% uncertainty interval [UI], 3,053.01–3,812.04) of ICH were reported worldwide, along with 3,308.37 thousand deaths (3,021.08–3,594.72) attributable to ICH. Males exhibited a higher disease burden, with an age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) per 100,000 population of 48.71 (95% UI, 43.07–54.27), compared to 33.61 (29.47–37.22) in females. The burden progressively increased with age, with notably higher mortality observed among individuals aged ≥ 65 years. There were substantial disparities in the ASIR by SDI level, with high SDI countries showing the lowest rates at 16.30 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 14.51–18.02), compared to 57.83 (51.95–63.27) in low SDI countries. Among behavioral and metabolic risk factors, high systolic blood pressure and smoking were identified as the leading modifiable risk factors for ICH. Forecasts based on the improved behavioral and metabolic risk scenario project a reduction in the age-standardized DALY rate to 166.13 per 100,000 population (95% UI, 132.31–209.64) by 2050, compared to 599.04 (482.89–744.16) projected under the baseline scenario.ConclusionAlthough the global burden of ICH has declined, it remains a major concern in several regions. Projections through 2050 suggest that improvements in the behavioral and metabolic risk profiles could substantially reduce the future burden of ICH, emphasizing the importance of targeted strategies to support evidence-based public health policy.Keywords: Disability-Adjusted Life Years, Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, Incidence, Intracerebral Hemorrhage, Mortality, Risk Factor

INTRODUCTION

Stroke, a major global health concern, ranks as the second-leading cause of death (CoD) and the third-leading cause of disability worldwide. Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), a subtype of stroke, is associated with exceptionally high mortality and long-term disability, contributing to almost half of all stroke-related deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) attributable to stroke.1 Studies have shown that behavioral and metabolic risk factors, including diet, smoking, and high systolic blood pressure, are the major contributors to stroke.2,3 Therefore, the effective management of modifiable risk factors for ICH is crucial for reducing the global burden of stroke-related morbidity and mortality.

Despite this, previous studies have not sufficiently investigated the different impacts of these risk factors for ICH across sexes and geographical regions, often restricting their analyses to certain populations, such as young adults, or specific geographies.1,4,5,6,7 Additionally, several studies have excluded dietary risk factors, one of the most important modifiable risk factors.7 These limitations hinder the development of tailored risk management strategies that cover the specific characteristics of different populations. In this study, we examined the age-standardized incidence, mortality, and DALYs of ICH attributable to behavioral and metabolic risk factors using data from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021. Additionally, we analyzed the differential impact of each risk factor by sex and geographical region. Furthermore, by projecting the regional and global ICH burden up to 2050 under improved behavioral and metabolic risk factor scenarios, this study provides valuable insights for future healthcare planning and prevention strategies.

METHODS

Overview

We used estimates from the GBD 2021 study to analyze the global, regional, and national burden of ICH from 1990 to 2021 across 204 countries.8 The GBD 2021 offers a comprehensive assessment of 371 diseases and conditions across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021, with the geographical classifications used in this study detailed in Supplementary Table 1.8 In this study, we focused on the key metrics, including incidence, mortality, and DALYs, which were stratified by age, sex, Socio-demographic Index (SDI) levels, and region.9 All the analytical procedures adhered to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting framework (Supplementary Table 2). All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.10.4; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA).8

Case definition

ICH was defined as non-traumatic bleeding due to the rupture of a blood vessel, resulting in the focal accumulation of blood within the brain parenchyma or ventricular system, clinically presenting as a stroke. We included first-ever, subtype-specific strokes, including cases that did not survive hospital admission, in our estimates of cause-specific mortality.8 The relevant International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes for ICH include I61, I62, I69.0–69.298 for ICD-10.8 These codes were uniformly applied to both mortality and incidence estimates, ensuring temporal and cross-regional consistency.10 The data sources for estimating ICH, including surveys, censuses, and vital statistics, are detailed in the GBD 2021 study.8

Modelling the burden of ICH

The GBD 2021 CoD database incorporates mortality data from vital registration, verbal autopsy, and vital statistics.11 ICH mortality was estimated using the Cause of Death Ensemble model (CODEm), a core component of the GBD 2021 analytical framework. The CODEm is designed to model cause-specific mortality through an ensemble of diverse statistical models.8 The model incorporates multiple data sources and covariates, with covariate details provided in Supplementary Table 3. The model weights are optimized based on out-of-sample predictive validity, and the spatial-temporal trends in mortality are systematically and robustly estimated across countries and years.8

In addition, ICH morbidity was estimated using the GBD Bayesian meta-regression tool DisMod-MR 2.1, which is the primary modeling framework used in the GBD 2021 to generate consistent estimates of non-fatal health outcomes.10 DisMod-MR 2.1 integrates heterogeneous epidemiological data to estimate the incidence and prevalence of disease across age, sex, year, and location.12 In settings with sparse data, additional tools such as spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression and linear mixed-effects regression (ST-GPR) were used to strengthen the estimates across space and time.10 The meta-regression–Bayesian, regularized, trimmed (MR-BRT) framework was also applied for cross-walking between case definitions and adjusting for methodological biases. DALYs represent the years of healthy life lost due to premature mortality and time lived in less than full health, serving as a comprehensive metric of disease burden. The estimation methods have been detailed in the GBD 2021.10

In GBD 2021, the redistribution of deaths initially recorded as hypertension was improved by incorporating multiple cause-of-death data from seven countries with high-quality vital registration systems.8 Consequently, a greater number of deaths previously attributed to hypertension were reassigned to stroke, particularly ICH. GBD 2021 also improved the noise reduction process by smoothing stochastic fluctuations in time series data, thereby enhancing the consistency and aligning the national mortality trends with regional patterns.8 The burden of ICH was estimated using the incidence count, mortality count, DALY count, age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR), and age-standardized disability-adjusted life year rate (ASDR), along with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs).1

APC

To assess the long-term trends in the burden of ICH, we estimated the APC in the age-standardized incidence, mortality, and DALY rates from 1990 to 2021. The APC was calculated separately for each country and GBD region using a log-linear regression model, and the statistical significance was evaluated using a two-sided test (P < 0.05).13 In this model, the natural logarithm of each metric was regressed against the calendar year.

Risk factors

The GBD 2021 study quantified the disease burden attributable to specific risk factors using a comparative risk assessment framework.14 In total, the GBD 2021 risk factor analysis used 54,561 distinct data sources, which included a small number of sources used to estimate both the relative risk and risk exposure.15 Exposure data were obtained from national surveys, environmental monitoring systems, and health examination data, while the relative risks were sourced from meta-analyses and large-scale prospective cohort studies.14 The data sources available for estimating the relative risks and exposure differed among risk factors, and the input data were highly heterogeneous, and the quality varied geographically and temporally.15 This method estimates the extent to which the disease burden could be reduced if exposure to a particular risk factor were lowered to the theoretical minimum risk exposure level.16 The risk–outcome pairs were selected based on robust and consistent evidence supporting a causal relationship. The population attributable fraction was derived by integrating the estimates of exposure distribution, relative risk, and the theoretical minimum risk exposure level.16

The risk factors are organized into a four-level hierarchical structure, ranging from level 1 (broad categories) to level 4 (specific exposure). In this study, we focused on level 4 risk factors associated with ICH, including tobacco use (e.g., smoking and second-hand smoke), metabolic risks (e.g., high fasting plasma glucose, high systolic blood pressure, and high body-mass index), and dietary risks (e.g., a diet high in sodium, a diet low in fruits, a diet low in fiber, a diet low in vegetables, and a diet high in red meat).14 Additional information on the definitions and data sources used for risk factors is provided in the Supplementary Data 1.

Forecasting ICH mortality and DALY to 2050

This study projected the estimated mortality and DALYs from ICH through 2050 by incorporating independent health drivers, sociodemographic indicators such as the SDI, and major public health measures, including vaccination rates and antiretroviral treatment.14 The projection model accounted for historical patterns, recent epidemiological changes, and unexpected external events.12 The trends in the risk factors were projected using summary exposure values, which were then used to calculate population-attributable fractions and adjust the cause-specific mortality estimates accordingly.14 The forecasting framework integrated historical trends, recent shifts, and external shocks. Initially, the future trajectories of risk factors were modeled via summary exposure values to estimate population-attributable fractions and cause-specific mortality multipliers.14

A comprehensive ensemble modeling framework was employed, incorporating 12 sub-models developed from two primary methodologies: one based on the annualized rates of change and the other utilizing a two-stage spline model implemented through the MR-BRT platform.14 To mitigate the risk of overestimating the contribution of specific risk factors, an ensemble strategy was also used to compute the mediator summary exposure values. Sub-model weights were determined through out-of-sample validation, with training conducted on data from 1990 to 2009 and validation performed using data from 2010 to 2019. The model performance was assessed using the root mean squared error.14

The final estimates through 2050 were produced using 500 Monte Carlo simulations per model. Mortality and DALY projections were conducted under two distinct scenarios: a baseline reference scenario and an alternative scenario reflecting the reductions in behavioral and metabolic risk factors. In the alternative setting, adult exposure to smoking, poor dietary habits, and elevated metabolic risks was assumed to decline linearly from 2023, reaching complete elimination by 2050. Furthermore, the model assumed that no new individuals would initiate smoking after 2022.17

RESULTS

Global burden of ICH from 1990 to 2021

In 2021, the global incidence of ICH was 3,444.34 thousand cases (95% UI, 3,053.01 to 3,812.04). The global ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR were 40.83 (95% UI, 36.20 to 45.21), 39.09 (35.65 to 42.45), and 923.64 (844.83 to 993.18) per 100,000 population, respectively (Fig. 1, Table 1). Males were identified as a significant risk factor for ICH, associated with higher incidence, mortality, and DALYs compared to females. In 2021, the ASIR per 100,000 population was 48.71 (95% UI, 43.07 to 54.27) in males and 33.61 (29.47 to 37.22) in females (Fig. 2, Table 1). Fig. 2 presents the age-specific ASIR per 100,000 population, showing the highest rate in individuals aged ≥ 95 years, reaching 677.51 (95% UI, 507.62 to 8,771.32) in 2021. In terms of the absolute number of cases, the age group of 70–74 years had the greatest incidence count, with 397.16 thousand cases (95% UI, 308.63 to 505.77; Supplementary Tables 4, 5, 6).

The regional level of ICH burden

The burden of ICH exhibited distinct regional patterns with particularly high rates observed in Central Asia and Oceania. In 2021, the ASIR per 100,000 population was 74.70 (95% UI, 69.34 to 79.87) in Central Asia and 74.42 (67.99 to 80.53) in Oceania, both substantially exceeding the global average of 40.83 (36.20 to 45.21) per 100,000 population (Table 1). Although the global ASIR of ICH declined across all SDI levels from 1990 to 2021, substantial disparities persisted by SDI level in 2021 (Fig. 3). In 2021, regions with higher SDI levels experienced significantly lower incidence rates. The ASIR was 16.30 (95% UI, 14.51 to 18.02) per 100,000 in high-SDI countries, compared to 38.55 (33.93 to 43.00) in high-middle SDI and 52.29 (45.87 to 58.52) in middle-SDI countries. Mortality and DALY rates followed similar SDI regional patterns, with detailed results presented in Supplementary Tables 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15.

Behavioral and metabolic risk factors attributable to ICH

The DALY rates associated with each risk factor are presented in Fig. 4. High systolic blood pressure emerged as the leading global risk factor for ICH in both sexes. In 2021, the ASDR per 100,000 population attributable to high systolic blood pressure was 521.09 (95% UI, 388.70 to 638.08). Smoking ranked second, with an ASDR per 100,000 population of 142.49 (95% UI, 120.18 to 167.75), followed by a diet high in sodium, which contributed a rate of 104.83 (28.74 to 223.47) globally for both sexes (Supplementary Tables 16 and 17).

ASDR = age-standardized disability-adjusted life-year rate.

The impact of high systolic blood pressure on the ICH burden varied considerably across regions for both sexes. High-income regions exhibited significantly lower DALYs attributable to high systolic blood pressure compared to the global average. The highest ASDR per 100,000 population among high-income regions was observed in Southern Latin America, at 208.41 (95% UI, 152.43 to 252.75). In contrast, substantially higher burdens were reported in Southeast Asia and Oceania. The ASDR per 100,000 attributable to high systolic blood pressure was 1,213.49 (95% UI, 883.87 to 1,493.43) in Southeast Asia, and 1,168.15 (770.58 to 1,626.47) in Oceania (Supplementary Table 17). The ICH burden attributable to smoking exhibited marked sex-related differences. Globally, the ASDR per 100,000 population attributable to smoking was 268.05 (95% UI, 226.44 to 318.58) in males and 25.97 (21.54 to 31.62) in females (Supplementary Tables 18, 19, 20, 21). A diet high in sodium also exhibited substantial regional variation in its impact on ICH. Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania experienced the greatest burden attributable to high sodium intake (Supplementary Tables 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27). The ASDR per 100,000 population attributable to a diet high in sodium was 239.79 (95% UI, 88.01 to 456.84) in East Asia, 219.62 (24.78 to 541.38) in Oceania, and 238.56 (46.30 to 504.90) in Southeast Asia. In contrast, Australasia showed a markedly lower impact, with the ASDR per 100,000 of 3.59 (95% UI, 0.02 to 13.79; Supplementary Table 16).

DISCUSSION

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the global and regional burden of ICH using estimates from the GBD 2021, including risk-attributable burdens by region and sex, and projected trends through 2050. In 2021, the global ASIR per 100,000 population was 40.83 (95% UI, 36.20 to 45.21), and the ASDR was 923.64 (844.83 to 993.18). Despite consistent declines in the ICH burden across all SDI regions from 1990 to 2021, the burden remained markedly higher in middle- and low-SDI regions compared to high SDI regions. Among the evaluated risk factors, elevated systolic blood pressure was identified as the primary contributor to the ICH burden, with a lower burden observed in high-income regions. The burden attributable to smoking ranked second, exhibiting significant sex-related differences. Of all dietary risk factors, a diet high in sodium had the greatest impact, showing considerable regional variations. Furthermore, projections through 2050 suggest that substantial reductions in the burden of ICH are achievable with improvements in behavioral and metabolic risk factors, compared to the reference scenario.

Although previous studies have reported conflicting findings regarding sex-based disparities in the ICH burden, our findings suggest a higher overall disease burden in males.18,19 This difference may be partly explained by the protective effects of endogenous estrogens, which offer metabolic advantages to females and may reduce susceptibility to ICH.20 The higher incidence among males may be attributed to the more pronounced impact of metabolic and behavioral risk factors, including a diet high in sodium, smoking, and high blood pressure. Notably, although males initially present with a higher incidence, the sex gap narrows with advancing age. This trend appears to be closely related to systolic blood pressure, in which a similar age-related pattern is observed.21

High systolic blood pressure had a lower impact on disease burden in high-income regions, suggesting that socioeconomic status influences risk factor management. In contrast, numerous people in low- and middle-income countries lack access to antihypertensive drugs and are often unaware of their hypertension despite the urgent need for treatment.22,23 Among the dietary risk factors, high sodium intake had the greatest global impact. High sodium consumption elevates the renal perfusion pressure and worsens hypertension by promoting immune cell infiltration into the kidney.24 Furthermore, by inducing innate immune memory, a high-sodium diet impairs the brain’s long-term repair mechanisms following stroke.25

Our projection scenario suggests that improvements in the behavioral and metabolic risk factors could lead to a substantial reduction in the global burden of ICH compared to the reference scenario. Therefore, it is imperative to implement comprehensive, region-specific public health strategies addressing both behavioral and metabolic risk factors. The Sustainable Development Goals have established a target to reduce premature mortality from noncommunicable diseases by one-third, while the World Health Organization has identified an unhealthy diet as one of the four major behavioral risk factors for noncommunicable diseases, emphasizing the urgent need for nutritional interventions.

Previous studies have not sufficiently investigated the differential impacts of behavioral and metabolic risk factors for ICH across sexes and geographical regions, often focusing solely on global trends.1,4 Moreover, some studies have limited their analyses to specific populations, such as young adults, or particular geographic areas.5,6 Our study reports the risk factor-attributable disease burden by sex and across GBD super regions, offering a more accurate representation of each population’s burden. Additionally, we included dietary risk factors in our analyses, which have often been excluded in previous studies. Dietary risk factors are among the most significant modifiable risk factors for noncommunicable diseases and should be thoroughly evaluated to inform policymakers.7 Furthermore, there is a lack of studies forecasting future trends in the behavioral and metabolic risk factor-related burden.26 These limitations hinder the development of tailored risk management strategies that cover the specific characteristics of different populations.

This study is the first to provide a comprehensive analysis of the varying impact of modifiable risk factors contributing to ICH, stratified by sex, region, and SDI level, thereby enabling policymakers to allocate health resources more effectively across diverse populations. However, several potential limitations should be acknowledged. First, the limited availability of data, particularly in low- and middle-income settings, may have introduced additional uncertainty and heterogeneity into the estimates. Nonetheless, GBD 2021 employed data adjustment procedures, including the matching of alternative and reference observations based on demographic characteristics, and utilized ST-GPR to smooth the estimates across age, time, and location in cases of incomplete data coverage. Second, the study did not incorporate all behavioral risk factors. Specifically, we did not assess the impact of physical activity on ICH, despite substantial research suggesting that regular exercise reduces the risk of stroke and lowers recurrence rates among stroke survivors.27 Third, the GBD dataset lacks data on the genetic predisposition and ethnicity. Although ICH disproportionately affects certain ethnic and racial groups, we were unable to assess these differential impacts due to data limitations.28 Fourth, there is a possibility that we overestimated the impact of dietary risk factors, as we assessed them individually. Individuals adhering to a healthy diet typically consider multiple dietary components in combination, rather than focusing exclusively on a single element. Finally, when assessing the risk factors, some GBD 2021 findings were based on self-reports, which may compromise reliability.

Despite the limitations, our study offers several notable strengths. We utilized a range of indicators to evaluate the disease burden, including age-standardized incidence, mortality, and DALY rates, and conducted analyses stratified by sex and SDI regions. Additionally, our findings indicate that the impact of high systolic blood pressure varies by income level, with a disproportionately lower burden observed in high-income regions. Moreover, while previous studies have often neglected dietary factors, our study incorporates these elements, revealing regional variations in the attributable burden of sodium on ICH. Furthermore, the study provides projections that account for potential improvements in behavioral and metabolic risk factors, emphasizing the importance of targeted health policies to address these modifiable determinants.

Globally, the burden of ICH has declined consistently from 1990 to 2021; however, it remained a substantial health concern across most regions. High systolic blood pressure was identified as a leading modifiable risk factor, with variations observed by sex and region. Projections through 2050 suggest that an enhanced management of behavioral and metabolic risk factors may result in greater reductions in the ICH burden than previously estimated. These findings underscore the importance of strengthening control over modifiable individual risk factors to mitigate the global burden of ICH and provide critical insights to inform the development of effective public health strategies.
