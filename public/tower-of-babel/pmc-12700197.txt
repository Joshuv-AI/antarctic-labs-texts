Phytophthora effector exploits WY module truncation to manipulate Nt-acetylation–mediated AtMBP-1 turnover and suppress plant immunity
Authors: Song Tian, Qin Peng, Xiaoran Du, Guangda Shao, Wenxin Gao, Qingyu Liu, Biao Gu, Xili Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Phytophthora pathogens deliver a repertoire of WY(L) modular effectors to disarm plant immunity. Understanding how these conserved modules mediate the interactions between Phytophthora effectors and host targets to manipulate plant immunity is crucial. Here, we identified two WY(L) modular paralogous effectors, PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b, essential for Phytophthora capsici virulence. Transposon insertion induced truncation of WY(L) module, enabling PcAvh337b to target Arabidopsis cMyc binding protein 1 (AtMBP-1). While the existence of AtMBP-1 has been debated, this study demonstrates that alternative transcription initiation and translation can generate AtMBP-1, further revealing that AtMBP-1 compromises plant immune responses as a susceptibility factor. AtMBP-1 homeostasis is antagonistically regulated by N-terminal acetyltransferases A and C (NatA and NatC). By modulating AtMBP-1–Nat complexes, PcAvh337b enhances NatA-mediated acetylation of AtMBP-1 to protect AtMBP-1 from ubiquitin-dependent degradation, thereby promoting P. capsici infection. These findings reveal a pathogenic mechanism through which an effector regulates AtMBP-1–Nat modules to suppress plant defense responses, highlighting virulence mechanisms diversification driven by effector dynamics.

INTRODUCTION

Phytophthora pathogens are highly destructive eukaryotic microbes that cause devastating losses in agriculture and forestry (1, 2). Phytophthora capsici is particularly notable for its ability to infect a wide range of economic vegetables by delivering an array of fast-evolving effectors into host cells (3, 4). RXLR effectors, which represent the largest class of cell-entering effectors (5), contain a signal peptide (SP) and a conserved RXLR motif in the N terminus, whereas their C-terminal regions exhibit remarkable sequence divergence (6, 7). Adaptive evolution has led to a structural hallmark, the WY(L) module fold, which is shared by more than 30% of Phytophthora RXLR effectors (6, 8). The individual WY units within multiple repeats contribute differently to the virulence function of RXLR effectors (8). Sequence divergence in the flanking regions of the core WY residues enables structurally similar effectors—such as PsAvh240 and PsAvh241—to target distinct host proteins and manipulate different immune signaling pathways (9, 10). In addition, the rearrangement of WY units in RXLR effectors determines the variation in effector targets. For example, Phytophthora suppressor of RNA silencing 1 (PSR1), which contains a single WY unit, interacts with PSR1-interacting protein 1 (PINP1) to inhibit small RNA biogenesis (11). PSR2, with seven tandem repeats of the WY unit, impairs secondary small interfering RNA production (12). Breakthrough research has recently demonstrated that a group of WY-containing effectors of Phytophthora pathogens hijack plant serine/threonine protein phosphatase 2A to form holoenzymes and regulate multiple phosphoproteins, promoting Phytophthora infection (13). The functional diversity and mechanistic versatility of RXLR modular effectors are increasingly understood (14, 15). A recent study by Li et al. (13) demonstrated that shuffling of WY tandem repeats promotes effector repertoire evolvability in Phytophthora through domain modularity. Nevertheless, whether additional mechanisms drive RXLR effector diversification and neofunctionalization requires further exploration.

To defend against diverse pathogens, plants have developed sophisticated immune systems intricately connected to primary metabolism, facilitating the generation of immune precursors and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (16). In Arabidopsis thaliana, the glycolytic protein enolase (AtEno2), encoded by the LOW EXPRESSION OF OSMOTICALLY RESPONSIVE GENES 2 (LOS2) gene, catalyzes the reversible conversion of 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate. Reducing enolase activity leads to a perturbation of glycolysis, which further activates nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR)–involved immune responses (16). AtEno2 is also involved in development, reproductive success, and abiotic stress responses (17–19). In addition, AtEno2 has been proposed to generate the N-terminal truncated isoform cMyc binding protein 1 (AtMBP-1) through alternative translation of the LOS2 gene (20). Several studies have suggested that AtMBP-1 modulates abscisic acid (ABA) sensitivity; contributes to cold, drought, and salinity stress; and coordinates energy metabolism (18, 20–22). However, a recent report showed that the AtMBP-1 protein is barely detectable under normal conditions and may not be an alternative translation product of the LOS2 gene (16). This finding challenges the notion that AtMBP-1 is a genuine alternative translation product of LOS2, warranting further investigation to validate the existence of AtMBP-1.

N-terminal acetylation (NTA) is an irreversible protein modification catalyzed by N-terminal acetyltransferase (Nat), which transfers an acetyl moiety from acetyl–coenzyme A to the N-terminal amino acid of the target protein (23, 24). Eukaryotes have three evolutionarily conserved Nat complexes (NatA, NatB, and NatC) whose substrate specificity is determined by the first two N-terminal residues of the substrate (25, 26). In A. thaliana, the down-regulation of NatA, NatB, or NatC complexes causes growth retardation, with NatA suppression inducing ABA-mediated drought responses (26), NatB deficiency conferring salt hypersensitivity and downregulating pathogen defense genes (27), and NatC depletion reducing thylakoid multiprotein complex abundance (28). Critically, NTA regulates protein stability and half-life (29). NatA-mediated acetylation stabilizes its substrates, and the depletion of NatA activity promotes substrates degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) (30). NatB-mediated acetylation stabilizes the salicylic acid–primed cell death regulator SIGMA FACTOR-BINDING PROTEIN1 (SIB1), ensuring the expression of SIB1-dependent immune-related genes (31). Alternative translation initiation generates two variants of suppressor of NPR1, constitutive 1 (SNC1), a critical Nod-like receptor, with distinct N termini (MMD-SNC1 and MD-SNC1). NatA and NatB differentially regulate the stability of MMD-SNC1 and MD-SNC1 through acetylation, with NatA promoting degradation and NatB enhancing stabilization. This antagonistic regulation is essential for maintaining SNC1 protein homeostasis, underscoring the pivotal role of Nats in plant immunity (32). Given the importance of NTA in plant immune regulation, it is valuable to explore whether pathogen virulence factors directly target Nat complexes or regulate NTA to disrupt plant immunity.

This study identified two paralogous RXLR effectors from P. capsici, PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b, which are critical for full pathogen virulence. PcAvh337b arises from a transposon insertion that causes premature termination, resulting in the loss of a C-terminal WY unit compared with PcAvh337a. Strikingly, the structural alteration in WY(L) module confers PcAvh337b, but not PcAvh337a, the unique ability to specifically target the plant susceptibility (S) factor AtMBP-1. NatA and NatC antagonistically regulate the homeostasis of AtMBP-1: NatA-mediated NTA stabilizes AtMBP-1 by reducing AtMBP-1 ubiquitination and degradation, whereas NatC counteracts this effect. PcAvh337b enhances NatA-mediated acetylation of AtMBP-1, thereby inhibiting UPS-mediated degradation and prolonging AtMBP-1 accumulation to promote P. capsici infection. This work reveals a pathogenic mechanism in which the truncation-driven diversification of effectors enables functional specialization in host target manipulation, and P. capsici exploits antagonistic Nats to stabilize the S factor AtMBP-1 to promote infection.

RESULTS

PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b are essential virulence factors of P. capsici

By transcriptome analysis of P. capsici during its interaction with A. thaliana and tomato (33, 34), we conducted a broad screen for up-regulated RXLR effectors and identified two sequence-similar effectors with divergent lengths, PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b. The amino acid sequences of PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b share 86% similarity. Both effectors have a secretion signal peptide at the N terminus, followed by the characteristic RXLR-dEER motif, whereas PcAvh337b lacks one WY unit at its C terminus compared with PcAvh337a (Fig. 1A and fig. S1). The genetic analysis of the PcAvh337 locus revealed that the class I transposon TNT1-94 is located adjacent to the C-terminal region of PcAvh337b, thereby separating the third WY unit from PcAvh337b and resulting in premature termination (Fig. 1B). In addition to the C-terminal truncation, 14 nonsynonymous substitutions were observed between the two paralogs (fig. S1). AlphaFold3 structural modeling demonstrated that these amino acid substitutions do not substantively alter the overall structural similarity between the two effectors (Fig. 1C and fig. S2). To investigate the evolutionary conservation of PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b, we conducted BLASTP searches via FungiDB against sequenced genomes of major Phytophthora pathogens, including Phytophthora sojae, Phytophthora infestans, and Phytophthora parasitica. The analyses showed that homologs of both effectors were exclusively identified in P. capsici. We further examined their distribution across 168 geographically diverse P. capsici isolates, revealing that PcAvh337a was universally present, while PcAvh337b occurred in only 50% of isolates (table S1).

To assess the virulence contribution of PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b, we generated C-terminal mCherry-fused PcAvh337 and mCherry-only transformants. Through immunoblot and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analyses (fig. S3, A and B), three independent overexpression lines for each effector (PcAvh337a-OE and PcAvh337b-OE) and one mCherry overexpression line control (CK) were selected for subsequent experiments. Phenotypic analyses indicated no notable impact of overexpressing either PcAvh337a or PcAvh337b on P. capsici development (fig. S3, C to G). Pathogenicity assays showed that A. thaliana leaves challenged with zoospores from either PcAvh337a-OE or PcAvh337b-OE transformants developed significantly more severe disease symptoms than those challenged with wild-type (WT) and CK strains (Fig. 1D and figs. S4 and S5).

To further elucidate the virulence function of these effectors, we used CRISPR-Cas9–mediated genome editing to generate single-knockout mutants of PcAvh337a (PcAvh337a-KO) and PcAvh337b (PcAvh337b-KO) as well as double-knockout mutants (PcAvh337-KO). The homozygous status of the transformants was confirmed via PCR amplification (fig. S6A) and Sanger sequencing. Phenotypic analyses indicated no notable impact of knockout of PcAvh337a, PcAvh337b, or both on P. capsici development (fig. S6, B to F). Pathogenicity assays showed that both single-knockout mutants exhibited significantly attenuated virulence on A. thaliana leaves compared with the WT strain and nonedited transformant (CK) (Fig. 1E and figs. S4 and S7). The double-knockout mutants exhibited an additive virulence defect, displaying an even greater reduction in pathogenicity than either single-knockout mutant. These genetic data suggest that both PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b contribute nonredundantly to P. capsici virulence, indicating that PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b potentially target distinct host pathways to collectively promote infection.

PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b translocate into host cells to promote P. capsici infection

To characterize the secretion capacity of PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b, we first performed yeast secretion assays and verified that the signal peptide of PcAvh337 successfully directed invertase secretion. This secretion enabled yeast strain YTK12 to grow on raffinose medium and the conversion of 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) to insoluble red-colored 1,3,5-triphenylformazan (TPF) (Fig. 2, A and B), confirming the functionality of the signal peptide. In addition, we observed that the fluorescence of PcAvh337a-OE and PcAvh337b-OE transformants specifically accumulated around the haustoria during infection (Fig. 2C). Further, immunoblot analysis showed that PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b were detectable in the mycelia and culture filtrate (CF), whereas control actin was detected only in the mycelia (Fig. 2D), indicating that PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b can be secreted from mycelia.

To demonstrate the entry to host cells, we linked full-length PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b with Avr1bCt, which triggers Rps1b-dependent resistance in soybean (35). The Avr1b-knockout transformant ∆114-1-7 (36) was used as the recipient strain to generate PcAvh337a-Avr1bCt– and PcAvh337b-Avr1bCt–overexpressing transformants (fig. S8). All homozygous transformants and the WT strain P7063 could infect Williams (rps1b) hypocotyls but not L77-1863 (Rps1b) hypocotyls (Fig. 2, E and F), suggesting the successful translocation of the chimeric proteins and initiation of Rps1b-dependent resistance. In contrast, ∆114-1-7 killed both Williams and L77-1863 soybean seedlings. Together, these results indicate that PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b can translocate into host cells.

To examine the in planta virulence activity of PcAvh337, we generated PcAvh337a- and PcAvh337b-overexpressing transgenic A. thaliana lines (At-PcAvh337a and At-PcAvh337b), with transgene integration and expression confirmed at the genomic, transcriptional, and protein levels (fig. S9). Pathogenicity assays showed that At-PcAvh337a and At-PcAvh337b transgenic lines exhibited significantly enhanced susceptibility compared with the WT and green fluorescent protein (GFP) control line (CK) (Fig. 2, G and H, and figs. S4 and S10), demonstrating that both effectors function as bona fide virulence factors that promote P. capsici infection.

PcAvh337b, rather than PcAvh337a, interacts with AtMBP-1

To explore the virulence mechanism by which PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b facilitate the infection of P. capsici, we performed liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to identify the potential interactors of PcAvh337. The results identified AtEno2 as a shared candidate interactor for both effectors. In addition, a yeast library screening using PcAvh337b as a bait also identified the candidate interacting protein AtEno2 (table S2). Given that AtEno2 undergoes alternative translation to produce AtMBP-1 (20, 21), we sought to distinguish which isoform interacts with each effector. We generated AtEno2M, a mutant that exclusively produces enolase through a Met93-Leu substitution that prevents AtMBP-1 translation. We then tested the interaction of PcAvh337a or PcAvh337b with AtEno2M or AtMBP-1 through yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays. Only the interaction between PcAvh337b and AtMBP-1 was detectable (Fig. 3A). We further validated the interaction in vivo through co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays. Consistent with the Y2H results, AtMBP-1–GFP specifically co-immunoprecipitated with red fluorescent protein (RFP)–PcAvh337b rather than with RFP-PcAvh337a (Fig. 3B). The AlphaFold3 structural modeling reveals that AtEno2 and AtMBP-1 share core structural similarity, with the primary difference being an N-terminal extension (residues 1 to 92) present in AtEno2 but absent in AtMBP-1 (fig. S11). Integrated with structural analyses of PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b, these results indicate that structural variations can profoundly remodel protein interactomes and lead to functional divergence.

Since the primary structural difference between PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b lies in the third WY unit present only in PcAvh337a, we further constructed a C-terminal truncation mutant (PcAvh337aM) mimicking PcAvh337b; this modified effector gained the ability to interact with AtMBP-1 in both yeast and plant systems (Fig. 3, C and D), suggesting that the truncation in tandem repeats of WY units may serve as a way to promote effector expansion and evolution, facilitating the adaptation of pathogens to hosts.

Confirmation of AtMBP-1 expression at the transcriptional and translational levels

Given the ongoing debate regarding the existence of AtMBP-1 (16, 20), we conducted a comprehensive analysis of its expression. We designed three primers targeting distinct transcriptional isoforms: (i) AtEno2-F at the canonical initiation codon, (ii) AtMBP-1-UTR-F, 92–base pair (bp) upstream of the putative AtMBP-1 start site, and (iii) LOS2-R spanning intronic regions to ensure cDNA-specific amplification (Fig. 4A). Gel electrophoresis and Sanger sequencing confirmed successful amplification of the AtMBP-1 transcript from cDNA, although at substantially lower abundance than AtEno2 (Fig. 4B). To validate the existence of AtMBP-1 at the protein level, we generated transgenic A. thaliana lines expressing pLOS2:LOS2-GFP (full-length LOS2 fused with a GFP tag driven by the native LOS2 promoter) and 35S:AtEno2-GFP (coding sequence of AtEno2 fused with a GFP tag under the control of the 35S promoter) (Fig. 4C). We initially attempted to generate constitutive AtMBP-1–GFP overexpression transgenic A. thaliana. However, despite successful transgene transcription, we failed to detect the fusion protein. Therefore, we developed an inducible expression system using a β-estradiol–responsive promoter (XVE:AtMBP-1–GFP) (Fig. 4C). The immunoblot analysis of the fusion proteins from the transgenic plants showed the expected protein sizes of pLOS2:LOS2-GFP, 35S:AtEno2-GFP, and XVE:AtMBP-1–GFP; however, additional bands were detected in the pLOS2:LOS2-GFP and 35S:AtEno2-GFP samples, one of which corresponded to the size of XVE:AtMBP-1–GFP (Fig. 4D), suggesting that AtMBP-1 can be produced by alternative transcription initiation and alternative translation. Furthermore, LC-MS analysis of band 3 (red arrow) in pLOS2:LOS2-GFP sample identified a N-terminal peptide of AtMBP-1 and with NTA on valine residue (Fig. 4E). These results provide conclusive evidence for the endogenous accumulation of AtMBP-1 protein in A. thaliana.

AtMBP-1 acts as an S factor to boost P. capsici infection

Previous studies have demonstrated that AtMBP-1 plays a role in abiotic stress responses (18, 20, 22). However, the function of AtMBP-1 in plant immunity remains poorly understood. Our investigation revealed that AtMBP-1 expression is significantly induced during P. capsici infection (Fig. 5A), suggesting potential involvement in plant defense responses. To clarify the role of AtMBP-1 in plant immunity, three independent XVE:AtMBP-1–GFP lines (AtMBP-1–OE) were selected for pathogenicity assay (fig. S12). Upon challenge with P. capsici zoospores, the leaves of AtMBP-1–OE lines exhibited larger infection lesions and increased pathogen biomass compared with the CK (estradiol-inducible GFP expression line) (Fig. 5, B and C, and figs. S4 and S13).

To obtain insight into the underlying molecular mechanisms giving rise to the susceptible phenotype of the AtMBP-1–OE lines, we performed a global transcriptome analysis of AtMBP-1–OE plants challenged with P. capsici. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed marked overrepresentation of immune-related genes (Fig. 5, D and E), particularly pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) components, including receptor-like kinases, receptor-like proteins, calcium-dependent protein kinases, mitogen-activated protein kinases, and WRKY transcription factors (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, single time-point RT-qPCR results showed that the expression of the WRKY70, FRK1, and PR12 genes was significantly repressed in AtMBP-1–OE plants compared with those in CK plants during P. capsici infection (Fig. 5G). Given that AtEno2 has been reported to associate with NLR-involved immune responses, we analyzed its transcript levels in AtMBP-1–OE transgenic lines. The results showed comparable AtEno2 expression between the AtMBP-1–OE lines and CK plants (fig. S14), indicating that the immune suppression observed in AtMBP-1–OE lines is independent of AtEno2 transcriptional regulation. Collectively, these findings establish AtMBP-1 as a bona fide susceptibility factor.

PcAvh337b prevents UPS-mediated degradation of the S factor AtMBP-1

Notably, LC-MS analysis confirmed the occurrence of ubiquitination at multiple sites on AtMBP-1 (fig. S15). To further investigate AtMBP-1 stability, we performed cell-free degradation and cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays using protein extracts from AtMBP-1–OE lines, which revealed a time-dependent decrease in AtMBP-1–GFP abundance. This degradation was markedly attenuated by MG132 (a proteasome inhibitor) but not by 3-methyladenine (3-MA; an autophagy inhibitor) (Fig. 6, A to C), demonstrating UPS-dependent turnover of AtMBP-1. Consistent with these findings, Co-IP and immunoblot analyses confirmed the presence of ubiquitin-conjugated AtMBP-1–GFP (Fig. 6D), providing direct evidence for UPS-mediated degradation of AtMBP-1. We further examined the stability of AtEno2M. Cell-free degradation and CHX chase assays showed a marked difference between AtMBP-1 and AtEno2M, with AtEno2M exhibiting high stability (fig. S16).

To investigate whether PcAvh337b regulates AtMBP-1 protein homeostasis, we performed a comparative analysis of AtMBP-1–GFP accumulation in AtMBP-1–OE protoplasts coexpressing RFP-PcAvh337b using double RFP (DRFP) and RFP-PcAvh337a as controls. As expected, we observed markedly increased AtMBP-1–GFP protein levels in the presence of PcAvh337b (Fig. 6E), indicating a potential role of PcAvh337b in stabilizing AtMBP-1. RT-qPCR analysis revealed comparable AtMBP-1 transcript levels between At-PcAvh337b transgenic lines and GFP control plants (fig. S17), confirming that PcAvh337b-mediated regulation occurs posttranslationally rather than at the transcriptional level. Given the established role of AtMBP-1 in plant PTI and the finding that PcAvh337b stabilizes AtMBP-1, we further investigated the impact of PcAvh337b on PTI responses. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the expression of defense-related genes (WRKY70, FRK1, and PR12) was significantly down-regulated in At-PcAvh337b transgenic A. thaliana compared with those in GFP control plants during P. capsici infection (Fig. 6F). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that PcAvh337b protects the S factor AtMBP-1 from UPS-mediated degradation to suppress defense gene expression and boost P. capsici infection.

NatA and NatC antagonistically regulate the stability of AtMBP-1

In addition to AtMBP-1, our yeast library screening identified AtNAA10, the catalytic subunit of NatA, as an interactor of PcAvh337b (table S2). Intriguingly, the LC-MS analysis of immunoprecipitated AtMBP-1 from AtMBP-1–OE lines revealed two distinct NTA states: one retaining initiator methionine (iMet) acetylation (ac-MV) and another with iMet cleavage followed by valine acetylation (ac-V) (Fig. 7A). Given the substrate specificity of different Nat complexes, we hypothesized that NatA and NatC act as functionally antagonistic acetyltransferases regulating AtMBP-1 protein stability, with PcAvh337b potentially modulating AtMBP-1 NTA status through its interaction with NatA’s catalytic subunit, AtNAA10. To test this hypothesis, we first confirmed the physical interaction between PcAvh337b and AtNAA10 through Co-IP assays (Fig. 7B). Subsequent Y2H and Co-IP assays further demonstrated that AtMBP-1 interacts with both AtNAA10 (NatA catalytic subunit) and AtNAA30 (NatC catalytic subunit) (Fig. 7, C and D), supporting a potential regulatory role of these acetyltransferases in AtMBP-1 turnover.

To explore the roles of NatA and NatC complexes in the turnover of AtMBP-1, gene silencing assays were performed. Given the pleiotropic functions of NatA and NatC (26, 28) and sequence conservation between A. thaliana and Nicotiana benthamiana (fig. S18), we verified the interactions between AtMBP-1 and the N. benthamiana homologs NbNAA10 (NatA catalytic subunit) and NbNAA30 (NatC catalytic subunit) (fig. S19), mirroring AtMBP-1’s interactions with AtNAA10 and AtNAA30 (Fig. 7, C and D). Next, hairpin RNA interference (hpRNAi) was performed to specifically silence NbNAA10 and NbNAA30 in N. benthamiana, with hpRNAi-GUS as a control. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed successful silencing, with 70 and 90% reductions in the NbNAA10 and NbNAA30 transcript levels, respectively, compared with those in the hpRNAi-GUS control (fig. S20). When AtMBP-1–GFP was expressed in these silenced plants, AtMBP-1–GFP accumulation decreased markedly in the NbNAA10-silenced plants but increased in the NbNAA30-silenced plants compared with the control (Fig. 7E). However, AtEno2M-GFP showed a comparable protein accumulation levels in the silenced plants (fig. S21), which indicates the specific regulation of AtMBP-1 stability by NatA and NatC. Ubiquitination analysis revealed that NbNAA10 silencing increased AtMBP-1 ubiquitination, whereas NbNAA30 silencing reduced it (Fig. 7F).

To further understand how NTA regulates AtMBP-1 stability, two AtMBP-1 variants, AtMBP-1V2A and AtMBP-1V2W, designed to be acetylated by NatA and NatC, respectively, were generated. The LC-MS analysis of immunoprecipitated proteins from transiently expressed in N. benthamiana confirmed their distinct acetylation patterns. AtMBP-1V2A showed all the N-terminal peptides with acetylation on alanine following iMet cleavage, whereas AtMBP-1V2W showed 73% of the N-terminal peptides with acetylation on the iMet residue and 27% without acetylation (fig. S22), validating their specific recognition by NatA and NatC, respectively. Strikingly, these acetylation states correlated with differential protein stability: AtMBP-1V2A (NatA-acetylated) exhibited enhanced stability and reduced ubiquitination, whereas AtMBP-1V2W (NatC-acetylated) exhibited decreased stability and increased ubiquitination (Fig. 7, G and H).

To assess the immune function of these acetylation variants against P. capsici, we expressed AtMBP-1V2A and AtMBP-1V2W driven by the native promoter in N. benthamiana leaves through agroinfiltration and challenged the leaves with P. capsici plugs. As shown in Fig. 7I, AtMBP-1V2A (NatA-acetylated) accumulated at higher levels (fig. S23) and conferred greater susceptibility, exhibiting larger lesion areas and increased P. capsici biomass compared with WT AtMBP-1 (Fig. 7I). In contrast, AtMBP-1V2W (NatC-acetylated), which showed reduced protein stability, did not significantly alter disease progression or pathogen biomass relative to the WT control (Fig. 7I and fig. S23). Overall, these results reveal that antagonistic acetylation-ubiquitination maintains the fine-tuning of AtMBP-1 homeostasis to modulate plant immunity.

PcAvh337b stabilizes AtMBP-1 by regulating AtMBP-1–Nat modules

Our findings confirmed that PcAvh337b interacts with the NatA catalytic subunit AtNAA10 and that NTAs of AtMBP-1 by NatA/NatC complexes regulate its stability. Hence, we investigated the molecular interplay between these components. Co-IP assays demonstrated that AtMBP-1–GFP forms complexes with RFP-PcAvh337b, AtNAA10-Flag (the NatA catalytic subunit), and AtNAA30-Flag (the NatC catalytic subunit) (Fig. 8A). Anti-Flag immunoblot analysis of the IP group revealed distinct patterns: The abundance of coprecipitated AtNAA10-Flag was increased in the presence of RFP-PcAvh337b (lane 3) compared to DRFP (lane 1) or RFP-PcAvh337a (lane 2), indicating that PcAvh337b promotes the interaction between AtMBP-1 and AtNAA10; conversely, the AtNAA30-Flag signal was reduced upon coexpression with RFP-PcAvh337b (lane 6) relative to DRFP (lane 4) or RFP-PcAvh337a (lane 5), suggesting that PcAvh337b attenuates the binding affinity between AtMBP-1 and AtNAA30 (Fig. 8A).

Furthermore, we compared the NTA levels of AtMBP-1 coexpressed with DRFP, RFP-PcAvh337a, or RFP-PcAvh337b in N. benthamiana. LC-MS analysis showed that AtMBP-1 exists in four isoforms (MV, ac-MV, V, and ac-V) when coexpressed with DRFP or RFP-PcAvh337a. In the presence of PcAvh337b, AtMBP-1 was detected in two isoforms (V and ac-V), which supports the result that PcAvh337b enhances the interaction between AtMBP-1 and NatA. Further quantitative analysis of the ac-V isoform relative to total detectable AtMBP-1 isoforms showed that the expression of PcAvh337b increased NTA modification on the valine residue of AtMBP-1 by 2.75-fold compared to the DRFP control (Fig. 8B and table S3). To further clarify the specificity of PcAvh337b–AtMBP-1 modulation, we examined whether PcAvh337b affects the interaction and the NTA of AtEno2M. Anti-Flag immunoblot analysis of the IP group showed comparable abundance of coprecipitated AtNAA10-Flag when coexpressed with DRFP, RFP-PcAvh337a, or RFP-PcAvh337b in N. benthamiana (fig. S24, A and B); LC-MS analysis showed consistent NTA levels of AtEno2M, with all detected N-terminal peptides of AtEno2M exhibiting acetylation on alanine following iMet cleavage (fig. S24, C and D). These data demonstrate that PcAvh337b enhances the interaction between AtMBP-1 and AtNAA10, thereby increasing NTA levels on the valine residue of AtMBP-1 to stabilize the S factor AtMBP-1, establishing a direct link between effector binding and acetylation status.

Given the cross-talk between NTA and ubiquitination in the regulation of AtMBP-1 homeostasis, we further examined the ubiquitination levels of AtMBP-1–GFP following coexpression with DRFP, RFP-PcAvh337a, or RFP-PcAvh337b in AtMBP-1–OE protoplasts. As expected, immunoblot analysis showed that PcAvh337b markedly decreased AtMBP-1 ubiquitination compared with the controls (Fig. 8C). Overall, we demonstrated that PcAvh337b reduces the ubiquitination of the S factor AtMBP-1 by modulating AtMBP-1–Nat modules to suppress plant immune responses (Fig. 9).

A working model illustrating how WY(L) truncation enables PcAvh337b to hijack AtMBP-1–Nat modules and suppress plant immunity.

Under normal conditions, the homeostasis of the AtMBP-1 protein is maintained by antagonistic NTAs, where NatA-mediated NTA enhances AtMBP-1 stability, whereas NatC-mediated NTA accelerates AtMBP-1 turnover. During P. capsici–host coevolution, gene duplication and transposon insertion generated PcAvh337b, a truncated isoform of PcAvh337a lacking one WY unit. Critically, the structural alteration enables PcAvh337b, but not PcAvh337a, to target AtMBP-1–Nat modules. PcAvh337b attenuates the interaction between AtMBP-1 and NatC while promoting AtMBP-1’s interaction with NatA. This results in increased NatA-mediated NTA, which reduces the ubiquitination of AtMBP-1 and prolongs the AtMBP-1 half-life, thereby suppressing plant defense responses and facilitating P. capsici infection.

MBP-1 is conserved in P. capsici–susceptible hosts

Enolase, a core glycolytic enzyme, exhibits remarkable evolutionary conservation across biological systems. The sequence alignment of plant enolase orthologs from P. capsici–susceptible hosts (A. thaliana, Capsicum annuum, Solanum melongena, Solanum tuberosum, Solanum lycopersicum, Cucumis sativus, Cucurbita moschata, and Benincasa hispida) revealed strict conservation of the critical methionine residue (M93 in Eno2) required for production of MBP-1 orthologs (fig. S25A). Notably, the overexpression of CaEno2-GFP in N. benthamiana produced multiple protein bands, including one corresponding to the predicted size of CaMBP-1–GFP, providing evidence for the existence of CaMBP-1 in chili pepper (fig. S25B). Moreover, the strict conservation of valine at position 2 in MBP-1 orthologs indicates a potential regulatory role for Nat-mediated NTA (fig. S25A). Crucially, Co-IP assays confirmed that PcAvh337b specifically binds CaMBP-1, but not with full-length CaEno2M (a mutant that exclusively produces enolase through a Met93-Leu substitution that prevents CaMBP-1 translation), mirroring the interaction specificity observed in A. thaliana (fig. S25C). Collectively, these findings suggest that the identified mechanism may operate in C. annuum and could be applicable to other P. capsici host systems.

DISCUSSION

Phytophthora pathogens have evolved unique reservoirs of effectors to facilitate the colonization and proliferation on their hosts (37). Diverse effectors interact with host proteins and target distinct cellular compartments, thereby serving as valuable molecular probes to uncover critical vulnerabilities in plant defense networks (38). In this study, we identified two paralogous RXLR effectors in P. capsici, PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b, which share 86% amino acid sequence similarity (fig. S1). Notably, a TNT1-94 retrotransposon was identified downstream of PcAvh337b, separating the third WY unit from PcAvh337b (Fig. 1B). TNT1-94 is a class I retrotransposon known to mobilize through a “copy-and-paste” mechanism that enables genomic insertion and contributes to gene function diversification (39, 40). This observation aligns with the dynamic architecture of the Phytophthora genome, where effector genes frequently reside in transposable element–rich regions prone to rearrangement, duplication, and rapid diversification driven by transposable element activity (41–44).

To date, sequence variations such as gene duplication, frameshifting, and point mutations have been documented in individual effectors. For example, the P. infestans RXLR effectors PiAvr1 and PiAvr1-like (Pi06432) interact with StSEC5 and StUDP, respectively, suggesting distinct strategies for immune suppression (45–47). Similarly, the P. sojae apoplastic effector PsXLP1, a duplicated paralog of PsXEG1, facilitates pathogen virulence by competitively binding the host inhibitor GmGIP1, thereby protecting PsXEG1 (48). The shuffling of WY tandem repeats has been shown to play a crucial role in maintaining Phytophthora effector repertoire dynamics (13). In this study, we found that the primary structural distinction between PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b is the deletion of a WY unit at the C terminus of the latter (Fig. 1, A and C). We characterized these two P. capsici effectors, PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b, as a case study for understanding RXLR effector expansion and virulence diversification.

Our study establishes that both PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b function as virulence factors (Figs. 1 and 2); however, they use distinct virulence mechanisms (Fig. 3). Structural predictions indicated that PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b share similar architectures, except for an additional WY unit at the C terminus of PcAvh337a. This structural difference was functionally validated by our observation that PcAvh337aM (lacking the C-terminal WY unit) interacts with AtMBP-1 (Fig. 3, C and D). Notably, AtMBP-1 and AtEno2 share identical sequences and core structural similarity except for a 92–amino acid N-terminal extension in AtEno2; however, AtMBP-1 and AtEno2 exhibit markedly different interaction partners and biological functions (16–22, 49). In human, the DNA binding domain at the N terminus of MBP-1 suggests that the N-terminal extension of Eno2 may physically occlude the DNA binding pocket and disrupt its DNA binding capability (50). These observations emphasize that subtle structural variations in proteins can fundamentally reshape their functions. Further investigation of the PcAvh337b–AtMBP-1 interaction interface may reveal valuable insights into the mechanistic differences between the virulence strategies of PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b.

Previous reports claimed that the first methionine-93 codon of AtEno2 is not an effective alternative translation initiation site of the AtMBP-1 gene (16). However, our results demonstrate that plants can generate AtMBP-1 through both independent AtMBP-1 mRNA and alternative translation of AtEno2 mRNA, indicating comprehensive regulation of the AtMBP-1 protein (Fig. 4). Moreover, AtMBP-1 was up-regulated during P. capsici infection, indicating its potential role in plant-pathogen interactions (Fig. 5A). Plants can adapt to pathogen challenges through translational reprogramming during immune responses. Under normal conditions, upstream open reading frames (uORFs) are selectively translated owing to hairpins immediately downstream of upstream start codons (uAUGs). Upon immune induction, RNA helicases resolve hairpin structures, allowing ribosomes to bypass uAUGs to translate downstream defense proteins (51, 52). While our data show AtMBP-1 induction during infection, whether this follows the established uORF regulatory paradigm requires further experimental validation.

The role of AtMBP-1 in plant immunity remains largely enigmatic. Pathogenicity assay and transcriptome analyses of AtMBP-1–OE plants revealed that AtMBP-1 acts as an S factor that hampers plant immune signaling (Fig. 5). Our data suggest a mechanism by which PcAvh337b prevents the degradation of the S factor AtMBP-1 through interference with the UPS, thereby suppressing immune-related gene expression and facilitating P. capsici colonization (Fig. 6). Protein modifications play pivotal roles in protein homeostasis and function (53), and pathogen effectors manipulate protein stability by modulating protein modification dynamics (37). Previous work established that lysine acetylation destabilizes AtMBP-1, whereas deacetylation by SRT1 enhances its stability (22). Our LC-MS data from AtMBP-1 combined with the yeast library screening identified NTA as a critical regulator of AtMBP-1 stability, with PcAvh337b potentially participating in this process (Fig. 7A and table S2).

NTA is a major protein modification that governs protein turnover. For example, NatB-mediated NTA stabilizes SIB1 to maintain immune gene expression, whereas NatA- and NatB-mediated NTAs of SNC1 antagonistically regulate its stability to maintain SNC1 homeostasis (31, 32). We found that AtMBP-1 stability is regulated similarly to that of antagonistic NTAs: NatA-mediated acetylation promotes stability, whereas NatC-mediated modification enhances degradation (Fig. 7). The depletion of NatA in A. thaliana has been shown to result in the accumulation of polyubiquitinated proteins and destabilized NatA substrates, accelerating their turnover by the UPS (30). Here, silencing of Nats and mutant assays demonstrated the cross-talk between NTAs and the ubiquitination of AtMBP-1. NatA-mediated NTA reduces AtMBP-1 ubiquitination, while NatC-mediated NTA promotes its UPS-dependent degradation (Fig. 7, F and H). In contrast to AtMBP-1, AtEno2 exhibits high stability (figs. S16 and S21). Further exploration of the potential implications of NTA on AtEno2 will shed light on the functional differences between AtEno2 and AtMBP-1.

Previous studies have documented that pathogen effectors can disrupt host protein complex functions to hamper plant immunity (13, 47, 54). Our findings provide mechanistic insight into this subversion strategy through the discovery that PcAvh337b manipulates the AtMBP-1–Nat regulatory network. Co-IP and LC-MS assays revealed that PcAvh337b modulates AtMBP-1–Nat modules by promoting the interaction between AtNAA10 and AtMBP-1 while weakening the association of AtNAA30 and AtMBP-1. This modulation increases NTA levels on the valine of AtMBP-1 mediated by NatA, resulting in decreased ubiquitination and consequent protein stabilization of AtMBP-1 (Fig. 8 and table S3). Meantime, the LC-MS quantification of AtMBP-1 in the control group (coexpressed with DRFP or RFP-PcAvh337a) showed that AtMBP-1 predominantly exists in the V isoform (table S3). This partial acetylation likely results from suboptimal recognition of the N-terminal residues of AtMBP-1 (MV) by both NatA and NatC, combined with competitive binding between NatA and NatC for the AtMBP-1 substrate. Together, these factors reduce acetylation efficiency, resulting in a lower proportion of the stable ac-V isoform—thereby supporting the observation that AtMBP-1 exhibits intrinsic instability and constitutive low abundance in planta (Figs. 4 and 6). However, NTA predominantly occurs in a cotranslational manner, our data show that PcAvh337b modulates NTA of AtMBP-1 by altering its interactions with NAT complexes. This raises two questions: whether the NTA of AtMBP-1 is in a posttranslational manner, or whether PcAvh337b directly regulates NatA enzymatic activity through AtNAA10 targeting to reprogram NTA patterns of AtMBP-1—both mechanisms merit further investigation.

In summary, we elucidated the molecular mechanism by which PcAvh337b stabilizes the S factor AtMBP-1 to hamper plant immunity. Our findings exemplify how WY(L) truncation facilitates effector expansion and diversity in the coevolutionary arms race between Phytophthora and plants, presumably allowing adaptation to a dynamic environment. We identified AtMBP-1 as a plant susceptibility factor and elucidated the mechanism by which Nats regulate its homeostasis, shedding light on a previously unrecognized pathway that could be pivotal in plant vulnerability during pathogen infection. Moreover, MBP-1 orthologs are evolutionarily conserved across multiple P. capsici host plants. However, the distinctive genomic architecture of the MBP-1 locus means that traditional CRISPR-Cas9 targeting MBP-1 would disrupt the essential gene Eno2. Thus, it would be valuable to apply state-of-the-art gene-editing tools to generate specific MBP-1 knockout lines for functional characterization in future studies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant cultivation

N. benthamiana was cultivated at 22° to 24°C under a 14-hour day/10-hour night photoperiod and 70% relative humidity for 4 to 6 weeks. A. thaliana and all the transgenic lines were cultivated at 22°C under a 10-hour light/14-hour dark photoperiod and 70% relative humidity for 5 weeks. Soybean plants, including Williams (rps1b) and L77-1863 (Rps1b) plants, were grown at 28°C with 16-hour light and 25°C with 8-hour darkness for 7 to 10 days.

A. thaliana Col-0 was used as the WT and to generate transgenic plants. The coding sequences of PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b without the signal peptide were cloned into the pCAMBIA2300 vector for the transformation of At-PcAvh337a and At-PcAvh337b transgenic A. thaliana. The LOS2 genomic DNA from 1.5-kb upstream of “ATG” site to “TAA” site (without “TAA”) was cloned into the pCAMBIA2300 vector for the transformation of pLOS2:LOS2-GFP transgenic A. thaliana. The coding sequence of AtEno2 was cloned into the pCAMBIA2300 vector for the transformation of 35S:AtEno2-GFP transgenic A. thaliana. The coding sequence of AtMBP-1 was cloned into the pER8 vector for the transformation of AtMBP-1–OE transgenic A. thaliana (55). All transgenic A. thaliana lines were generated by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation using the floral dip method (56). At-PcAvh337a, At-PcAvh337b, pLOS2:LOS2-GFP, and 35S:AtEno2-GFP transgenic seeds were selected on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/ml), and AtMBP-1–OE transgenic seeds were selected on one-half MS medium supplemented with hygromycin (30 μg/ml). Positive transgenic A. thaliana lines were identified by genomic DNA PCR, RT-PCR, and Western blot.

Microbial strain cultivation

The P. capsici strain (LT263), P. sojae strains (P7063 and ∆114-1-7), and all transformants were cultured on 10% (v/v) V8 juice medium at 25°C in darkness for 3 to 5 days.

Phytophthora transformations were performed as previously reported (57, 58). For CRISPR-Cas9–mediated gene knockout in P. capsici, single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed in EuPaGDT (http://grna.ctegd.uga.edu), and off-target sites were analyzed using the alignment search tool (BLASTN) of FungiDB (www.fungidb.org) to perform searches against the P. capsici genome. The secondary structure of the sgRNA was predicted with the RNA structure web tool (http://rna.urmc.rochester.edu/RNAstructureWeb/Servers/Predict1/Predict1.html). The sgRNA was cloned into the CRISPR-Cas9 vector pYF515, and the upstream/downstream flanking regions and the GFP sequence were fused into the pBluescript II KS vector. pYF515 and pBluescript II KS were cotransfected into P. capsici protoplasts and screened on 10% (v/v) V8 juice medium supplemented with G418 (50 μg/ml). Positive transformants were confirmed by genomic DNA PCR.

For gene overexpression in P. capsici, full-length genes were amplified from P. capsici cDNA and cloned into the pYF3 vector. The construct was transfected into P. capsici protoplasts and screened on 10% (v/v) V8 juice medium supplemented with G418 (50 μg/ml). Positive transformants were confirmed by Western blot and RT-qPCR.

Signal peptide validation using the yeast secretion trap system

The secretory function of the putative signal peptide from PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b was assessed using a yeast signal trap assay (59). The SP sequence alone or the full-length effector genes were fused to a modified SUC2 reporter. The construct was transformed into the invertase-deficient yeast strain YTK12. The transformants were selected on CMD-W medium [0.67% (w/v) yeast nitrogen base without amino acids, 0.075% (w/v) tryptophan dropout supplement, 2% (w/v) sucrose, 0.1% (w/v) glucose, and 2% (w/v) agar]. Positive transformants were confirmed by colony PCR and plated on YPRAA [1% (w/v) yeast extract, 2% (w/v) peptone, 2% (w/v) raffinose, antimycin A (2 mg/ml), and 2% (w/v) agar] plates, with raffinose replacing glucose to detect invertase secretion. Invertase enzymatic activity was confirmed by reducing TTC to insoluble red-colored TPF.

Effector secretion assay in Phytophthora

To verify the secretion of PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b, PcAvh337a-OE and PcAvh337b-OE were incubated in V8 liquid medium supplemented with G418 (50 μg/ml). After 48 hours of incubation, mycelia were transferred to fresh V8 liquid medium supplemented with G418 (50 μg/ml) and incubated for an additional 48 hours. Mycelia and CF were collected for protein detection (60).

Effector translocation assay

Effector translocation was tested using the established Avr1bCt fusion system (35). Full-length PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b were fused to the C-terminal sequence of P. sojae Avr1b (Avr1bCt) and transformed into the Avr1b-knockout P. sojae transformant Δ114-1-7 (36). The transformants were screened on 10% (v/v) V8 juice medium supplemented with G418 (50 μg/ml) and confirmed by RT-PCR and Western blot. The transformants were inoculated onto susceptible soybean Williams (rps1b) and resistant soybean L77-1863 (Rps1b). Translocation efficiency was quantified by the avirulence phenotypes, which were evaluated by the differences between the numbers of surviving Williams (rps1b) and L77-1863 (Rps1b) plants.

Agroinfiltration

The plant expression vectors pMDC43 and pCAMBIA2300 were used for transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves. The coding sequences of PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b without signal peptide, and the coding sequences of AtEno2, AtMBP-1, AtNAA10, and AtNAA30 were cloned into the pMDC43 vector. The LOS2 genomic DNA from 1.5 kb upstream of the “ATG” site to the “TAA” site (without “TAA”) was cloned into the pCAMBIA2300 vector to generate pLOS2:LOS2-GFP construct, and the desired mutations were generated by overlapping PCR. The Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 carrying the construct was cultured in Luria-Bertani medium supplemented with antibiotics [kanamycin (50 μg/ml) and rifampicin (25 μg/ml) at 28°C for ~40 hours. GV3101 cells were collected, washed twice with ddH2O, then resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl2, and 200 μM acetosyringone) with the optical density adjusted to optical density at 600 (OD600) = 0.1, and infiltrated into 5- to 6-week-old N. benthamiana leaves.

Gene silencing assays

Gene silencing assays were performed according to the method previously described (61). Specific 300-bp fragments targeting NbNAA10 and NbNAA30 were cloned and recombined into the entry vector pDONR201. Then, pDONR201-NbNAA10 and pDONR201-NbNAA30 constructs were recombined with pHellsgate8 to generate hpRNAi-NbNAA10 and hpRNAi-NbNAA30, respectively. A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 carrying hpRNAi gene construct (hpRNAi-GUS, hpRNAi-NbNAA10, and hpRNAi-NbNAA30) was resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl2, and 200 μM acetosyringone), with the optical density adjusted to OD600 = 0.2, and infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. Gene silencing efficiency was quantified at 72 hours postagroinfiltration by RT-qPCR using the specific primers listed in table S4.

P. capsici infection assays

The A. thaliana pathogenicity assay was performed according to the method previously described (62). P. capsici strain and transformants were cultured on 10% (v/v) V8 juice medium at 25°C in the dark for 3 days, after which the plates were placed under light for 5 days to induce sporangia formation. For the release of zoospores, the sporangia were subjected to cold shock by flooding with cold water (4°C) for 30 min, followed by an additional 30 min at room temperature. Zoospores were collected and adjusted to the appropriate concentrations (20 zoospores/μl). The abaxial surface of each detached A. thaliana leaf was challenged with 200 zoospores. The infected leaves were then photographed at 42 to 48 hours postinoculation under ultraviolet (UV) light, and the extent of disease symptoms was analyzed.

For the N. benthamiana pathogenicity assay, leaves of N. benthamiana were collected 30 hours postagroinfiltration. The abaxial surface of each detached leaf was challenged with P. capsici plugs (5 mm in diameter) from the edges of fresh mycelia. Inoculated leaves were photographed at 42 to 48 hours postinoculation under UV light, and the lesion areas were measured. The genomic DNA of P. capsici–inoculated leaves was extracted, and the biomass was quantified by RT-qPCR using the primers listed in table S4.

Yeast library screening and Y2H assays

Yeast library screening and Y2H assays were performed using the pGBKT7 (bait) and pGADT7 (prey) vectors according to the protocol provided by Clontech. For the yeast library screening, the coding sequence of PcAvh337b without the signal peptide was cloned into pGBKT7 (BD-PcAvh337b). BD-PcAvh337b was cotransformed with a pGADT7-based prey library into the yeast strain AH109 and plated on synthetic defined (SD) medium without His, Leu, and Trp (SD/-His-Leu-Trp) containing X-α-gal. Following incubation at 30°C for 4 to 7 days, putative positive colonies were isolated for sequence verification and subsequent BLAST analysis against the National Center for Biotechnology Information database. For the Y2H assays, the coding sequence of PcAvh337a without the signal peptide, the coding sequence of AtNAA10, AtNAA30, NbNAA10, and NbNAA30 were cloned into pGBKT7. The coding sequences of AtMBP-1 and AtEno2 with a 93rd amino acid substitution (M93L) were cloned into pGADT7. The pGBKT7 and pGADT7 constructs were cotransformed into the yeast strain AH109 and plated on SD medium without Leu and Trp (SD/-Leu-Trp). After 3 days, positive colonies were diluted and dropped onto SD/-Leu-Trp and SD medium without Ade, His, Leu, and Trp (SD/-Ade-His-Leu-Trp) containing X-α-gal to assess protein-protein interactions.

Protein extraction

For mycelia and CF extraction, 100 mg of mycelia was ground in liquid nitrogen, and the powder was transferred into a new tube with 100 μl of 2× SDS loading buffer [100 mM tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 4% SDS (w/v), 20% glycerol (v/v), and 0.2% bromophenol blue (w/v)] and boiled at 95°C for 10 min. CF was filtered through one layer of Miracloth, and the protein was precipitated with five times the sample volume of precooled acetone at −20°C overnight. The precipitate was separated by centrifugation at 12,000g for 10 min, resuspended in 50 μl of 2× SDS loading buffer, and boiled at 95°C for 10 min.

For plant protein extraction, protoplasts were centrifuged at room temperature and 100g for 5 min, the supernatant was removed, and protoplasts were disrupted in GTEN buffer [10 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40 (w/v), 10% glycerol (v/v), protease inhibitor cocktail (Bimake, B14001), and 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)] for total protein extraction; 0.5 g of leaves was ground in liquid nitrogen and incubated with 1 ml of GTEN buffer on ice for 30 min. The extraction mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 4°C and 12,000g for 10 min, after which the resulting supernatant was transferred into a new tube. 2× SDS loading buffer was then added, and the mixture was boiled at 95°C for 10 min.

Protoplast preparation and transformation

The vector pUC19 was used for transient expression in A. thaliana protoplasts. The coding sequences of PcAvh337a and PcAvh337b without signal peptide, and the coding sequences of AtMBP-1 and its variants were cloned into the pUC19 vector. Protoplasts were isolated from leaves of 5-week-old A. thaliana Col-0, AtMBP-1–OE transgenic plants, and transiently transformed via polyethylene glycol–mediated transformation according to the method previously described (63).

Protein stability assay

A. thaliana protoplasts were transformed with pUC19-gene (pUC19-PcAvh337a, pUC19-PcAvh337a, pUC19-AtMBP-1, pUC19-AtMBP-1V2A, and pUC19-AtMBP-1V2W) constructs and incubated for 14 hours, followed by treatment with 100 μM CHX alone or together with 50 μM MG132. Protein abundance was detected at 0, 6, and 10 hours posttreatment. Estradiol-pretreated AtMBP-1–OE lines were treated with a combination of 100 μM CHX and 50 μM MG132 or 100 μM CHX and 5 mM 3-MA, and five leaf discs with a diameter of 1 cm were collected as one sample at 0, 6, and 10 hours posttreatment. Agroinfiltrated leaves were syringed with 100 μM CHX, and five leaf discs with a diameter of 1 cm were collected as one sample at 0, 6, and 10 hours postsyringing. The sample was homogenized in 200 μl of GTEN buffer on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected for immunoblot analysis. For the cell-free degradation assay, proteolysis buffer [25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM adenosine 5′-triphosphate, and 5 mM DTT] was used for protein extraction. The supernatant was incubated with or without MG132 at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by mixing with 2× SDS loading buffer and boiling at 95°C for 10 min (64).

Co-IP and Western blot

For the Co-IP assay, 20 μl of GFP nanobody agarose beads (AlpalifeBio, KTSM1301) were precleared in 500 μl of washing buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA] three times, followed by a 1-hour incubation with the protein supernatant at 4°C. The beads were washed five times with washing buffer and pelleted at 1200g for 3 min. After the final wash, the pelleted beads were resuspended in 100 μl of 2× SDS loading buffer and boiled at 95°C for 10 min.

For Western blot, the samples were separated on a 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel for 3 hours at 80 V. The proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose filter membrane for 30 min at 25 V with Ponceau staining to demonstrate successful transfer and protein loading. The membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST buffer [25 mM tris-HCl, 137 mM NaCl, and 0.2% Tween 20 (pH 7.4)] for 2 hours at room temperature before incubation with primary antibody at 4°C overnight. The membrane was washed three times (5 min each) with TBST buffer before the addition of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 hour. After the final wash, the signals were developed using HRP substrate (Sigma-Aldrich, WBULS0100) and detected using ChemiScope 6100 (Shanghai, China).

The antibodies used in this study were anti-GFP (1:10,000; AB0005, Abways), anti-mCherry (1:5000; PTM-5619, PTM BIO), anti-Flag (1:8000; AB0008, Abways), anti-ubiquitin (1:8000; AB40290, Abbkine), anti-RFP (1:5000; produced by GeneCreate), and anti-mouse (1:10,000; B0102, Abways) and anti-rabbit (1:10,000; B0101, Abways) HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies.

Nucleic acid isolation and qPCR analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method (65). Total RNA was extracted using the SteadyPure Universal RNA Extraction Kit II (Accurate Biology, AG21022) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed using the Evo M-MLV RT Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR (Accurate Biology, AG11705). Real-time PCR was performed using ArtiCanA SYBR qPCR Mix (Tsingke, TSE501) on the Bio-Rad CFX Manager (Bio-Rad, CA, United States). Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method (66). The primers used are listed in table S4.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Five-week-old transgenic A. thaliana plants were used for transcriptome analysis. The CK (estradiol-inducible GFP expression line) and estradiol-inducible AtMBP-1–OE plants were pretreated with estradiol before challenge with P. capsici zoospores. Leaf tissues were collected at 6 hours postinoculation for analysis. Three biological replicates of each sample were used for total RNA extraction, library construction, and RNA sequencing (Novogene, Beijing, China). The data quality control was performed using Fastp to remove sequencing adapters and low-quality reads. Clean reads were mapped to the A. thaliana genome using HISAT2 and counted using StringTie. The DEGs were identified using DESeq2. The GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the DEGs were performed using clusterProfiler.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy

Fluorescent protein-tagged samples were imaged using the ZEISS LSM 900 confocal microscope (Zeiss Microsystems, Germany). Fluorescence imaging was performed using a 40× water-immersion objective with excitation/emission settings of 561 nm/570 to 630 nm for mCherry and RFP and 488 nm/500 to 540 nm for GFP. Leaf tissue with minimal P. capsici colonization was selected for the effector secretion assay. The pinhole was set at 5 airy units. Single optical sections and z-stacks were collected from cells exhibiting low-level protein fusion expression to minimize potential artifacts. The raw images were processed using ZEN 3.2 (blue edition).

LC-MS analysis

Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE and visualized with Coomassie brilliant blue staining. The target bands were excised into 1 mm3 pieces, destained with 30% acetonitrile, dehydrated with 100% acetonitrile, and then subjected to reduction using 10 mM DTT at 56°C for 1 hour, followed by alkylation with 60 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature for 30 min. The gel pieces were subsequently washed twice with 100% acetonitrile. In-gel digestion was performed with 150 ng of trypsin in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate at 37°C overnight. Peptides were extracted twice with 2% formic acid/67% acetonitrile, concentrated by vacuum centrifugation, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid for LC-MS/MS analysis. Peptides were analyzed on a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a nanoflow U3000 HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Buffer A (0.1% formic acid) was used for trapping and desalting, and buffer B (80% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid) was used for elution with a 5 to 38% formic acid gradient for 60 min. For a full MS scan, the scan mass ranged from 350 to 1500 m/z with a resolution of 70,000. For the MS2 scan, spectra with a charge state of 2 to 5 were selected for fragmentation by higher-energy C-trap dissociation.

LC-MS raw files were analyzed using PEAKS Studio 8.5 software. The mass tolerances of the precursor ions and fragment ions were set to 10 parts per million and 0.05 Da, respectively. The false discovery rate (FDR) for protein identification was set to 1.0%, allowing a maximum of two missed cleavages for trypsin digestion. The following variable modifications were selected: oxidation (M), acetylation (protein N-term), acetylation (K), phosphorylation (S/T/Y), and ubiquitination (K).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 and SPSS software. For the A. thaliana pathogenicity assay, disease severity and biomass were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and ordinary one-way analysis (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons, respectively. For the N. benthamiana pathogenicity assay, lesion size and biomass were evaluated using pairwise Student’s t tests. Gene expression data were analyzed with Student’s t test. Significance levels were denoted as follows: ns (not significant), P > 0.05; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; and ***P < 0.001.
