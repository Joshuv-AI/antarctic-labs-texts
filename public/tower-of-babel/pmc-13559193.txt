The settings approach to health promotion in schools: examining the role of school policies in fostering student health literacy
Authors: Didier Jourdan, Ting-Ya Kuo, Min-Chien Tsai, Fu-Li Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the settings approach to health promotion, which seeks to create supportive environments by addressing multiple health determinants within specific contexts. In schools, this involves not only policy and management but also the organization of the environment, pedagogy, engagement with families and communities, and the provision of health and social services. In this quantitative study, health literacy was selected as the dependent variable to examine the influence of environmental and personal determinants on students’ health. The sample comprised 46 primary schools (2325 students, 552 teachers, and 46 principals). The comprehensiveness of each school’s health promotion policy over the years preceding the study was assessed using the Health Promoting Schools (HPSs) framework. Comprehensiveness of the HPS policy refers to the extent to which the school’s health promotion policy addresses multiple and interacting determinants of health across the whole setting, including school organization, teaching and learning, the physical and social environment, family and community partnerships, and access to health and social services, in line with the settings approach to health promotion. Multivariate analyses showed that the breadth of HPS policies, collaborative practices with parents, teacher resources (training and well-being), and socio-economic context (mother’s education level and proportion of free meal subsidies at school level) significantly affected students’ health literacy. This study offers a key baseline for future research on how school health promotion policies affect students’ health literacy.

Introduction

The settings-based approach to health promotion, as articulated in the Ottawa Charter and reinforced by the socio-ecological model, emphasizes creating supportive environments where health can flourish rather than focusing solely on individual behaviour change (Kokko and Baybutt 2022). This strategic orientation advocates for multilevel, systems-based interventions across diverse settings such as schools, workplaces, and communities. However, translating this complex approach into practice requires robust frameworks for research, implementation, and evaluation to account for the intricate interplay of internal and external factors shaping each setting (Frohlich et al. 2022, Leahy 2022). At the empirical level, evidence from whole-school and settings-based reviews indicates that this approach can generate important population-level benefits, but the strength of evidence varies across outcomes (Smith et al. 2021, Khanal et al. 2025). More consistent effects are observed for some frequent health problems, whereas findings are less robust for other mental health and risk behaviours, reflecting both variability in implementation quality and the methodological challenges of evaluating complex interventions in dynamic school systems (Okan et al. 2018, Bollweg et al. 2020). In this context, being able to assess the comprehensiveness of the HPS policy is crucial, as HPS implementation is not a simple on/off phenomenon but a matter of quality and depth, and such assessment can guide tailored support to help schools strengthen their HPS policies over time.

Schools, where ∼20% of the population spends a significant part of their day (Jourdan and Gray 2024), represent a quintessential setting for health promotion interventions. The determinants of health in schools extend beyond the immediate educational environment and integrate broader community and family influences (Jourdan et al. 2021). At the school level, management style, physical and social environment, pedagogical methods, and the provision of auxiliary services all contribute to the school’s health-promoting potential. Externally, parental engagement, socio-economic status, and partnerships with community organizations further shape students’ health outcomes (World Health Organization and UNESCO 2021).

Despite this complexity, existing research offers limited empirical evidence about how these intersecting factors collectively influence student health within the formalized school context (Lekamge et al. 2025). Nevertheless, these studies are limited because they mainly evaluate programmes delivered by external experts or agencies, which do not necessarily transform the day-to-day culture, relationships, and routines within schools. As a result, they provide little insight into how the internal dynamics of schools, including leadership, staff collaboration, and teachers’ professional practices, contribute to health promotion. Within these internal processes, teachers play a pivotal role. International initiatives have emphasized integrating health literacy into teacher training and professional standards (Cheng and Wong 2015, Rodger et al. 2018, Kolbe 2019, Vamos et al. 2020, Hohensee and Schiemann 2021). Beyond training, factors such as teacher engagement and ownership in school initiatives, workload and job demands, and overall well-being influence their capacity to support student learning and well-being (Dreer 2023). Evidence indicates that teachers often feel underprepared to address health-related issues in the classroom and constrained by work-related stress (Bauer et al. 2020), while autonomy support is associated with improved student outcomes (Xu et al. 2024). Together, these findings highlight teacher-related resources as a key determinant of students’ health literacy.

A comprehensive evaluation of health promotion in schools requires methodologies capable of capturing this real-life layered reality. In this study, we examine a group of schools that are not subject to external intervention but have applied for HPS accreditation. The initiative comes from the principals and their teams and is part of the school’s policy as promoted by education professionals.

Quantitative approaches—especially those employing multilevel analyses—allow for systematic assessment of how factors at the school, family, and community levels interact to shape key health outcomes like health literacy. In the present study, we draw upon a large, diverse sample comprising 46 primary schools, with data from students, teachers, and principals. By leveraging the HPS framework to evaluate the comprehensiveness of each school’s health promotion policy over 2 years, this research examines how institutional, pedagogical, familial, and socio-economic factors converge to impact students’ determinants of health. In the present study, health literacy was chosen as the outcome variable because it is a core indicator capturing students’ capacity to access, understand, appraise, and apply health information for decisions about care, prevention, and health promotion across the life course (Kickbusch et al. 2013, Sørensen et al. 2013, Saboga-Nunes et al. 2021). It also reflects how school, family, and community environments shape a key personal and societal resource that underpins resilience and equity in health, making it highly relevant for analysing the effects of comprehensive HPS policy.

Substantial evidence shows that socio-economic disparities, reflected in family affluence and parental education, persistently shape children’s and adolescents’ health literacy, reinforcing health inequalities (Ran et al. 2018, Bröder et al. 2020, Fretian et al. 2020, Guo et al. 2021). Gender differences have also been observed, with girls frequently displaying higher levels of health literacy than boys in older student populations (Wong 2016, Svendsen et al. 2021, Kühn et al. 2022). However, gaps remain in understanding these dynamics at the primary school level, particularly regarding mechanisms through which the school context may mediate such inequalities.

Against this backdrop, the present study examines the multi-layered determinants of health literacy among school-age students, with an emphasis on the influence of the comprehensiveness of the HPS policy, collaborative practices involving families, and teacher training and well-being, alongside classic socio-economic factors. By advancing a multi-factorial, settings-based perspective, this research not only addresses critical gaps in the literature but also informs actionable strategies for future intervention and policy. Rooted in the logic of the health promotion settings approach as outlined in the Ottawa Charter and further theorized in the Health Promotion Handbook (Frohlich et al. 2022), this study embodies the principles of health promotion research (Potvin and Jourdan 2022), bridging organizational practice with empirical evaluation to guide transformative school health policy and practice.

Methods

Participants and procedure

This quantitative study is based on data collected in 2019 through the Taiwanese HPS scheme. The scheme is based on the WHO HPS approach (WHO 2009) that calls for integration of health into every aspect of school life—policy and leadership, curriculum, environment, and partnerships with parents and the broader community. Taiwan’s Health-Promoting School Accreditation System (HPSAS), established in 2011, provides structured recognition of the comprehensive HPS policy at bronze, silver, and gold levels, signalling adherence and commitment to HPS standards (Chen and Lee 2016).

Data were collected according to the following procedures. Forty-six elementary schools were purposively selected based on their HPS award level. An invitation letter describing the study aims and data collection procedures was sent to principals, and all 46 schools agreed to participate. Within each school, four classes from 5th and 6th grade (typically aged 10–12 years) were then randomly selected; each class included ∼10–25 students, depending on school size. A total of 2325 valid questionnaires were obtained, with a response rate of 95.1%. For school staff, the sampling strategy differed by school size. Schools were categorized as small (fewer than 25 classes) or medium–large (25 or more classes). In small schools, all teachers were invited to participate; in medium–large schools, teachers were purposively selected with a maximum of 23 per school. In total, 552 teachers and 46 principals completed the questionnaires, resulting in response rates of 98.4% and 100%, respectively. Teachers reported on HPS training, ownership, job demands, and well-being, while principals reported on student and parent involvement, community links, and collaboration with non-profit organizations (NPOs).

This non-experimental design compares schools along a continuum of HPS policy implementation without using an artificial control group, reflecting the reality that all schools address students’ health to some degree.

Measures

Our approach was to analyse the influence of a set of independent variables on students’ health literacy. Based on original scales to suit the school context and study purposes, all measures were slightly modified from original scales and translated into Chinese following the translation-back-translation procedure.

Dependent variable

Students’ Health Literacy. This scale was assessed using a scale adapted from the European Health Literacy Survey Questionnaire (HLS-EU-Q) developed by Sørensen et al. (2013), covering four competences: access, understand, appraise, and apply health information. To enhance cultural relevance and measurement validity in Asian contexts, item development was informed by the validated framework proposed by Duong et al. (2017), with minor modifications to item wording and the inclusion of two newly developed items to better reflect the study context.

The instrument consisted of 10 items across four competences in health promotion domain: access (two items; e.g. ability to find information on health-related activities and well-being), understand (two items; e.g. understanding health from family, peers, or media), appraise (three items; e.g. evaluating the impacts of environment, daily habits, and the credibility of health information), and apply (three items; e.g. making health-related decisions, influencing family members, and participating in community health promotion activities). Responses were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very difficult) to 4 (very easy), with higher scores indicating better health literacy.

Content validity was established through expert review, with specialists in school health promotion and education assessing item relevance and clarity. A pilot test was conducted with 121 5th- and 6th-grade students from two elementary schools that had previously received the Health Promoting School Award. The results demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a standardized Cronbach’s alpha of 0.74.

Health literacy was treated as a continuous variable. To classify students’ health literacy levels, we used a median split to dichotomize students into high- and low-health-literacy groups. This data-driven method ensured balanced group sizes and reduced potential bias associated with skewed distributions. Although dichotomization may lead to some loss of information, it was considered appropriate for the present analysis (MacCallum et al. 2002).

Independent variables

According to social determinant factors, we have chosen to study the influence of independent variables, as follows:

Socio-demographic variables (students’ questionnaire)

The socio-demographic information consisted of gender (male, female) and parental educational level. Parental educational level was assessed separately for fathers and mothers. Each parent’s educational attainment was categorized as no education, primary school or middle school, high school, and college/university or above. Both were included as separate variables in the analysis.

School contextual variables (national database)

School income level. The affluence of a school was measured by the average income of the county (district) in which the school is located and was classified as low income, low-middle income, medium-high income, or high income. Income data were obtained from the Fiscal Information Agency, Ministry of Finance (2017).

School policies (Health-Promoting School Accreditation System and principals’ questionnaires)

Comprehensiveness of the HPS policy (HPS award level). The overall quality of the school health promotion policy was operationalized using the three award levels (bronze, silver, and gold) of the Taiwan Health-Promoting Schools Accreditation System, which reflect the extent to which schools implement the six national accreditation standards for Health Promoting Schools. These six standards require schools to formulate a comprehensive school health programme based on their own needs, implement evidence-based health promotion programmes addressing key health issues and educational needs of students and staff, develop a life-skills-oriented health education curriculum, actively mobilize community resources for health promotion activities, organize a supportive school environment and management for health, and ensure participation of both staff and students in health promotion. The accreditation is awarded through a standardized national evaluation process led by health and education authorities, involving documentary reviews and on-site visits conducted by national and international accredited commissioners. Bronze level is awarded to schools scoring 80–84 points, silver level to schools scoring 85–89 points or 90 points and above without full scores on all core elements, and gold level to schools scoring at least 90 points with full scores on all core elements. Higher accreditation levels indicate a more comprehensive and systematically integrated implementation of HPS policy as a whole-school approach for the benefit of students and staff (Chen and Lee 2016).

Data analysis

Data were analysed in several steps to capture both individual-level and school-level patterns of health literacy.

First, descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. This was followed by univariate analyses (t-tests, ANOVA with Scheffé post hoc test, and correlation analyses) to explore bivariate associations among students’ health literacy, socio-demographic characteristics, social contextual factors, school policies, and human resource variables.

Second, multivariate logistic regression was used to estimate the independent contribution of these variables to the likelihood of students having high vs. low health literacy. Teacher-level responses were aggregated to the school level by calculating the mean score for each construct within each school. This approach assumes that the organizational context of a school is shaped by the shared perspectives and experiences of its teaching community. These aggregated school-level variables, particularly those related to human resources (e.g. teacher training, teacher ownership, teachers’ job demand, and teachers’ well-being), were then included in the multivariate logistic regression analyses (Bliese 2000, Trautwein et al. 2009).

Finally, we performed a hierarchical cluster analysis using Ward’s method (Everitt et al. 2011) to group schools based on significant social determinant variables in the multivariate logistic regression. These school-level variables were organized into four domains: socio-economic context (percentage of students receiving free meal subsidies and maternal education level), comprehensiveness of the HPS policy (HPS award level), teacher training, and teacher well-being. This approach complements regression analysis by identifying multidimensional patterns and heterogeneity across schools that cannot be captured through variable-entered methods alone, as demonstrated in previous health literacy research (e.g. Heinrichs et al. 2021).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Fu Jen Catholic University (No. C107133). All participants provided informed consent before the process began, and all responses were anonymous to maintain their confidentiality.

Results

General findings

Contextual variables

Socio-economic variables and award level of accreditation

Students in the sample attended schools located in high-income districts (40.8%), medium-high-income districts (34.0%), low-middle-income districts (18.5%), and low-income districts (6.8%). With respect to HPS accreditation, 48.1% of students were enrolled in silver-level schools, 45.0% in bronze-level schools, and 6.8% in gold-level schools.

School-level variables

Most principals (91.3%) reported collaborating with parents and the community (78.3%). More than half of them (54.3%) reported collaborating with non-profit organizations. Concerning the participation of students in the HPS policy of the school, 48.8% said that students are invited to participate in school health promotion-related meetings.

The average age of teachers in this study was 42.3 years, with school averages ranging from 34.1 to 48.8 years. Teacher questionnaire responses were aggregated to generate school-level scores for each construct. The mean school score for teacher training was 0.51 (SD 0.17), with values ranging from 0.24 to 1.00. The mean score for teacher ownership was 19.62 (SD 1.16), ranging from 16.86 to 22.67; for teacher job demand, 9.07 (SD 0.65), ranging from 8.14 to 12.00; and for teacher well-being, 7.29 (SD 0.49), ranging from 5.71 to 8.60.

Individual variables

Demographic variables

The study participants were fifth (49.4%) and sixth (50.6%) graders. More than half of the participants were male (51.4%). A total of 42.9% and 47.1% of the sample reported that their father’s and mother’s education levels were college/university or above, respectively.

Health literacy

Health literacy was analysed as a continuous variable. The mean health literacy score among students was 32.05 (SD 4.42), with individual scores ranging from 10 to 40. The median score was 32.0; accordingly, students scoring 32.0 or below were categorized as having low health literacy, and those scoring 33.0 or above as having high health literacy. Overall, 1045 students (45.1%) were classified as having high health literacy and 1273 (54.9%) as having low health literacy.

The average score of health literacy at the school level was 32.28 (SD 1.78), with school scores ranging from 29.62 to 37.25. Based on the average health literacy score in each school, a histogram was constructed to display the distribution of school-level health literacy (Fig. 1).

Students whose fathers had a college/university degree or above had higher health literacy than those whose fathers had lower education (32.88 vs. 31.89; P < .01). A similar pattern was observed for maternal education (32.89 vs. 31.52; P < .001). Health literacy also significantly differed by county-level income, with students in low-income counties reporting higher scores than those in high-income counties (33.02 vs. 31.84; P < .05). In contrast, students in schools with lower levels of free meal subsidies had higher health literacy than those in schools with higher subsidy levels (32.17 vs. 31.66; P < .05).

Compared with students whose mothers had low levels of education, those with mothers who had a high school or college/university education were more likely to have higher health literacy (OR = 1.603, 95% CI = 1.086–2.366; OR = 1.580, 95% CI = 1.031–2.423, respectively). Cluster analysis

Of the 46 participating schools, 43 had complete data and were included in the hierarchical cluster analysis; three were excluded due to missing values in one or more clustering variables. The hierarchical cluster analysis and inspection of the dendrogram indicated that a five-cluster solution provided the best compromise between statistical distinctiveness and interpretability (Fig. 2). This solution yielded five groups of schools (Clusters A–E) that differed systematically in their socio-economic context, HPS policy, teacher training, and teacher well-being.

Cluster A (nine schools) comprised schools characterized by an unfavourable socio-economic condition but relatively strong HPS policy and low levels of teacher training, combined with very low teacher well-being. Cluster B (nine schools) brought together schools with slightly favourable socio-economic conditions, weak HPS policy, high teacher training, and high teacher well-being. Cluster C (four schools) included schools facing very unfavourable socio-economic conditions, with only slightly favourable HPS policy, very high teacher training, and very high teacher well-being. Cluster D (10 schools) represented schools in a very favourable socio-economic context, with weak HPS policy, low teacher training, and moderate teacher well-being. Finally, Cluster E (11 schools) consisted of schools with favourable socio-economic conditions and strong HPS policy but very low teacher training and low teacher well-being.

Discussion

Interpretation of main findings

This study shows that primary school students’ health literacy is shaped by a complex constellation of determinants spanning the individual, family, school, and community levels, which aligns with the settings approach to health promotion and socio-ecological models of health. Several associations observed in the univariate analysis were small, suggesting that students’ health literacy is shaped by multiple interacting factors rather than any single determinant. In complex school systems, even modest associations may have practical significance, as their effects can accumulate across domains and shape students’ health literacy. Socio-economic status, parental education, HPS policy, collaboration with parents, and teacher-related resources (training and well-being) all emerged as significant predictors of students’ health literacy, underlining that health literacy is not only an individual attribute but also a product of the school setting and its broader social environment (Bröder et al. 2018, 2020). The central finding is that the HPS policy, as captured by accreditation level and selected components, and teachers’ training and well-being are associated with higher student health literacy, thereby confirming the relevance of whole-school health promotion for this key outcome (Chen and Lee 2016, Jourdan et al. 2021).

The lack of gender differences in health literacy among primary school students contrasts with studies in older populations that often report higher health literacy among girls and young women (Wong 2016, Svendsen et al. 2021, Kühn et al. 2022). This discrepancy suggests that gender differences may emerge later in the life course, possibly due to cumulative social and educational experiences that differentiate boys and girls during adolescence and early adulthood (Fretian et al. 2020, Guo et al. 2021). The strong influence of maternal more than paternal education is consistent with contexts in which mothers are the primary caregivers, highlighting their key role in shaping children’s health-related practices and competencies (Guo et al. 2021).

The cluster analysis shows that distinct combinations of socio-economic context, HPS policy, teacher training, and teacher well-being form different school profiles, each linked to a particular proportion of students with high health literacy, highlighting that configurations of setting-level determinants matter more than any single factor. Higher proportions of students with high health literacy tend to occur where a favourable balance is achieved between context, HPS policy, and strong teacher resources, and the profile corresponding to cluster illustrates that even very socio-economically disadvantaged schools can reach relatively high health literacy when robust school health policy and intensive, well-supported teacher training are in place.

Comprehensiveness of the HPS policy within the settings approach

A key contribution of this study is to operationalize and test the ‘HPS policy’ as a core feature of the settings approach. The HPS accreditation levels (bronze, silver, gold) reflect how extensively the six national standards are implemented, ranging from basic adoption to a fully integrated whole-school strategy that encompasses policy development, curriculum, environment, staff and student participation, and community partnerships (Chen and Lee 2016). The results indicate that students attending gold- and silver-award schools have higher odds of high health literacy than those in bronze-award schools. Although no clear difference was observed between silver and gold levels, likely due to the small number of gold-award schools and limited statistical power, the overall pattern suggests that more comprehensive implementation of HPS policies is associated with better student health literacy outcomes.

This finding supports the underlying logic of the settings approach, which emphasizes modifying organizational, social, and physical environments rather than focusing solely on individual behaviour change (Jourdan et al. 2021). In this perspective, HPS policy acts as structuring conditions that enable health literacy to be embedded in everyday school life—through health-oriented teaching, supportive relationships, and coherent health-related norms and routines (Bröder et al. 2018). The association between collaborative practices with parents and students’ health literacy further illustrates how the comprehensive HPS policy extends beyond the school walls to include family–school partnerships, another hallmark of the settings approach (Lee and Cheung 2017, Cruickshank et al. 2025). However, the effects of collaboration were not consistent across analyses. Some forms of collaboration were significant in the univariate analysis, but their effects were reduced in the multivariate model, suggesting that these associations may be mediated or confounded by other school-level factors. Most evidence-based studies highlight family involvement as one component of a broader whole-school strategy rather than as a stand-alone action (Elmer et al. 2021, Chen et al. 2022). This suggests that collaboration with parents or other partners is unlikely to be effective on its own if it is not embedded in a comprehensive, school-wide health promotion policy.

Comparison with previous research

Existing research on HPS has largely focused on institutional outcomes and on the health literacy of school leaders and staff rather than that of students (Dadaczynski et al. 2020, Betschart et al. 2022). These studies show that the attitudes and health literacy of school leaders are important for HPS implementation, yet the downstream effects on children’s health literacy remain underexplored (Dadaczynski et al. 2020, Betschart et al. 2022). The study of Prihanto et al. (2021) was conducted with high schools and not primary schools, as is the case in the present research. The team found that health literacy was associated with several specific health behaviours, whereas the national HPS programme showed only limited and inconsistent links with health literacy and health behaviour, partly because implementation levels were predominantly at the minimum standard and varied widely between schools (Prihanto et al. 2021). The current study adds to this debate by demonstrating that when the HPS policy is captured through accreditation level and key policy components, clear associations with student health literacy can be observed.

The findings also reinforce growing evidence that teacher resources are crucial for effective school health promotion. Research from different countries indicates that integrating health literacy and mental health literacy into teacher education and professional development can benefit student outcomes (Cheng and Wong 2015, Rodger et al. 2018, Vamos et al. 2020, Hohensee and Schiemann 2021). In this study, teacher training in the HPS framework and teacher well-being were both associated with higher student health literacy, even after controlling for socio-economic and policy variables, suggesting that teachers’ capacity and health status are important mechanisms linking HPS policy to student outcomes (Miller et al. 2019, Viac and Fraser 2020). This extends previous work showing links between teacher well-being and student well-being, and between teacher health literacy and student health literacy, by demonstrating that teacher training and well-being also contribute to students’ capacity to access, understand, appraise, and apply health information (Miller et al. 2019, Viac and Fraser 2020). However, our study found that neither teacher ownership nor job demand was significantly associated with students’ health literacy in the multivariate model. While teacher ownership reflects teachers’ engagement in their workplace conditions and job demand captures their workload and work-related pressures, both factors appear to indirectly influence students’ health literacy. Previous research has indicated that teacher ownership may contribute to implementation quality, its impact on students’ health literacy may be mediated or moderated, and that this impact may depend on how these factors are embedded within broader organizational structures, such as school leadership, school policies, and school health climate. (Li et al. 2017) Similarly, teacher job demands primarily influence teachers’ well-being and working conditions, which in turn serve as a key pathway influencing student health outcomes (Morris et al. 2025)

The results regarding socio-economic context are consistent with the broader health literacy literature, which documents persistent social gradients in health literacy among children and adolescents (Bröder et al. 2020, Fretian et al. 2020, Guo et al. 2021). In this study, the percentage of students receiving free meals—used as a school-level indicator of disadvantage—was more strongly associated with health literacy than the school district’s average income. Contrary to the expected socio-economic gradient, students attending schools in medium-high-income areas had significantly lower levels of health literacy than those in low-income areas. One possible explanation is that average income at the school-area level does not accurately capture students’ actual family socio-economic conditions, so indicators reflecting students’ lived economic realities may be more sensitive than broader contextual measures. In this regard, the proportion of students receiving free meals at the school level is likely to be a more valid indicator of relative socio-economic disadvantage than district-level income data.

In addition, the findings that the HPS policy and teacher resources remain significant predictors after accounting for socio-economic disadvantage suggest that whole-school approaches have the potential to mitigate, although not eliminate, the effects of social inequalities on health literacy (Okan et al. 2020, Jourdan et al. 2021).

School health literacy profiles and the life ecosystem

By applying cluster analysis, the study identified five distinct school profiles that combine different levels of socio-economic context, HPS policy, teacher training, and teacher well-being and linked these profiles to the proportion of students with high health literacy. This approach moves beyond single-variable analyses to capture the ‘life ecosystem’ of schools, in which multiple determinants interact to shape student outcomes, consistent with socio-ecological models and settings-based health promotion (Bröder et al. 2020, Jourdan et al. 2021). Clusters A and C, both located in unfavourable socio-economic contexts, differ because Cluster C combines slightly favourable HPS policy with very high teacher training and well-being, whereas Cluster A has stronger policy but only moderate training and very low well-being, suggesting that teachers’ capacity and health are critical for compensating structural disadvantage. Clusters B and D both operate in more favourable socio-economic conditions with relatively weak HPS policy: Cluster B couples this with high teacher training and well-being and reaches moderate health literacy, whereas Cluster D combines a very favourable context with low training and moderate well-being, indicating that socio-economic advantage alone does not guarantee high health literacy without investment in teachers and policy integration. Cluster E further shows that even where socio-economic conditions and HPS policy are favourable, very low teacher training and low well-being yield only mixed outcomes, underscoring that HPS policy must be matched by adequate support for teachers to translate into consistent gains for students. Overall, these configurations support a settings-based interpretation in which the balance between context, policy, and teacher resources determines each school’s health literacy potential.

These findings illustrate how the settings approach can be operationalized by examining configurations of determinants rather than isolated factors. In practice, schools rarely present a uniform picture; instead, they combine strengths and weaknesses across policy, resources, and context. Profiling schools in this way can support tailored interventions that build on existing assets (e.g. strong teacher engagement) while addressing gaps (e.g. limited community partnerships or partial policy implementation). It also underscores that comprehensiveness of the HPS policy should be understood not only in terms of formal accreditation but also in relation to the actual mix of resources and contextual conditions that make policies effective (Chen and Lee 2016, Betschart et al. 2022).

Implications for policy and practice

Several implications emerge for those seeking to advance the settings approach to health promotion in schools. First, the results underscore the added value of investing in comprehensive HPS policies rather than isolated or project-based health interventions (Jourdan et al. 2021). Strengthening policy comprehensiveness means integrating health across school governance, curriculum, environment, and partnerships, with explicit attention to health literacy as a core outcome (Sørensen et al. 2013, Okan et al. 2020). Accreditation systems such as the Taiwanese HPSAS can support this process by providing criteria and incentives that encourage schools to move towards higher levels of integration, but their effectiveness depends on support for implementation, including training, guidance, and time for school teams (Chen and Lee 2016, Betschart et al. 2022).

Second, the strong associations between teacher training, teacher well-being, and student health literacy call for systematic integration of health literacy and HPS principles into initial teacher education and ongoing professional development (Cheng and Wong 2015, Vamos et al. 2020, Hohensee and Schiemann 2021). Teacher training should not only provide knowledge about health topics but also develop pedagogical and relational competencies for embedding health literacy into everyday teaching and classroom practice (Okan et al. 2018, Bollweg et al. 2020). At the same time, policies should address teacher workload, psychosocial risks, and access to support services, as teachers’ own well-being is both a goal in itself and a condition for sustainable health-promoting practice (Miller et al. 2019, Viac and Fraser 2020). This perspective is echoed in large-scale international research, including the global International Barometer of the Health and Wellbeing of Education Personnel (I-BEST) survey on teachers’ health, well-being, and training (Education and Solidarity Network 2023), and in studies conceptualizing teachers as a key human resource by linking health to work engagement and extended working life (Tsai et al. 2022).

Third, the role of parents and families in shaping student health literacy needs to be more explicitly recognized within HPS strategies. The positive effect of school–parent collaboration suggests that schools can act as bridges to support parents in developing and using health literacy in ways that benefit children (Guo et al. 2021, Jourdan et al. 2021). This could include involving parents in school-based health education, providing accessible health information in multiple formats and languages, and creating opportunities for dialogue about health-related concerns.

Finally, because socio-economic inequalities remain a powerful determinant of health literacy, comprehensive HPS policies must be designed with equity as a central objective. This includes targeting additional resources and support to schools serving disadvantaged populations, ensuring that HPS accreditation processes are sensitive to context and do not inadvertently penalize schools facing structural constraints, and using school health literacy profiles to identify where intensified support is needed (Bröder et al. 2020, Fretian et al. 2020, Jourdan et al. 2021). The concept of ‘Health Literate Schools,’ which integrates health literacy into the school’s overall organization and culture, offers a useful framework for pursuing these equity-oriented strategies within the broader HPS model (Bröder et al. 2018, Okan et al. 2020).

Methodological considerations and limitations

The study has several methodological strengths. It uses a real-life, non-experimental design that compares schools along a continuum of HPS policy implementation rather than relying on an artificial control group assumed to be free of health promotion activities, reflecting the reality that all schools engage with students’ health to some degree. Data were collected from multiple stakeholders—students, teachers, and principals—allowing school-level constructs. Principal-reported data capture policy comprehensiveness and partnerships, whereas teacher-reported measures reflect teacher resources. Together, these perspectives provide a robust representation of the school context (Chen and Lee 2016, Betschart et al. 2022). In addition, the combination of univariate, multivariate, and cluster analyses offers both detailed and synthetic views of the determinants of student health literacy and their interactions.

Several limitations must also be acknowledged. First, the health literacy measure was adapted from an instrument developed for individuals aged 15 years and above, which may not fully capture the developmental specificities of primary school children (Sørensen et al. 2013). Although internal consistency was acceptable, future studies should use instruments specifically validated for children to ensure greater age-appropriateness and sensitivity (Okan et al. 2018, Bollweg et al. 2020, Fischer et al. 2022).

Second, the study relied on school-level indicators of affluence rather than detailed measures of family socio-economic status, which may have introduced some misclassification. While the percentage of students receiving free meals appears to be a sensitive proxy for economic disadvantage, richer data on family income, occupation, or material deprivation would allow more precise modelling of social gradients in health literacy (Fretian et al. 2020, Guo et al. 2021). However, collecting such data from younger students raises ethical and feasibility challenges.

Third, collaboration-related practices (including student participation, parental involvement, and links with community and non-profit organizations) were measured using dichotomous indicators, which may not fully capture the complexity, quality, and intensity of these practices. Future research should employ more fine-grained measures to better assess the depth and effectiveness of school-level partnerships and participation processes.

Fourth, although data were collected from multiple actors, the analyses did not fully exploit multilevel modelling techniques that can simultaneously account for individual-level and school-level variance. Future research using multilevel designs could provide a more nuanced understanding of how much of the variation in health literacy lies between schools vs. within schools and how specific school-level variables moderate or mediate the effects of individual and family factors (Bröder et al. 2020, Jourdan et al. 2021).

Fifth, the cross-sectional design prevents any firm conclusions about causality. Although health literacy was selected as a measurable outcome reflecting students’ capacity to access, understand, and use health information, it may not fully capture downstream health behaviours or health outcomes. Moreover, health literacy may also be considered an integral component of the health-promoting school approach and a capacity that may influence subsequent health behaviours, health conditions, and educational outcomes, which may in turn further shape students’ health literacy over time (Okan et al. 2020). While the theoretical rationale and temporal ordering (longer-term policy implementation and teacher resources influencing current student health literacy) lend plausibility to the assumed directions of effect, longitudinal designs would be needed to test whether changes in HPS policy and teacher resources over time lead to improvements in student health literacy (Fischer et al. 2022). Such designs would also allow examination of the medium- and long-term effects of HPS implementation, including in post-crisis contexts such as the COVID-19 pandemic (Jourdan et al. 2021).

Finally, the study was conducted within a specific national HPS scheme, which may limit direct generalizability to other countries. However, the underlying mechanisms—linking HPS policy, teacher resources, partnerships, and socio-economic context to student health literacy—are grounded in widely applicable principles of the settings approach and socio-ecological models.

Conclusion

This study reinforces the relevance of the settings approach to health promotion by demonstrating that the comprehensiveness of the HPS policy, together with teacher resources and school–family partnerships, is associated with primary school students’ health literacy over and above socio-economic differences (Chen and Lee 2016, Jourdan et al. 2021, Betschart et al. 2022). By analysing both individual determinants and school-level profiles, the findings show that health literacy emerges from the interaction of personal, familial, institutional, and contextual factors and that comprehensive, whole-school strategies can help create the conditions in which health literacy can flourish (Bröder et al. 2018, Okan et al. 2020). Despite limitations in measurement and design, the study provides empirically grounded support for investing in HPS policy and in the capacity and well-being of teachers as levers to promote health literacy and reduce health inequalities among children (Miller et al. 2019, Fretian et al. 2020, Viac and Fraser 2020).
