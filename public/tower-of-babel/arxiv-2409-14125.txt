1.
Introduction and statement of results

Let
H
be a complex Hilbert space,
and let
T
be a bounded linear operator on
H
.
It is well known that
\|e^{-tT}\|\leq 1
for all
t>0

if and only if the numerical range of
T

is contained in the right half-plane
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\}
.
This is a version of the celebrated Lumer–Phillips theorem
[
8
]
.

In this note, we study of operators of the form

(I+\lambda T)(I+\mu T)^{-1}
, where
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
Our goal is to obtain a necessary and sufficient condition for when

\|(I+\lambda T)(I+\mu T)^{-1}\|\leq 1
. In contrast with the
Lumer–Phillips theorem, our condition is expressed
terms of the numerical range of
T^{-1}
rather than that of
T
.
Here we assume that
T
is injective, so
T^{-1}
well-defined on
T(H)
,
though it may well be an unbounded operator.
We should therefore make precise the notion of numerical range
of an unbounded operator.

If
A
is a (possibly unbounded) operator defined on a domain
D(A)\subset H
,
then the numerical range of
A
is defined by

W(A):=\{\langle Ax,x\rangle:x\in D(A),\|x\|=1\}.

It is always a convex subset of
{\mathbb{C}}
. Furthermore, if
D(A)=H
and
A
is a bounded operator,
then the spectrum
\sigma(A)
of
A
is contained in the closure of
W(A)
. For details, see

[
2
, pp.264–265]
.

To state our results, we need to recall one more definition.
If
\Omega
is a non-empty convex subset of
{\mathbb{C}}
, then
its
support function

h_{\Omega}:{\mathbb{C}}\to(-\infty,\infty]

is defined by

h_{\Omega}(z):=\sup\{\operatorname{Re}(z\overline{w}):w\in\Omega\}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
H
be a complex Hilbert space, let
T
be an injective bounded operator on
H
,
and let
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Suppose that
(I+\mu T)
is invertible.
Then

\|(I+\lambda T)(I+\mu T)^{-1}\|\leq 1\quad\iff\quad 2h_{W(T^{-1})}(\lambda-\mu)\leq|\mu|^{2}-|\lambda|^{2}.

We single out two special cases of this result that are worthy of note.
In the first of these, we write

[-\overline{\mu},\mu)
for
\{-(1-t)\overline{\mu}+t\mu:t\in[0,1)\}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
H
be a complex Hilbert space, and let
T
be an injective bounded operator on
H

such that

W(T^{-1})=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\}
.
Then, given distinct
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

(I+\mu T)
is invertible and
\|(I+\lambda T)(I+\mu T)^{-1}\|=1
;

(2)

\operatorname{Re}\mu>0
and
\lambda\in[-\overline{\mu},\mu)
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
H
be a complex Hilbert space, and let
T
be an injective bounded operator on
H

such that
W(T^{-1})={\mathbb{C}}
.
If
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\lambda\neq\mu
and if
(I+\mu T)
is invertible, then

\|(I+\lambda T)(I+\mu T)^{-1}\|>1
.

Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
will all be proved in §
2
.

We now illustrate them using the Volterra operator

V:L^{2}[0,1]\to L^{2}[0,1]
,
defined by

Vf(x):=\int_{0}^{x}f(t)\,dt\quad(f\in L^{2}[0,1]).

For background information on
V
,
see e.g.
[
3
, Chapter 7]
.
Note, in particular, that
V
is an injective bounded operator
whose spectrum is equal to
\{0\}
,
so the abstract results above do indeed apply to
T=V
, and indeed to
T=V^{n}

for all
n\geq 1
.

We first need to
determine the numerical range of
V^{-n}
. This is the subject of
the following theorems.

Theorem 1.4
.

W(V^{-1})=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\}
.

Theorem 1.5
.

If
n\geq 2
, then
W(V^{-n})={\mathbb{C}}
.

These results will be proved in §
3
.
Combining Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
,
we immediately recover the following result,
originally due to Khadkhuu, Zemánek and the second author
[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\lambda\neq\mu
. Then
\|(I+\lambda V)(I+\mu V)^{-1}\|=1

if and only if
\operatorname{Re}\mu>0
and
\lambda\in[-\overline{\mu},\mu)
.

Also, Theorems
1.3
and
1.5
together immediately
lead to the following result.

Corollary 1.7
.

If
n\geq 2
, then

\|(I+\lambda V^{n})(I+\mu V^{n})^{-1}\|>1
for all
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\lambda\neq\mu
.

We conclude by remarking that Corollary
1.7

could also be deduced from a result of ter Elst and Zemánek
[
10
, Proposition 2.3]
.
The argument is quite different, and uses properties specific to the Volterra operaor.

2.
Proofs of Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We have the following chain of equivalences:

\displaystyle\|(I+\lambda T)(I+\mu T)^{-1}\|\leq 1

\displaystyle\iff\|(I+\lambda T)x\|^{2}\leq\|(I+\mu T)x\|^{2}\quad(\forall x\in H)

\displaystyle\iff 2\operatorname{Re}\Bigl((\lambda-\mu)\langle Tx,x\rangle\Bigr)\leq(|\mu|^{2}-|\lambda|^{2})\|Tx\|^{2}\quad(\forall x\in H)

\displaystyle\iff 2\operatorname{Re}\Bigl((\lambda-\mu)\langle y,T^{-1}y\rangle\Bigr)\leq(|\mu|^{2}-|\lambda|^{2})\|y\|^{2}\quad(\forall y\in T(H))

\displaystyle\iff 2\operatorname{Re}\Bigl((\lambda-\mu)\overline{w}\Bigr)\leq|\mu|^{2}-|\lambda|^{2}\quad(\forall w\in W(T^{-1}))

\displaystyle\iff 2h_{W(T^{-1})}(\lambda-\mu)\leq|\mu|^{2}-|\lambda|^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Fix
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
and, for the time being, suppose also that
(I+\mu T)
is invertible.

As
W(T^{-1})
is unbounded, we have
0\in\sigma(T)
, so
1\in\sigma((I+\lambda T)(I+\mu T)^{-1})
,
and consequently
\|(I+\lambda T)(I+\mu T)^{-1}\|\geq 1
.

By assumption,
W(T^{-1})=\{z:\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\}
, so

h_{W(T^{-1})}(z)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if~}z\in(-\infty,0],\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Using Theorem
1.1
, it follows that

\|(I+\lambda T)(I+\mu T)^{-1}\|\leq 1\iff\lambda-\mu\in(-\infty,0]\text{~and~}|\mu|\geq|\lambda|\\

It is easy to see that, if
\lambda,\mu
are distinct, then

\Bigl(\lambda-\mu\in[-\infty,0)\text{~and~}|\mu|\geq|\lambda|\Bigr)\iff\Bigl(\operatorname{Re}\mu>0\text{~and~}\lambda\in[-\mu,\mu)\Bigr).

Combining all these remarks, we deduce that, if
\lambda,\mu
are distinct complex numbers and

(I+\mu T)
is invertible, then

\|(I+\lambda T)(I+\mu T)^{-1}\|=1\iff\operatorname{Re}\mu>0\text{~and~}\lambda\in[-\mu,\mu).

Finally, we note that, although the above equivalence
was established under the assumption that
(I+\mu T)
is
invertible, in fact the invertibility of
(I+\mu T)
is automatic if
\operatorname{Re}\mu>0
.
Indeed, since
W(T^{-1})=\{z:\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\}
,
it follows that
W(T)\subset\{z:\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\}
, whence

\sigma(T)\subset\{z:\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\}

and so
(I+\mu T)
is invertible. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We have
h_{W(T^{-1})}(z)=h_{\mathbb{C}}(z)=\infty
for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
.
By Theorem
1.1
, it follows that
\|(I+\lambda T)(I+\mu T)^{-1}\|>1

whenever
(I+\mu T)
is invertible and
\lambda\neq\mu
.
∎

3.
Proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
g\in V(L^{2}[0,1])
, say
g=Vf
. Then

\operatorname{Re}\langle V^{-1}g,g\rangle=\operatorname{Re}\langle f,Vf\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle f,(V+V^{*})f\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl|\int_{0}^{1}f\Bigr|^{2}\geq 0.

Thus
W(V^{-1})\subset\{z:\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\}
.

For the reverse inclusion, we first note that a sufficient condition for
g
to belong to
V(L^{2}[0,1])

is that
g\in C^{1}[0,1]
with
g(0)=0
. In this case we have
V^{-1}g=g^{\prime}
.

In particular, if
n\geq 1
and
g_{n}(x):=e^{\pm 2\pi inx}-1
,
then
g_{n}\in V(L^{2}[0,1])
, with
\|g_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=2
and

\langle V^{-1}g_{n},g_{n}\rangle=\langle g_{n}^{\prime},g_{n}\rangle=\langle\pm 2\pi ine^{\pm 2\pi inx},\,e^{\pm 2\pi inx}-1\rangle=\pm 2\pi in.

It follows that
\pm i\pi n\in W(V^{-1})
.

Likewise, if
n\geq 1
and
h_{n}(x):=x^{n}
,
then
h_{n}\in V(L^{2}[0,1])
with
\|h_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=1/(2n+1)
and

\langle V^{-1}h_{n},h_{n}\rangle=\langle h_{n}^{\prime},h_{n}\rangle=\langle nx^{n-1},x^{n}\rangle=1/2.

It follows that
n+\frac{1}{2}\in W(V^{-1})
.

Finally, since
\{\pm\pi in,\,(n+\frac{1}{2}):n\geq 1\}\subset W(V^{-1})

and
W(V^{-1})
is convex, we deduce that
\{z:\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\}\subset W(V^{-1})
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Fix
n\geq 2
.
A sufficient condition for
g
to belong to
V^{n}(L^{2}[0,1])

is that
g\in C^{n}[0,1]
with
g^{(k)}(0)=0
for
0\leq k\leq n-1
.
In this case
V^{-n}g=g^{(n)}
.

Fix
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
,
and, for
r>0
, set
g_{r}(x):=x^{n}\exp(re^{i\theta}x)
.
Then
g_{r}\in V^{n}(L^{2}[0,1])
, and by Leibniz’s formula we have

g_{r}^{(n)}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{n!}{k!}x^{k}(re^{i\theta})^{k}\exp(re^{i\theta}x).

Hence

\displaystyle\langle V^{-n}g_{r},g_{r}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{n!}{k!}(re^{i\theta})^{k}\Bigl\langle x^{k}\exp(re^{i\theta}x),x^{n}\exp(re^{i\theta}x)\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{n!}{k!}(re^{i\theta})^{k}\int_{0}^{1}x^{n+k}\exp((2r\cos\theta)x)\,dx.

Now, for each
m\geq 0
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}x^{m}\exp((2r\cos\theta)x\,dx)\sim\int_{0}^{1}\exp((2r\cos\theta))x\,dx\quad(r\to\infty).

It follows that, as
r\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}x^{n+k}\exp((2r\cos\theta)x\,dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}x^{2n}\exp((2r\cos\theta))x\,dx\Bigl(1+o(1)\Bigl)

\displaystyle=\|g_{r}\|_{2}^{2}(1+o(1)).

Therefore

\langle V^{-n}g_{r},g_{r}\rangle=\Bigl(r^{n}e^{in\theta}+O(r^{n-1})\Bigr)\|g_{r}\|_{2}^{2}(1+o(1))\quad(r\to\infty).

Thus,
for each
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
,
the set
W(V^{-n})
contains complex numbers of the form
r^{n}e^{in\theta}(1+o(1))

as
r\to\infty
.
As
n\geq 2
and
W(V^{-n})
is convex, we deduce that
W(V^{-n})={\mathbb{C}}
, as claimed.
∎

Remark
.

The simple descriptions
of the numerical ranges of negative powers of
V
in Theorems
1.4

and
1.5
are in marked contrast with those of the positive powers of
V
,
which are more complex.
It has long been known that
W(V)
is the closed region bounded
by the vertical segment
[-i/2\pi,\,i/2\pi]
and the curves

t\mapsto\Bigl(\frac{1-\cos t}{t^{2}}\Bigr)\pm i\Bigl(\frac{t-\sin t}{t^{2}}\Bigr)\quad(t\in[0,2\pi]).

This was stated without proof in
[
4
, p.113]
.
Proofs can be found, for example, in
[
5
]
and
[
9
]
.
More recently, in
[
6
]
, Khadkhuu and the second author obtained
an analogous description of
W(V^{2})
.
It is considerably more complicated.
To the best of our knowledge, the precise identification of
W(V^{n})
remains
an open problem for
n\geq 3
.

The operator norms of
V^{n}

have been intensively studied by many authors
(see e.g.
[
1
, p.1058]
).
Analytic expressions for
\|V^{n}\|
are known for
n=1,2,3
.
For higher values of
n
, exact calculations become too complicated,
and one has to be satisfied with estimates. We suspect that the same may
turn out to be true
of
W(V^{n})
.
