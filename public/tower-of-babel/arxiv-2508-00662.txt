Giovanni Cerulli Irelli, Javier De Loera Chávez, Elena Pascucci

Abstract
. We provide a topological characterization of quivers whose path algebra satisfies a polynomial identity. This class includes the oriented cycle and acyclic quivers and, in the latter case, we describe the associated T-ideal. We introduce a generalization of Arnold’s A-graded algebras, which we call locally A-graded algebras, and prove that they are also PI. We give an example of a quiver algebra satisfying a polynomial identity, even if the path algebra of the quiver does not.

1.
Introduction

An algebra over a field
F
is called PI if there exists a non-commutative polynomial whose evaluation in every tuple of its elements is zero. Such a polynomial is then called a polynomial identity for the algebra. For example, if
A
is commutative then a polynomial identity for
A
is
x_{1}x_{2}-x_{2}x_{1}
. We say that a quiver
Q
is PI if its path algebra
FQ
is
PI
. Our first result gives a classification of finite PI-quivers.

Theorem 1.1
.

A finite quiver
Q
is
PI
if and only every vertex of
Q
lies in at most one oriented cycle.

Since subalgebras and quotients of PI algebras are PI, Theorem
1.1
provides a huge new class of PI algebras.

The quivers appearing in Theorem
1.1
are not new in the literature: Pere Ara informed us that they are precisely the quivers for which the corresponding Leavitt path algebra has finite GK-dimension
[
6
, Theorem 1.1]
,
[
4
]
. The class of Leavitt path algebras associated to a finite quiver which are PI are precisely those of GK dimension less or equal than one
[
9
, Theorem 4.1]
.

In the light of Theorem
1.1
, an acyclic quiver
Q
is PI. In this case we can improve considerably the result and give a description of the T-ideal
\Id(FQ)
of polynomial identities for its path algebra, at least in case the field
F
has characteristic zero.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
F
be a field of characteristic zero and let
Q
be an acyclic quiver. Then

\Id(FQ)=\langle[x_{1},x_{2}]\cdots[x_{2m-1},x_{2m}]\rangle_{T}

where
m-1
is the maximal length of a path in
Q
.

In the process of proving Theorem
1.1
we encountered a new class of algebras that we call
locally A-graded
(definition
5.1
), which are a generalization of Arnold’s A-graded algebras
[
7
]
. Examples of locally A-graded algebras include matrix algebras, upper triangular matrix algebras and their nilpotent subalgebras, the commutative square quiver algebra, the path algebra of an oriented cycle, the Nakayama algebras. Our second result is as follows

Theorem 1.3
.

A locally A-graded algebra is PI.

By Theorem
1.1
we know that the quiver
Q
obtained by gluing two oriented cycles in a vertex
v
is not PI. However, the last result of this paper states that a natural quotient of
Q
is indeed PI.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
Q
be the quiver obtained by gluing two oriented cycles
C(1)
and
C(2)
on a vertex
v
. Let
\alpha
be an arrow of
C(1)
ending in
v
and let
\beta
be an arrow of
C(2)
starting from
v
, then the algebra
A=FQ/(\alpha\beta)
is PI.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we recall facts about PI theory. In Section
3
we recall basic notions on quivers and quiver algebras. In Section
4
we introduce our main tool to prove Theorem
1.3
, namely the graph theoretic machinery used by Swan to prove Amitsur-Levitski’s Theorem; this section contains also Lemma
4.5
which is used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We prove Theorems
1.3
,
1.1
,
1.2
,
1.4
in Sections
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
, respectively.

2.
Polynomial Identities

In this section, we report basic notions about the theory of polynomial identities. Standard references are
[
3
]
and
[
13
]
. We fix a field
F
. By an algebra we mean an associative algebra over
F
. Given a countable set of non-commutative variables
X=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
we denote by
F\langle X\rangle=F\langle x_{1},x_{2},\dots\rangle
the free associative algebra generated by
X
. Its elements are hence non-commutative polynomials in
X
with coefficients in
F
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A
be an
F
-algebra and
f\in F\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
.
We say that
f
is a
polynomial identity for
A
(or simply a
PI for
A
) if
f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=0
for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A
. If there exists a non-zero polynomial identity for
A
then
A
is called
PI
.

Example 2.2
.

A commutative algebra is PI. Indeed, it satisfies the polynomial

[x_{1},x_{2}]\coloneqq x_{1}x_{2}-x_{2}x_{1},
called the
Lie commutator
of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
.

Example 2.3
.

A nilpotent algebra
A
is
PI
. Indeed, the polynomial
x_{1}^{n}
is a polynomial identity for
A
, where
n
is its nilpotency index.

Lemma 2.4
.

The algebra
M_{2}(F)
of
2\times 2
matrices over
F
is PI. Indeed, the polynomial in three variables
[x_{1},[x_{2},x_{3}]^{2}]
is a PI for it.

Proof.

By the Cayley-Hamilton theorem,
A^{2}-\mathrm{tr}(A)A+\mathrm{det}(A)\mathbf{1}_{2}=0_{M_{2}(F)}
, for every
A\in M_{2}(F)
. In particular, if
A=[B,C]
is a commutator then
A^{2}=-\mathrm{det}(A)\mathbf{1}_{2}
is a central element. Thus
[A,[B,C]^{2}]=0_{M_{2}(F)}
for every
A,B,C\in M_{2}(F)
.
∎

Example 2.5
.

A finite dimensional algebra is
PI
. Indeed, the determinant in
n+1
variables is a polynomial identity, where
n
denotes its dimension.

Non-example 2.6
.

The free algebra
F\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
in
n\geq 2
variables does not satisfy any polynomial identity. Indeed, the existence of such an identity would contradict its freeness.

Definition 2.7
.

Given an
F
-algebra
A
, we denote by

\Id(A)\coloneqq\{f\in F\langle X\rangle\ |\ \text{f is PI for A}\}

the
set of polynomial identities of
A
.

We recall that
\Id(A)
is a
T
-ideal
, which means that it is a two-sided ideal of
F\langle X\rangle
stable under every endomorphism of
F\langle X\rangle
. In simple terms, this means that
given a polynomial
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in Id(A)
, if each variable
x_{i}
is replaced by an arbitrary polynomial
g_{i}\in F\langle X\rangle
then

f(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\in\Id(A)
.
The following useful properties of T-ideals hold

(1)

\displaystyle\textrm{If }B\subseteq A\textrm{ is a subalgebra, }\Id(A)\subseteq\Id(B),

(2)

\displaystyle\textrm{If }B=A/I\textrm{ is a quotient algebra, then }\Id(A)\subseteq\Id(B).

In particular, subalgebras and quotients of PI algebras are PI algebras.

Given non-empty subset
Y\subseteq F\langle X\rangle
, we denote by
\langle Y\rangle_{T}
the
T
-ideal generated by
Y
in
F\langle X\rangle
.

The following remarkable result provides a description of the T-ideal of the algebra
UT_{n}(F)
of upper triangular
n\times n
matrices with entries in
F
.

Theorem 2.8
.

[
14
, Theorem 1]

Let
F
be a field of characteristic zero. Then

\Id(UT_{n}(F))=\langle u_{n}\rangle_{T}

where

(3)

u_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{2n})\coloneq[x_{1},x_{2}][x_{3},x_{4}]\dots[x_{2n-1},x_{2n}].

The hypothesis on the characteristic of the field
F
in Theorem
2.8
is mainly due to the following result, which holds only in characteristic zero. Recall that a polynomial
f
is said to be
multilinear
if it is linear in every variable, i.e. its degree with respect to each variable is one.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
F
a field of characteristic zero and let
A
be an
F
-algebra. Then
\Id(A)
is generated by multilinear polynomials.

An important example of multilinear polynomial is the
standard polynomial
of degree
n
:

(4)

St_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\coloneqq\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\text{sgn}({\sigma})x_{\sigma(1)}\cdots x_{\sigma(n)},

where
S_{n}
denotes the
symmetric group
of degree
n
and
\text{sgn}({\sigma})
is the sign of the permutation
\sigma
. Indeed, the following remarkable result holds about the algebra
M_{n}(F)
of
n\times n
matrices over
F
.

Theorem 2.10

(Amitsur-Levitski)
.

[
5
]

For every
n\geq 1
,
St_{2n}\in\Id(M_{n}(F))
.

Notice that the following Leibniz type formula holds

St_{n+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n+1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(-1)^{i+1}\,x_{i}\,St_{n}(x_{1},\dots,\hat{x_{i}},\dots,x_{n+1})

so that

(5)

St_{m}\in\langle St_{n}\rangle_{T},\;\forall\,m\geq n.

The standard polynomial is an example of an alternating polynomial.
Recall that a polynomial
f\coloneqq f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n};y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
, which is multilinear in the first
n
variables, is
alternating
in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
if, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{n};y_{1},\dots,y_{m})=0_{F\langle X\rangle}.

3.
Quivers and Path Algebras

In this section we fix notation and recall some basic facts about quivers and path algebras.
Standard references are
[
10
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
8
]
and
[
17
]
.

A
quiver

Q
is a quadruple
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,t)
where

•

Q_{0}
is a finite set of
vertices
,

•

Q_{1}
is a finite set of
arrows
,

•

s,t:Q_{1}\to Q_{0}
are two functions assigning to each arrow
\alpha\in Q_{1}
its
starting
vertex
s(\alpha)
and its
terminal
vertex
t(\alpha)
.

An arrow
\alpha\in Q_{1}
is graphically represented as:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(\alpha)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces t(\alpha)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}

Example 3.1
.

An example of quiver is given by:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 3}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}

where
Q_{0}=\{1,2,3\}
,
Q_{1}=\{\alpha,\beta\}
,
1=s(\alpha)
,
2=t(\alpha)=s(\beta)
,
3=t(\beta)
.

A
path

p
of length
n=l(p)\geq 1
in a quiver
Q
is a sequence
p=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})
of
n
arrows of
Q
such that
t(\alpha_{i})=s(\alpha_{i+1})
for all
1\leq i\leq n-1
; the vertex
s(p)\coloneq s(\alpha_{1})
is the
starting vertex
of
p
and the vertex
t(p)\coloneq t(\alpha_{n})
is the
terminal vertex
of
p
; the
support
of
p
is the subquiver of
Q
with arrows
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
and whose vertices are the starting and terminal vertex of each of its arrows. Instead of
p=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})
we use the shorthand notation
p=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{n}
. To every vertex
v\in Q_{0}
it is formally attached the path
e_{v}
which has length zero and starts and ends at
v
. The path
e_{v}
is called the
lazy path
at
v
. A
closed path
in
Q
is a path
c
such that
t(c)=s(c)
. The support of a closed path in
Q
is called an
oriented cycle
of
Q
. An oriented cycle with
n
vertices is called an
n
-cycle
and it will be denoted by
C_{n}
. A
loop
is a
1
-cycle. A quiver is said to be
acyclic
if it contains no oriented cycles.

Given a quiver
Q
and a field
F
, the
path algebra

FQ
of
Q
is the
F
-vector space spanned by all possible paths in
Q
, including lazy paths.
The multiplication of two paths
p
and
q
in
Q
is the path
pq
if
t(p)=s(q)
and it is zero otherwise. The multiplication in
FQ
is defined by extending linearly the path multiplication. The set of all paths in
Q
is called the
standard basis
of
FQ
. A path algebra
FQ
is finite dimensional if and only if
Q
is acyclic.

The path algebra
FQ
is graded by the length of the paths, i.e. there is a direct sum decomposition
FQ=\bigoplus_{d\geq 0}FQ^{d}
where
FQ^{d}
is the span of all paths in
Q
of length
d
. Moreover, the set of lazy paths
\{e_{i}\mid\,i\in Q_{0}\}
is a complete set of primitive orthogonal idempotents of
FQ
. Thus
FQ
admits a decomposition
FQ=\bigoplus_{i,j\in Q_{0}}e_{i}FQe_{j}
. Here,
e_{i}FQe_{j}
is hence the vector subspace of
FQ
generated by paths starting at vertex
i
and ending at vertex
j
. Combining these two decompositions we get

(6)

FQ=\bigoplus_{d,i,j}FQ^{d}_{i,j}

where
FQ^{d}_{i,j}\coloneq FQ^{d}\cap e_{i}FQe_{j}
.

Let
\mathfrak{m}
denote the two-sided ideal of
FQ
generated by
Q_{1}
. A two sided ideal
I\subset FQ
is called
admissible
if there exists a positive integer
k
such that
\mathfrak{m}^{k}\subseteq I\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{2}
. The quotient of a path algebra by an admissible ideal is called a
bound quiver algebra
. In particular, a bound quiver algebra is finite dimensional. A quotient
FQ/I
where
I\subseteq\mathfrak{m}^{2}
is simply called a
quiver algebra
.

If an admissible ideal
I
of
FQ
is generated by paths in
Q
then
I
is called a
monomial
ideal and
FQ/I
is called a
monomial quiver algebra
. It has a basis formed by paths called its
standard basis
.

Example 3.2
.

For the quiver

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 3}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}

the path algebra
FQ
has standard basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},\alpha,\beta,\alpha\beta\}
. The ideal
I=\langle\alpha\beta\rangle
is admissible and the bound quiver algebra
FQ/I
has standard basis
\mathcal{B}=\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},\alpha,\beta\}
.

Example 3.3
.

If
Q
is a loop,
FQ
is isomorphic to the polynomial ring
F[x]
.

4.
Unicursal paths

For convenience of the reader, in this section we collect some results and notions from
[
19
]
and also prove Lemma
4.5
, to be used later on.

Definition 4.1
.

[
19
, Definition 2]

Let
\Sigma=(\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{1},s,t)
be a finite quiver with arrow set
\Sigma_{1}=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\}
. A path
\gamma
in
\Sigma
is called
unicursal
if it contains all arrows of
\Sigma
exactly once. For a unicursal path
\gamma=\alpha_{\sigma(1)}\cdots\alpha_{\sigma(m)}
we define
\operatorname{sgn}(\gamma)\coloneq\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)
.

Unicursal paths are most commonly known as

5.
Locally A-graded algebras

In this section we introduce the notion of
locally A-graded algebras
and prove that they admit a polynomial identity.

Definition 5.1
.

Given a positive integer
n
, a
locally A-graded
algebra of size
n
, is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded
F
-algebra
A=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb{Z}}A^{d}
with the property that each homogeneous vector subspace is a direct sum
A^{d}=\bigoplus_{1\leq i,j\leq n}A_{i,j}^{d}
such that

(9)

\displaystyle\textrm{dim}(A_{i,j}^{d})\leq 1,

(10)

\displaystyle\textrm{there exists an homogenous linear basis }\mathcal{B}=\{e_{ij}^{(d)}\}\textrm{ of }A

\displaystyle\textrm{which is matrix-like, i.e. }e_{ij}^{(d)}\in A_{i,j}^{d},\textrm{ and}

e_{ij}^{(d_{1})}e_{k\ell}^{(d_{2})}=\begin{cases}\delta_{j,k}e_{i,\ell}^{(d_{1}+d_{2})}&\textrm{if }A_{i,\ell}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}\not=0,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\delta_{jk}
is the Kronecker delta. We say that
\mathcal{B}
is the
standard basis
of
A
.

Remark 5.2
.

If
A
is a locally A-graded algebra such that
A_{i,j}=\bigoplus_{t=0}^{d}A_{i,j}^{t}
, and
A_{i,j}^{t}\neq 0
for all
0\leq t\leq d
, for some
i
and
j
, then
A_{i,j}
is
A
-graded in the sense of Arnold
[
7
]
. This explains the choice of terminology.

Example 5.3
.

Let
Q
be the quiver

\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\bullet
\alpha_{1}
\alpha_{2}
\alpha_{3}
\alpha_{4}

The bound quiver algebra
A=FQ/\langle\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{3}\alpha_{4}\rangle
(also called the commutative square algebra)
is locally A-graded.

Non-example 5.4
.

The path algebra
FQ
of the quiver in the previous example is not locally A-graded (with respect to the standard basis) since there are two different paths with the same endpoints and of the same length.

Example 5.5
.

The algebra
M_{n}(F)
is locally A-graded with
\mathcal{B}=\{e_{ij}\}
.

Example 5.6
.

The path algebra of a quiver
T
whose underlying graph is a tree is locally A-graded. Indeed, given two vertices there is at most one path between them. In this case, the stronger condition
\mathrm{dim}\,e_{i}FTe_{j}\leq 1
holds.

Lemma 5.7
.

The path algebra of
C_{n}
is locally A-graded.

Proof.

Given two vertices
i
and
j
of
C_{n}
, there is at most one path of a given length
d
starting in
i
and ending in
j
.
∎

Open question 5.9
.

It would be interesting to characterize quivers whose path algebra is locally A-graded.

The next lemma provides more examples of locally A-graded algebras.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
A
be a locally A-graded algebra with standard basis
\mathcal{B}
. If
B\subset A
is a subalgebra of
A
spanned by a part of
\mathcal{B}
then
B
is locally A-graded. Dually, if
I\subset A
is a two-sided ideal spanned by a part of
\mathcal{B}
then
A/I
is locally A-graded.

Proof.

It follows immediately from the definition.
∎

Corollary 5.11
.

Let
Q
be a quiver whose path algebra
A=FQ
is locally A-graded. Then, for every monomial ideal
I\subset FQ
, the quiver algebra
A/I
is locally A-graded.

Proof.

By hypothesis,

is generated by paths in

. The result is hence a consequence of Lemma
5.10
.
∎

Example 5.12
.

Given

, the algebra

of upper triangular

is locally A-graded.

Example 5.13
.

The algebra of nilpotent upper triangular matrices is locally A-graded.

Proposition 5.14
.

Every basic Nakayama algebra is locally A-graded.

Proof.

Let
A
be a basic Nakayama algebra. By
[

, Theorem V.3.2]
there is an isomorphism
A\cong FQ/I
where

is either

or

for some

It is known that

The following is our first main result.

Theorem 5.15
.

Let
A
be a locally A-graded algebra with
n
idempotents. Then
\operatorname{St}_{2n}
is a polynomial identity for
A
.

Proof.

Let
A
be a locally
A
-graded algebra of size
n
. Since
\operatorname{St_{2n}}
is multilinear and alternating, we need only to check evaluations at pairwise different elements of the multiplicative basis
\mathcal{B}\coloneq\{e_{ij}^{(d)}\}
of
A
. Let
\beta=\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{2n}\}\subset\mathcal{B}
be such a choice. The Swan quiver
\Sigma_{\beta}
of
\beta
(see definition
4.6
) satisfies (
8
): Indeed,
\lvert(\Sigma_{\beta})_{0}\rvert\leq n
and
\lvert(\Sigma_{\beta})_{1}\rvert=\lvert\beta\rvert=2n
.

For every
\sigma\in S_{2n}
, the element
\gamma=\beta_{\sigma(1)}\beta_{\sigma(2)}\cdots\beta_{\sigma(2n)}
belongs to a graded piece
A_{i,j}^{d}
where
d=deg(\beta_{1})+\ldots+deg(\beta_{2n})
and
i
and
j
are suitable vertices of
\Gamma_{\beta}
. Such an element
\gamma
corresponds to a unicursal path from
i
to
j
in
\Gamma_{\beta}
.
By Theorem
4.4
, there is some unicursal path
\gamma^{\prime}

in
G_{\beta}
from
i
to
j
with
\sgn(\gamma^{\prime})=-\sgn(\gamma)
.
Since
A
is locally A-graded,
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}
as elements of
A
and hence they cancel each other out in the expansion of
\operatorname{St}_{2n}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{2n})
.
∎

As a corollary we find new examples of PI-algebras.

Corollary 5.16
.

The path algebra of the
n
-cycle
C_{n}
satisfies

. In particular,

Remark 5.17
.

The minimal degree of a polynomial identity on

of the set

By definition, Nakayama algebras are finite dimensional and thus PI. The following immediate corollary of Theorem

gives a polynomial identity whose degree is independent of the dimension of the algebra.

Corollary 5.18

A basic Nakayama algebra

.

6.
PI-quivers

In this section, we classify the quivers whose path algebras are PI. For simplicity, we introduce a name for them:

Definition 6.1
.

A quiver is called a
PI
-quiver if its path algebra is
PI
.

The following is our second main result.

Theorem 6.2
.

A quiver
Q
is
PI
if and only if each vertex of
Q
lies in at most one oriented cycle.

Proof.

If a quiver
Q
contains a vertex
v
which belongs to two oriented cycles
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, then the subalgebra
B(c_{1},c_{2})
of
FQ
generated by
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
is isomorphic to the free algebra in two generators. It follows from (
1
) that
\Id(FQ)\subseteq\Id(B(c_{1},c_{2}))=\Id(F\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle)=\{0\}
. It follows that
FQ
has no polynomial identities.

Let us prove the other implication. Let hence
Q
be a quiver such that every vertex of
Q
belongs to at most one oriented cycle. Let
n=\lvert Q_{0}\rvert
be number of vertices of
Q
. We prove that

St_{2n}\in\Id(FQ)
. More precisely, we claim that for any set
\beta=(p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{2n})
of
2n
paths in
Q
, we have

(12)

St_{2n}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{2n})=0.

If the elements of
\beta
are not all distinct, then (
12
) follows by the fact that
St_{2n}
is an alternating polynomial. Let us suppose that
\lvert\beta\rvert=2n
. If every product of all the elements of
\beta
is zero then (
12
) holds by definition (
4
). We may assume, up to a reordering of
\beta
, that
p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{2n}\neq 0
.
We notice that every non-zero summand of
S_{2n}(p_{1},\cdots,p_{2n})
corresponds to a unicursal path in the Swan quiver
\Sigma_{\beta}
.
We notice that the number of vertices of
\Sigma_{\beta}
is less or equal the number of vertices of
Q
, which is
n
and the number of arrows of
\Sigma_{\beta}
is
\lvert\beta\rvert=2n
; thus
\Sigma_{\beta}
satisfies (
8
) and we can apply Swan’s Theorem
4.4
.
The following is the key lemma to conclude the proof.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
Q
be a quiver in which each vertex lies in at most one oriented cycle. Let
\beta=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
be a set of paths in
Q
such that
p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{m}\neq 0
. Then given two vertices
i,j\in(\Sigma_{\beta})_{0}
of the Swan quiver of
\beta
, all unicursal paths in
\Sigma_{\beta}
from
i
to
j
represent the same path in
Q
.

For convenience of the reader, we postpone the proof of Lemma
6.3
to section
6.1
below and finish the proof of Theorem
6.2
as follows: By the key Lemma
6.3
all those paths represent the same path in
Q
and thus they cancel each other out in
S_{2n}(p_{1},\cdots,p_{2n})
. We conclude that (
12
) holds.
∎

6.1.
Proof of Lemma
6.3

We denote by
A_{\beta}
the subalgebra of
FQ
generated by
\beta
. We proceed by induction on
m=\lvert\beta\rvert\geq 1
. If
m=1
, then there is only one unicursal path in
\Sigma_{\beta}
and hence there is nothing to prove. Let us assume that
m\geq 2
. There are two possibilities,

(13)

\displaystyle\textrm{every arrow of }\Sigma_{\beta}\textrm{ lies in an oriented cycle},

(14)

\displaystyle\textrm{there exists an arrow }\alpha\textrm{ of }\Sigma_{\beta}

\displaystyle\textrm{that does not lie in an oriented cycle of }\Sigma_{\beta}.

If (
13
) holds, we claim that there exists an oriented cycle
C\subset Q
such that
\beta\subset FC
. Indeed, we notice that
\Sigma_{\beta}
is strongly path connected (to see this, we notice that there exists a path from
i
to
j
or from
j
to
i
because
\Sigma_{\beta}
has a unicursal path and now we conclude by the fact that every arrow of
\Sigma_{\beta}
is part of an oriented cycle). Now,
p_{1}
is part of an oriented cycle of
\Sigma_{\beta}
that we call
C(1)
and thus there exists an oriented cycle
C
in
Q
such that every arrow of
C(1)
is a path of
C
, in particular
p_{1}\in FC
. If
C(1)=\Sigma_{\beta}
we are done. Otherwise, let
p^{\prime}\in\beta
but not an arrow of
C(1)
. Then there exists a path
q_{1}
from any vertex
v\in(C(1))_{0}
to
s(p^{\prime})
and another path
q_{2}
from
t(p^{\prime})
to
v
and thus an oriented cycle
C(2)=q_{1}p^{\prime}q_{2}
. Since

is both in
C(1)
and in

,
v
is a vertex of
Q
lying in two oriented cycles; since there are no vertices in

lying in two different oriented cycles, we conclude that
C(1)
and

represent the same cycle

and thus

. It follows that

If (

, there is a decomposition of

7.
Acyclic quivers

In this section we find generators for the T-ideal of finite dimensional path algebras, i.e. path algebras of acyclic quivers. Notice that among the class of PI algebras the subclass for which is known a description of the generators of the T-ideal is quite small. For example, the set of generators of the T-ideal of
M_{n}(F)
is not known unless
n=2
in which case it is described in
[
11
]
. The following is our third main result.
(See (
3
) for the definition of
u_{n}
.)

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
F
be a field of characteristic zero and let
Q
be an acyclic quiver. Then

(15)

\Id(FQ)=\langle u_{m}\rangle_{T}

where
m-1
is the maximal length of a path in
Q
.

Proof.

A path of maximal length in
Q
gives an embedding of
\overrightarrow{A\,}_{m}
in
Q
, because
Q
is acyclic and hence the vertices of this path are all distinct. Hence
F\overrightarrow{A\,}_{m}\subseteq FQ
and, by (
1
),
\Id(FQ)\subseteq\Id(F\overrightarrow{A\,}_{m})
. By Lemma
3.4
and Theorem
2.8
, we get
\Id(FQ)\subseteq\langle u_{m}\rangle_{T}
.

To prove the other inclusion, we notice that, since
Q
is acyclic, for any paths
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
in
Q
, the element
[\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}]
is either zero or a single path; if it is a non-zero path, it has length
l(\gamma_{1})+l(\gamma_{2})\geq 1
, because
[e_{i},e_{j}]=0
for any
i,j\in Q_{0}
. Thus, a non trivial evaluation of
u_{m}
will produce a concatenation of
m
paths each of length at least 1, which is zero by hypothesis. Therefore
u_{m}\in\Id(FQ)
and hence
\langle u_{m}\rangle_{T}\subseteq\Id(FQ)
.
∎

Let

A glimpse into quiver algebras

In this section, we take a first look at the behavior of quotients of non-PI quivers. The simplest examples of such quivers arise by gluing two oriented cycles at a common vertex. We denote by
C_{n}\star_{v}C_{m}
the quiver obtained by gluing
C_{n}
and
C_{m}
at a vertex
v
(see figure
2
for the case
n=3
and
m=4
)

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
Q
be a quiver given by the gluing of two oriented cycles
C(1)
and
C(2)
at a vertex
v
. Let
\alpha
be the arrow of
C(1)
ending in
v
and let
\beta
be the arrow of
C(2)
starting from
v
. The monomial quiver algebra
A=FQ/\langle\alpha\beta\rangle
is PI. More precisely, if
C(1)=C_{n}
and
C(2)=C_{m}
and
k=\max\{n,m\}
, then
St_{2k}^{(1)}St_{2k}^{(2)}
is a polynomial identity for
A
.

Proof.

Let us use the shorthand

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{4k})\coloneq St_{2k}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{2k})St_{2k}(x_{2k+1},\cdots,x_{4k}).

We need to prove that
f\in\Id(A)
. Since
f
is multilinear in
4k
variables, it is enough to evaluate
f
on
4k
standard basis elements of
A
. By definition, these are either paths of
C(1)
, or paths of
C(2)
or of the form
\gamma_{2}\gamma_{1}
, where
\gamma_{i}\in FC(i)
, for
i=1,2
. Let, thus,
\mathcal{S}=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{4k})
be a tuple of
4k
such elements. If every product of all the elements of
\mathcal{S}
is zero, then
f(p_{1},\dots,p_{4k})
is zero. Up to a reordering of
\mathcal{S}
, we can hence assume that
p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{4k}\neq 0
. If
\mathcal{S}\subset FC(2)
then
St_{2k}(p_{1},\cdots,p_{2k})=0
by Corollary
5.16
applied to
C(2)
and (
5
). Otherwise, let
\ell
be the first index such that
t(p_{\ell})\in C(1)
. If
\ell\leq 2k
then
p_{s}\in FC(1)
for every
s>\ell
, and, again by Corollary
5.16
applied to
C(1)
and (
5
),
St_{2k}(p_{2k+1},\cdots,p_{4k})=0
. If
\ell>2k
then similarly
St_{2k}(p_{1},\cdots,p_{2k})=0
.
∎
