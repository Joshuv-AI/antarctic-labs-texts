Impact of small antral pseudocyst interventions on bone remodeling during sinus floor elevation: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Minna Fu, Fei Yang, Yuedi Yu, Xingwen Wu, Qianrong Zhou, Yang Sun, Zhihui Han, Dandan Song, Youcheng Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe study aims to assess the effects of different treatments on postoperative hard tissue surrounding implants. This will be achieved through a retrospective analysis of patients with small antral pseudocysts (APs) measuring 20 mm or less, who received various management strategies for APs during lateral maxillary sinus floor elevation (LSFE).MethodsThis retrospective study examined 107 patients who underwent LSFE with concurrent implant placement, categorized into removal, aspiration, and untreated groups. The three-dimensional volumetric change rate of the bone graft within the maxillary sinus, the endo-sinus bone gain (ESBG), apical bone height (ABH), and peri-implant bone height (PBH) were assessed to quantify bone volume change. The marginal bone loss (MBL) and implant failure rate were evaluated to compare the success rates of implant surgery.ResultsAll implants demonstrated functionality without any failure or occurrences of sinusitis. Six months after surgery, the untreated group exhibited a significant reduction in bone graft volume (-18.31% [-19.72%, -15.24%]) compared to the removal group (-14.11% [-17.42%, -11.05%]) and the aspiration group (-16.55% [-18.71%, -11.83%]) (P < .001), alongside greater changes in ESBG and ABH (P < .01). PBH and MBL exhibited comparable values across the groups. Over a period of two years, bone graft stability was maintained, whereas, PBH exhibited a significant increase from six months to one year.ConclusionsThe findings of our study suggested that in LSFE, routine removal of small APs (diameter ≤ 20 mm), might be not required. A conservative treatment strategy for APs was advised to streamline the surgical procedure and enhance the success rate of implantation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07229-y.Keywords: Maxillary sinus floor augmentation, Antral pseudocysts, Immediate implantation

Background

Lateral maxillary sinus floor elevation (LSFE) is an essential surgical procedure designed to augment bone volume in the posterior maxilla, facilitating the dependable placement of dental implants in atrophied ridges [1]. Maxillary sinus pneumatization is frequently responsible for a gradual decrease in residual bone height beneath the sinus floor [2]. Cysts represent the most common pathological conditions of the maxillary sinus, with incidences reported at 6.89% on panoramic radiographs and 10.1% on cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) [3, 4]. Although LSFE provides significant advantages, the presence of maxillary sinus cysts may increase the associated risks of this procedure [5].

Maxillary sinus cysts are typically classified as mucous cysts, retention cysts, antral pseudocysts (APs), and postoperative cysts [6]. Mucous cysts require comprehensive preoperative evaluation due to their potential to damage bone tissue and affect adjacent critical structures [7]. Therefore, preoperative treatment must be carefully evaluated prior to the execution of maxillary sinus floor elevation (MSFE) [8]. Retention cysts, generally small and resulting from obstructed mucosal glands, are frequently located adjacent to the maxillary ostium [9]. In contrast, APs typically present as asymptomatic, well-defined, dome-shaped radiolucent lesions that do not exhibit bone erosion [10]. Although treatment is deemed unnecessary for asymptomatic APs [11], the positioning of the AP at the base of the maxillary sinus and its proximity to the mucosa may complicate lateral sinus floor elevation (LSFE). The liquid in APs consistenly accumulates in the lamina propria of the mucoperiosteum, which may exert pressure on the sinus floor mucosa, resulting in thinning or localized expansion of the mucosa [12]. This may heighten the risk of mucosal perforation during LSFE and increase the probability of postoperative infection [13].

Large maxillary sinus cysts (diameter >20 mm) typically require removal prior to LSFE, as they may obstruct the sinus ostium and elevate the risk of postoperative sinusitis and complications [14]. However, the management for small cysts (diameter ≤ 20 mm) is contingent upon the clinical judgement of the surgeons. Currently, three main strategies are recognized for managing APs during LSFE: cyst removal followed by LSFE, LSFE without cyst intervention, and cyst aspiration in conjunction with LSFE [15–17]. Each method presents unique implications for sinus health and postoperative recovery. Cyst excision effectively removes space-occupying lesions, whereas aspiration diminishes cyst volume with minimal impact on the sinus membrane [15]. Untreated cysts maintain mucosal integrity; however, they may complicate surgical repair in the event of if perforations.

The management of small cysts is a subject of ongoing debate. Certain studies support the removal or aspiration of cysts to guarantee the stability of implant. Liu et al. [15] documented cyst resolution or shrinkage in 14 patients following aspiration, with all implants attaining osseointegration within one year. In contrast, Lin et al. [18] noted no complications in 11 patients after cyst removal over a period of 29.2 months. Some researchers argue that small cysts may not substantially affect outcomes and advocate for a conservative approach to minimize surgical risks. Choi et al. [19] proposed that untreated small cysts with normal ventilation may not affect LSFE outcomes. To date, the majority, of the studies referenced are case reports, lacking comparable research involving larger sample sizes. Consequently, there is insufficient consensus regarding the management asymptomatic small APs (diameter ≤ 20 mm), necessitating further studies to assess treatment outcomes.

This study conducted a retrospective analysis of CBCT imaging data to evaluate the outcomes of three management strategies for small APs (diameter ≤ 20 mm) during LSFE with immediate implantation: cyst removal, aspiration, and no treatment. The evaluation focused on alterations in bone volume, rates of AP recurrence, and variations in postoperative cyst size to determine the effectiveness of these strategies. The study seeks to offer evidence-based recommendations for clinicians overseeing small APs during LSFE, aiming to enhance implantation success rates and patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Research design

This research, consistent with our primary objectives, was a retrospective cohort study conducted in accordance with STROBE guidelines and the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital affiliated with Fudan University’s (approval number: B2021-767R) and involved the collection of patient records and radiological examinations at the same institution.

Participants

This study included patients who underwent sinus floor elevation at the Department of Oral Implantology, Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, between 2018 and 2020. Preoperative CBCT scans of patients in edentulous areas revealed anatomical variations of the maxillary sinus. This study established the following inclusion criteria: (1) Patients must have undergone treatment via the lateral wall approach; (2) Patients must have a remaining bone height greater than 2 mm and less than 5 mm [20]; (3) Patients must demonstrate a remaining bone width exceeding 6 mm; (4) Patients must have a maxillary sinus anteroposterior diameter of 20 mm or less; (5) Patients must have had tooth extractions in the implant areas at least three months prior to the operation; (6) Simultaneous implant placement and maxillary sinus floor augmentation were performed; and (7) Patients must have received treatment for anatomical variations during surgery, which may include the removal of anatomical variations, aspiration of cystic fluid, or the decision not to treat the anatomical variations.

Exclusion criteria for patients included: (1) presence of systemic diseases; (2) alcohol misused or smoked exceeding 20 cigarettes per day; (3) had uncontrolled periodontal disease; (4) prior radiation therapy in the head and neck region; and (5) treatment with bisphosphonates, monoclonal antibodies, or targeted therapies. Figure 1 illustrated the research process of this study. Of 121 patients evaluated for eligibility, 14 were excluded due to preoperative apical pathologies in the maxillary sinus measuring over 20 mm in diameter, the surgical method involving crestal sinus floor elevation, or delayed implantation. A cohort of 107 patients and 170 implants was established, with patients categorized into three distinct groups based on the management strategy for maxillary sinus APs: removal group, aspiration group, and untreated group. The removal group consisted of 36 patients with 59 implants, the aspiration group included 36 patients with 52 implants, and the untreated group consisted of 35 patients with 59 implants. In this retrospective study, patients were assigned to the surgical removal group, aspiration group, or untreated group, based on the treatment approach documented in the medical records.

Clinical procedures

All procedures were performed under local anesthesia, in accordance with established disinfection protocols. A mucoperiosteal flap was elevated to reveal the lateral aspect of the maxillary sinus, exhibiting an intact light blue mucosa. Options for managing maxillary sinus cysts during the procedure include removal, fluid aspiration, or observation without intervention. In the cohort undergoing cyst removal, a syringe was employed to extract the cyst fluid, while an aspirator and forceps were utilized to excise the cyst wall. The cyst fluid aspiration group utilized a syringe exclusively for fluid extraction. The untreated group received MSFE without cyst intervention, with the sinus mucosa meticulously elevated using a periosteal detacher. In both the cyst removal and fluid aspiration groups, perforation of the sinus membrane was unavoidable due to the respective surgical procedures. A 25 × 25 mm porcine collagen membrane (Bio-Gide, Geistlich Pharma, Wolhusen, Switzerland) was utilized to repair the perforated site after following cyst removal and fluid aspiration. Gelatamp gelatin sponges containing colloidal silver were subsequently applied to the lateral wall to absorb blood, after which they were mixed with 0.5 g of large particles (1–2 mm) of deproteinized bovine bone mineral (Bio-Oss, Geistlich Pharma, Wolhusen, Switzerland). In the untreated group, sinus membrane perforation did not occur. Following the elevation of the sinus floor membrane, the mixture was applied directly to the untreated group. All implants were placed concurrently in all groups during the surgical procedure. A Bio-Gide was employed to cover the maxillary sinus wall window, subsequently followed by the suturing of the mucoperiosteal flap. All patients underwent preventive intravenous antibiotic therapy post-surgery. Cefuroxime (3 g, once daily) was administered intravenously for three days post-surgery, in conjunction with dexamethasone (10 mg, once daily) for one day following the procedure. The oral prescriptions consisted of cefuroxime axetil (250 mg, twice daily) and paracetamol (300 mg, as needed for pain relief), administered orally for three days following intravenous administration. All patients were prescribed limonene enteric-coated capsules (0.3 g, three times daily) and furacilin nasal drops (0.05 ml, three times daily) for topical use following surgery, with these medications administered for seven days post-operatively.

Data acquisition

This study gathered baseline patient characteristics, encompassing age, sex, residual bone height, edentulous area, edentulous site, preoperative apical periodontitis volume, gray value of APs, location of APs, sinus width, maxillary sinus floor morphology, maxillary sinus floor mucosal thickness, implant brand, implant diameter, and implant length. All patients underwent CBCT imaging at four designated time points: T0 (preoperative), T1 (immediately postoperative), T2 (6 months postoperative), T3 (12 months postoperative), and T4 (24 months postoperative).

DICOM data were imported into Mimics Medical 21.0 to generate three-dimensional (3D) reconstructions of the bone graft in the maxillary sinus utilizing editing tools and to compute the volume. Radiographic parameters

Endo-sinus bone gain (ESBG) is quantified by measuring the distance from the initial sinus floor boundary to the apex of the endo-sinus bone on the implant oral surfaces. This measurement is averaged from ESBGm, ESBGd, ESBGb, and ESBGp (Fig. 4C) [22].

Apical bone height (ABH) is determined by measuring from the highest point of the bone to the apex of the implant, followed by averaging the values of ABHo, ABHm, ABHd, ABHb, and ABHp (Fig. 4D) [23].

Peri-implant bone height (PBH) refers to the average distance from the top of the implant to the implant-bone interface. This measurement is derived from the sum of PBHm, PBHd, PBHb, and PBHp (Fig. 4E) [24].

Marginal bone loss (MBL) is calculated by subtracting the bone height measured six months post-surgery (MBH2) from the height recorded immediately after surgery (MBH1), expressed as: MBL = MBH1 - MBH2. Marginal bone height (MBH) quantifies the average distance from the implant shoulder to the crest of the alveolar ridge at the buccal, palatal, mesial, and distal aspects. When the implant neck is positioned at bone level, the MBH is noted as 0 (Fig. 4F) [25].

Relative variations in the 3D reconstructions of the bone graft in the maxillary sinus were assessed by analyzing ESBG, ABH, and PBH, while excluding MBL. The rate of change between consecutive follow-up intervals (T1 to T2, T2 to T3, and T3 to T4) was calculated. Rate of implant failure

The failure of an implant was ascertained by the existence of any of these conditions: (1) pain during utilization; (2) displacement of the implant; (3) radiographic evidence of bone loss surpassing 50% of the implant’s length; (4) continuous discharge; or (5) total absence of the implant from the oral cavity [26].

Alteration and recurrence of maxillary sinus pseudocysts during monitoring

Gather the recurrence rates of cysts in the cyst removal cohort, together with the alterations in cyst size in both the aspiration cohort and the untreated cohort. Cyst recurrence was characterized by the emergence of a convex high-density shadow in the initial cyst region during postoperative radiographic follow-up. Alterations in cyst volume over 5% were recorded [27].

Calculation of sample size

This study examines the impacts of removal, aspiration, and untreated interventions on concurrent implants with LSFE in AP patients, necessitating a minimum of 105 samples based on a medium effect size, α = 0.05, and power = 0.90.

Statistical analysis

Analysis was performed utilizing IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.1.0; data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD, whereas non-normally distributed data were depicted as median (P25, P75). One-way ANOVA was employed for regularly distributed data, whereas the Kruskal-Wallis H test was utilized for non-normally distributed data. To address potential clustering effects from many implants in individual patients, we utilized Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) to assess relationships between preoperative maxillary sinus floor morphology, implant features, and treatment groups. Regression models included random intercepts for patients to accommodate the hierarchical data structure, with each anatomical and implant attribute evaluated using univariate generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) (refer to Supporting Information). The Friedman test was utilized to investigate temporal changes within groups, while inter-group differences were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, with significance set at P <.05. Data visualization was conducted utilizing Prism 10 software.

Alterations in ESBG, ABH, PBH, and MBL subsequent to maxillary sinus floor elevation

The violin plot depicts the changes in the ESBG, ABH, PBH, and MBL across the removal, aspiration, and untreated groups during two years period following surgery (Fig. 6C). A notable disparity in the change rates of the ESBG and the ABH was seen among the three groups during the T2-T1 interval (P <.01). The change rates of the ESBG in the untreated group (−20.83% [−21.31%, −17.22%]) throughout the T2-T1 interval was significantly greater than that in the removal group (−17.76% [−20.66%, −16.42%]) and the aspiration group (−17.94% [−20.67%, −14.99%]) (P <.05). During the T2-T1 interval, the ABH change rate in the untreated group (−33.39% [−47.03%, −23.71%]) was substantially greater than that in the removal group (−23.80% [−28.62%, −19.53%]) and the aspiration group (−26.34% [−35.51%, −16.96%]) (P <.05). Conversely, no statistically significant difference was seen in the change rates of the PBH and the MBL among the three groups within two years post-surgery (P >.05). The variations in the ESBG, ABH, PBH, and MBL across the removal, aspiration, and untreated groups are examined in the subsequent sections (Fig. 6D).

Rate of implant failure

Throughout the two-year follow up, no implant was lost, and all implants remained fully functioning. Consequently, the implant failure rate during a two-year period is regarded as nonexistent.

Postoperative complications

In the removal group, six patients displayed fluid levels on CBCT immediately following surgery, while five patients in the aspiration group demonstrated similar findings. No cases of acute or chronic maxillary sinusitis reported during the follow-up period.

Alterations in size and recurrence of APs within 2-year postoperative

In the removal cohort, APs completely resolved at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years without recurrence. The aspiration group exhibited diverse outcomes at various observation points (Fig. 7A): at 6 months, 72.2% remained unchanged, 16.7% experienced reduction, 5.6% resolved, and 5.6% enlarged; at 1 year, 55.6% remained unchanged, 22.2% reduced, 19.4% resolved, and 2.8% enlarged; and at 2 years, 36.1% remained unchanged, 33.3% reduced, and 30.6% resolved. In the untreated group (Fig. 7B), 65.7% remained constant post-operative, whereas 34.3% exhibited an increase in size. At 6 months, 51.4% were unchanged, while 48.6% had a reduction; by 1 year, 77.1% were unchanged, 17.1% reduced, and 5.7% resolved; at 2 years, 62.9% were unchanged, 11.4% reduced, and 25.7% resolved. Over a period of two years, the alterations in size and recurrence of APs in the untreated group are comparable to those in the aspiration group, with no advancement of cyst observed in either scenario.

Discussion

This study aims to compare the clinical outcomes of three treatment strategies (removal, aspiration of cyst fluid, and no treatment) for small APs with a diameter of ≤ 20 mm during LSFE, offering a theoretical framework for determining the necessity of intervention in small APs (diameter ≤ 20 mm) during implant placement and assisting in the optimization of treatment strategies.

Throughout the follow-up period, all implants were effective, aligning with the results of other investigations. Comprehensive studies have demonstrated that the success rate of implants following MSFE is typically elevated, especially when the bone graft material is proficiently integrated into the sinus floor [28, 29]. This study further corroborates the idea that individuals with contemporaneous anatomical anomalies might attain implant outcomes equal to those of patients receiving conventional sinus lifts, regardless of the preservation or control of these differences. Significantly, consistent with the findings of Hu et al. [30], postoperative maxillary sinusitis were not observed in any patient throughout the 2-year follow-up period. Multiple factors are recognized to contribute to the development of postoperative maxillary sinusitis. Initially perforation of the maxillary sinus floor mucosa may expose graft material to the sinus cavity, potentially provoking an inflammatory response. According to the previous research [31], preserving the health of the sinus cavity mucosa is essential for minimizing the incidence of sinusitis. Additionally, postoperative pharmacological intervention, utilizing Limonene and Pinene Enteric Capsules or Ephedrine Hydrochloride and Nitrofurazone Nasal Drops, may significantly mitigate the risk of sinus blockage or infection. Studies indicate that Limonene and Pinene may inhibit bacterial efflux pumps in both in vitro and in vivo settings, thereby augmenting the efficacy of medicines and improving clinical outcomes in respiratory infections with symptoms [32]. The concurrent use of ephedrine and nitrofuran in nasal drop formulation may enhance their pharmacological effects. The vasoconstructive effects of ephedrine, together with the antibacterial qualities of nitrofuran, collaboratively promote the swift alleviation of nasal congestion and the management of underlying infections, as evidenced by recent investigation [33]. In this study, all patients received postoperative limonene capsules (0.3 g, three times daily) and furacilin nasal drops (0.05 ml, three times daily) for seven days to prevent sinus blockage and infection after LSFE. Limonene modulates inflammation and inhibits bacterial pumps, enhancing other medications, while furacilin has broad antibacterial properties and reduces nasal colonization. This combined strategy may help maintain sinus patency and lower postoperative complications, but more controlled studies are needed to evaluate the agents’ independent effects. Furthermore, the management of postoperative infections is essential. An insufficient antibiotic regimen or the utilization of contaminated graft materials may increase the likelihood of sinusitis development. Current guidelines indicate that the length of antibiotic medication after lateral wall fenestration and MSFE should be 5 to 7 days. For high-risk individuals, this length should be prolonged to 10 days to provide comprehensive infection prevention [27, 34, 35]. The reduction in sinusitis observed in this study may be ascribed to the small size of the pseudocysts (diameter ≤ 20 mm), reduced surgical trauma to the sinus cavity, the adoption of standardized postoperative medication protocols (including agents aimed at preventing mucosal edema), and proficient management of postoperative infections. Additionally, we acknowledge that the location and health of the ostium are crucial factors in maxillary sinus augmentation. Previous studies, including Gutman et al. [36], have highlighted the importance of an open and healthy ostium in facilitating proper sinus function and improving surgical outcomes.

Moreover, six months to one year post-surgery, the untreated group had a markedly higher rate of change in bone graft volume than the removal and aspiration groups. This finding indicates that removal and aspiration methods may be more effective in preserving the volumetric stability of sinus floor bone grafts throughout this period. Significantly, within the same timeframe, the rate of bone graft volume alteration was less in the removal and aspiration groups compared to the untreated group. This observation suggests that managing cysts may influence the pressure conditions of the sinus floor, thereby enhancing the durability of the bone graft. The patterns in ESBG and ABH throughout the two-year follow-up period corroborated these findings. This outcome can be elucidated by the subsequent mechanisms: APs may cause a prolonged elevation of local pressure within the sinus cavity, hence increasing the surface tension of the mucosal lining on the sinus floor. This heightened pressure may mechanically damage the graft material, thereby jeopardizing the stability of osseointegration in the grafted area [37, 38]. Throughout the 1- to 2-year follow-up period, the rate of graft volume change in all three groups gradually converged, indicating that the body systematically restores the volume stability of the sinus floor bone via bone remodeling. This process presumably entails the gradual substitution of grafted bone material with new bone, thus reinstating the structure and function of the sinus floor [39, 40]. Efficient treatment of APs may enhance their influence on the bone stability of the maxillary sinus.

This study demonstrated that PBH displayed distinct variations across the follow-up period, with a significant rise observed between T2 and T3. These fluctuations can be attributed to several variables, including the healing process of submerged implants, which may lead to alterations in PBH from the immediate postoperative period to six months post-surgery. Between 6 months and 1-year post-surgery, the majority of implants began weight-bearing, resulting in an increase in PBH. Similar results have been recorded by Fu et al. [21]. No statistically significant difference in PBH change was seen among the three groups, indicating that the impact of tiny pseudocysts on peri-implant bone may be negligible. Furthermore, MBL across all groups remained within the standard guideline set by the International Team for Implantology (ITI), defined as less than 1.5 mm during the initial year post-surgery and an increase of no more than 0.2 mm in each following year [41]. This finding clarifies two fundamental elements. This safety of the cyst treatment methodology is underscored by its little effect on the natural resorption of bone surrounding the implant, irrespective of the cyst treatment condition. Secondly, the normal distribution of load stress indicates that a uniform stress suggests that a consistent stress distribution during implant loading may be essential for preserving the stability of MBL [42, 43].

The removal or aspiration of cysts resulted in notably reduced graft resorption at the six-month mark; however, when examining the treatment groups over a two-year period, no significant differences were found in implant survival, marginal bone loss, or peri-implant bone height. This indicates that while the short-term volumetric stability achieved through cyst removal or aspiration is beneficial, it does not necessarily lead to better long-term clinical outcomes. Ultimately, the clinical results demonstrate that all three treatment strategies yield comparable outcomes, despite the initial variations in graft resorption observed in the early stages.

The cyst removal cohort demonstrated complete shrinkage of the cyst within 6 months post-surgery, with no recurrence observed throughout the 2-year follow-up. This result corresponds with the current literature, which indicated that cyst excision can effectively resolve the problem by completely eradicating the lesions, hence decreasing the inflammatory response and alleviating pressure interference [16, 18]. The complete postoperative regression of cysts in the excision group may be ascribed to the direct removal of lesions, which effectively eliminated inflammatory chemicals and restored sinus floor pressure [44, 45]. Patients presenting with tiny APs (diameter ≤ 20 mm) had comparable rates of implant success and stability of the sinus floor bone, regardless of whether they underwent removal, aspiration, or remained untreated.

This study has several notable limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, its retrospective design is inherently susceptible to selection bias and lacks the rigor of a randomized controlled trial. The absence of randomization and control groups limits the ability to establish definitive causal relationships. Secondly, while the study provides initial insights, further research is crucial to validate the treatment efficacy for antral pseudocysts (APs) during lateral sinus floor elevation (LSFE). Specifically, long-term outcomes, such as the stability of the augmented sinus bone and the potential for cyst proliferation or recurrence over time, remain unassessed and are critical areas for future investigation. Finally, a significant limitation lies in the subgroup analysis. Despite a considerable overall sample size, the examination of how specific factors—such as the histologic type of the cyst, its precise location within the sinus, and the number of cysts present—influence the outcomes lacked sufficient detail and statistical power. This insufficiency means that these potentially important variables could not be robustly evaluated, and their true clinical significance remains unclear.

Conclusions

This work illustrated that during simultaneous implant placement in LSFE, three techniques for tiny APs (diameter ≤ 20 mm)—removal, aspiration, and untreated—could attain a favorable implant success rate and durable osseointegration. The excision or aspiration of APs might diminish short-term (within 6 months) postoperative peri-implant hard tissue resorption and improve graft stability; However, it elevated surgical risks and patient pain. Long-term follow-up (ranging from 6 months to 2 years) indicated no significant effect on hard tissue in the untreated group compared to removal and aspiration groups. Thus, the results of this study indicated that systematic excision was unwarranted for small maxillary sinus pseudocysts (diameter ≤ 20 mm). Instead, suitable conservative treatment options might be chosen according to specific patient situations to streamline the surgical process, increase the implant success rate, and elevate patient satisfaction.
