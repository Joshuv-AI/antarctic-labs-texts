A parameterization of anisotropic Gaussian fields with penalized complexity priors
L. Llamazares-Elias, J. Latz, F. Lindgren

Abstract
Gaussian random fields (GFs) are fundamental tools in spatial modeling and can be represented flexibly and efficiently as solutions to stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs). The SPDEs depend on specific parameters, which enforce various field behaviors and can be estimated using Bayesian inference. However, even under in-fill asymptotics, the likelihood only provides limited insights into the covariance structure. In response, it is essential to leverage priors to achieve appropriate, meaningful covariance structures in the posterior. This study introduces a smooth, invertible parameterization of the correlation length and diffusion matrix of an anisotropic GF and constructs penalized complexity (PC) priors for the model when the parameters are constant in space. The formulated prior is weakly informative, effectively penalizing complexity by pushing the correlation range toward infinity and the anisotropy to zero.



Keywords:
Anisotropy, Bayesian, Penalized Complexity, Prior, Spatial Modelling, Stochastic Partial Differential Equations.

1
Introduction

Gaussian random fields (GFs) are widely used to model spatial phenomena
(
Banerjee et al., 2003
;
Bhatt et al., 2015
;
Wang and Zuo, 2021
;
Rue and Held, 2005
)
while accounting for the uncertainty that may arise due to measurement error, model misspecification, or incomplete information. The prevalence of GFs is due to the fact that they are well understood theoretically, verify desirable properties, and are easily characterized – they are entirely specified by their mean and covariance
(
Cramér and Leadbetter, 2004
;
Adler and Taylor, 2007
;
Rasmussen, 2003
)
.

A convenient way of representing certain GFs is as solutions to stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs). This representation allows for a physical interpretation to be assigned to the parameters of the equation. Furthermore, it allows for computationally efficient inference, prediction, and uncertainty quantification using a finite element (FEM) approximation of the field
(
Lindgren et al., 2011
;
Simpson et al., 2012
)
.

In the literature, a common choice is to model using isotropic fields. That is, the correlation of the field at two locations depends only on the distance between said locations. While this may be an appropriate assumption in some cases, in others, it is inadvisable. This limitation can be overcome by introducing additional parameters to model the anisotropy present in the field
(
Higdon et al., 1999
;
Paciorek and Schervish, 2006
;
Fuglstad et al., 2015
)
. In the following, we consider the semi-parametric estimation of the random field and its anisotropy parameters. The existing work leaves us with two significant challenges:

1.

The anisotropy parameterizations in
Higdon et al. (1999)
,
Paciorek and Schervish (2006)
, and
Fuglstad et al. (2015)
are non-identifiable as they have multiple parameter combinations for each anisotropy matrix, leading to a multi-modal likelihood, making them unsuitable as general parameterizations. Furthermore, as a consequence of the lack of identifiability, under the aforementioned parameterizations, it is not possible to recover the parameters in a continuous fashion. This is especially problematic when the model parameters are extended to be spatially varying.

2.

Given that not all parameters can be recovered under in-fill asymptotics
(
Zhang, 2004
)
, the
choice of prior distribution
on the parameters of the model may significantly impact the posterior distribution. As a result, suitable priors need to be defined.

To address these issues, we make the following contributions:

1.

An Identifiable Parameterization
: We present a smooth and invertible parameterization of the anisotropy that preserves parameter interpretability.

2.

Prior definition
: We construct penalized complexity (PC) priors
(
Simpson et al., 2014
;
Fuglstad et al., 2019
)
for the parameters in the model. An additional benefit of this construction is that it avoids overfitting by favoring simpler base models.

3.

Validation and prediction
: We conduct a simulation study that shows that PC priors outperform “non-informative” priors. We then use the derived model to study precipitation in Norway and show that the anisotropic model outperforms the isotropic one in the presence of limited information.

The outline of the work is as follows. In
Section

2
, we introduce and motivate our anisotropic model. In
Section

3
, we address Contribution
1
and also construct a transformation that renders the parameters a Gaussian vector, providing the convenience of working with Gaussian random variables. In
Section

4
, we present Contribution
2
. Next, in
Section

5
, we conduct a simulation study of the designed PC priors and compare the results with those of other possible priors on the parameters. In
Section

6
, we study the performance of the model and priors on a real data set. Finally, in
Section

7
, we synthesize the obtained results and discuss future avenues of research.

2
Modeling anisotropy with SPDEs

One of the fundamental goals of spatial statistics is inferring some quantity
u(s)
defined over a spatially continuous domain
\mathcal{D}
. Since
u
is a function, this amounts to describing a probability distribution over the space of functions to which we allow the realizations of
u
to belong. A common approach is to assume that
u
is a GF, that is, a random function
u:\mathcal{D}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that for any finite set of points
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathcal{D}
, the vector
{\bm{u}}=(u(\bm{x}_{1}),\ldots,u(\bm{x}_{n}))
is a multivariate Gaussian random variable
{\bm{u}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}},\bm{K})
, where
\bm{K}
is the covariance matrix of
{\bm{u}}
. One of the fundamental challenges of such models is computational. The size of the covariance matrix scales with the number of observations, leading to a computational cost of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
for inference. This is prohibitive for large
n
.

To overcome this, one can assume that the discretized field
{\bm{u}}
is a Gaussian Markov random field (GMRF). That is, that the precision matrix
{\bm{Q}}={\bm{K}}^{-1}
is sparse. In two dimensions, this allows for inference with a cost of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3/2})

(
Rue and Held, 2005
)
. This provides a significant speed-up as compared to the
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
cost of working with a dense covariance matrix. The question is, then, how can one specify a continuous object, the GF
u
, such that the discretized field
{\bm{u}}
is a GMRF? As shown by
Rozanov (1977)
, a stationary GF
u
verifies the Markov property if and only if the inverse of its spectrum is a positive symmetric polynomial. Equivalently, if and only if
u
solves a SPDE of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}u=\dot{{\mathcal{W}}},

(1)

where
{\mathcal{W}}
is Gaussian white noise and
\mathcal{L}
is a partial differential operator whose symbol
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{{\bm{\xi}}}})
is a positive, symmetric polynomial in
{\bm{\xi}}
.
That is,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\mathcal{L}f)(\bm{x})\overline{g(\bm{x})}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{{\bm{\xi}}}})\widehat{f}({\bm{{\bm{\xi}}}})\overline{\widehat{g}({\bm{{\bm{\xi}}}})}\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{{\bm{\xi}}}},\quad\forall f,g\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),

where
C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is the space of smooth functions with compact support in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

The above fact motivates the SPDE approach
(
Lindgren et al., 2011
;
Lindgren et al., 2022
)
. The continuous GF
u
is modeled as the solution to an SPDE of the form (
1
). In this way, we can do the modeling using continuous GFs that are
independent of any spatial discretization
and
enforcing physical properties
such as reaction, advection, diffusion, smoothing, etc., through the operator
\mathcal{L}
and at the same time, reaping the
computational benefits
of working with GMRFs.

2.1
Model formulation

In this section, we address the first main contribution. How can we introduce anisotropy into our model and parameterize it such that each parameter has an interpretable effect? We work in
2
dimensions and within the framework of the SPDE approach where, most commonly,
u
is chosen to be the solution of

(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u=\dot{{\mathcal{W}}}.

(2)

Here,
\dot{{\mathcal{W}}}
is Gaussian white noise on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, and is defined such that given
f,g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
the random measure
{\dot{{\mathcal{W}}}}(f)
verifies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\dot{{\mathcal{W}}}}(f){\dot{{\mathcal{W}}}}(g)]=\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}.

The resulting field
u
has a mean of zero and is chosen to be isotropic, ensuring uniqueness
(
Lindgren et al., 2022
, Section 2.3)
. We recall that, by definition, the field
u
is isotropic if there exists some function
r
such that, for all
\bm{x,y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,

\displaystyle K(\bm{x},\bm{y}):={\mathrm{Cov}[u(\bm{x}),u(\bm{y})]}=r(\norm{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}).

(3)

The isotropy of
u
may be an appropriate assumption in some cases. However, it is inadvisable if the correlation of the field is not equal in all spatial directions. To model this anisotropy, we will consider the model given by

(\kappa^{2}-\nabla\cdot\bm{H}\nabla)\frac{u}{\sigma_{u}}=\sqrt{4\pi}\kappa\dot{{\mathcal{W}}},

(4)

where the parameters are
\kappa,\sigma_{u}\in(0,\infty)
, which are positive and bounded away from zero, and a symmetric positive definite matrix
\bm{H}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}
with determinant
1
and eigenvalues bounded away from zero. The parameters control the length scale, marginal variance, and anisotropy, respectively.
Equation

4
is derived by the geometric deformation of a stationary isotropic field. This provides a clear physical interpretation of the anisotropy matrix
{\bm{H}}
and the parameter
\kappa
as detailed in Section
S1
.

The formulation in (
4
) preserves the advantages of the SPDE approach. Namely, representing
u
as the solution of an SPDE gives it a physical interpretation. The term
\kappa^{2}u
represents reaction whereas
-\nabla{\cdot}\bm{H}\nabla u
represents diffusion
(
Evans, 2010
;
Roques et al., 2022
)
.

Furthermore, using a finite element method,
u
can be projected onto the finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{n}
spanned by the basis functions
\left\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}\right\}
linked to a mesh
M_{n}
of the domain. This gives a sequence of Gaussian Markov random fields
u_{n}
with sparse precision matrices, which converges in distribution to
u
as the mesh becomes finer and finer. This sparsity allows for a significant speed-up in computations (Kriging, posterior simulation, likelihood evaluation, etc.)
(
Lindgren et al., 2011
;
Simpson et al., 2012
)
.

3
Parameterization

In this section, we parameterize
\bm{H}
so that the parameters convey intrinsic geometric meaning about the field
u
. We recall that
{\bm{H}}
is symmetric positive definite and has determinant
1
. Suppose for example that
\kappa
is fixed to
1
so that
\bm{\Psi}=\sqrt{\bm{H}}
. Write

\{(\bm{v},\lambda^{2}),(\bm{v}_{\perp},\lambda^{-2})\}

for the eigensystem of
\bm{H}
, where
\bm{v}:=(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\bm{v}_{\perp}=(-v_{2},v_{1})
and we can suppose
\lambda\geq 1
by reordering if
\lambda<1
. Then, as shown in
Section

S1
,
u
corresponds to deforming and rescaling the stationary field
\widetilde{u}
through

u(\bm{x})=\sigma_{u}\cdot\widetilde{u}(\bm{\Psi}\bm{x}),\quad\bm{\Psi}\bm{x}=\lambda\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{v}\right\rangle\bm{v}+\lambda^{-1}\left\langle\bm{x},\bm{v}_{\perp}\right\rangle\bm{v}_{\perp}.

The rescaling by
\sigma_{u}
corresponds to changing the variance of the field. The deformation corresponds to stretching the initial domain by a factor of
\lambda
in the direction of
\bm{v}
and contracting, also by a factor of
\lambda
, in the orthogonal direction
\bm{v}_{\perp}
. The above shows that the eigensystem of
\bm{H}
carries fundamental geometric information, motivating a parameterization of
\bm{H}
in terms of its eigensystem.

Equation (
4
) was also considered by
Fuglstad et al. (2015)
. Here, the authors defined
\bm{v}(\alpha):=(\cos(\alpha),\sin(\alpha))
and parameterized
\bm{H}
as

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{\bm{v}(\alpha)}=\gamma\bm{I}+\beta\bm{v}(\alpha)\bm{v}(\alpha)^{T}.

(5)

However, the map
\alpha\mapsto\bm{H}_{\bm{v}(\alpha)}
is not injective as
\bm{H}_{\bm{v}(\alpha)}=\bm{H}_{-\bm{v}(\alpha)}
. Because of this, it is impossible to recover the sign of
\bm{v}
, and thus, this parameterization is not injective, typically leading to an at least bimodal likelihood. Similarly, in the case of
Higdon et al. (1999)
, with their notation, using
(\phi_{x},\phi_{y})
gives the same covariance as
(-\phi_{x},-\phi_{y})
. Likewise, in the case and with the notation of
Paciorek and Schervish (2006)
, using
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
gives the same covariance as using
(-\gamma_{1},-\gamma_{2})
.

The crucial step to obtain an invertible parameterization is to consider the “half-angle” version
\widetilde{\bm{v}}
of
\bm{v}
as an eigenvector of
\bm{H}
.

Theorem 1

(parameterization)
.

Given
\bm{v}=(v_{1},v_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
define

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{v}}:=\norm{\bm{v}}\exp({i \alpha/2 }),\quad\text{ where }\alpha:=\arg(\bm{v})\in[0,2\pi).

(6)

Write
\widetilde{\bm{v}}=(\widetilde{v}_{1},\widetilde{v}_{2})
and
\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{\perp}=(-\widetilde{v}_{2},\widetilde{v}_{1})
.
Then, the following defines a smooth, invertible parameterization on the space of symmetric positive definite matrices of determinant
1
.

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp({\norm{\bm{v}} })}{\norm{\bm{v}}^{2}}\widetilde{\bm{v}}\widetilde{\bm{v}}^{T}+\frac{\exp({-\norm{\bm{v}} })}{\norm{\bm{v}}^{2}}\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{\perp}\widetilde{\bm{v}}_{\perp}^{T}=\cosh\left(\lvert\bm{v}\rvert\right)\bm{I}+\frac{\sinh\left(\lvert\bm{v}\rvert\right)}{\lvert\bm{v}\rvert}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}&v_{2}\\
v_{2}&-v_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

(7)

The link with the parameterization by
Fuglstad et al. (2015)
is the following

Proposition 1
.

Let
\bm{H}_{\bm{v}(\alpha)},\bm{H}_{\bm{v}}
be as in (
5
), (
7
), then if we set

\displaystyle\bm{v}(\alpha)=\pm{\widetilde{\bm{v}}}/{\norm{\bm{v}}},\quad\gamma=\exp({-\norm{\bm{v}} }),\quad\beta=(1-\gamma^{2})/{\gamma}.

We obtain
\bm{H}_{\bm{v}(\alpha)}=\bm{H}_{\bm{v}}
.

The idea behind the usage of the half angle version
\widetilde{\bm{v}}
of
\bm{v}
is that it avoids any issues of identifiability in the sign as
\widetilde{\bm{v}}
and
-\widetilde{\bm{v}}
do not simultaneously belong to the parameter space. Parameterization using a Cholesky decomposition of
\bm{H}
is also possible and is more readily generalized to higher dimensions. However, it is not as easily interpretable in terms of the intrinsic properties of
u
.

In
Figure

, we show the half-angle vector field together with the parameterized diffusion matrices
\bm{H}_{\bm{v}}

\bm{H}_{\bm{v}}
is represented by the ellipse centred at
\bm{v}
whose main axis is

\bm{H}_{\bm{v}}
scaled by their respective eigenvalues.

. It can also be seen how the parameterization is injective (no two ellipses are the same) and smooth (the ellipses vary smoothly with
\bm{v}
).

S(\bm{\xi}):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\exp(-2\pi i\bm{\xi}\cdot\bm{x})r(\bm{x})\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\bm{\xi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},

4
Penalized complexity priors

PC priors were originally developed by
Simpson et al. (2014)
to construct weakly informative priors while adhering to certain principles. The main idea is that one has a parametric family
of models
M_{\bm{\theta}}
with parameter
\bm{\theta}
belonging to some parameter space
E
and a base model
M_{\bm{0}}
(by convention corresponding to
\bm{\theta}=\bm{0}
).

One views
M_{\bm{0}}
as the most suitable in the absence of any information. The larger the distance
\zeta(M_{\bm{\theta}},M_{\bm{0}})
between a model
M_{\bm{\theta}}
and
M_{\bm{0}}
, the smaller the prior density for
\bm{\theta}
should be, and the decrease is set to be exponential. That is, we choose the prior distribution for
\bm{\theta}
, such that:

d(\bm{\theta}):=\zeta(M_{\bm{\theta}},M_{\bm{0}})\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}).

(11)

Here, the rate
\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}>0
of the exponential distribution serves as a hyperparameter selected by the user, which governs the model’s flexibility. A smaller value of
\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}
increases the model’s flexibility, allowing for greater deviations of
M_{\bm{\theta}}
from the base model
M_{\bm{0}}
. Conversely, a larger value of
\lambda_{\bm{\theta}}
imposes stricter penalties on these deviations, thereby reducing the model’s flexibility. In the previously cited
Simpson et al. (2014)
, this notion of “distance” was taken to be

\eta(M_{\bm{\theta}},M_{\bm{0}}):=\sqrt{2\mathrm{KLD}(M_{\bm{\theta}}||M_{0})},

where
\mathrm{KLD}
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence

\text{KLD}(M_{\bm{\theta}}||M_{0}):=\int_{E}\log\quantity(\frac{\d M_{\bm{\theta}}}{\d M_0 })\,\mathrm{d}M_{\bm{\theta}}.

One possible complication of using the KL divergence to define the distance is that the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\frac{\,\mathrm{d}M_{\bm{\theta}}}{\,\mathrm{d}M_{\bm{0}}}
does not exist. In fact, in the case of our model, if the variances of
M_{\bm{\theta}}
and
M_{\bm{0}}
are equal, for any possible base model
u_{\bm{0}}
and
\bm{\theta}\neq\bm{0}
the measures
M_{\bm{\theta}}
and
M_{\bm{0}}
are singular. That is, with the usual convention,

\text{KLD}(M_{\bm{\theta}}||M_{\bm{0}})=\infty.

(12)

The proof of this fact is given in
Section

S3.1
.
Equation (
12
) makes an exact adherence to the previous steps impossible. As a result, we will merely adopt the principles of the PC prior construction (exponential penalization of complexity) while altering how we measure complexity
between models. The idea of modifying the metric used to measure complexity has also been explored in different settings, such as in
Bolin et al. (2023)
;
Uribe et al. (2021)
, where a Wasserstein distance was used. The possibility of using the Wasserstein distance to measure the complexity of the model (
4
) was also considered. However, the Wasserstein distance is bounded in this setting (see
Section

S3.3
in the supplementary material) and, as a result, was discarded. Thus, one of the main challenges is to find a computationally feasible distance that captures relevant information about our model. To this aim, we give the following definition.

Definition 1
.

Given two sufficiently regular models
u_{A}\sim M_{A},u_{B}\sim M_{B}
, with respective spectral densities
S_{A},S_{B}
and variances
\sigma_{A},\sigma_{B}
, we define the pseudometric

D_{2}(M_{A},M_{B}):=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\norm{2\pi\bm{\xi}}^{4}\left(\frac{S_{A}(\bm{\xi})}{\sigma_{A}^{2}}-\frac{S_{B}(\bm{\xi})}{\sigma_{B}^{2}}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(13)

The above definition uses the rescaled spectral densities of each field to define a Sobolev seminorm on the difference of the correlations of
M_{A}
and
M_{B}
. Sobolev norms are a common way to measure the distance between two functions due to their sensitivity to smoothness and shape. In this case, it measures the difference in speed of oscillation of the normalized covariance up to order
2
.

Other measures of complexity between models are also possible, and we give such an example in Appendix
S3
. If a particular application suggests the clear use of a specific distance, that distance should be used instead.

In this work, we considered many other common distances and showed that they were unsuitable. As we already mentioned, the KL divergence between two different models is infinite. Furthermore, the Wasserstein distance, the
L^{2}
distance, and the Hellinger distance are bounded, making them unsuitable for the exponential penalization of PC priors. Sobolev seminorms of integer order different from
2
can also be shown to be unsuitable for this model with this smoothness. For different smoothneess and dimension than the ones considered here, the distance can be adapted by modifying the weight used to make the integrand finite. The weight could also be adapted for models whose spectral density has a singularity at the origin of order
\alpha
by multiplying by a factor
\norm{\b{\xi}}^{\alpha}(1+\norm{\b{\xi}})^{-\alpha}
.

Finally, we note that the seminorm distinguishes different models (it defines a norm on our family of models) in the following sense. Write
M_{\kappa,\bm{v}}
for the model given by SPDE (
4
) with parameters
(\kappa,\bm{v})
and with variance fixed to
\sigma_{u}=1
. Due to the rescaling of the spectral densities in
Definition

1
, the choice of
\sigma_{u}
does not affect the distance between models and can be set to any other positive value. Then,

\displaystyle D_{2}(M_{\kappa_{A},\bm{v}_{A}},M_{\kappa_{B},\bm{v}_{B}})

\displaystyle=0\iff(\kappa_{A},{\bm{v}}_{A})=(\kappa_{B},{\bm{v}}_{B}).

We give a more detailed discussion in Section
S3
of the Appendix.

We define the base model as the limit in distribution of
M_{\kappa,\bm{v}}
as
\kappa,\bm{v}
go to zero

\displaystyle{M}_{\bm{0}}:=\lim_{(\kappa_{0},\bm{v}_{0})\to\bm{0}}{M}_{\kappa_{0},\bm{v}_{0}}=\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathbf{1}\otimes\mathbf{1}\right),

where

That is,

To reflect the dependency of this distance on the parameters, we use the notation

(14)

The exponential penalization in (

Fuglstad et al. (2019)
circumvented this issue by working iteratively, fixing one parameter while allowing the other to vary, building PC priors for each parameter, and then multiplying them together to obtain a joint prior. However, we prefer to work jointly from the start. This approach is made possible by the structure of

6.3
Model performance

To assess the performance of the models, we calculate various scores for each model. We recall that, given a (predictive) distribution
P
and an (observed) point
{y}
, a score is a function
S({P},{y})
that measures how closely the prediction
{P}
matches the observation
{y}
. If
{y}
follows the distribution
{Q}
, then the expected score is

\displaystyle S({P},{Q}):=\mathbb{E}_{{Q}}[S({P},{y})].

The score should be minimized when the true distribution
{Q}
matches the predictive distribution
{P}
. In this case, the score is said to be
proper
. It is
strictly proper
if it is minimized if and only if
{P}={Q}
. We consider the
squared error
(
\mathrm{SE}
),
continuous ranked probability score
(
\mathrm{CRPS}
, and
Dawid-Sebastiani score
(
\mathrm{DSS}
). These are defined in Appendix
Section

S7.1
.

Given a sample sample
\bm{y}=(y_{1},...,y_{n})
, a vector of predictive distributions
\bm{F}=(F_{1},...,F_{n})
and a score
S
, the
mean score
is defined as

\displaystyle\overline{S}(\bm{F},\bm{y}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S(F_{i},y_{i}).

By the linearity of the expectations, if
S
is a (strictly) proper scoring rule, then so is
\overline{S}
.

In this section, we calculate the mean scores

\displaystyle\mathrm{RMSE}:=\quantity(\overline{SE}(\bm{F},\bm{y}))^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\overline{\mathrm{CRPS}}(\bm{F},\bm{y}),\quad\overline{\mathrm{DSS}}(\bm{F},\bm{y}),

where

is the observation.

7
Discussion

In this study, we constructed a smooth, invertible, and geometrically interpretable parameterization for a 2D anisotropic spatial Gaussian model. We developed penalized complexity (PC) priors for these parameters, defining model complexity as a Sobolev seminorm of the correlation function. This distance was calculated in a closed form using the spectral density.

Our performance comparison of the PC prior against other priors demonstrated its effectiveness in penalizing model complexity. We found that priors designed to match the quantiles of the PC prior yielded similar results. Both these priors significantly outperformed the other non-informative priors considered, highlighting the necessity of incorporating penalization in prior information to prevent overfitting.

Applying the anisotropic model to a real dataset of precipitation in Southern Norway, we observed that the anisotropic model outperformed the isotropic model when the number of observations was small, with the PC prior slightly outperforming the other choices. However, with a larger number of observations, the isotropic model performed similarly to the anisotropic models. These results indicate that the anisotropic model is more informative when the data is scarce, but as the number of observations increases, the isotropic model becomes more competitive. The results also suggest that a non-stationary model could be better suited to capture spatially varying patterns in the data.

In conclusion, we advocate for the use of informative priors for the anisotropy parameters in spatial Gaussian models. The PC prior is highly effective, but other priors designed to match desired quantiles can also be effective and are simpler to construct.

Finally, we note that while the Matérn class allows for flexible local behavior, it restricts the field to exponential tail decay. Recent work by
Ma and Bhadra (2023)
introduces the Confluent Hypergeometric (CH) class, which generalizes the Matérn family to allow for polynomial tail decay. While the Matérn class is a limiting case of the CH class, the PC framework presented here relies on the existence of a standard spectral density, which is not guaranteed for the CH class in regimes of strong long-range dependence (
\alpha\leq d/2
) where the spectrum exhibits a singularity at the origin. Extending PC priors to such heavy-tailed classes remains a promising avenue for future research, likely requiring a complexity distance adapted to handle such singularities, for instance, via weighted spectral norms.

Looking forward, we aim to extend this study to the non-stationary setting where the model parameters vary spatially. This presents a challenge as there is no agreed-upon definition of correlation function or spectral density, necessitating a different definition of complexity. We are also interested in extending the parameterization to higher dimensions. The current construction relies on a “half-angle” parameterization of the anisotropy vector, which is not easily extendable to higher dimensions. Finally, we are interested in extending the construction to different orders of regularity.

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\quantity(K_A+K_B-2 \sqrt{K_AK_B})=\sum_{{\bm{j}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\absolutevalue{ \left[\sqrt{K}-\sqrt{K_0}\right]_\b{jj}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{j}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\quantity(\frac{\sqrt{4 \pi}\kappa_A \sigma_A }{\kappa_A ^2+ 4 \pi^2\bm{\xi}_\b{j}^T \bm{H}_{\bm{v}_A}\bm{\xi}_\b{j}}-\frac{\sqrt{4 \pi}\kappa_B \sigma_B }{\kappa_B ^2+ 4 \pi^2\bm{\xi}_\b{j}^T \bm{H}_{\bm{v}_B}\bm{\xi}_\b{j}})^{2}.

Making the side-length
{\bm{T}}
of the torus go to infinity heuristically shows that, by definition of
\bm{\xi}_{\bm{j}}
our discrete sum becomes an integral with

\displaystyle W_{2}(M_{A},M_{B})=\frac{\mathrm{vol}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\quantity(\frac{\kappa_A }{\kappa_A ^2+ \bm{\xi}^T \bm{H}_{\bm{v}_A}\bm{\xi}}-\frac{\kappa_B}{\kappa_B^2+ \bm{\xi}^T \bm{H}_{\bm{v}_B}\bm{\xi} })^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}+O(1).

The Wasserstein distance between

scales as
\mathrm{vol}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
times the Hellinger distance of the spectral densities of

. Thus, a reasonable option could be to define the scaling as the distance

\displaystyle D_{W_{2}}(M_{A},M_{B}):=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{W_{2}(M_{A},M_{B})}{\mathrm{vol}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})}=\mathrm{Hell}(S_{A},S_{B}).

However, a calculation in this simplified case shows that this will be bounded. For example, in the case where, as in the base model,
\bm{v}_{A}=\bm{v}_{B}=\bm{0}
, and
\sigma_{A}=\sigma_{B}=1
, we have the following.

\displaystyle D_{W_{2}}(u_{A},u_{B})=2\quantity(1-\frac{\kappa_A\kappa_B \left(\log\left(\kappa_A^2\right)-\log\left(\kappa_B ^2\right)\right)}{\kappa_A^2-\kappa_B ^2}).

The above expression takes a maximum of
2
. This makes putting an exponential distribution on the Wasserstein distance impossible, and as a result, the Wasserstein distance cannot be used to define PC priors for our model (

S3.4
Sobolev distance between fields

Given a stationary field

Write

S5

We show how to write

\mathbf{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{3})

Definition 2
.

Given a random variable
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

\displaystyle\varphi(\bm{X},\bm{x}):=\varphi_{\bm{X}}(\bm{x}):=(\mathbb{P}[X_{1}\leq x_{1}],\mathbb{P}[X_{2}\leq x_{2}|X_{1}=x_{1}]).

Observation 3
.

In the case where

\displaystyle\varphi_{\bm{X}}(\bm{x})=(F_{X_{1}}(x_{1}),F_{X_{2}}(x_{2})).

By construction

1

\bm{X}

In this section, we expand on the simulation study of
Section

5
. We discuss the details of the simulation of the SPDE, compare the priors, and present some additional results.

S6.1
Sampling from the SPDE

To simulate the Gaussian field resulting from (
4
), we use the
fmesher
package. This package uses a FEM method to approximate the precision matrix of
u
. That is, we approximate
u
to be in the Hilbert space
H
spanned by our finite element basis
\left\{\phi_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
and impose

\displaystyle\quantity[\br{\phi_i, \Ll u}, i\in\{1,...,n\}]=\quantity[\br{\phi_i, \kappa\dot{\Ww}}, i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}]

(47)

The condition that
u\in H
leaves us with

\displaystyle u(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{i}(\bm{x})u_{i},

(48)

where one can show that the vector of weights
\bm{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
is Gaussian. Let us write
\bm{Q}_{u}
for its precision matrix,
\bm{u}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{Q}_{u}^{-1})
. Then, from
(
48
), knowing
\bm{Q}_{u}
determines
\bm{u}
. The value of
\bm{Q}_{u}
is determined in turn by (
47
), as substituting in (
48
) and applying integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\bm{L}\bm{u}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{C}_{\kappa}),

(49)

where

\displaystyle\bm{L}=\bm{C}_{\kappa}+\bm{G}_{\bm{H}},\quad[\bm{C}_{\kappa}]_{ij}=\left\langle\phi_{i},\kappa^{2}\phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})},\quad[\bm{G}_{\bm{H}}]_{ij}=\left\langle\nabla\phi_{i},\bm{H}\nabla\phi_{j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}.

(50)

From (
49
) we deduce that the precision
Q
of the weight vector
w
is

\displaystyle\bm{Q}_{u}=(\bm{C}_{\kappa}+\bm{G}_{\bm{H}})^{\dagger}\bm{C}_{\kappa}^{-1}(\bm{C}_{\kappa}+\bm{G}_{\bm{H}})=\bm{C}_{\kappa}+2\bm{G}_{\bm{H}}+\bm{G}_{\bm{H}}\bm{C}_{\kappa}^{-1}\bm{G}_{\bm{H}},

(51)

where we used that since
\bm{H}
is symmetric, and we are considering real-valued functions,
\bm{C}_{\kappa},\bm{G}_{\bm{H}}
are symmetric.

The finite element basis is chosen so that
\bm{C}_{\kappa},\bm{G}_{\bm{H}}
are sparse. Since
\bm{C}_{\kappa}
is not sparse, it is approximated by the mass lumped version

[\widetilde{\bm{C}}_{\kappa}]_{ij}:=\delta_{ij}\sum_{k=1}^{n}[\bm{C}_{\kappa}]_{ik}.

After this approximation we obtain a sparse precision
\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{u}
which allows us to sample efficiently from
\bm{u}
(or rather, its approximation
\widetilde{u}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{u})
) by using a Cholesky decomposition
\widetilde{\bm{Q}}_{u}=\bm{L}\bm{L}^{T}
and solving
\bm{L}^{T}z=u
where
z\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathbf{I})
.

It remains to discuss how the integrals defining
\bm{C}_{\kappa},\bm{G}_{\bm{H}}
are calculated. Let
{\mathcal{T}}
be the mesh of the domain. For each triangle
T\in{\mathcal{T}}
we denote the average value of
\kappa,\bm{H}
on the nodes of
T
as
\kappa^{2}(T),\bm{H}(T)
and approximate the integrals in (
50
) by

[\bm{C}_{\kappa}]_{ij}\approx\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{T}}}\kappa^{2}(T)\int_{T}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},\quad[\bm{G}_{\bm{H}}]_{ij}\approx\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\in{\mathcal{T}}\\
k,l=1,2\end{subarray}}[\bm{H}(T)]_{kl}\int_{T}\partial_{k}\phi_{i}\partial_{l}\phi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(52)

Finally, we take
\phi_{i}
to be piecewise linear, equal to
1
on node
i
and equal to
0
on every other node. An explicit formula for the integrals in (
52
) can be found in
Lindgren et al. (2011, Appendix A2)
.

S6.3
Posterior sampling

Returning to the simulation, our parameters are
\bm{\theta}=(\log\kappa,\bm{v},\log(\sigma_u),\log(\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}))\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
. Bayes formula gives for any prior
\pi({\bm{\theta}})
on
\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle\pi(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(\bm{\theta},\bm{y})}{\pi(\bm{y})}\frac{\pi\left(\bm{u}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y}\right)}{\pi\left(\bm{u}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y}\right)}=\frac{\pi(\bm{\theta})\pi(\bm{u}|\bm{\theta})\pi(\bm{y}|\bm{u},\bm{\theta})}{\pi(\bm{y})\pi\left(\bm{u}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y}\right)}.

(54)

We note that the right-hand side of (
55

\bm{u}
. However, the resulting expression is independent of the value of
\bm{u}
chosen.
As a result, the log posterior
\ell(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})
is up to an additive constant given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})=\ell(\bm{\theta})+\ell(\bm{u}|\bm{\theta})+\ell(\bm{y}|\bm{u},\bm{\theta})-\ell(\bm{u}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y})

(55)

Observation 4
.

The maximum of the right-hand side of (
55
) is independent of
\bm{u}
. However, in the case where
\ell(\bm{u}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y})
were not known, the value of
\bm{u}
would affect the result. If
\bm{u}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y}
were approximated to be Gaussian, it would make the most sense to take
\bm{u}
as the mode
\bm{m_{u}}
of this Gaussian approximation.

In our case, all terms in (
55
) are known exactly. The log prior
\ell(\bm{\theta})
is determined by (
20
) and the remaining terms are given by

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{u}|\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\log\norm{\bm{Q}_u}-\norm{\bm{u}-\bm{m}_u}_{\bm{Q}_u}^2-n\log(2\pi))

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{y}|\bm{u},\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\log\norm{\bm{Q}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}} -\norm{\bm{y}-\bm{A}{\bm{u}}}_{\bm{Q}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}}^2-m\log(2\pi))

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{u}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\log\norm{\bm{Q}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta} }} -\norm{\bm{u}- \bm{m}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}}_{\bm{Q}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta} }}^2-n\log(2\pi)),

where we defined
\norm{\bm{x}}_{\bm{Q}}^{2}:=\bm{x}^{T}\bm{Q}\bm{x}
and

(56)

Maximizing the expression in (

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}:=\arg\max_{\bm{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\ell(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})=\arg\max_{\bm{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\widetilde{\ell}(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y}).

Using this, we do the following simulations. We fix as our domain the square

For the importance sampling, we use

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\quantity[f(\bm{\theta})|\bm{y}^{(j)}]\approx\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{IS}}\quantity[f(\bm{\theta})|\bm{y}^{(j)}]:=\sum_{s=1}^{S}w\quantity(\bm{\theta}^{(s)})f(\bm{\theta}^{(s)}).

where the first equality is the definition of the Kullback-Leibler divergence and the first and second approximations are the self-normalized importance sampling step (

Precipitation study details

S7.1
Score definitions

The scores considered in
Section

6
are

•

Given a distribution
\,\mathrm{d}F
and an observation
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
the
squared error
(SE) is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{SE}(F,x)

\displaystyle:=\left(x-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t\,\mathrm{d}F(t)\right)^{2}.

The expression above defines a proper scoring rule (its value is minimized when
x
follows the predictive distribution). However, it is not strictly proper (the minimum in
F
is not unique).

•

Given a CDF
F
and an observation
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the
continuous ranked probability score
(CRPS) is defined as
(
Gneiting and Raftery, 2007
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{CRPS}(F,x)

\displaystyle:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}(F(t)-\bm{1}\{x\leq t\})^{2}dt.

The CRPS is a strictly proper scoring rule on distributions with finite expectations.

•

Given a random variable
\bm{X}
with mean
\bm{\mu}
and precision
\bm{Q}
, the
Dawid-Sebastiani score
(DSS) is defined as
(
Dawid and Sebastiani, 1999
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{DSS}(\bm{X};\bm{x})

\displaystyle:=-\log(\norm{\bm{Q}} )+(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu})^{T}\bm{Q}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}).

The DSS is similar to a log-Gaussian density score and is a strictly proper scoring rule for Gaussian random variables. Still, it also defines a proper score for non-Gaussian random variables.

S7.2
Calculations

In this section, we show how to calculate scores of
Section

S7.1
. For what follows, we first define the leave-one-out mean of
y_{i}
having observed all locations except
y_{i}

\displaystyle m_{y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i}}

\displaystyle:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{5}}\quantity( \int_{\R} y_i \pi(y_{i}|\bm{y_{-i}},\bm{\theta})\d y_{i})\pi(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{5}}m_{y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}\pi(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}.

(59)

The expression of
m_{y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i}}
is approximately that of
\mathbb{E}\left[y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i}\right]
, with the exception that the integral in (
59
) is against
\pi(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})
as opposed to
\pi(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y}_{-i})
. This is a common approach to calculating the LOO predictions as it avoids the need to approximate
\pi(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y}_{-i})
for each
i
. The expression is justified by the fact that the posterior distribution of
\bm{\theta}
does not depend much on any single observation. That is,
\pi(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})\approx\pi(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y}_{-i})
. Similarly, we will need the posterior leave out one variance

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i}}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{5}}\sigma^{2}_{y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}\pi(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta},

(60)

where
\sigma^{2}_{y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}
is the posterior variance of
y_{i}
having observed all locations except
y_{i}
. This will be used later in the computations.

Because of the independence of
\bm{u},\bm{\beta}
from
\bm{\theta}
we have that

\displaystyle\left.\bm{u}_{\bm{\beta}}\right|\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{Q}_{\bm{u}_{\bm{\beta}}}^{-1}),\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{Q}_{\bm{u}_{\bm{\beta}}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{Q}_{\bm{\beta}}&\bm{0}_{2\times n}\\
\\
\bm{0}_{n\times 2}&\bm{Q}_{u}\end{bmatrix}.

We can then calculate
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
to maximize the (unnormalized) log-posterior as in (
55
)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\ell}(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})=\ell(\bm{\theta})+\ell(\bm{u}_{\beta}|\bm{\theta})+\ell(\bm{y}|\bm{u}_{\beta},\bm{\theta})-\ell(\bm{u}_{\beta}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y}).

(61)

To calculate the RMSE, CRPS, and DSS, it is sufficient to calculate the posterior mean and variance of
y_{i}
given
\bm{y}_{-i}
and
\bm{\theta}
. We show how to do this efficiently.

Due to the independence of
\bm{u}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}
and of
\varepsilon_{i}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}_{-i}
knowing
\bm{\theta}
, we deduce that
\bm{y}_{-i}=\bm{A}_{-i}\bm{u}+\varepsilon_{-i}
and
\varepsilon_{i}
are independent knowing
\theta
. As a result,

\displaystyle y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle=(\bm{A}_{i}\bm{u})|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}+\varepsilon_{i}|\bm{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{A}_{i}\bm{m}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}},\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}+\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2})

(62)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(m_{y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}},\sigma_{y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}^{2}),

where
\bm{A}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1\times N}
is the
i
-th row of
\bm{A}
. Furthermore, we have that, see (
56
)

\displaystyle\bm{Q}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\bm{Q}_{u}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\bm{A}_{-i}^{T}\bm{A}_{-i}=\bm{Q}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-2}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}\bm{A}_{i}

(63)

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{-2}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{-i}^{T}\bm{y}_{-i},

where we wrote
\bm{A}_{-i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n-1\times N}
for the matrix
\bm{A}
with the
i
-th row removed. To avoid calculating an inverse for each
i
, we can calculate
\bm{\Sigma}_{u|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}
once and then use the rank
1
correction provided by the Sherman-Morrison formula to obtain

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}+\frac{\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}}{\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2}-\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}}.

(64)

Using (
64
) in (
62
) and writing
V_{i}:=\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}
and
q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}:=\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{-2}
,
gives

\displaystyle\sigma_{y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}^{2}

\displaystyle=V_{i}+\frac{V_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2}-V_{i}}+\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2}=\frac{V_{i}}{1-q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}}+\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2}}{1-q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}}.

(65)

Furthermore, for the mean, we have from (
63
) that

\displaystyle m_{y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{-i}^{T}\bm{y}_{-i}=q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{y}-q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}{y}_{i}

(66)

Let us write

\displaystyle\eta_{i}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{A}_{i}\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}\right]=\bm{A}_{i}\bm{m}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}=q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{y},

Then, using the rank
1
correction in (
64
) we obtain for the first term in (
66
) that

\displaystyle q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{y}

\displaystyle=q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{y}+q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{A}_{i}\frac{\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}}{\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2}-\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{y}

(67)

\displaystyle=\eta_{i}+\frac{V_{i}}{\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2}-V_{i}}\eta_{i}=\frac{\eta_{i}}{1-q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}}.

Whereas, for the second term in (
66
), we have that

\displaystyle q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}{y}_{i}

\displaystyle=q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}{y}_{i}+q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{A}_{i}\frac{\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}}{\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2}-\bm{A}_{i}\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y},\bm{\theta}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}}\bm{A}_{i}^{T}{y}_{i}

(68)

\displaystyle=q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}y_{i}+q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\frac{V_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{2}-V_{i}}y_{i}=q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}y_{i}\quantity(1+ q_{\bm{\varepsilon}} \frac{V_i}{1-q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_i})=\frac{q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}y_{i}}{1-q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}}

Using (
67
) and (
68
) in (
66
) we obtain

\displaystyle m_{y_{i}|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta_{i}}{1-q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}}-\frac{q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}y_{i}}{1-q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}}=\frac{\eta_{i}-q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}y_{i}}{1-q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}}=y_{i}+\frac{\eta_{i}-y_{i}}{1-q_{\bm{\varepsilon}}V_{i}}.

(69)

The term
V_{i}
can be calculated efficiently using a Takahashi recursion on the Cholesky factor of the posterior precision
\bm{Q}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y,\theta}}
without the need to calculate a dense matrix inverse, as implemented by
inla.qinv
. To calculate
\eta_{i}
, we use a matrix-vector solve.

Next, since the expression of
\pi(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y}_{i})
is known up to a normalizing constant, we can use importance sampling and Riemann integration together with (
65
), (
69
) to calculate (
59
).
To estimate the CRPS, we use importance sampling and the expression for the posterior predictive distribution (
59
). We have

\displaystyle F_{i}(t):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{5}}\quantity(\int_{-\infty}^t \pi(y_i | \bm{y}_{-i}, \bm{\theta})\d y_i)p(\bm{\theta}|\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Phi\quantity(\frac{t-m_{y_i|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta}_j}}{\sigma_{y_i|\bm{y}_{-i},\bm{\theta_j }}})w_{j},

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of a standard Gaussian variable, and
\bm{\theta}_{j},w_{j}
are the importance samples and smoothed self-normalized weights.
Using this expression and the fact that there is an exact expression for the CRPS of a Gaussian mixture
(
Grimit et al., 2006
)
, we obtain the CRPS.
