The loss of rural vital places: A case study using the social determinants of health framework
Authors: Danielle Rhubart, Jennifer Kowalkowski, Hazel Velasco Palacios, Kristina Brant
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealthy food sources, recreational facilities, and social spaces serve as vital places for supporting health. Less is known about what happens when vital places are lost, especially in rural contexts that have experienced disinvestment and population decline. In this paper we use a case study of a small rural community in Pennsylvania that lost a substantial share of its vital places over a short period of time to explore the experiences of local residents. Using qualitative interviews with 26 local residents we show that the loss of vital places, which are part of the neighborhood and built environment, intersected with other social determinants of health with potential consequences for health and well-being. This was often because vital places were essential (i.e., few or no alternatives existed) and multifunctional (i.e., supporting health through multiple behavioral and social mechanisms). Examining experiences of local residents following the loss of vital places through a social determinants of health lens helps to identify consequences and inform adaptation strategies. We also use the findings to refine the vital places framework for rural contexts.Keywords: Vital places, Social determinants of health, Rural

Introduction

Within a community, vital places represent nearby destinations that are important to residents, are frequently used, and play a critical role in promoting health through behavioral and social mechanisms (Walton, 2014). Moreover, these places, characterized by their accessibility and relevance to daily life, serve as arenas where health-promoting actions and activities occur (Walton, 2014, 1). Walton (2014) identifies three types of vital places: healthy food sources, recreational facilities, and social spaces. Healthy food sources include markets and stores that provide access to fresh, nutritious food, which is essential for preventing chronic diseases and promoting overall health (Larson et al., 2009; Morland and Evenson, 2009). Recreational facilities, such as parks and sports centers, offer opportunities for physical activities which are important for maintaining physical health and enhancing psychological wellness (Berke et al., 2007; Dahmann et al., 2010). Lastly, social spaces like community centers and cafes provide venues for social engagement and where social networks can flourish. These interactions facilitate social support, protect mental health, and buffer against stress (Cohen, 2004; Uchino, 2006). By their nature, vital places are essential for encouraging healthy eating habits, fostering regular physical activity, and facilitating social interaction (Walton, 2014). The framework developed by Walton (2014) illuminates how vital places enable residents to engage in numerous health-related behaviors.

While substantial research has highlighted the importance of vital places within urban settings (Adams & Tiesdell, 2007; Galdini et al., 2023; Henderson & Frelke, 2000; Walton, 2014), less attention has focused on rural areas, many of which have experienced disinvestment and population decline. In this paper, we present a case study of a rural community in Pennsylvania that experienced substantial losses in its vital places over a short period of time. We find that vital places, which are part of the neighborhood and built environment, intersected with other social determinants of health (SDoH), with significant consequences on the health and well-being of both individual residents and the wider community. Examining the experiences of local residents through an SDoH lens following the loss of vital places can be useful in identifying consequences and informing adaptation strategies. It also builds on Walton’s (2014) original work to emphasize how essential vital places are to not only health but also the other social determinants of health in rural areas. We close with a discussion of the implications of these findings for future research and local and state leaders.

The context of rural vital places

As the concept of vital places was originally developed in an urban context, its application in rural settings requires attention to the unique challenges and opportunities these areas face. First, in rural communities, there are often fewer and more dispersed vital places (Rhubart et al., 2022). While lower population density partly explains this disparity, persistent population decline and rural disinvestment have also contributed to this trend. Formal recreation facilities may be the least available vital place in rural areas. Rural areas are significantly less likely to have physical fitness facilities, recreation clubs, dance studios, and public golf courses (Powell et al., 2006). This is problematic given that recreational facilities are significantly associated with physical activity in rural contexts (Abildso et al., 2021). Because vital places are more dispersed, residents must travel further distances to reach them, while also having less access to public transportation. In addition, closure of even a single vital place can ripple through the community, creating a desert for nutritious foods, physical activity, or social connection.

Second, rural vital places often serve multiple purposes. For example, a grocery store or park may also act as a stage for social interaction where neighbors can meet, exchange greetings, and share news or informational support. For many rural residents, these interactions in familiar settings contribute to their sense of security and belonging (Flora and Flora, 2003). Consequently, losing just one vital place could impact not only access to nutritious foods or physical activity but also social connection. In addition, loss could be felt not just in terms of physical absence – as few or no alternatives exist - but as a disruption to a community’s social fabric.

The loss of vital places in recent decades is a common theme for many rural communities, where long-standing population decline, disinvestment, and/or persistent poverty have made such places less economically viable (Brown and Shafft, 2019). Policies that prioritize social and economic opportunities in urban areas at the expense of rural areas result in young and working-age adults leaving rural and small-town America (Parsons, 2022). This hallowing of the middle exacerbates opportunities in strengthening or expanding vital places for rural communities. The disappearance of communal hubs could lead to not only fewer tangible resources to support health but also a fragmentation of social networks and a weakening of communal bonds that had been nurtured over generations. The subsequent lack of hubs could then further motivate outmigration and further disincentivize new-comers from moving in, leading to a cycle of decline—both in physical and social infrastructure and in the attitudes and feelings of those residents who stay behind (Tomaney et al., 2023; MacKinnon et al., 2024). Given the declining availability of vital places in many rural contexts, it is important to explore the experiences of residents who experience these losses, while remaining cognizant of the unique applications of the vital places concept to rural contexts.

Vital places and social determinants of health

The social determinants of health (SDoH) framework is used by public health agencies to understand and improve the social environments that shape health. The United States Department of Health and Human Services (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, n.d.) identifies five SDoH domains: healthcare access and quality, education access and quality, social and community context, economic stability, and neighborhood and built environment. Substantial research has examined residents’ experiences following the loss of elements within the first SDoH domain, like hospitals and other health care facilities (Kozhimannil et al., 2018; McCarthy et al., 2021; Adler et al., 2016; Baah et al., 2019; Brown and Homan, 2023). Less research has examined such experiences following the loss of vital places – a critical piece of the neighborhood and built environment. Given the presence of fewer vital places and their multifunctionality in rural areas, the loss of such places could also shape or be shaped by changes in the other non-healthcare related SDoH. Therefore, we use this opportunity to examine how rural residents experience the loss of vital places through the lens of these four non-healthcare related SDoH. In doing so, we not only affirm Walton’s (2014) assertion of the importance of vital places but also demonstrate that their impacts—and thus their losses—are felt acutely across many dimensions of residents’ social determinants of health. Indeed, we argue that individual vital places may be even more essential in rural areas than in non-rural areas, since fewer such places exist and those that do exist serve multiple functions across various social determinants of health.

Data & methods

This manuscript derives from a qualitative study approved by the Pennsylvania State University Institutional Review Board and conducted in conjunction with the launch of a mobile clinic by the Pennsylvania State University College of Medicine. The mobile clinic began by primarily visiting one rural Pennsylvania township that had recently lost its primary care clinic. The study aimed to assess health care access and other needs in this initial target community to inform the clinic’s work. However, while collecting and analyzing data, it became apparent that the loss of vital places merited further attention.

Study site description

We draw on interviews with residents of the target community first served by the mobile clinic. We mask the name of the township to preserve participant confidentiality, as it houses fewer than 1700 residents (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023). The township is defined as “rural” by the Center for Rural Pennsylvania, a state legislative agency. The Center considers counties and municipalities “rural” when their population density is less than the state average (291 people per square mile); the township’s population density is 20 people per square mile.

Demographically, the township looks much like an average rural community in Pennsylvania: 97 % of residents are white, the median age is 48, the median household income is $63,500, and the poverty rate is 9 % (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023). However, the demographics of this community differ when compared to the rural United States: 75 % white (Economic Research Center, 2024), median age of 42 years (Rural Health Information Hub, 2024a), median household income of $59,000 (Rural Health Information Hub, 2024b), and 15.4 % poverty rate (Economic Research Center, 2024). The income and poverty levels of this rural community would not suggest that the township is in great distress. While opportunities for local employment have dwindled, residents have adapted by making longer commutes to a university town approximately 30 miles away. The opportunity to do so may have buffered the economic impacts following the loss of vital places.

Yet, the township’s physical infrastructure tells a different story—one of decline. The township’s population has slowly decreased over the last three decades. Overall population decline, combined with retirements and accidents, have shuttered community institutions in recent years. In 2020, the grocery store caught fire and its owners could not financially justify rebuilding. The grocery store had housed a fast-food franchise, a bank, and a hardware store; these ancillary businesses could not reopen without reconstruction. In 2021, the pharmacy closed due to declining profits, and later that year, the primary care clinic closed following the local physician’s retirement. Since then, one of the two remaining restaurants and a church closed too. These institutions constituted many of the township’s vital places, providing an opportunity to explore the impact of their closures on residents.

Participant recruitment

Participants were recruited through purposive and convenience sampling methods. The mobile clinic began visiting the study site in May 2022, and study team members accompanied the clinic on biweekly visits throughout 2022. Community members who visited the clinic were provided information about the study. Interested individuals completed the written consent process in person. Study information was also shared on a community Facebook group. Participants recruited through Facebook completed the consent process online via REDCap. Finally, certain community members were contacted due to their specific roles in the community (e.g., local EMS employees and former clinic employees). These participants also provided consent via REDCap. Three of this manuscript’s authors conducted recruitment and the consent process.

In total, 31 people participated, but this manuscript draws on a subset of 26 participants who identified as residing in the study site. Those excluded from analysis were recruited at clinic events in nearby communities. All participants were white, and most (85 %) were women. Participants ranged in age from 27 to 90 years, with a mean of 57. Most participants (88 %) had lived in the study site for most of their lives, either from birth or after getting married to a local resident.

Data collection

After consenting, participants were called to set up a time for an interview. Almost all interviews were conducted via phone. One interview was conducted in person in a private office. The interviews were conducted by four study team members. All interviewers were faculty members, postdoctoral fellows, or PhD students and had training and experience conducting qualitative research. Interviewers did not have established relationships with participants, and participants did not know the backgrounds of interviewers. Participants were reminded they could skip any questions they did not wish to answer.

Interviews were grounded in a phenomenological approach—a participant-oriented style of interviewing which allows participants to express their lived experience as they see fit (Alase, 2017). By following participants’ narratives, this approach seeks “to capture as closely as possible the way in which the phenomenon is experienced within the context in which the experience takes place” (Giorgi and Giorgi, 2003, 27). Interviews prompted participants to describe their communities and imagine alternative futures (Bevan, 2014).

Interviewers followed a semi-structured interview guide with three sections. The section relevant to this manuscript focused on perceptions of one’s community. Following the phenomenological approach, questions were broad and open-ended. For example, interviewers asked, “Can you tell me about the community where you live? If you were talking to someone who had never been there before, how would you describe it to them? and “How have you seen 〈name of community〉 change over time?” Interviewers asked follow-up questions to flesh out participants’ narratives.

The interviews took place between May and December 2022 and lasted between 20 and 60 min. They were recorded using handheld audio recorders; phone interviews were conducted via speakerphone to allow for recording. Participants were compensated with a $40 gift card.

Data analysis

Recordings were transcribed through Rev.com and analyzed thematically using a coding reliability approach (Braun and Clarke, 2020). Four study team members devised primary codes both deductively using the interview guide and inductively by reading transcripts. Team members individually developed codebooks; these codebooks were compared and merged into one. One team member coded each transcript in NVivo using this primary codebook. A second team member checked each coded transcript. The two coders discussed discrepancies in interpretation to reach consensus.

Two team members reviewed primary codes relevant to the loss of vital places (e.g., community challenges, loss of local businesses and infrastructure, positive and negative emotions toward the community) and independently developed secondary codebooks, which were compared and merged. One team member coded each relevant primary code using this secondary codebook; the second team member checked the secondary coding, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Results

The results chronicle residents’ experiences following the loss of those vital places outlined in the Study Site Description. We use a conceptual framework (Fig. 1) to illustrate how – in the contexts of rural disinvestment and population decline – the loss of vital places not only shape the neighborhood and built environment as a social determinant of health, but also influence other SDoH domains. We find that understanding residents’ experiences through an SDoH lens can help identify consequences and adaptations following the loss of vital places and inform policy interventions to mitigate negative impacts. Additionally, this lens demonstrates how essential these vital places are in rural areas, as such places are fewer in number and serve multiple functions. Throughout, we highlight residents’ perceptions of the impacts of the loss of vital places on health and well-being.

Conceptual framework.

Neighborhood and built environment

Healthy Food Sources as Vital Places:

One of the community’s most important vital places was the local grocery store. The loss of this grocery store represented a significant impact on access to healthy food, especially fruits, vegetables, and meats. One resident explained, “Our store, our only store burnt, and nobody has ever come in to replace it. In order to shop, we have to go to the surrounding areas.” The nearest grocery store is a 20-min drive away, posing a challenge particularly for older adults who are less comfortable or unable to drive. One resident noted, “There are many people who aren’t able to go down off of the mountain for things like groceries.” Others said, “They could go days without good food,” and “A dozen people that I know personally don’t have a way to the grocery store.” Several noted the implications of the loss for resident health. One resident explained, “Obesity [is] the biggest problem, and because of the lack of places to get good food, they’re relying on snack foods and quick and easy foods.”

After the grocery store burned down, a dollar store opened in the community. Residents had mixed feelings. One described a sense of appreciation: “The dollar store has pretty much been a lifeline for a lot of people who can’t run off the mountain.” Another noted: “If it wasn’t for our Dollar General store, we wouldn’t have access to eggs, milk, bread, any kind of cereals, … it would be much harder.” But because so many residents rely on the dollar store, it struggles to keep its shelves stocked. One resident shared, “Yesterday I needed milk. No milk to be had. They can’t keep their shelves stocked fast enough and full enough for the population up here.” One resident who can travel to get groceries noted, “I try hard not to use the dollar store because I can get out and drive, and I know some of the older population can’t.” In addition, the dollar store does not supply fresh fruits, vegetables, or meats, so residents did not see it fully addressing the health implications of the lost grocery store. One lamented, “If they’re eating from the [dollar store], the only local store, they’re not getting very good food.” Another explained, “Some people rely strictly on the dollar store, which means they are definitely not eating the most nutritious items, which leads us to evolving disease and comorbidities as far as obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, [and] high cholesterol.”

Residents also adapted to the grocery store closure by utilizing other local places, including a road-side vegetable stand and food pantries. Each had their limitations. For example, the vegetable stand was limited in supply and only available during summer months. One resident noted that the stand’s distance from the town center made it difficult to utilize: “We could go down the other side of the mountain, there is a vegetable farm there, but [it’s] still not accessible unless you drive pretty far, and it’s very limited as to what they’ve grown or what’s available.” Many residents also explained that local food pantries served as a safety net, especially for low-income families and those without transportation. The pantries were well utilized; a resident commented that one pantry distribution was moved because the line of cars backed up into the road. Some pantries even offered to deliver food to residents’ homes. However, several residents explained that the pantries were open only once or twice a month and would run out of food. Residents also noted the limitations of the food received from pantries. One said, “It’s not like if you went to a store and you’re like, well, I don’t need celery this week, so I’ll buy what I need. You just get what you get and try to make it work.” While the vegetable stand and pantries serve as vital places, they do not fill the gap left by the loss of the grocery store.

Recreational Facilities as Vital Places:

Rural vital places that promote physical activity largely center around outdoor activities. This was also true for our study site. Residents described opportunities for hiking, kayaking, swimming, fishing, and hunting. Many residents noted the area’s beauty and said it was an ideal space for outdoor recreation. Some discussed a local trail system developed for residents that became dominated by four wheelers, compromising the safety of those wishing to walk or run. Yet, many of these activities are only available in nice weather, and indoor spaces for physical activity were absent. Residents connected this to health issues in the community. One explained, “The three most prominent health problems I know of in this area all directly relate to the fact that [we] live so remotely. Number one is obesity. These people don’t have anywhere to go. There’s no YMCA, Planet Fitness, no exercise establishments, so they sit and watch TV, and they sit on their porch. They don’t do anything.” Whereas a vital healthy food source had been lost, year-round recreational facilities had historically been absent, and therefore the community was always less able to support health and well-being.

Social Spaces as Vital Places:

The loss of several vital places (i.e., grocery store, restaurant, bank, church, etc.) limited spaces for social connection and information sharing. Residents explained, “There’s not really a lot of social things to do,” and “There’s no place where you just run into people and talk.” Residents saw how vital places served multiple purposes and recognized how their loss impacted the community in various ways. The most prominent example was that the loss of the grocery store also impacted their ability to connect with others. One resident explained, “Before the store burned down… you would run into people there and kind of build connections. You talk to people and see them and chit-chat and catch up on things. We don’t get that a whole lot now.” Another resident explained that the post office’s recently reduced hours lessened its ability to serve as a social space facilitating brief connections. In reflecting on the loss of vital places within their community, residents spoke about regular social activities with family and friends that they were no longer able do. One resident explained, “the little restaurant up here, it’s hardly opened at all. [My family] used to go and eat there after church and stuff.” Another talked about having to leave town to do things with friends, explaining, “There used to be a movie theater, that’s not there anymore.” Although no one explicitly described how the bank served as a social place, it was evident from how people described it, that it was a social hub for many residents.

There were some remaining opportunities for social connection, but these largely included churches and social clubs like the VFW and American Legion (residents also referred to the clubs as bars, as they serve alcohol). One resident described, “The only thing that we really have here are churches and bars… I think a lot of people go to the bars just to see other people. Even if they don’t really drink, they might go and just have water or soda and meet up with people.” These social spaces not only help people connect but also serve as safety nets during times of need. Another noted, “A lot of people congregate there and let the kids play while they have a meal, and the [club name] will be the first people to show up if something happens. They’ll do fundraisers for people in need, even if they’re not members.”

Two other social spaces mentioned were the pool and senior center; however, like churches and clubs, both only included specific subgroups of the community. Residents explained that the community pool – open only in the summer – supports social connection. One noted that while transportation barriers hinder access to many vital places, the pool addressed that barrier by providing transportation: “The pool is one of the things for our kids to do in the summer, and it’s very affordable, and it’s really nice because they run transportation for kids that are home.” Similarly, for older adults, the senior center provides important social activities and supports social connection. One resident described its importance, saying “It’s one of the only social places that we really have.” Finally, several residents noted periodic festivals and events that do provide important spaces for social connection but are not regularly available.

Transportation and Mobility:

Transportation and mobility are elements of the built environment integral for accessing resources. While public transportation (e.g., buses, ride sharing services) is ubiquitous in many urban contexts, the lack of transportation resources is an omni-present factor shaping rural residents’ experiences with their neighborhoods. In our study, residents commonly cited transportation and connectivity as issues, especially after losing several vital places. Residents lamented further travel distances—including both the time involved and added gas expenses. These new challenges particularly impacted older adults. One resident explained, “For some of them, it’s easy to drive five miles… but maybe going 25 miles is just beyond what they’re capable of. So, then they have to rely on someone else to take them.” These challenges are made worse by deteriorating roads and winter weather conditions. In addition to daunting travel, the need to visit vital places further from the community instilled in residents a need to plan purchases around when transportation was possible. One resident described, “When the winter starts coming, you start going, ‘Okay, do we have this and this?… Do you have enough milk, do you have enough this, do you have enough that?’”

While many rural areas lack any public transportation, residents in our study site had access to a van service, albeit with limitations. Residents noted that they needed to schedule pick-up times and were restricted in how much they could bring with them. One resident expressed their frustration: “The county van does pick people up, which is awesome. But that’s the planning too. You have to plan all that. Like, ‘This is what I’m going to do today.’” Beyond transportation issues, few choices for cable and internet, as well as intermittent access, also make staying connected challenging. One resident described, “[A neighbor] said that her internet has been out for the past day, and that is pretty typical. She said a lot of people she knows use this internet service provider, and the provider is not reliable.” These connectivity challenges were especially pronounced for older adults who also lacked technological devices or digital literacy.

Economic stability

Within our study site, residents experienced growing economic instability that was due to or coincided with the loss of vital places. Larger economic forces prompted local business closures, and these closures created further economic strain. One consequence was that residents faced a shrinking local job market. One resident described, “There used to be quite a few businesses that supported the community, but most of them are gone or really pared down…most of those places are done or nearly done.” Another respondent specifically shared that their daughter and wife lost their jobs at the grocery store when it shut down.

The jobs that remained were few. One resident shared, “[T]here’s very little employment here. The places that people are employed at are either small restaurants or clubs… We have a brickyard… but it is down to a skeleton crew. It no longer hires hundreds of people like they used to.” Residents described a situation where “people can’t make a go of it here,” and because “finding work close by [is a challenge]… people spend more money in gas to get to and from work.” Many expressed the need for local employment opportunities that offered living wages. One resident stated, “It would be nice to see maybe a factory or something come in that paid decent wages, so people didn’t have to travel so far to work.” For some, the cost of travel for work was too great a barrier: “I wouldn’t mind having a part-time job, but with gas being the price it is, nobody’s going to be willing to travel.”

Business closures also necessitated that residents travel for goods and services no longer available locally, which had economic consequences. One resident described, “So you’re paying for gas, you’re paying for rent, and you’re driving up and down the mountain.” Given rising gas prices and inflation, another noted, “It’s getting harder and costs more to go anywhere.” One resident observed that these added financial costs are not felt equally: “Not everybody has the economic means or even access to vehicles in some cases. Financially, [leaving the mountain] is not for them because they just don’t have the capital.” This was particularly true for older adults. One resident noted, “Especially our older population, they’re retired, so they don’t leave this area very much… it’s costing them more money to have to go to these places… [the closures are] really hurting the older population.”

Finally, residents recognized how the loss of vital places and the new custom of traveling for goods and services impacted remaining businesses. One resident noted that “usually it’s cheaper down the mountain,” making it difficult for remaining local businesses to survive. Another explained, “The grocery store… they fully intended to reopen, but insurance wouldn’t cover nearly what they needed. And the margins for profit had gotten so small because people go to Sam’s and Walmart and such. So [the owner] has said he wouldn’t recommend anybody reopening. The margins are just too tight.” Many residents described the incremental loss of businesses and services that has continued in their community since losing the grocery store. One recalled, “We called it a truck stop, that had been there for 50 years, it just closed its doors two weeks ago because they can’t keep it open, they don’t have enough business, they don’t have enough money.” There was consensus that the economic changes that occurred had dramatically changed the community. As one resident described, “We were a self-sustaining community, and now we’re not.”

Education quality and availability

The study site’s elementary school played an important role in the community. One resident described the school as one of “the best parts of [their community]”; another said, “Community wise, the school is a center.” Residents valued having a small, local school where people knew each other. As one resident noted, “It’s nice that we still have an elementary school up here on the mountaintop. Our kids don’t have to be bused down the mountain to [another school] or anything… It’s nice that everybody kind of knows everybody in town.” Another resident highlighted other important qualities of the local school, particularly the closeness that a small community affords: “The teacher-to-child ratio is really nice… close-knit enough that if you have any issues, everybody’s really easy to talk to.” However, the combination of population decline, the loss of vital places, and other economic shifts resulted in lower school enrollment. One resident reflected, “It’s hard to see the declining enrollment at school. When I was still teaching, which was 10 years ago, there were two classes of every grade: two first, two second, K through fifth. And now there is one of each.” The school was an important hub that fostered positive interpersonal relationships, community cohesion, and a sense of pride. Some residents worried about the longevity of the school if population decline and the loss of vital places were to continue.

Social and community context

The closeness and strong relationships within this community were apparent from resident interviews. One resident stated, “It is one of those communities where the neighbors all know each other, and we have what we call ‘life up on the mountaintop’”. More than a geographical descriptor, “mountaintop life” reflected the connections among residents and their commitment to each other. Statements from others mirrored this sentiment. One resident explained, “It’s a really community-oriented town;” another stated, “It’s a community that looks out for each other.” Coming together as a community to support others was also a shared value. One resident noted, “If you need help, you can go to your neighbors. [If] something disastrous would happen, the whole mountaintop really would come help you.”

Residents shared many examples of how the community came together to support one another as vital places closed. Many recounted community members helping those who had difficulty accessing food – especially older adults. One resident shared, “My grandmother and a lot of her neighbors are very elderly, so other people in the community help them out and get their groceries for them and do things like that”. Another recounted how community members ensured food pantry distributions reached all those in need: “I know people help [other] people get their stuff. They can pick it up, take it to them.” COVID-19 seemed to bring even greater awareness to the needs of other residents who are unable to travel for food and other goods. One resident, who identified as an essential worker, recounted how she utilized her trips to work as a chance to help others: “I have to be off the mountain. Who needs what while I’m there? [I’d get] a grocery list of things for different people. We all just took care of ourselves, and we still do. If somebody needs something, they’re not afraid to call and ask.” In interviews, residents often expressed pride in their community’s ability to support one another.

Several residents described how they overcame challenges maintaining the social and community context with others following the loss of these vital places. Social media, for example, became an important means for communicating: “We have a community Facebook group that we utilize, and that can range anywhere from letting each other know about issues with water lines, issues with roadways that were not properly treated during storms, accidents, power outages, internet outages.” Older adults and those lacking transportation also ask for help on the Facebook group. One resident explained, “People say, ‘I made a Walmart pickup order and it’s paid for. Is anybody going to be over that way and could pick it up for me?’ Usually five or six people will say, ‘I’m going to be over that way tomorrow,’ and someone is able to get it for them.” However, not all residents have internet or computer access, so the impact of this adaptation to the loss of vital places was limited.

The caring and supportive nature of the community was apparent in residents’ reflections of the health and well-being of their fellow community members. Several expressed concerns about others’ mental health challenges and related substance use. One resident shared, “I can’t even tell you the number of people I know that have depression and anxiety and struggle with mental health. That’s probably the number one issue, really.” Another noted, “Drug use is prevalent—illegal drugs I should say. And I think that is because of the mental health state of some people.” Importantly, concerns about mental health tied back to the loss of vital places. Despite residents’ pride in their community, their perceptions of the town’s future were often dire or fatalistic, and there were significant feelings of grief for what had once been. Common sentiments included “It’s sad”, “God, this is a depressing conversation”, and “It’s just really tough, you know what I mean?… It seems like we’re forgotten.” Several residents specifically tied these sentiments to the loss of vital places. One explained, “We lost the bank, we lost the hardware [store], we lost the grocery store. I mean, there’s nothing left.” Others noted that the losses they saw in their own community were not happening in the larger, more urbanized communities around them. The grief they felt in losing vital places in their own community was matched by frustration that growth was happening just 20 miles down the highway: “I think that there’s a caste system. [A nearby city] would be at the top of the caste system, and [our town] would be at the bottom, and [a mid-sized town in between] would be in the middle.”

Discussion

Vital places – as part of the neighborhood and built environment – are important for shaping health through both behavioral and social mechanisms (Walton, 2014). Using a case study of a small rural community in Pennsylvania that lost a substantial share of vital places over a short period of time, this manuscript addresses a gap in the literature on understanding the experiences of rural residents following the loss of vital places. We show that such losses not only have implications for how the neighborhood and built environment operates as a SDoH, but also shapes other SDoHs, including economic stability, education availability and quality, and social and community context. We build on previous vital places research (Walton, 2014) to show that in a context of disinvestment and population decline – the loss of vital places has impacts on not only health and well-being but also the other SDOH.

Essentialness and multifunctionality

As anticipated, one of the major findings of this research was that individual vital places in this rural community were essential (i.e., there were few or no alternatives) and multifunctional (i.e., they served multiple purposes). Regarding their essentialness, the loss of the grocery store was a major setback for the community. While the dollar store, food pantries, and vegetable stand provided some relief, none could adequately fill the resulting gap in healthy food access. These sentiments can be contextualized in a national trend of declining rural availability of grocery stores and growing reliance on dollar stores (Stevens, 2021). In addition, the community had no formal year-round recreational facilities that promoted physical activity, which aligns with previous research showing rural areas are less likely to be home to such facilities (Powell et al., 2006). After other restaurants closed, few places remained to purposefully gather with others. These examples speak to how essential vital places are in rural areas; their losses can have detrimental consequences for health and well-being as few or no alternatives remain available.

These vital places were also multifunctional. The grocery store served as a community hub where impromptu social connections could occur; residents recognized this loss and expressed related sadness. The remaining places to connect or run into others were largely exclusive spaces, such as social clubs/bars. While several residents noted that people do not have to drink at these establishments, the lack of alternative spaces for social connection requires people to use venues that may not promote positive health behaviors. There were clear interconnections between the multifunctionality of these vital places – as part of the neighborhood and built environment – and other SDoHs. For example, the loss of places for purposeful and happenstance interactions impacted the social and community context, leaving residents feeling despair about opportunities for social connection. And the loss of vital places coincided with and impacted the economic stability for both individuals and the broader community, as job opportunities became sparser and residents traveled further for goods, services, and places to connect. These findings align with previous work that ties economic and infrastructural decline of rural communities to deteriorating measures of health and well-being (Lee et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024)

Effects on health and well-being

Residents saw direct connections between the loss of vital places and health in their community. The most obvious example was the loss of the grocery store on health via a growing reliance on unhealthy, pre-packaged, grab-and-go foods from the dollar store. A lack of places for physical activity was also seen as a detriment to health, aligning with previous work on the connection between recreation environments and physical activity in rural environments (Abildso et al., 2021). Other residents saw a connection between the loss of and/or lack of things to do and opportunities to run into people, and general community decline was linked to declining social health and feelings of despair. This is an important contribution of this paper. Residents’ interpretations of these losses align with previous work that shows that social support found in vital places can shape physical and mental health and help buffer the effects of life’s stresses (Cohen, 2004; Uchino, 2006; Morenoff and Lynch, 2004). This contribution also builds on a growing body of literature that examines the construction of feelings of “collective embitterment” in “left-behind places” (Hannemann et al., 2023). We find that the loss of vital places—especially in comparison to seeing vital places remain or even grow in nearby, more urbanized areas—contributes to feelings of being left behind and forgotten, furthering a growing divide between disinvested communities and those around them.

Loss of vital places impacting other SDoH

We also found that the loss of vital places as part of the neighborhood and built environment influenced other SDoH. For example, the loss of vital places was closely aligned with economic stability. As businesses (including vital places) closed, local job opportunities were lost. And as residents had to travel further for their work, goods, and services, their transportation costs increased, which aligns with previous work on rising costs of transportation for rural residents (Smith et al., 2017) Changes, such as these, are associated with economic decline and contribute to rural outmigration and population decline (Johnson, 2022). We found that residents were acutely aware of how economic instability could further impact their community, as there were growing concerns about how long the school, a central feature of the community, would remain open if population loss persisted. These findings – the effects of the loss of vital places on other social determinants of health – build on Walton’s (2014) original work by showing that the potential health and well-being consequences of the loss of such places may function not only through the neighborhood and built environment, but also through other social determinants of health.

The role of transportation

Finally, another important contribution of this work is in highlighting the importance of transportation when considering the role of vital places in a rural community. Walton (2014) originally theorized that vital places must be within close proximity of residents. With dispersed populations in rural contexts, “close proximity” likely encompasses a broader geographic area and, access to such vital places likely requires access to transportation. For those with limited mobility due to age, disability, or economic disadvantage, transportation is especially key in shaping access to vital places. While residents in our study site were thankful that some form of public transportation was available, its limited functionality (i.e., needing to schedule in advance, being able to schedule trips only during limited hours, often committing a full day to the trip, and being able to carry only a limited amount on the van) often made it impractical. Residents had grown to rely on social capital via their social and community context in order to fill the gaps, such as picking up grocery orders for one another and creating delivery systems for local food pantries. Therefore, the vital places framework must be refined further to account for the vital role of transportation in accessing such places.

Implications

The findings presented here have important implications for future research. Efforts to understand the role of vital places in rural contexts must pay close attention to their essentialness, their multifunctionality, and the important role of transportation in facilitating access across widely dispersed populations. Such work should also attend to the interconnectedness of the social and physical health functions of vital places in rural areas, reflecting relevant mechanisms in sustaining or impacting the community’s overall well-being and resilience. This paper also has implications for local, county, and state leaders who oversee areas that have lost or are at risk of losing vital places. Investing in sustainable models for multi-purpose spaces in rural communities that support physical and social health is one potential pathway. Leaders must recognize that such places serve multiple purposes and can function through multiple SDoH in shaping community health and well-being. And of course, such investment should also attend to issues related to geographic access and transportation. Finally, this paper helps to show how loss of vital places can have broader implications for community attitudes and divides. Investing in spaces that preserve the health of communities can also help to preserve their place in the broader social fabric of the regions in which they exist.

Limitations

The results should be considered within certain study limitations. First, this study centered on the experiences of residents of one rural community in Pennsylvania, so findings may not generalize to other communities. The sampling method and recruitment strategies employed may also limit the results. Although purposive and convenience sampling were used to capture variation in experiences, important perspectives from under-represented or other marginalized groups may not have been captured. Moreover, residents who did not attend the mobile clinic or did not use the local Facebook page may not have known about the opportunity to participate in the study. However, our sample did include both homebound residents and residents without internet access, increasing confidence in the representativeness of our findings. Distrust of the interviewers, who were not from the community, may have limited participation or disclosure among respondents. Nonetheless, residents did share a broad range of perspectives, including both positive and negative experiences, and residents were frank in sharing how their disappointment and distrust of outside organizations grew as promises to bring local businesses to their community failed; both illustrate a degree of trustworthiness with the researchers.

Conclusion

As many rural communities face disinvestment and persistent decline in their populations and vital places, it is important to understand the implications for residents who remain. We have shown that the loss of vital places is not only experienced through the neighborhood and built environment, but also through coinciding or related impacts on economic stability, education quality and availability, and social and community context – all of which matter for health and well-being. We have also shown here that the vital places framework is not only relevant to a rural context, but that each vital place matters even more in rural areas than in urban, as there are fewer total vital places available, they often serve multiple purposes, and their loss may also effect health and well-being through other social determinants of health beyond the neighborhood and built environment. Understanding the essentialness of vital places in rural areas and their interconnections to the SDoH is important for theorizing the multiple mechanisms through which the loss of vital places impacts health for rural populations.
