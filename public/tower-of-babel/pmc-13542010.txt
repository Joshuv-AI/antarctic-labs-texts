Primary cilia connect epithelial mesenchymal plasticity with anoikis resistance and cancer stemness
Authors: Qiyuan Chen, Jiali Yang, Jiayi Yu, Shi Jiang, Jiabo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metastatic dissemination requires carcinoma cells to coordinate reversible changes in epithelial identity with survival following extracellular matrix detachment and retention of tumor-initiating capacity. However, the mechanisms integrating epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity (EMP), anoikis resistance, and cancer stemness remain incompletely defined. Primary cilia are spatially restricted signaling compartments whose assembly and function vary with cell state, tumor lineage, and pathway context. This review evaluates how primary cilia may connect these metastatic adaptations by organizing responses to microenvironmental cues. Evidence from mammary epithelial and breast cancer models links selected EMP states to increased ciliogenesis and to Hedgehog–GLI or cilium-dependent GLIS2 signaling, which may support stem-like properties and treatment resistance. Ciliary signaling can also intersect with adhesion, cytoskeletal, and pro-survival pathways involved in survival after detachment. However, these downstream networks are not specific to primary cilia, and direct evidence that ciliary signaling causes anoikis resistance remains limited. Hypoxia, matrix stiffness, and receptor-mediated signals may further modify ciliary function and cellular plasticity, but their effects are tumor- and model-dependent. Overall, primary cilia should be viewed as context-dependent organizers rather than universal drivers of metastatic behavior. Future studies should define ciliary status alongside EMP state, anchorage-independent survival, and functional stemness to determine when cilia constrain tumor progression, support malignant signaling, or provide therapeutically actionable dependencies.

Introduction

Primary cilia are solitary, microtubule-based projections that extend from the surface of many mammalian cells. Built from a basal body and an axonemal microtubule core, they provide a specialized membrane compartment where selected receptors, trafficking proteins, and signaling molecules are concentrated [1–4]. Although they are often described as sensory organelles, their function can be understood as context-dependent signal organization, not only passive signal reception. This architecture allows extracellular mechanical and biochemical cues to be interpreted in a spatially restricted manner.

Several cancer-relevant pathways intersect with ciliary biology. The relationship is strongest for Hedgehog (Hh) signaling, in which PTCH1 and SMO trafficking, together with GLI processing, depend closely on the ciliary compartment in many vertebrate cells [5, 6]. Wnt/β-catenin and Notch signaling, together with receptor tyrosine kinase pathways such as the PDGF-AA–PDGFRα and IGF-1–IGF1R axes, have also been linked to primary cilia; however, these relationships vary with cell type, tumor lineage, and pathway state [7–12]. Downstream survival modules may also intersect with ciliary signaling without being primary cilium-specific. These include phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) and mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (MAPK/ERK). In cancer, cilia can therefore support oncogenic signaling in selected settings, whereas ciliary loss or reduced ciliogenesis has been associated with tumor progression in others [7, 8].

Most cancer deaths are related to metastatic disease, but dissemination is not driven by one cellular program alone. In many carcinomas, a tumor cell that leaves an epithelial lesion must loosen cell-cell contacts, remodel its cytoskeleton, detach from the extracellular matrix (ECM), and survive while displaced from its original tissue environment [13]. These changes are often described in relation to epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), but epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity (EMP) is a more suitable framework for this review because it emphasizes reversible transitions among multiple epithelial, hybrid, and mesenchymal states rather than reducing this process to a binary shift between fixed endpoints [14–18]. These states are not merely combinations of markers: in vivo phenotypic profiling and lineage-tracing studies show that they differ in cellular plasticity, invasiveness, metastatic competence, and treatment response [17, 18]. Partial or hybrid states may retain epithelial cell–cell adhesion while acquiring mesenchymal motility and stress tolerance, allowing tumor cells to adapt across successive stages of dissemination. In this setting, cytoskeletal remodeling and reduced dependence on matrix attachment are closely related to anoikis resistance, because detached cells must suppress apoptotic signaling while maintaining sufficient survival pathway activity [14–16, 19–23].

Anoikis resistance is also linked to stem-like tumor cell states, although this relationship is not uniform across all cancers. Cancer stem cells (CSCs), or tumor cells with stem-like properties, are associated with self-renewal, tumor-initiating capacity, therapy tolerance, and increased metastatic potential [24–27]. Accordingly, stemness should be assessed functionally rather than inferred from a universal marker panel, because marker expression varies by tumor type and experimental model [24–27]. Stem-like states are maintained by developmental signaling, cellular plasticity, and cues from the tumor microenvironment (TME) [28]. Several microenvironmental conditions relevant to detachment survival and cellular plasticity may intersect with ciliary biology. Hypoxia and matrix stiffness can alter ciliary properties in defined cellular models [29, 30], whereas TGF-β, PDGF-AA, and IGF-1 provide examples of soluble mediators whose receptor signaling can be spatially coordinated at or near the ciliary compartment [11, 12, 31]. Whether ECM detachment itself directly remodels primary cilia remains unclear. Primary cilia may therefore act as one context-dependent site where microenvironmental signals and plasticity-related pathways intersect.

In this review, we discuss how primary cilia may connect EMP with anoikis resistance and cancer stemness. We first summarize core ciliary biology and major cilia-associated signaling pathways, then examine how these pathways intersect with adhesion remodeling, survival signaling, and stem-like states during metastatic dissemination. We also consider TME regulation and therapeutic approaches involving cilia-associated structures, ciliogenesis-related regulators, and downstream signaling pathways. Throughout the review, we distinguish established cilia-dependent mechanisms from tumor-specific findings and emerging links that require further validation.

Current state of research

Primary cilia as context-dependent signaling compartments in cancer

Primary cilia are now recognized as specialized signaling organelles rather than vestigial cellular structures. They organize selected receptors, trafficking machinery, and signaling proteins within a restricted membrane compartment, allowing extracellular cues to be interpreted in a spatially regulated manner [1, 7, 8]. In cancer, this function should not be viewed as a uniform driver of malignant progression. Instead, primary cilia appear to have tumor-type- and pathway-dependent roles. In some settings, intact cilia support cilia-dependent signaling, whereas in others, reduced ciliogenesis or ciliary loss may alter downstream pathways through non-canonical mechanisms [7, 8]. This context dependence is particularly relevant when considering the links between primary cilia, CSC-like states, epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity, and anoikis resistance.

The Hh pathway remains the clearest example of a cilia-dependent signaling pathway with relevance to cancer. Mechanistically, primary cilia provide an important compartment for PTCH1 and SMO trafficking and for GLI processing during canonical Hh signaling [5–6, 32, 33]. Under basal conditions, PTCH1 restrains SMO activity; after ligand engagement, SMO accumulates in the ciliary compartment and promotes GLI-dependent transcriptional output. This mechanism helps explain why Hh signaling is often discussed together with ciliary biology. However, the relationship between ciliary status and Hh pathway output is not identical across tumor types. In breast cancer, inhibition of ciliogenesis has been reported to promote Hh signaling rather than suppress it. This effect has been described in triple-negative breast cancer, where ciliary loss was associated with activation of a Hh-TGFA signaling loop and tumor progression [34]. This contrasts with many developmental contexts, in which ciliary trafficking and GLI processing are required for canonical Hh output [5]. These findings support a context-dependent interpretation rather than a simple model in which cilia always promote or always suppress Hh pathway activity.

Wnt/β-catenin signaling is also linked to primary cilia, although this relationship is less direct than that of Hh signaling [35]. In some models, the primary cilium has been proposed to restrain canonical Wnt/β-catenin activity, but the outcome depends on cell lineage, differentiation state, and the mechanism through which ciliogenesis is altered. Melanoma provides one tumor-specific example. In this setting, EZH2-mediated primary cilium deconstruction was reported to increase Wnt/β-catenin signaling and promote a more aggressive phenotype [36]. This observation supports the idea that ciliary loss can affect plasticity-related signaling pathways. At the same time, it should be interpreted as evidence from a defined tumor model, not as a general rule for all cancers.

The relationship between primary cilia and Notch signaling should be treated more cautiously than the relationship between primary cilia and Hh signaling. Notch activation is primarily contact dependent: ligand binding, for example through DLL1, induces proteolytic cleavage of the receptor and releases the Notch intracellular domain (NICD), which then enters the nucleus and regulates transcription with CSL [37]. This core activation process is not centered on the ciliary compartment. Even so, Notch remains relevant to this review because it is involved in cell fate control, stem-like phenotypes, survival, invasion, and tumor microenvironmental interactions in several cancers [37, 38]. More broadly, developmental signaling pathways, including Notch, can contribute to therapy resistance and malignant cell-state regulation in selected contexts [38]. Thus, Notch is best discussed here as a plasticity- and stemness-related pathway that may intersect with ciliary biology in selected settings, rather than as a clearly cilium-dependent pathway.

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma provides one example of this more limited type of connection. SOX9 has been reported to regulate cilia-related genes and to participate in developmental signaling programs that include Notch activity [39]. This does not prove a universal cilium-Notch mechanism. Rather, it indicates that ciliary gene expression, lineage state, and developmental signaling can be coordinated in particular tumor contexts. For this reason, the evidence in this section should be weighted by pathway. Canonical Hh signaling has the strongest mechanistic dependence on the primary cilium. Wnt/β-catenin and Notch signaling show more variable relationships with ciliary biology. Among receptor-mediated pathways, ciliary coordination has been demonstrated for PDGF-AA–PDGFRα signaling in quiescent fibroblasts [11] and for IGF-1–IGF1R signaling in fibroblast and epithelial models [12]. TGF-β receptors can localize to the ciliary tip and to endocytic vesicles at the ciliary base, where ligand stimulation promotes SMAD2/3 and ERK1/2 activation [31]. Because these mechanisms have been defined largely in nonmalignant cellular models, they should not be generalized to all tumor contexts. PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK are better described as downstream or intersecting survival modules, not as primary cilium-specific pathways.

Anoikis resistance as a survival requirement during metastatic dissemination

Under physiological conditions, the detachment of epithelial or endothelial cells from the ECM precipitates an immediate apoptotic cell death—a phenomenon termed anoikis. This anchorage-dependency acts as a physiological safeguard, enforcing tissue architecture and precluding the dissemination of detached cells. To colonize distant sites, however, disseminated tumor cells must circumvent this checkpoint [40–43]. The acquisition of anoikis resistance is not a singular event but relies on a concerted molecular reconfiguration: specifically, the remodeling of the integrin-cytoskeleton axis, the constitutive hyperactivation of pro-survival cascades, and the amplification of anti-apoptotic logic.

Bridging the extracellular architecture and the intracellular scaffold, integrins function not merely as adhesive anchors but as mechanochemical transducers that link ECM dynamics to the cytoskeleton [40, 44]. Malignant cells frequently dysregulate this signaling axis to decouple survival from attachment. This process is evident in prostate cancer, where the overexpression of TUBB3 potentiates the αvβ3/focal adhesion kinase (FAK)/Src cascade, effectively insulating cells against anoikis to fuel bone metastasis [45]. A distinct co-option strategy appears in highly metastatic colon carcinoma: even in the void of ECM attachment, integrins α2β1 and α5β1 physically recruit and activate EGFR. This aberrant interaction ignites downstream ERK/AKT survival signals, thereby reducing caspase-3 activation [46]. More broadly, FAK can couple integrin-derived adhesion signals to downstream pro-survival pathways [40, 44]. In the colon cancer model described above, however, the cited study demonstrated direct association of integrins α2β1 and α5β1 with EGFR and the consequent maintenance of ERK/AKT signaling, without establishing FAK as a required intermediary [46].

To evade the metabolic stress of detachment, tumor cells predominantly rely on two canonical survival pathways: the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling axes [20, 40, 47]. This systemic exploitation is exemplified in prostate malignancy, where Brain-derived neurotrophic factor engages the tropomyosin receptor kinase B receptor—not for neural maintenance, but to ignite an AKT-driven program of invasion and anchorage-independence [48]. Conversely, the therapeutic vulnerability of this network is highlighted by agents like moscatilin, which dismantles anoikis resistance in lung carcinoma specifically by dampening the dual hyperactivity of both the AKT and ERK modules [20, 40]. A receptor-specific connection to ciliary biology is provided by PDGF-AA–PDGFRα signaling. In growth-arrested fibroblasts, PDGFRα localizes to the primary cilium, and ligand stimulation is followed by activation of AKT and MEK1/2–ERK1/2 signaling [11]. However, this mechanism has not been shown to operate in detached tumor cells or to cause anoikis resistance.

The arbitration of cell fate hinges on the balance within the Bcl-2 family, where pro-apoptotic effectors, such as Bax and Bak, are opposed by anti-apoptotic members, such as Bcl-2 and Mcl-1. To enforce anoikis resistance, tumor cells can shift this ratio toward survival. Increased expression of anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2 and Mcl-1 can reduce the activation of pro-apoptotic effectors and suppress the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway [49]. This molecular regulation is often directed by upstream drivers; for instance, in ovarian carcinoma, the FZD7-TWIST1 axis functions as an upstream regulator, directly modulating Bcl-2 abundance to secure survival during detachment [50].

Anoikis resistance also involves molecular mechanisms beyond canonical signaling pathways. In glioma models, isocucurbitacin B promoted anoikis and suppressed tumor growth by downregulating CAV1 and altering cholesterol-associated survival mechanisms [51]. Parallel to this is a strategy of translational selectivity: metastatic breast cancer cells can use DAP5/eIF3d-dependent selective mRNA translation to sustain specific protein synthesis programs. Increased DAP5 expression has also been associated with poor metastasis-free survival [52]. Yet, these intrinsic adaptations tell only half the story. The TME functions not as a passive backdrop, but as an active source of selective pressure. Through physical and chemical stressors—ranging from hypoxia and acidic pH to matrix stiffness and immune infiltration—the TME can support the development of anoikis resistance [28, 40]. Taken together, these studies establish multiple adhesion, survival, apoptotic, translational, and microenvironmental routes to detachment survival, but they do not demonstrate that primary cilia are required for anoikis resistance. In the present framework, primary cilia are therefore considered candidate organizers of selected upstream signals rather than established components of the anoikis-resistance machinery.

Epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity and remodeling of adhesion, cytoskeleton, and survival signaling

Anoikis resistance should be considered across the EMP continuum rather than as a consequence restricted to complete EMT. Cells in partial or hybrid states can remodel adhesion, cytoskeletal organization, and survival signaling while retaining selected epithelial features, making EMP more informative than a binary EMT model for understanding detachment survival [14–20]. Through this coordinated but state-dependent remodeling, cells occupying distinct EMP states may reduce their susceptibility to the apoptotic stress of detachment without undergoing a uniform or complete mesenchymal conversion [19, 20].

A key molecular feature of EMT is the cadherin switch, characterized by reduced epithelial E-cadherin expression and increased mesenchymal N-cadherin expression [53]. This change can weaken cell-cell adhesion and may contribute to signaling changes that support migration and survival after detachment. In cervical cancer models, loss of miR-525-5p has been reported to promote metastasis and anoikis resistance through the UBE2C/ZEB1/2 axis [54]. The cadherin switch is therefore best treated as a useful readout, not as a complete definition of EMT. Depending on the tumor type and model, reduced E-cadherin may be accompanied by loss of cytokeratins, increased vimentin or fibronectin, and variable induction of SNAIL, SLUG, ZEB, or TWIST. These changes do not always occur together or in a fixed sequence, which is one reason why epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity is a more appropriate framework than a simple epithelial-to-mesenchymal conversion [14, 53, 55, 56]. Importantly, this state dependence also extends to ciliary biology. In mammary epithelial and breast cancer models, EMT programs induced by SNAIL, TWIST, or ZEB1 expression, or by E-cadherin depletion, promoted primary ciliogenesis [57]. Subsequent work showed that EMT programs induced primary ciliogenesis upon entry into intermediate epithelial–mesenchymal states and linked the ciliary compartment to the regulation and functional inactivation of GLIS2 [58]. More recently, analyses of human triple-negative breast cancer biopsies and patient-derived organoids identified quasi-mesenchymal ciliated stem-like cells as a subset of late-hybrid epithelial–mesenchymal cells, indicating that primary ciliogenesis was restricted to selected EMP states in these models rather than uniformly present across the continuum [59].

Adhesion changes are accompanied by remodeling of the actin cytoskeleton, which can support migration, altered cell shape, and survival under detachment stress [19, 20]. The therapeutic potential of targeting this machinery is highlighted in hepatocellular carcinoma, where Catechol acts to inhibit the EGFR-AKT-ERK axis, reducing cytoskeletal changes required for invasion [60]. Ultimately, these structural adaptations are not isolated; they feed into a broader survival network, where EMT-driven signals (via transforming growth factor beta and matrix metalloproteinases) directly recalibrate the apoptotic threshold to favor persistence over death [61]. In this sense, EMP provides a useful framework for linking changes in adhesion and cytoskeletal organization with the survival programs required for anchorage-independent growth. Together, these findings argue against treating ciliogenesis as a uniform consequence of EMT and instead support a state-specific relationship between EMP and primary cilia. However, the evidence remains concentrated in mammary epithelial and breast cancer models, and whether this relationship directly regulates anoikis resistance remains unresolved [57–59].

Cancer stemness and partial epithelial-mesenchymal states in anchorage-independent survival

Cancer stemness is therefore best understood as a context-dependent functional state rather than an invariant cellular identity [24–27, 62–64]. During metastatic dissemination, stemness and anoikis resistance provide complementary but non-equivalent functions: anoikis resistance enables survival after ECM detachment, whereas stemness maintains the capacity for self-renewal and tumor initiation at subsequent sites [24–28]. Developmental pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, Hh, and Notch can sustain cell-state plasticity and self-renewal, whereas PI3K/AKT and Bcl-2-family signaling can increase stress tolerance and raise the apoptotic threshold. These mechanisms provide potential points of convergence between stemness and detachment survival [38, 49, 65]. Metabolic plasticity may reinforce this overlap: CSC-associated metabolic adaptations support stress and therapy tolerance [66], whereas anoikis-resistant melanoma cells have been shown to shift toward oxidative metabolism using glutamine and fatty acids [67]. These observations indicate convergent stress-adaptation programs but do not establish that all stem-like cells are anoikis-resistant.

The relationship between EMP and stemness is state-dependent rather than monotonic: EMP can facilitate the reversible acquisition and loss of stem-like properties, but stemness does not necessarily increase as cells progress toward a fully mesenchymal phenotype [14–18, 68, 69]. Partial or hybrid states may be particularly relevant because they can retain epithelial adhesion and proliferative competence while acquiring increased motility and stress tolerance, thereby supporting both dissemination and subsequent tumor initiation [15–18, 68, 69]. In colorectal cancer, the Claudin-1–EPHA2 complex provides a tumor-specific example in which adhesion-associated signaling enhances AKT activity and promotes tumor-initiating capacity and chemoresistance [70]; however, the cited study did not directly establish this complex as a mediator of anoikis resistance. In mammary epithelial and breast cancer models, selected EMP states engage primary cilia to support stem-like functions through Hh–GLI signaling or cilium-dependent GLIS2 regulation [57, 58]. Human triple-negative breast cancer data further identify ciliated late-hybrid cells with stem-like and chemoresistant properties [59], although whether these ciliary programs also cause anoikis resistance remains unknown.

Taken together, EMP, anoikis resistance, and cancer stemness should be viewed as overlapping but non-equivalent adaptations. EMP enables reversible cell-state transitions, anoikis resistance allows detached cells to persist, and stemness maintains tumor-initiating capacity after dissemination. Their convergence may therefore be most consequential in plastic tumor cells that survive detachment while retaining tumor-initiating capacity. Primary cilia may organize part of this convergence in selected tumors, but current direct evidence is strongest for the EMP–stemness relationship and remains limited for a causal cilia–anoikis relationship [57–59]. Tumor microenvironmental regulation of ciliary signaling and cancer cell plasticity

Yet, these intrinsic adaptations tell only part of the story. The TME functions not as a passive backdrop, but as an active source of biochemical and physical signals. Through physical and chemical stressors ranging from hypoxia and acidic pH to matrix stiffness and immune infiltration, the TME can support or select for anoikis resistance [28, 40, 71, 72]. Stem-like states are rarely autonomous; they are influenced by signals from the TME, where extrinsic cues shape tumor cell plasticity and survival [28, 40, 73].

This relationship is relevant to primary cilia because cilia are positioned at the cell surface and contain selected receptors, trafficking machinery, and signaling proteins. Within the TME, primary cilia may help cells organize responses to mechanical and biochemical cues [1, 7, 8]. Among soluble mediators, TGF-β is particularly relevant because TGF-β receptors can localize to the ciliary tip and to endocytic vesicles at the ciliary base, where ligand stimulation promotes SMAD2/3 and ERK1/2 activation [31]. PDGF-AA–PDGFRα and IGF-1–IGF1R provide additional receptor-specific examples of signaling spatially coordinated by primary cilia [11, 12]. These mechanisms have been defined mainly in nonmalignant fibroblast and epithelial models and should not be assumed to operate identically in tumors. Hypoxia may influence ciliary biology, whereas matrix stiffness has been shown to alter ciliary length and centriole positioning in a chondrocyte model [29, 30]. By contrast, an IL-6–STAT3 feedback loop promoted EMT-associated changes and CSC expansion in human breast cancer cells with acquired resistance to PI3K inhibitors, but whether these effects require primary cilia remains unclear [74].

The TME therefore provides a useful framework for connecting ciliary signaling with cancer cell plasticity, but this connection should be interpreted carefully. Evidence that TME cues regulate cilia is not always the same as evidence that ciliary changes directly cause anoikis resistance or stemness. For this review, the strongest interpretation is that primary cilia may act as one context-dependent site where microenvironmental inputs intersect with signaling pathways involved in adhesion remodeling, survival after detachment, and stem-like states. Direct experimental links between TME-regulated ciliary changes and anoikis resistance remain limited and require further validation.

Therapeutic implications

Therapeutic strategies involving primary cilia or cilia-associated pathways remain at an early stage, but they provide a useful way to think about tumor plasticity, survival after detachment, and stem-like states. Because primary cilia can organize selected signaling pathways, changes in ciliary structure or signaling may influence how tumor cells respond to stress and treatment. A recent study of human triple-negative breast cancer biopsies and patient-derived organoids identified late-hybrid, ciliated stem-like cells as a chemoresistant population. Genetic ablation or pharmacological inhibition of primary ciliogenesis suppressed this resistance, providing a state-specific example of therapeutic ciliary dependency [59]. However, this therapeutic concept should be approached cautiously. The primary cilium is not a universal therapeutic target, and not every factor discussed in this section is primary cilium-specific. Some targets are better understood as part of broader cytoskeletal, centrosomal, chromatin-regulatory, or signaling programs, with ciliary relevance depending on the experimental context and whether ciliary readouts were directly measured.

One group of approaches involves factors related to microtubules, centrosomes, or ciliogenesis. TUBB3 is relevant here because it is a microtubule-related protein and has been linked to anoikis resistance and bone metastasis in prostate cancer [45]. In that study, bone-targeted lipid nanoparticles delivering siTUBB3 suppressed anoikis resistance and metastatic progression in vivo [45]. This finding supports the therapeutic relevance of TUBB3 in a defined tumor context, but it should not be interpreted as evidence that TUBB3 is a primary cilium-specific target. PLK4 provides another example. Because PLK4 is linked to centriole biology and centrosome homeostasis, it is relevant to cilia-associated biology but should not be treated as a primary cilium-specific target. PLK4 has also been reported to promote anoikis resistance in p53-deficient mammary epithelial cells [75]. In triple-negative breast cancer, PLK4 inhibition with CFI-400,945 has been explored as a radiosensitizing strategy [76]. These observations are therapeutically relevant, but PLK4 targeting also affects centrosome homeostasis, chromosome segregation, and cell-cycle progression, so ciliary effects need to be evaluated directly.

Pathway-directed strategies are most clearly connected to ciliary biology in the case of Hh signaling. SMO inhibitors such as vismodegib and sonidegib are established treatments for advanced basal cell carcinoma, a tumor type with strong Hh pathway dependence [6, 77–79]. However, resistance and limited efficacy in broader cancer contexts have encouraged attention to downstream GLI inhibition and combination strategies [6, 80]. Wnt-related approaches are also relevant because Wnt signaling can influence cancer stemness, plasticity, and therapy resistance [38, 65]. Strategies targeting different components of Wnt-related signaling, including Frizzled receptors, receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor family members, and β-catenin-associated transcriptional programs, have been investigated in different experimental settings [38, 65, 81]. These interventions may affect pathways that intersect with ciliary biology, but they should not automatically be considered cilia-directed therapies.

Unresolved questions and future directions

Metastasis represents an advanced stage of cancer progression that involves epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity, anoikis resistance, and stem-like states [13–16, 19, 20, 24, 25, 40]. Because EMP is dynamic, longitudinal or lineage-resolved models should track transitions among epithelial, hybrid, and mesenchymal states together with ciliary abundance and signaling output, rather than infer EMP–cilium relationships from static endpoint markers alone [14, 17, 18, 23, 59]. Translating these molecular insights into clinical utility demands more representative experimental systems that can reflect dynamic changes in disseminating tumor cells. Yet, a critical translational gap persists. The field currently struggles to engineer models that can fully capture the dynamic changes of these biological states, rendering the precise quantification of metastatic events—and the screening of drugs to stop them—a persistent bottleneck in oncological research.

Two-dimensional culture is widely used in cancer research but does not fully reproduce the spatial and microenvironmental complexity of tumors. Although experimentally convenient, it lacks the three-dimensional architecture, cell–cell interactions, extracellular matrix context, and oxygen gradients present in native tumors. Three-dimensional (3D) configurations, such as spheroids and organoids, represent a structural restoration, reintroducing critical variables like intercellular tension, ECM stiffness, and oxygen gradients. Yet, these self-organizing models often suffer from stochastic variability, making standardization a persistent hurdle. To bridge the gap between biological complexity and experimental control, the field is advancing toward engineered micro-physiologies—specifically microfluidic chips and bioprinted scaffolds—that allow for the precise, reproducible reconstruction of the metastatic niche [82–84].

The assessment of metastatic capability ultimately requires models that reflect the living organism. Xenograft models are widely used to evaluate metastatic capacity in vivo, but conventional systems provide a limited immune context [85]. The use of immunocompromised hosts can limit assessment of the crosstalk between tumors and the immune surveillance system. Moreover, interspecies metabolic dissonance frequently distorts pharmacokinetic profiles, leading to clinical failures. To bridge this translational gap, the next generation of models employs ‘Humanized’ Chimeras. By grafting human hematopoietic components into the murine host, these models reconstruct a functional immune environment, allowing for the evaluation of therapeutics in a system that more closely approximates the human patient [85].

The primary cilium ultimately emerges not as a static target, but as a context-dependent modulator of oncogenesis. Its influence is defined by a context-dependent pattern: cilia-associated Hh signaling can be therapeutically relevant in basal cell carcinoma, whereas ciliary loss or disruption has been associated with more aggressive behavior in selected tumor models [6, 8, 36, 77, 86, 87]. This context-dependent variability indicates that a uniform therapeutic approach is unlikely to be appropriate. The future of ciliary therapeutics, therefore, demands a more stratified approach—one that rigorously stratifies patients based on the specific ciliary dependency of their tumor. Such stratification may help identify settings in which cilia-associated pathways can be targeted without overlooking contexts where ciliary loss or disruption could have different effects.

Conclusion

Primary cilia provide a context-dependent signaling compartment in which selected extracellular cues and intracellular pathways can be organized. In cancer, their role is not uniform. The strongest mechanistic evidence concerns Hh signaling, where ciliary trafficking and GLI processing are closely linked to pathway activity. Beyond the well-established ciliary requirement of canonical Hh signaling, evidence for ciliary involvement remains pathway- and model-dependent. Within this evidence hierarchy, mammary epithelial and breast cancer studies currently provide the clearest direct connection between EMP and ciliary biology. Defined EMT-associated states can promote primary ciliogenesis and engage Hh–GLI signaling or cilium-dependent GLIS2 regulation, while recent human triple-negative breast cancer data extend this relationship to chemoresistant late-hybrid stem-like cells [57–59]. Primary cilia can spatially coordinate PDGF-AA–PDGFRα, IGF-1–IGF1R, and TGF-β receptor signaling in defined nonmalignant cell systems [11, 12, 31], whereas Wnt/β-catenin and Notch show more variable relationships with ciliary biology [7–10]. PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK are better regarded as downstream or intersecting modules rather than primary cilium-specific pathways. Whether these receptor-associated ciliary mechanisms directly regulate EMP, cancer stemness, or anoikis resistance in tumors remains unresolved.

Future studies should define when primary cilia constrain tumor progression, when they support malignant signaling, and when cilia-associated pathways can be targeted therapeutically. This will require models that measure ciliary status together with epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity, anchorage-independent survival, stem-like states, and tumor microenvironmental cues. Therapeutic interpretation should also remain cautious, because several targets discussed in this review, including TUBB3 and PLK4, affect broader cytoskeletal, centrosomal, or cell-cycle programs and are not primary cilium-specific. A clearer distinction between established cilia-dependent mechanisms, tumor-specific findings, and emerging hypotheses will be essential for translating cilia-associated biology into more precise cancer research and treatment strategies [7–8, 20, 45, 75, 76].
