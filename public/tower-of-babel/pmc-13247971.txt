Association between myocardial involvement and subclinical left ventricular dysfunction in patients with systemic sclerosis: a cardiac magnetic resonance study
Authors: Yafeng Peng, Huilin He, Xinyu Tong, Bowei Liu, Wushi Shao, Dong Xu, Xihai Zhao, Haiyan Ding, Jinzhi Lai, Zuo-Xiang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial involvement frequently occurs in patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc), leading to myocardial tissue changes and subclinical ventricular dysfunction. This form of primary cardiac involvement is one of the major determinants of mortality in SSc and may eventually progress to overt heart failure. To date, the pathophysiology and subsequent subclinical myocardial dysfunction in asymptomatic patients with SSc have not been extensively described. This study aimed to assess subclinical myocardial deformation and tissue abnormalities in asymptomatic patients with SSc using non-contrast cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) feature tracking and native T1 mapping, and to explore the relationship between native T1 values and myocardial strain.MethodsThe patients with SSc and age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs) underwent non-contrast CMR. Myocardial strain parameters and myocardial native T1 values were measured and compared between the HCs and patient subgroups based on skin involvement and disease duration. The association between the myocardial strain parameters and myocardial native T1 values was analyzed using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation and multivariate regression models.ResultsA total of 44 patients with SSc and 22 HCs were enrolled in the study. The patients with SSc had significantly lower absolute longitudinal peak strain (−17.5%±3.0% vs. −18.9%±2.3%, P=0.030) and peak diastolic strain rate (0.9±0.2 vs. 1.1±0.3 1/s, P=0.025) than the HCs, after adjustment for age, sex, and body mass index. The patients with SSc had significantly higher myocardial native T1 values compared with the HCs (1,278.4±59.7 vs. 1,233.1±27.9 ms, P<0.001). The myocardial native T1 values demonstrated moderate correlations with the longitudinal strain parameters (|r|=0.40–0.56) in the patients with SSc, but this association did not persist after adjustment for confounders in multivariate regression models. No significant differences were found in the myocardial strain parameters and myocardial native T1 values between the patient subgroups (all P>0.05).ConclusionsReduced absolute myocardial longitudinal strain and elevated myocardial native T1 values in patients with SSc indicate myocardial dysfunction and tissue impairment. These findings suggest that CMR may serve as a valuable tool for characterizing early alterations of the heart and optimizing the clinical management of patients with SSc.Keywords: Systemic sclerosis (SSc), myocardial involvement, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), feature tracking, T1 mapping

Introduction

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is an immune-mediated disease characterized by diffuse microangiopathy, leading to tissue ischemia and extracellular matrix deposition in the skin and internal organs, including the heart (1). Data from the European League Against Rheumatism Scleroderma Trials and Research database in 2010 showed that 26% of SSc-related deaths could be attributed to cardiac causes, mainly heart failure and arrhythmias (2). However, most cardiac involvement in SSc, particularly in early disease stages, is subclinical and cannot be detected using conventional modalities. Thus, the diagnosis and staging of cardiac involvement in SSc are challenging for clinicians. This underscores the importance of early detection of cardiac dysfunction using sensitive tools to identify a therapeutic window in which the disease course may be modified before irreversible damage occurs.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) provides an accurate, non-invasive, and radiation-free approach for evaluating ventricular volumes, function, myocardial perfusion, and tissue characterization. Among several techniques used in CMR, feature tracking has emerged as a powerful tool for assessing myocardial deformation and contractility. Unlike traditional measures of cardiac function that focus on global parameters such as ejection fraction (EF), strain and strain rate analysis allow for quantification of regional myocardial deformation across three spatial dimensions (longitudinal, circumferential, and radial) (3) and two cardiac phases (systolic and diastolic) (4). This approach enables the early identification of subtle myocardial abnormalities that may be misdiagnosed and/or underestimated by conventional imaging modalities (5-7). CMR feature tracking has been shown to detect early left ventricular (LV) diastolic and systolic myocardial dysfunction in patients with a variety of cardiovascular diseases (8). Native T1 mapping has been shown to quantify diffuse myocardial fibrosis in patients with SSc without manifest late gadolinium enhancement (9,10). As a non-contrast technique for quantifying myocardial fibrosis, it addresses concerns regarding nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in patients with SSc, who often have renal impairment.

While cardiac enzymes are less invasive and cost-effective markers of myocardial injury in SSc, Tipparot et al. found that cardiac enzymes alone are insufficient for identifying myocardial involvement (11). Therefore, CMR is still required to evaluate patients with SSc at risk of myocardial involvement, supporting its use as a complementary tool to biomarker assessment in SSc. This study aimed to detect subclinical myocardial deformation abnormalities using CMR feature tracking and myocardial tissue characteristics using native T1 mapping in asymptomatic patients with SSc undergoing non-contrast-enhanced CMR, and to investigate the relationship between myocardial native T1 values and myocardial strain parameters. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-aw-2421/rc).

Methods

Study population

SSc patients were prospectively recruited from the Rheumatology Outpatient Clinic of Peking Union Medical College Hospital between January 2022 and April 2024. Consecutive patients who met the 2013 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Association for Rheumatology classification criteria for SSc (12) were initially identified by a rheumatologist. These patients were then screened for eligibility by the study investigators. The exclusion criteria for all SSc patients were as follows: (I) coexistence of rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, or polymyositis/dermatomyositis; (II) history of ischemic or non-ischemic cardiac diseases; (III) cardiac or respiratory failure; and (IV) contraindications to CMR examination. Eligible patients who provided written informed consent were then enrolled in the study.

Based on the extent of skin involvement, patients were classified as having diffuse cutaneous (dc) SSc or limited cutaneous (lc) SSc (13). Based on disease duration, patients were classified as having early-stage SSc (≤5 years disease duration) or late-stage SSc (>5 years disease duration) (14). For comparison, age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs) without a history of autoimmune or cardiovascular disease were recruited from the community through local advertisements with a control-to-patient ratio of 1:2. All participants underwent CMR imaging after enrollment.

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (No. ZS-3245), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical data collection

Clinical characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), cardiovascular risk factors, disease duration, SSc subtypes (lc and dc), modified Rodnan skin score (mRSS), and laboratory data, were collected from medical records. The following laboratory data were collected: (I) immune and inflammation indicators: immunoglobulins G, A, and M, complement components 3 and 4, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein; (II) autoantibodies: anti-nuclear antibody, anti-centromere antibody, anti-double-stranded DNA antibody, anti-ribonucleoprotein antibody, anti-Sjogren’s syndrome A and B antibodies, and anti-topoisomerase antibody; and (III) other laboratory indicators: cardiac troponin I (CTnI), creatine kinase, serum creatinine, and N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation based on serum creatinine values (15). NT-proBNP ≤125 pg/mL, cTnI ≤0.05 ng/mL, and eGFR >90 mL/min/1.73 m2 were considered normal values.

CMR imaging protocol

CMR was performed using a 3.0-T whole-body magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanner (Ingenia CX, Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) with a 16-channel dStream Torso coil and a 12-channel embedded posterior coil in the supine position. To minimize skin irritation and avoid any potential discomfort or skin breakdown caused by adhesive electrocardiographic electrodes, a peripheral pulse unit triggering device was used, and all imaging data were acquired during breath-hold. A balanced steady-state free precession (b-SSFP) sequence was used to acquire a stack of short-axis cine, covering the entire left ventricle (from the base to the apex) and two long-axis cine views (two- and four-chamber views). The imaging parameters for the b-SSFP sequence were as follows: repetition time (TR) =3.5 ms, echo time (TE) =1.77 ms, flip angle (FA) =40°, field of view (FOV) =334×314 mm2, in-plane resolution =1.25×1.25 mm2, slice thickness =10 mm, inter-slice gap =6 mm for short-axis images, and 30 cardiac phases. Native T1 mapping at the mid-ventricular level was acquired using a modified Look-Locker inversion recovery sequence with a 5(3)3(3) acquisition scheme and the following imaging parameters: TR =2.2 ms, TE =1.03 ms, FA =35°, FOV =300×300 mm2, in-plane resolution =2×2 mm2, and slice thickness =10 mm.

CMR image analysis

The image analysis was performed by two reviewers (Y.P. and B.L.), each with more than 5 years of experience in CMR data analysis, using commercial software (CVI42, Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc., Calgary, Canada). Both reviewers were blinded to the clinical information. If disagreements arose between the two reviewers, a senior reviewer (H.D.), with more than 10 years of experience in CMR data analysis, provided arbitration. The endocardial and epicardial contours of the left ventricle were semi-automatically traced at the end-diastolic and end-systolic phases on short-axis cine images, avoiding inclusion of blood pool and pericardial partial volume effects. The LV functional parameters, including LV end-diastolic volume, end-systolic volume, stroke volume, and EF, were assessed automatically. The LV mass (LVM) was calculated by measuring the area between the endocardial and epicardial contours on each short-axis slice. The LV papillary muscles were included in the LV volume but excluded from the LVM.

The body surface area was calculated using the Mosteller formula, and the LV function and LVM were indexed to body surface area. For the LV myocardial strain analysis, the short-axis and long-axis cine images (two- and four-chamber views) were transferred to the tissue tracking module of the software. The LV endocardial and epicardial contours were semi-automatically traced at the end-diastolic phase on all series, while carefully excluding papillary muscles and moderator bands (Figure S1).

The global LV myocardial strain parameters were automatically calculated, including the global radial, circumferential, and longitudinal peak strain (PS), peak systolic strain rate (PSSR), and peak diastolic strain rate (PDSR). Subclinical myocardial deformation abnormality was defined as the early and subtle impairment of myocardial function detected by CMR feature tracking in the absence of overt abnormalities in conventional macroscopic functional parameters, such as LVEF.

For the native T1 mapping analysis, the T1 images were transferred to the T1 mapping module of the software. The native T1 values were automatically calculated by delineating the LV endocardial and epicardial contours of the myocardium.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and Origin 2025. The distribution of the continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as the median [interquartile range (IQR), 25th–75th percentile]. Independent t-tests (for normally distributed data) and Mann-Whitney U tests (for non-normally distributed data) were used to compare the continuous variables between the patients with SSc and HCs. One-way analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare the CMR measurements among patient subgroups and HCs with post-hoc Bonferroni correction as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared between patients and HCs using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Multivariable regression analysis was performed to evaluate differences in CMR parameters after adjustment for potential confounders, including sex, age, and BMI, disease duration, mRSS, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia. Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients and multivariate regression analyses were used to analyze associations between the LV strain parameters and native T1 values. Correlation strength was classified as follows: r=0.2–0.4, weak; r=0.4–0.6, moderate; r=0.6–0.8, strong; and r>0.8, very strong correlation. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 46 patients with SSc and 22 age- and sex-matched HCs were recruited for the study. Two patients were excluded due to non-ischemic cardiac diseases (severe valvular regurgitation and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy). Thus, 44 patients with SSc were ultimately included in the analysis. The study flowchart is shown in Figure S2. The mean age of the patients with SSc was 44.4±13.7 years (range, 18.1–69.4 years). Most of the patients were female (n=39, 88.6%). Of the 44 patients with SSc, 23 (52.3%) had the lc subtype and 21 (47.7%) had the dc subtype. The patients with SSc had a median disease duration of 5.2 years (IQR, 4.7–7.7 years; range, 0.3–28.7 years), and a median mRSS of 2.0 (IQR, 2.0–5.0).

There were no significant differences in age (44.4±13.7 vs. 46.4±12.8 years, P=0.995), sex (female, 88.6% vs. 86.4%, P=0.790), and BMI (22.4±3.3 vs. 23.1±2.1 kg/m2, P=0.227) between the patients with SSc and the HCs. The subgroup analysis revealed no significant differences in the demographic characteristics or laboratory data between the dcSSc and lcSSc groups (all P>0.05), or between the early- and late-stage SSc groups (all P>0.05), expect for a higher proportion of females in the late-stage SSc group compared with the early-stage SSc group (100% vs. 76.19%, P=0.019). LV remodeling, function, and myocardial strain measurements

In relation to the LV remodeling parameters, the diastolic and systolic LV mass index (LVMi) were lower in the patients with SSc than in the HCs (P=0.039 and P=0.005, respectively). However, these differences were not statistically significant after adjustment for sex, age, and BMI (P=0.250 and P=0.133, Table 2). There was no significant difference in LVEF between the patients with SSc and HCs (P=0.410). Nor were any significant differences observed in the global circumferential and radial PS, PSSR, and PDSR between the patients with SSc and HCs (all P>0.05, Figure 1). Conversely, the absolute longitudinal PS (−17.5%±3.0% vs. −18.9%±2.3%, P=0.058) and PDSR (0.9±0.2 vs. 1.1±0.3 1/s, P=0.054) were lower in the patients with SSc than in the HCs, and these differences remained statistically significant after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI (P=0.030 and P=0.025, respectively, Figure 2).

Myocardial tissue characteristics

The myocardial native T1 values were significantly higher in the patients with SSc than in the HCs (1,278.4±59.7 vs. 1,233.1±27.9 ms, P<0.001), and this difference remained statistically significant after adjustment for sex, age, and BMI (P=0.002, Figure 2). Comparisons of the native T1 values among the three groups revealed a significant overall difference (1,281.2±50.7 vs. 1,275.8±67.9 vs. 1,233.1±27.9 ms, P<0.001, Table S3). Post-hoc analysis revealed that both the early- and late-stage SSc groups had significantly higher native T1 values than the HC group (both P<0.05), while no such significant difference was detected between the early- and late-stage SSc subgroups (P>0.05) (Table S3). No significant differences were found in the myocardial native T1 values between the dcSSc and lcSSc patients (P>0.05, Table 2, Figure 3).

Association between LV myocardial strain and tissue characteristics

The myocardial native T1 values demonstrated significant moderate correlations with several strain parameters (|r|=0.40–0.56). Specifically, in the patients with SSc, a moderate negative correlation was observed between the myocardial native T1 values and longitudinal PSSR (r=−0.56, P<0.001), while a moderate positive correlation was observed between the myocardial native T1 values and longitudinal PDSR (r=0.40, P=0.008). Univariate linear regression analysis revealed that circumferential PSSR was significantly associated with myocardial native T1 [β =−0.323; 95% confidence interval (CI): −0.618 to −0.028; P=0.032]. The association was not significant after multivariable adjustment. Specifically, it was no longer significant in model 1, adjusted for age, sex, and BMI (β =−0.242; 95% CI: −0.596 to 0.112; P=0.174), or in model 2, further adjusted for age, sex, BMI, disease duration, mRSS, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia (β =−0.232; 95% CI: −0.612 to 0.149; P=0.229). A similar trend was observed in relation to longitudinal PSSR and longitudinal PDSR. These two parameters were found to be significantly associated with myocardial native T1 in the univariate analysis (longitudinal PSSR: β =−0.507, 95% CI: −0.775 to −0.238, P<0.001; longitudinal PDSR: β =0.378, 95% CI: 0.089 to 0.666, P=0.011). However, these associations were no longer significant after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI (model 1: longitudinal PSSR: β =−0.227, 95% CI: −0.558 to 0.034, P=0.079; longitudinal PDSR: β =0.208, 95% CI: −0.130 to 0.547, P=0.220), or after further adjustment for age, sex, BMI, disease duration, mRSS, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia (model 2: longitudinal PSSR: β =−0.221, 95% CI: −0.514 to 0.071, P=0.103; longitudinal PDSR: β =0.219, 95% CI: −0.111 to 0.549, P=0.140). Discussion

This study investigated subclinical alterations in myocardial strain and tissue characteristics in patients with SSc using CMR. We found that compared with the HCs, the patients with SSc had significantly higher myocardial native T1 values and lower myocardial longitudinal PS and PDSR, despite preserved systolic and global cardiac function. In the patients with SSc, the unadjusted analysis suggested a positive correlation between myocardial native T1 values and PDSR, an important strain-derived parameter reflecting LV diastolic relaxation and myocardial deformation; however, this association did not persist after adjustment for demographic and disease-specific confounders. Our findings indicate that subclinical cardiac structural impairment and dysfunction can be detected in patients with SSc. They also suggest that native T1 values may not be an independent determinant of diastolic function. Future studies with larger, prospective cohorts are needed to further explore this hypothesis and to identify the true determinants of diastolic dysfunction in this population.

In the present study, the longitudinal PS and PDSR were significantly lower in the patients with SSc than in the HCs after adjustment for sex, age, and BMI. No statistically significant differences were observed in the circumferential or radial PS, PSSR, and PDSR between the two groups. Physiologically, LV deformation is determined by the spiral architecture of myocardial fibers, with subendocardial and subepicardial fibers arranged in right-handed and left-handed helical patterns, respectively (16). Subendocardial fibers primarily induce longitudinal shortening of the myocardium during contraction, while subepicardial fibers primarily induce circumferential shortening. The combined mechanical forces from these two directions drive radial thickening of the myocardium (17). The presence of cardiac Raynaud phenomenon leads to cold-induced transient myocardial ischemia and reduced myocardial perfusion reserve index in patients with SSc (18,19). The subendocardial myocardium is particularly vulnerable to ischemia (20), and myocardial dysfunction typically occurs first in the subendocardium. Thus, we hypothesized that in the early stage of myocardial involvement in patients with SSc, longitudinal function of the subendocardium is mainly impaired. This trigger compensatory changes in the subepicardial myocardium to preserve global ventricular systolic function (17,21); thus, both radial and circumferential myocardial functions are preserved through these compensatory mechanisms. This pathophysiological adaptation may explain why the LV strain parameters (particularly longitudinal strain) are decreased in patients with early-stage SSc, whereas the LVEF remains within the normal range. Moreover, the longitudinal PDSR is a parameter of diastolic function. Some echocardiographic studies have indicated that LV diastolic dysfunction derived from speckle tracking is independently associated with an increased risk of mortality in patients with SSc (22,23). Our findings demonstrate that myocardial fibrosis and impaired strain are already present in asymptomatic SSc patients, with significantly greater strain impairment observed in late-stage SSc. This shows the importance of early CMR evaluation in patients with SSc, particularly asymptomatic patients, to facilitate the timely initiation of cardioprotective management.

To date, research comparing myocardial strain between early- and late-stage SSc based on CMR is limited. Our study showed a decreasing trend in the longitudinal PDSR from the HCs to the early-stage SSc patients and then to the late-stage SSc patients. This finding suggests that LV diastolic dysfunction in SSc is progressive and closely related to the disease course. Cardiac involvement occurs earlier and is more common in patients with dcSSc than in those with lcSSc (24), and is also more severe in dcSSc (23). However, CMR-based comparisons of myocardial strain parameters between dcSSc and lcSSc patients are limited. In the present study, the dcSSc patients had slightly lower strain values than the lcSSc patients; however, these differences were not statistically significant. Given that our cohort included patients with relatively mild disease (median mRSS =2), the phenotypic difference between the dcSSc and lcSSc subtypes may not have been fully apparent.

The present study showed that the myocardial native T1 values were higher in the patients with SSc than in the HCs. Theoretically, myocardial T1 values increase in the presence of water, whether in its free form or when bound to macromolecules such as collagen (24). While the precise molecular mechanisms of SSc-related cardiomyopathy remain unclear, microvascular dysfunction is recognized as a pivotal pathological driver. Structural microvascular alterations, including capillary loss and arteriolar stenosis, lead to tissue hypoxia, which in turn triggers inflammation and promotes excessive extracellular matrix production by cardiac fibroblasts (25). Reduced myocardial perfusion, as assessed using intravoxel incoherent motion diffusion-weighted MRI in patients with SSc, suggests the presence of underlying myocardial microvascular dysfunction (26). However, this finding appears paradoxical, given the pathophysiological expectation that edema is typically associated with increased myocardial perfusion.

CMR-derived comparative data on native T1 values between early- and late-stage SSc are limited. Patients with early-stage SSc exhibited the highest native T1 values, even surpassing those observed in late-stage SSc patients numerically. The native T1 value is a sensitive marker reflecting the total intra- and extracellular myocardial water content and interstitial fibrosis (24). The significant increase in native T1 values in early-stage SSc may primarily reflect active myocardial inflammation, edema, and/or early diffuse fibrotic changes during the active phase of the disease. As the disease progresses to later stages, fibrosis may become more established, while active inflammatory edema may subside. This may explain the comparatively lower native T1 values observed in late-stage SSc. However, this finding requires verification with a larger sample size due to the lack of statistical significance. In our study cohort (median mRSS =2), the myocardial native T1 values did not differ significantly between the dcSSc and lcSSc subgroups. This is consistent with the findings of Gotschy et al. (27), who suggested that because their study focused on less severe disease phases (median mRSS =3), it may not have captured phenotypic differences between the dcSSc and lcSSc subtypes, which typically become apparent in more advanced stages of the disease.

Further, significant correlations between myocardial native T1 values and myocardial strain parameters were observed in the present study. These findings suggest a link between tissue-level changes and myocardial mechanics in SSc. Notably, alterations in myocardial tissue characteristics may influence myocardial strain parameters in the early stage of SSc. Although unadjusted analyses suggested an assertion between myocardial native T1 values and diastolic function, this relationship did not persist after adjustment for demographic and disease-specific confounders in multivariate regression models. These findings suggest that native T1 values may not be an independent determinant of diastolic function in SSc. Future studies need to be conducted to further explore this relationship and to identify the key determinants of diastolic dysfunction in this population.

This study had several limitations. First, as a single-center study, it may be subject to selection bias. Second, the sample size of patients with SSc was small due to the rarity of the disease. The limited sample size, especially for subgroup analyses, may have resulted in insufficient statistical power. Validation in larger, independent cohorts with adequate power to detect clinically meaningful differences is thus warranted. Third, the study did not include T2-weighted imaging, a conventional sequence for assessing myocardial inflammation. Instead, it employed T1 mapping, which has been shown to be able to detect myocardial edema (28). Moreover, native T1 mapping was only acquired from a single mid-ventricular short-axis slice for each subject. Finally, given the slow progression of myocardial injury in SSc, the cross-sectional study design represents an inherent limitation. Thus, our results should be confirmed in longitudinal studies with patients with SSc. We intend to conduct follow-up examinations of these patients to verify our conclusions.

Conclusions

Reduced absolute myocardial longitudinal strain and elevated myocardial T1 values in patients with SSc indicate myocardial tissue and functional impairment. These findings suggest that CMR may serve as a valuable tool for characterizing early alterations of the heart and optimizing the clinical management of patients with SSc.
