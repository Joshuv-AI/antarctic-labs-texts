Dunkl-Schrödinger Equation in Higher Dimensions
B. Hamil, B. C. Lütfüoğlu, M. Merad

Abstract
This paper presents analytical solutions for eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Schrödinger equation in higher dimensions, incorporating the Dunkl operator. Two fundamental quantum mechanical problems are examined in their exact forms: the d-dimensional harmonic oscillator and the Coulomb potential. In order to obtain analytical solutions to these problems, both Cartesian and polar coordinate systems were employed. Firstly, the Dunkl-Schrödinger equation is derived in d-dimensional Cartesian coordinates, and then for the isotropic harmonic potential interaction, its solutions are given. Subsequently, using polar coordinates the angular and radial parts of the Dunkl-Schrödinger equation are obtained. It is demonstrated that the system permits the separation of variables in both coordinate systems, with the resulting separated solutions expressed through Laguerre and Jacobi polynomials. Then, the radial Dunkl-Schrödinger equation is solved using the isotropic harmonic, pseudoharmonic, and Coulomb potentials. The eigenstates and eigenvalues are obtained for each case and the behavior of the energy eigenvalue functions are illustrated graphically with the reduced probability densities.

Introduction

Quantum mechanics is a branch of mathematical physics that deals with the behavior of very small-scale atomic and subatomic systems, which cannot be described successfully within the framework of classical mechanics. The investigation of such systems necessitates the utilization of differential equations that are tailored to the characteristics of the particles under consideration. One such equation is the Schrödinger equation, which is frequently employed to examine the dynamics of non-relativistic systems irrespective of the spin feature of the system. The Klein-Gordon and Dirac equations are commonly deployed to investigate relativistic systems with spin-
0

1/2

It is worthy of note that the Dunkl formalization, which is integrated into the aforementioned equations and classifies the solutions according to the reflection symmetry, has been the subject of increasing interest, particularly over the past decade. The Dunkl formalization is founded upon the utilization of the Dunkl operator, which represents an extension of the ordinary partial spatial derivative with the incorporation of an additional term that involves the differential-difference operators associated with the reflection group

Although the Dunkl operator, named after the pure mathematician Charles Dunkl, who proposed it in 1989, has surprisingly its origins in the middle of the last century. 2
Dunkl Oscillator Hamiltonian in d-dimensions

The d-dimensional isotropic Dunkl oscillator model may be regarded as an extension of the original model, in which the standard derivatives are substituted with Dunkl derivatives, thereby introducing supplementary singular terms into the potential. In this context, the Hamiltonian of the d-dimensional isotropic Dunkl oscillator model is defined by

H=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(D_{1}^{2}+D_{2}^{2}+\cdots+D_{d}^{2}\right)+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2}\right),

(1)

where
D_{j}
is the Dunkl derivative
[
1
,
20
]

D_{j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\mu_{j}}{x_{j}}\left(\mathbf{1}-R_{j}\right),\qquad\text{for}\quad j=1,2,3,\cdots,d\,,

(2)

with the Wigner deformation constant
\mu_{j}
and the identity operator
\mathbf{1}
. Here, the reflection operator,
R_{j}
, acts on the eigenfunction as follows:

R_{j}f\left(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{j},x_{j+1},\cdots,x_{d}\right)=f\left(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,-x_{j},x_{j+1},\cdots,x_{d}\right),

(3)

thus,

R_{j}^{2}=\mathbf{1}.

(4)

In order to preserve the symmetry in the full Hamiltonian, we impose the condition that all deformation coefficients are equal

\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\cdots=\mu_{d}.

(5)

2.1
Solution with the Cartesian coordinates

In the d-dimensional Cartesian coordinates, the Schrödinger equation associated to the Hamiltonian given in Eq. (
1
) can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(D_{1}^{2}+D_{2}^{2}+\cdots+D_{d}^{2}\right)+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2}\right)\right]\psi\left(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}\right)=\mathcal{E}\psi\left(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}\right).

(6)

The most straightforward solution to this differential equation can be obtained through the separation of variables method. In this case, the total Hamiltonian can be expressed as the sum of
d
sub-Hamiltonian terms

H=H_{1}+H_{2}+\cdots+H_{d},

(7)

where each sub-Hamiltonian term

H_{j}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}D_{j}^{2}+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}x_{j}^{2},

(8)

corresponds to a simultaneous one-dimensional Dunkl oscillator

\displaystyle H_{j}\psi\left(x_{j}\right)=\varepsilon_{j}\psi\left(x_{j}\right).

(9)

Here, the total eigenfunction is taken as the multiplication of each separated one-dimensional solutions

\psi\left(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{d}\right)=\psi\left(x_{1}\right)\psi\left(x_{2}\right)\cdots\psi\left(x_{d}\right),

(10)

with the total eigenvalue

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{d}.

(11)

In the literature the solution of the non-relativistic one-dimensional Dunkl oscillator

\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}+\frac{2\mu_{j}}{x_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\mu_{j}}{x_{j}^{2}}\left(\mathbf{1}-R_{j}\right)\right)+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}x_{j}^{2}\right]\psi\left(x_{j}\right)=\varepsilon_{j}\psi\left(x_{j}\right),

(12)

were studied extensively. In order to avoid unnecessary repetition, we present a brief overview of the solution here. Given that the
H_{j}
and
R_{j}
operators commute, we select eigenfunctions with a specific parity

\displaystyle R_{j}\psi\left(x_{j}\right)=s_{j}\psi\left(x_{j}\right),

(13)

where
s_{j}=\pm 1.
Then, we express the parity-dependent solutions:

•

For the case
s_{j}=+1
, we have

R_{j}\psi^{+}\left(x_{j}\right)=\psi^{+}\left(x_{j}\right),

(14)

thus,

\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}+\frac{2\mu_{j}}{x_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\right)+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}x_{j}^{2}\right]\psi^{+}\left(x_{j}\right)=\varepsilon_{j}^{+}\psi^{+}\left(x_{j}\right).

(15)

Then, the complete solution of Eq. (
15
) stands in terms of the
associated Laguerre polynomials
[
50
]

\psi^{+}\left(x_{j}\right)=C_{j}^{+}e^{-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}x^{2}}\mathbf{L}_{n_{j}}^{\mu_{j}-1/2}\left(\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x^{2}\right),

(16)

with eigenvalues

\varepsilon_{j}^{+}=\hbar\omega\left(2n_{j}+\mu_{j}+1/2\right),\qquad n_{j}\in\left\{1,2,3,\cdots\right\}.

(17)

•

For the case
s_{j}=-1
, we have

R_{j}\psi^{-}\left(x_{j}\right)=-\psi^{-}\left(x_{j}\right),

(18)

so that

\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}+\frac{2\mu_{j}}{x_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{2\mu_{j}}{x_{j}^{2}}\right)+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}x_{j}^{2}\right]\psi^{-}\left(x_{j}\right)=\varepsilon_{j}^{-}\psi^{-}\left(x_{j}\right).

(19)

Then, the odd solution appears as

\psi^{-}\left(x_{j}\right)=C_{j}^{-}e^{-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}x^{2}}x\text{ }\mathbf{L}_{n_{j}}^{\mu_{j}+1/2}\left(\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x^{2}\right),

(20)

with eigenvalues

\varepsilon_{j}^{-}=\hbar\omega\left(2n_{j}+\mu_{j}+3/2\right),\qquad n_{j}\in\left\{1,2,3,\cdots\right\}.

(21)

The conjunction of the outcomes of the two subcases enables the general solution of Eq. (
12
) to be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\psi^{s_{j}}\left(x_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{j}^{s_{j}}e^{-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}x^{2}}x^{\frac{1-s_{j}}{2}}\text{ }\mathbf{L}_{n_{j}}^{\mu_{j}-s_{j}/2}\left(\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x^{2}\right),

(22)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{j}^{s_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbar\omega\left(2n_{j}+\mu_{j}+1-s_{j}/2\right).

(23)

The total energy eigenvalue expression is determined by substituting the specific
\varepsilon_{j}^{s_{j}}
expressions defined in Eq. (
11
). This calculation yields our final result

\mathcal{E}_{n}=\hbar\omega\left[2n+d+\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)-\left(s_{1}+s_{2}+\cdots+s_{d}\right)/2\right],

(24)

where
n=n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{d}.
Our results indicate that the total energy eigenvalue function of the Dunkl-oscillator is affected by the quantum numbers "
n
" as well as the Wigner parameters and parities. The highest contribution from the additional terms occurs when
s_{1}=s_{2}=\cdots=s_{d}=-1
, whereas the lowest contribution is achieved when
s_{1}=s_{2}=\cdots=s_{d}=+1.

2.2
Solution with the polar coordinates

We now examine the Dunkl-Schrödinger equation using d-dimensional polar coordinates. To do so, we will employ a set of coordinates with one spatial,
r
, and
d-1
angular,
\theta_{j}
, components. In this case, the reflection operators will act as follows:

\displaystyle R_{1}f\left(r,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{j},\cdots,\theta_{d-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\left(r,\pi-\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{j},\cdots,\theta_{d-1}\right),

\displaystyle R_{2}f\left(r,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{j},\cdots,\theta_{d-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\left(r,-\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{j},\cdots,\theta_{d-1}\right),

\displaystyle R_{j}f\left(r,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{j},\cdots,\theta_{d-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\left(r,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\pi-\theta_{j},\cdots,\theta_{d-1}\right),

(25)

\displaystyle R_{d}f\left(r,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{j},\cdots,\theta_{d-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\left(r,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{j},\cdots,\pi-\theta_{d-1}\right).

Let us start by defining the relationships between the Cartesian coordinates
x_{j}
and the d-dimensional polar coordinates
[
51
,
52
]
:

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\cos\theta_{1}\sin\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{3}\cdots\sin\theta_{d-1},

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\sin\theta_{1}\sin\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{3}\cdots\sin\theta_{d-1},

\displaystyle x_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\cos\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{3}\sin\theta_{4}\cdots\sin\theta_{d-1},

\displaystyle x_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\cos\theta_{j-1}\sin\theta_{j}\sin\theta_{j+1}\cdots\sin\theta_{d-1},

(26)

\displaystyle x_{d-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\cos\theta_{d-2}\sin\theta_{d-1},

\displaystyle x_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\cos\theta_{d-1},\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{for}\,\,d=3,4,5,\cdots,d-1.

Here, the range of the spatial component is
r\in\left]0,+\infty\right[,
while the angular components ranges are
0\leq\theta_{1}\leq 2\pi
, and
0\leq\theta_{j}\leq\pi
for
j=2,3,\cdots,d-1
. In this notation the sum of the squares of Eq. (
26
) gives

\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{2}=r^{2}.

(27)

We now consider the Dunkl derivative. Taking the square of the Dunkl derivative in d-dimensions, we get

\sum_{j=1}^{d}D_{j}^{2}=\Delta+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{2\mu_{j}}{x_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\mu_{j}}{x_{j}^{2}}\left(\mathbf{1}-R_{j}\right)\right).

(28)

Here, the first term is the conventional Laplacian operator and it can be expressed in d-dimensional polar coordinates with

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d-2}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta_{j+1}\sin^{2}\theta_{j+2}\cdots\sin^{2}\theta_{d-1}}\bigg[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{j}^{2}}+\left(j-1\right)\tan\theta_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\bigg]

(29)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\bigg[\frac{1}{\sin^{d-2}\theta_{d-1}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{d-1}}\sin^{d-2}\theta_{d-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{d-1}}\bigg].

In polar coordinates, for
d\geq 3
we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\partial\theta_{k}}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{k}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{h_{j}^{2}}\frac{\partial x_{k}}{\partial\theta_{j}}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}},

(30)

where

\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1,

\displaystyle h_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\sin\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{3}\cdots\sin\theta_{d-1},

\displaystyle h_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\sin\theta_{3}\sin\theta_{4}\cdots\sin\theta_{d-1},

\displaystyle h_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\sin\theta_{4}\sin\theta_{5}\cdots\sin\theta_{d-1},

\displaystyle h_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\sin\theta_{j+1}\sin\theta_{j+2}\cdots\sin\theta_{d-1},

(31)

\displaystyle h_{d-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\sin\theta_{d-1},

\displaystyle h_{d-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r,

and

\displaystyle h=r^{d-1}\sin\theta_{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{3}\sin^{3}\theta_{4}\cdots\sin^{d-2}\theta_{d-1}.

(32)

Then, for a particle that moves under a spherically symmetric potential
V\left(r\right),
the Dunkl-Schrödinger equation takes the form

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{A}_{r}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2mr^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{1}}}{\sin^{2}\theta_{2}\sin^{2}\theta_{3}...\sin^{2}\theta_{d-1}}+\frac{\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{2}}}{\sin^{2}\theta_{3}...\sin^{2}\theta_{d-1}}+\cdots+\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{d-1}}\bigg)\right]\psi\left(r,\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{d-1}\right)

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\psi\left(r,\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{d-1}\right),

where

\mathcal{A}_{r}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\bigg[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{d-1+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+...+\mu_{d}\right)}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\bigg]+V\left(r\right).

(34)

Here, the angular operators
\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{1}},\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{2}},\cdots,\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{d-1}}
, can be expressed in terms of polar coordinates as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{1}^{2}}+\frac{2\left(\mu_{1}\tan\theta_{1}-\mu_{2}\cot\theta_{1}\right)}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{1}}+\frac{\mu_{1}}{\cos^{2}\theta_{1}}\left(1-R_{1}\right)+\frac{\mu_{2}\left(1-R_{2}\right)}{\sin^{2}\theta_{1}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{2}^{2}}-\bigg[\left(1+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}\right)\right)\cot\theta_{2}-2\mu_{3}\tan\theta_{2}\bigg]\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{2}}+\frac{\mu_{3}\left(1-R_{3}\right)}{\cos^{2}\theta_{2}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{3}^{2}}-\bigg[\left(2+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}\right)\right)\cot\theta_{3}-2\mu_{4}\tan\theta_{3}\bigg]\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{3}}+\frac{\mu_{4}\left(1-R_{4}\right)}{\cos^{2}\theta_{3}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{d-2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{d-2}^{2}}-\bigg[\left(\left(d-3\right)+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+...+\mu_{d-2}\right)\right)\cot\theta_{d-2}-2\mu_{d-1}\tan\theta_{d-2}\bigg]\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{d-2}}+\frac{\mu_{d-1}\left(1-R_{d-1}\right)}{\cos^{2}\theta_{d-2}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{d-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{d-1}^{2}}+\bigg[\left(\left(d-2\right)+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+...+\mu_{d-1}\right)\right)\cot\theta_{d-1}-2\mu_{d}\tan\theta_{d-1}\bigg]\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{d-1}}+\frac{\mu_{d}\left(1-R_{d}\right)}{\cos^{2}\theta_{d-1}}.

(35)

We observe that each of these operators depends only on one of the angles of
\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{d-1}
, so their eigenstates can be denoted by
\Theta_{1}\left(\theta_{1}\right),\Theta_{2}\left(\theta_{2}\right),\cdots,\Theta_{d-1}\left(\theta_{d-1}\right)
.
Now, we assume the total wave function in the following form

\psi\left(r,\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{d-1}\right)=U\left(r\right)\Theta_{1}\left(\theta_{1}\right)\Theta_{2}\left(\theta_{2}\right)\cdots\Theta_{d-1}\left(\theta_{d-1}\right).

(36)

This allows us to transform the Schrödinger equation, given in Eq. (
33
), into a set of ordinary differential equations with one radial equation

\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{d-1+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)+V\left(r\right)+\frac{\hbar^{2}\varpi^{2}}{2mr^{2}}\right]U\left(r\right)=EU\left(r\right),

(37)

and
d-1
angular equations

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{1}}\Theta_{1}\left(\theta_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{2}\Theta_{1}\left(\theta_{1}\right),

(38)

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{2}}+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta_{2}}\right)\Theta_{2}\left(\theta_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}^{2}\Theta_{2}\left(\theta_{2}\right),

(39)

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{3}}+\frac{\lambda_{2}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta_{3}}\right)\Theta_{3}\left(\theta_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}^{2}\Theta_{d-1}\left(\theta_{d-1}\right),

(40)

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{J}_{\theta_{d-1}}+\frac{\lambda_{d-2}^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta_{d-1}}\right)\Theta_{d-1}\left(\theta_{d-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varpi^{2}\Theta_{d-1}\left(\theta_{d-1}\right).

where

and

3
Harmonic oscilator

Within the Dunkl formalism, the energy spectrum and the eigenfunctions of the harmonic oscillator in three dimensions were determined in
[
35
]
. Here, we extend those solutions to d-dimensions. Using Eqs. (
33
) and (
34
) we express the radial component of the Dunkl-Schrödinger equation with the isotropic harmonic oscillator potential

\left[\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{d-1+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)}{r}\frac{d}{dr}-\frac{m^{2}\omega^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}r^{2}-\frac{\varpi^{2}}{r^{2}}+\frac{2mE}{\hbar^{2}}\right]U\left(r\right)=0.

(49)

Here,
\omega
denotes the vibration frequency of the particle. We then introduce a new variable

\rho=\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}r^{2},

(50)

and transform Eq. (
49
) to

\left[\rho\frac{d^{2}}{d\rho^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(d+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)\right)\frac{d}{d\rho}-\frac{\rho}{4}-\frac{\varpi^{2}}{4\rho}+\frac{E}{2\hbar\omega}\right]U\left(\rho\right)=0.

(51)

Following an examination of the radial function’s behavior at the origin and infinity, we put forward the following solution to the wave function.

U\left(\rho\right)=e^{-\frac{\rho}{2}}\rho^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}}\digamma\left(\rho\right).

(52)

Subsequently, we obtain the following equation for
\digamma\left(\rho\right)
, which mimics the confluent hypergeometric differential equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\rho\frac{d^{2}}{d\rho^{2}}+\left[\frac{d+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)}{2}+2\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)-\rho\right]\frac{d}{d\rho}\\
+\frac{E}{2\hbar\omega}-\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)-\frac{d+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)}{4}\end{array}\right\}\digamma\left(\rho\right)=0.

(53)

So that we express the radial solution in the form of

U\left(\rho\right)=\mathcal{C}\text{ \ }\rho^{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}}e^{-\frac{\rho}{2}}\mathbf{F}\left(a,b;\rho\right),

(54)

where
\mathcal{C}
is the normalization factor to be determined and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}a=\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)+\frac{d+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)}{4}-\frac{E}{2\hbar\omega}\\
b=\frac{d+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)}{2}+2\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)\end{array}\right..

(55)

We now proceed to derive the energy spectrum. Given that the solutions are finite at infinity, we derive the general quantum condition from Eq. (
54

\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)+\frac{d+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)}{4}-\frac{E}{2\hbar\omega}=-n,\text{ \ with\ \ }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(56)

Therefore, the energy spectrum reads:

E_{n,\ell_{1},...,\ell_{d-1},d}=2\hbar\omega\left(n+\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}+\frac{d+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)}{4}\right).

(57)

Coulomb potential

In this section, we address another significant potential energy in physics. In one of our earlier papers

\left[\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{d-1+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)}{r}\frac{d}{dr}+\frac{2me^{2}}{\hbar^{2}r}-\frac{\varpi^{2}}{r^{2}}+\frac{2mE}{\hbar^{2}}\right]U\left(r\right)=0.

(65)

Here, let us assume the solution to be in the following form

U\left(r\right)=r^{2\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)}e^{-\eta r}\Xi\left(r\right),

(66)

where
\eta=\frac{\sqrt{-2mE}}{\hbar}.
Then, Eq. (

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\left(\frac{4\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)+d-1+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)}{r}-2\eta\right)\frac{d}{dr}\\
-\frac{4\eta\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)}{r}-\eta\frac{d-1+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)}{r}+\frac{2me^{2}}{\hbar^{2}r}\end{array}\right\}\Xi\left(r\right)=0.

(67)

Here, we notice that the current differential equation of
\Xi\left(r\right)

can be reduced to a confluent hypergeometric differential equation if the following substitution is made:

\zeta=2\eta r.

(68)

Once the elementary mathematical operations have been completed, we get the differential equation

\zeta\frac{d^{2}}{d\zeta^{2}}\Xi\left(\zeta\right)+\left(B-\zeta\right)\frac{d}{d\zeta}\Xi\left(\zeta\right)-A\Xi\left(\zeta\right)=0,

(69)

which has solution in terms of the confluent hypergeometric function
\mathbf{F}\left(A,B;\zeta\right)
with the arguments

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)+\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)+\frac{d-1}{2}-\frac{me^{2}}{\eta\hbar^{2}},

(70)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)+2\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)+d-1.

(71)

Subsequently, the eigenfunctions can be represented

U\left(r\right)=\mathcal{N}\text{ \ }r^{2\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)}e^{-\eta r}\mathbf{F}\left(A,B;2\eta r\right),

(72)

where the normalization constant, denoted by
\mathcal{N}
, is yet to be determined. Skipping this, we now turn our attention to the energy spectrum. In consideration of the finite nature of solutions at infinity, we put forth the following quantum conditions

2\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)+\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)+\frac{d-1}{2}-\frac{me^{2}}{\eta\hbar^{2}}=-n\text{, \ with\ \ }n\in\mathbb{N}.

E_{n,\ell_{1},...,\ell_{d-1},d}=-\left(\frac{me^{4}}{2\hbar^{2}}\right)\frac{1}{\left[n+2\left(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d-1}\right)+\left(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}+\cdots+\mu_{d}\right)+\frac{d-1}{2}\right]^{2}}.

This case suggests that the significance of the quantum numbers and Wigner parameters is diminished in higher dimensions.

5
Conclusion

The systems under investigation may yield disparate solutions contingent on the principle of parity. The conventional calculation techniques do not yield solutions that are directly dependent on parity. Nevertheless, research conducted in recent decades has demonstrated that utilizing the Dunkl operator in lieu of the conventional partial derivative operator facilitates the derivation of solutions that are dependent on parity. Furthermore, these solutions are influenced by additional free parameters, known as the Wigner deformation parameter. In this paper, we have solved the Dunkl-Schrödinger equation in any arbitrary dimension initially with the Cartesian coordinates. Subsequently, the d-dimensional polar coordinates were employed, demonstrating that the Dunkl-Schrödinger equation was separated into angular and radial parts, with the angular part equation solved in terms of Jacobi polynomials. The exact bound states solutions were then obtained for three systems: the isotropic harmonic oscillator, the pseudoharmonic oscillator, and the Coulomb potential. The results revealed that the energy eigenvalue functions are altered under the influence of Dunkl deformation in any arbitrary dimensions. Therefore, the deformation constant can be employed to achieve a precise alignment between experimental and theoretical outcomes.
