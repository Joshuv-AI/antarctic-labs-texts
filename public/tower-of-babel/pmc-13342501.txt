Potential of Rumex japonicus Houtt. in alkaline soil management
Authors: Yueming Zhao, Xianghua Zuo, Yuqiao Gong, Jian You, Xia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soil alkalization is a major abiotic factor that has a detrimental impact on soil health and crop productivity. To restore and enhance the use of alkaline soils, it is critical to search for alkaline-tolerant plant species with economic value. In this study, we investigated the potential of Rumex japonicus Houtt. as a commercial medicinal crop in alkaline soil. In this study, a pot experiment was conducted to compare the morphological traits of R. japonicus planted in alkaline and neutral soils. Specifically, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was used to determine the content of four main medicinal ingredients, while polymerase chain reaction-restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR-RFLP) was utilized to assess the soil microbial community diversity. Additionally, we analyzed the variations in soil physiochemical properties at different plant growth stages. Results showed that R. japonicus maintained robust morphological development under alkaline conditions, with no significant reduction in overall biomass compared to neutral soil. Furthermore, natural alkaline soil conditions significantly induced the accumulation of targeted secondary metabolites, increasing physcion content by 58.21% (S7 stage). The cultivation of R. japonicus improved soil fertility, enhanced soil enzyme activity, and induced distinct shifts in dominant soil microbial community profiles. These results indicate that R. japonicus has the potential to be grown as a commercial crop in alkaline soil. This study findings provide theoretical support for the selection of plant species for future utilization and restoration of alkaline soil.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52219-x.

Introduction

Soil alkalization has become a global concern with its ongoing expansion, as it may reduce agricultural production, cause soil desertification, and harm ecological health1–3. Approximately 800 million hectares of land have been affected by salinity, with more than half of that being alkaline4–6. Previous studies have shown that natural alkaline soil conditions can be more harmful to plants than saline stress due to its high pH stress in addition to osmotic stress and excess ion toxicity5–7. Therefore, plant performance in such environments is rarely controlled by pH alone; rather, it is shaped by a combination of these physicochemical factors. An elevated pH level can disrupt the integrity of root cell structure and hinder the availability of essential nutrients, resulting in a substantial reduction in agricultural productivity8,9. Soil pH may also lead to a decline in bacterial richness and diversity, due to its strong effect on soil physiochemical properties as well as soil microbial community and activity10–12. This can direct the suppression of soil ecological functions and affect plant growth13,14. Therefore, it is important to study the changes in plants and soil microorganisms under alkaline soil conditions.

Researchers are finding ways to make use of saline-alkaline soil and, where possible, improve the alkaline soil and generate economic benefits in the process. Growing salt-tolerant plants is considered an easy-to-use, cost-effective, and highly sustainable solution.15–17. Plants can modify soil to favor their survival through the production of aboveground and belowground litter, root activity, the secretion of various root exudates, and more18,19. These processes can lead to changes in soil physicochemical properties and the composition of its microbial communities19–21. Taking advantage of this, researchers are finding ways to make better use of saline-alkaline soil, and where possible, improve the alkaline soil and obtain economic benefits from the process. Take advantage of salt-tolerant plants is considered an easy-to-use, cost-effective, and highly sustainable solution. However, there are currently few plant materials suitable for this purpose22. At present, the research on cultivating stress-resistant plants in alkaline soil mainly focuses on food crops such as oat23, sorghum24, and rice25, and forage crops, such as Miscanthus26, tall wheat grass27 and alfalfa28. However, few medicinal plants have been evaluated for their potential in the ecological restoration and productive use of alkaline soils. Cultivating medicinal species adapted to such conditions can offer both pharmacological value and significant economic returns in marginalized environments. Therefore, identifying new species with high ecological resilience and commercial potential is essential for enhancing the sustainable utilization of alkaline soil.

Rumex japonicus Houtt., locally referred to as “Yang-Ti” in China, is a perennial herb widely distributed across East Asia and Russia. It naturally thrives in diverse habitats, including wetlands, riverbanks, and ditches, at elevations ranging from 30 to 3,400 m. Its inherent adaptation to these dynamic and often mineral-rich environments suggests a strong physiological potential for cultivation in natural alkaline soil conditions29. R. japonicus has been used as a kind of traditional Chinese medicine and has been widely employed in folk medicine to mediate jaundice, constipation, scabies, uterine hemorrhage, and other diseases29,30. Its antioxidant, antibacterial, antisepsis, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer effects have also been recognized and explored in modern medicine: It could provide effective treatments for human skin diseases31; and may suppress tumor formation, inflammation, and apoptosis via inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest32–37. 2-methoxystypandrone isolated from R. japonicus could potentially serve as an early lead compound for leukemia treatment38, and it also can protect dopaminergic neurons in both in vivo and in vitro Parkinson’s disease models39,40. In conclusion, R. japonicus has antioxidant, antibacterial, antisepsis, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer effects31,37. The root and leaf of R. japonicus contain phytoconstituents of pharmaceutical significance, such as emodin, chrysophanol, physcion, rhein, physcion 8-O-β-glucopyranoside, nepodin and more41,42. Specifically, emodin, chrysophanol, physcion, and rhein as anthraquinone derivatives are the main medicinal components in R. japonicus30,32. Apart from its medicinal value, R. japonicus as one of the forage crops, it also has a highly nutritious for animal feed43. However, little is known yet about the ecological applications of R. japonicus, except that it can be used as a microbial aggregation site for in-situ purification of river pollution44, let al.one its impact on soil ecosystem, especially in alkaline soil.

Therefore, it is important to study the changes in plants and soil microorganisms under alkaline soil conditions. In this study, we hypothesized that (1) under natural alkaline soil conditions, R. japonicus can maintain its morphological development by strategically accumulating specific secondary metabolites as a physiological defense mechanism; and (2) the cultivation of this plant will actively ameliorate the hostile rhizosphere by enhancing key soil enzyme activities and inducing distinct shifts in dominant microbial community profiles, thereby forming a positive plant-soil feedback loop to mitigate nutrient limitations.

In order to investigate the effect of alkaline soil on the morphological characteristics and medicinal ingredients of R. japonicus as well as to assess its influence on the quality and shifts in dominant microbial profiles of both neutral and alkaline soil. This study, polymerase chain reaction - restriction fragment length polymorphisms (PCR-RFLP) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) were conducted to analyze the soil microbial community and the content of four main medicinal ingredients of R. japonicus at different growth stages under alkaline and neutral soil conditions. The objectives of this study were: (1) to investigate the effect of alkaline soil on plant morphological characteristics and medicinal ingredients; (2) to assess the influence of R. japonicus on the soil quality and altered microbial banding patterns of both neutral and alkaline soil. The findings of this study will provide theoretical support for the potential use of new plant species in alkaline soil management.

Materials and methods

Experiment design, sampling, and determination of plant morphological characteristic

The experiment was conducted in the indoor laboratory of Jilin University (Changchun, Jilin Province, China). In this study, there were two experiment groups: the alkaline soil group (pH = 8.25 ± 0.02) and the neutral soil group (pH = 7.23 ± 0.01), and each experiment group has a corresponding control group (bare soil without planting anything).The physicochemical properties of the two soils are shown in Table 1. Rumex japonicus Houtt. (R. japonicus) were planted in the experiment groups, and all groups were cultured under the same environmental conditions. In each experiment group, there were three replicates, with each replicate containing two pots at every time point. The control group contained three pots at each time point. Two R. japonicus plants were planted in each pot. We established eight time points, from S0 to S7, with S0 representing the initial soil condition before planting R. japonicus. Each time point was 17 days apart. Altogether, there were 144 pots in this study (Experiment group: 2 soil types × 8 time points × 3 replicates × 2 pots = 96 pots; Control group: 2 soil types × 8 time points × 3 pots = 48 pots).

The plant material used in this study was rigorously identified as Rumex japonicus Houtt. by Dr. Wei Zhao (Director of the Germplasm Resource Bank, College of Life Sciences, Jilin University). A formal voucher specimen has been deposited in the publicly accessible National & Local United Engineering Laboratory for Chinese Herbal Medicine Breeding and Cultivation at Jilin University (Accession number: ZhaoW-2016-0325). This laboratory operates as an open public collaboration platform. The specific seed samples used in this experiment are available free of charge upon request to the corresponding author. We confirm that all experimental research and field studies on plants, including the sourcing of plant materials, were conducted in strictly accordance with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation. Plants were germinated and grown in sand before the experiment began. When the plants grew three leaves, they were transplanted into pots. The selected pot was a PVC pipe with a height of 30 cm and a diameter of 16 cm, containing a polyacrylamide plastic bag inside the pipe. Each pipe was filled with soil up to about 5 cm away from the top. The pot experiment was conducted in a controlled indoor environment, maintained at a constant temperature of 25 °C with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark photoperiod. The 250 W agricultural sodium lamp was selected as the supplemental light source equipment, providing a light intensity of 1784 lx. All the pots were watered every other week with a volume of 300 ml each time.

The first soil sample was collected on May 31, 2017. After that, every 17 days and during each sampling period, the topsoil (about 3 cm thick) in the pot was removed, and a soil sample was collected. In each group, the collected soil samples were evenly mixed into one sample, sieved (< 2 mm), and divided into two parts. One part was air-dried for the measurement of soil properties, and the other part was stored at -80 °C till DNA extraction.

Plant samples were collected at the same time as the soil samples. Number of leaves (LN), maximum leaf length (MLL), maximum leaf width (MLW), root length (RL), root diameter (RD), and root surface area (RSA) were recorded after sampling. The aboveground and belowground parts of the samples were separated, bagged, and marked, placed in the oven at 105 °C for 30 min, and then baked at 80 °C to constant weight. After cooling in the dryer, the dried samples were weighed (accurate to 0.0001 g), and then ground with a mortar, sieved (< 0.25 mm), bagged, and stored away from light for later use. The sum of the weight of all individual parts was considered the total plant biomass, and biomass allocation to aboveground (AGB) or belowground (BGB) parts was calculated as T = (biomass of each plant part/plant total biomass) × 100%.

Measurement of soil properties

Soil properties were measured as described in references45,46. Soil pH was measured with a soil: water ratio of 1:5 using a digital pH meter. The water content of the soil (WC) was determined by drying at 105 °C for 24 h. Soil organic carbon (SOC) was determined by the potassium dichromate-concentrated sulfuric acid oxidation method. Total nitrogen (TN) was quantified on an automatic Kjeldahl nitrogen meter K1306 (Sonnen, Shanghai, China). Catalase activity (CAT) was assessed through the potassium permanganate titration method. Alkaline phosphatase activity (ALP) was measured spectrophotometrically at 660 nm using the p-nitrophenyl phosphate salt method. Urease activity (UE) was determined spectrophotometrically at 578 nm.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

Leaf and root samples of R. japonicus were extracted by ultrasonic extraction for 1 h at 50 °C with methanol and then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was absorbed through the 0.22 μm filter membrane with an injector. The ACCHROM S6000 HPLC system and X-charge C18 5-µm column (150 mm × 4.6 mm; Acchrom Tech, Beijing, China) was used in this study. A gradient with solvents A (methanol) and B (0.1% phosphoric acid) was used to elute. The column temperature was maintained at a consistent 40 °C, the flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, the wavelength was 254 nm, and the sample size was 20 µL. Gradient elution conditions: 0–5 min, 60% A, 40% B; 5–22 min, 65% A, 35% B; 22–30 min, 75% A, 25% B; 30–35 min, 80% A, 20% B; 35–40 min, 90% A, 10% B. Standard curves were established by analyzing standards emodin, chrysophanol, physcion, and rhein with HPLC following the chromatographic conditions above, and were used for calculating the results of the samples. The HPLC analyses indicated that emodin, chrysophanol, physcion, and rhein accumulated in both aboveground and belowground parts of R. japonicus. In the following parts of this article, the content of medicinal ingredients is represented by a three-letter abbreviation. The first letter represents the medicinal ingredient, the second letter represents the soil conditions in which R. japonicus is grown, and the third letter represents whether the medicinal ingredient comes from the aboveground or underground part of the R. japonicus. For example, in the abbreviation EAB, “E” represents emodin, “A” represents alkaline soil conditions, and “B” represents the belowground part of R. japonicus.

Polymerase chain reaction–restriction fragment length polymorphisms (PCR-RFLP)

Soil DNA was extracted using the Soil DNA Kit D5625 (OMEGA, Guangzhou, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quantity and quality of genomic DNA was checked on 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis. The 16S rDNA gene of bacteria and actinomycetes was amplified using primers 63F (5’-CAGGCCTAACACATGCAAGTC-3’) and 1387R (5’-GGGCGGWGTGTACAAGGC-3’) as well as ACF (5’-GGCGGCCTATCAGCTTGTTG-3’) and ACR (5’-CCGTACTCCCCAGGCGGGG-3’), respectively. PCR amplification of the ITS region of fungi was performed using primers ITS1 (5’-TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG-3’) and ITS4 (5’-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3’).

The RFLP analyses of the 16 S rDNA gene of bacteria and actinomycetes were performed using the restriction endonuclease Rsa I and Hae III, respectively, while the RFLP analyses of the ITS region of fungi were performed using the restriction endonuclease Csp6 I (TaKaRa Bio, Beijing, China). A 3 µl/ 7 µl/ 5 µl aliquot of PCR-amplified product from bacteria/ fungi/ actinobacteria was digested with the restriction endonucleases for 3 h at 37 °C in a 10 µg reaction mixture resolved on 2% agarose gels for 2.5 h. The gels were stained with ethidium bromide, visualized with a Gel Imaging Analyzing System (WD-9403 F, LIUYI Biotechnology, Beijing, China), and analyzed by Quantity One 4.5.2 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., California, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data were tested for normality before performing statistical analyses. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was performed to assess the effects of soil conditions (alkaline vs. neutral) and time, or plant part and time, on plant traits and medicinal ingredients (applied in Fig. 1). Differences in medicinal ingredient content from different parts of R. japonicus grown in different soil conditions were determined using Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. The mean values and standard deviation (SD) of the means were calculated. All aforementioned statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad PRISM 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., Boston, USA).

To evaluate the relative influence of R. japonicus on soil properties and microbial diversity, the response ratio (RR) was calculated according to previously established methods47. The RR was calculated using the natural logarithm of the ratio between the treatment and control groups, specifically:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left[RR=\mathrm{ln}\left(\frac{{X}_{treatment}}{{X}_{control}}\right)\right]$$\end{document}

Xtreatment is the value of the variable in the specific treatment group, and Xcontrol is the value of the variable in the corresponding control group. Spearman correlation analysis was used to examine the correlations among plant traits, soil properties, microbial profiles, and medicinal ingredient content, and was performed using the corrplot package of R statistical language (version 4.3.3)48. P < 0.05 was considered significant for all analyses.

Crucially, the two natural soils utilized in this study possessed different baseline properties (Table 1). Directly comparing absolute temporal changes across these soils could introduce systematic bias. The application of the ln RR metric effectively resolved this issue. By mathematically normalizing the temporal data against their respective initial baseline or control, ln RR eliminates the background differences. This allowed us to rigorously compare the relative magnitude of the plant-driven rhizosphere modifications across the two distinct soil ecosystems.

Results

Plant characteristics of R. japonicus

Alkaline soil conditions significantly increased the maximum leaf length, maximum leaf width, and root surface area of R. japonicus, while soil pH has little influence on other plant characteristics tested in this study (Fig. 1). All plant characteristics underwent significant changes over time. The combined effect of soil pH and time only has a significant impact on root surface area (Fig. 1f). Moreover, the aboveground biomass of R. japonicus planted in alkaline soil conditions appeared to be higher than those planted in neutral soil conditions, but these differences were not significant (P = 0.0847; Fig. 1g).

Soil physiochemical properties and enzyme activities

Response ratios of R. japonicus planting effect were calculated to demonstrate the relative changes in soil properties compared to the control groups (Fig. 2). In general, water content and soil organic carbon markedly decreased over time in both alkaline and neutral soil conditions (Fig. 2a, b). Planting R. japonicus has positive effects on soil total nitrogen, catalase activity, alkaline phosphatase activity, and urease activity. Notably, R. japonicus has a stronger effect on urease activity in alkaline soil than in neutral soil (Fig. 2f).

Soil microbial profiles

R. japonicus significantly altered the α-diversity (Shannon-Wiener index, Pielou evenness index) of the soil microbial community, and the effect was mostly positive across various time points (Fig. 3). R. japonicus has stronger effects on the bacterial Shannon-Wiener index and actinobacterial Pielou evenness index in alkaline soil than in neutral soil (Fig. 3a, c). The Fungal Shannon-Wiener index and Pielou evenness index in alkaline soil both reached their highest values at S3, while the neutral soil group did not exhibit a similar trend (Fig. 3b, e).

Medicinal component content

We used HPLC to measure the content of four medicinal ingredients (chrysophanol, emodin, physcion, and rhein) in the above and belowground parts of R. japonicus grown in neutral and alkaline soils, and their changes over time. Generally, the content of chrysophanol, physcion, and rhein in the belowground part of R. japonicus was significantly higher than that in the aboveground part, while the aboveground part of R. japonicus contained more emodin than the belowground part (Fig. 4a-d).

There was no significant difference in the content of chrysophanol in the R. japonicus grown in alkaline soil and neutral soil (Fig. 4a). CBA content at each time point decreased in the following order: S2 (2.7070 µg·g− 1) > S7 (1.9820 µg·g− 1) > S3 (1.2958 µg·g− 1) > S6 (1.2426 µg·g− 1) > S1 (1.2191 µg·g− 1) > S5 (1.0495 µg·g− 1) > S4 (0.8329 µg·g− 1) (Fig. 4e). Plant part, time, and their interaction all has significant effects on the content of chrysophanol (Fig. 4e, i).

Alkaline soil conditions significantly decreased the emodin content in the aboveground part of R. japonicus, while having a limited effect on the emodin content in the belowground part (Fig. 4b). Even though emodin has a higher content in the aboveground part of the R. japonicus, we focused on its content in both parts because the content of other medicinal ingredients was higher in the belowground part of the R. japonicus. EAA content at each time point decreased in the following order: S5 (2.7515 µg·g− 1) > S7 (2.6527 µg·g− 1) > S4 (1.4811 µg·g− 1) > S2 (1.3056 µg·g− 1) > S1 (1.0968 µg·g− 1) > S3 (0.9300 µg·g− 1) > S6 (0.5112 µg·g− 1) (Fig. 4f). EBA content at each time point decreased in the following order: S2 (1.6337 µg·g− 1) > S7 (1.3547 µg·g− 1) > S1 (1.2791 µg·g− 1) > S3 (1.0883 µg·g− 1) > S6 (0.8690 µg·g− 1) > S5 (0.7218 µg·g− 1) > S4 (0.5218 µg·g− 1). Plant part, time, and their interaction all has significant effects on the content of emodin (Fig. 4f, j).

Alkaline soil conditions has a significant positive effect on the physcion content in the belowground part of R. japonicus while having a limited effect on the emodin content in the belowground part (Fig. 4c). PBA content at each time point decreased in the following order: S2 (1.9153 µg·g− 1) > S7 (1.7172 µg·g− 1) > S1 (1.5718 µg·g− 1) > S6 (1.1836 µg·g− 1) > S3 (0.8140 µg·g− 1) > S5 (0.7944 µg·g− 1) > S4 (0.6104 µg·g− 1) (Fig. 4g). Plant part, time, and their interaction all has significant effects on the content of physcion (Fig. 4g, k).

Soil pH has no significant effect on the content of rhein (Fig. 4d). Relationships between medicinal component content, soil variables, and microbial properties

We further verified the interaction pattern of plant morphological variables, soil properties, soil microbial profiles, and medicinal component content through Spearman correlation analysis (Fig. 5). Aboveground biomass and belowground biomass of R. japonicus, regardless of the soil condition in which it was planted, were strongly correlated with each other and with plant morphological variables (P < 0.05; Fig. 5). Surprisingly, soil pH only has a significant correlation with RBA (P < 0.05; Fig. 5a). Catalase activities showed significant negative correlations with PBA and RBA, while urease activities were positively correlated with CBA (P < 0.05; Fig. 5a).

Under neutral soil conditions, EBN was strongly correlated with plant morphological variables (P < 0.05; Fig. 5c). Soil TN was negatively correlated with CBN, PBN, and RBN under neutral soil conditions. The most surprising is catalase activities has strong positive correlations with both plant aboveground biomass and belowground plant biomass under neutral soil conditions. EAN has positive correlations with bacterial and actinobacterial community evenness, as well as aboveground plant biomass (P < 0.05; Fig. 5c, d). RBN and CBN were significantly correlated under neutral soil conditions (ρ = 0.89, P < 0.01; Fig. 5d), while the relationship was strong but not statistically significant in alkaline soil (ρ = 0.75, P = 0.052; Fig. 5b).

Discussion

Soil is home to a diverse variety of microbial communities11,49. Changes in soil environmental variables may lead to alternations in soil microbial community and its functions. Soil pH has been proven to have a striking effect on soil processes such as the decomposition of organic matter and nitrification, which might indirectly affect plant growth50–53. Plants themselves can also affect soil pH through plant residues, litter, and root exudates54. These processes may all affect the activity and community of soil microorganisms. However, little is known about how plants, especially medicinal plants, and soil microbiomes would change when facing high soil pH.

In this study, natural alkaline and neutral soils were utilized instead of artificially simulated stress substrates. We acknowledge that these natural soils differ in baseline properties, making it a multi-variable environment rather than a single-factor pH stress. However, this experimental design was intentionally chosen to maximize ecological realism. Using authentic soils better simulates the complex wild environment of saline-alkaline fields. This approach provides a more accurate and practical evaluation of the ecological adaptation and medicinal value of R. japonicus for future agricultural cultivation. This study shows that the soil type (primarily differing in pH, along with variations in TN, OM and MC; Table 1) has a limited effect on most of the tested plant morphological traits (Fig. 1). While high pH is a critical stressor, the overall plant performance is fundamentally shaped by the comprehensive physicochemical profile of the soil. R. japonicus growing in alkaline soil conditions showed a significant increase in maximum leaf length, maximum leaf width, and root surface area, compared to those in neutral soil (Fig. 1b,c,f). Higher maximum leaf length and higher maximum leaf width result in a larger leaf area, which contributes to increased biomass accumulation55. The aboveground biomass of R. japonicus was slightly higher in alkaline soil than in neutral soil (Fig. 1g). This result is consistent with the findings related to leaf traits and suggests the yield of R. japonicus as animal feed does not reduce even when planted in alkaline soil. However, in previous studies, alkaline soil conditions significantly reduced total plant biomass and shoot biomass, and may have a negative effect on root biomass3,56. Furthermore, plants experiencing natural alkaline soil conditions may develop smaller leaves to minimize water absorption, aiding in moisture conservation and preventing additional soil salinity escalation57.

Classical literature widely reports that natural alkaline soil conditions inhibits plant growth. It typically reduces leaf size and impairs root development in conventional crops. However, R. japonicus defied this typical inhibition pattern in our study. It exhibited increased leaf size and root surface area under alkaline conditions. This paradoxical robust growth can be explained by the plant’s evolutionary background. R. japonicus naturally inhabits wetlands, riverbanks, and ditches. These native environments frequently experience alkaline conditions. Consequently, the plant has evolved strong alkaliphilic traits. For this specific species, high pH is not necessarily a severe environmental stressor. Instead, it represents a compatible ecological niche. To capitalize on this, R. japonicus actively expanded its root surface area. This functional adaptation allowed for efficient nutrient foraging. Therefore, the combination of its evolutionary tolerance and the specific soil fertility explains its exceptional performance under alkaline conditions. Furthermore, it should be noted that while pH is a primary distinguishing factor between the two experimental soils, plant performance is rarely governed by a single variable. The soils also differ in other physicochemical properties, such as MC and nutrient availability. Therefore, the observed morphological adaptations and biomass maintenance of R. japonicus in the alkaline soil are likely a result of the combined effects of elevated pH, distinct nutrient profiles and other influencing factors, rather than pH alone. It is important to note that the differences in plant performance observed between the two experimental treatments stem from the overall soil conditions—a complex combination of pH, MC, nutrient profiles (Table 1). Nevertheless, soil pH is recognized as the primary overarching driver in this system. High pH not only directly induces physiological stress on root cell integrity but also acts as the master variable that dictates the solubility and availability of essential nutrients, as well as shaping microbial niches. Therefore, while the synergistic effects of other soil traits cannot be ignored, the extreme pH level remains the fundamental mechanism underlying the morphological adaptations of R. japonicus in alkaline soils.

Soil conditions regulate plant growth, and plant growth can in turn affect soil properties and soil microbial community. In the case of R. japonicus,, water content and soil organic carbon in both alkaline and neutral soil decreased, whereas their total nitrogen, catalase activities, alkaline phosphatase activities, and urease activities increased (Fig. 2). Given that nitrogen limitation occurs in most ecosystems, the increased soil nitrogen resulting from the growth of R. japonicus may enhance soil fertility and subsequently boost soil productivity58–60. Soil enzyme activity is a window to understanding the biochemical processes that occur in the soil61, and catalase can serve as a biochemical marker for intracellular biological activity, providing insights into the community responses to exogenous stress62. Alkaline phosphatase is useful for assessing organic phosphorus mineralization potential and biological activity63,64, and hence catalyzes the hydrolysis of urea to produce nitrogen available for plant growth, making it a crucial component in agricultural production65. This study findings suggest that R. japonicus has the ability to promote soil nitrogen and phosphorus cycling, be it in alkaline or neutral soil.

Plant growth profoundly impacts soil microbiomes. This study reveals that R. japonicus induced significant shifts in dominant microbial profiles in both soils (Fig. 3). This aligns with previous research44. These shifts suggest a potential recruiting effect on specific soil microbes. Plant roots actively alter soil properties and stimulate microorganisms67. Specifically, root exudates influence microbial community structures. This selection process is highly species-specific66,67. However, we must acknowledge the methodological limitations of our microbial analysis. We utilized PCR-RFLP to assess these microbial changes. This technique is relatively coarse. It only visualizes shifts in dominant microbial groups. It cannot identify rare species or provide precise taxonomic resolution. Therefore, our observations represent preliminary profiling. Future studies must employ high-throughput sequencing, such as 16 S or ITS amplicon sequencing. This will provide a comprehensive and high-resolution understanding of the root-associated microbiome.

Regarding medicinal value, alkaline conditions influenced compound accumulation. Various types of exudation profiles may favor the growth of microorganisms that have positive effects on plant development68. The concentration of four underground ingredients peaked at S2. However, their levels at S7 remained relatively high (Fig. 4). Crucially, overall plant biomass reached its maximum at S7 (Fig. 1). As previously discussed, total medicinal yield depends on both concentration and biomass. Therefore, harvesting R. japonicus at S7 maximizes the total medicinal yield (Table 2). Additionally, emodin content in the aboveground parts was notably high at S7. Consequently, S7 represents the optimal harvest time. This timing ensures maximum economic benefits for both medicinal extraction and animal feed utilization.

Plants can adjust the sort and quantities of secondary metabolites in response to changes in their growth environment69. Therefore, environmental conditions can induce alterations in the growth of medicinal plants, affecting the concentration and efficacy of their bioactive compounds70. Soil pH has been considered as one of the key factors that influence medicinal components69,71–73. This study finds that, soil pH improved the physcion content but has no significant effect on the chrysophanol, emodin, and rhein contents in the belowground part of R. japonicus (Fig. 4). These results might indicate that planting in alkaline soil does not harm the economic value of R. japonicus as medicinal plants, and can even enhance it. Soil pH has no significant effect on the medicinal ingredients of R. japonicus other than RBA (Fig. 5). It is possible that R. japonicus is not sensitive to pH changes, as it can inhabit wetlands along roadsides, riverbanks, and ditches, where soils can often be saline-alkaline. There were also researchers who found that Rumex dapibus, which belongs to the same genus as R. japonicus, can thrive on saline-alkaline soil74.

So, when we want to know if medicinal plants can be grown profitably under environmental stress, we have to tell two aspects: is the plant just making more active compounds because it’s stressed, and is it truly producing a stable amount that works for agricultural cultivation? Usually, abiotic stress induces the synthesis of secondary metabolites as a physiological defense mechanism, but at a severe cost to overall plant growth, ultimately resulting in a lower total medicinal yield (Total Yield = (Aboveground Biomass × Aboveground Concentration) + (Belowground Biomass × Belowground Concentration)). However, the results demonstrate a highly favorable balance for R. japonicus. As shown in Fig. 1, the overall biomass of R. japonicus in alkaline soil was effectively maintained. Consequently, the stable biomass, coupled with the enhanced or maintained concentrations of key active ingredients, translates to a robust and stable total medicinal yield per plant. This physiological coordination indicates that the accumulation of bioactive compounds in R. japonicus under alkaline conditions is not a desperate survival trade-off, but rather a stable production profile, fully validating its potential for large-scale commercial cultivation in alkaline soils.

The experiment results in this study show that different soil pH conditions did not affect most of the correlation patterns between variables, but the significance of some correlations changed (Fig. 5). Soil pH may indirectly influence the medicinal ingredient content of R. japonicus by affecting catalase activity and bacterial alpha diversity (Figure S1, 2). Previous researchers have found that pH affects the conformation of the enzyme, its binding to solid surfaces, and the ionization and solubility of substrates and cofactors75,76. Catalase has been found to improve bacterial growth and can positively impact microbial distribution, which aligns with the results in this study77–79. However, another plausible explanation might be that the pH gradient in this experiment was not wide enough to induce significant changes in R. japonicus. In addition, only one generation of R. japonicus has participated in this experiment, which may not be sufficient to induce variations in the mechanism. Otherwise, plant morphological traits has no significant correlation with medicinal component content under alkaline soil conditions, whereas no such correlation was observed in neutral soil conditions. It is also highlighted that root morphology does not appear to have a direct connection to the root metabolism involved in exudation, while there exists a clear correlation between the root exudate ability and composition and associated microbial communities66,80.

Beyond demonstrating basic morphological tolerance, the resilience of R. japonicus in alkaline soils is deeply intertwined with its pharmacological properties. The primary bioactive compounds of R. japonicus, particularly medicinal components such as physcion and emodin, are widely recognized not only for their medicinal efficacy but also for their crucial roles in plant stress physiology. Natural alkaline soil conditions typically induces the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative damage and the disruption of root cell integrity. Under such conditions, these secondary metabolites act as potent non-enzymatic antioxidants. This study finding that the physcion content significantly increased under alkaline conditions strongly suggests a stress-induced accumulation strategy. By upregulating the biosynthesis of these specific bioactive compounds, R. japonicus effectively scavenges ROS and mitigates cellular damage, utilizing its “pharmacological” traits as a primary physiological defense mechanism against high pH.

Furthermore, we hypothesize a potential plant-soil-microbe feedback mechanism. Secondary metabolites may act as key drivers in this process. Plants often exude these compounds to modify their rhizosphere. Our correlation analysis (Fig. 5) supports this possibility. Specific anthraquinones might act as signaling molecules. They could potentially recruit specific soil microbes. This recruitment may then stimulate soil biochemical processes. This hypothesis aligns with the enhanced soil enzyme activities observed in our study. Consequently, a modified microbiome could accelerate localized nutrient mineralization. However, we acknowledge that correlation does not establish causation. Direct profiling of root exudates was not performed in this study. Future research must measure these exudates directly. Functional microbial inoculation experiments are also required. These steps are essential to definitively confirm this ecological strategy.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that R. japonicus exhibits adaptability to alkaline soil conditions, maintaining stable morphological traits and biomass production comparable to those in neutral soils. While high soil pH acts as a primary stressor, it strategically induces the accumulation of specific secondary metabolites, such as physcion, without compromising the overall yield of other key medicinal ingredients. For practical agricultural application, harvesting R. japonicus at the S7 growth stage provides an optimal balance between maximal biomass yield and desirable bioactive compound concentrations for both medicinal and forage uses. Furthermore, cultivate R. japonicus could actively modifies its surrounding soil environment, enhancing enzyme activities, promoting nutrient cycling (particularly nitrogen), and inducing distinct shifts in the dominant soil microbial community profiles. According to these findings, R. japonicus is highly promising in two respects: it can be cultivated as a commercially plant material under alkaline conditions, and it can also serve as a suitable candidate for cultivation in alkaline soils and ecological restoration. Future investigations utilizing high-throughput sequencing technologies such as metagenome, along with long-term field trials, are warranted to further clarify the above-ground and below-ground interactive mechanisms.
