Precision neoadjuvant treatment with AI-assisted subtyping in HR+/HER2−breast cancer: A randomized, phase 2 trial FASCINATE-N
Authors: Jun-Jie Li, Lin-Xiaoxi Ma, Peng Ji, Li Chen, Jiong Wu, Guang-Yu Liu, Yi-Feng Hou, Gen-Hong Di, Zhen Hu, Yi-Zhou Jiang, Lei Fan, Zhong-Hua Wang, Ke-Da Yu, Zhi-Ming Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn this phase 2 trial (NCT05582499), patients with stage II–III hormone-receptor-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HR+/HER2−) breast cancer were categorized into endocrine-based and targeted-based groups based on previously proposed similarity network fusion (SNF) subtyping by digital pathology classification and were then randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive precision or control treatment. The primary endpoint was pathological complete response (pCR). Among 251 randomized patients, 49 were classified into an endocrine-based group and 202 into a targeted-based group. Patients receiving precision treatment had a significantly higher pCR rate (11.1% [90% confidence interval (CI), 6.8–16.8] vs. 4.0% [90% CI, 1.6–8.2]; p = 0.033), with the benefit mainly derived from the targeted-based group (13.9% in precision vs. 4.0% in control; p = 0.026). Toxicity was manageable. Our findings highlight that guiding neoadjuvant precision treatment by SNF subtyping in patients with HR+/HER2− breast cancer showed potential clinical benefits, with improvement of pCR in the targeted-based group and better tolerance in the endocrine-based group.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignant tumor for women worldwide, of which 70% are hormone-receptor-positive (HR+)/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2−) subtype.1 Despite advances in treatment for breast cancer, the cumulative risk of distant recurrence at 20 years remains around 40% in high-risk HR+/HER2− early breast cancer.2 Neoadjuvant therapy may improve the operable rate and breast-conserving rate, and the current strategies for HR+/HER2− disease include neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NCT), neoadjuvant endocrine therapy (NET), and NCT/NET combined with targeted therapies or immune checkpoint inhibitors.3,4 However, the pathological complete response (pCR) rate is relatively low, ranging from 0% to 25%, while the appropriate precision-based treatment strategies have not yet been conclusively determined.4,5,6,7 Therefore, it is important to further explore appropriate neoadjuvant treatment strategies.

HR+/HER2− breast cancer exhibits significant heterogeneity, and numerous efforts have been made to interpret its molecular characteristics. While cohorts like Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC), Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC), and Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) have provided insights, limitations in multi-omics data or sample size have constrained a comprehensive understanding.8,9,10 Our team previously applied multi-omics data and similarity network fusion (SNF) to divide HR+/HER2− breast cancer into two groups.11 Endocrine-based group (SNF1: canonical luminal) represented a group of patients resembling the traditional luminal breast cancer most likely to be sensitive to endocrine therapy, while the targeted-based group included several SNF subtypes (SNF2: immunogenic, SNF3: proliferative, and SNF4: RTK-driven) with distinct biological and clinical features and might derive benefit from subtype-specific targeted treatments, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, PARP inhibitors, and RTK inhibitors. The SNF subtypes were further validated in independent datasets as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), METABRIC, and CPTAC cohorts, indicating the robustness and generalizability of the SNF subtyping system.

However, the cost and complexity of multi-omics sequencing might limit clinical application. To overcome this hurdle, we developed a digital pathology classification approach employing convolutional neural networks (CNNs) derived from pathology whole-slide images (WSIs) to classify molecular subtypes. Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have introduced specialized deep neural networks that surpass human performance in cancer cell identification, classification, prognosis, and treatment prediction using gigapixel WSIs. AI allows for combining image analysis with molecular data to uncover the genotype-phenotype relationships and assess genomic alterations quickly. Utilizing routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-processed slides, which reflect morphological changes, can enhance these discoveries.12,13,14 This technique achieved cross-validation area under the curve scores of 0.87 (SNF1), 0.81 (SNF2), and 0.78 (SNF3 and SNF4), indicating the feasibility for digital pathological and deep-learning-based subtype differentiation. Besides, previous trails like LINUX trial (NCT05594095) for metastatic patients and FINEST study (NCT04215003) for neoadjuvant patients of HR+/HER2− laid the efficacy of SNF subtyping.15,16 These works have laid foundational insights for the classification and personalized treatment of HR+/HER2− breast cancer, yet the efficacy of AI-assisted precision treatment based on the SNF subtyping system for HR+/HER2− breast cancer demands additional clinical validation.

We have conducted an umbrella phase 2 trial to evaluate subtyping-based neoadjuvant treatments for breast cancer.17 Here, we present the findings on AI-assisted subtyping in HR+/HER2− to identify effective neoadjuvant precision treatments based on endocrine-based and targeted-based groups.

Results

Patients characteristics

Between November 1, 2022 and July 31, 2024, 302 patients were screened, and 251 patients were randomly assigned (Figure 1). Through Bayesian monitoring method, the pCR rates of the first 60 patients in each group of target-based therapy indicated that the pCR rate was significantly higher in patients receiving precision treatment compared with those receiving control treatment, so the enrollment was stopped, and 251 patients were ultimately enrolled (Figure S1). Based on subtyping, 49 (19.5%) patients were assigned to the endocrine-based group, and 202 (80.5%) patients were assigned to the targeted-based group, which included 50 (19.9%) patients with the SNF2 subtype, 73 (29.1%) with the SNF3 subtype, and 79 (31.5%) with the SNF4 subtype. The baseline characteristics were well balanced (Table 1). The median age was 45 years (range, 25 to 70), and 144 (57.4%) patients were at stage III. Efficacy outcomes

The pCR rate was significantly higher in the precision treatment group compared with the control group (14/126, 11.1% [90% confidence interval (CI), 6.8–16.8] vs. 5/125, 4.0% [90% CI, 1.6–8.2], p = 0.033) (Figure 2A). In the endocrine-based group, no patients receiving endocrine treatment achieved pCR, whereas one patient in the control group did (0/25, 0.0% [90% CI, 0.0–11.3] vs. 1/24, 4.2% [90% CI, 0.2–18.3], p = 0.490) (Figure 2B). In the targeted-based group, the pCR rate was significantly higher in patients receiving precision treatment compared with those receiving control treatment (14/101, 13.9% [90% CI, 8.6–20.8] vs. 4/101, 4.0% [90% CI, 1.4–8.8], p = 0.026) (Figure 2C). Safety

Grade 3–4 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) occurred in 40.0% (10/25) of endocrine treatment group and 45.8% (11/24) of control group in the endocrine-based group and 40.6% (41/101) of precision treatment group and 43.6% (44/101) of control group in the targeted-based group (Table 3). The most common grade 3–4 TRAEs were hematological toxicities in all groups. A complete list of TRAEs in the targeted-based group is provided in Table S3. Among patients with SNF2 tumors who received SHR-1316 plus chemotherapy, grade 3–4 immune-related adverse events included hyperglycemia (7.7%, 2/26), an increase in thyroid-stimulating hormone (11.5%, 3/26), and hypothyroidism (11.5%, 3/26). For patients with SNF3 tumors who received fuzuloparib plus chemotherapy, the grade 3–4 TRAEs did not differ from that in the control group. Discussion

This study prospectively applies endocrine-based and targeted-based subtyping to guide precision neoadjuvant treatment with increased pCR rate for HR+/HER2− breast cancer based on the Bayesian approach. The results further demonstrated that in the endocrine-based group, endocrine therapy combined with CDK4/6 inhibitors was found equivalent to chemotherapy with better tolerance. In the targeted-based group, subtype-specific precision treatments led to improved pCR with tolerable toxicity.

We employed this method based on digital pathology and deep learning for SNF subtyping in the current study, as well as LINUX trial (NCT05594095) involving metastatic patients who progressed with CDK4/6 inhibitor treatment with endocrine therapy for metastatic disease and trials exploring precision therapy in the first-line setting (NCT05949541, NCT05205200, NCT05759546, NCT06612814, NCT05759572, and NCT06447623), confirming the value of SNF subtyping in guiding precision treatment. Confirmed ORRs in the LINUX trial were higher in targeted-based subtype therapy who received precision therapy instead of treatment of physician’s choice (TPC) (SNF2: precision therapy 65% versus 30%; SNF3: 40% versus 30%; and SNF4: 70% versus 20%), when precision therapy showed no benefit in endocrine-based subtype (SNF1: 10% versus 10%). Data from advanced HR+/HER2− breast cancer also notified the significance of precision therapy in targeted-based subtypes. Importantly, the neoadjuvant treatment strategy based on the SNF subtyping could be iteratively updated with advances in drug development, highlighting its potential for widespread application.

Previous research has demonstrated that endocrine-based group, also known as SNF1 subtype, was enriched with PAM50 luminal A subtype and most likely to be sensitive to endocrine therapy,11 and this trial verified the hypothesis that patients in the endocrine-based group are suitable for NET plus CDK4/6 inhibitor and could exempt NCT. The clinical value of NET with CDK4/6 inhibitor as neoadjuvant treatment remains debatable.18,19 Several clinical trials showed that NET plus CDK4/6 inhibitor led to an ORR of around 50% and pCR of around 5%, without selection of patients.20,21,22,23 Nevertheless, most patients included in these trials had relatively low tumor burden, with tumor stage of I to II. The phase III DAWNA-2 trial (NCT03966898) proved the efficacy and safety of CDK4/6 inhibitor dalpiciclib as a first-line treatment of HR+/HER2− breast cancer, with an absolute PFS benefit of 12.4 months (30.2 months with dalpiciclib and aromatase inhibitor AI versus 18.2 months with aromatase inhibitor; hazard ratio [HR] = 0.51, p < 0.0001). The DARLING 01 study (NCT05512780) with dalpiciclib and aromatase inhibitor for neoadjuvant therapy with 35 patients reached ORR of 55.2% and pCR of 2.7% with acceptable safety.24 Our trial included 67.3% of patients with stage III tumor and 96% patients with estrogen receptor (ER) >50% in the endocrine-based group, hindered the achievement of pCR, and resulted in a modest decrease in Ki67 expression. Still our data indicated that patients with high tumor burden breast cancer could also benefit from NET plus CDK4/6 inhibitor with similar spectrum of adverse events and lower rate of grade ≥3 hematological toxicities compared to NCT.20,21,22 This observation is of clinical importance because identifying patients suitable for NET based on intrinsic molecular characteristics provides an equivalent and less toxic option for them. The absence of pCR improvement in the endocrine-based group limits definitive conclusions regarding superiority, and these findings should be considered hypothesis generating.

Defining a subgroup of patients who will benefit from addition of targeted therapy is equally crucial. Our previous study revealed that targeted-based subtype was quite different from the endocrine-based subtype by intrinsic biological characteristics, which was made up by SNF2, SNF3, and SNF4 subtypes.11 SNF2 subtype was enriched in adaptive immune response pathways with higher expression of immune checkpoint biomarkers, which indicated the potential application of immunotherapy. According to our previous trial, patients with SNF2 subtype were more sensitive to immunotherapy (MP score 4–5: 45.5% versus 6.3%). Our results also revealed that patients with SNF2 subtype achieved a relatively higher pCR rate of 15.4% in chemotherapy with immunotherapy than 4.2% in chemotherapy alone. Our trial allowed to include patients with grade 2 disease with ER expression >10%, as well as a higher proportion of stage III diseases (57.4%), which was less likely to achieve pCR compared to KEYNOTE-756 and CheckMate 7FL.6,7 SNF3 subtype exhibited more cell-cycle-pathway activations with higher homologous recombination deficiency and chromosomal instability scores, revealing potential sensitive to PARP inhibitors.25 Several trials have been conducted to explore PARP inhibitor efficacy in cases of high homologous recombination deficiency score or germline or somatic BRCA mutations in the neoadjuvant setting.26,27 However, the efficacy and biomarkers of PARP inhibitors in HR+/HER2− breast cancer need to further clarify. The phase III FABULOUS study proved the efficacy and safety of fuzuloparib in metastatic HER2− breast cancer with germline BRCA1/2 mutations, with the improvement of median PFS (with an absolute improvement of 8.0 months in fuzuloparib and apatinib versus chemotherapy, HR = 0.27, p < 0.0001 and 3.7 months in fuzuloparib alone versus chemotherapy, HR = 0.49, p = 0.0004). Our study showed the pCR rate could achieve triple with the addition of PARP inhibitor in SNF3 subtype, indicating the potential value of SNF3 subtype as the biomarker of PARP inhibitor. Compared with other subtypes, SNF4 subtype was associated with the worst prognosis, suggested by retrospective cohort and real-world cohort and proven in prospective clinical trials like the LINUX trial, and upregulated RTK pathway with enrichment of stromal cells.11,15,28 Apatinib, a highly selective inhibitor to vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR)-2, has been demonstrated effective and tolerable in the treatment of metastatic breast cancer patients and neoadjuvant treatment of triple-negative breast cancer.29,30,31,32 In this study, we demonstrated that adding apatinib led to a higher pCR in patients with SNF4 tumors.

Given the disease heterogeneity and relatively low pCR rate, optimized neoadjuvant treatment strategies with the inclusion of novel agents for HR+/HER2− breast cancer are needed. The KEYNOTE-756 and CheckMate 7FL trials showed that adding immunotherapy to neoadjuvant chemotherapy significantly improved pCR rates for HR+/HER2− breast cancer.6,7 However, both trials had bias in enrollment, with KEYNOTE-756 including only grade 3 disease and CheckMate 7FL including grade 3 disease or grade 2 disease with ER expression of 1% to ≤10%. Although drugs of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) exhibited excellent efficacy in the metastatic setting, trials applying ADCs in the neoadjuvant setting, such as SOLTI VALENTINE, TALENT, and HELEN-015, demonstrated pCR rates of only around 5%.33,34,35,36,37 Thus, identifying patients suitable for new therapeutic agents is crucial for tailoring treatment. Moreover, since HR+/HER2− breast cancer showed susceptibility to hormones, NET could be an appropriate, de-intensified, less-toxic approach for highly HR-dependent breast cancers, amenable to chemotherapy-free options.38 NET showed less pCR rate of 1%–17% even with CDK4/6 inhibitor, which was more tolerable compared with NCT.4,39 The WSG-ADAPT-HR+/HER2− trial reported data on an OncotypeDX recurrence score and response to 3-week preoperative endocrine-therapy-driven NET strategy, but only patients with lower tumor load (T1-2N0-1) were included in this trial.40 Therefore, identifying which patients are more likely to benefit from NET remains of paramount importance in clinical practice.

To assess the efficacy of subtype-specific precision therapies and to identify tailored treatment regimens for distinct subtypes, our previous research applied AI-assisted SNF subtyping to divide HR+/HER2− breast cancer into endocrine-based group and targeted-based group.11 We employed this method based on digital pathology and deep learning for SNF subtyping in the current study, as well as LINUX trial (NCT05594095) involving metastatic patients who progressed with CDK4/6 inhibitor treatment with endocrine therapy for metastatic disease and trials exploring precision treatment in first-line (NCT05949541, NCT05205200, NCT05759546, NCT06612814, NCT05759572, and NCT06447623), confirming the value of SNF subtyping in guiding precision treatment. Confirmed ORRs in the LINUX trial were higher in targeted-based subtype therapy who received precision treatment instead of treatment of physician’s choice (TPC) (SNF2: precision treatment 65% versus 30%; SNF3: 40% versus 30%; SNF4: 70% versus 20%), when precision treatment showed no benefit in endocrine-based subtype (SNF1: 10% versus 10%).15 Data from advanced HR+/HER2− breast cancer also notified the significance of precision treatment in targeted-based subtypes. Importantly, the neoadjuvant treatment strategy based on the SNF subtyping could be iteratively updated with advances in drug development, highlighting its potential for widespread application.

Based on the current data, we found it was promising for future phase III study, especially in targeted-based group. Thus, a phase III study (NCT06913777) with conventional taxane and anthracycline regimens as the backbone of chemotherapy was designed. This randomized controlled trial to further verify the efficacy and safety of target therapy involves larger number of patients and centers, which set long-term outcomes like overall survival (OS).

The spectrum of adverse events associated with precision treatment were consistent with those reported in previous trials, with no new safety concerns identified.6,7,26,30 There was no evidence that the addition of targeted therapy to NCT exacerbated chemotherapy-associated toxicity. TRAEs with a potentially immune-mediated mechanism, including endocrine disorders, occurred more frequently among patients with immune therapy in SNF2 subtype, while apatinib-related adverse events, including hand-foot syndrome and hypertension, occurred more frequently among patients in SNF4 subtype. Importantly, the addition of precision treatment did not increase the incidence of serious adverse events or compromise the patients’ ability to receive chemotherapy.

Despite the convincing results obtained from our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was an explorative phase II study using a Bayesian approach, and pCR was considered as the primary endpoint. However, it should be noticed that whether the improvements in pCR rate would translate into improvements in long-term survival in HR+/HER2− breast cancer is still unknown. We have designed another phase III study (NCT06913777) with more samples and long-term follow-up to confirm its value. Second, our study did not involve conventional sequential anthracycline-taxane regimens, although nab-paclitaxel with carboplatin administrated in our study is widely accepted in neoadjuvant setting, more suitable to combine with various targeted therapies, especially in triple-negative and HER2+ breast cancer. Another phase III study (NCT06913777) involves taxane and anthracycline sequentially regimens as the backbone of chemotherapy. Third, adjuvant treatments in this trial were determined based on individualized clinical assessments and therapeutic guidelines; in the study group, adjuvant chemotherapy might omit for patients achieving pCR; in non-pCR patients who received NET plus CDK4/6 inhibitor, four cycles of anthracyclines followed by four cycles of taxanes were recommended; in non-pCR patients who received targeted-based therapy, adjuvant endocrine therapy plus CDK4/6 inhibitor was recommended, while the appropriate adjuvant treatment strategies are worthy of future exploration.

In conclusion, this study explored an insight for the neoadjuvant therapy for HR+/HER2− early breast cancer via subtyping it to endocrine-based group and targeted-based group. Precision treatment could improve the benefit of neoadjuvant therapy for HR+/HER2− breast cancer patients. In the endocrine-based group, NET plus CDK4/6 inhibitor demonstrated the potential to replace chemotherapy while offering improved tolerability. In the targeted-based group, precision treatment showed promising clinical activity and manageable safety profile .

Limitations of the study

While the FASCINATE-N trial provides proof-of-concept evidence for AI-assisted SNF subtyping to guide neoadjuvant therapy in HR+/HER2− breast cancer, the current clinical utility and generalizability of these findings remain limited, necessitating cautious interpretation regarding immediate changes to standard practice. First, the clinical relevance of the observed pCR improvement is constrained by the lack of validated survival endpoints in HR+/HER2− disease. Although the increase in pCR within the targeted-based group suggests enhanced treatment activity, pCR is not a universally accepted surrogate for long-term survival benefit in this intrinsically less chemosensitive subtype. Consequently, the pivotal question of whether SNF-guided precision therapy improves invasive disease-free survival or overall survival remains unanswered, limiting the current clinical utility of these findings to hypothesis generation rather than practice-defining evidence. Second, the endocrine-based group failed to demonstrate a pCR advantage over chemotherapy, restricting its current clinical utility as a definitive chemotherapy-sparing strategy. While the favorable toxicity profile supports its role in specific frail populations, the absence of a statistically significant efficacy improvement means that NET plus CDK4/6 inhibitor cannot yet be recommended as a standard replacement for chemotherapy in high-burden, stage II–III disease outside of clinical trials. Third, the precision treatment regimens in the targeted-based group represent exploratory, subtype-directed hypotheses rather than definitive standards of care. The sample sizes for individual SNF2, SNF3, and SNF4 subtypes were modest, and the study was not powered for pairwise comparisons against chemotherapy within each biomarker-defined subgroup. Furthermore, the use of nab-paclitaxel plus carboplatin, while effective, differs from the global standard anthracycline-taxane backbones, complicating the integration of these findings into existing treatment algorithms. Finally, the translational impact of this subtyping system is contingent upon additional specific analyses and trials.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and participants

FASCINATE-N is a randomized, open-label, single-center, phase 2 umbrella trial (NCT05582499, with the ethical approval number of 2209261-7 approved by Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center Institutional Review Board) designed to evaluate precision-based treatment strategies in the neoadjuvant setting for breast cancer.17 Female patients with HR+/HER2-breast cancers were stratified into two distinct therapeutic cohorts: an endocrine-based therapy group and a targeted-based therapy group, then randomized to receive either precision treatment or control treatment.

Eligible patients were aged 18 years or older with pathologically confirmed HR+/HER2-breast cancer. HR + status was defined as estrogen receptor (ER) positivity >10% and/or progesterone receptor (PR) positivity >10% on immunohistochemistry (IHC). HER2 negativity was defined as IHC 0/1+ or IHC 2+ with negative fluorescence/chromogenic in situ hybridization, according to the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO)/College of American Pathologists (CAP) guidelines.41 Patients were required to have an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1, a left ventricular ejection fraction of 55% or higher, and no prior exposure to chemotherapy, a histologically confirmed early-stage breast cancer (T2-4, N0-2, M0).42,43 A detailed list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in protocol (supplemental information).

The trial was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Before enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study received approval from the Institutional Review Board. An independent data monitoring committee provided trial oversight.

Method details

Randomization and masking

We adopted our previously published deep learning model to infer the SNF subtypes from pathological WSIs.11,44,45 This model was developed via a deep learning algorithm based on 243 samples that have both WSIs and multi-omics SNF classification. The subtyping system is based on H&E-stained histopathological slides, with a digital pathology scanner, a server capable of deep learning inference (preferably equipped with a GPU of 2080 or higher) and a storage device of approximately 500GB for temporarily storing WSI files. An efficient internal information transmission system was also required, which the following components: a) the outpatient/inpatient hospital information system for submitting classification requests, b) the data terminal in the pathology department for receiving classification requests, scanning images, and uploading them, and c) the AI guided subtyping platform in the data analysis and report feedback. Through the digital pathology subtyping, HR+/HER2-breast cancers were classified into four SNF subtypes, with SNF1 assigned to the endocrine-based group and SNF2-4 allocated to the targeted-based group. Patients were randomized at a 1:1 ratio by an independent biostatistician through an interactive web-based system. Patients and investigators were not masked to the treatment assignments.

Procedures

Patients in the endocrine-based group were randomly assigned to receive endocrine treatment: dalpicilib (CDK4/6 inhibitor, 150 mg orally once daily from day 1 to day 21) plus letrozole (2.5 mg orally once daily) with or without goserelin (for premenopausal patients, 3.6 mg intramuscularly every 4 weeks) in a 28-day per cycle for six cycles; or chemotherapy in the control group with nab-paclitaxel (100 mg/m2 intravenously on day 1, day 8, and day 15) plus carboplatin (AUC = 1.5 intravenously on day 1, day 8, and day 15), in a 28-day per cycle for six cycles (PCb). Patients in the targeted-based group were randomly assigned to receive subtype-specific precision treatments, listed as follows: SHR-1316 (PD-L1 inhibitor, 10 mg/kg intravenously on days 1 and 15) plus PCb in a 28-day per cycle for six cycles for SNF2; fuzuloparib (PARP1 inhibitor 50 mg orally twice daily) plus PCb in a 28-day per cycle for six cycles for SNF3; and apatinib (VEGFR inhibitor, 250 mg orally daily) plus PCb in a 28-day per cycle for six cycles for SNF4; or PCb for six cycles in the control group. Treatment was continued until study completion, confirmed disease progression, intolerable toxicity, physician decision, or patient withdrawal, with no crossover permitted. For patients who did not achieve pCR, subsequent treatment strategies were determined at the discretion of the attending physician, based on individualized clinical assessments and therapeutic guidelines.

Dose reduction was permitted. Details of treatment choice, toxicity management and dose modifications are stated in the protocol. Adverse events were recorded as per the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE), version 5.0. Immune-mediated adverse events were identified according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) terminology. Tumor assessments, including CT or MRI scans, were conducted at baseline and every two cycles thereafter until disease progression, patient withdrawal, initiation of new therapy, or death, as per the response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (RECIST; version 1.1).

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was pCR rate, defined as pathological stage (post neoadjuvant, abbreviated yp) ypT0/Tis ypN0. The secondary endpoints included invasive disease-free survival, ORR accessed via the response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (RECIST; version 1.1), and safety. Efficacy was assessed in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, which included all patients who underwent randomization. Patients who did not undergo surgery were considered as non-pCR. And the analysis of long-term follow-up will be performed on the full ITT population.

Safety was assessed in the as-treated population, comprising all patients who were randomized and received at least one dose of the study drug. Adverse events were monitored continuously throughout the study and for up to 30 days after the last dose of treatment, extended to 90 days for serious adverse events.

Exploratory endpoints include MP score, RCB score and absolute change in Ki-67, which were obtained from pathological methods. Confidence intervals of exploratory endpoints are pointwise 95% CIs and have not been adjusted for multiplicity. They reflect estimation precision rather than formal hypothesis tests.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated based on the hypothesis that the pCR rate would be 10% in the control group and 20% in the precision treatment group. A two-sided alpha level of 0.10, a power of 75%, and a 1:1 randomization ratio, with an additional 5% accounted for potential dropout, resulting in an estimated sample size of 118 patients per group.

Efficacy was also evaluated periodically using a Bayesian approach. Each cohort was planned to be tested on a minimum of 20 patients and a maximum of 120 patients, and we periodically evaluated treatment efficacy after every 20 patients underwent surgery. If the probability of a precision-based treatment arm achieving a higher pCR rate than control arm was greater than 95%, the treatment arm graduated from the trial for efficacy. If the probability was less than 5%, the treatment arm was dropped for futility. The full statistical analysis plan is detailed in the supplemental information.

Statistical analysis

The 90% confidence intervals (CIs) for single-sample proportions were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method, and approximate 90% CIs for differences between rates were calculated using the Hauck-Anderson method. The two-sided Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test was employed to compare pCR rates between groups. Patients not evaluable for pathological response (i.e., did not undergo surgery) were considered negative responders. Data analysis was performed using SAS version 9.4 or higher. All of the statistical details of experiments can be found in the Statistical Analysis Plan (supplemental information, Data S2).
