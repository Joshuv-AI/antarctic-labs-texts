Compact breast parenchyma: imaging and transcriptomic correlates
Authors: Joanna N. Modi, Dyutika Kantamneni, Kathleen M. Capaccione, Alison M. Taylor, Mary Salvatore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer is the second leading cause of cancer-related death among women in the United States. High mammographic density, one of the most notable risk factors, is associated with a four to sixfold increased likelihood of developing breast cancer. Tissue compactness is a related but distinct, newly developed tool to assess density in a specific location. However, the transcriptomic differences between more and less compact tissue are not well understood.MethodsWe performed RNA sequencing on paired more and less-compact breast tissue from three individuals to identify genes that correlate with compactness.ResultsWe identified 8 genes that were differentially expressed between more and less compact tissue. Less-compact tissue had an upregulation of genes associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment, consistent with higher estimated fractions of t-regulatory cells. Pathway analysis revealed that more compact tissue is significantly enriched for cholesterol homeostasis genes; cholesterol biosynthesis has been demonstrated to promote breast cancer and its metastasis.ConclusionsThis study provides novel insights into the transcriptomic differences between more and less compact breast tissue and represents an important step towards understanding the molecular distinctions between tissue compactness and breast cancer risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08220-z.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide [1]. Breasts are composed of fibroglandular tissue, which is white on mammography, and fatty tissue, which is a shade of grey. Breast density is the extent of fibroglandular tissue compared to total breast tissue [2]. Mammography classifies breast density into four categories using the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS): fatty, scattered fibroglandular density, heterogeneously dense, and more dense breast tissue [3, 4]. High breast density (HBD) is associated with a 4- to 6-fold increased risk of developing breast cancer [5]. Our previous work demonstrated that chest CT grading of breast density is comparable to mammographic grading with good inter-reader agreement [6, 7]. In addition, GWAS-studies have identified an overlap between Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with HBD and breast cancer risk [8].

High mammographic breast density (MBD) is not the only feature that will predict breast cancer risk [9, 10]. Breast compactness (BC), a relatively new term, reflects collagen organization within the breast fibroglandular tissue and is quantified by the maximum Hounsfield units (HU) (Table 1) [11, 12]. Collagen in areas with high HU is more organized and densely packed, which contributes to increased stromal stiffness, a risk factor for breast cancer [12–15]. Histologic evidence that collagen fiber density is heterogeneously distributed within breast tissue may explain why some areas of the breast parenchyma are at greater risk than others [16]. Additionally, since HU measurement reflects collagen organization and stiffness, it may be able to predict where cancer is most likely to occur [11]. Increased HU (breast compactness) on chest CT may be a more precise measurement than MBD for the potential risk of breast cancer [6, 7, 11].

Molecular signatures of compactness in breast tissue remain a key knowledge gap [17]. To address this, we performed RNA sequencing on paired breast tissues with more or less compactness from the same individuals. With this approach, we identified gene expression patterns that correlate with compactness.

Materials, methods, and statistical analysis

Breast tissue was collected from both less compact and more compact areas of resections from three patients. All six samples were immediately stored in RNAlater Stabilizing Solution (Invitrogen). Tissue was removed from the stabilizing solution and minced using a scalpel and 1 mL of TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen). RNA was isolated according to the manufacturer’s TRIzol protocol, and the resulting RNA was purified using the Norgen RNA Purification Kit. RNA concentration and quality were assessed via bioanalyzer, with all samples having an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) greater than 6. Samples were sent for library preparation using polyA pull-down and subsequent sequencing on the Element AVITI at Columbia’s Sulzberger Genome Center. The sequencing produced an average of 33 million reads per sample. A pseudoalignment to a kallisto index was created from transcriptomes (Ensembl v113) using kallisto (0.50.1). Estimated counts from the results were normalized using DESeq2 [18]. Both edgeR [19] and paired t-tests were used to identify significantly expressed genes between the less compact and more compact RNA samples, using an FDR < 0.05 for edgeR and a p-value < 0.001 for the paired t-test. Normalized expression data from DESeq2 was also analyzed via Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) [20] to determine enriched pathways and via xCell [21] to determine enriched cell types. With three sets of paired samples and a two-sided Type I error of 0.1, our gene expression analyses had a power of 0.72.

Results

To classify by levels of tissue compactness, surgical breast resections (Fig. 1) were subjected to micro-computed tomography (microCT) imaging. Segmentation of tissue was based on radiodensity; areas exhibiting higher attenuation (appearing brighter on CT) were classified as fibrous or more compact regions, while areas with lower attenuation (appearing darker) were classified as less-compact, fatty regions. This classification and segmentation process was performed on three independent surgical resections. For each resection, paired tissue biopsies were extracted from the defined less-compact (fatty) and more-compact (fibroglandular) regions, which were derived from the same patient. RNA was subsequently isolated from each paired biopsy (Fig. 2A), for a total of two tissue samples each from three patients representing more and less compact areas of the breast.

We performed RNA-sequencing on all six samples and first assessed principal components. Samples clustered by individual rather than compactness (Supplemental Fig. 1A). We then took two approaches to identify differentially expressed genes. First, we performed paired t-tests of individual gene expression. Second, we used the paired function in the edgeR algorithm. Eight genes were found to be differentially expressed in both analyses (Fig. 2B, Supplemental Fig. 1B). PRRT1, TCN2, and SF1 were enriched in high compact tissue, while DNM1L, CORO7, JMJD8, NRCAM, and ADAM3A were enriched less compact tissue.

The genes enriched in more compact tissue have diverse functions. Proline-rich transmembrane protein 1 (PRRT1) regulates glutamate receptors [22]; its expression has also been identified as a biomarker distinguishing high and low risk breast cancer [23]. TCN2 binds vitamin B12, which may be involved in breast cancer development; however, the data is conflicting [24–26]. SF1, Steroidogenic factor 1, plays an important role in gonadal development and steroidogenesis, though its role in breast cancer is unknown [27]. Pathway analysis revealed that the cholesterol homeostasis pathway was enriched in more compact tissue compared to less compact tissue (normalized enrichment score = 1.88, p = 0.007, Fig. 4A). Increased cholesterol biosynthesis has been implicated in breast cancer proliferation and metastasis [28, 29]; its enrichment in more compact tissue may serve as an additional contributor to breast cancer risk.

In less compact tissue, several enriched genes were related to actin and cell adhesion. NRCAM is a glial-related cell adhesion molecule with two known somatic mutations related to breast cancer [30, 31]. CORO7 is a member of the coronin family, which regulates actin and actin filament binding [32]. It also functions as a scaffolding protein that regulates the HIPPO pathway [33]. Lastly, ADAM3A is a pseudogene belonging to the ADAM gene family, which are widely expressed and involved in processes such as cell adhesion and signaling. The role of ADAM3A is not well-characterized, however other family members have been implicated in breast cancer [34]. Consistent with increased expression of these genes, top pathways more highly expressed in less compact tissue relate to cell adhesion and collagen production (Fig. 3). We previously showed that collagen protein is higher in more compact tissue, cells may turn down collagen RNA expression once collagen is present at sufficient levels [12]. This is consistent with our pathway analysis of another study comparing more and less dense breasts as measured by mammography (Supplemental Fig. 1C) [35].

The other genes enriched in less compact tissue have been associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Elevated expression of DNM1L, dynamin-1-like protein, correlated with poorer overall survival and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [36]. JMJD8 plays a role in regulating the AKT/NF-κB pathway [37], and has been positively associated with infiltration of M2 macrophages, immune evasion, and breast tumorigenesis [38, 39].

To estimate immune cell populations from bulk RNA-sequencing, we applied the xCell algorithm [21]. Consistent with having less expression of genes associated with immunosuppression, more compact tissue had significantly higher fractions of CD8 + central memory T-cells (“Tcm”, p = 0.047) and higher fractions of CD8 + T-cells overall (p = 0.074, Fig. 4B). Interestingly, less compact tissue had significantly higher fractions of mesenchymal stem cells (“MSC”, p = 0.039) and higher fractions of regulatory T-cells (“Tregs”, p = 0.073) and lymphatic (ly) endothelial cells (p = 0.059) (Fig. 4B). The gene expression data suggest immune differences correlating with breast compactness.

Discussion

Our study employed a novel, CT-guided approach using breast compactness to study transcriptomic differences between paired more-compact and less-compact tissue from the same individuals. Our analysis identified 8 genes and several expression pathways significantly differentially expressed between more and less compact tissue, with implications for regulation of cholesterol biosynthesis, collagen formation, and immune surveillance.

Breast stiffness, driven by collagen organization and fibrous stromal remodeling, is said to be pro-carcinogenic [11, 12, 40, 41]. One example of this has been seen with preventative tamoxifen treatment. Tamoxifen, a selective estrogen modulator and first-line treatment for hormone receptor-positive breast cancer, is also prescribed as a preventative measure for people at risk for developing breast cancer, such as those with more breast density [42–44]. Clinical trials have demonstrated that tamoxifen treatment in the preventative context can successfully decrease the incidence of invasive and noninvasive breast cancer by 49% [43]. Importantly, tamoxifen has also been shown to decrease breast density over time [45–47]. Tamoxifen reduces collagen organization, indicative of tissue stiffness in breast tissue of healthy women [48]. This highlights the significance of breast density and stiffness as risk factors for the development of cancer and the importance of determining the underlying molecular signatures.

High cholesterol is thought to be correlated with higher risk of breast cancer, though there is some debate about this [49]. Higher expression of some cholesterol biosynthesis genes was previously observed in breast tissue [50]. Our analysis of additional gene sets found similar results [35]. We have now validated this finding within tissue from the same individual – more compact regions have more expression of cholesterol pathways. This is consistent with findings showing radiologically dense breasts have an increased expression of cholesterol biosynthesis genes [50]. Whether or not increased cholesterol in more compact tissue contributes to risk remains an open and intriguing question.

While our study provides novel insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. Our most significant limitation is sample size, with three pairs of samples leaving analyses underpowered. Consistency of our results with studies comparing gene expression in individuals with different breast density suggests robustness, and in the future, we hope to sequence larger cohorts of samples. In addition, we performed RNA-seq on bulk tissue containing a heterogeneous mix of cells. Future studies via single-cell RNA-seq can classify gene expression changes in individual cell types, including epithelial cells, immune cells, and fibroblasts. Our analyses also do not directly address cancer risk. Next steps could include similar analysis in tissue resections from breast cancer patients after measuring compactness by CT in regions of tumor and adjacent normal tissue. In addition, it would be interesting to incorporate murine breast cancer models with the development of new models of stiffness in the mouse. While CT-based HU quantifies tissue compactness, elastography can measure the stiffness, and future studies could leverage elastography guided tissue sampling to precisely identify stiff regions for transcriptomic analysis [6, 7, 51, 52]. In this study, we identified distinct gene expression patterns between more and less compact breast tissue, revealing correlations with signaling ranging from fibrotic to cholesterol to immune. The comparative framework of our study advances our understanding of how compaction may drive breast cancer susceptibility, with the ultimate goal of informing precision-guided screening and prevention strategies.
