Evaluating the Molecular Potential and Interpretability of DNA in Historical Spirit Collection Media
Authors: Rachel L Tulloch, Jack Rojahn, Linda E Neaves, Alejandro Trujillo‐González, Clare E Holleley, Erin E Hahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdvancements in historical genomics increasingly leverage museum collections to study past ecosystems, species interactions and biodiversity. Formalin‐fixed, ethanol‐preserved specimens, once thought inaccessible to molecular analyses due to DNA degradation, are emerging as valuable genomic resources. If recoverable and reliably attributable, DNA within preservation media could provide a non‐destructive alternative to conventional tissue sampling, with the potential to expand molecular access to valuable or irreplaceable specimens. We tested whether preservation media contains recoverable DNA suitable for taxonomic inference. We coupled passive adsorption and active filtration of specimen media with hot alkaline lysis DNA extraction followed by metabarcoding and shotgun metagenomics. DNA was recoverable across samples, including 41 of 61 (~67%) targets in a composite sample. However, detections were dominated by non‐target taxa, indicating that preservation media retain a layered mixture of specimen‐derived DNA and broader collection‐level background. Detection success tracked with preservation chemistry (near‐neutral pH and low residual formaldehyde) rather than specimen age. Method choice influenced detections: active filtration increased target detections but admitted more background; passive capture was sparser but more selective; shotgun sequencing retrieved broader vertebrate signals, including reptiles, but was heavily enriched for non‐targets. Because both target and non‐target taxa were often abundant, read‐abundance cut‐offs were unreliable for attribution. Spirit‐media DNA is therefore best interpreted as a collection‐level signal and a screening tool to identify jars with molecular potential (e.g., taxa of conservation or biosecurity interest), rather than as a definitive non‐destructive proxy for specimen identity. Prioritising chemically favourable jars and implementing rigorous contamination controls should improve signal interpretability and help unlock the value of preservation media for historical genomics.Keywords: archival DNA, biodiversity, formalin, metabarcoding, museum genomics, non‐destructive sampling

Introduction

Natural history collections represent the most comprehensive temporal record of Earth's biodiversity. Although most of the more than three billion global specimens were collected prior to the discovery of DNA, modern molecular techniques are now enabling evolutionary and phylogenetic research across centuries at increasingly rapid scales (Card et al. 2021; Fong et al. 2023; Raxworthy and Smith 2021; Wheeler et al. 2012). Among museum holdings, specimens preserved in spirit (alcohol‐based media often following fixation with formaldehyde) constitute one of the world's largest untapped molecular archives (Holleley and Hahn 2025a). For more than a century, zoological and botanical spirit collections have been used primarily for morphological and anatomical study, but recent advances have begun to demystify and unlock their molecular potential (Hahn et al. 2022; Hahn, Alexander, et al. 2024; Hykin et al. 2015; O'Connell et al. 2022; Ruane and Austin 2017; Straube et al. 2021). DNA and even RNA can persist for over a century within formalin‐fixed tissues, revealing both genomic sequence data and tissue‐specific gene regulation information (Hahn, Stiller, et al. 2024; Holleley and Hahn 2025a, 2025b; Porter et al. 2025).

Despite these advances, most molecular analyses of historical specimens still rely on destructive sampling of preserved tissues, which can be impractical or undesirable for rare, fragile, or type specimens. This has driven increasing interest in minimally invasive or non‐destructive molecular approaches that can expand access to genomic information while preserving specimen integrity. At the same time, there is growing demand for high‐quality reference sequences to support metabarcoding and biodiversity genomics (Porter and Hajibabaei 2018a; Weigand et al. 2019), alongside coordinated efforts to develop comprehensive DNA reference libraries for biodiversity monitoring (e.g., the National Biodiversity DNA Library, https://research.csiro.au/dnalibrary/) and large‐scale initiatives to catalogue global biodiversity at the genomic level (Darwin Tree of Life Project 2022; Lewin et al. 2018). Yet generating these data from historical specimens often requires destructive sampling of limited material. Non‐destructive approaches that can recover molecular information without compromising specimen integrity therefore offer a critical pathway to expand reference databases while preserving irreplaceable specimens.

In parallel with advances in historical DNA research, it is increasingly recognised that preserved specimens can retain DNA not only from the focal organism but also from associated biotic communities. Studies of preserved material have shown that host‐associated tissues can retain molecular traces of their original environments. For example, metabarcoding of preserved mussels (Junk et al. 2023) and Antarctic sponges (Jeunen et al. 2024) recovered DNA from associated fish and invertebrates, effectively using the specimens themselves as natural historical environmental DNA (heDNA) samplers (Mariani et al. 2019) to reconstruct temporal biodiversity. These findings demonstrate that specimens can act as reservoirs of multi‐source DNA consistent with the ‘extended specimen’ concept (Lendemer et al. 2020; Webster 2018) and highlight the potential to recover additional ecological information beyond the target organism.

Building on this, preservation media offers a non‐destructive and accessible means of capturing DNA released from preserved specimens. Studies have shown that preservation media can retain DNA shed by specimens (Hajibabaei et al. 2012; Linard et al. 2016; Parekh et al. 2019; Rayo et al. 2021; Shokralla et al. 2010). Recovery of DNA from liquid media therefore offers a non‐destructive alternative to sampling tissue directly, with particular relevance for valuable or irreplaceable specimens (Jeunen et al. 2026). Early studies demonstrated successful identification of target invertebrate and plant taxa from preservation media using DNA barcoding (Hajibabaei et al. 2012; Shokralla et al. 2010), while subsequent shotgun sequencing and metabarcoding approaches revealed that preservation media can contain complex and heterogeneous DNA mixtures, including from bulk insect collections (Linard et al. 2016) and medical or transplant media (Parekh et al. 2019; Rayo et al. 2021). Together, these studies illustrate both the promise of preservation fluids as molecular time capsules and the challenges associated with interpreting mixed and variable DNA signals in long‐term collections.

Amid these emerging possibilities lies a central dilemma: although preservation media clearly contains DNA, the composition and persistence of this DNA are likely influenced by preservation chemistry and collection‐specific practices and the extent to which that DNA reflects the enclosed specimens rather than accumulated environmental or collection‐derived contamination remains uncertain. Early studies largely validated this approach by comparing media‐derived DNA to verified reference sequences for the target organisms (Hajibabaei et al. 2012; Linard et al. 2016), demonstrating that preservation fluids can yield recognisable barcodes under controlled conditions. However, extending this approach to historical spirit collections introduces the additional challenge of applying an unbiased discovery framework, one capable of detecting and characterising DNA from unknown assemblages. Such an approach is essential for exploring the full potential of heDNA as a tool for reconstructing past biodiversity, yet it first requires understanding the extent of background or ‘junk’ DNA present in the media. Resolving this uncertainty is critical not only for demonstrating feasibility, but for determining when and under what conditions preservation media can be expected to yield reliable and interpretable specimen‐derived DNA signals.

Museum collections are dynamic and inherently unsterile environments. Specimen jars are routinely topped up or refilled from shared ethanol stocks, sometimes reusing the same jars or fluid across specimens. Specimen handling is frequent and often side‐by‐side, using tools that may not be sterilised between specimens. Critically, the detailed history of specimen preservation and handling is rarely recorded at this level of resolution, meaning that events such as ethanol replacement, specimen transfer between jars and cumulative handling are often undocumented. Aerosolised DNA and splashing or spillover during curatorial activities further contribute to a pervasive background of collection‐level DNA. Over decades or centuries, these practices likely generate complex mixtures of authentic and exogenous sequences within spirit media, making it difficult to infer specimen‐specific signals with confidence. Indeed, Hajibabaei et al. (2012) detected human, domestic dog and poplar sequences within ethanol from macroinvertebrate assemblages, while Parekh et al. (2019) found microbial DNA likely associated with curatorial equipment or original ethanol sources, highlighting how even well‐controlled systems accumulate exogenous DNA. Beyond contamination, little is known about how specimen‐ and media‐related factors influence recovery of DNA from preservation fluids. While preservation chemistry (pH and formaldehyde concentration) has been shown to affect DNA preservation more greatly than specimen age (Hahn et al. 2022), their impacts on DNA detectability or composition within the surrounding media remain unexplored. Such variability reflects the reality of historical collections and presents both a challenge and an opportunity for evaluating DNA recovery under representative, real‐world conditions.

Here, we test the potential for historical spirit media to serve as a molecular archive. We compared metabarcoding and shotgun metagenomic profiles from specimen media from the Australian National Wildlife Collection against the known identities of the contained specimens to assess whether DNA in spirit media reflects the organisms it preserves. We further examined how factors such as residual formaldehyde concentration, media pH and specimen age influence detection success. To evaluate the interpretability of media‐derived signals under real‐world conditions, we also analysed a series of unlabelled or ‘unknown’ media containers to determine whether recovered DNA could reveal their likely contents. Together, these analyses provide an empirical basis for assessing both the promise and limitations of spirit media as a source of genomic information and for identifying the conditions under which meaningful biological signal can be recovered.

Methods

Test Media Sourcing

We collected and processed liquid specimen preservation media from the Australian National Wildlife Collection (ANWC) to evaluate DNA detectability and composition. To assess detection success and reliability, we tested samples with two levels of associated specimen metadata. We created a combined sample from multiple specimen jars with extensive specimen metadata (‘Known’ sample) and, in contrast, sampled waste media lacking specimen metadata from the collections' specimen preparation laboratory (‘Unknown’ samples 1–5).

We designed a combined ‘Known’ sample to capture a representative mixture of preservation conditions present within the collection, rather than to isolate the effects of individual variables such as pH, formaldehyde concentration, or storage history. To construct the Known sample, we selected 61 jars from the ANWC's spirit vault containing vouchered specimens spanning multiple vertebrate classes with available species‐specific 12S rRNA reference sequences (Table S1). For each of the 61 jars, we measured pH and residual formaldehyde concentration ([F]; mg/L) of the original media using an Orion Versa Star Pro benchtop pH meter (Thermo Scientific) and MQuant test strips (Merck), respectively. From each specimen jar, we then transferred approximately 250 mL of preservation media, sampling from the bottom to capture settled debris, to create a combined sample with a final volume of approximately 15 L, which we assembled in a clean, unused 25 L plastic container. To minimise cross‐jar contamination introduced during the sampling process itself, while reflecting typical collection handling conditions, we conducted all media transfers for the Known sample under standard curatorial conditions using single‐use sterile serological pipettes while wearing gloves.

We assembled five Unknown samples from the ANWC's specimen preparation waste stream, which primarily consists of discarded 70% ethanol with trace formaldehyde and biological debris from routine collection operations (e.g., specimen re‐housing or jar replacement). This waste is not linked to specific specimen jars, making the contributing DNA sources unknown. We sampled five plastic waste media containers in late November 2021, each containing 25 L of waste media. These containers were not sterile but were not reused between waste batches. Except for Unknown sample 1, the containers had been stored outdoors at ambient temperature for an unspecified duration.

We measured the pH and [F] of the combined Known sample and the five Unknown samples prior to further processing (Table 1). We conducted all measurements using single‐use sterile consumables, with glove changes between samples to minimise cross‐sample contamination.

Archival DNA Capture

To test how different collection approaches influence the recovery of archival DNA from specimen preservation media, we compared two methods: passive adsorption and active filtration. Passive collection allowed us to evaluate whether DNA (or cells containing DNA) present in preservation fluids can adsorb onto filter materials without applied pressure or flow, while active filtration concentrated suspended particulates within the media.

For passive capture, we aliquoted 50 mL from each of the six samples into sterile 50 mL centrifuge tubes using single‐use serological pipettes within the curatorial environment prior to transfer to the laboratory, reflecting practical collection sampling practices while minimising disturbance or contamination of specimen jars with laboratory contaminants. We transported the aliquots at room temperature to a molecular laboratory where, within a PCR cabinet decontaminated with 10% bleach and UV sterilisation, we inserted sterile filters (5 μm polycarbonate track‐etched (PCTE) and 0.45 μm cellulose ester (CE)) using sterile forceps and incubated them under gentle rocking at room temperature for 4 days to allow adsorption. Alongside these experimental samples, we prepared passive filtration blanks by incubating filters in 50 mL of laboratory‐grade 70% ethanol (prepared using MilliQ water) under identical conditions. We then transferred the filters to 2 mL screwcap tubes and stored them at −20°C until extraction.

For active capture, we transported the media containers from the ANWC to the University of Canberra Animal Holding Facility for filtration, where we conducted processing in a dedicated workspace. We filtered media through sterile 10 and 20 μm PCTE filters mounted on filter funnel manifolds (Pall Life Sciences) using a Geotech Geopump peristaltic pump. Prior to filtration, we sterilised all equipment (funnels, tubing, tweezers, etc.) with a 10% bleach solution then rinsed with UV‐sterilised water. We trialled filtration with 5 μm PCTE filters but the media proved too viscous. We filtered the Known sample through 15 × 10 μm filters in 100–150 mL aliquots, followed by 18 × 20 μm filters in 500–750 mL aliquots. We filtered the Unknown samples through 15 × 10 μm filters per sample in 300–700 mL aliquots. To monitor for equipment contamination, we collected equipment negative controls by filtering 500 mL of UV‐sterilised water through each of the five funnels before and after active filtration. In addition, we processed a 50 mL 70% ethanol blank to monitor for possible media contamination. Following filtration, we transferred filters to 2 mL tubes using sterile forceps and transported them to a molecular laboratory for further processing within a PCR cabinet decontaminated with 10% bleach and UV sterilisation, where we pooled five of each filter type per sample into 2 mL screwcap tubes and stored them at −20°C until extraction.

DNA Extraction

To recover heavily crosslinked DNA, we employed a hot alkaline lysis extraction method (Campos and Gilbert 2012; Hahn et al. 2022). To each 2 mL screwcap tube containing one (controls) or five (experimental samples) filters, we added 70 μL alkaline lysis buffer (0.1 M NaOH with 1% SDS, pH 13), vortexed vigorously and heated the tubes to 120°C for 25 min using an autoclave. After allowing the tubes to cool, we purified DNA from the lysates via phenol‐chloroform extraction and SPRI bead clean‐up as previously described (Hahn, Alexander, et al. 2024). We processed extraction blanks (no filter added) alongside samples to monitor for contamination introduced during extraction. We performed all extraction manipulation steps using single‐use sterile consumables within a UV‐and bleach‐sterilised PCR cabinet. We quantified extracted dsDNA using a Qubit fluorometer and high sensitivity DNA kit (Invitrogen).

Amplification of Mitochondrial Targets for Metabarcoding

We amplified a ~100 bp fragment of the 12S mitochondrial gene using 12SV5 primers (Riaz et al. 2011). We assembled reactions in a total volume of 25 μL, with 10 μM of each primer, Q5 High‐Fidelity 2× Master Mix (NEB) and 2 μL of template DNA (or PCR water for negative controls), with thermocycling conditions set to 98°C for 30 s, followed by 45 cycles of 98°C for 30 s, 53°C for 40 s and 72°C for 60 s, with a final extension step of 72°C for 5 min. We used agarose gels stained with SYBRSafe (Invitrogen) to confirm amplification prior to library prep. We included PCR blanks in each amplification batch to monitor for contamination introduced during amplification and conducted all pre‐PCR steps in a dedicated pre‐PCR laboratory, assembling PCR reactions within a UV‐and bleach‐sterilised PCR cabinet.

Sequencing

We constructed sequencing libraries from the 12S amplicons as well as extracted DNA aliquots for shotgun sequencing with the IDT xGEN cfDNA and FFPE DNA Library Prep kit. We generated amplicon libraries only for samples that produced a visible PCR product, to ensure sufficient template for downstream sequencing and to avoid sequencing libraries dominated by background or stochastic amplification artefacts, whereas we prepared shotgun libraries for all samples, including pooled control extracts. Prior to shotgun library preparation, we pooled DNA extracts from extraction and ethanol blanks to generate an Extraction/Ethanol Blank pool and from active filtration controls to generate Equipment Control (Pre) pool and Equipment Control (Post) pool libraries. We pooled all libraries in equal volume and sequenced on an Illumina 300 cycle (150 bp paired end) NovaSeq S1 Lane. To ensure computational feasibility while maintaining comparable depth across samples, we randomly subsampled each amplicon library to approximately 1 M reads using BBMap (reformat.sh, v39.13, https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/) with a fixed random seed (seed = 20,240,715). To assess reproducibility of random sampling, we additionally generated triplicate subsamples for two representative libraries (sample IDs 16 & 27) using two independent seeds (20,240,716 and 20,240,717).

Metabarcoding Analysis

We processed the raw amplicon data using the DADA2 pipeline (Callahan et al. 2016) in R. We filtered and trimmed forward and reverse reads using truncation lengths of 105 bp and primer trimming of 18 bp for both forward and reverse reads, maximum expected error of 3 and 4 for forward and reverse reads, respectively and a truncation quality threshold of 2. We chose these settings to remove low‐quality bases while retaining sufficient overlap for merging. We dereplicated within samples to collapse identical sequences, then denoised independently for forward and reverse reads. We merged denoised reads, retaining only those with exact overlap. From these merged reads, we constructed an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table and identified and removed potential chimeric sequences using the consensus method.

We exported ASVs from the chimera‐filtered table as a FASTA file and assigned taxonomic identity using rBLAST (v0.99.4) in R with the NCBI nucleotide (nt) database. Briefly, we queried ASV sequences against a local BLAST+ (v2.15.0) installation, joined the BLAST results with NCBI taxonomy information and linked them to the ASVs using the taxonomizr package.

We filtered the raw BLAST results to reduce spurious or low‐confidence taxonomic assignments. We first retained hits if they had an e‐value < 1e−5, ≥ 98% identity and alignment length ≥ 50% of the query sequence. For each query sequence, we kept the top five hits and selected the best hit per species (lowest e‐value). We then classified sequences into three categories: unambiguous (all top hits were identical, ≥ 98% identity), ambiguous (multiple high‐identity species), or poor (no strong match). We resolved ambiguous hits manually using a structured set of adjudication rules. Where multiple species‐level matches showed equivalent alignment quality, we collapsed to the lowest taxonomic level supported (typically genus‐level or higher). Where appropriate, we retained top hits based on lowest e‐value. We further refined assignments using biological and contextual information, including known species distributions (in this case—Australian occurrence or ANWC holdings) as a conservative plausibility filter and corrected obvious misassignments or taxonomic inconsistencies. We removed all bacterial, viral and fungal hits as well as non‐target metazoan groups (Cnidaria) and fish. We further refined the ASV table by removing low‐abundance ASVs (fewer than 10 total reads across all samples) and excluding any ASVs detected in the negative controls from all downstream analyses. We then calculated relative abundance of the ASVs within each sample.

Shotgun Sequencing Analysis

We analysed shotgun reads without pre‐trimming using Kraken2 (Wood et al. 2019) against the NCBI nt database to assess overall taxonomic composition. We further parsed the Kraken2 outputs in R, only retaining species‐level assignments within non‐fish classes of chordates and eliminating human reads.

Statistical Analyses

To assess factors influencing DNA detection success in the Known sample, we performed non‐parametric tests in R. We treated detection outcome as a binary variable indicating whether DNA was recovered for each specimen. We evaluated differences in specimen collection year, media pH and residual formaldehyde concentration between detected and undetected specimens using Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests. We assessed associations between detection outcome and taxonomic class using Fisher's exact test. We produced boxplots using ggplot2.

Results

DNA Yield and Amplification Success

DNA yield and amplification success varied among samples and collection methods (Table S2). Active filtration generally produced higher DNA concentrations than passive filtration, with total yields ranging from 61.5–915 ng across active filters, compared with ≤ 6.12 ng for passive filters. All 12S assays produced visible amplification products of the expected size except for four reactions, which failed to amplify despite repeated attempts. The passive 0.45 μm filter from the Known sample and several filters from the Unknown samples showed no detectable bands: the active 10 μm filters for Unknown sample 1, both passive (5 and 0.45 μm) filters for Unknown sample 3 and the passive 0.45 μm filter for Unknown sample 4. We observed weak amplification in several extraction and PCR controls, which we retained for sequencing and subsequently assessed bioinformatically to identify and remove potential contaminants.

Amplicon Library Sequencing Output and ASV Recovery

Sequencing of pooled 12S amplicon libraries yielded a median of approximately 38 million reads per sample. After randomly subsampling the amplicon libraries and processing them through the DADA2 pipeline, we retained a total of 16,666,717 reads across all amplicon samples, including controls, with 36%–97.8% of reads per experimental sample and 30.8%–97.5% of reads per control sample retained as non‐chimeric merged read pairs (Table S2). Following denoising, we recovered 4159 unique ASVs across all samples, including controls, of which we successfully assigned 4098 (98.5%) to taxonomy based on BLAST matches.

Applying conservative BLAST quality filters (e‐value < 1e−5, ≥ 98% identity, alignment length ≥ 50% of query), we retained 992 ASVs (24.2%). Notably, applying the e‐value and identity filters reduced the ASV set only slightly (to 4041), whereas the major loss occurred at the alignment length threshold. This pattern suggests that many ASVs align only across a high‐identity region, likely reflecting end‐biased errors, residual chimeras or off‐target products, or true divergence from available references.

Of the 992 ASVs that passed the BLAST quality filters, 194 (19.6%) were classified as unambiguous, 445 (44.9%) as ambiguous and 353 (35.6%) as poor matches based on the concordance and identity of their top five BLAST hits. The relatively high proportion of ambiguous and poor assignments likely reflects the short nature of the 12S amplicons, which limits resolution and increases the number of equally high‐scoring hits among closely related taxa.

After manual curation of ambiguous ASVs, we retained only those resolved to species or genus level. The curated dataset comprised 773 ASVs, including 682 (88.1%) assigned to species and 91 (11.9%) to genus level. Following removal of low‐abundance ASVs (< 10 reads in any sample) and those detected in controls, 422 ASVs remained. These corresponded to 50 taxa identified to species and 23 identified to genus level. ASVs detected in controls were associated with human ( Homo sapiens ), pig ( Sus scrofa ), bandicoot ( Isoodon macrourus ), spectacled flying‐fox ( Pteropus conspicillatus ) and several passerine taxa ( Smicrornis brevirostris , Corvus sp., Malurus sp.).

To assess reproducibility of read subsampling, we compared technical replicates for two representative samples: the Known sample and Unknown sample 4 both with active filtration with a 10 μm filter. Species complement was highly consistent across replicates. For the Known sample, replicates yielded 42–43 species each, while for the Unknown sample 4, all three replicates recovered the same five species. Jaccard distances between replicates were ≤ 0.07 for the Known sample (> 93% overlap) and 0 for Unknown sample 4 (identical species lists). Bray–Curtis dissimilarities were uniformly low (≤ 0.012 across both samples), indicating near‐identical relative abundance profiles. Together, these results demonstrate that replicate subsamples produced highly reproducible community compositions, supporting the reliability of downstream analyses.

Taxonomic Composition of Amplicon Libraries

Across all amplicon libraries, we detected vertebrate DNA from three classes (Aves, Mammalia and Amphibia) spanning 18 orders, 36 families and 61 genera. Avian taxa dominated the dataset, representing 88% of detections by relative abundance, followed by mammals (9%). Amphibian sequences (all Rhinella marina ) occurred only in one actively filtered sample from Unknown sample 4. This taxon composition is consistent with the general abundance of taxa held within the ANWC, which has historically had a focus on avian species.

The Known sample yielded DNA from 62 vertebrate taxa spanning two classes (Aves and Mammalia; Figure 1), of which 40 were identified to species‐level. Among these identified taxa, nine corresponded to target species—those from which specimen media was deliberately added to the sample—and a further nine to the genus of a target species. The remaining 44 taxa (71%) represented non‐target detections.

The eight absent species—great penguins (Aptenodytes sp.), Lumholtz's tree‐kangaroo ( Dendrolagus lumholtzi ), paradise drongo ( Dicrurus megarhynchus ), wonga pigeon ( Leucosarcia melanoleuca ), black kite ( Milvus migrans ), Australian shelduck ( T. tadornoides ), wood sandpiper ( Tringa glareola ) and red fox ( Vulpes vulpes )—are, however, held in the collection as other preparation types (dry skins, skeletons, eggs, etc.) This suggests that most detections likely originated from DNA in the preservation media itself. Specimen and Media Factors Influencing Detection Success via Amplicon Sequencing

Within the Known sample, amplicon detection success varied significantly with both specimen media chemistry (Figure 3A–C) and taxonomic class. To assess the effect of specimen age on detection success, we restricted our analysis to jars containing a single specimen to avoid potential confounding from mixed‐age samples. We observed no significant relationship between specimen age and detection outcome (Figure 3A; Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, W = 93.5, p = 0.0668). Media pH was strongly associated with detection outcome (Figure 3B; Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, W = 232.5, p = 0.0025). Successfully detected specimens had higher mean pH (7.11) than undetected specimens (6.66). Residual formaldehyde concentration was also a strong predictor of detection success (Figure 3C; Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, W = 628.5, p = 0.0021). Successfully detected specimens had lower mean formaldehyde levels (168 mg L−1) than undetected specimens (2634 mg L−1). Detection success differed significantly among vertebrate classes (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.046). Birds were overrepresented among successful detections (52% of avian specimens detected) relative to mammals (38%), while we detected no reptiles.

Taxonomic recovery in the Unknown samples showed no clear relationship with media pH (range 6.67–10.62) or formaldehyde concentration (200–20,000 mg L−1), likely due to small sample size, although we note that the sample with the highest formaldehyde concentration (Unknown sample 2; 20,000 mg L−1) yielded no detections.

Effects of Collection Method on Detections via Amplicon Sequencing

Collection method strongly influenced both the number and selectivity of detections in the Known sample (Table 2). Active filtration with 10 and 20 μm filters yielded the highest numbers of taxa overall (42 and 45 taxa, respectively), but only approximately one‐third of these detections corresponded to the target species. Each active filter recovered approximately one‐fifth of all target taxa (23.0% and 21.3%, respectively). In contrast, passive collection using a 5 μm filter produced far fewer detections (three taxa in total) yet showed greater selectivity, with two‐thirds of its detections corresponding to target species, though this represented only 3.3% of the full target set. The passive 0.45 μm filter failed to yield any detections.

Amplicon ASV Relative Abundance of Target and Non‐Target Taxa

Relative abundance was significantly higher among target taxa compared with non‐target taxa in the Known sample (Figure 3D; Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, W = 469.5, p = 0.00029). Target taxa had a mean relative abundance of 0.033 ± 0.059, an order of magnitude greater than that of non‐target taxa (0.0043 ± 0.015), consistent with the expectation that DNA from deliberately spiked specimens dominated the amplicon pool.

Shotgun Sequencing Yields and Taxonomic Composition

Shotgun libraries generated between 2 and 26 million read pairs per sample (Table S3). Active filtration libraries consistently produced an order of magnitude more reads (~18–26 million) than passive collections (~2–4 million), reflecting greater DNA recovery through active capture. Kraken2 classification identified a total of 44,442 unique species‐level taxids (i.e., unique NCBI species identifiers) across all samples, of which 9071 (20.4%) were identified in controls and excluded from further analyses. Control‐associated taxids were dominated by bacteria (61.9%), with a smaller contribution from eukaryotes (31.0%; Chordata 9.1%, Streptophyta 5.3%, Arthropoda 4.3%, fungi 8.2%). Taxonomic assignments within experimental samples were dominated by eukaryotes, with 23,288 unique species‐level taxids (65.8%). Prokaryotic and viral taxa were less common, comprising 6982 bacterial (19.7%), 4532 viral (12.8%) and 469 archaeal (1.3%) species‐level taxids. Among eukaryotic taxa, diversity was dominated by Chordata (6467; 27.7%) and Arthropoda (5741; 24.6%) and Streptophyta (4724; 20.3%). Fungal taxa were also well represented (2491; 10.6%).

Shotgun Sequencing Composition of Dominant Eukaryotic Groups

Across the dominant eukaryotic phyla, species‐level detections reflected a broad range of vertebrate, invertebrate, plant and fungal taxa (Figure S1). Within Chordata, the top 10 species by relative abundance accounted for ~4% of all classified reads. The most abundant detection, Hoplopterus spinosus (0.95%), corresponds to an older synonym for Vanellus spinosus but almost certainly represents the congeneric masked lapwing ( Vanellus miles ), which is common in Australia and well‐represented in the ANWC, although predominantly as dry‐preserved specimens. Several other high‐abundance vertebrate taxa closely matched specimens held in the ANWC, consistent with DNA derived from preserved material. These included eastern barred bandicoot ( Perameles gunnii ; 0.67%), spiny‐tailed monitor ( Varanus acanthurus ; 0.58%), southern grass skink (Pseudemoia entrecasteauxii; 0.19%), limbless snake‐tooth skink (Coeranoscincus frontalis; 0.18%) and common dwarf skink (Menetia greyii; 0.16%).

Several other chordate identifications were less consistent with the known holdings of the ANWC and are likely due to taxonomic misclassification or low‐level contamination. These included many‐banded krait ( Bungarus multicinctus ; 0.36%), black‐banded sea krait ( Laticauda semifasciata ; 0.14%), a lizard species endemic to Turkey (Darevskia unisexualis; 0.12%) and spotted skink (Carinascincus ocellatus; 0.29%). Another identification, the western barred bandicoot ( Perameles bougainville ; 0.20%), is similarly unlikely given its rarity and absence from the collection and may represent misassignment to the congeneric P. gunnii . In some cases, detections such as grey‐bellied sun skink (Lampropholis robertsi; 0.14%) and rough‐scaled sun skink (Eutropis rugifera; 0.13%) may reflect database‐level substitutions for congeners present in the collection. Overall, the top chordate detections are dominated by DNA consistent with the holdings of the ANWC, particularly small mammals and lizards, while a minority of taxa likely reflect taxonomic or database artefacts rather than true biological presence.

Non‐chordate groups were characterised by widespread and environmentally persistent taxa. Arthropoda detections were dominated by ants, flies and moths (Crematogaster, Anopheles, Pieris). Notably, several of the most abundant arthropod detections correspond to recognised collection pests and commensals, including the mould mite (Tyrophagus putrescentiae; 0.0016%), booklouse ( Liposcelis decolor ; 0.00086%) and ants of the genus Crematogaster (0.0036%) all of which can be associated with museum cabinetry and specimen containers. Low‐level detections of blowflies (Calliphora stygia; 0.0013%) and termites (Coptotermes sp.; 0.00078%) further reflect taxa known to exploit or inhabit collection spaces. Fungal detections were dominated by cosmopolitan moulds and wood‐decay fungi (Aspergillus, Penicillium, Ganoderma), while Streptophyta included common vegetation and timber taxa such as Pinus, Cedrus and Musa.

Comparison Between Amplicon and Shotgun Sequencing Approaches

Across all collection methods, shotgun sequencing recovered far more target taxa than amplicon sequencing from the Known sample (Tables 2 and 3). While the 12S amplicon data detected up to 14 target species per collection method, shotgun sequencing detected up to 27 target species. However, shotgun sequencing yielded substantially more non‐target species per collection method (up to 2190). In relative abundance terms, target taxa accounted for only 0.01%–0.02% of classified reads, while non‐target chordate taxa comprised 1.2%–1.5% of classified reads in the active filtrations and < 0.01% in the passive collections.

Shotgun sequencing detected 3020 chordate species in the Known sample across collection methods, whereas amplicon sequencing detected 62 vertebrate taxa to species or genus‐level. Only 22 species were shared between the two methods. Among the target species, amplicon sequencing detected 18 and shotgun sequencing detected 28, with five species shared between datasets (Figure 3E). Overlap among non‐target taxa was minimal, with 15 species shared between the 44 amplicon detections and 2992 shotgun detections (Figure 3F). Notably, while the amplicon approach recovered only avian and mammalian target taxa, the shotgun libraries detected four of the six target reptiles (Tiliqua rugosa, T. scincoides, Lampropholis guichenoti and L. delicata ) (Figure S2).

Similar to the results from the amplicon sequencing, detection success with shotgun sequencing varied strongly by collection method, with active filtration recovering the most target taxa (44% and 38% for the 10 and 20 μm filters, respectively), compared to a single species detection for both passive collections (Figure S2).

Comparing the shotgun and amplicon detections in the Unknown samples, both amplicon detections in Unknown sample 1 ( T. tadornoides , Turdus sp.) were confirmed in the shotgun data, whereas no overlap was observed for Unknown samples 3 and 5. Unknown sample 4 showed partial correspondence, with one of four amplicon detections ( R. marina ) also detected by shotgun sequencing. Overall, shotgun sequencing confirmed only 3 of 11 amplicon‐detected taxa (27%) across the Unknown samples, suggesting low reproducibility of species‐level identifications between methods under these preservation conditions and data filtering approaches.

Across the five Unknown samples, the cumulative relative abundance of the top 20 non‐fish vertebrate taxa varied by three orders of magnitude (0.00002–0.024), indicating strong differences in total DNA signal among samples (Table S4). Despite this variation, the composition of dominant taxa was broadly consistent: between 20%–40% of the top detections per sample corresponded to species represented in the ANWC holdings and 40%–65% matched at least at genus level. Many of these taxa were also represented by spirit‐preserved material (15%–40% at species level, 40%–60% at genus level), suggesting moderate alignment between detections and the collection's liquid‐preserved specimens.

Discussion

Our results confirm that DNA is recoverable from museum preservation media, yet the signal is compositionally complex. Both metabarcoding and shotgun sequencing detected extensive DNA, but the overwhelming majority of reads corresponded to non‐target taxa. This pattern reflects a composite molecular signature: a mixture of specimen‐derived fragments, environmental DNA from the field or laboratory exposure and cross‐contamination accumulated through curatorial history. While preservative ethanol can retain DNA for taxonomic identification under controlled conditions (Hajibabaei et al. 2012; Jeunen et al. 2026; Linard et al. 2016; Shokralla et al. 2010), historical collections introduce a century or more of uncontrolled variables. In contrast to recent heDNA surveys directly from specimen tissue collected under standardised conditions (Jeunen et al. 2024; Junk et al. 2023), DNA contamination within museum specimen media is more sensitive to having been repeatedly opened, topped up and handled with shared equipment. Even in sterile medical settings, preservation media can yield variable DNA profiles containing exogenous taxa (Parekh et al. 2019; Rayo et al. 2021). Together, these findings suggest that spirit‐media DNA represents not a snapshot of a single organism but a molecular record of layered biological and curatorial history.

Across our samples, detection success was driven by chemical rather than temporal factors. Specimens in media with near‐neutral pH and lower residual formaldehyde yielded stronger DNA signals, whereas chronological age showed no significant effect. This mirrors results from formalin‐fixed tissues, where chemical cross‐linking and hydrolytic cleavage are key drivers of molecular degradation alongside postmortem decay prior to fixation and where specimen age is not a strong predictor of DNA yield or fragment length (Hahn et al. 2022; Straube et al. 2021). Despite the ‘Known’ sample representing a composite of specimens with differing preservation histories, clear associations with media chemistry were detectable across samples. Our findings reinforce that DNA persistence in preservation media depends primarily on fixative chemistry and pH, while detection success is further influenced by multiple interacting factors including specimen biology and assay performance. Notably, DNA was recoverable from a high‐pH (~10) waste media sample (Unknown sample 3), indicating that elevated pH does not preclude detection and is consistent with experimental evidence that DNA degradation in formalin is strongly influenced by low pH (Koshiba et al. 1993). For future applications, chemically favourable jars, those with low residual formaldehyde and near‐neutral/buffered pH, offer the greatest potential for heDNA recovery regardless of specimen age. However, preservation chemistry and storage practices vary among collections, including differences in ethanol composition and additives and may influence DNA persistence and recovery; consequently, collection‐specific optimisation will be required.

Hajibabaei et al. (2012) demonstrated that detection probability can scale with organismal abundance and biomass. This pattern aligns with broader observations from eDNA studies, where signal strength associations with biomass are increasingly recognised as both a challenge and an opportunity for ecological inference (Deagle et al. 2019; Elbrecht and Leese 2015; Ershova et al. 2021). We did not quantify biomass here, as weighing all specimens from the 61 jars contributing to the Known sample would have required additional handling and contamination risk. Given the wide size range, from small reptiles to large mammals, any biomass effect is likely confounded by shedding rate, handling history and media chemistry. Future work could test whether accounting for biomass improves predictability of detection success in historical media and compare individual and composite samples to better resolve factors influencing downstream detection outcomes, such as DNA competition and differential fragmentation.

The method of DNA capture and sequencing strongly influenced both yield and interpretability of signals from specimen media. Active filtration maximised DNA recovery and taxonomic breadth but also increased environmental background, whereas passive capture yielded fewer taxa but stronger correspondence with known specimen identities. Comparable sampling method‐dependent differences have been reported in eDNA studies, where sampling method influences both DNA yield and taxonomic composition of recovered signals (Bessey et al. 2022). Beyond sampling method, sequencing approach further shaped both the breadth and interpretability of recovered signals. Shotgun sequencing detected a broader range of vertebrates, including reptiles absent from the amplicon dataset, yet was dominated by low‐abundance, non‐target DNA. These contrasts highlight the trade‐off between sensitivity and specificity inherent in capture and sequencing strategies, where increased taxonomic breadth comes at the cost of greater background signal, a pattern consistent with broader comparisons of metabarcoding and metagenomic approaches (Stat et al. 2017) and with known differences in taxonomic resolution, bias and detection efficiency (Porter and Hajibabaei 2018b). Similar advantages of targeted approaches have been demonstrated in ancient DNA research, where enrichment improves recovery of low‐abundance, fragmented DNA relative to shotgun sequencing (Carpenter et al. 2013). While targeted approaches may improve signal recovery from degraded DNA, they depend on the availability of verified assays using short, informative loci. In this study, we focused on short (~100 bp) amplicons to maximise amplification success from highly fragmented DNA, as longer amplicons tested during method development did not yield reliable amplification. However, the effectiveness of such targeted assays is constrained by the limited taxonomic resolution of short amplicons and while this can be improved through the development of additional loci, this in turn depends on the availability of comprehensive reference databases for assay design and validation. Together, these findings show that sampling design and sequencing strategy jointly shape the composition and interpretability of preservative‐derived DNA signals and are therefore critical for improving identification accuracy and confidence in spirit‐media detections, necessitating careful validation of downstream biological inferences.

As with all metabarcoding and metagenomic analyses, distinguishing genuine biological detections from artefacts requires rigorous controls and careful validation, as sequencing errors and contamination can introduce spurious signals (Cooper and Poinar 2000; Goldberg et al. 2016). Relative read abundance alone cannot reliably separate true signals from noise, as amplification bias, DNA degradation and stochastic effects can decouple sequence counts from organismal abundance (Deagle et al. 2019). The contrasting taxonomic composition of control and experimental samples in our shotgun dataset suggests that much of the background signal reflects ubiquitous environmental DNA introduced during handling and filtration. Environmental DNA studies often incorporate ecological plausibility filters, such as known species distributions, alongside methodological frameworks to limit false positives and support detection validation (Ficetola et al. 2015; Thomsen and Willerslev 2015). While collections such as the ANWC maintain detailed records of vertebrate holdings that can aid such vetting, equivalent references do not exist for the diverse taxa associated with those specimens (their parasites, microbiota and commensals). In principle, detection of such taxa within spirit media could derive from host‐associated microbiota or pathogens preserved alongside specimens, but robust evaluation will require targeted approaches designed specifically for characterising associated biological communities. Interpretation is further complicated by the fact that our media samples were taken from a terrestrial vertebrate collection housed at a dedicated site. Collections that co‐house multiple taxonomic groups would likely yield even more entangled molecular signals, amplifying attribution challenges. Natural history collections therefore present a unique interpretive problem: their purpose is to concentrate and preserve biodiversity across time and space. As a result, taxa from distant regions or eras may legitimately co‐occur, limiting the effectiveness of ecological plausibility filters.

Given this complexity, specimen‐level attribution requires stringent criteria. In most cases, spirit‐media DNA should be interpreted as a collection‐level signal, offering insights into the curatorial environment rather than individual organisms. Such data could support monitoring of collection pest taxa, microbial colonisation and routine quality‐assurance checks, similar to how medical collections use preservation media sequencing to screen for microbial contaminants and historical infections (Parekh et al. 2019; Rayo et al. 2021). Spirit‐media sequencing may also serve as an initial screening tool to identify jars with potential to contain specimens or associations of interest. For example, screening could reveal bulk assemblages containing taxa of conservation or biosecurity concern, or flag jars likely to harbour microbial commensals, parasites, or pathogens that are otherwise invisible to curators. As reference databases and metadata systems expand, probabilistic assignment of sequences to specimens or storage contexts may become feasible. Importantly, museum specimens can also represent valuable biochemical archives, retaining bioactive compounds such as metabolites, venoms and toxins (Zabinski and Bowers 2025), with initial preservation fluids likely to contain relatively high concentrations of these signals. Large‐volume filtration or disposal of preservation fluid should therefore be approached cautiously, with consideration given to preserving or returning filtered media. With appropriate handling and improved metadata integration, spirit‐media analyses could complement traditional molecular approaches by enabling detection of taxa of conservation or biosecurity concern, identifying associated communities and revealing biochemical signatures retained in preservation media.

Spirit media clearly retains amplifiable DNA, affirming their value as molecular archives, but interpretation is constrained by pervasive background signals. Chemical preservation factors, not specimen age, govern detectability and cross‐contamination is pervasive. Future work should prioritise chemically favourable and well‐sealed containers, such as recent accessions or holotypes and employ comprehensive contamination controls, including ethanol‐stock and room‐air blank samples. For applications targeting highly degraded or potentially ancient DNA, additional precautions may be required, including the use of dedicated trace DNA facilities with controlled air handling capabilities to minimise contamination from modern DNA (Knapp et al. 2012). Multi‐marker metabarcoding, spike‐in standards and paired tissue‐and‐media sequencing could further refine interpretation. More broadly, recognising spirit collections as dynamic ecosystems rather than static repositories reframes their molecular potential: the mixed DNA signal is not a flaw but a record in its own right, one that deepens our understanding of how biodiversity and curatorial practices intersect across time.
