Estimating the Share of Social Surplus Generated by Pharmaceutical Innovation Accruing to Manufacturers: A Systematic Literature Review
Authors: Robert J. Nordyke, Tyler D. Wagner, Janine Ross, Sreeranjani Menon, Jonathan D. Campbell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPharmaceutical innovation operates within an implicit social contract that seeks to balance incentives for future drug development with equitable access to current therapies. A key metric for evaluating this balance is producer surplus (PS), which represents the share of total social surplus (the sum of consumer surplus [CS] and PS) captured by manufacturers. We explore the determinants and distribution of estimated supply-side surplus share across therapeutic areas, drug classes, and product life cycles.MethodsWe conducted a systematic literature review of empirical studies on PS in pharmaceuticals. Embase and EconLit databases were searched from database inception to April 2025. Search practices followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidance.ResultsA total of 24 studies were included, representing 68 unique PS estimates. Our analysis reveals wide variation in PS estimates, from 0.3% to 260%, with a median of approximately 15%. The range of estimates is driven primarily by disease-specific factors and methodological inconsistencies. Notably, most studies fail to account for the full spectrum of social benefits or to accurately identify manufacturer revenues, often conflating PS with broader supply chain gains. Only one study met rigorous criteria for both PS and CS definitions.ConclusionThe limitations of current studies on PS hinder the utility of PS in value assessment and policymaking. We argue for greater methodological rigor and consistency to improve PS estimation and inform sustainable public policy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40258-026-01064-0.

Introduction

Background

The implicit social contract in the pharmaceutical industry, balancing the incentives to develop new medicines for future health improvements alongside providing access to life-improving prescription drugs today, is embedded in public policy in most wealthy nations [1, 2]. Yet, while components of the societal benefits of pharmaceutical innovation are well-demonstrated [3–8], whether pharmaceutical manufacturers are appropriately incentivized to develop new medicines remains a point of debate [9]. A common measure of how well the pharmaceutical industry meets its social contract obligations is the concept of producer surplus, the net financial benefit that manufacturers derive from selling pharmaceuticals at prices above their production and operating costs [10]. In welfare economics, producer surplus is typically reported as the portion of total social surplus (defined as the sum of consumer and producer surpluses) that accrues to manufacturers.

The share of total social surplus accruing to pharmaceutical manufacturers is likely shaped by innovation incentives, regulatory frameworks, and market dynamics that vary by country. Additionally, the benefits of new pharmaceuticals, as well as their development and manufacturing costs, will vary depending on the disease state and drug class. Empirical analyses reveal that measures of producer surplus vary significantly across therapeutic areas and over the product life cycle. For instance, studies have found that, in the management of high cholesterol and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, producer surplus averaged approximately 25% and 37% of total health-related social surplus, respectively, during periods of exclusivity, with the remainder accruing to consumers in the form of improved health and reduced costs [11]. However, following the entry of generic competitors and the expiration of patents, the producer surplus declines sharply—often to less than 5% of total surplus—while consumer surplus rises as prices fall and access broadens. Philipson et al. highlight several recent empirical studies that, despite variation in point estimates of producer surplus, collectively suggest that only a small portion of the total surplus of pharmaceutical innovation accrues to manufacturers [12].

There is growing interest in incorporating concepts of producer surplus into health technology assessment (HTA) [10, 13], where, ideally, value-based pricing would seek to align drug prices with their therapeutic and long-term societal benefits. While both health authorities and pharmaceutical companies recognize the importance of economic and cost considerations in pricing decisions, an ongoing debate persists regarding the relative weight assigned to cost-effectiveness, disease characteristics, and patient outcomes within these frameworks [9]. This lack of consensus underscores the challenge of establishing equitable and sustainable pricing strategies that balance innovation incentives with affordable access.

Objectives

Understanding the determinants and distribution of producer surplus is critical for informing health policy, guiding reimbursement decisions, and fostering a more transparent dialog between stakeholders. This paper aims to elucidate the factors that shape producer surplus and to contribute to the ongoing discourse on balancing present and future health improvements.

Methods

Study Design

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidance [14]. Analytic methods, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and search terms were all specified a priori.

The subject of review was empirical studies of producer surplus as a share of total social surplus arising from pharmaceutical innovation. Literature searches were conducted in April 2025 in Embase and EconLit. A flow diagram of the study selection and inclusion process is shown in Fig. 1. The included search terms used in each database are found in Supplemental Table S1 (see the electronic supplementary material). Review Process

Studies were screened for eligibility by two independent reviewers at the title/abstract and full-text levels, and a third reviewer resolved any disagreements. Study exclusions at the abstract and full-text review stages were review papers, editorials, non-empirical or hypothetical analyses, non-pharmaceutical interventions, and studies that did not report or did not allow the calculation of producer surplus as a proportion of total social surplus. Data elements were extracted into a spreadsheet developed in advance of the review. Data elements extracted included the following: study setting (country, disease and patient characteristics, drug intervention); willingness to pay (WTP) for health gains; study methods (including data sources, modeling/analytic methods, and study-specific definitions for consumer and producer surplus); and the primary outcome of producer surplus estimate as a proportion of total surplus. Several studies reported multiple estimates, either for different countries, drug–disease combinations, or differing levels of WTP for health gains; multiple estimates are noted in supplemental Table S2 (see the electronic supplementary material).

Analyses

Descriptive analyses of producer surplus estimates were conducted using Microsoft Excel. Stratified analyses were also performed on variables selected a priori that may affect the outcomes of interest, including country, disease, drug class, WTP for health gains, and modeling/analytic methods. As all data were obtained from published scientific articles, this study was exempt from institutional review board review in accordance with the United States (US) Code of Federal Regulations 45 CFR 46.101(b)(4).

Results

Included Studies

The review identified 24 studies that yielded 68 distinct estimates of supply-side surplus share. Key study characteristics are summarized in supplemental Table S2 (see the electronic supplementary material). Overall, most studies were conducted in the US, representing 75% of studies (18 of 24) and 71% of all estimates (48 of 68). Included studies examined several diseases, including prevalent chronic conditions, mental health, infectious diseases, and oncology. There were two methodological issues that were not anticipated during the design of the study that were identified during data abstraction and analysis. These issues are discussed below.

Methodologies of Included Studies

A notable feature of the included studies was their lack of consensus on analytic methods and data sources. Some studies evaluated the welfare economic implications of a pharmaceutical innovation for a disease state (N = 8, 33%), while others assessed the impact of individual drugs or drug classes (N = 16, 67%). Studies employed a variety of time periods (ranging from 1980 to the present) and time horizons (ranging from 4 to 60 years and the patient lifetime). Several estimates were derived from retrospective analyses, often using aggregate data on health benefits and drug expenditures. In contrast, two studies derived measures of producer surplus from prior cost-effectiveness analyses (CEAs) conducted at the time of drug launch. Counterfactual analyses (i.e., a world without drug X) were used in 11 studies (46%), while an active drug comparator was used in the remaining 13 studies (54%).

The first methodological issue identified ex post involves one study with 12 drug-specific estimates that adopted a unique approach among the 24 included studies [13]. Opportunity costs were accounted for on both the demand side (WTP for health gains) and a supply-side measure that assumes the drug expenditures are entirely offset by comparable reductions in non-drug healthcare services elsewhere in the health system. Including both demand- and supply-side opportunity costs in estimates of a measure of net consumer surplus may be appropriate in cases of a strictly rationing healthcare system or in resource-constrained countries, where the relationship between demand-side WTP for health diverges from the productivity of the system [15]. However, the authors’ definition of “health foregone due to drug expenditures” [13] is not a measure of manufacturers’ revenue and may lead to estimates of producer surplus that are biased upward. This approach differs fundamentally from the theory of welfare economics and the empirical studies based on it, and it yields supply-side surplus shares that exceed the theoretical maximum of 100%. Consequently, the estimates of supply-side surplus share presented below will be stratified accordingly.

Taxonomy of Methods to Estimate Consumer and Producer Surplus

A second important methodological concern identified during abstraction is how these empirical studies define and operationalize the surplus that accrues to demand-side and supply-side stakeholders. Empirical studies of surplus share accruing to manufacturers are hampered by limited analytic methods and data availability, and many studies do not follow definitions of consumer or producer surplus that are strictly consistent with the theory of welfare economics. Instead, studies employ varying definitions of benefit, both for patients/society on the demand side and for manufacturers on the supply side, either alone or combined with the broader pharmaceutical supply chain. Empirical Estimates of Producer Surplus

Overall, only six of 24 studies produced estimates of the true net surplus accruing to manufacturers, and seven estimated net surpluses accruing to consumers; only one study, Romley (2018), estimated both true consumer and producer surpluses. Note that 20 studies estimated surplus accruing to the overall supply side, not specifically to manufacturers. These distinctions are meaningful in that adopting a full societal perspective on consumer benefits would likely increase total consumer surplus and consequently reduce estimates of producer surplus [4, 16]. Similarly, not distinguishing between gross expenditures on drugs and actual manufacturer profits will complicate the interpretation of producer surplus results, due to rebates and other fees, ranging from about 10% to 50% depending on drug class and payer,which accrue to other stakeholders in the pharmaceutical supply chain [17–20].

Estimates of supply-side surplus shares are shown in Table 2 by key study characteristics, including country, disease, and drug class factors. Overall, the mean surplus share is 34.9% (median 15.1%). After excluding one study that double-counted drug expenditures to account for strict rationing in the UK (N = 12 estimates), the mean is 18.9% (median 12.6%). The range of estimates is large, spanning from 0.3% to 260% across all studies, or from 0.3% to 98.3% excluding Woods et al. Supply-side surplus share estimates tend to be higher in oncology among therapeutic areas, while estimates for biologics and cell therapies were higher among drug classes. The use of an active comparator in the analysis and development of a new economic model yields slightly higher point estimates; however, these differences are not statistically significant. Estimates do not vary significantly based on industry funding, with variation appearing to be due to study methods. Excluding estimates from Woods et al., there is little variation in mean point estimates at lower (< $150k/life year [LY]) values of WTP for health, and the lower estimates at higher WTP may be due to outliers with WTP > $500,000/quality-adjusted LY (QALY) (Fig. 2). All studies calculated the value of health gains based on WTP for improvements in QALYs (20 studies) or LYs (4 studies). Across all supply-side surplus share estimates, there is a weak relationship with WTP for health gains (Fig. 2), whereas a clear linear relationship exists between surplus and WTP within studies that included multiple WTP levels. Reflective of their inclusion of both demand- and supply-side opportunity cost measures, the estimates from Woods et al. [13] tend to be much greater than all other estimates. Due to their distinct methodological approach and its resulting impact on estimates, descriptive results are stratified accordingly. The mean supply-side surplus share estimate was 34.9% inclusive of all estimates, 18.9% excluding Woods et al., and 110% for Woods et al. alone. Results from UK studies, as well as those for oncology treatments, yield higher estimates, regardless of the opportunity cost of treatment. There is little meaningful variation in estimates based on comparator or retrospective analyses compared to prospective analyses. That prospective CEAs, with projected cost and benefit data, and retrospective studies, with potentially more accurate historical evidence on costs and benefits, yield such similar results may be explained since most studies focus on health surplus, not total societal benefits accruing to consumers.

Discussion

Median estimates of supply-side surplus shares, based on the current empirical literature, range from approximately 13% to 15%, with the lower range being more prevalent when methods more aligned with conventional economic theory are employed. The current empirical literature exhibits a wide variation in supply-side surplus share estimates, ranging from 0.3% to 260%, making their use in health policy particularly challenging, especially at the drug level and early in the life cycle. We found that this variation is driven primarily by two factors.

First, the total social surplus and supply-side surplus shares of pharmaceutical innovation depend to a large degree on variations in disease characteristics and drug classes. For example, we found the supply-side surplus share to be higher for oncology treatments than for other therapeutic areas and for biologics and cell therapies than for other drug classes. However, the sample size is so limited, even for more common drug classes (small molecules, biologics) and therapeutic areas (chronic conditions, infectious diseases), that clear trends are not statistically apparent.

Second, the diversity of analytic methods used makes broad interpretation difficult. At one level, analytic methods and data sources may contribute to some variability, particularly between studies that employ active comparators and those that do not. Perhaps more important are specific methodological shortcomings that further complicate interpretation across studies and likely lead to an overestimation of the supply-side surplus share in most studies. The first shortcoming is that most studies of surplus shares fail to account for the full range of social benefits arising from pharmaceutical innovation. Studies that do not account for key social benefits are likely to bias the allocation of surplus between consumers and producers. Secondly, most studies do not accurately account for true net manufacturer revenues. Three-quarters of the estimates (Fig. 3, upper panel) are based on drug expenditures that do not reflect common rebates, discounts, and clawbacks that reduce manufacturers’ net revenues. By doing so, these are effectively estimates of surplus accruing to the entire pharmaceutical supply chain. Among the included studies, only one—evaluating two cell therapies in advanced tumors—implemented both true consumer surplus and producer surplus definitions. While detailed data on rebates and ex post clawbacks, for example, are typically not public, information is becoming adequate to make reasonable estimates, and several studies demonstrate how this can be done in different markets [21–25]. Whether we have the tools to estimate the total societal benefits of pharmaceutical innovation is debated [26]; however, the “life income” approach [24] is a promising way to account for non-wage benefits accruing to individuals receiving treatment.

The lack of consistency in methods and limited clarity in empirical estimates of supply-side surplus share may be expected in such a niche and developing area. Nevertheless, addressing these methodological issues is essential as the broad focus on appropriate drug pricing continues with growing attention toward dynamic efficiency. In 2008, Jena and Philipson [27] demonstrated that the theoretical first-best solution to optimal producer surplus to ensure dynamic efficiency during the patent-protected period is a producer surplus of 100% of total social surplus. That is, full surplus appropriation by manufacturers using perfect price discrimination allows all consumers to access current treatments at a price they are willing to pay and maximizes investment funding for future innovations. In practice, several factors limit producer surplus to less than the 100% theoretical optimum: patent races and insurance may both lead to an over-investment in R&D, while public R&D funding may point to lower manufacturer appropriation [26], in addition to market and political feasibility. Expanding on theory, two recently published models attempt to estimate the level of producer surplus that achieves dynamic efficiency in pharmaceutical markets [28, 29]. Both models are early in development and refinement, and we therefore emphasize their methods, units of analysis, and potential data requirements. Woods et al. estimate, in base-case analyses, an optimal producer surplus of 22%. Basu puts the estimate at 70–80%. These new models, which seek to determine whether investment in R&D is socially optimal, highlight a disconnect with empirical studies of producer surplus in that existing empirical studies exclude R&D costs. That is, the body of evidence we have on producer surplus that excludes R&D costs is unlikely to be a good real-world comparator for theoretical models that explicitly address the optimality of R&D expenditures.

The median empirical estimates of producer surplus may be biased upwards yet are still much less than 100%, suggesting, under assumptions of a pure monopoly producer, that there is an imbalance toward current health consumption and too little on future innovation [21]. However, no individual empirical study can imply that pharmaceutical markets are or are not dynamically efficient. As our review of empirical studies reveals, the various estimates across studies may not be directly comparable and may not be consistent with the assumptions underlying the multi-period models that attempt to estimate optimality.

This analysis has several limitations. As highlighted above, the main limitation is the heterogeneity of study methodologies, which limits the ability to generalize results. The available empirical studies of producer surplus address only a small sample of countries and disease states, which further limits the ability to generalize our findings to other markets and health conditions. In addition, there may be publication bias in the available literature. However, some measure of standardization of analytic methods may be required before this issue can be assessed in future reviews.

Conclusions

Our findings underscore the need for greater rigor and consistency in comprehensively capturing elements of value and costs to estimate producer surplus with greater validity. Further research is required to align empirical work grounded in classical one-period microeconomic frameworks that exclude R&D costs with developing models that seek to determine whether R&D expenditures are socially optimal. Currently, existing approaches are insufficient to inform HTAs and health policy, potentially diverting efforts from the goal of providing patients with access to innovative technologies.
