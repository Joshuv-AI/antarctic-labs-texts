Radioiodine‐Refractory Differentiated Thyroid Cancer: Definition, Molecular Mechanisms, and Advances in Precision Therapy
Authors: Yamin Li, Zuoxiang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRadioiodine‐refractory differentiated thyroid cancer, defined by loss of responsiveness to ¹³¹I therapy or by heterogeneous iodine uptake, accounts for the majority of thyroid cancer‐related deaths; 10‐year survival is approximately 10% with distant metastases. Its definition is not uniform: the 2014 expert consensus and the 2015 American Thyroid Association guideline diverge on heterogeneous uptake and on progression despite substantial uptake, and the 2019 Martinique principles reframe these as risk‐stratification triggers rather than absolute disqualifiers. Refractoriness reflects mitogen‐activated protein kinase‐driven silencing of the sodium iodide symporter; TERT promoter mutations, often co‐occurring with BRAF V600E, are associated with dedifferentiation. The therapeutic landscape has changed over the past decade: antiangiogenic multikinase inhibitors (lenvatinib, sorafenib, and cabozantinib) are established first‐ and second‐line options; molecular profiling supports genotype‐driven and tumor‐agnostic treatment of RET, NTRK, and ALK fusions and BRAF V600E mutations; redifferentiation with MEK or combined BRAF/MEK inhibition can restore radioiodine uptake in selected patients, with responses that vary by molecular subtype; and immune checkpoint inhibitors benefit the rare microsatellite instability‐high or mismatch repair‐deficient subset. Most syntheses organize these options by molecular driver and evidence level, whereas the patient's experience of therapy (toxicity, disruption of daily life, decisional preferences) is acknowledged but seldom carried into recommendations. This review compares those definitions, appraises the evidence for each therapeutic class, defines where ¹²⁴I lesional dosimetry can refine treatment timing, and adds the patient dimension as an explicit third axis, operationalized through two tables: a patient‐centered agent comparison and a scenario‐specific decision matrix. The evidence supports, without yet establishing, early broad molecular profiling, a selective inhibitor where an actionable alteration is present, a multikinase backbone otherwise, and redifferentiation only where tested. Randomized evidence is distinguished throughout from expert opinion. Head‐to‐head comparisons and sequencing data remain limited; circulating tumor DNA and imaging‐derived biomarkers, so far supported only by small cohorts, and adaptive trial designs are the most plausible routes forward. By making treatment burden as explicit as genotype and evidence level, the framework is intended to make treatment selection and shared decision‐making more reproducible.Keywords: differentiated thyroid cancer, precision medicine, radioiodine‐refractory thyroid cancer, redifferentiation therapy, targeted therapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors

Introduction

Differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) typically has an excellent prognosis with surgery, suppression of thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH), and radioiodine (131I) therapy. However, a subset of patients develop dedifferentiation and loss of avidity for iodine, leading to radioiodine‐refractory (RAIR) disease, which is characterized by progressive metastatic lesions, imaging heterogeneity, and a significant symptom burden. When metastases are present, the estimated 10‐year survival rate is approximately 10% [1].

Emergence of RAIR signals that further 131I therapy is unlikely to confer benefit, and clinicians must weigh disease control, cumulative toxicity, quality of life, and, where applicable, eligibility for redifferentiation or enrollment in a trial. Imaging is heterogeneous (the “flip‐flop” pattern, with lesions losing iodine uptake yet showing intense avidity for 18F‐FDG on positron emission tomography/computed tomography [PET/CT]), and systemic therapy must balance efficacy against tolerability in coordination with local interventions.

The pivotal Phase III SELECT and DECISION trials demonstrated that vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR)‐targeted therapy significantly prolongs progression‐free survival (PFS) [2, 3], while the subsequent Phase III COSMIC‐311 trial established cabozantinib as the standard second‐line option after progression on VEGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy [4]. Nonetheless, most patients eventually become treatment‐resistant.

Concurrently, molecular classification has transformed the former one‐size‐fits‐all approach: tumors harboring RET or NTRK fusions derive deeper and more durable responses with selective inhibitors, and BRAF V600E enables tumor‐agnostic BRAF/MEK inhibition and redifferentiation therapy where blockade of the mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway restores iodine‐handling genes and 131I uptake. The rare microsatellite instability‐high (MSI‐H)/deficient mismatch repair (dMMR) and high tumor mutational burden (TMB‐H) subsets may derive durable benefit from immune checkpoint blockade.

Recent reviews have mapped the molecular landscape and the expanding inventory of targeted, redifferentiating, and immunotherapeutic options for RAIR‐DTC in detail. This review does not seek to duplicate this synthesis. Rather, we have observed that the bulk of the contemporary literature is organized along a two‐axis logic, namely, molecular driver and trial‐level evidence, within which the patient's lived experience of therapy (cumulative toxicity, treatment‐related disruption to work and daily routines, decisional preferences, and the realities of living with chronic metastatic disease in the long term) is acknowledged but not systematically integrated into recommendations. This review further integrates this patient dimension into a three‐axis decision framework of molecular driver × evidence level × patient dimension and operationalizes it through two complementary tables, that is, a patient‐centered comparison of the principal systemic agents and an integrated, scenario‐specific decision matrix. These are intended for use as tools for structured shared decision‐making rather than as prescriptive rules.

This narrative review synthesizes the clinically actionable evidence for the contemporary management of RAIR‐DTC. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase for English‐language articles published between January 2013 and April 2026, using combinations of the following terms: “radioiodine‐refractory,” “differentiated thyroid cancer,” “tyrosine kinase inhibitor,” “redifferentiation,” “RET fusion,” “NTRK fusion,” “ALK fusion,” “BRAF V600E,” “NRAS,” and “immune checkpoint inhibitor,” together with the names of the individual agents, including lenvatinib, sorafenib, cabozantinib, selpercatinib, pralsetinib, larotrectinib, entrectinib, dabrafenib, trametinib, selumetinib, pembrolizumab, and spartalizumab. The reference lists of pertinent articles, together with the international guidelines and consensus documents issued by the American Thyroid Association (ATA), the European Society for Medical Oncology, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network, the European Association of Nuclear Medicine, and the International Thyroid Oncology Group (ITOG), were screened for additional sources. Evidence was weighted in the following order: Phase III randomized trials > Phase II trials and pooled analyses > prospective cohort studies > retrospective cohort studies > case series; therapeutic evidence published within the preceding 5 years and current international guidelines and consensus statements were given preference. On this basis, we juxtapose the 2014, 2015, and 2019 consensus definitions of refractoriness and identify the two points of substantive divergence between them; appraise each systemic option in turn, distinguishing conclusions established by randomized trials from those resting on single‐arm registration data and expert opinion; and translate that appraisal into the two decision tools introduced above. The intended readership is the clinician who must choose between options of comparable trial‐level evidence and be able to defend that choice.

Definition and Clinical Classification of RAIR‐DTC

RAIR‐DTC is defined by one or more of the following: (1) metastatic lesions that fail to concentrate 131I outside the thyroid bed on the first post‐therapy whole‐body scan (WBS) [5]; (2) loss of 131I uptake in previously 131I‐avid tumor tissue that is not attributable to stable iodine contamination; (3) heterogeneous uptake, whereby some lesions accumulate 131I while others do not [6, 7, 8]; or (4) progression despite substantial radioiodine uptake, with evidence showing that progression after an adequate course of therapy predicts that further radioiodine will be ineffective [9]. Supportive findings include the absence of post‐therapy uptake in structurally or metabolically evident disease (demonstrated by CT, magnetic resonance imaging, or 18F‐FDG PET/CT) and partial uptake confined to select lesions. These categories align with the criteria in the 2015 ATA guideline (recommendation 91) [10], although the two frameworks diverge on how “heterogeneous uptake” and “progression despite substantial uptake” are operationalized, with only the Schlumberger consensus explicitly addressing cumulative 131I activity; the 2019 ATA/European Association of Nuclear Medicine/Society of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging/European Thyroid Association Martinique principles reframe these features as triggers for risk stratification rather than absolute disqualifiers (Table 1). In all three frameworks, the definitions should be applied alongside structural imaging, the kinetics of thyroglobulin, and 18F‐FDG PET/CT [11].

A frequently cited adjunctive criterion is cumulative 131I activity exceeding approximately 22 GBq (600 mCi) in the setting of structurally or biochemically progressive metastatic disease. This threshold appears in the Martinique principles as one of the common clinical scenarios suggesting 131I‐refractory behavior and is reflected in our management algorithm (Figure 1) [12]. However, the 600 mCi value is acknowledged to be somewhat arbitrary: it derives from empiric expert practice rather than from prospectively defined efficacy or toxicity cutoffs, and dose–outcome analyses in patients with radioiodine‐avid pulmonary metastases have shown a continuous, near‐linear association between cumulative 131I activity and overall survival across roughly 300–1000 mCi, without an identifiable inflection point that would justify a single fixed threshold [13]. Conversely, aggressive histologies, including poorly differentiated, tall‐cell, and BRAF V600E (or TERT‐promoter) co‐mutated tumors, may exhaust meaningful benefit from radioiodine at substantially lower cumulative activity levels, whereas young patients with highly avid micronodular pulmonary metastases can continue to derive survival gains beyond 600 mCi. Therefore, consistent with the current consensus, the 600 mCi value should be operationalized as a prompt for structured re‐evaluation of probable benefit, integrating lesional avidity, disease kinetics, previous dose–response, cumulative marrow/pulmonary toxicity, and molecular context, rather than as a rigid disqualifier from further 131I therapy [12].

In practice, decisions should integrate FDG‐PET, CT/magnetic resonance imaging, thyroglobulin, and thyroglobulin doubling time; where available, 124I PET/CT‐based lesional dosimetry can identify patients who are still able to absorb therapeutic doses before retreatment [14, 15]. Predictors of a poor 131I response include older age, poorly differentiated histology, large or multiple metastases, and high 18F‐FDG uptake; a short thyroglobulin doubling time (< 1 year) is associated with aggressive, FDG‐positive biology and can trigger closer surveillance or consideration of systemic therapy [16, 17, 18, 19].

Molecular and Cellular Mechanisms

Approximately 96%–99% of papillary thyroid cancers (PTCs) harbor a single, mutually exclusive driver alteration when mutations and gene fusions are jointly considered [20]. In adult cohorts, BRAF accounts for 61%–62% of cases, RAS (HRAS, NRAS, and KRAS) for approximately 13%, and gene fusions for approximately 15% (RET, NTRK, and ALK fusions together accounting for approximately 9%), segregating tumors into “BRAF‐like” (high MAPK output) and “RAS‐like” (lower MAPK output) classes [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Constitutive driver activation stimulates MAPK signaling, which suppresses thyroid biosynthetic genes, including the sodium iodide symporter (NIS) and thyroid peroxidase; MAPK output inversely tracks with the thyroid differentiation score, linking pathway intensity to loss of radioiodine avidity [20, 25]. MEK inhibitors or combinations of BRAF/MEK blockade upregulate the NIS and restore 131I uptake, providing the biological rationale for redifferentiation strategies [26]. Defective NIS glycosylation, dimerization, and basolateral membrane trafficking further limit functional iodide transport, explaining the discordance between expression of the NIS and 131I uptake [27, 28]. TERT‐promoter mutations, which often co‐occur with BRAF V600E in adults, associate with dedifferentiation and the behavior of RAIR [29, 30].

Alterations in RET and NTRK warrant focused discussion in view of their therapeutic actionability. RET is activated in the cysteine‐rich or kinase domains and by structural rearrangements generating 5′ RET fusions [31, 32]. These fusions are detected in 5%–10% of non‐medullary DTCs in adults [20, 33, 34, 35]. NTRK fusions are relatively rare in adults (2%–3%) but are not uncommon in pediatric and radiation‐associated PTC [36, 37]. RET and NTRK fusions function as potent oncogenic drivers and show high responsiveness to selective inhibitors [31, 38, 39]. Targeted inhibition can partially re‐establish thyroid gene programs and radioiodine avidity in molecularly selected patients [40, 41, 42].

Beyond MAPK output, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis is a parallel and clinically consequential determinant of refractoriness to radioiodine, particularly in follicular‐pattern and oncocytic (Hürthle cell) carcinomas. Whereas BRAF‐driven tumors follow a “BRAF‐like,” MAPK‐dominant trajectory, RAS‐mutant and follicular‐pattern lesions co‐activate PI3K/AKT alongside MAPK, with downstream engagement of mTOR (in part through ERK‐driven p90RSK‐mediated inhibition of TSC2) that compounds the transcriptional suppression of the NIS, thyroid peroxidase, and other thyroid‐differentiation effectors [20]. In a survey of 55 18F‐FDG‐PET‐positive RAIR metastases in 42 patients, Ricarte‐Filho et al. [43] detected the transforming AKT1 E17K allele exclusively in metastatic foci (not in matched primaries) and observed discordant PIK3CA/AKT1 mutations between paired sites in 5/6 patients, indicating that the PI3K pathway is activated during malignant progression rather than at initiation. Consistent with this stepwise model, alterations in PIK3CA and PTEN are uncommon in well‐differentiated PTC (approximately 2% each) and follicular carcinoma (approximately 7%–8%) but are enriched in poorly differentiated and anaplastic disease (approximately 16% and 14%), tracking with loss of differentiation [43]. Simultaneous activation of MAPK and PI3K produces deeper dedifferentiation than either pathway alone [44], and this co‐activation pattern is hypothesized to predict a reduced response to MAPK‐only redifferentiation, motivating interest in combined MAPK and PI3K/mTOR inhibition for follicular‐pattern and Hürthle cell carcinomas, in which PI3K/AKT engagement is most prevalent.

Layered upon these transcriptional programs, epigenetic mechanisms can silence iodide‐handling genes even when a canonical driver mutation is absent; this is an important consideration given that approximately 30%–40% of DTCs lack an identifiable BRAF, RAS, or RTK‐fusion driver [44]. The SLC5A5 (NIS) gene contains a CpG‐rich proximal promoter and a distal NIS enhancer, both of which are targets for DNA hypermethylation in RAIR tumors, with methylation levels inversely correlated with NIS expression; preclinical demethylating agents such as 5‐azacytidine restore the NIS transcript and partial iodide transport in dedifferentiated thyroid cell‐line models. In parallel, BRAF V600E drives histone deacetylation at the NIS promoter, and pharmacological BRAF inhibition (PLX4032), MEK inhibition (AZD6244), or the HDAC inhibitor SAHA (vorinostat) all restore promoter acetylation and NIS expression, supporting chromatin‐level repression as an integral effector of oncogene‐driven NIS silencing [45]. These layers help to explain how tumors without classical MAPK hyperactivation can nonetheless exhibit refractoriness to radioiodine and how they have motivated early clinical exploration of HDAC inhibitors (valproic acid, vorinostat, panobinostat, romidepsin) as redifferentiation agents. However, clinical experience has been modest, with a small romidepsin study restoring 131I avidity in a minority of patients with no objective Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) responses, indicating that single‐agent epigenetic redifferentiation is insufficient and that combination with MAPK pathway inhibitors is likely to be required for durable clinical benefit [44, 45]. Epigenetic regulation thus complements MAPK and PI3K signaling as a mechanism of NIS silencing, and represents a plausible, if still investigational, therapeutic axis for selected patients with RAIR‐DTC.

Diagnostic and Evaluation Pathways

Initial management of advanced or metastatic RAIR‐DTC is guided by symptomatology, tumor kinetics, and lesion burden. Asymptomatic patients with stable disease are monitored by cross‐sectional imaging at approximately 3‐month intervals and, if stability is confirmed, 6‐monthly thereafter, and undergo periodic serum thyroglobulin and anti‐thyroglobulin antibody assessments [10]. 18F‐FDG PET/CT may be used selectively for risk stratification. Although the supporting evidence is largely retrospective and methodologically heterogeneous, emerging data suggest that total metabolic tumor volume and total lesion glycolysis are associated with shorter PFS in patients with RAIR‐DTC [16, 17]. However, prospective multivariate validation and standardization of segmentation/standardized uptake value cut‐offs are still lacking. Extrathyroidal extension of the primary tumor and distant metastases detected on 18F‐FDG PET/CT provide additional prognostic information that may guide treatment intensity.

Disease progression, as defined by RECIST version 1.1, prompts therapeutic intervention [46]. Single‐lesion or oligometastatic progression is generally managed using locoregional approaches, including surgery, thermal ablation, or stereotactic body radiotherapy. In contrast, multifocal progression warrants systemic therapy.

When systemic therapy is indicated, the first‐line agent follows a biomarker‐driven triage. RET fusion tumors are treated with a selective RET inhibitor, NTRK fusion tumors with a tumor‐agnostic TRK inhibitor, and BRAF V600E potentially with combined BRAF/MEK inhibition, which can additionally function as a redifferentiation‐induction strategy to restore 131I avidity before administration of therapeutic radioiodine. Tumors without an actionable driver proceed to a multitargeted VEGFR‐TKI, with the choice of agent based on patient‐specific factors (i.e., comorbidities, anticipated toxicities, and previous therapy). Disease kinetics, thyroglobulin doubling time, 18F‐FDG‐PET activity, and the RECIST‐defined progression rate determine the urgency of initiation of therapy versus continued surveillance. Systemic Therapy: Evidence‐Based Approaches and Sequential Strategies

Building on the biomarker‐driven triage discussed in the previous section, this section presents multitargeted VEGFR‐TKIs, which are the backbone of systemic therapy in biomarker‐negative or post‐progression disease, and the selective inhibitors directed against actionable alterations in RET, NTRK, and BRAF V600E. A direct comparison of molecularly driven precision therapy and multitargeted VEGFR‐TKIs is provided in Table 2.

Multitargeted VEGFR‐TKIs

Early therapeutic efforts in RAIR‐DTC focused on the VEGF/VEGFR axis. However, VEGF‐mediated angiogenesis is only one facet of thyroid oncogenesis, with aberrations in BRAF, RAS, RET/PTC, fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR), and platelet‐derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) also driving tumor proliferation and survival [43, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65]. Therefore, multikinase inhibitors (MKIs) targeting these nodes concurrently are the empirically derived class of agents used to treat biomarker‐negative RAIR [3].

Lenvatinib, an oral MKI targeting VEGFR1–3, FGFR1–4, PDGFRα, RET, and KIT, was evaluated in the pivotal Phase III SELECT trial, which demonstrated a median gain in PFS of 14.7 months over placebo (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.21; 99% confidence interval [CI]: 0.14–0.31; p < 0.001) and an objective response rate (ORR) of 64.8% [2]. SELECT was also the first Phase III RAIR‐DTC TKI trial to record complete responses, an efficacy advantage commonly attributed to the broader inhibitory profile of lenvatinib, including FGFR blockade [47]. The PFS benefit was preserved across all subgroups, including patients who had previously received TKI therapy and all RAIR strata [66], and was independent of BRAF/RAS mutation status. Treatment‐related adverse events occurred in 97.3% of patients (Grade ≥ 3 in 75.9%), with hypertension and proteinuria being the most common, and 14.2% discontinued because of adverse effects [67, 68]. Differences in overall survival did not reach statistical significance (adjusted HR: 0.62), likely because of placebo‐to‐active crossover.

Sorafenib, an oral MKI of VEGFR1–3, RET (including PTC RET fusion proteins), RAF, and PDGFRβ [48], was the first systemic therapy shown to delay progression of RAIR‐DTC. This agent was observed to have activity in three single‐arm Phase II studies in advanced DTC or RAIR‐DTC [69, 70, 71], providing the rationale for the subsequent Phase III evaluation. In the Phase III DECISION trial, sorafenib improved median PFS over placebo (10.8 months vs. 5.8 months; HR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.45–0.76; p < 0.0001) with an ORR of 12.2% versus 0.5% and a disease control rate of 54.1% versus 33.8% [3]. Overall survival did not differ significantly (HR: 0.80) between the study groups, likely because of a 71.4% placebo‐to‐active crossover rate. Adverse events, most commonly hand–foot skin reaction (the principal driver of dose modification), diarrhea, alopecia, rash, fatigue, weight loss, and hypertension, occurred in 98.6% of treated patients, with dose reductions, interruptions, and discontinuations in 64.3%, 66.2%, and 18.8%, respectively. The PFS benefit was independent of BRAF/RAS status [3].

The PFS gain associated with lenvatinib in SELECT (HR: 0.21) was substantially larger than that associated with sorafenib in DECISION (HR: 0.59), an advantage typically attributed to the broader inhibitory profile of lenvatinib (FGFR in addition to VEGFR, PDGFR, and RET) [47]. The ORR is higher for lenvatinib than for sorafenib (64.8% vs. 12.2%); however, lenvatinib is associated with more frequent Grade ≥ 3 toxicity, particularly hypertension, fatigue, and proteinuria [67, 68, 72], whereas sorafenib carries more dermatological toxicity (hand–foot skin reaction, alopecia). Both agents benefit BRAF, RAS, and wild‐type subgroups, establishing multikinase TKIs as the standard biomarker‐negative first‐line backbone, with lenvatinib offering greater efficacy at the cost of higher toxicity.

Cabozantinib is the established second‐line option after progression on first‐line VEGFR‐TKI therapy. The Phase III COSMIC‐311 trial demonstrated that cabozantinib significantly prolonged PFS over placebo (median 11.0 months vs. 1.9 months; HR: 0.22, 96% CI: 0.15–0.32; p < 0.0001) in patients with RAIR‐DTC who had progressed after lenvatinib and/or sorafenib. Updated analyses with extended follow‐up confirmed sustained efficacy with no new safety signals [4]. Importantly, the PFS benefit was consistent regardless of the type of previous VEGFR‐targeted therapy (lenvatinib alone, sorafenib alone, or both sequentially) and across tumor histologies, including papillary, follicular, oncocytic, and poorly differentiated subtypes [49].

Across the VEGFR‐TKI class, clinical implementation occurs within a multidisciplinary care framework that emphasizes proactive monitoring and management of toxicity, given the significant adverse event profiles associated with MKIs, particularly hypertension (often requiring aggressive management), diarrhea, fatigue, decreased appetite, stomatitis, and palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia. Real‐world studies have confirmed that maintaining an adequate relative dose intensity through proactive management of toxicity optimizes clinical outcomes with lenvatinib. On the basis of the SELECT efficacy data and the favorable cumulative experience with toxicity management, current National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines designate lenvatinib as the Category 1 preferred first‐line systemic therapy for progressive RAIR‐DTC, with sorafenib retained as an alternative first‐line option and cabozantinib established as the standard second‐line agent after progression on VEGFR‐TKI therapy. Molecularly Driven Precision Therapy

In RET fusion‐positive DTC, selpercatinib produces strong, durable responses with favorable tolerability and is preferred before broad‐spectrum VEGFR‐TKIs when a fusion is detected [31]; safety monitoring centers on liver function, the QT interval, and drug–drug interactions. In NTRK fusion‐positive disease, the tumor‐agnostic tropomyosin receptor kinase (TRK) inhibitors larotrectinib and entrectinib have yielded consistently high ORRs with durable benefit [38, 50]. Early next‐generation sequencing (NGS), when systemic therapy is first indicated, maximizes access to these agents. In BRAF V600E‐mutant DTC, dabrafenib plus trametinib may serve as a tumor‐agnostic option and can function uniquely as a redifferentiation–induction regimen restoring 131I uptake before therapeutic administration of 131I [51].

Evidence for the efficacy of RET‐directed therapy in RET fusion‐positive DTC rests on the thyroid cohort in the Phase I/II LIBRETTO‐001 trial, which enrolled patients with RET‐mutant medullary and RET fusion‐positive non‐medullary thyroid cancer in pre‐specified, separately analyzed cohorts [31]. Long‐term follow‐up revealed an ORR of 95.8% in treatment‐naïve fusion‐positive patients and a 3‐year PFS rate of 87.3% at a median of 24.9 months [52]. The class‐level activity of selpercatinib is further supported by the Phase III LIBRETTO‐531 trial in advanced RET‐mutant medullary thyroid cancer, in which the effect of selpercatinib on PFS was superior to that of the physician's choice of cabozantinib/vandetanib (HR: 0.28; 95% CI: 0.16–0.48; p < 0.001) [73]; moreover, selpercatinib had a more favorable toxicity profile. The findings of LIBRETTO‐531 cannot be directly extrapolated to RET fusion‐positive DTC, which is a biologically and clinically distinct entity. Therefore, first‐line selpercatinib in fusion‐positive DTC rests on evidence from LIBRETTO‐001 and not LIBRETTO‐531.

Pralsetinib, a second‐generation selective RET inhibitor, provides complementary evidence in this setting. In the Phase I/II ARROW trial, patients with previously treated RET fusion‐positive thyroid cancer achieved an ORR of 89% (8/9; 95% CI: 52–100) in the initial registration analysis [53] and 90.9% (20/22; 95% CI: 70.8–98.9) in the updated analysis with a median duration of response of 23.6 months (95% CI: 15.1–not estimable) [54]. Both selpercatinib and pralsetinib have been studied predominantly in small single‐arm cohorts, with no head‐to‐head randomized comparison reported to date. The clinically relevant safety distinction is a class‐specific but agent‐prominent risk of pulmonary toxicity with pralsetinib: the risk of Grade ≥ 3 pneumonitis is approximately 4% with this agent, and interstitial lung disease emerged as a treatment‐limiting adverse event in ARROW, including discontinuations [53, 54]. In contrast, the profile of selpercatinib is dominated by hypertension, elevated transaminases, and QT prolongation [31, 52]. Therefore, the choice of agent reflects accessibility, pre‐existing pulmonary comorbidity (favoring selpercatinib in patients with chronic lung disease or previous thoracic radiotherapy), and the cardiovascular risk profile (favoring pralsetinib when QT or uncontrolled hypertension is a concern), rather than differences in efficacy.

NTRK‐directed therapy in DTC is grounded in tumor‐agnostic registration data, where fusions are present in 2%–3% of DTCs [36, 37]. Analysis of pooled data from the Phase I/II trials of larotrectinib found an ORR of 79% in the thyroid subgroup (95% CI: 58–93; n = 24 evaluable), with median duration of response not reached [38]; a thyroid‐specific pooled analysis (n = 28) found an overall ORR of 71% (95% CI: 51–87), which increased to 86% (95% CI: 64–97) in the PTC/follicular thyroid cancer (FTC) subset and decreased to 29% (95% CI: 4–71) in patients with anaplastic disease [55]. These estimates derive from small cohorts with mixed histology and should be interpreted with the corresponding precision in mind. Entrectinib has shown comparable activity (ORR: 57% across tumor types) [50].

ALK fusions occur in a small minority of DTCs, accounting for approximately 0.8% in The Cancer Genome Atlas PTC cohort [20], with STRN‐ALK being most frequent and EML4‐ALK and others also documented [56]. By the same tumor‐agnostic logic that prioritizes selective inhibitors for RET and NTRK fusions, ALK‐rearranged DTC is biologically a candidate for ALK‐directed therapy. There have been isolated reports of meaningful activity with first‐generation and second‐generation ALK inhibitors, including a complete response to crizotinib in an ALK‐rearranged oncocytic thyroid carcinoma [57], a partial response to lorlatinib after development of resistance to crizotinib in an EML4‐ALK‐rearranged tumor [58], and a sustained partial response to alectinib in metastatic STRN‐ALK PTC following radioiodine, chemotherapy, and external beam radiotherapy [59]. However, the evidence base remains confined to case reports, and no prospective trial has formally evaluated the response rate or its durability. Therefore, the consistent recommendation is to include ALK alongside RET, NTRK, and BRAF in routine NGS panels for advanced DTC or RAIR‐DTC, offering an ALK inhibitor on a compassionate‐use/off‐label basis or enrollment in tumor‐agnostic basket trials rather than elevating ALK‐directed therapy to the same evidence tier as selpercatinib or larotrectinib.

A precision medicine gap of growing clinical relevance concerns the RAS‐mutated subgroup, for which the molecular driver‐to‐matched drug paradigm has yet to deliver an approved targeted agent. RAS mutations, predominantly NRAS Q61, with smaller contributions from HRAS and KRAS, account for approximately 13% of adult cases of PTC and a larger proportion of follicular and poorly differentiated thyroid carcinomas, making this the second most common MAPK driver after BRAF V600E [20, 35, 63]. In contrast with the RET‐positive, NTRK‐positive, and BRAF V600E‐positive subgroups discussed earlier, no approved targeted agent analogous to selpercatinib or larotrectinib currently exists for RAS‐mutant DTC. The KRAS G12C‐specific covalent inhibitors sotorasib and adagrasib, which have reshaped practice in non‐small‐cell lung cancer and colorectal cancer, are not applicable here because the RAS mutations seen in thyroid cancer are almost exclusively at codon 61 (most commonly NRAS Q61R/K/L) rather than at the codon‐12 cysteine required by these agents. The proof‐of‐concept of selumetinib‐driven redifferentiation by Ho et al. [26] identified a notable signal specifically in NRAS‐mutant tumors (5/5 with increased 124I uptake; 4/5 with a confirmed partial response after subsequent 131I). However, the negative results of the Phase III ASTRA trial in the specific setting of high‐risk adjuvant DTC [74], together with more modest 6‐month partial response rates of approximately 10% in less selected RAS‐mutant cohorts treated with trametinib as monotherapy [42], have tempered enthusiasm for MEK‐only strategies as a routinely deployable option in this subgroup. Therefore, in current practice, patients with RAS‐mutant RAIR‐DTC are managed predominantly with multitargeted VEGFR‐TKIs (lenvatinib first‐line; cabozantinib as the established second‐line agent after progression on a VEGFR‐TKI), which is the same default applied to driver‐negative disease, despite the molecular distinction. Emerging RAS(ON) multiselective inhibitors, most notably daraxonrasib (RMC‐6236), which has broad‐spectrum activity against mutant and wild‐type KRAS, HRAS, and NRAS and is currently undergoing Phase III evaluation in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and RAS‐mutant non‐small‐cell lung cancer, with earlier‐phase evaluation ongoing in additional RAS‐mutant tumor types, including colorectal cancer [75], represent a biologically rational future avenue for treatment of NRAS‐mutant DTC. However, no thyroid cancer‐specific prospective clinical data have yet been reported. Additional experimental strategies, including T‐cell receptor‐based immunotherapies directed against the recurrent NRAS Q61 neoantigen and inhibitors targeting the SHOC2‐RAS interaction, remain at the preclinical or early‐phase stage. Therefore, the RAS‐mutant subgroup stands out as a clearly defined unmet need within the otherwise rapidly expanding DTC precision therapy landscape, with rational therapeutic pathways now in development but no immediately deployable matched targeted option available in 2026.

When RET or NTRK fusions are identified, selective inhibitors are prioritized over nonselective VEGFR‐TKIs (Table 2). Transitions between classes should account for overlapping hepatic, cardiovascular, QT, and dermatological toxicities; combination strategies (a TKI combined with a selective inhibitor or immunotherapy) remain investigational and should be pursued within clinical trials.

Overall, fusion‐driven and mutation‐driven targeted therapy is a paradigm shift from broad‐spectrum kinase inhibition to molecularly defined precision therapy. Selpercatinib, larotrectinib, and the dabrafenib plus trametinib combination offer higher response rates and improved tolerability when matched, and routine NGS enables early identification of actionable alterations.

Several challenges persist, in that a subset of patients lack actionable molecular alterations, resistance mechanisms to fusion‐targeted inhibitors are still emerging, and optimal combination or sequential strategies for resistant or relapsed disease require prospective validation. The success of selective inhibition in fusion‐positive disease has not been mirrored consistently across other molecular subgroups. The diversity of redifferentiation outcomes reviewed in the following section reflects unresolved biological heterogeneity in iodine‐handling restoration rather than a consistently mature therapeutic class.

Redifferentiation Strategies

Redifferentiation therapy occupies a distinct conceptual space within the RAIR‐DTC armamentarium. Rather than replacing 131I, it attempts to re‐enable 131I in selected patients whose tumors have lost iodine‐handling competence, a goal for which the evidence base is mechanistically compelling but clinically inconsistent across molecular subtypes and study designs. Early non‐MAPK strategies (retinoids [76, 77, 78] and lithium [79]) produced at best modest benefit and were constrained by an inability to quantify uptake and retention of 131I.

In the proof‐of‐concept study reported by Ho et al. [26], selumetinib, a selective MEK1/2 inhibitor, was shown to restore 131I avidity in patients with RAIR‐DTC by enhancing iodine uptake through inhibition of the MAPK pathway. All five NRAS‐mutant patients had increased iodine uptake (4/5 confirmed partial response, 1/5 stable disease after 131I), whereas only 1/9 BRAF‐mutant patients reached the therapeutic dosimetry threshold and achieved a partial response, consistent with preclinical evidence that RAS suppresses thyroid‐specific gene expression in an MEK‐dependent manner [80, 81]. The variable BRAF response may reflect incomplete inhibition of the MAPK pathway at the higher pathway flux of BRAF‐driven tumors. The research by Ho et al. [82] established that a short induction course can produce durable 131I resensitization, distinguishing this approach from long‐term kinase inhibitor therapy.

Building on the proof‐of‐concept research by Ho et al. [26], larger prospective and real‐world cohorts have refined the picture of MAPK pathway‐driven redifferentiation, with the response now most informatively reported by molecular subtype rather than as a single pooled success rate. A recent ITOG consensus statement summarizing prospective trials and case reports across BRAF V600E, RAS, and more recently RTK fusion‐driven RAIR‐DTC, reports restoration of iodine uptake in 33%–95% of treated patients and tumor response rates of 11%–80% [42]. The breadth of these ranges reflects substantial methodological heterogeneity between studies in terms of inclusion criteria, the redifferentiating agent used and the duration of treatment, activity of administered 131I, and whether dosimetry was used to gate retreatment. Therefore, data obtained by cross‐trial pooling should be interpreted cautiously. For BRAF V600E‐mutated tumors, BRAF inhibition (vemurafenib and dabrafenib) or combined BRAF/MEK inhibition (dabrafenib plus trametinib) has yielded best RECIST partial response rates ranging from 0% to 57% across prospective trials, with confirmed partial response rates of 33% and 38% at 6 months in the two larger Phase II studies; vemurafenib monotherapy produced a RECIST partial response of approximately 20%, and in a Phase II randomized comparison, the addition of trametinib to dabrafenib did not improve response over dabrafenib alone (modified‐RECIST objective response rate 48% vs. 42%, p = 0.67; RECIST 1.1 objective response rate 30% vs. 35%) [42, 83, 84]. Notably, real‐world cohorts treating less stringently selected patients with BRAF V600E tumors have shown markedly lower benefit, with a best partial response of approximately 6% and restoration of iodine uptake in approximately 37%, highlighting the fact that figures from prospective trials reflect carefully selected populations [42, 85]. For RAS‐mutated (predominantly NRAS) tumors, the Ho et al. [26] proof‐of‐concept study of selumetinib indicated near‐universal restoration of iodine uptake and a high partial response rate after subsequent 131I; however, follow‐up cohorts using trametinib monotherapy in less selected RAS‐mutant populations have reported a considerably more modest tumor shrinkage rate of approximately 10% during on‐treatment exposure, and real‐world series similarly show high restoration of iodine uptake (approximately 82%) that is decoupled from the structural response (best partial response approximately 8%) [42, 85]. For RTK fusion‐driven disease (RET and NTRK), evidence from case series suggests that selpercatinib and larotrectinib can similarly re‐induce 131I avidity; however, the data remain anecdotal and prospective evaluation is ongoing. Taken together, the often‐cited “33%–95%” redifferentiation success envelope should be read as a cross‐study span rather than a clinically actionable expectation. The benefit realized depends on the molecular subtype, drug regimen used, threshold used to define “successful” redifferentiation, and the rigor of patient selection in the original study. The ITOG statement accordingly endorses redifferentiation as a viable strategy for selected patients with metastatic RAIR disease lacking 131I uptake while emphasizing that randomized data establishing its effectiveness against standard‐of‐care VEGFR‐TKI therapy are still lacking.

An important counterpoint to enthusiasm about MAPK‐driven redifferentiation comes from the Phase III ASTRA trial, which evaluated selumetinib in the adjuvant (not RAIR) setting [74]. ASTRA enrolled 400 patients with high‐risk DTC (primary tumor > 4 cm, gross extrathyroidal extension, or at least one metastatic lymph node ≥ 1 cm or ≥ 5 nodes of any size, with exclusion of Hürthle, medullary, and anaplastic carcinomas). In this study, 233 patients were randomized in a ratio of 2:1 to receive selumetinib 75 mg twice daily (n = 155) or matching placebo (n = 78) for approximately 5 weeks, with recombinant human TSH‐stimulated 131I (100 mCi) administered on treatment Day 31. The primary endpoint of complete remission at 18 months after 131I was not met: remission was achieved in 40% of patients (62/155) who received selumetinib and in 38% of those (30/78) who received placebo (odds ratio: 1.07; 95% CI: 0.61–1.87; p = 0.82). Grade ≥ 3 treatment‐related adverse events occurred in 16% of selumetinib‐treated patients (most commonly acneiform dermatitis, 7%) and in 0% in the placebo arm, with only 83 of 154 patients (54%) in the selumetinib arm meeting the prespecified criterion for treatment compliance (study drug taken for ≥ 7 days before, on the day of, and for five consecutive days after 131I) [86]. The findings of ASTRA temper enthusiasm for a single agent‐agnostic “MEK‐inhibitor plus RAI” approach and highlight three considerations that bear directly on RAIR‐specific redifferentiation: (1) the population in ASTRA (high‐risk adjuvant DTC, not metastatic RAIR) differs fundamentally from the populations in which the redifferentiation rationale was developed, so the negative result does not invalidate molecularly selected RAIR redifferentiation but does caution against extrapolating enthusiasm for MEK inhibitors across DTC populations; (2) the toxicity profile of selumetinib compromised dose intensity in roughly half of treated patients, raising the question of whether a more tolerable MEK or BRAF/MEK regimen, administered with rigorous monitoring of adherence, might perform differently; and (3) molecular subgroup selection (BRAF V600E vs. RAS vs. driver‐negative) was not the primary enrollment criterion in ASTRA, whereas accumulated redifferentiation evidence indicates that the response is strongly subtype‐dependent, a point that the ASTRA investigators themselves emphasized in calling for “tumor genotype‐tailored drug selection” in future redifferentiation trials [74].

A persistent methodological challenge across the redifferentiation literature is the use of 124I PET/CT for lesional dosimetry to gate retreatment: although 124I PET‐derived absorbed dose estimates are more quantitative than 131I or 123I scintigraphy, and can detect, for example, the approximately ninefold post‐redifferentiation increase in the lesional absorbed dose reported with dabrafenib plus trametinib in the ERRITI study, the technology is available at only a limited number of specialist centers globally and is not standard of care in most national‐level health care systems [42]. For centers without access to 124I PET, practical alternatives that have been used in published redifferentiation protocols include the following: (1) qualitative or semiquantitative visual assessment of post‐redifferentiation 123I scintigraphy with SPECT/CT, with iodine avidity defined operationally by a target‐to‐background ratio > 4 and lesion uptake at least twice the mean uptake in the liver parenchyma; (2) empirical 131I activity prescription protocols administered under recombinant human TSH stimulation when dosimetric gating is unavailable; and (3) serial stimulated thyroglobulin trajectories combined with RECIST 1.1 imaging as surrogate biochemical and structural efficacy endpoints. Two further caveats apply even when dosimetry is available: lesional dosimetry performed while the patient is still receiving an MAPK pathway inhibitor has been shown to overestimate post‐therapeutic dosimetry by approximately one‐third, and interlesional heterogeneity within a single patient is not captured by single‐lesion dosimetry approaches and remains an unresolved limitation independent of the imaging modality chosen [42].

Redifferentiation therapy is most appropriate for patients with a controllable tumor burden and moderately progressive disease who can tolerate a short induction course. Systemic therapy or local intervention should be prioritized for patients with rapidly progressive disease or an imminent threat to organ function. It is important to emphasize that redifferentiation is not a “one‐time” strategy; in selected patients, repeated attempts at redifferentiation may be feasible during subsequent therapeutic windows. Biomarker‐Driven Immunotherapy

In DTC, the baseline mutational burden is generally low, which helps to explain the limited activity of single‐agent PD‐1 blockade in unselected RAIR‐DTC. The Cancer Genome Atlas analysis of PTC characterized a comparatively low somatic alteration rate and MAPK‐driven biology, supporting modest immunogenicity a priori [20].

Immunotherapy becomes a rational therapeutic option when specific biomarkers are positive. The multi‐cohort Phase II KEYNOTE‐158 trial demonstrated that pembrolizumab produced durable antitumor activity across MSI‐H/dMMR solid tumors, establishing the tumor‐agnostic indication [87]. For TMB‐H disease (≥ 10 mutations/Mb), a prespecified KEYNOTE‐158 analysis showed that a high tissue tumor mutational burden was associated with improved outcomes with pembrolizumab, underpinning the tumor‐agnostic approval [88].

In thyroid‐specific cohorts, activity has been modest overall. KEYNOTE‐028 (Phase Ib, PD‐L1‐positive DTC) reported an ORR of approximately 9% (2/22) with manageable safety, demonstrating benefit in a minority of patients [89]. The KEYNOTE‐158 thyroid cohort (Phase II) further suggested manageable toxicity with responses observed in a small subset of previously treated thyroid cancers [90]. In summary, immunotherapy in RAIR‐DTC is biomarker‐driven. Pembrolizumab is appropriate for MSI‐H/dMMR or TMB‐H disease. Outside these subsets, responses are uncommon, and VEGFR‐TKIs or fusion‐targeted agents remain the default therapeutic backbone, with immune‐related adverse events managed per American Society of Clinical Oncology guidance.

Perspectives

Three questions remain unresolved: how multitargeted VEGFR inhibition, driver‐selective inhibition, and redifferentiation should be sequenced; why redifferentiation outcomes differ so markedly between molecular subtypes; and how narrow the biomarker‐defined role of immunotherapy currently is. Each is considered below, with recommendations supported by randomized data distinguished from those resting on expert opinion.

SELECT, DECISION, and COSMIC‐311 established lenvatinib/sorafenib as first‐line therapy and cabozantinib as second‐line therapy in biomarker‐negative disease [2, 3, 4]. Selective inhibitors for RET and NTRK fusions deliver deeper and more durable responses with tolerability superior to that of broad‐spectrum TKIs [31, 38, 50]. The cumulative evidence, albeit as yet not from head‐to‐head trials, supports a working hierarchy in which selective inhibitors are used first‐line for actionable fusions while VEGFR‐MKIs remain the default for biomarker‐negative or post‐fusion lines. The optimal sequencing of these modalities, and the position of redifferentiation within that sequence, has not been examined in randomized comparative trials and is determined on a case‐by‐case basis.

Redifferentiation therapy is a second therapeutic pillar. MEK or combined BRAF/MEK blockade can re‐express the NIS and restore 131I uptake in selected patients, occasionally producing durable benefit after a short induction course followed by 131I [26, 51]. The key unanswered question is timing, that is, before any VEGFR‐TKI, between lines, or after initial targeted therapy. Where accessible, 124I PET/CT dosimetry quantifies the dose absorbed at the lesion level, allowing individualized selection; without it, visual 123I SPECT/CT, empirical recombinant human TSH‐stimulated 131I, and serial stimulated thyroglobulin trajectories remain pragmatic surrogates.

Immunotherapy plays a biomarker‐restricted role. The low mutational burden and MAPK‐dominant biology of DTC align with the modest activity of immunotherapy in unselected cohorts, whereas MSI‐H/dMMR or TMB‐H subsets derive the clinically meaningful benefit that underpinned the tumor‐agnostic approvals of pembrolizumab [20, 87, 88, 89, 90]. MSI/TMB testing should accompany NGS when systemic therapy is first contemplated.

Several features of the current evidence base warrant explicit acknowledgment, both to calibrate the reader's confidence in the recommendations made and to signal where the field has not yet converged. First, the systemic therapy backbone for biomarker‐negative RAIR‐DTC rests on placebo‐controlled Phase III trials (SELECT for lenvatinib, DECISION for sorafenib, and COSMIC‐311 for cabozantinib). However, no randomized head‐to‐head trial has compared lenvatinib with sorafenib in the first‐line setting or compared cabozantinib with continued lenvatinib or sorafenib re‐challenge in the second‐line setting. Therefore, current first‐line and second‐line choices reflect cross‐trial comparisons, timing of registration, and the tolerability profile rather than direct comparative efficacy. Second, the operational definition of “successful redifferentiation” varies across studies. Some authors require restoration of 124I uptake above a dosimetric absorbed‐dose threshold, others require a RECIST partial response after subsequent 131I, and others require a decline in serum thyroglobulin. Therefore, the wide cross‐study response rate envelope reflects definitional and methodological heterogeneity at least as much as biological variation in response. Third, the resistance landscape for selective RET, NTRK, and BRAF inhibitors in DTC is far less mature than that in non‐small‐cell lung cancer or melanoma. On‐target gatekeeper and solvent‐front mutations have been described, but molecular triage at progression, sequencing of next‐generation selective inhibitors, and the role of VEGFR‐TKI rechallenge after failure of fusion‐directed therapy are not yet supported by prospective DTC‐specific data and are largely extrapolated from other tumor types. Fourth, the relative positioning of redifferentiation against continued VEGFR‐MKI therapy in molecularly eligible patients has not been tested in a randomized trial, and the negative Phase III ASTRA result in the adjuvant setting tempers, without invalidating, enthusiasm for the broader paradigm in metastatic disease. Taken together, these gaps mean that several treatment‐sequencing decisions in contemporary management of RAIR‐DTC remain driven by expert opinion, and the framework presented in this review is intended to organize rather than prescribe such decisions.

Several knowledge gaps warrant future investigation. First, adaptive sequencing that integrates fusion‐targeted therapy, redifferentiation, and VEGFR‐TKI therapy requires prospective validation using patient‐centered endpoints (quality of life, time to next treatment) rather than PFS alone [2, 4]. Second, liquid biopsy and image‐derived biomarkers in RAIR‐DTC are supported chiefly by small thyroid‐specific cohorts. Proof‐of‐concept series show that serial BRAF V600E circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) detects progression earlier than thyroglobulin [91] and that plasma BRAF V600E achieves 100% tissue concordance in anaplastic thyroid carcinoma [92]. Nevertheless, a synthesis of seven thyroid ctDNA studies found a low yield in early‐stage disease and wide variation in the assays used, positioning ctDNA as a follow‐up adjunct rather than a stand‐alone diagnostic procedure [93]. For imaging biomarkers, ultrasound radiomics predicts recurrence of PTC (area under the curve: 0.85–0.89; n = 554) [94], and early 18F‐FDG PET/CT predicts the response to lenvatinib (area under the curve: 0.71 for the standardized uptake value; n = 21) [95]; however, both require external validation. Third, adaptive basket and platform trial designs should address the heterogeneity of RAIR‐DTC, which ranges from indolent oligoprogressive disease to rapidly progressive, high‐burden phenotypes that may warrant early escalation of systemic therapy.

Two clinical cautions merit emphasis. First, cross‐toxicity accumulates across lines (hypertension and proteinuria for VEGFR‐TKI, hepatic and QT effects for RET inhibitors, pyrexia and dermatological events for BRAF/MEK inhibitors, and PD‐1 immune‐related adverse events), requiring proactive monitoring and dose modification algorithms from the outset. Second, while the 10‐year survival rate remains poor when metastases are present, earlier molecular triage and judicious use of redifferentiation strategies offer tangible opportunities to prolong disease control with acceptable quality of life [1, 26, 31].

Three broad orientations emerge and are summarized across molecular, evidentiary, and patient–dimension axes in Table 5. While acknowledging that none has been confirmed by randomized comparative trials in RAIR‐DTC, these three orientations are as follows: broad NGS with MSI/TMB assessment can identify the minority of patients for whom matched‐driver or biomarker‐driven therapy is rational; the timing of redifferentiation attempts and line transitions is best individualized, informed by lesion‐level dosimetry where available and by stimulated thyroglobulin trajectories and visual 123I scintigraphy elsewhere; and selective inhibitors are preferred when an actionable fusion is detected, with VEGFR‐MKI serving as the durable backbone in biomarker‐negative disease, alongside ongoing attention to quality of life and multidisciplinary local control. These orientations are best understood as a shared‐decision framework rather than as definitive sequencing rules.

Conclusion

Management of RAIR‐DTC has evolved from empiric post‐radioiodine salvage to an evidence‐based, molecularly informed paradigm. Current evidence supports, without yet formally establishing, early broad molecular profiling, a selective inhibitor as a reasonable first‐line therapy when an actionable RET, NTRK, BRAF V600E, or ALK alteration is identified, and VEGFR‐MKI as the durable backbone for biomarker‐negative disease. MAPK pathway redifferentiation can restore 131I avidity in molecularly selected patients, although the response is heterogeneous across subtypes. The negative results of the Phase III ASTRA in the adjuvant setting caution against uncritical extrapolation; pembrolizumab offers a meaningful option for the small MSI‐H/dMMR or TMB‐H subset. Two caveats apply: the optimal sequencing of selective inhibitors, redifferentiation, and VEGFR‐multikinase backbones has not been tested in randomized head‐to‐head trials, and the strongest recommendations rest on evidence from the Phase III RCTs (SELECT, DECISION, and COSMIC‐311), whereas selective inhibitor positioning, redifferentiation timing, and biomarker‐driven immunotherapy rest on single‐arm registration trials, real‐world cohorts, or expert consensus. Within these limits, early molecular profiling, individualized sequencing, attention to cumulative toxicity and quality of life, and multidisciplinary collaboration remain the most defensible principles to guide care.
