Ladder‐Like Structural Architecture of Layered Magnetic A 2.4 Cr8Te14 (A = Rb, Cs) Compounds by Self‐Flux Synthesis
Authors: Kai D Röseler, Felix Eder, Fabian O von Rohr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe discovery and control of intergrowth structures represent an important avenue for the targeted synthesis of new, more complex structure types. When including magnetic framework metal atoms, this enhanced complexity can transfer to rich magnetic ground states. Here, we show that the subtle adjustment of the composition of alkali–tellurium fluxes enables the synthesis of a new family of alkali chromium tellurides, A 2.4 Cr8Te14 (A = Rb, Cs). Their ladder‐like crystal structures integrate the 2D character of delafossite‐like ACrTe2 with the tunnel motifs of hollandite‐like AxCr5Te8 phases. This results in a previously unobserved unique hybrid framework. Direction‐dependent magnetization measurements on oriented single crystals reveal distinct magnetic ground states: Rb2.4Cr8Te14 is antiferromagnetic with TN = 114.5 K, while Cs2.4Cr8Te14 is ferrimagnetic with TC = 125.0 K. This work underscores the simplicity and effectiveness of flux growth as a design strategy for discovering low‐dimensional materials.Keywords: chromium tellurides, flux synthesis, intergrowth phases, layered materials, low‐dimensional magnetism

Introduction

The search for new functional materials increasingly turns to modular “synthesis‐by‐design” strategies, in which known structural units are deliberately combined to generate targeted properties [1, 2, 3]. One structural realization of this principle is the formation of complex structures of compounds that can be interpreted as intergrowth of segments of simpler structures [4, 5]. Such intergrowth phases often serve as structural bridges between known phases and provide a rational route toward new, more complex architectures [6, 7].

For this study, we considered the family of ternary alkali‐chromium tellurides. In this system, all known phases up to date follow one of two structure types: The first known structure type is found for the smaller alkali cations A = Li, Na, K and can be considered as delafossite‐like layered phases with a composition of ACrTe2. The crystal structure of these delafossite‐type phases consists of CrTe2 sheets separated by layers of intercalated alkali cations [8, 9]. We have recently established a synthetic approach to obtain large single crystals of delafossite‐type ACrX2 (X = chalcogen) phases by employing a A/X‐self‐flux synthesis approach [10, 11, 12]. It is worth noting that the deintercalation of these phases by soft‐chemical methods has become a central strategy for accessing metastable, magnetic, CrIV‐based vdW materials such as CrTe2 [13, 14].

The second known structure type for A–Cr–Te phases — exhibited by the heavier alkali metals — follows a composition of AxCr5Te8 (A = K, Rb, Cs; 0.5≤x≤1) and corresponds to a hollandite‐like architecture. Here, the CrTe2 layers are interconnected by Cr2Te2 slabs forming a tri‐periodic framework perforated by channels occupied by alkali cations [15, 16]. The synthesis route reported for these phases either used a solid‐state reaction of the elements [15] (A = K, Rb, Cs), or a reaction of elemental Cr, Te, and either Cs2CO3 or CsTe4 as Cs‐sources [16]. By altering the amount of Cs‐source used, different under‐stoichiometric CsxCr5Te8 phases with 0.73 < x < 1 have been synthesized. Both structure types contain CrTe2 layers of edge‐sharing CrTe6 octahedra, but the number of Cr2Te2 bridges connecting them controls the dimensionality – from 2D in delafossite‐type to 3D in hollandite‐like phases. This increase in dimensionality is accompanied by a decrease in alkali content. A structure with alternating Cr2Te2 bridges and layers of alkali atoms, however, has not yet been reported, and motivated our search for new 2D magnetic materials.

In this work, we apply flux synthesis strategies to these alkali‐chromium‐tellurides, revealing a novel ladder‐like hybrid framework with tunable dimensionality and magnetic ground states — opening a new avenue in single‐anion materials design, and bridging a logical gap in the (Rb,Cs)–Cr–Te phase space. Specifically, we obtained large crystals of the previously unreported layered phases Cs2.4Cr8Te14 and Rb2.4Cr8Te14. These compounds exhibit the desired double‐layered ladder‐like architecture, which can be interpreted as a structural intergrowth phase consisting of the structural elements of both the hollandite‐ and the delafossite‐like structures. In direction‐dependent magnetization experiments on oriented single crystals we find that Rb2.4Cr8Te14 is antiferromagnetic with TN = 114.5 K, while Cs2.4Cr8Te14 orders ferrimagnetically at T C = 125 K.

Experimental

Synthesis of Cs2.4Cr8Te14

As a first step, a Cs x Te y precursor was prepared by carefully adding drops of liquid Cs (Alfa Aesar, 99.98%) to crushed Te pieces (Alfa Aesar, 99.999%) in an agate mortar inside an Ar‐filled glovebox, which led to the rapid formation of unspecified Cs–Te binary compounds, which were ground into a fine powder. Cr (powder, Alfa Aesar, 99.95%) and the Cs x Te y precursor were then placed in a Canfield alumina crucible set comprising a bottom and top crucible and a frit disc in between [17]. The setup was then sealed in a quartz ampule under an atmosphere of 300 mbar Ar. Inside a muffle furnace, the ampule was heated at 30/h to 1000, and was subsequently slow‐cooled to 750 within 96 h. After this, the ampule was removed from the oven, and the excess flux was separated by immediate high‐temperature centrifugation.

Synthesis of Rb2.4Cr8Te14

Single crystals of Rb2.4Cr8Te14 were synthesized using a Rb/Te self flux starting from a molar Rb:Cr:Te ratio of 3.3:1:8. Rb (Strem, 99.9+%), Cr (powder, Alfa Aesar, 99.95%) and Te (pieces, Sigma Aldrich, 99.999%) were placed in a Canfield crucible set inside a glovebox with an Ar atmosphere. The crucible was then sealed in a quartz ampule under an atmosphere of 300 mbar Ar and placed in a muffle furnace. The temperature program followed by the subsequent centrifugation was identical to the synthesis of Cs2.4Cr8Te14.

Synthesis of CsCr5Te8

Single crystals of CsCr5Te8 were synthesized using a Cs/Te self‐flux from molar Cs:Cr:Te ratios of 3.3:1:8 by the same procedure as Cs2.4Cr8Te14. By this, black bar‐shaped single crystals were obtained. Powder X‐ray diffraction (PXRD) measurements confirmed an almost phase‐pure product with small impurities of remaining Cs2Te3 and Cs2Te2 flux (see Supporting Information). By applying a sawtooth‐like cooling profile (see, Figure 1), the crystal size of CsCr5Te8 platelets could be increased moderately.

Adjusted temperature program used for the self‐flux synthesis of larger single crystals of CsCr5Te8 compared to two‐step synthesis heating up to 1000 with 30/h and subsequent cooling to 750 over 96 h. Axes not to scale.

Powder X‐Ray Diffraction (PXRD)

Capillary PXRD data of Rb2.4Cr8Te14 and Cs2.4Cr8Te14 were collected on a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer equipped with a LynxeyeXE detector using Mo‐Kα radiation (λ = 0.710806 Å) in a 2θ scan in the range of 2.5–49. The beam was shaped by a 6 mm focussing Göbel mirror and size selected by a 0.6 mm slit. Capillary PXRD measurements on CsCr5Te8 were performed on a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer with Cu‐Kα radiation (λ = 1.54187 Å) on a D/teX Ultra 250 detector in the range of 5–80. For this, CsCr5Te8 was diluted with amorphous quartz dust. Calculated patterns were simulated using the Mercury software developed by the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre [18]. Full width half maximum (FWHM) values were estimated to match the experimental pattern best. Patterns of Rb2Te3, Cs2Te3, and Cs2Te2 were generated based on crystal structures from the ICSD database [19]: Rb2Te3 (90806), Cs2Te3 (53244), and Cs2Te2 (83351).

Single Crystal X‐Ray Diffraction (SXRD)

SXRD experiments were performed under N2 cooling at 100 K on an Oxford Diffraction Supernova diffractometer using Mo‐Kα radiation (λ = 0.71072 Å). Pre‐experiment screenings, data collection, data reduction, and absorption correction were performed using the program suite CrysAlisPro [20]. The crystal structure was solved with the dual space method in SHELXT [21]. Least squares refinements of F2 were performed using SHELXL [22].

Magnetization Measurements

Magnetization versus temperature and magnetization versus magnetic field measurements were carried out in a physical property measurement system (PPMS DynaCool) from Quantum Design equipped with the vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) option. The measurements were performed in a temperature range of 1.8–300 K in the sweep mode at rates of 5 K·min−1 and 50 Oe·s−1 in the range of −9 T to 9 T.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Energy‐Dispersive X‐Ray Spectroscopy (EDS)

Electron images were obtained from a JEOL JSM‐IT800 scanning electron microscope. Energy dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) data were collected with an X‐MaxN 80 detector from Oxford Instruments with an acceleration voltage of 20 kV. Composition measurements are based on 80 measurement points at eight sites on four crystals from two batches each.

Results and Discussion

Crystal Structures of Rb2.4Cr8Te14 and Cs2.4Cr8Te14

Metallic‐black crystals of Rb2.4Cr8Te14 (starting from a flux with molar Rb:Cr:Te ratios of 3.3:1:8) with sizes up to 4 × 3 × 0.2 mm3 and Cs2.4Cr8Te14 (starting from a flux with molar Cs:Cr:Te ratios of 6:1:8; size up to 3 × 1 × 0.2 mm3) were obtained from self‐flux syntheses followed by hot‐centrifugation (Photographs Figure 2). Powder X‐ray diffraction (PXRD) identified Rb2Te3 or Cs2Te3 and Cs2Te2, as the only detectable side products, resulting from residual flux material (see Supporting Information). When the Cs‐based synthesis is performed with a molar 3.3:1:8 ratio of the starting elements instead, crystals of the hollandite‐like phase CsCr5Te8 are obtained; these were characterized by PXRD and thanks to increased crystal size subjected to anisotropic magnetic measurements for the first time. This demonstrates that in the (Rb,Cs)–Cr–Te system, the flux composition operates as a control parameter on phase formation.

Our SXRD (single crystal X‐ray diffraction) measurements of Rb2.4Cr8Te14 and Cs2.4Cr8Te14 revealed that both phases crystallize in new structure types. While they share the same space group Cm and the same basic structural building blocks, they are nonetheless not isotypic due to a different stacking arrangement of the ACr8Te14 double layers. Figure 3a depicts the building blocks derived from the hollandite‐ and delafossite‐like phases, which combine to form the ladder‐like architecture characteristic of these compounds.

The lattice parameters of A2.4Cr8Te14 (A = Rb, Cs) are very similar: a = 20.2039(5) Å, b = 3.91660(10) Å, c = 19.8178(6) Å, β = 109.152(3), V = 1481.40(7) Å for Cs2.4Cr8Te14 and a = 20.1741(3) Å, b = 3.91560(10) Å, c = 19.6823(4) Å, β = 110.827(2), V = 1453.19(6) Å for Rb2.4Cr8Te14 (see Table 1). The solution and refinement of both crystal structures were complicated by the soft‐mechanical behavior of the crystals, residual flux, and several possible sources of disorder. These challenges are discussed in detail in the Supporting Information. In the crystal structures of A2.4Cr8Te14, all atoms are located at crystallographic sites corresponding to the 2a Wyckoff position with site symmetry m, in total 8 Cr, 14 Te, and 4 A sites. Like in other ternary A–Cr–Te phases, CrTe2 layers, which consist of edge‐sharing CrTe6 octahedra, form the structural backbone.

In delafossite‐like ACrTe2 phases, the CrTe2 layers are intercalated by layers of A cations (Figure 3a). In contrast, in Ax Cr5Te8 phases, these CrTe2 layers are instead interconnected by alternating Cr2Te2 bridges and A cations. Through this, a tri‐periodic Cr–Te framework permeated by channels along [010] is formed. In A2.4Cr8Te14, both of these two possibilities of linking CrTe2 layers are realized alternately. Pairs of CrTe2 layers are interconnected by Cr2Te2 bridges leading to a ladder‐shaped Cr8Te14 double‐layer. Like their counterparts within the CrTe2 layers, the CrTe6 coordination polyhedra of the two bridging Cr atoms are connected to each other by edge‐sharing. However, the contact between CrTe2 layers and Cr2Te2 bridges is facilitated by plane‐sharing instead, thus resulting in much shorter Cr–Cr distances at these points. Instead of 3.8–4.0 Å, the Cr–Cr contacts are as close as 3.078(15) and 3.087(15) Å (Cs‐phase) or 3.061(10) and 3.065(10) Å (Rb‐phase).

The Cr2Te2 bridge further influence the Cr–Cr distances within the CrTe2 layers: contacts along <010> are near 3.92 Å, while those along <130> alternate between shorter (3.8–3.9 Å) and longer (≈ 4.0 Å) separations. This distance disproportionation reflects the influence of the interconnecting Cr2Te2 bridges bound by plane‐sharing, as the Cr sites with the most distorted in‐plane Cr–Cr contacts are those directly involved in the cross‐connections.

The Cr8Te14 ladders are isolated from each other by a layer of A cations. While the A sites located inside the hollandite‐type tunnels are fully occupied, as they are in CsCr5Te8 as well, those forming the separating layer are occupationally disordered with site occupation factors close to 0.5. This leads to the non‐integer sum formula of A2.4Cr8Te14, as a full occupation of all A sites would instead lead to a sum formula of A4Cr8Te14. The A content is very similar in the presented SXRD refinements of Rb2.4Cr8Te14 and Cs2.4Cr8Te14. The A2.4Cr8Te14 stoichiometry of both phases was later confirmed by EDS measurements discussed in detail below. Under‐stoichiometry of the A cations is frequently observed in delafossite‐like phases, including the cathode materials NaxCoO2 and LixCoO2 [23, 24], or the chromium‐based phases K0.6−0.8CrSe2 [25, 26], K0.87CrSe2 [11], and K1−xCrSe2 [27], as well as in hollandite‐like AxCr5Te8 (A = Rb, Cs; 0.5 <x< 1) [15, 16]. In all cases, the removal of A cations leads to a gradual increase of the formal Cr oxidation number from pure CrIII to a mixed CrIII/CrIV valence state. These interlayer A sites are the only part of the crystal structure that breaks C2/m symmetry, which is followed by the Cr8Te14 ladders. The consequences of this on the refinement are discussed in detail in the Supporting Information.

Differences Between Cs2.4Cr8Te14 and Rb2.4Cr8Te14

At first glance, the unit‐cell metrics and crystal structures of Cs2.4Cr8Te14 and Rb2.4Cr8Te14 appear very similar. The a and b lattice parameters, which are mostly defined by the dimensions of the Cr8Te14 ladders, are almost identical. The larger value of c for Cs2.4Cr8Te14 can be attributed to the higher ionic radius of Cs compared to Rb.

However, the most striking difference concerns the orientation of the Cr2Te2 bridges relative to c. While in Cs2.4Cr8Te14, they are located parallel to c, in Rb2.4Cr8Te14, they are tilted in the other direction relative to c*. This indicates that the stacking of the whole Cr8Te14 layers is different for the two phases (see Figure 3b,c). This difference cannot be resolved by a different setting of the unit cell. When progressing along the tilted direction of the Cr2Te2 bridges (dashed arrows in Figure 3b, c), the Cr atoms alternate pairwise between y = 0 and y = 1/2 in Cs2.4Cr8Te14, whereas in Rb2.4Cr8Te14 this alternation follows a repeating sequence of y = 0, 1/2, 1/2, 0 along the bridge direction. This clear difference in the stacking arrangement of the double‐layers underlines the presence of two different structure types in Cs2.4Cr8Te14 and Rb2.4Cr8Te14.

A similar distinction between two closely related structure types is known for the pseudo‐hollandite ACr5Te8 phases. There, the difference between the so‐called A‐ and B‐type structures [15] lies in whether neighboring Cr2Te2 bridges connect to the same CrTe6 octahedron (B) or from neighboring ones (A).

Microstructure and Elemental Composition

EDS analysis was performed on Cs2.4Cr8Te14 and Rb2.4Cr8Te14 crystals to verify the elemental composition, especially the alkali content determined by the SXRD experiments. EDS measurements based on 80 measurement points at eight sites on four crystals from two batches of search found the elemental composition to be Cs2.9(4)Cr8Te14.6(8), which shows that the Cs and Te contents are in very good agreement with our SXRD experiment. The elementary composition of Rb2.4Cr8Te14 based on EDS was found to be Rb2.56(20)Cr8Te13.7(3), which fits the values from SXRD within one standard deviation. In Cs2.4Cr8Te14, both the alkali metal, as well as the Te contents are slightly elevated in comparison with the refined structural model from SXRD, which might be caused by some residual flux stuck to the uncleaved sample surface. This is in line with traces of Cs2Te2 observed in PXRD, which is expected to be solid at the centrifugation temperature [28]. It should be noted that the chemical composition, especially for Rb2.4Cr8Te14, was found not to vary much when comparing different spots on the same crystal, different crystals as well as crystals from different batches. This suggests homogeneity of the composition within the crystals and the batches as well as the reproducibility of the synthesis. Maps of the elemental compositions with two different magnifications further underlining the homogeneity of the crystals are depicted in the Supporting Information. The chemical composition of flux‐grown CsCr5Te8 was determined as Cs1.01(11) Cr5Te8.1(4), which exactly fits the expected stoichiometry and does not indicate any under‐occupation of the Cs sites in the channels. A list of the individual elemental compositions within the batches, crystals, and sites for all three compounds is given in the Supporting Information.

Using scanning electron microscopy, we further studied the microstructure of Cs2.4Cr8Te14 and Rb2.4Cr8Te14, the results of which are depicted in Figure 2b, c. Both compounds form overall plate‐shaped crystals that show large areas of flat surfaces. Large magnifications show steps suggesting stacks of multilayered, stacked crystal slabs, which is a common microstructural feature of 2D‐materials. On top of the surface, smaller on‐grown crystals can be observed, which were identified as remaining flux Ax Tey with no Cr‐content. The layered nature of the crystal structure is further highlighted by stair‐like features resulting from the increasing number of multi‐layers on top of each other.

Magnetic Properties

Given the uncommon isolated ladder‐like architecture of these compounds, their magnetic behavior is of particular interest, and we have studied it using direction‐dependent magnetization on oriented single crystals. This is especially relevant in light of the rich and tunable magnetism known in structurally related intercalated chromium dichalcogenides, where small variations in size and amount of alkali cations lead to diverse magnetic ground states. Analogously, the closely related ladder‐like telluride frameworks of Rb2.4Cr8Te14 and Cs2.4Cr8Te14 provide an excellent platform to explore the interplay between crystal structure and magnetism. As shown in Figure 4, the magnetic moment reveals that the two compounds, despite their structural similarity, order magnetically in different ways: Rb2.4Cr8Te14 orders antiferromagnetically at TN = 114.5 K, whereas Cs2.4Cr8Te14 exhibits ferrimagnetic ordering at TC = 125.0 K.

Temperature‐dependent measurements of the magnetic moment of Cs2.4Cr8Te14 (Figure 4a) show a transition to a ferromagnetic state below TC = 125.0 K. The ferromagnetic transition temperature can be estimated based on the maxima of the first derivative dmdT corresponding to the inflection point in the m(T) curves at TC = 125.0 K.

The field‐dependent magnetization measurements of Cs2.4Cr8Te14 are presented in Figure 4b for T = 1.8 and 150 K, with the external magnetic field parallel and perpendicular to c*, respectively. Only a small hysteresis loop is visible at T = 1.8 K. For both crystal orientations in the external field, H ∥ c* = 9 T and H ⊥ c* = 9 T the magnetic moment at 1.8 K saturates at about m = 1.24 μB/Cr. Since the magnetic moment saturates much faster, when the external field is applied parallel to the layer plane (H ⊥ c*), the magnetic easy‐axis and direction of the magnetic moments are likely to lie within the layer plane as well. The theoretical magnetic moment can be estimated using the spin‐only formula m=g·S·μ B (assuming no spin‐orbit coupling) with S being the total spin and g being the Lande factor, which can be approximated as being 2. Since the formal oxidation number of Cr in A2.4Cr8Te14 amounts to +3.2, we estimate the magnetic moment from a mixture of the adjacent +3 and +4 oxidation states. For Cr4+ and Cr3+, the expected moments are 2 and 3 μ B, respectively, leading to a theoretically expected moment of 2.8 μ B/Cr atom, which is significantly higher than the observed saturation moment. This difference can be rationalized by ferrimagnetic ordering within the Cr8Te14 ladders, instead of a pure ferromagnetic ground state. The two types of CrTe6 octahedra‐linking by edge‐ and face‐sharing and the resulting two groups of nearest‐neighbor Cr–Cr distances provide the structural basis for such a magnetic ordering. According to the Goodenough–Kanamori–Anderson rules [29, 30, 31, 32, 33], the edge‐sharing CrTe6 octahedra with Cr–Te–Cr angles close to 90 favor ferromagnetic superexchange, while the CrIII–CrIII contacts evoke antiferromagnetic direct exchange; the latter contribution becomes increasingly relevant as the Cr–Cr distance decreases. Within the CrTe2 layers, the large Cr–Cr distances of 3.8–4.0 Å make ferromagnetic interactions favorable. In contrast, the short Cr–Cr contacts (< 3.1 Å) that connect the Cr2Te2 slabs to the CrTe2 layers are very likely antiferromagnetically coupled. Assuming two spin orientations along the easy axis, this geometry would couple the two CrTe2 layers ferromagnetically, while the bridging Cr2Te2 units adopt the opposite spin orientation. With six Cr atoms per formula unit in the CrTe2 layers and two in the bridges, the net moment is reduced by a factor of 6−28=12, which is consistent with the reduced saturation magnetization observed in Cs2.4Cr8Te14.

This hypothesis is underpinned by the magnetic saturation moments of CsCr5Te8 observed in the magnetic field‐dependent measurements in Figure 4d. The saturation moment for an applied magnetic field H ⊥ c* (along the easy magnetization axis) corresponds to slightly above 0.5 μ . Due to the same distribution of Cr–Cr distances, we can rationalize the reduced saturation value again with ferromagnetically coupled CrTe2 layers and Cr2Te2 bridges exhibiting the opposite spin orientation. With three Cr atoms per formula unit in the layers and two in the bridges, the net moment is reduced to a factor of 3−25=15. The expected value of 0.56 μ /Cr is in excellent agreement with our observed ∼0.5 μ /Cr. The Curie temperature of our flux‐grown CsCr5Te8 was determined at T C = 116.7 K based on the temperature‐dependent magnetic moment data displayed in Figure 4c, which is slightly lower than previously reported (125 K) [15]. A more detailed magnetic investigation of CsCr5Te8 can be found in the Supporting Information.

Temperature‐dependent measurements of the magnetic moment normalized to the applied field of Rb2.4Cr8Te14 are illustrated in Figure 4e. For an applied field of 1 T, the magnetic moment shows the characteristic peak shape of an antiferromagnet with a maximum reached at the Néel temperature T N = 114.5 K. This maximum is sharp for μ0H = 1 T and H ⊥ c*, but becomes broadened for H ∥ c* and increasing field strengths. The plateauing of m(T) at about 1 μB/Cr at μ0H = 9 T with H ∥ c* indicates a shift to a spin‐polarized ferromagnetic state. Figure 4d shows the magnetic moment for applied fields between 0 T and 9 T at temperatures of 10, 70, and 130 K with fields applied H ⊥ c* and H ∥ c*, respectively. Other than for Cs2.4Cr8Te14, no saturation was observed up to fields of 9 T in either crystal orientation. With increasing temperature, the curvature of m(H) changes from positive to negative, indicating a signature of a metamagnetic transition like a spin‐flop. The change of curvature appears at different temperatures, approximately around 100 K for H ⊥ c*, while already at ca. 60 K for H ∥ c*. Supplementary magnetic analyses of all three phases are collated in the Supporting Information.

Conclusion

We have extended the family of alkali chromium tellurides by discovering two phases with new structure types, A 2.4 Cr8Te14 (A = Rb, Cs). These compounds were obtained as millimeter‐sized single crystals via A/Te self‐flux syntheses, highlighting the potential of flux growth not only for producing high‐quality crystals but also as a materials discovery tool.

Their crystal structures consist of a ladder‐like hybrid framework that integrates motifs from both delafossite‐like A CrTe2 and hollandite‐like A Cr5Te8 phases, representing a flux‐guided intergrowth of 2D and 3D endmembers, and thus highlighting the possibility of a new structure series from these building blocks.

Our large single crystals enabled us to perform anisotropic magnetic measurements of these phases. Despite their close structural relationship, the two compounds exhibit distinctively different magnetic ground states: Rb2.4Cr8Te14 undergoes antiferromagnetic ordering below TN = 114.5 K with field‐induced metamagnetic transition at higher fields, while Cs2.4Cr8Te14 exhibits a ferrimagnetic ground state with TC = 125.0 K and reduced saturation magnetization. These findings underscore the sensitivity of magnetic order to subtle structural and electronic differences within the ladder framework. The competition between ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic interactions at different Cr–Cr distances drives the observed long‐range magnetic orders.

By extending intergrowth concepts to magnetic chromium–chalchogenides through simple flux synthesis control, this study opens new directions for targeted synthesis in magnetic 2D quantum materials research. The layered architecture of A 2.4 Cr8Te14 provides a promising foundation for further structural series of intergrown alkali–chalchogenide ternaries, as well as for manipulation by deintercalation, metathesis, or intercalation chemistry, offering a pathway to tune their dimensionality and electronic states. Such chemically engineered derivatives may unlock access to new families of 2D spintronic or quantum materials.
