Guideline-based clinical reasoning in periodontology education: a comparative study of residents and large language models
Authors: Turan Emre Kuzu, Beyza Nur Esen, Zeynep Kurtaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn healthcare education, clinical practice guidelines play a central role in the development of clinical reasoning skills by providing structured, evidence-based decision-making frameworks. Successful management of peri-implantitis requires not only the acquisition of knowledge but also the ability to interpret and apply guideline recommendations within a clinical context. Large language models (LLMs), as artificial intelligence systems capable of generating clinically meaningful responses, have recently attracted attention; however, their educational performance when compared with learners at different levels of clinical training has not yet been sufficiently clarified. In this study, the performance of different LLMs was compared with that of periodontology assistants within a guideline-based clinical reasoning framework.MethodsBased on the European Federation of Periodontology’s (EFP) clinical practice guidelines for peri-implantitis, a total of 46 assessment items comprising multiple-choice questions (MCQs) and short-answer questions (CRQs) were developed. The questions were structured according to the five clinical stages of peri-implantitis management. Four LLMs (ChatGPT-5.1, Gemini 1.5 Flash, DeepSeek V3.2 and Claude Sonnet 4.5) and periodontology assistants at early, intermediate and advanced training levels were assessed using a standardised application protocol. In quantitative analyses, performance was compared across groups; in qualitative assessments, the clinical consistency, clarity, and clinical appropriateness of the explanatory responses were examined by academic staff.FindingsIn multiple-choice questions, generally similar performance was observed between residents and LLMs, suggesting comparable levels of success in recognising structured, guideline-based recommendations. In contrast, performance differences became more pronounced in open-ended questions requiring explanation, justification and the application of knowledge within a clinical context. Whilst a general difference in performance was observed across resident training levels in open-ended questions, no statistically significant differences were detected between specific resident groups in multiple comparison analyses. LLMs demonstrated stronger performance, particularly in tasks requiring the structured expression of guideline-based reasoning. Qualitative assessments also revealed differences between models in terms of explanation organisation and clinical consistency, particularly in tasks requiring higher levels of interpretation.ConclusionLarge language models can serve as valuable educational tools for organising and interpreting structured clinical information within guideline-based learning environments. However, the outputs of these systems should not be regarded as a direct substitute for real clinical reasoning, contextual decision-making, or experiential clinical judgement. Therefore, their educational integration must be conducted under the supervision of teaching staff and within a framework of critical evaluation. Hybrid educational models combining human-led clinical training with supervised AI-assisted learning can contribute to periodontology education whilst preserving the development of independent clinical reasoning skills.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09616-7.

Introduction

Peri-implantitis is a multifactorial disease characterised by inflammatory changes in peri-implant tissues. The clinical course of the disease can vary significantly due to its multifactorial nature. This variability has increased the need for evidence-based and systematic approaches [1]. The European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) has created a thorough classification system for peri-implant disorders in response to this requirement, providing a clear diagnostic framework and standardised clinical nomenclature [2]. However, clinicians’ ability to appropriately evaluate recommendations and incorporate them into clinical reasoning is just as important to the efficacy of clinical guidelines as the quality of the evidence [3]. Consequently, one of the primary objectives of health professions education is now to develop clinical reasoning grounded in guidelines.

Large Language Models (LLMs), which are artificial intelligence systems capable of generating text resembling that of a human using multi-layer neural network architectures trained on massive datasets, have emerged alongside this shift in education [4]. Thanks to advancements in natural language processing, these models have the potential to serve as valuable tools in educational, therapeutic, and research settings. Although ChatGPT remains one of the most popular models in medical research, there is growing interest in more recent systems such as Claude Sonnet, Gemini, and Copilot [5]. Although these models share a similar transformer-based architecture, they differ in terms of training data, alignment strategies, and task optimisation [6, 7].

In dental education, teaching clinical guidelines now encompasses not only the transfer of information but also the interpretation of recommendations, the analysis of clinical scenarios, and the application of structured decision-making skills within context. Therefore, guideline awareness and interpretation competence are considered key learning outcomes; scenario-based and competency-oriented assessment methods are increasingly used [8]. Whilst previous studies have examined the accuracy of medical knowledge in large language models (LLMs), research evaluating AI systems alongside residents at different stages of clinical training within a guideline-based training context remains limited. This gap is particularly evident in the management of peri-implantitis, where standard treatment protocols strongly guide clinical reasoning. Consequently, evaluating AI performance in parallel with human learning progress constitutes an important yet insufficiently studied area in health professions education research.

This study aimed to assess and compare guideline-based knowledge and clinical reasoning outcomes among periodontology residents at three levels of training and four major large language models (ChatGPT-5.1, Gemini 1.5 Flash, DeepSeek V3.2, and Claude Sonnet 4.5), and to analyse human and artificial intelligence performance within an educational evaluation framework based on the EFP clinical practice guidelines for peri-implantitis.

Materials and methods

Study groups

This study was designed to comparatively evaluate the levels of guideline-based knowledge and clinical decision-making approaches of artificial intelligence-based LLMs and periodontology residents in accordance with the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) clinical practice guidelines for peri-implantitis. Four LLMs were included: ChatGPT-5.1, Gemini 1.5 Flash, DeepSeek V3.2, and Claude Sonnet 4.5. All AI systems were assessed using their publicly available versions during the study period and were evaluated within a clinical education context. A total of 18 periodontology residents who had completed undergraduate dental education were enrolled in the study. Participants were categorised into three training-level groups based on the duration of their specialist training: 0–1 year of training: 6 residents; 1–2 years of training: 6 residents; more than 2 years of training: 6 residents. This grouping was established to represent progressive stages of clinical competence development throughout specialist training.

Development of assessment questions

The EFP clinical guidelines on peri-implant diseases served as the basis for the development of a total of 46 questions. All questions were developed in accordance with the EFP clinical practice guidelines on peri-implant diseases and peri-implantitis. Of these, 37 were multiple-choice questions (MCQs) and 9 were clinical reasoning questions (CRQs). The questions were organised according to the five clinical stages of peri-implantitis management defined in the guidelines: prevention of peri-implant diseases (3 MCQs, 2 CRQs), treatment of peri-implant mucositis (9 MCQs, 0 CRQs), non-surgical treatment of peri-implantitis (12 MCQs, 0 CRQs), surgical treatment of peri-implantitis (10 MCQs, 3 CRQs), and supportive peri-implant therapy following treatment (3 MCQs, 4 CRQs). The aim of the question formulation process was to reflect clinical reasoning and competency development, particularly in relation to treatment planning based on guidelines. The purpose of the multiple-choice questions was to assess participants’ understanding of the EFP guidelines’ definition of the strength of recommendations. Consequently, the German Association of Scientific Medical Societies’ (AWMF) recommendation rating system was used to structure the answer options. The following answer choices directly correspond to the terminology used in the guidelines: Strong recommendation (“We recommend”); strong counter-recommendation (“We do not recommend”); recommendation (“We recommend”); counter-recommendation (“We do not recommend”); and clear recommendation (“Considered”) are denoted by A, B and O, respectively [9]. This format enabled each multiple-choice question to assess the participant’s ability to understand the clinical weight and strength of the recommendation within the guideline-based decision-making process, in addition to accuracy. Experienced periodontology specialists reviewed the questions to ensure their content validity and assessed their clinical relevance and alignment with guidelines. In a single session, all questions were presented to AI systems and assistants in the same sequence and to the same subject. Each question was asked only once to avoid learning or adaptation effects from repeated exposure. This method was used to evaluate clinical decision-making skills in real time in an objective manner. A consistent prompt structure was used to present all questions for the AI evaluation in separate, freshly started sessions, and the models were not provided with any further contextual information.

Assessment of responses

Responses to multiple-choice questions (MCQs) were scored as 1 for a correct answer and 0 for an incorrect answer. The number of correct answers and success rates were calculated for each clinical sub-domain and overall performance. In the evaluation of open-ended questions (CRQs), answers were determined based on the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) clinical practice guidelines. In the evaluation of open-ended questions, a rubric-based, five-point Likert-scale ordinal rating approach was used to ensure that performance could be scored in a more standardised and comparable manner across groups. This approach is consistent with educational assessment principles that categorise complex performance outcomes according to predefined levels of achievement. Accordingly, raw performance scores ranging from 0 to 10 were normalised and converted into five ordinal categories; scores between 0 and 2 were coded as 0.2 points, scores between > 2–4 as 0.4 points, scores between > 4–6 as 0.6 points, scores between > 6–8 as 0.8 points, and scores between > 8–10 as full marks (1.0). This structure enabled continuous performance data to be interpreted in a standardised manner as ordered levels of achievement.To minimise assessor bias, open-ended responses were assessed in a blinded manner, independent of participant identity and level of specialist training. The scoring process was carried out independently by three periodontology faculty members, each with over 10 years of clinical and academic experience. Each response was scored separately by the three assessors, and the final CRQ score was calculated as the average of these three scores.Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The MCQ and CRQ scores obtained were used in comparative analyses between resident training levels and artificial intelligence systems.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The analyses were conducted using participant-level aggregated MCQ and CRQ scores, which reflect each participant’s overall performance, rather than at the individual question level. This approach was chosen to prevent potential pseudo-replication bias that might arise from treating multiple responses from the same participant as independent observations. The assumption of normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and it was determined that the assumption of normal distribution was not met. Therefore, non-parametric statistical methods were used in the analyses. Descriptive statistics were reported as the median and interquartile range (Q1–Q3). The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare the MCQ and CRQ performance of periodontology trainees at different levels of specialist training (early, intermediate and advanced). When significant differences were detected in overall group comparisons, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Dunn–Bonferroni post-hoc test. As performance data for artificial intelligence models were derived from repeated model outputs that did not constitute independent participant observations, the performance of the artificial intelligence systems was evaluated using a descriptive benchmark approach based on overall average scores rather than inferential statistical analysis for these groups, and presented in comparison with human participant groups.

Inter-rater reliability in the scoring of open-ended questions (CRQ) was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). Reliability analysis was conducted using a two-way random effects model based on the absolute agreement definition and mean measures (ICC[2,k]), and reported with 95% confidence intervals. A statistical significance level of p < 0.05 was accepted in all analyses.

Artificial intelligence model use and standardisation

LLMs were included exclusively as experimental entities and had no role in study design, data analysis, interpretation of results, or manuscript preparation. Model responses were generated using standardised prompts delivered within newly initiated independent sessions to minimise potential contextual carryover effects. No additional background information beyond the predefined question content was provided. Each item was presented a single time to prevent iterative refinement or response optimisation. All outputs were recorded verbatim and evaluated using the same assessment criteria applied to human participants. All responsibilities related to data analysis, interpretation, and manuscript preparation remained solely with the research team.

Artificial intelligence model evaluation

ChatGPT-5.1, Gemini 1.5 Flash, DeepSeek V3.2 and Claude Sonnet 4.5 were evaluated under standardised test conditions using their publicly available web-based user interfaces. All models were evaluated using identical prompt structures presented on the same day and in the same format. For multiple-choice questions, the models were instructed: ‘Please evaluate the following multiple-choice clinical question and select the single most appropriate answer. Please state only the answer option you have selected without providing any additional explanation.’ For open-ended questions, the prompt ‘Please evaluate the following open-ended clinical question and provide your answer in written form based on your clinical reasoning.’ was used. No model-specific prompt modifications, iterative prompt refinement or response re-generation were applied. To ensure procedural consistency, only the first response generated by each model was included in the analysis. As the evaluation was conducted via publicly available web interfaces rather than API-based programmable environments, certain background model parameters—such as temperature and response sampling configurations—could not be directly controlled by the researchers.”

Discussion

This study examined the guideline-based knowledge level and clinical reasoning performance of periodontology residents at different levels of seniority using assessment items derived from the European Federation of Periodontology’s (EFP) clinical practice guidelines on peri-implantitis; it also comparatively evaluated the performance of artificial intelligence models using a descriptive benchmark approach. In the analyses of multiple-choice questions, no statistically significant difference in performance was detected between periodontology residents at different levels of specialist training. In open-ended clinical reasoning questions, although a significant difference was observed in the overall group comparison, no statistically significant pairwise differences could be demonstrated between individual resident groups following multiple comparison corrections. Artificial intelligence models, however, demonstrated higher descriptive benchmark performance compared to resident groups, particularly in open-ended assessments.

The absence of a significant performance difference between resident groups in multiple-choice questions should not be interpreted as direct equivalence in clinical competence. Instead, it may reflect a relative similarity in the area of guideline-based knowledge, owing to the highly structured nature of evidence-based treatment approaches in the management of peri-implantitis. Whilst multiple-choice questions primarily assess recall of standard recommendations, the open-ended question format requires the justification of decisions and the application of guideline knowledge within a clinical context [10]. In this regard, whilst a general difference in performance was observed across resident training levels in open-ended questions, no significant differences were detected between specific resident groups after adjusting for multiple comparisons; therefore, it is inappropriate to make a definitive statement that one training stage is superior to another. These findings suggest that the duration of training may not be a linear determinant of clinical reasoning performance on its own, and that individual knowledge of guidelines, cognitive adaptation to the assessment format, and differences in clinical reasoning strategies may also play a role. Similarly, the higher discriminative performance demonstrated by artificial intelligence models, particularly in open-ended assessments, should not be accepted as a direct indicator of actual clinical reasoning or experiential clinical judgement; rather, it should be interpreted as reflecting the organisation of structured guidance and performance in response generation.

From an educational perspective, this suggests that performance in standardised assessments may not progress in direct parallel with clinical experience, and that different cognitive strategies may come into play at different stages of training. In this context, the Dual-Process Theory, which defines decision-making as an interaction between analytical System 2 reasoning and more intuitive System 1 processes, can be considered one possible interpretation of this variation [11]. As CRQs require contextual interpretation and justification, they are more likely to involve System 2 processing. According to this paradigm, the relatively good performance of LLMs can be attributed to their capacity to generate structured linguistic responses resulting from comprehensive pattern recognition. According to previous research, LLM outputs are largely dependent on probabilistic correlations resembling System-1-like mechanisms [12, 13]. However, logically consistent responses do not necessarily indicate a genuine understanding of clinical concepts; rather, they highlight the distinction between fluent language production and genuine conceptual understanding, even though superior performance in System-2-focused tasks might give the impression of analytical reasoning [13].

In a recent study from an educational assessment perspective, large language models were compared with human assessors in the rubric-based scoring of clinical short-answer questions, and it was demonstrated that model performance varied significantly.3 In particular, it was highlighted that although some models demonstrated high ICC values, they exhibited a tendency to over-score incorrect answers and may not be reliable in high-stakes educational assessments without human oversight. 2 These findings also support the methodological approach of our current study; as it appears more appropriate to interpret LLM outputs not as an absolute performance indicator, but comparatively and contextually alongside human expertise [14].

Although interest in the use of artificial intelligence in healthcare education has grown significantly, most of the material currently being published evaluates the performance of learning-based models primarily through accuracy-based tests or short-term knowledge recall measures [15, 16]. These methods provide very little insight into context-dependent decision-making and clinical reasoning [17, 18]. Furthermore, due to the inadequate integration of pedagogical and cognitive theoretical perspectives, study results are frequently reduced to simple comparisons of accuracy measures. Studies using only multiple-choice tests provide limited educational interpretation [19, 20]. This is because clinical decision-making in dental education is complex. Furthermore, according to recent systematic reviews, generative artificial intelligence technologies are still in their infancy and exhibit significant methodological differences [21]. The scenario-based assessment approach used in this study has made it possible to distinguish between recognition-based performance and reasoning-based performance. Open-ended questions required justification and interpretative reasoning, whereas multiple-choice questions primarily assessed the recognition of standardised statements. Through the assessment of accuracy, clinical appropriateness and explanatory consistency, the instructor-led quantitative assessment used in this study has enhanced pedagogical validity. Competency-based assessment models in health professions education are compatible with this strategy [22, 23]. The findings of this study suggest that large language models can be considered as potential auxiliary tools within structured educational settings, rather than as systems capable of replacing clinical education. However, this interpretation is limited to observational comparative findings, and specific educational intervention studies are required to assess the impact of LLMs on actual educational effectiveness.

Current literature on the clinical use of AI-supported clinical decision-making systems reveals that large language models have shown remarkable development in the field of dentistry, yet their performance exhibits significant variability depending on the task type, evaluation methodology, and clinical context. A recent systematic review examining multimodal large language models in the diagnosis of oral lesions reported that advanced LLMs can approach expert performance in some scenarios, particularly demonstrating potential in structuring differential diagnosis processes and supporting clinical communication. However, the same study emphasises that expert clinical assessment remains indispensable in areas requiring high accuracy, such as the interpretation of morphological details, severity grading, and independent clinical decision-making [24]. These findings support the interpretation that the performance of LLMs in our current study should be viewed not as an indicator of independent clinical superiority, but as a benchmark for structured clinical reasoning. Similarly, in a multicentre diagnostic accuracy study comparing ChatGPT-5 with experts in periapical radiographs, it was reported that although the model demonstrated high sensitivity, it exhibited very low specificity and was not deemed reliable for independent clinical use due to a tendency towards false positives [25]. This situation demonstrates that, although LLMs may perform strongly at the level of information recall or pattern recognition, they may have limitations regarding the contextual accuracy of clinical decisions. In our study too, the fact that artificial intelligence models perform well in specific question formats does not imply that these systems demonstrate absolute clinical competence; rather, it may reflect their ability to process structured information in standardised, guideline-based scenarios.

It is generally known that LLMs can generate responses that are grammatically competent but contextually inappropriate [26, 27]. When students accept outputs generated by artificial intelligence without critically analysing them, a phenomenon known as LLM-induced hallucination may give rise to pedagogical concerns. Over-reliance on such outputs may hinder the development of independent clinical judgement [26, 28]. The fluency of the generated text may create a false sense of assurance that outweighs the reliability of the underlying logic. Therefore, it seems more appropriate to present LLMs as auxiliary tools supervised by educators.

Differences in response generation techniques and model architecture optimisation may be partly responsible for the performance hierarchy observed in this study (Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek and Gemini). According to previous research, advanced LLMs generally have similar baseline accuracy rates, but differences become more pronounced in tasks requiring better descriptive quality [29–31]. The stronger performance observed in Claude models may be related to their tendency to generate more systematically structured explanations [32–34]. Claude models may perform better due to architectural features that encourage the generation of more methodically organised explanations. The variability seen in Gemini may be related to differences between model versions [35, 36]. The similar performance between ChatGPT and DeepSeek may suggest convergent optimisation techniques. Previous clinical studies have also found that Claude models offer better diagnostic quality [37, 38]. However, as LLM performance is highly sensitive to task design [39–41], the observed ranking should be interpreted as context-dependent rather than as evidence of universal model superiority.

Overall, the findings of this exploratory comparative study suggest that LLMs may have a potential role as supporting tools in supervised hybrid learning environments within the context of periodontal education. However, these findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence of educational effectiveness, pedagogical superiority, or an optimal educational model. Whilst it is considered that LLMs may have a potential supportive role in early learning processes, the development of advanced clinical reasoning and reflective judgement continues to rely on experiential learning and instructor guidance [42, 43].

Certain limitations of this study should be noted. Firstly, the study was designed as a single-centre, comparative and exploratory educational research study, and the sample consisted of accessible periodontology residents who met the eligibility criteria during the study period. The fact that no formal a priori sample size or power analysis was conducted should be borne in mind regarding the generalisability of the findings to broader educational populations. Nevertheless, the study provides important preliminary data on the comparison of resident performance with artificial intelligence models within a real academic training environment. Secondly, although a standardised scoring rubric, a blind assessment approach, and a high level of inter-rater agreement were employed in the evaluation of open-ended questions, the assessment of open-ended responses inherently involves a certain degree of interpretative elements. Thirdly, the assessment was structured based on the S3 clinical practice guidelines of the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP), which are internationally recognised. Whilst this approach provides a robust framework for methodological standardisation, as the study was conducted in a controlled and guideline-based assessment setting, it may not fully reflect all patient-based and contextual variability encountered in real-world clinical practice. Furthermore, due to the limited number of assessment items in some clinical treatment stages, stage-based statistical comparisons were not performed. Future studies with a more balanced distribution of questions could examine performance patterns across different clinical stages in greater detail. Finally, findings regarding artificial intelligence models are based on outputs obtained from specific model versions and publicly available user interfaces available during the analysis period. Given the rapid development in large language model technologies, future performance levels may differ.
