Generalized Multi-Linear Models for Sufficient Dimension Reduction on Tensor Valued Predictors
Daniel Kapla, Efstathia Bura, Institute of Statistics, Mathematical Methods in Economics

Abstract
We consider supervised learning (regression/classification) problems with tensor-valued input. We derive multi-linear sufficient reductions for the regression or classification problem by modeling the conditional distribution of the predictors given the response as a member of the quadratic exponential family. We develop estimation procedures of sufficient reductions for both continuous and binary tensor-valued predictors. We prove the consistency and asymptotic normality of the estimated sufficient reduction using manifold theory. For continuous predictors, the estimation algorithm is highly computationally efficient and is also applicable to situations where the dimension of the reduction exceeds the sample size. We demonstrate the superior performance of our approach in simulations and real-world data examples for both continuous and binary tensor-valued predictors.

5.1
Multi-Linear Normal

The
multi-linear normal
is the extension of the matrix normal to tensor-valued random variables and a member of the quadratic exponential family (
4
) under (
8
).
[
15
]
and
[
3
]
introduced the term matrix normal and, in particular,
[
3
]
provided several theoretical results, such as its density, moments and conditional distributions of its components. The matrix normal distribution is a bilinear normal distribution; a distribution of a two-way (two-component) array, each component representing a vector of observations
[
54
]
.
[
38
,
28
,
54
]
presented the extension of the bilinear to the multi-linear normal distribution, using a parallel extension of bilinear matrices to multi-linear tensors
[
10
]
.

The defining feature of the matrix normal distribution, and its multi-linear extension, is the Kronecker product structure of its covariance. This formulation, where the vectorized variables are normal with multiway covariance structure modeled as a Kronecker product of matrices of much lower dimension, aims to overcome the significant modeling and computational challenges arising from the high computational complexity of manipulating tensor representations
[
26
,
61
]
.

Suppose the conditional distribution of tensor
\mathcal{X}
given
Y
is multi-linear normal with mean
\mathcal{\mu}_{y}
and covariance
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathchoice{\bigotimes_{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle k=r$}}^{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
. We assume the distribution is non-degenerate which means that the covariances
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
are symmetric positive definite matrices. Its density is given by

f_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathcal{X}\mid Y=y)=(2\pi)^{-p/2}\prod_{k=1}^{r}\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{k})^{-p/2p_{k}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{\mu}_{y},(\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{\mu}_{y})\mathchoice{\operatorname*{\lx@scalerel@obj{\times}}_{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle k=1$}}^{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle r$}}}{\operatorname*{\lx@scalerel@obj{\times}}_{k=1}^{r}}{\operatorname*{\lx@scalerel@obj{\times}}_{k=1}^{r}}{\operatorname*{\lx@scalerel@obj{\times}}_{k=1}^{r}}\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\right\rangle\right).

For the sake of simplicity and w.l.o.g., we assume
\mathcal{X}
has 0 marginal expectation; i.e.,
\E\mathcal{X}=0
. Rewriting this in the quadratic exponential family form (
4

. The relation to the GMLM parameters

and

is

(14)

where we used that
\overline{\mathcal{\eta}}=0
due to

in combination with

. Additionally, the positive definiteness of the

\displaystyle=\E[\mathcal{X}\circ\mathcal{X}\mid Y=y]\equiv\mathchoice{\bigotimes_{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle k=r$}}^{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}\bm{\Sigma}_{k}+(\vec{\mathcal{\mu}}_{y}){(\vec{\mathcal{\mu}}_{y})^{T}}.

In practice, we assume we have a random sample of

,

\displaystyle=(\bm{v}_{1}(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{1}(\mathcal{X})),\ldots,\bm{v}_{q_{k}}(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q_{k}}(\mathcal{X}))),\,\bm{V}_{k}=(\bm{v}_{1}(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{Y}),\ldots,\bm{v}_{q_{k}}(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q_{k}}(\mathcal{F}_{Y}))

\displaystyle=\diag(\bm{v}_{1}(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{1}(\mathcal{X}))\bm{v}_{1}(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{Y})),\ldots,\bm{v}_{q_{k}}(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q_{k}}(\mathcal{X}))\bm{v}_{q_{k}}(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k}(\mathcal{F}_{Y}))).

Equating the partial gradient of the

on

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{i}=\mathcal{X}_{i}-\widehat{\mathcal{\mu}}_{y_{i}}=\mathcal{X}_{i}-\mathcal{F}_{y_{i}}\mathchoice{\operatorname*{\lx@scalerel@obj{\times}}_{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle k=1$}}^{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle r$}}}{\operatorname*{\lx@scalerel@obj{\times}}_{k=1}^{r}}{\operatorname*{\lx@scalerel@obj{\times}}_{k=1}^{r}}{\operatorname*{\lx@scalerel@obj{\times}}_{k=1}^{r}}\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{k}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}.

The estimates are computed via
\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{i})_{(j)}{(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{i})_{(j)}^{T}},
where
\tilde{s}\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{j}=\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{j}^{-1}
. To decide on the scaling factor
\tilde{s}
we use that the mean squared error has to be equal to the trace of the covariance estimate,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{i},\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\rangle=\tr\mathchoice{\bigotimes_{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle k=r$}}^{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{k}^{-1}=\prod_{k=1}^{r}\tr{\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{k}^{-1}}=\tilde{s}^{r}\prod_{k=1}^{r}\tr{\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k}},

so that

\tilde{s}=\biggl(\Bigl(\prod_{k=1}^{r}\tr{\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k}}\Bigr)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{i},\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\rangle\biggr)^{1/r}

resulting in the estimates
\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{j}=(\tilde{s}\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{j})^{-1}

A technical detail for numerical stability is to ensure that the scaled values
\tilde{s}\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{j}
, assumed to be symmetric and positive definite, are well conditioned. Thus, we estimate the condition number of
\tilde{s}\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{j}
before computing the inverse. In case of ill-conditioning, we use the regularized
\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{j}=(\tilde{s}\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{j}+0.2\lambda_{1}(\tilde{s}\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{j})\bm{I}_{p_{j}})^{-1}

\lambda_{1}(\tilde{s}\tilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_{j})
is the first (maximum) eigenvalue. Experiments showed that this regularization is usually only required in the first few iterations.

5.2.3
Small Data Sets

In the case of a finite number of observations, specifically in data sets with a small number of observations

The first situation where this needs to be addressed is in (

The same problem is present in gradient optimization. Therefore, before starting the optimization, we detect degenerate combinations. We compute upper and lower bounds for the “degenerate” element in the Kronecker product
\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}=\mathchoice{\bigotimes_{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle k=r$}}^{\makebox[0.0pt][c]{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}{\bigotimes_{k=r}^{1}}\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{k}

\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}_{k}
, corresponding to the “degenerate” element of their Kronecker product, to fall inside the precomputed bounds. While doing so, we try to alter every component as little as possible to ensure that the non-degenerate elements in
\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}

5.2.4
Slightly Bigger Dimensions

A big challenge for the Ising model is its high computational complexity as it involves summing over all binary vectors of length

in the partition function (
16

When

This, though, is inaccurate and may only be used to get a rough idea of the log-likelihood. Regardless, for our method, we only need the gradient for optimization where appropriate break conditions, not based on the likelihood, lead to a working method for MLE estimation.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{\bm{\theta}}(Z_{i}).

Discussion

In this paper, we propose a generalized multi-linear model formulation for the inverse conditional distribution of a tensor-valued predictor given a response and derive a multi-linear sufficient reduction for the corresponding forward regression/classification problem. We also propose estimators for the sufficient reduction and show they are consistent and asymptotically normal. Obtaining the asymptotic results required leveraging manifolds as a basis for resolving the issue of unidentifiable parameters. This in turn led to an even more flexible modeling framework, which allows building complex and potentially problem-specific parameter spaces that incorporate additional domain-specific knowledge into the model.

We allude to this feature of our approach in
Section

6.2
, where we also tabulate different matrix manifolds that can be used as building blocks
\mathfrak{B}_{k}
and
\mathfrak{O}_{k}
of the parameter space in
Table

1
. For example, our formulation can easily accommodate longitudinal data tabulated in matrix format, where the rows are covariates and the columns are consecutive time points with discrete AR(
k
) dependence structure.

Our multi-linear Ising model can be thought of as the extension of the Ising model-based approach of
[
9
]
, where a
q
-dimensional binary vector is regressed on a
p
-dimensional continuous vector. Yet, our model leverages the inherent structural information of the tensor-valued covariates by assuming separable first and second moments. By doing so, it bypasses requiring sparsity assumptions or penalization, despite the tensor high-dimensional nature of the data. Moreover, it can accommodate a mixture of continuous and binary tensor-valued predictors, which is the subject of future work.

A special case of the Ising model is the one-parameter Ising model without an external field. Given our theory, it is possible to represent this special case as well by constructing a one-dimensional parameter manifold. More precisely, if
\bm{A}
is a fixed symmetric
p\times p
matrix with zero main diagonal elements, the parameter space for the one-dimensional Ising model has the form
\Theta=\{\bm{0}\}\times\{\theta\bm{A}+0.5\bm{I}_{p}:\theta\in\mathbb{R}\}
. As a result (a) the model is a vector-valued multi-variate binary model for predictors
\bm{X}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
, and (b) the predictors
\bm{X}
are independent of the response
y
. In the unsupervised setting, the usual question is to determine the single parameter
\theta
. This is the model considered by
[
62
]
, whose finding that the asymptotic distribution of the MLE depends on the true value of the parameter and is not necessarily normal, at first glance, may seem contradictory to our asymptotic result that the MLE
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
is asymptotically normal.
[
62
]
answer a different question in that they determine the limiting experiment distribution
[
60
, Ch 9]
, where they let the dimension
p
of the binary vector-valued predictors go to infinity (
p\to\infty
). We, on the other hand, let the sample size; i.e., the number of observations go to infinity (
n\to\infty
).

An additional powerful extension of our model involves considering a sum of separable Kronecker predictors. This is motivated by the equivalence of a Kronecker product to a rank
1
tensor. By allowing a sum of a few separable Kronecker predictors, we remove the implicit rank
1
constraint. However, if this extension is to be applied to the SDR setting, as in this paper, it is crucial to ensure that the sum of Kronecker products form a parameter manifold.

which concludes the proof.
