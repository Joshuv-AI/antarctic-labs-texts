Abstract
. This paper investigates atomic factorizations in the monoid ℐ ⁡ ( R ) {\mathcal{I}}(R) of nonzero ideals of a multivariate polynomial ring R R , under ideal multiplication. Building on recent advances in factorization theory for unit-cancellative monoids, we extend techniques from [ 21 ] to construct new families of atoms in ℐ ⁡ ( R ) {\mathcal{I}}(R) , leading to a deeper understanding of its arithmetic. We further analyze the submonoid ℳ ​ on ​ ( R ) {\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R) of monomial ideals, deriving arithmetic properties and computing sets of lengths for specific classes of ideals. The results advance the extensive study of ideal monoids within a classical algebraic framework.

1.
Introduction

By the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, every positive integer admits a unique factorization into prime numbers, up to units. This result, first formulated in Euclid’s
Elements
, was later found not to extend to more general settings. In particular, when considering the ring of integers of a number field (a number ring, for short), factorizations into prime elements may fail to be unique. Initially regarded as a pathological exception, starting with Narkiewicz’s work in the 1960s, this phenomenon became a subject of systematic study. The resulting field, now known as
factorization theory
, investigates the structure of non-unique factorizations. Its main object of study is the following notion, suitably generalizing that of factorization into primes in (multiplicative monoids of) number rings: an
(atomic) factorization
of an element of a commutative cancellative monoid is, up to units, a finite, unordered sequence of
atoms
(i.e., non-unit elements which are not the product of two non-units) whose product is equal to the element. We refer the reader to the monograph
[
20
]
for a comprehensive overview of the subject.

In recent years, while the classical theory of factorization has continued to thrive (see, for instance,
[
9
,
11
,
10
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
19
]
),
several attempts have been made to expand its scope, considering non-commutative (see
[
4
,
7
,
35
,
6
]
) and non-cancellative monoids (see
[
3
,
12
,
36
]
), even multiplicative monoids of rings with zero divisors, as in
[
5
]
. In the latter direction, the definition of
unit-cancellativity
was introduced in
[
15
]
as a weakening of the usual cancellativity hypothesis, and a substantial portion of the classical theory has since been developed in this more general setting; we refer
[
24
]
for a gentle introduction to the topic.

We aim to study atomic factorizations in the (commutative, unit-cancellative) monoid of nonzero ideals of the polynomial ring
K[X_{1},\dots,X_{N}]
, where
K
is an arbitrary field, under ideal multiplication. We assume throughout the paper that
N\geq 2
, as
K[X]
is a PID and the factorizations of its ideals correspond to the factorizations of their generating polynomials.

Our work contributes to the extensive literature on monoids of ideals: we refer to
[
29
]
and the monograph
[
17
]
for an overview, and to
[
33
,
1
,
30
,
31
]
for more recent develpoments. A common approach in the literature is to focus on specific classes of ideals within a given ring, or semigroup ideals within a given monoid. Among these, divisorial ideals are perhaps the most prominent example. By suitably choosing a ring (or monoid) and a class of its ideals, one can obtain a fairly well-behaved monoid of ideals, and in some cases one is able to completely determine its structure. As an example, we recall two well-known results: a domain
R
is Dedekind if and only if all its nonzero ideals are invertible, and this happens only if the monoid of all nonzero ideals is factorial, and a domain
R
is Krull if and only if the monoid of its invertible divisorial ideals is factorial.

Compared to the “classical” examples of monoids of ideals, the structure of atomic factorizations in the monoid
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
consisting of all nonzero ideals of a ring
R
, remains poorly understood in general. This is true even in seemingly simple cases, such as when
R
is a polynomial ring over a field, where surprisingly little is currently known.

In the paper
[
21
]
, a first step was made in the study of the arithmetic properties of
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
when
R
a multivariate polynomial ring. In particular, the results collected in
[
21
, Theorem 5.1]
reveal a noticeable similarity between the arithmetic of
\mathcal{I}(R)
and that of Krull monoids with infinite class group and prime divisors in each class, despite
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
not even being transfer Krull. More specifically,
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
is fully elastic and each of its unions of sets of lengths is the whole set
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
.

Building on these initial results, we refine the demonstrative techniques and adapt them to broader contexts. Following a review of the necessary preliminaries in Section
2
, we extend the methods introduced in
[
21
]
to find several new families of atoms in
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
. In particular, we highlight in in Section
3.1
an interesting class built from sum-free subsets of
\mathbb{N}
, which includes some previously known examples.

Motivated by the problem of computing the length sets of certain ideals (see Theorem
3.7
), in Section
4
we focus on the submonoid
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}(R)
of
monomial ideals
of
R
(i.e., ideals generated by monomials).
We first provide some arithmetic results in the spirit of
[
21
, Theorem 5.1]
, and then we determine several classes of atoms in this monoid, allowing us to compute the set of lengths of the families of ideals introduced in Theorem
3.7
. The main technical ingredient which makes the study of factorizations in
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
easier than in
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
is Lemma
4.1
(see also Remark
4.4
) greatly constricting the possible generators of the monomial ideals occurring in a given factorization.

We conclude the paper by outlining, in Section
5
, possible directions for future research into atomic factorizations in the monoids
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
and
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
, with the present work serving as a foundation.

2.
Notation and preliminary results

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{N}^{+}
denote the sets of nonnegative and positive integers, respectively. For
a,b\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
we let
\llbracket a,b\rrbracket:=\allowbreak\{x\in\mathbb{N}\colon a\leq x\leq b\}
be the discrete interval from
a
to
b
. For sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
A+B:=\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B\}
their sumset, by
\Delta(A)\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{+}
the set of distances of
A
, i.e., the set of all
d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
such that
A\cap[a,a+d]=\{a,a+d\}
for some
a\in A
, and by
\rho(A):={\sup A}/{\min A}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}\cup\{\infty\}
the elasticity of

(by convention, we set

2.1.
Factorization theory

In order to make the paper as self-contained as possible, we recall the basic notions of factorization theory. For further details, examples, and references, the reader can consult the already mentioned monograph
[
20
]
.

Let
H
be a commutative semigroup with an identity element, written multiplicatively. We denote by
H^{\times}
the subgroup of invertible elements of
H
, also called
units
of
H
. If
H^{\times}=\{1\}
, we say that
H
is
reduced
. An element
h\in H
is
cancellative
if
h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime}\in H
and
hh^{\prime}=hh^{\prime\prime}
implies
h^{\prime}=h^{\prime\prime}
, while it is
unit-cancellative
if
u\in H
and
h=hu
implies
u\in H^{\times}
. Moreover, we say that
H
is
cancellative
(respectively,
unit-cancellative
) if all its elements are cancellative (respectively, unit-cancellative).

In this paper, a
monoid
is always a commutative unit-cancellative semigroup with an identity element. Note that a subsemigroup of a monoid inherits its unit-cancellativity; the subsemigroups of monoids we consider will always contain the identity element, and we will therefore speak of submonoids.
A monoid
H
is cancellative if and only if it
has a quotient group, which we denote by
\mathbf{q}(H)
.

Let
H
be a cancellative monoid. An
ideal
of
H
is a subset
J\subseteq H
such that, for any
j\in J
,
jH\subseteq J
; they are also commonly referred to as
s
-ideals
(
semigroup
ideals), especially when
H
also carries a ring structure, to distinguish them from usual ring ideals. For ideals
J,K
of
H
, denote by
(J:K)
the
ideal quotient

\{g\in\mathbf{q}(H):gK\subseteq J\}
. An ideal
J
of
H
is
divisorial
if
J=(H:(H:J))
. We say that
H
is a
Mori
monoid if it satisfies the ascending chain condition on divisorial ideals. Furthermore, we denote by
\hat{H}:=\{g\in\mathbf{q}(H):hg^{n}\in H\text{\ for all\ }n\in\mathbb{N}\text{\ for some\ }h\in H\}
the
complete integral closure
of
H
, and we say that
H
is
completely integrally closed
if
H=\hat{H}
. Finally, we say that
H
is a
Krull
monoid if it is Mori and completely integrally closed.

Let
H
be a monoid, not necessarily cancellative. A submonoid
S\subseteq H
is
divisor-closed
if, whenever
h\in S,h^{\prime}\in H
and
h^{\prime}\mid h
, we have
h^{\prime}\in S
. For a subset
A\subseteq H
, we denote by
\llbracket A\rrbracket
the smallest divisor-closed submonoid generated by
A
. A monoid homomorphism
\varphi:H\to H^{\prime}
, where
H^{\prime}
is cancellative, is a
divisor homomorphism
if
h,h^{\prime}\in H
and
\varphi(h)\mid\varphi(h^{\prime})
in
H^{\prime}
implies that
h\mid h^{\prime}
in
H
.

An element
h\in H
is
prime
if
h\notin H^{\times}
and
h\mid h^{\prime}h^{\prime\prime}
with
h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime}\in H
implies
h\mid h^{\prime}
or
h\mid h^{\prime\prime}
. An element
h\in H
is an
atom
if
h\notin H^{\times}
and
h=h^{\prime}h^{\prime\prime}
with
h^{\prime},h^{\prime\prime}\in H
implies
h^{\prime}\in H^{\times}
or
h^{\prime\prime}\in H^{\times}
; the set of all atoms of
H
is denoted by
\mathscr{A}(H)
. A
factorization
of an element
a\in H
is, up to units, a finite unordered sequence of atoms of
H
, say
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
, such that
a=u_{1}\dots u_{n}
. The
length
of a factorization
z
is the number of atoms occurring in it, and it is denoted by
\lvert z\rvert
. We denote by

•

\mathsf{Z}_{H}(a)=\mathsf{Z}(a)
the
set of factorizations
of
a\in H
;

•

\mathsf{L}_{H}(a)=\mathsf{L}(a):=\{\lvert z\rvert:z\in\mathsf{Z}(a)\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
the
set of lengths
of
a
;

•

\mathcal{L}(H):=\{\mathsf{L}(a):a\in H\}
the
system of sets of lengths
of
H
;

•

\rho(H):=\sup\{\rho(L)\colon L\in\mathcal{L}(H)\}
the
elasticity
of
H
.

Note that, if
S\subseteq H
is a divisor-closed submonoid and
a\in S
, then
\mathsf{Z}_{S}(a)=\mathsf{Z}_{H}(a)
and
\mathsf{L}_{S}(a)=\mathsf{L}_{H}(a)
. We say that
H
is

•

atomic
if
\mathsf{L}(a)
is nonempty for every
a\in H
;

•

a
\mathsf{BF}
-monoid
if
\mathsf{L}(a)
is finite and nonempty for every
a\in H
;

•

an
\mathsf{FF}
-monoid
if
\mathsf{Z}(a)
is finite and nonempty for every
a\in H
;

•

half-factorial
if
\lvert\mathsf{L}(a)\rvert=1
for every
a\in H
;

•

factorial
if
\lvert\mathsf{Z}(a)\rvert=1
for every
a\in H
;

•

locally finitely generated
if
{\llbracket a\rrbracket}
is finitely generated up to units, for every
a\in H
;

•

fully elastic
if, for any
q\in\mathbb{Q}
with
1<q<\rho(H)
, there is an
L\in\mathcal{L}(H)
such that
\rho(L)=q
.

Every Mori monoid is a
\mathsf{BF}
-monoid (
[
20
, Theorem 2.2.5.1]
), Krull monoids are locally finitely generated and locally finitely generated monoids are in turn
\mathsf{FF}
-monoids (
[
20
, Prop. 2.7.8]
). We note that sets of elasticities of locally finitely generated monoids have been studied in great detail in
[
37
]
.

A monoid homomorphism
\theta:H\to K
is called a
transfer homomorphism
if
K=\theta(H)K^{\times}
,
\theta^{-1}(K^{\times})=H^{\times}
, and, whenever
a\in H
,
\beta,\gamma\in K
are such that
\theta(a)=\beta\gamma
, there exist
b,c\in H
such that
a=bc
,
\theta(b)\in\beta K^{\times}
and
\theta(c)\in\gamma K^{\times}
. In Section
4
we will use the following elementary result.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\theta:H\to K
is a transfer homomorphism, then
\mathsf{L}_{H}(a)=\mathsf{L}_{K}(\theta(a))
for any
a\in H
. In particular, an element
a\in H
is an atom of
H
if and only if
\theta(a)
is an atom of
K
.

A monoid
H
is
transfer Krull
if there exist a Krull monoid
K
and a transfer homomorphism
\theta:H\to K
. Clearly, any Krull monoid is transfer Krull, since the identity is a transfer homormophism. Conversely, by Lemma
2.1
all transfer Krull monoids are
\mathsf{BF}
-monoids, but they need neither be Mori nor completely integrally closed, as noted in
[
24
, Example 5.4]
. All transfer Krull monoids are fully elastic, by
[
23
, Theorem 3.1]
. Some notable examples of monoids which are
not
transfer Krull are the integer-valued polynomials, by
[
18
, Remark 12]
, and
\mathcal{P}_{\rm fin}(\mathbb{N})
, by
[
16
, Prop. 4.12]
.

2.2.
Multivariate polynomial rings and their ideals

Let
D
be an integral domain with quotient field
K
, and let
R=D[X_{1},\dots,X_{N}]
and
S=K[X_{1},\dots,X_{N}]
be the polynomial rings in
N\geq 2
indeterminates
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
over
D
and
K
respectively.
For
m=(m_{1},\dots,m_{N})\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
, let
\mathbf{X}^{m}:=X_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots X_{N}^{m_{N}}
and set, for every
t\geq 0
,

R_{t}:=\{\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{N}}r_{m}\mathbf{X}^{m}\colon r_{m}\in D\text{ and }m_{1}+\cdots+m_{N}=t\},

S_{t}:=\{\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{N}}q_{m}\mathbf{X}^{m}\colon q_{m}\in K\text{ and }m_{1}+\cdots+m_{N}=t\}.

Then

R=\bigoplus_{t\geq 0}R_{t}\text{ and }S=\bigoplus_{t\geq 0}S_{t}

are graded rings, equipped with the
standard grading
. It is clear that
R_{t}\subseteq S_{t}
for every
t
, that
R_{0}=D
and
S_{0}=K
, and that
\deg X_{1}=\dots=\deg X_{N}=1
. It follows that every
f\in S
can be uniquely written as
f=\sum_{t\geq 0}f_{t}
, with
f_{t}\in S_{t}
for every
t
.

We can then define the
min-degree
of a nonzero polynomial
f\in S
as the smallest nonnegative integer
d
such that
f_{d}
is nonzero, and we denote it as
\mdeg(f)
. We set
\mdeg(0):=+\infty
. For all
f,g\in S\setminus\{0\}
, the following properties hold:

(a)

\mdeg(fg)=\mdeg(f)+\mdeg(g)
;

(b)

\mdeg(f+g)\geq\min\{\mdeg(f),\mdeg(g)\}
, with equality if
\mdeg(f)\neq\mdeg(g)
.

Let
I\subseteq R
be an ideal of
R
. We define the
min-degree

\mdeg(I)
of
I
as the smallest nonnegative integer
d
such that
I
contains a polynomial whose min-degree is
d
. The min-degree of the zero ideal is set equal to
+\infty
.

Remark 2.2
.

It is easy to see from the properties of the min-degree, that if
I
is a nonzero finitely generated ideal of
R
, i.e.,
I=\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\rangle
with
n\geq 1
and
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in R
, then

\mdeg(I)=\min\{\mdeg(g_{i})\colon i\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\}.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
be the semigroup of nonzero (ring) ideals of
R
, with the usual ideal multiplication. This semigroup is well known (see, e.g.,
[
21
, Prop. 2.1 (4)]
) to be reduced and unit-cancellative, and thus a monoid in the sense specified in Section
2.1
, being obviously commutative.

Remark 2.3
.

It is of interest to understand how far is
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
from being cancellative. We recall here a remarkable characterization of the cancellative elements of
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
due to Anderson and Roitman, valid for
any
commutative ring
R
, not necessarily even a domain: an ideal
I
of
R
is cancellative if and only if it is a regular principal ideal at each localization at a maximal ideal
[
2
]
. If we consider
R=D[X_{1},\dots,X_{N}]
, with
D
a noetherian domain and
N\geq 2
, it is easy to check that the cancellative elements of
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
are precisely the principal ideals of
R
.

It was proved in Lemma 5.5 of
[
21
]
that the map
\mdeg\colon\mathcal{I}(R)\to\mathbb{N}\text{ defined by }I\mapsto\mdeg(I)

is a semigroup homomorphism, i.e., for every
I,J\in\mathcal{I}(R)
,

\mdeg(IJ)=\mdeg(I)+\mdeg(J).

(1)

For a nonzero ideal
I
of
R
and a nonnegative integer
i
, we let

I[i]:=\{f_{i}\colon f\in I\}

be the set of the homogeneous components of degree
i
of the elements of
I
, which is a
D
-module contained in
R_{i}
. For
I,J\in{\mathcal{I}}(R)
and
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
,

I[i]\cdot J[j]:=\left\{\sum_{s=1}^{k}x_{s}y_{s}\colon k\geq 1,x_{s}\in I[i],y_{s}\in J[j]\right\}

is also a
D
-module.
Moreover, we let

I_{K}[i]:={\rm Span}_{K}\{I[i]\}

be the
K
-linear span of the elements of
I[i]
. It is clear that
I_{K}[i]
is a finite dimensional
K
-vector space contained in
S_{i}
.

Remark 2.4
.

If
I=\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\rangle
is a nonzero finitely generated ideal of
R
and
\mdeg(I)=m\in\mathbb{N}
, then

I_{K}[m]={\rm Span}_{K}\{(g_{i})_{m}\colon\mdeg(g_{i})=m\}.

Lemma 2.5

(Lemma 5.6 of
[
21
]
)
.

Let
I,J\in\mathcal{I}(R)
and set
\mdeg(I)=d
,
\mdeg(J)=e
and
\mdeg(IJ)=m
. The following hold:

(i)

(IJ)[m]=I[d]\cdot J[e]
;

(ii)

(IJ)_{K}[m]=I_{K}[d]\cdot J_{K}[e]
.

Remark 2.6
.

Throughout the rest of the paper, we will consider ideals of
R
generated by polynomials in two indeterminates, say
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
. To slightly ease the notation, we will rename these indeterminates to
X
and
Y
respectively. Note that if an ideal is generated only by polynomials in
X
and
Y
, ideals occurring in any of its factorizations are necessarily also generated by polynomials in
X
and
Y
only. We will use this fact in the rest of the paper without further explanation.

Finally, let
\leq
be the
lexicographic order
on monomials of
S
defined by
X>Y>X_{3}>\dots>X_{N}
; we denote by
\ini(f)
the
initial monomial
of
f\in S
with respect to
\leq
, that is, the largest monomial occurring in
f
with a non-zero coefficient.

2.3.
A link between polynomial ideals and power monoids

Keeping the notation of the previous subsection, we let
R=D[X_{1},\dots,X_{N}]
, with
D
an integral domain, be a polynomial ring in
N\geq 2
indeterminates. As mentioned in the introduction, the study of
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
from the perspective of factorization theory was initiated
[
21
, Sect. 5]
, where it was shown that when
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
is a
\mathsf{BF}
-monoid, it is fully elastic and each of its unions of sets of lengths equals
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}

[
21
, Theorem 5.1]
.

The proof of the main theorem relies on the construction of several families of atoms in
\mathcal{I}(R)
:

\mathfrak{b}_{i}(X,Y):=\langle X^{i},Y^{i}\rangle\ {\rm for}\ i\in\mathbb{N}^{+},

\mathfrak{c}_{2i+1}(X,Y):=\langle\{X^{2i+1},X^{2i}Y\}\cup\{X^{2i-j}Y^{j+1}\colon j\in\llbracket 1,2i+1\rrbracket\cap 2\mathbb{N}\}\rangle\ {\rm for}\ i\in\mathbb{N}^{+},

\mathfrak{c}_{2i}(X,Y):=\langle\{X^{2i},X^{2i-1}Y\}\cup\{X^{2i-j}Y^{j}\colon j\in\llbracket 1,2i\rrbracket\cap 2\mathbb{N}\}\rangle\ {\rm for}\ i\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}.

These families of atoms
share a common structure: as explained in
[
21
, Example 5.13]
, they correspond to families of atoms in the monoid of finite nonempty subsets of
\mathbb{N}
endowed with set-wise addition, known as the
(finitary) power monoid
of
\mathbb{N}
and denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})
. More precisely,
\mathcal{I}(R)
has a submonoid which is isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})
. Indeed, the map

\Phi\colon\ \mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})\to{\mathcal{I}}(R),\ A\mapsto I_{A}

(2)

that sends a set
A\in\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})
, say
A=\{n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell}\}
with
\ell\geq 1
and
0\leq n_{1}<\dots<n_{\ell}
, to the ideal

I_{A}:=\langle X^{n_{\ell}-n_{1}}Y^{n_{1}},X^{n_{\ell}-n_{2}}Y^{n_{2}},\dots,X^{n_{\ell}-n_{\ell-1}}Y^{n_{\ell-1}},Y^{n_{\ell}}\rangle,

is readily seen to be an injective monoid homomorphism. The image of this map, denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{2}(X,Y)
, consists precisely of those ideals which can be generated by
Y^{m}
and a (possibly empty) set of monomials in
X
and
Y
of degree
m
, for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular,
\mathcal{M}_{2}(X,Y)\subseteq{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}(R)
, where
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
is the monoid of
monomial ideals
of
R
(i.e., ideals generated by monomials).

The study of the arithmetic properties of
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})
was started by Fan and Tringali in
[
16
]
. In particular, it follows from
[
16
, Prop.s 3.2 and 3.5]
that
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})
is a commutative, reduced, unit-cancellative semigroup with
\{0\}
as the identity element. Among their results, Fan and Tringali show that for any
i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, the sets
\{0,i\}
and
\{0,1,3,\dots,2i+1\}
are atoms, while
\{0,1,2,\dots,2i\}
is an atom for
i\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
(see
[
16
, Prop.s 4.1(iv) and 4.2]
). The correspondence
\Phi
in (
2
) sends these sets to the ideals
\mathfrak{b}_{i}(X,Y)
,
\mathfrak{c}_{2i+1}(X,Y)
, and
\mathfrak{c}_{2i}(X,Y)
, respectively. In
[
21
, Prop. 5.10]
, it is proved that
\mathfrak{b}_{i}(X,Y)
and
\mathfrak{c}_{2i+1}(X,Y)
are atoms of
\mathcal{I}(R)
for any
i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, while
\mathfrak{c}_{2i}(X,Y)
is an atom only for
i\geq 3
.
Note that,
a priori
, the above correspondence ensures only that these ideals are atoms of the submonoid
\mathcal{M}_{2}(X,Y)
, as monoid isomorphisms are clearly atom-preserving. Indeed, it turns out that in general
\Phi
does
not
send atoms of
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})
to atoms of
\mathcal{I}(R)
, as the set
C=\{0,1,2,4\}
is easily seen to be an atom of
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})
, while, when
2
is a unit of
D
,
(see
[
21
, Remark 5.11]
)

I_{C}=\mathfrak{c}_{4}(X,Y)=\langle X^{4},X^{3}Y,X^{2}Y^{2},Y^{4}\rangle=\langle X^{2},XY+Y^{2}\rangle\langle X^{2},XY-Y^{2}\rangle.

On the other hand, we will show in Remark
4.3
that
I_{C}
is an atom of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
. Thus, it might be the case that the map
\Phi
sends atoms of
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})
to atoms of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
.

3.
Atoms and sets of lengths in
{\mathcal{I}}(R)

Throughout this section, we maintain the notations introduced in Section
2.2
, and assume that
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
is a
\mathsf{BF}
-monoid. This assumption is clearly satisfied if, for instance,
D
is noetherian (in particular, if
D=K
). Our aim is to find new classes of atoms in
\mathcal{I}(R)
, by extending the methods introduced in
[
21
, Sect. 5]
to further families of ideals of
R
, corresponding (via the map
\Phi
in (
2
) from
\mathcal{P}_{\rm fin}(\mathbb{N})
to
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
) to atoms of the
reduced power monoid

\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
of
\mathbb{N}
, i.e., the monoid of nonempty finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
containing
0
with setwise addition. Note that
\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{P}_{\rm fin}(\mathbb{N}))=\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(\mathbb{N}))\cup\{\{1\}\}
, since every finite set of positive integers
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
with
n\geq 2
or
a_{1}\geq 2
can be written as
A=(A-1)+\{1\}
. It is also worth noting that the monoid homomorphism
\Phi
restricts to a monoid isomorphism between
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
and the ideals of
R
generated, for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, by
X^{m}
,
Y^{m}
and a set of monomials in
X
and
Y
of degree
m
(cf.
[
21
, Example 5.13]
).

The following lemma, generalizing the arguments used in
[
21
]
, collects a number of necessary conditions for the existence of a nontrivial factorization for an ideal
I_{A}
, where
A\in\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
. They will be a key tool in the proofs of the main results of this section, namely, Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.7
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
\mathcal{N}
be a set of monomials of degree
m
in
X
and
Y
, and
I=\langle X^{m},Y^{m},\mathcal{N}\rangle
be an ideal of
R
. Suppose that
I=\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
, for some
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in\mathcal{I}(R)
such that
I\subsetneq\mathfrak{a}
,
\mathfrak{b}\subsetneq R
,
\mdeg(\mathfrak{a})=d
,
\mdeg(\mathfrak{b})=e
,
{\rm dim}_{K}(\mathfrak{a}_{K}[d])=r
and
{\rm dim}_{K}(\mathfrak{b}_{K}[e])=s
. Then, the following hold.

(i)

m=d+e
and
I_{K}[m]=\mathfrak{a}_{K}[d]\cdot\mathfrak{b}_{K}[e]
.

(ii)

d\geq 1,e\geq 1
.

(iii)

\mathfrak{a}_{K}[d]
has dimension
r\geq 2
and admits a
K
-basis
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}
with
X^{d}=\ini(f_{1})>\ini(f_{2})>\dots>\ini(f_{r})
. Analogously,
\mathfrak{b}_{K}[e]
has dimension
r\geq 2
and admits a
K
-basis
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{s}\}
with
X^{e}=\ini(g_{1})>\ini(g_{2})>\dots>\ini(g_{s})
.

(iv)

For every
j\in\llbracket 2,r\rrbracket
there exists
a_{j}\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
such that
\ini(f_{j})=X^{d-a_{j}}Y^{a_{j}}
. Analogously, for each
k\in\llbracket 2,s\rrbracket
there exists
b_{k}\in\llbracket 1,e\rrbracket
such that
\ini(g_{k})=X^{e-b_{k}}Y^{b_{k}}
.

(v)

Set
a_{1}=b_{1}=0
; then, for every
j\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
and every
k\in\llbracket 1,s\rrbracket
we have

X^{m-(a_{j}+b_{k})}Y^{a_{j}+b_{k}}\in\{X^{m},Y^{m}\}\cup\mathcal{N}.

Proof.

It follows from Remark
2.2
that
\mdeg(I)=m
, and item
(i)
then follows from equation (
1
) and Lemma
2.5
(i)
; item
(ii)
follows from
[
21
, Lemma 5.8]
, while item
(iv)
is a consequence of item
(iii)
. We are thus left to prove items
(iii)
and
(v)
.

Since
{\rm dim}_{K}(\mathfrak{a}_{K}[d])=r
and
{\rm dim}_{K}(\mathfrak{b}_{K}[e])=s
, we get from
[
21
, Lemma 5.9]
that there are bases
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}
of
\mathfrak{a}_{K}[d]
and
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{s}\}
of
\mathfrak{b}_{K}[e]
with
\ini(f_{1})>\ini(f_{2})>\dots>\ini(f_{r})
and
\ini(g_{1})>\ini(g_{2})>\dots>\ini(g_{s})
. Note that the
\ini(f_{j})
and
\ini(g_{k})
are monomials of degree
d
and
e
respectively, for all
j\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
and
k\in\llbracket 1,s\rrbracket
. If
r=1
, we get from
(i)
that

I_{K}[m]\ni X^{m}=\sum_{k=1}^{s}\alpha_{k}f_{1}g_{k}\ \quad{\rm for\ some}\ \alpha_{1},\dots.\alpha_{s}\in K.

Thus, we obtain
f_{1}=X^{d}
, implying in turn that
Y^{m}\notin{\rm Span}_{K}\{f_{1}\}\cdot\mathfrak{b}_{K}[e]
, contradicting
(i)
. Therefore, it must be
r\geq 2
, and similarly we obtain that also
s\geq 2
. On the other hand, if
\ini(f_{1})\neq X^{d}
, then for every
f\in\mathfrak{a}_{K}[d]\setminus\{0\}
we have
\ini(f)<X^{d}
, hence
\ini(fg)<X^{m}
for every
f\in\mathfrak{a}_{K}[d]\setminus\{0\}
,
g\in\mathfrak{b}_{K}[e]\setminus\{0\}
, and so
X^{m}\notin I_{K}[m]
, contradicting
(i)
. Thus,
\ini(f_{1})=X^{d}
, and analogously we can prove that also
\ini(g_{1})=X^{e}
. This concludes the proof of item
(iii)
.

To prove item
(v)
, consider, for any
j\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
and
k\in\llbracket 1,s\rrbracket
,

\ini(f_{j}g_{k})=\ini(f_{j})\ini(g_{k})=X^{d-a_{j}+e-b_{k}}Y^{a_{j}+b_{k}}=X^{m-(a_{j}+b_{k})}Y^{a_{j}+b_{k}},

where we have used
(iv)
and
(i)
. Since
f_{j}g_{k}\in I_{K}[m]={\rm Span}_{K}(\{X^{m},Y^{m}\}\cup\mathcal{N})
, we must also have
\ini(f_{j}g_{k})\in I_{K}[m]
, and the conclusion follows from the above equalities.
∎

3.1.
Atoms constructed from sum-free sets of positive integers

We are now in the position to extend the results on the atomicity of
\mathfrak{b}_{i}(X,Y)
and
\mathfrak{c}_{2i+1}(X,Y)
to a larger family of ideals. Before proceeding, let us recall a classical definition from additive combinatorics: a nonempty subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
sum-free
if
A\cap(A+A)=\emptyset
. Easy examples of sum-free sets include any set of odd positive integers and any subset of
\{\lceil{n/2}\rceil,\dots,n\}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lceil\ \rceil
denotes the usual ceiling function. In fact, Green proved in
[
28
]
that these subsets account for “most” of the sum-free sets; more precisely, he proved that the number of sum-free subsets of
\{1,\dots,n\}
is
O(2^{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, as predicted by the Cameron-Erdös Conjecture.

Clearly, a finite sum-free set is contained in
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})\setminus\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
. It follows that no sum-free set different from
\{1\}
is an atom of
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})
(see the remarks at the beginning of the section).
In preparation for Theorem
3.3
, we note that adjoining the zero element to sum-free subsets of
\mathbb{N}
turns them into atoms of
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
A
is finite and sum-free, then
\{0\}\cup A
is an atom of
\mathcal{P}_{\rm fin}(\mathbb{N})
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exist
B,C\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm fin}(\mathbb{N})\setminus\{\{0\}\}
such that

\{0\}\cup A=B+C.

Firstly, note that
0\in B\cap C
, for otherwise
0
cannot be an element of
B+C
. Furthermore, since
B
and
C
both differ from
\{0\}
, both
\beta:=\max{B}
and
\gamma:=\max{C}
are positive integers. The set
B+C
contains, in particular, the elements
\beta,\gamma
and
\beta+\gamma
; since they are all positive, they necessarily belong to
A
, but this is a contradiction, as
A
was supposed to be sum-free.
∎

For every
i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, the sets
\beta_{i}=\{i\}
and
\delta_{2i+1}=\{2\llbracket 0,i\rrbracket+1\}
are clearly sum-free. We then get from Lemma
3.2
an alternative proof of the fact (mentioned in the introduction and proved in
[
16
]
) that
\{0\}\cup\beta_{i}
and
\{0\}\cup\delta_{2i+1}
are atoms of
\mathcal{P}_{\rm fin}(\mathbb{N})
. The next theorem (the first of the main results of this section) provides a general method for constructing atoms in
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
from sum-free subsets of
\mathbb{N}
and yields, as a special case, the atomicity of
\mathfrak{b}_{i}(X,Y)=I_{\{0\}\cup\beta_{i}}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{2i+1}(X,Y)=I_{\{0\}\cup\delta_{2i+1}}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
A
is a finite sum-free subset of
\mathbb{N}
, then
I_{\{0\}\cup A}
is an atom of
\mathcal{I}(R)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
I_{\{0\}\cup A}=\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
for some
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in\mathcal{I}(R)\setminus\{R\}
. Set
m=\max{A}=\mdeg(I_{\{0\}\cup A})
,
d=\mdeg(\mathfrak{a})
and
e=\mdeg(\mathfrak{b})
. Since
\{0\}\cup A\in\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, we can apply Lemma
3.1
. Therefore, we obtain
d\geq 1
,
e\geq 1
,
m=d+e
,

{(I_{\{0\}\cup A})}_{K}[m]=\mathfrak{a}_{K}[d]\cdot\mathfrak{b}_{K}[e],

\mathfrak{a}_{K}[d]={\rm Span}_{K}\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}
, where
r\geq 2
and
X^{d}=\ini(f_{1})>\dots>\ini(f_{r})
,
\mathfrak{b}_{K}[e]={\rm Span}_{K}\{g_{1},\dots,g_{s}\}
, where
s\geq 2
and
X^{e}=\ini(g_{1})>\dots>\ini(g_{s})
. Let
\ini(f_{2})=X^{d-a}Y^{a}
for some
a\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
and
\ini(g_{2})=X^{e-b}Y^{b}
for some
b\in\llbracket 1,e\rrbracket
. Then Lemma
3.1
(
(v)
) yields

X^{m-a}Y^{a},X^{m-b}Y^{b},X^{m-(a+b)}Y^{a+b}\in\{X^{m-c}Y^{c}\colon c\in\{0\}\cup A\},

i.e.,
a,b,a+b\in\{0\}\cup A
. Since both
a
and
b
are positive, we actually get
a,b,a+b\in A
, but this contradicts the assumption that
A
is a sum-free set.
∎

In light of the remarks at the beginning of this section, the above theorem gives a substantial number of examples of atoms of
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
. As a further corollary, we also obtain an
\mathcal{I}(R)
-analogue of
[
16
, Prop. 4.1(v)]
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
. For any integer
j\in\llbracket 1,m-1\rrbracket
, the ideal
I=I_{\{0,j,m\}}=\langle X^{m},X^{m-j}Y^{j},Y^{m}\rangle
is an atom of
\mathcal{I}(R)
if and only if
m\neq 2j
.

Proof.

If
m=2j
, then
I={\langle X^{j},Y^{j}\rangle}^{2}
is clearly not an atom. Conversely, if
m\neq 2j
, then the set
\{j,m\}
is sum-free, and
I=I_{\{0,j,m\}}
is an atom by Theorem
3.3
.
∎

3.2.
A further family of atoms

For every
i\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, the set
\delta_{2i}=\{1,2\llbracket 1,i\rrbracket\}
is clearly not sum-free. Nonetheless, as recalled in Section
2.3
,
\mathfrak{c}_{2i+1}(X,Y)=I_{\{0\}\cup\delta_{2i}}
is an atom for every
i\geq 3
. In this section, we will provide a further class of atoms not accounted for by Theorem
3.3
.

Following
[
16
, Prop. 4.10]
, fix a positive integer
n\geq 2
and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},a_{n+1}
be positive integers satisfying the following conditions:

(C1)

a_{n+1}=a_{1}+\dots+a_{n-1}+2a_{n}
,

(C2)

a_{i+1}>2(a_{1}+\dots+a_{i})
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
.

Further, define

A_{n}:=\left\{a_{I}\colon I\subseteq\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket\right\},

B_{n}:=A_{n}\cup\{a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}\}\cup(A_{n}+a_{n+1}),

C_{n}:=\left\{a_{I}\colon I\subseteq\llbracket 1,n+1\rrbracket\right\},

where we set
a_{\emptyset}=0
and
a_{I}=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}
for every nonempty subset
I\subseteq\llbracket 1,n+1\rrbracket
.
In
[
16
, Prop. 4.10(i)]
, it is proved that, for every
n\geq 2
, the set
B_{n}
is an atom of
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
.

Remark 3.5
.

By the definition of the
a_{i}
’s it is clear that
\max(B_{n})=a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}+a_{n+1}=a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket\cup\{a_{n+1}\}}
. By condition (C1), if
a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}
is odd (resp. even), then
a_{n+1}
is odd (resp. even), hence
m=\max(B_{n})
is always even. In particular,
m=a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}+a_{n+1}=2a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}+2a_{n}=2a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}
.

Let us focus our attention to the ideals of
R

I_{B_{n}}=\langle X^{m-b}Y^{b}\colon b\in B_{n}\rangle\text{ and }I_{C_{n}}=\langle X^{a_{\llbracket 1,n+1\rrbracket}-c}Y^{c}\colon c\in C_{n}\rangle.

Remark 3.6
.

The following identities can be easily verified by a direct computation or using
[
16
, Prop. 4.10 (ii)]
and the monoid homomorphism
\Phi\colon\ {P}_{{\rm fin}}(\mathbb{N})\to{\mathcal{I}}(R)
.

I_{C_{n}}=\mathfrak{b}_{a_{1}}\cdot\dots\cdot\mathfrak{b}_{a_{n+1}}=\mathfrak{b}_{a_{n}}\cdot I_{B_{n}}.

(3)

We recall here that
\mathfrak{b}_{i}=\langle X^{i},Y^{i}\rangle\in\mathscr{A}({\mathcal{I}}(R))
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

The next theorem provides a new class of atoms of
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
, namely the ideals
I_{B_{n}}
, and gives bounds on the set of lengths of the ideals
I_{C_{n}}
.

Theorem 3.7
.

(1)

I_{B_{n}}
is an atom of
\mathcal{I}(R)
for every
n\geq 2
.

(2)

\{2,n+1\}\subseteq\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{I}(R)}(I_{C_{n}})\subseteq\llbracket 2,n+1\rrbracket
.

Before proving this result, we need some technical lemmas based on the properties of the positive integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n+1}

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
n,k
be positive integers such that
n\geq 2
and
k\leq n
and let
I,J\subseteq\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
. Then
a_{I}+a_{J}=a_{H}
for some
H\subseteq\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
if and only if
I\cap J=\emptyset
.

Proof.

One of the implications is trivial: if
I\cap J=\emptyset
, then
a_{I}+a_{J}=a_{I\cup J}
. Conversely, let
I\cap J\neq\emptyset
and assume to the contrary that there exists
H\subseteq\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
such that

a_{H}=a_{I}+a_{J}=a_{I\cup J}+a_{I\cap J}=a_{(I\cup J)\setminus(I\cap J)}+2a_{I\cap J}.

(4)

Since
a_{I\cap J}>0
, in particular
H\neq\emptyset
. Let
h:=\max H
and
\ell:=\max(I\cup J)
. Then equality (
4
) and condition
(C2)
imply
h=\ell
and
h\in(I\cup J)\setminus(I\cap J)
. In fact, if
h>\ell
, then (using the fact that both
h
and
\ell
are at most
k
) we obtain
I,J\subseteq\llbracket 1,h-1\rrbracket
and

a_{H}\geq a_{\{h\}}>2a_{\llbracket 1,h-1\rrbracket}\geq 2a_{(I\cup J)\setminus(I\cap J)}+2a_{I\cap J}\geq a_{I}+a_{J};

if
\ell>h
, then
H\subseteq\llbracket 1,\ell-1\rrbracket
and

a_{H}<2a_{\llbracket 1,\ell-1\rrbracket}<a_{\{\ell\}}\leq a_{I\cup J}<a_{I}+a_{J};

if
h=\ell
and
h\in I\cap J
, then

a_{H}\leq a_{\{h\}}+a_{\llbracket 1,h-1\rrbracket}<2a_{\{h\}}\leq 2a_{I\cap J}\leq a_{I}+a_{J}.

Now, since
h=\ell\in(I\cup J)\setminus(I\cap J)
, it follows that
((I\cup J)\setminus(I\cap J))\setminus\{h\}=((I\cup J)\setminus\{h\})\setminus(I\cap J)
; moreover, equality (
4
) yields

a_{H\setminus\{h\}}=a_{(I\cup J)\setminus\{h\}}+a_{I\cap J}=a_{((I\cup J)\setminus\{h\})\setminus(I\cap J)}+2a_{I\cap J}.

If
H\setminus\{h\}=\emptyset
, we immediately get a contradiction. Hence, set
h^{\prime}:=\max(H\setminus\{h\})
and
\ell^{\prime}:=\max((I\cup J)\setminus\{h\})
(note that
(I\cup J)\setminus\{h\}\neq\emptyset
since
I\cap J\neq\emptyset
). Arguing as before, we obtain
h^{\prime}=\ell^{\prime}\in((I\cup J)\setminus\{h\})\setminus(I\cap J)
. Iterating this argument, we arrive at the following equation

a_{x}=a_{(I\cup J)\setminus(H\setminus\{x\})}+a_{I\cap J},

(5)

where
x\in H
and
x\in(I\cup J)\setminus(I\cap J)
. By subtracting
a_{x}
from both sides of equation (
5
), we finally obtain
a_{I\cap J}=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

For a positive integer
n\geq 2
and
I,J,H\subseteq\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
, assume that

a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}+a_{H}=a_{I}+a_{J}=a_{I\cup J}+a_{I\cap J}.

Then,
I\cup J=\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
and
H=I\cap J
.

Proof.

We first observe that
n-1\in I\cup J
. If
n=2
this is trivial, otherwise, if
n>2
and
I\cup J\subseteq\llbracket 1,n-2\rrbracket
, we get the contradiction

a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}+a_{H}\geq a_{n-1}>2a_{\llbracket 1,n-2\rrbracket}\geq a_{I}+a_{J}.

Moreover
n-1\in H
if and only if
n-1\in I\cap J
. In fact if
n-1\in H
and
n-1\notin I\cap J
, then

a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}+a_{H}\geq 2a_{n-1}>a_{n-1}+2a_{\llbracket 1,n-2\rrbracket}\geq a_{I}+a_{J},

while if
n-1\in I\cap J
and
n-1\notin H
, then

a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}+a_{H}\leq a_{n-1}+2a_{\llbracket 1,n-2\rrbracket}<2a_{n-1}\leq a_{I}+a_{J},

which is a contradiction in both cases. Thus,
a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}+a_{H}=a_{I}+a_{J}=a_{I\cup J}+a_{I\cap J}
implies that
a_{\llbracket 1,n-2\rrbracket}+a_{H\setminus\{n-1\}}=a_{I\setminus\{n-1\}}+a_{J\setminus\{n-1\}}=a_{(I\cup J)\setminus\{n-1\}}+a_{(I\cap J)\setminus\{n-1\}}
. Arguing recursively as in the previous lemma, we prove the statement after
n-1
steps.
∎

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
n\geq 2
. The following statements hold:

(iii)

For every
I,J\subseteq\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
,
a_{I}+a_{J}\in A_{n}
if and only if
I\cap J=\emptyset
.

(iv)

For every

and

(note that

is a disjoint union),
a_{I}+a_{J}\in B_{n}
if and only if

. Moreover,
a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}+a_{I}\in B_{n}
if and only if

.

(v)

For every
I,J\subseteq\llbracket 1,n+1\rrbracket
,
a_{I}+a_{J}\in C_{n}
if and only if either
I\cap J=\emptyset
or
I\cup J=\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
and
n\in I\cap J
.

Proof.

(iii)
The statement follows from Lemma
3.8
with

. In fact,
a_{I}+a_{J}\in A_{n}
if and only if there is

.

(iv)
We first assume

by

. If
J=X\cup\{n+1\}
for some
X\in\mathcal{P}(\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket)
, then
I\cap X=\emptyset
and, by
(iii)
,
a_{I}+a_{X}\in A_{n}
. Thus
a_{I}+a_{J}=a_{I}+a_{X}+a_{n+1}\in A_{n}+a_{n+1}\subseteq B_{n}
.

On the other hand, let
I\subseteq\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
,
J\in\mathcal{P}(\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket)\cup\{Y\cup\{n+1\}\colon Y\in\mathcal{P}(\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket)\}
and assume
a_{I}+a_{J}\in B_{n}
. Then
a_{I}+a_{J}=a_{H}
, where either
H\subseteq\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
, or
H=\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
or
H=X\cup\{n+1\}
, with
X\in\mathcal{P}(\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket)
. Let us first observe that
H\neq\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. Indeed, if we assume that
H=\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
, if
n+1\in J
, then
a_{I}+a_{J}\geq a_{n+1}>a_{H}
, while if
n+1\notin J
(so
I,J\subseteq\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
),
a_{I}+a_{J}\leq 2a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}<a_{n}\leq a_{H}
.

If
H\in\mathcal{P}(\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket)
, then
J\in\mathcal{P}(\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket)
and
a_{H}\in A_{n}
, hence
I\cap J=\emptyset
, by
(iii)
. If
H=X\cup\{n+1\}
for some
X\in\mathcal{P}(\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket)
, then
J=Y\cup\{n+1\}
for some
Y\in\mathcal{P}(\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket)
and
a_{I}+a_{Y}=a_{X}
. Note that
a_{X}\in A_{n}
, so, by
(iii)
,
I\cap Y=\emptyset
and hence
I\cap J=\emptyset
.

Finally, we prove that
a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}+a_{I}\in B_{n}
only if
I=\emptyset
(the reverse implication is trivial). Assume
a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}+a_{I}=a_{H}
, where either
H\subseteq\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
,
H=\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
or
H=X\cup\{n+1\}
, with
X\subseteq\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
. First observe that
H
cannot contain
n+1
, otherwise
a_{H}\geq a_{n+1}=a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}+a_{n}>a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}+2a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}\geq a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}+a_{I}
, so we are left to check only the first two of the above conditions. If
H\subseteq\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
, Lemma
3.8
implies
\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\cap I=\emptyset
and this can happen only if
I=\emptyset
. If
H=\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
, then
a_{I}=0
and
I=\emptyset
.

(v)
Firstly, if
I\cap J=\emptyset
, then
a_{I}+a_{J}=a_{I\cup J}\in C_{n}
, while if
I\cup J=\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
and
n\in I\cap J
, then
a_{I}+a_{J}=a_{I\cup J}+a_{I\cap J}=a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}+a_{n}+a_{((I\cap J)\setminus\{n\})}=a_{((I\cap J)\setminus\{n\})\cup\{n+1\}}\in C_{n}
.

On the other hand, assume
a_{I}+a_{J}\in C_{n}
, i.e., there exists
H\subseteq\llbracket 1,n+1\rrbracket
such that

a_{H}=a_{I}+a_{J}=a_{I\cup J}+a_{I\cap J}=a_{(I\cup J)\setminus(I\cap J)}+2a_{I\cap J}.

(6)

If
H=\emptyset
, then
I,J=\emptyset
and
I\cup J=\emptyset
. Assume then
H\neq\emptyset
, implying
I\cup J\neq\emptyset
. Set
h:=\max H
and
h^{\prime}:=\max I\cup J
. We first observe that it must be
h\geq h^{\prime}
. In fact, if
h<h^{\prime}
and
h^{\prime}\leq n
, then
a_{I}+a_{J}\geq a_{h^{\prime}}>2a_{\llbracket 1,h\rrbracket}\geq a_{H}
, while if
h^{\prime}=n+1
(and so
h\leq n
), then
a_{I}+a_{J}\geq a_{n+1}=a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}+a_{n}>a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}\geq a_{H}
. Both cases contradict the assumption.

If
h=h^{\prime}
, we distinguish two sub-cases. If
h=h^{\prime}\leq n
, then
I,J,H\subseteq\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
and we get from Lemma
3.8
(with
k=n
) and (
6
) that
I\cap J=\emptyset
. If
h=h^{\prime}=n+1
, then
n+1\notin I\cap J
(otherwise
a_{I}+a_{J}=a_{I\cup J}+a_{I\cap J}\geq 2a_{n+1}>a_{H}
). Thus, (
6
) becomes
a_{H\setminus\{n+1\}}=a_{(I\cup J)\setminus\{n+1\}}+a_{I\cap J}
and we get from Lemma
3.8
with
k=n
, that
(I\cup J)\setminus\{n+1\}
and
I\cap J
have no elements in common, i.e.,
I\cap J=\emptyset
.

It remains to consider the case
h>h^{\prime}
. This can occur only if
h=n+1
and
h^{\prime}=n
. In fact, if
h=n+1
and
h^{\prime}\leq n-1
, then
a_{H}\geq a_{n+1}>a_{n}>2a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}\geq a_{I}+a_{J}
, while if
h\leq n
, then
a_{H}\geq a_{h}>2a_{\llbracket 1,h-1\rrbracket}\geq a_{I}+a_{J}
. Moreover, it must be
n\in I\cap J
, otherwise we get the following contradiction:

a_{H}\geq a_{n+1}>2a_{n}>a_{n}+2a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}\geq a_{I}+a_{J}
. In addition, we have
n\notin H
, otherwise
a_{H}\geq a_{n}+a_{n+1}=2a_{n}+a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}>2a_{n}+3a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}\geq 2a_{\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}\geq a_{I}+a_{J}
, which is again an absurd. Finally, summing up all the observations, we have
\max H=n+1
,
\max I\cup J=n
,
H\setminus\{n+1\}\subseteq\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
,
I\cup J\subseteq\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
and
n\in I\cap J
. Then, (
6
) becomes
a_{H}=a_{H\setminus\{n+1\}}+a_{n+1}=a_{H\setminus\{n+1\}}+a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}+2a_{n}=a_{I\setminus\{n\}}+a_{J\setminus\{n\}}+2a_{n}=a_{I}+a_{J}
, and we get

a_{H\setminus\{n+1\}}+a_{\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket}=a_{I\setminus\{n\}}+a_{J\setminus\{n\}}.

It then follows from Lemma
3.9
that
(I\setminus\{n\})\cup(J\setminus\{n\})=\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
, thus
I\cup J=\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
and we already know that
n\in I\cap J
. All possible cases have been examined.
∎

We are finally ready to prove Theorem
3.7
.

(I_{B_{n}})_{K}={\rm Span}_{K}\{X^{m-b}Y^{b}\colon b\in B_{n}\}=\mathfrak{a}_{K}[d]\cdot\mathfrak{b}_{K}[e],

(7)

4.
Arithmetical properties of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)

In this section we focus on the arithmetical properties of the monoid
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
of nonzero monomial ideals in
R=K[X_{1},\dots,X_{N}]
, with
K
an arbitrary field and
N\geq 2
.
We start by recalling a basic fact on monomial ideals which we will repeatedly use in the following discussion. We refer to
[
13
, Cor. 1.8]
for a proof.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
I\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
, and
\mathcal{M}
a set of monomials in
I
. Then
\mathcal{M}
generates
I
if and only if for each monomial
v\in I
there exists
u\in\mathcal{M}
such that
u\mid v
.

Remark 4.2
.

If
I\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
and
\mdeg{(I)}=0
, then
I=R
. Indeed,
I
contains an element of min-degree zero, i.e., a polynomial
f\in R
with
f(0)\neq 0
; since
I
is generated by monomials and
f\in I
, necessarily
f(0)\in I
, whence
1\in I
.

Example 4.3
.

As a first application of the lemma, we show that the ideal

\mathfrak{c}_{4}=I_{\{0,1,2,4\}}=\langle X^{4},X^{3}Y,X^{2}Y^{2},Y^{4}\rangle

is an atom of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
, while back in Section
2
we recalled that it is not an atom of
\mathcal{I}(R)
(at least when the characteristic of
K
is not
2
), despite
\{0,1,2,4\}
being an atom of
\mathcal{P}_{\rm fin}(\mathbb{N})
. Suppose that
\mathfrak{c}_{4}=\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
for some
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)\setminus\{R\}
, with
\mdeg(\mathfrak{a})=d
and
\mdeg(\mathfrak{b})=e
. By equation (
1
) and Remark
4.2
,
d+e=4
,
d\geq 1
and
e\geq 1
. By symmetry, we can also assume
d\leq e
, so the only possibilities for
(d,e)
are
(1,3)
and
(2,2)
.

Case
(d,e)=(1,3)
: first, we claim that
Y^{3}\in\mathfrak{b}
. Indeed, if this is not the case, for every monomial
X^{i}Y^{j}\in\mathfrak{b}
we have either
i\geq 1
or
j\geq 4
, as
e=3
; the product of such elements with monomials of
\mathfrak{a}
, all of which have positive min-degree, yields elements of the form
X^{k}Y^{l}\in\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
, with either
k\geq 1
or
l\geq 5
. In particular,
Y^{4}\not\in\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
, a contradiction. Now suppose
X\notin\mathfrak{a}
; since
d=1
, for every monomial
X^{i}Y^{j}\in\mathfrak{a}
we have either
i\geq 2
or
j\geq 1
. The product of such elements with monomials of
\mathfrak{b}
yields elements of the form
X^{k}Y^{l}
, with either
k+l\geq 5
or
l\geq 1
. In particular,
X^{4}\not\in\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
, again a contradiction; therefore,
X\in\mathfrak{a}
. We then obtain
XY^{3}\in\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
, absurd by Lemma
4.1
, as none of the monomials
X^{4},X^{3}Y,X^{2}Y^{2},Y^{4}
divide
XY^{3}
, despite forming a generating set of
\mathfrak{c}_{4}=\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
.

Case
(d,e)=(2,2)
: by arguments analogous to those used in the previous case, we obtain
X^{2},Y^{2}\in\mathfrak{a}\cap\mathfrak{b}
. Since
\langle X^{2},Y^{2}\rangle^{2}=\langle X^{4},X^{2}Y^{2},Y^{4}\rangle\subsetneq\mathfrak{c}_{4}
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\langle X^{2},Y^{2}\rangle\subsetneq\mathfrak{a}
. By Lemma
4.1
, there exist nonnegative integers
i
and
j
such that
X^{i}Y^{j}\in\mathfrak{a}
, and neither
X^{2}
nor
Y^{2}
divides
X^{i}Y^{j}
; since
\mdeg(X^{i}Y^{j})\geq d=2
, the only possibility is
(i,j)=(1,1)
, i.e.,
XY\in\mathfrak{a}
. Multiplying with
Y^{2}\in\mathfrak{b}
, we obtain
XY^{3}\in\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
, a contradiction (as in the previous case).

As we have found a contradiction in all the possible cases, we conclude that
\mathfrak{c}_{4}
does not admit a factorization as the product of two nonunit monomial ideals.

Remark 4.4
.

An important consequence of Lemma
4.1
is that every monomial ideal
I
admits a unique generating set of monomials that is minimal with respect to divisibility
[
13
, Prop. 1.11]
; we denote this set by
G(I)
. As a consequence of Dickson’s Lemma
[
13
, Theorem 1.9]
,
G(I)
is finite for every
I\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
.

Next, we see to what extent can
[
21
, Theorem 5.1]
be adapted to the monoid
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
. Let us define a further class of monomial ideals, namely
\mathfrak{a}_{k}={\langle X,Y\rangle}^{k}
, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

The monoid of monomial ideals
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
of the ring
R=K[X_{1}\dots,X_{N}]
, where
K
is a field and
N\geq 2
, is a BF-monoid. Moreover,

(1)

{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
is neither locally finitely generated nor transfer Krull;

(2)

\mathcal{U}_{k}({\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R))=\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
for all
k\geq 2
;

(3)

\mathsf{L}_{{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)}(\mathfrak{a}_{k})=\llbracket 2,k\rrbracket
for all
k\geq 2
;

(4)

{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
is fully elastic.

Proof.

Let
I\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
with
m=\mdeg{(I)}
. Firstly, since every nonunit (in particular, every atom) of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
has positive min-degree by Remark
4.2
, equation (
1
) implies that the length of any factorization (into atoms of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
) of
I
is at most
m
. We will now show by induction on
m
that
I
always admits at least one factorization, thus proving that
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
is a BF-monoid.

If
m=0
, then
I=R
, by Remark
4.2
. When
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, either
I
is itself an atom, or there exist
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)\setminus\{R\}
such that
I=\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
. By equation (
1
) and Remark
4.2
, we have
m=\mdeg{(\mathfrak{a})}+\mdeg{(\mathfrak{b})}
,
\mdeg{(\mathfrak{a})}\geq 1
and
\mdeg{(\mathfrak{b})}\geq 1
. Thus,
\mdeg{(\mathfrak{a})}<m
and
\mdeg{(\mathfrak{b})}<m
, whence by the inductive hypothesis both
a
and
b
can be factorized into atoms.

(
1
) The fact that
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
is not locally finitely generated follows from the proof of the corresponding statement for
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
in
[
21
, Lemma 5.3.2]
, since all the ideals involved are monomial, and a monomial ideal which is an atom in
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
is clearly also an atom in
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
. For the second part of the statement, assume to the contrary that there are a Krull monoid
H
and a transfer homomorphism
\theta\colon{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)\to H
. Consider the monomial ideals

\mathfrak{a}_{1}=\langle X,Y\rangle,\ \mathfrak{a}_{2}={\langle X,Y\rangle}^{2}={\mathfrak{a}_{1}}^{2},\ \mathfrak{b}_{2}=\langle X^{2},Y^{2}\rangle.

A straightforward computation shows that
\mathfrak{a}_{1}\mathfrak{a}_{2}=\mathfrak{a}_{1}\mathfrak{b}_{2}
, whence
\theta(\mathfrak{a}_{1})\theta(\mathfrak{a}_{2})=\theta(\mathfrak{a}_{1})\theta(\mathfrak{b}_{2})
in
H
. Since Krull monoids are cancellative, we infer
\theta(\mathfrak{a}_{2})=\theta(\mathfrak{b}_{2})
. By Lemma
2.1
, for any
I\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
we have that
I\in\mathcal{A}({\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R))
if and only if
\theta(I)\in\mathcal{A}(H)
. Since
\mathfrak{b}_{2}
is an atom of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
, it follows that
\theta(\mathfrak{b}_{2})
is an atom of
H
. Thus,
\theta(\mathfrak{a}_{2})
is also an atom of
H
, whence
\mathfrak{a}_{2}
is an atom of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
, a contradiction.

(
2
) One can proceed exactly as in
[
21
, Lemma 5.3.3]
to show that
\mathcal{U}_{2}({\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R))=\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, as all the ideals occurring in that proof are monomial. Since
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
is a BF-monoid, the conclusion then follows by
[
21
, Lemma 3.1.3]
.

(
3
) Clearly,
\mdeg{(\mathfrak{a}_{k})}=k
and, for
k\geq 2
, the ideal
\mathfrak{a}_{k}
is not an atom of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
. Since the length of a factorization of a monomial ideal is bounded by its min-degree and
1\in L_{{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)}(\mathfrak{a}_{k})
if and only if
\mathfrak{a}_{k}
is an atom (i.e., if and only if
k=1
), we infer that
L_{{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)}(\mathfrak{a}_{k})\subseteq\llbracket 2,k\rrbracket
, for any
k\geq 2
. For the converse inclusion we refer to the proof of
[
21
, Theorem 5.1.3]
, noting that all the ideals therein are monomial, with one exception: when proving that
2\in L_{{\mathcal{I}}(R)}(\mathfrak{a}_{5})
, the authors use a factorization into genuine polynomial ideals to get around the fact that
\mathfrak{c}_{4}
is generally not an atom of
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
. In our case, by considering the identity

\mathfrak{a}_{5}={\langle X,Y\rangle}^{5}=\langle X,Y\rangle\langle X^{4},X^{3}Y,X^{2}Y^{2},Y^{4}\rangle=\mathfrak{a}_{1}\mathfrak{c}_{4},

involving only monomial ideals, and recalling Remark
4.3
, we can conclude that
2\in L_{{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)}(\mathfrak{a}_{5})
.

(
4
) Set
\mathfrak{p}=\langle X\rangle
, denote by
H_{1}
the free monoid with basis
\{\mathfrak{p}\}
and let
H_{2}=\{\mathfrak{a}\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)\colon\mathfrak{p}\nmid\mathfrak{a}\}
. The ideal
\mathfrak{p}
is principal, thus invertible in the monoid of nonzero fractional ideals of
R
. In particular, it is a cancellative element of this monoid, hence also a cancellative element of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{p}
is clearly prime, whence we obtain
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)=H_{1}\times H_{2}
. Now observe that
\mathfrak{a}_{k}\in H_{2}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, as
Y^{k}\in\mathfrak{a}_{k}
. By item (
3
), we can then apply
[
21
, Prop. 3.2]
to conclude that
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
is fully elastic.
∎

4.1.
Atoms and sets of lengths in
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)

We continue our investigation of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
by constructing new classes of atoms in this monoid. Fix a positive integer

and

verifying conditions (C1),
a_{n+1}=a_{1}+\dots+a_{n-1}+2a_{n}

. Keeping the notation of Section

while we set

let us define:

\tilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{r}:=\langle\mathfrak{b}_{a_{1}}\cdot\mathfrak{b}_{a_{3}}\cdots\mathfrak{b}_{a_{r}},X^{a_{\llbracket 3,r\rrbracket}-a_{2}}Y^{a_{3}-a_{2}}\rangle.

(8)

Note that

, i.e.,

(9)

Thus, if there exists

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
n\geq 3
be a positive integer. The following hold for every

:

(5)

\mathfrak{b}_{a_{2}}\cdot\tilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{r}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathfrak{b}_{a_{i}}
.

(6)

I_{C_{n}}=\mathfrak{b}_{a_{2}}\cdot\tilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{r}\cdot\prod_{i=r+1}^{n+1}\mathfrak{b}_{a_{i}}
.

Proof.

(5)
By definition
\mathfrak{b}_{a_{i}}=\langle X^{a_{i}},Y^{a_{i}}\rangle
and
\tilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{r}=\langle\mathfrak{b}_{a_{1}}\cdot\mathfrak{b}_{a_{3}}\cdots\mathfrak{b}_{a_{r}},X^{a_{\llbracket 3,r\rrbracket}-a_{2}}Y^{a_{3}-a_{2}}\rangle
, whence

\mathfrak{b}_{a_{2}}\cdot\tilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{r}=\langle\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathfrak{b}_{a_{i}},X^{a_{\llbracket 3,r\rrbracket}}Y^{a_{3}-a_{2}},X^{a_{\llbracket 3,r\rrbracket}-a_{2}}Y^{a_{3}}\rangle.

Therefore,
\mathfrak{b}_{a_{2}}\cdot\tilde{\mathfrak{b}}_{r}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathfrak{b}_{a_{i}}
as claimed.

(6)
It follows from item
(5)
and Remark
3.6
.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Fix a positive integer
n\geq 4
. Let
I,J\subseteq\{1\}\cup\llbracket 3,r\rrbracket
with
r\in\llbracket 4,n\rrbracket
. If
a_{J}-a_{I}>a_{1}
and
a_{J}-a_{I}\neq a_{3}-a_{1}
, then
a_{J}-a_{I}\geq a_{3}
.

Proof.

We first observe that
a_{J}-a_{I}=a_{J\setminus I}-a_{I\setminus J}
. If
I\setminus J=\emptyset
, then
a_{J}-a_{I}=a_{J\setminus I}
and
a_{J\setminus I}>a_{1}
trivially implies
a_{J\setminus I}\geq a_{3}
, as
J\setminus I\subseteq\{1\}\cup\llbracket 3,r\rrbracket
. Assume then
I\setminus J\neq\emptyset
. Clearly our assumptions imply that also
J\setminus I\neq\emptyset
(otherwise
a_{J}-a_{I}<0
), so there exist
s,t>0
such that
s=\max(J\setminus I)
and
t=\max(I\setminus J)
. Note that, since
(J\setminus I)\cap(I\setminus J)=\emptyset
,
s\neq t
.
Moreover, it must be
s>t
. In fact, if
s<t
(implying that
t\geq 3
), then

a_{I\setminus J}\geq a_{t}>a_{\llbracket 1,t-1\rrbracket}\geq a_{\llbracket 1,s\rrbracket}\geq a_{J\setminus I},

but this is absurd since we are assuming
a_{J}-a_{I}=a_{J\setminus I}-a_{I\setminus J}>0
.
We can also prove that
s\geq 4
. If this is not the case, in fact, the only admissible alternative is
s=3
and
t=1
. However, this leads to
I\setminus J=\{1\}
and
J\setminus I=\{3\}
, i.e., to
a_{J}-a_{I}=a_{3}-a_{1}
, a case that we discarded by assumption.

Therefore, we can conclude that

a_{J}-a_{I}=a_{J\setminus I}-a_{I\setminus J}\geq a_{s}-a_{\llbracket 1,s-1\rrbracket}>a_{\llbracket 1,s-1\rrbracket}\geq a_{\llbracket 1,3\rrbracket}>a_{3}.

∎

{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
of

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
such that
m>n
, and let
I\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
with
X^{m},Y^{n}\in G(I)
.
Assume
I=\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
for some
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)\setminus{R}
. Then:

(12)

\mdeg{(I)}=\alpha+\beta
, with
\alpha=\mdeg(\mathfrak{a})\geq 1
and
\beta=\mdeg(\mathfrak{b})\geq 1
.

(13)

there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, with
a\geq\alpha
,
b\geq\beta
, and
a+b=m
such that
X^{a},Y^{\alpha}\in\ G(\mathfrak{a})
and
X^{b},Y^{\beta}\in\ G(\mathfrak{b})
.

Proof.

(12)
This follows from equation (
1
) and Remark
4.2
.

(13)
The minimal generators of
\mathfrak{a}
are necessarily monomials of the form
X^{a}Y^{c}
, with
a+c\geq\alpha
, while those of
\mathfrak{b}
are monomials of the form
X^{b}Y^{d}
, with
b+d\geq\beta
. We observe that there must be a minimal generator of
\mathfrak{a}
of the form
Y^{c}
, for some
c\geq\alpha
. Otherwise,
X
would divide every element of
\mathfrak{a}
and thus of
\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
, but this is impossible since
Y^{n}\in I
. Similarly, there must be a minimal generator of
\mathfrak{a}
of the form
X^{a}
for some positive integer
a\geq\alpha
. Arguing in the same way,
\mathfrak{b}
also has among its minimal generators a monomial of the form
Y^{d}
and one of the form
X^{b}
, for some
b,d\geq\beta
. Thus,
Y^{c+d}
is a generator of
I
, while all its other minimal generators are monomials divisible by
X
. Since
Y^{n}\in I
, by Lemma
4.1
,
Y^{c+d}
must divide it, i.e, it must be
c+d\leq n
. But this can clearly happen only if
c=\alpha
and
d=\beta
. Analogously,
X^{a+b}
is a generator of
I
and so it must divide
X^{m}
, whence
a+b\leq m
. But if
a+b<m
, none of the generators of
I
divides
X^{a+b}
, so the only possibility is that
a+b=m
.
∎

Remark 4.14
.

It is worth noting that for an ideal
I\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
with
X^{m},Y^{n}\in\ G(I)
for some
m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, in order to check whether
I
is an atom one needs only to compute a finite number of products
\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
. Indeed, by Lemma
4.13
, if
I=\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
, then
X^{a},Y^{\alpha}\in\ G(\mathfrak{a})
, so every monomial
X^{c}Y^{\gamma}\in\ G(\mathfrak{a})
satisfies
c\leq a
and
\gamma\leq\alpha
, and analogously a monomial
X^{d}Y^{\delta}\in\ G(\mathfrak{b})
satisfies
d\leq b
and
\delta\leq\beta
.

Proof of Theorem
4.9
.

We already know from
[
21
, Prop. 5.10.1]
that
I
is an atom of
I(R)
, hence also of
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
, when
m=n
. Therefore, by symmetry, it is enough to consider the case
m>n
.
If
n=1
, equation (
1
) ensures the atomicity of
I
over
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
: if
I=\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
for some
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
, one among
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
has min-degree zero and must be the whole ring
R
.
Let
n\geq 2
and assume as a contradiction that there exist proper nonzero monomial ideals of
R
, say
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}
, such that
I=\mathfrak{a}\cdot\mathfrak{b}
. By Lemma
4.13
, we have that
X^{a},Y^{\alpha}\in G(\mathfrak{a})
and
X^{b},Y^{\beta}\in G(\mathfrak{b})
, where
\alpha=\mdeg(\mathfrak{a})\geq 1
,
\beta=\mdeg(\mathfrak{b})\geq 1
,
\alpha+\beta=n
,
a\geq\alpha\geq 1
,
b\geq\beta\geq 1
and
a+b=m
. It follows that
X^{a}Y^{\beta}\in I
, but this is a contradiction since
m>a
and
n>\beta
, so none of the generators of
I
divides it.
∎

5.
Prospects for future research

The results of this paper suggest several directions for future research, as they provide large families of atoms in
\mathcal{I}(R)
and
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
, along with techniques to construct them. In light of the recent breakthrough by Reinhart
[
34
]
on the arithmetic of
P_{\rm fin,0}(\mathbb{N})
, we expect that evaluating finer arithmetical invariants of these monoids, such as the (monotone) catenary degree (see
[
22
]
for the definition and some examples in which it can be computed), should be feasible. Moreover, in view of Theorem
3.7
(2)
and Corollary
4.8
, it would be interesting to attempt the construction of ideals whose sets of lengths are not intervals. Such results could, in turn, yield information about the sets of distances in the monoids under consideration. Another possible line of research would consist in detecting the
strong atoms
of
{\mathcal{I}}(R)
or
{\mathcal{M}{\rm on}}(R)
; in the terminology of
[
14
]
