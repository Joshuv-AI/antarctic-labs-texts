Kidney Dysfunction-Associated Intracerebral Haemorrhage: Global Trends and Regional Disparities (1990–2021)
Authors: Lili Tang, Hu Jiang, Hongtao Zhang, Weiwei Wan, Lingting Zang, Xiaoyue Li, Qiang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionKidney dysfunction is an increasingly recognized risk factor of intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH), but its global burden remains underexplored. This study systematically evaluated the burden, trends, health disparities, and future projections related to kidney dysfunction-associated ICH (KD-ICH) from 1990 to 2021.MethodsData were extracted from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. We estimated deaths, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and age-standardized mortality (ASMR) and DALY rates (ASDR) across sex, age, region, and sociodemographic index (SDI) strata. Temporal trends were analysed using the estimated annual percentage change. Health inequality was assessed using the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and concentration index. Future projections to 2040 were generated using ARIMA models.ResultsBetween 1990 and 2021, the absolute burden of KD-ICH increased significantly, with deaths rising from 241,229 to 329,737 and DALYs increasing from 6.23 million to 8.05 million, despite a decline in global ASMR and ASDR. Older adults, especially those aged 60–79 years, and males were disproportionately affected. Nearly 75% of the global burden was concentrated in low-, middle-low-, and middle-SDI regions, which also experienced slower declines in ASR burden. The SDI and the KD-ICH burden were strongly negatively correlated, and healthy inequality metrics confirmed persistent and widening global disparities. The ARIMA model projected a continued decline in age-standardized burden through 2040.ConclusionThe growing absolute burden and pronounced health inequities of KD-ICH highlight the need for context-specific prevention strategies, improved chronic disease management, greater health system investment, and equitable allocation of resources to address disparities and mitigate the future burden of KD-ICH.

Introduction

Intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH), a condition characterized by spontaneous bleeding into the brain parenchyma, is the most devastating stroke subtype [1]. Compared with the other two major stroke subtypes, ischaemic stroke and subarachnoid haemorrhage, ICH is associated with significantly higher case fatality, more severe disability, and a disproportionately greater socioeconomic burden [2]. Despite advances in stroke prevention and management, ICH remains a major global health concern, with 30-day and 1-year mortality rates of 40% and 54%, respectively, and only 12–39% of survivors achieve long-term functional independence [3]. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study, ICH accounted for 28.8% of all incident strokes, less than half the proportion of ischaemic stroke (65.3%), but contributed to a nearly equivalent mortality rate and comparable disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [4]. Among the 7.25 million global stroke deaths reported in 2021, ICH was responsible for 3.31 million (45.6%), nearly matching the 3.59 million deaths attributed to ischaemic stroke (49.5%) [4]. In terms of DALYs, ICH contributed 49.6% of the 160.5 million stroke-related DALYs, surpassing the 43.8% associated with ischaemic stroke [4]. Notably, although the global age-standardized incidence rates of ICH has fallen since 1990, this reduction has progressed substantially more slowly in low- and middle-sociodemographic index (SDI) regions [4]. The burden of ICH disproportionately falls upon socially disadvantaged and other vulnerable populations, as evidenced by 94.2% of ICH-related DALYs occurring outside high-SDI settings [4].

Kidney dysfunction is increasingly recognized as an independent and potent contributor to cerebrovascular events, particularly ICH [5, 6]. Pathophysiological mechanisms that have been proposed to mediate this association include faster atherosclerosis, cerebrovascular remodelling, endothelial dysfunction, impaired cerebral autoregulation, and chronic systemic inflammation [7]. A shared microvascular pathobiology make both the renal and cerebral systems susceptible to convergent traditional arteriosclerotic risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, ageing, and smoking [8]. Non-traditional kidney dysfunction-related risk factors, such as dysregulated calcium-phosphate metabolism, elevated blood pressure variability, elastinolysis, platelet dysfunction, and chronic inflammation, have been proposed to contribute to the development of ICH by triggering vascular injury and endothelial dysfunction [9, 10]. These processes synergistically promote vascular injury and loss of autoregulatory control, predisposing individuals with kidney dysfunction to haemorrhagic stroke phenotypes. Notably, this predisposition is largely mediated by treatment-resistant hypertension and uraemia-associated platelet dysfunction [11, 12]. Data from the INTERSTROKE study confirmed a strong association between kidney dysfunction and stroke, particularly for ICH (OR 1.60; 95% CI: 1.35–1.89) than for ischaemic stroke (OR 1.29; 95% CI: 1.17–1.42) [12]. The outcomes of patients who experience ICH are significantly worse in patients with kidney dysfunction, with mortality and disability inversely correlated with the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [13, 14]. Patients with an eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 have a 5.5-fold higher 30-day mortality and a 7.5-fold higher 1-year mortality than those with preserved renal function, as well as greater stroke severity and larger haematoma volumes [14]. Furthermore, moderate-to-severe kidney dysfunction is associated with a 2.3-fold increase in haematoma volume and a predominance of lobar haemorrhage patterns [15, 16]. These clinical vulnerabilities are compounded by structural inequities, including limited access to kidney disease screening and treatment, an insufficient therapeutic infrastructure, workforce shortages, and low health literacy, all of which contribute to the disproportionate burden of kidney dysfunction and its cerebrovascular sequelae in low-resource and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations [17, 18].

Although the dangerous interplay between kidney dysfunction and ICH is increasingly recognized, epidemiological data quantifying the global burden of kidney dysfunction-associated ICH (KD-ICH) remain limited. Given the marked disparities in healthcare accessibility, affordability, and quality, the burden of KD-ICH likely varies significantly across countries and regions, reflecting long-standing structural and socioeconomic inequalities. A comprehensive assessment of this dual pathology is critical for generating robust epidemiological evidence that can guide the development of targeted prevention strategies and equitable health system responses. However, KD-ICH has not been systematically quantified in the GBD study or other national disease burden estimates, limiting evidence-based prioritization and impeding effective resource allocation at the global, regional, and national levels. Therefore, the aim of this study was to estimate the global burden and cross-national health inequalities of KD-ICH from 1990 to 2021 using data from the GBD database.

Methods

Data Sources

Data for this study were obtained from the GBD 2021 database (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/), which is coordinated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) at the University of Washington [19]. GBD 2021 systematically quantified health losses from 371 diseases and injuries using data from 100,983 sources across 204 countries and territories to support the strengthening of health systems worldwide and address global health disparities. To generate internally consistent estimates, GBD integrates diverse sources of data – including health service utilization records, disease registries, vital registration systems, population-based surveys, and censuses – through advanced statistical modelling techniques such as spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression, the Cause of Death Ensemble Model (CODEm), and Bayesian meta-regression via DisMod-MR 2.1, which allow the comprehensive estimation of disease burden across varying data quality and geographic contexts.

Burden Description

The disease burden related to KD-ICH, including mortality and DALYs, was extracted from the GBD 2021 database. KD-ICH refers to the burden of ICH attributable to kidney dysfunction as a risk factor, estimated using the comparative risk assessment framework of the Global Burden of Disease study. Metrics are reported as absolute numbers, crude rates (per 100,000 populations), and age-standardized rates (ASRs), including age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs) and age-standardized DALY rates (ASDRs), on the basis of the GBD 2021 global standard population. Age standardization was performed using the direct method, applying weights from the World Health Organization’s 2021 standard population structure to adjust for differences in age distribution, thereby improving comparability across countries, regions, and time periods. All estimates are reported with accompanying 95% uncertainty intervals (UI), derived from 1,000 draws from the posterior distribution; the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles are used to define the lower and upper bounds, respectively.

DALYs are computed as the sum of years of life lost and years lived with disability, incorporating mortality counts and severity-specific disability weights. The socioeconomic context is assessed using the SDI, a composite indicator representing national development status. The SDI is calculated as the geometric mean of three normalized components: total fertility rate in females under age 25, mean educational attainment in individuals aged ≥15 years, and lag-distributed income per capita. Countries and territories are categorized into five SDI quintiles including low-, low-middle-, middle-, high-middle-, and high- based on their relative SDI scores, which ranged from 0 to 1.

Descriptive Analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to comprehensively assess the disease burden of KD-ICH across five SDI regions, 21 GBD regions, and 204 countries. KD-ICH-related deaths and DALYs, both as absolute counts and ASR, were compared globally by sex, SDI region, and GBD region for the years 1990–2021. Data from all 204 countries were visualized on a world map to illustrate geographic variation in the burden. Additionally, the distribution of KD-ICH cases over time and across SDI regions was depicted in stacked bar charts to highlight trends and regional disparities.

Trend Analysis

To evaluate temporal trends in the burden of KD-ICH from 1990 to 2021, the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) was computed by fitting a linear regression model to the natural logarithm of the ASMR and ASDR over time [20]. A trend was defined as increasing if both the EAPC and the lower bound of its 95% confidence interval (CI) were >0 and decreasing if both the EAPC and upper bound of the CI were <0. If the 95% CI spanned zero, the trend was considered statistically stable.

Correlation Analysis

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the association between the ASRs of KD-ICH and the SDI at two levels: across 204 countries and 21 GBD regions. This analysis aimed to quantify the linear relationship between the SDI and disease burden, thereby identifying potential socioeconomic gradients in the global distribution of KD-ICH. The p value indicates the probability of observing a correlation coefficient as extreme as the one calculated, assuming the null hypothesis of no true linear association. A p value ≤0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance, suggesting that the observed association is unlikely to be due to chance; in contrast, a p value >0.05 implies insufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis. To visually depict the strength and direction of the correlations, scatter plots with fitted regression lines were generated.

Cross-Country Health Inequality Analysis

Cross-country health inequalities in the burden of KD-ICH were assessed using the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and the concentration index, in accordance with WHO guidelines (https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241548632). The SII quantifies absolute inequality by regressing ASR on the relative socioeconomic rank of countries, weighted by population size, with the regression coefficient representing the absolute difference in disease burden between the lowest and highest positions on the SDI spectrum. The Lorenz curve was constructed by fitting a cubic spline to the cumulative distribution of the ASMR or ASDR plotted against the cumulative population ranked by the SDI. The concentration index, a measure of relative inequality, was calculated as twice the area between the Lorenz curve and the line of equality obtained via numerical integration. A positive concentration index (Lorenz curve below the diagonal) indicates a higher burden concentrated in high SDI countries, whereas a negative index (curve above the diagonal) suggests a disproportionate burden in low SDI countries. Together, these indices provide a quantitative basis for monitoring global health disparities and inform equitable policy responses to reduce the unequal distribution of the KD-ICH burden.

Predictive Analysis

To forecast trends in the burden of KD-ICH through 2040, an autoregressive (AR) integrated moving average (MA) (ARIMA) model was applied. ARIMA models address non-stationary time series data by applying differencing to achieve stationarity, followed by fitting AR and MA components [21]. Model parameters – p (AR order), d (degree of differencing), and q (MA order) – were determined on the basis of model fit indices, including the Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion. The optimally fitted ARIMA model enhances the understanding of long-term disease trajectories and provides evidence-based projections to inform health system planning and preventive strategies.

Data Processing and Visualization

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1) within the RStudio environment. Data preprocessing and manipulation were performed using the data.table, dplyr, reshape, tidyr, tidyverse, and devtools packages. Data visualization was carried out using the ggplot2 and ggmap packages. A two-sided p value ≤0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

The Global KD-ICH Burden Declined Overall from 1990 to 2021, but the Reductions Were Slower in Underdeveloped Regions

From 1990 to 2021, the global burden of KD-ICH increased while the ASR substantially decreased. Globally, KD-ICH-related deaths rose from 241,229 (95% UI: 176,354 to 307,083) to 329,737 (95% UI: 236,342 to 426,396), whereas DALYs increased from 6,234,145 (95% UI: 4,743,943 to 7,810,998) to 8,052,724 (95% UI: 5,962,331 to 10,179,526) years (shown in Table 1). Despite this increase in crude burden, the ASMR decreased from 6.34 (95% UI: 4.52–8.16) to 3.86 (95% UI: 2.75–5.03) per 100,000 populations, and the ASDR decreased from 153.25 (95% UI: 115.44–192.35) to 92.48 (95% UI: 68.49–117.13) per 100,000 populations, corresponding to EAPCs of −1.65 (95% CI: −1.78 to −1.53) and −1.69 (95% CI: −1.80 to −1.58) (shown in Table 1).

Global and regional burden change of KD-ICH from 1990 to 2021

DALYs, disability to adjusted life years; ASR, age to standardized rate; EAPC, estimated annual percentage change; SDI, sociodemographic index.

All SDI regions demonstrated declining trends in the burden of KD-ICH between 1990 and 2021, although the pace of decline was markedly slower in lower-SDI settings. The low-middle-SDI regions exhibited the slowest reduction, with the ASMR decreasing from 8.41 (95% UI: 6.04–10.99) to 5.83 (95% UI: 4.10–7.63) and an EAPC of −1.25 (95% CI: −1.31 to −1.20) (shown in Table 1). Similarly, the ASDR decreased from 208.40 (95% UI: 153.88–266.38) to 142.55 (95% UI: 104.19–181.45), with an EAPC of −1.29 (95% CI: −1.34 to −1.24) (shown in Table 1). The low-SDI regions experienced the second slowest decline, with the ASMR decreasing from 9.36 (95% UI: 6.51–12.22) to 6.19 (95% UI: 4.30–8.38) and an EAPC of −1.44 (95% CI: −1.53 to −1.35), whereas the ASDR decreased from 230.21 (95% UI: 164.03–296.34) to 149.50 (95% UI: 105.68–198.24), with an EAPC of −1.54 (95% CI: −1.63 to −1.45) (shown in Table 1). In contrast, high-middle-SDI regions achieved the most rapid reductions, with the ASMR decreasing from 6.45 (95% UI: 4.52–8.55) to 3.14 (95% UI: 2.19–4.20), for an EAPC of −2.52 (95% CI: −2.82 to −2.22), while the ASDR decreased from 146.77 (95% UI: 110.04–187.98) to 69.53 (95% UI: 50.66–90.81), with an EAPC of −2.63 (95% CI: −2.91 to −2.35) (shown in Table 1). High-SDI regions were the only regions that exhibited concurrent decreases in both the absolute and age-standardized KD-ICH burden (shown in Table 1).

Across the 21 GBD regions, Southern sub-Saharan Africa was the only region with no statistically significant change in the burden of KD-ICH from 1990 to 2021, with EAPCs of 0.32 (95% CI: −0.17–0.81) for the ASMR and 0.17 (95% CI: −0.31–0.65) for the ASDR (shown in Table 1). In contrast, Central Europe exhibited the most pronounced declines, with EAPCs of −3.88 (95% CI: −4.14 to −3.61) for the ASMR and −4.07 (95% CI: −4.35 to −3.78) for the ASDR (shown in Table 1). In 2021, Oceania exhibited the highest burden of KD-ICH, with an ASMR of 11.37 (95% UI: 7.81–15.61) and an ASDR of 269.54 (95% UI: 192.12–369.87), followed by Southeast Asia (ASMR: 10.41 [95% UI: 7.41 to 13.47]; ASDR: 257.82 [95% UI: 187.10 to 329.68]) and central sub-Saharan Africa (ASMR: 10.07 [95% UI: 6.59 to 14.57]; ASDR: 233.72 [95% UI: 157.40 to 327.18]) (shown in Table 1). In comparison, Australasia reported the lowest ASMR (0.56; 95% UI: 0.34–0.79) in 2021, followed by Western Europe (0.67; 95% UI: 0.43–0.93) and high-income North America (0.98; 95% UI: 0.66–1.30) (shown in Table 1). The lowest ASDR of KD-ICH in 2021 were observed in high-income Asia Pacific (9.97; 95% UI: 6.82–13.37), Western Europe (12.36; 95% UI: 8.83–16.11), and high-income North America (21.41; 95% UI: 15.81–26.96) (shown in Table 1).

Nationally, the burden of KD-ICH decreased in most countries from 1990 to 2021. The most substantial reductions occurred in the Republic of Korea, Estonia, Hungary, Singapore, and Portugal, with EAPCs ranging from −5.36 to −7.71 for the ASMR and −5.12 to −7.65 for the ASDR (shown in Fig. 1c and Fig. 2c). In contrast to the global trend, several nations demonstrated stable or increasing burdens. Zimbabwe exhibited the most pronounced increase in burden, with EAPCs of 2.11 (95% CI: 1.48–2.74) for the ASMR and 2.37 (95% CI: 1.68–3.07) for the ASDR, followed by Lesotho, Mozambique, and Honduras, all of which showed similar increasing trends (shown in Fig. 1c and Fig. 2c). A stable burden trend was observed in Turkmenistan, the Philippines, Eswatini, Kenya, the Dominican Republic, South Africa, Sierra Leone, and Chad (shown in Fig. 1c and Fig. 2c). In 2021, China, India, the USA, Mexico, and Vietnam had the highest absolute KD-ICH mortality (ranging from 8,897 to 103,958 deaths) and DALYs (ranging from 215,082 to 2,213,573) (shown in Fig. 1a and Fig. 2a). The highest age-standardized KD-ICH burden was observed in Nauru in 2021, with an ASMR of 17.23 (95% UI: 12.25–23.75) and an ASDR of 470.05 (95% UI: 334.62–639.18) (shown in Fig. 1b and Fig. 2b). The Solomon Islands, Lesotho, the Marshall Islands, and the Central African Republic followed, with ASMRs ranging from 15.12 to 16.84 and ASDRs ranging from 368.08 to 403.97 (shown in Fig. 1b and Fig. 2b). Conversely, Iceland, Switzerland, and Andorra reported relatively low burdens in 2021, with ASMRs ranging from 0.40 to 0.44 and ASDRs ranging from 7.40 to 8.66 (shown in Fig. 1b and Fig. 2b).

An Inverse Association between the SDI and KD-ICH Burden Was Observed, with the Majority of the Burden Concentrated in Underdeveloped Regions

At the GBD regional level, both the ASMR (r = −0.715; p < 0.001) and the ASDR (r = −0.725; p < 0.001) for KD-ICH were significantly inversely correlated with the SDI between 1990 and 2021 (shown in Fig. 3a and Fig. 4a). On the basis of the fitted regression model, the observed burden exceeded expected levels in the Southeast Asia, Central Asia, East Asia, Oceania, and several sub-Saharan African regions, as well as in high-income Asia Pacific. Conversely, South Asia, multiple Latin American subregions, North Africa and the Middle East, Australasia, and Western Europe presented lower than expected burdens (shown in Fig. 3a and Fig. 4a).

At the national level in 2021, the relationship between the SDI and KD-ICH burden followed a nonlinear pattern: both the ASMR (r = −0.747; p < 0.001) and the ASDR (r = −0.762; p < 0.001) increased gradually as the SDI increased to approximately 0.37, beyond which the burden decreased sharply (shown in Fig. 3b and Fig. 4b). Between 1990 and 2021, the proportional burden of KD-ICH decreased in high- and high-middle-SDI regions but increased steadily in low-, low-middle-, and middle-SDI regions. Although the absolute gap in KD-ICH burden between the highest and lowest SDI countries has narrowed over the past 30 years, the persistently negative SII underscores persistent and pronounced health inequalities across SDI levels, disproportionately affecting lower-SDI regions. Discussion

This study comprehensively quantified the global burden and temporal trends of KD-ICH from 1990 to 2021, evaluated the distribution of burden and health disparities across SDI levels, and projected future trajectories through 2040. Despite a substantial increase in the absolute burden – reflected by an increase in deaths from 241,229 to 329,737 and DALYs from 6.23 million to 8.05 million – a notable decline in ASR was observed and is projected to continue. This analysis also revealed pronounced global health inequities, with approximately 75% of the KD-ICH burden concentrated in underdeveloped regions. Both the ASMR and the ASDR were strongly and inversely correlated with the SDI, underscoring the disproportionate impact on socioeconomically disadvantaged populations. While previous research has focused largely on the clinical link between chronic kidney disease (CKD) and ICH, few studies have systematically examined the global epidemiological patterns, long-term trends, and socioeconomic disparities specific to KD-ICH [5, 22]. By addressing this critical gap, our study offers the most comprehensive assessment to date, incorporating inequality metrics and forecasting future burden across SDI strata. These findings advance the recognition of KD-ICH as a distinct global health challenge and provide evidence to inform more equitable and targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

The marked increase in the absolute global burden of KD-ICH over the past 3 decades, primarily concentrated in underdeveloped regions, is likely driven by a combination of demographic shifts, epidemiologic transitions, and persistent healthcare disparities. Population growth and ageing have substantially expanded the number of individuals at risk, particularly in low- and middle-SDI regions, where the burden is rising most rapidly [23–25]. Additionally, the global increase in the CKD prevalence, largely fuelled by poorly controlled hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, has expanded the pool of individuals vulnerable to ICH [26–28]. In many low-SDI regions, limited access to routine CKD screening, effective blood pressure control, and timely stroke care further amplify this risk, resulting in higher mortality and long-term disability [29, 30]. Moreover, advances in diagnostic imaging and broader disease surveillance may also contribute to the rising detection of KD-ICH, especially in previously underreported regions [31, 32]. These converging factors not only explain the overall increase in deaths and DALYs but also highlight the disproportionate impact on socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.

The sustained decline in the ASMR and ASDR associated with KD-ICH, which is particularly evident in high- and high-middle-SDI regions, likely reflects cumulative advances in integrated public health strategies, clinical advancements, and robust health system infrastructure [33]. Widespread implementation of primary prevention programs targeting hypertension, diabetes, and obesity has led to earlier detection and more effective management of CKD, thereby reducing the population at highest risk for ICH [34–36]. Mature acute stroke systems, including stroke units, emergency response networks, and standardized care protocols, have significantly improved access to timely care and post-stroke outcomes [37, 38]. Additionally, the expanded use of advanced neuroimaging (e.g., CT and MRI) has facilitated earlier diagnosis and prompt intervention, particularly in resource-rich settings [39]. Furthermore, population-wide policies such as public education, sodium reduction initiatives, dietary sugar control, smoking cessation, and weight management promotion have helped reduce the incidence and severity of kidney and vascular comorbidities [40–43]. Emerging cardiorenal-protective therapies, particularly sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, are poised to reshape future treatment paradigms and disease trajectories, owing to their demonstrated efficacy in reducing stroke hospitalizations and attenuating renal function decline, especially among patients with underlying metabolic factors such as diabetes and hypertension, as substantiated by large-scale randomized trials [44, 45]. The past two decades have witnessed substantial reductions in anticoagulation-related bleeding, driven by the transition from warfarin to direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), a therapeutic advance that has likely contributed to the global decline in age-standardized KD-ICH burden. The COMBINE AF meta-analysis (2,025) demonstrated that standard-dose DOACs reduce ICH risk by 42–58% compared with warfarin across all CKD stages, with benefits persisting to a creatinine clearance of 25 mL/min [46, 47]. These improvements highlight the critical role of therapeutic innovation, health infrastructure, and policy implementation in alleviating the KD-ICH burden and highlight the persistent disparities faced by underdeveloped regions, where such interventions remain inconsistently implemented.

Notable sex-based disparities in the burden of KD-ICH were observed, with males consistently exhibiting higher mortality and DALY burden than females. This disproportionate burden likely reflects a complex interplay of biological, behavioural, and healthcare-related factors. Biologically, oestrogen in females exerts vascular protective effects through anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and endothelial-stabilizing pathways, potentially mitigating the impact of CKD-related cerebrovascular injury, particularly before menopause [48, 49]. Behaviourally, men are more frequently exposed to major modifiable risk factors including uncontrolled hypertension, smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, psychosocial stress, and poor adherence to medical care, which accelerate both CKD progression and the risk of ICH [50]. Clinically, sex-specific differences in renal disease progression have been reported, with men showing faster declines in kidney function and worse clinical outcomes [51]. However, women are more likely to experience delayed CKD diagnosis, reduced referral to nephrology care, underuse of guideline-recommended therapies, and a higher risk of adverse drug reactions [52]. To reduce the sex-based burden of KD-ICH, policy efforts should promote earlier CKD detection and better engagement in risk factor management among men, while addressing diagnostic delays and treatment gaps experienced by women. Gender-sensitive public health strategies and equitable access to nephrology and stroke care are essential to narrowing these disparities.

Age plays a pivotal role in shaping the burden of KD-ICH, with deaths and DALYs disproportionately concentrated on older adults. This pattern reflects both the cumulative impact of vascular comorbidities and the progressive decline in physiological reserve. The major vascular risk factors including hypertension, diabetes, and CKD increase in prevalence and severity with age, each of which is independently associated with ICH and collectively exerts synergistic effects [24]. Physiological renal decline with ageing leads to impaired filtration, endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and systemic inflammation, all of which amplify cerebrovascular fragility [53]. Concurrently, neurovascular changes including small vessel disease, cerebral microbleeds, and impaired autoregulation further increase susceptibility to haemorrhage, particularly in the presence of CKD [54]. Neuroimaging evidence has shown that older individuals carry a greater burden of white matter lesions, microbleeds, and silent infarctions, which are pathological substrates that translate to an increased risk of symptomatic ICH [55]. This biological vulnerability is compounded by clinical factors, such as atypical symptom presentation, cognitive impairment, frailty, and delays in recognition or treatment, which together worsen outcomes [56]. Age therefore functions not merely as a measure of exposure but also as an active amplifier of systemic vulnerability. These mechanisms explain the disproportionate KD-ICH burden borne by older populations and underscore the need for prevention, early detection, and management strategies that are explicitly tailored to the ageing population.

The disproportionate burden of KD-ICH in underdeveloped regions reflects entrenched inequities in healthcare infrastructure, health literacy, and environmental exposures, embedded within broader socioeconomic and cultural divides. Foundational systems for the prevention, early detection, and management of metabolic comorbidities, particularly hypertension and diabetes, are often absent or fragmented. Limited screening programs, weak primary care networks, and under-resourced referral pathways contribute to underdiagnosis and poor control of these key risk factors [57]. CKD, a critical intermediary in KD-ICH pathogenesis, is rarely detected in its early stages; and even when diagnosed, access to nephrology care and renal replacement therapy remains severely limited, particularly in low-resource settings [18]. For example, the benefits of DOACs have not been equitably distributed worldwide. Global surveys reveal that 29% of institutions in low- and middle-income countries lack DOAC access, compared with only 9% in high-income countries [58]. Acute stroke systems are similarly underdeveloped. Restricted access to neuroimaging, organized stroke units, and timely emergency response infrastructure results in delayed diagnosis and treatment, directly exacerbating mortality and long-term disability [59, 60]. These clinical deficiencies are compounded by low health literacy and limited public awareness, which delay symptom recognition and care-seeking [61, 62]. Environmental risks, including such as air pollution, exposure to nephrotoxins, and unsafe water sources, further exacerbate renal and cerebrovascular vulnerability in these regions [63, 64]. Collectively, these structural, clinical, and environmental inequities not only shape the global geography of KD-ICH burden but also underscore the urgency of equity-focused health system strengthening and targeted prevention strategies.

To effectively mitigate the disproportionate burden of KD-ICH in underdeveloped regions, a multilayered strategy that encompasses both national and global responses is needed. At the national and regional levels, strengthening the primary healthcare system is important. This includes expanding access to screening and longitudinal management of key metabolic comorbidities – particularly hypertension, diabetes, and CKD – that substantially elevate KD-ICH risk but remain underdiagnosed and poorly managed in many low-SDI regions because of limited infrastructure and fragmented care pathways [57, 65]. Early detection of CKD must be emphasized through systematic surveillance and integration into existing NCD programs, alongside improved access to nephrology care and scale-up of renal replacement therapies in low-resource regions [66]. Parallel investment in acute stroke systems is equally critical. Prehospital emergency response, neuroimaging capacity, and organized stroke units remain limited in many low-SDI regions, directly contributing to delayed diagnosis, higher mortality, and long-term disability [67, 68]. Efforts to improve health literacy through culturally tailored education programs can empower individuals to recognize symptoms, manage chronic conditions, and seek timely care [61]. At the global level, coordinated action is required to address structural inequities in kidney and cerebrovascular care. International agencies and donors should prioritize capacity building, technical assistance, and sustainable financing for chronic disease management in low-resource settings. Initiatives such as the WHO Global NCD Action Plan and the ISN Global Kidney Health Atlas provide frameworks to guide evidence-based interventions and track progress. Furthermore, expanding global data-sharing platforms, strengthening epidemiologic surveillance, and investing in implementation research will be critical to adapt proven strategies to local contexts. Collectively, these multi-tiered interventions not only hold promise for reducing KD-ICH burden in disadvantaged populations but also represent an essential pathway to advancing health equity worldwide.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the global burden, health inequalities, and projected trends of KD-ICH. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the accuracy of the estimates depends on the availability and quality of input data, which vary considerably across regions. In particular, data from low-SDI countries and conflict-affected areas may be subject to underreporting, incomplete case ascertainment, and diagnostic limitations, introducing uncertainty into the estimates. Second, due to data constraints, this study was unable to include incidence and prevalence, thereby limiting the assessment of the full spectrum of disease burden. Third, the GBD database does not currently provide risk-attributable burden estimates disaggregated by severity of kidney dysfunction (e.g., CKD stages G1–G5). Given that the risk of ICH and its poor outcomes increase progressively with declining renal function, our estimates – averaged across all CKD stages – may mask the true burden concentrated among those with more severe impairment. Future iterations of GBD should consider providing stage-stratified estimates to enhance clinical and policy relevance. Fourth, although the ARIMA-based projections offer valuable insights into future trends, they are predicated on the assumption that historical patterns will persist. This assumption may not hold in the context of rapidly evolving healthcare systems, epidemiologic transitions, or policy interventions. Fifth, our study did not assess the effectiveness of region-specific public health strategies, limiting the direct translation of findings into targeted policy recommendations. Future research should integrate high-quality, longitudinal clinical data and evaluate the impact of specific prevention and intervention programs. Moreover, investment in global surveillance infrastructure, particularly in data-scarce regions, will be essential to improve the accuracy, granularity, and policy relevance of KD-ICH burden estimates.

Conclusions

Our systematic assessment reveals that the global burden of KD-ICH is characterized by two opposing trends: a decline in age-standardized rates alongside a rising absolute burden, underscoring the tension between public health gains and aging populations. Furthermore, significant health disparities persist, with lower SDI regions bearing a disproportionate share of this burden. Addressing these persistent inequalities and mitigating the future impact of KD-ICH will require context-specific health strategies, equitable resource distribution, and reinforced global surveillance systems.
