How to see the forest for the trees
Erika Bérczi-Kovács, András Frank

Abstract
One of the major starting points of discrete optimization is the theorem of Nash-Williams and Tutte on the existence of k k disjoint spanning trees of a graph along with its counterpart on the existence of k k forests covering all edges of the graph. These elegant results triggered a comprehensive research that gave rise to far-reaching generalizations and found applications at seemingly far-fetched areas. There are well over a thousand papers in the literature, including quite a few brand-new ones. Our first goal is to enlighten some aspects and links of these developments with the hope that the melody finds its way to non-experts. But we hope that experts will also find some novelties in our orchestration.



1
Introduction

Graphs (directed, undirected, mixed, hyper-) are fundamental
mathematical tools for modelling problems of real-life networks. For
example, a GPS seeks to find the fastest/shortest path from one
location of a map to another.

For managing practical problems, the very first step is to capture
(define) formally a certain intuitive feature of the network. For
example, the feeling that a graph
G=(V,E)
with node-set
V
and
edge-set
E
‘consists of one piece/component’ or that
G
‘is connected’ can be formally defined by requiring that
V
cannot
be cut (partitioned) into two non-empty parts in such a way that no
edge connects the two parts. Another possibility is to require that
there is a path connecting any two nodes. These two formal
definitions of connectedness are easily seen to be equivalent.

A
tree

G=(V,T)
is a critically (or minimally) connected graph
in the sense that deleting any of its edges destroys connectivity.
This is equivalent to requiring that
G
is connected and includes no
circuit. Yet another equivalent definition is that
G
is connected
and every non-empty subset
X\subseteq V
of nodes is sparse in the
sense that
X
induces at most
|X|-1
edges. The
connectivity of a graph
G
is equivalent to requiring that
G

includes a spanning tree. A
forest
is a graph whose components
are trees. An equivalent definition is that the graph includes no
circuit, that is, the graph is sparse.

Node, edge, degree, path, circuit, loop, cut, tree, forest,
arborescence, branching, (connected) component: these are some of the
most important basic concepts of graph theory. An easy property is
that an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with an
n
-element node-set
V

and edge-set
E
admits an
st
-path (a path connecting two specified
nodes
s,t\in V
) if and only if there is no empty cut separating
s

and
t
, that is, the number
d_{G}(X)
of edges between
X
and
V-X

is positive whenever
s\in X\subseteq V-t
. There are simple
efficient algorithms for checking local connectivity (between nodes

s
and
t
) that either find an
s\overline{t}
-set
X
with
d_{G}(X)=0
or
find an
st
-path.
G
is called (globally) connected if there is a
path between any two nodes
s,t\in V
.

A basic graph optimization problem is finding a spanning tree of
G

which is cheapest with respect to a cost-function on
E
. A simple
greedy algorithm, due to Kruskal, does this job. By Dijkstra’s
classic algorithm, the problem of finding algorithmically a cheapest

st
-path is also efficiently manageable, at least for non-negative
cost-functions. However, the sensitivity of this class of problems is
nicely reflected by the fact that the cheapest (or shortest) path
problem is NP-hard for general cost-functions. On the positive side,
when negative components are allowed and only circuits with negative
total costs are forbidden, then there is a polynomial algorithm, which
relies, however, on deeper ideas.

The need for capturing intuitively more ‘massive/strong/solid’ connectivity concepts appeared as early as 1927, when Menger
characterized those graphs admitting
k
openly disjoint
st
-paths.
Here it is straightforward to figure out a natural necessary
condition: no
k-1
nodes (distinct from
s
and
t
) can block all

st
-paths. The deep part of Menger’s theorem is the proof of the
sufficiency of this condition.

Naturally, there are several other variants for extending connectivity
concepts. For example, a variation of Menger’s theorem is about the
existence of
k
edge-disjoint
st
-paths, which is often referred to
as the (undirected) edge-Menger theorem. These results gave rise to a
huge discipline: network flows (in directed graphs), in particular,
the max-flow min-cut theorem. It should be emphasized that in this
area it is rather typical that figuring out a necessary condition for
the existence of the investigated property is easy, and the essence is
finding a (possibly algorithmic) proof of sufficiency (see, for
example, Hall’s theorem on the existence of a perfect matching in a
bipartite graph, or Dilworth’s theorem on the partionability of a
poset into
k
chains).

Global connectivity has also been extended to higher connectivities.
A graph
G
is
k
-edge-connected
if
d_{G}(X)\geq k
whenever

\emptyset\subset X\subset V
, which is equivalent, by the edge-Menger
theorem, to requiring that there are
k
edge-disjoint
st
-paths for
every pair
\{s,t\}
of nodes.
G
is
k
-node-connected
if it
has at least
k+1
nodes and
G
remains connected after leaving out
any subset of less than
k
nodes.

On this way of possible extensions of connectivity concepts, a natural
question arises: what would be a right generalization of the above
mentioned trivial statement that a graph is connected if and only if
it has a spanning tree?

A graph is called
k
-tree-connected
if it contains
k

edge-disjoint spanning trees. Following the melody of the edge-Menger
theorem, one may surmise at first sight that a graph is

k
-edge-connected if and only if it is
k
-tree-connected. However,
this is plain wrong already for
k=2
, as is shown by a triangle.
This tiny example indicates that one must also require that the graph
should have at least
k\cdot(|V|-1)
edges. However, even
this strengthened necessary condition is not sufficient, and the
question arises: what is a necessary and sufficient condition for a
graph to be
k
-tree-connected?

For Menger (also for Hall or for Dilworth), it was easy to formulate a
necessary condition, for the problem of finding
k
disjoint spanning
trees, however, just figuring out a promising conjecture is already a
great challenge. A bit surprisingly, the answer was provided by two
authors, W.T. Tutte and C.St.J.A Nash-Williams, independently of each
other. Their papers appeared in the same issue (number 36) of the
Journal of London Mathematical Society, in 1961. The paper of Tutte
was submitted on April 29, 1960, and the paper of of Nash-Williams was
submitted on December 4, 1960.

Tutte writes on page 223 of his paper:

”Note. -Dr. C. St. J. A. Nash-Williams has sent me another proof of
Theorem I (obtained quite independently), which does not use the
concept of a multiple graph.”

Nash-Williams writes at the beginning of his paper:

”Tutte [3] proved a theorem equivalent to Theorem 1 of the present
paper. A few months later, the present author (unaware of Tutte’s
work) obtained a different proof, which is the object of this paper to
present.”

By using notation fitting to the present overview, Theorem I of Tutte
and Theorem 1 of Nash-Williams are as follows.

THEOREM 1.1
(Tutte
[
49
]
,
Theorem I
)

An undirected graph
G=(V,E)
includes
k
edge-disjoint spanning
trees if and only if

|F|\ \geq\ k\cdot(q(G,F)-1),

(1.1)

holds for every subset
F\subseteq E
of edges, where

q(G,F)
denotes the number of connected components of the subgraph of

G
induced by
E-F
.

We hasten to emphasize that Tutte proved this result actually in a
significantly more general form (Theorem II), see below Theorem

2.1
.

THEOREM 1.2
(Nash-Williams
[
42
]
,
Theorem 1
)

A graph
G
has
k
edge-disjoint spanning trees if
and only if

e_{G}({\cal P})\geq k\cdot(|{\cal P}|-1)

(1.2)

for every partition
\cal P
of
V(G)
, where
e_{G}({\cal P})
denotes the number of edges connecting distinct members of
\cal P
.

We assume throughout that the graphs are always loopless. It is also
assumed that a partition consists of non-empty sets and has at least
two members. In what follows, we shall refer to the edges connecting
distinct parts of a partition
\cal P
as
cross-edges
of
\cal P
.

In an equivalent formulation, the condition in the theorem is that the
number of cross-edges of every
q
-partite (
q=2,3,\dots,n
)
partition of
V(G)
is at least
k\cdot(q-1)
. Such graphs are
sometimes called
k
-partition-connected
.

It is seen immediately, without refering to the theorems themselves,
that the conditions in the two theorems are equivalent. Namely, the
Tutte condition may be viewed as a redundant form of the Nash-Williams
condition. In fact, even the latter one is redundant a bit since it
suffices to require (
1.2
) only for partitions where each
part induces a
(k+1)
-edge-connected subgraph.

The form of Theorem
1.2
immediately implies that a

2k
-edge-connected graph
G=(V,E)
is
k
-tree-connected and actually
it remains so after deleting an arbitrary subset
K
of at most
k

edges. Indeed, for a partition
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{q}\}
of
V
, the
number of cross-edges in
G^{\prime}:=(V,E-K)
is
\sum_{i}d_{G^{\prime}}(V_{i})/2\geq\sum_{i}d_{G}(V_{i})/2-k=q(2k)/2-k=k\cdot(q-1)
, and Theorem

1.2
can be applied to
G^{\prime}
in place of
G
.

The theorem of Tutte and Nash-Williams can be formulated in a concise
way, as follows.

THEOREM 1.3
(Tree-packing theorem of Tutte and Nash-Williams)

A graph
G
is
k
-tree-connected if and only if
G
is

k
-partition-connected.

For later generalization, it is useful to introduce a natural
extension of
k
-partition-connectivity
[
18
]
. Beside the
non-negative integer
k
, let
l
be a non-negative integer. A graph

G=(V,E)
is called
(k,l)
-partition-connected
if

e_{G}({\cal P})\geq k\cdot(|{\cal P}|-1)+l

(1.3)

holds for any partition
\cal P
of
V
. Obviously,

(k,0)
-partition-connectivity is the same as

k
-partition-connectivity,
(0,l)
-partition-connectivity is
equivalent to
l
-edge-connectivity, and

(k,k)
-partition-connectivity is
2k
-edge-connectivity, or more
generally, for
k\leq l
,
(k,l)
-partition-connectivity is equivalent
to
k+l
-edge-connectivity. Theorem
1.3
immediately shows for a
given non-negative integer
l
that a graph
G
remains

k
-tree-connected after leaving out any subset of
l
edges if and
only if
G
is
(k,l)
-partition-connected.

This observation on
2k
-edge-connected graphs has far-reaching
applications, for example, it served as a starting point for the
design of min cut algorithms
[
28
]
and remained the basis
for many others. Karger
[
28
]
observed that given a
maximum set of edge-disjoint spanning trees, a minimum cut intersects
at least one of them in at most 2 edges, thus to find a minimum cut it
is enough to find the minimum of those cuts containing at most 2 edges
from one of the given trees.

A natural counterpart of the disjoint-tree theorem investigates the
problem of decomposing the edge-set of a graph
G=(V,E)
into
k

forests, which is equivalent to cover
E
by
k
spanning trees when

G
is connected. This was solved by Nash-Williams in a (one-page
long) paper that also appeared in the Journal of London Math.
Society.

THEOREM 1.4
(Tree-covering theorem of Nash-Williams
[
43
]
)

The edge-set of a connected graph
G=(V,E)

can be covered by
k
spanning trees (or equivalently,
E
can be
partitioned into
k
forests) if and only if
G
is
k
-forest-sparse
(or just
k
-sparse) in the sense that

i_{G}(X)\leq k\cdot(|X|-1)

(1.4)

holds for every non-empty subset
X
of
V
, where
i_{G}(X)

denotes the number of edges induced by
X
.

Proof outline
The necessity is straightforward,
while sufficiency follows once we observe that
G
may be assumed to
be saturated in the sense that adding any new edge destroys

k
-sparsity. For such a graph the supermodularity of
i_{G}
implies
that
|E|=k\cdot(|V|-1)
in which case

k
-sparsity is just equivalent to
k
-partition-connectivity, and
then the tree-packing theorem implies the tree-covering theorem.

For integers
0\leq l<k
, we call a graph
G=(V,E)

(k,l)
-forest-sparse
if the graph obtained from
G
by adding any
set of
l
new edges is decomposable into
k
forests. By Theorem

1.4
of Nash-Williams, this is equivalent to requiring that

i_{G}(X)\leq k\cdot(|X|-1)-l

(1.5)

holds for every subset
X
of
V
with
|X|\geq 2
. In the literature (e.g.
[
33
]
) a graph is
called
(k,l)
-sparse if
i_{G}(X)\leq k\cdot|X|-l
holds for
every nonempty subset
X\subseteq V
(where
0\leq l\leq 2k-1
).
Clearly, a graph is
(k,l)
-forest-sparse if and only if it is

(k,k+l)
-sparse.

It should be noted that there is yet another closely related paper
that appeared in the Journal of London Math. Society, actually
earlier than the above-mentioned works of Nash-Williams and Tutte. A.
Horn proved the following.

THEOREM 1.5
(Horn
[
25
]
)

A (finite) set
S
of
vectors can be partitioned into
k
linearly independent subsets if
and only if

k\cdot r(X)\geq|X|

(1.6)

holds
for every subset
X
of
S
, where
r(X)
denotes the rank of
X
.

A bit surprisingly, the same theorem was published in the same journal
by R. Rado in 1962
[
44
]
, without referring to Horn’s
paper. (For a profound historic overview, see section 42.6f of
Schrijver’s book.
[
46
]
).

Actually, it suffices to require (
1.6
) only for closed and
non-separable subsets
X
. It is also known for a graph
G=(V,E)

that there is a matrix
M_{G}
whose columns correspond to the edges of

G
in such a way that a subset
X\subseteq E
is a forest if and only
if the set of columns of
M_{G}
corresponding to
X
is linearly
independent. These two observations show that Horn’s theorem implies
Theorem
1.4
.

These starting results of Nash-Williams and Tutte on trees and forests
triggered an immense advancement in various related areas: matroid
optimization, directed counterparts, hypergraph extensions. A rich
overview of the area can be found, for example, in the books of Frank

[
15
]
and Schrijver
[
46
]
, and there are
thousands of papers referring to the above-mentioned results of
Nash-Williams and Tutte. This interest has not quieted down in recent
times either. Google Scholar, for example, found more than 300 papers
of the past 15 years that cited the article
[
49
]
of Tutte.
Obviously, the present summary cannot be appropriate for providing a
general overview, and our goal is only to pick up and outline some of
the most exciting related results.

1.1
Some graph problems triggered by the tree-packing and
tree-covering theorems

We close this introductory session by a list of naturally emerging
questions related to the tree-packing and the tree-covering theorems,
which will be answered in the remaining parts. Some notions used
here are also defined later.

Problem 1.1

How can we find (algorithmically)
k

disjoint spanning trees, when they exist? How can we find a deficient
partition (that is, one violating (
1.2
)) when no
k

disjoint spanning trees exist? In the latter case, what is the
minimum number (total cost) of new edges whose addition makes the
graph
k
-tree-connected?
\bullet

Problem 1.2

Given
k
edge-disjoint forests

F_{1},\dots,F_{k}
of a graph
G=(V,E)
, when is it possible to extend
these forests (A) to
k
edge-disjoint spanning trees, (B) to

k
forests covering all edges? More generally, for given edge-sets

E_{1},\dots,E_{k}
with
F_{i}\subseteq E_{i}
, one may expect that the
extension of
F_{i}
be a subset of
E_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,k
).
\bullet

Problem 1.3

Given non-negative lower bounds

f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
and upper bounds
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}
, when do there
exist
k
disjoint forests
F_{1},\dots,F_{k}
in
G
for which
f_{i}\leq|F_{i}|\leq g_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,k
?
\bullet

Problem 1.4

What is the maximum cardinality
K
of
the union of
k
forests of
G
? Clearly,
K=|E|
precisely
when
G
can be decomposed into
k
forests, and
K=k\cdot(|V|-1)
when
G
includes
k
spanning trees. In an equivalent
formulation, what is the minimum number of new edges which are
parallel to existing ones, whose addition to
G
results in a

k
-tree-connected graph?
\bullet

Problem 1.5

Given a non-negative weight-function on

E
, what is the maximum total weight of the union of
k
forests?
Given a non-negative cost-function on
E
, what is the minimum total
cost of the union of
k
disjoint spanning trees?
\bullet

Problem 1.6

Given
k
cost-functions
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}
on
E
, find
k
disjoint spanning trees
F_{1},\dots,F_{k}

for which the total cost
\widetilde{c}_{1}(F_{1})+\widetilde{c}_{2}(F_{2})+\dots+\widetilde{c}_{k}(F_{k})
is minimum, where
\widetilde{c}_{i}(F_{i}):=\sum[c_{i}(e):e\in F_{i}]
.
\bullet

Problem 1.7

What are the counterparts of the
theorems of Nash-Williams and Tutte concerning hypergraphs?
\bullet

Problem 1.8

What are the counterparts of the
theorems of Nash-Williams and Tutte concerning directed and mixed
graphs?
\bullet

Problem 1.9

(A directed counterpart of the
second part of Problem
1.5
) For a given cost-function, how
can we compute a cheapest rooted
k
-arc-connected subgraph of a
digraph?
\bullet

Problem 1.10

(A directed counterpart of
Problem
1.6
) For given cost-function
c_{1},\dots c_{k}
, how
can we compute
k
arc-disjoint spanning arborescences
F_{1},\dots,F_{k}
with given root of a digraph for which
\widetilde{c}_{1}(F_{1})+\cdots+\widetilde{c}_{k}(F_{k})
is minimum?

2
Matroids in the background

As already indicated, Tutte actually proved the tree-packing theorem
in a more general form. He called a set of graphs
G_{1}=(V_{1},E,\varphi_{1})
, …,
G_{k}=(V_{k},E,\varphi_{k})
a
multiple graph
where

\varphi_{i}(e)=\{u,v\}
denotes the end-nodes of
e\in E
in
V_{i}
.

THEOREM 2.1
(Tutte
[
49
]
,
Theorem II.
)

Suppose that the graphs
G_{1}=(V_{1},E,\varphi_{1})
,
…,
G_{k}=(V_{k},E,\varphi_{k})
form a multiple graph. There exist

k
disjoint edge-sets
F_{1}\subseteq E,\dots,F_{k}\subseteq E
for
which
F_{i}
is a spanning tree of
G_{i}

(i=1,\dots,k)
if and only
if

|F|\ \geq\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}(q(G_{i},F)-1)

(2.1)

holds for every subset
F\subseteq E
of edges, where

q(G_{i},F)
denotes the number of (connected) components of the
subgraph
(V_{i},E-F,\varphi_{i})
of
G_{i}
induced by
E-F
.

This theorem rather easily implies, for example, a solution to Part
(A) of Problem
1.2
. A bit surprisingly, despite the numerous
works in the literature citing the paper
[
49
]
of Tutte,
only very few of them mention Theorem
2.1
. A reason of
this may be that the notion of Tutte’s multiple graph is not
particularly intuitive.

As it is so typical in mathematics, a phenomenon is more naturally
understandable in an appropriately generalized framework. In the
present case, this wonder framework is matroids. Already the paper

[
8
]
of Edmonds contains the following extension of
Theorem
1.4
of Nash-Williams.

THEOREM 2.2
(Edmonds
[
8
]
)

The
ground-set of a matroid
M=(S,r)
with rank function
r
can be
partitioned into
k
independent sets if and only if
k\cdot r(X)\geq|X|
holds for every subset
X\subseteq S
.

As noted after Theorem
1.5
of Horn, it suffices to require the
inequality only for closed and non-separable sets
X
. When
M
is a
graphic matroid, we are back at Nash-Williams’ theorem, and when
M

is a matroid representable over a field, we are back at Theorem

1.5
of Horn. This theorem immediately provides a
characterization of graphs whose edge-set can be partitioned into
k

forests, each consisting of at most
l
edges.

However, a straight extension of Tutte’s Theorem II to a matroidal
framework was described only in the fundamental paper

[
14
]
of Edmonds and Fulkerson, who proved the
following result (formulated here in our terms).

THEOREM 2.3
(Edmonds and Fulkerson
[
14
]
,
Theorem
2c
)

Let
M_{1},\dots,M_{k}
be matroids on a
common ground-set
S
. There is a sub-partition
\{B_{1},\cdots B_{k}\}

of
S
for which
B_{i}
is a basis of
M_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,k
) if and
only if

|X|\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}(X),

holds for every subset
X\subseteq S
, where
t_{i}
denotes
the co-rank function of
M_{i}
defined by

t_{i}(X):=\min\{|X\cap B|:B
a basis of
M_{i}\}.

After this theorem, Edmonds and Fulkerson explicitly mention that in
the special case when each
M_{i}
is a graphic matroid, Theorem

2.3
is nothing but Theorem II of Tutte (= Theorem
2.1
.

Edmonds and Fulkerson also proved a significant extension of Theorem

2.2
, which result may be viewed as the covering
counterpart of Theorem
2.3
.

THEOREM 2.4
(Edmonds and Fulkerson
[
14
]
,
Theorem
1c
)

Let
M_{1},\dots,M_{k}
be matroids on a
common ground-set
S
. There is a partition
\{I_{1},\cdots I_{k}\}
of

S
for which
I_{i}
is an independent set of
M_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,k
) if
and only if

|X|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}(X),

holds for every subset
X\subseteq S
, where
r_{i}
denotes
the rank function of
M_{i}
.

In the special case, when the matroids are graphic, this theorem
implies not only Theorem
1.4
but its extension as well
when, for given upper bounds
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}
, we want to find a
decomposition of
E
into forests
F_{1},\dots,F_{k}
for which
|F_{i}|\leq g_{i}
(
i=1,\dots,k
).

It should be emphasized that there are constructive proofs of the two
theorems of Edmonds and Fulkerson which give rise to polynomial
algorithms to compute the
k
independent sets or the
k
bases in
question.

These general matroidal results of Edmonds and Fulkerson are extremely
helpful in properly understanding the background of the tree-covering
and tree-packing theorems but the genuine recognition behind the real
power of the matroidal view is the following theorem of Edmonds from
1968.

THEOREM 2.5
(Matroid-sum theorem of Edmonds
[
10
]
,
Theorems 1 and 2
)

Let
M_{1}=(S,r_{1}),

\dots,M_{k}=(S,r_{k})
be
k
matroids on a common ground-set
S
, and let

{\cal F}_{i}
denote the set of independent sets of
M_{i}
. Let

{\cal F}_{\Sigma}:=\{F_{1}\cup\cdots\cup F_{k}\ :\ F_{i}\in{\cal F}_{i}\}.

(2.2)

Then
{\cal F}_{\Sigma}
forms the set of independent sets of
a matroid (called the sum or union of matroids
M_{1},\dots,M_{k}
). The
rank function
r_{\Sigma}
of
M_{\Sigma}
is as follows.

r_{\Sigma}(Z)=\min\{|Z-X|+\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}(X)\ :\ X\subseteq Z\}.

(2.3)

Finally, it is useful to recall that Kruskal’s algorithm has also been
extended to matroids to compute a maximum weight (or minimum cost)
basis of a matroid, see the paper
[
11
]
of Edmonds.

In this light, we can safely say that the theorems of Nash-Williams
and Tutte became the root and a major driving force of the huge area
of matroid optimization.

At this point, it is useful to remark, that a fundamental feature of
matroids is that the rank-function of a matroid is a submodular
function (that is,
r(X)+r(Y)\geq r(X\cap Y)+r(X\cup Y)
holds
whenever
X,Y\subseteq S
). We note that submodular functions (coming
from graphs) were already used by Tutte and Nash-Williams in their
proofs.

2.1
Some consequences on graphs

The matroidal background outlined above gave rise to solutions to the
first six problems listed in Section
1.1
. Here we mention
explicitly only one concrete extension of Theorem
1.3
that can
be derived from the matroid results.

THEOREM 2.6

For integers
k\geq 1
and
h\geq 0
, a graph

G=(V,E)
can be made
k
-tree-connected by adding at most
h
new
edges if and only if

e_{G}({\cal P})\geq k\cdot(|{\cal P}|-1)-h

(2.4)

holds for every partition
\cal P
of
V
.

By relying on the matroid-sum theorem of Edmonds, one can observe
that, for any spanning tree
(V,F)
of the complete graph on
V
, the

h
new edges in the theorem whose addition to
G
makes the graph

k
-tree-connected can be chosen to be parallel to some edges of
F
.
In particular, the new edges may be chosen in such a way that they
form a star (or that they are parallel to some edges of a spanning
tree of
G
).

For a partition
\cal P
, the value
(k(|{\cal P}|-1)-e_{G}({\cal P}))^{+}
is called the
k
-deficit
of
\cal P
,
where
x^{+}:=\max\{0,x\}
. We call the largest
k
-deficit over all
partitions of
V
the
k
-partition-deficiency
of
G
and denote
it by
\Pi_{k}(G)
.

Let us consider a partition
\cal P
with maximum
k
-deficit. It can
be shown that each node-set
V_{i}\in{\cal P}
induces a

k
-tree-connected graph, and the contraction of sets
V_{i}
gives a

k
-sparse graph. Jackson and Jordán
[
26
]
showed
that there is a unique partition
{\cal P}^{*}
among them with

|{\cal P}^{*}|
minimum (which they called the
brick-partition of
G
). These observations give an alternative proof
of the following theorem.

THEOREM 2.7

The minimum number of new edges whose addition to a graph

G=(V,E)
results in a
k
-tree-connected graph is
\Pi_{k}(G)
. The
maximum cardinality of the union of
k
spanning trees of a connected
graph
G
is
k(n-1)-\Pi_{k}(G)
.

To demonstrate how actively investigated this area is even nowadays,
we cite a recent theorem of Akrami, Raj and Végh
[
1
]

stating that if the ground-set
S
of a matroid
M
can be partitioned
into
k
bases, then, for any subset
Z
of
S
, there is a partition

\{B_{1},\dots,B_{k}\}
of
S
into bases which is
Z
-equitable in the
sense that
\lfloor{\frac{|Z|}{k}}\rfloor\leq|B_{i}\cap Z|\leq\lceil{\frac{|Z|}{k}}\rceil
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
For graphic matroids, this means that if a graph is decomposable into

k
spanning trees, then there is an equitable decomposition for any
subset of edges.

3
Hypergraphs and directed graphs

After exploring and understanding the matroidal background of the
tree-packing and tree-covering theorems, the question naturally
emerges: how can the connectivity concepts and theorems concerning
undirected graphs be extended to hypergraphs, to directed graphs and
to dypergraphs (
=
directed hypergraphs.)

3.1
Hypergraphs

Let
H=(V,{\cal E})
be a hypergraph with node-set
V
where
\cal E

denotes the set of hyperedges. We assume throughout that each
hyperedge has at least two elements. By
trimming
a hyperedge

X
to a graph-edge, we mean the operation of deleting
|X|-2
elements of
X
, or in other words, replacing
X
with a
graph-edge connecting two elements of
X
.

We say that a hypergraph
H
is a
hyperforest
if its hyperedges
can be trimmed to a forest. Lovász
[
37
]
proved that
H

is a hyperforest if and only if the union of any
j>0
hyperedges has
at least
j+1
elements. A
spanning hypertree
is a hyperforest
having exactly
|V|-1
hyperedges.

A natural extension of the notion of graph connectivity is as follows.

H
is
connected
if
d_{H}(X)\geq 1
for every non-empty proper
subset
X
of
V
, where
d_{H}(X)
is the number of hyperedges
intersecting both
X
and
V-X
.
H
is
partition-connected
if,
for every partition
\cal P
of
V
, the number of hyperedges
intersecting at least two members of
\cal P
is at least
|{\cal P}|-1
. While these two connectivity concepts are equivalent for
graphs, a hypergraph on a 3-element node-set
V
with a single
hyperedge
V
is connected but not partition-connected.

THEOREM 3.1
(Lorea
[
35
]
)

The hyperforests of a hypergraph
H=(V,{\cal E})
form the independent sets of a matroid on the ground-set

\cal E
of hyperedges.

A matroid arising in this way is called the
hypergraphic
matroid
of
H
.

THEOREM 3.2
(Whiteley
[
51
]
)

The rank of
the hypergraphic matroid of a hypergraph
H=(V,{\cal E})
is equal to

\min\{|V|-|{\cal P}|+e_{H}({\cal P}):{\cal P}\hbox{ a partition of}\ V\}

where
e_{H}({\cal P})
denotes the number of hyperedges
intersecting at least two members of the partition.

This implies that a hypergraph
H=(V,{\cal E})
is partition-connected
if and only if the rank of its hypergraphic matroid is
|V|-1
. The following theorem may be considered as a straight
generalization of the tree-packing and tree-covering theorems of Tutte
and Nash-Williams (Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
) to
hypergraphs.

THEOREM 3.3
(Frank, Király, Kriesell
[
18
]
)

Let
H=(V,{\cal E})
be a hypergraph.

(A)

\cal E
can be decomposed into
k
spanning
partition-connected subhypergraphs (or, equivalently,
H
includes
k

disjoint spanning hypertrees) if and only if
H
is

k
-partition-connected.

(B)

\cal E
can be decomposed into
k
hyperforests if
and only if we have

i_{\cal E}(X)\leq k\cdot(|X|-1)

for every non-empty
subset
X
of
V
, where
i_{\cal E}(X)
denotes the number of
hyperedges included in
X
.

This theorem provides a solution to Problem
1.7
. As for
algorithmic aspects, see the paper
[
3
]
of Baïou
and Barahona. Note that the problem of decomposing a hypergraph into

k
connected hypergraphs is
NP
-hard already for
k=2

[
18
]
.

3.2
Directed and mixed graphs

A directed tree is an
arborescence
or
r_{0}
-arborescence

if it has a node
r_{0}
of in-degree 0 (called the root-node) and the
in-degree of every other node is one. The union of node-disjoint
arborescences is a
branching
. A digraph
D=(V,A)
is called

root-connected
(from a root-node
r_{0}\in V
) if the in-degree

\varrho_{D}(X)
of every non-empty set
X\subseteq V-r_{0}
is positive.
This is equivalent to requiring that
D
has a spanning

r_{0}
-arborescence, or that every node of
D
is reachable from
r_{0}

along a directed path.
D
is
rooted
k
-arc-connected
if

\varrho_{D}(X)\geq k
for every non-empty set
X\subseteq V-r_{0}
.
This is equivalent (by Menger) to requiring that there are
k

arc-disjoint
r_{0}v
-dipaths for each
v\in V-r_{0}
.

The following fundamental theorem of Edmonds may be viewed as the
directed counterpart of the tree-packing theorem of Tutte and
Nash-Williams.

THEOREM 3.4
(Arborescence-packing theorem of Edmonds
[
12
]
)

(Weak form)
A digraph
D=(V,A)
with a
root-node
r_{0}
contains
k
arc-disjoint spanning
r_{0}
-arborescences
if and only if
D
is rooted
k
-arc-connected.
(Strong form)
Let
(V,A_{1}),\dots,(V,A_{k})
be
k
arc-disjoint
r_{0}
-arborescences
of
D
, and let
A_{0}:=A-(A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{k})
. They can be
extended to
k
arc-disjoint spanning arborescences if and only if

\varrho_{A_{0}}(X)
plus the number of arborescences
A_{i}
entering

X
is at least
k
for every non-empty subset
X\subseteq V-r_{0}
.

Lovász
[
38
]
found a stunningly short and simple proof of
this theorem, but a major difference between the directed and the
undirected cases is that, unlike the tree-packing theorem of Tutte and
Nash-Williams, no matroidal result is known that implies Edmonds’
theorem.

We show how Edmonds’ theorem helps solve Problem

1.9
. Let
c
be a non-negative cost-function on
the arc-set of an
r_{0}
-rooted
k
-arc-connected digraph
D=(V,A)

with no arc entering
r_{0}
. Let
G=(V,E)
denote the undirected graph
obtained from
D
by de-orienting each arc. Let
M_{1}
be the matroid
on
A
in which a set of arcs is a basis of the corresponding set of
edges in
G
is the union of
k
disjoint trees (that is,
M_{1}
may be
viewed as
k
times the graphic matroid of
G
). Let
M_{2}
be a
partition matroid on
A
in which a subset of arcs is a basis if the
in-degree of every non-root node is
k
. By relying on Theorem

3.4
of Edmonds, it is not difficult to see for a subset

J\subseteq A
of arcs that the subgraph
(V,J)
of
D
is a minimally
(with respect to arc deletion) rooted
k
-arc-connected digraph if and
only of it is a common basis of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
. Therefore an
algorithm for computing a minimum cost common basis of two matroids
can be applied
[
13
]
.

An essential difference between the directed and the undirected cases
is that, while there is an efficient algorithm (via matroids) for
solving Problem
1.6
, its directed counterpart in Problem

1.10
is NP-hard already for
k=2
.

As far as the directed counterpart of Nash-Williams tree-covering
theorem is concerned, there are two results.

THEOREM 3.5
(Vidyasankar
[
50
]
)

Let
r_{0}
be a root-node of
a digraph
D=(V,A)
such that no arc enters
r_{0}
. The arc-set of
D

can be covered by
k
spanning
r_{0}
-arborescences if and only if
(a)

\varrho_{D}(v)\leq k
for every
v\in V-r_{0}
and

(b)

k-\varrho_{D}(X)\leq\sum[k-\varrho_{D}(v):v\in\Gamma^{-}(X)]
holds for every non-empty set
X\subseteq V-r_{0}
where
\Gamma^{-}(X)
denotes the set of heads of the arcs
entering
X
.

THEOREM 3.6
(Frank
[
17
]
)

The arc-set of a digraph
D=(V,A)

can be covered by
k
branchings if and only if
(a)

the in-degree of each node is at most
k
and
(b)

i_{D}(X)\leq k\cdot(|X|-1)
holds for every set
\emptyset\subset X\subseteq V
, where
i_{D}(X)
denotes the number of arcs
induced by
X
.

It is possible to merge the tree-packing and the arborescence-packing
theorems. A
mixed graph

M=(V,A+E)
is a graph with possible
directed and undirected edges. A mixed
r_{0}
-arborescence is a mixed
tree in which it is possible to orient the undirected edges to get
an arborescence
[
17
]
.

THEOREM 3.7
(
[
17
]
)

In a mixed graph
M=(V,A+E)
with root-node

r_{0}
, there are
k
edge-disjoint spanning mixed
r_{0}
-arborescences
if and only if
e_{G}({\cal P})\geq\sum[k-\varrho_{A}(V_{i}):\ i=1,\dots,q]
holds for every partition
{\cal P}=\{V_{0},V_{1},\dots,V_{q}]
of
V
, where
r_{0}\in V_{0}
and
e_{G}({\cal P})
denotes the number of edges from
E
connecting distinct parts of

\cal P
.

Note that these results provide an answer to Problem
1.8
.

3.3
Dypergraphs

Analogously to digraphs, one may consider directed hypergraphs or
dypergraphs. A
dyperedge
(directed hyperedge)
(Z,z)
is a pair
of a set
Z
with
|Z|\geq 2
and an element
z
of
Z
,
where
z
is called the
head
of
Z
while the remaining elements
of
Z
are its
tails
. A dyperedge
(Z,z)
is said to enter a
set
X\subset V
if
z\in X
and
Z-X
is non-empty. A dypergraph
D=(V,{\cal A})
consists of a node-set
V
and a family
{\cal A}
of
dyperedges. The in-degree
\varrho_{D}(X)
of a subset
X\subseteq V

is the number of dyperedges entering
X
.

Given a root-node
r_{0}
, we say that a dypergraph is
out-rooted

k
-arc-connected
if the in-degree of every non-empty set
X\subseteq V-r_{0}
is at least
k
. When
k=1
,
D
is out-rooted arc-connected.
The dypergraphic extension of Edmonds’ arborescence-packing theorem is
as follows.

THEOREM 3.8
(
[
18
]
)

Suppose that every dyperedge of a
dypergraph
D=(V,{\cal A})
has at least two elements. Let
r_{0}\in V
be a given root-node. Then
D
can be decomposed into
k

out-rooted arc-connected dypergraphs if and only if
D
is out-rooted

k
-arc-connected.

4
Orienting graphs and hypergraphs

4.1
Graphs

Orienting an undirected edge
e=uv
means that we replace
e
with one
of the two directed edges (
=
arcs)
uv
and
vu
. Orienting a
hyperedges
Z
means that we replace
Z
by a dyperedge
(Z,z)
for
some
z\in Z
.

Graph (hypergraph) orientation problems serve as a bridge between
directed graphs and undirected graphs (hypergraphs). For example, the
weak form of Nash-Williams’ orientation theorem
[
41
]
states
that a graph has a
k
-arc-connected orientation if and only if
G
is

2k
-edge-connected. A general graph orientation result

[
16
]
implies the following.

THEOREM 4.1

A graph
G
has a rooted

k
-arc-connected orientation if and only if
G
is

k
-partition-connected.

On one-hand, if
G
is
k
-partition-connected, then, by Theorem

1.3
, there are
k
disjoint spanning trees, and we can orient
them separately to obtain
k
disjoint spanning arborescences. On the
other hand, this orientation theorem and the disjoint arborescence
theorem of Edmonds immediately implies Theorem
1.3
.

For non-negative integers
k
and
l
, a digraph or a dypergraph
D

on node-set
V
with a specified root-node
r_{0}\in V
is
rooted

(k,l)
-arc-connected
if
\varrho_{D}(X)\geq k
and
\varrho_{D}(V-X)\geq l
whenever
\emptyset\subset X\subseteq V-r_{0})
.

THEOREM 4.2
(
[
16
]
)

For non-negative integers

k
and
l
, an undirected graph
G
with a root-node
r_{0}
has a
rooted
(k,l)
-arc-connected orientation if and only if
G
is

(k,l)
-partition-connected.

Incidentally, we note that the concept of
k
-edge-connectivity is
simpler than
k
-tree-connectivity. However, if we want a concise
certificate for a graph to be
k
-edge-connected, then the simplest
one (so far) is a set of
k
edge-disjoint
r_{0}v
paths for every node

v\in V-r_{0}
. But there is a more concise certificate implied by Theorem
4.1
.

THEOREM 4.3

A graph
G=(V,E)
is
k
-edge-connected if and only if, for
an arbitrarily specified node
r_{0}\in V
, the digraph
D=(V,A)

arising from
G
by replacing each edge by two oppositely directed
parallel edges contains
k
disjoint spanning arborescences of root

r_{0}
.

4.2
Hypegraphs

As for orientation problems of hypergraphs are concerned, one has the
following.

THEOREM 4.4
(
[
18
]
)

(A)

A
hypergraph
H=(V,{\cal E})
has a
k
-arc-connected orientation if and
only if
e_{H}({\cal P})\geq k\cdot|{\cal P}|
holds for
every partition
\cal P
of
V
, where
e_{H}({\cal P})
denotes the
number of hyperedges intersecting at least two members of
\cal P
.

(B)

H
has an out-rooted
k
-arc-connected orientation
(with respect to a specified root-node) if and only if
H
is

k
-partition-connected.

A trivial observation is that a digraph is rooted

(k,l)
-arc-connected if and only if the digraph obtained by reversing
all arcs is
(l,k)
-arc-connected. Because of the asymmetric role of
tails and heads in a dypergraph, this observation does not extend to
dypergraph. For example a dypergraph on node-set
V:=\{r_{0},s,t\}

having the single hyperedge
V
has a
(0,1)
-partition-connected
orientation but it has no
(1,0)
-partition-connected orientation.

In a paper of Guo et al.
[
24
]
a hypergraph
H
is called

h
-weakly-partition-connected
if
\sum[d_{\cal P}(Z)-1:\ Z\in{\cal E}]\geq h\cdot(|{\cal P}|-1)
for every
partition
\cal P
of
V
, where
d_{\cal P}(Z)
denotes the number of
members of
\cal P
intersecting
Z
. By relying on this
concept, they formulated a general conjecture concerning Reed-Solomon
codes. The question whether there is a good characterization of this
property is answered in a more general form by the following result.

THEOREM 4.5
(
[
19
]
)

Let
H=(V,{\cal E})

be a hypergraph with a root-node
r_{0}
, and let
k
and
l
be
non-negative integers.

(A)

In case
l\leq k
,
H
has a
(k,l)
-arc-connected
orientation if and only if

e_{H}({\cal P})\geq k\cdot(|{\cal P}|-1)+l

(4.1)

holds for every partition
\cal P
of
V
. In the special
cases when
l=k
or
l=0
, we are back at Parts (A) and (B),
respectively, of Theorem
4.4
.

(B)

In case
l>k
,
H
has a
(k,l)
-arc-connected
orientation if and only if (
4.1
) holds and

\sum_{Z\in{\cal E}}[d_{\cal P}(Z)-1]\geq l\cdot(|{\cal P}|-1)+k

(4.2)

holds for every partition
\cal P
of
V
, where
d_{\cal P}(Z)
denotes the number of members of
\cal P
intersecting
Z
. In the special case when
k=0
, we obtain that
H
has an orientation in which the out-degree of every non-empty subset
of
V-r_{0}
is at least
l
if and only if
H
is

l
-weakly-partition-connected.

We remark that the special case
k=0
of Part (B) (for characterizing

l
-weakly-partition-connected hypergraphs) was applied in a recent
paper of Alrabiah et al.
[
2
]
in the theory of
Reed-Solomon codes.

The proof of Theorem
4.5
gives rise to a polynomial
algorithm that finds either a
(k,l)
-arc-connected orientation or a
partition
\cal P
violating the conditions.

5
Constructive characterizations

By a constructive characterization of a graph with a certain property,
we mean a simple construction to build up the graph with the given
property step by step. For example, a graph is connected if and only
if it can be built up from a node by adding consecutively new edges
for which at least one end-node is an already existing nodes.
Constructive characterizations may be useful for deriving theorems
concerning the graph property in question.

THEOREM 5.1
(Lovász
[
36
]
)

An undirected graph is

2k
-edge-connected if and only if it can be built up from an initial
node by consecutively applying the following two operations.

(A) Add a new edge (possibly a loop) connecting two
existing nodes.

(B) Pinch together
k
existing edges with a new node.

Note that this constructive characterization immediately implies a
theorem of Nash-Williams stating that a
2k
-edge-connected graph has
a
k
-arc-connected orientation.

THEOREM 5.2
(Mader
[
39
]
)

A digraph is

k
-arc-connected if and only if it can be built up from an initial
node by consecutively applying the following two operations.

(A) Add a new arc (possibly a loop) connecting two
existing nodes.

(B) Pinch together
k
existing arcs with a new node.

This result rather easily implies the following.

THEOREM 5.3

A digraph
D=(V,A)
with a root-node

r_{0}
is rooted
k
-arc-connected if and only if it can be built up
from
r_{0}
by consecutively applying the following two operations.

(A) Add a new arc (possibly a loop) connecting two
existing nodes.

(B) Add a new node
z
along with
k
arcs entering
z

for which their tails are existing nodes.

(C) Pinch together
j

(0<j\leq k)
existing arcs with
a new node
z
and add
k-j
new arcs entering
z
whose tails are
existing nodes.

By combining Theorems
5.3
and
4.1
,
one obtains the following.

THEOREM 5.4

An undirected graph
G
is
k
-partition-connected if and
only if it can be built up from a node by consecutively applying the
following operations.

(A) Add a new edge (possibly a loop) connecting two
existing nodes.

(B) Add a new node
z
along with
k
edges connecting
z

with existing nodes.

(C) Pinch together
j
existing edges (
0<j\leq k
) with a
new node
z
and add
k-j
(possibly parallel) new edges connecting

z
with existing nodes.

This result rather easily implies the tree-packing theorem of Tutte
and Nash-Williams (Theorem
1.3
). A significant extension of
Theorem
5.3
is as follows.

THEOREM 5.5
(Kovács and Végh
[
30
]
)

Let
0\leq l\leq k-1
be integers. A digraph
D

is
(k,l)
-arc-connected with respect to a root-node
r_{0}
if and only
if
D
can be built up from
r_{0}
by consecutively applying the
following operations.

(A) Add a new arc connecting two existing nodes.

(B) Pinch together
j
existing arcs (
l\leq j\leq k-1
)
with a new node
z
and add
k-j
(possibly parallel) new arcs
entering
z
for which their tails are existing nodes.

By combining Theorems
4.2
and
5.5
, one gets the following.

THEOREM 5.6

Let
0\leq l<k
be integers.
An undirected graph
G
is
(k,l)
-partition-connected if and only if
it can be built up from a node by consecutively applying the following operations.

(A) Add a new edge (possibly a loop) connecting two
existing nodes.

(B) Pinch together
j
existing edges (
l\leq j\leq k
)
with a new node
z
and add
k-j
(possibly parallel) new edges
connecting
z
with existing nodes.

The proof of this theorem is deep and difficult, however, a
significantly simpler proof is available for its special case
l=1
in
the paper of Frank and Szegő
[
20
]
. This special case
has an interesting application in rigidity theory, see Section
6
.

6
Tree-connectivity in rigidity theory

Tree-connectivity finds applications in seemingly distant areas such
as rigidity theory. Rigidity examines the stability of structures
modeled as graphs embedded in the Euclidean space with straight-line
edges. The primary goal is to determine whether these structures
(called frameworks) can be deformed without changing the edge lengths.
If such a deformation does not exist, the framework is called rigid.
In a bar-and-joint framework, nodes of a graph (the joints) are
embedded in a
d
-dimensional space, where the edges (the bars) have
fixed lengths. Intuitively, such a framework is rigid if a continuous deformation of the framework preserves pairwise distances of the nodes. An embedding of a graph is generic if all coordinates of
the node positions are algebraically independent over the rational
numbers. Rigidity is a generic property
[
51
]
, and in
some cases, generic rigid graphs can be characterized by sparsity.
Here we call a graph
G=(V,E)

(k,l)
-forest-tight if it is

(k,l)
-forest-sparse and
|E|=k\cdot(|V|-1)-l
.
Laman
[
32
]
proved that
(2,1)
-forest-tight graphs are the
generic minimally rigid graphs for bar-and-joint frameworks in the
plane, and a graph is rigid in the plane if and only if it contains a

(2,1)
-forest-tight subgraph.

A body-and-bar framework is similar to a bar-and-joint framework,
except the nodes of a graph represent full-dimensional bodies that are
connected by fixed-length bars such that the attachment points of the
bars on the bodies are pairwise distinct. Tay showed
[
47
]

that a multi-graph (a graph with possibly parallel edges but no loops)
can be realized by a rigid body-bar framework in dimension
d
if and
only if it contains
d(d+1)/2
edge-disjoint spanning trees.
Informally, a framework is globally rigid if for every other embedding
with the same bar lengths also the distances of every pair of nodes
are the same. A graph
G
is highly
k
-tree-connected if
e_{G}({\cal P})\geq k\cdot(|{\cal P}|-1)+1
. There is also a
strong relation between generic global rigidity of body-bar frameworks
and highly tree-connected graphs. A framework is called redundantly
rigid if it is rigid and remains rigid after the removal of any edge.
For a multi-graph
H
, a so-called body-bar graph
G_{H}
can be
associated with
H
. (Its definition can be found for example in

[
6
]
.)

Connelly et al.
[
6
]
showed that the following are equivalent:
(a)
G_{H}
is generically globally rigid in
{\bf R}^{d}
, (b)
G_{H}

is generically redundantly rigid in
{\bf R}^{d}
, (c)
H
is highly

\binom{d+1}{2}
-tree-connected. Their proof relies on the
constructive characterization of highly
k
-tree-connected graphs,
that is,
(k,1)
-partition-connected graphs (see Theorem

5.6
for the special case
l=1
). We note that
generic globally rigid body-hinge frameworks can also be characterized
by highly tree-connected graphs
[
27
]
.

We saw above that connectivity and constructive characterizations are
closely related, and the latter turned out to be a useful tool in
rigidity. Interestingly, this connection is bidirectional: recent
results in rigidity were the key to prove some long-standing
conjectures about connectivity. Kriesell conjectured that a graph
with high node-connectivity can always be partitioned into a spanning
tree and a
k
-connected subgraph
[
40
]
. His conjecture has
recently been proven by Garamvölgyi et al.
[
23
]
. They
actually prove a stronger statement about subgraphs with higher
dimensional rigidity.

THEOREM 6.1
(Garamvölgyi, Jordán, Király, Villányi
[
23
]
)

Every
(t\cdot 10d(d+1))
-connected graph contains
t
edge-disjoint
d
-rigid (and hence

d
-connected) spanning subgraphs.

They use a probabilistic approach in their proof. We note that with
this tool they also answer an old conjecture of Thomassen

[
48
]
about highly connected orientations.

THEOREM 6.2
(Garamvölgyi, Jordán, Király, Villányi
[
23
]
)

Every
(320\cdot k^{2})
-connected graph has a
k
-connected orientation.

For a more detailed description of these recent results see

[
7
]
.

7
Switching games

Shannon (see
[
22
]
) introduced a two-person game on connected
undirected graphs with two specified nodes
s
and
t
. The players,
Short and Cut, alternately tag an untagged edge, as long as there are
untagged edges. Short wins if she tagged all edges of an
st
-path,
and Cut wins if she tagged all edges of a cut separating
s
and
t
.

A special case of the switching game is the Bridge-It game of Gale
played on a square grid
[
21
]
. A matroidal extension is due
to Lehman
[
34
]
while Edmonds
[
9
]
described
a general framework to explore the link between Lehman’s results and
the theorem
1.3
of Nash-Williams and Tutte.

For technical simplicity, here we discuss a version of Shannon’s
switching game, where no specified nodes
s
and
t
are designated,
Short wins if she tagged all the edges of a spanning tree of
G
and
Cut wins if she tagged all the edges of a cut of
G
. Clearly, at
most one of the two players can win, and an immediate observation
shows that one of them will definitely win.

Actually, there are two variants of the game, depending on who the
first player is. Since these two variants are close to each other, we
assume that Cut tags first.

THEOREM 7.1

Suppose that
G=(V,E)
is a connected graph with
|V|\geq 3
.

(A)

There is a winning strategy for Short if
and only if
G
is 2-tree-connected.

(B)

There is a winning strategy for Cut if and
only if
V
has a deficient partition
{\cal P}=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{q}\}

for
k=2
, that is, one for which
e_{G}({\cal P})<2q-2.

Proof.
(outline) By Theorem
1.3
,
G
is 2-tree-connected if and
only if there is no deficient partition. Therefore, exactly one of
the two configurations can occur.

If
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are two disjoint spanning trees, and Cut tags an
edge
e
, say, from
F_{1}
, then Short should tag an edge
f\in F_{2}

that connects the two components of
F_{1}-e
. (If
e
is not in
F_{1}\cup F_{2}
, then any edge
f
will do). Now the graph
G^{\prime}
arising from

G-e
by contracting
f
is also 2-tree-connected, and by using
iteratively this approach, Short can win.

If there is a deficient partition
{\cal P}
, a winning strategy for
Cut is that she always tags an untagged cross-edge of
\cal P
. After
her first move, there are at most
2q-4
untagged cross-edges. During
the entire game Short can tag at most half of these edges, but every
spanning tree contains at least
q-1
cross-edges, therefore Short
will loose, and Cut wins.

It is not difficult to formulate an analogous theorem for the
case when Short tags first. Roughly, in this case, there is a winning
strategy for Cut if and only if there is a partition with
2
-deficit
at least 2 (which is equivalent to requiring that every two spanning
trees have at least two edges in common), and there is a winning
strategy for Short if and only if there are two spanning trees with at
most one edge in common (which is equivalent to requiring that the
2-deficiency of
G
is at most 1).

For the original Shannon switching game (for cutting or connecting
s

and
t
) when Cut is again the first player, one has the following.

THEOREM 7.2

(A)

There is a
winning strategy for Short if and only if there exists a subset
U
of
nodes containing
s
and
t
for which
G|U
is 2-tree-connected,
where
G|U
denotes the restriction of
G
to
U
.

(B)

There is a winning strategy for Cut if and
only if
V
has a partition
{\cal P}=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{q}\}
separating

s
and
t
such that the graph arising from
G
by contracting each

V_{i}
to a single node can be decomposed into two forests
F_{1}
and

F_{2}
where
s
and
t
belong to distinct components of
F_{1}
.

This theorem is a consequence of Edmonds’
[
9
]
general
results concerning games on matroids. From an algorithmic aspect, it
is useful mentioning that if the unique maximum
2
-deficit partition

{\cal P}^{*}
ensured by Jackson and Jordán

[
26
]
(that is the brick partition of
G
) happens
to separate
s
and
t
, then
{\cal P}^{*}
will meet the
requirements in Part (B).

Theorem
7.2
gives a characterization of the winning
cases of player Cut when she starts the game. However, the result
itself does not provide an algorithm for deciding whether property (A)
or (B) holds. It can be decided by finding a pair of maximally
disjoint spanning trees
[
4
]
. The approach of
Bruno and Weinberg
[
5
]
uses the structural
result of Kishi and Kajitani
[
29
]
on maximally
disjoint trees, which is motivated by an application in electrical
networks, where the above distances of the spanning trees correspond
to certain degrees of freedom of a given electric network

[
31
,
45
]
.
