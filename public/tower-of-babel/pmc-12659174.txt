Efficacy and safety of stereotactic radiosurgery for hypothalamic hamartomas: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bardia Hajikarimloo, Salem M Tos, Mohammadamin Sabbagh Alvani, Alireza Kooshki, Ibrahim Mohammadzadeh, Amir Hesam Zare, Amir Hosein Zare, Ali Mortezaei, Ehsan Bahrami Hezaveh, Azin Ebrahimi, Mohammad Amin Habibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypothalamic hamartomas (HHs) are congenital, non-neoplastic tumors originating from the hypothalamic region. While surgical resection remains the standard treatment, it is associated with substantial morbidity, leading to the exploration of minimally invasive approaches such as radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RFTC), laser interstitial thermal therapy (LiTT), and stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS). This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluate the efficacy and safety of SRS in managing HHs, with a focus on seizure control, endocrine function, and treatment-related complications.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted on December 6, 2024, using PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science. Studies that evaluated the role of SRS in HH patients were included. The R program was used to calculate the pooled estimates.ResultsSeven studies with 152 HH patients were included. The meta-analysis revealed a pooled post-SRS seizure improvement rate of 77% (95% CI: 61%-91%) and a seizure-free status rate of 48% (95% CI: 19%-78%). In addition, the meta-analysis showed a pooled good outcome, Engel I/II, rate of 67% (95% CI: 48%-84%), and a pooled adverse radiation effect (ARE) rate of 0% (95% CI: 0%-1%).ConclusionSRS provides favorable seizure control and a promising safety profile for managing patients with HH.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-025-02754-0.Keywords: Radiosurgery, Hypothalamic hamartoma, Seizure, Meta-analysis, Adverse radiation effect

Introduction

Hypothalamic hamartomas (HHs) are congenital, non-neoplastic, non-progressive lesions arising in the hypothalamic region [1]. HHs are rare lesions with a prevalence of one case per 100,000 individuals [1]. Most cases occur sporadically; however, 5% of cases are associated with Pallister-Hall syndrome, which results from a mutation in the GLI3 gene [2]. HHs are correlated with gelastic seizures, cognitive impairments, and endocrinological dysfunctions that severely impact the quality of life of the patients [1]. HHs are classified into two clinical phenotypes: intrahypothalamic and parahypothalamic [1]. The intrahypothalamic HHs are typically drug-resistant and associated with epilepsy concurrent with developmental regression, psychiatric impairment, cognitive dysfunction, and precocious puberty [1]. Parahypothalamic HHs primarily manifest as endocrinological dysfunction, while epilepsy and cognitive impairments are scarce [1].

Microscopic resection (MS) is the gold-standard therapeutic option for HHs, providing the best seizure-free rates and preventing further development of neurocognitive and psychological impairments [1]. Despite promising resection outcomes, this approach is associated with a considerable morbidity rate [1]. On the other hand, several minimally invasive options have been introduced during the past decades for HHs [3]. Radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RFTC), laser interstitial thermal therapy (LiTT), brachytherapy, and stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) are minimally invasive options that have demonstrated encouraging seizure-related outcomes [3].

SRS has emerged as one of the main therapeutic options for managing HHs [4–11]. In SRS, high doses of radiation are delivered, targeting the lesion while minimizing radiation-related damage to surrounding brain structures [4–13]. Nevertheless, the diversity in treatment protocols, radiation doses, and outcome evaluations across studies makes it challenging to interpret the available literature. A comprehensive synthesis of current evidence was necessary to evaluate the overall effect and safety profile of SRS in HHs. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the clinical outcomes of SRS for HHs, focusing on seizure control, endocrine function, and treatment-related complications.

Materials and methods

Objective

The objective of the current study was to systematically evaluate the clinical outcomes of SRS modalities in patients with HHs. The study was conducted by the “Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)” guidelines [14].

Search strategy

On December 6, 2024, a comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science. The keywords were “radiosurgery,” “hypothalamic,” and “hamartoma,” along with their equivalents (Supplementary Table 1).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Studies that have evaluated the seizure-related outcomes and adverse events of SRS in HH individuals regardless of whether SRS was used as a primary or salvage option, (2) Publications that reported seizure-free rate, seizure improvement rate, Engel I + II seizures, Engel III + IV seizures, and adverse radiation effect (ARE), and (3) Clinical trials, cohort studies, retrospective studies, or case reposts with five or more patients.

The exclusion criteria were (1) Case series with less than five individuals, case reposts, book chapters, conference abstracts, preprints, commentaries, and editorials, (2) Lack of reporting the outcome data, and (3) Inability to separate the data of HHs from other lesions, (4) Overlap between the participants of the included studies, (5) Studies published in a language other than English.

Study selection process

The search results were imported into the Covidence Software following the comprehensive literature search. Two independent reviewers (M.S. and A.K.) conducted the title and abstract screening, and a third reviewer (B.H.) resolved the conflicts. Studies that met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the full-text screening process. Similarly, two independent reviewers performed the full-text screening, and a third reviewer resolved the conflicts. Studies that fulfilled the eligibility requirements were included for data extraction.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers (B.H. AND I.M.) meticulously performed the data extraction process using an Excel spreadsheet, and the conflicts were resolved by a third author (A.M.). The spreadsheet consisted of two sections: Demographic data and SRS outcomes. The demographic section contained information such as publication year, study design, age, gender, and SRS characteristics, including mean prescription dose and maximum dose. The outcome section comprised seizure-free rate, seizure improvement rate, Engel I + II, and Engel III + IV seizures.

The Engel classification categorizes post-SRS seizure outcomes as follows: Class I (seizure-free or non-disabling auras), Class II (rare disabling seizures with significant improvement), Class III (worthwhile seizure reduction but continued disabling seizures), and Class IV (no significant improvement or worsening of seizures). According to Engel’s classification, seizure freedom was defined as Engel Class I, which indicates the complete resolution of disabling seizures. This meta-analysis was performed following the MetaComp Checklist [15]. Treatment-related adverse events were mainly evaluated by the presence or absence of AREs, determined by new radiation-related clinical and radiological presentations.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias (RoB) was assessed using the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies—of Interventions tool (ROBINS-I) [16]. Two authors independently performed the RoB assessment, and any conflicts were resolved through discussion. The ROBINS-I tool has seven domains, including “D1: Bias due to confounding”, “D2: Bias in selection of participants”, “D3: Bias in classification of interventions”, “D4: Bias due to deviations from intended interventions”, “D5: Bias due to missing data”, “D6: Bias in measurement of outcomes”, and “D7: Bias in selection of reported results”. Each domain was evaluated as either “Low risk,” “Moderate risk,” or “Serious risk.”

Statistical analysis

The R language (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, V 4.4.2) was used for the meta-analysis utilizing the “meta” and “metafor” packages. Heterogeneity was considered significant whenever I2 > 50% or Cochran’s Q was substantial (p < 0.05), and a random-effects model was applied. The robustness of the outcomes was evaluated through a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. Publication bias was recognized through visual evaluation of funnel plots and interpretation of Egger’s test and the trim-and-fill method. Meta-regression was performed to evaluate possible sources of heterogeneity. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection process

The comprehensive search yielded 754 studies. Of these, 369 were identified as duplicates by Covidence and subsequently resolved (Fig. 1). Consequently, 385 studies were included in the title and abstract screening process. During this process, 268 studies were excluded, and 117 were included for full-text screening. Of these 117 studies, 109 were excluded during full-text screening. Among these 109 studies, 20 were excluded either due to overlapping populations or because they were conference abstracts [4, 17–28]. Ultimately, seven studies were included.

PRISMA flowchart of the current study

Quality assessment

The RoB assessment for the included studies underscored diverse levels of bias across various domains (Supplementary Fig. S1). The RoB assessment resulted in a moderate overall RoB. The main concerns stemmed from “confounding,” “classification of interventions,” and “selection of reported results,” highlighting potential challenges in controlling for confounders, defining interventions, and selective reporting. Nevertheless, “participant selection, “missing data,” and “measurement of outcomes” were consistently evaluated as low risk, demonstrating reliable study inclusion, data handling, and outcome assessment. While these studies showed an acceptable level of bias, the presence of moderate concerns warrants a cautious interpretation of the findings.

Our study included seven studies with 152 HH patients. Table 1 demonstrates the baseline characteristics of the included studies. The mean age at seizure onset ranged from 0.92 to 15 years across the included studies. The mean age at the SRS ranged from 7.3 to 26.1 years. Regarding gender, males and females comprised 61.2% (90/147) and 38.8% (57/147) of the patients, respectively. Regarding the Regis classification, type II (32%, 33/103) was the most common, followed by type III (30.1%, 31/103) and type I (15.5%, 16/103). Regarding the type of seizure, 47.9% (45/94) experienced a gelastic seizure only, 43.6% (41/94) had a gelastic seizure in combination with other types of seizures, and 8.5% (8/94) had seizures other than gelastic seizures.

Meta-analysis of outcomes

Six studies were included in the meta-analyses of visual impairment and new hormonal deficits post-SRS (Fig. 1A-B). The meta-analysis revealed a pooled post-SRS visual impairment of 0% (95%CI: 0%-1%) with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.99) (Fig. 1A). The meta-analysis for the post-SRS new hormonal deficits showed a pooled rate of 0% (95%CI: 0%-5%) with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.94) (Fig. 1B).

Seven studies were included in the meta-analysis of seizure improvement following SRS (Fig. 1C). The meta-analysis revealed a pooled post-SRS seizure improvement rate of 77% (95%CI: 61%-91%) with relatively high heterogeneity (I2 = 55.5%, P = 0.04) (Fig. 1C). The meta-regression identified Regis type IV and prior psychological impairment as sources of heterogeneity for seizure improvement following SRS (Supplementary Table S2). It showed that a higher number of Regis type IV (Estimate= -4.77, P = 0.037) was associated with a lower seizure improvement rate, and the presence of prior psychological impairment (Estimate = 3.062, P: 0.002) was accompanied by higher rates of seizure improvement.

Six studies were included in the meta-analysis for post-SRS seizure-free status rate (Fig. 1D). The meta-analysis showed a pooled post-SRS seizure-free status rate of 48% (95%CI: 19%-78%) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 71%, P = 0.004) (Fig. 1D). The meta-regression showed that older age at the onset of seizure (Estimate = 0.896, P = 0.001) and lower number of Regis type III HHs (Estimate=-7.3, P < 0.001) were associated with a higher post-SRS seizure-free rate (Supplementary Table S2).

Seven studies were reported in the meta-analysis of good (Engel I/II) and poor outcomes (Engel III/IV) (Figs. 1E-F). The meta-analysis revealed a pooled good outcome rate of 67% (95%CI: 48%-84%) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 54.1%, P = 0.04) (Fig. 1E). The meta-analysis for poor outcome demonstrated a pooled rate of 33% (95%CI: 16%-52%) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 54.1%, P = 0.42) (Fig. 1F). Meta-regression showed that the presence of prior psychological impairment (Estimate = 1.699, P: 0.046) was accompanied by higher rates of post-SRS good outcomes (Supplementary Table S2). Seven studies were included in the meta-analysis of the post-SRS ARE (Fig. 1G). The meta-analysis revealed a pooled post-SRS ARE rate of 0% (95% CI: 0%-1%), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.99) (Fig. 1G).

Similarly, the sensitivity analysis for post-SRS seizure improvement and seizure-free status demonstrated that the results were moderately robust after excluding each study (Supplementary Figs. The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for both good and poor outcomes also showed that despite high heterogeneity, the results remained consistent through the sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figs. Publication bias

The evaluation of publication bias revealed that the meta-analysis for post-SRS visual impairment exhibited an asymmetrical pattern, which was confirmed by Egger’s test, indicating the presence of publication bias (P < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. S3A). The trim-and-fill analysis adjusted the post-SRS visual impairment by adding three studies, resulting in a pooled rate of 10% (95% CI: 5.7%-14.3%). The funnel plots for post-SRS new hormonal deficit, seizure improvement, seizure-free status, good outcome, and poor outcome showed symmetrical patterns (Supplementary Figs. S3B-F). Additionally, the corresponding Egger’s test confirmed the absence of publication bias for post-SRS new hormonal deficit (P = 0.3), seizure improvement (P = 0.70), seizure-free status (P = 0.29), good outcome (P = 0.2), and poor outcome (P = 0.2). In contrast, the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for the ARE revealed an asymmetrical pattern, as the Egger’s test (P < 0.01) indicated the presence of publication bias (Supplementary Fig. S3G). The trim-and-fill analysis adjusted the post-SRS ARE by adding three studies, resulting in a pooled rate of 8.9% (95% CI: 5%-12.6%).

Discussion

Management of HHs remains controversial due to variability in lesions, treatment modalities, and seizure-related outcomes. Our study highlighted the efficacy and safety of SRS in patients with HHs. Our meta-analysis revealed a post-SRS seizure-free status rate of 77% (95% CI: 61%-91%) and a pooled seizure-free rate of 48% (95% CI: 19%-78%) following SRS. In addition, the proportion of patients achieving Engel class I or II outcomes was 67% (95% CI: 48%-84%), suggesting a significant reduction in seizure burden.

MS encompassing transcallosal and personal techniques has been historically the main therapeutic option for the management of HHs [29, 30]. However, this approach was associated with considerable morbidity, leading to investigations into less invasive alternatives [30]. Transventricular endoscopic surgery (ES) has emerged as a novel, less invasive approach for the resection of HHs, which has been utilized for small and unilateral lesions [30]. Although the endoscopic approach mitigated the length of stay and complications, 10% of the patients experienced post-interventional complications [30]. A recent meta-analysis by Niazi et al. showed that MS and ES achieved a pooled seizure-free rate of 60.1% and 41.8%, respectively [30]. On the other hand, the major complication rate was significantly lower in ES (11.1%) than in MS (33%) [30]. A recent meta-analysis by Rizzi et al. showed that despite the higher number of seizure-free rates in the MS (58.7%) than in ES (47.7%), the hypothalamic function impairment (MS: 31.8% vs. ES: 0%) and neurological complications (MS:15.5% vs. ES: 4.17%) were significantly lower in the ES patients [31]. On the other hand, our findings showed that, despite lower seizure-free rates following SRS, the complication rates are considerably lower than those of MS and ES.

LiTT is another minimally invasive procedure extensively used for patients with HH [3, 29–31]. Magnetic Resonance-Guided LiTT (MRg-LiTT) is a novel approach characterized by prompt efficacy and accurate ablation with low damage to surrounding tissues [3, 29–31]. This approach mitigates the length of stay and costs, while concurrently achieving favorable outcomes [3, 29–31]. A recent meta-analysis by Ahmed et al. demonstrated that MRg-LiTT resulted in a pooled seizure-free rate of 77.1% [3]. Another study by Rizzi et al. showed that LiTT led to a pooled Engel I seizure outcome in 75.86%, with a pooled hypothalamic function impairment rate and neurological complication rate of 6.96% [31]. They also highlighted that LiTT was associated with the highest Engel I seizure outcomes compared to MS, ES, SRS, and RFTC [31]. A meta-analysis by Niazi et al. showed that MRg-LiTT achieved a pooled seizure-free rate of 74.5%, which was slightly inferior to RFTC (78.5%) but significantly greater than MS (60.1%), ES (41.8%), and SRS (43.4%). They also demonstrated that the significant complication rate following MRg-LiTT (16.9%) was higher than that of SRS (1.8%) and RFTC (4.5%) [30]. Our results were consistent with prior studies and showed that, despite lower seizure-free rates following SRS, this approach is associated with lower complication rates than LiTT.

RFTC is another minimally invasive approach typically used in patients with HHs. Niazi et al. showed that RFTC achieved the highest pooled seizure-free rate (78.5%) compared to MRg-LiTT, SRS, MS, and ES [30]. In addition, in their study, RFTC (4.5%) had the lowest major complication rate after SRS (1.8%) [30]. In contrast, a study by Rizzi et al. found that the Engel I seizure outcome was slightly lower in RFTC (72.4%) [31]. They also showed that although hypothalamic function impairment was higher in the RFTC (RFTC: 10.1% vs. LiTT: 6.96%), neurological complications were lower (RFTC: 3.37% vs. LiTT: 6.96%) [31]. Both Niazi et al. and Rizzi et al. demonstrated that RFTC is correlated with higher seizure-free rates compared to SRS; however, SRS was associated with considerably lower complication rates [30, 31].

This study represents a dedicated meta-analysis that focuses exclusively on SRS for HHs, differentiating it from prior meta-analyses that included mixed surgical approaches or small case series without separate SRS-specific pooled estimates. Unlike previous studies, our analysis evaluated outcomes from 152 patients treated with SRS alone, demonstrating a pooled seizure improvement rate of 77%, a seizure-free rate of 48%, and a low ARE rate. These results highlight that SRS is an effective and minimally invasive treatment option with a favorable safety profile, particularly for patients with small or surgically inaccessible HHs. Our study suggests consideration of SRS as a therapeutic option when resection is not feasible. Future studies should prioritize prospective registries and comparative studies that assess SRS against other approaches, including LiTT or RFTC, with an emphasis on standardized outcome reporting and long-term follow-up.

The heterogeneity in the meta-analysis can arise from variations in patient characteristics and HH-related features such as Régis type. Additionally, variations in SRS protocols and follow-up durations may have influenced outcomes. Meta-regression analysis demonstrated that specific factors, such as psychological comorbidity and lesion type, significantly impacted the response, partially explaining the observed heterogeneity.

Our study has several limitations. Most included studies were conducted retrospectively, which may introduce selection bias and affect the generalizability of our results. Another limitation is the variability in the follow-up period of the included studies, which can directly impact the seizure-related outcome rates as SRS tends to be effective gradually, and after months to years. Another limitation is that some studies were conducted in a single-center setting, which can impact the results and introduce selection bias, as protocols may vary between institutions. Future research should focus on prospective direct comparisons between SRS, LiTT, RFTC, and surgical resection to determine the optimal treatment for various HH subtypes.

Conclusion

SRS is a practical therapeutic option for individuals with HHs, offering favorable seizure control and a promising safety profile. SRS remains an effective alternative for patients with small, well-defined lesions. Nevertheless, the delayed therapeutic effect and variability in clinical response underscore the necessity of meticulous patient selection. Future prospective trials and long-term studies are required to optimize treatment strategies and enhance outcomes in this challenging patient population​.
