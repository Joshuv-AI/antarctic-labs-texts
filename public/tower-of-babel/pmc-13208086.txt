Primary hepatic neuroendocrine tumor with multifocal liver involvement: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Asmae Guennouni, Hatim Essaber, Soukaina Bahha, Fatima Zahra Laamrani, Youssef Omor, Rachida Latib, Sanae Amalik, Youssra Aroudan, Mouna Salihon, Nawal Kabbaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary hepatic neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are extremely rare, representing approximately 0.3% of all NETs. We report the case of a 30-year-old woman who presented with asymptomatic abdominal distension. Imaging revealed multiple hepatic masses, the largest measuring 195 × 100 × 230 mm in the left lobe. Laboratory tests, including liver function and tumor markers, were normal. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry confirmed a well-differentiated grade 1 neuroendocrine carcinoma with positivity for synaptophysin, CD56, and chromogranin A and a Ki-67 index of 1%. A comprehensive workup excluded an extrahepatic primary tumor. The patient was treated with long-acting lanreotide, and follow-up imaging demonstrated stable lesions with minimal hormonal symptoms. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of primary hepatic NETs, their often asymptomatic course, and the importance of multidisciplinary evaluation, histological confirmation, and targeted therapy, with surgical resection remaining the mainstay for localized disease while systemic therapy and interventional approaches may be considered in multifocal or unresectable cases.Keywords: Primary hepatic neuroendocrine tumor, PHNET, Liver neoplasm, Neuroendocrine tumor, Somatostatin analogues, Hepatic mass

Introduction

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) account for approximately 1%-2% of all gastrointestinal neoplasms [1]. The liver is the most common site of metastasis for digestive NETs. In contrast, primary hepatic NETs (PHNETs) are an exceedingly rare entity, representing approximately 0.3% of all NETs. The first case was reported by Edmondson in 1958 [2], and since then, fewer than 150 cases have been documented in the literature [3].

Owing to their rarity, standardized diagnostic and therapeutic algorithms for PHNETs are lacking, posing significant challenges in clinical practice. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment whenever feasible. Despite their malignant nature, these tumors generally demonstrate a favorable prognosis, with a reported 10-year survival rate of up to 70% [2].

Case report

A 30-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented to the gastroenterology department for evaluation of abdominal distension. She was clinically asymptomatic, with no nausea, vomiting, vasomotor flushing, fever, diarrhea, constipation, or abdominal pain.

On physical examination, hepatomegaly was noted with palpable masses predominantly involving the left lobe of the liver. The remainder of the examination was unremarkable.

Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated multiple solid hepatic masses, the largest located in the left hepatic lobe. Multidetector abdominal computed tomography (CT) confirmed multiple well-circumscribed, heterogeneous hepatic lesions, the largest measuring 195 × 100 × 230 mm in the left lobe. These lesions exhibited marked enhancement during the arterial and portal phases, followed by contrast washout and peripheral enhancement in the delayed phase (Fig. 1). The surrounding hepatic parenchyma showed no evidence of cirrhosis.

An ultrasound-guided liver biopsy was performed. Histological and immunohistochemical analysis (Fig. 2, Fig. 3) demonstrated tumor cells positive for neuroendocrine markers, including synaptophysin, CD56, and chromogranin A (CgA). The Ki-67 proliferation index was estimated at 1%, consistent with a well-differentiated, grade 1 neuroendocrine carcinoma.

A comprehensive systemic workup was undertaken to identify a potential primary NET, including upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy and enterography, all of which were unremarkable. CT angiography revealed no extrahepatic lesions beyond the liver masses.

After follow-up and exclusion of an extrahepatic primary tumor, a diagnosis of PHNET with multifocal liver involvement was established. Following a multidisciplinary team discussion, treatment with lanreotide (Somatuline) 120 mg was initiated every 4 weeks, with close clinical and radiological monitoring.

At 1-month follow-up, the patient remained in good general condition and reported mild hormonal symptoms, including diarrhea and flushing. Liver function tests and blood glucose levels remained normal. Follow-up liver magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated stability of the lesions. Surveillance was planned with clinical and imaging assessments every 3 months, including repeat imaging at 6 months.

Discussion

NETs originate primarily from the gastrointestinal tract and bronchopulmonary system, accounting for approximately 1%-2% of gastrointestinal tumors [4]. Among gastrointestinal NETs, the small intestine is the most common primary site (45%), followed by the rectum (20%), appendix (17%), colon (11%), and stomach (7%) [4]. These tumors are classified based on morphological features and embryological origin.

The WHO recommends using “neuroendocrine tumor” rather than the term “carcinoid tumor” to reduce confusion, although both terms remain in use in the literature [5].

PHNETs appear to affect both sexes similarly, although a slight male predominance has been reported. The mean age at diagnosis is approximately 47 years [6].

Clinically, PHNETs are usually asymptomatic and rarely produce endocrine manifestations, contrasting with hepatic metastases from extrahepatic NETs, which frequently present with hormonal symptoms [7]. These tumors are often discovered incidentally during imaging performed for unrelated reasons. Due to their indolent growth, PHNETs may reach considerable size before detection. In some cases, a palpable right upper quadrant mass or abdominal pain may occur, occasionally associated with carcinoid syndrome [8]. Carcinoid syndrome occurs in <10% of patients with gastrointestinal NETs and is typically linked to hepatic metastases. NETs may secrete various hormones, with gastrin most frequently detected (10.1%), followed by CgA [9]. However, hormonal symptoms in PHNETs are exceptional [8].

The pathogenesis of PHNETs remains unclear. Proposed mechanisms include malignant transformation of neuroendocrine cells within intrahepatic bile ducts, origin from ectopic pancreatic or adrenal tissue, and secondary neuroendocrine differentiation of malignant stem cells [10]. Associations with underlying liver disease, such as chronic hepatitis B or C infection, have also been reported [11].

Biological diagnosis of NETs traditionally relies on 24-hour urinary 5-HIAA, though this is limited in nonfunctioning tumors [8]. Serum CgA is a more versatile marker, useful for both functional and nonfunctional NETs, though false positives can occur in hepatic or renal insufficiency, atrophic gastritis, or prolonged proton pump inhibitor use [8].

Radiologically, ultrasound, CT, and MRI are of limited specificity in distinguishing PHNETs from other liver tumors [12]. Multidetector CT is the primary modality for disease localization and extent assessment [8]. On MRI, lesions appear hypointense on T1 and hyperintense on T2 sequences [13]. Hepatic metastases from NETs show strong arterial enhancement followed by washout in portal and delayed phases, reflecting hypervascularity [13].

PHNETs generally present as solitary lesions >3 cm; multinodular forms can mimic metastatic disease. The right lobe is most commonly involved, though left-sided or bilateral disease has been reported [9].

PET/CT’s role in PHNET diagnosis is unclear, though increased FDG uptake may aid in selected cases for staging. Somatostatin receptor scintigraphy, particularly OctreoScan (iodine-111–labeled octreotide), demonstrates high sensitivity (85%-90%) for lesion detection and may predict response to somatostatin analogues [14].

Histopathology remains the diagnostic gold standard. Hematoxylin–eosin staining aids classification but is nonspecific; immunohistochemistry for NSE, CgA, and synaptophysin is essential [10].

Management of PHNETs requires a multidisciplinary approach. Surgical resection is the only potentially curative option for localized disease [15]. In metastatic disease, surgery is primarily palliative. Chemotherapy may be considered for high-proliferation tumors, often using 5-fluorouracil, doxorubicin, and streptozotocin, though evidence is limited [8].

Transarterial chemoembolization is well established for extrahepatic NET metastases but has limited data for PHNETs. Somatostatin analogues (octreotide, lanreotide) control hormonal symptoms but rarely reduce tumor burden [16]. Liver transplantation may be considered in select patients with multifocal disease and hepatic insufficiency. Radiofrequency ablation can complement surgery in patients with ≤3 lesions ≤5 cm, but is limited near major vessels [8].
