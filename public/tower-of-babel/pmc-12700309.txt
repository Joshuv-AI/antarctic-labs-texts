Macrophage Subtypes Distinctly Impact Intervertebral Disc Inflammation and Extracellular Matrix Composition
Authors: Lauren E Lisiewski, Leonardo Campos, Timothy D Jacobsen, Min Kyu M Kim, Nadeen O Chahine
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntervertebral disc (IVD) degeneration is characterized by changes in extracellular matrix (ECM) composition and an inflammatory microenvironment, including macrophage recruitment. While inflammation is known to amplify ECM degradation, the individual roles of IVD cells and macrophages, and contributions of crosstalk are unknown. Additionally, the impacts of polarized pro‐inflammatory M1 or anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages on the IVD are unknown, highlighting a need for an in vitro macrophage‐IVD explant co‐culture system.MethodsIVDs were dissected from the lumbar spine of rats and cultured encapsulated in agarose. Primary bone marrow‐derived macrophages were isolated and polarized to M1 or M2. IVD explants were cultured for 14 days with or without TNFα. M1 or M2 macrophages were co‐cultured with unstimulated or TNFα‐stimulated IVDs. Changes in ECM were evaluated using glycosaminoglycan (GAG) loss into media, IVD GAG and collagen content, and histology. Inflammatory responses were assessed by measuring nitric oxide (NO) and cytokines (TNFα, IL‐6, IL‐13, CCL2) in media supernatants.ResultsTNFα stimulation of IVDs increased NO, TNFα, and CCL2, indicating an impact on inflammation. TNFα stimulation of IVDs also affected IVD ECM, with changes observed in both GAG and collagen. M1 and M2 co‐culture counteracted TNFα‐induced changes in collagen content, while macrophage subtypes distinctly modulated inflammation. Specifically, M1 macrophages increased inflammatory cytokines (NO, IL‐6), while M2 macrophages decreased pro‐inflammatory (NO, TNFα) and increased anti‐inflammatory (IL‐13) cytokines. Macrophage‐IVD crosstalk had unique effects on IL‐6 and IL‐13 levels compared to IVDs or macrophages cultured alone.ConclusionsInflammatory stimulation of IVDs and co‐culture with macrophages in vitro impacted both inflammation and ECM composition. Both macrophage subtypes reversed TNFα‐induced changes in collagen content, while M1 and M2 macrophages had detrimental and protective effects on inflammation, respectively. The macrophage‐IVD explant co‐culture model in this study provides a novel system for evaluating crosstalk exclusively through paracrine signaling.Keywords: crosstalk, extracellular matrix, inflammation, macrophage

Introduction

Low back pain is the leading cause of disability with intervertebral disc (IVD) degeneration often being associated with the condition [1, 2]. IVD degeneration is characterized by morphological alterations to the IVD structure and composition [3]. The ECM composition of the healthy IVD varies between the nucleus pulposus (NP) and annulus fibrosis (AF) regions. While both regions consist of proteoglycans, including glycosaminoglycan (GAG) and collagen, GAG is more prevalent in the NP and collagen content is greater in the AF [4, 5, 6]. When IVD degeneration occurs, matrix‐degrading enzymes, including matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTSs), break down the ECM components leading to decreased GAG content, altered distribution of collagen, and a generally fibrotic tissue structure [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. One of the primary mediators of ECM degradation is the inflammatory microenvironment, which drives the increase in MMPs and ADAMTSs by cells within the IVD [10].

In human degenerative disc tissue, expression of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines has been observed [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. The production of chemokines, such as C‐C motif ligand 2 (CCL2), promotes recruitment of immune cells, including macrophages, and amplifies the inflammatory and degradative cascade through production of additional inflammatory cytokines and matrix degrading enzymes [14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Importantly, expression of the inflammatory cytokines, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) and interleukin‐1 beta (IL‐1β), as well as macrophage markers has been shown to change with disease, with increasing levels in more severely degenerated human surgical samples [10, 11, 19, 20]. These findings emphasize the contributions of both IVD cells and macrophages in promoting and maintaining chronic local inflammation in degenerating IVDs.

The inflammatory and immune response after IVD injury has been recapitulated in vivo using a variety of rodent models. Most prominently, puncture injury has been used to induce increased expression of inflammatory cytokines, including TNFα, interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), and IL‐6, along with structural changes such as decreased disc height and increased histological score [21, 22, 23, 24]. Other in vivo models have focused on the induction of IVD degeneration through modulation of inflammation using either transgenic mice or intradiscal injection of inflammatory stimuli, such as TNFα or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [25, 26]. For example, chronic inflammation was induced in a transgenic mouse model through prolonged upregulation of nuclear factor κB (NF‐κB), a transcription factor known to mediate the inflammatory and immune response within the IVD [27]. Separately, a rat model utilized intradiscal injection of LPS with a small gauge needle to minimize structural disruption while inducing inflammation locally within the IVD [28]. In both of these inflammation‐driven models, the induction of local inflammation increased IVD tissue expression of inflammatory cytokines and induced macrophage recruitment. Ultimately, structural changes similar to those resulting from puncture injury were observed, supporting the role of inflammation and macrophages in ECM degradation. However, while the presence of macrophages in the IVD during degeneration is well established, the coexistence of IVD cells and macrophages in vivo makes it challenging to distinguish the contributions of the individual cell types within the inflammatory environment. Additionally, macrophages exist on a spectrum spanning from pro‐inflammatory M1 to anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages. The impact of the inflamed IVD on the maintenance of macrophage polarization, and subsequent effects of crosstalk between these unique macrophage subtypes and IVD cells remains elusive.

For this reason, many studies have explored these questions through macrophage conditioned media or co‐culture studies. In vitro, the inflammatory microenvironment of the degenerating IVD has been simulated using TNFα or IL‐1β stimulation of IVD cells or tissue explants [29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. In response to stimulation, IVD cells had increased expression of additional inflammatory mediators, including IL‐6 and NO release, accurately representing the inflammatory profile that has been observed in response to in vivo injury. In conditioned media studies, treatment of macrophages with conditioned media from IL‐1β‐treated IVD cells increased macrophage expression of inflammatory cytokines (TNFα, IL‐1β, IL‐6), chemokines (CCL2), and matrix degrading enzymes (MMP‐3), while decreasing expression of anti‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐10, IL‐4), indicating potential polarization of macrophages towards a pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotype [36]. Conditioned media from LPS‐stimulated macrophages increased expression of a similar profile of pro‐inflammatory cytokines in IVD cells, reflecting the possibility for a positive feedback loop between macrophages and IVD cells in the degenerative disc environment [37, 38]. Expression and production of the inflammatory mediators TNFα, IL‐1β, IL‐6, and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) was also increased in co‐cultured IVD cells and macrophages compared to either cell type alone [29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. This response was enhanced with the addition of inflammatory stimulation in the culture system. Also, production of matrix degrading enzymes increased and IVD cell expression of ECM markers, Acan and Col2, decreased after co‐culture, supporting the role of macrophages in ECM degradation [39, 40].

While conditioned media studies allow for separation of the effects of IVD cells on macrophages and vice versa, they do not allow for crosstalk between cell types, which is an important regulator of cell signaling. Indeed, in a study comparing conditioned media and co‐culture models of IVD explants and macrophages, differing effects were observed between study designs [39]. Additionally, 2D culture of IVD cells oversimplifies the IVD microenvironment due to a lack of physiological cues from the native ECM [41, 42]. Similarly, macrophage cell lines such as THP‐1 or RAW264.7 are easily accessible, but have been shown to have different expression and protein release profiles than primary macrophages [43]. Previous studies on the interaction of macrophages and IVD cells also mostly focused on unpolarized macrophages, whose functionality differs from that of polarized pro‐inflammatory M1 and anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages [16]. The few studies that have explored the effects of M1 and M2 macrophages on IVD cells observed detrimental effects of M1 macrophage conditioned media on NP cell gene expression of inflammatory and catabolic markers, while M2 macrophage conditioned media had protective effects [44, 45].

To address the limitations of previous conditioned media and co‐culture studies, this paper aimed to create an in vitro macrophage‐IVD explant co‐culture model that can be used to study both inflammatory and ECM changes resulting from macrophage‐IVD crosstalk. Using a transwell system that restricts crosstalk to exclusively paracrine signaling, IVDs stimulated with TNFα were co‐cultured with primary bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMDMs) polarized to the pro‐inflammatory M1 or anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype. The use of primary‐derived macrophages minimized the discrepancies in gene expression and immunomodulatory capabilities observed in macrophage cell lines [43]. Additionally, utilizing an IVD explant model maintains the physiological ECM structure, while also facilitating measurement of changes in ECM composition over 14 days in culture [41, 42]. The macrophage‐IVD explant co‐culture system developed in this study, provides a novel in vitro model for the study of the inflammatory and degradative impacts of crosstalk between cell types present within the degenerative disc environment.

Materials and Methods

L929 Conditioned Media (LCM)

L929 mouse fibroblasts were cultured in Eagle's Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM, Corning) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, GeminiBio) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S). At confluence, media was exchanged and cells were cultured for an additional 3 days. This media was then collected, filtered using a 0.2 μm polyethersulfone (PES) bottle top filter, and frozen at −20°C to be used as LCM in macrophage media. LCM contains macrophage colony stimulating factor (mCSF) which is required for macrophage viability [46].

Primary Macrophage Isolation and Stimulation

“Macrophage media” was prepared using low‐glucose DMEM, 10% FBS (GeminiBio, Cat#. 100‐106), 1% P/S, and LCM (30% during cell expansion, 20% during experiments). Femurs were dissected from 300 to 325 g, skeletally mature, male Sprague Dawley rats, and bone marrow was isolated by flushing with 3 mL fresh macrophage media without LCM using a 21G syringe needle 3 times, followed by 3 mL cell suspension 3 times, alternating ends of the femur. The collected bone marrow suspension was then strained through a 40 μm cell strainer, treated with red blood cell lysis buffer, and then equally divided and plated on 6 non‐treated petri dishes in macrophage media. The resulting adhered cell population were M0 macrophages, with confluence achieved after approximately 5 to 7 days of culture in macrophage media containing 30% LCM. M0 macrophages were then lifted using 0.05% Trypsin‐Ethylenediaminetetraacetic Acid (EDTA), removed from petri dishes using a cell scraper, and used immediately in experiments without freezing. For each co‐culture experiment, M0 macrophages from a single donor were plated in a 12‐well plate at a density of 300 000 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. Following adherence, they were polarized to the M1 phenotype through overnight chemical stimulation with 100 ng/mL LPS (MilliporeSigma, Cat#. L2630) and 20 ng/mL IFN γ (PeproTech, Cat#. 400‐20), or the M2 phenotype with 20 ng/mL IL‐4 (PeproTech, Cat#. 400‐04) and IL‐13 (PeproTech, Cat#. 400‐16L). Polarized cells were used in co‐culture studies immediately after chemical stimulation.

Macrophage Phenotype Validation

Successful isolation of macrophages after expansion was validated using flow cytometry. At confluence, macrophages were lifted using trypsin–EDTA and cell scraping as described above. Suspended cells were strained using a 40 μm cell strainer and treated with an Fc blocker (1 μg/1 million cells) on ice for at least 5 min. Following blocking, cells were stained with anti‐CD45 (1:200, BioLegend, Cat#. 202 225), anti‐CD68 (1:20, BioRad, Cat#. MCA341A647), and propidium iodide (PI, 1:400, BioLegend, Cat#. 421 301), indicating cell viability, on ice for 30 min while protected from light. After incubation, cells were washed 3 times with staining buffer and resuspended for analysis. Flow cytometry was performed using a BioRad ZE5 Cell Analyzer and live cells dually expressing the leukocyte marker, CD45, and macrophage marker, CD68, were considered M0 macrophages.

Proliferation of M1‐ and M2‐polarized macrophages was determined through analysis of DNA content in cell lysates. Macrophages were polarized to the M1 and M2 phenotype as described above. At Day 0, 4, 7, 11, and 14, macrophage media was exchanged, or cells were lysed using 0.1% Triton X‐100 in macrophage media incubated for 30 min at 37°C. DNA content of cell lysates was quantified using the Quant‐iT Picogreen dsDNA assay kit (Invitrogen, Cat#. P7589). Normality was assessed using a Shapiro–Wilk normality test within each group and timepoint, and statistical significance was determined using a Student's t‐test at each timepoint.

Gene expression of M1 and M2 phenotypic markers was evaluated in M0, M1, and M2 macrophages after overnight chemical polarization to the desired phenotypes (Day 0), and 1, 4, 7, 11, or 14 days after polarization. At each timepoint, media was collected and exchanged, or RNA was isolated from macrophages using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat#. 74106) according to the manufacturer's protocol. After isolation, RNA was converted to cDNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio‐Rad, Cat#. 1708891). Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐qPCR) was performed using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio‐Rad, Cat#. 1725121) to determine gene expression changes in macrophage phenotypic markers. Macrophage phenotypic markers were determined from previous literature, including bulk RNA sequencing of polarized rat bone marrow‐derived macrophages, and primer sequences can be found in Table 1 [47, 48]. Normality was assessed using a Shapiro–Wilk normality test within each group and timepoint, and statistical significance was determined using a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Fisher's least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test comparing macrophage subtypes within each timepoint.

Rat IVD Isolation and Co‐Culture Conditions

IVDs were isolated from level thoracic (T)13 to lumbar (L)6 of the spine of male Sprague Dawley rats weighing approximately 300 to 325 g. IVDs within each experimental group were isolated from multiple biological donors (n = 3–5). Vertebral bodies and endplates were removed from lumbar IVDs, and IVDs containing the AF and NP regions were individually cast in 2% agarose to minimize tissue swelling and associated cell death and ECM loss [49]. Agarose‐encapsulated discs were cultured overnight in “disc media” containing high‐glucose DMEM, 10% FBS (R&D Systems), and 1% P/S. Explants were then transferred to the top of transwell inserts with 0.4 μm pore size in a 12‐well plate for the start of experimental conditions, denoted Day 0 (Figure 1).

Schematic of the IVD explant and co‐culture models with timelines.

On Day 0 of co‐culture studies without TNFα stimulation, agarose‐encapsulated IVDs in transwell inserts were put into co‐culture above polarized macrophages adhered to the bottom of a 12‐well plate. Macrophage‐IVD explant co‐cultures were maintained in macrophage media for 14 days with media supernatants collected and replaced with fresh media on Day 4, 7, and 11. On Day 14, the final day of co‐culture, media was collected for supernatant analysis, IVDs were collected for tissue biochemical analysis or histology, and macrophage RNA was isolated for gene expression analysis (Figure 1).

In some studies, IVD explants or macrophage‐IVD explant co‐cultures were treated with TNFα to simulate an inflammatory environment in vitro. In IVD explant experiments, on Day 0, agarose‐encapsulated IVDs were transferred to transwell inserts in a 12‐well plate containing disc media or macrophage media plus 10 ng/mL TNFα (R&D Systems). Media supernatants were collected and replaced with more TNFα stimulation media on Day 4, 7, and 11. On Day 14, media was collected for supernatant analysis and IVDs were collected for tissue biochemical analysis or histology (Figure 1).

In studies with TNFα stimulation of macrophage‐IVD explant co‐cultures, on Day 0, agarose‐encapsulated IVDs were transferred to transwell inserts in a 12‐well plate above M1‐ or M2‐polarized macrophages for co‐culture. IVDs, along with M1 or M2 macrophages were cultured in macrophage media containing 10 ng/mL TNFα. Media was collected and replaced with fresh macrophage media containing TNFα on Day 4, 7, and 11. On Day 14, media was collected for supernatant analysis, and IVDs were collected for tissue biochemical analysis or histology. In experiments where samples were collected for both biochemical analysis and histology at the terminal timepoint, the sample number between supernatant and biochemical analyses differed due to the allocation of some samples to histology. RNA was also isolated from macrophages for phenotypic analysis. All IVD explant stimulation and co‐culture conditions are outlined with timelines in Figure 1.

Supernatant Analyses

Supernatants were collected from all IVD‐only and macrophage co‐culture experiments on days 4, 7, 11, and 14. Changes in inflammation were primarily evaluated by measuring nitric oxide (NO) release into the media using the Griess Reagent System (Promega, Cat#. G2930). All NO release results are presented as cumulative release over the entirety of the culture period by summing measurements from each timepoint (i.e., Day 4, Day 7, Day 11, Day 14), or as an average of daily NO release which was computed by dividing NO measurements by the number of days between media changes for each timepoint. Production of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines was also evaluated using the Rat CCL2 DuoSet ELISA (R&D Systems, Cat#. DY3144‐05) and the LEGENDPlex Rat Th Cytokine Panel V02 (BioLegend, Cat#. 741220) multiplex assay containing 13 analytes: TNFα, IL‐6, IL‐13, IFNγ, IL‐17A, IL‐17F, IL‐5, IL‐2, IL‐4, IL‐10, IL‐22, GM‐CSF, and IL‐9. Statistical significance was evaluated using Student's t‐tests between IVD and IVD + TNFα, and using one‐way ANOVAs with Fisher's LSD post hoc tests within each timepoint for all co‐culture experiments.

ECM biochemical changes over time in culture were measured using GAG loss into media supernatants collected on days 4, 7, 11, and 14 of culture. The amount of GAG lost from IVDs into the media was measured using the Dimethylmethylene Blue (DMMB) assay [50]. All GAG loss results are presented as cumulative loss throughout the duration of the culture period calculated by summing measurements from each timepoint (i.e., Day 4, Day 7, Day 11, Day 14), or as average daily GAG loss by dividing GAG measurements by the number of days between media changes for each timepoint.

Normality was assessed using a Shapiro–Wilk normality test within each group and timepoint for each type of data. Statistical significance of cumulative NO release and GAG loss was evaluated using Student's t‐tests for analysis of IVD macrophage versus disc media and IVD TNFα stimulation, and one‐way ANOVAs with Fisher's LSD post hoc tests within each timepoint for co‐cultures. Statistical significance of average daily NO release and GAG loss was determined using two‐way ANOVAs with Fisher's LSD post hoc tests, with treatment group and time as variables.

Disc Digest Analyses

At the completion of the culture period, IVDs intended for tissue biochemical analysis were snap frozen. Before digestion, IVDs were thawed, agarose was removed, and the AF and NP regions were separated. Samples were then digested in a 1:50 dilution of papain aqueous suspension (MilliporeSigma, Cat#. P3125) in papain buffer containing sodium acetate anhydrous (MilliporeSigma, Cat#. S2889), cysteine hydrochloride (MilliporeSigma, Cat#. C9768), and EDTA (MilliporeSigma, Cat#. E6758) at 60°C for approximately 48 h. 500 or 300 μL of papain digestion solution was used per AF or NP, respectively. Tissue digests were then analyzed for DNA content using the Quant‐iT Picogreen dsDNA assay kit (Invitrogen, Cat#. P7589), GAG content using the DMMB assay, and collagen content using the hydroxyproline (OHP) assay [50, 51]. Normality was assessed using a Shapiro–Wilk normality test within each group and timepoint for each type of data, and statistical significance was determined for TNFα stimulations using a Student's t‐test, and for Day 0 versus Day 14 and co‐cultures using a one‐way ANOVA with Fisher's LSD post hoc within each tissue region.

IVD Histology

For histological analysis, agarose was removed at the end of culture, and IVD explants were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight. Following fixation, explants were transferred to 70% ethanol for transportation to the Molecular Pathology Shared Resource (MPSR) facility at the Columbia University Herbert Irving Comprehensive Cancer Center (HICCC) where samples were embedded in paraffin. 7 μm IVD histological sections were transferred to slides then stained with Alcian Blue (pH 1) or Picrosirius Red, and imaged on a Zeiss Axio Observer for qualitative evaluation.

Co‐Cultured Macrophage Gene Expression

RNA from macrophages co‐cultured with IVDs was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat#. 74106) according to the manufacturer's protocol, and macrophage phenotype was evaluated using the markers in Table 1. In co‐cultures without TNFα stimulation, statistical significance between macrophage phenotypes was determined using Student's t‐tests between macrophages alone and co‐cultured macrophages. In co‐cultures with TNFα treatment, one‐way ANOVAs with Fisher's LSD post hoc tests within each macrophage subtype were used to determine significance.

Results

Macrophage Phenotype Over Time in Culture

Validation of the macrophage isolation from bone marrow, along with proliferation and phenotype of M1‐ and M2‐polarized macrophages, was evaluated over time in culture. Flow cytometry confirmed that 99.8% of cells were double positive for the leukocyte marker, CD45, and macrophage marker, CD68, confirming the purity of the macrophage population (Figure 2A). M2 macrophages consistently proliferated more than M1 macrophages in culture over a 14‐day period, as demonstrated by significantly greater DNA levels from M2 than M1 macrophages at Day 4 (p < 0.01), 7 (p < 0.01), and 11 (p < 0.001), with a trend at Day 14 (p = 0.095), despite a greater number of M1 than M2 macrophages at Day 0 (p = 0.093; Figure 2B).

(A) Expression of CD68 and CD45 by unstimulated BMDMs using flow cytometry. (B) Macrophage proliferation shown through DNA content of polarized M1 and M2 macrophages over 14 days in culture (n = 3; *p < 0.05 M1 significantly different from M2). Gene expression of pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophage markers (C) Inos and (D) Ccr7. Expression of anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophage markers (E) Arg1, (F) Maob, and (G) Spint1 (n = 3; *p < 0.05 Indicated group significantly different from M0, represented by dashed line).

Expression levels of M1 phenotypic markers, Inos and Ccr7, and M2 phenotypic markers, Arg1, Maob, and Spint1, changed over the 14‐day culture duration. Inos expression was significantly higher in M1 macrophages compared to M0 at Day 0 (p < 0.0001), 1 (p < 0.0001), 4 (p < 0.0001), and 7 (p < 0.05), but was comparable at Day 11 and 14, while M2 macrophages showed no change in Inos expression over 14 days in culture (Figure 2C). Ccr7 expression in M1 macrophages was significantly higher at Day 0 (p < 0.0001) and 14 (p < 0.05) compared to M0 macrophages, while M2 macrophages had a transient increase of Ccr7 expression at Day 1 (p < 0.01) and 7 (p < 0.01) compared to M0 macrophages (Figure 2D). Expression of the M2 marker, Arg1, was higher in M2 macrophages at Day 0 (p < 0.0001), 1 (p < 0.01), and 4 (p < 0.05); however, M1 macrophages also had increased expression compared to M0s at all timepoints (Day 0: p < 0.0001, Day 1: p < 0.05, Day 4: p = 0.095, Day 7: p < 0.01, Day 11: p < 0.001, Day 14: p < 0.01; Figure 2E). Maob expression was increased at Day 0 (p < 0.0001) and 1 (p < 0.01) in M2 macrophages and in M1 macrophages at Day 7 (p < 0.01), 11 (p < 0.05), and 14 (p < 0.001; Figure 2F). Similarly, Spint1 expression was increased in M2 macrophages at Day 0 (p < 0.0001) and 1 (p < 0.01), while expression in M1 macrophages was decreased at Day 0 (p = 0.094) and 1 (p = 0.080), but increased at Day 7 (p < 0.05) and 14 (p = 0.083; Figure 2G). Overall, these results demonstrated the plasticity of rat bone marrow‐derived macrophage phenotype over a 14‐day culture duration.

Validation of IVD Explant Culture Conditions

IVD ECM was evaluated over time in culture through GAG changes using Alcian Blue staining, measurement of GAG loss into the media, and tissue GAG content, while collagen content was evaluated using Picrosirius Red staining and tissue collagen content. A partial loss in Alcian Blue staining intensity was observed in the AF and NP regions after 14 days of culture in both disc and macrophage media compared to Day 0 (Figure S1A). Further, GAG content measurements showed a significant decrease in AF and NP GAG content after culture in disc media (NP: p < 0.01; AF: p < 0.001) and macrophage media (NP: p < 0.01; AF: p < 0.01). There was also significantly greater GAG loss into disc media than macrophage media (p < 0.05; Figure S1B). An increase in Picrosirius Red staining intensity of IVD explants was observed after 14‐day culture in disc and macrophage media (Figure S1C). However, this was not reflected in collagen content measurements, where no significant differences were identified in AF collagen content. NP collagen content decreased after 14 days of culture in disc media (p < 0.05), with a trend towards decreased collagen content after culture in macrophage media (p = 0.068; Figure S1D).

Effect of Macrophage Co‐Culture Without Inflammatory Stimulation

Pro‐inflammatory M1 and anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophage co‐cultures with unstimulated IVDs were evaluated for IVD ECM degradation using GAG loss, tissue GAG content, tissue collagen content, Alcian Blue staining, and Picrosirius Red staining. M1 co‐culture caused a significant decrease in average daily GAG loss compared to IVD alone at Day 14 (p < 0.05; Figure 3A). Despite minimal differences between treatment groups, within group differences in daily GAG loss were observed over time. In the IVD group, average daily GAG loss decreased from Day 4 to all other timepoints (Day 7: p < 0.001, Day 11: p < 0.0001, Day 14: p < 0.01), and from Day 7 to Day 11 (p < 0.0001) and 14 (p < 0.01). In the IVD + M1 group, significant differences in average daily GAG loss were observed between all timepoints (Day 4–7: p < 0.01, Day 4–11: p < 0.01, Day 4–14: p < 0.01, Day 7–11: p < 0.05, Day 7–14: p < 0.01, Day 11–14: p < 0.001; Figure 3A). No significant differences in cumulative GAG loss between IVD + M1 co‐culture and IVD alone were observed (Figure 3B). There were also no differences in DNA or GAG content of the AF or NP regions in IVD + M1 co‐culture compared to IVD alone, but when GAG was normalized to DNA, a significant decrease in GAG/DNA content was observed in the AF (p < 0.05; Figure 3C–E). AF and NP collagen content did not significantly differ in IVD + M1 versus IVD groups, even when normalized to DNA content (Figure 3F,G).

(C) DNA, (D) GAG, (E) normalized GAG/DNA, (F) collagen, and (G) normalized collagen/DNA content of IVD alone and co‐cultured with M1 macrophages (n = 5). (H) Daily and (I) cumulative GAG loss from IVD explants alone and co‐cultured with M2 macrophages (n = 7). (O) Alcian Blue and (P) Picrosirius Red staining of IVD explants after 14‐day culture alone or with M1 or M2 macrophages.

The effects of M2 co‐culture on GAG loss were similar to the effects of M1 co‐culture, with a significant decrease in average daily GAG loss at Day 14 in IVD + M2 compared to IVD alone (p < 0.05, Figure 3H). Over time, average daily GAG loss from the IVD alone group decreased from Day 4 to all other timepoints (Day 7: p < 0.001, Day 11: p < 0.0001, Day 14: p < 0.01) and from Day 7 to Day 11 (p < 0.0001) and 14 (p < 0.01). For IVD + M2, average daily GAG loss decreased from Day 4 to all other timepoints (Day 7: p < 0.05, Day 11: p < 0.05, Day 14: p < 0.01) and from Day 7 (p < 0.01) and 11 (p < 0.01) to Day 14 (Figure 3H). However, no significant differences in cumulative GAG loss were observed (Figure 3I). There were no significant differences in DNA or GAG content, but when normalized, GAG/DNA content in the AF decreased after M2 co‐culture compared to IVD alone (p < 0.05; Figure 3J–L). M2 macrophage co‐culture also had no significant effect on IVD collagen content in either the AF or NP regions (Figure 3M,N).

Contrasting changes in GAG content, Alcian Blue staining intensity in histology suggested a decrease in tissue GAG after 14‐day co‐culture with M1 macrophages, and better maintenance of tissue GAG after co‐culture with M2 macrophages (Figure 3O). Similarly, M2 macrophage co‐culture appeared to prevent the increase in Picrosirius Red staining intensity that occurred in IVD alone over 14‐day culture, although this was not shown in collagen content measurements (Figure 3P).

M1 and M2 macrophage co‐cultures with unstimulated IVDs were also evaluated for inflammation using NO release. M1 macrophage co‐culture significantly increased daily NO release at Day 4 (p < 0.0001) and 7 (p < 0.01) and cumulative NO release at all timepoints (Day 4: p < 0.0001, Day 7: p < 0.0001, Day 11: p < 0.0001, Day 14: p < 0.0001) compared to IVD alone (Figure 4A,B). NO release also significantly decreased over time in the IVD group from Day 4 to Day 11 (p < 0.05) and 14 (p < 0.05). In the IVD + M1 group, NO release decreased from Day 4 to all other timepoints (Day 7: p < 0.0001, Day 11: p < 0.0001, Day 14: p < 0.0001) and from Day 7 to Day 11 (p < 0.0001) and 14 (p < 0.0001; Figure 4A). Minimal differences in NO release were observed between IVD + M2 macrophage co‐culture and IVD alone, likely because an inflammatory stimulus was not present in the culture system. Discussion

The inflammatory microenvironment within the degenerating IVD is known to promote cytokine and chemokine production by IVD cells leading to macrophage recruitment [16, 17, 18, 52]. While inflammation and ECM degradation have been shown to be exacerbated as a result, the contributions of the individual IVD and macrophage cell types remain unknown. Additionally, modulation of the degenerative disc environment by the M1 and M2 macrophage subtypes has not been fully elucidated. The aim of this paper was to create a macrophage‐IVD explant co‐culture model to investigate the impact of crosstalk on both inflammation and IVD ECM composition. TNFα inflammatory stimulation and macrophage co‐culture with IVD explants had an impact on both inflammation and IVD ECM composition. TNFα treatment of IVDs increased concentrations of inflammatory cytokines in media supernatants and decreased IVD GAG content, while collagen content was regionally increased in the AF and decreased in the NP. Macrophages had negligible effects on GAG content; however, both macrophage subtypes reversed the detrimental effects of TNFα stimulation on IVD collagen content. The effects of macrophage co‐culture on inflammation were dependent on macrophage polarization state, with pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages exacerbating inflammation through increased NO and IL‐6 production, while anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages reduced levels of the inflammatory mediators, NO and TNFα, and increased levels of the anti‐inflammatory cytokine, IL‐13. The model development outlined herein sheds light on the importance of considering the requirements of multiple cell and tissue types within in vitro culture, in the creation of co‐culture models that more closely recreate in vivo microenvironments.

Inflammation in Macrophage‐IVD Explant Co‐Cultures

In an in vitro setting, inflammatory stimulation with TNFα is relevant to simulate the IVD inflammatory environment, as increased expression has been previously identified in degenerating human IVDs [10, 12, 14, 53, 54]. In this study, TNFα stimulation of IVD explants caused increased NO, TNFα, and CCL2 concentrations in media supernatants (Figures 6A–C,F and S3A,B). This is consistent with previous studies utilizing TNFα or IL‐1β treatment of IVD cells isolated from bovine or human IVD tissue that showed increased expression of multiple inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including NO, IL‐6, and CCL2, for up to 7 days [14, 53, 54, 55]. The findings of the current study demonstrate a more sustained inflammatory response starting at Day 4 and continuing for 14 days, using TNFα stimulation of IVD explants.

While inflammatory stimulation of IVD explants with TNFα caused a sustained inflammatory response, macrophage co‐culture modulated this effect, with M1 and M2 macrophages having contrasting impacts. Both with and without TNFα inflammatory stimulation, M1 macrophage–IVD explant co‐culture promoted NO release for up to 7 days (Figure 4A). However, the impact was sustained more effectively in the presence of inflammatory stimulation with TNFα, where a trend towards increased NO in IVD + TNFα + M1 samples was still observed at Day 11 compared to IVD + TNFα alone (Figure 8A). As disc cells typically produce inflammatory cytokines and chemokines prior to recruitment of macrophages, the relevance of macrophage co‐culture without TNFα stimulation may be limited, leading to few additional findings (Figures 3 and 4) [16, 17, 18, 52]. Indeed, production of the chemokine CCL2 was more prominent from TNFα‐treated IVDs than from macrophages, supporting the importance of the inflammatory microenvironment in promoting macrophage recruitment (Figures 6F and 8F,L).

Importantly, M1 and M2 macrophages appeared to have detrimental and protective effects, respectively, in co‐culture with TNFα‐stimulated IVD explants. Namely, concentrations of the inflammatory cytokines, NO and IL‐6, were increased in IVD + TNFα + M1 co‐cultures compared to IVD + TNFα alone (Figure 8A,B,D). Interestingly, IL‐6 production from both M1 macrophages and TNFα‐treated IVDs was negligible, but was substantially increased in co‐culture, suggesting crosstalk between M1 macrophages and the IVD is required for IL‐6 production (Figures 6D and 8D). Similar findings have been demonstrated in the literature, where it was suggested that most IL‐6 producing cells were macrophages and IL‐6 production was dependent on secreted factors from inflamed IVDs [19, 31, 33, 56, 57]. In contrast, M2 macrophage co‐culture appeared to more robustly regulate concentrations of other inflammatory cytokines. M2 macrophage–IVD explant co‐culture significantly decreased TNFα concentrations compared to TNFα‐stimulated IVDs alone, suggesting a protective, anti‐inflammatory effect of M2 macrophage co‐culture (Figure 8I). While other studies have shown decreased gene expression of inflammatory cytokines from treatment of IVD cells with M2 macrophage conditioned media, this study offers validation of such observations at the protein level in a co‐culture model, increasing the biological and translational relevance [44, 45].

Macrophage polarization also appeared to be influenced by crosstalk with TNFα‐treated IVDs. For example, concentrations of the M2 macrophage stimulant, IL‐13, were significantly increased in M2 macrophages alone compared to M2 macrophages co‐cultured with TNFα‐treated IVDs, suggesting IVD co‐culture caused suppression of IL‐13 production, and a potential shift away from the M2 macrophage phenotype (Figure 8K). Similarly, at Day 4, IL‐4 production was increased in groups containing M2 macrophages, and IFNγ concentrations were greater in groups containing M1 macrophages, but further increases from Day 4 to 14 were not observed, suggesting a loss of the original macrophage polarization state in culture over time (Figure S4A,F′). Additionally, while macrophage cell number decreased from Day 11 to Day 14 when cultured independently, macrophage viability appeared higher when co‐cultured with IVD explants, suggesting that biological cues from IVD tissues delay macrophage cell death (Data Not Shown). These observations in combination with the change in phenotype observed in M1‐ and M2‐polarized macrophages over time, demonstrate the plasticity of the macrophage phenotype, and its relationship with the inflammatory microenvironment (Figure 2).

Effect of M1/M2 Macrophage Co‐Culture on IVD ECM Composition

The inflammatory microenvironment and recruited macrophages in degenerated IVDs are thought to contribute to altered ECM composition [14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. ECM changes characteristic of IVD degeneration include degradation of proteoglycans leading to decreased GAG content and altered collagen content that varies regionally within the IVD. Specifically, decreased AF collagen and fibrosis of the NP have previously been observed [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. In this study, changes in GAG content were not observed in IVD explant cultures in macrophage media; however, a significant increase in GAG loss by Day 14 could be indicative of GAG degradation (Figure 5A–E). Additionally, collagen content decreased in the AF and increased in the NP in TNFα‐stimulated IVDs compared to unstimulated IVDs, mimicking observations from human degenerated samples (Figure 5F,G). Importantly, these findings o ECM changes expand on prior studies where measurement of altered ECM composition was limited to gene expression analysis [39, 40, 44, 45].

Macrophages are known to produce matrix‐degrading enzymes, indicating an ability to modulate IVD ECM composition [14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. The impact of macrophages on ECM can vary greatly due to the plasticity of the macrophage phenotype; however, pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages are generally regarded as pro‐fibrotic and involved in degradation, while anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophages are pro‐healing and regulate ECM turnover [58]. In this study, neither macrophage subtype significantly affected IVD GAG content; however, analysis of collagen content showed both M1 and M2 macrophage co‐culture with IVD explants prevented the degradative and fibrotic effects from TNFα stimulation alone. M1 macrophage co‐culture appeared to have a greater effect on the AF region, with increased collagen content compared to TNFα‐treated IVDs alone potentially suggesting better maintenance of collagen over 14 days in culture (Figure 7F,G). While M1 macrophages are typically involved in matrix degradation, this could be indicative of a shift in macrophage phenotype over time in culture, altering the macrophages' impact on ECM composition [58]. M2 macrophage co‐culture more prominently impacted the NP region, with decreased collagen content in the IVD + TNFα + M2 versus IVD + TNFα group, indicating potential prevention of fibrosis, which is consistent with the role of M2 macrophages in matrix remodeling (Figure 7M,N). Together these findings indicate that the novel macrophage‐IVD explant co‐culture model used in this study effectively recapitulates both the inflammatory response and IVD ECM compositional changes known to occur during IVD degeneration.

Impact of Media Composition on IVD Response

Interestingly, the magnitude of inflammatory effects as well as the mechanism of ECM degradation in TNFα‐treated IVDs appeared to be dependent on media composition (i.e., disc or macrophage media). Specifically, the magnitude of NO release from IVD explants after TNFα stimulation in disc media was greater than in macrophage media. The main differences between the media compositions were the addition of LCM and lower glucose concentration in macrophage media, while disc media contained higher concentrations of glucose and no LCM. Previous efforts have been made to better understand the best culture conditions for IVD cells and tissues from a variety of sources [59]. These studies concluded that media containing high concentrations of glucose was recommended for 2D culture of NP cells from rodents, while low glucose concentrations were recommended for 3D culture of NP cells encapsulated in alginate. However, neither of these recommendations addressed the needs and additional considerations for a whole IVD explant model containing native NP and AF ECM structures. Additionally, the impact of culture conditions on the inflammatory response of IVD cells was not addressed. Other previous literature has demonstrated an impact of low glucose concentrations on AF and NP cell viability, and inflammatory and catabolic processes, providing a possible explanation for the difference in NO release observed in response to TNFα stimulation of IVD explants in this study [60, 61].

Another important consideration of the media compositions that could account for the differing inflammatory responses, is the addition of LCM in macrophage media. LCM is produced from the culture of L929 mouse fibroblasts, and contains the cytokine, mCSF, required for macrophage viability. While media supplementation with recombinant mCSF has been used as an alternative to LCM, the polarization potential of rat macrophages cultured in LCM is better established in previous literature, making it the more reliable option in this study [47]. In addition to mCSF, LCM also contains an abundance of other secreted factors that have the potential to impact both the inflammatory response and ECM degradation. Previous studies have used proteomic analysis to identify these additional components within LCM [46]. These included the immunomodulatory factors, MIF, CCL2, and TGF‐β1, which have the potential to influence inflammation, providing a possible explanation for the dampened inflammatory response of IVD explants to TNFα treatment in macrophage media compared to disc media.

LCM also contains the ECM proteins, Fibronectin, Actin, and Collagen II, indicating possible factors that could have impacted ECM degradation of IVD explants within macrophage media as well [46]. While TNFα stimulation of IVD explants in disc media primarily affected GAG content, stimulation in macrophage media had a greater impact on GAG loss (Figures 5A–E and S3C–G). The presence of ECM proteins within the macrophage media could have influenced the dynamics of ECM degradation, impacting GAG loss rather than GAG content. Alternatively, the higher glucose content of disc media may have caused ECM production in unstimulated IVDs that was suppressed by stimulation with TNFα (Figure S3G). Supporting this theory, the sum of AF GAG, NP GAG, and released GAG from unstimulated IVDs in disc media was greater than in macrophage media after 14 days of culture, which could suggest GAG production over time in culture (Figure S1B).

The impact of media composition on both inflammatory response and ECM degradation is especially important to consider in the configuration of a co‐culture model containing multiple tissue or cell types (i.e., IVD explants and macrophages) with differing culture requirements. Nevertheless, in both disc and macrophage media, TNFα stimulation of IVD explants in vitro promoted both inflammation and alterations to IVD ECM composition within 14 days of culture (Figures 5, 6, and S3).

Limitations

There are many considerations in the optimization of a co‐culture system, which lead to inherent limitations. While this study investigated broad inflammatory changes within the co‐culture system, it did not address differences in inflammation between the AF and NP regions. Increased expression of the inflammatory cytokines, Tnfa, Il1b, and Il6, has been observed in NP cells compared to AF cells in response to inflammatory stimulation, indicating possible differences in the inflammatory response between IVD regions [62]. Additionally, M2 macrophages can be further divided into 2 subtypes: M2a and M2c [63]. While the effects of M2a macrophages were investigated extensively in the current study, the other M2 subtype was not explored. Both M2a and M2c macrophages have anti‐inflammatory and matrix remodeling properties that make their individual roles difficult to distinguish. As both are known to be present in the degenerating IVD, future exploration of the M2c phenotype may be warranted. Further, DNA content was used as a normalization factor for ECM content; however, previous studies have shown changes in cellularity during IVD degeneration, indicating that DNA content is not the optimal normalizer and wet weight or dry weight may be better alternatives [6]. Finally, the small sample size limited the significance of some findings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study advance understanding of how crosstalk between the IVD and macrophages modulates inflammation and IVD ECM composition. The use of primary bone marrow‐derived macrophages increased the physiological relevance of the model, while the use of IVD explants allowed for analysis of ECM content, adding to the novelty of this co‐culture system. Applying this model, inflammatory stimulation of IVD explants with TNFα promoted increased inflammation and ECM changes, which were dependent on media composition, where glucose concentration and LCM may have contributed to the differences observed. Co‐culture of IVD explants with either macrophage subtype prevented the detrimental effects of TNFα stimulation on IVD collagen content. Further, pro‐inflammatory M1 and anti‐inflammatory M2 macrophage‐IVD explant co‐cultures had detrimental and protective effects on inflammation, respectively. The secretion of cytokines, such as IL‐6 and IL‐13, appeared to be dependent on crosstalk between macrophages and the IVD, supporting the physiological relevance of co‐culture over unidirectional conditioned media studies. Overall, the macrophage‐IVD explant co‐culture model created herein recapitulates many of the characteristics of inflammation and altered ECM composition observed after in vivo injury, while providing additional insight into the contributions of cell types (i.e., IVD cells and macrophages) present within the degenerative disc environment.
