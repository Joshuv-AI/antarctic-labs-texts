Zebrafish larvae reveal early neuropathology and host responses to nervous necrosis virus
Authors: Jie Gao, Jing Jiang, Junzhe Zhang, Rui Zheng, Siyou Huang, Qijin Miao, Bingya Wu, Jian He, Jianguo He, Junfeng Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNervous necrosis virus (NNV) is a major neurotropic pathogen in aquaculture, but early host responses to infection remain poorly defined. Here, we used zebrafish larvae at 4 days post-fertilization and a bath immersion approach to examine the early phase of NNV infection. Infected larvae showed progressive viral accumulation, capsid protein production, and recovery of infectious virus from larval homogenates. Histological and ultrastructural analyses revealed early neural injury, including retinal disruption, mitochondrial swelling, and virus-like particles in brain tissue. Infection was also associated with altered locomotor behavior. Transcriptomic profiling further revealed activation of innate antiviral, oxidative stress, cytokine, and stress-response pathways, together with suppression of genes involved in neuronal maintenance, DNA replication, and cell cycle progression. These findings define an early pathological and transcriptional profile of NNV infection and highlight zebrafish larvae as a useful vertebrate system for studying early host-virus interactions in aquaculture.Keywords: nervous necrosis virus, neurotoxicity, innate immunity, transcriptomics

Introduction

Nervous necrosis virus (NNV), an RNA virus of the genus Betanodavirus, is a waterborne pathogen responsible for significant disease outbreaks in aquaculture. NNV is primarily transmitted through the aquatic environment, which acts both as a vector and reservoir, enabling efficient viral spread among farmed fish populations.1,2,3 NNV mainly infects larval and juvenile fish, causing extensive damage to the central nervous system (CNS) and leading to viral nervous necrosis (VNN). Infected fish exhibit characteristic symptoms such as whirling swimming, loss of balance, pigmentation changes, retinal degeneration, and high mortality.4,5,6 To date, NNV has been reported to infect more than 140 economically important marine and freshwater fish species including the orange spotted grouper (Epinephelus coioides),3 European seabass (Dicentrarchus labrax),7 Senegal sole (Solea senegalensis),8 and gilthead seabream (Sparus aurata),9 among others resulting in severe economic losses and threatening the sustainability of global aquaculture.

Although significant progress has been made in understanding NNV biology, most current studies rely on in vitro experiments using highly susceptible fish cell lines such as Grouper spleen (GS) cells1 and Grouper brain (GB) cells.10 However, Epinephelus species, the natural hosts of NNV, are difficult to maintain and breed under laboratory conditions, making it challenging to obtain a stable supply of sensitive fish for controlled infection studies. These limitations restrict systematic investigations into infection mechanisms, replication dynamics, and host immune responses.11 Moreover, cell-based systems cannot fully capture the complexity of whole organism host-pathogen interactions, hindering comprehensive studies on viral pathogenesis, antiviral immunity, and the development of effective therapeutic or preventive strategies.12,13,14

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) has long been widely used in developmental biology15 and has emerged as an ideal model for studying aquatic virus host interactions under controlled laboratory conditions, including infections with spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV).16 Zebrafish possess a mammal-like innate and adaptive immune system, with conserved interferon mediated antiviral pathways, and their fully sequenced genome allows versatile genetic manipulation including knockouts, knock ins, and transgenic lines for in vivo tracking of specific cell populations.17,18,19 These advantages have led to their widespread application in virology research involving human norovirus (HuNoV),20 Zika virus (ZIKV),21 influenza A virus (IAV),22 cyprinid herpesvirus 3 (CyHV-3),23 covert mortality nodavirus (CMNV),24 and viral hemorrhagic septicemia virus (VHSV).25

Several studies have evaluated zebrafish as a model for NNV infection. These studies indicate that susceptibility depends strongly on larval stage and route of challenge, with direct injection routes generally producing more evident mortality than bath exposure.26,27 Later work further showed that larvae can also be infected by bath exposure and develop typical clinical signs and CNS vacuolization.28 Natural infections have also been documented, with zebrafish exhibiting classical VNN pathology and testing positive for red-spotted grouper NNV (RGNNV) like viruses.29 While these studies confirm that zebrafish is a permissive host for betanodaviruses, they mainly focused on mortality, histopathological verification, or innate immune responses. Systematic analyses of early larval infection including replication kinetics, neural injury, locomotor impairment, and the establishment of a standardized platform suitable for downstream mechanistic studies remain limited.

In this study, we used zebrafish larvae as the experimental host and the orange-spotted grouper nervous necrosis virus Hainan strain (OGNNV-HN1) as the challenge virus to refine and comprehensively evaluate a controlled bath exposure infection model. By integrating viral load quantification, histopathology, ultrastructural characterization, locomotor behavioral assays, and olig2-based neural imaging, we demonstrate that zebrafish larvae recapitulate key pathological features observed in farmed fish, including active viral replication, CNS damage, and movement deficits. This immersion-based larval system provides a tractable vertebrate framework for studying early host-virus interactions and NNV-associated neurotoxicity in vivo.

Results

Establishment of an NNV infection model in zebrafish

To evaluate the pathogenicity of NNV in zebrafish larvae, 4 days post-fertilization (dpf) AB and Tg (olig2:DsRed2) larvae were exposed to the undiluted viral stock or serially diluted viral suspensions, and mortality rates were recorded (Figures 1A and S1A). Mortality increased in a dose-dependent manner. Analysis of the mortality data showed that the estimated median lethal concentration (LC50) at 12 h was approximately 1.43 × 105 TCID50/mL, as indicated by the dashed line in Figure 1A. This concentration was therefore selected for subsequent formal infection experiments.

NNV RNA and CP protein accumulation in zebrafish larvae

To further investigate the early infection dynamics of NNV in zebrafish larvae, we quantified the relative RNA levels of viral CP and RdRp at 0, 4, 8, and 12 h post-infection. CP RNA levels increased progressively within the measured time window and reached the highest level at 12 h, indicating progressive viral RNA accumulation during early infection (Figure 1B). Similarly, RdRp RNA levels showed a time-dependent increase, with the highest level observed at 12 h (Figure 1C).

Western blot analysis further showed a gradual increase in CP protein abundance following infection. The CP protein band intensity increased progressively from 4 to 12 h, supporting the accumulation of viral structural protein in zebrafish larvae during early infection (Figure 1D). Together, these results indicate that NNV RNA and CP protein accumulate in a time-dependent manner in infected zebrafish larvae.

Absolute quantification confirms rapid NNV accumulation in zebrafish larvae

The results showed that the Ct values of the CP and RdRp targets exhibited a strong linear correlation with the logarithm of plasmid copy numbers. The linear equations were y = −3.9875x + 34.911 (R2 = 0.998, CP) (Figure S1B) and y = −4.5776x + 35.969 (R2 = 0.998, RdRp) (Figure S1C), indicating good linearity of the absolute quantitative PCR (AQ-PCR) assays.

Absolute quantification further supported progressive viral accumulation in infected larvae. Based on the standard curves, both CP and RdRp copy numbers increased over time from 0 to 12 h post-infection (Figures 1E and 1F; Table 1). Detectable viral copies were already present at 0 h, consistent with the initial larva-associated viral input after immersion exposure and washing. By 8 h, both targets had increased to the 103 range, supporting rapid early viral accumulation after immersion challenge. By 12 h, CP and RdRp copy numbers further increased to the 105 and 104 ranges, respectively. Notably, the first larval deaths were observed at 8 h, when both viral targets had reached the 103 range, whereas the higher mortality observed at 12 h was accompanied by a further increase in viral burden. These data support a close temporal association between rapid viral expansion and early larval death in this model.

Recovery and titration of infectious NNV from infected zebrafish larvae in SC1 cells

To assess whether infectious NNV could be recovered from infected zebrafish larvae, SC1 cells were inoculated with filtered larval homogenates collected at 12 h post-infection. In the mock group, SC1 cells maintained normal morphology throughout the 5-day observation period, forming tightly packed monolayers with no detectable cytopathic changes (Figure 2A, top row). In contrast, SC1 cells exposed to homogenates from NNV-infected larvae developed progressively pronounced cytopathic effects (CPE), beginning with mild cell rounding and reduced confluency at 3 days post-inoculation (dpi) and progressing to severe cell shrinkage, detachment, and widespread lysis by 5 dpi (Figure 2A, bottom row). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis of SC1 culture supernatants revealed increasing levels of viral CP and RdRp RNA from 1 to 5 dpi (Figures 2B and 2C). In addition, endpoint dilution assay based on CPE observed in SC1 cells at 5 dpi showed that the 12 h infected larval homogenate contained detectable infectious virus, with a calculated titer of approximately 2.37 × 104 TCID50/mL (Table S1).

NNV infection is associated with neural and retinal tissue damage in zebrafish larvae

NNV infection was associated with marked alterations in olig2-labeled neural-lineage cells and retinal tissue in zebrafish larvae. In Tg (olig2:DsRed2) transgenic larvae, DsRed fluorescence driven by the olig2 promoter was clearly visible and continuous in the control group (Figure 3A). In contrast, NNV-infected larvae showed a marked reduction in DsRed fluorescence intensity (Figure 3B), suggesting disruption or reduction of olig2-associated neural-lineage cells. This observation was quantitatively supported by flow cytometry, which showed that the proportion of DsRed+ cells in the infected group at 12 h post-infection was significantly lower than that in controls (3.3% vs. 14.34%; Figures 3C–3E). Consistently, qRT-PCR analysis revealed a progressive decline in olig2 mRNA expression during infection, with a significant decrease at 12 h post-infection (Figure 3F). Western blot analysis further supported this trend, showing a time-dependent reduction in Olig2 protein levels (Figure 3G). Together, these results indicate that NNV infection is associated with disruption of olig2-labeled neural-lineage populations during early infection.

H&E staining of eye tissue revealed well-organized retinal layers with intact cellular morphology in the control group (Figures 4A and 4B). In contrast, infected larvae exhibited disorganized retinal structure, reduced cellular density, and signs of necrotic damage (Figure 4C), indicating structural disruption of visual tissue following NNV infection. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further revealed ultrastructural alterations in brain tissue (Figure 4D). Mitochondria in the infected group displayed swelling, vacuolization, and disruption of cristae structure (Figure 4E). In addition, virus-like particles consistent with NNV morphology were observed in infected samples (Figure 4F, red arrow), supporting the presence of viral particles in neural tissue. Collectively, these observations indicate that NNV infection is associated with early injury to neural and visual tissues in zebrafish larvae.

Effects of NNV infection on zebrafish locomotor behavior

To investigate the behavioral effects of NNV infection in zebrafish larvae, locomotor activity was monitored at 0, 4, 8, and 12 h post-infection and analyzed using representative heatmaps (Figure 5). The color scale indicates swimming velocity (mm/s), with blue representing low movement and red representing high movement. At 0 h, larvae showed relatively limited movement. As infection progressed, swimming trajectories became broader, more dispersed, and more irregular. At 8 and 12 h, NNV-infected larvae showed more frequent high-velocity regions and more pronounced alterations in locomotor patterns than control larvae. These representative heatmaps suggest that NNV exposure was associated with altered larval locomotor behavior during early infection. Morphological observation showed that control larvae maintained normal body morphology, whereas NNV-infected larvae exhibited spinal curvature, shortened body length, and pigment aggregation (Figure S2).

Host molecular responses to NNV infection in zebrafish larvae

Based on the zebrafish larval NNV infection model established in this study, we further applied RNA-seq to analyze transcriptomic changes between the 0 h post-exposure baseline group and the 12 h post-infection group (Figure 6A). More than 90% of the clean reads were mapped to the zebrafish reference genome, and most mapped reads were uniquely aligned (Table S2). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between the 0 h baseline group and the 12 h post-infection group, indicating substantial transcriptional remodeling during early NNV infection (Figure 6B). Using DESeq2 with Q ≤ 0.05 as the significance threshold, we identified 1,521 upregulated genes and 1,274 downregulated genes at 12 h relative to the 0 h baseline group (Figures 6C and 6D), indicating extensive changes in host gene expression during early infection.

GO enrichment analysis showed that upregulated genes were significantly enriched in terms related to mitochondrial electron transport, oxidative phosphorylation, intracellular iron ion sequestration, death receptor agonist activity, and ubiquitination-related processes (Figure 7A). Conversely, downregulated genes were predominantly enriched in terms associated with DNA repair and cell-cycle processes, including double-strand break repair via break-induced replication, CMG complex assembly, DNA replication initiation, cytoskeletal organization, and microtubule-organizing center formation (Figure 7B).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further supported these findings. Upregulated genes were mainly enriched in metabolic pathways, oxidative phosphorylation, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, and the FOXO signaling pathway (Figure 7C), suggesting activation of antiviral and cytokine-related responses together with metabolic remodeling following NNV infection. In contrast, downregulated genes were primarily enriched in pathways related to the cell cycle, cellular senescence, p53 signaling, Wnt signaling, and DNA replication (Figure 7D).

Validation of RNA-seq results by qRT-PCR analysis

To assess the reliability of the RNA-seq dataset, 12 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected for qRT-PCR validation. These included six upregulated genes (foxo1a, tnfα, hsp70, c-fos, il10, and mlycd) and six downregulated genes (olig2, sox10, sox9b, mapk3, wnk1a, and tap1) identified from the RNA-seq analysis. The qRT-PCR results showed expression trends consistent with those obtained from RNA-seq (Figure 8). Although modest quantitative differences were observed between the two approaches for some genes, the overall expression patterns were consistent, supporting the reliability of the RNA-seq dataset.

Discussion

Advantages of zebrafish larvae for modeling early NNV infection

NNV is a major neurotropic pathogen in aquaculture and causes high mortality in a wide range of marine and freshwater fish species. Although larval groupers and other farmed fish are natural or highly susceptible hosts, their maintenance and experimental manipulation under laboratory conditions are technically demanding, limiting their utility for controlled mechanistic studies of viral pathogenesis and host responses. As a result, many NNV studies still rely heavily on susceptible fish cell lines, such as GB and GS cells.1,10,30 Although these in vitro systems have provided important insights into viral replication and innate immune regulation, they cannot fully capture organism-level neural pathology, behavioral consequences, or systemic host responses.

Zebrafish larvae provide a complementary vertebrate system for studying early NNV infection in vivo. In the present study, an immersion-based larval workflow enabled integrated analysis of viral accumulation, infectious virus recovery, neural and retinal tissue injury, locomotor alterations, and host transcriptional responses during the early stage of infection. Compared with injection-based approaches, this bath exposure system more closely reflects waterborne viral exposure and allows early host-virus interactions to be examined in an intact vertebrate organism. Thus, the present model provides a tractable in vivo framework for studying early NNV-associated neuropathology and host responses.

NNV replication dynamics in infected zebrafish

The rapid accumulation of NNV in zebrafish larvae during early infection highlights their capacity to support viral amplification, as evidenced by progressive increases in CP and RdRp RNA copy numbers within the 0–12 h observation window. Consistent with nodavirus biology, CP accumulated to higher levels than RdRp, reflecting the transcriptional bias toward RNA2 and structural protein production.31,32 Similar replication patterns have been reported in other susceptible hosts, including hybrid grouper33 and Asian seabass,34 suggesting that this replication profile may be conserved across permissive fish hosts.

The mortality kinetics observed in our immersion model differ from those reported in some previous zebrafish-NNV studies, in which bath immersion induced limited or delayed mortality.27 Such discrepancies may reflect differences in viral strain or genotype, inoculum preparation, viral dose, host developmental stage, immersion conditions, and mortality criteria. In our model, 4 dpf larvae were challenged with OGNNV-HN1 under a defined immersion protocol, and the 12 h LC50 was 1.43 × 105 TCID50/mL. These conditions may favor rapid early pathological progression compared with other experimental settings.

Importantly, the 12 h infection window was supported by direct virological evidence. In addition to increased CP and RdRp RNA and CP protein accumulation, infectious virus was recovered from 12 h infected larval homogenates, with an infectious titer of approximately 2.37 × 104 TCID50/mL in SC1 cells. Although this does not fully explain the acute mortality observed in the immersion model, it demonstrates that infectious virus was already present within this early time frame, rather than reflecting detection of viral nucleic acid alone.

Developmental susceptibility further helps contextualize these results. Previous work showed that zebrafish larvae exhibit stage-dependent susceptibility to NNV bath exposure, with mortality largely restricted to the fourth day post-fertilization.28,35 In addition, another zebrafish NNV-related study reported rapid early viral progression, with early viral genes predominating at 2 h and late structural genes increasing toward 12 h.36 Together, these findings indicate that zebrafish-NNV infection outcomes are highly context-dependent and shaped by specific combinations of viral genotype, host developmental stage, and exposure conditions. Therefore, the OGNNV-HN1 immersion infection model in 4 dpf zebrafish larvae should be viewed as a complementary system for studying rapid early NNV amplification and host responses after waterborne exposure.

Impact of NNV infection on nervous system damage

NNV is a strongly neurotropic virus that primarily targets the CNS and induces characteristic neuropathological changes in a wide range of teleosts.37,38 In our zebrafish model, infected larvae showed a pronounced reduction in olig2-driven DsRed fluorescence, together with disorganization of retinal layers and decreased cellular density. These lesions are consistent with previous observations in NNV-infected groupers and other farmed species, where neuronal apoptosis or necrosis, axonal degeneration, glial proliferation, and severe tissue disruption have been reported.39,40,41 Similarly, retinal degeneration and loss of structural integrity have been described in shi drum and European sea bass following NNV infection,11,42,43 supporting the view that NNV exhibits strong tropism for CNS and visual system structures across host species.

The behavioral observations are also consistent with neural tissue injury in infected larvae. During the early stage of infection, NNV-infected larvae displayed altered locomotor patterns, including broader swimming trajectories and more irregular activity than controls. Although these observations were based mainly on representative heatmaps, they are consistent with the neurological abnormalities reported in NNV-infected fish.6,44

A notable feature of our model is the decline in DsRed+ olig2-labeled cells in the head region during the early phase of infection. Olig2 is a key transcription factor involved in the development and maintenance of neural-lineage populations, including motor neuron-associated and oligodendrocyte-lineage cells. Its expression can be sensitive to inflammatory and viral insults in the CNS. Therefore, the early decline in olig2 expression and Olig2 protein levels in infected zebrafish may reflect disruption of olig2-associated neural-lineage populations rather than damage to a single cell type. Together with mitochondrial ultrastructural alterations, these findings suggest that disruption of olig2-associated neural programs is an early component of NNV-associated neuropathology in zebrafish larvae.

Host transcriptional responses during early NNV infection

RNA-seq analysis revealed extensive transcriptional remodeling in zebrafish larvae during early NNV infection, with many genes significantly altered in expression. One prominent feature of this response was the enrichment of mitochondrial energy-related pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation and electron transport. Similar metabolic shifts have been reported during other RNA virus infections and are often interpreted as reflecting increased energetic demands associated with viral RNA synthesis, protein translation, and virion assembly.45 In our data, this metabolic remodeling was accompanied by enrichment of terms related to intracellular iron sequestration and death receptor activity, suggesting the involvement of oxidative stress-related and cell death-associated processes during early infection, in agreement with previous observations in fish virus infections.46

In parallel, immune and cytokine-related signaling pathways were prominently enriched. The JAK-STAT signaling pathway and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction were both significantly enriched after NNV exposure. Previous studies have shown that the JAK-STAT pathway is a central mediator of antiviral innate immunity, linking interferon signaling to the induction of antiviral interferon-stimulated genes and proinflammatory cytokines during viral infection.47 The enrichment of this pathway in zebrafish larvae suggests that an innate antiviral program is engaged during early NNV infection. The enrichment of the FOXO signaling pathway is also consistent with reports that FOXO transcription factors integrate cytokine and stress signals to regulate apoptosis, oxidative stress resistance, and immune modulation in fish,48 indicating that stress-adaptive mechanisms may be involved in the larval response to NNV.

By contrast, pathways associated with cell-cycle control, DNA replication, and DNA repair were predominantly downregulated. Suppression of cell-cycle regulators and DNA synthesis has been described for several other fish viruses, including Iridoviridae49,50 and carp viruses,51 and is thought to reflect a shift of infected cells away from proliferation toward stress and damage responses, or a viral strategy to impair host cell renewal and facilitate cytopathic effects.52,53 The transcriptomic signatures observed in NNV-infected zebrafish larvae are therefore consistent with a coordinated host response that couples metabolic remodeling and immune signaling with suppression of cell-cycle progression.

Limitations of the study

Despite providing important insight into early NNV infection in zebrafish larvae, this study has several limitations. First, our analyses were restricted to the early phase of infection (0–12 h) and therefore do not address later pathological progression or long-term infection outcomes. Second, although the immersion model reproduced key features of infection, including viral accumulation, infectious virus recovery, neural injury, and locomotor alterations, future studies incorporating more detailed quantitative behavioral analyses would further strengthen functional assessment of infection-induced locomotor changes. Third, transcriptomic analysis focused on a single post-infection time point, providing a snapshot of the host response rather than the full temporal dynamics of specific immune, metabolic, and stress-related pathways. In addition, because zebrafish larvae at 4 dpf are not sexually differentiated, sex-associated effects could not be assessed in this study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Finally, because infection outcome may vary with viral strain, larval developmental stage, and exposure conditions, broader comparative studies will be needed to further evaluate the generalizability of the infection kinetics and pathological features observed in this zebrafish NNV infection model.

Experimental model and study participant details

Zebrafish

AB wild-type zebrafish and Tg(olig2:DsRed2) transgenic zebrafish larvae were used in this study. Adult AB zebrafish were obtained from the National Aquatic Biological Resource Center (China), and Tg(olig2) zebrafish were obtained from the China Zebrafish Resource Center. Fish were maintained in the zebrafish facility of the School of Life Sciences, Sun Yat-sen University (China), under standard laboratory conditions. Healthy 4 days post-fertilization (dpf) larvae were used for NNV infection experiments. Because zebrafish larvae at 4 dpf are not sexually differentiated, sex was not determined or used as a biological variable in this study. All fish handling procedures followed the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Sun Yat-sen University and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. SYSU-LS-IACUC-2025-0056).

Cell lines

The SC1 cell line, a clonal line derived from SSN-1 cells by limiting dilution in our laboratory, was used for NNV propagation, virus isolation, and infectivity assays. SC1 cells were maintained in Leibovitz’s L-15 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) under standard culture conditions, as described in Method details. The SC1 cell line was authenticated based on its origin, morphology, growth characteristics, and characteristic cytopathic effects following NNV infection. The cells were routinely monitored for microbial contamination and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination before use in this study.

Method details

Zebrafish husbandry

Adult zebrafish were maintained according to the standard operating procedures of the National Zebrafish Resource Center. Fish were kept at 28.5°C under a 14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod. For breeding, adult males and females were separated overnight and paired at a 1:1 male-to-female ratio the following morning. Fertilized eggs were obtained by natural spawning, and normally developing embryos were selected and transferred into E2 medium supplemented with 0.5 mg/L methylene blue. Embryos were incubated at 28.5°C under controlled light conditions until use in subsequent experiments.54 During incubation, embryonic development was regularly monitored, and dead or abnormal embryos were promptly removed to minimize contamination risks and ensure the health and consistency of experimental subjects.

Cell culture and virus

SC1 cells were cultured at 28°C in Leibovitz’s L-15 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The OGNNV-HN1 used in this study was isolated from a natural outbreak of viral nervous necrosis in larval E. coioides in Hainan Province, China. Brain tissues from moribund larvae were homogenized, filtered, and inoculated onto SC1 cells for virus isolation following the procedure described by Frerichs et al.55,56 The identity of the isolated virus was confirmed by RT-PCR detection of NNV-specific genomic regions before use in subsequent experiments30. Working viral stocks were subsequently propagated in SC1 cells.57,58 The viral stock used in this study had a titer of 7 × 106 TCID50/mL, as determined by the Kärber method (see supplemental methods for detailed procedures).

Determination of the median LC50 of NNV in zebrafish

To determine the LC50 of NNV in zebrafish, a standardized immersion-based infection assay was performed using 4 dpf larvae. A preliminary dose-response experiment was conducted with seven viral concentrations generated via serial 7-fold dilutions of the NNV stock solution (initial titer: 7 × 106 TCID50/mL). Final viral titers in each treatment group were calculated as follows: 7 × 106, 1 × 106, 1.43 × 105, 2.04 × 104, 2.92 × 103, 4.16 × 102, and 5.95 × 101 TCID50/mL. Viral inocula were prepared in sterile E2 embryo medium, and final titers represent theoretical concentrations per well.

The assay employed 96-well plates, with one larva allocated per well and three biological replicates per experimental group (n = 96 larvae/replicate), totaling 2,304 larvae. Larvae were exposed to viral suspensions for 4 h, followed by replacement of the inoculum with fresh E2 medium. Mortality was monitored at 2-h intervals up to 12 h post-exposure, and cumulative mortality rates were recorded. The relationship between viral concentration and larval survival was analyzed using probit regression to calculate the LC50 value, which was subsequently applied to standardize viral dosing in all formal infection experiments.

Formal experiment for NNV infection in zebrafish

Based on the LC50 value determined above, a total of 720 healthy 4 dpf zebrafish larvae were used across the formal infection experiments and allocated to different downstream assays, including viral load quantification, protein analysis, histological and ultrastructural examination, behavioral analysis, flow cytometry, and transcriptomic profiling. Larvae were distributed into 12-well plates at 10 larvae per well. Larvae in the infection group were immersed in 4 mL of viral suspension per well at the 12 h LC50 concentration (1.43 × 105 TCID50/mL) for 4 h. Control larvae were treated in parallel with E2 embryo medium under the same handling conditions. After exposure, the viral suspension was removed, larvae were washed three times with PBS, and transferred to fresh culture water. This time point was defined as 0 h post-infection (0 hpi, Figure S3).

Subsequent samples for time-course analyses were collected at 4, 8, and 12 h after 0 h. For viral RNA quantification at each time point, nine larvae were randomly selected and pooled into three biological replicates, with three larvae per replicate. The 0 h group represents larvae collected immediately after viral exposure and washing and was used as the post-exposure baseline representing the initial larva-associated viral input after washing. All samples were stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Viral load quantification and replication dynamics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from NNV-infected and control zebrafish larvae using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and concentration were assessed spectrophotometrically by measuring absorbance at 260 and 280 nm. Samples with a 260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were used for subsequent analysis. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37°C for 15 min, followed by 85°C for 5 s, and the resulting cDNA was stored at −20°C until downstream quantitative analysis.

To monitor NNV accumulation and replication dynamics, hydrolysis probe-based AQ-PCR was performed to detect viral CP and RdRp RNA targets. Reactions were run on a LightCycler 480 real-time PCR system (Roche) using target-specific primer-probe sets designed for the CP and RdRp regions (Table S3) and a commercial probe-based qPCR master mix, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Standard curves were generated using 10-fold serial dilutions (106–101 copies/μL) of plasmids containing the CP or RdRp target fragments. Ct values were plotted against the logarithm of template copy number to obtain linear regression equations, which were then used to calculate viral copy numbers in samples collected at 0, 4, 8, and 12 h post-infection. The resulting time-course profiles of CP and RdRp copy numbers were used to describe the in vivo accumulation and replication kinetics of NNV.

qRT-PCR

Relative quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out on a Roche LightCycler 480 II system (Roche Diagnostics, Shanghai, China) in 10 μL reactions containing 5 μL 2× Polarsignal qRT-PCR Mix (MIKX, China), 1 μL cDNA, 0.5 μL forward primer, 0.5 μL reverse primer, and 3 μL nuclease-free water. The cycling program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s, and a melting curve analysis to confirm amplification specificity. Each sample was analyzed in three technical replicates. Primer sequences are listed in Table S3, and all reactions showed amplification efficiencies above 90%. β-actin was used as the reference gene because its expression remained stable under NNV infection in this study. Relative transcript levels were calculated by the 2-ΔΔCt method using the 0 h group as the calibrator.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Total protein extraction was performed using 4 dpf zebrafish larvae, which were sampled at 0, 4, 8, and 12 h post NNV infection. For each time point, 10 larvae were pooled as one sample, and three biological replicates were prepared. Larvae were lysed in pre-chilled RIPA buffer supplemented with PMSF and incubated on ice for 30 min. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay kit (Solarbio, PC0020). The lysates were then mixed with 5 × SDS sample buffer and denatured by heating at 100 °C for 15 min. Equal amounts of protein (25–40 μg per lane) were loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels, separated by electrophoresis, and transferred onto PVDF membranes.

After protein transfer, the PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk prepared in PBST for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was then incubated for 2 h with primary antibodies diluted in PBST, including an anti-NNV CP antibody prepared in our laboratory using virus-like particle (VLP) protein as the antigen, an anti-zebrafish Olig2 antibody (CUSABIO, China), and an anti-β-actin antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). After three washes with TBST, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (Santa Cruz, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the membrane was washed three times with TBST, and signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagents (Invitrogen, USA).

Histological examination and TEM

In this study, histological sections were prepared from zebrafish collected at 0 h and 12 h post-infection, following a slightly modified version of the protocol described by Tanaka et al.59 Whole larvae were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 24 h, rinsed in 70% ethanol, dehydrated through an ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin using standard histological protocols. Sections were cut at 5 μm thickness, mounted on glass slides, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Microscopic images of the sections were acquired using a Nikon ECLIPSE C1 microscope, following previously established methods.

For TEM analysis, zebrafish larvae were collected and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C for 24 h, followed by post-fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. The samples were then dehydrated and embedded in Spurr’s resin. Ultrathin sections (50 nm) were obtained, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined under a JEOL JEM-1400FLASH transmission electron microscope.

Virus isolation from zebrafish larvae and SC1 cell infection assay

To evaluate the infectivity of NNV recovered from zebrafish larvae, 60 larvae from each group were collected at 12 h post-infection for virus isolation. Mock-treated larvae were processed in parallel as negative controls. Larvae were transferred to 400 μL of pre-chilled PBS (pH 7.4) under sterile conditions, thoroughly homogenized, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was sequentially filtered through 0.45 μm and 0.22 μm syringe filters to remove tissue debris and potential bacterial contaminants.

The filtered supernatant was inoculated onto SC1 cell monolayers in 96-well plates and incubated at 28°C for 2 h to allow viral adsorption. After adsorption, the inoculum was removed and replaced with fresh maintenance medium. Cells were then cultured at 28°C and monitored daily under an inverted microscope for up to 5 days. CPE were recorded to assess viral infectivity. In addition, culture supernatants were collected at 1, 3, and 5 dpi for qRT-PCR analysis of viral CP and RdRp RNA levels, as described above. The infectious titer of the 12 h infected larval homogenate was also determined on SC1 cells by endpoint dilution assay based on CPE observed at 5 days post-inoculation, and the TCID50 was calculated using the Kärber method, as described in the supplemental methods.

NNV infection in olig2-labeled zebrafish

In this study, the transgenic zebrafish line vu19Tg carrying the Tg(olig2:DsRed2) allele was used.60 In this line, the olig2 promoter drives DsRed expression in olig2-expressing neural-lineage cells, including motor neuron-associated populations during early development. For the experiment, 4 dpf vu19Tg larvae were infected with NNV as described above and sampled at 12 h post-infection. The larvae were then anesthetized with 20 mg/L MS-222, and fluorescence changes were examined under a microscope using z stack maximum intensity projection images to assess the effect of NNV infection on olig2-labeled neural structures.

Flow cytometry analysis

To quantitatively analyze changes in olig2-labeled neural-lineage cells, head tissues from control and NNV-infected Tg(olig2:DsRed2) larvae collected at 12 h post-infection were dissected and dissociated into single-cell suspensions. Cells were filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer and resuspended in PBS containing 2% FBS. Flow cytometry was performed using a CytoFLEX LX high-performance multicolor flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, China) to quantify the proportion and fluorescence intensity of DsRed+ cells. Cell debris was excluded based on forward and side scatter, and DsRed+ cells were quantified using the same gating strategy across all groups. Three biological replicates were included for each group.

Behavioral analysis

Behavioral assays were performed to evaluate the effects of NNV infection on locomotor activity in 4 dpf larvae using a zebrafish tracking and activity recording system (DanioVision, Noldus, Netherlands) equipped with temperature-control and illumination modules. Before the experiment, zebrafish larvae were individually placed into 12-well plates, with one larva per well, to allow precise observation of individual locomotor behavior. The environmental temperature was maintained at 28°C, and the light intensity was adjusted to 20% of the system’s maximum to reduce stress responses in the larvae.

Each data recording session lasted for 180 min, during which the Viewpoint tracking system continuously monitored and recorded zebrafish movement trajectories in real time. Locomotor parameters were exported at 1-min intervals to obtain detailed locomotion data. Additionally, the activity duration of each zebrafish larva was recorded using the Viewpoint activity monitoring system, enabling the assessment of their activity status and behavioral characteristics at various time points. At the conclusion of the experiment, collected trajectory data were processed and analyzed using the Tracking Mask behavioral analysis software to generate 3D trajectory plots, providing comprehensive information on swimming paths, speed, and range of movement.

Transcriptomic profiling of zebrafish larvae in response to NNV infection

Transcriptomic analysis was performed on zebrafish collected at 0 h and 12 h post-infection. At each time point, three biological replicates were generated, with each replicate comprising a pool of 10 larvae. Total RNA was extracted from the samples using the TaKaRa RNAiso Plus reagent, following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA library preparation and sequencing were conducted by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China). mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT)-conjugated magnetic beads, followed by reverse transcription with random hexamer primers and second-strand cDNA synthesis to construct cDNA libraries. The library was then amplified to generate DNA nanoballs (DNBs) and loaded onto a patterned nanoarray. Sequencing was carried out on the BGISEQ-500 platform, and raw sequencing data were processed using SOAPnuke (version 1.5.2) to remove low quality reads. Gene expression levels were quantified using StringTie (version 2.1.2), and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.4.5) between the 0 h and 12 h post-infection groups with a significance threshold of Q ≤ 0.05. Genes meeting this criterion were considered significantly differentially expressed, and upregulated and downregulated genes were classified according to the direction of the log2 fold change. Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using Phyper based on the hypergeometric distribution.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA), unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t test was used. For comparisons among more than two groups or multiple time points, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. Values labeled with different letters (a, b, c and d) are significantly different (p < 0.05). Exact n values and the meaning of n are provided in the figure legends, tables, or Method details, where appropriate. For larval experiments, n refers to the number of larvae or biological replicates as specified in each figure legend. For pooled molecular assays, each biological replicate represents an independent pool of larvae. For flow cytometry, n refers to independent biological replicates prepared from dissected larval head tissues. For representative images, at least three independent biological replicates or independent experiments were examined unless otherwise stated. LC50 values were calculated by probit regression, and TCID50 values were calculated using the Kärber method. Differential expression analysis of RNA-seq data was performed using DESeq2, with Q ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant.
