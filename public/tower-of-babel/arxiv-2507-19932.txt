Equivariant Parameter Families of Spin Chains: A Discrete MPS Formulation Thanks: YITP-25-114
Ken Shiozaki

Abstract
We analyze topological phase transitions and higher Berry curvature in one-dimensional quantum spin systems, using a framework that explicitly incorporates the symmetry group action on the parameter space. Based on a G G -compatible discretization of the parameter space, we incorporate both group cochains and parameter-space differentials, enabling the systematic construction of equivariant topological invariants. We derive a fixed-point formula for the higher Berry invariant in the case where the symmetry action has isolated fixed points. This reveals that the phase transition point between Haldane and trivial phases acts as a monopole-like defect where higher Berry curvature emanates. We further discuss hierarchical structures of topological defects in the parameter space, governed by symmetry reductions and compatibility with subgroup structures.

Introduction

In an isolated quantum many-body system, symmetry is expressed by the requirement that the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}

\displaystyle\hat{g}\,\hat{H}\,\hat{g}^{-1}=\hat{H},\quad g\in G,

(1)

G

\displaystyle\hat{g}\,\hat{H}(\tau)\,\hat{g}^{-1}=\hat{H}(g\tau),\quad g\in G,

For example, consider the one-dimensional Heisenberg model with a uniform magnetic field

\displaystyle\hat{H}(J,\bm{h})=J\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j+1}+\bm{h}\cdot\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\mathbf{S}}_{j}\,.

In this paper, we systematically investigate the relationship between topological invariants of families of Hamiltonians and the role of the
G

In this paper, we formalize these relations. As an application, we prove that the phase transition point between the Haldane phase and the trivial phase, protected by either time-reversal symmetry or

As a methodological framework, we follow Ref.
[

and adopt a discrete formulation based on matrix product state (MPS) representations
[

, assuming translational invariance. The parameter space is discretized into a sufficiently fine mesh compared to the variation of the Hamiltonian, and topological invariants are constructed from MPS data assigned to the vertices. A key advantage of this formulation is that gauge invariance is manifest, and it can be straightforwardly implemented numerically, for instance using DMRG.

We note that the assumption of translational symmetry is a technical simplification to facilitate the formulation; a generalization to non-translationally invariant systems remains an important direction for future work.

The structure of this paper is as follows.
Sec. 2, as a preliminary to the formulation for one-dimensional spin systems, reviews the relationship between topological invariants of families of pure states in quantum mechanical systems and group actions, particularly describing a discrete formulation based on a triangulation of the parameter space.
In Sec. 3, using the injective MPS, we carry out a discrete formulation with group action for parameter families of MPSs.
In Sec. 4, as an application of Sec. 3, we derive a fixed-point formula for the DDKS number and some new invariants introduced by group action.
Sec. 5 summarizes general aspects of defect structures in theory space.
Finally, Sec. 6 provides a summary and outlook of this work.

2.1
Discrete formulation

Below, we describe the discrete formulation for the topological number of the family of Hamiltonians
\hat{H}(\tau)
. (In the next subsection
2.2
, we will introduce the group action.) We divide the parameter space
{\cal M}
into small simplices (triangulate it), denoting the vertices by
\tau_{j}
, etc. In the following, for a
q
-simplex
\Delta^{q}=(\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{q})
, we abbreviate the action of
g
as
g\Delta^{q}:=(g\tau_{0},\dots,g\tau_{q})
. Similarly, we write
gc
for the action of
g
on any
q
-chain
c
.

Assign a state
|\psi(\tau_{0})\rangle
to each vertex
\tau_{0}
. The
U(1)
phase of
|\psi(\tau_{0})\rangle
can be chosen arbitrarily and independently at each vertex. For any edge (1-simplex)
\Delta^{1}=(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
, define the (discrete) Berry connection along that edge as the
U(1)
phase of the inner product between the states at its endpoints:

\displaystyle{\cal A}(\Delta^{1}):=\arg\Big(\langle\psi(\tau_{0})\mid\psi(\tau_{1})\rangle\Big)\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},\quad\Delta^{1}=(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})\,.

(9)

Note that reversing the orientation of an edge
\Delta^{1}=(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
(i.e., considering
-\Delta^{1}=(\tau_{1},\tau_{0})
) flips the sign of the Berry connection
4
4

4

Hereafter, for any quantity valued in
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, the equality sign “
=
” is always understood as an equality modulo
2\pi
.
:
{\cal A}(-\Delta^{1})=-{\cal A}(\Delta^{1})
. Under the gauge transformation

\displaystyle|\psi(\tau_{0})\rangle\mapsto|\psi(\tau_{0})\rangle\,e^{i\chi(\tau_{0})}\,,

(10)

the Berry connection changes as
{\cal A}(\Delta^{1})\mapsto{\cal A}(\Delta^{1})+d\chi(\Delta^{1})
, where
d\chi(\Delta^{1})=\chi(\partial\Delta^{1})=\chi(\tau_{1})-\chi(\tau_{0})
. For any loop (1-cycle)
\ell=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+(\tau_{2},\tau_{3})+\cdots+(\tau_{N},\tau_{1})
, define the
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
-valued (discrete) Berry phase
\gamma(\ell)
as the sum of Berry connection along the loop:

\displaystyle\gamma(\ell):=\sum_{\Delta^{1}\in\ell}{\cal A}(\Delta^{1})\,.

(11)

Nice properties of this definition are that invariance under the gauge transformation (
10
) is manifest, and it is easily implemented numerically. Reversing the orientation of a closed loop
\ell
flips the sign of the Berry phase:
\gamma(-\ell)=-\gamma(\ell)
. For any triangle (2-simplex)
\Delta^{2}=(\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
, the Berry phase around its boundary loop
\partial\Delta^{2}=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-(\tau_{0},\tau_{2})+(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
is, if the triangle
\Delta^{2}
is sufficiently small, close to
0
mod
2\pi
, so there is no branch ambiguity and one can take a logarithm. We thus define the Berry flux (the integral of Berry curvature) on the triangle
\Delta^{2}
as a real number by lifting the
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
-valued Berry phase to a branch
(-\pi,\pi]
: writing this lift as

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}\ni\theta\mapsto\tilde{\theta}\in\mathbb{R},\quad-\pi\leq\tilde{\theta}<\pi.

(12)

With this, we define the Berry flux

\displaystyle{\cal F}(\Delta^{2}):=\widetilde{\,d{\cal A}(\Delta^{2})\,},

(13)

where
d{\cal A}(\Delta^{2})={\cal A}(\partial\Delta^{2})={\cal A}(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})+{\cal A}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+{\cal A}(\tau_{2},\tau_{0})
. By definition, note that
{\cal F}(\Delta^{2})\equiv d{\cal A}(\Delta^{2})
modulo
2\pi
holds. For any oriented closed 2D surface (2-cycle)

, the Chern number (a topological number of the family) is defined as the sum of Berry fluxes on
\Sigma

[
47
]

\displaystyle\nu(\Sigma):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\Delta^{2}\in\Sigma}{\cal F}(\Delta^{2})\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

(14)

The quantization of this value is evident from the equality

\displaystyle\sum_{\Delta^{2}\in\Sigma}{\cal F}(\Delta^{2})\equiv\sum_{\Delta^{2}\in\Sigma}d{\cal A}(\Delta^{2})\equiv\sum_{\Delta^{1}\in\partial\Sigma}{\cal A}(\Delta^{1})\equiv 0\quad\bmod\,2\pi.

(15)

Note that the Chern number flips sign under orientation reversing of
\Sigma

\nu(-\Sigma)=-\,\nu(\Sigma)

2.2
Group action

Next, let us implement the
G
-equivariant structure. We choose the triangulation so that it is compatible with the
G
-action on the parameter space
{\cal M}
. Namely, we require:

•

The group
G
acts on the vertex set
\{\tau_{j}\}_{j}
.

•

For each

-simplex
\Delta^{q}_{a}
labeled by
a
, the action of a group element

is either:

–

to fix the interior of
\Delta^{q}_{a}
pointwise (i.e.
g\tau=\tau

\tau\in\mathring{\Delta}^{q}_{a}
), or

–

to map
\Delta^{q}_{a}
to another
q
-simplex (i.e.
g(\mathring{\Delta}^{q}_{a})=\mathring{\Delta}^{q}_{b}
with some
b
).

A triangulation obtained in this way is compatible with a
G
-simplicial structure, and the set of
q
-simplices is invariant under
G
.

The
G
-equivariance (
2
) means that for any parameter point
\tau_{0}
, the state
\hat{g}|\psi(\tau_{0})\rangle
is the same up to a

|\psi(g\tau_{0})\rangle
at the point

. Therefore, we have the equality

\displaystyle\hat{g}\,|\psi(\tau_{0})\rangle=|\psi(g\tau_{0})\rangle\,e^{i\alpha_{g}(\tau_{0})},\quad g\in G\,,

(16)

thereby introducing a
U(1)

e^{i\alpha_{g}(\tau_{0})}\in U(1)
. Under the gauge transformation (
10
),
\alpha_{g}(\tau_{0})
changes as

\displaystyle\alpha_{g}(\tau_{0})\mapsto\alpha_{g}(\tau_{0})+(\delta\chi)_{g}(\tau_{0})=\alpha_{g}(\tau_{0})+\phi_{g}\,\chi(\tau_{0})-\chi(g\tau_{0})\,,

(17)

and it satisfies the cocycle condition

\displaystyle(\delta\alpha)_{g,h}(\tau_{0})=\phi_{g}\,\alpha_{h}(\tau_{0})-\alpha_{gh}(\tau_{0})+\alpha_{g}(g\tau_{0})=0\,.

(18)

In particular, for group elements of the stabilizer
G_{\tau_{0}}=\{g\in G\mid g\tau_{0}=\tau_{0}\}
of
\tau_{0}
(so that
g
leaves the point
\tau_{0}
invariant) which are unitary (
\phi_{g}=1
), the
U(1)

\displaystyle e^{i\alpha_{g}(\tau_{0})}=\langle\psi(\tau_{0})\mid\hat{g}\mid\psi(\tau_{0})\rangle,\quad g\in G_{\tau_{0}},\ \phi_{g}=1,

(19)

is a one-dimensional representation (“charge”) of
G_{\tau_{0}}
and is gauge-invariant.

The Berry connection
{\cal A}(\Delta^{1})
and
\alpha_{g}(\tau_{0})
satisfy the following “descendant equation”:

\displaystyle\delta{\cal A}\equiv d\alpha\,.

(20)

In components:

\displaystyle(\delta{\cal A})_{g}(\Delta^{1})

\displaystyle=\phi_{g}\,{\cal A}(\Delta^{1})-{\cal A}(g\Delta^{1})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{g}(\tau_{1})-\alpha_{g}(\tau_{0})=d\alpha_{g}(\Delta^{1})\,.

(21)

Furthermore, from (
21
) we find for the Berry flux that
(\delta{\cal F})_{g}(\Delta^{2})=\phi_{g}\,{\cal F}(\Delta^{2})-{\cal F}(g\Delta^{2})=0
, i.e.

\displaystyle{\cal F}(g\Delta^{2})=\phi_{g}\,{\cal F}(\Delta^{2})\,.

(22)

Therefore, the action of
g
on the Chern number is

\displaystyle\nu(g\Sigma)=\phi_{g}\,\nu(\Sigma)\,.

(23)

It follows that if there exists an antiunitary element
g\in G
such that a closed surface
\Sigma
is invariant as a set (not necessarily pointwise) under

, or if there exists a unitary

that reverses the orientation of
\Sigma
, then the Chern number is zero:

\displaystyle\exists\,g\in G\ \text{s.t.}\quad g\Sigma=-\,\phi_{g}\Sigma\quad\text{then}\quad\nu(\Sigma)=0\,.

(24)

Similarly, integrating both sides of (
21

on the Berry phase:

\displaystyle\gamma(g\ell)=\phi_{g}\,\gamma(\ell),\quad g\in G.

(25)

Therefore, the same constraint arises as the Chern number:

\displaystyle\exists\,g\in G\ \text{s.t.}\quad g\ell=-\,\phi_{g}\ell\quad\text{then}\quad\gamma(\ell)\in\{0,\pi\}.

(26)

Fixed-point formula for the Berry phase

Consider the case in Eq. (

2.4

\displaystyle R_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{2\pi}{n}&-\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}&0,\\

3
Equivariant family of MPS: discrete formulation

In this section, we review the gauge redundancy of MPS and how the
G
-equivariant structure is encoded in the parameter space. Following Ref.
[
30
]
, we employ a discrete formulation based on a triangulation of the parameter space. In Sec.
4
, we derive fixed-point formulas for family topological invariants.

Consider a parameter family of gapped Hamiltonians
\hat{H}(\tau\in{\cal M})
in a one-dimensional quantum spin system with
{\cal M}
the parameter space. Assume that over the whole parameter space (or the subspace of interest), the ground state of
\hat{H}(\tau)
is unique and there is no SSB (i.e., no degeneracy of the macroscopic quantum state). As a technical assumption of this paper, we assume the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(\tau)
is translationally invariant and also that the ground state
|\psi(\tau)\rangle
is translationally invariant. In other words, letting
\hat{T}_{\rm r}
be the translation operator, we assume
\hat{T}_{\rm r}\,\hat{H}(\tau)\,\hat{T}_{\rm r}^{-1}=\hat{H}(\tau)
and
\hat{T}_{\rm r}\,|\psi(\tau)\rangle=|\psi(\tau)\rangle
. Under these assumptions, the ground state wavefunction
\psi(\tau)
can be described by an injective MPS, which we introduce shortly.

3.1
Fundamental theorem of MPS

First, we summarize the gauge degrees of freedom in an MPS, which is analogous to the
U(1)
gauge ambiguity (
10
) in pure states of a quantum mechanical system.

In a one-dimensional quantum spin system, let
\{\ket{i_{x}}\}_{i_{x}=1}^{n}
denote a local basis at site
x
. A translationally invariant matrix product state (MPS) is specified by a set of
n
matrices
\{A^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with
A^{i}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{D\times D}(\mathbb{C})
, where
D
is called the bond dimension, as follows:

\displaystyle\ket{\{A^{i}\}_{i}}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}=1}^{n}\mathrm{Tr}[A^{i_{1}}\cdots A^{i_{N}}]\,\ket{i_{1}\cdots i_{N}}\,.

(44)

Here,
N
is the system size. It is known that if a quantum state
\ket{\psi}
satisfies the area law of entanglement entropy, meaning that the entanglement entropy remains bounded independently of the system size, then
\ket{\psi}
can be approximated by an MPS in the following sense: if we define the approximation error between
\ket{\psi}
and the MPS
\ket{\{A^{i}\}_{i}}
as
\epsilon=1-|\langle\psi|\{A^{i}\}_{i}\rangle|
, the required bond dimension
D
grows polynomially with the system size
N
and the inverse error
\epsilon^{-1}
, that is,
D=\mathrm{poly}(N,\epsilon^{-1})

[
50
]
. Therefore, since the bond dimension
D
depends on both
N
and
\epsilon
, any physically meaningful quantity such as correlation functions must be formulated in a way that is insensitive to variations in
D
.

An appropriate weighting to extract the dominant components in the bond Hilbert space is given by the Schmidt eigenvalues: An MPS can always be brought, without changing the physical state, into the following right-canonical form that satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}A^{i}A^{i\dagger}=1_{D},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}A^{i\dagger}\Lambda^{2}A^{i}=\Lambda^{2},

(45)

or the left-canonical form

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}B^{i\dagger}B^{i}=1_{D},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}B^{i}\Lambda^{2}B^{i\dagger}=\Lambda^{2},

(46)

such that
\ket{\{A^{i}\}_{i}}=\ket{\{B^{i}\}_{i}}
. Here
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix with positive real entries

\displaystyle\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{D}),\quad\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{D}>0,

(47)

and it satisfies the normalization condition

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\,\Lambda^{2}=\sum_{a=1}^{D}\lambda_{a}^{2}=1\,.

(48)

The set of eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{a}\}_{a}
in an MPS in canonical form coincides with the Schmidt eigenvalues obtained by the Schmidt decomposition at a bond
(x,x+1)
for an infinitely 1D quantum state:
\ket{\{A^{i}\}_{i}}=\sum_{a=1}^{D}\lambda_{a}\,\ket{L,a}\otimes\ket{R,a}
.
The right- and left-canonical forms are related by the following transformation
6
6

6

\Lambda^{-1}
involves reciprocals of arbitrarily small Schmidt eigenvalues, so one should avoid using it in numerical computations.
:

\displaystyle B^{i}=\Lambda\,A^{i}\,\Lambda^{-1},\quad i=1,\dots,n.

(49)

In this paper, we proceed using the right-canonical form, i.e.,
\{A^{i}\}_{i}
.

Moreover, to exclude cat states (i.e., linear combinations of macroscopically distinct states), we assume the property called injectivity. Injectivity is defined in terms of the transfer matrix. Define the transfer matrix
T\in\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{Mat}_{D\times D}(\mathbb{C}))
as

\displaystyle T(X):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A^{i}\,X\,A^{i\dagger}\,.

(50)

We say
T
is injective if the eigenvalue
\mu_{\rm max}
of
T
with the largest absolute value is unique and its eigenspace is one-dimensional
7
7

7

Let
{\rm spr}(T)=\max\{|\mu|\mid\mu\in{\rm Spec}(T)\}
denote the spectral radius of
T
. We assume that the eigenvalue
\mu\in{\rm Spec}(T)
satisfying
|\mu|={\rm spr}(T)
is unique and that the eigenvector
X
with eigenvalue
\mu
,
T(X)=\mu X
, is unique up to a scalar multiplication.
. In this case, by positivity of
T
,
\mu_{\rm max}
is positive real (
\mu_{\rm max}>0
), and one can choose the eigenvector
X
satisfying
T(X)=\mu_{\rm max}X
to be positive definite (
X>0
). If the MPS
\{A^{i}\}_{i}
is in canonical form, then
\mu_{\rm max}=1
and we can take
X=1_{D}
as the positive definite eigenvector.

For an injective MPS, the following gauge structure theorem holds
[
43
]
. Let
\{A^{i}\}_{i}
and
\{\tilde{A}^{i}\}_{i}
be two injective MPSs in right-canonical form with bond dimension
D
, with Schmidt matrices
\Lambda
and
\tilde{\Lambda}
, respectively. For a sufficiently large system size
N>O(D^{4})
, if there exists some phase
e^{i\alpha}
such that
|\{A^{i}\}_{i}\rangle=e^{i\alpha}|\{\tilde{A}^{i}\}_{i}\rangle
(i.e., the two MPS represent the same physical state for a system size
N
), then there exist some
e^{i\theta}\in U(1)
and some
V\in U(D)
such that

\displaystyle A^{i}=e^{i\theta}\,V^{\dagger}\,\tilde{A}^{i}\,V,\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(51)

\displaystyle V\,\Lambda=\tilde{\Lambda}\,V.

(52)

Furthermore,
e^{i\theta}
is unique, and
V
is unique up to an overall
U(1)
phase
[
51
]
. Since
\Lambda,\tilde{\Lambda}
are arranged in descending order, Eq. (
52
) implies
\Lambda=\tilde{\Lambda}
, i.e.,
\Lambda
is gauge-invariant. Eq. (
52
) means that the unitary matrix
V
is block-diagonal in the eigensectors of the Schmidt eigenvalues.

Since
V
is unique up to an overall phase, the equivalence class
[V]=\{zV\mid z\in U(1)\}
is unique; in other words, one can regard it as an element of the projective unitary group
PU(D)=U(D)/(z\sim zU,z\in U(1))
. Thus, the gauge ambiguity of an MPS is given by the pair
(e^{i\theta},[V])\in U(1)\times PU(D)
.

Moreover, for a pair of gauge-equivalent MPSs
\{A^{i}\}_{i},\{\tilde{A}^{i}\}_{i}
, the gauge transformation
(e^{i\theta},[V])
can be computed numerically using the following mixed transfer matrix
[
51
]
:

\displaystyle T_{A\tilde{A}}(X):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A^{i}\,X\,\tilde{A}^{i\dagger}\,.

(53)

From Eq. (
51
), the eigenvalue of
T_{A\tilde{A}}
with the largest absolute value is
\mu=e^{i\theta}
, and the corresponding eigenvector is given by
X=V^{\dagger}
.

In the rest of this section,
\{A^{i}\}_{i}
will always denote an injective MPS in the right-canonical form.

3.2
Overlap matrix

We introduce the
overlap matrix
, which plays a central role in constructing family topological invariants.

Let
\{A_{0}^{i}\}_{i}
and
\{A_{1}^{i}\}_{i}
be two injective MPSs in right-canonical form, with bond dimensions
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
, respectively. Define the mixed transfer matrix
T_{A_{0}A_{1}}\in\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{Mat}_{D_{0}\times D_{1}}(\mathbb{C}))
similarly to Eq. (
53
) as:

\displaystyle T_{A_{0}A_{1}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{0}^{i}\otimes A_{1}^{i*}\,.

(54)

Explicitly,

\displaystyle T_{A_{0}A_{1}}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{0}^{i}\,X\,A_{1}^{i\dagger},\quad X\in{\rm Mat}_{D_{0}\times D_{1}}(\mathbb{C}).

(55)

We say that the two MPSs
\{A^{i}_{0}\}_{i}
,
\{A^{i}_{1}\}_{i}
are “close” if, similarly to the injective condition, the eigenvalue of the mixed transfer matrix
T_{A_{0}A_{1}}
with the largest absolute value is unique and its eigenspace is one-dimensional. In that case, there exists a rectangular matrix
X_{01}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{D_{0}\times D_{1}}(\mathbb{C})
as the eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue
\mu_{01}
with the largest absolute value:

\displaystyle T_{A_{0}A_{1}}(X_{01})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{0}^{i}\,X_{01}\,A_{1}^{i\dagger}=\mu_{\rm max,01}\,X_{01}\,.

(56)

We call this matrix
X_{01}\in{\rm Mat}_{D_{0}\times D_{1}}(\mathbb{C})
the
overlap matrix
, by analogy with the inner product of pure states. (Note that, being the solution of a linear eigenvalue equation, the complex coefficient of
X_{01}
is undetermined.)

To align with the formulation for quantum mechanical systems, we introduce the bra-ket notation. Endow the matrix space
{\rm Mat}_{D_{0}\times D_{1}}(\mathbb{C})
with the Hilbert=Schmidt inner product

\displaystyle(Y|X):=\mathrm{Tr}[Y^{\dagger}X]\,,\quad X,Y\in{\rm Mat}_{D_{0}\times D_{1}}(\mathbb{C})\,.

(57)

With this inner product, the matrix elements of the mixed transfer matrix in an operator basis
e_{ab}=|a)(b|
are:

\displaystyle[T_{A_{0}A_{1}}]_{ab,cd}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[e_{ab}^{\dagger}\,T_{A_{0}A_{1}}(e_{cd})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a|A^{i}_{0}|c)\,(d|A_{1}^{i\dagger}|b)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}[A^{i}_{0}]_{ac}\,[A_{1}^{i}]^{*}_{bd}\,.

(58)

The eigenvalue equation (
56
) is written in bra-ket notation as a right-eigenstate of the mixed transfer matrix:

\displaystyle T_{A_{0}A_{1}}\,|X_{01})=\mu_{\rm max,01}\,|X_{01})\,.

(59)

We summarize the gauge redundancy of the overlap matrix. Under gauge transformations on the MPSs
\{A^{i}_{0}\}_{i}
and
\{A^{i}_{1}\}_{i}
:

\displaystyle A_{m}^{i}\mapsto e^{i\theta_{m}}W_{m}^{\dagger}\,A_{m}^{i}\,W_{m},\quad e^{i\theta_{m}}\in U(1),\ W_{m}\in U(D_{m}),\quad m=0,1,

(60)

the mixed transfer matrix transforms as

\displaystyle T_{A_{0}A_{1}}\mapsto e^{i(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})}\,(W_{0}\otimes W_{1}^{*})^{\dagger}\,T_{A_{0}A_{1}}\,(W_{0}\otimes W_{1}^{*})\,.

(61)

Therefore, the eigenvalue and eigenvector transform as

\displaystyle\mu_{\rm max,01}\mapsto e^{i(\theta_{0}-\theta_{1})}\,\mu_{\rm max,01}\,,

(62)

\displaystyle|X_{01})\mapsto W_{0}^{\dagger}\otimes W_{1}^{\top}\,|X_{01})

\displaystyle\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad X_{01}\mapsto W_{0}^{\dagger}\,X_{01}\,W_{1}\,,

(63)

respectively. Thus, we find that any product of the form

\displaystyle A^{i_{0}}_{0}\,X_{01}\,A^{i_{1}}_{1}\mapsto W_{0}^{\dagger}\,A^{i_{0}}_{0}\,X_{01}\,A^{i_{1}}_{1}\,W_{1}

(64)

transforms covariantly under gauge transformations (up to an overall
U(1)
phase). Thanks to this property, the overlap matrix
X_{01}
plays a role in constructing an MPS that transforms covariantly under independent gauge transformations defined at each site. In other words,
X_{01}
plays the role of a “gauge field” defined on the bond between 0 and 1. This point was discussed at the end of subsection
4.3
, in the context of constructing a topological invariant for an adiabatic pump.

3.3
Discrete formulation with group action

We approximate the parameter space
\mathcal{M}
by a triangulation, and at each vertex

\displaystyle A^{i}(\tau_{0})\mapsto e^{i\chi^{(0,0)}(\tau_{0})}\,W(\tau_{0})^{\dagger}\,A^{i}(\tau_{0})\,W(\tau_{0})\,,\quad e^{i\chi^{(0,0)}(\tau_{0})}\in U(1),\quad W(\tau_{0})\in U(D(\tau_{0})),

(66)

For any adjacent vertices

\displaystyle T(\Delta^{1}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A^{i}(\tau_{0})\otimes A^{i}(\tau_{1})^{*}\,.

\displaystyle T(\Delta^{1})\,\left.\middle|X(\Delta^{1})\right)=\mu_{\rm max}(\Delta^{1})\,\left.\middle|X(\Delta^{1})\right)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{n}A^{i}(\tau_{0})\,X(\Delta^{1})\,A^{i}(\tau_{1})^{\dagger}=\mu_{\rm max}(\Delta^{1})\,X(\Delta^{1})\,.

(68)

Taking the Hermitian conjugate of (
68

\displaystyle\mu_{\rm max}(-\Delta^{1})=\mu_{\rm max}(\Delta^{1})^{*},\quad X(-\Delta^{1})=X(\Delta^{1})^{\dagger}\,,

\displaystyle{\cal A}^{(0,1)}(\Delta^{1}):=\arg\Big(\mu_{\rm max}(\Delta^{1})\Big)\,.

Note that since the rectangular matrix

8

8

3.4.1
Comment on inversion-type symmetry action

An inversion-type group action that flips the position
x\mapsto-x
acts on an MPS via transpose:

\displaystyle\sum_{j}[u_{g}]_{ij}(A^{j})^{\top}.

(120)

This is in the left canonical form, and the transfer matrix
T
is mapped to the transpose
T^{\top}
(with a similarity transformation). As a result, the higher Berry connection defined using the right eigenvector
X
of the transfer matrix (see Eq. (
71
)) is mapped, under spatial inversion, to the higher Berry connection constructed using the left eigenvector. In general, no clear relation can be found between the higher Berry connections defined using the right and left eigenvectors, and no nice structure was found. For this reason, inversion-type group actions are not considered in this paper.

3.5

The
G
-action on the higher Berry flux is, from

\displaystyle{\cal F}^{(3)}(g\Delta^{3})=\phi_{g}\,{\cal F}^{(3)}(\Delta^{3})\,.

SO(3)\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}
equivariance:

\displaystyle\hat{T}\hat{H}(\bm{n})\hat{T}^{-1}=\hat{H}(n_{0},-n_{1},-n_{2},-n_{3}),

(134)

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{\bm{m},\theta}\hat{H}(\bm{n})\hat{q}_{\bm{m},\theta}^{-1}=\hat{H}(n_{0},(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})R_{\bm{m},\theta}),

(135)

where
R_{\bm{m},\theta}
is the SO(3) rotation matrix acting from the right on the row vector
(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
. Therefore, the model (
130
) is equivariant under any subgroup
G
of
SO(3)\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}
. Below, we list several examples that will be addressed later. We denote the rotation axes in spin space by
\hat{x}=(1,0,0),\hat{y}=(0,1,0),\hat{z}=(0,0,1)
, and introduce the notation
C_{2\mu}=q_{\hat{\mu},\pi}
for
\pi
-rotations.

•

The composition
C_{2z}T
leads to a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

\displaystyle\widehat{C_{2z}T}\hat{H}(\bm{n})\widehat{C_{2z}T}^{-1}=\hat{H}(n_{0},n_{1},n_{2},-n_{3}).

(136)

The
C_{2z}T

•

U(1)_{z}
rotations about the
z

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{\hat{z},\theta}\hat{H}(\bm{n})\hat{q}_{\hat{z},\theta}^{-1}=\hat{H}(n_{0},\cos\theta n_{1}+\sin\theta n_{2},-\sin\theta n_{1}+\cos\theta n_{2},n_{3}).

(137)

The
U(1)_{z}

S^{1}
.

•

\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
spin rotations:

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{2x}\hat{H}(\bm{n})\hat{C}_{2x}^{-1}=\hat{H}(n_{0},n_{1},-n_{2},-n_{3}),

(138)

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{2y}\hat{H}(\bm{n})\hat{C}_{2y}^{-1}=\hat{H}(n_{0},-n_{1},n_{2},-n_{3}).

(139)

The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}

(\pm 1,0,0,0)
.

A crucial fact is that, at the two points
(1,0,0,0)
and
(-1,0,0,0)

130
), the point
\bm{n}=(-1,0,0,0)

\bm{n}=(1,0,0,0)
corresponds to a nontrivial model. That is,

\displaystyle\mu^{\rm RP}_{T}(-1,0,0,0)=0,\quad\mu^{\rm RP}_{T}(1,0,0,0)=2\pi S,

(140)

\displaystyle\mu^{\rm T}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(-1,0,0,0)=0,\quad\mu^{\rm T}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(1,0,0,0)=2\pi S.

(141)

4.3
One-parameter family: Thouless pump

We consider the subgroup that is unitary and preserves the parameter space pointwise:

\displaystyle G_{0}=\{g\in G\mid\phi_{g}=1,\;g\tau=\tau\text{ for }\tau\in{\cal M}\}.

(142)

We allow
G_{0}
to include continuous groups in this section. For such
G_{0}
, the cocycle condition (
119
) and gauge transformation (
118
) of the
(1,1)
-cochain reduce to

\displaystyle d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{g}=0,

(143)

\displaystyle{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{g}\mapsto{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{g}-d\chi^{(1,1)}_{g},\quad g\in G_{0}.

(144)

Thus, for each
g\in G_{0}
, one obtains a gauge-invariant and quantized quantity defined for a loop
\ell
in the parameter space:

\displaystyle\eta_{g}(\ell):=\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{g},\ell\right)\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},\quad g\in G_{0}.

(145)

Due to the flatness condition
d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{g}=0
,
\eta_{g}
is invariant under continuous deformations of the loop
\ell
, i.e.,
\eta_{g\in G_{0}}(\ell)
depends only on the homotopy class
[\ell]\in[S^{1},{\cal M}]
and is a topological invariant of the family. The allowed values of
\eta_{g}
satisfy

\displaystyle\eta_{h}(\ell)-\eta_{gh}(\ell)+\eta_{g}(\ell)=\left((\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{g,h},\ell\right)=\left(-d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{g,h},\ell\right)=0,

(146)

and hence
e^{i\eta}
defines a one-dimensional representation of
G_{0}
for each homotopy class of loops:

\displaystyle e^{i\eta}:[S^{1},{\cal M}]\to\mathrm{Hom}(G_{0},U(1)).

(147)

Thus, the “
G_{0}
charge”
e^{i\eta(\ell)}
is assigned to each loop.

In particular, when
G_{0}=U(1)
, this setting reduces to the original Thouless pump. Parameterizing the group elements of
U(1)
by
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, the integer-valued
U(1)
charge is given by

\displaystyle N(\ell)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\eta_{\theta}(\ell)}{d\theta}\in\mathbb{Z},

(148)

which means that the adiabatic time evolution along the loop
\ell
pumps
U(1)
charge by an amount
N(\ell)
.

4.3.1
Realization as an MPS soliton

The topological number of a family over
S^{1}
can also be formulated as a
G_{0}
charge localized on a “soliton,” where the local Hamiltonian in real space depends on the adiabatic parameter. In the context of MPS, this corresponds concretely to giving an
x
-dependent MPS as follows. Let the loop
\ell
be discretized as
\ell=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+\cdots+(\tau_{N},\tau_{1})
and introduce

\displaystyle\ket{\ell}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}=1}^{n}\mathrm{tr}\left[A^{i_{1}}(\tau_{1})X(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\cdots A^{i_{N}}(\tau_{N})X(\tau_{N},\tau_{1})\right]\ket{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}.

(149)

The state
\ket{\ell}
is invariant under the gauge transformation (
65
) up to a
U(1)
phase, and hence is well-defined as a physical state. The
G_{0}
charge of the state
\ket{\ell}
, using (
90
) and (
107
), is given by

\displaystyle\Braket{\ell|\hat{g}|\ell}=e^{-i\sum_{x=1}^{N}{\cal A}^{(1,0)}_{g}(\tau_{x})}\times e^{i\eta_{g}(\ell)},\quad g\in G_{0}.

(150)

The first factor corresponds to the
G_{0}
charge per site and is unrelated to the topological number of the family over the loop
\ell
. To eliminate this contribution, one may, for instance, consider the MPS at a single point in the parameter space, i.e., a trivial loop such as
\tau_{1}\in\ell
:

\displaystyle\ket{\tau_{1}}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}=1}^{n}\mathrm{tr}\left[A^{i_{1}}(\tau_{1})\cdots A^{i_{N}}(\tau_{1})\right]\ket{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}},

(151)

whose
G_{0}
charge is

\displaystyle\Braket{\tau_{1}|\hat{g}|\tau_{1}}=e^{-i\sum_{x=1}^{N}{\cal A}^{(1,0)}_{g}(\tau_{x})},\quad g\in G_{0}.

(152)

The ratio of the two quantities gives the desired result.

Let us comment on the relation to the ground state of a Hamiltonian with spatial modulation. Denote the Hamiltonian at parameter point
\tau_{x}
as

\displaystyle\hat{H}(\tau_{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{h}_{j}(\tau_{x}),

(153)

where
\hat{h}_{j}(\tau_{x})
is an interaction term depending on
\tau_{x}
and supported near site
j
. Note that
\tau_{x}
is a parameter and
\hat{H}(\tau_{x})
is translationally invariant. Correspondingly, one can consider a non-uniform Hamiltonian that varies in space along the loop
\ell
:

\displaystyle\hat{H}(\ell)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\hat{h}_{j}(\tau_{j}),

(154)

where the local term
\hat{h}_{j}(\tau_{j})
depends on the parameter
\tau_{j}
assigned to site
j
. The ground state of this non-uniform Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(\ell)
is not exactly
\ket{\ell}
, but if the spatial variation is sufficiently slow compared to the energy gap (i.e., for sufficiently large
N
), the state
\ket{\ell}
may approximate the ground state of
\hat{H}(\ell)
well. In this sense,
\ket{\ell}
can be viewed as the ground state in the “semiclassical approximation” of
\hat{H}(\ell)
.

4.3.2
Fixed-point formula for Thouless pump

In addition to the symmetry of the group
G_{0}
, consider the situation where there exists a group element
h\in G
such that the loop
\ell
is given by the difference between an arc
C
and its image under
h
, that is,
\ell=C-hC
(see Fig.
1
[a]). Note the following identities for
g\in G_{0}
:

\displaystyle(\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{g,h}(\Delta^{1})={\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{h}(\Delta^{1})-{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{gh}(\Delta^{1})+{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{g}(h\Delta^{1}),

(155)

\displaystyle(\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{h,g}(\Delta^{1})=\phi_{h}{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{g}(\Delta^{1})-{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{hg}(\Delta^{1})+{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{h}(\Delta^{1}).

(156)

When
h
is unitary (
\phi_{h}=1
) and
gh=hg
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{h,g}(\Delta^{1})-(\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{g,h}(\Delta^{1})={\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{g}(\Delta^{1})-{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{g}(h\Delta^{1}).

(157)

In this case, the pump invariant
\eta_{g}(\ell)
for elements
g\in G_{0}
that commute with
h
, i.e.,
gh=hg
, is completely determined by the SPT invariants at the endpoints
\partial C=Q-P
of the arc
C
:

\displaystyle\eta_{g}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{g,h},C-hC\right)=\left((\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{h,g}-(\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{g,h},C\right)=\left(-d{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{h,g}+d{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{g,h},C\right)

\displaystyle=\mu^{\rm T}_{g,h}(Q)-\mu^{\rm T}_{g,h}(P).

(158)

For example, consider the case of the Haldane phase protected by the unitary
\mathbb{Z}_{2}[C_{2x}]\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}[C_{2y}]
symmetry, which is characterized by the nontrivial invariant
\mu^{\rm T}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}=\pi
. There must exist a phase transition point between nontrivial and trivial phases. If one breaks one of the symmetries, say
\mathbb{Z}_{2}[C_{2y}]
, then an adiabatic path
C
can exist. The combination of this adiabatic path
C
and its image under
\sigma_{2}
forms the loop
\ell=C-\sigma_{2}C
. According to the fixed-point formula (
158
), the pump invariant
\eta_{\sigma_{1}}(\ell)
associated with the unbroken symmetry
\mathbb{Z}_{2}[\sigma_{1}]
along the loop
\ell
must take the nontrivial value
\eta_{\sigma_{1}}(\ell)=\pi
.
See also prior works
[
53
,
54
,
55
]
.

4.4
Two-parameter family:
\pi
-higher Berry phase

According to the constraint (
124
), when there exists an antiunitary symmetry
a\in G
that leaves each point in parameter space invariant, i.e.,
a\tau=\tau
for all
\tau\in\mathcal{M}
, with
\phi_{a}=-1
, the higher Berry phase
\gamma^{(2)}(\Sigma^{(2)})
is quantized to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
values
\{0,\pi\}
. Hence, in the presence of such a symmetry
a
, there exists a codimension-3 defect in parameter space characterized by a
\pi
-quantized higher Berry phase.

4.4.1
Model example

As a model exhibiting a nontrivial
\pi
-quantized higher Berry phase, consider the half-integer spin model (
130
) restricted to the
n_{3}=0
subspace, where the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
C_{2z}T
(see Eq.
136
) is preserved. Since this is a codimension-3 defect, it is stable against any
C_{2z}T
-invariant perturbation, such as an Ising interaction
J_{z}\sum_{j}(\hat{s}^{L,z}_{j}\hat{s}^{R,z}_{j}+\hat{s}^{R,z}_{j}\hat{s}^{L,z}_{j+1})
.

4.4.2
Fixed-point formula for
\pi
-higher Berry phase

More generally, when there exists an antiunitary group action
b\in G
with
\phi_{b}=-1
that leaves the closed two-dimensional surface
\Sigma^{(2)}
(on which the higher Berry phase
\gamma^{(2)}(\Sigma^{(2)})
is defined) invariant as a set, and preserves its orientation, then by Eq. (
124
), the higher Berry phase is quantized to values in
\{0,\pi\}
. When
b
acts nontrivially on
\Sigma^{(2)}
, the
\pi
-quantized higher Berry phase is expected to be determined solely by the fixed points of the action
b
.

For simplicity, consider the two-sphere
S^{2}\subset\mathcal{M}
, with coordinates
\bm{n}=(n_{0},n_{1},n_{2})
satisfying
\bm{n}^{2}=1
. Let
b
act as a “
180^{\circ}
rotation about the
n_{0}
axis”:

\displaystyle b:(n_{0},n_{1},n_{2})\mapsto(n_{0},-n_{1},-n_{2})

(159)

and assume
b^{2}=e
for the reason that will be explained shortly. Take the upper hemisphere
D=\{\bm{n}\in S^{2}\mid n_{3}\geq 0\}
as an independent integration region for the higher Berry phase, and the semicircular arc
C=\{\bm{n}\in S^{2}\mid n_{2}\geq 0,n_{3}=0\}
as an independent region on the boundary of
D
. Noting that
(\delta{\cal A}^{(0,2)})_{b}(\Delta^{2})=-{\cal A}^{(0,2)}(\Delta^{2})-{\cal A}^{(0,2)}(b\Delta^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma^{(2)}(S^{2})

\displaystyle=({\cal A}^{(0,2)},D+bD)=(-(\delta{\cal A}^{(0,2)})_{b},D)=(-d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{b},D)=(-{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{b},C-bC).

(160)

Furthermore, using

(\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{b,b}(\Delta^{1})=-{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{b}(\Delta^{1})-{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{b^{2}}(\Delta^{1})+{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{b}(b\Delta^{1})
, and noting that
{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{e}=0
when
b^{2}=e
, we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma^{(2)}(S^{2})=((\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{b,b},C)=(-d{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{b,b},C)=\mu^{\mathrm{RP}}_{b}(P)-\mu^{\mathrm{RP}}_{b}(Q),

(161)

meaning that the higher Berry phase is determined by the difference of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariants (
127
) at the fixed points
P
and
Q
.

If
b
is regarded as a time-reversal operation, then the meaning of the fixed-point formula (
161
) is as follows: along the fixed-point line of the
b
-action in parameter space, given by
(x,0,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
x\in\mathbb{R}
, there must exist a phase transition point between the Haldane phase (
\mu^{\mathrm{RP}}_{b}=\pi
) and the trivial phase (
\mu^{\mathrm{RP}}_{b}=0
). However, once the
b
-symmetry is broken, one can find an adiabatic path connecting the Haldane and trivial phases. Since this path can be taken in two “independent” directions, it forms a closed sphere
S^{2}
in parameter space surrounding the transition point. In such a case, the higher Berry phase
\gamma^{(2)}(S^{2})
on this
S^{2}
must be equal to
\pi
.

In the model (
130
), the elements
a
and
b
correspond to
C_{2z}T
and
T
, respectively. When
C_{2z}T
-equivariance is present, the higher Berry phase
\gamma^{(2)}(S^{2})
is quantized in the subspace
n_{3}=0
. If the additional
T
-equivariance is also imposed, then by the fixed-point formula (
161
), one obtains
\gamma^{(2)}(S^{2})=S\times 2\pi
.

4.5
Three-parameter family: DDKS number

The DDKS number (
81
) is an integer-valued topological invariant defined for three-dimensional closed manifolds, independent of any symmetry or group action. In this section, we consider the case where the three-dimensional closed manifold is
S^{3}
, and examine the relationship between the DDKS number and the topological invariants on subspaces invariant under group actions. We introduce the Cartesian coordinates of
S^{3}
as
\bm{n}=(n_{0},n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
with
\bm{n}^{2}=1
.

4.5.1
C_{2z}T
-equivariance:
\pi
-higher Berry phase

Consider the case where the following antiunitary
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
action exists on
S^{3}
:

\displaystyle C_{2z}T:\bm{n}\mapsto(n_{0},n_{1},n_{2},-n_{3}),\quad\phi_{C_{2z}T}=-1.

(162)

Since
C_{2z}T
is antiunitary and reverses the orientation of
S^{3}
, the higher Berry curvature is invariant under the
C_{2z}T
action. Therefore, the integration of the DDKS number can be restricted to the independent region
D^{3}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{3}\geq 0\}
, yielding

\displaystyle 2\pi\nu^{(3)}(S^{3})=\left({\cal F}^{(3)},D^{3}-C_{2z}TD^{3}\right)=2\left({\cal F}^{(3)},D^{3}\right).

(163)

Thus,

\displaystyle\pi\nu^{(3)}(S^{3})\underset{\bmod 2\pi}{\equiv}\left(d{\cal A}^{(0,2)},D^{3}\right)=\left({\cal A}^{(0,2)},\partial D^{3}\right).

(164)

Therefore, the
\pi
-higher Berry phase on the
C_{2z}T
-invariant two-sphere
\partial D^{3}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{3}=0\}
gives the parity of the DDKS number.

4.5.2
C_{nz}
-equivariance: Pump invariant

Consider the case where the following unitary
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
action exists on
S^{3}
:

\displaystyle C_{nz}:\bm{n}\mapsto(n_{0},\cos\frac{2\pi}{n}n_{1}+\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}n_{2},-\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}n_{1}+\cos\frac{2\pi}{n}n_{2},n_{3}),\quad\phi_{C_{nz}}=1.

(165)

Since
C_{nz}
preserves the orientation of
S^{3}
and is unitary, the integration of the DDKS number can be restricted to the independent region
D^{3}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{1}\geq\cos\frac{2\pi}{n},n_{2}\geq 0\}
. The boundary of
D^{3}
consists of a contractible 2D region
D^{2}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{1}\geq 0,n_{2}=0\}
and its image under
C_{nz}
, i.e.,
\partial D^{3}=D^{2}-C_{nz}D^{2}
. The boundary of
D^{2}
is a loop invariant under
C_{nz}
,
\partial D^{2}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{1}=n_{2}=0\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{n}\nu^{(3)}(S^{3})

\displaystyle\underset{\bmod 2\pi}{\equiv}\left(d{\cal A}^{(0,2)},D^{3}\right)=\left({\cal A}^{(0,2)},D^{2}-C_{nz}D^{2}\right)=\left((\delta{\cal A}^{(0,2)})_{C_{nz}},D^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{nz}},D^{2}\right)=\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{nz}},\partial D^{2}\right)=\eta_{C_{nz}}(\partial D^{2}),

(166)

establishing the mod-
n
relation between the DDKS number and the
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
Thouless pump invariant.

4.5.3
T
-equivariance: Fixed point formula (1)

As an example of the fixed point formula for the DDKS invariant, we consider the case where a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
group acts on
S^{3}
as

\displaystyle T:\bm{n}\mapsto(n_{0},-n_{1},-n_{2},-n_{3}),

(167)

and
T
is an antiunitary operation with
\phi_{T}=-1
. The only fixed points are
(\pm 1,0,0,0)
. Since
T
reverses the orientation of
S^{3}
, no restriction is imposed on the DDKS invariant. Let us define the
q
-dimensional disks
D^{q}
and fixed points as follows:

\displaystyle D^{3}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{3}\geq 0\},

(168)

\displaystyle D^{2}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{2}\geq 0,n_{3}=0\},

(169)

\displaystyle D^{1}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{1}\geq 0,n_{2}=n_{3}=0\},

(170)

\displaystyle P_{\pm}=(\pm 1,0,0,0).

(171)

Note the chain relations:

\displaystyle\partial D^{3}=D^{2}-TD^{2},

(172)

\displaystyle\partial D^{2}=D^{1}+TD^{1},

(173)

\displaystyle\partial D^{1}=P_{-}-P_{+}.

(174)

Since the independent integration domain of the DDKS invariant
\nu^{(3)}(S^{3})
is
D^{3}
, we obtain by straightforward calculation:

\displaystyle\pi\nu^{(3)}(S^{3})

\displaystyle=\left({\cal F}^{(0,3)},D^{3}\right)\underset{\bmod 2\pi}{=}\left(d{\cal A}^{(0,2)},D^{3}\right)=\left({\cal A}^{(0,2)},\partial D^{3}\right)=\left({\cal A}^{(0,2)},D^{2}+TD^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-(\delta{\cal A}^{(0,2)})_{T},D^{2}\right)=\left(-d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{T},D^{2}\right)=\left(-{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{T},D^{1}-TD^{1}\right)=\left((\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{T,T},D^{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-d{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{T,T},D^{1}\right)=\left(-{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{T,T},P_{-}-P_{+}\right)=\mu^{\rm RP}_{T}(P_{+})-\mu^{\rm RP}_{T}(P_{-}).

(175)

That is, the parity of the DDKS invariant coincides with the difference of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
SPT invariants
\mu^{\rm RP}_{T}
evaluated at the fixed points
(\pm 1,0,0,0)
.

4.5.4
C_{2x}\times C_{2y}
-equivariance: Fixed point formula (2)

It is known that the Haldane phase is also protected by a unitary
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry.
As another example of the fixed point formula for the DDKS invariant, we consider the action of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
group on
S^{3}
given by

\displaystyle C_{2x}:\bm{n}\mapsto(n_{0},n_{1},-n_{2},-n_{3}),

(176)

\displaystyle C_{2y}:\bm{n}\mapsto(n_{0},-n_{1},n_{2},-n_{3}),

(177)

where both transformations are unitary:
\phi_{C_{2x}}=\phi_{C_{2y}}=1
. As in the previous section, the only fixed points under the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
action are
P_{\pm}=(\pm 1,0,0,0)
. Since the independent integration domain for the DDKS invariant
\nu^{(3)}(S^{3})
is one fourth of
S^{3}
, a formula modulo 4 is anticipated.

Let us take the independent domain of
S^{3}
to be the 3-disk

\displaystyle D^{3}_{*++*}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{1},n_{2}\geq 0\}.

(178)

Its boundary is separated into two parts:

\displaystyle\partial D^{3}_{*++*}=-D^{2}_{*+0*}+D^{2}_{*0+*},

(179)

\displaystyle D^{2}_{*+0*}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{1}\geq 0,n_{2}=0\},

(180)

\displaystyle D^{2}_{*0+*}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{1}=0,n_{2}\geq 0\}.

(181)

Each of these can be expressed as a sum of an independent region and its group image:

\displaystyle D^{2}_{*+0*}=D^{2}_{*+0+}-C_{2x}D^{2}_{*+0+},\quad D^{2}_{*+0+}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{1}\geq 0,n_{2}=0,n_{3}\geq 0\},

(182)

\displaystyle D^{2}_{*0+*}=D^{2}_{*0+-}-C_{2y}D^{2}_{*0+-},\quad D^{2}_{*0+-}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{1}=0,n_{2}\geq 0,n_{3}\leq 0\}.

(183)

The boundaries of
D^{2}_{*+0+}
and
D^{2}_{*0+-}
are

\displaystyle\partial D^{2}_{*+0+}=D^{1}_{*+00}-D^{1}_{*00+},

(184)

\displaystyle\partial D^{2}_{*0+-}=D^{1}_{*00-}-D^{1}_{*0+0}=C_{2y}D^{1}_{*00+}-D^{1}_{*0+0},

(185)

\displaystyle D^{1}_{*00+}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{1},n_{2}=0,n_{3}\geq 0\},

(186)

\displaystyle D^{1}_{*+00}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{2},n_{3}=0,n_{1}\geq 0\},

(187)

\displaystyle D^{1}_{*0+0}=\{\bm{n}\in S^{3}\mid n_{1},n_{3}=0,n_{2}\geq 0\}.

(188)

A straightforward computation yields the mod 4 formula

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}\nu^{(3)}(S^{3})

\displaystyle=\left(d{\cal A}^{(0,2)},D^{3}_{*++*}\right)

\displaystyle=\left({\cal A}^{(0,2)},-D^{2}_{*+0+}+C_{2x}D^{2}_{*+0+}+D^{2}_{*0+-}-C_{2y}D^{2}_{*0+-}\right)

\displaystyle=\left((\delta{\cal A}^{(0,2)})_{C_{2x}},-D^{2}_{*+0+}\right)+\left((\delta{\cal A}^{(0,2)})_{C_{2y}},D^{2}_{*0+-}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x}},-D^{2}_{*+0+}\right)+\left(d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2y}},D^{2}_{*0+-}\right)

\displaystyle=\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x}},-D^{1}_{*+00}+D^{1}_{*00+}\right)+\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2y}},C_{2y}D^{1}_{*00+}-D^{1}_{*0+0}\right)

\displaystyle=\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x}},-D^{1}_{*+00}\right)+\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2y}},-D^{1}_{*0+0}\right)+\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x}C_{2y}}+(\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{C_{2x},C_{2y}},D^{1}_{*00+}\right)

\displaystyle=\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x}},-D^{1}_{*+00}\right)+\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2y}},-D^{1}_{*0+0}\right)+\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x}C_{2y}},D^{1}_{*00+}\right)+{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{-})-{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{+}).

(189)

It is easy to verify that this expression is gauge invariant under the gauge transformation (
118
). The integration path
C=-D^{1}_{*+00}-D^{1}_{*0+0}+D^{1}_{*00+}
consists of three arcs intersecting at the fixed points
P_{\pm}
and thus
C
is not a cycle and has boundary
\partial C=-P_{+}+P_{-}
. This boundary gauge redundancy is corrected by the boundary term
{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{-})-{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{+})
, making the whole expression gauge invariant.

Now, although the mod 4 formula (
189
) involves integrals over one-dimensional paths, the following fixed point formula modulo 2 is obtained: Using

\displaystyle(\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{C_{2x},C_{2x}}(D^{1}_{*+00})=2{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x}}(D^{1}_{*+00}),

(190)

\displaystyle(\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{C_{2y},C_{2y}}(D^{1}_{*0+0})=2{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2y}}(D^{1}_{*0+0}),

(191)

\displaystyle(\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{C_{2x}C_{2y},C_{2x}C_{2y}}(D^{1}_{*00+})=2{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x}C_{2y}}(D^{1}_{*00+}),

(192)

we obtain

\displaystyle\pi\nu^{(3)}(S^{3})

\displaystyle=\left(2{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x}},-D^{1}_{*+00}\right)+\left(2{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2y}},-D^{1}_{*0+0}\right)+\left(2{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x}C_{2y}},D^{1}_{*00+}\right)+2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{-})-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{+})

\displaystyle=\left((\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{C_{2x},C_{2x}},-D^{1}_{*+00}\right)+\left((\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{C_{2y},C_{2y}},-D^{1}_{*0+0}\right)+\left((\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{C_{2x}C_{2y},C_{2x}C_{2y}},D^{1}_{*00+}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{-})-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{+})

\displaystyle=\left(-d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x},C_{2x}},-D^{1}_{*+00}\right)+\left(-d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2y},C_{2y}},-D^{1}_{*0+0}\right)+\left(-d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{C_{2x}C_{2y},C_{2x}C_{2y}},D^{1}_{*00+}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{-})-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{+})

\displaystyle=\left({\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2x}}(P)+{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2y},C_{2y}}(P)-{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x}C_{2y},C_{2x}C_{2y}}(P)-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P)\right)\Big|_{P=P_{-}}^{P=P_{+}}

\displaystyle=\left({\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P)-{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{C_{2y},C_{2x}}(P)\right)\Big|_{P=P_{-}}^{P=P_{+}}

\displaystyle=\mu^{\rm T}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{+})-\mu^{\rm T}_{C_{2x},C_{2y}}(P_{-}).

(193)

Thus, as in the previous section, the parity of the DDKS invariant coincides with the difference of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
topological invariants evaluated at the fixed points
(\pm 1,0,0,0)
.

Together with Sec.
4.5.3
, this proved that the phase transition between the Haldane phase and the trivial phase, protected by time-reversal symmetry or unitary
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry, behaves as a source of higher Berry curvature.

4.6
Topological invariants and geometric phases defined by group actions

Neither the higher Berry phase
\gamma^{(2)}(\Sigma^{(2)})
nor the DDKS number
\nu^{(3)}(\Sigma^{(3)})
requires
G
-equivariance for their definitions.
In this section, we introduce certain types of higher Berry phases and topological invariants that arise only in the presence of
G
-equivariance.

4.6.1
Free
G
-action and Higher Berry Phase

Let us consider the case where a unitary group element
\sigma_{1}\in G
with
\phi_{\sigma_{1}}=1
acts freely on the parameter space
S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
of a two-dimensional cylinder as

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}:(\theta,t)\mapsto(\theta,t+1).

(194)

Then, a gauge-invariant higher Berry phase on a finite cylinder of unit length in the
\mathbb{R}
-direction can be defined using the action of
\sigma_{1}
as

\displaystyle\gamma^{(2)}\left(S^{1}\times[0,1];\sigma_{1}\right):=\left({\cal A}^{(0,2)},S^{1}\times[0,1]\right)+\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{\sigma_{1}},S^{1}\times\{0\}\right).

(195)

Indeed, under a gauge transformation (
118
), this quantity transforms as

\displaystyle\gamma^{(2)}\left(S^{1}\times[0,1]\right)\mapsto\gamma^{(2)}\left(S^{1}\times[0,1]\right)+\left(d\chi^{(0,1)},S^{1}\times[0,1]\right)+\left((\delta\chi^{(0,1)})_{\sigma_{1}}-d\chi^{(1,0)}_{\sigma_{1}},S^{1}\times\{0\}\right),

(196)

but the gauge-dependent terms cancel out.

Similarly, consider the planar parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
on which two commuting unitary group elements
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in G
, with
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}
and
\phi_{\sigma_{1}}=\phi_{\sigma_{2}}=1
, act freely as

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}:(s,t)\mapsto(s+1,t),

(197)

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}:(s,t)\mapsto(s,t+1).

(198)

In this case, a gauge-invariant higher Berry phase on the unit plaquette
[0,1]^{2}
is defined as

\displaystyle\gamma^{(2)}\left([0,1]^{2};\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\right)

\displaystyle:=\left({\cal A}^{(0,2)},[0,1]^{2}\right)+\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{\sigma_{1}},[0,1]\times\{0\}\right)-\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{\sigma_{2}},\{0\}\times[0,1]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}(0,0)-{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma_{2},\sigma_{1}}(0,0).

(199)

It is easy to verify that this expression is invariant under the gauge transformation (
118
).

4.6.2
Free
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
Action and a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
Topological Invariant

Consider the case where the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
group acts freely on the parameter space
S^{3}
(a three-dimensional sphere), that is, the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
element
\sigma
acts on the Cartesian coordinates
\bm{n}=(n_{0},n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
of
S^{3}
(with
\bm{n}^{2}=1
) as

\displaystyle\sigma:\bm{n}\mapsto-\bm{n},

(200)

and the action is antiunitary, i.e.,
\phi_{\sigma}=-1
. Since the action
\sigma
preserves the orientation of
S^{3}
, the DDKS number on
S^{3}
vanishes due to (
122
). On the other hand, the following
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-valued invariant can be defined:

\displaystyle\xi(S^{3};\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\left({\cal F}^{(0,3)},D^{3}\right)-\left({\cal A}^{(0,2)},D^{2}\right)-\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{\sigma},D^{1}\right)-{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,\sigma}(P_{+}).

(201)

Here,
D^{3},D^{2},D^{1},P_{+}
are defined in the same way as in (
168
)–(
171
). That
\xi(S^{3};\sigma)
takes values in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{0,\pi\}
is directly verified as follows. Noting the boundary relations of the chains:

\displaystyle\partial D^{3}=D^{2}-\sigma D^{2},\quad\partial D^{2}=D^{1}+\sigma D^{1},\quad\partial D^{1}=\sigma P_{+}-P_{+},

(202)

we compute:

\displaystyle 2\xi(S^{3};\sigma)

\displaystyle=\left(d{\cal A}^{(0,2)},D^{3}\right)-2\left({\cal A}^{(0,2)},D^{2}\right)-2\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{\sigma},D^{1}\right)-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,\sigma}(P_{+})

\displaystyle=\left({\cal A}^{(0,2)},D^{2}-\sigma D^{2}\right)-2\left({\cal A}^{(0,2)},D^{2}\right)-2\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{\sigma},D^{1}\right)-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,\sigma}(P_{+})

\displaystyle=\left((\delta{\cal A}^{(0,2)})_{\sigma},D^{2}\right)-2\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{\sigma},D^{1}\right)-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,\sigma}(P_{+})

\displaystyle=\left(d{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{\sigma},D^{2}\right)-2\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{\sigma},D^{1}\right)-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,\sigma}(P_{+})

\displaystyle=\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{\sigma},D^{1}+\sigma D^{1}\right)-2\left({\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{\sigma},D^{1}\right)-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,\sigma}(P_{+})

\displaystyle=\left((\delta{\cal A}^{(1,1)})_{\sigma,\sigma},D^{1}\right)-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,\sigma}(P_{+})

\displaystyle=\left(-d{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,\sigma},D^{1}\right)-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,\sigma}(P_{+})

\displaystyle=\left(-{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,\sigma},\sigma P_{+}-P_{+}\right)-2{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,\sigma}(P_{+})

\displaystyle=(\delta{\cal A}^{(2,0)})_{\sigma,\sigma,\sigma}(P_{+})=0.

(203)

Here we used
{\cal A}^{(1,1)}_{e}(\Delta^{1})=0
and
{\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{e,\sigma}(\Delta^{2})={\cal A}^{(2,0)}_{\sigma,e}(\Delta^{2})=0
. Gauge invariance is similarly established.

If there exists no symmetry that leaves any point inside
S^{3}
invariant, then the only allowed type of defect structure is a codimension-4 defect carrying a DDKS number. Since the DDKS number flips sign under the
\sigma
action, it follows that when the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant
\xi(S^{3};\sigma)
takes the nontrivial value
\pi
, the interior of
S^{3}
generically hosts a pair of codimension-4 defects with DDKS numbers
+1
and
-1
related under the
\sigma
action. At the fixed point of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
action, namely the center of the sphere
S^{3}
,
\tau=(0,0,0,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
, the pair annihilation of DDKS defects with values
+1
and
-1
is prohibited by the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
equivariance. Thus, a pair of such defects is topologically stable.

5
Discussion: defect structures in the parameter space

Finally, we summarize general properties of defect structures that may arise in the parameter space of one-dimensional quantum systems.

In this paper, a “non-defective” parameter point refers to a point
\tau
at which the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(\tau)
is gapped and does not exhibit SSB. Hence, a “defect” corresponds to a point where the system is either gapless or SSB.

5.1
Symmetry

Firstly, let us focus on the subgroup
G_{0}=\{g\in G\mid g\tau=\tau\}
of the symmetry group
G
that preserves a given parameter point
\tau
. The following types of topological defects can stably exist under the symmetry
G_{0}
:

•

If
H^{2}_{\mathrm{group}}(G_{0},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})
is nontrivial: codimension-1 SPT defects can exist.

•

If
H^{1}_{\mathrm{group}}(G_{0},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})
is nontrivial: codimension-2 pump defects can exist.

•

If antiunitary symmetries are present: the higher Berry phase
\gamma^{(2)}(S^{2})\in\{0,\pi\}
is quantized, and codimension-3 defects can exist.

•

If no antiunitary symmetries are present: the DDKS number
\nu^{(3)}(S^{3})\in\mathbb{Z}
leads to codimension-4 defects.

Based solely on logical possibilities (ignoring physical constraints that may further restrict realizability), the following general rules hold:

(i)

If a topological charge
\nu
characterizing a defect can be decomposed into small charges as
\nu=\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}
with
|\nu_{1}|,|\nu_{2}|<|\nu|
, then the corresponding defect can be split into two or more defects.

(ii)

When both codimension-
p
and codimension-
q
defects (
p<q
) are allowed under a given symmetry, the lower-codimension defect (codimension
p
) is generally realized. Furthermore, since a codimension-
q
defect is defined on
S^{q-1}
, it may enclose a closed submanifold composed of codimension-
p
defects (e.g., a conformal manifold) that carry the topological charge of the codimension-
q
defect.

As an example of rule (i), a codimension-4 defect with DDKS number
\nu^{(3)}(S^{3})=2
can be decomposed into two separated defects in the parameter space, each carrying
\nu=1
.

As for rule (ii), in the presence of time-reversal symmetry, codimension-3 defects characterized by
\gamma^{(2)}(S^{2})=\pi
cannot generally be realized without fine-tuning; instead, they appear as closed surfaces formed by codimension-1 defects inside
S^{2}
. Similarly, under a unitary
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry, the closed surface of codimension-2 defects corresponding to a Thouless pump may exist inside
S^{3}
, collectively carrying a nontrivial DDKS number.

Such general rules for defect structures are well-known in the context of Fermi surface topology in band theory, where lower-codimension defects emerge as Fermi surfaces under generic Bloch Hamiltonians respecting symmetry. On the other hand, in one-dimensional quantum many-body systems, the extent to which such rules apply remains an open question, as it may involve SSB defects and renormalization group flows.

5.2
G
-Equivariance

Let us consider the case where the symmetry group
G_{0}
that leaves a parameter point invariant is a subgroup of a larger group
G
with an equivariant structure. As discussed in Sec.
4
, topological invariants defined under the lower symmetry
G_{0}
may be related to those defined in the
G
-invariant subspace under the full group action. On the other hand, as shown in Sec.
4.6.2
, there also exist topological invariants that are defined only in the presence of
G
-equivariance.

We do not repeat the discussion of the latter type of defect structures here, since they were commented on at the end of Sec.
4.6.2
. Instead, we elaborate on the former case.

In general, suppose we have a subgroup inclusion
H\subset G
. Whether a codimension-
q
defect defined in the
G
-invariant subspace
{\cal M}^{G}=\{\tau\in{\cal M}\mid g\tau=\tau,\ g\in G\}
can be stably extended into the
H
-invariant subspace
{\cal M}^{H}=\{\tau\in{\cal M}\mid g\tau=\tau,\ g\in H\}
is determined by the compatibility between the sets of topological invariants
\{\nu_{G,i}(S^{q-1})\}_{i}
defined under
G
and
\{\nu_{H,i}(S^{q-1})\}_{i}
defined under
H
. Since the
G
-symmetry implies the
H
-symmetry, there exists a homomorphism between topological invariants

\displaystyle f:\{\nu_{G,i}(S^{q-1})\}_{i}\to\{\nu_{H,i}(S^{q-1})\}_{i},

(204)

and the kernel of this map, i.e., those defect charges
x
such that
f(x)=0
, corresponds to defects that are unstable under the
H
-symmetry and thus disappear in
{\cal M}^{H}
. In contrast, if
f(x)\neq 0
, then the same codimension-
q
defect structure remains stable.

5.2.1
Example

Let us consider the chain of subgroups of the equivariant structure of the model in Eq. (
130
):

\displaystyle\{e\}\subset G^{\prime\prime}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}[C_{2z}T]\subset G^{\prime}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}[C_{2z}T]\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}[C_{2y}T]\subset G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}[C_{2z}T]\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}[C_{2y}T]\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}[T].

(205)

We take the parameter space to be a four-dimensional vector
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. The
G
-invariant subspaces are given by:

\displaystyle(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{G}=\{(x_{0},0,0,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}\mid x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\},

(206)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{G^{\prime}}=\{(x_{0},x_{1},0,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}\mid x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}\},

(207)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{G^{\prime\prime}}=\{(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}\mid x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

(208)

On the line
(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{G}
, four
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
SPT invariants are defined:
(\mu^{\rm RP}_{C_{2z}T},\mu^{\rm RP}_{C_{2y}T},\mu^{\rm RP}_{T},\mu^{\rm T}_{C_{2z},C_{2x}})
. On the plane
(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{G^{\prime}}
, two
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
SPT invariants
(\mu^{\rm RP}_{C_{2z}T},\mu^{\rm RP}_{C_{2y}T})
and one
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
pump invariant
\eta_{C_{2x}}
are defined. On the 3D space
(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{G^{\prime\prime}}
, one
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
SPT invariant
\mu^{\rm RP}_{C_{2z}T}
and a quantized higher Berry phase
\gamma^{(2)}
are defined. Accordingly, the defect structure in the full parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{4}
depends on the topological charges on the line
(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{G}
as follows:

•

For
(\mu^{\rm RP}_{C_{2z}T},\mu^{\rm RP}_{C_{2y}T},\mu^{\rm RP}_{T},\mu^{\rm T}_{C_{2z},C_{2x}})=(0,0,\pi,0)
or
(0,0,0,\pi)
, the defect does not extend along the
\mathbb{R}^{4}
directions and becomes a codimension-4 defect, where the DDKS number
\nu^{(3)}(S^{3})
is defined. This is consistent with the fixed-point formulas (
175
) and (
193
).

•

For
(\mu^{\rm RP}_{C_{2z}T},\mu^{\rm RP}_{C_{2y}T},\mu^{\rm RP}_{T},\mu^{\rm T}_{C_{2z},C_{2x}})=(0,\pi,0,0)
, the defect extends into
(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{G^{\prime}}
and becomes a codimension-2 defect.

•

For
(\mu^{\rm RP}_{C_{2z}T},\mu^{\rm RP}_{C_{2y}T},\mu^{\rm RP}_{T},\mu^{\rm T}_{C_{2z},C_{2x}})=(\pi,0,0,0)
, the defect extends into
(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{G^{\prime\prime}}
and becomes a codimension-3 defect.

Moreover, since the pump invariant
\eta_{C_{2x}}
under symmetry
G^{\prime}
is not defined under
G^{\prime\prime}
, we obtain:

•

A point defect in the plane
(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{G^{\prime}}
with
\eta_{C_{2x}}=\pi
cannot extend into
\mathbb{R}^{4}
and thus becomes a codimension-4 defect, where the DDKS number is defined. This is consistent with the relation (
166
) between the DDKS number and the pump invariant.

Finally, the quantized higher Berry phase
\gamma^{(2)}
under symmetry
G^{\prime\prime}
is no longer quantized once the symmetry is broken. Thus:

•

A point defect in the three-dimensional space
(\mathbb{R}^{4})^{G^{\prime\prime}}
with
\gamma^{(2)}=\pi
cannot extend into
\mathbb{R}^{4}
and hence becomes a codimension-4 defect, where the DDKS number is defined. This is consistent with the relation (
164
) between the DDKS number and the quantized higher Berry phase.

We remark that the above hierarchical structure is consistent with the nonlinear
\sigma
-model description of SPT phases protected by symmetries defined on the sphere target space
[
56
]
.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have developed a discrete formulation of higher Berry connection and curvature that incorporates the
G
-equivariant structure of the symmetry group
G
, and systematically analyzed the structure of topological defects in the parameter space of one-dimensional quantum spin systems.

In Sec.
3
, we constructed a framework to incorporate
G
-equivariance into the discrete formulation of the higher Berry connection. We explicitly formulated the gauge transformation rules under group actions for the definitions of higher Berry connections and curvatures, and confirmed that the resulting constructions on the discretized parameter space are naturally described in terms of
G
-simplicial complexes. This framework enables a systematic discussion of various topological invariants, including their behavior under equivariant symmetry and the construction of topological invariants that require equivariance for their definition.

In Sec.
4
, we derived the relation between the DDKS number and the topological invariants defined on the invariant subspaces of the group action. In particular, we derived a fixed point formula in the case where the group action has isolated fixed points. Using this result, we explicitly demonstrated that the quantum critical point between the Haldane phase and the trivial phase manifests as an emergence of higher Berry curvature.

In Sec.
5
, we systematically discussed the structure of topological defects in the theory space (i.e., parameter space) of quantum spin systems. In particular, we illustrated with examples how defect structures are constrained and organized according to the subgroup relations among symmetries.

Appendix A
Simplicial
G
-complex

Let
{\cal M}
be a simplicial complex and
A
an abelian group. A
q
-simplex is denoted in shorthand as
\Delta^{q}=(\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{q})
. The
j
-th face of a
q
-simplex is written as

\displaystyle\partial_{j}\Delta^{q}:=(\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{j-1},\tau_{j+1},\dots,\tau_{q}).

(209)

The coboundary operator
d:C^{q}({\cal M},A)\to C^{q+1}({\cal M},A)
acting on the cochain complex
C^{q}({\cal M},A)
is defined by

\displaystyle(df)(\Delta^{q+1})=\sum_{j=0}^{q+1}(-1)^{j}f(\partial_{j}\Delta^{q+1}).

(210)

Let
G
be a discrete group with a left action on
{\cal M}
written as
(g,\tau)\mapsto g\tau
. Let
G
also act on
A
from the left, denoted by
(g,a)\mapsto g.a
. Then, left and right actions of
G
on the cochain complex
C^{q}({\cal M},A)
are defined by

\displaystyle(g\cdot f)(\Delta^{q}):=g.\big(f(\Delta^{q})\big),\quad(f\cdot g)(\Delta^{q}):=f(g\Delta^{q}),\quad f\in C^{q}({\cal M},A),

(211)

where we write
g\Delta^{q}:=(g\tau_{0},\dots,g\tau_{q})
as shorthand.
Based on this structure, we define the double complex combining group cochains and spatial cochains as

\displaystyle C^{p,q}_{G}({\cal M},A):=C^{p}(G,C^{q}({\cal M},A)).

(212)

For
f\in C^{p,q}
, the differential in the spatial direction
d:C^{p,q}_{G}({\cal M},A)\to C^{p,q+1}_{G}({\cal M},A)
is given by the standard cochain differential:

\displaystyle(df)_{g_{1},\dots,g_{p}}(\Delta^{q+1})=\sum_{j=0}^{q+1}(-1)^{j}f_{g_{1},\dots,g_{p}}(\partial_{j}\Delta^{q+1}).

(213)

On the other hand, the differential in the group direction
\delta:C^{p,q}_{G}({\cal M},A)\to C^{p+1,q}_{G}({\cal M},A)
is defined by the group cochain differential as

\displaystyle(\delta f)_{g_{1},\dots,g_{p+1}}=

\displaystyle\ g_{1}\cdot f_{g_{2},\dots,g_{p+1}}-f_{g_{1}g_{2},g_{3},\dots,g_{p+1}}+\cdots

\displaystyle+(-1)^{p-1}f_{g_{1},\dots,g_{p-1},g_{p}g_{p+1}}+(-1)^{p}f_{g_{1},\dots,g_{p}}\cdot g_{p+1}.

(214)

In low degrees, this gives:

\displaystyle(\delta f)_{g}(\Delta^{q})=g.\big(f(\Delta^{q})\big)-f(g\Delta^{q}),\quad f\in C^{0,q}_{G}({\cal M},A),

(215)

\displaystyle(\delta f)_{g,h}(\Delta^{q})=g.\big(f_{h}(\Delta^{q})\big)-f_{gh}(\Delta^{q})+f_{g}(h\Delta^{q}),\quad f\in C^{1,q}_{G}({\cal M},A),

(216)

\displaystyle(\delta f)_{g,h,k}(\Delta^{q})=g.\big(f_{h,k}(\Delta^{q})\big)-f_{gh,k}(\Delta^{q})+f_{g,hk}(\Delta^{q})-f_{g,h}(k\Delta^{q}),\quad f\in C^{2,q}_{G}({\cal M},A).

(217)

It is important to note that the differentials commute:

\displaystyle d\delta=\delta d.

(218)

The total differential on the entire double complex

\displaystyle D:\bigoplus_{p+q=n}C^{p,q}_{G}({\cal M},A)\to\bigoplus_{p+q=n+1}C^{p,q}_{G}({\cal M},A)

(219)

is defined as

\displaystyle D:=\delta+(-1)^{p}d.

(220)

That is, for an
n
-cochain

\displaystyle f^{n}=(f^{0,n},f^{1,n-1},\dots,f^{n,0}),\quad f^{p,q}\in C^{p,q}_{G}({\cal M},A),

(221)

the total differential is given by

\displaystyle Df^{n}=(df^{0,n},\ \delta f^{0,n}-df^{1,n-1},\ \dots,\ \delta f^{n-1,1}+(-1)^{n}df^{n,0},\ \delta f^{n,0}).

(222)

This total differential satisfies
D^{2}=0
. Thus, we define the
D
-coboundaries and
D
-cocycles by

\displaystyle B^{n}_{G}({\cal M},A):=\{Df^{n-1}\mid f^{n-1}\in\bigoplus_{p+q=n-1}C^{p,q}_{G}({\cal M},A)\},

(223)

\displaystyle Z^{n}_{G}({\cal M},A):=\{f^{n}\in\bigoplus_{p+q=n}C^{p,q}_{G}({\cal M},A)\mid Df^{n}=0\},

(224)

and the equivariant cohomology group is defined by

\displaystyle H^{n}_{G}({\cal M},A):=Z^{n}_{G}({\cal M},A)/B^{n}_{G}({\cal M},A).

(225)

A.1
Families of pure states

The gauge transformation and cocycle condition for
G
-equivariant families of pure states, as discussed in Section
2
, can be summarized from the perspective of the double complex as follows.

Let the coefficient abelian group be
A=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, and define the left action of the group
G
on
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
via the homomorphism
\phi:G\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}
(cf. Eq. (
6
)) that specifies whether each
g\in G
is unitary or anti-unitary:

\displaystyle g.\theta=\phi_{g}\theta,\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},\quad g\in G.

(226)

The discrete Berry connection (
9
) and the
U(1)
phase (
16
) define a pair of cochains of bidegree
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
:

\displaystyle{\sf A}=(A,\alpha)\in C^{0,1}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})\oplus C^{1,0}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}).

(227)

The gauge transformation (
10
) is generated by a
(0,0)
-cochain

\displaystyle\chi\in C^{0,0}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}),

(228)

and is compactly written as

\displaystyle{\sf A}\mapsto{\sf A}+D\chi.

(229)

The Berry curvature is given by a
(0,2)
-cocycle with real coefficients:

\displaystyle F\in C^{0,2}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}),\quad dF=0.

(230)

The cocycle condition for the cochain
{\sf A}
is written as

\displaystyle D{\sf A}\equiv F\quad\bmod 2\pi.

(231)

A.2
Families of MPS

The gauge transformation and cocycle condition for families of injective MPS with
G
-equivariance, as discussed in Section
3
, can similarly be formulated using the double complex.

As in the previous subsection, let the coefficient abelian group be
A=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
and define the left
G
-action on
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
via Eq. (
226
).
The
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
cochains are defined as:

\displaystyle{\sf A}^{(1)}=({\cal A}^{(0,1)},{\cal A}^{(1,0)})\in C^{0,1}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})\oplus C^{1,0}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}),

(232)

and transform under gauge transformation as

\displaystyle{\sf A}^{(1)}\mapsto{\sf A}^{(1)}-D\chi^{(0,0)},\quad\chi^{(0,0)}\in C^{0,0}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}).

(233)

The Berry curvature is a
(0,2)
-cocycle with real coefficients:

\displaystyle{\cal F}^{(2)}\in C^{0,2}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}),\quad d{\cal F}^{(2)}=0.

(234)

The cocycle condition is given by

\displaystyle D{\sf A}^{(1)}\equiv{\cal F}^{(2)}\quad\bmod 2\pi.

(235)

The cochains of bidegree
(0,2)
,
(1,1)
, and
(2,0)
are collectively organized as a degree-2
D
-cochain:

\displaystyle{\sf A}^{(2)}=({\cal A}^{(0,2)},{\cal A}^{(1,1)},{\cal A}^{(2,0)})\in C^{0,2}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})\oplus C^{1,1}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})\oplus C^{2,0}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}).

(236)

The corresponding gauge transformation is

\displaystyle{\sf A}^{(2)}\mapsto{\sf A}^{(2)}+D{\sf X}^{(1)},\quad{\sf X}^{(1)}=(\chi^{(0,1)},\chi^{(1,0)})\in C^{0,1}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})\oplus C^{1,0}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}).

(237)

The higher Berry curvature is a
(0,3)
-cocycle with real coefficients:

\displaystyle{\cal F}^{(3)}\in C^{0,3}_{G}({\cal M},\mathbb{R}),\quad d{\cal F}^{(3)}=0,

(238)

and the cocycle condition is

\displaystyle D{\sf A}^{(2)}\equiv{\cal F}^{(3)}\quad\bmod 2\pi.

(239)
