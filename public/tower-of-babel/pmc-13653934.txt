Artificial intelligence for detecting fall events, assessing fall risks, and preventing fall occurrences in the elderly: A narrative review
Authors: Chen-Chang Shih, Syaun-Huei Lin, Wei-Jang Yen, Guang-Chih Cheng, Whai-En Chen, Chih-Chung Shiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Falls in the elderly represent a critical public health crisis, necessitating a shift from reactive to proactive management. This review evaluates artificial intelligence (AI) across four primary modalities: wearable sensors, vision-based systems, ambient devices, and natural language processing and large language models applied to electronic health records. Machine learning and deep learning algorithms enable real-time detection, near-fall identification, and predictive risk stratification. Hybrid edge–cloud frameworks and multimodal fusion enhance accuracy and scalability. However, challenges include the ʻsimulation gap’ in training data, ʻblack box’ interpretability, and privacy concerns, ʻalarm fatigue’ among nursing staff, and ʻclass imbalance.’ Future directions emphasise hybrid systems, integrating multiple sensor streams with edge-cloud computing, to enhance ecological validity and responsiveness. Ultimately, seamless integration of interpretable AI into clinical workflows offers a transformative path toward personalised prevention, reducing fall-related morbidity and empowering elderly independence.

Introduction

Falls in older adults are a major public health crisis, causing significant morbidity and high healthcare costs. Annually, one-third of individuals over 65 fall once, with 15% falling repeatedly.1 Community-dwelling adults face a 33% fall rate,2 while hospitalised patients have amplified risks.3 Consequences include fractures, head trauma, and psychological impacts.1, 2 Falls are multifactorial, resulting from a complex mix of intrinsic factors and extrinsic factors.1, 4 Traditional assessments rely on clinical observation and gait tools, which are often subjective or time-consuming.4

The emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) has transformed fall risk management by offering innovative solutions for fall detection, prediction, and prevention. AI technologies like machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), natural language processing (NLP), and large language models (LLMs) can analyse vast datasets from electronic health records (EHRs), wearable sensors, and video footage. This capability allows for more objective, continuous, and personalised risk assessments.

This review outlines the current landscape of fall management and explores how AI can overcome existing challenges through advanced detection, assessment, and prevention strategies. Given the widespread impact of falls on the elderly, integrating AI is crucial. While recent reviews have advanced our understanding of specific AI applications focusing narrowly on either fall prediction or detection,5, 6 a critical gap remains in synthesising the entire fall management lifecycle. This review provides a holistic integration of detection, assessment, and prevention strategies. Furthermore, it expands the traditional technological scope by evaluating the emerging role of NLP and LLMs in clinical data analysis, addressing real-world challenges and future directions that are vital for clinical adoption.

Detecting fall events by wearable sensor-based approaches

AI-driven systems using wearable sensors detect fall events in real time. The key challenge is distinguishing falls from normal activities of daily living (ADLs). Early ML algorithms like Support Vector Machine (SVM) achieved high classification accuracy using triaxial accelerometer data.7 Advanced DL architectures, such as a DL algorithm combining a one-dimensional Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) with an attention mechanism and a specialised loss function, have been proposed and achieved superior performance.8 Lee et al.9 disclosed a promising result by applying a DL model for fall monitoring using a custom-made skin-worn electronic patch. For clinical utility, systems must be real-time. Mrozek et al.10 proposed a scalable, Cloud-based system using mobile Internet of Things (IoT) devices and ML algorithms like Boosted Decision Trees (DTs), with an Edge-based approach reducing latency by processing data directly on the device. Additionally, Alfayez et al.11 proposed an IoT-blockchain-empowered system (Trinet) to utilise data collected from wearable devices with good performance for fall detection.

Research also focuses on identifying near-fall states. Palazzo et al.12 developed a CNN-based framework for pathological gait recognition using five body-worn inertial sensors. Nineteen healthy subjects simulated four gait disorders. The sequential CNN achieved 100% accuracy and rapid inference. Nevertheless, it is worth mentioning that these results were obtained via simulated falls, which may not reflect the biomechanical complexities of spontaneous falls in frail older adults.

Assessing fall risk by wearable sensor-based approaches

AI is instrumental in moving from simple detection to quantitative fall risk assessment, and AI’s analysis extends to clinical tests and daily life. For the application of traditional ML algorithms, Hauth et al.13 used wearable IMUs and a Random Forest (RF) algorithm to classify fall risk based on gait features like speed and stride length. Similarly, Lockhart et al.14 also used an RF model with wearable IMU data to predict fall risk in community-dwelling older adults, achieving 81.6% accuracy and 86.7% sensitivity. Unsupervised ML using smartphone data is associated strongly with risk factors.15 A gradient boosting decision tree analysing the three-dimensional pelvic motion characteristics during a single gait cycle can accurately classify risk.16

Nait Aicha et al.17 investigated DL models (CNNs, LSTM, and ConvLSTM) to automatically derive features from raw accelerometer data, finding that multi-task learning improves fall risk assessment over handcrafted features. Maiora et al.18 found that DL approaches, especially Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BLSTM), yielded the most accurate results when processing IMU data from the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test. Roshdibenam et al.19 developed a CNN model using three IMU sensors during a TUG test to predict fall-risk status, comparable to a geriatrician's expert assessment. Buisseret et al.20 showed that parameters from the TUG and six-minute walking tests (6MWT) using a single wearable IMU and an AI algorithm can improve fall risk assessment in nursing home residents. A systematic review concluded that AI is a valuable tool for creating highly accurate and low-cost predictive models for fall risk, with reviewed studies reporting accuracy exceeding 70%.5 Nevertheless, it is essential to note that accuracy is a limited metric for rare events like falls and that future studies should consider prediction horizon definition, calibration, decision thresholds, and workload impact.

Besides, the emergence of wearable plantar pressure-based assessment represents a significant advancement in objective fall risk stratification. In-shoe pressure sensors offer a discreet and comfortable modality for continuous monitoring.21, 22 Recent studies have addressed the challenge of subject variability through innovative architectures like MhNet, which utilises multi-scale spatio-temporal features to achieve real-time assessment with high sensitivity within just nine gait cycles.21 Furthermore, the introduction of the ʻweak foot’ concept has refined the extraction of temporal consistency and gait symmetry features, significantly outperforming traditional bipedal centre-of-pressure models.22 To bridge the gap between clinical scores and real-world data, semi-supervised learning frameworks have been successfully deployed to predict Berg Balance Scale scores while mitigating the inherent ʻlabel noise’ and subjectivity of traditional clinical evaluations.23 These technologies transition fall risk assessment from periodic, subjective clinical observations to continuous, data-driven insights, facilitating timely community-based interventions.23

Preventing fall occurrence by wearable sensor-based approaches

AI-driven systems contribute to prevention by identifying precursors to falls. The wearable IMUs and an RF algorithm by Hauth et al.,13 which automatically identify subtle ʻloss-of-balance events’ in real-world walking, serve as an early warning system. Also, Choi et al.24 developed a hybrid CNN-LSTM DL model using a single IMU to detect ʻnear-falls’ of simulated near-falls with good accuracy.

While some studies report a significant reduction in falls using sensor-enabled wearable systems in hospitals,25 others, such as the trial of the Ambient Intelligent Geriatric Management (AmbIGeM) system,26 found no significant reduction in fall rates. The mixed or non-significant results for wearables in hospital settings were supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis by Morris et al.27 The divergence in in-hospital fall-reduction outcomes often stems from a lack of seamless workflow integration. Even high-accuracy AI systems can fail due to ʻalarm fatigue,’ where excessive false positives lead nursing staff to desensitise to warnings, or a lack of intervention capacity to act on real-time data.

Detecting fall event by vision-based approaches

Vision-based systems using AI have an effect on fall detection in simulated settings. Espinosa et al.28 used multiple cameras and a CNN to classify simulated fall events. To enhance privacy, alternative systems are employed: Yang et al.29 proposed a method for infra-red videos using a Spatial-Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN) to analyse human skeletons, offering an effective, privacy-conscious solution. Similarly, Newaz et al.30 used statistical distributions of thermal signatures from a low-resolution infra-red array sensor with high accuracy.

Assessing fall risk by vision-based approaches

Vision-based systems excel at non-contact extraction of biomechanical parameters. Dubois et al.31 used a depth sensor and ML algorithms to monitor daily activities and identify patterns in gait and mobility that serve as fall risk predictors. Kanko et al.32 validated that video-based markerless motion capture can accurately measure spatiotemporal gait parameters, showing good agreement with traditional systems.

Preventing fall occurrence by vision-based approaches

Vision-based systems are designed for real-time fall detection, which allows for immediate alerts to caregivers or emergency services, thereby preventing prolonged periods of immobility after a fall. For example, the systems described by Yang et al.29 and Newaz et al.30 are specifically designed to provide these real-time alerts, which are a direct form of fall prevention. Beyond immediate detection, the analysis of movement patterns for risk assessment also serves a preventative role. The work of Dubois et al.,31 which monitors daily activities with a depth sensor and ML algorithms, provides a proactive approach by alerting clinicians to increasing risk before a fall occurs.

Ambient sensor-based approaches

Detecting fall event by ambient sensor-based approaches

Droghini et al.33 used a two-stage classifier with a Floor Acoustic Sensor (combining a One-Class SVM and a template-matching algorithm) to discriminate falls based on acoustic signals, improving performance by over 10%. Ultrasonic-based sensor systems detect sudden changes in height with good accuracy.34 Radar-based systems offer advanced detection: a continuous wave (CW) Doppler radar sensor analyses Doppler signatures and can track post-fall breath rate.35

An Impulse Radio-Ultra-Wideband (IR-UWB) radar system with a multi-task learning (MTL) approach uses DL to simultaneously perform fall detection and identify the person who fell.36 A robust millimetre-wave (mmWave) radar system uses DL for simultaneous multi-human tracking and fall detection in multi-occupant environments.37 Li et al.38 developed a convolutional bidirectional long short-term memory (CB-LSTM) DL model on Doppler radar sensor data that achieved a high accuracy of 98.83%. Finally, an ultra-wideband (UWB) radar system using an adaptive weighted fusion (AWF) algorithm significantly improved performance by combining data from multiple sensors.39

Assessing fall risk by ambient sensor-based approaches

Long-term monitoring with ambient sensors and predictive analytics can anticipate a decline in an individual's mobility, thus predicting future falls. This provides an automatic and efficient method for fall risk assessment. A study by Eichler et al.40 described a system using ambient sensors to analyse data on daily activities and mobility patterns over time, concluding that this approach can effectively predict future falls.

Preventing fall occurrence by ambient sensor-based approaches

Ambient sensor systems contribute to fall prevention in two main ways. First, they enable a rapid response through real-time detection and post-fall monitoring. For instance, a Doppler radar system can track a person's breath rate after a fall, which is crucial for assessing their condition.35 Second, these systems provide a proactive approach to prevention by analysing mobility patterns over time. The work of Eichler et al.40 demonstrated that these systems can alert clinicians to increasing fall risk before a fall occurs. When a risky situation is detected, they can trigger automated responses, such as adjusting lighting or alerting caregivers. These environmental AI systems can also integrate with smart home technologies to create adaptive living environments that help prevent falls.

NLP and LLMs

Beyond physical sensors, AI can be applied to other data sources, such as EHRs, mobility assessments, and patient histories, to assess fall risk. NLP is a powerful tool for analysing unstructured clinical data to identify key risk factors and patterns that may indicate an increased likelihood of falls. LLMs represent a cutting-edge approach that can process and analyse vast amounts of clinical data, including patient histories and notes, to generate comprehensive fall risk assessments (Supplementary Table 1).

Detecting fall event by NLP and LLMs

Patterson et al.41 developed a simple, rules-based NLP algorithm to automatically identify fall events in emergency department notes. The algorithm demonstrated excellent performance, proving its potential for research, surveillance, and clinical interventions.41

Assessing fall risk by NLP and LLMs

ML combined with an NLP algorithm can effectively predict hospital falls by analysing unstructured nursing records, showing high accuracy and extracting morphemes related to known risk factors.42 Jahandideh et al.43 used deep neural networks and RF models to predict hospital falls from massive EHR data. Liu et al.44 used ML models to predict inpatient falls at four different stages of hospitalisation, finding ensemble classifiers optimal for timely, individualised prevention. LLMs are also being explored for their potential. Kaiser et al.45 reported that LLMs have the potential to streamline Multidisciplinary teams' workflows, enabling specialists to focus on complex cases and patient-centred discussions, and assisting in treatment decision-making for prostate cancer patients.

Preventing fall occurrence by NLP and LLMs

ML models can use readily available clinical and demographic data from sources like EHRs to accurately assess the fall risk of hospitalised patients. The predictive models developed by Nakatani et al.42 can directly contribute to fall prevention by identifying at-risk patients from their clinical notes, allowing for targeted interventions. Similarly, a clinical decision support system that uses ML models trained on EHR data has demonstrated significant reductions in fall rates by enabling timely interventions. The ability of these models to predict the severity of inpatient falls is also a crucial aspect of prevention.3, 46 Wang et al.46 used an ML classifier that integrated multi-view ensemble learning to predict the severity of falls, providing a tool to identify patients at risk of severe injuries.

While LLMs are still being refined for clinical use, they have potential applications in healthcare workflows that could indirectly support fall prevention. LLMs can simplify documentation, aid in patient communication, and assist with medical diagnosis.

Although traditional ML models currently outperform LLMs in some diagnostic tasks, future hybrid approaches may improve their accuracy and reliability for clinical use.

The customisation of LLMs may also help overcome limitations such as bias, ethics, and cost-effectiveness. For example, Kaiser et al.45 found that two LLMs, given clinical data and guidelines, achieved 93% adherence with the actual recommendations of a multidisciplinary team for prostate cancer treatment. The findings suggest that LLMs could streamline workflows and help specialists focus on complex cases.

Hybrid approaches for comprehensive fall management

Hybrid approaches, which combine different sensing technologies or data sources, are emerging as a way to enhance accuracy and robustness in fall prevention systems, as a single sensing modality may not be sufficient for a comprehensive system. Some hybrid approaches combine different types of sensors to improve performance. For example, a system called ʻSmartFall’ uses transfer learning to combine data from multiple wearable devices. This approach can significantly improve the accuracy of smartwatch-based fall detection and enables the development of generalised models that function effectively across diverse device types, which is crucial for widespread implementation.

The collaborative edge-cloud framework presented by Mrozek et al.10 can also be seen as a hybrid approach, as it combines local processing on a smartwatch (ʻedge’) with centralised model training and updates in the ʻcloud.’ Another type of hybrid approach involves fusing AI with other technologies, such as blockchain and the IoT. For instance, the IoT-blockchain-empowered system by Alfayez et al.11 uses a combination of wearable sensors and a blockchain framework for fall detection, providing data security and integrity, which is crucial for health-related information.

The simulation gap and ecological validity

A critical limitation in current AI fall-detection research is the Simulation Gap. Most high-performing models rely on datasets from young, healthy volunteers performing ʻstaged’ falls on padded surfaces, which fail to replicate the complex biomechanics of geriatric events.

In clinical practice, geriatric falls are frequently characterised by: (1) Compensatory dynamics, including near-fall ʻstaggering’ or grasping strategies that are often misclassified by AI. (2) Slow-motion descents caused by sarcopenia or muscle weakness, which lack the high-velocity impact signature of a simulated fall. Besides, environmental noise, including the use of mobility aids (walkers/canes) and the ʻlong-lie’ phenomenon, introduces variables that current laboratory-trained models struggle to interpret. To improve ecological validity, future research must prioritise ʻin-the-wild’ datasets and utilise transfer learning to adapt models to the heterogeneous movement patterns of the elderly population.48

Socio-technical-ethical barriers and concrete risk-mitigation points

A successful fall management process includes increased detection, prompt response, adequate risk management, and improved clinical outcomes. The clinical translation of AI from bench to bedside is hindered by a complex intersection of systemic and ethical hurdles. (1) The ʻBlack box’ problem denotes a lack of interpretability in DL models, eroding clinician trust and creating legal vulnerabilities regarding the duty of care.49 (2) Privacy considerations, particularly with vision-based systems, limit environmental suitability. (3) ʻAlarm fatigue’ among nursing staff and delays in the behavioural adoption of digital workflows stifle real-time intervention.50 (4) ʻClass Imbalance’ of statistically rare fall events compared to daily activities often leads to excessive false positives, further undermining clinical utility.

To ensure clinical safety, AI implementation must transition from black-box models to transparent frameworks. Privacy governance utilises edge computing and skeletal mapping to protect patient autonomy. To combat hallucinations in NLP, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) ensures data fidelity. Addressing version drift requires continuous performance auditing to maintain accuracy as clinical environments evolve. Finally, auditability and human-in-the-loop decision support ensure that AI flags act as expert adjuncts rather than autonomous agents, preserving clinician oversight and the duty of care.49, 50

Strategic directions: towards integrated, interpretable AI

Future directions in AI-driven fall prevention must focus on overcoming current challenges by creating more interpretable AI models that balance predictive accuracy with ethical considerations.49

A key strategy is to develop generalisable and robust models through hybrid approaches that fuse data from multiple sensors, EHRs, and ambient systems.39 To address the data imbalance problem, exploring synthetic data generation techniques like Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) is a promising avenue.51 Scalability and privacy concerns can be mitigated through edge-based processing10 and the refinement of ambient, non-intrusive sensor systems such as those utilising radar or thermal imaging.30, 37 Ultimately, for these technologies to have a real-world impact, future efforts must prioritise their seamless integration into existing clinical workflows and EHRs to enable proactive, personalised interventions.3, 43

Conclusions

The integration of AI represents a paradigm shift in fall management for older adults, moving beyond traditional, reactive approaches. By leveraging AI-driven technologies like wearable sensors, vision-based sensors, and ambient systems, we can achieve more objective, continuous, and proactive fall risk assessments. While challenges in data privacy and algorithm interpretability persist, the continued evolution of ML, hybrid sensor systems, and seamless integration into clinical workflows holds immense promise for personalised, preventative care. This will ultimately reduce fall-related injuries and empower the elderly to maintain independence and safety.
