Social inequities in general health and oral health among Australian adults over time
Authors: Xiangqun Ju, Liana Luzzi, Lisa M. Jamieson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial inequality in health is a persistent and growing concern worldwide. The study aimed to describe and analyse social disparities in general and oral health among Australian adults over time.MethodsThis study utilised two population-based cross-sectional survey data: the National Survey of Adult Oral Health (NSAOH) conducted in 2004-06 (NSAOH-1, n = 14,123) and 2017-18 (NSAOH-2, n = 15,731) in Australia. Representative samples of adults aged 15 years and over were recruited from metropolitan and regional areas in each state and territory using a three-stage, stratified sampling design. Outcome variables were self-rated general and oral health conditions. Explanatory variables included socioeconomic status- assessed using equivalised household income and divided into roughly equal quartile groups from lowest to highest- along with sociodemographic characteristics, culturally and linguistically diverse status, and oral health-related behaviours. Both unadjusted and adjusted absolute prevalence differences (PDs) of fair/poor general and oral health with 95% confidence intervals were calculated to assess income-related inequality. Adjustments were made for age and sex to the average covariate distribution of the four household income groups combined.ResultsThe prevalence of fair/poor general and oral health increased from 10.6% to 15.0% and from 16.4% to 23.9%, respectively, over the 14-year survey period. Across all household income quartiles, the prevalence of fair/poor general and oral health was higher in NSAOH-2 than in NSAOH-1. The adjusted model showed that the prevalence increase was most marked for the second lowest household income group (Q2), from 18.7% to 26.7% (PD = 8.0%) for oral health and the second highest household income group (Q3), from 6.4% to 10.8% (PD = 4.4%) for general health.ConclusionOur study revealed social inequalities in general and oral health among Australian adults over 14 years, with age, sex, place of residence, irregular dental visits, and dental insurance being significant factors contributing to these inequities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25523-w.

Introduction

Health inequities are a persistent and growing concern worldwide. Social inequality is a determinant of people’s health levels, not only for general health but also for oral health. Despite advancements in medical science and healthcare delivery, certain populations experience poorer health outcomes than others. Social determinants of health, such as income, education, employment, and social support, play a crucial role in shaping these disparities [1]. These social determinants significantly affect both general and oral health, leading to disparities in morbidity, mortality, and quality of life [1]. According to estimates by the World Health Organisation, 30–55% of health outcomes were accounted for by Socioeconomic Status (SES). In Australia, inequities in both general and oral health are more evident in the social factors that influence access to care and treatment than in the measured prevalence of diseases themselves [2, 3].

Socioeconomic status and health

Socioeconomic status is widely recognised as one of the most influential determinants of health outcomes. Individuals from lower SES backgrounds often face barriers to accessing healthcare services, nutritious food, safe living environments, and quality education, which are associated with lower levels of health literacy [4–8]. These barriers collectively contribute to increased rates of chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and respiratory illnesses [9–11]. Additionally, poorer oral health outcomes, including a higher prevalence of dental caries and periodontal disease, are more frequently observed among individuals with lower SES, largely due to the same structural and social barriers outlined above [2, 12].

Oral health and general health

Although often viewed as distinct from general health, oral health is closely linked to overall well-being and is equally shaped by social inequities [13]. Inequity refers to unjust or unfair differences in health that are avoidable and systematically associated with social disadvantage [14], whereas inequality refers to measurable differences in health or oral health outcomes between population groups, such as those observed across income levels [15]. Oral health is a critical component of general health, and the two are interconnected. Poor oral health can exacerbate systemic conditions, such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, while systemic conditions can also negatively impact oral health [16–18]. For example, individuals with diabetes are more susceptible to periodontal diseases, and poor oral health can complicate diabetes management [17]. Similarly, tobacco smoking, a behaviour influenced by social determinants, is a risk factor for both oral cancer and other cancers, such as lung, blood or cervical cancers [19].

This study is motivated by the well-established impact of social determinants on both general and oral health, and the ongoing disparities observed among different population groups in Australia. Although overall health has improved, inequities persist—particularly in access to care and health outcomes—underscoring the need for current evidence to guide policy and resource allocation. Accordingly, this study aims to provide up-to-date evidence on social disparities in general health and oral health among Australian adults, with objectives to examine trends over time, identify associated factors, and assess potential differences across data collection methods to inform policy decisions.

Methods

This study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for transparent and comprehensive reporting.

Study design and participants

This study drew on data from the National Survey of Adult Oral Health (NSAOH) in Australia, specifically NSAOH-1 (2004–06) and NSAOH-2 (2017–18) [20, 21]. Both population-based cross-sectional surveys recruited adults aged 15 years and over from metropolitan and regional areas in each state and territory using a three-stage, stratified sampling design. Data were weighted following standard procedures for clustered samples. Individuals who were selected were invited to respond to a questionnaire.

Data collection

Computer-assisted telephone interviews (CATI) were used to collect self-reported data on general and oral health in 2004–06, while in 2017–18, data were collected using either an online questionnaire or CATI. CATI refers to a method of administering the primary questionnaire by telephone, in which trained interviewers use a computer-administered script to ask respondents questions and enter their responses directly into a computer system. Additionally, an online questionnaire was offered as an alternative mode for selected participants to complete the interview component of NSAOH [20].

Ethics approval

Both NSAOH-1 and NSAOH-2 were reviewed and approved by the University of Adelaide Human Research Ethics Committee. All participants provided written informed consent.

Study variables

The outcome variables were self-rated general health (SRGH) and oral health (SROH). Both variables were assessed using the questions ‘How would you rate your general health?’ and ‘How would you rate your dental health?’, and then dichotomised into ‘Excellent/very good/good’ or ‘Fair/poor’.

The exposure variable was socioeconomic status, measured by equivalised household income. Income was calculated using the OECD-modified scale [22], which assigns a weight of 1 to the first adult, 0.5 to the second adult, and 0.3 to each child, and then divided it into roughly equal quartile groups from lowest (Q1) to highest (Q4).

Covariates included socio-demographic characteristics (age, sex, and residential location), culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) status, and oral health-related behaviours (such as last dental visit and dental insurance), with details reported in our previous study [23]. CALD refers to individuals and communities in Australia whose cultural or linguistic backgrounds differ from the dominant Anglo-Celtic culture. This group typically includes people born in non-English-speaking countries, those who speak a language other than English at home, and those who identify with diverse cultural heritages or statuses [24].

Statistical analysis

The univariate statistics describing the percentage of sample characteristics and associated prevalence and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of fair/poor self-rated oral health and general health. Adjusted estimates accounted for age and sex by standardising to the average covariate distribution across the four household income groups. The Cochran-Armitage test [25] for trend was considered significant at a p-value < 0.05. Additionally, multivariable logistic regression models were employed to estimate associations between fair/poor general and oral health and explanatory variables. Separate models were also fitted to estimate associations for “fair” and “poor” general and oral health individually. Odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported from the multivariable analyses. The goodness-of-fit of the multivariable logistic regression model was evaluated using the Pearson Chi-Square statistic [26].

Sensitivity analysis was performed using imputed data. Missing values were addressed through Multiple Imputation (MI) under the assumption that data were missing at random (MAR). The Fully Conditional Specification (FCS) method was applied, utilising logistic regression for categorical variables and linear regression for continuous variables. All missing data, including both outcome and explanatory variables, were imputed.

Data files were managed, and summary variables were computed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Weights were used to account for the complex sampling methodology of the survey.

Results

There were 14,123 participants in NSAOH-1 and 15,731 participants in NSAOH-2, respectively. The sample characteristics and prevalence of fair/poor general and oral health are shown in Table 1. The average ages in NSAOH-1 and NSAOH-2 were 44.5 and 45.4 years, respectively. Statistically significant differences between the two time points were observed for participants identifying as CALD (9.7% vs. 14.4%), those in the lowest household income quartile (20.3% vs. 30.4%), and those whose last dental visit was more than two years ago (59.4% vs. 56.4%).

Overall, the prevalence of fair/poor general and oral health increased from 10.6% to 15.0% and 16.4% to 23.9%, respectively, with oral health deficits consistently higher. The largest deficit was seen in those whose last dental visit was over 12 months ago (9.6% to 13.5%). Fair/poor general health increased across all age groups, sexes, residential locations, CALD status, income levels, dental visit recency, and dental insurance coverage. A similar pattern was observed for oral health, with the greatest increase among those without dental insurance (20.9% to 32.8%) and the smallest among those with insurance (11.7% to 15.5%).

Figure 1 presents the prevalence of fair/poor general (‘a’) and oral (‘b’) health across all household income quartiles and the two time points. With the increase in household income from the lowest (Q1) to the highest (Q4), the prevalence of fair/poor general and oral health showed an obvious downward trend in both NSAOH-1 and NSAOH-2, with p-values < 0.0001 in each case.

Table 2 presents both the unadjusted and adjusted prevalence of fair/poor self-rated oral health at two time points. Across all household income quartiles, both unadjusted and adjusted prevalence of fair/poor oral health was higher in NSAOH-2 than in NSAOH-1. This increase was most marked for the second lowest household income group (Q2), from 18.7 to 26.7 (PD = 8.0); followed by the lowest household income group (Q1), from 26.9 to 33.6 (PD = 6.8); and the third was the second highest household income group (Q3), from 13.5 to 19.6 (PD = 6.2). After adjusting for age and sex, a similar pattern was observed. Comparing the lowest to highest household income quartiles, the prevalence difference was 14.9 in NSAOH-1 (from 26.9 to 12.0) and 18.7 in NSAOH-2 (from 33.3 to 14.6). This indicates that social inequality in the prevalence of fair/poor oral health increased over the 14-year survey periods (PDs = 3.8). Similar patterns were also observed in the sensitivity analysis, as shown in Table 2.

Discussion

Main finding of this study

Our study found social inequalities in fair/poor general and oral health among Australian adults from 2004 to 06 to 2017-18. The inequalities were observed between household groups at each time point, with a decrease in household income from the highest (Q4) to the lowest (Q1) equating to an increase in the prevalence of fair/poor self-rated general and oral health. Ultimately, however, the inequalities increased across 14 years. Age, sex, place of residence, and dental health-related behaviours (irregular dental visits and lack of dental insurance) were important factors affecting these inequities.

Our findings highlight the pivotal role of socioeconomic status in shaping both general and oral health, with lower household income associated with an increased risk of fair or poor health outcomes. This income-related inequity is likely driven by structural and financial barriers that limit access to healthcare and preventive services. In particular, financial constraints restrict the ability to obtain health insurance, including dental coverage, which is a key determinant of timely and regular care. Previous evidence [27, 28] demonstrates that socioeconomic status strongly influences access to health and dental insurance, perpetuating disparities that favour wealthier populations. Individuals without coverage are more likely to delay care, rely on irregular or emergency sources of treatment, and forgo preventive services [27, 29]. These barriers cumulatively reduce opportunities for early detection, prevention, and treatment, ultimately exacerbating both general and oral health inequalities.

Our research revealed an upward trend in the prevalence of fair or poor general and oral health among Australian adults between 2004 and 06 and 2017-18. Several interrelated factors may underlie this pattern. First, the prevalence of chronic conditions—including asthma, cancer, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease- rose from 42% in 2007-08 to 47% in 2017-18 [30], reflecting an increasing burden of multimorbidity that likely contributes to declines in both general and oral health. Second, oral disease indicators worsened over the same period: the mean number of decayed teeth increased from 0.6 to 0.8, and the prevalence of moderate or severe periodontitis rose from 22.9% to 30.1% [31]. These trends suggest that improvements in overall health have not translated into reductions in dental disease, highlighting gaps in preventive care and access to dental services. Third, demographic shifts, particularly population aging, may intensify these trends: the proportion of Australians aged 65 years and older grew from 12.8% in 2004 to 15.4% in 2017 [32], a change associated with higher risks of both chronic and dental conditions. Collectively, these findings indicate that increasing disease burden, inadequate preventive care, and demographic pressures are likely interacting to exacerbate general and oral health inequities over time.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths of the study include assessing and comparing two large, representative samples of Australian adults by using self-reported general and oral health conditions, which has been a valid assessment used in the Australian context for over 20 years [33]. This study has several limitations: (1) the use of cross-sectional data limits the ability to assess the temporality and direction of relationships between variables, (2) Missing data on household income (7.5%) may introduce bias; however, the use of survey weights and a large sample size helps reduce random error, (3) We also acknowledge that weighting cannot fully adjust for unmeasured factors influencing participation (e.g., oral health status). Consequently, even after weighting, individuals with poorer oral health may remain under-represented, potentially leading to biased prevalence estimates. (4) This study relied on self-reported measures for general and oral health. Although these measures are commonly used and have been shown to correlate with clinical outcomes, using validated scales would have allowed for a more precise assessment and potentially reduced measurement bias. Future research could incorporate objective or standardised health assessments to strengthen the evidence base, and (5) using income alone as an exploratory variable may not fully reflect the multidimensional nature of social inequities. Future studies that incorporate multiple indicators of socioeconomic position, such as education and occupation, would enable a more comprehensive assessment of health inequities.

Social inequities in general and oral health are complex and multifaceted issues that require comprehensive and collaborative solutions. Addressing the root causes of these disparities, such as poverty, education, and systemic discrimination, is essential for achieving health equity. Our study is important to provide evidence and inform policymakers to make general and oral health plans and policies to mitigate these inequalities.

Conclusion

Our study indicated social inequalities in both general and oral health among Australian adults across 14 years, with age, sex, irregular dental visits and dental insurance being important factors associated with fair/poor general and oral health.

Addressing social inequality in general and oral health requires a multifaceted approach, such as policy interventions, community-based approaches, integrated care models, and health education initiatives to work towards a future where everyone can achieve optimal health and well-being.
