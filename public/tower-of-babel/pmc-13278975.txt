A damage accumulation model identifies distinct aging regimes across species
Authors: Naveh Raz, Yifan Yang, Glen Pridham, Ben Shenhar, Shachaf Frenkel, Tomer Levy, Tsvi Tlusty, Avi Mayo, Uri Alon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDifferent species age in similar ways but their lifespans differ by orders of magnitude. It is not clear how these similarities and differences arise from the accumulation of damage that underlies aging. Does long lifespan arise from reduced damage production, increased removal or enhanced robustness to damage? Here we apply the saturating removal model—a stochastic model of damage accumulation and removal—and fit it to survival data from well-studied species. Several parameters have near-universal values including ratios of removal rate, noise amplitude and death threshold. The model parameter that best predicts lifespan is the damage production rate, which spans seven orders of magnitude. We identify two distinct aging regimes: ballistic aging where damage production outpaces removal, characterizing yeast, nematodes, flies and mice, and quasi-steady-state aging, where damage tracks a moving set point of balanced production and removal, characterizing humans, dogs, guinea pigs and cats. These results provide a mechanistic model-based basis of comparative aging that awaits experimental validation.Subject terms: Population dynamics, Bayesian inference, Stochastic modelling, Numerical simulations, Ageing

Main

Different animals age in a roughly similar way, with loss of function and a sharp increase in death probability with age. However, their median lifespans vary by many orders of magnitude. Explaining the similarities and differences in aging dynamics across the tree of life is one of the main challenges in research into longevity1–6.

Lifespan—or mechanisms that delay death—seem to have evolved to allow organisms to reproduce and benefit their kin postreproductively before they die from extrinsic causes such as predation, infection, accident or starvation. Molecular explanations for extended lifespan stem from comparing short- and long-lived animals7,8. Long-lived animals share enhanced DNA repair3,5,9–11, reduced molecular error rates and other processes6,12–14.

Aging and mortality dynamics are often studied by examining the survival curve—the fraction of organisms alive at each age. Most previous approaches to compare survival curves of different species have fit functional forms such as Gompertz or Weibull curves15–18, but these functional forms have no mechanistic basis. It would be of interest to connect molecular mechanisms of damage accumulation to the shapes of survival curves, to identify principles of aging processes in different organisms.

Recently, a connection between damage and demographic patterns such as survival curves was formed using a mathematical model of damage production and removal called the saturating removal (SR) model. This model was defined and calibrated based on longitudinal damage measurements in mice (senescent cells) and Escherichia coli (membrane damage)19,20.

The SR model is based on the simplifying hypothesis that the damage that causes aging can be summarized by a scalar x and that life cannot persist above a certain level of x. The biochemical nature of x can be different in each species. The possibility of such a one-dimensional representation of the complex biochemistry and phenotypes of aging21–25 is supported by analyses of aging data26–30.

The SR model describes the dynamics of damage by a stochastic differential equation that includes production, removal and noise. Production rises linearly with age, whereas removal saturates at high damage. Death occurs when damage exceeds a threshold. The SR model explains many quantitative patterns of aging including Gompertz and Weibull hazard curves19, distributions of human frailty index2, disease incidence curves31 and heritability of lifespan32. It was applied to invertebrates to explain rapid shifts in hazard curves in Drosophila and scaling of survival curves in Caenorhabditis elegans2,19. In mice it was applied to parabiosis33, compression of morbidity and combinations of longevity interventions34. However, the SR model has not been used to compare the aging of different species systematically.

Here we assembled a set of high-quality survival curves for model organisms and humans and fit the damage production, removal, threshold and noise parameters for each species using a Bayesian approach. We find that several parameter combinations are nearly universal between species, and that the strongest single parameter predictor of lifespan differences between species is the damage production rate, which varies by seven orders of magnitude between yeast and humans. We find evidence for two regimes of aging: ballistic aging in which production dominates removal over most of the lifespan (yeast, C. elegans, flies and mice) and quasi-steady-state aging in which damage tracks a moving set point defined by a balance between production and SR (humans, dogs, guinea pigs and cats). The former have Weibull-like survival curves, and the latter have Gompertz-like curves. This analysis provides insight into the ways that evolution has sculpted strategies to resist aging-related decline across the tree of life, and provides a framework to compare damage production, removal and noise in different organisms.

Results

Bayesian approach to estimate SR model parameters from mortality data

The SR model posits a damage x that drives aging. The rate of change of x is production minus removal plus noise. The SR equation, developed by model selection from longitudinal damage data19, is dxdt=ηt−βxκ+x+2ϵξ. The production of damage ηt rises linearly with age. Damage removal βxκ+x saturates at high damage, with a halfway saturation point of κ. Noise is modeled as Gaussian white noise with amplitude ϵ. Death in the model is a first passage process when x crosses a death threshold Xc.

We also allowed for extrinsic mortality mex, a parameter (also called a Makeham term) that describes death probability per unit time from extrinsic causes15,18. The model schematic is in Fig. 1 left panel, and the parameters are summarized in Table 1.

SR model parameters and their meaning

The linear rise of damage with age is required in the model to produce the Gompertz law, in which mortality hazard grows exponentially with time exp(αt). If one changes the linear rise ηt to, say, a quadratic rise ηt2, one would get h ~ exp(αt2) instead of the Gompertz law. Biologically, the rise of production rate with age may represent the accumulation of damage producing units (DPUs) that are not removed. The DPUs are produced at a constant rate and therefore their number grows linearly with age. Each DPU produces damage, and thus the total damage production rate rises linearly with time.

The saturation of removal at high damage in the SR model is a common feature of biological processes. Mathematically, if damage did not saturate, that is, if removal was −βx, the steady-state damage would rise linearly with age, whereas experiments indicate that damage accelerates with age2,19,20.

We distinguish between removal and repair; for the purpose of this model, we define removal as processes that specifically remove the damage x (immune system removal of damaged and/or senescent cells in mammals), whereas repair refers to intracellular processes that affect the damage production per DPU and thus affect the production parameter η (DNA repair that prevents a cell from becoming damaged and/or senescent).

The noise term in the model ϵ describes biological noise. The choice of additive Gaussian noise is for convenience, because other types of noise such as Poisson noise or multiplicative noise terms give similar quality of fits to aging patterns (Supplementary Note 1). In practice, this term can also absorb some of the technical measurement noise in the data19.

In this study we estimated the SR model parameters from survival curves of different organisms. To do so we used a Bayesian approach using simulations of the SR model. Each simulation tracks damage over time (Fig. 1), according to its random noise realization, and death occurs when damage first crosses the death threshold Xc.

We repeated this for 20,000 simulated individual organisms for each parameter set and compared the simulated death time distribution to the data. We then use a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approach to adjust the parameters and repeat the simulations until good fits are obtained (Methods). We thus obtain posterior distributions for each parameter and parameter combination (Supplementary Note 2)35,36.

We fit the model to survival data from well-studied organisms

To compare organisms, we assembled high-quality mortality datasets for well-studied species (data and fit to the model described below is shown; Fig. 2). For E. coli (n = 4,744) we use starvation microfluidic experiments from ref. 37. For yeast replicative aging (Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4742) we used all controls from a genomic deletion screen studied by manual separation of daughter and mother cells (n = 24,512)38. For C. elegans (QZ0) we used controls from a large-scale aging-machine experiment (n = 2,908)30. For Drosophila melanogaster we obtained data for four fly strains with different lifespans (n ~ 1000 for all strains)39.

These organisms span four orders of magnitude of median lifespan, from 100 h for starving E. coli to 80 years for humans. Their survival and hazard curves have different shapes (Fig. 2).

The model also captures the hazard curves, which are the logarithmic derivative of the survival curves. These show a diversity of intercepts and shapes ranging from power law Weibull-like curves (Fig. 2j) to exponential Gompertz-like curves with appreciable extrinsic mortality (Fig. 2k).

As can be seen in Fig. 2, the model generally describes the data well. One exception is the decrease in mortality at old ages in C. elegans, which is not captured (Fig. 2j). This may be due to phenotypic heterogeneity between plates and/or individuals we do not model.

The damage production parameter is the best single predictor of lifespan differences across species

The production parameter η varied by seven orders of magnitude between species. The longer the lifespan, the lower the η (Fig. 3a). The other model parameters show smaller trends and generally decrease more mildly with lifespan (Extended Data Fig. 1 and Extended Data Tables 2 and 3).

To quantify the contribution of each parameter to lifespan across species, we used Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis. SHAP values quantifies predictive importance within the fitted model and lets us decompose lifespan into the additive contribution of each parameter to the lifespan prediction, accounting for all possible feature interactions and multicollinearity45,46.

The production parameter η had highest mean SHAP importance at 2.85, effectively matching the summed importance of the next two most important parameters, threshold Xc (1.53) and removal β (1.38), with noise ϵ (0.33) showing the lowest SHAP importance (Fig. 3b). These findings are robust across analytical frameworks including single parameter substitution and analysis of variance (ANOVA), which identify η as the strongest predictor of lifespan (Supplementary Note 3).

We conclude that the of the model parameters, the production rate η, is the best single predictor of lifespan differences between species.

Species show different aging regimes—ballistic or quasi-steady-state

Because the production parameter η varies widely, whereas removal and noise vary less, the production rate ηt is much larger than removal in some organisms, and much smaller in others. Therefore, the ratio of production to removal creates two regimes of model behavior.

In the first regime, found in yeast, C. elegans and most fly strains, damage production rate ηt is much larger than removal throughout most of the lifespan. As a result, to a good approximation, mean damage rises ballistically (quadratically with time like a free-falling object) x∼12ηt2. Damage crosses the death threshold Xc at time L (the characteristic lifespan), when 12ηL2=Xc. This provides an inverse quadratic relationship between production and mean lifespan η∼2Xc/L2. As Xc is on the same order of magnitude in most species (Xc∼101), we expect that η∼1/L2. We call this ‘ballistic aging.’

In contrast, in longer-lived mammals and one Drosophila strain, we find that removal balances production and tracks it over most of the organism’s lifespan—they are at quasi-steady-state. Damage production rate rises so slowly that removal can adapt to it, become more saturated and damage rises slowly accordingly. Only at a very old age when production is large compared to removal does the dynamics become more similar to ballistic aging.

Roughly speaking, at quasi-steady-state there is no change dxdt≈0, and hence production approximately balances removal ηt−βxκ+x≈0, and therefore mean damage rises with age 〈x〉∼κηt+ϵβ−ηt diverging at tc=βη(ref. 19). As a result, lifespan is roughly determined by the time that production reaches maximal removal ηt = β, so that lifespan L ∼ β/η. We therefore expect an inverse-linear relationship η ∼ 1/L. We call this ‘quasi-steady-state aging.’

Plotting the production parameter η versus lifespan is consistent with these predictions (Fig. 3a). There is a roughly inverse quadratic relation of η with lifespan L, η ∼ 1/L2, for yeast, C. elegans, flies and mice. This relationship switches (around L of a few years) to η ∼ 1/L for guinea pigs, cats, dogs and humans (Fig. 3).

Thus, we define two broad aging regimes. In the first—ballistic aging—damage production dominates. Hazard curves are more Weibullian than Gomperzian in shape—hazard first rises sharply and then transitions to a power law before the median lifespan (Fig. 2j).

In the second regime—quasi-steady-state aging—production and removal are balanced throughout most of the lifespan and damage tracks the rising production rate (Extended Data Fig. 2). The hazard curve resembles Gompertz exponential rise over several decades which slows down at very old ages beyond the median lifespan (Fig. 2k)2,19,29.

Each organism can lie on a continuum between these regimes, defined by the fraction of median lifespan in which production exceeds removal. Most organisms studied here lie squarely in one regime or the other; one exception is female mice, which spend the first ~2 years of their life in quasi-steady-state aging and the remainder in ballistic aging (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Tables 2 and 3) resulting in about half the population dying in a quasi-steady-state regime and half in the ballistic regime.

A tightly conserved timescale is the mean time it would take noise to reach the death threshold Tϵ=Xc2/ϵ. This timescale is on the order of 2 years in mice, dogs, guinea pigs, cats and humans (Fig. 4a). If we had an organism with only noise driving damage, and no production or removal, Tϵ would be its approximate mean lifespan. This corresponds with the notion that animals with a lifespan close to 2 years, such as male mice, have a different aging regime than longer-lived animals in which this noise timescale is shorter than the lifespan.

A second conserved feature is the ratio of removal and noise timescales,βXcϵ~10 (Fig. 4a). The time for removal processes working at full speed to remove damage equal to the threshold (on the order of months) is ten times faster than the time for noise to reach the threshold.

The approximate constancy of this ratio between removal and noise parameters across species raises the possibility that noise in mammals arises from systemic fluctuations in the removal process. One hypothesis is that noise is due to circadian fluctuations in immune functions that remove damaged and/or senescent cells47–51 (Supplementary Note 4).

Yeast replicative aging has negligible removal

Yeast replicative aging shows a hazard curve that rises quickly for about ten generations (about half the median lifespan) and then slows to a polynomial rise for at least 50 more generations (Fig. 4b).

We find that in yeast the best-fit removal parameter β is very small and can be neglected nearly without compromising the fit quality. Yeast aging is therefore an extreme case of ballistic aging—production drives damage with negligible removal. Thus, although yeast has many repair systems, the specific damage x that drives aging seems not to be countered by a strong removal process in the mother cell.

An analytical approximation of the SR model with β = 0 shows an asymptotic hazard at late times of ht=316(ηϵ)2t2+O1 (Supplementary Note 5) in reasonable agreement with the observed hazard curve (Fig. 4b).

The lack of removal in yeast suggests a mechanism where damage accumulates in the mother cell without a sizable opposing process that clears it. Candidates for such damage include elements that tend to remain in the mother cell including disrupted mitochondria, DNA epigenetic (histone modification) states and alkaline vacuoles52,53.

Several strains and species show aging patterns similar to humans whereas others do not

The SR model has four parameters; if we use Xc as a unit of damage, and median lifespan L as a unit of time, we remain with three parameters in dimensionless units: removal βLXc, noise ϵLXC2 and production ηL2Xc (the fourth parameter XC is now effectively equal to 1). Plotting all species in the space of these three parameters uncovers a loglinear relationship, roughly L∼cϵ2Xc2ηβ(days) with c ≈ 30 (Fig. 5a). Outliers are species with very low removal. Organisms thus seem to lie on a continuum between two extremes; at one extreme all parameters are small in units of threshold and lifespan (C. elegans) and at the other extreme all parameters are large (humans).

To obtain further insight, we plot species according to two criteria—how deterministic their aging process is as quantified by the relative magnitude of noise to production, and where the species lie on the range between ballistic and quasi-steady-state aging.

The ballistic-to-quasi-steady-state aging parameter is the fraction of life spent with removal comparable to production P1=βη/L. It is high for organisms with quasi-steady-state aging, and low for organisms with ballistic aging. The second criterion is the ratio of noise and production timescales s=TϵTη=η0.5Xc1.5ϵ. The larger s, the more deterministic the damage accumulation process.

One may hypothesize that animals with similar dimensionless parameters to humans might serve as better model organisms for dynamical aspects of human aging. In engineering this principle is called similitude analysis54,55, where systems with the same dimensionless parameter groups tend to behave in a similar manner and respond similarly to perturbations.

Plotting species in the space of these two criteria, we find that the closest species to humans are dogs (except the largest breed tested German Shepard), cats, Guinea pigs, starving E. coli and Drosophila strain 853 (Fig. 5b). These species have Gompertz-like hazard curves that slow down only at very old ages, long after the median lifespan. Mice; C. elegans; Drosophila strains 707, 441 and 217 and yeast are closer to the ballistic regime and have different mortality patterns than humans. Female mice are closer to human-like aging than male mice.

Discussion

We compare aging of different organisms using a damage accumulation model and determine its parameters in a set of high-quality survival curves for different species. We find that the parameter that changes the most between species is the damage production parameter η, which is inverse to lifespan. Other parameters are more similar between species. Ratios between noise and removal timescales are conserved across mammals, suggesting that noise may represent fluctuations in the removal process. We identify two aging regimes: a ballistic regime found in short-lived species where damage production dominates removal, and a quasi-steady-state aging regime found in mammals where damage tracks a moving set point determined by the balance of rising production and saturating removal.

Aging is thought to result from the accumulation of damage over time. Within this framework, it has been unclear whether long lifespan is due to reduced damage production, increased damage removal, low noise or high death threshold (damage tolerance). Our findings suggest that the single parameter that best explains lifespan differences between species is the damage production rate, which is seven orders of magnitude higher in yeast than in humans.

The damage accumulation model we use can apply to different forms of damage x in different organisms. In mammals, one candidate for x is senescent cells—a broad category of damaged cells that enter growth arrest and cause inflammation and stem cell exhaustion56,57. To avoid confusion, we suggest the terminology of repair (intracellular) versus removal (immune elimination of damaged cells). Senescent cells are removed by the immune system, so that a damage half-life of days is plausible and supported experimentally in mice19. Reduced production of senescent cells in long-lived mammals can be due to enhanced repair of cellular damage, such as double-stranded breaks3,9–11,58. Removal rates seem roughly constant across mammals, whereas repair affects production and is improved in long-lived organisms.

Long-living mammals have indeed been found to have enhanced repair and reduced error rates in several processes (some of which may not drive aging). Cellular error rates that have been found to be inverse to mammalian species lifespan include somatic mutations12, epigenetic dysregulation14, transcription and translation errors13.

The finding of two aging regimes—ballistic aging and quasi-steady-state aging—can help resolve a debate around the shape of hazard curves. Hazard curves are Weibull-like (power law) in some organisms and Gompertz-like (exponential) in others15,59. We find that organisms with ballistic aging have Weibull-like hazard curves. In contrast, quasi-steady-state agers (dogs, cats, guinea pigs, humans) have a Gompertz-like exponential rise in hazard that slows at very old ages.

Not all short-lived organisms are ballistic; starving E. coli and some Drosophila strains have quasi-steady-state aging parameters and a Gompertz-like hazard curve quite similar to humans. Female mice are on the transition between the regimes, with quasi-steady-state aging up to the median lifespan and ballistic aging thereafter.

Death threshold Xc in all organisms is at least ten times bigger than the damage removal saturation point κ (Extended Data Fig. 1 and Extended Data Tables 2 and 3). The saturation of removal occurs in young organisms, and κ is the damage scale in youth and reproductive age. One reason may be that selection to delay aging pushes death to occur far beyond the saturation of removal (Xc/κ ≫ 1). Biologically, Xc describes robustness mechanisms that allow an organism to survive at a given level of damage x.

This study suggests a hypothesis about the origin of biological noise in the mammalian aging process. The conserved relationship of noise and removal timescales, Tϵ∼10Tβ suggest a link between noise and removal. Removal in mammals is thought to be due to the immune system, which has a circadian rhythm. Stochastic variations in circadian peaks on consecutive days may drive the noise. This connects to a large field of study on the role of circadian rhythm in aging60–62.

One conclusion based on this comparative analysis is that the production parameter may be the main knob that evolution uses to vary lifespan by orders of magnitude between organisms. The other estimated parameters also vary between organisms in a correlated way, suggesting that longevity is achieved by a coordinated change in production, removal, threshold and noise; production is the single parameter that best explains lifespan differences between species. Therefore, future putative interventions aimed at extending lifespan by a large amount need to focus on the production parameter rather than exclusively on removal or threshold. Recent analysis indicates that reducing production alone would stretch the lifespan but also the sickspan (time of poor health at end of life) by the same factor34. Thus, to extend life and compress sickspan requires not only slowing production but also adding interventions that raise threshold, reduce noise and/or enhance removal.

Despite the differences between organisms, when plotted in the space of three dimensionless parameters, organisms seem to fall on a loglinear relationship. This may hint at a continuum of strategies between two archetypes63: humans at the extreme of large parameters (in units of threshold Xc and lifespan L), and yeast at the extreme of small parameters. One may hypothesize that organisms close to the human extreme might age most similarly to humans, including certain strains of Drosophila, cats, most dog breeds and Guinea pigs.

It is interesting to compare this study to other stochastic models of aging that reproduce the Gompertz law. Classical Strelher–Mildvan models posit linearly reducing vitality and death caused by exponentially distributed events23,64. Recently Fedichev et al. proposed a stochastic ODE model derived from expansion of modes in an interacting system, with similarities to the SR model, and noted that mice and humans have quasi-stable versus stable dynamics21,22. Another recent stochastic damage accumulation model based on queue theory provides a similar framework for the Gompertz law24. This model posits constant production and removal that drops with age, a mechanism that Karin et al. found to be at odds with damage trajectories in mice19. Other frameworks include failure models in networks of components65–67. In principle one can apply the present approach to find how the parameters of these models vary across species.

A limitation of our approach is that both species’ lifespans and the SR parameters are estimated from the same survival curves. Consequently, our inferred parameters—such as damage production and removal—are mathematical constructs rather than directly measured biological quantities. Although our model describes how these theoretical variables track lifespan variation, it does not constitute independent validation of unique biological causation. Instead, these mathematically grounded hypotheses await future experimental verification.

In summary, we compared species survival curves to determine parameters in the SR model of damage accumulation. We find two regimes of aging: ballistic aging, in which production dominates, and quasi-steady-state aging, in which production is balanced by removal until very old ages. The best single predictor of lifespan is the production rate, which spans seven orders of magnitude; the other parameters also vary in a coordinated way. This sheds light on the origins of stochastic noise in aging and indicates near-universality of certain aging parameters between clades. Evolution may be tuning lifespan primarily by changing damage production rates (by enhanced molecular repair) rather than removal rates or death thresholds, hinting at the putative target for interventions aimed at extending maximal lifespan.

Methods

Full details in of all methods can be found in the Supplementary Information.

Stochastic model simulation

Simulation of mortality data was done using Python. We simulated the SR model stochastic differential equation as an Itô process dx=(ηt−βxκ+x)dt+2ϵdW and calculated the death time of each individual as a first passage time of Xc. When simulating we constrained the value of x to be non-negative. We used a Brownian bridge correction to reduce sensitivity to simulation step size68. We used normal Gaussian noise, although different noise types produced similar results (Supplementary Note 1).

As we can choose κ or Xc as a scaling parameter, we used κ = 0.5 in all simulations to be consistent with ref. 19. Where relevant, survival and hazard functions were calculated using the Python Lifelines library69.

In datasets acquired in laboratory conditions we assumed no external hazard. For dogs, cats and humans we also simulated a constant external hazard. Simulation of external hazard was done by assigning a rate mex to die. For human data we performed the simulations with heterogeneity in the parameters to represent genetic and environmental variations—each individual’s parameters are drawn from a normal distribution as in ref. 32. We repeated the simulation without heterogeneity and obtained qualitatively similar results (see full results in git in Supplementary Information). For full simulation details see code in Supplementary Information.

Bayesian estimation of parameters

To calculate the likelihood of the data given a set of model params θi⃗, we simulated the death times, then used a Gaussian kernel density estimator to generate a smoothed death times distribution Pθi and a survival function Sθi. When the data are given as a list of death times and censoring times D=d0,d1…dn,c0,c1…cm, the log likelihood is given by: LL=∑j=0nlogPθi(dj)Δt+∑k=0mlogSθick where Δt is the time resolution of the data70.

The SR model parameter space was scanned with MCMC using python Emcee library with stretch move35,36. Walker initial position was sampled from a log-uniform prior (noninformative prior) centered around an initial guess (parameter guess values found in Supplementary Information). The allowed range of the scan constituted four orders of magnitude.

Samples probabilities were averaged to a grid and posterior probabilities were calculated using Bayes’s law Pθi|D=P(D|θi)P(θi)Δθ∑iP(D|θi)P(θi)Δθ where P(D|θi) is the likelihood (grid size consisted of 100 bins in log space, full details per each simulation in Supplementary Information). The posterior was then marginalized to calculate the probabilities and confidence intervals (CIs) for each parameter.

To address infant mortality and external hazard, for species with substantial early mortality we only used the death times above a cutoff age. For these cases in Fig. 2, survival curves were renormalized to the relevant interval. Specific ranges used per each organism are detailed in Extended Data Tables 4.

Yeast data were measured in generations, conversion to days for Fig. 3 and Extended Data Table 3 used an average generation time of ~3 h calculated based on data from Li et al52.

To calculate human parameters, we used the joint distribution of parameters for several cohorts: Denmark 1890, 1900 birth cohorts and Sweden 1910 birth cohort (Extended Data Table 1). An identifiability challenge we encountered is the existence of several sets of parameters that provide good fits. The parameter sets with the best-fit scores formed elongated valleys in parameter space, denoting a continuum of parameters with a good fit (Supplementary Note 2). This resulted in certain parameter combinations having well-defined best fit values, whereas the uncertainty in certain parameters led to large CIs.

We assessed that most of the identifiability issue was due to uncertainty in estimating the death threshold XC, which provides a damage scale. To resolve the identifiability issues, we can normalize by Xc, resulting in a damage range from 0 (no damage) to 1 (death); we therefore show in Fig. 3a parameters normalized by Xc. Using the unnormalized values did not change the main result presented in Fig. 3a, (Extended Data Fig. 1, black markers).

Using simulated data with known parameters we find that one needs at least n = 500 individuals to obtain errors smaller than twofold for the well-defined parameter combinations and less than fivefold for most other parameters.

To calculate CIs for each parameter, we started with the mode (highest probability) of the marginalized posterior distribution and then opened the interval by taking steps on the grid; at each iteration the interval opened one grid point to the side that had higher probability. This was repeated until the interval consisted of 95% of the estimated probability. In this manner we ensured the CI was capturing the region of highest probability surrounding the mode.

Full details of estimation method, simulation parameters and validation process (including code) is found in the Supplementary Information.

Datasets and preparation

For data on species that included several cohorts we tested for outlier cohorts and removed them from the data. To find outlier cohorts we first aggregated the data and generated thousands of sampled cohorts randomly from the aggregate. We then calculated the median lifespan and steepness defined as the median divided by the interquartile range for the real and sampled cohorts. Cohorts with median lifespan or steepness that were not covered by the 99.9% interval of the sampled data were removed. We then used the aggregated death times of all remaining cohorts. Supplementary Note 6 provides more details on per dataset cohort definition and outlier detection.

For male mice we used ITP data from the Jackson Laboratory and the University of Texas and excluded data from University of Michigan as this site was found to have longer lifespan for male mice then the other sites71.

For humans we obtained data in the form of life tables. We used the life tables to generate synthetic death times and applied the same analysis as for the other datasets. Full details regarding the data and cohorts used can be found in Extended Data Table 1.

To address infant mortality and external hazard, for species with substantial early mortality we only used the death times above a cutoff age. Full details of number of individuals excluded at each stage and/or outlier exclusion and cutoff age can be found in Extended Data Table 4.

We used replicative aging data for yeast38,52 rather than chronological aging. For E. coli the dataset uses survival time under starvation conditions37.

In Fig. 2j,k we show the hazard from the time ten deaths were observed and until the time ten individuals remain alive. We also trim the hazard to the fitted range, so regions that were not fitted such as those exhibiting high infant mortality are not shown. The full fits for the hazards with the noisy sections is found in Supplementary Note 7.

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Instead, we maximized the available power by including all eligible cohorts that met our pre-established quality and homogeneity criteria.

These sample sizes are sufficient to provide statistical power for the results presented in this paper. To ensure the robustness of our findings, we provide comprehensive error analysis for our methodology in Supplementary Note 2, including dependence of error on sample size, and report 95% CIs for all primary results.

Detailed descriptions of the specific datasets used, including the final number of individuals and/or records per cohort, are provided in Extended Data Tables 1 and 4.

Classification of the hazard functions to Gompertz and Weibull

Models were compared using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and classified according to the lower value. Some species, such as humans, mice and yeast had a large gap between the models (ΔBIC > 1,000); the gap was smaller for Guinea pigs, German Shepherd dogs and Drosophila strains 853 and 441 (50 < ΔBIC < 100) and they have the closest margins in our dataset. See Supplementary Note 8 for details. Fits and BIC calculations were performed using the Python lifelines library69.

SHAP analysis

To quantify the relative importance of the calibrated parameters on lifespan, we performed a global sensitivity analysis using SHAP using the KernelSHAP algorithm from the Python SHAP library46.

Predictive model and response variable

In this framework, the ‘predictive model’ evaluated by the SHAP explainer was a wrapper function around our stochastic differential equation simulator. For a given set of input parameters, the function simulated a population to generate a survival distribution. The target response variable returned to the SHAP explainer was the natural logarithm of the simulated median lifespan.

Data transformations

The input features to the SHAP computation were the base model parameters (η, β, ϵ and Xc). These input features and the resulting response variable (median lifespan) were log-transformed. This transformation ensures that the SHAP values reflect sensitivity to proportional (fold) changes in the parameters rather than absolute scalar differences.

Summarization across datasets

The SHAP analysis was conducted globally across the 19 independently calibrated parameter sets, which represent the diverse biological conditions analyzed in this study (varying species, sexes and genotypes). KernelSHAP computed the local additive contribution of each parameter for all 19 configurations. To summarize global parameter importance across the entire dataset, we calculated the mean absolute SHAP value for each parameter, representing its average impact on the model’s predicted change in log median lifespan.

Code is available at the accompanying repository. See Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. 3 for detailed results.

Calculation of lifespan for figures

To put all species on equal footing we used the median lifespan from simulations after excluding external mortality (L). All results are nearly indistinguishable from those using median lifespan calculated from data (Extended Data Tables 2 and 3).
