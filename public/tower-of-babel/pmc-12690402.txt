The Use of Parabulbar and Sub‐Tenon Injections to Correct Isolated Resistant Diplopia in Patients With Generalized Myasthenia Gravis: A Long Road to Clinically Meaningful Success
Authors: M. Marchuk, V. Sanin, V. Mamilov, M. Zinchenko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTParabulbar betamethasone provided rapid and durable relief of isolated, treatment‐resistant diplopia in seronegative myasthenia gravis after multiple systemic therapy failures, suggesting that targeted periocular corticosteroid delivery may be a steroid‐sparing option for carefully selected patients when standard treatments are ineffective and close ophthalmic monitoring is available.

Introduction

Diplopia in ocular or generalized myasthenia gravis (MG) is a frequent clinical challenge that significantly reduces quality of life. Standard MG scales such as the Myasthenia Gravis‐Activities of Daily Living (MG‐ADL) and the Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis score (QMG) often fail to capture short‐term ocular fluctuations [1, 2].

Systemic treatments (glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants) may help but often cause side effects, act slowly, and show inconsistent benefit when diplopia dominates [3, 4, 5, 6]. Non‐pharmacologic measures (prisms, occlusion, surgery) also frequently provide only partial or unstable relief due to fluctuating ocular alignment [6, 7, 8].

Periocular corticosteroid injections offer a steroid‐sparing option with reduced systemic exposure. Small studies suggest benefit, though techniques and outcomes vary [9]. We present a case‐based comparison of periocular injection methods, evaluating efficacy, duration, and safety.

Case Presentations

Patient 1

A 34‐year‐old man (65 kg, 175 cm) presented in 2021 with disabling diplopia worsened in lateral gaze, affecting his work as a programmer, along with limb pain and mild weakness.

On examination: cushingoid features from steroids, no ptosis or bulbar symptoms, mild ophthalmoparesis in horizontal gaze, normal swallowing, speech and limb strength.

Baseline function was near normal (dynamometry: 33.2/37.4 kg; VC: 109%–128% predicted). MG‐ADL [1] = 3 (constant diplopia), QMG [2] = 3 (Dynamometry, right arm in a man 15–44 kgB, diplopia with lateral gaze to the left or right occurs in 1–10 s).

We acknowledge that SFEMG and low‐frequency RNS are sensitive but not disease‐specific; accordingly, we qualify our conclusion as “findings consistent with MG,” consistent with ocular‐MG literature and treatment reviews [3, 4, 5]. Prolonged glucocorticoid exposure contributed to steroid‐induced myopathy and secondary systemic osteoporosis.

The working diagnosis was generalized seronegative MG with predominant ocular involvement; disease activity was incompletely controlled at presentation (Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America [MGFA] class IIIA; Osserman class II). Complications included cataracts, steroid‐induced myopathy, and osteoporosis; comorbidities included multinodular goiter.

Conclusion and Results

Serial photographs after five parabulbar dexamethasone injections showed improved left‐eye abduction by weeks 3–4 (Figure 1). Further injections consolidate the positive therapeutic effect (Figure 2).

Post: Progressive improvement to near‐full abduction with reduced diplopia. Timing: Improvement most evident during Weeks 2–4 of the once‐weekly regimen.

Dexamethasone provided about 30 days of benefit but required weekly injections, prompting a switch to sub‐Tenon triamcinolone (0.5 mL/eye) for longer durability (Table 1, Figure 3).

Time course of left‐eye adduction after sub‐Tenon triamcinolone acetonide (0.5 mL per eye). Baseline (injection day): Limited left‐eye adduction in right gaze with diplopia. Post: Full adduction at Day 30; mild decline at Day 48 with slight nasal (medial) scleral show and mild diplopia. Timing: Day 0, Day 30, and Day 48 after sub‐Tenon triamcinolone acetonide 0.5 mL per eye.

Sub‐Tenon triamcinolone caused small, self‐limited hemorrhages and visible drug retention, a benign effect that requires patient counseling (Figure 4).

Visual consequences of sub‐Tenon's injection of triamcinolone acetonide at a dose of 0.5 mL.

When diplopia recurred at 76 days, sub‐Tenon betamethasone (1.0 mL/eye) was administered, providing a 67‐day improvement (Figure 5).

Post: Improved adduction at Day 30 with ~1 mm nasal (medial) scleral show and reduced diplopia. Timing: Day 0 and Day 30 after sub‐Tenon betamethasone 1.0 mL per eye.

A subsequent triamcinolone injection (0.5 mL/eye) produced the longest effect—110 days (Figure 6).

Patient Perspective

The patient responded to this method as follows: “By the third day after the parabulbar betamethasone injection I felt a marked improvement, and within a week my diplopia had almost disappeared – something I had never achieved with other treatments. The benefit has persisted for four months: I can run and work out on the horizontal bar, and double vision now occurs only at extreme gaze positions. Although I sense a gradual return of symptoms, this sustained four‐month effect remains the best result I have experienced.”

Patient 2

A female patient, 168 cm tall, weighing 59 kg, first noted diplopia at the age of 30 (born in 1989) during the second trimester of pregnancy. On 22.05.2023, she complained of constant diplopia, intermittent right‐sided ptosis, fatigable weakness of the arms after repetitive tasks (e.g., combing her child's hair), and pain in the lumbar muscles after physical exertion.

Clinical data: No ptosis was observed at examination. Ophthalmoparesis was present, with incomplete ocular movements to the right and left in extreme abduction. Diplopia was constant. No bulbar symptoms were detected; the voice timbre was normal. Strength of the facial muscles (eyebrow elevation, eye closure, teeth baring, and cheek puffing) was preserved at 5/5 (Medical Research Council scale).

Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG‐ADL) – 4 points (constant diplopia, intermittent ptosis).

Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis Score (QMGS) – 5 points (immediate diplopia on sustained gaze; right and left arm outstretched time 90–239 s).

Although the overall diagnostic picture in Patient 2 remained uncertain, the combination of clinical fatigability, constant diplopia, and SFEMG abnormalities was consistent with impaired neuromuscular transmission and therefore compatible with myasthenia gravis (Table 5). The antibody panel was negative (AChR, MuSK, LRP4), and decrement was observed only at high‐frequency stimulation, while jitter—although a non‐specific marker—was clearly abnormal. MRI of the orbits excluded structural lesions but demonstrated retrobulbar edema of the left optic nerve without contrast enhancement, which could not fully explain the persistent diplopia. Immunological studies further revealed lymphopenia, CD8+ predominance over CD4+ T‐cells, reduced neutrophil myeloperoxidase activity, elevated circulating immune complexes, and increased neuroautoimmune reactivity, supporting an autoimmune mechanism.

The working diagnosis was: “Myasthenia gravis, seronegative for antibodies to the AChR, MuSK, LRP4, with predominant involvement in the pathological process of the extraocular muscles, to a lesser extent—the muscles of the extremities, incomplete control of disease activity (MGFA IIA and II Class according to Osserman). Thus, while a working diagnosis of seronegative myasthenia gravis was made, alternative or coexisting ocular pathology could not be fully excluded.

As summarized in Table 6, systemic glucocorticoid therapy (0.41 mg/kg) and multiple trials of anticholinesterase agents did not result in clinically meaningful improvement. Thymectomy revealed thymolipoma but had no effect on ocular symptoms, and subsequent plasmapheresis likewise failed to provide benefit. Overall, diplopia remained persistent and treatment‐refractory despite conventional approaches.

Patient 3

A female patient, 178 cm tall and weighing 70 kg, first noted diplopia and generalized muscle weakness at the age of 14 (born in 1990). On April 21, 2025, she presented with complaints of moderate limb weakness, bilateral ptosis, and persistent double vision.

Clinical data: Examination revealed ptosis involving the right upper eyelid (one‐third) and the left upper eyelid (two‐thirds). Ophthalmoparesis was present, with limited ocular motility to the right and left. Diplopia was constant. No bulbar symptoms were detected; the voice timbre remained normal. Strength of the facial muscles (eyebrow elevation, eye closure, teeth baring, cheek puffing) was reduced to 4/5 on the Medical Research Council scale.

Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG‐ADL) – 6 points (constant diplopia and ptosis).

Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis Score (QMGS) – 9 points (instantaneous ptosis and diplopia; weak but complete eye closure with slight resistance to passive opening; right and left arm outstretched time 90–239 s).

The diagnosis of myasthenia gravis in Patient 2 (Table 8) was supported by ENMG findings consistent with a myasthenic‐type transmission disorder, demonstrating a marked decrement and post‐tetanic facilitation/exhaustion, as well as by the presence of high‐titer AChR antibodies (56.0 and 69.5 nmol/L; Ref ≤ 0.5), confirming an autoimmune mechanism. Thymectomy was undertaken despite normal MRI of the thymus, in accordance with international recommendations (MGFA and EFNS guidelines), which support thymectomy in patients under 65 years of age with generalized disease and seropositivity for AChR antibodies, particularly when disease activity is not adequately controlled with standard therapy [1, 10].

An acute deterioration occurred, with severe ophthalmoparesis and disabling diplopia that prevented the patient (a programmer) from performing computer work. In view of the functional impact, we implemented a multimodal strategy after shared decision‐making: parabulbar betamethasone 0.5 mL per eye; discontinuation of azathioprine due to moderate hepatotoxicity that precluded dose escalation; initiation of tacrolimus 3 mg/day; and methylprednisolone 24 mg/day, with the upper limit constrained by pre‐existing steroid‐related cataracts and osteoporosis.

Parabulbar betamethasone 0.5 mL per eye was administered on April 22 and May 6, 2025 (Table 9). Photographic documentation demonstrated measurable improvement already on the following day, with restoration of horizontal alignment and reduction of vertical deviations (Figure 9). By May 13, the patient reported that diplopia had decreased by approximately 70%, allowing her to return to full‐time computer work. This outcome confirms the clinical effectiveness of parabulbar betamethasone in a seropositive patient with disabling ocular manifestations of myasthenia gravis.

Discussion

In Patient 1, repeated periocular corticosteroid injections provided a therapeutic benefit, with objective improvement of ocular motility and reduction of diplopia documented on serial photographs. Among the tested regimens, parabulbar betamethasone (0.5 mL per eye) produced the most rapid, sustained, and pronounced effect, whereas parabulbar dexamethasone was associated with the shortest duration of benefit. These observations confirm that local corticosteroid therapy can serve as an effective adjunct in seronegative MG with disabling ocular symptoms, although efficacy and durability differ depending on the drug and route of administration.

The lack of clinical response in Patient 2 highlights diagnostic uncertainty in cases with seronegative status and atypical electrophysiological findings. Possible explanations include a non‐myasthenic etiology of ocular motility disturbance (such as coexisting strabismus or microstructural orbital changes), insufficient local drug bioavailability, differences in the injection regimen compared to Patient 1, or irreversible neuromuscular transmission impairment. In contrast to Patient 1, who showed a robust response to periocular corticosteroids, Patient 2 exhibited no clinical benefit despite multiple injections. This divergence underscores the heterogeneity of seronegative MG and highlights the importance of precise differential diagnosis and an individual approach when ocular symptoms predominate. Compared with Patient 1, who responded favorably to repeated periocular corticosteroid injections, and Patient 2, who failed to show benefit despite multiple local treatments, Patient 3 demonstrated a clear and rapid clinical response after parabulbar betamethasone. The improvement was evident both objectively (photographic documentation of ocular alignment) and subjectively (improvement was already noted the day after injection, progressively increased, and by May 13 the patient reported a 70% reduction in diplopia with restoration of work capacity). This contrast underscores the role of antibody status and disease phenotype: while Patient 2 was seronegative with atypical electrophysiological findings, Patient 3 was seropositive with classic ENMG features, which may partly explain the robust treatment response. Taken together, these cases illustrate the heterogeneity of therapeutic outcomes and highlight the importance of individualized treatment strategies in ocular myasthenia gravis.

Our strategy for a seronegative MG patient with diplopia as the predominant disabling symptom was guided by evidence on ocular‐predominant disease and by standardized outcomes—MG‐ADL and the QMG—to track change [1, 2]. Systemic approaches can help but are constrained by adverse effects, delayed onset, and uncertain cost‐effectiveness when diplopia is the main problem; in our patient, glucocorticoids were poorly tolerated and azathioprine ineffective, consistent with variable responses reported in ocular MG [3, 4, 5].

Quantitative surgical series help define when strabismus surgery becomes reasonable. In Park et al. (n = 28), exotropia with vertical heterotropia was most common; mean horizontal deviation was 20.1 ± 17.9 prism diopters (PD) and mean vertical deviation 14.8 ± 11.1 PD; limitation of eye movement occurred in 71.4% [6]. After conventional MG therapy, 21.4% achieved resolution of diplopia, 14.3% had a partial response, and 64.3% had minimal or no response; among the latter, 35.7% had deviation ≥ 15 PD. Six patients underwent strabismus surgery: four became symptom‐free and satisfactorily aligned, one had intermittent diplopia despite small deviation, and one recurred during 10‐year follow‐up; on multivariable analysis only the initial deviation angle predicted surgical need (p = 0.016) [6]. Merino Sanz et al. (n = 14; mean age 55.6 years; 10 bilateral) reported horizontal deviations in 11 cases (8 exotropia) and vertical in 4; four pharmacologically refractory cases underwent surgery with good final results, six resolved on medication, one with prisms, and one required ptosis surgery; overall good outcomes were 78.6% [7]. Morris and O'Day noted diplopia in about 90% of MG patients, complete remission in only about 37% despite systemic therapy, and highlighted that surgical successes typically followed at least 5 months of angle stability, although they also reported two longstanding successes in unstable diplopia [8]. Earlier work likewise supports surgery after unsuccessful prolonged (approximately 6‐month) conservative therapy [11]. Botulinum toxin has been included in “active management” paradigms, but dosing uncertainty and diffusion into already weak extraocular muscles are practical concerns in MG; we therefore did not pursue this modality in our patient [12].

Against this background, periocular corticosteroid delivery offers a pragmatic, steroid‐sparing option to stabilize ocular symptoms while limiting systemic exposure. In an open‐label series (n = 14), weekly peribulbar or extraocular‐muscle dexamethasone for 4–6 weeks improved symptoms in 12/14 (85.7%), with one partial responder (7.1%) and one non‐responder (7.1%); relapses occurred in 2/14 (14.2%) during follow‐up, indicating benefit in many patients but underscoring the need for standardized technique and durability data [9]. Our within‐patient comparisons extend this signal by contrasting planes and agents across multiple episodes.

Pathophysiologically, pathogenic antibodies reduce the safety factor of neuromuscular transmission, producing fatigable weakness; systemic steroids broadly suppress autoimmunity, whereas periocular injections plausibly create a focal anti‐inflammatory depot adjacent to extraocular muscles, potentially modulating perimuscular edema and inflammation and stabilizing transmission. Short‐term changes immediately after injection may reflect mechanical/volume or placebo effects; however, in our case the most meaningful benefit emerged over days to weeks and lasted up to 128 days after parabulbar betamethasone 0.5 mL per eye, which is more compatible with a corticosteroid pharmacodynamic effect. Nonetheless, natural fluctuation and regression to the mean cannot be excluded in a single‐patient timeline.

Choice of technique in practice balances onset, durability, and safety. Parabulbar injections distribute drug within orbital fat and broadly expose extraocular muscles; sub‐Tenon injections create an episcleral depot with slower release. Dexamethasone (more soluble) may act faster but shorter, triamcinolone (particulate) may yield longer depot effects, and some betamethasone preparations combine soluble and particulate fractions. Smaller volumes (about 0.5 mL per eye) may limit pressure‐related effects; larger volumes (about 1.0 mL per eye) could prolong exposure at the cost of transient mass effects. Published series remain limited and heterogeneous, reinforcing the need for protocol standardization and agreed ocular outcome metrics [6, 9].

Safety considerations include local events (transient intraocular pressure [IOP] elevation and steroid response, cataract with repeated exposure, infection, hemorrhage or hematoma, nerve or muscle injury, transient ptosis, and‐rarely–lipoatrophy with enophthalmos and cosmetic or functional sequelae) and infrequent systemic effects at these doses. Risk mitigation comprises the lowest effective dose and frequency, aseptic technique, IOP monitoring in at‐risk patients, varying injection sites for repeats, and prudent agent and volume selection. Within stepped care, periocular steroids complement optical measures and may defer or refine the timing of surgery once alignment stabilizes [5, 6, 7, 8, 12].

Limitations include reliance on small series and retrospective cohorts with heterogeneous methods and follow‐up [3, 5, 6, 9]. Prospective studies should define candidate selection, standardized dosing (agent, plane, volume), safety monitoring, and validated ocular‐specific endpoints aligned with adaptations of MG‐ADL and QMG for ocular symptoms [1, 2, 5, 6, 12].

Conclusion

In refractory diplopia due to ocular involvement in myasthenia gravis, periocular corticosteroid delivery may offer clinically meaningful, temporizing benefit when systemic options fail or are poorly tolerated. Careful patient selection, standardized outcome measures, and prospective evaluation are needed before broader adoption.
