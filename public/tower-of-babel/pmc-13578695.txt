Plasma neurofilament light chain in early Parkinson’s disease predicts motor complications: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Ningning Che, Jingxuan Huang, Shichan Wang, Qirui Jiang, Tianmi Yang, Yi Xiao, Ruwei Ou, Chunyu Li, Huifang Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhether blood biomarkers can predict the development of motor complications in early-stage Parkinson’s disease (PD) remains unclear. In this prospective longitudinal cohort study, 173 patients with early-stage PD were followed for up to seven years. Plasma neurofilament light chain (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), phosphorylated tau181 (p-tau181), amyloid beta 42 (Aβ42), and amyloid beta 40 (Aβ40) were measured at baseline, 1-year, and 2-year follow-up using an ultrasensitive single-molecule array platform. Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of motor fluctuations and dyskinesias. Motor fluctuations and dyskinesias occurred in 41.6% and 17.9% of participants, respectively. Patients who developed motor complications had higher baseline plasma NfL, GFAP, and Aβ40 levels. However, after adjustment for confounders, only higher baseline NfL independently predicted the future development of motor complications, whereas longitudinal changes in plasma biomarkers were not associated with these outcomes. Higher baseline levodopa daily dose and greater anxiety and depression scores were also identified as clinical predictors. These findings suggest that plasma NfL may serve as a minimally invasive biomarker for early risk stratification of motor complications in PD.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a common neurodegenerative disorder, primarily characterized by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and striatum, accompanied by the formation of Lewy bodies1. Anti-PD medications, particularly levodopa (L-dopa), play an essential role throughout the disease course2. However, as the progression of PD and increased dosage of L-dopa, patients frequently develop motor complications including motor fluctuation and dyskinesia, with reported incidence of 54.3–100% and 14.5–55.7%3–5, respectively. Motor complications seriously disturb the quality of life of PD patients, and may even lead to disability3. Consequently, early prediction and effective therapeutic management of motor complications are crucial to patients with PD.

Previous studies have identified various clinical characteristics and non-motor symptoms for predicting motor complications, including age at onset, disease duration, anxiety, and apathy6,7. Moreover, neuroimaging studies using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have demonstrated that alterations in white matter connectivity within corticostriatal regions and intrinsic brain functional connectivity are associated with motor complications8,9. Additionally, a positron emission tomography study indicated that lower dopamine transporter binding might predict the development of motor complications in early-stage PD10. However, the subjectivity of clinical scales and the high technical demands of neuroimaging infrastructure limit their efficiency and widespread use in the clinical practice. Consequently, the development of reliable and convenient biomarkers has become increasingly essential.

In addition to neuroaxonal degeneration, astrocytic activation and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) related co-pathology are increasingly recognized in PD, and biomarkers reflecting these pathways (GFAP, Aβ40, Aβ42, and p-tau181) may provide complementary insights into disease heterogeneity, although their relationship with motor complications remains unclear. Neurofilament light chain (NfL), a well-established marker of neuroaxonal injury, is considered one of the most effective blood biomarkers for predicting disease progression of PD11–13. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) serves as a marker of reactive astrocyte and neuroinflammation, which has been associated with cognitive decline and disease progression in PD14,15, AD16, and frontotemporal dementia17. Phosphorylated tau181 (p-tau181), a biomarker for tau pathology, and amyloid beta 42 (Aβ42) and amyloid beta 40 (Aβ40), biomarkers for amyloid deposition, are all considered to as a biomarker for detecting AD co-pathology in PD18, particularly in cases of dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB)19. Nevertheless, no studies have specifically examined whether these plasma biomarkers correlate with motor complications in PD.

Furthermore, it remains unclear whether these biomarkers can predict the future occurrence of motor complications. The current study aimed to identify risk plasma biomarkers in early-stage PD for future motor complications through a longitudinal Chinese PD cohort, which ultimately paves the way for more individualized treatment strategies.

Results

Clinical and demographic profiles

Clinical and plasma data were available for all 173 patients at baseline and the 1-year follow-up. At the 2-year follow-up, clinical data were available for 167 patients, while plasma data were available for 115 patients. Of the 173 participants, 90 (52.02%) were male. The mean age of PD patients at baseline was 58.64 ± 10.79 years, with a mean onset age of 56.72 ± 11.12 years, a mean disease duration of 1.92 ± 1.15 years, and a median H-Y stage of 2 (0) (Supplementary Table 1).

Among the 173 patients over seven years of follow-up with a mean disease duration of 8.08 ± 0.78 years, motor fluctuation occurred in 72 (41.61%) patients, while dyskinesia was observed in 31 (17.91%) patients. Patients with motor complications had significantly higher baseline L-dopa daily dose, H-Y stage, UPDRS-III scores, HAMA scores, and HAMD scores compared to those without motor complications (P < 0.05) (Table 1). Additionally, baseline disease duration and UPDRS-I scores were significantly higher in patients with dyskinesia compared to those without dyskinesia (P < 0.05), however, no such differences were observed between patients with and without motor fluctuation (Table 1). There were no significant differences in the age, age of onset, sex, BMI, education, MoCA scores, REM, and EDS scores (P > 0.05) at baseline between PD patients with and without motor complications (Table 1).

Baseline plasma biomarkers and motor complications

At baseline, plasma levels of NfL, GFAP, and Aβ40 were significantly higher in patients with motor fluctuation compared to those without (P < 0.05) (Table 1, Fig. 1A–C). Similar changes in baseline plasma levels of NfL, GFAP, and Aβ40 were observed for patients with and without dyskinesia (P < 0.05) (Table 1, Fig. 1F–H). However, no significant differences were found in baseline plasma levels of p-tau181 and Aβ42 between patients with or without motor fluctuation or dyskinesia (P > 0.05) (Table 1, Fig. 1D, E, I, J).

Predictors of motor complications

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the predictive value of baseline plasma biomarkers for motor complications. Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests showed that patients with the highest tertile of baseline NfL had a significantly higher risk of developing motor fluctuation (P = 0.047) and dyskinesia (P = 0.005) compared to those with the lowest tertile of NfL (Fig. 2A, B). The results showed that patients in the intermediate tertile of baseline NfL had lower risk of developing dyskinesia compared with those in the highest tertile (P = 0.047), but not for motor fluctuations (P = 0.589). In contrast, when comparing to the lowest tertile, only a higher risk of developing into motor fluctuations (P = 0.110) and dyskinesia (P = 0.349) was observed, with no statistically significant. No significant differences in the risk of motor fluctuation and dyskinesia were observed for baseline plasma GFAP, p-tau181, Aβ42, and Aβ40 between patients with the highest and lowest tertile (Supplementary Table 2).

Kaplan-Meier curve for conversion to motor fluctuations and dyskinesias in PD.

Kaplan-Meier analysis illustrating the time to conversion from without motor fluctuations and without dyskinesias to motor fluctuations (A) and dyskinesias (B) in PD patients over a seven-year follow-up.

Univariable Cox regression analysis showed that longer disease duration, higher UPDRS-I scores, UPDRS-III scores, HAMA scores, HAMD scores, L-dopa daily dose, and higher levels of baseline NfL, GFAP, and Aβ40 were significantly associated with both motor fluctuation and dyskinesia (Tables 2 and 3). Higher H-Y stage was associated with motor fluctuation, but not dyskinesia, while higher UPDRS-II scores was associated with dyskinesia but not motor fluctuation. However, changes in plasma levels of NfL, GFAP, p-tau181, Aβ42, and Aβ40 over time were not associated with the onset of motor fluctuation or dyskinesia (P > 0.05). After adjusting the significant predictors mentioned above, multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that higher baseline L-dopa daily dose (HR: 1.002, 95% CI [(1.000–1.003)], P = 0.015), NfL levels (HR: 1.056, 95% CI [1.024–1.088], P < 0.001), and higher HAMD scores (HR: 1.055, 95% CI [1.017–1.097], P = 0.005) were significantly correlated with motor fluctuation. Similarly, after adjusting the significant predictors mentioned above, higher L-dopa daily dose (HR: 1.003, 95% CI [1.001–1.005], P = 0.009), HAMA scores (HR: 1.065, 95% CI [1.010–1.124], P = 0.021), and baseline NfL levels (HR: 1.131, 95% CI [1.074–1.191], P < 0.001) were significantly associated with dyskinesia in multivariate Cox regression.

Diagnostic utility of multivariate Cox regression models

ROC analysis demonstrated that baseline plasma NfL levels predicted the motor fluctuation with an AUC of 0.63 (95% CI: 0.5650–0.7316, sensitivity = 88.89%, specificity = 35.64%). Combining the three variables (plasma NfL levels, L-dopa daily dose, and HAMD score), which were most strongly associated with motor fluctuation in multivariate Cox regression analysis model, obtained an improved AUC of 0.70 (95% CI: 0.6078–0.7663, sensitivity = 45.83%, specificity = 83.17%) (Fig. 3A). Compared with the LD and HADA model, the addition of NfL showed a modest improvement in risk reclassification for motor fluctuations, with a continuous NRI of 0.251 (95% CI: −0.045 to 0.547, P = 0.096). The event and non-event components were −0.056 and 0.307, respectively, indicating that the improvement was mainly driven by better reclassification of patients without motor fluctuations, although the overall difference was not statistically significant.

Discussion

The longitudinal study investigated the predictive value of baseline plasma biomarkers for the occurrence of motor complications of PD patients. Higher baseline plasma NfL levels independently predicted the occurrence of future motor fluctuation and dyskinesia in PD patients after adjusting for relevant variables. Additionally, our study showed that higher baseline L-dopa daily dose and HAMD scores were associated with an increased risk of motor fluctuations, while higher baseline L-dopa daily dose and HAMA scores were associated with an increased risk of dyskinesias.

Our results indicated that motor fluctuation occurred in 41.61% of participants, while dyskinesia was observed in 17.91% over the seven-year follow-up with a mean disease duration of 8.08 ± 0.78 years, which was similar to the previous findings4,20. The development of motor complications is closely related to several factors, including higher L-dopa daily dose, longer disease duration, more serious disease severity, and younger age at disease onset7,20,21. Additional contributors, such as lower body weight, anxiety, and genetic susceptibility have also been reported22–24. In the study, higher baseline NfL levels, L-dopa daily dose, and elevated HAMD, HAMA scores were identified as risk factors for motor complications. Although the mechanisms of motor complications remain unclear, presynaptic dopaminergic denervation and chronic pulsatile stimulation of dopamine receptors are considered to play a key essential role25.

As a marker of neuroaxonal degeneration, plasma NfL levels reflect the extent of neuronal loss and has been implicated in a variety of neurodegenerative diseases, including AD26, frontotemporal dementia27, multiple system atrophy28, and PD12,29. Recent studies have indicated that plasma NfL correlates with motor symptoms and predicts disease progression in PD12,30, especially plasma NfL was a better predictor of progression in PD than p-tau181 and GFAP31. Elevated CSF NfL levels and higher NfL immunoreactivity have been associated with presynaptic dopamine loss in PD32,33. Additionally, serum NfL levels have been shown to predict motor deficit and reductions in dopamine transporter binding in early-stage PD34. These findings suggest that higher NfL levels reflect the degree of neurodegeneration and functional decline in the dopaminergic system. Axonal damage reduces neuronal excitability by disrupting ion channel distribution and function, as well as axonal transport, leading to impaired responsiveness of dopaminergic neurons35–37. This reduced excitability may contribute to motor complications by weakening dopamine signaling in the basal ganglia. Chronic pulsatile stimulation of dopamine receptors, which was caused by disease progression and long-term dopaminergic therapy, likely worsens this disruption and exacerbates motor complications38.

The depletion of presynaptic dopamine plays a critical role in the development of motor complications39–41, which could be caused by the neurodegeneration of dopaminergic neurons. Higher NfL levels may serve as an indicator of this depletion, revealing the extent of neuronal injury. We hypothesize that reduced excitability of dopaminergic neurons, driven by axonal injury and neuroinflammation, mediates the relationship between plasma NfL levels and motor complications in PD. Although higher NfL levels may reflect the threshold of neuronal damage at which motor fluctuation and dyskinesias begin to develop, they represent indirect indicators of neurodegeneration rather than direct measures of central pathology. Future studies combining plasma biomarkers with neuroimaging or cerebrospinal fluid markers may provide a more comprehensive understanding of disease mechanisms.

In this study, although baseline plasma GFAP and Aβ40 levels were elevated in PD patients with motor complications compared to those without, no significant predictive relationship between motor complications and baseline plasma GFAP or Aβ40 levels was found after adjusting for confounding factors. GFAP primarily reflects astrocytic activation and neuroinflammatory processes, which have been more consistently associated with cognitive decline and non-motor manifestations in PD rather than treatment-related motor complications14,42, Similarly, Aβ40, Aβ42, and p-tau181 reflect Alzheimer’s disease-related co-pathology, which is increasingly recognized in PD but is more strongly linked to cognitive impairment and dementia18. These findings suggest that biomarkers reflecting neuroinflammation or AD-related pathology may be less directly linked to motor complications in PD. In contrast, motor complications are thought to arise mainly from progressive dopaminergic denervation and long-term pulsatile dopaminergic stimulation, processes that may be more directly reflected by biomarkers of neuroaxonal injury, such as NfL. Therefore, it is biologically plausible that biomarkers reflecting neuroaxonal damage show stronger associations with the development of motor complications.

Non-motor symptoms, such as anxiety, apathy, and poor sleep quality, not only impact the quality of life in PD, but also can predict the development of motor complications24,43,44. Our study indicated that baseline higher HAMA and HAMD scores were associated with an increased risk of motor complications. Previous studies indicated that a high non-motor symptom burden in early PD predicts faster motor progression of PD45. Therefore, early identification and treatment of anxiety and depression may help to reduce the risk of motor complications, though the underlying mechanisms require further investigation.

Previous studies have suggested that higher L-dopa daily dose and longer disease duration, rather than the duration of L-Dopa treatment, have a greater impact on the risk of dyskinesia46,47. Consistent with such a finding, we also identified a higher baseline L-dopa daily dose as a predictor of dyskinesia and motor fluctuations in PD. In this study, it indicated that patients have motor fluctuations and dyskinesia have higher baseline L-Dopa daily dose (150 mg/day, 300 mg/day, respectively). Although STRIDE-PD trial indicates that early use of low-dose L-dopa (<400 mg/day) did not increase the risk of dyskinesia22, however, a study on Chinese patients with PD indicated that L-Dopa daily dose with less than 275 mg was considered a safe level for motor complications48. The L-Dopa daily dose in the Chinese population is lower than that in the North American population, suggesting heterogeneity in L-dopa dosage among different ethnic groups. Our findings suggest that early adjustments to medication regimens may be necessary for the future occurrence of motor complications in PD patients. In addition, compared with clinical characteristics alone, the combination of NfL improved risk reclassification for motor complications, suggesting that a multidimensional model integrating neuroaxonal injury with clinical features may offer more accurate individualized risk stratification.

The study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center. The results of the study should be validated in future research on a larger sample size across multiple centers. Second, subgroup analyses based on age of onset were not performed. Future research should include more detailed subgroup analyses to facilitate more personalized treatment strategies for PD patients. Another limitation is that plasma biomarkers were measured only at baseline, 1-year, and 2-year follow-ups, whereas clinical follow-up extended to seven years. This limited our ability to assess long-term biomarker trajectories. Future studies with more comprehensive longitudinal biomarker sampling are needed. Additionally, the relationship between genetics and motor complications was not explored in the study, and further investigation is needed in the future. Despite these limitations, our study is the first to explore the relationship between plasma NfL, GFAP, P-tau181, Aβ42, and Aβ40 levels and motor complications over a prolonged follow-up period.

In conclusion, PD patients with motor complications have higher baseline plasma NfL levels compared to those without motor complications. Elevated baseline plasma NfL levels, rather than the change rate of these biomarkers, were associated with an increased risk of motor complications in early-stage PD. These findings suggest that plasma NfL may serve as a valuable biomarker for predicting future occurrence of motor complications, and could provide a significance for developing prevention and treatment strategies in PD.

Methods

Participants

All PD patients were enrolled from February 2015 to September 2024 at the Department of Neurology, West China Hospital of Sichuan University. The diagnosis of PD patients was made clinically by experienced movement disorder specialists based on the 2015 Movement Disorder Society clinical diagnostic criteria49 and the UK Brain Bank criteria for PD50. The exclusion criteria were set as following: (1) patients with Parkinson-plus syndromes, including multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, cortical basal ganglia degeneration, dementia with Lewy bodies; (2) Patients with secondary parkinsonism, including vascular, drug-induced, inflammatory, immune-mediated, normal pressure hydrocephalus, infectious, and traumatic causes; (3) patients with severe cognitive decline, significant depression or anxiety, and those who had a history of heart failure, liver disease, renal failure, and lung disease; (4) participants undergone surgical procedures for PD, including deep brain stimulation therapy.

This is an ongoing prospective longitudinal cohort study, and the patients were followed up to seven years. The study received ethical approval from the hospital’ s Ethics Committee (No.2015-236), and all participants provided written informed. A total of 180 PD patients with a disease duration less than 3 years and a Hoehn & Yahr (H&Y) stage ≤2 were initially included in the study; however, 7 patients were excluded due to the presence of motor complications at baseline. Finally, 173 patients were enrolled final analysis. Annual follow-up assessments were conducted for all patients. For participants unable to attend in-person visits, motor complication outcomes were obtained through structured telephone follow-up, thereby reducing the risk of loss to follow-up.

Clinical evaluation

Detailed methods regarding the clinical assessment and data collection during follow-up has been described previously45. Demographic information (See Table 1) was collected from all PD patients at baseline. Motor symptoms were assessed using the Movement Disorders Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS-III), and Hoehn & Yahr (H&Y) staging in the “OFF” state, while non-motor symptoms were assessed in the “ON” state. The presence and severity of motor complications, including dyskinesia and motor fluctuation, were evaluated using MDS-UPDRS part IV, specifically questions 1 and 351. We considered patients with motor complications who developed motor complications during any follow-up period. Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA, Beijing Version), 14-item Hamilton anxiety (HAMA-14) rating scale, 24-item Hamilton depression (HAMD-24) rating scale, Rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD) screening questionnaire, Excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) scale was performed to assess non-motor symptoms including cognition, anxiety, depression and sleep. The L-dopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) was calculated based on the L-dopa conversion formula52.

Measurement of plasma biomarkers

Fasting venous blood samples (4 ml) were collected in EDTA tubes from all PD patients. Samples were centrifuged at 4 °C within 10 min (2000 rpm) to isolate plasma, which was then stored at −80 °C to prevent degradation from repeated freeze-thaw cycles. Plasma levels of NfL, GFAP, p-tau181, Aβ42, and Aβ40 were measured at baseline and during 1-year, and 2-year follow-ups using an ultrasensitive single-molecule array (Simoa™; Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) on the automated Simoa™ HD-X platform (GBIO, Hangzhou, China). The Neurology 4-Plex E Assay Kit (Cat No:103670) and the p-tau181 Advantage V2 Assay Kit (Cat No:103714) were used (Quanterix) for biomarker measurements. Plasma samples were randomized, blinded, and measured using reagents from the same lot to ensure consistency.

Statistical analysis

The normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) depending on the normality. Group comparisons were performed using independent sample t-tests or the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using Chi-square tests. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to compare the cumulative risk of motor complications during follow-up. Univariable and multivariate Cox regression analysis were performed to identify predictors of the onset of motor complications. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in univariable analysis were included in the multivariate analysis. To evaluate the predictive capability of baseline clinical characteristics and plasma biomarkers for motor complications, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated.

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Scatter plots were created using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
