Comparing functional impairments related to social network use disorder and internet gaming disorder
Authors: Lara Zumrode, Leah Reinicke, Anja Bischof, Gallus Bischof, Samantha Schlossarek, Hannah Schmidt, Stefan Borgwardt, Hans-Jürgen Rumpf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsThe current study aimed to analyze impairments in daily life functioning (DLF) in vocational students with Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) and Social Network Use Disorder (SNUD). While diagnostic requirement for IGD has been defined and the functionally impairing nature has been demonstrated, SNUD is a relatively new phenomenon with less evidence.MethodsA diagnostic interview (n = 937) was conducted in a sample of students, of which 279 met the adapted DSM-5 criteria, with 70 reporting social networking and 29 gaming as their main activity. For screening, the Compulsive Internet Use Scale (CIUS) was used and the Internet Use Disorder – Criterion-based Assessment Tool (I-CAT) served as diagnostic measure. Impairment was assessed using a subset of items taken from the WHO Disability Scale.ResultsIn a prior analysis, overall impairments in DLF among students with and without IUD were compared, indicating higher impairments for the IUD-group. In our study, the DSM-5 criteria appear to be appropriate to differentiate between pathological (IUD-group) and healthy internet users (non-IUD group) in terms of DLF. Odds Ratios ranged between 0.597 and 1.340 for impairments in DLF. When testing for statistical significance despite low statistical power, no differences of DLF were observed between students with IGD and SNUD in logistic regression analyses.ConclusionThe results must be regarded as preliminary and might indicate that students with IGD and SNUD show comparable impairments in DLF. However, higher sample sizes could have led to different results which needs to be investigated in future studies.

Introduction

Our comprehension of addiction has expanded beyond substances to include compulsive behaviors, commonly referred to as behavioral addictions. These addictions involve excessive engagement in activities and are characterized by repetitive, harmful patterns that persist despite efforts to exert control over them (Grant, Potenza, Weinstein, & Gorelick, 2010). A ‘functionally impairing nature’ (Kardefelt-Winther et al., 2017) has been suggested as a requirement that needs to be present to meet criteria for a behavioral addiction. Furthermore, several theoretical frameworks have been established to comprehend and elucidate the emergence of behavioral addictions. The Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model (Brand et al., 2016, 2019) states that addictive behaviors develop as a consequence of an interaction between three elements: predisposing variables, affective and cognitive responses to specific stimuli, and executive functions. Among these behaviors, internet use has been evidenced as exhibiting addictive potential. The internet plays a significant role in our daily lives, providing a wealth of information, entertainment, and possibilities to socially interact and has therefore been linked to well-being (Hennemann, Farnsteiner, & Sander, 2017). However, there is growing concern about potential negative aspects of internet use. Recent research has unveiled adverse effects of addictive internet use on mental health and well-being (Montag et al., 2024). Thus, studies have illuminated its negative impact on overall well-being and life satisfaction (Montag, Demetrovics, et al., 2024), as well as associations with psychological issues such as depression and anxiety (Ghimire, Sapkota, Shakya, Joshi, & Nepal, 2023; Peterka-Bonetta, Sindermann, Elhai, & Montag, 2019). These negative consequences may culminate in the manifestation of what is referred to as an Internet Use Disorder (IUD). Recognizing the wide array of content and applications the internet offers, however, scholars have increasingly advocated for a focus on specific IUDs (Brand et al., 2020; Montag et al., 2015). The most researched and cited IUDs are the Internet Gaming Disorder and the Social Network Use Disorder (SNUD), of which IGD is by far more researched. IGD has been classified as research diagnosis in the fifth Edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR; American Psychiatric Association, 2024), while Gaming Disorder (GD) has been fully recognized as a clinical diagnosis in the 11th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11, World Health Organization, 2022). Accordingly, a great body of research exists that evidences the addictive potential of gaming and the negative consequences, consolidating its status as a mental health disorder (Brand et al., 2020). Regarding the development of IGD, studies have demonstrated the complex interactions between gaming-related, individual, and environmental factors (Király, Koncz, Griffiths, & Demetrovics, 2023). Depressive and (social) anxiety symptoms, have been consistently associated with IGD among psychopathological conditions (Király et al., 2023; Ohayon & Roberts, 2021).

Besides IGD, SNUD does not hold such a classification and relatively few research exists on the addictive use of social network sites (SNS). However, a growing body of literature hints at potential negative consequences of SNUD (Weinstein, 2023), and it has been associated with hyperactivity-inattention and depressive symptoms (Shensa et al., 2017; Van Den Eijnden, Lemmens, & Valkenburg, 2016). Furthermore, SNS use has unique potential harms such as higher probability to excessively draw social comparisons with others (Clark, Algoe, & Green, 2018). Possibly as a consequence of this, it has been associated with feelings of loneliness (Huang, 2022) and negative body image (Tiggemann, 2018). Boer et al. (2022) emphasized that when analyzing the relationship between social media use and adolescent well-being, it is important to recognize the heterogeneity of effects and to distinguish between the intensity of social media use and the problems associated with it. Also, elevated levels of Fear of Missing Out (FoMO) have been demonstrated to be generally associated with a more pronounced adverse impact of social networks on an individual's daily life and work productivity in a cross-sectional study (Rozgonjuk, Sindermann, Elhai, & Montag, 2020). These findings indicate that SNUD might hold clinical importance, suggesting that it should be considered for potential inclusion as an official diagnosis (Brand et al., 2020).

Functional impairment

The concept of daily life functioning (DLF) encompasses a broad spectrum of an individual's everyday activities, spanning tasks like social interactions, occupational or academic achievements, responsibilities management, as well as emotional well-being and stress coping (Brand et al., 2020) An inverse relationship between the presence of a mental health disorder and an individual's ability to perform daily life tasks and activities has been proven. It is therefore important to investigate potential mental disorders with regard to this construct. Among behavioral addictions, distinguishing an involved but healthy from a pathological use or behavior needs to be a core feature of diagnosis (Brand et al., 2020). Kardefeld-Winter et al. (2017) consequently stress the importance of considering this criterion in behavioral addictions, as it constitutes a crucial factor in avoiding over-pathologizing. This is important for both IGD and SNUD, as the mere use of games or social networks is not an indication of pathology. Scholars raised concerns about the capability of IGD criteria to effectively distinguish healthy and pathological gamers. They criticize the mixing of crucial, so-called core criteria (Billieux, Flayelle, Rumpf, & Stein, 2019) with less important, peripherical criteria (Besser, Loerbroks, Bischof, Bischof, & Rumpf, 2019), which diminishes the diagnostical accuracy. Also, Castro-Calvo et al. (2021) and colleagues showed that some IGD criteria are less clinically relevant. Nevertheless, studies have evidenced that IGD in fact leads to functional impairment (Ko, Lin, Lin, & Yen, 2020). Regarding SNUD, the core features including diminished control, increased priority, and continued use despite negative consequences have been recognized (Andreassen, 2015). However, empirical evidence on specific aspects of problematic social network use is limited compared to IGD (Wegmann & Brand, 2016), SNUD has qualified as a specific IGD with good levels of evidence besides gaming disorder, buying-shopping disorder, and pornography use disorder (Brand et al., 2020). Functional impairment caused by excessively using SNS is less extensively studied compared to other behavioral addictions and more empirical evidence is needed. Moreover, this review pointed out that data from clinical samples are scarce. At the same time, a current study characterizing individuals undergoing an online-treatment for IUDs found 15.8% of those suffering from a severe symptomatology preferred the use of social networks (Basenach et al., 2024) and SNS is considered for treatment guidelines (Rumpf et al., 2021).

Research aim

The research aim of the present study is to investigate impairments in DLF among students with IGD and SNUD. To do so, we will identify two different groups of addictive internet users: those who show excessive behavior in online gaming (IGD group) and those for whom the same applies regarding social network applications (SNUD group). We will analyze whether these two groups are affected to the same extent in their daily lives. Based on the literature described above, we hypothesize that students diagnosed with IGD will show different impairments in DLF as students diagnosed with SNUD. Further, we hypothesize that emotional well-being, one of the six domains of DLF, will be more affected among students with SNUD. This assumption is based on established links between SNUD with factors such as FoMO and neuroticism (Rozgonjuk et al., 2020), as well as body dissatisfaction (Tiggemann, 2018) and alexithymia (Gori & Topino, 2023). Given the relationships described above, the research hypotheses are as follows:Students with IUD (IUD group) show overall significant higher impairments in DLF compared to students without IUD (non-IUD group).The impairments in DLF predict the assignment of students to the GD or SNUD group.

Method

Participants were recruited at vocational schools in Schleswig-Holstein, Germany. The recruitment procedure is displayed in Fig. 1 and will be described in detail in the following. The data used in this paper was generated within the intervening in Problematic INTernet use (iPIN)-study (Schmidt et al., 2022), which tested an intervention for vocational students with pathological and problematic internet use behavior. We used only the part of the data that is relevant to assess and test our hypotheses. Hence, we will only describe those procedures of iPIN involved in collecting the data relevant for our research question.

Flowchart of the selection of participants

Participants and procedures

Recruitment took part from March 2018 to March 2019. Sufficient knowledge of the German language and a minimum age of 16 years were requirements to be included. In total, 8,607 students from 12 different schools finished the screening carried out on site in the classrooms, of these, 3,078 students exceeded the cut-off value of ≥21 of the Compulsive Internet Use Scale (CIUS; Meerkerk, Van Den Eijnden, Vermulst, & Garretsen, 2009).The CIUS was assessed as an initial filter. Subsequently, 937 students participated in an in-depth diagnostic interview via telephone as a second and last filter. Based on the diagnostic interview, 279 students were assigned a diagnosis of IUD, hence met five or more of the nine DSM-5 criteria. Of these, a combined total of 99 students reported using either gaming or social networks as their only or primary online activity of which 29 were assigned to the IGD group and 70 to the SNUD group.

Screening

The screening in vocational schools was carried out by employees of the research group: project staff, research assistants, and graduate and undergraduate students. Individual schedules were settled with each school as to when to conduct the screenings. In every classroom, study staff informed students and teachers about goals and procedures of the study, the voluntariness of participation and the protection of privacy. Afterwards, an iPad to answer the questionnaires was handed to every student who agreed to participate. The questionnaires were preceded by an information section emphasizing once again the voluntariness of participation and the duty of confidentiality on behalf of the study staff towards the school and school staff. During completion study staff was at disposal for potential questions or uncertainties. Sociodemographic data was collected as well as student's internet use behavior. The CIUS has been used as a screening tool to screen for problematic Internet use (Meerkerk et al., 2009). The 14 items of the scale represent the following criteria for compulsive Internet use: Salience, Withdrawal, Loss of Control, Conflict, and Coping with an Unpleasant Mood. All questions were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “never” (0) to “very often” (4). Participants scoring at least 21 on the CIUS were defined as having at least elevated levels of Internet use and at increased risk for IUD. The low cut-off was used to ensure optimal sensitivity and full reach of the target group (Schmidt et al., 2022). Internal consistency was good in the present sample (CIUS Cronbach's α = .87). Those who reached a cut-off value of ≥21 on the CIUS, a scale to assess the severity of compulsive internet use, were categorized as potential participants of the study and hence informed about the further process. They were given the relevant information material on the study and on data privacy. Adult students were asked to provide written consent and contact details. Underage students were given the information material in duplicate, as well as a consent form for their parents and a return envelope.

In-depth diagnostical interview

Those students that gave written consent – the underaged ones through their parents – were contacted later by telephone to conduct the in-depth diagnostical interview, for which each participant was paid €20. Initially, further demographical data were collected before continuing with the diagnostical interview. In order to assign the diagnosis Internet Use Disorder, the Internet related disorders – Criterion-based Assessment Tool (I-CAT) was applied. This tool is based on the structure of the sections on substance use disorders from the Munich Composite International Diagnostic Interview (M-CIDI; Wittchen, Lachner, Wunderlich, & Pfister, 1998). The I-CAT assesses the frequency of internet use as well as the nine diagnostic criteria of the IGD of the DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2024) however, modified for internet applications in general (e.g., social network, pornography use etc.). I-CAT is a fully structured interview which allows for precise diagnostic assessments that has been used in a couple of studies and proved to distinguish between individuals with and without Internet use disorders (Röttinger et al., 2021). Included in our main statistical analysis were vocational students who fulfilled five or more of the nine criteria of DSM-5 criteria for IGD and indicated gaming or social network use as their sole main activity on the internet. The data of students that did not indicate IGD or SNUD as their main activity as well as those that underwent the diagnostical interview and did not exceed four DSM criteria were used in a preliminary analysis to compare impairments among students with IUD with the group of students with non-IUD. Excluded in general were students who were currently accessing psychological or psychotherapeutic support. The threshold of five out of nine criteria on one hand is proposed by the APA guidelines (2013), being the same threshold to assign the diagnosis. On the other hand, this cut-off demonstrated the highest levels of sensitivity and specificity in effectively distinguishing individuals with healthy gaming from those gamers who exhibited clinically significant problems (Koo, Han, Park, & Kwon, 2017). Ultimately, for statistical analyses we used the 12-month period of the DSM criteria of each participant, which is also the official timeframe for diagnosis assignment (American Psychiatric Association, 2024).

Impairment in daily life

Impairments in DLF were assessed already during the screening in the classrooms (N = 8,607), using a questionnaire developed within the research group based on the WHODAS-S Questionnaire (Janca et al., 1996). In total, six domains of impairments were assessed, namely: 1) household duties, 2) social activities, 3) concentration ability, 4) friendships, 5) work/school, and 6) emotional well-being. The questions assessing these domains of impairments concerned the last 30 days and had to be answered on a 5-point scale. For example, the first impairment was inquired asking: ‘In the past 30 days, how much difficulty did you have in performing your household duties due to your internet or smartphone use?’. These items operationalize the variables analyzed in hypothesis 2. The additional three questions of the WHODAS-S Questionnaire (Janca et al., 1996) measure the severity and frequency of these impairments that occurred within the last 30 days. For example: ‘On approximately how many days within the last 30 days did you have to reduce everyday activities or your work because of your internet use?’. Internal consistency was acceptable in the present sample WHODAS-S Cronbach's α = .72).

Covariates

The four covariates – gender, anxiety and depression, mental health, and severity of the disorder – that will be controlled for in the regression analyses were collected partly in the screening and partly during the telephone interview. Gender was one of the first variables to be queried in the screening. Its inclusion in our models is driven by consistent findings of unequal distributions of males and females in the two internet use disorders (Andreassen, Pallesen, & Griffiths, 2017; Mérelle et al., 2017) so we will control for potential impact of gender on disruptions in DLF. Also, mental health was assessed during the screening using the self-report questionnaire Mental Health Inventory (MHI-5; Veit & Ware, 1983). Internal consistency was good in the present sample (MHI-5 Cronbach's α = .81). As already seen earlier, mental health and psychological problems can be a trigger for as well as a consequence of IUD (Gentile et al., 2011). We will hence control for their influence on DLF, as they have been clearly evidenced. Self-reporting of depressive and anxiety symptoms was performed during the telephone interview using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS; Snaith, 2003). Internal consistency was good in the present sample (HADS Cronbach's α = .80). These control variables were significantly related to the impairment of DLF and IUD in our sample. Significant differences were also found for the IUD and non-IUD groups (no data shown).

The severity of the respective IUD was measured with the I-CAT. More precisely, it was defined by how many DSM-5 criteria were met. The severity was controlled for as it might be crucial for the extent to which it impairs people's daily life (Dieter et al., 2017; Rozgonjuk et al., 2020).

Assurance of quality

To ensure maximum objectivity, telephone interviews were conducted using computer-based tools with standardized questions and introductory texts. Prior to conducting interviews, all interviewers underwent comprehensive training in the computer-assisted interview version. Possible challenges and appropriate interviewer responses were discussed and practiced in advance. As part of their training, all interviewers were provided with Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) containing standardized phrases for greetings, introductions, and potential follow-up questions and responses during the interviews.

For optimal sample coverage, various methods of contact were employed, including phone calls, emails, and postal communication. If participants could not be reached at the provided phone numbers on different days and at different times, initial contact attempts were made via email, followed by postal correspondence. After a total of ten unsuccessful contact attempts, participants were reassigned to different interviewers. Following an additional five unsuccessful contact attempts, further contact attempts were ceased. In cases where postal addresses were found to be invalid after baseline interviews, address inquiries were made to the local registration office to enable sending letters to participants at their current addresses. To ensure a scientifically sound project implementation, regular intra-team project conferences were conducted. The completed screening and telephone interviews were critically discussed, and adjustments were deliberated. SOPs were updated to align with the experiences of previous data collection phases. Furthermore, gender-specific aspects were considered in the planning and execution of the study. The results of the PINTA study indicated an elevated prevalence of internet-related disorders among young women, particularly regarding the preference for social networking on the internet. Accordingly, vocational schools predominantly attended by women (e.g., training in healthcare professions) were included.

Ethics

The research project underwent a thorough examination by the responsible ethics committee at the University of Lübeck and was approved on December 15, 2017, under the reference number 17-339. In order to facilitate the process of recruiting study participants from vocational schools, formal applications were submitted to the Ministries of Education in both Schleswig-Holstein and Hamburg, and these applications received the necessary authorizations. This collaborative effort ensured compliance with ethical standards and regulatory requirements, allowing for the inclusion of participants from these educational institutions in the research study.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for Mac. A first analysis was conducted to investigate whether students that were assigned an IUD were more impaired in DLF than those positive in the screening but diagnosed negatively on IUD in the in-depth diagnostical interview using the I-CAT. Hence, we used data from the 658 participants without an IUD – five were excluded due to missing data so 653 remained – and compared them to the 279 students that had been assigned an IUD. For illustrative purposes see Fig. 1. We deliberately chose not to include the 5,152 participants who did not exceed the CIUS cut-off in this analysis, as the research question aims to investigate impairments in DLF among students who showed an IUD (IGD or SNUD). Participants who do not exceed the CIUS cut-off are not expected to have an addictive use of the Internet.

Prior the main analyses, a comparison of impairments in DLF among students with and without IUD was performed. Therefore, Mann-Whitney U tests (one-sided) were performed, assuming stronger impairments in the group of students with IUD. Binomial logistic regression analyses were performed to predict differences in impairment measures, respectively the frequency and severity of their occurrence. With the exception of gender, which was incorporated as a two-level factor variable, the covariates were included in the analyses as ordinal scaled control variables. In the regression models, the dependent variable, our criteria, was the dummy variable ‘group’, coded 0 for SNUD and 1 for IGD. The six impairment measures were assessed on an ordinal scale and served as independent variables. The three impairment questions about their frequency and severity of occurrence were measured on an interval scale. First, bivariate analyses (with one independent variable in each model) were calculated to investigate each impairment on its own. Then, multivariate analyses (multiple regression analysis, with all independent variables in a single model) were performed to account for potential interactions between variables. Effect sizes are represented by Odds Ratio (OR) (Cheng et al., 2010). proposed guidelines for its interpretation by making it comparable to the effect size of Cohen's d. They propose that an OR of 1.5–2.0 is a weak effect, an OR of 3.0–3.5 a medium effect, and an OR of 4.0–7.0 a strong effect.

Results of the regression analysis

In a first step, for each impairment, separate binomial logistic regression analyses were performed. Results are reported in Tables A1–6 in the Appendix. Based on the small sample size, testing for missing differences between groups has to be regarded with caution. Nevertheless, data will be reported. When testing for statistical significance despite low statistical power, no statistically significant effects (p > .107) were observed concerning the impairment measures. Hence, none of the predictor variables included could significantly predict whether a person would rather exhibit IGD or SNUD in our analysis. The covariate gender was consistently found to be significant. As previously shown in the descriptive analyses, men and women were notably unevenly distributed across the two groups. More precisely, the IGD group displayed a significantly higher proportion of males, while the SNUD group exhibited a markedly larger contingent of females. As soon as gender was excluded as a covariate, the Pseudo R2 of McFadden was close to zero. Also, after an exploratory comparison between IGD and SNUD, when we combined the first five impairment measures that were expected to be higher in IGD into a single variable, no significant difference was detected. There was a tendency of the IGD group exhibiting slightly greater impairment, however none of significance. Subsequently, binomial regression analyses were calculated for the three metric measures, reported in Tables A7–9 which are also displayed in Appendix. The results were the same for the measures for frequency and severity of the occurrence. Again, no significances were found for the variables of interest, only gender revealed significant effects. Finally, a multiple logistic regression analysis was performed to test for potential interactions between impairments. Just like in the binomial regression analyses, no significances except for gender were found. The analysis is displayed in Table 4. Further analyses are presented in Tables A1–9 in the Appendix. Alternative statistical approaches were tested (findings not shown). Using a negative binomial regression model did not alter the results.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate impairments in DLF between students with IGD and SNUD, to contribute to the investigation of distinct profiles of IUD (Brand et al., 2020; Kuss & Lopez-Fernandez, 2016). IGD was chosen as the comparative disorder due to empirical evidence and its classification as a disorder in the two prominent classification systems. Our initial analysis confirmed that participants with an IUD were significantly more impaired than those without an IUD (hence did not exceed four DSM-5 criteria). For the comparison of the IUD and non-IUD group, we applied the nine DSM-5 IGD criteria, which have been criticized to potentially leading to an over-pathologization. We found the criteria to effectively distinguish between students without an IUD from addictive internet users in our sample considering the impairments. Nevertheless, we consider it necessary to note that several studies, such as those by Billieux et al. (2019) and Kardefelt-Winther et al. (2017) raised concerns about the IGD criteria. In an international Delphi study, in which an expert panel evaluated the clinical applicability of the DSM-5 criteria for IGD (Castro-Calvo et al., 2021), there was an agreement that some had higher diagnostic validity, clinical utility and prognostic value than others (e.g. tolerance, deception). Crucially, some DSM-5 criteria (e.g. escapism/mood regulation, tolerance) were emphasized as incapable of distinguishing between addictive and healthy gaming. However, our results align with those of Ko et al. (2020), emphasizing substantial functional impairments among individuals diagnosed with IGD. Considering the concerns about over-pathologizing gaming behavior, our study indicates significant differences in functional impairment between students with and without an IUD, according to DSM-5 criteria. Our hypothesis that impairments in DLF would predict assignment to the GD or SNUD group, considering the domains, the severity, and frequency of impairment, was not confirmed in our analysis. It has to be noted that our findings are derived from a sample with limited size resulting in low statistical power to observe differences between groups. Given this limitation, our data might suggest that students with these two types of IUD show equal impairments in DLF. However, they stay inconclusive due to the methodological shortcoming of observing small groups. Higher sample sizes could have led to different results which needs to be investigated in future studies. Nevertheless, our results may also indicate that certain IUD could be associated with similar impairments, which requires further investigation and re-evaluation in a clinical context. Gender was the sole significant predictor to predict the assignment to the IGD or the SNUD group in our logistic regression analyses. In the IGD group, markedly more students were male, and significantly more were female in the SNUD group, which is consistent with previous findings (Andreassen et al., 2017; Mérelle et al., 2017). Further investigation of the role of gender appears to be crucial, as recent research has highlighted gender-specific psychological mechanisms of certain IUDs, in addition to specific prevalence rates (Brand et al., 2025). The preliminary trend that we observed in terms of impairments in DLF coincide with the other research findings such as Wartberg, Kriston, and Thomasius (2020), who compared IGD and SNUD and found more similarities than differences in the two disorders. Also, Dieter et al. (2017) compared IGD and SNUD and simultaneously specific IUD with healthy controls. They did not find notable differences, but rather similarities between IGD and SNUD, for example low scores in emotional competence. Nevertheless, it should also be noted that addictive behaviors are complex and multifactorial (Brand et al., 2025). The revised I-PACE model (Brand et al., 2019) suggests that multiple addictive online behaviors are underpinned by common psychological processes and are driven by akin mechanisms. According to the theoretical assumptions of the model, the use of different internet applications can lead to addictive behavior, including social networks and games (Wegmann, Billieux, & Brand, 2022). In our sample, more students were affected by SNUD compared to IGD. A significant aspect of social networks compared to games that may increase addictive potential is the constant smartphone accessibility with ongoing message notifications, potentially impacting concentration ability, impulsivity and causing stress. In fact, interruptive notifications have been linked to mental health issues (Elhai, Rozgonjuk, Alghraibeh, & Yang, 2021). The ease of access and availability may explain the relatively higher number of students with SNUD compared to those with gaming in the present sample. Consistent with this, a study of online therapy patients also found that SNUD was more common than IGD (Basenach et al., 2024). Taking together these findings, SNUD might have similar clinical relevance as the already classified (Internet) Gaming Disorder. Ultimately, within the realm of social networking, it is essential to remember that sophisticated strategies are used to promote addictive-like usage (Montag & Hegelich, 2020).

Limitations

One limitation arises from the sample size. In the logistic regression analyses we calculated, effect sizes represented by the OR ranged between 1.0 and 1.3. This indicates that, if an effect would be present, it would be very small, hence a large sample is needed to detect it. Due to low effect sizes and a small sample, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.3) to test the statistical power of our models. The crucial values we used was the sample size of N = 99 and the average observed effect size of OR = 1.2. Results revealed a power of 0.1, which is in fact very low and indicates under-powered analyses. It is, however, important to note that calculating post hoc power, which is also called observed power and retrospective power, has its limitations. It has been criticized to vary within the practical range of interest and may diverge significantly from the actual power (Zhang et al., 2019). As described in our methods, the different variables in our study were not collected simultaneously. Importantly, the functional impairment measures were collected during the screening whereas the diagnoses were assigned in the telephone interview. In fact, there was an average gap of nine weeks between the two time points. While this temporal gap may introduce potential time-dependent changes, it is less likely to capture significant alterations in internet use patterns or DLF.

Furthermore, previous research suggested unequal gender distributions between the two groups (Andreassen et al., 2017; Mérelle et al., 2017), which was confirmed in our study. The IGD group consisted of 93.1% males, while in the SNUD group only 14.2% were male. This disparity may be influenced by gender-related factors like gaming preferences and social network usage patterns. Although we controlled for gender, it is important to acknowledge this as a limitation since comparing two groups with essentially opposite gender distributions might affect the generalizability of our findings.

It has to be noted that the DSM-5 approach has been criticized to over-pathologize behavior and includes criteria not reflected in the official ICD-11 approach to diagnose GD or other disorders due to addictive behaviors (Billieux et al., 2019; Saunders, Rumpf, Carragher, & Poznyak, 2025). An approach to use only four criteria that better align with ICD-11 (Király, Potenza, & Demetrovics, 2022) would have resulted in a loss of precision provided by the diagnostic interview and an uncertainty which cut-off to use. Moreover, it is rather unlikely that this would have had an impact on the general findings of our study.

Future research

Research should continue to investigate the increasing problem of addictive internet use. The present study highlights the importance of researching SNUD, which lacks formal diagnosis status. Future studies should look at their unique characteristics and behaviour, to deepen our understanding and facilitate comparisons with other IUDs. Also, research should aim for more balanced gender distributions in IGD and SNUD groups, as well as in other IUDs with uneven gender representation. This would aid in isolating unique disorder characteristics and clarifying differences between IGD and SNUD while reducing potential gender-related confounding variables. When comparing different IUDs, it is crucial to employ large samples, since effects for differences might be relatively small. In general, more precision and consistency in use of terminology is needed (Rumpf et al., 2021). This would facilitate further research and comparability between literature. Research should continue to compare gaming and SNS use, since it can be expected that, even though impairment might be similar, the quality or the nature of the addiction is different, for instance with gamers playing for a long time straight and SNS being opened over and over again during the day. Further internet-related disorders should be investigated to determine if observed impairments are unique or common across various forms of IUDs. This broader investigation may encompass online shopping, pornography consumption, and other emerging issues (Brand et al., 2020; Rumpf et al., 2021; Wegmann et al., 2022) studying them comparatively and as independent disorders. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine long-term effects of addictive internet use, especially in the context of SNS. This would clarify whether impairments persist or evolve over time and assess the effectiveness of interventions and treatments.

Conclusion

The results must be regarded as only preliminary due to the limitations described above. In this sample, no statistically significant differences emerged between students with IGD and SNUD with respect to DLF impairments. Further research involving larger samples is necessary to investigate impairment differences among certain IUDs and to provide evidence for considering SNUD a behavioral addiction. Our preliminary findings contribute to research on SNUD and highlight the need for further studies investigating clinical symptoms of SNUD. Further research is needed to strengthen the evidence base for SNUD within frameworks such as DSM-5 or ICD-11 (Brand et al., 2020; Montag, Demetrovics, et al., 2024). Initial results suggest that it would be helpful to strive for a standardized framework for the classification of IUDs in clinical practice (Brand et al., 2020). Future research should focus on understanding and differentiating the different manifestations of IUDs and their impact on DLF. Finally, our study highlights the importance of raising awareness of the potential risks of addictive Internet use in general, regardless of specific online activities.
