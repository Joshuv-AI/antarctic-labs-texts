Intramural Hematoma of the Esophagus: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Ian Seng Lam, Hoi Ip Leong, Tam Fei Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntramural esophageal hematoma is a rare differential diagnosis for retrosternal pain with dysphagia or hematemesis, especially in patients on antiplatelets. Computed tomography excludes aortic dissection and confirms the diagnosis. Temporary aspirin withdrawal and conservative management led to complete resolution by Day 3, with no recurrence at 6‐month follow‐up, avoiding unnecessary intervention.Keywords: cardiothoracic surgery, critical care medicine, emergency medicine, gastroenterology

Introduction

Intramural hematoma of the esophagus (IHE) is a rare but clinically significant condition characterized by blood accumulation within the esophageal wall, representing a distinct form of acute mucosal injury [1, 2, 3, 4]. The classic clinical triad includes acute retrosternal pain, sudden dysphagia or odynophagia, and mild hematemesis [1, 2, 3, 4].

IHE may be secondary to trauma, foreign body ingestion, endoscopic intervention, severe vomiting, ingestion of caustic substances, or aortoesophageal fistula. Spontaneous cases have been associated with the use of anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, and underlying coagulopathy [1, 2, 4]. It is important to distinguish IHE from other acute cardiovascular conditions such as acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, and pulmonary embolism.

Several imaging modalities are available for diagnosis, including endoscopy, contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT), endoscopic ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [1]. Endoscopy typically reveals a dark red to purple, smooth, non‐pulsatile submucosal mass [2].

We report the case of a 45‐year‐old man who presented with acute retrosternal pain and hematemesis that occurred while eating at a restaurant. A contrast‐enhanced CT scan of the thorax revealed a soft tissue mass in the posterior mediastinum. The diagnosis was subsequently confirmed by gastroscopy, which demonstrated a longitudinal submucosal hematoma extending from 25 cm distal to the incisors to the lesser curvature side of the gastric cardia.

Case History/Examination

A 45‐year‐old man with a history of hypertension and coronary artery disease, taking aspirin, amlodipine, and atorvastatin, presented to the emergency department with sudden‐onset retrosternal pain and hematemesis that occurred while he was eating. He denied fever, cough, hemoptysis, dyspnea, palpitations, syncope, abdominal pain, dysphagia, foreign body sensation, or melena. Importantly, he also reported no vomiting or retching prior to symptom onset, and he had not undergone any recent instrumental examinations, such as endoscopy or nasogastric intubation. On physical examination, his blood pressure was 168/95 mmHg; other vital signs were normal. Abdominal examination revealed no tenderness or rebound tenderness. Cardiovascular examination was unremarkable, and digital rectal examination showed no melena. Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm without ST‐T changes. Initial laboratory tests, including complete blood count (hemoglobin, 15.8 g/dL; platelet count, 191 × 109/L), electrolytes, liver and renal function tests, cardiac enzymes, and coagulation profile (international normalized ratio, 0.9; activated partial thromboplastin time, 30.5 s; prothrombin time, 11.2 s), were all within normal limits.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Based on the initial presentation, differential diagnoses included aortic dissection, acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism, Boerhaave syndrome (esophageal rupture), bleeding peptic ulcer disease, and Mallory‐Weiss syndrome.

A contrast‐enhanced CT scan of the thorax revealed no aortic dissection or esophageal rupture, a non‐enhancing soft tissue mass in the posterior mediastinal space, extending from the T3 level along the esophagus to the lesser curvature of the stomach, causing extrinsic compression of the esophageal lumen. No mediastinal lymphadenopathy or aortic abnormality was noted. These findings were suggestive of an intramural esophageal hematoma (Figures 1 and 2).

A strip of slightly high‐density shadow is seen on the esophageal lumen in contrast‐enhanced thoracic CT (yellow and blue arrows).

While in the emergency department, after performing the CT scan, the patient experienced two episodes of hematemesis (approximately 100 mL fresh blood) and was intubated for airway protection. After excluding aortic dissection and confirming an intact esophageal wall, urgent upper endoscopy was subsequently performed due to the patient recurrent hematemesis, which demonstrated a longitudinal submucosal hematoma extending from 25 cm distal to the incisors to the lesser curvature side of the gastric cardia, confirming the diagnosis of intramural hematoma of the esophagus (Figure 3). On Day 1, he was admitted to the ICU for supportive care, including stopping the aspirin, administering intravenous proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), analgesia, and mechanical ventilation. The thoracic surgery service was consulted and recommended conservative management with close monitoring. No surgical or endoscopic intervention was required.

Gastroscopy: A longitudinal submucosal hematoma extending from the point 25 cm distal to the incisors to the lesser curvature side of the gastric cardia (white arrow); blood clots were noted in the gastric cavity.

Conclusion and Results

The patient's symptoms improved rapidly. A repeat contrast‐enhanced CT scan on Day 3 demonstrated complete resolution of the previously noted posterior mediastinal soft tissue mass (Figure 4), he was successfully extubated on the same day. On Day 4, he was transferred to the general ward and resumed an oral liquid diet and aspirin. Finally, he was discharged on Day 6, as he remained asymptomatic and showed no signs of anemia progressive on blood tests (hemoglobin, 15.2 g/dL).

Follow‐up contrast‐enhanced CT of the neck and chest demonstrated complete resolution of the previously noted posterior mediastinal soft tissue mass. A nasogastric tube was observed in situ (blue arrow).

During the 6‐month follow‐up in the outpatient department, the symptoms did not recur. This case highlights that prompt CT imaging is crucial for accurate diagnosis and exclusion of vascular emergencies, and that conservative management leads to excellent outcomes in esophageal intramural hematoma.

Discussion

IHE, also referred to as dissecting intramural hematoma of the esophagus, is a rare but clinically significant condition characterized by blood accumulation within the esophageal wall [1, 2, 3, 4]. A review of 174 reported cases indicates that patients are typically middle‐aged (mean age 58.8 years), with a bimodal age distribution peaking at 45 and 70 years. The condition demonstrates a slight female predominance [5].

The clinical presentation of IHE commonly includes acute retrosternal pain, dysphagia, and hematemesis. However, a review of 91 cases found that only 35% of patients present with the classic triad of symptoms; 80% present with at least two of these features [5, 6]. In patients presenting with retrosternal pain, critical differential diagnoses include acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, and pulmonary embolism.

IHE is classified as spontaneous or secondary. Proposed mechanisms for spontaneous IHE include sudden intraluminal pressure changes, such as increased intra‐abdominal pressure from vomiting, and underlying coagulation disorders. Antiplatelet therapy is also considered a risk factor [1, 2]. Secondary IHE is thought to result from acute esophageal injury, with a mechanism similar to that of the Mallory‐Weiss tear and Boerhaave syndrome, potentially representing an intermediate stage of mural injury. Consequently, the differential diagnosis includes these conditions [1, 2, 3, 7, 8].

Several modalities aid in the diagnosis of IHE. Contrast‐enhanced CT is the first‐line investigation, as it can directly visualize the hematoma, typically demonstrating concentric or eccentric thickening of the esophageal wall. Oral contrast administration is recommended when perforation is suspected. Endoscopy may reveal a bluish submucosal swelling, with or without a mucosal tear; however, it should be deferred until the integrity of the esophageal wall is confirmed to avoid iatrogenic perforation [1, 8]. Other useful modalities include endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), which is superior to conventional endoscopy for evaluating submucosal lesions and adjacent structures, and MRI, which can help distinguish IHE from other mediastinal pathologies by showing intermediate signal intensity on T1‐ and T2‐weighted images.

Most patients with IHE recover with conservative management, which includes nil per os (NPO), intravenous fluids, acid suppression with PPIs, and correction of any coagulopathy [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Antibiotics are not routinely indicated unless there is evidence of infection within the submucosal layer [3]. Symptoms typically resolve within 2 weeks with this approach [5]. Repeat CT imaging is useful for monitoring hematoma progression or resolution. Oral intake can be gradually reintroduced, starting with liquids and advancing to a soft diet as tolerated [1, 3].

Although rare, complications of IHE can be severe, including esophageal obstruction and major bleeding [9]. Esophageal perforation, while uncommon, is a life‐threatening complication.

For IHE that does not resolve spontaneously with conservative treatment, endoscopic incision of the overlying mucosal bridge has been described as a therapeutic option [3]. Successful endoscopic management has also been reported in cases of esophageal obstruction [10].

Therapeutic angiography with transarterial embolization (TAE) may be required for recurrent massive hematemesis [1, 9]. A recent meta‐analysis comparing TAE with surgery for refractory non‐variceal upper gastrointestinal bleeding reported that TAE was associated with significantly higher rebleeding rates but fewer complications and comparable mortality, supporting its use as a safe first‐line option, particularly in poor surgical candidates [11]. The utility of TAE has also been demonstrated in refractory Mallory‐Weiss syndrome and in a reported case of recurrent massive bleeding from IHE [9, 12, 13]. Surgery is reserved for patients with massive hemorrhage and hemodynamic instability, severe esophageal obstruction, or esophageal perforation [1, 9, 14, 15, 16].

IHE is a rare but clinically important condition that should be considered in the differential diagnosis of acute retrosternal pain with hematemesis, particularly in patients with risk factors such as antiplatelet therapy or underlying coagulopathy. This case illustrates the diagnostic challenge posed by IHE, as its presenting symptoms can closely mimic life‐threatening conditions such as aortic dissection, acute coronary syndrome, and pulmonary embolism. Contrast‐enhanced CT serves as a valuable first‐line imaging modality, enabling both the identification of IHE and the assessment of esophageal integrity, which is essential for guiding subsequent management. In this case, the decision to perform endoscopy required caution and was guided by the differential diagnosis. The patient initially presented with retrosternal pain and hematemesis. Electrocardiography and contrast‐enhanced CT of the thorax excluded acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, and esophageal rupture, revealing instead a non‐enhancing soft tissue mass in the posterior mediastinal space, suggestive of an intramural esophageal hematoma. At that time, his hemoglobin level was 15.8 g/dL and vital signs were stable. However, due to recurrent hematemesis, endotracheal intubation was performed for airway protection. After confirming esophageal wall integrity on CT, endoscopy could be safely performed to confirm the diagnosis of IHE and exclude peptic ulcer disease. This case also demonstrates that in hemodynamically stable patients without evidence of perforation or uncontrolled gastrointestinal bleeding, conservative management including NPO, intravenous fluids, acid suppression, and close monitoring leads to excellent outcomes. Surgical or radiological intervention should be reserved for patients with massive gastrointestinal hemorrhage, hemodynamic instability, severe esophageal obstruction, or esophageal perforation.

Early recognition of IHE, a systematic diagnostic approach, and multidisciplinary collaboration between emergency physicians, gastroenterologists, radiologists, and surgeons are key to avoiding unnecessary interventions and ensuring optimal patient recovery.
