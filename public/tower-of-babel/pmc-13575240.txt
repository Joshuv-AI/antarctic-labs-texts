Column switching liquid chromatography dual mass spectrometry system for simultaneous untargeted metabolomics and targeted exposomics
Authors: Yuting Wang, Daidi Hou, Yuan Yuan, Pengwei Guan, Tiantian Chen, Xingyu Guo, Xin You, Xianzhe Shi, Xiaolin Wang, Qi Wang, Xinyu Liu, Guowang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exposome-wide association studies (ExWAS) require the detection of metabolites and exposures with diverse chemical properties across wide concentration ranges, a task that typically demands multiple analytical methods. To address this challenge, we develop an integrated column-switching two-dimensional liquid chromatography-dual mass spectrometry (2DLC-dual-MS) system. This system employs a 2DLC setup to sequentially separate polar and non-polar compounds with log P ranging from −8 to 15. The separated fractions are directed via a three-way valve to a high-resolution MS (HRMS) and a triple quadrupole MS (TQMS), enabling simultaneous untargeted metabolome analysis and targeted quantification of 601 exposures. The method is particularly suited for the concurrent analysis of metabolome and exposome in human blood, where their concentrations typically differ by 2–3 orders of magnitude. In a demonstration application on lung adenocarcinoma ExWAS, the system exhibits good stability over more than 300 consecutive injections for both metabolome and exposome analysis, confirming its robustness for ExWAS applications.

Introduction

As a complement to the genome, the exposome refers to the whole range of exposures from all sources reaching the human being, including the environment, diet, behavior, and endogenous processes1. Exposomics studies have shown that environmental exposures are key contributors to most chronic noncommunicable diseases, such as cancer2,3, immune-mediated disease4, cardiometabolic diseases5, reproductive health issues6, metabolic syndrome7,8, and aging-related outcomes9. Metabolomics is the comprehensive identification and quantitative analysis of all metabolites in a biological system10, aiming to capture changes in endogenous metabolites driven by genetic regulation or external environmental stimulation. In this context, exposome-wide association studies (ExWAS), which systematically investigate the associations between multiple environmental exposures and human health or disease outcomes and are increasingly integrated with multi-omics approaches such as metabolomics, have garnered increasing attention in recent years7,11–14. Despite some overlap between metabolomics and exposomics in chemical space (molecular weights ≤ 1500 Da)15,16 and the blurring of analytical boundaries through high‑coverage high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS)‑based approaches, the simultaneous and effective measurement of the metabolome and exposome—with their diverse chemical properties and wide concentration ranges in human samples—remains challenging17.

Untargeted methods are widely used in metabolomics research18–20, while exposomics studies often rely on targeted methods tailored to specific exposure groups—such as insecticides21, environmental phenols22, plasticizers23, and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs)24—which require high-sensitivity biomonitoring and accurate quantification25. Most of the current ExWAS studies analyze endogenous metabolites and exogenous chemical exposures by using multiple independent analytical methods26,27. This approach leads to extended experimental durations, reduced analytical efficiency, and increased sample consumption. Recently, more studies have attempted to analyze endogenous metabolome and exogenous exposome simultaneously in a single run based on HRMS28,29. However, this strategy often sacrifices quantification accuracy for exposure detection30, as exposure concentrations are 2–3 orders of magnitude lower than metabolites in human15,31. Although the ZenoTOF 7600 MS enables simultaneous untargeted metabolomics (full‑scan acquisition) and targeted exposomics (MRM) in a single injection32, the sensitivity improvement using TOF‑based MRM mode is limited. Our previous work showed that only 22% of chemical exposures exhibited >10‑fold higher detection sensitivity, with a further 32% showing only 3–10‑fold improvement33, which remains insufficient to monitor and quantify pg-level human exposures. Thus, achieving simultaneous high-coverage untargeted metabolome analysis and highly sensitive exposome quantification remains elusive with a single MS.

Human metabolome and exposome are highly complex, encompassing compounds of widely differing polarities17. Single-column-based one-dimensional liquid chromatography (1DLC) analysis, whether hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC)- or reversed-phase (RP) LC-based, cannot adequately separate compounds that elute near dead time. A key trend for expanding analytical coverage is the combination of complementary chromatographic techniques, such as integrating gas chromatography (GC) with LC34,35, or coupling RPLC with HILIC21,36. Flasch et al. described a dual-column approach in which RPLC and HILIC columns are rapidly operated in parallel to separate polar and non-polar compounds37, offering a new strategy for high-coverage small-molecules analysis, albeit with substantial redundant data. A core advantage of two-dimensional (2D) LC methods is their broader separation window, enabling higher coverage and selectivity by combining different separation mechanisms38. HILIC-RPLC column-switching 2DLC methods have been developed and applied to metabolomics and environmental exposure analysis39–41. This mode avoids analyte elution at dead time and effectively expands the log P range of separable small molecules. To our knowledge, no column-switching 2DLC approach has been reported for ExWAS. Therefore, achieving simultaneous high-coverage, high-sensitivity analysis of endogenous metabolites and exogenous chemical exposures with vastly different physicochemical properties and concentrations in a single run remains a challenge42.

In this work, we design and develop an online column-switching 2DLC-dual MS system comprising an HRMS and a triple quadrupole (TQ) MS for simultaneous untargeted metabolomics and precise quantification of trace-level exposures across a broad polarity range in a single injection−a task that traditionally requires four independent analyses (1D-HILIC-HRMS, 1D-RPLC-HRMS, 1D-HILIC-TQMS, 1D-RPLC-TQMS). HILIC and RPLC are employed sequentially for the separation of polar and non-polar/weakly polar compounds. The 2DLC effluent is then stably split via a three‑way valve, with one stream directed to the HRMS for untargeted metabolomics and the other to the TQMS for targeted exposomics, enabling relative quantification of metabolites and accurate quantification of 601 chemical exposures. The performance of this method is verified by evaluating analytical coverage across three representative biological matrices (blood, tissue, and urine) and conducting comprehensive method validation. Finally, the method's applicability is demonstrated through an ExWAS-based association analysis of 276 lung adenocarcinoma (LA)-related serum samples. Results

Design and optimization of the 2DLC system for high-coverage analysis

In this study, an online column-switching 2DLC system was developed for the comprehensive analysis of polar and non-polar/weakly polar compounds in a single injection, improving coverage and reducing redundant or dead-time signals. The system integrates two complementary chromatographic modes: an amine‑column‑based HILIC system for separating polar compounds and a C18‑column‑based RP system for separating weakly polar and non‑polar compounds, thereby extending coverage across a broad log P range. HILIC was selected as the first-dimension and RPLC as the second-dimension/trapping column because RP columns generally provide higher column efficiency and better tolerance to mobile-phase changes, making this HILIC–RPLC configuration more suitable for solvent replacement on the trapping column and for maintaining good peak shape and separation performance. Detailed operations are as follows:

First, during the pre-separation step, weakly polar/non-polar compounds eluting near dead time on the HILIC column are initially separated from polar compounds and transferred to a trapping column for capture after dilution with water (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Polar compounds are retained on the HILIC column for subsequent separation. Isocratic elution is maintained on both the HILIC and trapping columns during pre-separation, ensuring process stability and controllability. After polar compounds are separated on HILIC (Supplementary Fig. 1b), the trapped compounds are backflushed into the RP column for separation (Supplementary Fig. 1c). The key to this workflow is to ensure that weakly polar/non-polar fractions are effectively separated from polar fractions on the HILIC column and retained on the RP trapping column. Using log P as a practical, unified polarity descriptor, we optimized the cut boundary for complementary HILIC and RPLC separations.

Optimization of pre-separation cutting boundary and time

The retention and separation performance of the HILIC and RPLC systems was evaluated using 221 representative standards (log P: −6 to 8). The results indicate that HILIC is suitable for separating polar compounds with log P < 2 (Supplementary Fig. 2a), while RPLC is more suitable for separating weakly polar and non-polar compounds with log P > 0 (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Thus, the log P range of the HILIC-RPLC fraction boundary was narrowed to 0–2. The separation performance of each standard on HILIC and RPLC is summarized in Fig. 2a using three criteria of extracted ion chromatograms (EICs): (i) MS response with peak intensity > 1e5, (ii) absence of multiple or broad peaks (full width at half maximum < 0.15 min), (iii) retention factor > 1. Different colors indicate whether all three criteria (green), two (yellow), one (red) or none (black) were met, corresponding to classifications of good, acceptable, unacceptable, and undetected, respectively. Specifically, polar compounds with log P from −6 to 1 were separated better on HILIC, while non-polar compounds with log P from 1 to 8 were well separated on RPLC. This was particularly evident for carnitines (Cars) with different carbon chain lengths: those with eight or more carbons are highly susceptible to solvent effects on HILIC, resulting in multiple peaks and necessitating transfer to RPLC. Finally, Car C8:0 (log P = 1.1) was selected as the fraction-cutting boundary. When Car C8:0 is fully eluted from HILIC, pre-separation stops, and the valve is switched to the polar compound separation position. Using Car C8:0 as the boundary, the effect of water phase proportion in HILIC-based pre-separation on fraction-cutting performance was evaluated over the range of 3–15% (Fig. 2b). A low water phase proportion improved separation between non-polar/weakly polar and polar components, but prolonged pre-separation time, potentially increasing the loss of compounds eluting from the trapping column to waste. Conversely, a high-water phase proportion accelerated pre-separation but caused more overlap, leading incompletely separated peaks to split into two parts. Ultimately, isocratic elution with 10% water phase and 90% organic phase was used for pre-separation on HILIC. Under this condition, the boundary compound Car C8:0 eluted from the HILIC at 3.2 min, marking the end of pre-separation. Thus, Car C8:0 and other weakly polar and non-polar compounds eluting by 3.2 min were transferred to RPLC, while Car C6:0 and other polar compounds remained on HILIC, with data acquisition starting at 3.2 min (Fig. 2c, d). A mixed standard solution of Car C6:0 and Car C8:0 was used to monitor fraction-cutting stability throughout the experiment.

Optimization of trapping column

During pre-separation, weakly polar and non-polar components eluted with HILIC mobile phase (0.15 mL/min) near HILIC dead time entered the trapping column after mixing with diluent (pure water, 1 mL/min). To ensure maximal capture of compounds from the HILIC fraction, especially the weakly polar compounds, the retention capacity of four potential trapping columns with different stationary phases and column lengths was simultaneously evaluated using 136 weakly polar compounds (log P from 0 to 2): PFP (3 µm, 2.1 ⅹ 50 mm, Supelco), XBridge BEH C18 (5 µm, 2.1 ⅹ 30 mm, Waters), Acquity HSS T3 (1.8 µm, 2.1 ⅹ 5 mm, Waters) and Acquity BEH C8 (1.7 µm, 2.1 ⅹ 5 mm, Waters). Compared with the shorter T3 and C8 columns, almost no weakly polar compounds were eluted from the PFP and C18 columns to waste before the fraction-cutting time, indicating their stronger retention capabilities for these compounds (Fig. 2e, f). However, using the PFP column as a trapping column tended to increase column pressure and broaden chromatographic peaks. Therefore, among the available trapping columns in this study the 3-cm C18 column was found to have the best overall performance of retention, analyte coverage and chromatographic behavior and was therefore selected to retain weakly polar and non-polar compounds from the HILIC pre-separation.

Separation performance

The online column-switching 2DLC system was established under optimized conditions, including pre-separation eluent, fraction cutting and trapping column. By switching valve positions and applying gradient elution (Supplementary Fig. 1d), excellent compound separation with different polarities was achieved in a single analysis by the integration of HILIC and C18 systems, with no compounds eluting at dead time (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Compounds eluted near dead time in the 1DLC-HILIC analysis were transferred to the RPLC column of the 2DLC system and detected after 11 min (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Similarly, unretained compounds in 1DLC-RP system were retained and separated between 3.2 and 10 min on the HILIC column of the 2DLC system (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Thus, the analytical coverage for metabolites and exposures across a wide log P range was extended using this column-switching 2DLC system.

Establishment and evaluation of a simultaneous untargeted metabolomics and targeted exposomics method based on 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS system

Analytical performance, including limit of detection (LOD), linearity, lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ), upper limit of quantitation (ULOQ), precision, extraction recovery, accuracy, and matrix effect, was evaluated for the column-switching 2DLC-dual MS system with spiked serum samples. For untargeted metabolomics validation in human serum, 21 representative isotope-labeled metabolite standards (Supplementary Data 5) encompassing different metabolite classes and covering a range of retention times across the elution gradient were used. For targeted exposomics validation, all 601 exposures were tested in fetal bovine serum, which served as a blank human serum substitute (Supplementary Data 6). Quantitative performance including linearity and accuracy was further evaluated for the 242 chemical exposures observed in actual samples (Supplementary Data 7). Overall, except for 15 exposures excluded from the quantitative scope of the method due to lower quantitative accuracy or stronger matrix effects, acceptable analytical performance was achieved for both untargeted metabolomics and targeted exposomics analyses (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5), with details provided in Supplementary Note 1 and 2.

To further evaluate the quantitative performance of the targeted exposomics workflow, the method characterization results were compared with those reported for conventional environmental biomonitoring assays for PFAS and bisphenols in serum/plasma, which typically employ analyte-specific isotope-labeled internal standards (ISs) (Supplementary Data 8). Although direct comparison of LOQs across studies is limited by differences in sample preparation, enrichment and instrumentation, the inter- and intra-precision observed were generally within the range reported for biomonitoring assays. However, a little greater uncertainty was observed in accuracy and matrix-effect correction because this high-coverage method was based primarily on surrogate ISs rather than analyte-specific isotope-labeled ISs. The surrogate IS-based quantitation was associated with slightly lower accuracy, broader variation across the linear range (typically within ±20% versus ±10%), and weaker correction of matrix effects for several analytes, including 1H,1H,2H,2H-Perfluorodecanesulfonic acid (8:2 FTS), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), perfluoro-n-pentanoic acid (PFPeA) and bisphenol M (Supplementary Data 9). Despite these limitations, this trade-off between chemical coverage and quantitative rigor is still acceptable for a high coverage exposomics screening method.

Comparison of annotation coverage between 2DLC-HRMS and 1DLC-HRMS methods in three biological matrices

Untargeted metabolomics is traditionally performed on 1DLC-HRMS methods using a HILIC43 or RP44,45 column. To evaluate the capability of the developed 2DLC method for high-coverage untargeted metabolomics analysis, annotation numbers in serum, tissue, and urine were compared between the 2DLC-HRMS method and 1DLC-HRMS methods (HILIC and RPLC). This comparison was made under the respective optimized conditions of each workflow and therefore reflects overall analytical performance. Only compounds with a signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) > 30 and annotated at levels 1 and 2 were retained46.

As shown in Fig. 4a, approximately 1000 or more compounds were annotated in each matrix, with the tissue displaying the richest profile (nearly 1300 compounds). Across all matrices, the 2DLC-HRMS method annotated more compounds at both level 1 and level 2 than the two 1DLC-HRMS methods, covering a log P range from −8 to 15 without dead-time elution. In serum, 986 compounds (levels 1 and 2) were annotated by the 2DLC-HRMS method, compared with 652 by 1DLC-RPLC-HRMS and 483 by 1DLC-HILIC-HRMS, representing a 51 to 104% increase in annotation coverage. A total of 589 compounds were co-annotated in both 1DLC-RPLC-HRMS and 2DLC-HRMS methods, and 436 in both 1DLC-HILIC-HRMS and 2DLC-HRMS methods (Fig. 4b). More than 90% of compounds that cannot be analyzed by 1DLC are detectable with the 2DLC method. Undetectable polar compounds (mainly with log P < 2) in 1DLC-RPLC method were detected before 9.4 min in the HILIC module of the 2DLC method, and undetectable weakly polar and non-polar compounds in 1DLC-HILIC method (mainly with log P > 0) were detected after 9.4 min in the RPLC module of the 2DLC method (Fig. 4c). In tissue, 1204, 689, and 663 compounds were annotated by 2DLC, 1DLC-RPLC, and 1DLC-HILIC methods, respectively, with 2DLC method increasing annotation coverage by 75 to 82% compared to 1DLC methods (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Similar improvement was observed in urine, with 2DLC increasing annotation coverage by 54–92%(Supplementary Fig. 6). Approximately 1% to 6% of compounds were not detected in 2DLC analysis, mainly due to slight peak broadening (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 6e, g) and insufficient retention of some weakly polar compounds on both the HILIC and trapping columns (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 6f, h). Despite these limitations, the detection coverage of the 2DLC-HRMS system still far exceeds that of individual 1DLC-HRMS systems. Furthermore, the column-switching strategy combines the complementary advantages of HILIC and RPLC, avoiding the dead-time elution in 1DLC and preventing data redundancy inherent in dual‑1DLC parallel setups. It should be emphasized that, in the above comparison of method performance, differences in column chemistry, mobile phases, and gradient programs may influence analyte retention, separation selectivity, and final coverage. For example, the 1DLC-RPLC method employed a T3 column, which is better suited to practical applications and more favorable for the retention of relatively hydrophilic compounds. Thus, the 459–652 annotated compounds were achieved on a 1DLC-RPLC method with a T3 column under conditions relatively favorable to this column system. If the 1DLC were forced to use the same RP column and mobile-phase conditions as 2DLC, the performance of 1DLC would likely be further compromised. Therefore, the comparison in this study is in fact more rigorous and more practically relevant than a comparison focused solely on the 2D configuration alone. Detailed discussion is given in Supplementary Note3.

Comparison of analytical sensitivity for metabolites and exposures between TQMS and HRMS in the 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS system

The detection sensitivity of this 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS system was evaluated by comparing the LODs of 293 metabolites and 568 targeted chemical exposures obtained under the individually optimized conditions of the TQMS and HRMS modules. We found that in HRMS mode, the LODs of the metabolites and exposures ranged from 10⁻³ to 1 μg/mL and 10-4 to 10-1 μg/mL, respectively (Fig. 4F). In the TQMS mode, the LODs of exposures analyzed in the targeted mode ranged from 10-5 to 10-3 μg/mL, 1 to 2 orders of magnitude lower than those obtained with HRMS method. The concentration of exposures in blood is generally 2–3 orders of magnitude lower than that of metabolites31, and detection by TQMS is 1–2 orders of magnitude more sensitive than by HRMS, constructing a system in which metabolites are analyzed by HRMS and exposures by TQMS enables similar LODs for both classes, facilitating simultaneous analysis of the metabolome and exposome. The developed 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS-based method provides a sensitive, customized analytical approach for simultaneous untargeted metabolomics and targeted exposomics, making it particularly suitable for human ExWAS research.

Demonstrative application of the developed 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS method in ExWAS of lung adenocarcinoma

Reliability monitoring of analytical sequence

A total of 276 serum samples from lung adenocarcinoma (LA) patients and controls were analyzed using the developed 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS method to explore the association among metabolic profiling, chemical exposures and LA status. Before instrumental analysis, LA and control samples were randomized using a random-number-based injection sequence to minimize potential bias related to injection order, instrumental drift, and batch effects. Unspiked human serum QCs (MQCs) and human serum QCs spiked with 601 exposures (EQCs) were inserted after every 15 samples to assess data reliability for simultaneous untargeted metabolomics and targeted exposomics (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Annotated metabolites with relative standard deviation (RSD) < 30% in MQCs and EQCs were kept for further analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7b–d). Features in MQCs with an RSD of less than 30% accounted for 92.6% and 95.6% of the total peak area in positive and negative ion modes, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). For MRM transitions of exposures in EQCs, all transitions except four showed RSD < 30% after internal standard correction. Specifically, 94.6% and 99% of MRM transitions had RSD < 15% in positive and negative ion modes, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7d). The stability and reliability of the 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS method for untargeted metabolomics and targeted exposomics were confirmed by continuous QC monitoring throughout the analysis sequence.

In targeted exposomics, 242 exposure features were initially detected. The remaining 218 exposures were therefore retained for further quantitative analysis, of which 205 exposures with signal intensities above the LLOQ were accurately quantified, and 41 of them had a quantitation rate >30% across all samples (Fig. 5b) and were considered as frequently quantified exposures and used for subsequent statistical analyses. Among all exposuree features detected with TQMS (Fig. 5e), only 12.2% with intensities over 200,000 were detected in the HRMS module, 19.8% had low intensities below 200,000 and were insufficient for accurate quantification, while the remaining 68% were undetectable. A further comparison of detectability versus concentration for 8 frequently quantified exposures showed that exposures with concentrations below 0.5 ng/mL were difficult to detect in HRMS-based untargeted analysis (Fig. 5g). In contrast, targeted analysis by the TQMS module enabled quantification of exposures at concentrations 2–3 orders of magnitude lower than the LODs achieved by untargeted analysis, underscoring the necessity of TQMS-based targeted methods for high-sensitivity exposure quantification.

Demonstrative ExWAS-based association analysis related to LA

To demonstrate the applicability of the 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS method for ExWAS, a demonstrative ExWAS-based association analysis was performed to explore preliminary relationships among chemical exposures, metabolic alterations, and LA status based on relative concentrations of 853 metabolites and absolute concentrations of 41 frequently quantified exposures from 276 serum samples. The analysis comprised three key components: identifying LA status-associated metabolites through metabolome‑wide association analysis, screening exposures associated with LA status through exposome‑wide association analysis, and applying a meet-in-the-middle approach together with statistical mediation analysis to explore candidate metabolic markers and related pathways potentially involved in the observed exposure–LA associations. These exploratory results for hypothesis generation still require further validation in independent epidemiological cohorts and mechanistic studies.

(i) Metabolome-wide association analysis

Through multivariate analysis and non-parametric testing, 373 metabolites were found to differ between the LA and control groups (p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.1, two-sided, Supplementary Fig. 9a–c), with enrichment in amino acid-related pathways (Supplementary Fig. 9d). 92 differential metabolites with log2 fold change < −0.5 or >0.5 were further defined (Supplementary Data 12), and 86 metabolites associated with LA status were identified (p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.1, two-sided) via binary logistic regression (BLR) adjusted for sex, age, and BMI. Collectively, these metabolic alterations were characterized by altered peptide accumulation, bile acid metabolism, and lipid metabolism, including decreased carnitines and fatty acids, elevated eicosanoids, lyso-phosphatidylcholines and lyso-phosphatidylethanolamines. These patterns are consistent with metabolic alterations previously reported in LA, including changes in nutrient utilization, metabolic pathway rewiring, and antioxidant defense-related processes48–52. Detailed discussion is given in Supplementary Note 4.

(ii) Exposure association analysis

The exposure association analysis was carried out based on the 41 frequently quantified exposures. For exposures detected at concentrations below the LOQ, their concentrations were set as LOQ/√2. All regression models were adjusted for sex, age, and BMI. Twelve exposures were identified (p < 0.05, FDR < 0.1, two-sided) as significantly different between the control and LA groups (Fig. 6a), of which 9 were further associated with the odds of LA in BLR analysis (p < 0.05, FDR < 0.1, two-sided). Among these, lufenuron, furmecyclox, and 4,4’-dichlorobenzophenone (4,4’-DCBP) were negatively associated with the odds of LA, whereas mephosfolan, 4,6-dinitro-o-cresol, bisphenol S (BPS), bisphenol A (BPA), perfluoroheptanoic acid (PFHpA), and 8:2 FTS showed positive associations (Fig. 6b). Several of these associations are consistent with previously reported potential toxicological evidence, including BPA-associated pulmonary fibrosis and metastasis, PFAS-mediated activation of integrin/FAK-PI3K-Akt signaling, and mitochondrial uncoupling by 4,6-Dinitro-o-cresol53–57. Stratified analyses were further conducted by sex and age across subgroups. Higher OR estimates for mephosfolan and BPA were observed among females (Fig. 6c), and BPA has been reported to exhibit endocrine-disrupting activity58. Higher OR estimates for BPA and BPS were also observed in individuals aged ≤55 years (Fig. 6d), potentially reflecting age-related differences in exposure patterns. In contrast, PFASs showed higher OR estimates in individuals older than 55 years, which may be partly attributed to age-dependent accumulation or slower elimination in the elderly59,60. (iii) Meet-in-the-middle and statistical mediation analyses

To investigate metabolic changes linked to the observed associations between exposures and LA, a meet-in-the-middle approach was applied to exposures positively associated with the odds of LA, aiming to identify candidate metabolic markers related to both exposure levels and LA status and to preliminarily assess their involvement in these associations. After multiple linear regression (MLR) analysis (p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.1, two-sided), 34 candidate metabolic markers were obtained from differential fatty acids (FAs), bile acids (BAs) and peptides (Fig. 6e). Peptides and eicosanoids were positively associated with mephosfolan, 4,6-Dinitro-o-cresol, and BPA, whereas several fatty acids were additionally associated with BPA. Notably, 11 peptides were positively correlated with BPA in the full cohort, while only four associated peptides were observed for BPS, with all correlation coefficients below 0.2. However, in the age ≤55 subgroup, the number of BPS-associated peptides increased to 12, and overlapping BPA- and BPS-related metabolic patterns were observed across peptides, eicosanoids, and fatty acids, suggesting greater convergence of their metabolic perturbations among younger participants. Additionally, most FAs were associated with PFHpA and 8:2 FTS. Mediated proportions were then calculated using statistical mediation analyses to evaluate the extent to which each candidate metabolic marker may statistically account for the observed chemical exposure–LA association61. Twenty‑eight candidate metabolic markers and their corresponding mediated proportions are summarized in Fig. 6f and Supplementary Data 16 (p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.1, two-sided).

Multiple peptides (Arg-Gln, Arg-Gly, Ile-Arg, Ile-Val, Leu-Leu, Phe-Arg, Pro-Pro, Ser-Arg, Ser-Asn, γ-Glu-Met) were identified as candidate metabolic markers in the BPA–LA association, with mediated proportions ranging from 10% to 28%. Notably, several BPA-associated peptides, including Arg-Gln, Arg-Gly, Ile-Arg, Phe-Arg, and Ser-Arg, contained arginine, suggesting arginine-related peptide metabolism may be involved in the BPA–LA association. Similarly, although only Arg-Tyr-Arg yielded a significant mediated proportion of 25% in the BPS–LA association, arginine-containing peptides were also correlated with BPS in the previous MLR analysis (Fig. 6e), and these correlations were more apparent among participants aged ≤55 years. This aligns with our finding that some LA-associated differential metabolites were enriched in arginine metabolism pathways (Supplementary Fig. 9d), and with recent studies reporting altered arginine metabolism after BPA or BPS exposure in experimental models62,63. Given the reported role of arginine in cancer–immune cell interactions, these results may indicate a potential connection among bisphenol exposure, arginine-containing peptide alterations and immune-metabolic regulation in LA64.

Various peptides, including arginine-containing peptides were also identified as candidate metabolic markers in the mephosfolan–LA association, with mediated proportions ranging from 15% to 46%, consistent with previous reports linking organophosphate pesticide exposure to disturbances in amino acid metabolism and arginine-related pathways65,66. 8:2 FTS, a fluorotelomer sulfonate PFAS, was significantly associated with the odds of LA, with four FAs yielding mediated proportions of 24% to 60%. PFASs have been reported to regulate FA metabolism and synthesis through peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) activation67, providing potential context for these FA-associated findings. Glycoursodeoxycholic acid (GUDCA) was also identified as a candidate metabolic marker potentially involved in the 8:2 FTS LA association, with a mediated proportion of 37%. While PFAS exposure has been linked to perturbed plasma BA levels68, the specific role of BAs in the 8:2 FTS LA association remains unclear.

Discussion

Current ExWAS workflows typically rely on multiple independent analytical methods to characterize the metabolome and exposome separately. In practice, metabolome data are typically obtained by using an untargeted HRMS method, whereas exposome data are obtained using several targeted TQMS methods, for example, separate methods for bisphenols, plasticizers or PFASs, due to low concentrations and different chemical properties. Therefore, several independent measurement techniques have been used to obtain a comprehensively annotated metabolome and accurately quantified exposures, a process that is time-consuming and labor-intensive, thereby hindering the rapid development of ExWAS research to some extent.

In this work, we present an integrated analytical strategy based on the combination of multiple separation modes and complementary detection platforms. On this basis, we developed a column-switching 2DLC-dual MS system for simultaneous analysis of metabolome and exposome. The established system is able to separate small molecules with log P ranging from -8 to 15 by employing tandem HILIC and RPLC in a 2DLC setup. The fractions separated by 2DLC are directed into the dual-MS system (HRMS and TQMS) via a three-way valve. Compared to HRMS, the use of TQMS for detecting the exposures improved sensitivity by 1-2 orders of magnitude. Therefore, by analyzing the metabolome with HRMS and the exposome with TQMS, simultaneous analysis of the untargeted metabolomics and targeted exposomics – with concentration differences spanning 2–3 orders of magnitude – is achieved in a single injection. In contrast, the conventional approach requires at least four independent methods (1D-HILIC-HRMS, 1D-RPLC-HRMS, 1D-HILIC-TQMS, and 1D-RPLC-TQMS). Real serum sample analysis showed that over 800 metabolites were relatively quantified and 601 chemical exposures were absolutely quantified in a single analysis. The 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS method provides an integrated analytical workflow for LC-MS-based ExWAS research, featuring large-scale, high-coverage, and matched sensitivity. Thus, this method demonstrates the value of combining orthogonal separation and different detection modes within a single workflow. A demonstrative application performed in an LA-related cohort enabled exploratory assessment of associations between exogenous chemical exposures and endogenous metabolites directly from a single injection. To fully understand environmental factors associated with complex diseases, it is necessary to monitor a wider diversity of endogenous and exogenous compounds, quantify trace-level exposures, and analyze large population cohorts. Therefore, the improved analytical coverage, sensitivity and efficiency of this method provide technical support for expanding the scale of ExWAS research and generating more potential evidence on environmental disease associations.

The 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS method also represents progress and plays an important role in independent metabolomics and exposomics studies. High-coverage untargeted metabolomics profiling and targeted quantification of key disease biomarkers can be integrated into a single analytical method, improving analytical efficiency. The evolving definition of the exposome has expanded the scope of health research, highlighting the need for a comprehensive, discovery‑oriented approach to identify environmental determinants of human health, rather than multiple piecemeal, hypothesis‑driven approaches; thus, the productivity and integrity of exposomics research programs are further emphasized69. Although the quantification method used for exposures remains a hypothesis-driven, targeted approach, it covers up to 600 common chemical exposures and is customizable for analyzing other polar and non-polar exposures, improving the efficiency and expandability of targeted exposure studies. In the future, as HRMS sensitivity improves, unknown exposures can be further screened and annotated through untargeted analysis and incorporated into studies of environmental effects on health, thereby supporting broader discovery-driven exposome research.

However, the limitations of this method should not be overlooked. First, some moderately polar compounds may be lost (though not too numerous) due to insufficient retention on both the HILIC and trapping columns, and some chromatographic peak broadening was caused by the physical separation between the two MS platforms in this study. Second, because surrogate internal standards were used to maximize chemical coverage rather than analyte-specific isotope-labeled internal standards for each compound, the quantitative performance, including accuracy and correction for matrix effects, may be compromised for some exposures. In addition, although the current QC results support the overall analytical stability and reliability of the method in this large-scale run, the frequency of pooled QC insertion was relatively low for untargeted HRMS analysis compared with the current common practice. These limitations can be addressed in future research by further optimizing the trapping column, system configuration, internal standard strategy, and the frequency of quality control insertion. More importantly, the complexity of the method may not be practical for many laboratories. Nevertheless, the broader value of this work lies in promoting the concept of a multi-mode integration strategy to improve the coverage, sensitivity, and data richness of a single run for combined metabolomics and exposomics analysis, thus providing a general methodological reference for developing simpler and more widely applicable integrated analysis workflows for future high-coverage ExWAS research. With the continuous development of high-resolution and high-sensitivity MS platforms, such as Astral-based system, achieving higher-throughput ExWAS analysis on a single MS platform will become increasingly feasible.

Furthermore, this LA-related study served as both a technical validation and a methodological demonstration of the integrated multi-omics workflow. Preliminary associations were observed among several exposures, metabolites, and LA status, and candidate metabolic markers were explored for their potential involvement in these associations. The analysis showed that LA patients exhibited metabolic characteristics such as peptide accumulation, abnormalities in bile acid and lipid metabolism, and identified nine quantified exposures associated with the odds of LA. Arginine-containing peptides were identified as candidate metabolic markers potentially involved in the BPS–LA and mephosfolan–LA associations, whereas fatty acids may be involved in the 8:2 FTS–LA association. Together, these findings provide a multidimensional, exploratory framework for exposure-metabolite associations related to LA. However, given the relatively modest cohort size (n = 276) and the observational nature of the analysis, these findings should be considered preliminary and exploratory. Larger independent prospective cohort studies and mechanistic studies are needed to further validate the identified exposure, metabolite, and LA associations and clarify their biological relevance.

Methods

Sample collection and preparation

This study complied with all relevant ethical regulations for research involving human participants and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University (Approval No. 128, 2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before sample collection. Participants did not receive financial compensation for participation. Sex information was obtained from clinical records. Sex was considered in the study design and statistical analysis: cases and controls were balanced by sex, sex was included as a covariate in regression models, and sex-stratified analyses were performed where applicable. Gender information was not separately collected.

A total of 276 serum samples were collected from The Second AffiliatedHospital of Dalian Medical University (Dalian, China) between 2022 and 2024, including 138 lung adenocarcinoma patients and 138 healthy controls. Cases and controls were recruited using the same blood collection materials/tubes and harmonized collection and processing protocols. Specifically, the lung adenocarcinoma patients were diagnosed with primary lung adenocarcinoma via pathological examination (e.g., needle biopsy or surgical specimen pathology). Health controls were individuals with no history of lung disease, malignant tumors or other major organ diseases and with normal chest imaging and routine laboratory test results (complete blood count, liver function and kidney function). Clinical information of LA patients is summarized in Supplementary Table 1 (Supplementary Information).

The pretreatment protocol for both untargeted metabolomics and targeted exposomics was as follows: 100 µL serum was pipetted into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube, 300 µL methanol with internal standard (Supplementary Data 1, 2) and 100 µL pure methanol were added to precipitate proteins. After vortexing for 60 s to ensure complete protein precipitation, the mixture was centrifuged at 18,700 g and 4 °C for 15 min. 400 µL supernatant was transferred directly to low-temperature freeze concentrator (JM CV600, Beijing, China) for freeze-drying at vacuum condition (4 °C). Finally, the dried mixture was stored at −80 °C and reconstituted in 50 µL of methanol/water (50:50, v/v) prior to analysis. The injection volume was 5 µL, and each sample was analyzed only once.

Chemicals and reagents

HPLC-grade acetonitrile and methanol were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). Formic acid (HPLC-grade, >98%), ammonium formate and ammonium bicarbonate were from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, Missouri, U.S.A.). Mill-Q water (Millipore, Billerica, MA, U.S.A.) was used to prepare mobile phases. Metabolite standards were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, Alabama, U.S.A.). Chemical exposure standards were purchased from J&K Scientific (Beijing, China), ANPEL Laboratory Technologies (Shanghai, China) and ALTA Scientific (Tianjin, China). All standards ranged in purity from 90% to 99.9%. Isotope-labeled internal standards were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Toronto Research Chemicals (North York, Ontario, Canada), CDN Isotopes (Pointe-Claire, Quebec, Canada), Cambridge Isotope Laboratories (Tewksbury, Massachusetts, U.S.A.), Avanti Polar Lipids, International Laboratory (Beverly, Massachusetts, U.S.A.), CHIRON (Emeryville, California, U.S.A.) and Witega (Görlitz, Saxony, Germany), and summarized in Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Data 2, respectively.

Quality control strategy

For the analysis of 276 serum samples from LA patients and controls, a quality control (QC) scheme was designed using QC samples prepared from a pool of all subject sera. A complete set of QC samples (comprising five items) was inserted after every 15 sample injections to ensure data reliability, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 7a: (i) Unspiked serum QC (MQC) was used to monitor the stability of untargeted metabolomics. (ii) Serum QC spiked with 601 exposures (EQC) was used to verify the reliability of targeted exposome quantification. (iii) A carnitine C8:0/C6:0 mixed standard solution (Car-mix) was used to monitor the fraction-cutting and split stability of the 2DLC-dual-MS system. Unlike endogenous carnitines in MQCs/EQCs, this independent standard avoided potential abnormal interference from serum matrix. Finally, two solvent blanks (methanol/water = 50:50, v/v) were inserted after every 15 samples, EQC and Car-mix to prevent pressure elevation and residue contamination. The injection sequence followed a complete cycle of “MQC → 15 samples→EQC →Car-mix→ 2 solvent blanks “, repeated 18 times in each MS ionization mode.

2DLC instrument configuration and workflow in the 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS system

The column-switching 2DLC system was an upgraded version of our previously reported design, optimized for complementary analysis of both endogenous and exogenous polar and non-polar compounds in complex matrices39. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, the system operates in three valve positions. For position 1 (pre-separation position, Supplementary Fig. 1a), samples are injected and eluted on the HILIC column at the initial mobile phase ratio for preliminary separation of polar and non-polar/weakly polar fractions. Polar compounds are retained on the HILIC column, and unretained weakly polar and non-polar compounds are mixed with water diluent via pump P5 through a mixer and then captured on the trapping column. After pre-separation, the system is switched to position 2 (polar compound separation, Supplementary Fig. 1b). Pump P5 is stopped, and polar compounds are eluted from HILIC column to the MS detector. During positions 1 and 2, the RPLC column is continuously equilibrated. Once the polar compound analysis is complete, the system is switched to position 3 (weakly polar and non-polar compound separation, Supplementary Fig. 1c). Weakly polar and non-polar compounds captured on the trapping column are backflushed and transferred to the RPLC column for further separation, while the HILIC column is constantly equilibrated. Finally, all valves are returned to their initial positions to prepare the trapping column for the next analysis.

The column-switching 2DLC system was equipped with an SIL-30A autosampler (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), a CTO-30A column oven (Shimadzu), five solvent delivery pumps, pump P1 to P4 were LC-30AD (Shimadzu) and pump P5 was LC-20AD (Shimadzu), three different columns, two electronically controlled 6-port2-position valves (Valco, Houston, USA), and two CBM-20A lite system controllers (Shimadzu). XBridge BEH C18 (5 µm, 2.1 ⅹ 30 mm), Acquity BEH Amide (1.7 µm, 2.1 ⅹ 100 mm) and Acquity BEH C18 (1.7 µm, 2.1 ⅹ 100 mm) column were used as the trapping, HILIC and RPLC columns for unretained compounds capturing, polar compounds separation, and weakly polar/non-polar compounds separation, respectively. Mobile phases A1 and B1 for the HILIC column were water and 5% water in acetonitrile both containing 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.125% formic acid. Mobile phases A2 and B2 of the RPLC column were water and methanol, both containing 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in positive ion mode, and water and 5% water in methanol, both containing 6.5 mM ammonium bicarbonate in negative ion mode. Elution steps for the 2DLC system are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 1d and Supplementary Data 3. The entire analysis process was performed at a column oven temperature of 50 °C, with a total runtime of 25 min.

MS data acquisition

After 2DLC separation, the eluate is split into two streams via a three-way valve, directed to the HRMS module and the TQMS module of the dual-MS system. The dual-MS system comprised a Q Exactive HF System (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, U.S.A.) for untargeted metabolomics and a QTRAPTM 6500+ system (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) for targeted exposome quantification.

On the Q Exactive HF equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, data acquisition was performed by three strategies in both positive and negative ion mode: (1) Full scan and data-dependent acquisition (ddMS2) were used to generate MS2 fragments of metabolite standards for metabolite annotation. (2) Full scan and ddMS2 with inclusion list were used to acquire MS2 information in QCs. All full scan features were divided into multiple lists (each containing 300–500 precursor ions) and added to the ddMS2 method. (3) A single full scan mode was used for metabolomics analysis of actual serum samples.

Full scan acquisition covered an m/z range of 85-1250, with a mass resolution of 120,000 FWHM at m/z 200. The AGC target and maximum ion injection time were set to 3 ⅹ 106 and 100 ms, respectively. For MS² acquisition, ddMS2 was performed with a mass resolution of 60,000 FWHM at m/z 200. The AGC target and maximum IT were set to 1 ⅹ 105 and 50 ms, respectively. Top 10 most abundant ions were selected for ddMS2, and a stepped normalized collision energy (SCE) was applied, with values set at 15, 30, and 45%. Isolation window was set to 1 m/z and inclusion lists were enabled. For ESI source parameters: the spray voltage was 3.5 kV (ESI + ) and 3.0 kV (ESI-), the ion transfer tube temperature was 320 °C (ESI + ) and 350 °C (ESI-), the sheath gas and auxiliary (aux) gas flow rates were set to 45 and 10 (in arbitrary units), respectively.

For targeted exposomics quantification on QTRAPTM 6500 + , ionization was performed in both ESI+ and ESI- modes. MRM transitions, declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) were manually optimized for each exposure using standards. Retention times were determined on the optimized column-switching 2DLC method. Detailed information is presented in Supplementary Data 4. Additional ESI source parameters were set as follows: the electrospray voltage was 5500 V (ESI + ) and -4500 V (ESI-), the Curtain gas pressure was 40.0 psi; the ion source temperature was 350 °C (ESI + ) and 300 °C (ESI-); both ion source gas 1 and gas 2 were set to 50 psi.

Analysis of metabolites and exposures using 1DLC-HRMS

Two 1DLC separations (HILIC and RPLC, respectively) were performed under optimized conditions reported previously70,71 using an ACQUITY UPLC (Waters, Milford, MA) and a Q Exactive HF System (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, U.S.A.). To enable fair comparison with our developed 2DLC-MS method, MS parameters for 1DLC-HRMS were identical to those of the HRMS module in the 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS, as detailed in the “MS data Acquisition” section.

The HILIC-based 1DLC method, adapted from the metabolomics method reported by Fiehn et al.70, was performed on an Acquity BEH Amide column (2.1 ⅹ 150 mm, 1.7 µm, Waters, Milford, U.S.A.) with the column oven set to 45 °C. Mobile phases A and B were the same as the HILIC of our 2DLC method. The elution gradient started at 100% B and held for 2 min, then decreased linearly to 70% B from 2 to 7.7 min, further to 40% B from 7.7 to 9.5 min, and rapidly to 30% B from 9.5 to 10.25 min for washing. Finally, it increased linearly back up to 100% B from 10.25 to 12.75 min and held for 4.25 min, with a total analysis time of 17 min. Flow rate was maintained at 0.4 mL/min throughout the analysis.

The RPLC-based 1DLC method, modified from our previous work71, had a total cycle time of 25 min. An Acquity BEH C8 (1.7 µm, 2.1 ⅹ 100 mm) and an Acquity HSS T3 (1.8 µm, 2.1 ⅹ 100 mm) column were used for 1DLC-RPLC analysis in positive and negative ion mode, respectively, with the column oven at 50 °C. For positive ion mode, mobile phases A1 and B1 were water and acetonitrile both containing 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid. The elution gradient started at 5% B1 and held for 1 min, then increased linearly to 100% B1 from 1 to 18 min, and held for 4 min, then decreased back to 5% B1 and held for 3 min. For negative ion mode, mobile phases A2 and B2 were water and 5% water in methanol both containing 6.5 mM ammonium bicarbonate. The elution gradient started with 2% B2, followed the same increasing profile as in positive ion mode, and finally decreased back to 2% B2 and held for 3 min. Flow rate was set as 0.35 mL/min in both positive and negative ion mode.

Metabolite annotation

After obtaining metabolomics data, metabolite annotation was performed based on in-house and public databases combined with experimental MS2 information, followed by manual verification. The in-house database was established by analyzing reference standards based on the same 2DLC-MS method developed in this study, and contains information on retention time, accurate mass, and MS2 fragments. MassBank of North America (MoNA) (https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/), NIST (https://chemdata.nist.gov/), and LipidBlast (https://fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/projects/LipidBlast) were used as public databases for metabolite annotation. All features were annotated hierarchically following standards proposed by the Chemical Analysis Working Group (CAWG) of the 2007 Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI)46. Annotations from the in-house database were designated as level 1 (identified compounds), while those from public databases were level 2 (putatively annotated compounds). Level 3 (putatively characterized compound classes) and level 4 (unknown compounds) were excluded to prioritize data reliability. For features with multiple annotations, the result with the higher-confidence annotation level or score was selected. Specific parameter settings were as follows:

(1) Level 1 annotation (in-house database): Annotation was performed by matching accurate mass, retention time and MS2 fragments. A custom-code program was developed for targeted database extraction, with parameters: MS1 and MS2 tolerance: ±10 ppm, retention time matching tolerance: ±0.2 min, MS2 similarity score threshold: 0.6.

(2) Level 2 annotation (public database): Since public databases lack retention time, only accurate mass and MS2 fragmentation patterns were used for targeted database extraction by MSDIAL 472. Parameters were: MS1 tolerance: ±10 ppm, MS2 tolerance: ±0.02 Da, identification score cutoff: 60%. For isomers, the structure with the highest MS2 similarity score was selected as the most probable candidate to avoid redundant annotations that would interfere with downstream analysis.

Method validation

The performance of the 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS system for simultaneous untargeted metabolomics and targeted exposomics analysis was validated based on 21 representative isotopically labeled metabolite standards and 601 chemical exposure standards. Evaluation parameters included linearity, LOD, LLOQ, ULOQ, precision, accuracy, extraction recovery, and matrix effect. All experiments used spiked samples of varying concentrations, with core parameters defined and calculated as follows:

LOD was defined as the first concentration (starting from the lowest concentration) with a S/N greater than 3.

LLOQ was the lowest concentration on the calibration curve with an S/N greater than 10 and accuracy between 80 and 120%.

Linearity was evaluated using the correlation coefficient (r²) of the calibration curve over the range from ULOQ to LLOQ, with a 1/x weighting factor applied in regression to minimize low-concentration calculation errors.

Accuracy was assessed by analyzing 6–8 replicate samples and calculated by substituting the corrected peak area into the calibration curve.

Extraction recovery was evaluated by analyzing four replicate samples and calculated as the ratio of the peak areas of spiked analytes before extraction to those after extraction.

Intra-day precision was evaluated by analyzing 6 replicate samples in a single run and calculated by RSD%.

Inter-day precision was evaluated by analyzing 18 replicate samples over three consecutive days and calculated by RSD%.

Matrix effects were calculated by dividing slopes of matrix-matched calibration curves by slopes of solvent-only calibration curves.

For untargeted metabolomics validation, 21 isotopically labeled standards spanning different metabolite classes and a range of retention times across the elution gradient were selected and spiked into human serum. Human serum QC samples spiked with serial concentrations across 6 orders of magnitude were prepared to generate internal calibration curves and determine the linear range, LLOQs, and ULOQs. Accuracy and extraction recovery were evaluated at low, medium, and high concentrations. Intra-day and inter-day precisions were assessed using unspiked human serum QC samples, and the stability of all metabolite features was evaluated.

For targeted exposomics validation with the 2DLC-HRMS/TQMS method, fetal bovine serum was used as the matrix for spiked samples due to challenges in obtaining human serum free of background interference. For the 601 chemical exposure standards, LOD was determined using bovine serum-spiked samples at 16 concentration levels of 0.001, 0.0025, 0.005, 0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 ng/mL. Intra-day precision, inter-day precision and extraction recovery were evaluated at four concentrations, 0.05 (close to the LOD), 0.5, 5 and 50 ng/mL. For the 242 exposures observed in actual samples, the quantitative performance of the targeted exposomics method, including linear range, LLOQ, and ULOQ, was further evaluated using the same 16 bovine serum-spiked concentrations, with internal standard calibration curves established. Accuracy was evaluated at the above four concentrations by internal standard calibration. Additionally, matrix effects of these 242 exposures in bovine serum and human serum were calculated and compared to verify the feasibility of fetal bovine serum as a substitute for human serum in exposure quantification.

Data processing

Peak areas of metabolites and exposures were corrected using isotopic internal standards (ISs, Supplementary Data 1, 2) to account for technical variation arising from sample preparation, injection volume, ionization efficiency, and short-term instrumental response fluctuations, thereby improving the comparability of peak areas across samples. The rule of IS selection followed the method reported in the reference73, where the internal standard yielding the smallest RSD of corrected peak areas in MQC or EQC samples was chosen for the corresponding metabolite or exposure. In the untargeted metabolomics analysis, peak areas of features and ISs were extracted from raw data using Compound Discoverer software (version 3.1, Thermo Fisher), and only peaks with areas exceeding 500,000 were retained. As shown in Supplementary Table 2, raw features were initially filtered using the 80% rule47, where features detected in at least 80% of samples in either the control group or the LA group were retained. Subsequently, feature peak areas were normalized using ISs to obtain relative quantification results. Only features with RSD < 30% in MQCs were kept for further annotation to generate the peak table. TraceFinder 5.1 EFS (Thermo Fisher) was used to quantify isotopically labeled metabolites for method validation of untargeted metabolomics analysis. In the targeted exposomics analysis, peak areas of exposures and ISs were integrated using SCIEX OS (version 3.0, AB SCIEX) software. After the peak areas were normalized using ISs, precise quantification of each exposure was achieved by constructing matrix-spiked internal-standard calibration curves using weighted linear regression (1/x). Potential contamination effects were controlled by ensuring that the limits of quantitation for the target compounds exceeded the corresponding background concentrations observed in blank samples. Missing values and concentrations below LLOQ were set to LLOQ/√2. For downstream statistical analyses, the relative quantification data of metabolites and the absolute quantification data of exposures were ln-transformed to reduce right-skewness and the influence of extreme values, and subsequently z-score standardized to remove scale differences and enhance comparability across analytes.

Statistical analysis

SIMCA-P software (version 14.1, Umetrics, Malmö, Sweden) was used to evaluate the stability of the untargeted metabolomics analysis and overall metabolic perturbation. Metabolite enrichment analysis was carried out using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (https://www.kegg.jp/). A unified analytical workflow was applied for differential metabolite and exposure discovery and LA-related association analysis. Differential metabolites and differential exposures were first identified using two-sided non-parametric Mann–Whitney U tests in Multi Experiment Viewer software (MeV, version 4.7.4, TIGR, Boston, MA, USA). Binary logistic regression (BLR) was then performed in R software (version 4.1.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to evaluate metabolite-LA and exposure-LA associations. In the metabolome-exposure association analysis, multiple linear regression (MLR) was used to examine associations between significant exposures and metabolites associated with LA status, and to identify candidate metabolic markers. Single mediator models were used to calculate the mediated proportion to evaluate the extent to which the candidate metabolic marker may statistically account for the observed exposures-LA association using the “mediation” package74 in R. Its formula is:

Mediated proportion = [(total effect of environmental exposure − direct effect of environmental exposure) / total effect of environmental exposure] × 100%

All regression models were adjusted for sex, age, and BMI to reduce potential confounding effects. A consistent multiple-testing correction strategy was applied throughout the analysis using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, with statistical significance defined as a two-sided p value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.10.
