Collective Buckling in Metal-Organic Framework Materials
Nico Hahn, Lars Öhrström, R. Matthias Geilhufe

Abstract
We develop a framework to describe collective buckling in metal-organic frameworks (MOFs). Starting from the microscopic structure of a single organic linker, we define a buckling coordinate governed by an effective double-well potential. Coupling between linkers arises from dipole-dipole interactions, leading to a lattice Hamiltonian. We analyze the transition between ordered and disordered phases within a mean-field approximation and determine the critical temperature. As an example for our theory, we discuss the collective buckling instability for the prototypical cubic framework MOF-5 under different values of uniaxial strain. Our approach enables a quantitative description of collective buckling in framework materials.



I
Introduction

Buckling is a ubiquitous phenomenon in applied mechanics, in which structural elements adopt deformed configurations under sufficient axial load
2
;
11
. Such a transition involves the spontaneous breaking of a symmetry and leads to the emergence of new stable states. Similar mechanisms are also possible at the molecular scale, for example in the organic linkers of metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)
29
;
7
.

MOFs are relatively new materials consisting of metallic coordination centers connected by organic linkers, often forming porous crystalline structures with large internal surface area
1
. Their flexible design has led to more than 90.000 discovered structures with diverse functionalities. Technological applications include gas storage and separation, catalysis and chemical sensing, among others
22
;
6
.

Beyond that MOFs also provide a promising platform for novel quantum materials
13
. To date, phenomena such as topological electronic states
14
, strong correlations
17
;
5
;
21
and superconductivity
12
;
26
have been explored in both theoretical and experimental works. While conventional quantum materials exhibit macroscopic order composed of microscopic degrees of freedom such as spin, charge or orbital, the unconventional degrees of freedom in MOFs, in combination with their flexible design, can give rise to novel types of ordering phenomena. In this work, we demonstrate one example: a collective buckling transition.

For a single linker, buckling typically leads to the emergence of multiple stable configurations related by a symmetry operation. In framework materials, however, the buckling state of each linker is influenced by its neighbours. Such interactions give rise to collective phases reminiscent of magnetic ordering
7
. In the ferrobuckling phase, linkers tend to buckle in the same direction, while in the antiferrobuckling phase they tend to buckle in opposite directions. In the parabuckling phase, quantum tunneling between symmetry-related configurations dominates over interactions and the linkers remain in a superposition of buckling states without selecting a definite orientation.

The molecular buckling in MOFs is schematically illustrated in Fig.
1
. The metal secondary building units (SBUs), or metallic coordination centers, are arranged in a lattice and connected to their nearest neighbours by organic linkers. The framework responds to external stress through a shift of the SBUs, which in turn induces a buckling of the organic linkers. In Fig.
2
we show the distribution of linker angles in the 1,4-benzenedicarboxylate (bdc) linker of MOF-5
19
obtained from 750 datasets of the Cambridge Structural Database (CSD)
10
.

We show that the molecular buckling in MOFs can be described classically by the lattice Hamiltonian

H=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}b_{i}b_{j}+\sum_{i}V_{\text{eff}}(b_{i})

(1)

with an effective double-well potential

V_{\text{eff}}(b)=-\mu\,b^{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}b^{4},

(2)

see Fig.
1
, and a real-valued buckling coordinate
b\in\mathbb{R}
. The interaction between linkers
i
and
j
originates from a dipole-dipole interaction and is linear in both buckling coordinates. For
\mu>0
there are two degenerate minima corresponding to a buckling to the left or the right. For
\mu\leq 0
there is no stable buckling configuration.

At low temperatures, quantum fluctuations can suppress the transition into the classically ordered state, giving rise to a parabuckling phase
7
. This is analogous to the quantum paraelectric state observed in perovskites such as SrTiO
3

24
;
4
. Qualitatively, this occurs when the quantum ground state energy exceeds the depth of the double-well potential, preventing localization in either minimum.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we present a rigorous derivation of the buckling coordinate of a single linker. In Sec.
III
, we derive the interaction
J_{ij}
microscopically by approximating each linker as an electric dipole. These two ingredients lead to the lattice model (
1
). In Sec.
IV
, we consider the collective buckling phases of this model within a mean-field approximation. The technical details of this section are relegated to the appendix. In Sec.
V
, we demonstrate our findings on the example of the metal-organic framework MOF-5, for which we obtain the model parameters via density functional theory (DFT). In Sec.
VI
, we discuss the classical to quantum cross-over at low temperatures and recover the transverse field Ising model, which has been considered in the context of buckling in Ref.
7
. Finally, we conclude in Sec.
VII
.

II
The Buckling Coordinate

In this section, we derive the collective buckling coordinate of a molecular linker and map the buckling dynamics onto an effective single-particle problem characterized by an effective potential.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
be the coordinates of the
i
th (
i=1,\ldots,N
) atom and
V(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N})
the potential of the unbuckled molecule. The equilibrium configuration of the molecule
(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N}^{(0)})
is a local minimum of the potential. Without loss of generality, we set the end points to
\mathbf{x}_{1}^{(0)}=0
and
\mathbf{x}_{N}^{(0)}=L\,\hat{e}_{1}
, where
L
is the length of the molecule and
\hat{e}_{1}
an arbitrary unit vector indicating the chain direction.

Buckling is introduced by constraining the endpoints to

\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathbf{x}_{1}^{(0)}=0,\qquad\mathbf{x}_{N}=L(1-\kappa)\hat{e}_{1},

(3)

where the parameter
\kappa
with
0\leq\kappa\ll 1
quantifies the imposed compression. In the case of multiple atoms at the ends of the chain, we assume that they are rigid and define
\mathbf{x}_{1}
and
\mathbf{x}_{N}
as their geometric centers. This defines a constrained potential

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=V_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N-1})

\displaystyle=V(\mathbf{x}_{1}=0,\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N-1},\mathbf{x}_{N}=L(1-\kappa)\hat{e}_{1}),

(4)

which depends only on the inner coordinates
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{3(N-2)}
. The equilibrium in the unbuckled configuration (
\kappa=0
) is denoted as
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}=(\mathbf{x}_{2}^{(0)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N-1}^{(0)})
.

Figure 3:
Illustration of the atomic displacement for the bdc linker of MOF-5. The relaxed configuration is shown on the left and in the center. The buckled configuration is shown on the right with atomic displacements
\mathbf{\delta x}_{j}
.

For small
\kappa>0
, the equilibrium position will shift by
\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x}
. Fig.
3
illustrates the atomic displacement due to buckling. We find this displacement vector by expanding the potential to first order around
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
and equating to zero

\nabla V_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x}^{(0)}+\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x})=\nabla V_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x}^{(0)})+\mathcal{H}\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x}+\mathcal{O}\left(\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x}^{2}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0,

(5)

where

\mathcal{H}_{i\alpha,j\beta}=\frac{\partial^{2}V_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i\alpha}\partial x_{j\beta}}\Bigg|_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{(0)}}

(6)

is the Hessian of the potential at the equilibrium. For the sake of clarity, we do not include the dependency on
V_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x})
into the notation of the Hessian. Furthermore, we follow the convention that Latin indices (
i,j,\ldots
) label atoms and Greek indices (
\alpha,\beta,\ldots
) denote Cartesian coordinates. The displacement vector is thus given by

\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x}=-\mathcal{H}^{-1}\nabla V_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x}^{(0)}).

(7)

Before defining the buckling coordinate, it is convenient to switch to mass-weighted coordinates

\mathbf{q}=M^{1/2}\mathbf{x},

(8)

where
M=\text{diag}(m_{2},\ldots,m_{N-1})\otimes\mathds{1}_{3}
is the diagonal mass matrix. This transformation is also used in the discussion of vibrational modes and phonons. The corresponding mass-weighted Hessian

\mathcal{D}=M^{-1/2}\mathcal{H}M^{-1/2}

(9)

retains its real symmetric form. We denote the eigenvalues as
\lambda^{(i)}
and the normalized eigenvectors as
\mathbf{v}^{(i)}
and order them as
\lambda^{(1)}\leq\lambda^{(2)}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda^{(3(N-2))}
. The eigenvector
\mathbf{v}^{(1)}
belonging to the smallest eigenvalue defines the soft buckling mode, i.e. the direction of the instability. Here we assume that the eigenvalues are non-degenerate. A comment on this point follows at the end of this section.

The scalar buckling coordinate is defined as the projection of the displacement
\bm{\delta}\mathbf{q}
(now in mass-weighted coordinates) onto the normalized soft eigenmode

b=\mathbf{v}^{(1)}\cdot\bm{\delta}\mathbf{q}=-\frac{1}{\lambda^{(1)}}\mathbf{v}^{(1)}\cdot M^{-1/2}\nabla V_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x}^{(0)}),

(10)

which is measured in units of
[b]=\sqrt{\text{u}}\,\text{\AA}
. Conversely, the displacement vector can be written in a single-mode approximation as

\bm{\delta}\mathbf{q}\approx b\,\mathbf{v}^{(1)}.

(11)

The single-mode approximation is good when the gap between the smallest and the second smallest eigenvalue is large,
\lambda^{(2)}\gg\lambda^{(1)}
. We define the effective potential as

V_{\text{eff}}(b)=V_{\kappa}\left(\mathbf{x}^{(0)}+b\,M^{-1/2}\mathbf{v}^{(1)}\right).

(12)

The relevant physics is captured by the low-energy expansion, the double well potential

V_{\text{eff}}(b)=-\mu\,b^{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}b^{4}+\mathcal{O}\left(b^{6}\right),

(13)

where we ignore the constant energy shift. Henceforth, we will not explicitly state terms of
\mathcal{O}(b^{6})
, which become important only in the case
\nu\leq 0
.

The transformation to mass-weighted coordinates (
8
) guarantees that the effective mass of the buckling coordinate is unity. This can be checked quickly by considering the kinetic energy of the system in the soft mode approximation (
11
)

T=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{T}M\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^{2}\approx\frac{1}{2}\dot{b}^{2}.

(14)

Finally, we discuss the role of symmetry for the soft mode. Let
G
denote the point group that leaves the equilibrium configuration
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}
invariant. The group elements
g\in G
act on the displacement
\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x}
via an orthogonal matrix
R(g)

\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x}\to R(g)\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x}.

(15)

Since the potential at the equilibrium is invariant, the Hessian commutes with all
R(g)

\mathcal{H}=R(g)\mathcal{H}R^{T}(g).

(16)

Therefore the Hessian becomes block-diagonal in the basis where
R(g)
is reduced to its irreducible components. The same holds true for the mass-weighted Hessian (
9
) as long as the mass distribution respects the symmetry, i.e.
M
commutes with all
R(g)
. As a consequence of Schur’s lemma, the symmetry enforces degeneracies that coincide with the dimensions of the real irreducible representation of
G
.

In particular,
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
has only one-dimensional irreducible representations and thus the eigenvalues of the Hessian are non-degenerate. In contrast, higher symmetry groups such as
C_{n}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{n}
(
n\geq 3
) or
\rm SO(2)
have also two-dimensional real irreducible representations, which enforces twofold degeneracies of the soft mode. This latter case requires two buckling coordinates, defined as the projections of
\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x}
onto the two eigenvectors belonging to the smallest eigenvalue. In this situation, our formalism applies in a similar manner, but details are beyond the scope of this work.

III
Dipolar Approximation of Linker-Linker Interaction

Each linker carries a charge distribution
\rho(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{x})
with zero net charge. The distribution has an ionic contribution depending directly on the atomic coordinates
\mathbf{x}
, and thus on the buckling coordinate, and a continuous contribution of the electrons relaxing around these positions. The dipole moment of a linker in the configuration
\mathbf{x}
is

\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x})=\int d^{3}r\,\rho(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{x})\mathbf{r}.

(17)

The equilibrium position changes in the presence of buckling and we expand

\mathbf{p}\left(\mathbf{x}^{(0)}+\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathbf{p}\left(\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\right)+Z\,\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x}+\mathcal{O}\left(\bm{\delta}\mathbf{x}^{2}\right),

(18)

where we introduced the effective charge tensor

Z_{\alpha,i\beta}=\frac{\partial p_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i\beta}}\Bigg|_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{(0)}}.

(19)

This quantity is the molecular analogue of the Born effective charge defined for the polarization per unit cell
8
;
9
.

The atomic displacement is dominated by the soft buckling mode (
11
) and we obtain

\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{p}^{(0)}+b\,\mathbf{d},\qquad d_{\alpha}=\sum_{i,\beta}\frac{Z_{\alpha,i\beta}}{\sqrt{M_{i}}}v^{(1)}_{i\beta},

(20)

where we suppress the argument of
\mathbf{p}
and use the abbreviation
\mathbf{p}^{(0)}=\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}^{(0)})
. We define a mode dipole vector
\mathbf{d}
and take the mass-weighted coordinates into account. The dipole vector is measured in units of
[d]=e/\sqrt{\text{u}}
, which combines to units of a true dipole moment when multiplied with the buckling coordinate.

Finally, we label different linkers by
i
and
j
and consider their dipole-dipole interaction

E_{ij}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon}\frac{\mathbf{p}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{j}-3\left(\mathbf{p}_{i}\cdot\hat{e}_{ij}\right)\left(\mathbf{p}_{j}\cdot\hat{e}_{ij}\right)}{r_{ij}^{3}},

(21)

where
\epsilon
is the permittivity of the material,
r_{ij}
the distance and
\hat{e}_{ij}
the normalized orientation vector between the linkers. Inserting (
20
) we obtain

E_{ij}=-J_{ij}^{(0)}-J_{ij}^{(1)}b_{i}-J_{ji}^{(1)}b_{j}-J^{(2)}_{ij}b_{i}b_{j}

(22)

with

\begin{split}J^{(0)}_{ij}&=\frac{3(\mathbf{p}_{i}^{(0)}\cdot\hat{e}_{ij})(\mathbf{p}_{j}^{(0)}\cdot\hat{e}_{ij})-\mathbf{p}_{i}^{(0)}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{j}^{(0)}}{4\pi\epsilon\,r_{ij}^{3}},\\
J^{(1)}_{ij}&=\frac{3(\mathbf{d}_{i}\cdot\hat{e}_{ij})(\mathbf{p}_{j}^{(0)}\cdot\hat{e}_{ij})-\mathbf{d}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{j}^{(0)}}{4\pi\epsilon\,r_{ij}^{3}},\\
J^{(2)}_{ij}&=\frac{3(\mathbf{d}_{i}\cdot\hat{e}_{ij})(\mathbf{d}_{j}\cdot\hat{e}_{ij})-\mathbf{d}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{j}}{4\pi\epsilon\,r_{ij}^{3}},\end{split}

(23)

where we included a sign so that we have positive interaction parameters for an attractive interaction. The interaction is symmetric
J^{(k)}_{ij}=J^{(k)}_{ji}
and we take
J^{(k)}_{ii}=0
for
k\in\{0,1,2\}
. The parameters are expressed in units of
[J^{(0)}_{ij}]=\text{eV}
,
[J^{(1)}_{ij}]=\text{eV}/(\sqrt{\text{u}}\,\text{\AA})
and
[J^{(2)}_{ij}]=\text{eV}/(\text{u}\,\text{\AA}^{2})
. Eq. (
22
) simplifies tremendously when
\mathbf{p}^{(0)}=0
for all linkers. This is the case when the linkers have spatial inversion symmetry, since then we have
\rho(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{x})=-\rho(-\mathbf{r};-\mathbf{x})
, so that a vanishing dipole moment follows immediately from (
17
).

IV
Macroscopic Phase Transition

Taking the interactions and the local effective potential into account, we obtain the lattice Hamiltonian (
1
)

H=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}b_{i}b_{j}+\sum_{i}V_{\text{eff}}(b_{i}),

(24)

where we assume the situation of linkers with spatial inversion symmetry and have included a factor
1/2
to avoid double counting.

In analogy to magnetization in spin models, we define a global buckling order parameter by

m=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\langle b_{i}\rangle=\langle b\rangle,

(25)

which is normalized by the system size
N
. Since we consider bulk properties, there is translation invariance and the order parameter reduces to the thermal expectation value
\langle b\rangle
of any linker, indicated by the absence of a site index.

We apply a mean-field approximation

b_{i}=\langle b_{i}\rangle+\delta b_{i},

(26)

i.e. each buckling coordinate is written as its average plus thermal fluctuations
\delta b_{i}=b_{i}-\langle b_{i}\rangle
. In the mean-field Hamiltonian, all but the leading order of
\delta b_{i}
are neglected. It then becomes an effective single-particle Hamiltonian

H_{\text{MF}}=\sum_{i}h(b_{i})+\frac{N\tilde{J}}{2}m^{2}

(27)

with a local Hamiltonian

h(b_{i})=-\tilde{J}\,m\,b_{i}+V_{\text{eff}}(b_{i})

(28)

and

\tilde{J}=\sum_{j}J_{0j}.

(29)

The order parameter is determined by the self-consistency equation of the mean-field theory

m=\frac{\displaystyle\int d[b]b_{i}\,e^{-\beta H_{\text{MF}}}}{\displaystyle\int d[b]e^{-\beta H_{\text{MF}}}}=\frac{\displaystyle\int db\,b\,e^{-\beta h(b)}}{\displaystyle\int db\,e^{-\beta h(b)}}

(30)

where we use the notation
d[b]=db_{1}\cdots db_{N}
. Since we are dealing with an effective single-particle theory, the integral factorizes and we are left with a single integral over a buckling coordinate.

In Appendix
A
, we perform a cumulant expansion of the free energy and obtain the critical temperature as the solution of the fixed-point equation

k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{C}=\tilde{J}\left\langle b^{2}\right\rangle_{0},

(31)

where

\left\langle f(b)\right\rangle_{0}=\frac{1}{Z_{0}}\int db\,f(b)e^{-\beta V_{\text{eff}}(b)},\qquad Z_{0}=\int db\,e^{-\beta V_{\text{eff}}(b)}

(32)

denotes the thermal expectation value of a single linker in the absence of interactions. The moments, such as
\langle b^{2}\rangle_{0}
, can be evaluated analytically in terms of parabolic cylinder functions, providing a closed-form description of the mean-field theory.

3

19

28
.

To estimate the effective buckling potential
V_{\text{eff}}(b)
, we performed ab initio calculations in the framework of the density functional theory using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)
15
. The generalized gradient approximation of Perdew, Burke and Ernzerhof
23
was employed with a cut-off energy of 400 eV. For the unstrained configuration, we relaxed the bdc molecule to its computational groundstate, which is
\approx 4.5\%
larger than experimentally observed in MOF-5
20
. In all calculations the linker axis was aligned in the
x
-direction, while the benzene ring was constrained to the
xy
-plane. The displacement due to buckling was applied in
z
-direction, perpendicular to the molecular plane. We then decreased the molecular length successively and calculated the total energy for a sine-shaped displacement with varying amplitude. The energy against the buckling mode coordinate is shown in Fig.

V_{\text{eff}}(b)=-\mu\,b^{2}+\nu/2\,b^{4}
. For sufficiently high strain
\geq 2\%

\mu>0
and a corresponding non-zero minimum in the buckling coordinate.

VI
Classical to Quantum Cross-Over

The crossover from classical to quantum buckling is governed by the local quantization energy of a single linker. This scale is obtained from the harmonic approximation around one of the buckling minima. For the effective potential (
13
) it is

V_{\text{eff}}(b)=-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\nu}+\frac{\omega_{1}^{2}}{2}(b-b_{0})^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left((b-b_{0})^{4}\right),

(34)

where the two degenerate minima and the local well frequency are given by

b_{0}=\pm\sqrt{\mu/\nu},\qquad\omega_{1}=\sqrt{V^{\prime\prime}_{\text{eff}}(b_{0})}=2\sqrt{\mu}.

(35)

Once the thermal energy becomes comparable to or smaller than this scale

k_{\mathrm{B}}T\lesssim\hbar\omega_{1},

(36)

each linker effectively behaves as a two-level system described by the two lowest eigenstates of the double-well potential. The crossover temperature is therefore

T_{1}=\hbar\omega_{1}/k_{\mathrm{B}}.

(37)

Figure 6:
Effective potential and wave functions of the ground state
\psi_{-}
and the first excited state
\psi_{+}
.

The total energy of a single linker is

E=\frac{1}{2}\dot{b}^{2}+V_{\text{eff}}(b)

(38)

and for unit effective mass the canonical momentum of the buckling coordinate is
p_{b}=\dot{b}
. Canonical quantization
p_{b}\to-i\hbar\partial/\partial b
yields the Schrödinger equation

\left[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial b^{2}}+V_{\text{eff}}(b)\right]\psi(b)=E\psi(b)

(39)

with a buckling wave function
\psi(b)
.

In the limit of a large potential barrier, the two lowest eigenstates are strongly localized near
b_{0}
. Each well can be approximated by a harmonic oscillator with ground state energy
E_{0}=V(\pm b_{0})+\hbar\omega_{1}/2=-\mu^{2}/(2\nu)+\hbar\sqrt{\mu}
. Lowering the potential barrier enables tunneling between the two wells and lifts the degeneracy

E_{\pm}=E_{0}\pm t,

(40)

where
t>0
is the tunneling amplitude. Due to the symmetry of the potential, the wave functions are either even or odd in
b
. Sturm-Liouville theory guarantees that the ground state is even, while the first excited state is odd
27
. These states are well approximated by symmetric and antisymmetric combinations of the localized harmonic oscillator ground states. An effective Hamiltonian for this two-level system is

h=\begin{bmatrix}E_{0}&-t\\
-t&E_{0}\end{bmatrix}

(41)

with eigenstates
\psi_{\pm}=1/\sqrt{2}(1,\pm 1)
and energy splitting
2t
. Fig.
6
shows the double-well potential (
13
) and the wavefunctions of the ground state
\psi_{+}
and the first excited state
\psi_{-}
.

The tunnel amplitude
t
can be estimated semiclassically within the WKB approximation
18

\begin{split}t&\approx\frac{\omega_{2}\hbar}{2\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\limits_{-b_{1}}^{b_{1}}db\left|p(b)\right|\right)\\
&=\frac{\omega_{2}\hbar}{2\pi}\exp\left(\pi\frac{E_{0}}{\hbar\omega_{2}}+\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\right),\end{split}

(42)

where
p(b)
is the momentum in the classically forbidden region,
\pm b_{1}
are the turning points and
\omega_{2}=\sqrt{2\mu}
the barrier frequency. To reproduce the result in Ref.
25
we applied a harmonic approximation of
V_{\text{eff}}(b)
around the local maximum. Once the thermal energy becomes comparable to or smaller than this scale, quantum tunneling dominates over thermal hopping. This defines a second temperature scale
T_{2}=t/k_{\mathrm{B}}
with
T_{2}\ll T_{1}
.

Below this temperature,
t
becomes relevant and we write the local Hamiltonian in the two-level subspace of
\psi_{\pm}
as
-t\sigma^{x}
. It remains to couple the linkers according to the results of Sec.
III
. Substituting
b_{i}\to b_{0}\sigma_{i}^{z}
yields the transverse field Ising model

H=-\frac{b_{0}^{2}}{2}\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}J_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{j}^{z}+t\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{x}.

(43)

This model has been discussed in the context of collective buckling in Ref.
7
. The competition of the coupling
J_{ij}
, favoring an ordered state, and the transverse field
t
leads to a quantum phase transition into the parabuckling state.

We estimated
t
by the WKB approximation for MOF-5 and found neglibily small values. This is in accordance with a numerical solution of the Schrödinger equation (
39
), which shows no energy splitting between the two lowest eigenstates. We conclude that a quantum crossover is absent for the present example.

VII
Conclusion

We have developed a framework to describe collective buckling in MOFs. Starting from the microscopic structure of a single organic linker, we defined a buckling coordinate governed by an effective double-well potential. The coupling between linkers was derived from a dipole-dipole approximation, resulting in a lattice Hamiltonian.

We introduced a ferrobuckling order parameter and analyzed the phase transition within a mean-field approximation. Applying our theory to MOF-5, we extracted the model parameters from DFT and obtained the critical temperature as a function of applied strain for a decoupled (100) plane. At low temperatures, quantum effects may drive a crossover to a transverse-field Ising model, giving rise to a parabuckling phase.

Our work provides a quantitative description for the macroscopic buckling behaviour in terms of microscopic details. Future work could extend this approach beyond decoupled (100) planes to the full three-dimensional structure of MOF-5, allowing for more general order parameters and potentially richer collective phases.

Appendix A
Free Energy Expansion in Mean-Field Approximation

The free energy of the mean-field Hamiltonian (
27
) is given by

F=-k_{\mathrm{B}}T\ln\,Z=\frac{N\tilde{J}}{2}m^{2}-k_{\mathrm{B}}TN\ln\,Z_{\text{loc}},

(44)

where the local partition function is

Z_{\text{loc}}=\int db\,e^{-\beta\,h(b)}.

(45)

Instead of the free energy, we consider in the following the free energy density
f=F/N
, which is an intensive quantity. Adopting the notation of the main text (
32
), we write this as

Z_{\text{loc}}=Z_{0}\left\langle e^{\beta\tilde{J}mb}\right\rangle_{0}.

(46)

Close to the phase transition the order parameter
m
is small and we can perform a cumulant expansion
16

\ln\left\langle e^{\beta\tilde{J}mb}\right\rangle_{0}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\beta\tilde{J}m)^{n}}{n!}\kappa_{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\beta\tilde{J}m)^{2n}}{(2n)!}\kappa_{2n}

(47)

with the cumulants
\kappa_{n}
. Since
V_{\text{eff}}(b)
is even, odd terms vanish.

The partition function
Z_{0}
for a single linker has an analytical solution (
a=\beta\nu/2>0
,
c=\beta\mu\in\mathbb{R}
)

Z_{0}=\int db\,e^{-ab^{4}+cb^{2}}=\sqrt{\pi}\,(2a)^{-1/4}e^{c^{2}/(8a)}D_{-1/2}\left(-\frac{c}{\sqrt{2a}}\right)

(48)

in terms of parabolic cylinder functions
D_{n}(z)
, which are a generalization of the Hermite functions to non-integer order
3
. The moments and cumulants can be obtained from the derivatives

\left\langle b^{2n}\right\rangle_{0}=\frac{1}{Z_{0}}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial c^{n}}Z_{0},\qquad\kappa_{2n}=\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial c^{n}}\ln Z_{0}.

(49)

To obtain a recursion formula for the moments, we compute the derivative

\begin{split}&\frac{d}{db}\left(b^{2n+1}e^{-ab^{4}+cb^{2}}\right)=\\
&\left[(2n+1)b^{2n}-4a\,b^{2n+4}+2c\,b^{2n+2}\right]e^{-ab^{4}+cb^{2}}\end{split}

(50)

and integrate over the real numbers. This yields

\left\langle b^{2n+4}\right\rangle_{0}=\frac{c}{2a}\left\langle b^{2n+2}\right\rangle_{0}+\frac{2n+1}{4a}\left\langle b^{2n}\right\rangle_{0}.

(51)

Therefore it suffices to compute the derivative for
n=1

\begin{split}\langle b^{2}\rangle_{0}=&\frac{c}{2a}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2a}}R\left(-\frac{c}{\sqrt{2a}}\right)\\
=&\frac{\mu}{\nu}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta\nu}}R\left(-\mu\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\nu}}\right),\\
R(z)=&\frac{D_{1/2}(z)}{D_{-1/2}(z)}.\end{split}

(52)

We also explicitly state the fourth moment

\begin{split}\langle b^{4}\rangle_{0}=&\frac{1}{4a}+\frac{c^{2}}{4a^{2}}+\frac{c}{(2a)^{3/2}}R\left(-\frac{c}{\sqrt{2a}}\right)\\
=&\frac{1}{2\beta\nu}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{\nu^{2}}+\frac{\mu}{\beta^{1/2}\nu^{3/2}}R\left(-\mu\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\nu}}\right).\end{split}

(53)

At low temperatures we can use the asymptotics of the parabolic cylinder functions for
z\to\infty
to find
R(z)=1/(2z)+\mathcal{O}(z^{-3})
. This coincides with a saddle-point approximation around both minima of the double-well.

The cumulant expansion (
47
) up to
\mathcal{O}(m^{4})
yields

f=-T\ln Z_{0}+\frac{a_{2}(T)}{2}m^{2}+\frac{a_{4}(T)}{4}m^{4}+\mathcal{O}(m^{6})

(54)

with the temperature-dependent Landau coefficients

a_{2}(T)=\tilde{J}\left(1-\beta\tilde{J}\kappa_{2}\right),\qquad a_{4}(T)=-\frac{\beta^{3}\tilde{J}^{4}}{6}\kappa_{4}.

(55)

The first two non-vanishing cumulants are given by

\kappa_{2}=\langle b^{2}\rangle_{0},\qquad\kappa_{4}=\langle b^{4}\rangle_{0}-3\langle b^{2}\rangle_{0}^{2}.

(56)

A change of sign in
a_{2}(T)
, i.e. its root, as a function of temperature determines the phase transition. We arrive at

k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{C}=\tilde{J}\langle b^{2}\rangle_{0},

(57)

which is to be understood as a fixed-point equation as the right-hand side depends on temperature as well. By minimizing the free energy, we obtain an expression for the order parameter

m(T)=\sqrt{-\frac{a_{2}(T)}{a_{4}(T)}}=\sqrt{\frac{6}{\beta^{3}\tilde{J}^{3}}\frac{1-\beta\tilde{J}\kappa_{2}}{\kappa_{4}}},

(58)

which is valid only close to the phase transition. A first order expansion around
T_{C}
reveals the characteristic mean-field behaviour of the order parameter

m(T)\sim\sqrt{T_{C}-T},

(59)

i.e. the critical exponent is
1/2
. The condition
a_{4}>0
guarantees that the expansion is stable and that the phase transition is continuous.
