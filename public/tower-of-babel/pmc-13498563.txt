Transverse and vertical cracking behavior of railway rails due to geometrical imperfections and bending
Authors: Memduh Karalar, Ali Motameni, Murat Buyuk, Rıza Gürbüz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRail flaws and unexpected failures occurred at a quick pace as a result of the rapid growth of high-speed and heavy-haul railroads. It is of the utmost importance to prevent a situation like this since any possible breakdown of a rail carries the possibility of trucks being derailed devastatingly. As a consequence of this, there is a need for more sophisticated rail inspection, which involves the precise detection and assessment of rail problems in real-time. The purpose of this investigation is to provide an introduction to the most significant problems concerning the propagation of cracks and the fracture of rails. The loading conditions that fall under this category include contact forces from the wheel as well as residual stresses resulting from on-site welding and fabrication procedures. To ascertain the factors that led to the occurrence of fracture and failure damage, a series of tests were carried out. These tests included spectral, tensile, hardness and fracture toughness tests. Apart from the mentioned tests, fractographic analysis of the surfaces was investigated using a scanning electron microscope (SEM). Furthermore, stress distributions were ascertained, and the impact of residual stresses and geometric discontinuities on crack development was assessed using finite element modeling. The findings show that residual bending stresses considerably speed up fracture propagation, whereas stress concentration zones caused by geometric imperfections are strongly correlated with the beginning of cracks. In conclusion, the steps of crack propagation, beginning with the commencement of the crack and ending with the ultimate rupture, were described. According to the findings, stress concentration locations that are connected with geometric flaws have a significant role in the onset of cracks, while residual stresses contribute to the acceleration of fracture propagation.Keywords: Rail, Fatigue crack propagation, Fracture, Damage toleranceSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

In addition to being an expensive and time-consuming investment, rail infrastructure has a lengthy lifespan. It is necessary to perform efficient maintenance to profit from this investment. As a result of their intimate association with the movement of passengers and goods, railways pose a significant threat to the possible loss of human life and the damage or destruction of assets. Railways are a significant mode of transportation in many nations. There are significant challenges that all rail stakeholders across the globe must face, including the detection and correction of rail flaws and degradations. There are a numerous different types of rail defects as worn out rails, weld defects, internal flaws, corrugations, rolling contact fatigue (RCF), surface cracks, head checks, squats, spalling, and shelling1. These defects can result in rail breakage and derailments if not discovered and/or remedied correctly. In the form of rail deterioration, a potential risk is the danger that builds up over a period of time without being addressed. Even though all of the service providers of rail infrastructure across the globe are always working to save costs, a significant amount of the funding available for railways is allocated to the maintenance of rails. The yearly examination of rail systems in the European Union is estimated to cost between 375 and 850 million €2. Because of this specific reason, a significant number of researchers are now working on the process of developing procedures for the maintenance of railway infrastructure that are particularly cost-effective such as Podofillini et al.3, Zarembski4. Chattopadhyay et al.5, in their research, brought attention to the need of reducing the number of flaws that were not recognized and those that were detected incorrectly. To facilitate economic decision making, the purpose of this research was to identify the elements that influence rail deterioration, build models for rail failures, and conduct an analysis of the costs associated with varied grinding intervals. This research established a total cost model that considered a variety of expenditures associated with rail maintenance. These costs included rail grinding, downtime, inspection, rail failures, derailment, and the replacement of worn-out rails. Ma et al.6 conducted several tests to study the high-speed rail material’s fatigue crack development velocity and rolling contact fatigue destruction characteristics under a variety of temperature settings. Based on the findings, it can be concluded that the stress and strength of rail components rise as the temperature of the surrounding environment decreases. At three different temperatures, it is possible to deduce three alternative theories of the crack formation process. When the temperature drops, the brittle fracture mechanism becomes more noticeable that is occurring. Another investigation was performed by Xiong et al.7. In this study, it was presented that there were several forms of rail problems. Following that, a summary was provided of the techniques that have the ability to accomplish speedy and accurate detection and assessment of rail problems. These methods included ultrasonic testing, electromagnetic testing, visual testing, and certain combined approaches that are used in the field. It was discovered that the magnetic flux leakage and visual testing methods may be employed in conjunction with one another to identify and assess surface and subsurface faults. Additionally, UT is used to examine the rail for interior problems. Full rail information will be obtained to avoid abrupt failure and assure train trip safety. To study wheel and rail elastic-plastic fatigue fracture propagation, Zhou et al.8 presented a two-stage simulation technique. First, a three-dimensional finite element model of the wheel-rail rolling contact was made to investigate the changes in tangential traction and the pressure and flexibility of the wheel-rail contact. Next, a simplified 3D finite element model of the wheel and rail with vertical cracks was made to look into how fatigue cracks grow under the same moving load. The findings of the simulation reveal that there are two crucial places for crack development that arise during the contact load approaching and leaving from the crack. It was also found that the deepest fracture point has a limited plastic zone with the highest crack development rate because the Mises stress exceeds the yield stress. Grossoni et al.9 identified and discussed the most frequently observed failures at the switch and crossing panels, along with possible causes and mitigation measures. For this reason, a seven-year period of rail fault data gathered from the network has been statistically assessed with the use of free-text analysis algorithms to extract more information from the manual inputs. This study revealed potential reasons for both switch and crossing panels and topics for further research to help the next generation of switches and crossings. In another investigation, Research conducted by Orringer et al.10 revealed the findings of an exhaustive investigation on the crack propagation behavior of minor fractures in railroad rails. For the purpose of this research, full-scale crack development experiments were conducted on a test track while heavy freight train operation was simulated. Additionally, identical field testing and observations were conducted on revenue tracks. Finally, static tests were conducted to assess the breaking strengths of rails that included detailed cracks. The results of laboratory studies to identify the fundamental crack development rate attributes of rail steel were discussed, and a fracture mechanics model of the detailed crack was provided. It is discovered that the three most important variables are residual stress in the rail head, curve high rail position (with respect to tangent track), and thermal stress in continuous welded rail. Liu et al.11 examined transverse surface fracture development in rail steel and thermite weld under in-plane and out-of-plane loads during structural fatigue. Finite element model analysis was used to calculate Stress Intensity Factor (SIF) solutions for transverse surface cracks at five crack initiation points in rail steel and thermite weld in the cross-section. At the end of the investigation, the fatigue crack growth method and propagation life estimation reveal crack form progression. Trollé et al.12 addressed the fractured rail issue, computed the stress intensity components, performed crack propagation, and fatigue life prediction using a two-scale frictional contact fatigue crack model created inside the X-FEM framework. The investigation focused on the impact of the real plastic stresses on the crack propagation route and rate. Analysis was also done on interactions among various network breaks. It was found that plastic stress fields have a significant influence in the fatigue crack propagation behavior, as shown by the injection of plastic stresses into the fracture network. Peixoto et al.13 studied fatigue fracture propagation in AVE wheel steel and UIC60 rail steel, including numerous R-values and near threshold behaviors. Mode-I fatigue crack growth experiments were conducted to achieve this goal. It was discovered that compared to the wheel material, the rail material exhibits greater fatigue crack propagation rates. The possibility of fatigue fractures starting in the rail head and web in the weld zone of a rail was investigated by Josefson et al.14. Through the use of a nonlinear finite element (FE) analysis, the interaction between the welding residual stress field and the zone of stress generated by induced by service loads was imitated. Observations indicate that welding residual stresses enhance fatigue crack initiation risk, with higher magnitude (σA) indicating greater fatigue failure risk. As shown above, a lot of researchers have conducted extensive research on many forms of rail faults15–37. Nezhad et al.38 investigated the impact of different operating loads on the development of rolling contact fatigue cracks in a rail head. In order to achieve this objective, a numerical framework based on 3D finite elements is implemented, which includes a 60E1 rail with a semi-circular gauge corner fracture that is inclined and causes surface fracturing. Under various support situations, the effects of wheel-rail tractive forces, heat loads, rail bending, and the size and location of (normal) contact load were examined. Crack development rate was shown to be primarily influenced by contact stress amount and location. Gharawi et al.39 performed another investigation to provide a comprehensive experimental and computational analysis of the flexural response of UIC 60 rail connections under three-point bending. Under static loads of up to 1400 kN, full-scale laboratory experiments were implemented to evaluate the rail joints’ deformation capacity, rigidity, and load–deflection behavior. In addition, the experimental configuration was replicated and internal stress states were evaluated through finite element simulations conducted with ANSYS. Bolted joints were found to have the most deformation and the lowest stiffness, with deflection reaching around 24.3 mm at peak load—roughly 42% more than that of their hot-rolled counterparts. In every instance, the load-deflection behavior in the elastic regime was precisely represented using finite element analysis. Nazari and Tehrani40 performed another study to observe the features of the crack start in railway curves. For this aim, this study used a three-dimensional coupled thermomechanical finite element analysis. The objective of the proposed numerical simulation of wheel–rail contact was to investigate the impact of varying curvature radii and slip ratios on the thermal increase, fatigue parameters, and fatigue life of fracture initiation. It was observed that the direction of fracture development shifts away from the surface and toward the depth of the rail when the fatigue parameter increases under critical circumstances. Zhu et al.41 created a quick crack propagation analysis technique specifically for turnout rails and creatively suggested a new fatigue reliability evaluation method based on the ASO-SVR machine learning algorithm. The findings help fatigue life prediction, failure risk mitigation, and turnout structure maintenance strategy improvement. Lunga and Ilunga42 examined railway track deterioration using finite element analysis, focusing on the performance and resilience of rail parts under different loads. To find high-stress areas, especially at rail joints and sleeper interfaces, which are most vulnerable to increased wear and failure, static, dynamic, and fatigue simulations were performed. The discovery of crucial stress locations at rail joints and fastening systems, where maximal von Mises stresses occurred under dynamic loading, is one of the key results. The track components preserved enough safety margins in spite of these high pressures, confirming their structural integrity. The assessment of prior research indicated that rail deterioration and fault production rates must be reduced by failure estimation for track components to design an efficient track maintenance approach. Furthermore, while the behavior of vertical and transverse cracks in railroad tracks under geometric defects and bending effects has been studied in the literature, it is noted that most of the studies that are currently available focus on these types of cracks independently and do not accurately depict how defects change over time and under dynamic loading conditions. Nevertheless, there are serious issues in using field data to validate the established models and incorporating the results into maintenance and failure prediction plans.

Scope of the study

In this study, an examination of the damage was carried out on three samples of corrugated metro rail, one of which was broken, and the other two were fractured horizontally. Three distinct rail samples that exhibited damage in the field were subjected to comprehensive experimental and analytical investigations in this study. The initial sample was obtained from the middle segment of an 18-meter-long grooved rail of the RI59E2 (R260) type. The rail was already assembled and revealed vertical fragmentation during the passage of a vehicle weighing approximately 35 tons. This rail was found to be situated in a stretch of the track that was subjected to bending and classified as a small curve. Transverse fracture forms were seen after the bending operation in the second and third samples. The tested rails were bent in accordance with a curve geometry with a radius of around 45 m, without any preheating, and at ambient circumstances between 20 and 35 °C. Nevertheless, it was also noted that certain rails that were bent under the identical circumstances did not exhibit surface crack development.

To determine the causes of fracture and cracking damage, examination tests like spectral analysis, tensile tests, hardness tests, fracture toughness tests, fractographic, and metallographic were performed, and the stressed areas were determined using the finite element method. Following that, the areas of fracture and crack damage were identified, and a detailed comparison was made with the findings of the finite element model. In the following sections, certain analyses are presented extensively. The goal of this research is to identify the underlying processes that, despite comparable manufacturing and assembly circumstances, cause certain rails to sustain damage while others do not. In order to identify the underlying causes of fracture and cracking damage, mechanical testing, material characterisation, and microstructural analyses are methodically addressed in this context. By elucidating the interplay between geometric flaws, bending circumstances, and material behavior, the results are anticipated to further knowledge of rail damage processes. Additionally, it is thought that by providing recommendations for enhancing rail manufacturing, bending, and maintenance procedures, the study’s findings would advance field-based damage assessments, which is very briefly discussed in the literature.

Significant novelty is added to the research by the fact that the rails under investigation are brand-new and have not experienced any service loads. This makes it possible to precisely link the observed crack and fracture behaviors with effects resulting from manufacturing, bending operations, and geometric flaws by removing the impacts of wear, rolling contact fatigue, and cumulative damage that may occur throughout the operating process. In this manner, the mechanisms of pre-service damage formation can be more clearly elucidated, and a substantial contribution is made to the comprehension of early-stage damage processes, which are typically challenging to isolate in studies conducted on rails subjected to operating conditions in the literature.

Limitations of the study

There are certain constraints associated with this investigation. First and foremost, the investigation is restricted to only three samples in which damage was observed, and it does not offer a dataset that is sufficiently large to facilitate statistical generalization. Additionally, the direct generalization of the findings to various rail types and configurations is impeded by the fact that the examined rails are comprised of a specific material class (R260) and a specific bend geometry (a curve with a radius of approximately 45 m). Nevertheless, load repetitions, dynamic effects, and long-term fatigue behavior that arise under operational circumstances cannot be evaluated since the rails examined in this research were in their pre-service state. Instead of measuring the stresses created during the bending process, the damage that resulted was used to interpret the results. Additionally, there was little quantitative research done on production fluctuations, environmental impacts, and possible residual strains. Notwithstanding these drawbacks, the study’s emphasis on pre-service damage accumulation fills a gap in the literature and lays the groundwork for future research employing bigger datasets, real-time observations, and numerical modeling.

Microstructure investigations

Within the scope of this investigation, metallographic sections that were generated from the fracture start zone, which were perpendicular to the crack plane, subjected to microstructural analysis. Pearlitic formations has been identified as the microstructure of the rail. Even at the grain boundaries, there is just a trace quantity of ferrite phase present. The ferrite ratio, on the other hand, has a tendency to increase as it approaches the surface at the foot of the rail. The process of decarburization is responsible for the increase in the amount of ferrite as shown in Figs. 6 and 7. Characteristic fracture contact fatigue damage cracks are shown in Fig. 6. These cracks include fractures and cracks that propagate parallel to the fracture, as well as fragments from the fracture plastic deformation zone that are located below the surface and are subjected to maximal shear loads. One of the most visible formations near the rail foot is the folds that are present on the surface. The fracture surface is parallel to the folds, as well as the crack-like indentations that are the consequence of the folds. In Figs. 6 and 7, arrows illustrate some examples of these crack-shaped indentations that were found in the material. Iron oxide deposits were observed piled up within the crevices of the structure. It has been presented that the folds and indentations were produced as a result of rolling, and that they constitute a flaw in the manufacturing process. Not only have falls of a similar kind been discovered on the surface, but they have also been discovered just below the surface, at depths totaling several hundred microns as shown in Fig. 8.

Transversely cracked rails

The cross-sectional region of fracture weld joints is the most common location for the development of transverse fractures. There are two possible causes of a welding fault: a variance in the weld material or a flaw in the fabrication of the rail. The center of the rail head or the rail foot is the origin of transverse cracks.

Finite element model

In earlier sections, it was reported that the bending process resulted in the formation of rail samples that were horizontally fractured. Furthermore, it was mentioned that the fractures occurred at the point where the rail body meets the rail base. Figure 17 illustrates the technique of bending the rails, which is discussed in this section, as well as the relative arrangement of the rails. The mechanism for bending the rail operates according to the idea that three cylindrical rollers spin and grab in the direction of the rail length axis. This causes the rail to bend at three different spots without any interruptions. In the numerical analysis, these components and the force flow were modeled with the contact algorithm. It is not the purpose of this research to carry out a comprehensive validation of fracture mechanics; rather, the objective is to get to the bottom of the observed fracture behavior by locating crucial stress areas that take place during bending. It was for this reason that the model was used to identify the precise site of stress concentrations, rather than to forecast the absolute fracture load or the course that cracks would take to propagate.

The device used for bending the rails: (a) Top view, (b) Side view, (c) Examples of bent rails.

The mesh pattern used in modeling, the geometry of the bending rollers and the way they fit into the rail profile can be observed in Fig. 18. During the process of developing the numerical model, the rail structure was discretized by hexagonal finite elements with eight nodes, and the cylindrical rolling mill structures were represented by shell elements with four nodes. In the numerical model; a thermo-viscoplastic material model with integrated regularized failure and damage algorithm (*MAT_224) is utilized to represent the rails46. This model is selected since it can provide thermal and strain rate dependent behavior with coupled failure model that is also temperature, strain rate and state-of-stress dependent while it is regularized to avoid any mesh size dependency. Rigid material models were used for the rollers. The generic mechanical properties of the materials used were taken as shown in Table 3. A surface_to_surface47 contact algorithm was defined that enables the transfer of loads between the rail material and the three rolling elements as shown in Fig. 18-f representing the contact pairs that are interacting and force flow is directly maintained by the finite element segments shown. In the algorithm, a time-dependent progression was defined to perform three-point bending operation with the rotational motion of the rolls. One roller will remain fixed, while the other two rollers will move in the same direction and try to bend the rail. As mentioned in Fig. 12, the rail section does not comply with standard dimensions. The vertical axis was off-center, although it should pass through the middle of the body wall thickness. Therefore, the spaces A and B where the cylindrical bending mandrels fit were not of equal size as shown in Fig. 19. This defect causes one of the mandrels to fit only the middle part of the rail body, while the other fits the mushroom and foot part. When the rail is bent in this way, the stress distribution is different from the normal 3-point bending distribution and the stress is concentrated on the line where the crack occurs (body-base junction line) as shown in Fig. 20. This stress distribution, combined with internal structural defects in the line, where transverse cracks were present, was seen as the main reason for the cracking of the rails during bending. The places that are represented by the color red are those that have the greatest comparable stress. This line cracked horizontally.

The key findings of finite element analysis show the stress distribution and areas favorable for fracture initiation. Extensive fracture mechanics parameters and particular crack development models are required for quantitative modeling of crack propagation; however, they are not within the scope of this work.

Figures 21 and 22 illustrate the location of the crack initiation at the root of the rail, and then the transient analyses show how the crack gradually propagated towards the column section of the profile. It is widely recognized in the literature that deterioration does not entirely vanish following heat rolling48. The omission to simulate the fracture as an obvious geometric discontinuity and the disregard for residual stresses are intentional modeling decisions made within the parameters of the research and have no effect on the validity of the findings. Instead of utilizing a discrete geometry technique to portray crack activity, this investigation uses a continuous damage approach. It should be noted that the imperfection due to prior production of the rail by hot rolling provides an off-center column geometry. There is no other imperfection considered within the analysis. This off-center geometry is due to prior rolling of the rails in hot rolling. Therefore, no residual stresses were expected within the cross-section, but an eccentric column defect has been modelled on the finite element model as an input for the bending procedure. It is shown that the bending procedure of the rail then forced the eccentric section in a way that the force flow was not perfectly achieved, and the crack initiated first in the lateral direction due to stress localization and then propagated longitudinally towards the column section.

Conclusion

Considering the investigations, the following conclusions were reached regarding rail damage. It was observed that the vertically fracture rail material complies with the EN 13674 R260 standard in terms of chemical composition and mechanical values. It was found that the cause of the breakage was a crack that had grown at the toe of the rail. The fracture developed in two phases and eventually reached a size that was considered to be serious. Critical circumstance occurred during 35-ton machine passing, breaking to the point of fracture. The fracture, which begins at the top corner of the rail support, is thought to be a manufacturing problem that resulted from the rail’s lack of prior usage and was exacerbated during the bending process as a result of a manufacturing mistake. Crack formation was hastened by discontinuities in the region where the fractures originated, such as curves and sharp indentations. These gaps were rail production related. For transversely cracked rails, it was found that the rail material complies with the EN 13674 R260 standard in terms of chemical composition and mechanical values. In the section where the crack occurred, it was observed that there were internal structural defects and discontinuities originating from rail production in the crack plane. The crack formed during the bending process followed these discontinuities. In addition, the profile of the rail did not comply with the dimensions specified in the standard. The vertical axis of the rail section was 2–3 mm off. It is believed that the bending mandrels’ improper seating and the uneven gap between the rail profiles are caused by the geometric abnormalities in the rail. The rail is believed to have developed cracks along a transverse line from the body-base connection as a result of this alignment issue. The development of this mechanism is thought to have been aided by internal structural flaws along the crack route.

Scope for future work

It is recommended that a more comprehensive and meticulous extraction of data and information pertaining to each rail break be carried out. The differences in hardness between the rails and the wheels of railway vehicles should be investigated to see how it affects the wear on the wheels.
