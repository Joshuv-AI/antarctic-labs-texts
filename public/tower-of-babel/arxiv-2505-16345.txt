Abstract
The finite element solution of Helmholtz problems near resonant or quasi-resonant frequencies poses significant challenges, as iterative solvers typically suffer from severely degraded convergence. We analyze the convergence behavior of GMRES applied to linear systems arising from such configurations. Theoretical convergence estimates are derived based on harmonic Ritz values, highlighting their proximity to small eigenvalues as a key determining factor. We further examine deflation strategies and their interplay with preconditioning techniques, using the Complex Shifted Laplacian preconditioner as a case study. Numerical experiments on resonant and quasi-resonant test cases validate the theoretical framework and demonstrate the effectiveness of deflation strategies. This study provides new insights and practical guidance for analyzing and improving iterative solvers for time-harmonic problems near resonances.



Keywords:

Helmholtz equation, resonances, finite elements, harmonic Ritz values, deflation, preconditioning

1
Introduction

The solution of time-harmonic wave propagation problems is of paramount importance in many fields of science and engineering, including acoustics, electromagnetism, and structural mechanics.
One of the most fundamental equation for modeling scalar waves is the Helmholtz equation,

\displaystyle-\Delta u-k^{2}u=f,

(1)

where
k\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the wavenumber,
u
is an unknown field, and
f
is a given source.
This equation is completed with boundary conditions.
Here, we are interested in the solution of (
1
) when
k^{2}
is close to an eigenvalue of the Laplacian.
Discretizing the resulting problem with the finite element method (FEM) leads to a linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}
, where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is a sparse non-singular matrix which may be non-symmetric or non-Hermitian,
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
is unknown,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
is given.
To ensure solution accuracy, the number of degrees of freedom per wavelength must be sufficiently high, as it must increase faster than the wavenumber to mitigate the pollution effect
[
1
]
.
For example, in dimension
2
, the element diameter
h
should reduce faster than
k^{-3/2}
as the wavenumber
k
increases for piecewise linear elements (
P_{1}
).
Consequently, for large wavenumber problems, the resulting linear system can be extremely large.

Direct solvers are expensive and difficult to parallelize.
Solving finite element problems on structured grids yields an algorithmic complexity of
\mathcal{O}(N^{3/2})
in 2D and
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
in 3D
[
17
]
.
Iterative methods, such as Krylov subspace methods, are more commonly used to solve large-scale problems.
These methods are easier to parallelize since they only require matrix-vector product computations.
The key challenge lies in controlling the number of iterations needed to achieve a given tolerance
[
41
]
.

For non-normal matrices, the Generalized Minimal Residual (GMRES) method is a Krylov method of choice
[
40
]
.
The GMRES convergence has been widely studied and several bounds have been established, for instance
[
44
,
53
,
46
,
4
,
20
,
9
]
.
However, there is no universally applicable estimate for all classes of matrices
[
19
]
.
In particular, GMRES convergence in the case of discretized Helmholtz problems for different scenarios remains an open problem due to its highly indefinite or non-self-adjoint nature, as seen in previous studies
[
25
,
26
,
32
]
.
In addition, when the wavenumber is close to a resonance value, the number of GMRES iterations required to achieve a given tolerance can significantly increase.
Efficient acceleration techniques tailored for this class of problems must be used
[
41
,
55
]
.
This topic is currently the focus of extensive research within the community. Acceleration techniques have been developed specifically for Helmholtz problems and more generally for non-normal cases, for example Complex Shifted Laplacian (CSL) preconditioning strategies
[
22
,
23
,
13
,
29
,
38
,
39
]
, domain decomposition methods
[
16
,
15
]
, and deflation techniques
[
30
,
18
,
46
]
.

In this work, we focus on GMRES convergence for Helmholtz problems close to resonances.
In a closed domain, a resonance is a (real) wavenumber
k
such that
k^{2}
is an eigenvalue of the Laplace operator. In an unbounded domain, resonances are complex, but some of them approach the real axis. Their real parts are called quasi-resonances, and are wavenumbers for which the norm of the inverse of the Helmholtz operator grows rapidly.
Previous studies
[
32
]
provided estimates on the increase in the number of GMRES iterations for a sequence of quasi-modes as the frequency increases.
Here, we aim at a new understanding of these convergence behaviors based on
GMRES approximation of the underlying the properties of the linear system.
We also provide suggestions for studying the robustness of iterative solvers in these cases.
The contribution of deflation, which can be combined with preconditioning techniques, is highlighted.
This is particularly interesting for cases involving quasi-modes and modes.

The main aim of this paper is to investigate the relationships between GMRES convergence rates and the properties of linear systems associated with Helmholtz problems near resonances.
More specifically our main contributions are:

•

to generalize a GMRES residual bound based on harmonic Ritz values;

•

to show that the approximation of every eigenvalue or group of eigenvalues by harmonic Ritz values affects convergence;

•

to apply deflation techniques with a suitable choice of eigenvectors adapted to resonance problems to accelerate GMRES convergence and combine them with a preconditioner;

•

to understand the numerical behavior of GMRES for different scenarios and acceleration methods.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows.
Section

2
introduces the GMRES method and reviews key convergence properties. We then extend a GMRES residual bound based on harmonic Ritz values, showing that when these values approach a subset of eigenvalues closely enough, the corresponding eigenvalues cease to hinder convergence. In
Section

3
, we present the continuous and discretized Helmholtz problem and the settings in which resonances and quasi-resonances arise.
To efficiently solve the problem for cases near to resonances or quasi-resonances, we study and highlight the utility of a deflation technique, and we investigate its combination with a preconditioning strategy.
For this analysis, we selected the Complex Shifted Laplacian (CSL) preconditioner with an incomplete LU (ILU) factorization as a case of study.
Finally, in
Sections

4
and
5
, we investigate numerical benchmarks near resonant regimes. These cases illustrate how the proposed tools influence GMRES convergence and allow us to assess the effectiveness and robustness of the acceleration techniques.

2
GMRES

The Generalized Minimal Residual (GMRES) method
[
40
]
is a popular iterative algorithm for solving general linear systems.
In this section, we introduce its main components and discuss a standard convergence result.
We then propose new convergence results based on harmonic Ritz (HR) values, which allow a better interpretation of the GMRES residual evolution.

2.1
Key ideas

The GMRES method belongs to the class of Krylov subspace iterative methods, leveraging the Krylov subspace defined as

\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{r}_{0}):=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{r}_{0},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{r}_{0},\mathbf{A}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{A}^{l-1}\mathbf{r}_{0}\},\quad\text{for $l<N$},

where
\mathbf{r}_{0}:=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{0}
is the initial residual and
\mathbf{x}_{0}
is an initial guess.
At each iteration
l<N
, the approximate solution
\mathbf{x}_{l}
belongs to
\mathbf{x}_{0}+\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{r}_{0})
, and it is computed such that the residual
\mathbf{r}_{l}=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{l}
has minimum norm, i.e.

\mathbf{x}_{l}:=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{x}_{0}+\mathcal{K}_{l}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{r}_{0})}\|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}.

(2)

A critical component of GMRES is the Arnoldi process, which generates an orthonormal basis for the Krylov subspace.
The basis vectors are the columns of a matrix
\mathbf{U}_{l}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times l}
.
This basis is used to compute the restriction of
\mathbf{A}
to the Krylov subspace,
\mathbf{H}_{l}=\mathbf{U}_{l}^{*}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{U}_{l}\in\mathbb{C}^{l\times l}
, which is an upper Hessenberg matrix.
Each iteration of the GMRES algorithm can be summarized as follows: perform the Arnoldi process to compute
\mathbf{U}_{l}
and
\mathbf{H}_{l}
, then solve the least-squares problem
\min_{\mathbf{y}}\|\beta\mathbf{e}_{1}-\mathbf{H}_{l}\mathbf{y}\|_{2}
with
\beta=\|\mathbf{r}_{0}\|_{2}
and
\mathbf{e}_{1}
the first canonical vector, and finally form the approximate solution
\mathbf{x}_{l}=\mathbf{x}_{0}+\mathbf{U}_{l}\mathbf{y}
, see e.g.
[
41
]
.

2.2
Convergence results

Several convergence bounds have been developed to analyze the behavior of GMRES, often by expressing the residual vector in terms of polynomials; see, for example,
[
19
]
. The residual at iteration
l
satisfies the following relation:

\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}=\min_{q_{l}\in\mathcal{P}_{l}^{1}}\left\lVert q_{l}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{r}_{0}\right\rVert_{2},

(3)

where
\mathcal{P}_{l}^{1}
is the set of polynomials of degree at most
l
with the constraint
q_{l}(0)=1
. Throughout this paper, we denote by
p_{l}\in\mathcal{P}_{l}^{1}
the polynomial that achieves this minimum, such that

\mathbf{r}_{l}=p_{l}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{r}_{0}.

(4)

GMRES convergence can be understood in terms of the eigenvalue distribution of
\mathbf{A}
and the conditioning of its eigenvectors. If
\mathbf{A}
is diagonalizable, we can write
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{-1}
. For any polynomial
q_{l}\in\mathcal{P}_{l}^{1}
, it follows that:

\left\lVert q_{l}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{r}_{0}\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\mathbf{V}q_{l}(\mathbf{\Lambda})\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{r}_{0}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\kappa_{2}(\mathbf{V})\left\lVert q_{l}(\mathbf{\Lambda})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{0}\right\rVert_{2},

where
\kappa_{2}(\mathbf{V}):=\left\lVert\mathbf{V}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{V}^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}
is the condition number of the eigenvector matrix. This leads to the classical convergence bound
[
40
]
:

\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{0}\right\rVert_{2}}\leq\kappa_{2}(\mathbf{V})\min_{q_{l}\in\mathcal{P}_{l}^{1}}\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(\mathbf{A})}\left\lvert q_{l}(\lambda)\right\rvert,

(5)

where
\sigma(\mathbf{A})
denotes the set of eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
. If
\mathbf{A}
is normal, then it is possible to choose
\kappa_{2}(\mathbf{V})=1
, and the convergence depends solely on the eigenvalue distribution. In contrast, for non-normal matrices,
\kappa_{2}(\mathbf{V})
can be large, making the bound in (
5
) less predictive of actual convergence behavior
[
19
]
.

Alternative convergence bounds relate to other properties of
\mathbf{A}
, such as its field of values
[
4
,
20
,
14
]
or its pseudospectrum
[
50
]
.
While these bounds have distinct advantages, none of them provides an accurate or systematic prediction of GMRES convergence.
Moreover, they describe global decay rates of the GMRES residual
[
19
]
, by considering the norm of the residual at iteration
l
relative to the initial residual.
They do not capture specific and local convergence behaviors, such as stagnation phases followed by rapid decays.
Such patterns can be observed in the GMRES residual history, and can occur close to resonances (see
Figure

3
and
Sections

4
and
5
).
To better understand this phenomenon, it is crucial to highlight the role of small eigenvalues related to these resonances.
This need motivates our interest in harmonic Ritz values.
These results also highlight how both spectral and non-normality properties of
\mathbf{A}
shape the convergence of GMRES.

2.3
Harmonic Ritz values

Harmonic Ritz (HR) values provide estimates of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
at a given iteration
l
(see for example
[
50
, Chapter 26]
).
In the following, we assume that GMRES does not stagnate up to iteration
l
.
They are defined as follows:

Definition 2.1

(Harmonic Ritz (HR) values)
.

At iteration
l>0
, the
harmonic Ritz values

{\{\nu_{j}^{(l)}\}}_{j=1}^{l}
are the roots of the minimizing polynomial
p_{l}\in\mathcal{P}_{l}^{1}
from (
3
).

By construction, the number of HR values increases with each GMRES iteration. Since
p_{l}(0)=1
, none of the HR values can be zero. As a result, the polynomial
p_{l}
can be factored as:

p_{l}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left(1-\frac{z}{\nu_{j}^{(l)}}\right).

(6)

The HR values satisfy a useful lower bound:

Proposition 2.2

(Lower bound
[
10
, Theorem 4]
)
.

Assume
\mathbf{A}
is non-singular.
At iteration
l>0
, the HR values satisfy

\left\lvert\nu_{j}^{(l)}\right\rvert\geq s_{\min}(\mathbf{A}),\quad\forall j=1,\ldots,l,

where
s_{\min}(\mathbf{A})
is the smallest singular value of
\mathbf{A}
.

As iterations progress, the HR values tend to approach the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
increasingly well. They converge to the full spectrum after
N
iterations, or earlier in cases of “lucky breakdown”
[
50
, Chapter 26]
. Proposition
2.2
guarantees that these approximations remain bounded away from zero as long as
\mathbf{A}
is non-singular.

Remark 2.3

(Ritz values)
.

The Ritz values are the eigenvalues of the upper Hessenberg matrix
\mathbf{H}_{l}
, and they coincide with the roots of the minimizing polynomial in the Full Orthogonalization Method (FOM). Like the HR values, Ritz values serve as approximations of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
. In
[
52
]
, Ritz values are used to analyze the convergence of the conjugate gradient method for symmetric positive definite systems. This approach is extended in
[
53
]
to study the convergence of GMRES for general non-normal matrices. However, because Ritz values are associated with FOM rather than GMRES, the analysis must relate the residual norms of the two methods.
As a result, the residual bounds derived from Ritz values involve a factor larger than
1
that depends on the iteration
[
53
, Theorems 2.5 and 2.6]
.
A more natural bound can be obtained using HR values
[
10
]
, leading to a similar result as in
[
53
]
, but improved in that the factor no longer appears
[
10
, Theorem 6]
.

2.4
Convergence results with HR values

For the reasons stated in Remark
2.3
, Cao
[
10
]
proposed such a bound by linking GMRES convergence to the approximation of the smallest eigenvalue by an HR value. Building on this idea, we introduce two generalizations that consider the approximation of an entire subset of eigenvalues by corresponding HR values, rather than just a single one. This leads to a natural partition of the spectrum into two groups: the eigenvalues closely approximated by HR values, and the remaining ones. This strategy is reminiscent of spectral clustering techniques that separate eigenvalue distributions into dominant clusters and peripheral outliers
[
9
,
32
]
.

To formalize this spectral separation and derive quantitative bounds, we now consider the application of GMRES to a linear system of the form
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
, with
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
. We denote the residual vector at iteration
l
by
\mathbf{r}_{l}
.
Ultimately, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
will come from the finite elements discretization of a physical problem.
The convergence results presented in this section then rely on the following assumption.

Assumption 2.4
.

The matrix
\mathbf{A}
is non-singular and diagonalizable.

We aim to analyze the residual evolution between two iterations,
l
and
l+m
, assuming
0<l<l+m<N
. We begin with a preliminary result that sets the stage for the generalized bounds.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.4
holds, and let
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{-1}
be an eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{A}
, with
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})
.
Let
J\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
, and consider a set
N_{J}^{(l)}
of
J
HR values at iteration
l
, along with a set
\Lambda_{J}
of
J
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
, such that no HR value in
N_{J}^{(l)}
is an eigenvalue in
\Lambda_{J}^{c}:=\sigma(\mathbf{A})\setminus\Lambda_{J}
.
Then, for any
m>0
such that
l+m<N
, the following bound holds:

\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\rVert_{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}}\leq\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\left\lVert q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}\right\rVert_{2},

where

\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}:=\mathbf{V}\,\mathrm{diag}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{N})\,\mathbf{V}^{-1},\quad\text{with}\quad s_{i}:=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J},\\
s_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(7)

and

s_{J}^{l}(z):=n_{J}(z)/d_{J}^{l}(z)

(8)

with

n_{J}(z):=\prod_{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{J}}\left(1-\frac{z}{\lambda_{j}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad d_{J}^{l}(z):=\prod_{\nu_{j}^{(l)}\in N_{J}^{(l)}}\left(1-\frac{z}{\nu_{j}^{(l)}}\right).

(9)

Proof.

Recall from (
4
) that
\mathbf{r}_{l}=p_{l}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{r}_{0}
, where
p_{l}\in\mathcal{P}_{l}^{1}
is the GMRES minimizing polynomial at iteration
l
.
Since the HR values in
N_{J}^{(l)}
are roots of
p_{l}
(
6
), one can write
p_{l}=d_{J}^{l}\,\hat{p}_{l-J}
, where
\hat{p}_{l-J}
is a polynomial of degree
l-J
.
For any
q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}
, the polynomial

\tilde{p}_{l+m}:=q_{m}\,n_{J}\,\hat{p}_{l-J}

has degree at most
l+m
and satisfies
\tilde{p}_{l+m}(0)=q_{m}(0)\,n_{J}(0)\,\hat{p}_{l-J}(0)=1
, since
\hat{p}_{l-J}(0)=p_{l}(0)/d_{J}^{l}(0)=1
.
Therefore,
\tilde{p}_{l+m}\in\mathcal{P}_{l+m}^{1}
.
For any eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
, one has

\tilde{p}_{l+m}(\lambda_{i})=q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\,s_{i}\,p_{l}(\lambda_{i}).

(10)

Indeed, if
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}
, then one has
s_{i}=0
by definition and
\tilde{p}_{l+m}(\lambda_{i})=0
because
n_{J}(\lambda_{i})=0
.
If
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}
, then one can write

\tilde{p}_{l+m}(\lambda_{i})=q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\,\frac{n_{J}(\lambda_{i})}{d_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})}\,d_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})\,\hat{p}_{l-J}(\lambda_{i})=q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\,s_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})\,p_{l}(\lambda_{i})=q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\,s_{i}\,p_{l}(\lambda_{i})

since
d_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})\neq 0
by assumption.
Applying (
10
) to all the eigenvalues and using the basis of eigenvectors gives

\tilde{p}_{l+m}(\mathbf{A})=q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}\,p_{l}(\mathbf{A}),

(11)

with
\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}
defined by (
7
).
From the GMRES optimality property (
3
) at iteration
l+m
, we have:

\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\rVert_{2}=\min_{q_{l+m}\in\mathcal{P}_{l+m}^{1}}\left\lVert q_{l+m}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{r}_{0}\right\rVert_{2}.

In particular, using
\tilde{p}_{l+m}
yields:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\tilde{p}_{l+m}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{r}_{0}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}\,p_{l}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{r}_{0}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}\,\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}\right\rVert_{2}\,\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}.

Taking the minimum over all
q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma

2.5
provides a general residual estimate based on the positions of the HR values relative to the eigenvalues. To make this bound more explicit and interpretable, we now use the eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{A}
to express the norm of
q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}
in spectral terms. This leads to our first main result, which quantifies the effect of the remaining eigenvalues—those not approximated by HR values—on the residual evolution.

Theorem 2.6

(First residual estimate)
.

If the assumptions of
Lemma

2.5
hold, then, for iterations
l
and
l+m
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\rVert_{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}}\leq\left(\sum_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}\kappa(\lambda_{i})\right)\ \max_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}\left\lvert s_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})\right\rvert\ \min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\max_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}\left\lvert q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\right\rvert,

with
\Lambda_{J}^{c}:=\sigma(\mathbf{A})\setminus\Lambda_{J}
and
\kappa(\lambda_{i}):=\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}
, where
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
are the left and right eigenvectors associated to
\lambda_{i}\in\sigma(\mathbf{A})
, with
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{i}=1
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{A}
is diagonalizable, it admits the decomposition
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{-1}
, where
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
is the matrix of right eigenvectors, and
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})
is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. We denote by
\mathbf{v}_{i}
the
i
-th column of
\mathbf{V}
(the right eigenvector associated with
\lambda_{i}
), and by
\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{*}
the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{V}^{-1}
, corresponding to the conjugate transpose of the left eigenvector associated with
\lambda_{i}
(see
[
50
, Chapter 52]
).

By using the eigenvalue decomposition and
Lemma

2.5
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\rVert_{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\left\lVert\mathbf{V}q_{m}\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}\right)\mathrm{diag}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{N})\mathbf{V}^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\left\lVert\sum_{\lambda_{i}\in\sigma(\mathbf{A})}q_{m}(\lambda_{i})s_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}.

Because
s_{i}=0
for all
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}
and
s_{i}=s_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})
for all
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}
, by (
7
), the sum can be restricted to
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}
, leading to

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\rVert_{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\left\lVert\sum_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}q_{m}(\lambda_{i})s_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})\mathbf{v}_{i}\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\sum_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}\left\lvert q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\right\rvert\left\lvert s_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})\right\rvert\left\lVert\mathbf{v}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i}^{*}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\sum_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}\left\lvert q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\right\rvert\left\lvert s_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})\right\rvert\kappa(\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}\kappa(\lambda_{i})\right)\max_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}\left\lvert s_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})\right\rvert\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\max_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}\left\lvert q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\right\rvert.

∎

Interpretation

If, at iteration
l
, the HR values in
N_{J}^{(l)}
closely approach the eigenvalues in
\Lambda_{J}
, then the ratio
\max_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}\left\lvert s_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})\right\rvert
approaches 1.
Moreover, these eigenvalues are no longer involved in the
\min\max
problem, and their condition numbers are removed from the sum:
the convergence rate for subsequent iterations is effectively governed only by the remaining eigenvalues in
\Lambda_{J}^{c}
. For highly non-normal problems, the influence of the eigenvalues in
\Lambda_{J}
may be indirectly reflected in high values of the remaining
\kappa(\lambda_{i})
, but in any case, the term related to non-normality decreases as the number of iterations increases.

An important advantage of expressing the convergence rate using the ratio
\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\rVert_{2}/\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}
is that it allows for a stage-by-stage analysis of the convergence process. This formulation captures the evolution of the convergence rate between any two iterations and helps interpret the typically nonlinear behavior of GMRES convergence.

Remark 2.7

(Link with Cao
[
10
]
)
.

The convergence bound proposed in
[
10
, Theorem 6]
is given by:

\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\rVert_{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\rVert_{2}}\leq\kappa_{2}(\mathbf{V})\max_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{1}}\left\lvert\frac{1-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{1}}}{1-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\nu_{1}^{(l)}}}\right\rvert\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\max_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{1}}\left\lvert q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\right\rvert,

where
\lambda_{1}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
, and
\nu_{1}^{(l)}
is an HR value at iteration
l
.
This bound focuses on the approximation of a single eigenvalue and includes the global condition number
\kappa_{2}(\mathbf{V})
, which reflects the influence of all eigenvectors and the overall non-normality of
\mathbf{A}
.

Note that the bound in
Theorem

2.6
also holds with
\sum_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}\kappa(\lambda_{i})
replaced by
\kappa_{2}(\mathbf{V})
.
An advantage of
\sum_{\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}}\kappa(\lambda_{i})
is that as the iterations progress, more and more eigenvalues are well approximated by the HR values, so their condition numbers are removed from the sum.
Thus, the non-normality measured by the bound decreases.
We can expect that, asymptotically, this version of the bound is more accurate.

The bound in
Theorem

2.6
provides a residual estimate based on eigenvalue separation using the eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{A}
. However, when
\mathbf{A}
is non-normal or nearly defective, its eigenvalues alone may not fully capture the convergence behavior of GMRES. In such cases, a more robust analysis can be achieved using the concept of the
pseudospectrum
, which accounts for the sensitivity of the spectrum to perturbations and highlights the influence of non-normality.

Theorem 2.8

(Second residual estimate)
.

Let the assumptions of
Lemma

2.5
hold and let
\Gamma_{J}^{c}
be a finite union of disjoint, positively oriented Jordan curves with disjoint interiors, enclosing the eigenvalues in
\Lambda_{J}^{c}
of
\mathbf{A}
and no other eigenvalues.
Suppose there exists a constant
\varepsilon_{J}^{c}>0
such that

\left\|\left(\mathbf{A}-z\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{J}^{c}},\quad\forall z\in\Gamma_{J}^{c}.

We assume that the HR values in
N_{J}^{(l)}
lie outside
\Gamma_{J}^{c}
and the regions it encloses.
Then, for iteration indices
l
and
l+m
, the residuals satisfy:

\frac{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\|_{2}}\leq\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{J}^{c})}{2\pi\varepsilon_{J}^{c}}\cdot\max_{z\in\Gamma_{J}^{c}}|s_{J}^{l}(z)|\cdot\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\max_{z\in\Gamma_{J}^{c}}|q_{m}(z)|,

where
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{J}^{c})
denotes the total length of
\Gamma_{J}^{c}
.

Proof.

The spectral projector associated with
\Lambda_{J}^{c}
(see
[
42
, Sections 3.1.3–3.1.4]
) is defined by

\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}:=\frac{-1}{2\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{J}^{c}}{\left(\mathbf{A}-z\mathbf{I}\right)}^{-1}\mathrm{d}z.

Let
\mathbf{v}_{i}
denote the
i
-th column of
\mathbf{V}
, so that
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}
and
\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{e}_{i}
, the
i
-th canonical vector of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
Then, for any polynomial
q_{m}
and all
z\notin\sigma(\mathbf{A})
,

q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{v}_{i}=q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\mathbf{v}_{i},\qquad{(\mathbf{A}-z\mathbf{I})}^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{i}}{\lambda_{i}-z},\qquad\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}\mathbf{v}_{i}=s_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i},

(12)

where the last identity follows from
\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{V}\,\mathrm{diag}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{N})\,\mathbf{e}_{i}
by (
7
), with
s_{i}=0
when
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}
.
Moreover, by the residue theorem, since
\Gamma_{J}^{c}
is positively oriented and encloses the eigenvalues in
\Lambda_{J}^{c}
and no other eigenvalues (see also
[
42
, Theorem 3.3, Lemma 3.1]
),

\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\left(\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{J}^{c}}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z-\lambda_{i}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{i}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{v}_{i}&\text{if }\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c},\\
\mathbf{0}&\text{if }\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}.\end{cases}

(13)

Consequently,
\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}\mathbf{v}_{i}=s_{i}\,\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}\mathbf{v}_{i}=s_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}=\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}\mathbf{v}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
, and since the
\mathbf{v}_{i}
form a basis of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
,
\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}=\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}
.
Besides,
\mathbf{A}
commutes with
\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}
(see
[
42
, Section 3.1.4]
).
Then, from
Lemma

2.5
,
\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}=\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}
and
q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}=\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}\,q_{m}(\mathbf{A})
, we have:

\frac{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\|_{2}}\leq\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\left\|\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}\,q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}\right\|_{2}.

(14)

We now show that

\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}\,q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}=\frac{-1}{2\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{J}^{c}}(\mathbf{A}-z\mathbf{I})^{-1}q_{m}(z)s_{J}^{l}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

(15)

As above, it is enough to check that both sides of (
15
) coincide on each
\mathbf{v}_{i}
.
For the left-hand side, (
12
) and (
13
) give

\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}\,q_{m}(\mathbf{A})\,\mathbf{S}_{J}^{l}\,\mathbf{v}_{i}=q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\,s_{i}\,\mathbf{P}_{J}^{c}\mathbf{v}_{i}=q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\,s_{i}\,\mathbf{v}_{i},

since
s_{i}=0
when
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}
.
For the right-hand side, by (
12
), it remains to show that

\frac{-1}{2\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{J}^{c}}\frac{q_{m}(z)\,s_{J}^{l}(z)}{\lambda_{i}-z}\,\mathrm{d}z=q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\,s_{i}=\begin{cases}q_{m}(\lambda_{i})\,s_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})&\text{if }\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c},\\
0&\text{if }\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}.\end{cases}

By assumption, the poles of
q_{m}\,s_{J}^{l}
, which are HR values in
N_{J}^{(l)}
, lie outside
\Gamma_{J}^{c}
, so that
q_{m}\,s_{J}^{l}
is holomorphic inside and on
\Gamma_{J}^{c}
.
The first case then follows from Cauchy’s integral formula, since
\lambda_{i}
lies inside
\Gamma_{J}^{c}
.
In the second case,
\lambda_{i}
lies outside
\Gamma_{J}^{c}
, so that
z\mapsto q_{m}(z)\,s_{J}^{l}(z)/(z-\lambda_{i})
is holomorphic inside and on
\Gamma_{J}^{c}
, so the integral vanishes by Cauchy’s theorem.
Note that
s_{J}^{l}
is never evaluated at the eigenvalues in
\Lambda_{J}
, so that this holds even if some of them are HR values in
N_{J}^{(l)}
.

Using (
15
) in (
14
), we derive:

\frac{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\|_{2}}\leq\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{J}^{c}}|q_{m}(z)|\cdot|s_{J}^{l}(z)|\cdot\left\|{(\mathbf{A}-z\mathbf{I})}^{-1}\right\|_{2}\,\mathrm{d}z.

Bounding the integrand by its maximum on
\Gamma_{J}^{c}
, we find:

\frac{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\|_{2}}\leq\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{J}^{c})}{2\pi}\cdot\max_{z\in\Gamma_{J}^{c}}|s_{J}^{l}(z)|\cdot\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\max_{z\in\Gamma_{J}^{c}}|q_{m}(z)|\cdot\max_{z\in\Gamma_{J}^{c}}\left\|{(\mathbf{A}-z\mathbf{I})}^{-1}\right\|_{2}.

Applying the resolvent bound yields the desired result:

\frac{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{l+m}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\mathbf{r}_{l}\right\|_{2}}\leq\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{J}^{c})}{2\pi\varepsilon_{J}^{c}}\cdot\max_{z\in\Gamma_{J}^{c}}|s_{J}^{l}(z)|\cdot\min_{q_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}^{1}}\max_{z\in\Gamma_{J}^{c}}|q_{m}(z)|.

∎

Interpretation

In
Theorem

2.8
, the convergence bound involves a maximum over the contour
\Gamma_{J}^{c}
, which is a union of closed curves in the complex plane. The choice of this contour is restricted by the requirement that the resolvent norm
\|{(\mathbf{A}-z\mathbf{I})}^{-1}\|_{2}
remains bounded above by
1/\varepsilon_{J}^{c}
for all
z\in\Gamma_{J}^{c}
.
Different choices of
\varepsilon_{J}^{c}
lead to different bounds.
In cases where
\mathbf{A}
is highly non-normal, especially when eigenvalues are clustered around a non-normal eigenvalue, ensuring that this bound is useful in practice may force
\varepsilon_{J}^{c}
to be very small.
As a result, even if an HR value is close to such an eigenvalue, its influence can still be felt implicitly in the convergence bound via the smallness of
\varepsilon_{J}^{c}
.

Then, both the
pseudospectrum-based
bound (
Theorem

2.8
) and the
spectrum-based
bound (
Theorem

2.6
) are influenced by non-normal eigenvalues in
\bm{\Lambda_{J}}
indirectly through the geometry (i.e. angles between eigenvectors) and norm behavior on the contour.
This influence decreases as increasingly more eigenvalues are approximated by the HR values.

3
Helmholtz problem and acceleration techniques

In this section, we introduce the Helmholtz boundary value problem, the linear system arising from its finite element discretization, and techniques to accelerate the iterative solution process. We also discuss the difficulties caused by resonances and quasi-resonances occurring near the wavenumber, which can significantly impair convergence.

3.1
Boundary value problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma
, which is partitioned into three disjoint parts:
\Gamma=\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}
, corresponding to Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin boundary segments, respectively.
The Helmholtz problem is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u-k^{2}u&=f&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=g_{\mathrm{D}}&&\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}},\\
\partial_{\bm{n}}u&=g_{\mathrm{N}}&&\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}},\\
\partial_{\bm{n}}u+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muku&=0&&\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}},\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(16)

with source term
f
and boundary data
g_{\mathrm{D}}
and
g_{\mathrm{N}}
.

A variational formulation is obtained by multiplying the Helmholtz equation by a test function
v\in V_{0}:=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\;|\;v=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\}
and integrating by parts.
The solution space is
V:=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\;|\;v=g_{\mathrm{D}}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\}
.
Taking into account the boundary conditions yields the weak form:

\left|\ \begin{aligned} \text{Find $u\in V$ such that $a(u,v)=\ell(v)$ for all $v\in V_{0}$.}\end{aligned}\right.

Here, the sesquilinear form
a
and linear form
\ell
are defined by

a(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\bar{v}-k^{2}u\bar{v}\right)\mathrm{d}\Omega+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muk\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}}u\bar{v}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma,\quad\ell(v)=\int_{\Omega}f\bar{v}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}g_{\mathrm{N}}\bar{v}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma.

We also consider problems defined in unbounded domains.
In that case, the Helmholtz equation is supplemented with the Sommerfeld radiation condition at infinity
[
45
,
43
]
:

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}r\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muku\right)=0,\quad r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}},

and the solutions are sought in
H^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
, the space of functions locally in
H^{1}(\Omega)
.
In practice, numerical simulations are carried out on truncated computational domains equipped with artificial boundary conditions—such as absorbing boundary conditions
[
21
,
34
]
or perfectly matched layers (PMLs)
[
8
,
7
,
6
,
3
,
5
,
33
,
51
]
.
In this work, we employ PMLs (see
Section

5
).

The Helmholtz problems may be ill-posed for certain wavenumbers
k
.
For the bounded case (Problem (
16
)), these values, called
resonances
, appear if
\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}=\emptyset
. They are real and correspond to the eigenvalues of the associated Laplace problem on
\Omega
.
Similar phenomena occur in unbounded cases involving configurations such as open cavities or inhomogeneous media
[
35
]
.
These resonances arise from nonphysical complex values of
k
(called
scattering resonances
) with small negative imaginary parts.
These values imply the existence of real values of
k
, called
quasi-resonances
, which are associated with localized quasi-modes, and for which the Helmholtz problems are well-posed
[
47
]
. Quasi-modes are defined as follows:

Definition 3.1

(Quasi-modes
[
32
, Definition 1.1]
)
.

A family of quasi-resonances with associated quasi-modes of quality
\epsilon(k)
is a sequence

{\left\{\left(k_{j},u_{j}\right)\right\}}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}\times H^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega),

such that
k_{j}\to\infty
when
j\to\infty
, and there exists a compact set
K\subset\Omega
such that, for all
i,j
,

\textnormal{supp}(u_{i})\subset K,\quad\left\lVert-\Delta u_{i}-k_{i}^{2}u_{i}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\epsilon(k_{i}),\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lvert\left(u_{i},u_{j}\right)-\delta_{i,j}\right\rvert\leq\epsilon(k_{i}),

where
\left(u,v\right):=\int_{\Omega}u\bar{v}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega
denotes the
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product.

Depending on the problem, the decay of
\epsilon(k_{j})
when
j\rightarrow\infty
can be polynomial (known as
weak-trapping
) or exponential (
strong-trapping
)
[
12
]
.

As we shall see, when the wavenumber
k
approaches a resonance or quasi-resonance, the linear system resulting from the discretization of the problem becomes significantly harder to solve.

3.2
Discretization

To numerically solve the Helmholtz problem introduced above, we apply a polynomial finite element discretization on a triangular conforming mesh of the domain.
The average element size is
h
. We use shape functions based on integrated Legendre polynomial
[
48
,
37
]
.
The discrete solution
u_{h}
is sought in a finite-dimensional subspace
V_{h}\subset V
and satisfies the variational formulation

\displaystyle a(u_{h},v_{h})=\ell(v_{h}),\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h,0}:=V_{h}\cap V_{0}.

The solution
u_{h}
can be expanded as

\displaystyle u_{h}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}\phi_{j}(\bm{x})+\sum_{j=N+1}^{N+N_{\mathrm{D}}}\hat{u}_{j-N}\phi_{j}(\bm{x}),

with
{\{\phi_{j}\}}_{j=1}^{N}
forming a basis for
V_{h,0}
and
{\{\phi_{j}\}}_{j=N+1}^{N+N_{\mathrm{D}}}
are the
N_{\mathrm{D}}
basis functions associated with the degrees of freedom lying on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}
, which do not vanish on that boundary.
The coefficients
\{u_{j}\}
are unknowns, while
\{\hat{u}_{j}\}
encode the Dirichlet data on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}
.
Substituting this representation into the variational formulation and choosing the test functions from
V_{h,0}
leads to the linear system

\displaystyle\left|\ \begin{aligned} \text{Find $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}$ such that }\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b},\end{aligned}\right.

(17)

with system matrix and right-hand side defined by

A_{i,j}=a(\phi_{j},\phi_{i}),\quad b_{i}=\ell(\phi_{i})-\sum_{j=N+1}^{N+N_{\mathrm{D}}}a(\phi_{j},\phi_{i})\,\hat{u}_{j-N},\quad\text{for }i,j=1,\dots,N.

The properties of the discrete system mirror those of the continuous problem:

•

The matrix
\mathbf{A}
is
non-singular
, since
k
is assumed not to correspond to a resonance.

•

The sesquilinear form is typically not coercive. The matrix
\mathbf{A}
is often highly
indefinite
, and this indefiniteness increases with
k
. The numerical range
W(\mathbf{A})=\left\{\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\,:\,\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|_{2}=1\right\}
contains zero.

•

The matrix
\mathbf{A}
may also be
non-normal
, meaning its eigenvectors are not mutually orthogonal, for instance when PML are used (see
Section

5
).

These features make the Helmholtz linear system particularly challenging for iterative solvers.
At high frequencies or near (quasi-)resonances, these difficulties become more pronounced:
[
28
]
shows that quasi-modes give rise to small eigenvalues.
In the boundary element setting,
[
32
]
analyzes how GMRES convergence deteriorates near quasi-modes.
Here, with a finite element discretization, we likewise observe the emergence of small eigenvalues and corresponding GMRES stagnation.

3.3
Acceleration techniques and preconditioning

In this section, we describe two techniques to accelerate the convergence of GMRES for solving the discrete problem (
17
): the deflation technique and the CSL preconditioning technique.
We also assume that the Assumption
2.4
holds in this section.

Deflation

The principle of the deflation is to remove a part of the spectrum of
\mathbf{A}
in order to speed up the GMRES convergence on the modified matrix.
This is done by solving a small problem in a subspace, called the
deflation subspace
, which is designed to remove the part of
\mathbf{A}
that slows down convergence.
This process relies on two bases, corresponding to the columns of matrices
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Z}
below, which must be chosen with care.
The method was initially developed for symmetric problems
[
27
,
36
,
49
]
, but the theoretical framework was later generalized to non-Hermitian problems
[
24
,
30
,
46
]
.
First, we provide a general overview of deflation. After that, we discuss the choice of
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Z}
. Then, in
Sections

4
and
5
, the deflation subspaces will be spanned by modes or quasi-modes of the physical problems.

Definition 3.2
.

(Deflation matrices)
Let
\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times n_{\mathrm{def}}}
be two full rank matrices such that
\ker(\mathbf{Y}^{*})\cap\operatorname{range}\left(\mathbf{AZ}\right)=\{0\}
, and let

\mathbf{E}:=\mathbf{Y}^{*}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{\mathrm{def}}\times n_{\mathrm{def}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{Q}:=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{Y}^{*}.

The
deflation matrices
are defined as

\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}:=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}:=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{A}.

Matrix
\mathbf{E}
is the restriction of
\mathbf{A}
to the deflation subspace. It is non-singular and a priori easy to invert, since
n_{def}
is assumed to be small.

The deflation matrices are projectors onto the complement of the deflation subspace and satisfy

\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}^{2}=\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}
,

\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}^{2}=\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}
and

\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}
, see
[
30
, Proposition 3.1]
.
Applying
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}
or
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}
to
\mathbf{A}
removes the components associated with the deflation subspace. The deflated problem is
[
30
, Lemma 3.2]

\displaystyle\left|\ \begin{aligned} \text{Find $\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}$ such that $(\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A})\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=(\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{b})$},\end{aligned}\right.

(18)

and the solution of the original problem (
17
) is obtained by using

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}.

This can be shown by noting that
\mathbf{u}
can be decomposed as

\mathbf{u}=\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}\right)\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{u}.

(19)

Using the property
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}
, (
18
) gives

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{u}.

Since
\mathbf{A}
is assumed to be non-singular by Assumption
2.4
, one has
\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{u}
.

Because
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}
projects out the deflation subspace, the matrix
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A}
is singular, with zero as an eigenvalue of multiplicity
n_{\mathrm{def}}
.
But GMRES applied to this system still converges to a solution if the following condition is satisfied
[
30
, Theorem 3.4]
:

\ker(\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A})\cap\operatorname{range}\left(\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A}\right)=\{0\}.

(20)

The choice of
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Z}
is not trivial; several options have been explored in the literature, such as exact eigenvectors, vectors derived from the multigrid method or domain decomposition, see e.g.
[
54
,
24
,
31
,
49
,
30
]
.
In the numerical experiments we consider in
Sections

4
and
5
, matrix
\mathbf{A}
has small eigenvalues.
The associated eigenvectors can be approximated by modes or quasi-modes of the physical problems, and can be used empirically to deflate the small eigenvalues.
Thus, in what follows, the columns of
\mathbf{Z}
will be the projections of these modes or approximated quasi-modes onto the finite-element space.
Based on this physical intuition, we take
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{Z}
.

If exact eigenvectors are chosen, then
\mathbf{E}
is non-singular (diagonal when
\mathbf{A}
is normal), since the problem is well-posed.
In this case, the hypothesis of
Definition

3.2
holds and the condition (
20
) is automatically satisfied, so GMRES is guaranteed to converge to the solution.
A similar situation can be expected when using approximate eigenvectors.

Although the deflated system resembles a system preconditioned on the left by
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}
, GMRES applied to (
18
) still minimizes the residual norm of the original problem.
This follows from the decomposition (
19
) and the definition of
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}
; see
[
24
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Each GMRES iteration becomes more expensive due to the application of
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}
.
Nevertheless, if
n_{\mathrm{def}}
is small, computing
\mathbf{E}^{-1}
is inexpensive.
In addition, if
\mathbf{Z}
is a full matrix (as is the case if its columns are eigenvectors), noting that
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}-(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z})(\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{A})
, the
N\times n_{\mathrm{def}}
matrix
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z}
and the
n_{\mathrm{def}}\times N
matrix
\mathbf{E}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{A}
can be stored.
Then, applying
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A}
requires only
4Nn_{\mathrm{def}}-N-n_{\mathrm{def}}
additional operations compared to applying
\mathbf{A}
.
In practice,
\mathbf{Z}
can be sparse (as in
Section

5
), and this reduces the computational cost.

Deflation and preconditioning

Having described deflation as a technique to accelerate GMRES by removing selected eigenvalues, we now turn to another complementary approach: preconditioning.
That approach aims to improve the spectral properties of the system by pre- or post-multiplying the matrix system by an approximation of its inverse.
We then investigate how deflation can be combined with preconditioning.

We consider the CSL preconditioner as a representative example, following
[
22
,
23
,
13
,
29
,
38
,
39
]
.
The preconditioner is based on solving a modified Helmholtz problem with a complex-shifted wavenumber.

\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\bar{v}-(k^{2}+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\varepsilon)u\bar{v}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muk\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{R}}}u\bar{v}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma=\int_{\Omega}f\bar{v}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}g_{\mathrm{N}}\bar{v}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma,\quad\forall v\in V,\ \varepsilon>0.

As suggested in
[
29
]
, we use
\varepsilon=k
. Discretizing this problem yields the matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}
, and the original system is then right-preconditioned as:

\displaystyle\left|\ \begin{aligned} \text{Find $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}$ such that }(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}^{-1})\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},\end{aligned}\right.

(21)

with the solution recovered as
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{x}
.

The CSL preconditioner aims to reduce iteration counts without introducing excessive computational cost.
Its assembly is inexpensive, since the mass and stiffness matrices are reused from the original Helmholtz discretization.
However, applying
\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}
can be as costly as solving the original system.
Multigrid methods are often employed to efficiently deal with this challenge, but they can be difficult to implement and tune effectively.
Here, we rather use an incomplete LU (ILU) factorization as an efficient approximation.
Each GMRES iteration is then more expensive due to the cost of applying this approximate inverse.

To combine the CSL preconditioning and deflation techniques, we substitute the preconditioned formulation (
21
) into the deflated system (
18
).
This leads to the doubly modified system:

\displaystyle\left|\ \begin{aligned} \text{Find $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}$ such that }(\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}^{-1})\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{b}.\end{aligned}\right.

(22)

The final solution is reconstructed using

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{def}}\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}.

If the conditions

\ker(\mathbf{Z}^{*})\cap\operatorname{range}\left(\mathbf{AZ}\right)=\{0\}\quad\text{and}\quad\ker(\mathbf{Z}^{*})\cap\operatorname{range}\left(\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}\right)=\{0\},

hold, then GMRES applied to this system converges for all initial guesses
[
30
, Theorem 3.4]
.
In practice,
\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}
is approximated by its ILU factorization.
Each GMRES iteration then involves the additional cost of deflation mentioned in
Section

3.3
plus the cost of applying the preconditioner.

Alternatively, the deflation and preconditioning ideas can be combined additively, yielding the so-called
additive coarse grid correction
method
[
2
]
.
This leads to the modified system:

\displaystyle\left|\ \begin{aligned} \text{Find $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}$ such that }\mathbf{A}\left(\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}+\mathbf{Q}\right)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},\end{aligned}\right.

with the solution given by
\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}+\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{x}
.
This approach has the same additional cost per iteration of (
22
), but it does not require the initial computation of
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z}
and
\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{A}
.
However, since the numerical results obtained with this method are nearly identical to those with deflation, and because deflation enjoys a more developed theoretical foundation, we do not pursue this approach further in this work.

4
Numerical experiments: a cavity problem with resonances

In this section and the next, we present numerical experiments designed to illustrate the specific challenges of solving Helmholtz problems.
These benchmarks serve to validate the interpretation tools and acceleration techniques discussed previously.
The first set of experiments focuses on a simple cavity problem with a wavenumber chosen close to resonance, a setting known to lead to slow convergence.

Description of the benchmark

We solve the Helmholtz equation on the unit square domain
\Omega={(0,1)}^{2}
, subject to homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
and a constant source term equal to one in the interior.
The problem admits a unique solution provided that the square of the wavenumber does not coincide with an eigenvalue of the Laplacian operator.

For this configuration, the Laplacian eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are given by

k_{n,m}^{2}=\pi^{2}(n^{2}+m^{2}),\quad u_{n,m}(x,y)=\sin(n\pi x)\sin(m\pi y),\quad n,m\in\mathbb{N}.

After discretization, the system matrix
\mathbf{A}
is real, symmetric, and therefore normal.
Its eigenvalues are real, and it admits an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

All experiments are conducted using a Matlab implementation.
We use
P_{2}
continuous finite elements on a uniform mesh with characteristic size
h=1/32
, leading to approximately
N_{\mathrm{dof}}=5{,}000
degrees of freedom (see
Figure

1
).
The mesh resolution is chosen so that the number of degrees of freedom per wavelength satisfies
D_{\lambda}=(\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{dof}}}-1)\,\lambda\geq 30
, where
\lambda=2\pi/k
denotes the wavelength.

(23)

,
\mathrm{II}
, and
\mathrm{III}
in both plots of

Theorem

2.6
.

•

Phase I: HR values are far from
\lambda_{1}
.
At this stage,

is not yet close to
\lambda_{1}
, and both terms in
Equation

23
are large. The bound from
Theorem

2.6
is then loose, and cannot provide an accurate description of the convergence rate.

•

Phase II: HR value approaches
\lambda_{1}
.
As the iteration progresses, one HR value
\nu_{1}^{(l)}
begins to converge towards
\lambda_{1}
. However, the term
\left|\nu_{1}^{(l)}/\lambda_{1}\right|
remains large, and the convergence bound still does not fully capture the residual behavior. This corresponds to the plateau in the residual curve, where the influence of
\lambda_{1}
is strong but not yet neutralized.

•

Phase III: HR value accurately captures
\lambda_{1}
.
Once
\nu_{1}^{(l)}\approx\lambda_{1}
, we have
\left|s_{J}^{l}(\lambda_{i})\right|\approx 1
for all
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{J}^{c}
. The impact of
\lambda_{1}
is effectively removed from the bound, and the convergence behavior improves significantly. The residual drops, and the theoretical prediction becomes tight.

This analysis reveals a direct link between the evolution of HR values and the shape of the GMRES residual curve.
Each plateau reflects the presence of a small eigenvalue not yet well captured by the Krylov subspace.
Once a small eigenvalue is well approximated, its effect is factored out of the residual bound, and the solver progresses more efficiently. The same mechanism explains the subsequent plateaus and drops: each corresponds to another small eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
being successively captured by HR values.
The more small eigenvalues are well approximated, the smaller the quantity in the convergence bound of
Theorem

2.6
, and the more precise the theoretical prediction becomes.

In summary, GMRES convergence slows down when a small eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
is in the process of being resolved by the Krylov subspace.
Once the associated HR value converges, the eigenvalue’s influence fades, and the residual decays more rapidly, as if the eigenvalue were no longer present.

Building on the previous observations, we now examine the effect of combining preconditioning with deflation.

Influence of the restarted version of GMRES

Having explored the effects of preconditioning and deflation in full GMRES, we now turn to a more practical variant commonly used in large-scale applications: restarted GMRES.

In the standard implementation of GMRES, the cost per iteration grows with the number of iterations, as each step requires storing and orthogonalizing against an expanding Krylov basis.
To limit memory and computational overhead, GMRES is often restarted every
m
iterations, retaining only the last Krylov vector to start the next cycle.
This variant, known as GMRES
(m)
, reduces per-iteration cost but sacrifices theoretical guarantees of convergence.

Figure

4(b)
and
Figure

4(c)
show the residual histories for GMRES
(m)
with a restart parameter
m=25
, in both unpreconditioned and ILU-preconditioned settings (blue and yellow curves, respectively).
In both cases, and without deflation, convergence stagnates rapidly and the residual plateaus—even when preconditioning is applied.
We observed similar behavior with larger restart values (e.g.,
m=80
, not shown), indicating that the issue is robust.

However, when deflation is introduced, GMRES
(m)
recovers its ability to converge.
In both the unpreconditioned and preconditioned cases, the residual drops more steadily, and the stagnation phases are significantly reduced or eliminated.
This improvement can be explained as follows: in the restarted setting, the Krylov subspace may not have enough iterations to accurately approximate the small eigenvalues responsible for stagnation.
Deflation explicitly removes these difficult modes, making GMRES
(m)
more effective.
This highlights an important practical benefit: deflation enhances the robustness of restarted GMRES, particularly near resonant wavenumbers where convergence is otherwise problematic.

Numerical experiments: scattering problem with quasi-resonances

We now turn to a more challenging configuration: the diffraction of a plane wave by an open cavity. Unlike the previous benchmark, where the system matrix was real, symmetric, and associated with exact resonance modes, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
here is complex and non-normal. Although exact resonances are absent, the problem features
quasi-modes
, which can lead to similar convergence difficulties for iterative solvers. As before, we use numerical experiments to study GMRES convergence and the effect of acceleration techniques.

Description of the benchmark

The problem consists of the scattering of a time-harmonic plane wave
u_{\mathrm{inc}}(x,y)=e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0muk(\cos(\theta)x+\sin(\theta)y)}
by an obstacle
\mathcal{O}
in free space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\theta
is the incident angle. The boundary of the obstacle is assigned either a Dirichlet condition (Dirichlet case) or a Neumann condition (Neumann case).

To model the unbounded domain, we truncate the computational domain to a square region
\Omega_{\mathrm{dom}}={(-L,L)}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{O}
and surround it with perfectly matched layers (PMLs) of thickness
L_{\mathrm{pml}}
, denoted
\Omega_{\mathrm{pml}}

[
8
,
7
]
.
The PMLs absorb outgoing waves and eliminate reflections from the artificial boundary, on which a homogeneous Dirichlet condition is imposed. The PML formulation introduces complex coordinate stretchings:

\gamma_{x}(x):=1+\frac{i\sigma_{x}(x)}{k},\quad\gamma_{y}(y):=1+\frac{i\sigma_{y}(y)}{k},

with absorption profiles:

\sigma_{x}(x):=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{pml}}(x)}{L_{\mathrm{pml}}-|x|+L},\quad\sigma_{y}(y):=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\mathrm{pml}}(y)}{L_{\mathrm{pml}}-|y|+L}.

The scattered field
u
then satisfies:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\gamma_{y}}{\gamma_{x}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\gamma_{x}}{\gamma_{y}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)+\gamma_{x}\gamma_{y}k^{2}u&=0\quad&\text{in }\Omega_{\mathrm{dom}}\cup\Omega_{\mathrm{pml}},\\
u=-u_{\mathrm{inc}}\quad\text{or}\quad\partial_{\bm{n}}u&=-\partial_{\bm{n}}u_{\mathrm{inc}}\quad&\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{obs}},\\
u&=0\quad&\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{ext}}.\end{aligned}\right.

The obstacle
\mathcal{O}
is an open rectangular cavity of length
L_{\mathcal{O}}
and opening width
l_{\mathcal{O}}
(see
Figure

5
).
In contrast to closed domains, this setup does not admit true resonance modes but supports
quasi-modes
, i.e., modes that approximately satisfy the homogeneous equation and are spatially localized.
This configuration is a case of a weak-trapping (and, more specifically, parabolic)
[
12
]
. The decay of
\epsilon(k_{j})
in
Definition

3.1
is therefore polynomial.

In the Dirichlet case, quasi-modes are known to accumulate near wavenumbers
\pi n/l_{\mathcal{O}}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}

[
11
, Theorem 5.1]
.
However, numerical simulations indicate the presence of additional quasi-modes at other wavenumbers.
These are often close to the resonance frequencies of a closed rectangular cavity with the same dimensions.
In that case, the Dirichlet resonance wavenumbers are given by

k_{n,m}=\pi\sqrt{\frac{m^{2}}{L_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}}+\frac{n^{2}}{l_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}}},\quad m,n\in\mathbb{N}.

For the Neumann case (used in our experiments), the expected quasi-modes are close to the resonance frequencies of a closed rectangular cavity with Neumann boundary conditions on all sides except the left edge (Dirichlet), given by

k_{n,m}=\pi\sqrt{\frac{{(m+1/2)}^{2}}{L_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}}+\frac{n^{2}}{l_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}}},\quad m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

In both cases, the quasi-modes inside the cavity resemble the eigenmodes of the corresponding closed configuration.

After discretization using finite elements, the problem leads to a linear system
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}
.
The resulting matrix
\mathbf{A}
is complex, non-normal, and has a spectrum that includes complex-valued eigenvalues.

All numerical results presented in this section correspond to the Neumann case.
The Dirichlet case shows similar behavior and is omitted for brevity.
We use the following parameters
L_{\mathcal{O}}=1.3,\quad l_{\mathcal{O}}=0.4,\quad\theta=\frac{4\pi}{10},\quad\mathsf{h}=\frac{1}{20},\quad\text{$P_{3}$ continuous elements},\quad\mathsf{tol}=10^{-6},\quad D_{\lambda}\geq 30
.
This configuration leads to approximately 13,500 degrees of freedom.

6
Conclusion

This study examined the convergence behavior of GMRES for the finite element solution of Helmholtz problems, emphasizing the impact of resonance and quasi-modes.

Novel convergence bounds based on harmonic Ritz values have been proposed for general systems.
These bounds are able to describe nonlinear residual histories, offering a detailed explanation of residual plateaus.
Through numerical experiments on both closed and open cavity benchmarks, we observed that convergence stalls align with wavenumbers associated with resonances or quasi-modes.
These stalls were accurately related to the evolution of harmonic Ritz values.

The main observations of the numerical studies can be summarized as follows:

•

GMRES convergence is highly slowed and plateaus because of small eigenvalues due to resonances or quasi-resonances. In particular, restarted GMRES cannot achieve convergence in some cases.

•

Deflation of the eigenvectors corresponding to resonances or quasi-resonances, even when approximated, removes plateaus and accelerates convergence.
This approach requires knowledge of the eigenvectors and slightly increases the cost per iteration, which depends on the number of the deflated eigenvectors.

•

Although deflation and preconditioning techniques improved convergence when used individually, combining them proved to be the most effective approach. For open domain problems, ILU
(\mathbf{A}_{\varepsilon})
preconditioning enhanced performance, albeit at the cost of an additional matrix product. However, the convergence of restarted GMRES was only obtained when used in conjunction with deflation.

These results underscore the value of combining an a priori knowledge of spectral properties with tailored numerical techniques to design robust solvers for high-frequency wave propagation problems.
Future work includes extending these strategies to 3D configurations and exploring recent preconditioning techniques based on domain decomposition.
A more detailed study of the factors of the convergence bounds that account for the non-normality of the problem, such as the sum of the eigenvalue condition numbers, would also be of interest.
