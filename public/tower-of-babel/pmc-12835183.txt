Effect of Er, Cr: YSGG laser–activated final irrigation on gingival crevicular fluid RANKL/OPG levels
Authors: Furkan Evrendilek, Esin Özlek, İsmet Meydan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to investigate the effect of Er, Cr: YSGG laser activation of NaOCl during final irrigation on the level of RANKL/OPG in the gingival crevicular fluid of mandibular molars with periapical lesions at 1 and 3 months after root canal treatment. Thirty patients with periapical lesions in mandibular molars in the study. Root canal treatment was completed in two visits. Participants were randomly allocated into two groups (n = 15). In the experimental group, NaOCl was activated using an Er, Cr: YSGG laser, while in the control group no activation was performed during final irrigation. Gingival crevicular fluid samples were collected at baseline, one month, and three months after treatment, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. RANKL and OPG concentrations were quantified by ELISA. Statistical comparisons were made using the Mann–Whitney U and Fisher’s Exact tests. Laser activation of NaOCl did not produce a statistically significant difference in the RANKL/OPG ratio compared with the control group (p > 0.05). Within-group analysis indicated a non-significant reduction in the RANKL/OPG ratio at 3 months after treatment compared to pre-treatment levels in both groups (p > 0.05). Er, Cr: YSGG laser–activated NaOCl irrigation did not produce a statistically significant difference between groups after three months. However, the early decrease in the RANKL/OPG ratio observed in the laser group suggests that laser activation may influence early inflammatory responses associated with the healing process following root canal treatment. Introduction

Apical periodontitis is an inflammatory reaction that develops in response to the presence of microorganisms within the root canal system1. Histologically, it is classified as either acute or chronic, depending on the predominant cellular components involved in the periapical inflammatory response. Acute inflammation is primarily characterized by the infiltration of neutrophilic leukocytes, whereas chronic inflammation is marked by the presence of macrophages and lymphocytes2.

Apical osteolytic lesions represent a hallmark of chronic apical periodontitis and arise as a consequence of progressive bone resorption. This bone destruction is regulated by activated osteoclasts. Various cytokines—including interleukin (IL)−1, IL-11, IL-17, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)—as well as the OPG/RANKL/RANK signaling axis, stimulate the differentiation and activation of osteoclast progenitor cells3.

The RANK/RANKL/OPG complex plays a crucial role in regulating bone metabolism in both marginal and apical periodontitis. The interaction between RANK and RANKL promotes osteoclastogenesis and enhances bone resorption. In contrast, osteoprotegerin (OPG) acts as a decoy receptor by binding to RANKL, thereby inhibiting its interaction with RANK and suppressing osteoclast activity4. An increased RANKL/OPG ratio leads to enhanced bone resorption, whereas a decreased ratio is associated with reduced bone degradation. This balance, governed by osteoblasts, is a critical determinant of osteoclastogenic progression5. Studies have reported increased levels of RANKL, decreased levels of OPG, and an elevated RANKL/OPG ratio in the gingival crevicular fluid of teeth affected by marginal periodontitis6.

Gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) contains a variety of mediators, host-derived enzymes, and byproducts of tissue degradation, the concentrations of which are elevated during inflammatory processes. This fluid reflects the anatomical and functional interplay between the vascular and neural networks of the endodontium and periodontium7. In cases of apical periodontitis, where inflammation and bone resorption advance in the periapical region, the analysis of GCF may serve as a valuable diagnostic tool for evaluating the periapical status of the affected tooth. Recent studies have increasingly employed GCF analysis for this purpose3–6.

The aim of this study is to evaluate the effect of final irrigation with sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), activated by Er, Cr: YSGG laser, during root canal treatment of mandibular molars with periapical lesions on RANKL/OPG levels in gingival crevicular fluid at 1 and 3 months post-treatment. The null hypothesis of this study was that activation of NaOCl with Er, Cr: YSGG laser during final irrigation does not significantly influence the RANKL/OPG ratio, reflecting the balance of bone resorption and formation, in the early and intermediate phases (1 and 3 months) of healing following root canal treatment.

Materials and methods

This prospective, single-blind, single-center randomized clinical trial was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Van Yuzuncu Yil University (Decision no: 10/15.12.2021). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The protocol was registered on the clinical trials website (http://www.clinicaltrials.gov, Identifier: NCT05478811, Date: 08/01/2022). This manuscript was prepared following the PRIRATE 2020 guidelines8. Figure 1 shows a flowchart depicting the key stages of the trial.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using the G*Power program (3.1.9.2; Heinrich Heine University, Dusseldorf, Germany) with a 5% type 1 alpha error, 90% test power (1-β), and an effect size of f = 1,252, as reported by Arslan et al.9. The minimum number of patients was set at 30, with 15 in each group.

Eligibility criteria

Patients who presented to the Department of Endodontics between 1 January 2022 and 30 June 2022, were considered for inclusion in this study. Only voluntary participants were enrolled, and written informed consent was obtained from all individuals after they were thoroughly informed about the purpose, procedures, potential benefits, and possible risks of the study.

Randomization and allocation concealment

A total of 34 patients were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 17) based on the method of NaOCl activation, using a computer-generated randomization tool (www.randomizer.org); Group 1: Er, Cr: YSGG laser activation, Group 2: Control. Due to the nature of the clinical procedure, blinding of the operators was not feasible, as the clinicians needed to be aware of the group allocation prior to treatment. Therefore, complete blinding could not be implemented at the intervention stage. However, to ensure objectivity in data interpretation, allocation information was not disclosed to the individuals responsible for data collection and analysis. This approach was employed to reduce potential bias and minimize information-related influence among the investigators. All endodontic procedures were performed by the same experienced operator to ensure standardization and eliminate variability in instrumentation and irrigation quality.

Collection of gingival crevicular fluid samples

Before the administration of local anesthesia, gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) samples were collected from the tooth scheduled for root canal treatment to obtain baseline measurements. The sampling site was gently cleaned with cotton pellets to remove debris and dried for 10 s with a mild air stream. A Periopaper strip (Oraflow, New York, USA) was carefully inserted into the gingival sulcus until slight resistance was felt and left in place for 30 s to absorb the crevicular fluid. Care was taken to avoid contamination with blood and saliva; contaminated samples were discarded, and new samples were collected. The collected strips were immediately placed into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes (Labosel, Istanbul, Turkey) containing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Each tube was labeled according to the sampling time point (baseline, 1 st month, or 3rd month) and stored in a deep freezer at − 80 °C until analysis.

Treatment protocol

All patients received local anesthesia via inferior alveolar nerve block and buccal infiltration using 2 mL of articaine hydrochloride with 1:100,000 epinephrine (Ultracaine DS Forte; Haver, Istanbul, Turkey). Following rubber dam isolation, access cavities were prepared using sterile diamond burs. Working length was determined using an electronic apex locator (Propex Pixi, Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) and a #10 K-file (VDW GmbH, Munich, Germany), and verified with periapical radiographs, ensuring a position 0.5–1 mm short of the radiographic apex.

The root canals were shaped using ProTaper Next (Dentsply, Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) files. The mesial canals were shaped up to X2, and the distal canal up to X3. All files were used with an endodontic motor (X-Smart Plus, Dentsply Sirona) at the manufacturer’s recommended speed and torque settings. To prevent torsional stress, the files were withdrawn from the canal when resistance was encountered during shaping, and the apical patency was checked using a #10 K-file before continuing with the procedure.

During instrumentation, each file change was followed by irrigation with 2 mL of 5.25% NaOCl (Microvem AF, Istanbul, Turkey) for 1 min. Irrigation was performed using a 2.5 mL dental syringe (Ayset Tibbi Ürünleri A.S., Adana, Turkey) and a side-vented needle (Endo-Top, Cerkamed, Stalowa Wola, Poland) inserted 2 mm short of the working length. After completion of root canal preparation, calcium hydroxide powder (Kalsin, Spot Dis Deposu A.S., Izmir, Turkey) was mixed with distilled water at a 1:1 ratio as per the manufacturer’s instructions and placed into the canals using a #25 K-file, positioned 1 mm short of the working length. The access cavity was temporarily sealed with glass ionomer cement, and the patient was scheduled for a follow-up appointment in 2 weeks.

At the second appointment, rubber dam isolation was applied, and the temporary restoration was removed using a diamond round bur (Brasseler, USA). The calcium hydroxide from the root canals was flushed out with the 5.25% NaOCl solution and the finishing files used during the first session. The procedure then proceeded to the final irrigation phase, which was performed sequentially using 3 mL of 5.25% NaOCl followed by 3 mL of 17% EDTA (Imicryl, Konya, Turkey).

Er,Cr:YSGG laser group

The NaOCI final irrigation activation was performed using an Er, Cr: YSGG laser (Waterlase MD, Biolase Technology Inc., USA) at a wavelength of 2780 nm. An RFT-2 optical fiber (275 μm in diameter, 21 mm in length) was used for activation. The laser parameters were set to a power of 2 W and a frequency of 20 Hz, with 10% air and 10% water spray. The energy density was calibrated to 23.15 J/cm². The fiber tip was positioned 1 mm short of the working length and moved in an apico-coronal direction using a helical motion during activation. Each cycle consisted of 8 s of irrigation with 1 mL of solution followed by 12 s of laser activation. This 20-second cycle was repeated three times per canal, totaling 60 s of activation time.

Control group

Final irrigation was performed with 3 mL of 5.25% NaOCl using a 30-gauge needle (0.4 mm diameter). Activation was achieved by manually moving the needle in an apico-coronal direction for 1 min.

Following irrigation, all root canals were dried with sterile paper points and obturated using the single-cone technique with gutta-percha (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) and AH Plus sealer (Dentsply DeTrey GmbH, Konstanz, Germany). Access cavities were restored with composite resin (Filtek Z250, 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, Minnesota, USA). Periapical radiographs were taken post-operatively, and patients with inadequate or overextended root canal fillings were excluded from the study.

Root canal post-treatment follow-up appointments

Patients who underwent root canal treatment were scheduled for follow-up appointments at 1 and 3 months post-treatment. As previously described, GCF samples were collected using Periopaper strips (Oraflow, New York, USA). Each strip was placed in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube containing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and samples were immediately stored at −80 °C until biochemical analysis.

Analysis of GCF samples

All GCF samples were analyzed after the completion of follow-up appointments to quantify RANKL and OPG concentrations using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) technique. Commercial ELISA kits (ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) were used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Prior to the assay, frozen samples were thawed at room temperature and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to obtain clear supernatants. Then, 100 µL of standard solution and 100 µL of each sample were added to their respective wells. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 80 min, washed three times with the supplied wash buffer, and treated with 100 µL of biotinylated antibody solution. After incubation for 50 min at 37 °C and washing, 100 µL of streptavidin–HRP conjugate was added, followed by another 50-minute incubation. The washing procedure was repeated, and 90 µL of TMB substrate solution was applied. The color reaction was stopped after 20 min by adding 50 µL of stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek ELx800, USA). A standard calibration curve was constructed based on the absorbance of the known standards, and the concentrations of RANKL and OPG were automatically calculated from this curve. While RANKL concentrations were obtained in pg/mL, OPG results were expressed in ng/mL. To allow for direct comparison and ratio calculation (RANKL/OPG), OPG values were converted to pg/mL to ensure unit consistency between both mediators. Changes in mediator levels between time points were calculated as absolute differences (Δ = post-treatment value − pre-treatment value).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed with IBM SPSS V23. Conformity to the normal distribution was examined with Shapiro-Wilk test. Independent Samples t Test was used to compare normally distributed variables according to groups, and Mann-Whitney U Test was used to compare variables that did not fit the normal distribution. Intra-group comparisons of the RANKL/OPG rations at different time points were performed using the Wilcoxon test. Fisher’s Exact Test was used to compare categorical variables by groups. The results of the analysis are presented as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables, mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum-maximum) for quantitative variables. The significance level was calculated as p < 0.050.

Discussion

This study evaluated the effect of Er, Cr: YSGG laser activation of NaOCl during the final irrigation of root canal treatment in mandibular molars with chronic apical periodontitis, focusing on its impact on the RANKL/OPG ratio in gingival crevicular fluid at 1 and 3 months after treatment. Since no statistically significant differences were found between the groups, the null hypothesis—that Er, Cr: YSGG laser activation does not significantly influence the RANKL/OPG ratio, reflecting the balance of bone resorption and formation during healing—could not be rejected.

The use of immunological assessment in endodontics for diagnostic and healing evaluation represents a promising advancement. Traditional imaging modalities, such as periapical radiographs, expose patients to ionizing radiation and require a certain time interval before healing can be assessed radiographically. Moreover, the interpretation of periapical images can vary depending on the angulation of the radiograph. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), while more accurate, demands clinical expertise and incurs high costs, which limit its routine clinical use13. In contrast, immunological evaluation through biochemical mediators provides a simple, non-invasive, and clinically applicable alternative capable of reflecting the inflammatory and healing dynamics of periapical tissues. Earlier investigations mainly employed immunological markers in gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) for diagnostic purposes—to identify pulpal and periapical inflammation14–17. However, recent studies have increasingly focused on their use in monitoring treatment outcomes and assessing periapical healing18–20.

In endodontic research, various biological fluids have been utilized for the analysis of inflammatory mediators, including gingival crevicular fluid, pulpal blood, dentin fluid, and periapical exudate21. Among these, GCF represents a clinically practical and minimally invasive option for longitudinal assessment, particularly in post-treatment follow-up, where direct sampling from the root canal or periapical tissues is not feasible. Therefore, in the present study, GCF was chosen as the biological medium to evaluate post-treatment healing dynamics in a non-invasive yet biologically informative manner.

Numerous studies over the years have reported that the RANKL/OPG ratio in the gingival crevicular fluid of teeth with periodontal inflammation is higher than that observed in healthy teeth4,6,13,22. Research investigating the effect of periodontal treatment on the RANKL/OPG ratio has shown a significant post-treatment decrease in this ratio5,22,23. These findings are partially consistent with the present study, in which a decrease in the RANKL/OPG ratio was observed in both groups compared to baseline values, although this reduction did not reach statistical significance.

Although numerous studies have investigated the RANKL/OPG ratio in the context of periodontal infections, research focusing on this ratio in teeth with apical periodontitis remains limited. To date, the study by24 is the only investigation that evaluated the RANKL/OPG ratio in periapical fluid from asymptomatic teeth diagnosed with apical periodontitis. Their findings revealed no statistically significant difference between pre- and post-treatment RANKL/OPG ratios. Furthermore, they reported that the use of various intracanal medicaments—calcium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide combined with ibuprofen, and calcium hydroxide combined with ciprofloxacin—had no impact on this ratio. Similarly, our study found no statistically significant difference in the RANKL/OPG ratio between the two groups, both of which received calcium hydroxide as an intracanal medicament. This outcome may be attributed to the antibacterial properties of calcium hydroxide and its potential to promote new bone formation24. Interestingly, while Ulukoylu et al. reported an increase in the RANKL/OPG ratio post-treatment—suggesting that apical debris might impair periapical bone healing—our findings diverged from this, as both groups in our study exhibited a decrease in the RANKL/OPG ratio following treatment.

Currently, various irrigation activation systems are recommended to enhance the effectiveness and overall success of root canal treatment. In the present study, the Er, Cr: YSGG laser was selected as the activation method. This laser, belonging to the erbium family, operates at a wavelength of 2780 nm and is known for its ability to achieve tissue ablation and disinfection through water-mediated micro-explosions25. By generating cavitation bubbles within the irrigant, it enhances fluid dynamics inside the root canal system, thereby facilitating the mechanical disruption and removal of biofilms26. In addition, Er, Cr: YSGG laser activation has been reported to eliminate the vapor lock effect in the apical region, improving irrigant penetration27.

Although numerous studies have investigated the physical and antibacterial effects of Er, Cr: YSGG laser activation28,29, its potential biological influence on periapical healing and immunomodulation has not been fully elucidated30. While no previous research has directly examined the immunological effects of Er, Cr: YSGG laser in root canal therapy, Arslan et al.8 evaluated the impact of intracanal laser application and low-level laser therapy on the levels of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) in gingival crevicular fluid. CGRP is a pro-inflammatory neuropeptide synthesized by T cells, mast cells, and dendritic cells, playing a key role in neurogenic vasodilation during inflammation. In their study, significant alterations in CGRP concentrations were observed in laser-treated teeth compared to placebo, suggesting that laser application may modulate the local immune response during the acute inflammatory phase.

In the present study, Er, Cr: YSGG laser activation resulted in a reduction in the RANKL/OPG ratio during the early (1-month) post-treatment period, although this change was not statistically significant. By the third month, this decreasing trend continued similarly in both groups. The mediator evaluated by Arslan et al.8 (CGRP) represents an early-phase neuropeptide associated with acute inflammation, whereas the RANKL/OPG ratio assessed in the current study reflects the balance between bone resorption and regeneration, serving as a late-phase indicator of healing. This distinction in the timing and nature of the biomarkers may explain the differences between the two studies. Although Er, Cr: YSGG laser activation may have the potential to induce measurable biochemical changes in the early phase of periapical healing, its physical advantages during irrigation may not necessarily translate into significant long-term immunological modulation.

The present study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, RANKL/OPG levels were measured only in teeth affected by apical periodontitis, and no samples were obtained from contralateral healthy teeth. Although this restricts direct comparisons between diseased and healthy sites, it does not substantially affect the clinical interpretation of the results, as the primary objective of this study was to investigate biological changes occurring in teeth with apical periodontitis. Second, the assessment of periapical healing was based exclusively on the measurement of RANKL and OPG levels in gingival crevicular fluid (GCF). Radiographic healing parameters, such as periapical index scores or cone-beam computed tomography findings, were not included. Consequently, a direct correlation between biochemical changes and clinical or radiographic healing outcomes could not be established. Future studies combining immunological markers with clinical and radiographic assessments may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of periapical healing. Lastly, the study focused on short-term outcomes; therefore, further well-designed, long-term clinical trials are required to evaluate the sustained effects of laser-activated irrigation.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that Er, Cr: YSGG laser–activated final irrigation with NaOCI did not result in a statistically significant difference in gingival crevicular fluid RANKL/OPG ratios compared with the control group at the three-month follow-up. However, the statistically significant reduction in the RANKL/OPG ratio observed at the first month in the laser group suggests that laser activation may influence early biochemical responses associated with periapical inflammation. These findings indicate that Er, Cr: YSGG laser–activated irrigation does not provide a clear biological advantage within the follow-up period, but may contribute to the modulation of early inflammatory processes following root canal treatment.
