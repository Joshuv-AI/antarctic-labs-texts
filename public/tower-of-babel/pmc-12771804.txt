Latent profile analysis of resilience and its relationship with social support, coping style, and death attitude among Chinese palliative nurses
Authors: Shu Zhang, Jianjun Jiang, Chang Guan, Juejin Li, Xiaolin Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNurses assume primary responsibility for palliative care. Owing to the complexity of palliative care and frequent exposure to death, there is a need to explore the latent resilience profiles of nurses to create targeted protective systems for improving mental health and the quality of palliative care.MethodsA cross-sectional study was performed. A total of 320 palliative care nurses were recruited. The sociodemographic questionnaire, the Connor and Davidson’s Resilience Scale, the Perceived Social Support Scale, the Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire, and the Death Attitude Profile-Revised were used for data collection. A latent profile analysis of resilience among palliative nurses was performed, and the factors influencing the latent profiles were explored via multivariate logistic regression analysis.ResultsThe results of latent profile analysis revealed three distinct resilience subgroups among palliative nurses: a “low resilience - difficulty adapting to work stress” group (39.6%); a “moderate resilience - challenged in regulating negative emotions” group (42.7%); and a “high resilience - well coping in adversity recovery” group (17.7%). Multiple logistic regression showed that protective factors for resilience included good family support, positive coping, and high death avoidance acceptance, and risk factors included suboptimal health conditions, the intention to leave the job, and the fear of death.ConclusionsPalliative nurses exhibit individual differences in resilience. Protective and risk factors for resilience among nurses should be evaluated to identify unique needs and targeted intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04115-4.

Background

According to the World Health Organization, palliative care is a comprehensive health service involving early identification, appropriate evaluation and addressing of physical, psychosocial or spiritual problems to optimize quality of life and relieve suffering for seriously ill and dying patients along with their families [1]. Currently, with population aging and the increased burden of chronic diseases, deploying palliative care has emerged as one of the priorities for global health systems [2]. Nevertheless, access to quality palliative care remains a universal challenge, particularly in regions where palliative services are in early developmental stages [3]. The situation in China exemplifies these global challenges while revealing distinctive contextual factors. China has the largest elderly population in the world, accounting for approximately one-fifth of the total worldwide [4]. As chronic diseases at older ages become a significant concern, the need of palliative care in China is particularly high [5]. However, the support system for palliative care is underdeveloped, a challenge commonly observed in many resource-limited settings [6, 7]. Besides, rooted in traditional East Asian Confucian culture, attitudes toward death in China tend to be conservative in some contexts [8], further resulting in the unique considerations for palliative care delivery.

Nurses assume the leading responsibility to provide diversified palliative care for patients and their families from diagnosis through end-of-life [9], including coordinating patient care, providing direct clinical interventions, emotional support, and education. However, they face painstaking dilemmas. Globally, most palliative nurses lack specialized training and education [10], leading to poor competence in the holistic and complex nature of care. Besides, palliative nurses are frequently exposed to death and despair, resulting in far more distress than nurses in other departments [11]. In addition, in certain countries such as China, open discussion about death is often limited [8], which can affect public acceptance of palliative care and the professional recognition of nurses. Given these concerns, palliative nurses are prone to report exacerbated stress, compassion fatigue, and burnout [12], which are closely correlated with absenteeism, high staff turnover, and degraded levels of health care delivery [13]. Thus, to improve the well-being of health care professionals and promote palliative care, it is necessary to address this dilemma.

Addressing these challenges requires a focus on enhancing psychological defenses, with resilience being a key factor. Resilience refers to the ability to cope with adversity, overcome difficulties, and recover from setbacks [14, 15], and it has been shown to alleviate anxiety, depression, and burnout among nurses in high-pressure settings [16, 17]. However, existing research on nurse resilience has several limitations (Table S1). First, while a wealth of studies exists across various specialties (e.g., ICU, emergency, mental health), there is a notable scarcity of research focusing on palliative care nurses, a group consistently exposed to high emotional and ethical challenges [18]. Second, most studies operate at the micro-system level, emphasizing individual traits or coping mechanisms, without integrating environmental and cultural factors [18]. Furthermore, previous studies have assessed resilience predominantly based on a group’s total scores, overlooking the heterogeneity among subgroups. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) offers a novel approach to identify heterogeneous resilience profiles [19], which is critical for developing targeted support strategies. The Kumpfer’s resilience framework pointed out that [20], the level of resilience is impacted by the intersection of external environmental factors (community, family, and cultural factors) and internal self-factors (physical, behavioral, cognitive, and emotional factors. According to the framework, social support, coping styles, and death attitudes, respectively as important environmental, internal self and cultural factors, may affect resilience. Therefore, considering current research gaps, by adopting a multi-center design to enhance sample diversity and generalizability, this study used LPA under the guidance of Kumpfer’s framework to (1) identify resilience profiles among palliative care nurses using LPA and (2) examine the influence of social support, coping styles, and death attitudes on these profiles.

Methods

Design

This research was designed as a multicentre cross-sectional study and was undertaken as per the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Setting and participants

Palliative nurses from 23 hospitals were recruited via a convenience sampling method. The study included participants who satisfied the following criteria: (1) adult registered nurses (≥ 18 years), (2) providing direct palliative care for patients in the palliative units for more than 1 year, and (3) informed consent and voluntary participation. Nurses were excluded for being absent from duty due to personal leave or continuing education lasting more than 6 months.

Sample size

The sample size was determined by the statistical methods adopted in this study, namely LPA and multivariate analysis. Based on LPA requirements, a minimum sample size of 300 was targeted to ensure profile detection accuracy [21]. For multivariate analysis, the conventional principle that the number of observations should be not less than five times the number of variables was adopted [22]. In this study, 19 variables were included, and a sample size of at least 95 cases was required. To concurrently meet both methodological requirements, the larger sample size of 300 was established as the recruitment goal. This study recruited 320 palliative nurses, satisfying the sample size criteria for all subsequent analyses.

Measurement

Sociodemographic characteristics

The information form was structured into two sections: a personal information section, including age, sex, marital status, education level, and health status; and a work-related information section, including professional title, working years, monthly income, and turnover intention.

Perceived Social Support Scale

The 12-item Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS) developed by Zimet et al. (1988) was utilized to assess social support for nurses with respect to three dimensions: family support, friend support, and other support [23]. Each item is rated on a scale ranging from 1 to 7. The total score of the scale ranges from 12 to 84, positively correlating with the level of social support. For this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the PSSS was estimated to be 0.914.

Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire

The 20-item Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ) compiled by Xie et al. (1998) was employed to evaluate coping styles among nurses [24]. The SCSQ comprises two dimensions: positive coping (Items 1–12) and negative coping (Items 13–20). Each item is assigned a score from 1 to 3. The total scores for the two dimensions were calculated separately, and a higher score on the positive coping subscale indicates a greater tendency to adopt positive coping strategies, while a higher score on the negative coping subscale reflects a greater reliance on negative coping strategies. An acceptable validity was identified in the present study, with Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.868 and 0.852 for the positive coping dimension and the negative coping dimension, respectively.

Death Attitude Profile-Revised

The Death Attitude Profile was originally designed by Gesser et al. (1987), and in this study [25], the Death Attitude Profile-Revised (DAP-R) was adopted to measure the attitudes towards death among nurses [26]. The scale consists of 32 items with 5 subscales, including fear of death, death avoidance, escape acceptance, neutral acceptance, and approach acceptance. Each item is graded on a 5-point Likert scale. Total scores were calculated separately for each subscale, with higher scores for fear of death and death avoidance representing a tendency towards negative acceptance and higher scores for escape acceptance, neutral acceptance, and approach acceptance demonstrating the likelihood of positive acceptance. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the five subscales in this study varied between 0.849 and 0.959.

Connor–Davison Resilience Scale

Resilience was assessed using the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC), originally developed by Connor and Davidson (2003) [14]. In this study, the Chinese version of the CD-RISC translated by Yu et al. (2007) was administered [27]. The scale comprises 25 items that consider three dimensions of resilience: tenacity, strength and optimism. Responses to each item were rated on a scale of 0 to 4 points. The total score of the scale is 0-100, and the score is proportional to the level of resilience. In this study, the CD-RISC exhibited acceptable reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.943.

Data collection

Nursing administrators from palliative units in 23 hospitals approved the data collection after the purpose and methods of the study were explained. Two trained nurses with master’s degrees distributed printed questionnaires to the palliative nurses and used uniform verbiage to give them instructions for completion. To ensure maximum response rates and study quality, nurse administrators led regular meetings to review the data collection process and check the questionnaire in a timely manner. The details of the participant selection process are presented in Figure S1. In total, 353 questionnaires were obtained, and after discarding responses with missing items or highly repetitive answers, 320 qualified questionnaires remained, resulting in a valid response rate of 90.65%.

Ethical considerations

Prior to the survey, the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital (No. 2022996), and it was authorized by other involved hospitals in the same manner. In the data collection process, all participants were informed of the study purpose and signed an informed consent form. Moreover, the nurses were assured that their information was strictly anonymized and that they could withdraw from the study if they preferred.

Data analysis

The collected questionnaires first underwent a rigorous quality control process. Specifically, two researchers independently examined all questionnaires for completeness and logical consistency. Any identified discrepancies or missing responses were resolved through team discussion and cross-verification against the original records. Following this procedure, the data were reviewed for completeness, cleaned to address any inconsistencies or missing values, and then entered into Excel before being analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.3. Sociodemographic information and questionnaire data were characterized with descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were presented as the means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages.

Potential profiles of resilience were distinguished via LPA. LPA is a clustering analysis method developed to classify individuals with similar characteristics into different subgroups [28]. A single profile model was initially fitted, and additional profiles were added incrementally. To ascertain the optimal model, the following fit indices were evaluated: (1) the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC), with lower values indicating a superior model; (2) the entropy, ranging from 0 to 1; a value exceeding 0.8 means that the model has acceptable accuracy [29]; and (3) the Lo–Mendell–Rubin test (LMRT) and bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), whose p < 0.05 suggest that the k model is preferable to the k-1 model. In addition, the interpretability of the profiles was also considered [30].

After the best-fitting models were identified, chi-square tests, Fisher’s exact tests, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the Kruskal‒Wallis H tests were applied to examine differences in sociodemographic variables, social support, coping styles, and death attitudes across each profile. Afterwards, independent variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were selected for multinomial logistic regression to determine the final influencing factors on the resilience profile.

Results

Participant characteristics

In this study, the average age of the included 320 palliative nurses was 31 years (SD = 7.23), ranging from 18 to 56 years. The majority of recruited nurses were female (n = 315, 98.44%), and a total of 200 nurses reported a partnered status (62.5%). Regarding educational attainment, 30 nurses had completed technical secondary school (9.38%), 118 had attended junior college (36.88%), and 172 held a bachelor’s degree or higher (53.75%). Fifteen nurses reported poor health, accounting for 4.69%, while 51.88% rated themselves as healthy (n = 166). In addition, 26.56% of the nurses had intermediate titles (n = 85), and 51.88% responded as being always or sometimes willing to leave their employment (n = 166). The working experience of participants ranged from 3 months to 38 years, with 31.88% having served in palliative care units for more than 10 years (n = 102), and 39.38% of nurses having less than five years of work experience (n = 126). The monthly salary for the majority of nurses (n = 227) was concentrated in the range of 3,000–7,000 RMB, 15.31% (n = 49) earned a lower income (1,000–3,000 RMB), while 13.75% (n = 44) earned a higher income (≥ 7,000 RMB) (Table 1).

Results of latent profile analysis

The LPA model was fitted using the scores of the 25 items in the CD-RISC. As presented in Table 2, the values of the AIC, BIC, and aBIC decreased as the number of profiles increased. The entropy values for all models were greater than 0.9, and 3-profile model presented the highest entropy value (0.972), indicating optimal accuracy. The AIC, BIC, and aBIC scores were notably higher in the 2- profile model, and the classification was overly simplistic to be of clinical relevance. The p value for LMR-LRT was > 0.05 in the model with 4 profiles, suggesting that the 3-profile model was superior to the 4-profile model. After further consideration of the practical implications of the model, model with three profiles was determined to be the most reliable potential one for identifying the heterogeneous characteristics of resilience in palliative nurses.

The distribution of the 25 items in each of the three profiles is shown in Fig. 1. For a clearer presentation, the 25 items of the Connor–Davison Resilience Scale were reordered by dimension. Profile 1 included 127 participants. The overall scores for Profile 1 were relatively low, and the items “You work to attain your goals” (Item 20) and “Pride in your achievements” (Item 21) scored significantly lower than the other items. This group of nurses may not have adapted to the palliative care work environment and may have low motivation and recognition of their work; hence, Profile 1 was named “low resilience - difficulty adapting to work stress”.

Profile 2 included 136 participants. The scores on the 25 items in Profile 2 were between those of Profile 1 and Profile 3, but the items “Not easily discouraged by failure” (Item 6), “Can handle unpleasant feelings” (Item 9) and “Coping with stress strengthens” (Item 16) received relatively low scores. This group of nurses demonstrated a certain ability to cope with difficulty but demonstrated significant fluctuations in negative emotions; therefore, Profile 2 was named “moderate resilience - challenged in regulating negative emotions”.

Profile 3 involved 57 participants. The 25 items in Profile 3 yielded relatively high scores, indicating that nurses in this profile are hopeful in addressing difficulties and promoting recovery from adversity. We named Profile 3 “high resilience - well coping in adversity recovery”.

Factors influencing the latent profile membership

Differences in sociodemographic and key variables across the three resilience profiles were examined. For categorical sociodemographic variables, the chi-square tests revealed significant differences in the education level, self-rated health condition, professional title, monthly income, and turnover intention across the three profiles (P < 0.05) (Table 1). For continuous variables, a one-way ANOVA found that three dimensions of social support (family support, friend support, and other support); the positive coping; and death avoidance significantly differed among the resilience profile (P < 0.05) (Table 1). Besides, the Kruskal‒Wallis H tests showed that the scores of two dimensions of death attitudes, including fear of death, and escape acceptance, were significantly different among resilience profile (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Multiple logistic regression revealed that compared to Profile 3, nurses with self-rated fair health (OR = 3.548, 95% CI: 1.307–9.631), turnover intention (OR = 3.123, 95% CI: 1.063–9.176), low levels of family support (OR = 0.848, 95% CI: 0.729–0.987), low levels of positive coping (OR = 0.709, 95% CI: 0.642–0.782), high levels of fear of death (OR = 1.238, 95% CI: 1.094–1.401), and low levels of escape acceptance of death (OR = 0.773, 95% CI: 0.678–0.881) were more likely to be categorized Profile 1. Besides, compared to Profile 3, nurses with turnover intention (OR = 3.194, 95% CI: 1.246–8.185), low levels of positive coping (OR = 0.855, 95% CI: 0.788–0.928), high levels of fear of death (OR = 1.139, 95% CI: 1.022–1.269), and low levels of escape acceptance of death (OR = 0.785, 95% CI: 0.704–0.877) had a higher likelihood to be classified in Profile 2 (Table 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to ascertain potential resilience profiles among palliative nurses via LPA. Similar to the previous study results from the LPA of resilience among nurses [31, 32], our study also identified three profiles, including low resilience - difficulty adapting to work stress, moderate resilience - challenged in regulating negative emotions, and high resilience - well coping in adversity recovery. This classification was further supported by the Kumpfer’s resilience framework, which posits that individuals may exhibit three different states of resilience, namely resilience enhancement, adaptation, or reduction following stressful stimuli. Besides, the Entropy value of 3-profile model is 0.972, revealing the distinct classification between the identified profiles an relative accuracy of the model categorization.

Profiles of resilience

In this study, 127 palliative nurses were categorized into a subgroup with low resilience - difficulty adapting to work stress”. Undoubtedly, this group of nurses requires attention, as they are vulnerable to the strain of palliative care work. As a common characteristic, this group demonstrated significantly low levels of competence and acknowledgement of their occupations. Currently, there is still a gap between the availability of education in palliative care in developed countries, resulting in Chinese nurses possessing insufficient palliative care skills to perform well at work [33]. Moreover, cultural factors often contribute to negative public perceptions and self-stigma regarding palliative care among nurses themselves [34], which further reduces occupational recognition. Therefore, to increase resilience among these nurses, managers are encouraged to accelerate the popularization of palliative care among the general public, integrate education in palliative care into work schedules, pair nurses with experienced colleagues to teach skills, and emphasize altruism in palliative settings [35, 36].

Profile 2 encompassed 136 palliative nurses who demonstrated moderate capacity to overcome challenges but were distinguished by their poor control of negative emotions. Palliative nurses frequently interact with suffering patients and caregivers and are tasked with delivering bad news [37]. Confronted with profound grief and emotional distress in patients and families, they are often presented as powerless and overwhelmed [38]. Repeated exposure to loss and death increases their susceptibility to significant emotional distress [39]. Besides, palliative nurses may also be under ethical pressure because they are sometimes involved in assisting patients with end-of-life decisions [40]. All these cumulative challenges exacerbate the emotional load of palliative nurses, causing them to cope poorly with stress. For this group of nurses, administrators need to focus on developing communication strategies and grief counselling abilities and provide staff with timely emotional screening and stress management training.

Profile 3 included 57 palliative nurses. Nurses in this group have the potential to face challenges squarely and maintain a positive mindset under workplace adversity, thereby defusing clinical suffering [41]. For these palliative nurses, managers need to identify and acknowledge their strengths, thus maintaining their high levels of resilience [36]. In addition, nurses with high resilience could lead unit gatherings and provide support for low-resilience colleagues. However, nurses in the “high resilience - well coping in adversity recovery” group accounted for only 17.81% of all participants, which reinforces that the positive psychological trait of palliative nurses are suboptimal and emphasises the strong need to promote resilience.

Influencing factors of latent profiles

In the present study, relatively unfavourable health, tendency towards turnover, lack of family support, low levels of positive coping, high perception of fear of death, and low levels of escape acceptance of death were identified as influencing factors that weaken resilience.

Unfavourable health is usually associated with chronic illnesses, emotional burdens, and impaired social functioning. Nurses in poor health have limited capacity to perform intense palliative care tasks due to insufficient energy and resources [42]. This also directly undermines the productivity of nurses, making it unlikely for them to demonstrate high resilience. Notably, health status declines with age [43], and with the delayed retirement policy in China [44], the health problems of nurses should receive special attention. Therefore, health status should be tracked dynamically throughout the professional careers of nurses, and they should be given corresponding support, such as health checkups, education for healthy life behaviour, emotional counselling, and flexible workload distribution.

The intention to leave usually reflects career pressures and unfair reward [45]. Unsurprisingly, palliative care is stressful both skill-wise and emotionally. Chronic stress leads to reduced self-efficacy and may even trigger feelings of illness [46], making it difficult for nurses to mobilize positively to achieve high resilience. Furthermore, despite the intensity of palliative care work, the monthly income of nurses in this study was clustered in the range of 5,000–7,000 RMB, which is lower than the average for nurses in China [47]. Unsatisfactory rewards may hinder fulfilment and optimism, which are important components of resilience. Considering the present context, to reduce the tendency for separation and promote resilience, nurses must improve their self-efficacy to convert stress into positive motivation [46], and be adequately rewarded according to their workload.

In terms of social support, family support is an enabler of resilience. Families are the core force that supports individuals in maintaining their physical and mental health [48], especially in the context of traditional Chinese culture. Nurses can confide in family members about work-related challenges, enjoy family companionship and engage in many forms of recreational activities with them [37, 49]. Through these efforts, the negative emotions of nurses may be alleviated, and their confidence in problem solving may be increased, thus positively affecting their resilience. For nursing managers, easing work‒family conflict can be used as an entry point to help nurses better access family support. Specifically, creating a harmonious work environment and assisting nurses in balancing work and family to reduce conflict indirectly contributes to the stabilization of family support for nurses [50].

Positive coping contributes to resilience. In contrast to negative individuals, positivist persons concentrate upon self-improvement to achieve favourable outcomes rather than experiencing unpleasant conditions [51]. That is, positive coping strategies help nurses maintain emotional stability while facing the demands of palliative care [52], which enables them to realize full potential and use initiative to overcome challenges. Hence, it may be difficult for them to avoid emotional exhaustion when dealing with high workloads, which is linked with the core of resilience. Therefore, palliative nurses should be instructed in positive coping approaches centred on end-of-life care to synergistically promote resilience.

In terms of attitudes towards death, fear of death was negatively related to resilience, whereas escape acceptance was positively associated with resilience. In Confucian-influenced Chinese culture, which emphasizes on “life-oriented” values, death is not only considered inauspicious but also a taboo subject that disrupts social harmony [53]. Consequently, nurses often internalize this cultural stigma, leading to a natural fear of death that impairs their ability to cope with frequent death exposure, ultimately manifesting as psychological distress and reduced professional efficacy in adversity [54]. Whereas escape acceptance is a type of positive acceptance of death, which is consistent with the Buddhist idea to redefine death as a natural transition. Nurses with a higher acceptance of death are often more motivated to engage in end-of-life care and adjust more effectively to the palliative care environment, thereby fostering greater resilience [55]. Given the impact of death attitudes on resilience, it is appropriate to implement death education [56]. To shift palliative nurses’ attitudes towards death, the philosophical values of life and death based on traditional Buddhist naturalism ethics are encouraged to be incorporated into the future curriculum. For instance, introducing the concept of impermanence (Anitya) may help nurses reframe their understanding of life cycles and frame death as a natural transition and a new beginning rather than an endpoint [57]. Meanwhile, mindfulness yoga rooted in Buddhist practice should be integrated into the curriculum to facilitate death acceptance [57]. Furthermore, creating facilitated group reflection sessions such as Peace of Mind Tea House gatherings are also encouraged to offer nurses a safe and structured space to articulate and reconstruct their perceptions of death [58], thereby strengthening resilience.

Implication for future clinical practice

The findings of this study offer several implications for future clinical practice. First, tailored interventions should be developed for nurses in different resilience profiles. For those in the “low resilience” profile, continuous palliative care skills education should be established to enhance clinical competencies. These initiatives should encompass symptom management, effective communication with patients, and approaches to address spiritual distress [59]. For nurses in the “moderate resilience” profile, supportive programs should focus on emotional regulation. Mindfulness exercises, positive psychology, and cognitive behavioral therapy, which have been proven effective in latest evidence, are recommended [60]. For the “high resilience” group, leadership opportunities should be given to leverage their strengths. Specifically, these nurses could be appointed as clinical mentors to guide newly hired staff. They also could serve as “resilience promoters” within peer support sessions, leading stress scenario simulations and coping strategy sharing. Second, healthcare institutions should foster supportive environments for nurses through equitable compensation, accessible mental health counseling, and flexible scheduling to mitigate work-family conflict. Moreover, death education including philosophical discussions rooted in culture, clinical simulations, and reflective practices should be implemented to empower nurses to enhance professional identity and provide more compassionate care.

Limitations

Several limitations of the study should be clarified. First, assessing resilience on the basis of self-reports may lead to certain reporting bias. Second, this study was a cross-sectional investigation, whereas resilience changes dynamically, longitudinal studies are needed to further explore the developmental patterns of resilience. Third, although this study included a multicentre sample from Southwest China, further expansion of the participants is still in need to support the application of findings.

Conclusions

The resilience of palliative nurses was categorized into three profiles: low resilience - difficulty adapting to work stress, moderate resilience - challenged in regulating negative emotions, and high resilience - well coping in adversity recovery. The three profiles present remarkably different characteristics, and health condition, turnover intentions, social support, coping styles, and death attitudes are influencing factors. Nursing managers should implement systematic screening to identify at-risk nurses and provide tiered support. Core palliative care skills training, psychoeducation, peer support, and culture-oriented systematic death education represent crucial directions for future clinical practice to address the specific needs of each profile.
