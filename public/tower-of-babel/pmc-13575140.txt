Sintilimab plus Bevacizumab biosimilar IBI305 and chemotherapy as a first-line treatment in advanced gastric or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma (SBAGA): a single-arm, phase Ib/II study
Authors: Mo Cheng, Pengfei Zhang, Qiancheng Hu, Jitao Zhou, Ruihong Dai, Wenke Li, Jing Wei, Menghui Xu, Yanna Lei, Xiaoying Li, Xiufeng Zheng, Qian Huang, Zhiping Li, Bo Zhang, Wen Zhuang, Zhixin Chen, Xiaolong Chen, Chaoyong Shen, Weihan Zhang, Kai Liu, Linyong Zhao, Xiaonan Yin, Boqiang Peng, Zhaolun Cai, Yuan Yin, Ji Chen, Chunjuan Liu, Deying Kang, Mojin Wang, Hongfeng Gou, Kun Yang, Jiankun Hu, Feng Bi, Ming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdding anti-PD-1 or anti-VEGF therapy to chemotherapy improves survival in patients with advanced gastric cancer, yet outcomes remain poor. This Phase Ib/II trial aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of combining sintilimab, the bevacizumab biosimilar IBI305, and CapeOX chemotherapy as a first-line treatment for patients with HER2-negative advanced gastric or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma (GA/GEJA). In phase Ib, no dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) that served as the primary endpoint were observed (N = 9). In phase II, the primary endpoint of the objective response rate (ORR) in the efficacy-evaluable population (N = 54) was 87.0% (95% CI: 75.1–94.6%), achieving the pre-specified target was reached. Secondary endpoints included progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), disease control rate (DCR), duration of response (DoR), and safety. The DCR was 98.1% (95% CI: 90.1–100.0%). At a median follow-up of 20.3 months, median PFS and DoR were 12.2 months (95% CI: 9.2–18.1) and 12.5 months (95% CI: 9.4–NE), respectively, while the median OS was not reached. A clinical benefit was observed regardless of PD-L1 CPS, Claudin-18.2 expression status, or the presence of peritoneal metastases. Treatment-related adverse events of grade ≥3 occurred in 66% of patients; the most common were neutropenia (14%), fatigue (13%), and thrombocytopenia (11%). The limitations of the study include the single-arm, non-randomized design and the modest sample size, which necessitate confirmation in larger randomized controlled trials. Nonetheless, sintilimab plus IBI305 and chemotherapy demonstrated promising antitumor activity and favorable PFS with manageable toxicity in HER2-negative advanced GA/GEJA. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is among the most frequently diagnosed types of cancer and represents the fourth leading cause of global cancer-related deaths1. While most patients are diagnosed with advanced-stage disease and surgery remains the primary curative treatment2, recent studies have shown improved survival outcomes with chemoimmunotherapy than with chemotherapy alone (e.g., KEYNOTE-8593, CheckMate 6494, RATIONALE-3055, and ORIENT-166). Despite these promising results, the majority of patients develop disease progression within 6–8 months, and the combination regimen with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and chemotherapy has been more beneficial for patients with high programmed cell deathligand 1 (PD-L1) expression (defined as combined positive score [CPS] ≥5 or ≥1) than those with low PD-L1 expression (i.e., with CPS < 5 or <1). However, approximately 50% of advanced GC patients have low PD-L1 expression (CPS < 5)7,8. The above considerations highlight an urgent need for additional therapeutic strategies in advanced or metastatic GC in order to enhance the efficacy of current treatments, particularly for patients with low PD-L1 expression.

In the later-line treatment of advanced GC, antiangiogenic targeted agents—including monoclonal antibodies and tyrosine kinase inhibitors—have achieved important progress. The REGARD and RAINBOW studies established the role of ramucirumab in the second‑line therapy of GC, and apatinib has also demonstrated favorable efficacy in later-line settings9–11. However, in the first-line treatment of GC, based on the results of the RAINFALL, AVAGAST, and AVATAR clinical trials, the combination of ramucirumab or bevacizumab with chemotherapy did not show superior efficacy compared with chemotherapy alone11–13. Consequently, the value of antiangiogenic therapy in the first-line treatment of advanced GC remains undetermined.

GC is often classified as a cold tumor with suppressed T-cell generation and infiltration. Abnormal tumor vasculature plays a key role in maintaining this immunosuppressive environment. VEGF not only regulates angiogenesis but also mediates tumor immunosuppression through multiple mechanisms, including suppressing dendritic cell maturation, reducing T‑cell infiltration, promoting the proliferation of immunosuppressive cells such as myeloid‑derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs), upregulating programmed cell death-1 (PD‑1) expression on T cells, and exacerbating tumor hypoxia14,15. Antiangiogenic therapy can reverse these processes: it relieves the VEGF‑mediated suppression of dendritic cell maturation, thereby promoting T‑cell priming and activation; meanwhile, it reduces the immunosuppressive activity of MDSCs and Tregs, leading to remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. Moreover, anti‑VEGF therapy can induce “tumor vessel normalization,” which alleviates tumor hypoxia, improves tumor perfusion, and enhances T‑cell infiltration into tumors16,17. Together, these changes synergistically potentiate the T‑cell‑dependent tumor‑killing effect of PD‑1/PD‑L1 inhibitors. Combinations of antiangiogenic agents and PD-1 blockers have shown encouraging results in cancers like hepatocellular carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, and nonsquamous non-small cell lung cancer, leading to their approval as standard treatments18–20. These findings suggest that combining antiangiogenic agents with PD-1 inhibitors and chemotherapy may similarly offer a promising approach for advanced GC.

The anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody IBI305 is a biosimilar to bevacizumab, exhibiting comparable pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, and safety profiles to bevacizumab in Phase I studies21. In a notable Phase III trial in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), IBI305 combined with paclitaxel/carboplatin demonstrated similar efficacy and safety to bevacizumab: objective response rates (ORR) were 44.3% for IBI305 and 46.4% for bevacizumab, and there was no significant difference in progression-free survival (PFS) (7.64 vs. 7.77 months, p = 0.9987)22. Furthermore, IBI305 has shown preliminary efficacy and acceptable safety in EGFR-mutated nonsquamous NSCLC after EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy and in unresectable HBV-associated hepatocellular carcinoma in the ORIENT-31 and ORIENT-32 trials, respectively23,24.

Here, we initiate a Phase Ib/II trial to assess the efficacy and safety of sintilimab, IBI305, and chemotherapy as a first-line treatment for advanced gastric adenocarcinoma or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma (GA/GEJA). In this work, the combination of sintilimab, IBI305, and chemotherapy demonstrates notable clinical activity with a manageable safety profile, supporting further investigation of this regimen.

Results

Dose escalation

Nine patients were enrolled for the IBI305 dose-escalation part of the study: three each in the 7.5 mg/kg, 10 mg/kg, and 15 mg/kg groups. Within 6 weeks of treatment initiation, at the 7.5 mg/kg dose level, grade 3 fatigue was observed in one of three patients, and all three patients experienced grade 2 nausea. At the 10 mg/kg dose level, one of three patients experienced grade 3 fatigue, while two of three patients developed grade 2 neutropenia. At the 15 mg/kg dose level, one patient experienced grade 3 thrombocytopenia, and another developed grade 2 hypertension. No dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) were observed in any group. However, considering the risk of bleeding and perforation in gastrointestinal tumors caused by long-term, high-dose use of the anti-angiogenic agent IBI305, the dose of 10 mg/kg was determined as the recommended phase II dose (RP2D).

Baseline characteristics

Between March 10, 2023, and March 16, 2024, a total of 57 patients were enrolled in the study. This comprised 9 patients in the Phase Ib dose-escalation cohort and 48 patients in the Phase II expansion cohort. In Phase II, one patient was excluded due to discordant final pathology, and two patients withdrew informed consent after completing one cycle of treatment. Fifty-six patients received at least one cycle of treatment and were included in the safety analysis. Fifty-four patients were evaluable for the primary endpoint (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of patients evaluable for efficacy are summarized in Table 1. The majority of patients in this study are male (68%, 38/56) and under 65 years old (75%, 42/56), with a median age of 59 years (IQR: 55–64). Most have metastatic disease (86%, 48/56) and gastric adenocarcinoma (GA) (77%, 43/56) as the primary tumor location. Common metastatic sites include lymph nodes (57%, 32/56) and the peritoneum (48%, 27/56). Most patients (70%, 39/56) have an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0. Regarding molecular characteristics, PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1 is observed in 66% (37/56), while 93% (52/56) are proficient mismatch repair (pMMR)/microsatellite stable (MSS), and 91% (51/56) are Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) negative. Signet ring cell carcinoma is present in 20% (11/56) of cases. Claudin-18.2 expression analysis shows negative/low (N/L) expression in 50% (28/56) of the patients, while moderate/high (M/H) expression is seen in 34% (19/56). In terms of body mass index (BMI), most patients are classified as normal weight (75%, 42/56).

Trial profile.

A total of 176 patients were screened, and 57 were enrolled. One patient was excluded from phase II due to discordant final pathology, and 56 patients started treatment and were included in the safety analysis. Two patients withdrew informed consent after one cycle in phase II, leaving 54 patients evaluable for efficacy (9 from phase Ib, 45 from phase II). Among these, 38 discontinued treatment (34 due to disease progression, 2 received other treatments, and 2 died from other causes), and 16 remained on treatment at data cutoff.

Efficacy results

A total of 54 patients were included in the efficacy analysis. The ORR was 87.0% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 75.1–94.6%), and the disease control rate (DCR) was 98.1% (95% CI: 90.1–100.0%) (Table 2 and Fig. 2a). At the data cut-off date of July 23, 2025, the median follow-up duration was 20.3 months (95% CI: 19.2–21.3). The median PFS was 12.2 months (95% CI: 9.2–18.1), while the 6-month and 12-month PFS rates were 77.8% (95% CI: 67.4–89.7%) and 51.9% (95% CI: 40.1–67.0%), respectively (Figs. 2b and 3a). The median overall survival (OS) was not reached. The 6-month and 12-month OS rates were 94.4% (95% CI: 88.5–100.0%) and 75.9% (95% CI: 65.3–88.2%; Figs. 2b and 3b), respectively. The ORR based on the intention-to-treat (ITT) population was 83.9% (95% CI: 71.7–92.4%), with a median PFS of 12.0 months (95% CI: 8.9–17.1) and median OS not reached (Supplementary Fig. 1). After excluding 4 patients with dMMR and 2 patients with EBV-positive status, we re-analyzed efficacy in the remaining cohort. The analysis showed a median PFS of 11.8 months (95% CI: 8.9–16.1), with 6-month and 12-month PFS rates of 77.1% (95% CI: 66.1–89.9%) and 47.9% (95% CI: 35.7–64.4%), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The median OS was not reached, and the 6-month and 12-month OS rates were 95.8% (95% CI: 90.3–100.0%) and 75.0% (95% CI: 63.7–88.3%), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2b). The median time to response was 1.8 months (range, 1.3–5.5). The median duration of response (DoR) was 12.5 months (95% CI: 9.4–not estimable [NE]). At 6 and 12 months, 70.2% (95% CI: 58.3–84.6%) and 53.2% (95% CI: 40.7–69.6%) of patients, respectively, maintained a response (Fig. 3c). As of the data cut-off date, 63% (34/54) of patients had experienced disease progression, with 63% (34/54) of those receiving second-line therapy. At the time of analysis, 16 patients (30%) were still receiving the investigational combination treatment (Figs. 1 and 2b).

Safety results

All 56 patients who received at least one dose of the study treatment were included in the safety analysis (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 1). 96% (54/56) patients experienced at least one treatment-related adverse event (TRAE) of any grade, with the most common being fatigue (44/56, 79%), nausea (38/56, 68%), neutropenia (35/56, 63%), decreased white blood cell count (34/56, 61%), anorexia (29/56, 52%), and paresthesia or peripheral neuropathy (28/56, 50%). TRAEs of Grade 3 or higher occurred in 66% (37/56) patients, most frequently including neutropenia (8/56, 14%), fatigue (7/56, 13%), and thrombocytopenia (6/56, 11%). Twenty per cent (11/56) of patients experienced serious adverse events (SAEs), including gastrointestinal bleeding (4/11, 36%), gastric perforation (1/11, 9%), pulmonary embolism (1/11, 9%), immune-related pneumonitis (1/11, 9%), thrombocytopenia (1/11, 9%), rash (1/11, 9%), and hepatotoxicity (1/11, 9%). Twenty-three per cent (13/56) of patients experienced immune-related AEs associated with sintilimab, including hypothyroidism (9/13, 69%), skin rash (2/13, 15%), myocarditis (1/13, 8%), pneumonitis (1/13, 8%), and colitis (1/13, 8%). Additionally, two patients died due to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), a cause unrelated to the study treatment.

Dose reductions of at least one component of the regimen were required in 20 patients (36%), primarily due to fatigue (7/20, 35%), gastrointestinal bleeding (6/20, 30%), neutropenia (5/20, 25%), vomiting (4/20, 20%), palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome (1/20, 5%), oral mucositis or ulceration (1/20, 5%), hypertension (1/20, 5%), or elevated creatinine (1/20, 5%). Dose reductions of IBI305 were required in six patients (11%) due to renal impairment (1/6, 17%), thrombotic events (2/6, 33%), gastrointestinal bleeding (1/6, 17%), hypertension (1/6, 17%), or weight loss (1/6, 17%). Considering TRAEs that led to discontinuation, four patients (7%) stopped oxaliplatin for reasons including fatigue (2/4, 50%), drug hypersensitivity (1/4, 25%), and thrombocytopenia (1/4, 25%). Sintilimab was discontinued in four patients (7%) due to dermatitis (2/4, 50%), concurrent pneumonitis and myocarditis (1/4, 25%), or colitis (1/4, 25%). Seven patients (13%) discontinued IBI305 following gastrointestinal bleeding (5/7, 71%), gastric perforation (1/7, 14%), and otorrhagia (1/7, 14%).

We described in detail the clinical characteristics and management of six cases with gastrointestinal bleeding events (Supplementary Table 2). All events occurred in patients with the primary gastric tumor present, with most occurring between the 7th and 11th treatment cycles. The severity of bleeding was predominantly grade 3 (3 cases), grade 2 in 2 cases, and grade 4 in 1 case. Clinical management measures included embolization, transfusion, and medication support; IBI305 was permanently discontinued for patients who experienced ≥ grade 3 events, whereas those who experienced grade 2 events either had their dose reduced or IBI305 was temporarily withheld. Four patients withdrew from the study due to disease progression, and two patients are currently under maintenance therapy. No fatal outcomes or admissions to the intensive care unit occurred.

Subgroup analysis

An exploratory analysis was conducted to evaluate potential genomic and immunohistochemical biomarkers. PD-L1 expression was assessed using the CPS in a cohort of 51 patients. Among patients with PD-L1-negative tumors (CPS < 1), the median PFS was 14.5 months (95% CI: 10.4–NE), compared with 12.1 months (95% CI: 9.2–NE) in the PD-L1-positive group (CPS ≥ 1), with no statistically significant difference observed (p = 0.836; hazard ratio [HR] (95% CI): 0.92 (0.45–1.91); Fig. 4a). Further stratification by PD-L1 expression levels using a CPS cutoff of 5 revealed a median PFS of 12.2 months (95% CI: 9.2–NE) in patients with CPS < 5, versus 16.1 months (95% CI: 8.4–NE) in those with CPS ≥ 5 (p = 0.272; HR (95% CI): 0.68 (0.34–1.32); Fig. 4b).

The tumor mutational burden (TMB) was detected for 38 patients, resulting in a median value of 4.6 mutations per megabase (mut/Mb). Correspondingly, patients benefited from the combination therapy irrespective of either high TMB (TMB-H) or low TMB (TMB-L) status, as defined via a cutoff value of 4.6 mut/Mb (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The ORR was 80.0% (95% CI: 56.3–94.2%) in the TMB-L subgroup and 83.3% (95% CI: 58.6–96.4%) in the TMB-H subgroup, while the median PFS was 11.0 months (95% CI: 6.7–NE) in the TMB-L group and 14.4 months (95% CI: 10.5–NE) in the TMB-H group (p = 0.371; HR (95% CI): 0.72 (0.34–1.52); Supplementary Fig. 3b). Among the 46 patients who underwent testing for Claudin-18.2 levels, those with N/L expression and those with M/H expression all benefited from combination therapy, as shown by the ORR of 85.7% (95% CI: 67.3–96.0%) in the Claudin-18.2 N/L subgroup and 88.9% (95% CI: 65.2–98.6%) in the Claudin-18.2 M/H subgroup, as well as the median PFS values of 13.2 months (95% CI: 9.6–NE) vs. 13.2 months (95% CI: 8.3–NE) for the same two subgroups, respectively (p = 0.686; HR (95% CI): 0.86 (0.42–1.76); Supplementary Fig. 3c).

However, patients with postoperative recurrence exhibited poorer median PFS months 7.8 (95% CI: 6.6–NE) than those with initially advanced disease [13.9 months (95% CI: 10.5–NE); p = 0.073; HR (95% CI): 0.48 (0.17–1.41); Supplementary Fig. 3d]. The median PFS was 10.4 months (95% CI: 8.3–15.0) in patients with peritoneal metastasis and 16.6 months (95% CI: 9.6–NE) in those without peritoneal metastasis (p = 0.057; HR (95% CI): 0.54 (0.29–1.04); Supplementary Fig. 3e).

Exploratory biomarker analysis

Targeted gene capture sequencing was used to conduct a comprehensive genomic analysis of baseline paired samples from 38 patients. The most common mutations were in TP53 (63%), ARID1A (18%), CCNE1 (16%), CDH1 (16%), FGFR2 (13%), and PIK3CA (13%; Supplementary Fig. 4a). No improvement in PFS was associated with mutations in TP53 or ARID1A (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). CDH1 mutations were associated with shorter median PFS [WT CDH1 vs. MUT CDH1: 13.9 months (95% CI: 11.9–NE) vs. 5.9 months (95% CI: 5.4–NE)], while CCNE1 mutations (including amplifications) corresponded with longer median PFS [WT CCNE1 vs. MUT CCNE1: 11.8 months (95% CI: 7.5–15.0) vs. 17.6 months (95% CI: 14.9–NE), Supplementary Fig. 4d, e]. In addition, MET amplification was detected in two patients, neither of whom achieved an objective response (one with progressive disease (PD) and one with stable disease (SD)).

Discussion

Despite recent advances with immune checkpoint inhibitors in the treatment of advanced HER2-negative GA/GEJA, clinical outcomes remain suboptimal, with mPFS from 6 to 8 months and mOS from 15 to 18 months only3–6. In this Phase Ib/II trial, the triplet regimen of sintilimab (a PD-1 inhibitor) combined with the bevacizumab biosimilar IBI305 (an anti‑VEGF monoclonal antibody) and CapeOX chemotherapy, as the first-line treatment in advanced HER2-negative gastric/gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma, demonstrated promising efficacy and a manageable safety profile. The RP2D of the combination was established with no unexpected toxicities. The trial met its primary endpoint, with an ORR of 87.0% (95% CI: 75.1–94.6%). After a median follow‑up of 20.3 months, the secondary endpoint, the median PFS, was 12.2 months (95% CI: 9.2–18.1), while a median OS was not reached. Previous studies have explored PD‑1 inhibitors combined with chemotherapy and multi‑kinase VEGF inhibitors25–27. Although phase II trials of apatinib and regorafenib showed promising efficacy, the phase III trial of lenvatinib combined with immunotherapy and chemotherapy failed to demonstrate superiority over chemotherapy alone25–27. Our regimen differs from TKI-based combinations in three key aspects: (1) Differences in mechanisms of action: TKIs such as lenvatinib and regorafenib are multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitors that act not only on VEGFR but also broadly inhibit other targets. For example, the inhibition of VEGFR-3 may affect lymphangiogenesis, indirectly hindering the migration and infiltration of immune cells into tumor tissues, thereby weakening the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors28. (2) Treatment intensity: we used longer induction (4–6 cycles) and a chemotherapy-containing maintenance regimen, while LEAP‑015 limited chemotherapy to 4 cycles. (3) Toxicity and exposure: multi-target TKIs showed higher ≥G3 AE rates (79%) and treatment discontinuation, whereas our regimen had lower toxicity-driven discontinuation, allowing more sustained therapy. Regarding VEGF monoclonal antibodies, the AVAGAST and AVATAR studies did not show a benefit for bevacizumab plus chemotherapy over chemotherapy alone, and while ramucirumab succeeded in second‑line GC, it failed in the first‑line setting12,13. This may be associated with dose selection; the present study used IBI305 at 10 mg/kg, whereas AVAGAST and AVATAR employed 7.5 mg/kg. Furthermore, anti‑angiogenic therapy may sensitize tumors to immunotherapy, thereby enhancing efficacy. The field has moved rapidly to a focus on bispecific VEGFxPD-1 agents. Ivonescimab (AK112), a first-in-class PD-1/VEGF bispecific antibody, has been approved for non-small cell lung cancer, and related trials in GC are ongoing (NCT06846346)29. Compared with monoclonal antibody combinations, bispecific VEGFxPD-1 agents offer theoretical advantages, including dual pathway blockade to overcome resistance, enhanced specificity with potentially lower toxicity, and improved convenience with reduced costs. However, challenges remain, including the potential upregulation of PD-L1, the expansion of Tregs, and T-cell exhaustion. Clinical efficacy in GC requires validation in prospective trials29.

In addition, clinical benefit was observed across key subgroups, including patients with PD-L1–low tumors and those with peritoneal metastases, subpopulations that typically derive minimal benefit from chemoimmunotherapy. Although the PD‑L1 positivity rates in our cohort (66% for CPS ≥ 1 and 38% for CPS ≥ 5) were lower than those reported in phase III trials such as KEYNOTE‑8593, CheckMate 6494, RATIONALE‑3055, and ORIENT‑166, the objective response rate remained notably high at 87.0% (95% CI: 75.1–94.6%). For the PD-L1-negative (CPS < 1) and PD-L1-positive (CPS ≥ 1) subgroups, the median PFS rates were 14.5 months (95% CI: 10.4–NE) and 12.1 months (95% CI: 9.2–NE), respectively (p = 0.836). While a benefit was generally observed only for patients with high PD-L1 levels (CPS ≥ 1 or CPS ≥ 5) in the above Phase III trials, this study demonstrated that patients may benefit from the addition of IBI305 regardless of PD-L1 status. In our study, 48% of patients had peritoneal metastasis, which is notably higher than the proportions reported in the CheckMate 649 (24%) and ORIENT-16 (19.9%) trials4,6. Peritoneal metastases in GC have traditionally been associated with poor responsiveness to systemic chemoimmunotherapy30,31. This resistance is often attributed to the restrictive blood-peritoneal barrier and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment within the peritoneal cavity30,31. However, in our study, patients with peritoneal metastases demonstrated survival outcomes comparable to those without such involvement when treated with the combination regimen. Notably, progression-free survival was similar between the two groups, suggesting that peritoneal metastases did not diminish the therapeutic efficacy. This result is consistent with the findings of the RATIONALE-305 study5. This finding is clinically relevant, as more than 50% of patients with stage T3 or T4 GC eventually develop peritoneal metastases following curative resection. These results indicate that adding IBI305 to chemoimmunotherapy may help overcome the traditional barriers to treatment efficacy in cases of peritoneal dissemination. Several studies have linked Claudin-18.2-positive GC to poor prognosis and reduced effectiveness of immunotherapy32. However, in our study, 31% of patients were characterized as Claudin-18.2 M/H, yet the ORR and PFS were comparable between these and the Claudin-18.2 N/L GA/GEJA patients. This suggests that the addition of IBI305 to chemotherapy and sintilimab offers similar therapeutic benefits, irrespective of Claudin-18.2 levels.

Several potential synergistic mechanisms may underlie the enhanced efficacy observed with this triplet regimen. Abnormal tumor vasculature has been shown to inhibit the recruitment and antitumor effect of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and promote the accumulation of regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, thus maintaining the tumor immunosuppressive environment33. An anti-angiogenic inhibitor has been shown to normalize disorganized and permeable tumor vasculature. This vascular normalization can enhance the intratumoral delivery of cytotoxic agents and promote infiltration of immune effector cells, such as T lymphocytes, into the tumor microenvironment, thereby amplifying the anti-tumor immune response34. Chemotherapy-induced tumor cell death contributes to this process by releasing tumor-associated antigens, which enhance antigen uptake and presentation by dendritic cells and other antigen-presenting cells, leading to the priming and activation of tumor-specific T cells. Sintilimab, a monoclonal antibody targeting PD-1, sustains and strengthens this immune response by blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, thereby preventing T-cell exhaustion and restoring anti-tumor activity. Moreover, bevacizumab biosimilar IBI305 may further support immune activation by modulating the tumor microenvironment. Preclinical evidence indicates that it reduces immunosuppressive cell populations, such as regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, while increasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, thus fostering a more immune-permissive environment14,17. These results underscore the potential of combining anti-VEGF therapy (IBI305) with immunotherapy and chemotherapy as a first-line treatment strategy for advanced GC. This approach is consistent with evolving treatment paradigms in other malignancies, such as HCC and NSCLC, where the combination of antiangiogenic agents and immunotherapy has already become standard care. A Phase III trial is warranted in order to validate these findings and confirm their efficacy in the frontline setting.

In this study, TRAEs were observed in most patients (96%), with a 66% incidence of grade 3 or higher, closely aligning with the rates seen in the CheckMate 649 (60%)4, RATIONALE-305 (53%)5, and ORIENT-16 (59.8%) trials6. In addition, a range of AEs commonly associated with antiangiogenic agents was observed, which is consistent with findings from the AVAGAST studies12. However, the incidence of gastrorrhagia was slightly higher, with symptomatic gastrointestinal bleeding events occurring in 6 patients (11%), particularly during the maintenance therapy, which may be related to the prolonged exposure to IBI305 and the acidic gastric contents resulting from the longer PFS observed in this study. Importantly, none of these bleeding events resulted in fatal outcomes. An increased bleeding risk has also been documented in other studies combining chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and anti-angiogenic therapy, suggesting that this is a class effect associated with such combination regimens rather than a unique safety signal specific to IBI305. This highlights the necessity for enhanced monitoring and preventive strategies in clinical practice.

In terms of predictive biomarker analysis, a comprehensive genomic analysis using targeted-capture sequencing was conducted on paired samples to identify the potential predictors of benefit from this regimen. Our results revealed that mutations or amplifications of CCNE1 were associated with longer PFS, while CDH1 mutations were linked to shorter PFS. These findings are consistent with the previous studies, indicating that CDH1 and CCNE1 may serve as promising predictive biomarkers for GC35–37. In addition, one patient with MET gene amplification experienced disease progression after the first assessment. MET gene amplification has been associated with protein overexpression, which can increase tumor invasiveness, metastasis, advanced disease stages, and poor prognosis38. Regrettably, this study did not analyze biomarkers related to the VEGF pathway, such as tumor VEGF‑A and VEGFR expression, circulating VEGF levels, or microvessel density. Although circulating VEGF has shown strong prognostic value across various cancer types, its role as a predictive biomarker for anti-VEGF therapy or ICIs remains unclear. Future studies should integrate multi-omics indicators—including VEGF-related markers, the tumor immune microenvironment, and other indicators—to develop artificial intelligence (AI) predictive models for the efficacy of such combination regimens and enable precise patient stratification.

Despite the exploratory nature of this early-phase trial, the standardized 3 + 3 dose-escalation design and rigorous patient selection support internal validity. Historical benchmarks and consistency between observed efficacy and mechanistic insights from biomarker analyses strengthen the findings. Although the cohort was small, population uniformity enabled focused efficacy assessment. These results provide a strong rationale for future randomized studies. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, this was a single-arm, single-center phase Ib/II trial lacking a randomized control group, which may introduce selection bias and limit subgroup analysis reliability. The two-sided α = 0.2 may increase false-positive risk. Second, with a median follow-up of 20.3 months, the median overall survival was not reached, which limits the assessment of long-term benefits and potential late-onset toxicities. Third, subgroup analyses were not based on formally prespecified hypotheses; all reported p-values are nominal and should be interpreted as exploratory. Finally, biomarker analyses were exploratory in nature, relied on a limited set of paired samples, were not adjusted for multiple testing, and involved subgroups with small sample sizes. Therefore, the identified biomarkers should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Moreover, key angiogenesis-related biomarkers—such as tumor VEGF-A/VEGFR expression, circulating VEGF levels, and microvessel density—were not assessed, constraining the mechanistic understanding of the antiangiogenic therapy.

In conclusion, this Phase Ib/II trial demonstrates that the combination of sintilimab, the bevacizumab biosimilar IBI305, and chemotherapy is both feasible and active as a first-line treatment for advanced GA/GEJA. This triplet regimen achieved a high objective response rate with manageable toxicity, supporting its potential for further evaluation in larger, randomized trials.

Methods

Study design and participants

This trial single-arm, open-label, phase Ib/II study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 2022 [1307]) and is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05640609). The study protocol is provided in the supplementary appendix. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This trial is no longer enrolling participants; the only ongoing activity is survival follow-up.

The Phase Ib portion of this trial utilized a standard 3 + 3 dose-escalation design to evaluate safety and to determine the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and RP2D of the combination. The starting dose of IBI305 was 7.5 mg/kg, with planned escalation to 10 mg/kg and 15 mg/kg. Cohorts of 3 patients received escalating doses sequentially. If ≥1 DLT occurred in a cohort, 3 additional patients were enrolled at that dose level. Escalation proceeded if ≤1/6 patients experienced DLTs. The MTD was defined as the highest dose level at which ≤1/6 patients had DLTs. The Phase II part of this study adopted a single‑arm, open‑label design. The final planned enrollment was 48 patients. All Phase Ib patients were allowed to continue at RP2D after the MTD was established.

The main inclusion criteria for participants were: age 18–70 years; histologically or cytologically confirmed GA/GEJA with the presence of at least one measurable lesion, as defined by the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST 1.1); unresectable recurrent or metastatic disease based on imaging or surgical assessment; no prior systemic therapy for advanced GA/GEJA; and an ECOG performance status of 0 or 1. Patients having received previous adjuvant or neoadjuvant treatment (including chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy) for GA/GEJA were deemed eligible if their last treatment had concluded at least 6 months prior to enrollment. Key exclusion criteria included: established HER2-positive status (i.e., HER2 3+, or HER2 2+ with FISH positivity); prior treatment with ICIs; a history of other malignancies; active infections or interstitial lung disease; and significant clinically relevant bleeding or an obvious bleeding tendency in the 3-month period before enrollment. Sex was not considered in the study design, and the sex of participants was determined based on self-report. Both male and female participants who met the inclusion/exclusion criteria were eligible for this study. For a full list of inclusion/exclusion criteria, refer to the trial protocol (supplementary materials).

Procedures

In this study, patients were treated in 21-day cycles with the CapeOX regimen, which included oxaliplatin (130 mg/m², administered via intravenous infusion on day 1) and capecitabine (1000 mg/m², taken orally twice daily from days 1 to 14). Additionally, patients received sintilimab (200 mg) and the bevacizumab biosimilar IBI305, both administered via intravenous infusion on day 1. During the Phase Ib dose-escalation portion, IBI305 was given once every three weeks at varying doses (7.5 mg/kg, 10 mg/kg, or 15 mg/kg) to evaluate DLTs within the first six weeks of treatment, following a 3 + 3 design. The starting dose of IBI305 was 7.5 mg/kg. In Phase II, patients continued treatment with the combination of CapeOX, sintilimab, and IBI305 at the recommended dose established in Phase Ib. They received 4 to 6 cycles of the full triplet regimen, after which they transitioned to maintenance therapy with capecitabine, sintilimab, and IBI305. Maintenance treatment continued until patients experienced intolerable toxicity, disease progression, or withdrew informed consent. If tumor progression was detected, patients exited the study and proceeded to receive subsequent treatments.

Response assessments were conducted every six weeks or after every two treatment cycles using computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in accordance with RECIST 1.1 criteria. Dose reductions for oxaliplatin, capecitabine, and IBI305 were allowed when necessary, and if any drug was discontinued for any reason, the remaining treatments could continue. Patients could withdraw from the study due to severe or intolerable toxicity, medical necessity as determined by investigators, disease progression, or the voluntary withdrawal of their consent.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint of phase Ib was the assessment of DLTs, which were defined as: grade ≥3 non-hematologic adverse events (AEs) (excluding alopecia, fatigue, nausea/vomiting responsive to therapy, or electrolyte imbalances corrected within 48 h). Grade 4 hematologic AEs included neutropenia lasting >7 days, febrile neutropenia, or thrombocytopenia with bleeding. DLTs were assessed during the first 6 weeks of treatment (Cycle 1–2). The primary endpoint of Phase II was the ORR, assessed according to RECIST 1.1 criteria. The secondary endpoints included PFS, OS, DCR, DoR, and safety. PFS was defined as the time from the first dose to either radiologically documented disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Patients without an event at the data‑cutoff date were censored at the date of their last evaluable tumor assessment. Patients could withdraw from the study due to severe or intolerable toxicity, medical necessity as determined by investigators, disease progression, or the voluntary withdrawal of their consent. PFS was also censored at the last tumor assessment prior to starting a new anticancer therapy if such therapy began before documented progression. OS was measured from the first dose to death from any cause. Patients alive at the data‑cutoff date were censored at their last contact date. DCR represented the proportion of patients achieving a best overall response of complete response (CR), partial response (PR), or SD, while DoR was defined as the time from the first occurrence of CR or PR until disease progression or death, whichever came first. All primary and secondary endpoints have been completely reported in the manuscript. Additionally, post-hoc subgroup analyses were performed for PD-L1 expression, peritoneal metastasis, recurrence, Claudin-18.2 expression, and TMB. An exploratory analysis was conducted to investigate the potential association of biomarkers with treatment efficacy.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical assays for PD-L1 (clone 22C3; DAKO Autostainer Link48; RTU) and Claudin-18.2 (clone d313D22; Katush Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) were conducted at baseline prior to treatment. The PD-L1 CPS was calculated by dividing the number of PD-L1-positive tumor cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages by the total number of viable tumor cells and then multiplying the resultant value by 100. Claudin-18.2 positivity, hereafter termed Claudin-18.2 M/H expression, was defined as 2+ to 3+ membranous staining with a positive membranous area of ≥40%. Claudin-18.2 N/L expression was defined as membranous staining intensity of 0 or 1+; or, despite having an intensity of 2+/3+, having a positive membranous area of <40%.

Targeted capture sequencing

Paired formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue specimens from 38 patients were subjected to detailed genomic analysis of a 1021-gene panel via targeted capture sequencing at baseline prior to treatment. Specifically, Genomic DNA was extracted from FFPE tissue samples using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (catalog No. 56404, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit fluorometer with the Qubit dsDNA HS (High Sensitivity) Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and fragment size distribution and total yield were assessed. Custom-designed biotinylated oligonucleotide probes (Geneplus-Beijing Biomedical Engineering Co., Ltd., China) were designed to cover 1021 commonly mutated genes. The panel included: (1) all 4847 exons of 312 genes (enabling CNV detection), (2) intronic, promoter, and fusion breakpoint regions of 38 genes, and (3) 1778 coding regions of 709 additional genes. The complete gene list is provided in Supplementary Data 1. Libraries were constructed using the Human 1021 Gene Mutation Detection Kit (catalog No. KB0009048, Geneplus-Beijing Biomedical Engineering Co., Ltd., China), quantified, and hybridized to the probes. DNA sequencing was performed on the Gene + Seq-2000 sequencing system (Geneplus-Beijing Biomedical Engineering Co., Ltd., China) with 2 × 100 bp paired-end reads. Reads were aligned to GRCh37 (hg19) using BWA-MEM (version 0.7.12), with reads of mapping quality <30 filtered out. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (indels) were detected using MuTect (version 1.1.6) and GATK (version 4.5.0.0), respectively. germline variants were filtered using matched blood sequencing and public databases (dbSNP, gnomAD). All candidate variants were manually reviewed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV). TMB was calculated as the total number of somatic non-synonymous mutations per megabase of the captured coding region. MSI status was assessed using MSI sensor software.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated based on the primary endpoint of ORR using an exact one-sample binomial test comparing ORR with a historical benchmark. Data from existing clinical trials indicated an ORR of 58% for anti-PD-1 agents in combination with chemotherapy. We anticipated an increased ORR of 73% when adding IBI305 to a PD-1 inhibitor (sintilimab) and CapeOX regimen; therefore, assuming a two-sided alpha value of 0.2 and a statistical power (i.e., 1−β) of 0.8, the estimated sample size was 45 patients. Allowing for a 5% dropout rate, the final target sample size was determined to be 48 patients. Efficacy analyses were performed in both the ITT, defined as all patients who received at least one dose of study treatment, and the efficacy-evaluable population, defined as patients who received at least one post-baseline efficacy assessment. Safety analyses were conducted in the ITT population. For subgroup and biomarker analyses, missing data were not imputed; biomarker subgroup analyses were exploratory and based on available data, with no adjustment for multiplicity (p values are descriptive). The experiments were not randomized, and investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Demographic, disease, and treatment characteristics were summarized as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, while continuous variables were reported as medians with IQR or 95% CI. Exact two-sided 95% confidence intervals for ORR and DCR were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method. The Kaplan–Meier method was employed to estimate OS, PFS, and DoR. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare ORR across subgroups. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.2), SPSS Statistics (version 22.0), GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.0), and Microsoft Excel.
