Which insulin resistance indices best predict future frailty progression in a cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stage 0–3 population: a national prospective cohort and machine learning study
Authors: Ruikang Liu, Guangyi Yang, Yiying Liu, Jun Li, Botan Xu, Guancheng Ye, Xuanchun Huang, Shiyi Tao, Tiantian Xue, Yonghao Li, Fuyuan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsulin resistance (IR) is implicated in frailty progression within Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome populations. While multiple non-insulin-based IR indices have been proposed, their comparative utility in predicting frailty across early CKM stages (0–3) remains unclear. Identifying reliable, non-insulin-based IR indices for predicting frailty index (FI) across early CKM stages (0–3) remains challenging.MethodsThis prospective cohort study analyzed 4,354 adults (≥ 45 years) from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (2011–2015). We evaluated and compared 12 IR indices for predicting frailty progression. Associations were assessed using multivariable logistic regression. Machine learning (RFE, Boruta, and LASSO) identified optimal predictors, and a Random Forest (RF) model incorporating key covariates was developed and validated.ResultsAfter full adjustment, CTI (OR = 1.19), TyG-WHtR (OR = 1.12), TyG-WC (OR = 1.00), and eGDR (OR = 0.87) significantly predicted FI (all p < 0.05). Among all indices, TyG-WHtR demonstrated the most stable discriminative performance across CKM stages 0–3 (AUCs: 0.52–0.59, all p < 0.001). Machine learning consistently selected TyG-WHtR as the top predictor. The final 13-variable RF model achieved an AUC of 0.74. SHAP analysis confirmed TyG-WHtR, age, depressive symptoms, and renal biomarkers as key predictors.ConclusionTyG-WHtR is a robust, stable predictor of frailty progression in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Its integration into clinical practice, potentially via the developed web tool, could enhance early frailty risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01993-1.

Introduction

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, a newly defined systemic disorder by the American Heart Association (AHA), underscores the bidirectional interplay between metabolic dysregulation, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular dysfunction, driving accelerated multimorbidity and mortality [1]. Individuals with CKM syndrome frequently exhibit subclinical metabolic and vascular disorders that predispose them to adverse health outcomes, affecting more than 20% of the adult population worldwide [2, 3]. This high prevalence reflects the synergistic effects of the global rise in obesity, diabetes, and hypertension, which collectively drive the pathogenesis of CKM syndrome [3]. Notably, these abnormalities contribute to an elevated risk of frailty, a geriatric condition characterized by diminished physiological reserves and heightened vulnerability to stress [4]. Insulin resistance (IR), a hallmark of metabolic syndrome, is defined by diminished responsiveness to insulin in peripheral tissues, resulting in impaired glucose uptake and utilization [5, 6]. Chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction associated with IR are potential drivers of frailty in CKM syndrome populations [7]. Thus, the potential of the IR index to predict frailty index (FI) in this population at various stages of CKM syndrome warrants further investigation.

Traditional approaches to quantifying IR face notable constraints in clinical and epidemiological research. The hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp and the homeostatic model assessment of IR (HOMA-IR), although widely regarded as reference standards, require fasting insulin measurements or specialized protocols, making them costly and impractical for large-scale studies [8, 9]. To overcome these limitations, non-insulin-based indices derived from routine metabolic parameters have emerged as promising alternatives, including triglyceride-glucose (TyG)-related indices, IR metabolic scores (METS-IR), and the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), which are derived from conventional metabolic parameters, have garnered considerable interest in metabolic research [9–12]. Although the above indices can strongly predict cardiovascular events and renal dysfunction in the CKM syndrome cohort, there is a gap in their utility in predicting FI [13, 14]. This knowledge gap is clinically significant, as early identification of frailty could enable targeted interventions aimed at mitigating functional decline in high-risk CKM populations. A secondary analysis of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohort suggests that TyG index is related to accelerated development of frailty, but assessments analyses of such indices across CKM syndrome remain to be explored [15]. Preliminary evidence suggests that TyG-related indices, particularly the TyG-waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR), may be associated with frailty risk [15, 22]. However, whether TyG-WHtR consistently outperforms other indices across CKM stages 0–3, and whether it provides stable predictive utility in heterogeneous populations, has not been systematically tested.

Identifying the most reliable predictive markers among these indices remains a critical challenge in clinical practice, with crucial implications for early screening and risk mitigation of frailty in patients with CKM syndrome, ultimately enhancing patient life expectancy. By integrating traditional statistical approaches with machine learning (ML), our methodology shifts the focus from hypothesis-driven inference to predictive accuracy, enabling the algorithmic identification of complex covariate interactions [16, 17]. ML techniques are well-suited to high-dimensional data and can uncover subtle patterns often overlooked by traditional approaches, providing a robust framework for biomarker prioritization. This approach facilitates a comprehensive evaluation of each variable’s contribution to FI prediction in the CKM syndrome population.

To address critical gaps in the literature, we analysed data from the CHARLS using ML approaches to evaluate 12 metabolic indices of insulin resistance. These included the TyG index and its derivatives, METS-IR, the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio, lipid accumulation product (LAP), visceral adiposity index (VAI), Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI), eGDR, and the non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio. We systematically compared these non-insulin-based indices in predicting frailty progression and assessed the stability and clinical utility of TyG-WHtR across CKM stages 0–3. Our aim was to identify the most robust predictor of frailty progression in populations at risk of CKM syndrome. The findings provide evidence to support the development of clinically actionable tools for frailty risk stratification, with implications for improving patient outcomes and guiding resource allocation in increasingly burdened healthcare systems.

Methods

Study population

CHARLS is a nationally representative, longitudinal survey of community-dwelling adults aged 45 years and older, encompassing 28 provinces across China. Operating under the auspices of the National Institute for Development Studies at Peking University, CHARLS secured ethical clearance from the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment [18]. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to ensure the rigor and transparency of reporting. Further details on CHARLS, including access to datasets upon registration and application, are available at https://charls.pku.edu.cn/. [19, 20]

Our investigation performed a secondary analysis utilizing CHARLS data from the 2011 and 2015 waves. Exclusion criteria for participants were as follows: [1] age below 45 years [2], FI (wave 1) and CKM syndrome stage 4 at or prior to baseline [3], missing or aberrant exposure indicator values [4], absence of required indicators for CKM syndrome staging [5], lack of 2015 FI. An initial cohort of 17,705 participants from wave 1 was screened, with 4,354 individuals incorporated into the final analysis. The participants selection flowchart is depicted in Fig. 1.

CHARLS includes fasting venous blood samples collected by researchers from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (China CDC), which were analyzed utilizing an enzyme colorimetric assay at the Youanmen Clinical Trial Center of Capital Medical University, Beijing [19]. During the physical examination, height, weight, and waist circumference (WC) were each measured three times, with the mean value utilized as the final measurement, using a vertical height gauge and a weight scale for height and weight, respectively, and a flexible tape measure encircling the waist at the level of the navel for WC, while the participant remained in a standing position [21]. In this research, the selected TyG-related indices, including the TyG index, C-reactive protein-TyG index (CTI), TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG-WC, and TyG-waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR), among which TyG-WHtR has been recognized as a risk factor for FI in European populations [22].

A score of 0 denoted the absence of a deficit, whereas 1 signified its presence, except for item 32, which was modeled as a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1, with higher values reflecting greater cognitive impairment. In this investigation, participants with missing data exceeding 20% of the items on a given scale were excluded from the analysis [23]. The 32-item FI for each participant was computed by dividing the total number of present health deficits by 32, multiplying the quotient by 100, and converting this value onto a scale ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores reflecting greater FI, which was classified as a 32-frailty score ≥ 0.25, consistent with established guidelines in the literature [24].

Definition of CKM syndrome stages 0–3

As outlined in the AHA Presidential Advisory Statement, CKM syndrome is stratified into 5 progressive stages, spanning from stage 0 to 4 [1]. Stage 0 is characterized by the absence of risk factors, comprising blood pressure (BP), normal body weight, blood glucose levels, lipid profiles, and renal function, with no signs of cardiovascular disease (CVD), highlighting the emphasis on primary prevention and the promotion of cardiovascular health. Stage 1 comprises individuals with obesity and impaired glucose metabolism, reflecting markers of excess or dysfunctional adiposity. Stage 2 encompasses individuals at moderate to high risk of CKD, defined by an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 30–60 ml/min/1.73 m2 and/or a self-reported CKD diagnosis, alongside metabolic risk factors including hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), with eGFR serving as a key indicator for evaluating renal function. Stage 3 encompasses individuals who exhibit subclinical CVD, such as those at high 10-year CVD risk (Framingham risk score ≥ 21.5 for females and ≥ 21.6 for males) [22], or those with extremely high-risk CKD, defined by an eGFR < 30 ml/min/1.73 m², with the Framingham risk score estimating CVD risk based on various factors (Table S2-Table S4). Lastly, stage 4 involves individuals with clinical CVD. The FI assessment, the primary variable of interest in this study, encompassed clinical CVD, necessitating the exclusion of individuals classified as stage 4 in the CKM syndrome framework.

Covariates

Drawing upon existing literature, clinical expert assessments, and retrospective data analysis, a range of sociodemographic, socioeconomic, health behavior and lifestyle factors, as well as blood biomarkers, were considered as potential confounders [1]. Sociodemographic variables encompassed age (years) and sex (male or female) [2]. Socioeconomic parameters comprised place of residence (rural or urban), educational attainment (“no formal or primary education” or “middle school or above”), marital status (“married and cohabiting with spouse,” “married but separated from spouse,” or “unmarried, divorced, and widowed”), Regional category (“east,” “midland,” or “west”) and annual household expenditure [3]. Health behavior and lifestyle factors comprised smoking status (“non-smoker” or “smoker”), alcohol consumption (“non-drinker,” “drinking < once a month,” or “drinking >once a month”), PA, type of cooking fuel used (“solid fuel” or “clean fuel”), sleep duration (“≤6 h,” “6–8 h,” or “>8 h”), and social isolation [25]. Tobacco use classification was based on participants’ history of tobacco consumption, including chewing tobacco, pipe smoking, self-rolled cigarettes, and commercial cigarettes or cigars. Alcohol intake was determined through self-reported consumption over the past year, accounting for beverage type (spirits, wine, or beer). Smoking status was recorded as current or former use of cigarettes, cigars, pipes, or chewing tobacco. PA was quantified using the International PA Questionnaire (IPAQ), with time intervals in the CHARLS converted to median values: “≥4 hours”, “≥2 hours and < 4 hours”, “≤30 minutes and < 2 hours”, and “≥10 minutes and < 30 minutes”. PA scores were derived using metabolic equivalent values: PA score = 8.0 × total hours of vigorous activity per week + 4.0 × total hours of moderate activity per week + 3.3 × total hours of walking per week [26, 27]. Indoor air pollution was evaluated based on cooking fuel type, with solid fuels (coal, crop residue, wood) classified as “solid fuels” and cleaner alternatives (natural gas, biogas, liquefied petroleum gas) as “clean fuels” [28]. Sleep duration was assessed by self-reported average nightly hours during the past year and categorized as ≤ 6 h, 6–8 h, or >8 h. Depressive symptoms were evaluated utilizing the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) [29]. Social isolation was measured by a validated four-item scale, incorporating [1] living alone [2], marital status [3], frequency of contact with children (< 1 time/week), and [4] participation in social activities (< 1 time/month). A composite score (0–4) was calculated, with ≥ 2 indicating isolation [4, 30, 31–]. Blood biomarkers comprised white blood cell count (WBC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), platelets, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), glycated hemoglobin (GHb), uric acid (UA), hematocrit, hemoglobin (Hb), cystatin C (CysC), C reactive protein (CRP), and eGFR. In summary, we chose a total of 24 covariates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.9.12) and R (version 4.4.1). For continuous variables, data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and group differences were assessed through analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables, expressed as counts and corresponding percentages, were compared using the chi-square (χ²) test.

Logistic regression analyses were performed to investigate the association between IR indices and FI across individuals categorized into CKM syndrome stages 0 to 3. A series of five hierarchical models were specified. Model 1 incorporated adjustments for demographic and socioeconomic factors. Model 2 further accounted for behavioural covariates, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, and cooking fuel use. In Model 3, additional adjustments were applied for PA, social isolation, sleep duration, and depressive symptoms. Model 4 expanded upon the previous models by including pertinent blood biomarkers. We did not include BMI index and HDL in the model for adjustment because the exposure in this study involved TyG-BMI index and TG/HDL ratio. If any of the aforementioned factors were components of the IRs, they were excluded from the adjustment process to prevent collinearity. For example, as the exposure variables in this study included the TyG-BMI and TG/HDL ratio, BMI and HDL were not included as covariates in the regression models.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were conducted to evaluate the discriminative performance of all IRs. Optimal threshold values for each index were determined using the OptimalCutpoints R package, based on criteria including maximal sensitivity and specificity, diagnostic odds ratios (positive and negative), and the Youden index for predicting frailty in individuals at CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Thresholds were further stratified by CKM syndrome stages (0–3), and their predictive validity was evaluated through logistic regression modelling [33].

The final cohort comprised 4,354 participants, randomly split into training (70%) and validation (30%) sets to minimize overfitting. To determine the most predictive IR index for frailty in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, we employed an integrative approach combining multiple embedded ML-based feature selection methods [16]. We used 24 covariates as reference variables to compare our IR indices. For embedded feature selection, we initially applied recursive feature elimination (RFE) to screen variables. RFE improves classification performance by ranking a large set of features according to their importance within a given ML algorithm [34]. Subsequently, the Boruta algorithm was employed to introduce permuted “shadow” features by shuffling the values of each original variable. The RFE model was then trained on both the original and shadow features, and their relative importances were evaluated across multiple iterations. Features consistently exhibiting higher importance than their shadow counterparts were retained [35]. Finally, we applied the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), a regularization technique grounded in linear regression, which penalizes less informative features by shrinking their coefficients to zero, thereby selecting variables with non-zero coefficients [36]. Drawing on the results from three feature selection methods, the optimal IR index for predicting FI among individuals across CKM syndrome stages 0 to 3 was identified.

Subsequently, 24 covariates were incorporated as input features for model development. To refine the model, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were employed to evaluate feature importance, enabling a reduction from 24 to 12 features. Ten ML algorithms, including K-nearest neighbor (KNN), decision tree (DT), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGB), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), multi-layer perceptron (MLP), bootstrap aggregating (Bagging), and random forest (RF), were evaluated for their performance in predicting FI in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Model performance and interpretability were performed utilizing the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and SHAP values. To enhance clinical applicability, the final predictive model was deployed as a web-based application utilizing the Streamlit Python framework.

Result

Associations of IR indices with FI across CKM syndrome stages 0–3

Table 1 provided an overview of the baseline characteristics for the 4,354 participants incorporated into the present analysis. Compared to non-frailty participants, participants with frailty were older, more frequently women and rural residents, reported higher social isolation and depression, used solid fuels more frequently, engaged in less PA, spent less annually, and had higher CRP and LDL-C levels. Table 2 presented the associations of IR indices with FI across CKM syndrome stages 0 to 3. Within the crude model, 7 IR indices, including CVAI, CTI, eGDR, LAP, TyG, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR, demonstrated statistically significant links to FI. After adjustment for demographic and socioeconomic variables in Model 1, 6 indices (CVAI, CTI, eGDR, TyG, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR) remained statistically significant. Following additional adjustment for behavioral covariates, these associations remained robust in Model 2. In Model 3, which additionally accounted for PA, social isolation, sleep duration, and depressive symptoms, all 7 IR indices continued to show significant associations. When blood biomarkers were incorporated into the Model 4, 4 indices remained independently linked to FI status: CTI (OR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.05–1.35, P = 7.95 × 10−3), eGDR (OR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.83–0.91, P = 1.67 × 10−9), TyG-WC (OR: 1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.00, P = 7.16 × 10−3), and TyG-WHtR (OR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.03–1.23, P = 1.2 × 10−2).

Figure 2A–E further depicted the ROC curves of 12 IR indices for predicting FI across CKM stages 0–3 and in the overall cohort, while Fig. 2F compared the AUCs for these 5 analytic groups. In the overall cohort, discriminative accuracy was highest for eGDR (AUC: 0.61, 95% CI: 0.58–0.63), followed by CVAI (AUC: 0.58, 95% CI: 0.55–0.61) and TyG-WHtR (AUC: 0.57, 95% CI: 0.55–0.60). However, eGDR’s discriminative ability fell to 0.42 in CKM0, and CVAI dropped to 0.49 in CKM1. By contrast, TyG-WHtR maintained consistently high AUCs across all stages (0.52–0.59) with P values < 0.001 for every stage, underscoring its superior stability and overall predictive strength among the 12 indices. Supplement Table S5 provides additional detail for the ROC-curve analysis by delineating the optimal cut-off thresholds for 12 IR indices in the overall cohort and at CKM syndrome stages 0 through 3.

Figure 3 summarized the ML feature selection of IR indices workflow. Figure 3A identified eGDR, TyG-WHtR, CVAI, and METS-IR as the most informative variables by the Boruta algorithm; each exhibited a mean Z-score that was significantly higher than those of the remaining indices. Figure 3A illustrated the LASSO coefficient profile, in which TyG-WHtR retained the largest standardized coefficient (β = 0.119) after L1 penalization, indicating the strongest association with FI. Based on RF-RFE, Fig. 3C ranked TG/HDL-C ratio, TyG-WHtR, TyG-WC, TyG, and TyG-BMI as the 5 most informative IR indices. An ensemble voting strategy across the three methods consistently selected TyG-WHtR as the optimal predictor. Spearman correlation analysis (Fig. 3D) indicated no substantial collinearity between TyG-WHtR and any covariate (all |r| < 0.8). In addition, to assess potential collinearity between each IR index and the covariates, we computed variance inflation factors (VIFs) for the logistic models. VIFs were uniformly low across the crude model through Model 4 (range 1.0–1.4.0.4, Table 2), suggesting negligible multicollinearity and little risk that collinearity materially biased the estimated associations between each IR index and FI.

Model development and performance comparison

Data from 4 354 participants were used to train 10 ML models. Sequential feature elimination was subsequently applied to identify the optimal model. As shown in Fig. 4A, the 28-feature model outperformed the 8-feature model yet conferred no additional predictive value over the 13-predictor model for detecting FI in adults with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Given it exhibited comparable net benefit and decision thresholds, the model with 13 predictors, including age, annual household expenditure, C-reactive protein, cooking-fuel type, residential setting, educational attainment, depressive symptoms, creatinine, GHb, CysC, and eGFR, was selected for subsequent analyses.

Across the 10 algorithms, the RF model provided the greatest discrimination (AUC, 0.741), followed by Bagging (AUC, 0.734), MLP (AUC, 0.731), and XGB (AUC, 0.731). Figure 4B depicted ROC curves for the 6 best-performing models, wherein the RF model persistently attains the greatest overall accuracy. Performance evaluation of the final RF model yielded an AUC of 0.74; sensitivity, specificity, overall accuracy, and F1-score were 0.87, 0.86, 0.87, and 0.81, respectively (Fig. 4C).

Both 5-fold and 10-fold cross-validation demonstrated stable performance, with mean AUCs of 0.691 ± 0.044 and 0.690 ± 0.047, respectively (Supplement Figure S1A–B), supporting the adequacy of sample size and robustness across sites.

Model explanation

SHAP analysis was employed to determine the influence of each predictor on the final RF model for forecasting FI in participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Two interpretive levels were generated. Global, feature-level interpretation described overall model behavior: mean absolute SHAP values were calculated and ranked (Fig. 5A and B), showing that age, depressive symptoms, education status, TyG-WHtR, eGFR, and cystatin C exerted the greatest influence. Local, observation-level interpretation was provided by SHAP dependence plots (Fig. 5C), which illustrated how changes in individual predictor values affected the estimated risk. Positive SHAP values shifted the prediction toward FI, whereas negative values favored a non-FI outcome. Higher age, presence of depressive symptoms, higher BMI, cystatin C, C-reactive protein, and use of solid fuel increased predicted risk while lower education, lower creatinine, and lower annual household expenditure decreased it.

Supplement Figure S2A–B depicted model outputs for two participant who was classified as non-FI and FI, separately. Figure S2A assigned the case to the non-FI class with a predicted probability of 74.2%, whereas Figure S2B assigned it to the FI class with a probability of 94.9%. Waterfall plots displayed the participants’ actual feature values: lower TyG-WHtR, higher eGFR, lower glycated hemoglobin, lower BMI, absence of depression, lower CRP, and greater annual household expenditure all shifted the estimate toward non-FI. Whereas in Figure S2B, the balance reversed. Advanced age, a renal profile marked by higher eGFR together with elevated CysC, depressive symptoms, solid-fuel use, higher TyG-WHtR, raised CRP and GHB collectively pushed the prediction toward FI.

To improve clinical applicability, the final predictive model was deployed as an online application (Fig. 6). Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that TyG-WHtR consistently outperforms other IR indices in predicting FI progression across CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Its robust stability and high discriminatory power support its utility as a clinically relevant tool for early frailty risk stratification (AUC 0.74 in ML validation). Our results highlight the utility of integrating metabolic and anthropometric parameters to enhance the predictive accuracy of FI assessments in clinical settings, particularly where specialized biomarkers are unavailable.

Alternative IR markers, TyG-WHtR, was employed to assess obesity, reflecting the established correlation between IR and a broad range of obesity-related metrics [37]. Unlike traditional indices such as HOMA-IR, TyG-WHtR avoids reliance on insulin measurements, reducing logistical barriers and costs while maintaining strong predictive validity [15, 22, 38]. This practical advantage is especially pertinent in resource-limited settings where access to insulin assays is restricted, potentially democratizing frailty risk assessment. It is consistent with emerging evidence that composite indices combining anthropometric and metabolic markers offer superior predictive performance over single-parameter models in forecasting adverse health outcomes [39].

The predictive advantage of TyG-WHtR likely derives from its capacity to capture both visceral adiposity and systemic inflammation, factors known to exacerbate insulin resistance and accelerate physical decline [7]. Visceral adipose tissue, for instance, secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines that disrupt insulin signalling and drive muscle atrophy, a cardinal feature of FI [6]. Our results corroborate prior evidence linking TyG-related indices to adverse cardiovascular and renal outcomes in CKM syndrome, while further extending these associations to FI, a critical yet underexplored endpoint in metabolic pathology [22]. Importantly, the consistent performance of TyG-WHtR across CKM stages underscores its potential utility for early disease detection, where preventative strategies may confer maximal benefit [4].

The incorporation of ML techniques further reinforces our findings. By applying RFE, Boruta, and LASSO algorithms, we reduced the risk of overfitting and consistently identified TyG-WHtR as the most robust predictor. The optimized RF model yielded an AUC of 0.74, surpassing the performance of conventional logistic regression. These results underscore the utility of ML approaches in resolving the intricate interdependencies among metabolic, inflammatory, and socio-economic determinants, which are often inadequately captured by traditional statistical models [16]. Notably, SHAP analysis uncovered a non-linear association between estimated glomerular filtration rate eGFR, social isolation, and frailty risk, highlighting the multifactorial dynamics that underpin the progression of FI [12].

The simplicity and low cost of the TyG-WHtR index render it well-suited for implementation in large-scale screening initiatives. Its integration into routine clinical evaluations may assist in the early identification of individuals with stage 0–3 CKM syndrome who are at heightened risk, thereby enabling more precise targeting of preventive strategies. To support clinical application, we developed a web-based tool that facilitates real-time risk stratification and informs individualized management. Patients exhibiting elevated TyG-WHtR values may particularly benefit from tailored interventions, including dietary modification and resistance training, aimed at mitigating frailty progression [26]. Future implementation research could evaluate whether TyG-WHtR-guided interventions reduce frailty incidence compared to standard care, a crucial step toward value-based CKM management.

The RF model achieved an AUC of 0.74, a value that, while clinically acceptable, falls short of the threshold (>0.80) typically regarded as excellent for prediction models. Several factors likely contribute to this performance. Frailty is inherently multifactorial, shaped not only by metabolic and cardiovascular pathways but also by psychosocial, behavioural, and environmental determinants that were only partially represented in the dataset [15]. In addition, population heterogeneity within CHARLS, spanning lifestyle diversity and regional socioeconomic disparities, may have attenuated model accuracy [21]. Nonetheless, an AUC of 0.74 remains clinically informative, particularly given the model’s robustness and reliance on routinely collected variables, which supports its utility in early risk screening.

The discriminative performance of TyG-WHtR across CKM stages (AUC 0.52–0.59) warrants a similar interpretation. Although modest, these values demonstrate a stable signal across heterogeneous CKM subgroups, a feature of practical importance in real-world screening where early-stage CKM often presents without overt symptoms [22]. Comparable AUC ranges have been reported in previous evaluations of metabolic indices for frailty, underscoring the inherent challenge of achieving high discrimination in this domain [15]. The moderate performance of TyG-WHtR therefore, highlights its value as a pragmatic, low-cost biomarker that complements rather than replaces multidimensional frailty assessment. Future research that integrates more factors is expected to further enhance its prediction accuracy.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, due to the CHARLS cohort consists solely of Chinese adults, extrapolation of these findings to other ethnic groups may be constrained. Second, despite adjustment for numerous covariates, bias arising from unmeasured confounders may persist (such as dietary patterns or genetic susceptibility) cannot be ruled out. Third, the observational design inherently precludes definitive causal inference. Further prospective investigations are required to validate the discriminative performance of TyG-WHtR for FI.

Conclusion

In summary, TyG-WHtR represents a practical and non-invasive index for predicting frailty progression among individuals with CKM syndrome. Its incorporation into routine clinical assessment may facilitate earlier identification and targeted intervention, potentially enhancing health outcomes and quality of life in this vulnerable population.
