Muscle Mass, Adiposity, and Bone Health in Surgical Care Setting: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Ngo Thi Thu Thuy, Le Van Thieu, Tran Vuong The Vinh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoporosis and sarcopenia are interrelated conditions that significantly affect surgical outcomes by impairing bone strength, mobility, and postoperative recovery. Understanding how body composition and metabolic factors influence bone mineral density (BMD) is essential for improving perioperative risk assessment and rehabilitation. This study aimed to evaluate the relationships between regional muscle mass, fat mass (FM), and circulating adipokines with BMD.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in 199 patients. Whole-body dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) was used to assess regional lean and FM and BMD at multiple skeletal sites. Serum leptin and adiponectin were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Correlations were examined using Pearson’s coefficients, and stepwise multiple linear regression identified independent predictors of T-score.ResultsTrunk and gynoid muscle mass exhibited the strongest positive correlations with T-score (r=0.490 and r=0.475, both P<0.001). FM showed weaker associations, while adiponectin correlated inversely with BMD (r= −0.196, P=0.005). In multivariable analysis, trunk muscle mass (β=0.48, P<0.001), gynoid muscle mass (β=0.36, P=0.002), body mass index (β=0.18, P=0.031), and adiponectin (β=−0.22, P=0.008) remained independent predictors (adjusted R2=0.45).ConclusionsSkeletal muscle, particularly in the trunk and hip regions, is the primary determinant of bone density, while adiponectin negatively influences BMD. Incorporating muscle mass assessment and metabolic optimization into perioperative care may enhance fixation stability and postoperative recovery.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Osteoporosis and sarcopenia are major public health challenges in aging populations, each contributing to fragility fractures, disability, and impaired quality of life. In surgical practice, particularly in orthopedic, reconstructive, and general surgery, these conditions have additional implications: reduced bone mineral density (BMD) increases fracture risk and implant failure, while loss of muscle mass delays mobilization, prolongs rehabilitation, and worsens postoperative recovery. Understanding the interplay between muscle, fat, and bone is therefore crucial for perioperative risk stratification and for optimizing long-term functional outcomes.[1,2]

Skeletal muscle plays a central role in maintaining bone strength through both mechanical loading and endocrine crosstalk. Several studies have confirmed that lean mass (LM) is a stronger predictor of BD than adiposity, supporting the concept of a functional muscle–bone unit.[3–5] Muscle contraction imposes mechanical strain that stimulates osteogenesis, while myokines such as irisin and myostatin further influence bone remodeling. Conversely, the contribution of fat mass (FM) remains inconsistent. While moderate fat loading can enhance bone density through weight-bearing, excessive or visceral adiposity may exert harmful effects on bone quality by promoting low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and altered hormonal signaling.[6–8]

Adipose tissue also functions as an endocrine organ, secreting adipokines such as leptin and adiponectin, which modulate both energy metabolism and bone turnover. Leptin has been reported to exert both anabolic and catabolic effects depending on its site of action and the physiological context, whereas adiponectin is more consistently associated with reduced BMD and higher fracture risk. [9,10] However, the relative contributions of these adipokines compared with regional muscle and fat distribution remain poorly defined.

Most existing studies have focused on community-dwelling adults or postmenopausal women, with limited data on patients undergoing surgery, who often exhibit complex metabolic alterations, inflammation, and immobilization that may influence the muscle–bone–fat axis. Furthermore, evidence from low- and middle-income countries is scarce, where perioperative metabolic evaluation is not routinely integrated into clinical decision-making.

The present study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the relationships of regional muscle mass, FM, and circulating adipokines with BMD using dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). By identifying which body composition compartments most strongly determine bone strength, this study can provide clinically relevant evidence to guide preoperative assessment, risk stratification, and perioperative optimization strategies that integrate musculoskeletal and metabolic health.

METHODS

1. Study design and participants

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Hai Phong University of Medicine and Pharmacy (IRB No. 5/HDDD). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. This was a cross-sectional observational study conducted at Viet Tiep Friendship Hospital and Hai Phong University of Medicine Hospital, including 199 patients who underwent clinical evaluation and body composition assessment between October 2017 and September 2022.

2. Inclusion criteria

• Adults aged ≥18 years;

• Adults aged ≥18 years;

• Patients who underwent whole-body DXA scanning;

• Patients who underwent whole-body DXA scanning;

• Availability of complete demographic data, biochemical laboratory results, and body composition indices

• Availability of complete demographic data, biochemical laboratory results, and body composition indices

3. Exclusion criteria

• Chronic systemic conditions known to affect bone metabolism, including advanced chronic kidney disease (stage 4–5), chronic liver disease, hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, malignancy, or long-term corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy;

• Chronic systemic conditions known to affect bone metabolism, including advanced chronic kidney disease (stage 4–5), chronic liver disease, hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, malignancy, or long-term corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy;

• Active inflammatory, infectious, or rheumatologic disease;

• Active inflammatory, infectious, or rheumatologic disease;

• History of metabolic bone diseases other than osteoporosis;

• History of metabolic bone diseases other than osteoporosis;

• Incomplete DXA or laboratory records

• Incomplete DXA or laboratory records

4. Laboratory analysis

Fasting venous blood samples were collected between 7:00 and 9:00 AM after an overnight fast of 8 to 12 hr. Serum glucose, triglycerides, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were determined using enzymatic assays on automated analyzers (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Serum leptin and adiponectin concentrations were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (Biovendor, Modrice, Czech Republic), with all measurements performed in duplicate according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were <8% and <10%, respectively. All samples were stored at −80°C until batch analysis.

5. Bone and body composition assessment

DXA was performed using a Hologic Discovery QDR scanner (Hologic Inc., Bedford, MA, USA). The DXA system operates using two low-dose X-ray beams at different energy levels, allowing the proprietary tissue-decomposition algorithm to differentiate bone mineral content, LM, and FM. For BMD analysis, the standard diagnostic regions of interest (ROIs)—lumbar spine L1–L4 and femoral neck—were used to calculate BMD values expressed in g/cm2 and corresponding T-scores based on an Asian reference database. Whole-body DXA scanning simultaneously provides additional regional BMD outputs (pelvis, ribs, arms, legs, and whole body). These values are commonly used for research purposes only and were not used for diagnostic interpretation in this study. Body composition indices (total and regional LM and FM, android and gynoid distribution) were extracted from the whole-body scan using the manufacturer’s automated segmentation software.

DXA also provided detailed body composition indices, including total and regional FM, LM, android and gynoid fat distribution. Normalized indices were calculated as follows:

• FM Index (FMI)=FM (kg)/height2 (m2)

• FM Index (FMI)=FM (kg)/height2 (m2)

• LM Index (LMI)=LM (kg)/height2 (m2)

• LM Index (LMI)=LM (kg)/height2 (m2)

6. Data statistics

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation or median (interquartile range) as appropriate, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine associations between serum adipokines, anthropometric indices, and BMD. Specifically, correlations were classified as weak when r<0.30, moderate when 0.30≤r<0.50, and strong when r≥0.50. These cut-off values are widely used in biomedical and epidemiological research to standardize the interpretation of correlation magnitude.[11] To identify independent predictors of BMD, a stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with T-score as the dependent variable, including candidate predictors such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), trunk muscle mass, gynoid muscle mass, leptin, and adiponectin. Collinearity diagnostics were evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and model performance was assessed by R2, adjusted R2, and Akaike information criterion. Statistical significance was set at P value of less than 0.05 (two-tailed).

RESULTS

1. Patient characteristics

A total of 199 patients were included in the analysis. The mean age was 59.3±6.8 years, and the majority were female (81.9%) with males comprising 18.1% of the cohort. The mean BMI was 22.6±2.6 kg/m2, with 42.7% of patients classified as overweight or obese (BMI≥23). Mean waist circumference was 82.3±7.8 cm, and mean hip circumference was 91.3±6.0 cm, yielding a mean waist-to-hip ratio of 0.90±0.06, indicating a tendency toward central adiposity.

Regarding biochemical parameters, the mean fasting glucose was 8.5±5.4 mmol/L, total cholesterol 5.4±1.8 mmol/L, triglycerides 2.4±1.7 mmol/L, HDL-C 1.1±0.3 mmol/L, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol 3.3±1.2 mmol/L. Mean serum leptin was 4.1±4.5 ng/mL, while mean adiponectin was 4.3±4.2 μg/mL. The BMD assessment revealed a mean T-score of −1.42±1.47 and a mean Z-score of −0.20±1.33, indicating a substantial proportion of patients with osteopenia or osteoporosis (Table 1).

2. Metabolic parameters and BMD

Among anthropometric measures, both BMI and hip circumference showed moderate positive correlations with T-score (r=0.335 and 0.320, respectively; P<0.001), while waist circumference demonstrated a weaker but significant association (r=0.157, P=0.026). Adiponectin was the only biochemical marker significantly correlated with bone density, showing a modest inverse relationship (r=−0.196, P=0.005). Other metabolic disturbances, including hyper-triglyceridemia, hyperglycemia, hypertension, low HDL-C, and leptin, did not exhibit significant associations with T-score (Table 2).

3. FM distribution and BMD

Analysis of regional FM revealed weak but significant positive correlations with BMD. Total FM and trunk FM were positively associated with T-score (both r=0.188, P= 0.008), while gynoid FM (r=0.182, P=0.010) and leg FM (r=0.167, P=0.018) also showed similar trends. Android FM demonstrated a weaker association (r=0.145, P=0.041). In contrast, abdominal FM was not significantly correlated with T-score (r=0.084, P=0.238). These findings indicated that regional fat deposition, particularly in the trunk and lower body, contributed modestly to bone density, while abdominal fat accumulation does not appear to play a significant role (Table 3).

4. Muscle mass and BMD

Muscle mass exhibited the strongest correlations with BMD among all body composition parameters. Trunk muscle mass (r=0.490, P<0.001) and gynoid muscle mass (r=0.475, P<0.001) showed the highest positive correlations with T-score. Total muscle mass was also strongly correlated (r=0.459, P<0.001), followed by leg muscle mass (r=0.401, P<0.001). Android muscle mass, although slightly weaker, remained significantly associated with bone density (r=0.299, P<0.001). These results highlighted that skeletal muscle mass, particularly in the trunk and pelvic regions, plays a pivotal role in supporting bone density, underscoring the mechanical and metabolic contributions of muscle to skeletal health (Table 4).

5. Multiple linear regression analysis

To further identify the independent predictors of BMD, a stepwise multiple linear regression was performed using T-score as the dependent variable. Candidate predictors included age, sex, BMI, trunk muscle mass, gynoid muscle mass, adiponectin, and leptin.

The final model retained BMI, trunk muscle mass, gynoid muscle mass, and adiponectin as significant independent variables (Table 5). The model explained approximately 46% of the variance in T-score (adjusted R2=0.45).

Both trunk muscle mass (β=0.48, P<0.001) and gynoid muscle mass (β=0.36, P=0.002) showed strong positive associations with T-score, confirming that higher muscle mass, particularly in the trunk and hip regions, is associated with higher bone density. BMI had a modest positive effect (β=0.18, P=0.031), while adiponectin was inversely correlated with bone density (β=−0.22, P=0.008). Leptin and age were excluded from the final model during the stepwise selection process.

No multicollinearity was observed among the final predictors (all VIF<2), indicating a stable and independent contribution of each variable (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

The study identified muscle mass, especially in the trunk and gynoid regions, as the strongest independent determinant of BMD, while adiponectin showed a significant inverse association and BMI contributed only modestly. These findings reinforced the concept of a muscle–bone functional unit, in which skeletal muscle exerts both mechanical and endocrine influences on bone remodeling.

The findings were consistent with recent literature emphasizing the dominant role of LM over FM in maintaining bone strength.[3]. Han et al. [4] identified that muscle mass and handgrip strength were found to correlate positively with BMD across multiple skeletal sites. Similarly, Gandham et al. [12] noted that regional muscle volume, particularly of the trunk and hip, was directly associated with bone microarchitecture using high-resolution imaging. These results aligned with our findings that trunk and gynoid muscle mass are the most predictive parameters of bone density, underscoring the region-specific nature of muscle–bone coupling.

In contrast, the contribution of adiposity to bone health remained ambiguous. Although BMI showed a weak positive relationship with T-score in our model, this effect was attenuated after adjusting for LM, suggesting that the apparent benefit of higher BMI largely reflects greater muscle loading rather than fat accumulation. This was consistent with recent Mendelian randomization analyses by Zhao et al. [13], which reported that the causal effect of BMI on BMD was primarily mediated by LM. Excess visceral fat may even impair bone quality through chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, as observed in previous imaging and histologic studies.

The negative association between adiponectin and BMD aligned with accumulating evidence that this adipokine exerts catabolic effects on bone metabolism. He et al. [14] demonstrated a causal relationship between elevated adiponectin and lower BMD in Mendelian randomization analysis, while Lee et al. [2] confirmed through meta-analysis that adiponectin consistently predicts low bone mass and increased fracture risk, independent of body composition. Mechanistically, adiponectin enhances osteoclastogenesis and suppresses osteoblast differentiation, resulting in net bone loss. Conversely, leptin, though once thought to promote bone formation, showed no significant relationship in our cohort.[15]

From a clinical and surgical perspective, these findings have considerable implications. Preoperative assessment of muscle mass may improve risk stratification for osteopenia, delayed union, or implant failure. Several surgical outcome studies support this: patients with sarcopenia or osteosarcopenia exhibit higher rates of postoperative complications and prolonged rehabilitation.[16,17] Therefore, muscle-focused prehabilitation, combining resistance exercise and protein optimization, may strengthen both muscle and bone, improving fixation stability and postoperative recovery. This approach aligns with the paradigm shift toward functional recovery and metabolic optimization in perioperative care.

The study had some limitations. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and confounders such as physical activity, nutritional status, and menopausal stage were not fully assessed. Additionally, DXA cannot capture muscle quality or infiltration, which have been shown to affect bone microarchitecture independently of muscle mass. The predominance of female participants may limit generalizability and preclude robust sex-specific analysis. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to determine whether improving trunk and gynoid muscle mass can directly enhance bone density and reduce surgical complications.

Muscle mass, particularly in the trunk and gynoid regions, was identified as the strongest determinant of BMD, while adiponectin showed a negative association, and BMI contributed modestly. These findings highlighted the central role of skeletal muscle in maintaining bone strength and suggest that muscle preservation should be an integral focus in perioperative management. Assessing and optimizing muscle mass before surgery may improve fixation stability and postoperative recovery. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the causal pathways linking muscle, adipokines, and bone metabolism, and to determine whether muscle-centered prehabilitation can enhance skeletal integrity and surgical outcomes.
