Traction-assisted versus conventional endoscopic submucosal dissection for colorectal lesions: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials
Authors: L. Fadeli, M. Desouky, L. Alfarone, C. Hassan, R. A. Alqandouz, M. A. Elshwihdi, A. Elghezewi, W. Frandah, A. Sherif, M. Elhadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) allows en bloc, organ-sparing removal of large superficial colorectal lesions; however, conventional ESD (C-ESD) is often time intensive, technically challenging, and associated with clinically relevant adverse events. Traction-assisted ESD (TA-ESD) was developed to enhance submucosal visualization and maintain a stable dissection plane. Still, its overall impact on efficiency, effectiveness, and safety, particularly across different levels of operator experience, remains unclear. We therefore compared TA-ESD with C-ESD for superficial colorectal lesions and examined whether endoscopist experience influences outcomes.MethodsIn line with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidance, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched from inception through 8 November 2025. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) enrolling patients with superficial colorectal lesions undergoing TA-ESD versus C-ESD and reporting outcomes on an intention-to-treat (ITT) or modified intention-to-treat (mITT) basis were included. Pooled estimates were generated using random-effects models, with prespecified subgroup analyses according to endoscopist experience.ResultsFrom 930 screened records, 10 RCTs met the inclusion criteria, totaling 1008 participants (499 TA-ESD; 509 C-ESD). TA-ESD was associated with shorter procedure time than C-ESD (MD = −19.23 min; 95% CI −26.7 to −11.8; P < 0.0001), although heterogeneity was substantial and certainty was very low. Resection quality was high and similar between approaches, with no meaningful differences in en bloc or R0 resection rates. In the C-ESD arm, 13% of cases required rescue conversion to traction. TA-ESD increased overall resection speed (MD = 2.06 mm2/min; 95% CI 0.59–3.53; P = 0.006). TA-ESD showed a borderline reduction in intraoperative perforation (RR = 0.46; 95% CI 0.215–0.999; P = 0.049), whereas delayed perforation and delayed bleeding were infrequent and did not differ between techniques.ConclusionsIn colorectal RCTs, TA-ESD was associated with shorter procedure time than C-ESD, consistent with prior meta-analyses. However, certainty for this endpoint was very low, and heterogeneity was substantial. The main demonstrated advantage was procedural efficiency, whereas resection quality and overall safety were broadly comparable. Implementation-related outcomes, including rescue conversion to traction and handover to another operator, were reported in only a limited number of trials and should therefore be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Future multicenter RCTs should standardize workflow-related endpoint definitions and determine which lesions, procedural settings, and operator contexts derive the greatest incremental benefit from dedicated adjunctive traction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10151-026-03363-z.

Introduction

Colorectal endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) has become an established organ-preserving treatment for large non-pedunculated colorectal lesions and selected early cancers, particularly superficial lesions ≥ 20 mm with suspected limited submucosal invasion that snare-based techniques cannot completely remove. ESD achieves higher en bloc and R0 resection rates than piecemeal endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR), but at the cost of longer procedure times and a substantial perforation risk, especially in the thin-walled and mobile colon [1]. Poor submucosal visualization, fibrosis, and limited countertraction with a single endoscope impair identification of the dissection plane, increasing technical complexity and steepening the learning curve [2–5].

Saito et al. [6] introduced traction-assisted ESD (TA-ESD) using a sinker-assisted clip-and-line method to provide traction, improve submucosal exposure, and facilitate en bloc resection of superficial lesions. Since then, a wide range of TA-ESD techniques has been developed, using both external and internal methods to provide dynamic countertraction, open the submucosal space, and stabilize the lesion during dissection [2, 3]. For conceptual clarity, this review does not compare “traction” with the “absence of traction.” Some degree of tissue tension, gravitational exposure, flap-based countertraction, or pocket-related stabilization is inherent to most colorectal ESD procedures. Rather, the present comparison is between ESD performed with a dedicated adjunctive traction device or method (TA-ESD) and standard non-device-assisted ESD (C-ESD). Accordingly, “conventional ESD” (C-ESD) in this review should be understood as standard ESD without a dedicated traction device, not as a traction-free procedure. Several meta-analyses have evaluated the impact of traction on ESD across the gastrointestinal tract and generally report that TA-ESD shortens procedure time compared with C-ESD while maintaining en bloc and R0 resection rates, and in some analyses, reducing perforation risk [7–10]. However, most of these syntheses either pool esophageal, gastric, and colorectal lesions or mix randomized and nonrandomized designs, and include diverse traction techniques, which limits the applicability of their pooled estimates to colorectal ESD, where anatomical constraints and complication profiles differ from those in the upper gastrointestinal tract [7–11]. The only colorectal-specific RCT meta-analysis to date included relatively few trials and predated several more recent randomized studies [12]. In addition, device-specific and network meta-analyses focusing on specific traction systems do not isolate the overall effect of dedicated adjunctive traction strategies from conventional non-device-assisted ESD solely on the basis of randomized evidence [11, 13, 14]. In parallel, the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) guidelines and technical reviews, together with contemporary narrative reviews, emphasize optimization of technical strategies for colorectal ESD and highlight traction as a promising means of improving efficiency and safety, while acknowledging that the evidence base remains constrained by small RCTs and mixed study designs [1–3, 15].

Against this background, we conducted an updated colorectal-focused meta-analysis that included only RCTs comparing traction-assisted and C-ESD. Prior meta-analyses have generally shown that traction shortens ESD procedure time. Still, many pooled studies included mixed gastrointestinal sites and/or were nonrandomized, and fewer data are available from colorectal-specific RCTs. In addition, implementation-oriented outcomes, such as rescue conversion to traction and handover to another operator, have rarely been synthesized and are too sparsely reported to support definitive inferences. Accordingly, our aim was not to reestablish the existence of a procedural time benefit per se, but to provide an updated colorectal-specific RCT-only estimate of its magnitude and certainty, while describing, in an exploratory manner, underreported workflow-related outcomes. We also explored whether comparative effects varied according to lesion location or operator expertise.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 statement guidelines [16]. The PRISMA checklist is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Search strategy and selection criteria

On 8 November 2025, a systematic literature search was conducted across five electronic databases—PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov—to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing traction-assisted endoscopic submucosal dissection (TA-ESD) with C-ESD in the treatment of superficial colorectal lesions. Detailed search strategies for each database are available in Supplementary Table 2. Additionally, the reference lists of all relevant articles were screened to identify any further eligible studies.

Screening was carried out using the Covidence platform (covidence.org, Melbourne, Australia). Two researchers independently reviewed each article in two stages: title and abstract screening, and then by full-text screening. The senior author resolved any discrepancies.

Predefined inclusion criteria were RCTs comparing TA-ESD with C-ESD for the treatment of patients with colorectal lesions and reporting outcomes on an intention-to-treat (ITT) or modified-intention-to-treat (mITT) basis. For this review, TA-ESD was defined as colorectal ESD performed with a dedicated traction device or traction method intentionally added to provide additional countertraction or exposure. C-ESD was defined as standard colorectal ESD performed without a dedicated traction device. We acknowledge that C-ESD may still involve intrinsic exposure or countertraction generated by gravity, patient repositioning, mucosal flap tension, cap-assisted visualization, or technical approaches such as the pocket-creation method.

Our exclusion criteria were nonrandomized designs, studies performed per-protocol (PP) analysis set, reviews, protocols, letters, meeting abstracts, animal or ex vivo studies, overlapping publications, and articles in a language other than English.

Data extraction

Two independent researchers extracted the data using standardized Excel spreadsheets. We recorded the following data: (1) study characteristics: first author, year of publication, country, center design, study period, analysis set, traction technique, and operator experience; (2) patient baseline characteristics: sample size, age, gender, lesion location, lesion size, macroscopic type, and pre-resection lesion diameter; (3) histopathological characteristic of resected lesion including grading and depth; (4) outcomes: procedure time, en bloc resection, R0 resection, horizontal and vertical margin involvement (HM1 and VM1), rescue conversion to TA-ESD, handover to another operator, resection speed, specimen diameter and size, post-resection lesion diameter, detachment of the traction device, traction-related damage to the specimen, submucosal dissection time and speed, muscularis propria injury, and curative resection; and (5) adverse events (AEs): intraoperative and delayed perforation, and delayed bleeding. For continuous data, when studies reported medians along with ranges or interquartile ranges, well-established formulas were used to estimate the means and standard deviations [17, 18]. Controversial issues were resolved through discussion, and the senior author made the final decision.

Critical appraisal

The methodological quality of included RCTs was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB2) [19] by two independent researchers. Five standard domains of bias were evaluated: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias from missing outcome data, bias in outcome measurement, and bias in the selection of reported results. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk” of bias on the basis of an algorithm informed by responses to signaling questions. Finally, an overall risk-of-bias assessment was made. Any discrepancies between the researchers were discussed, and a joint consensus was reached. The certainty of the evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [20]. Outcomes reported in only two studies or as single-arm (without a comparator) were not subjected to GRADE assessment due to insufficient evidence to provide a reliable certainty rating.

Outcomes and adverse events

Our primary outcome was procedure time, defined as the duration from the initial submucosal injection to completion of lesion resection, including traction-device handling when applicable. Across trials, definitions varied in the start/stop points and in the inclusion of device/ancillary steps [21–24], with explicit exclusions in one trial [25] and unspecified criteria in another [26], which may partly explain the substantial heterogeneity. As secondary outcomes, we assessed en bloc resection, defined as the removal of the entire lesion in a single piece; R0 resection, defined as en bloc resection of the lesion with both horizontal and vertical margins free of tumor cells on pathological examination; horizontal (HM1) and vertical (VM1) margin involvement, defined as the presence of tumor cells at the horizontal (lateral) and vertical (deep) resection margin on pathological examination; rescue conversion to TA-ESD, defined as applying a traction-assisted method during a procedure initially allocated to C-ESD due to poor visualization, uncontrolled bleeding or perforation, or prolonged procedural difficulty; handover to another operator, defined as transferring the procedure to a more experienced endoscopist due to safety concerns, delayed progress, or inability to manage complications; and resection speed, defined as the area of the resected specimen divided by procedure time. Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed in RStudio using the meta package. For continuous variables, mean differences (MDs) and 95% CIs were calculated using the inverse-variance method. Categorical variables were assessed with the Mantel–Haenszel model and reported as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs. For studies with zero events in one arm, a continuity correction was applied; studies with zero events in both arms were not included in the RR pooling (assigned zero weight), as they provide no information for relative effect estimation. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the Q-test and the I2 statistic. I2 values of 0–25% were considered low, 26–50% moderate, 51–75% substantial, and 76–100% considerable heterogeneity. A random-effects model was used to account for potential between-study variability. All P-values were two-sided, and a P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Subgroup analyses were prespecified to explore potential clinical heterogeneity by endoscopist experience and lesion location. Subgrouping by endoscopist experience was performed for outcomes and adverse events when stratified data were available, whereas subgrouping by lesion location was performed only for resection speed. Two RCTs [21, 26] did not report operator experience and were therefore excluded from endoscopist experience subgroup analyses, but remained included in the overall meta-analyses. Where applicable, differences between subgroups were assessed using a formal test.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine how excluding each study affected the overall effect size. A publication bias assessment using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test was planned but not performed, as no outcome included ten or more studies.

Results

Study selection

The database search identified 930 records, of which 439 duplicates were removed. After title and abstract screening of 491 records, 469 irrelevant articles were excluded. A full-text review of 22 studies, using predefined inclusion criteria, identified 10 eligible for data extraction and analysis [21–30]. No additional studies were identified through manual reference screening. The reason for full-text exclusion is detailed in Supplementary Table 4. Study characteristics and quality of included studies

The 10 included RCTs (4 multicenter) comprised 1008 patients (499 TA-ESD, 509 C-ESD) with a total of 781 (77.48%) colonic lesions (387 TA-ESD, 394 C-ESD) and 227 rectal lesions (112 TA-ESD, 115 C-ESD). Study-level mean age ranged from 51 to 74 years, and participants were predominantly male (57.7%). Regarding endoscopist experience, four trials were performed by experts only [22, 24, 29, 30], three involved mixed operators (trainees and experts) [23, 25, 28], and one was conducted by trainees only [27]. Two trials did not report operator experience [21, 26]. The baseline characteristics of the included trials are summarized in Supplementary Table 5.

Regarding the overall risk of bias, seven of ten RCTs were of some concern, whereas three were high risk. Deviation from intended interventions and selective reporting of results were the main sources of bias and were assessed as having some concerns in six and eight studies, respectively. In one RCT [25], deviations from the intended intervention were identified as a high risk of bias. All RCTs were found to be at low risk of bias for missing outcome data. Outcomes

Procedure time

TA-ESD was associated with shorter procedure time compared with C-ESD (mean difference (MD) = −19.23 min, 95% CI −26.7 to −11.8; P < 0.0001) with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 77%, τ2 = 89.7, P < 0.0001; GRADE: very low; Fig. 3A). The 95% prediction interval ranged from −43.37 to 4.91 min, indicating substantial variability in the effect across future studies. Heterogeneity may partly reflect nonuniform definitions of procedure time across trials.Fig. 3(A) Procedure time forest plot. (B) En bloc resection rates forest plot. The reduction in procedure time with TA-ESD was broadly consistent across operator experience: experts-only (MD = −18.77 min, 95% CI −28.12 to −9.42; P < 0.0001), trainees-only (MD = −12.35 min, 95% CI −17.08 to −7.62; P < 0.0001), and mixed operators (MD = −23.39 min, 95% CI −62.83 to 16.06; P = 0.25). There was no evidence of subgroup differences (P for interaction = 0.43; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Mori et al. [30] did not report procedure time; only submucosal dissection time was provided, thus this trial was excluded from the procedure-time meta-analysis. Submucosal dissection time was synthesized separately and pooled with the other trial reporting this metric [29].

En bloc and R0 resection

En bloc (risk ratio (RR) = 1.01, 95% CI 0.99–1.03; P = 0.46) and R0 resection rates (RR = 1.02, 95% CI 0.99–1.06; P = 0.23) did not differ significantly between groups, with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = 0.77; GRADE: moderate) and (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = 0.48; GRADE: moderate), respectively (Fig. 3B, C).

Across resection-quality outcomes, there was no evidence that endoscopist experience modified the comparative effect of TA-ESD versus C-ESD for either en bloc resection (mixed-operators: RR = 1.01, 95% CI 0.98–1.04; P = 0.7 versus experts-only: RR = 1.01, 95% CI 0.98–1.04; P = 0.55; P for interaction = 0.89) or R0 resection (mixed-operators: RR = 1.02, 95% CI 0.95–1.09; P = 0.63 versus experts-only: RR = 1.01, 95% CI 0.96–1.06; P = 0.69; P for interaction = 0.88; Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3).

Horizontal and vertical margin involvement (HM1 and VM1)

TA-ESD did not significantly reduce horizontal (HM1: RR = 0.51, 95% CI 0.22–1.18; P = 0.11) or vertical margin involvement (VM1: RR = 1.39, 95% CI 0.4–4.83; P = 0.61). The heterogeneity between groups was low (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = 0.78; GRADE: low and I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = 0.46; GRADE: low, respectively; Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5).

Rescue conversion to TA-ESD

Rescue conversion from C-ESD to TA-ESD was required in 13% (34/255) of procedures (95% CI 0.04–0.33) with considerable heterogeneity across trials (I2 = 87%, τ2 = 1.29; P < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 6).

Handover to another operator

Handover to another operator did not differ significantly with TA-ESD (11.5% versus 15.6%; RR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.47–1.30; P = 0.35), events: 25/218 (11.5%) versus 35/225 (15.6%), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 6%, τ2 = 0.02, P = 0.34; GRADE: low; Supplementary Fig. 7).

Resection speed

TA-ESD achieved a higher resection speed overall (MD = 2.06 mm2/min, 95% CI 0.59–3.53; P = 0.006) with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = 0.99; GRADE: moderate; Supplementary Fig. 8). The effect estimate was larger in expert-only trials (MD = 3.28 mm2/min, 95% CI 0.34–6.22; P = 0.03) than in trainee-only trials (MD = 1.6 mm2/min, 95% CI −0.17 to 3.37; P = 0.08). Subgroup analysis by lesion location showed a numerically larger effect in cecal lesions (MD = 9.14 mm2/min, 95% CI 2.36–15.92; P = 0.008) and sigmoid lesions (MD = 6.36 mm2/min, 95% CI 0.38–12.34; P = 0.04). However, there was no evidence of subgroup differences by operator experience or lesion location (P for interaction = 0.34 and 0.22, respectively). Heterogeneity was mostly low across all subgroups (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). These subgroup analyses were exploratory, involved multiple comparisons, and were underpowered to detect interactions; therefore, subgroup inferences should be interpreted cautiously.

Two RCTs [29, 30] reported submucosal dissection speed rather than overall resection speed (speed based on procedure time). These were analyzed as a separate, conceptually related endpoint and were not pooled with trials reporting overall resection speed.

Specimen and post-resection lesion diameters

Specimen diameter did not differ significantly between groups (MD = −0.40 mm, 95% CI −2.06 to 1.25; P = 0.63) with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = 0.82; GRADE: low) (Supplementary Fig. 11). Similarly, post-resection lesion diameter was not significantly different (MD = −1.12 mm, 95% CI −4.41 to 2.17; P = 0.51), although heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 59%, τ2 = 4.97, P = 0.09; GRADE: very low; Supplementary Fig. 12).

Traction-specific events

Detachment of the traction device and specimen damage were infrequent, occurring in 4.5% (95% CI 0.03–0.08) and 2.5% (95% CI 0.01–0.11) of cases, respectively, with low heterogeneity for both outcomes (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0; P = 0.997 and P = 0.44, respectively; Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14).

Adverse events

TA-ESD was associated with a borderline reduction in intraoperative perforation (RR = 0.46, 95% CI 0.22–0.999; P = 0.049), events: 7/436 (1.6%) versus 20/446 (4.5%), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = 0.96; GRADE: low; Fig. 4A). However, delayed perforation was comparable between the two groups (RR = 0.74, 95% CI 0.14–4.04; P = 0.73), events: 2/319 (0.6%) versus 4/333 (1.2%), also with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = 0.37; GRADE: very low; Fig. 4B). Delayed bleeding similarly showed no significant difference (RR = 0.95, 95% CI 0.33–2.76; P = 0.92) events: 6/436 (1.4%) versus 7/446 (1.6%), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, τ2 = 0, P = 0.77; GRADE: very low; Fig. 4C).Fig. 4(A) Intraoperative perforation forest plot. On the basis of two contributing studies, TA-ESD was associated with shorter submucosal dissection time (P = 0.03) and higher dissection speed (P < 0.0001). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed for muscularis propria injury, specimen size, or curative resection (Supplementary Figs. 17–21).

Sensitivity analysis

Forest plots for sensitivity analysis are shown in Supplementary Figs. (22–43). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that the statistical results for all outcomes were consistent with the overall pooled analysis, except for resection speed and intraoperative perforation. For resection speed, excluding the Zheng 2025 [27] trial changed the effect from significant to nonsignificant (MD = 1.86 mm2/min, 95% CI −0.11 to 3.83; P = 0.06; Supplementary Fig. 22). For intraoperative perforation, exclusion of any single study, except for Ito 2025 [25] and Tamaru 2022 [22], rendered the pooled effect nonsignificant, indicating that this safety outcome is statistically fragile and should be interpreted with caution (Supplementary Fig. 23). Sensitivity analyses excluding high-risk trials showed that the reduction in procedure time remained significant (Supplementary Fig. 37). However, resection speed was no longer statistically significant (P = 0.149 versus 0.006 in the primary analysis; Supplementary Fig. 38), and the borderline reduction in intraoperative perforation also lost statistical significance (P = 0.101 versus 0.049; Supplementary Fig. 39). A sensitivity analysis of procedure time, excluding trials with inclusion of device/ancillary steps or unspecified criteria in their definition, showed that procedure time remained statistically significant (P = 0.0008; Supplementary Fig. 40). Finally, rare-events sensitivity analysis using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) supported a lower risk of intraoperative perforation (RR = 0.36, 95% CI 0.15–0.84; P = 0.018; Supplementary Fig. 41), while delayed perforation and delayed bleeding remained nonsignificant (Supplementary Figs. 42 and 43).

Discussion

In this updated colorectal-specific meta-analysis of 10 RCTs including 1008 patients, with prespecified ITT and modified mITT analyses, traction-assisted endoscopic submucosal dissection (TA-ESD) was associated with shorter procedure times and higher resection speed compared with C-ESD, without compromising en bloc or R0 resection rates and with only a borderline reduction in intraoperative perforation. The main comparative advantage observed in this review was therefore procedural efficiency rather than broader clinical superiority. These findings were robust in sensitivity analyses for efficacy outcomes, and the direction of effects was broadly consistent across lesion location and operator experience, with numerically larger resection-speed gains in some strata (cecal or sigmoid lesions and expert-only trials), without evidence of effect modification. Overall, TA-ESD may offer a time-efficient approach to colorectal ESD without an apparent tradeoff in resection quality or safety, while any reduction in intraoperative perforation should be considered hypothesis generating, given statistical fragility.

Our finding that traction shortens colorectal procedure time aligns with the colorectal RCT synthesis by Singh et al. [12] and is consistent with the broader colorectal evidence. For consistency, we use TA-ESD to denote any traction-assisted colorectal ESD technique, noting that some meta-analyses label this category differently. In Wu et al. [11], direct comparisons showed shorter procedure time with traction than with C-ESD, consistent with our head-to-head findings; however, these ranking-based findings should be interpreted as a supportive context rather than primary evidence.

Pan-GI meta-analyses that reported explicit colorectal subgroups [7–9] similarly demonstrated a time benefit favoring traction in the colorectum, while effects in the stomach were often weaker or nonsignificant, supporting the interpretation that traction is most valuable in technically demanding settings. As a supportive context, a recent RCT-only pan-GI network meta-analysis by Ramai et al. [31] reported directionally similar findings in the colorectal subgroup, with traction associated with shorter procedure time than C-ESD in the network estimates (Table 2); however, the subgroup ranking of TA-ESD reflects indirect, ranking-based evidence rather than colorectal-specific head-to-head estimates. In a mixed-design study, Lopimpisuth et al. [10] also reported shorter colorectal procedure time but noted substantial heterogeneity related to operator experience, traction methods, and inconsistent time definitions; pooled estimates should therefore be interpreted cautiously given the susceptibility of nonrandomized data to differences in patient and procedure characteristics and to expertise confounding. Finally, device-specific evidence from Hasan et al. [13] (S–O clip) suggested shorter colorectal procedure time with borderline precision, reinforcing a consistent direction of effect even when certainty varies by device and dataset.

In our study, traction did not confer a clear advantage for en bloc or R0 resection, which is expected given the high baseline en bloc and R0 rates in colorectal ESD, making further gains difficult to demonstrate. This mirrors Singh et al. [12], who reported no meaningful differences for either endpoint. Wu et al. [11] similarly reported comparable en bloc and R0 resection in direct comparisons, aligning with our head-to-head results. Any apparent R0 advantage suggested by network estimates likely reflects indirect evidence and between-study differences rather than a consistent head-to-head effect. In the same network framework, Ramai et al. [31] reported that TA-ESD ranked highest for en bloc resection in the colorectal subgroup; however, this colorectal finding was presented as a surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA)-based ranking rather than subgroup-specific pairwise estimates. Nunes et al. [9] reported a small improvement in colorectal en bloc resection, whereas Su et al. [8] found no change in complete resection within the colorectal subgroup. When observational data are incorporated, Lopimpisuth et al. [10] still showed unchanged en bloc rates and only borderline evidence for an R0 advantage by lesion location, consistent with residual confounding and heterogeneous practice patterns. Hasan et al. [13] (S–O clip) reported a higher colorectal en bloc rate without a consistent improvement in complete (R0) resection, but this should be interpreted cautiously, given the heterogeneity and nonrandomized design.

Whenever resection speed was reported, it generally favored traction, including in our study. Singh et al. [12] showed faster resection speed with traction than C-ESD in colorectal RCTs, while Wu et al. [11] found that network estimates favored traction; however, their direct pairwise analysis did not show a statistically significant difference, and heterogeneity was substantial, underscoring uncertainty in the magnitude and generalizability of this endpoint. Hasan et al. [13] (S–O clip) reported a directionally concordant increase in dissection speed in the colorectal subgroup. Variation in effect size is expected because resection speed is sensitive to the traction method used, operator technique, and largely unblinded procedural trial designs; mechanistically, different devices can also apply countertraction in different directions.

Lesion-size surrogates were broadly comparable between TA-ESD and C-ESD in our synthesis, arguing against case-selection of smaller or less complex lesions as the primary explanation for the observed efficiency gains. This is consistent with Su et al. [8], who reported that the colorectal subgroup showed no meaningful difference in resected specimen size.

Intraoperative perforation was rare in colorectal RCTs, and our pooled estimate suggested a lower risk with TA-ESD, though with limited precision, warranting cautious interpretation. This is broadly consistent with Singh et al. [12], which found no significant difference in perforation, underscoring the limited power to detect uncommon safety events. Similarly, the colorectal subgroup in Su et al. [8] trended toward traction but remained nonsignificant due to very low event counts. By contrast, Nunes et al. [9] reported a statistically significant reduction in colorectal perforations and attributed this to improved visualization of the submucosal plane with traction. Lopimpisuth et al. [10] also reported fewer colorectal perforations in a mixed-methods study, though confounding from case selection, expertise, and technique variability remains a concern. Finally, the device-focused S–O clip synthesis by Hasan et al. [13] found no evidence that traction increases colorectal perforation.

Bleeding risk was not increased with traction in our study, reinforcing a reassuring safety profile. This is consistent with Singh et al. [12], who found no significant difference in delayed bleeding between TA-ESD and C-ESD, and with colorectal subgroup findings from Su et al. [8] and Nunes et al. [9], which showed similarly nonsignificant effects despite sparse events. Lopimpisuth et al. [10] likewise reported no location-dependent bleeding signal while highlighting heterogeneity in bleeding definitions, and the S–O clip synthesis did not indicate excess post-ESD bleeding in the colorectal subgroup [13].

An additional exploratory aspect of this review was the synthesis of implementation-related outcomes that have been infrequently addressed in prior meta-analyses, including rescue conversion to traction, handover to another operator, and traction device-specific adverse events. However, these endpoints were available only from a limited subset of trials, were subject to operator discretion in unblinded procedural settings, and should therefore be interpreted as descriptive rather than definitive. Their main value lies in informing the design of future trials, reporting standards, and the development of standardized escalation algorithms, rather than in establishing firm practice recommendations.

In contrast to our strictly colorectal RCT synthesis, Awad et al. [14] pooled rubber-band-clip traction across mixed GI sites; predominantly colorectal, with only one gastric study; and reported overall improvements favoring traction, including shorter procedure duration and faster resection speed, higher en bloc and R0 resection, no increase in bleeding outcomes, and a lower perforation risk. However, they explicitly did not conduct lesion-location subgroup analyses due to insufficient data, precluding colorectal-specific effect estimates for any endpoint and limiting direct comparability with our colorectal-only conclusions. Accordingly, when contextualizing our findings against the pan-GI literature, we relied primarily on meta-analyses that reported explicit colorectal subgroups.

From a clinical perspective, these findings are broadly consistent with current international guidance, which increasingly considers traction a useful adjunct in technically demanding ESD, particularly in the esophagus and colon. The 2022 ESGE guideline on ESD reports that traction shortens procedures without sacrificing R0 resection and recommends its use in difficult esophageal and colorectal cases [1], while a more recent narrative review by Myung et al. [3] goes further, highlighting traction as a strongly recommended strategy in these settings.

Our organ-specific results reinforce these recommendations in several ways. First, the reduction in procedure time and increase in dissection speed may be clinically meaningful in the colon, where luminal angulation, respiratory motion, and thin-wall anatomy often make ESD particularly laborious [4, 5]. Using the sample-size–weighted mean C-ESD procedure time across the included trials as a reference (~64.8 min), the pooled estimate corresponds to an average reduction of roughly 30%. However, the magnitude is likely to vary across settings given substantial heterogeneity and very low certainty. Shorter procedures may translate into less sedation exposure, improved patient comfort, and more efficient use of endoscopy resources in high-volume centers. Second, the absence of any signal of harm in en bloc or R0 resection suggests that dedicated traction may be used selectively in technically challenging colorectal lesions, rather than being reserved exclusively as a bailout option. However, the current evidence does not justify routine or universal use, particularly because the main demonstrated advantage in this review was procedural efficiency rather than broader superiority in core clinical outcomes. Third, the borderline reduction in intraoperative perforation, although based on relatively few events, should be considered hypothesis-generating and is in line with the mechanistic rationale that improved visualization and stable counter-traction help maintain dissection within the correct submucosal plane and avoid deep muscular injury, consistent with the rationale for traction and with the lower perforation risk observed in the colorectal subgroup of the RCT meta-analysis by Nunes et al. [9].

Reported rescue conversion and handover to another operator underscore the need for ready access to traction and consideration of early escalation in technically challenging cases, particularly when submucosal exposure is suboptimal, as C-ESD may evolve into a hybrid strategy.

In our included RCTs, 13% of cases assigned to C-ESD required rescue conversion to traction, which reframes traction from a purely optional adjunct to a frequently necessary component of real-world colorectal ESD workflows. This shifts the practical question from whether traction can improve efficiency to in which procedural contexts its selective early use provides meaningful incremental benefit over C-ESD and protocolized conversion when needed.

Importantly, between-trial heterogeneity likely reflects meaningful variation in what triggered rescue conversion, ranging from early time-based difficulty thresholds (e.g., 5–10 min) to prolonged dissection/total procedure time cutoffs (e.g., ≥ 60–90 min after expert takeover or > 120 min total), as well as safety/visibility indications (poor visualization, bleeding/perforation control, or failure to improve after circumferential incision). This variability limits comparability and reinforces the need for standardized escalation criteria.

From a clinical standpoint, the more relevant question is not whether dedicated traction should be used routinely in all colorectal ESD cases, but in which situations its incremental benefit is most likely to justify its use. On the basis of the current evidence, TA-ESD is best viewed as a selective adjunct for procedures in which maintaining stable submucosal exposure is expected to be difficult, rather than as a universal default strategy. Conversely, when exposure is already adequate with the standard technique, the additional benefit of a dedicated traction device may be limited. Although the current RCT evidence base does not allow for derivation of a validated decision rule, the available data suggest that future trials should evaluate predefined selective-use criteria for dedicated traction. Candidate contexts may include suspected fibrosis or non-lifting in previously manipulated or recurrent lesions, anticipated poor scope stability due to severe angulation or limited access, gravity-disadvantaged orientation, or early intraprocedural failure to achieve adequate submucosal exposure despite standard maneuvers. These factors should be viewed as hypotheses for prospective testing rather than as established indications for routine use.

Dedicated traction strategies also have practical limitations that should temper any overly broad recommendation. Their feasibility and incremental value may vary according to lesion location, endoscope position, and procedural approach. In some settings, device deployment may be technically cumbersome or may offer less additional benefit when exposure is already optimized by standard methods such as pocket creation. These procedural realities reinforce the view that traction should be seen as a context-dependent adjunct rather than a one-size-fits-all solution.

In all of these syntheses, as in our own, converted C-ESD cases remain in the conventional arm under ITT principles, which is methodologically appropriate but may attenuate the apparent benefit of planned traction and dilute safety differences, particularly for rare events such as perforation.

By explicitly describing rescue conversion and handover to another operator frequencies alongside ITT and mITT effect estimates, our review more closely reflects the practical question faced by clinicians: in which cases dedicated traction may reasonably be chosen early, and in which cases C-ESD with conversion when needed remains appropriate. It suggests that traction use may be associated with shorter procedures and at least comparable safety profiles.

Finally, we pooled traction-specific events, such as device detachment and specimen damage, and found them infrequent and rarely clinically consequential. When reported, these events were managed endoscopically without documented long-term sequelae, mirroring the safety patterns described in device-focused studies of S–O clip and rubber-band-clip traction [13, 14, 24, 27]. Overall, this safety profile may reassure practitioners who remain hesitant to adopt traction because of concerns about device-related complications.

A key strength of this review is the prespecified restriction to RCTs with ITT or mITT analyses, which preserve randomization, minimize confounding, and provide an effectiveness-oriented estimate that better reflects real-world treatment performance than per-protocol analyses. This directly addresses one of the major methodological limitations of some prior syntheses that combined RCTs with heterogeneous observational designs [10, 11, 13, 14].

We also focused exclusively on colorectal ESD, aligning the study population with contemporary practice in which anatomical and technical considerations differ markedly from the upper GI tract, and systematically evaluated outcomes of direct relevance to everyday decision-making, including rescue conversion, handover to another operator, and traction-specific events, endpoints that have been under-reported in previous meta-analyses.

However, several limitations of the underlying evidence warrant emphasis. First, all included trials were conducted in East Asia, predominantly in high-volume Japanese and Chinese centers with extensive ESD experience; generalizability to Western centers with lower procedural volumes, different training structures, and variable device availability remains uncertain.

Second, blinding of endoscopists was impossible, and decisions regarding rescue conversion or handover to another operator were at the operator’s discretion, introducing potential performance bias. These implementation-oriented outcomes are particularly vulnerable to performance bias because they depend partly on operator judgment, local workflow, and threshold for procedural escalation; therefore, pooled estimates for these endpoints should be viewed as exploratory. Although outcome assessment was also unblinded, most efficacy and safety endpoints (procedure time, en bloc and R0 resection, perforation, delayed bleeding) were objective. Hence, the risk of detection bias is likely low.

Third, an additional conceptual limitation is that the comparator arm was categorized as “conventional ESD,” although standard ESD is not uniform across trials. In particular, some control techniques, including the pocket-creation method, may themselves generate substantial intrinsic traction, stabilization, or improved exposure. As a result, the present comparison should not be interpreted as dedicated traction versus no traction, but rather as dedicated device-mediated traction versus a heterogeneous group of C-ESD strategies. This may have attenuated observed between-group differences and limits the precision of broad conceptual labels.

Fourth, there was substantial clinical heterogeneity across trials in traction methods (e.g., clip-with-line, S–O clip, rubber-band clip, and multi-loop devices), lesion size and location, and operator experience. We addressed these factors qualitatively, and where feasible, through subgroup analyses, but statistical power was limited, and our pooled estimate of dedicated adjunctive traction necessarily averages across techniques that may not be equivalent.

Existing meta-analyses, including ours, were not designed or powered to compare individual traction modalities head-to-head. Network analyses of colorectal ESD, including Wu et al. [11] and Ramai et al. [31], have generally grouped all traction devices into a single TA-ESD category rather than distinguishing specific systems, and device-specific meta-analyses such as those by Hasan et al. [13] on S–O clip-assisted ESD and Awad et al. [14] on rubber-band-clip traction suggests that individual traction systems may have distinct efficiency and safety profiles. However, these findings are not supported by large, head-to-head randomized comparisons, which should be a priority for future multicenter RCTs with adequate sample sizes for each technique. Most importantly, despite the 13% rescue conversion rate shown in our analysis, trials do not provide a standardized, validated decision rule for the selective use of dedicated adjunctive traction versus C-ESD with conversion when needed. This limits translation of evidence on traction efficacy into clinically actionable guidance on which cases derive the greatest incremental benefit from adjunctive traction and should be a primary focus of future comparative-effectiveness trials.

Fifth, despite including ten RCTs, the number of adverse events remained low, particularly for perforation and delayed bleeding, resulting in wide confidence intervals and borderline P-values for some safety outcomes. A similar level of imprecision is evident in earlier RCT-only meta-analyses [7–9, 12]. Rare but clinically important complications and very late recurrences are better captured in large observational cohorts; their exclusion here was deliberate to preserve internal validity but limits conclusions about very-low-frequency outcomes. In addition, the modest sample size (1008 patients) limited power to detect infrequent events and increased uncertainty around pooled estimates. Moreover, none of the included trials were judged to be at an overall low risk of bias, which should temper confidence in the effect estimates. Sensitivity analyses excluding high-risk trials confirmed the reduction in procedure time.

In contrast, effects on resection speed and intraoperative perforation were no longer statistically significant, indicating that these findings are sensitive to risk-of-bias exclusions. Notably, intraoperative perforation remained reduced in a rare-events GLMM sensitivity analysis, but this signal was not robust to risk-of-bias exclusions and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. This RoB2 pattern is particularly relevant for time-based and workflow outcomes (procedure time, resection speed, rescue conversion, and handover to another operator), which are susceptible to performance bias in unblinded procedural trials (D2) and selective reporting (D5). In contrast, missing outcome data were consistently low risk (D3), suggesting attrition is unlikely to explain the observed effects. Accordingly, estimates for procedure time and resection speed should be interpreted as potentially influenced by deviations from intended interventions and outcome reporting choices.

Sixth, the certainty of evidence for our primary outcome, procedure time, was very low due to high heterogeneity and risk of bias, indicating that the true effect may differ. Other outcomes had low-to-moderate certainty, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation and further high-quality trials.

Finally, the relatively small number of eligible trials precluded a reliable formal assessment of small-study effects using funnel plots or regression-based methods, a limitation shared with previous RCT-only meta-analyses of traction [7–9, 12].

Taken together, our colorectal RCT-only meta-analysis and prior meta-analyses [7–14] suggest that traction may improve procedural efficiency without clear safety penalties, but confidence is limited by low/very low certainty for some outcomes, and further high-quality trials are needed.

For experienced operators, traction appears to translate into faster dissection with at least equivalent oncologic quality and possibly reduced perforation risk. However, effect sizes appeared larger among experts; traction may also partially mitigate the learning curve for less experienced endoscopists by improving visualization and stabilizing the dissection field.

Future research should move beyond the binary comparison of “dedicated traction strategies” versus “conventional ESD approaches” and define which traction method offers the greatest net benefit for specific lesion characteristics and operator profiles. Priorities include high-quality multicenter RCTs directly comparing commonly used traction modalities, with standardized definitions of operator expertise and submucosal fibrosis severity, and that incorporate cost-effectiveness alongside patient-centered outcomes (e.g., sedation exposure, post-procedural pain, and time to recovery).

Individual-participant-data meta-analyses combining RCTs with carefully controlled observational cohorts could further clarify which lesion subsets (e.g., very large, right-sided, fibrotic, or recurrent lesions) benefit most and help define traction’s role within competency-based training curricula.

Conclusions

In colorectal randomized trials, TA-ESD was mainly associated with improved procedural efficiency, reflected by shorter procedure time than with C-ESD, in a direction consistent with previous meta-analyses. However, the certainty of evidence for this endpoint was very low, and heterogeneity was substantial. Resection quality appeared similar between approaches, whereas safety differences remained imprecisely estimated. Workflow-related outcomes such as rescue conversion to traction and handover to another operator were reported infrequently and should be considered exploratory rather than definitive. Thus, the principal contribution of this review is to provide an updated colorectal-specific synthesis of RCTs only and to highlight priorities for future multicenter trials, including standardized definitions and validated criteria for the selective use of dedicated adjunctive traction.
