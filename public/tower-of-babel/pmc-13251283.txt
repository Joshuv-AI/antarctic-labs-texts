Feasibility and role of the lateral arm free flap following pull-through oropharyngectomy for tonsillar carcinoma: a clinical analysis
Authors: Xiaofeng Yuan, Xiaoran Zhang, Zheng Jiang, Mailudan Ainiwaer, Chenyili Xiong, Xi Yang, Longhao Wang, Jun Liu

Background

The traditional mandibular swing approach is a well-established technique for resecting advanced oral and oropharyngeal cancers and is frequently considered the standard of care. However, this technique carries multiple postoperative morbidities including dental loss, malocclusion, mandibular nonunion, plate exposure, and labial dysesthesia and scarring [1]. The mandible-preserving pull-through oropharyngectomy (MPPO), as described by Masuda et al., utilizes a combined transoral and transcervical approach for advanced oropharyngeal cancer, demonstrating favourable postoperative outcomes [1]. According to our clinical experience, this approach has proven particularly well-suited for tonsillar carcinoma.

Postoperative tissue defects in head and neck surgery impacts patient’s physiological functions. Recent advancements in microsurgery and surgical techniques have facilitated the widespread use of free flaps for soft tissue reconstruction. The Lateral Arm Free Flap (LAFF), based on the posterior radial collateral artery (PRCA), a branch of the profunda brachii, and its accessory veins, was first described in the 1980s [2]. In recent years, the LAFF has been used to repair the skin, limbs, penis, and oral and maxillofacial soft tissue defects [3, 4]. While the radial forearm free flap (RFFF) and anterolateral thigh free flap (ALTFF) are standard and more commonly used in oropharyngeal reconstruction, their specific drawbacks in tonsillar reconstruction, such as donor-site morbidity for RFFF and excessive bulk/hair for ALTFF, necessitate the exploration of the LAFF as a volume-matched, low-morbidity alternative. While currently, the data on using LAFF to reconstruct tonsil area is lacking.

To measure the psychological wellbeing and QoL of postoperative patients, the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire Core 30 (QLQ-C30) is a widely used patient-reported outcome which was first introduced in 1994 as a means of measuring the QoL in cancer patients [5], this questionnaire has been translated and validated in over 120 languages and used in more than 5000 studies worldwide, signifying it as a good indicator of health-related QoL (HR-QoL) [5]. To assess the HR-QoL in head and neck cancer patients more precisely, the QLQ-H&N35 was introduced and has been extensively validated in head and cancer survivors worldwide [6].

In this study, we aim to share our pragmatic institutional experience using the LAFF to reconstruct the surgical defects resulted from MPPO approach on tonsil carcinoma patients. We detail the reconstruction process, recipient and donor site complications, and postoperative follow-up using the EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-H&N35 quality of life questionnaires. We conducted an longitudinal assessment of quality-of-life scores at 1, 3, and 6 months postoperatively to identify trends and assess the long-term impact of LAFF reconstruction in tonsil carcinoma patients.

Methods

This study is a retrospective study. Data from 23 patients with tonsil carcinoma diagnosed and treated at a tertiary center between January 2019 and November 2024 was collected. This study has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital.

Treatment

Upon admission, all patients underwent a comprehensive evaluation including complete blood count, biochemical tests, coagulation studies, electrocardiogram, chest computed tomography (CT), laryngoscopy, neck CT/MRI, gastroscopy (if indicated), and cardiac color Doppler ultrasound or lung function assessment (as needed).

All patients underwent R0 resection of tonsil carcinoma using mandible preserving pull-through surgery as described by Masuda et al. [1] (Fig. 1a-c), with/without unilateral or bilateral neck lymph node dissection as indicated. Intraoperative frozen section analysis ensured clear surgical margins. Routine tracheostomy was made to prevent dyspnea after surgery. The preparation of the LAFF is consistent with the prior report [7] (Fig. 1d), the reconstruction of the tonsil defects bases on the individual’s condition of the surgical site (Fig. 1e). Following anastomosis (Fig. 1f), the LAFF donor site and neck incision were sutured with primary attention, a drain was placed (Fig. 1g and h).

Postoperatively, the surgical site was maintained clean and dry to minimize infection risk. Flap color, temperature, and capillary refill were closely monitored to detect potential vascular complications. Rehabilitation efforts focused on oral function and swallowing recovery, including exercises to improve swallowing and vocalization functions.

Decannulation was protocol-driven, requiring 24–48 h of successful tube plugging without respiratory distress. NGT removal was based on a standardized bedside swallow evaluation and successful transition to a soft diet.

Timepoint of inventories administration

The official Chinese version 3.0 of QLQ-C30, QLQ-H&N35, have been initially administered at three different timepoints: at 1-month postoperatively, 3-month postoperatively, 6-month postoperatively. Patients received the packet of questionnaires from the receptionist during routine clinic visits and completed them with the help of nursing staff while waiting to see the physician. Any questions or concerns were answered by either the nurse or the physician.

QLQ-C30 and QLQ-H&N35 inventories

In QLQ-C30, the questions are divided into five functional scales (physical, social, role, emotional and cognitive functioning), three symptom scales (fatigue, pain and nausea and vomiting), a global health status scale and six single items (dyspnea, appetite loss, insomnia, constipation, diarrhea and financial difficulties) [8]. While in QLQ-H&N35, questions are divided into seven scales (pain, problems with swallowing, senses, speech, social eating, sexuality, and social contact) as well as eleven single items (problems with the teeth, mouth opening, dry mouth, sticky saliva, coughing, feeling unwell as well as the use of painkillers, feeding tube, nutritional supplement, loss of weight and weight gain) [9]. All questions are scored on a 4-point Likert scale. Scores from both questionnaires are then translated to a 0–100 points scale as per the scoring manual (3rd Edition), missing items were handled according to the EORTC scoring manual’s multi-item scale rules, where a scale score is calculated if at least half of the items are completed. Higher scores in the functional and global health status scales indicated towards better levels of functioning, whereas higher scores in the symptom scales demonstrated higher levels of symptoms.

Statistical methods

The patient-related information and quality-of-life results are presented in a descriptive manner. A Kaplan-Meier (KM) curve was used to demonstrate the tumor recurrence of the patients, however, due to the limited follow-up period (mean 11.6 months), the KM analysis is explanatory and is statistically underpowered.

Results

Demographics

The patient cohort consisted of 18 males and 5 females, with an average age of 56.7 ± 8.9 years (range: 42–75 years) (Table 1). With 95.65% of patients having advanced tonsil carcinoma. Among all the patients, 60.87% of the patients had neoadjuvant therapy before surgery, and 39.13% had upfront surgery. The average follow-up time was 11.61 ± 9.06 months (range: 1–30 months).

Postoperative conditions

Flap-related information and outcomes

Among all the included patients, all patients had their flap harvested from non-dominant left arm, with recipient artery being mainly from superior thyroid artery (95.65%) due to the adjacency. Similarly, superior thyroid, facial and external jugular veins were the most commonly used veins for venous anastomosis. In terms of the flap size, the greatest flap size was 6*11 cm, with the width (perpendicular to the long axis of the arm) ranging from 4 to 6 cm and the length (parallel to the long axis of the arm) ranging from 6 to 11 cm. Twenty-two patients (95.65%) had survival flap, with one patient having flap necrosis (Grade III) 5 days after surgery, which required debridement and subsequent pedicled submental flap reconstruction. Though the hospital stay was prolonged, the patient recovered successfully. Two patients complained mild swelling (Grade I) of the donor site, which resolved without further intervention, two patients experienced recipient-site complication, both having bleeding (Grade II) from the flap reconstructed area within 24 h postoperatively, which were successfully managed with iced adrenaline-saline solution rinsing, No transfusions or surgical hemostasis were required. Five (21.74%) patients experienced grade I-II hypoalbuminemia (< 35 g/L) within 1 postoperative week due to preoperative insufficient nutrition intake. All five patients were managed with high-protein enteral nutrition, two patients with levels < 30 g/L received intravenous albumin infusions. All patients’ albumin level were corrected to above 30 g/L prior to discharge. One patient experienced pulmonary embolism (Grade III) 2 days after surgery and initially managed with low-molecular weight heparin, then transited to rivaroxaban, patient recovered without complications.

Postoperative follow-up

After surgery, all patients underwent successful decannulation and NGT extraction. The average decannulation time was 28.22 ± 9.13 days and NGT extraction time was 12.09 ± 7.52 days (Table 2). One patient underwent a local tumor recurrence at 8th postoperative month, and two patients experienced lung metastasis at 12th postoperative month and 23rd postoperative month respectively (Fig. 2), those patients were managed with chemoradiation. Postoperative follow-up and examinations indicated that the LAFF was initially bulky, but after several months’ recovery, the anastomosis healed well and the structure of the palatine arch was restored (Fig. 3).

Quality-of-life assessment

A total of ten patients completed all three consecutive QoL assessments, with detailed exclusion process reported in Figure S1, and comparison of baseline clinical characteristics of “complete QoL” vs. “incomplete QoL” groups was provided in Table 3. Of ten patients completing three consecutive QoL assessments, global QoL at six months postoperatively was favorable, with progressive improvement observed in physical, role, emotional, cognitive, and social functioning, as measured by the QLQ-C30 (Fig. 4a, Table S1). Concomitantly, fatigue and insomnia exhibited a decreasing trend, indicative of symptomatic amelioration. QLQ-H&N35 assessment revealed resolution of initial speech, social, and cough-related issues, potentially attributable to tracheostomy, but documented a progressive worsening of xerostomia over the six-month period, potentially reflecting the diminished secretory capacity of the flap compared to native mucosa (Fig. 4b, Table S2).

Discussions

Effective surgical intervention for early-stage tonsillar carcinoma necessitates comprehensive management of the primary tumor site and regional lymph nodes. Traditionally, the most often used surgical techniques employ tumor excision with reconstruction of the pharynx through mandibulotomy for advanced tonsil cancer, and the transoral robotic surgery (TORS) approach is increasingly being used to treat HPV-positive OPSCC as a de-escalation strategy [10]. Also, many studies have demonstrated good surgical outcomes by adopting different mandible-preserving approaches [1, 11, 12]. Comparative analysis of mandible-sparing techniques revealed that the combined transoral and lateral pharyngotomy approach offers superior potential for achieving R0 resection in tonsillar carcinoma which also facilitates the vascular anastomosis while mitigating morbidities associated with mandibulotomy, including dental complications, malocclusion, mandibular non-union, plate exposure, and labial dysesthesia and scarring [1]. Thus, this approach became standard approach for tonsil carcinoma at our center back in year 2011.

Initially, we reconstructed the surgical defect primarily with RFFF and ALTFF, which carried certain disadvantages. The RFFF, despite their versatility for a variety of oropharyngeal defects, the donor site morbidity and the demand for skin graft have been limiting its application [13, 14]. Though multiple efforts have been made to mitigate the donor-site complications and achieve primary closure, like using pre-operative tissue expansion [15], a local hatchet flap [16], a bilobed flap [17] or a more recently introduced snake flap [18], the maximal size of the flap is still limiting its application in advanced oropharyngeal cancer reconstruction. The ALTFF also presents limitations for oropharyngeal reconstruction, especially its substantial bulk, which can lead to excessive volume at the reconstruction site and potentially impair deglutition. Clinically, some patients report a foreign body sensation potentially attributable to hair growth from the ALTFF, which can be a source of discomfort. Furthermore, ALTFF use may delay postoperative ambulation, potentially increasing the risk of complications such as deep vein thrombosis and aspiration pneumonia [19]. In terms of other commonly used regional pedicled flap like submental island flap, patients always complain about foreign body sensation due to beard growth [20]. None of the patients undergoing LAFF reconstruction ever complained about marked foreign body sensation, while no hair growth was observed during follow-up visit.

The lateral arm free flap is a versatile and reliable option for soft tissue reconstruction, particularly for small-to-medium-sized defects of the upper extremity and head and neck region. This flap, first described by Song et al. in 1982 [21], has become an increasingly popular choice among reconstructive surgeons due to its advantages [7]. Based on the posterior radial collateral artery (PRCA), a terminal branch of the deep brachial artery, the LAFF can typically be harvested with dimensions up to 8 cm in width and 20 cm in length, providing a pedicle length of up to 11 cm with a caliber of 2–2.5 mm [22]. In our clinical experience, compared with other commonly used flaps, especially RFFF and ALTFF, the LAFF carries certain advantages. Compared with ALTFF, the LAFF offers advantages including avoidance of lower limb positioning (reducing contamination risk and improving efficiency), less hair (leading to better aesthetic outcomes and quality of life), earlier postoperative mobilization (reducing risks like deep vein thrombosis and aspiration pneumonia), and smaller tissue volume (making it more suitable for tracheal and esophageal reconstructions where ALTFF may be too bulky). Compared with RFFF, LAFF offers greater tissue volume for larger defect repair, with improved scar concealment (easily covered by T-shirts), minimal impact on hand and wrist function and sensation (due to preservation of major blood vessels), and a reduced risk of donor site complications, as primary closure is often feasible without skin grafting or nerve/tendon exposure.

In head and neck reconstruction, the LAFF, RFFF, and ALTFF each offer exceptionally high success rates, typically exceeding 95% in experienced centers. Recent data revealed that flap survival is comparable across all three options, with large series reporting loss rates as low as 1.8% for LAFF and success rates of 95–97% for ALTFF and RFFF [23–26]. Shifting the clinical focus from reliability toward specific functional outcomes and donor-site morbidity. When comparing RFFF and ALTFF for intra-oral reconstruction, meta-analyses reveal no significant differences in the recovery of a regular diet or overall quality of life [26]. The LAFF serves as a less studied but a versatile alternative, however, the comparison data between LAFF and the other two flap types are currently lacking. Future studies are encouraged to compare the functional outcomes between LAFF and those traditionally used RFFF or ALTFF.

This study found the LAFF to be an good candidate flap for tonsil carcinoma reconstruction, offering a perfect volume fit for the peritonsillar area and a favorable flap survival rate (consistent with a 96% success rate reported in a meta-analysis of 429 LAFF cases) [27]. All donor sites were primarily closed without skin grafting, with minimal complications. Patients experienced successful decannulation and oral feeding, along with gradual improvement in quality-of-life scores postoperatively, aligning with previous findings [28]. While a potential worsening of xerostomia was noted (possibly due to the LAFF’s lack of secretion), this is likely primarily attributable to either diminished secretory capacity of the flap or radiotherapy-related xerostomia, the current well-accepted etiology [29]. Our findings reflect the management complexities of oropharyngeal cancers. As noted by Filippini et al. (2025) [30], rare head and neck malignancies often face interobserver variability and require centralized expertise, highlighting the importance of standardized reconstructive protocols like the LAFF in high-volume centers. Other treatment modalities have also demonstrated good treatment outcomes like electrochemotherapy (ECT), which represents a significant advancement in the locoregional management of oral and oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma, particularly for patients with advanced disease who are poor surgical candidates due to age or comorbidities. In palliative contexts, ECT serves as an essential tool for symptom management, achieving objective responses in 74% of patients and providing relief from pain, bleeding, and tumor-associated distress, even in heavily pre-treated or recurrent cases. As a well-tolerated procedure with manageable adverse events, ECT’s integration into multimodal treatment strategies offers a promising avenue to improve both oncological outcomes and quality of life for this challenging patient population [31].

This study, a purely descriptive analysis of LAFF outcomes in 23 patients, has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The small sample size of 23 patients precludes any comparative analysis and may lead to an under- or overestimation of both outcomes and the rates of rare complications. This limited number of patients may not be representative of the broader population undergoing LAFF procedures. In addition, it is important to explicitly state that the outcomes, particularly the free flap failure rates, may be influenced by factors such as surgeon skill and luck. These variables, which are difficult to quantify, may have played a critical role in the observed results and could potentially lead to an underestimation of the true free flap failure rates. Moreover, like any similar study, this research is subject to selection bias. The patients included in this study may not be a random representation of all individuals undergoing this procedure, which could affect the generalizability of our findings. Finally, the 56.5% attrition rate in QoL data collection reflects the geographic reality of our tertiary center, where many patients travel from rural provinces and prioritize clinical survival over elective survey completion. This attrition necessitates that our functional data be viewed as a preliminary longitudinal trend rather than a definitive cohort analysis. Due to those innate flaws of the study design, the perceived benefits are based on qualitative clinical observations and existing literature, rather than a direct statistical comparison, which require careful interpretation.

Future studies will employ a prospective design with a larger sample size, control groups and extended follow-up. Further analysis of categorized postoperative quality of life variables will provide valuable insights for clinicians.

Conclusions

The application of LAFF for tonsil carcinoma defect reconstruction is a potent candidate for tonsil cancer reconstruction, showing satisfactory flap survival rates and aesthetic or functional outcomes from our pragmatic institutional experience. The worsening xerostomia over time requires further attention by head and neck surgeons. Despite this, overall quality-of-life and symptom scores improved over time, establishing LAFF as a competitive candidate for oropharyngeal defect repair and potentially enhanced patient quality of life.
