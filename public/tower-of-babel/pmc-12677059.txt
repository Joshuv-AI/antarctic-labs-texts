Tumor Microscopically-absent On Resection Specimen After Bronchoscopic Cryobiopsy: TuMORS-ABC
Authors: Peter P Olivieri, Darwin Ashbaker, Monika Nageshwar, Ashley D Gable, Lola A Hoover, Gavin Henry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe advent of shape-sensing robotic-assisted bronchoscopy (SSRAB) and its combination with integrated mobile cone beam CT (mCBCT) have made it possible for chest clinicians to biopsy smaller lung nodules than was previously possible. The 1.1 mm cryoprobe (Erbe, Tuebingen, Germany) can be deployed through the SSRAB catheter and produces larger biopsies than conventional forceps. We present three cases where tumors <10 mm in diameter were biopsied with the 1.1 mm cryoprobe via SSRAB, such that no residual tumor could be found at the biopsy site in the subsequent surgical resection specimens. We discuss the potential relevance of this newly-described phenomenon and propose the name Tumor Microscopically-absent On Resection Specimen After Bronchoscopic Cryobiopsy: TuMORS-ABC.Keywords List: Bronchoscopy, Cryobiopsy, Lung biopsy, Robotic-assisted bronchoscopy

Background

Recent advancements in bronchoscopy technology and technique have allowed operators to accurately biopsy smaller pulmonary nodules with improved diagnostic yield compared to conventional bronchoscopy [[1], [2], [3]]. The combination of shape-sensing robotic-assisted bronchoscopy (SSRAB) with integrated mobile cone beam computed tomography (mCBCT) has facilitated navigation success of 98–100 % and intra-operative confirmation of biopsy tool-in-lesion (TIL) in 96.7–99.4 % of nodules with a median diameter of 14–17.5 mm [[1], [2], [3]].

The 1.1 mm cryoprobe (Erbe, Tuebingen, Germany) freezes tissue at −79 °C and creates a circumferential ice ball of tissue that can be retrieved through the working channel of the SSRAB catheter. This technique has been associated with larger biopsy pieces, higher diagnostic yield, and less crush artifact compared to conventional forceps biopsies [4,5].

Here, we present three patients who underwent SSRAB with cryobiopsies for the diagnosis of peripheral pulmonary nodules measuring <10 mm in diameter. In all three cases, biopsy confirmed stage I lung cancer, and subsequent lobectomy or segmentectomy showed no evidence of residual tumor at the biopsy site. Though a similar phenomenon has been described after image-guided core needle biopsy of ductal carcinoma in situ of the breast, it has not been reported in lung cancer [6,7]. We propose the name Tumor Microscopically-absent On Resection Specimen After Bronchoscopic Cryobiopsy (TuMORS-ABC) for this newly-described phenomenon.

After successful navigation was confirmed via mCBCT, transbronchial needle aspiration (TBNA) was performed under two-dimensional fluoroscopic guidance with 21 and/or 23-gauge needles (Intuitive Surgical, Sunny Vale, CA) for a minimum of 3 passes. The radial endobronchial ultrasound (rEBUS) probe (Olympus Medical, Japan) was inserted through a hole in the airway wall that had been created by the TBNA needle to confirm lesion location. The 1.1 mm cryoprobe was then inserted into the lesion, to an optimal depth determined by rEBUS, and used to take 5–10 cryobiopsies with a 3–6 second freezing time.

After stage I lung cancer was diagnosed, all three patients underwent robotic resection of the lobe or anatomic segment where the tumor had been. In all three cases, the pathologist evaluating the resection specimen was able to identify the site where the tumor had been by the presence of inflammation, fibrosis, and reactive changes on histologic examination at a location corresponding to where the nodule had been on pre-operative CT. None of the three resection specimens demonstrated any evidence of residual tumor and, in all three cases, the aggregate size of the cryobiopsy sample was of similar or greater size than the radiographic diameter of the nodule, suggesting that the entire tumor had been removed by the biopsies. Discussion

As new technologies, such as SSRAB and mCBCT, enable more chest clinicians to accurately target sub-centimeter tumors while obtaining relatively large biopsy samples with novel tools such as the 1.1 mm cryoprobe, we believe the phenomenon of TuMORS-ABC will be seen with increasing frequency. Awareness of the phenomenon is important for multiple reasons. First, it will be important for clinicians, pathologists, and patients to avoid the erroneous conclusion that the preoperative biopsy was a false positive or that the surgical resection failed to remove the portion of the lung containing the cancer. Additionally, our observation has potential implications regarding future bronchoscopy-based therapeutics for early-stage lung cancer. Though TuMORS-ABC does not exclude the possibility that residual tumor cells below the level of histologic detection have been left behind, one could imagine a technique where numerous cryobiopsies are taken with therapeutic intent, potentially in combination with other bronchoscopy-based ablation modalities to eliminate any remaining malignant cells. This series has several limitations including its small size, observational nature, and lack of longitudinal follow up. Nonetheless, it contributes to our understanding of the growing capabilities of peripheral bronchoscopy in the diagnosis, and potential future treatment, of early-stage lung cancer.
