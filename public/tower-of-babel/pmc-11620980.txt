18F‐FAPI versus 18F‐FDG PET/CT in the Diagnosis of Relapsing Polychondritis
Authors: Xiaotong Guo, Juan Chen, Qian Zhao, Ying Liu, Xiuyan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRelapsing polychondritis (RP) is a rare immune‐mediated systemic inflammatory disease with diverse clinical manifestations. Independent involvement of the respiratory system in RP is uncommon. In the event of respiratory involvement as the initial airway‐only manifestation, the diagnosis of RP is challenging and might be delayed, and patients with respiratory involvement exhibit a poor prognosis. However, no specific diagnostic method is currently available for RP with respiratory system involvement as the main clinical manifestation. We present a 49‐year‐old female with the complaint of chronic dry cough accompanied by shortness of breath after exercise that has persisted for over a year. The patient was treated using corticosteroids. The patient's symptoms improved rapidly with the administration of 5 days of methylprednisolone sodium succinate at a dose of 40 mg/day. The treatment was then switched to methylprednisolone tablets at a dose of 40 mg/day, and the dosage was reduced by 4 mg every week until the cessation of therapy. Meanwhile, oral cyclophosphamide tablets were administered once every day at a dose of 100 mg each time. After 1 month of treatment, the symptoms of cough disappeared, the modified british medical research council (mMRC) grade dropped from 4 to 2, and the COPD assessment test (CAT) score dropped from 30 to 17. Repeated CT of the chest revealed that the tracheal wall thickening had alleviated. No recurrence was revealed in the follow‐up visit 12 months after drug withdrawal. The patient underwent 18F‐FDG PET/CT examination before hormone and immunosuppressive therapy, and 18F‐FAPI PET/CT examination was performed 5 days later. The 18F‐FDG PET/CT method revealed slight thickening of the local wall of the trachea and the left and right main bronchus, with no increase in the FDG metabolism, and no abnormalities in the rest of the cartilage. 18F‐FAPI PET‐CT imaging showed increased FAPI uptake in various parts of the body, including trachea and bronchus. The present study reports that compared to 18F‐FDG PET/CT, the 18F‐FAPI PET/CT revealed more lesions and provided a better image contrast, suggesting the latter as a suitable diagnostic method for RP, which could assist in improving the clinical management of RP patients.

Introduction

RP is an immune‐mediated systemic inflammatory disease that mainly affects the cartilage and proteoglycan‐rich tissues such as the ears, nose, airways, eyes, and joints [1, 2]. The rarity, unknown etiology, diverse clinical manifestations, and unpredictable evolution of RP render its diagnosis and treatment challenging [3]. High rates of misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis are reported for patients having relapsing polychondritis (RP) with airway involvement. 18F‐labeled FAPI has demonstrated promising prospects in the detection of benign diseases in several recent studies. In a similar context, the present report describes a case of RP with respiratory involvement as the initial presentation. 18F‐FAPI and 18F‐18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) were used as diagnostic methods to evaluate the different features of the affected patient's lesions.

Case History

The study subject was a 49‐year‐old female with the complaint of chronic dry cough accompanied by shortness of breath after exercise that has persisted for over a year. The patient was a housewife and presented with no wheezing, chest pain, fever, or any other discomfort. After visits to several hospitals, the patient was diagnosed with chronic pharyngitis, chronic bronchitis, reflux esophagitis, cough variant asthma, etc., for which she had received antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors, inhaled corticosteroid and long‐acting beta‐2 receptor agonists therapy, although the outcomes were not satisfactory. The patient was then treated with an empirical steroid for 2 weeks, which resulted in mild and temporary improvement in her symptoms. The patient was also infected with the 2019 novel coronavirus 1 month prior to her visit to our hospital, following which her cough and shortness of breath worsened significantly, accompanied by a small amount of white phlegm and wheezing. The patient was otherwise in good health. She was not a tobacco user and also denied consuming any drugs, having any food or other allergies, bird and pet ownership, and contact history. The patient did not have arthralgia or myalgia. She also denied any contact with patients having tuberculosis or any other respiratory illness. No genetic history was on record.

Physical Examination Findings

The results showed that the respiratory rate of the patient was 23 times/min, the transcutaneous oxygen saturation was 93% (2 L/min nasal catheter oxygen), and three depressions sign was positive (+). A wheezing sound was noted in both lungs during inspiration and exhalation. A prominent saddle‐nose deformity was also observed, there were no abnormalities in the right or left ears (Figure 1), and physical examination did not conform to the signs of auricular chondritis. The remaining physical examinations presented normal results.

Later, in January 2023, a chest CT was conducted at our hospital, which revealed thickening of the tracheal and bilateral bronchial tube walls and airway stenosis (Figure 4). The results of urine microscopy report were normal, with no proteinuria or sediment. Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

The 18F‐FDG PET/CT method revealed slight thickening of the local wall of the trachea and the left and right main bronchus, with no increase in the FDG metabolism. No abnormalities were noted in the rest of the cartilage (Figure 8A). After communicating with the patient and the family members, an 18F‐FAPI PET‐CT examination was performed free of charge for the patient 5 days later. The 18F‐FAPI PET‐CT imaging revealed the following: FAPI uptake had increased to varying degrees in the proximal nasal cartilage, nasal septum cartilage, bilateral inferior turbinate, bilateral auricle root, thyroid cartilage, cricoid cartilage, trachea, bilateral left and right main bronchi, right intermediate bronchi, right medial segment bronchi of the middle lobe of the lung, and bilateral fifth rib cartilage (Figure 8B). Based on the above findings and the diagnostic criteria proposed by Michet et al. [4] and Damiani and Levine [5], the patient was diagnosed with RP that involved the nose, ears, cricoid cartilage, trachea, bronchus, and costal cartilage. The patient was treated using corticosteroids. The patient's symptoms improved rapidly with the administration of 5 days of methylprednisolone sodium succinate at a dose of 40 mg/day. The treatment was then switched to methylprednisolone tablets at a dose of 40 mg/day, and the dosage was reduced by 4 mg every week until the cessation of therapy. Meanwhile, oral cyclophosphamide tablets were administered once every day at a dose of 100 mg each time. After 1 month of treatment, the symptoms of cough disappeared, the modified british medical research council (mMRC) grade dropped from 4 to 2, and the COPD assessment test (CAT) score dropped from 30 to 17. Repeated CT of the chest revealed that the tracheal wall thickening had alleviated. No recurrence was revealed in the follow‐up visit 12 months after drug withdrawal.

Discussion

The etiopathogenesis of RP remains unknown to date due to its rarity, resulting in diverse clinical manifestations, unpredictable evolution, lack of standard treatment strategies, and ongoing challenges for the clinician in terms of diagnosis and treatment. RP is considered a part of the spectrum of systemic autoimmune disorders and could, therefore, present with clinical features similar to those noted in other autoimmune rheumatic diseases such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (ChurgStrauss syndrome), etc. [6]. The rarity of recurrent polychondritis renders it difficult to obtain accurate epidemiological data, with most reports presenting a small number of cases or individual cases. with an estimated annual incidence of 0.7 ~ 4.5 per million person‐years [2, 7, 8, 9]. The disease is reported across all races and age groups, although it is usually reported in the 40 ~ 50 year age group, and no significant difference in incidence is noted between the genders [10]. On average, 2.9 years exist between the onset of RP and the time of its diagnosis, with the delay in the diagnosis ranging from 12.6 ~ 22.8 months. The 5‐year survival rate reported for RP is 66% ~ 74%, while the 10‐year survival rate is 55% [4, 10, 11].

Among the patients with RP, 18%–26% of the patients present with respiratory involvement [12]. The respiratory tract is involved, with the probable involvement of the larynx, trachea, and bronchus, or any part of the respiratory tree, including the distal bronchus [13]. Two large‐sample studies conducted in China reported that approximately 38%–67% percent of the patients with RP present with airway involvement [14, 15]. RP often begins insidiously, and its early manifestations include localized thickening, softening, and tenderness of the thyroid cartilage and cricoid cartilage. In the later stage of RP, the airway could become narrower or even collapse due to inflammation, edema, and scar formation, which may lead to hoarseness, persistent dry cough, inspiratory wheezing, dyspnea, obstructive sleep apnea, etc. Both trachea and bronchus are rich in cartilaginous components. With the grading of the bronchus, the number of cartilage rings decreases, while the shape of the cartilage rings becomes incomplete. The cartilage pieces begin to disappear in the bronchioles and are absent in the bronchioli terminales. Recurrent inflammation destroys the cartilage rings, which essentially serve as a scaffold for maintaining airway patency. These results in tracheobronchial malacia, which then affects the drainage of the secretions, thereby rendering the affected patients prone to repeated infections [16]. Meanwhile, the existing infection aggravates this tracheobronchial malacia, and this turns into a vicious cycle of infection and progressing malacia [17]. Irreversible damage to the tracheal cartilage could result in tracheal collapse and treatment failure, which complicates airway treatment, thereby increasing the financial burden on the patient, and could also lead to death [2]. The rates of misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis are high among patients with RP with airway involvement, along with prolonged disease course, increased rate of re‐hospitalization, and poor prognosis [18]. Airway involvement is the main risk factor for death in patients with RP [16]. However, independent respiratory involvement in recurrent polychondritis is uncommon. In the event of respiratory involvement as the initial manifestation, diagnosis is challenging and often delayed.

The clinical features and the occult nature of RP render it difficult to have a standard specific laboratory examination, histopathology findings, or reference imaging for this condition. Therefore, the diagnosis of recurrent polychondritis often relies on its clinical features, although this is often challenging for the diagnosing clinicians. Currently, no unified diagnosis standard for RP is available. Certain diagnostic criteria for RP include histological findings, although these are not specific, and a biopsy of the inflammatory site is not always safe. Thoracic CT is the main imaging method used for the diagnosis of RP patients with tracheobronchial involvement. CT images reveal the thickening of the airway wall, airway stenosis, airway malacia, airway wall calcification, and trapped air. A combination of CT, bronchoscopy, and pulmonary function could, therefore, facilitate the early detection of respiratory involvement in patients with recurrent polychondritis. However, suitable laboratory examinations to define active cartilage catabolism in patients with RP are lacking. RP is characterized by inflammatory changes, chondrolysis, replacement of the cartilage with fibrous tissue, loss of basophilic cells, calcification, and bone formation. PET/CT could reveal these abnormal changes in the cartilage and be used, therefore, to track the disease activity of RP and its response to treatment as well. Prior studies have demonstrated that 18F‐FDG PET/CT images revealed the characteristic features of RP [19, 20]. The relevant guidelines also suggest that PET‐CT be used for the diagnosis, disease assessment, and follow‐up of patients with compound polychondritis [21, 22].

Fibroblast activation protein (FAP) inhibitor (FAPI) PET imaging is a strategy that involves targeting the cell population in the tumor surrounding stroma, which is termed cancer‐associated fibroblasts. These cells, though heterogeneous in origin, are identifiable based on the upregulation of several surface markers, among which FAP, a Type II transmembrane serine protease belonging to the dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4) family, represents the most specific surface marker [23]. FAP is expressed highly in the stroma of various types of human cancers and is, therefore, considered promising for guiding targeted therapy [24]. FAPI PET/CT provides a higher tumor‐to‐background ratio (TBR) compared to 18F‐FDG PET/CT in various cancers [25, 26], due to which it has attracted substantial attention. Activation of fibroblasts occurs in the tissues surrounding the tumor as well as in benign situations such as wound healing, inflammation, or ischemia. FAPIs are molecules capable of binding to FAP, a membrane serine protease that is expressed highly on activated fibroblasts under a wide range of pathophysiological conditions, such as wound healing, inflammation, and cancer. This explains the increased uptake of 18F‐FAPI observed in the present study. Therefore, it is understood that FAPI PET imaging might also have the potential to characterize several common benign disease processes associated with a wide range of morbidities. If used properly, FAP‐targeting tracers would serve as an effective non‐invasive diagnostic strategy for the aforementioned diseases [27]. In the present study, 18F‐FAPI PET/CT revealed more lesions along with better image contrast compared to F‐FDG PET/CT, demonstrating its potential evaluation value for RP.

In the case reported in the present study, 18F‐FAPI PET/CT and 18F‐FDG PET/CT examinations were performed successively for the patient. The 18F‐FDG PET/CT examination indicated only the slight thickening of the local tube wall of the trachea and the left and right main bronchus while revealing no increase in the FDG metabolism. However, 18F‐FAPI PET/CT suggested that the FAPI uptake was increased to varying degrees in the proximal nasal cartilage, nasal septum cartilage, bilateral inferior turbinate, bilateral auricle root, thyroid cartilage, cricoid cartilage, trachea, bilateral left and right main bronchi, right intermediate bronchi, right medial segmental bronchi of the middle lobe of the lung, and bilateral fifth rib cartilage. According to this result, combined with clinical practice experience, the diagnosis of recurrent polychondritis was clear.

In the present study, 18F‐FAPI PET/CT exhibited more lesions that were involved and a better image contrast compared to 18F‐FDG PET/CT. Accordingly, compared to 18F‐FDG PET/CT, 18F‐FAPI PET/CT has more advantages in terms of the impact on the initial stage of patients with chondritis. Therefore, 18F‐FAPI PET/CT exhibits improved diagnosis rate and clinical management level for patients with chondritis, and the accuracy of FAPI PET/CT appears promising. Accordingly, it is recommended to use 18F‐FAPI PET/CT in combination with other clinical or imaging examinations for the diagnosis and monitoring of such patients with respiratory manifestations. 18F‐FAPI PET/CT provides functional and morphological information in a single and non‐invasive examination. However, most studies conducted so far to evaluate the effectiveness of using FAPI PET for patients with RP are case report series, and further systematic research in this direction is warranted to validate and optimize the incidental findings on FAPI PET/CT. Undoubtedly, the role of FAPI PET‐CT in the diagnosis and prognosis of RP has to be further explored.

Conclusion

Independent respiratory involvement in recurrent polychondritis is uncommon. In the event of respiratory involvement as the initial manifestation, diagnosis is challenging and often delayed. This paper reports a case of RP with respiratory symptoms as the only clinical manifestation. The patient was misdiagnosed by several medical institutions during the course of diagnosis and treatment. No specific diagnostic method for recurrent polychondritis with respiratory system involvement is currently available.

PET/CT was used in our center to evaluate the patient's condition and was expected to clarify the diagnosis through this examination, so the patient was first examined with 18F‐FDG PET/CT, but the results showed no obvious cartilage involvement, and then 18F‐FAPI PET/CT was performed. 18F‐FAPI PET/CT revealed more lesions with a better image contrast compared to 18F‐FDG PET/CT in the present study, suggesting that 18F‐FAPI PET/CT has good evaluation value for the diagnosis of RP and could thereby facilitate improved clinical management of RP patients.
