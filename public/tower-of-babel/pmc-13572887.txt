High‐Throughput Multi‐Capillary Electrophoresis
Authors: Jana Drewes, Sarah Meyer, Isobel Roberts, Cleo Fanny, Philip A Doble, Dayanne Mozaner Bordin, Janice I McCauley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe potential of multi‐capillary electrophoresis (CE) for high‐throughput analysis of legacy CE applications was investigated by optimisation of the separation of six pharmaceutical compounds on a single‐capillary instrument, followed by transference of the method to an eight‐capillary BioPhase 8800 system from SCIEX. Multi‐CE had superior performance, allowing construction of calibration curves in a single run, and a five‐fold reduction of run time per sample, improved linearity, accuracy and limits of detection. Method transfer from the single‐capillary system required minor instrumental parameter adjustments using the same background electrolyte. The potential of multi‐CE was shown via quantification of caffeine in a variety of energy drinks, demonstrating broad applicability where high‐throughput and rapid analyses are desirable.Keywords: analytical chemistry, calibration curve, capillary action, capillary electrophoresis, chemistry, chromatography, detection limit, electrolyte, electrophoresis, linearity

Introduction

Capillary electrophoresis (CE) gained considerable attention from analytical chemists when first introduced by Jorgenson and Lukas in 1981 [1]. Here, the authors reported high‐resolution separations of fluorescamine‐labelled n‐alkylamines and peptide tryptic digests of chicken albumin. The superior separation efficiency and selectivity of CE when compared against industry gold standard high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) were quickly recognised, leading to the development of many complementary methods for the analyses of pharmaceuticals. Some examples include quantification of impurities in drug formulations, structural confirmations, stereoselective separations, peptide mapping, protein characterisation, vitamin, analgesic and antibiotic analyses and many others [2, 3, 4, 5, 6].

Technological advancements of CE and allied technologies were in part motivated by the development and manufacture of biological medical products or biologics. Accordingly, industry requirements for fit‐for‐purpose validated methods for novel biologics and for quality assurance in manufacture of vaccines [7], as well as gene [8] and protein [9] therapeutics, necessitated next‐generation multi‐CE instrumentation.

Recently, developments in multi‐capillary UV detection have enabled the simultaneous analysis of eight capillaries with negligible crosstalk (<0.01%), a wide linear dynamic range and analytical sensitivity suitable for routine applications [10].

Earlier studies have also demonstrated the versatility of multiplexed CE across applications, including high‐throughput peptide mapping [11], as well as directed enzyme evolution studies [12]. Collectively, such advances demonstrate the feasibility of robust, high‐throughput CE platforms and laid the foundation for commercial multi‐capillary instruments.

SCIEX introduced the BioPhase 8800 system in 2021, configured with a capillary cartridge for simultaneous analysis of eight samples in a single acquisition. The instrument employs step‐scanning optics for UV or fluorescence detection, targeted specifically for biopharma applications (BioPhase 8800 system).

Regardless of the target market, the instrument is suitable for legacy CE applications in the food, environmental and traditional pharmaceutical sciences.

Despite the potential advantages of multi‐capillary CE systems, there remains limited information regarding the transferability of methods developed on conventional single‐capillary instruments to multi‐capillary platforms. In particular, few studies have evaluated whether improvements in analytical throughput can be achieved without compromising method performance characteristics such as resolution, precision, accuracy and sensitivity.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to develop an optimised method for separation and quantification of beta blockers, respiratory and antidiabetic medications, and caffeine on a single‐capillary instrument, followed by transference to the BioPhase 8800 system for rapid and high‐throughput analyses. The utility and performance of both instruments were compared and applied to the analysis of caffeine in energy drinks.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Acebutolol hydrochloride, atenolol, propranolol hydrochloride, caffeine, dextromethorphan hydrobromide, tolbutamide, sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (Darmstadt, Germany); sodium phosphate (Na3PO4·12H2O) was obtained from May and Baker (Dagenham, England), 18.2 MΩ H2O water was dispensed as required from a Sartorius Arium Pro ultra‐pure lab water system (Göttingen, Germany).

Background Electrolyte (BGE) Preparation

A total of 100 mM stock solutions of Na3PO4, SDS and NaOH were diluted as required for fresh preparation of all BGEs, 5 mM Na3PO4, 20 mM SDS, 20 mM NaOH and pH 12.62. All BGEs were degassed for 2 min prior to use.

Calibration Standards

A total of 1000 mg/L mixed stock solutions of the pharmaceutical standards, acebutolol hydrochloride, atenolol, propranolol hydrochloride, caffeine, dextromethorphan hydrobromide and tolbutamide were diluted in ultra‐pure water to obtain calibration standards of 150, 125, 100, 75, 50, 25 and 10 mg/L. All standards were vacuum degassed for 2 min prior to usage.

Caffeine Samples

Red Bull sugar free, Oxyshred Energy Kiwi Strawberry, Musashi Energy Zero Sugar Purple Grape Flavour, Monster Energy Zero Sugar Ultra Peachy Keen, Kami Kaze Energy Hawaiian Splice, Mother Energy Drink Zero Sugar, V Refresh Green Apple Lemonade and V Guarana Energy Drink Zero Sugar were purchased from Woolworths Metro, Sydney, Australia. The beverages were degassed for 3 min and diluted 1:4 in ultra‐pure water.

Instruments and Experimental Parameters

A conventional single‐capillary PA 800 Plus system (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) was used for method development and optimisation of the separation of the pharmaceutical compounds. The instrument was fitted with a 29 cm and 50 µm i.d. bare fused silica (BFS) capillary, with an effective length of 21 cm. The capillary was washed with 0.1 M NaOH for 2 min at 20 psi, followed by a 5 min flush of the BGE at 20 psi before each injection. Standards and samples were injected for 5 s at 0.2 psi, and separations were performed at +22 kV. UV detection for each pharmaceutical compound was at 214 nm collected at 4 Hz. The acquisition time was 5 min, and the total method duration was 12 min.

A multi‐capillary BioPhase 8800 system (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) was fitted with a BFS cartridge consisting of eight capillaries, each with a total length of 30 cm, 50 µm i.d. and effective length of 20 cm (Figure 1). Each capillary within the cartridge was washed with 0.1 M NaOH for 2 min at 20 psi, followed by a 5 min flush of the BGE at 20 psi before each injection. Standards and samples were injected for 3 s at 0.5 psi, and separations were performed at +11 kV. UV detection for each pharmaceutical compound was at 220 nm, and data were collected at 8 Hz. The acquisition time was 12 min, and the total method duration was 19 min.

Results and Discussion

Preliminary Experiments

Preliminary optimisation experiments involved systematic variations of the pH, phosphate and SDS concentrations of the BGE conducted using the single‐capillary PA 800 Plus system (data not shown). Transference of the method to the BioPhase 8800 plus system required considerations of instrument design modifications for multi‐CE and data acquisition across eight channels. The cartridge consisted of eight gold plated cannula electrodes for concentric positioning of each BFS capillary into a 96‐well standard/sample plate or a 96‐well BGE/reagent plate on the inlet side. For separation steps, the front wells of the inlet plate were filled with 2.8 mL of BGE or sample solution, while during rinsing steps the wells were filled with 1.5 mL of rinse solution. A corresponding 12‐well outlet plate, consisting of 6 mL troughs served as overflow reservoirs. The total current was limited to 600 µA across the eight capillary cartridge. Detection was performed using a step‐scanning approach that focused the UV light source through each capillary with acquisition of data points limited to 8 Hz. These factors allowed application of +11 kV across the capillary array, producing baseline resolution of all six pharmaceuticals with a longer separation time of 12 min as shown in Figure 2B. However, the relative speed of analysis was improved by five times, that is, from 12 min using a conventional CE to 2.4 min using the BioPhase 8800 system, calculated from the total method duration for both systems, and the number of samples/standards per run. The electropherograms were acquired using the same BGE and have similar resolution, selectivity and performance.

Calibration and Quantification

External calibration curves for both instruments were prepared using eight standard solutions covering the range 10–150 mg/L. For the BioPhase 8800 system, rapid calibration was achieved by loading each of the eight capillaries in the cartridge with a different concentration of the standard solutions, allowing all calibration points to be collected in a single run. To account for minor absorbance differences between capillaries, signal normalisation was applied. For this, a mid‐range mixed pharmaceutical standard (75 mg/L) was analysed in triplicate to calculate capillary‐specific normalisation factors, defined as intensity (standard or sample)/average intensity (75 mg/L standard) (n = 3) (Figure S1). The capillary‐to‐capillary variation observed in Figure S1 was consistently observed across replicate measurements and was, therefore, corrected using capillary‐specific normalisation factors. To validate this rapid‑calibration approach, conventional external calibration curves were also generated by running each capillary individually in eight consecutive analyses (see Table S1). The calibration results obtained from the rapid single‑run method were then compared with those from the single‐capillary instrument, as summarised in Table 1.

The correlation coefficients obtained from the PA 800 Plus system ranged from 0.9918 to 0.9986 for all compounds, demonstrating excellent linearity that was consistent with typical CE quantification workflows. Similarly, the correlation coefficients of calibration curves obtained using conventional consecutive runs on the BioPhase 8800 system ranged from 0.9931 to 0.9999. The rapid calibration approach yielded uncorrected correlation coefficients that ranged from 0.9869 to 0.9960. This was significantly improved when normalised, ranging from 0.9993 to 0.9999.

Limits of detection (LODs) and quantification (LOQ) for each pharmaceutical on both instruments were calculated from the respective calibration curves and detailed in Table 2. The analytical performance of the BioPhase 8800 method was comparable or superior to previously reported CE methods for caffeine and related analytes. For example, a simple CE method for simultaneous determination of caffeine, vanillin and ethyl vanillin in beverages reported LODs and LOQs of 2.6–4.3 and 4.1–5.2 mg/L, respectively, with calibration linearity (r 2 > 0.9986) and analysis times of less than 3 min [13]. Herein, the LODs and LOQs on the single‐capillary instrument ranged from 4.3 to 10.5 mg/L, and from 13.1 to 31.8 mg/L, respectively. The raw BioPhase 8800 system rapid calibration approach had similar LODs and LOQs; however, after normalisation, the LODs were significantly improved by three to four times, ranging from 1.1 to 3.0 mg/L, with a concomitant improvement of LOQs. The conventional calibration approach on the BioPhase 8800 system had slightly improved LOQs and LODs when compared to the single‐capillary instrument but was inferior to the normalised data. The accuracy and precision of the analyses for each pharmaceutical were calculated and compared on the single‐capillary instrument and the BioPhase 8800 system using both calibration methods, at 75 and 150 mg/L, and shown in Tables S3 and S4. On the single‐capillary CE, the accuracy ranged from 92.7% to 105.5% recovery, whereas the precision ranged from 0.6% to 13.2% RSD, again consistent with expectations of typical quantification workflows. The conventional calibration method on the BioPhase 8800 system improved accuracy and precision, ranging from 94.7% to 109.0% recovery and 0.1% to 12% RSD, respectively. Similarly, the normalised rapid calibration method accuracy and precision ranged from 97.3% to 101.0% recovery and 0.1% to 1.8% RSD, respectively.

This demonstrates that the multi‐capillary BioPhase 8800 system outperformed the single‐capillary instrument using both conventional and rapid calibration methods, the latter providing the best analytical figures of merit.

Rapid Analysis of Caffeine in Energy Drinks

The enormous potential for rapid and high‐throughput analyses of multi‐CE was demonstrated via quantification of eight brands of sugar free energy drinks, with expected caffeine concentrations ranging from 306 to 320 mg/L. These were as follows: Red Bull sugar free (RB), Oxyshred Energy Kiwi Strawberry (OX), Musashi Energy Zero Sugar Purple Grape Flavour (MU), Monster Energy Zero Sugar Ultra Peachy Keen (MS), Kami Kaze Energy Hawaiian Splice (KK), Mother Energy Drink Zero Sugar (MT), V Refresh Green Apple Lemonade (VR) and V Guarana Energy Drink Zero Sugar (VG). The detection wavelength was changed to 280 nm for higher specificity of caffeine detection and minimisation of unwanted matrix peaks.

Each energy drink was diluted 1:4 in ultra‐pure water, and rapid calibration and quantification were performed as described above using the method parameters and seven standard solutions of 10–150 mg/L caffeine. Conclusions

The separation of six pharmaceutical compounds was optimised on a single‐capillary instrument and transferred to an eight‐capillary BioPhase 8800 system. The transfer required minor adjustments of the instrumental method parameters using the same BGE. Multi‐CE had superior performance with improved analytical figures of merit, including linearity, accuracy, precision, LODs and LOQs. The most significant improvement was a five‐fold decrease of acquisition time per sample using a rapid calibration approach, where a calibration curve was constructed in a single run. Linearities up to 0.9999 and LODs down to 1.1 mg/L were obtained. The method was applied to the quantification of caffeine in energy drinks with results that were consistent with other literature methods. Collectively, these data demonstrated facile transference of legacy CE methods and excellent potential for application to a wide range of analytical methods where high throughput is desirable, including pharmaceutical quality control, food and beverage analysis, and other routine analytical testing applications.
