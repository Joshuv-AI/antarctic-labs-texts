Lymph node metastasis from a small papillary renal cell carcinoma mimicking pancreatic infiltration: A case report
Authors: Fumihiro Ito, Koki Kobayashi, Gaku Hayashi, Shunsuke Kamijo, Takashi Fujita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall renal masses (SRMs), typically defined as tumors ≤4 cm, are generally considered indolent and often managed with active surveillance. However, metastasis can occur even in small tumors. We report a 76-year-old man with a 26-mm renal mass and a periduodenal lesion initially suspected as pancreatic invasion. EUS-FNA suggested metastatic renal cell carcinoma. Nephrectomy revealed a 17-mm papillary RCC with high nucleolar grade (WHO/ISUP grade 2), tumor necrosis, venous invasion, and lymph node metastasis. This case underscores that biological aggressiveness may outweigh tumor size and that atypical imaging findings warrant careful evaluation.Keywords: Small renal mass, Papillary renal cell carcinoma, Lymph node metastasis, Active surveillance

Introduction

Small renal masses (SRMs), defined as renal tumors ≤4 cm in diameter, are generally considered to have low malignant potential and are frequently managed with active surveillance in appropriately selected patients.1 Nevertheless, population-based studies demonstrate that synchronous metastasis can still occur in T1a tumors, indicating that tumor size alone does not eliminate metastatic risk.2, 3, 4 Increasing evidence suggests that tumor biology—particularly histologic features and adverse pathological features such as necrosis and vascular invasion—may be more predictive of aggressive behavior than size alone.5, 6, 7, 8

We report a rare case of a 17-mm papillary renal cell carcinoma presenting with lymph node metastasis mimicking pancreatic infiltration on imaging. This case highlights that even very small renal tumors may harbor metastatic potential, underscoring the importance of biological risk assessment when considering surveillance strategies for SRMs.

Case presentation

A 76-year-old man was referred for evaluation after multiple pulmonary cysts were detected on a routine health checkup. Computed tomography revealed a 26-mm enhancing mass in the upper pole of the right kidney (Fig. 1A). Magnetic resonance imaging further characterized the renal lesion (Fig. 1B). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated a mass anterior to the horizontal segment of the duodenum (Fig. 1C). Positron emission tomography–computed tomography showed increased fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in the periduodenal lesion (Fig. 1D). Additional axial and sagittal CT images demonstrated that the lesion was located anterior to the inferior vena cava (IVC), clarifying the anatomical relationship that was not clearly evident on the initial imaging (Fig. 2). The patient had a medical history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and a brain tumor. Although FDG PET has limited sensitivity in renal cell carcinoma, it was performed in this case to aid in the differential diagnosis of the periduodenal lesion and to evaluate potential metastatic disease.

(B) Magnetic resonance imaging showing the right renal mass with corresponding signal characteristics. (C) Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealing a mass adjacent to the horizontal segment of the duodenum, radiologically suggestive of pancreatic invasion. (D) Positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) demonstrating increased fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in the periduodenal mass.

Further evaluation using endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) of the periduodenal lesion revealed CD10 positivity, AE1/AE3 negativity, and histologic features consistent with metastatic renal cell carcinoma (Fig. 3A). Dermatologic consultation ruled out Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome based on skin biopsy findings. Given the suspicion of pancreatic infiltration, a combined surgical approach was undertaken by urology and gastrointestinal surgery teams. Preoperative imaging suggested localized disease with suspected direct organ invasion rather than distant systemic metastasis; therefore, curative-intent surgical resection was considered appropriate.

Cytological and histopathological findings. (A) Endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) specimen from the periduodenal mass showing tumor cells with membranous CD10 positivity on immunohistochemistry, consistent with metastatic renal cell carcinoma. (B) Low-power hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) view of the renal tumor demonstrating a papillary architecture with fibrovascular cores. (C) High-power H&E view demonstrating coagulative tumor necrosis and microscopic venous invasion (tumor cells within a vascular lumen).

During open nephrectomy, the mass was found to be located anterior to the inferior vena cava (IVC), clearly separated from the pancreas, without evidence of direct invasion, in contrast to the preoperative imaging findings. The operation lasted 4 hours and 13 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 150 mL.

Histopathological examination confirmed papillary renal cell carcinoma measuring 17 mm in diameter, with high nucleolar grade (WHO/ISUP grade 2), accompanied by tumor necrosis and venous invasion (Fig. 3B and C). Metastasis to a lymph node located anterior to the IVC was also confirmed (pT1aN1). The discrepancy between radiologic (26 mm) and pathological (17 mm) size likely reflects post-fixation shrinkage and measurement differences.

This case highlights the rare potential of small renal tumors to metastasize to regional lymph nodes, even when initial imaging findings may mimic direct invasion into adjacent organs.

Discussion

Small renal masses (SRMs), defined as renal tumors measuring ≤4 cm, are frequently regarded as biologically indolent and are often managed with active surveillance, particularly in elderly or comorbid patients.1 The rationale behind this approach stems from population-based data demonstrating a relatively low incidence of synchronous metastasis among T1a tumors.2, 3, 4 However, several large analyses indicate that metastatic risk is not eliminated by small tumor size alone.2, 3, 4

Nguyen and Gill demonstrated a clear correlation between tumor size and metastatic prevalence at diagnosis; nevertheless, even tumors ≤4 cm retained a measurable risk of metastasis.2 Similarly, Kume et al. reported distant metastases in renal cell carcinomas measuring ≤3 cm, underscoring that very small tumors can occasionally exhibit aggressive clinical behavior.9 Thompson et al. further confirmed that metastatic potential persists even in small renal masses.3 These findings suggest that tumor diameter should be interpreted as a continuous risk variable rather than a binary determinant of safety.

Histologic features further refines risk assessment. Papillary RCC accounts for approximately 10–15% of renal malignancies.10 According to the 2022 WHO Classification of Renal Tumors, papillary RCC is no longer subclassified into type 1 and type 2, and grading is instead based on nucleolar features (WHO/ISUP grades 1–4). Higher nucleolar grade has been associated with more aggressive tumor behavior and worse oncologic outcomes.5,8 Therefore, although SRMs are often presumed low risk, the presence of high-grade histologic features may substantially modify prognosis, even when tumor size is limited.

In addition to histologic grade, adverse pathological features such as tumor necrosis and venous invasion are well-established predictors of recurrence and cancer-specific mortality in renal cell carcinoma.6,7 Tumor necrosis reflects aggressive tumor biology and rapid cellular proliferation, while venous invasion provides a direct route for hematogenous dissemination. The coexistence of these features in the present 17-mm tumor strongly suggests that biological aggressiveness outweighed size-based expectations. Notably, this case was staged as pT1aN1, illustrating that nodal involvement may occur even when the primary lesion is minimal in diameter.

Radiologically, the metastatic lymph node mimicked pancreatic invasion, further complicating clinical interpretation. Cross-sectional imaging alone may not reliably distinguish between direct organ invasion and nodal metastasis in atypical presentations. This emphasizes the importance of comprehensive evaluation, including tissue diagnosis when feasible, and multidisciplinary discussion in cases with discordant imaging findings. FDG PET may have limited sensitivity for primary renal tumors; however, it can provide additional information in atypical presentations or when evaluating suspected metastatic lesions.

Current European Association of Urology guidelines advocate individualized decision-making for SRMs, incorporating radiologic characteristics, growth kinetics, comorbidity profile, and, when available, histologic information obtained by biopsy.1 While active surveillance remains an appropriate strategy for many patients, this case highlights its potential limitations when adverse biological markers are present.

To our knowledge, the combination of an extremely small pathological tumor size (17 mm), papillary histology with tumor necrosis and venous invasion, and radiologic mimicry of pancreatic invasion has not been previously reported. This constellation of findings distinguishes the present case from prior reports of small metastatic RCC.

Importantly, this case does not challenge the established role of active surveillance for appropriately selected SRMs but emphasizes that biological risk factors should be carefully integrated into individualized decision-making. Rather, it illustrates that tumor biology—reflected by histologic grade, necrosis, and vascular invasion—may supersede tumor size in determining metastatic potential. Clinicians should remain cautious when small renal tumors display atypical imaging features, high-grade biopsy findings, or aggressive histologic characteristics.

In conclusion, this case reinforces the concept that small tumor size does not guarantee indolent behavior. Risk stratification of SRMs should extend beyond diameter alone and incorporate biological determinants to optimize individualized management strategies.

Conclusion

This case underscores the fact that small renal masses (SRMs) can exhibit metastatic potential, despite their generally indolent nature. The presence of histopathological features such as tumor necrosis and venous invasion may indicate a higher risk of lymphatic spread, even in tumors measuring less than 2 cm. Clinicians should exercise caution when selecting active surveillance for SRMs and consider a comprehensive assessment of radiologic and pathologic risk factors. Our findings highlight the need for individualized management strategies and suggest that tumor biology may be more important than size alone in predicting behavior.
