The resilient subset: cancer stem cells at the core of immunotherapy resistance
Authors: Julián A Gajón, Diego Esperante, Nahuel Ko, Aaron Jimenez-Miranda, Samira Muñoz-Cruz, Ezequiel Fuentes-Panana, Laura C Bonifaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer stem cells (CSCs) are a small subset of cells that possess unique characteristics, including the ability to initiate and sustain tumor growth, self-renew, and differentiate into various types of cancer cells. They play a crucial role in tumor progression, metastasis, and recurrence. In recent years, CSCs have garnered significant attention due to their involvement in therapeutic resistance to both conventional and novel cancer treatments, including immunotherapy. This review delves into the fundamental properties of CSCs, molecular, cellular, and metabolic, focusing particularly on their immunomodulatory effects. We propose a comprehensive model that reflects the involvement of CSCs in various interlinked contexts, including key signaling pathways, the suppressive microenvironment, and the surrounding stromal components. Through this triaxial conceptualization, we highlight the key role of the stemness-orchestrated architecture in contributing to therapeutic failure. We present evidence gathered from diverse clinical settings, demonstrating a tight correlation between a stem tumor phenotype and poor clinical outcomes, including reduced survival rates and dampened response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICB). Finally, we propose therapeutic interventions specifically designed to disrupt the architecture of cancer stemness. These include vaccination against CSC antigens, differentiation therapies, cellular therapies, and combinatorial approaches. In summary, this review apprises an integral, up-to-date perspective that redefines the role of CSCs, positioning them at the core of immunotherapeutic resistance.Keywords: cancer stem cells, cancer signaling, immune evasion, immunotherapy resistance, therapeutic failure

Introduction

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are a subset of tumor cells defined by their capacity for quiescence, self-renewal, asymmetric division, multilineage differentiation, and long-term tumor propagation in serial transplantation assays. These cells are thought to be responsible for sustaining tumor growth, promoting invasion and metastasis, and driving resistance to therapy [1–4]. Two conceptual models have historically shaped our understanding of CSC biology [5–10]. The hierarchical model posits that tumors are organized in a unidirectional lineage structure, where a small population of CSCs gives rise to transient amplifying and terminally differentiated cells, with CSCs residing at the apex and maintaining long-term clonogenicity. In contrast, the plasticity model proposes that stemness is not confined to a fixed subset, but rather exists as a dynamic and reversible state that can be acquired or lost at different stages of carcinogenesis depending on microenvironmental cues such as hypoxia, inflammation, nutritional depletion, stromal remodeling, or therapeutic stress [11]. Current evidence supports a hybrid model, in which both hierarchical organization and phenotypic plasticity co-exist: while some tumors maintain stable CSC populations, others exhibit dynamic transitions between stem-like and nonstem states driven by niche-dependent signaling and epigenetic reprogramming. This integrated perspective aligns with the conceptual framework of cancer stemness as a dynamic and adaptable cellular state sustained by multiple, interlocking regulatory axes [12–14].

CSCs are better understood if compared to their ‘healthy’ counterpart. Stem cells (SCs) are necessary for embryonic and fetal development, where different categories of stem cells give rise to the entirety of the organism. When development is complete, SCs participate in tissue growth during childhood and tissue repair in adulthood [15]. These SCs are usually multipotent, i.e. they can give rise to cells of a specific lineage, be this hematopoietic, neural, mesenchymal, etc. Because of their critical role in tissue repair and regeneration, damage to SCs is severely detrimental to tissue health [16]. Furthermore, SCs express specific antigens that may be recognized as foreign in the context of MHC presentation, such as epitopes derived from SC-specific proteins (e.g. embryonic factors such as Oct-4)[17]. To surpass this limitation, SCs down-regulate MHC-I expression. While this may act as a double-edged sword, given that a main signal for NK-mediated cytotoxicity is reduced or absent MHC-I expression, SCs take advantage of NK biology by limiting the expression of NK-activating receptors (e.g. NKG2D) and secreting immunosuppressive molecules, such as prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) [18].

Another layer of immune resistance in normal SCs is conferred by their localization within specialized immune-privileged niches. Prototypical examples include the hematopoietic niche in the bone marrow and epithelial niches in barrier tissues, such as the skin and intestine, where long-lived SCs are physically sheltered and embedded in highly regulated stromal-immune cell networks protecting them from immune-mediated damage during homeostasis and tissue repair [19–21]These niches are anatomically and molecularly configured to limit immune activation and effector function, for instance through: restricted lymphocyte access, enrichment of regulatory T cells, and high local concentrations of anti-inflammatory mediators and inhibitory ligands. As such, SCs represent a prime example of cells endowed with an extraordinary immune evasion capacity.

SCs also express a plethora of membrane channels and transporters that allow for the extrusion of noxious molecules. Specifically, elevated expression of members of the ABC transporter family, including ABCG2, ABCB1, ABCB5, and ABCC1, allows for the elimination of xenobiotics and other harmful substances [22]. While this trait may help preserve normal SC reservoirs, this characteristic may be exploited in the context of cancer therapy, allowing CSCs to resist antineoplastic agents through active drug efflux [23]. While differentiated cells are more susceptible to damage, aging, and replacement, SCs are specifically equipped to withstand these stresses and preserve their long-term regenerative capacity, ensuring their continued existence. These adaptations, which are essential for tissue maintenance under physiological conditions, can be co-opted by CSCs to resist endogenous and therapy-induced antitumor pressures.

Indeed, CSCs are a formidable adversary, exploiting stemness mechanisms to hinder the efficacy of antitumor responses and antineoplastic therapies[24, 25]. Their role in carcinogenesis and tumorigenesis is well documented, although the specific dynamics of the stemness-oncogenesis process remain to be thoroughly studied. In this regard, cancer stemness may diverge across the spectrum of cancers. Transformed hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) have been pinpointed as the initiator cells of hematologic malignancies, such as leukemic cells [26]. However, in solid tumors, where tissue hierarchy is not as well-defined, the generation of CSCs may be the product of a dedifferentiation process by progressively transformed cells, or of direct oncogenic transformation of SCs within specific tissue niches [27]. It is likely that these processes occur simultaneously, leading to diverse courses of oncogenesis through phenotypic flexibility of stem components. If plasticity is a prerogative for CSC formation, then adaptability is a natural consequence of this process. In this scenario, CSC plasticity is likely governed by a combination of mutually regulated factors, including immunosuppressive, epigenetic, and metabolic adaptations.

Cancer stemness is a dynamic cellular state

CSC behavior is governed by a highly conserved network of signaling pathways that regulate transcriptional, metabolic, epigenome, and functional programs essential for sustaining the CSC state across diverse tumor types[28–30]. Many of these pathways are shared with normal stem cells, orchestrating asymmetric division, maintaining a reservoir of quiescent tumor-initiating cells, and balancing survival, self-renewal, and differentiation. Critically, they also confer the metabolic flexibility that enables CSCs to adapt to hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, therapy, and other hostile microenvironmental conditions [28, 29]. The CSC phenotype thus emerges as a dynamic, plastic state driven by extensive crosstalk and feedback among core pathways, including WNT/β-catenin, Sonic Hedgehog (SHH), YAP/TAZ, TGF-β, NOTCH, JAK/STAT, and PI3 K/AKT, which together form a self-sustaining regulatory network responsive to both intrinsic and extrinsic cues (Fig. 1a). Additional modulators, such as NF-κB, BMPs, HIFs, and epigenetic regulators, further refine stemness features and fuel adaptation or immune evasion in a context-dependent manner.

Within this integrated network, WNT/β-catenin acts as a central orchestrator of pluripotency and metabolic adaptation, inducing targets like OCT4, c-MYC, and HIF-1α and promoting stemness through feedback loops. NOTCH signaling fosters quiescence and stress resistance, while Hedgehog (HH) drives both pluripotency and metabolic reprogramming in response to intrinsic and stromal signals. Integrator pathways such as PI3 K/AKT/mTOR and YAP/TAZ sense metabolic stress and environmental changes, amplifying or stabilizing stemness programs as needed. In parallel, JAK/STAT and the TGF-β/BMP superfamily respond primarily to microenvironmental inputs, facilitating immune evasion, cellular plasticity, and therapy resistance through key transcription factors, including SOX, NANOG, and KLF4. Collectively, these pathways function hierarchically, as autocrine drivers, intracellular integrators, and stromal mediators, imparting CSCs with their characteristic adaptability and persistence within adverse clinical settings. CSCs are defined by their capacity to sense, adapt, and rewire their programs in response to environmental pressures. Therefore, cancer stemness is not a binary switch, but rather a fluid state, orchestrated by interconnected signaling networks that confer resilience and plasticity under selective stress. Understanding this regulatory architecture is essential for delineating CSC biology and for uncovering therapeutic vulnerabilities within the evolving tumor ecosystem (Fig. 1b).

Epigenetic and metabolic plasticity as emergent layers of CSC resilience

As previously discussed, phenotypic plasticity is a hallmark of CSCs; these plastic states, including de-differentiation capacity, are tightly governed by epigenetic mechanisms that act as rheostats of the signaling pathways described above [5]. These epigenetic mechanisms stabilize cancer stemness cues, establishing positive feedback loops that ultimately consolidate highly adapted cellular states capable of withstanding selective pressures such as immune surveillance. Epigenetic regulation may therefore be considered an additional layer of CSC-driven immune escape, one that ensures the perpetuation of resistance across successive generations. Conversely, this epigenetically sustained phenotypic plasticity is itself subject to metabolic inputs that actively shape the transcriptional landscape of CSCs, adding yet another layer of resilience that is deeply integrated within the conceptual framework described throughout this review.

Epigenetic drivers of CSC plasticity

Epigenetic control of gene expression in CSCs arises from diverse mechanisms that would deserve their own review, such as the one recently published by Galassie et al [88]; nonetheless, in order to explain its relationship with immune escape, we can broadly state that this control is mediated by modifications in DNA, histones, or chromatin structure that do not alter the DNA sequence itself, but rather its chemical interactions. Among this diversity of epigenetic modifications, at least some have been described as key controllers of CSC plasticity [89].

DNA promoter methylation is a nonpermissive modification for gene expression. CSCs display a differential methylation landscape compared even to nonmalignant stem cells [90, 91], suggesting that this epigenetic mark is actively remodeled to sustain stemness programs and, consequently, immune evasion. This remodeling is driven by specialized enzymes that methylate or demethylate DNA. Among them, DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) actively methylates DNA and, while playing supportive roles in normal cells [92], its loss in CSCs results in compromised survival [93, 94]. In CSCs, DNMT1 promotes hypermethylation of differentiation-associated genes and transcriptional programs, including ISL1 and FOXO3, which in turn stabilizes SOX2 expression [93, 95]. Hypermethylation of WNT/β-catenin repressor promoters by DNMT1 has been observed in colorectal cancer (CRC) and hepatic cancer (HCC) [96, 97], suggesting that this enzyme represses both differentiation programs and inhibitory regulators of cancer stemness-associated pathways.

Conversely, demethylation mediated by TET2 enzymes has been found to be compromised in several CSC-enriched cancers [98, 99]. In GBM, TET2 dysfunction leads to hypomethylation of SOX2 and OCT4 [100], TET indicating that a finely tuned balance between DNMT1-driven methylation and TET2-driven demethylation is critical for sustaining stemness programs. Notably, TET2 activity is tightly regulated by metabolites such as α-ketoglutarate, derived from BCAT1 enzyme activity or IDH function, positioning TET2 as a central node linking metabolic activity with epigenetic rewiring [101, 102].

Histone modification represents another key epigenetic mechanism underlying CSC plasticity and adaptation. H3K4 methylation, a mark canonically associated with transcriptional activation [103], has been described in CSC-enriched tumors across multiple cancer types. Several KMT2 family enzymes are hyperactivated in leukemia, CRC, GBM, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and breast cancer (BC) stem cells [104–106], driving the expression of CSC-related genes such as OCT4 and SOX2, as well as positive regulators of WNT/β-catenin signaling such as LGR5 [107–109]. Furthermore, these enzymes may facilitate the establishment of hypoxic and proangiogenic transcriptional responses, creating a self-reinforcing circuit of stemness induction and hypoxic-angiogenic signaling that ultimately promotes CSC dissemination [110]. Paradoxically, H3K4 demethylation by KDM family enzymes has also been shown to support CSC biology, by removing activating marks from WNT/β-catenin suppressors such as DKK1 [111]. Moreover, KDM5 demethylases activate epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) transcriptional regulators such as SNAIL1 and SMAD3 [112, 113], reinforcing the notion that epigenetic balance in CSCs is skewed to preferentially impose repressive marks on stemness-inhibitory programs while sustaining transcriptional activity at stemness-driving loci, a selectivity whose underlying mechanisms remain a central open question in the field.

A similar paradigm governs repressive histone marks. H3K9 and H3K27 methylation, which normally silence differentiation-associated transcriptional programs [114, 115], are either aberrantly deposited or lost in CSCs, leading to dysregulated expression of key stemness transcription factors and effector molecules. In melanoma and CRC, e.g. H3K9 methyltransferase EHMT2 sustains WNT/β-catenin activation and induction [116, 117]. On the other hand, KDM3-mediated H3K27 demethylation can sustain c-MYC, IHH, and WNT/β-catenin activity, or directly suppress cyclin D (CCND) transcription depending on context [118, 119].

Histone acetylation follows an analogous logic. Activating acetylation marks are deposited by histone acetyltransferases (HATs), which have been shown to act selectively at stemness-associated promoters, including that of FOXM1, a central transcription factor controlling metabolic rewiring in CSCs [120, 121], hereby bridging epigenetic plasticity with metabolic adaptation [122–124]. Consistently, acetylated nucleosomes have been detected at pluripotency gene loci and WNT/β-catenin promoters [125], further linking this modification to the maintenance of stemness transcriptional programs. Conversely, histone deacetylases (HDACs) are induced downstream of CSC transcription factors such as NANOG and once expressed, deacetylate p53 promoter regions, effectively silencing this tumor suppressor and consolidating immune evasion [126].

Taken together, these findings underscore a recurring and unresolved question: what are the mechanisms driving this selective, context-dependent gain of epigenetic function in CSCs, whereby both repressive and activating histone modifications converge to reinforce stemness and adaptation? The answer to this question may arise from a fundamental principle: epigenetic reprogramming could represent the final step in the three-layered signaling cascades described above. As previously discussed, several of these pathways function as molecular integrators of extracellular cues. STAT3 transduces inflammatory signals, PI3 K senses metabolic inputs, and YAP/TAZ responds to matrix stiffness, while TGF-β integrates ECM remodeling and immunosuppressive cues. The convergence of these signaling adaptors on epigenetic remodeling complexes may therefore constitute the mechanistic basis by which CSCs consolidate and perpetuate their evasive and adaptive transcriptional identity across successive cellular generations. This epigenetic-signaling integration is not, however, independent of the metabolic state of the cell. As introduced above, key epigenetic enzymes, including TET2 and HATs, are directly regulated by metabolic intermediates, suggesting that metabolic rewiring in CSCs does not merely support biosynthetic demands but actively shapes the epigenetic landscape that sustains stemness and immune evasion. Collectively, these data support a model in which epigenetic reprogramming in CSCs not only stabilizes stemness and EMT programs but also entrenches immune-evasive transcriptional states that blunt responses to immunotherapy. A dedicated review of the mechanisms driving immune scape by epigenetic rewiring on CSCs is available from Galassi et al [127].

Metabolic regulation of CSC resilience

Metabolism thus emerges as a foundational layer of CSC resilience, mechanistically coupled to both the signaling cascades and epigenetic reprogramming discussed above. Nonetheless, CSC metabolism is a debated topic: while some research suggests that CSCs are predominantly glycolytic, other evidence points to a greater dependency on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [128]. In truth, this apparent contradiction is itself a reflection of the CSC plasticity characterized by the ability to switch between metabolic states in response to contextual cues such as nutrient availability, microenvironmental conditions, and pharmacological pressure [129], supporting that enhanced metabolic adaptation is a defining feature of CSC bioenergetics.

Glycolysis is nonetheless essential for the acquisition and maintenance of stemness properties. CSCs reside in specialized hypoxic niches deep within the tumor architecture, and hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) serves as the master transcriptional integrator of this adaptation, upregulating key glycolytic effectors including hexokinase 2 (HK2), pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) [130]. Beyond ATP production, glycolytic intermediates feed into ancillary biosynthetic routes that are critical for stemness persistence, most notably glucose-6-phosphate, which fuels the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and thereby sustains NADPH-dependent regeneration of glutathione reductase, a central antioxidant defense. Indeed, PPP inhibition through resveratrol administration led to diminished stemness marker expression in a nasopharyngeal carcinoma model [131], underscoring the nonenergetic functions of glycolytic metabolism in CSC biology. This antioxidant dependency is not incidental: CSCs are acutely susceptible to oxidative stress, as elevated ROS production drives differentiation and loss of pluripotency [132]. OXPHOS, by contrast, appears to be engaged in slow-cycling or quiescent CSC states, and under conditions of metabolic stress such as chemotherapeutic exposure. Although OXPHOS yields greater ATP per glucose molecule, it does so at the cost of increased ROS production [132], a tradeoff that may itself function as a selective switch, favoring OXPHOS-dependent states only when the energetic demand justifies the oxidative burden.

Lipid metabolism adds a further layer of complexity to this picture. CSCs accumulate intracellular triacylglycerols in the form of lipid droplets (LDs), ensuring a readily available reservoir of lipid-derived metabolites [133]. Stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) emerges as a central node in this context, catalyzing the synthesis of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) that directly interface with core stemness signaling cascades, activating Notch1, stabilizing the Wnt co-receptors LRP5 and LRP6, and mediating nuclear translocation of the YAP/TAZ heterodimer [134]. Reciprocal induction of SCD1 expression by the Wnt/β-catenin establishes a lipid-stemness feedback loop, highlighting that CSC adaptation relies on interconnected positive feedback circuits rather than on single linear pathways.

Amino acid metabolism completes this metabolic portrait. Glutamine supports stemness by buffering oxidative stress, while serine acts as an indirect epigenetic regulator through methyl group donation via S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), directly linking metabolic flux to the methyltransferase activity discussed in the preceding section [133]. Attempts to therapeutically exploit these dependencies, including HMG-CoA reductase inhibition with statins, prostaglandin synthesis inhibition with celecoxib, OXPHOS inhibition with metformin, and glycolysis inhibition with 2-deoxyglucose, have thus far failed to demonstrate significant clinical benefit [135]. This reinforces a central argument of this review: no single axis, whether signaling, epigenetic, or metabolic, is sufficient to account for CSC resilience, and it is precisely their integration that renders these cells so formidably adapted to withstand immune pressure and resist immunotherapy.

CSCs are architects of immune evasion

Stromal signals are known to influence the identity and maintenance of CSCs, but whether CSCs can actively reshape the immune landscape around them is less clear. This creates a paradox: although CSCs typically harbor the same oncogenic driver mutations as the bulk tumor that should render them highly immunogenic, CSCs often persist despite ongoing immune surveillance. This suggests that CSCs are not merely passive targets but instead have evolved sophisticated mechanisms of immune resistance. Rather than simple evading detection, CSCs appear to be active architects of immunosuppressive niches. Through immune checkpoint ligand expression, cytokine modulation, and stromal niche remodeling, CSCs actively shape an environment that ensures their survival under immune pressure.

Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), immunological modulation by CSCs can be topologically classified according to spatial proximity. Proximal mechanisms are localized within the CSC niche, the local environment surrounding CSCs where direct interactions with stromal factors occur, including immune cells. This niche is defined not only by its cellular composition but also by its unique ecological characteristics, for instance, hypoxia, nutrient deprivation or high metabolic stress. Together, these conditions foster the emergence of an immunological niche, a specialized microdomain where immune cell activity is tightly regulated or repressed. Key signaling pathways such as WNT/β-catenin, NOTCH, HH, JAK/STAT, and PI3 K/AKT are enriched and dynamically activated in this environment, allowing CSCs to sense and adapt to hostile cues while orchestrating immune evasion and local immune cell polarization. In contrast, outer or distal mechanisms operate beyond the core CSC compartment, imposing additional physical and biochemical barriers through dense matrix deposition, regulation of immune cell trafficking and activity, and the secretion of further immunomodulatory factors (see below). This topological distinction underscores a layered architecture of immune modulation, ranging from local, niche-centered suppression to the establishment of tumor-wide immunoprotected regions. The resulting environment facilitates CSC formation and maintenance and serves as a critical zone for immune escape that ultimately governs tumor persistence.

The inner circle: core immunosuppressive partners in the CSC-protective niche

CSC immunomodulatory strategies are integrated into the biology of stemness itself. It has been shown that, under physiological conditions, mesenchymal stem cells are capable of inducing the formation of T CD4 + regulatory cells (Tregs), and this process is capable of subverting polarization toward Th1 and Th17 subsets[136]. Strikingly, CSCs employ a similar strategy, driving Treg accumulation to create immune-privileged tumor niches [137, 138]. This phenomenon was associated with high expression of CD80 on CSCs, a molecule typically involved in co-stimulation, but here co-opted to reinforce immunosuppression by serving as a ligand for CTLA4+ Tregs [139–141]. Further studies strengthened the link between CSCs and Tregs, particularly through the activation of TGF-β, a cytokine essential for Treg differentiation. For instance, in breast cancer, CSC-derived TGF-β promotes the conversion of naïve CD4+ T cells into Tregs [142]. Additionally, CSCs can directly recruit Tregs into the TME by secreting the chemokine CCL1, which in turn suppresses CD8+ cytotoxic responses [137].

Tregs are characterized by the high expression of the immune checkpoint molecules PD-1 and CTLA-4. While these molecules inhibit cytotoxic function in CD8+ T cells, in Tregs they enhance suppressive activity, creating a feedback loop that further protects CSCs [143–145]. Across multiple tumor types, FOXP3 expression often correlates with CSC transcriptional programs [142], reinforcing the idea that immune suppression is not simply superimposed on CSCs, but encoded within the core biology of cancer stemness. Thus, Treg induction represents not only a buffer against immune attack but a mechanism of immunological insulation that is not only compatible with CSC biology, but rather enhanced by it.

Within the immunosuppressive inner circle that protects CSC, two myeloid populations stand out as key allies: myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Although heterogeneous and still incompletely defined, MDSCs are broadly recognized as immature myeloid cells with potent suppressive functions, particularly against cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) [73]. CSCs actively recruit MDSCs via the secretion of CCL2, CXCL5, and GM-CSF [146, 147]. Once present in the TME, MDSCs reinforce the stem-like state by secreting TGF-β, IL-6, and PGE2 [148], soluble mediators known to promote stemness traits, EMT, and resistance to immune clearance [73]. Furthermore, WNT signaling within tumor cells has been implicated in the induction of MDSC-like phenotypes, establishing a direct connection between stemness pathways and myeloid education [149–152]. In parallel, PI3 K/AKT, NOTCH, and TGF-β core stemness pathways are known to drive MDSC recruitment and function across multiple tumor types. MDSCs also function as active sentinels of the CSC niche. These cells operate as corrupt antigen-presenting cells, inducing T cell tolerance, exhaustion, and T cell polarization away from Th1-type responses. MDSCs also disrupt T cell metabolism by depleting essential nutrients, such as arginine and cysteine, producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), and establishing hypoxic microdomains that impair effector T cell function [153].

TAMs are also reprogrammed in the TME skewed toward an M2-like state, characterized by a wound-healing, angiogenic, tissue repair/remodeling and anti-inflammatory profile that suppresses cytotoxic responses and supports tumor progression [154–158]. M2-like polarization frequently arises in contexts rich in stemness-associated signals, and CSCs actively induce M2-like TAM differentiation by secreting M-CSF, CCL2, and IL-10 [147, 159]. In turn, M2-like TAMs sustain CSC traits by releasing TGF-β, CCL18, IL-6 and other stemness-promoting cues [155, 160, 161]. These factors reinforce EMT, fuel β-catenin activity, and stabilize stem-like transcriptional programs in tumor cells [157].

The CSC-Treg-MDSC-M2-like TAM tetrad constitutes a core immunosuppressive module that not only silences antitumor immunity but also reinforces the transcriptional and metabolic programs sustaining cancer stemness. Central stemness pathways have been actively implicated in the polarization and functional programming of these immune cells, suggesting that CSC-intrinsic cues extend outward to educate their immune environment. Moreover, MDSCs and Tregs engage in a reciprocal feedback loop that amplifies their suppressive capacity, consolidating an immunological architecture that shields CSCs from immune attack. This raises a provocative question: are these immune populations within the CSC niche merely recruited in response to immunogenic cues, or do they represent a self-sustaining immunological companion to stemness? Intriguingly, while CSCs express multiple embryonic antigens (e.g. OCT4 and NANOG) that should be highly immunogenic postnatally, these are often tolerated as ‘self,’ implying that the coordinated Treg-MDSC-M2-like TAM formation may serve as an intrinsic safeguard of stem-like state [162, 163]. Therefore, the co-existence of CSCs and Tregs, initially contradictory, is likely to depend on their shared capacity to adapt and thrive under similar metabolic conditions, such as hypoxia, abundance of adenosine and its derivatives, and immunoregulatory molecules, all of which are abundant in the TME [,66,66,142,164–166]. Altogether, the CSC and Treg-MDSC-M2-like TAM coordinated alliance constitutes a formidable barrier to immune clearance. Whether CSCs actively reprogram this axis or mimic physiological stem-immune cell crosstalk remains an open and critical question.

Distal immune exclusion: architectural and microenvironmental barriers

CSCs do not rely solely on immune suppression. A second line of CSC-orchestrated defense is immune exclusion, a mechanism that does not directly suppress effector functions but rather restricts the access, retention, or survival of antitumor immune cells within the TME. One of the key drivers of this exclusion is the subversion of dendritic cells (DCs). As the sentinels of adaptive immunity, type 1 conventional DCs (cDC1s) are indispensable for antigen sensing and cross-priming of CTLs [167].

Accumulating evidence indicates that CSCs, either directly or through their stromal network, disrupt DC biology[168, 169]. In melanoma and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), stabilization of the WNT/β-catenin pathway impairs the recruitment of cDC1s. This occurs via the downregulation of chemokine axes such as CXCL9/11–CXCR3 and CCL5–CCR5, leading to a collapse of intratumoral CD8+ T cell and NK cell infiltration [170–173]. In lung adenocarcinoma, WNT1 expression similarly reprograms DCs, suppressing the same chemokine axes and dampening inflammatory signatures, positioning WNT/β-catenin as a master pathway of DC suppression [174]. Notably, WNT-driven tumors often fail to respond to ICB therapy, placing DC dysfunction as a promoter of therapeutic resistance by subverting effective antigen presentation [175].

Recent findings highlight PGE2 as a potent suppressor of DC maturation and migration. PGE2 synthesis is tightly coupled to WNT activity and CSC biology [176]. Exposure to PGE2 blocks the differentiation of functional cDC1s and arrests them in an aberrant state within the tumor, preventing their trafficking to lymph nodes and impairing CD8+ T cell priming [168, 177]. Moreover, PGE2 has been linked to the induction of mature regulatory DCs (mregDCs) concomitant with Treg infiltration[178].

Beyond quantitative defects and impaired migration, DCs in tumors often undergo qualitative reprogramming that skews their function toward immune tolerance. A prominent example is the emergence of mregDCs, which are characterized by the expression of immunosuppressive markers such as IL-10, PD-L1, and IDO1[179]. These mregDCs fail to prime effective CTL responses and instead promote the expansion and stabilization of Tregs within the TME [180, 181]. Notably, mregDCs are enriched across diverse tumors and are associated with poor prognosis and resistance to immunotherapy [182]. In this setting, CSC-derived factors may not only impair the maturation and migration of conventional DC subsets but also actively shape a tolerogenic DC network that cooperates with Tregs to entrench local immunosuppression[181].

Beyond immune cell manipulation, CSCs deploy a network of structural accomplices to create physical and biochemical barriers. Chief among them are cancer associated fibroblasts (CAFs), nonimmune stromal cells that function as both physical and functional gatekeepers, demarcating the tumor parenchyma from surrounding tissue. Being the most abundant nonimmune cell in the TME, CAFs respond to stemness cues such as WNT and TGF-β [,80,80,183–185]. CAFs deposit a dense extracellular matrix (ECM), forming an impermeable scaffold that limits immune infiltration [186–188]. Furthermore, they secrete immunosuppressive cytokines such as TGF-β and IL-10, and contribute to the formation of hypoxic niches, further entrenching stemness and immune resistance [189, 190]. Simultaneously, CAFs secrete IL-6 and IL-8, both inflammatory cytokines, which help to sustain CSC phenotype [191].

Remarkably, CAFs respond not only to stemness signals but they also amplify them. WNT ligands and TGF-β secreted by CSCs can be sensed and secreted by surrounding CAFs, creating a feedforward loop that diffuses the stem-like program throughout the microenvironment. In some contexts [185, 192, 193], CAFs also induce Treg expansion through MHC class II-dependent antigen presentation and upregulate inhibitory molecules such as PD-L1, extending immunosuppressive signaling from the CSC core to the tumor periphery [184].

Beyond their secretory role, CAFs act as mechanosensitive integrators that translate TME cues into cellular responses. They interpret ECM stiffness, hypoxia, and damage-associated cues to calibrate their output through YAP/TAZ signaling and other mechano-transduction pathways [194–196]. In doing so, CAFs provide microenvironmental signals back to CSCs, indicating whether to remain in a quiescent, protected state or initiate migration, invasion, and tumor spread. Emerging data also suggest that CAFs are not a monolithic population. Single-cell profiling has identified at least four distinct CAF subsets within tumors, with two exhibiting direct roles in sustaining cancer progression and possibly CSC support [197–200]. It is therefore plausible that CSC-adjacent CAFs perform specialized functions distinct from those in the broader tumor stroma, acting not only as structural scaffolds, but as biochemical messengers and niche maintainers. In this sense, CAFs do not act merely as physical obstacles, rather they have an active role in determining CSC function and fate.

At the same time, cytokine-driven polarization, shaped by CAFs, TAMs, and CSCs themselves, skews the T cell landscape toward noncytotoxic, Th2-like phenotypes, further weakening antitumor immunity. Several tumor types, particularly breast and colon, exhibit a remarkable enrichment of Th2 CD4+ T cells [201–204]. In these contexts, Th2 infiltration strongly correlates with tumor progression and immune suppression [205–207]. Th2-derived cytokines, such as IL-4 and IL-13, are potent inhibitors of both CTL and Th1 responses, and their accumulation is a hallmark of immune-excluded or immune-suppressed tumors [208–211].

The Th2 program is functionally intertwined with M2-polarized TAMs, as both cell types produce and respond to IL-4 and IL-13. TAMs are major inducers of Th2 polarization in aggressive tumors, positioning Th2 infiltration as a potential surrogate of CSC-driven myeloid education [212–214]. However, recent evidence suggests that CSCs may also act directly: CD133+ CSCs from colorectal carcinoma (CRC) and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) secrete IL-4, which promotes therapeutic resistance by enhancing mitochondrial fitness and metabolic adaptability [214–216].

Immune exclusion thus constitutes the second axis of CSC-mediated evasion: one that impairs immune positioning, reshapes cellular recruitment, and reprograms inflammatory orientation to favor tolerance.

Programmed immune escape: hardwired mechanisms of CSC immune privilege

A third mechanism of immune escape is not mediated by cellular allies or stromal architecture, but arises from a cell-intrinsic program of immune cloaking and resistance hardwired within CSC molecular features. A central component of this mechanism is the downregulation of MHC class I molecules and other key associated antigen-processing machinery, thereby evading CD8+ T cell recognition [217]. MHC-I loss should normally sensitize CSCs to natural killer (NK) cells, which target cells lacking ‘self’ signals as demonstrated in in vitro and ex vivo studies in which NK cells preferentially eliminate CSCs in glioblastoma and pancreatic tumors [218]. However, CSCs can evade NK killing by downregulating NK-activating ligands, such as MICA/B, ULBP1, and ULBP2, as reported in acute myeloid leukemia and glioblastoma models [219, 220]. Simultaneously, CSCs upregulate several immune checkpoint molecules, most notably PD-L1, but also TIM-3, LAG-3, CTLA-4 and NKG2A, creating a molecular shield that suppresses immune effector cell function even in the event of T cell recognition and engagement [221, 222].

CSCs also exploit their stemness-associated cytokines to directly suppress T cell activation and proliferation. For instance, CSC-secreted TGF-β, IL-10, WNT, and IL-4 can inhibit T cell and NK cell activation and proliferation by dampening TCR signaling, promote Treg differentiation, or shift CD8+ T cells toward an exhausted phenotype[223–231]. Thereby, these signals act dually; while they are essential to sustain the stem-like state, they are also potent suppressive agents against immune attack. As a result, even T and NK cytotoxic cells that infiltrate the tumor and reach CSCs are rendered functionally inert (Fig. 2a).

Taken together, CSCs immune resistance does not emerge from a single mechanism, but from the combined effects of diverse immune-evasion mechanisms acting in concert within the TME. At the immunosuppressive core, CSCs enlist and educate Tregs, MDSCs, and M2-like TAMs to create a hostile microenvironment for effector cells. In spatially excluded niches from the stemness core, they manipulate CAFs and disrupt DC networks to exclude or misdirect immune infiltration. And at the intrinsic level, CSCs activate a hardwired program that disables recognition pathways and directly subverts immune effector function. Notably, many of these immune evasion strategies are not unique to cancer. Normal tissue-resident and developmental stem cells also rely on the same immunoregulatory mechanisms to preserve their integrity and avoid immune-mediated clearance. These mechanisms are thought to have evolved to protect the stem cell compartment from immune attack during tissue regeneration, provide immune privilege during gestation and early postnatal development. In this light, the immune evasiveness of CSCs does not represent a stochastic consequence of malignant transformation, but the reactivation of an evolutionarily conserved program rooted in the core biology of stemness, evasion by design (Fig. 2b).

Cancer stemness as the resilient core of immunotherapy resistance

As seen in previous sections, CSCs are inherently resilient: they withstand cytotoxic insults that eliminate bulk tumor cells. This resilience extends to not only conventional treatments such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy, but also to emerging immune-based therapies. The following section explores how stemness-related mechanisms underlie resistance to ICB and other immunotherapies, highlighting the role of CSCs as active drivers of treatment resistance. Given that CSCs also possess the critical capacities to invade, seed metastases, and regenerate tumors at distant sites or even after apparent remission, they represent a central therapeutic target, making CSC-directed strategies an urgent priority in clinical oncology.

Cancer immunotherapy has transformed oncological care by harnessing the immune system to recognize and eliminate tumor cells, enabling durable remissions in subsets of patients. ICB strategies targeting PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4, or LAG3, have shown remarkable efficacy [232–236]. Despite these advances, a substantial fraction of patients either fail to respond or eventually relapse. Even dual PD-1/CTLA-4 inhibition only yields durable responses in ∼60% of selected patients with metastatic melanoma [233, 237]. These outcomes suggest that reactivating T cell function is necessary but insufficient, and that resistance is rooted in more fundamental, evolutionarily conserved programs within the tumor ecosystem.

Reinvigorating the immune response through ICB can be viewed as initiating a new round of immunoediting, further reshaping the tumor landscape under renewed immune pressure. Due to their resilience to physiological and pathological stressors, CSCs are likely to be shielded from the renewed immune surveillance. Once immune pressure subsides or the effector response is exhausted, CSCs can reconstitute the tumor through their inherent self-renewal and tumor-initiating capacity. Thus, immunotherapy failure may not reflect a lack of immune (re)activation, but rather the unintended consequence of immune selection of a fortified, resilient stem-like state.

We propose that the three layers of CSC-mediated immune evasion may operate synergistically to shield CSCs from immune clearance. In this model, stemness is not a mere byproduct of therapeutic resistance, but rather its structural and functional blueprint (Fig. 3a and c). At the intrinsic level, CSCs may exploit stemness-associated signaling pathways to sustain self-renewal while activating autocrine circuits and transcriptional programs that suppress antigen presentation, via MHC-I downregulation and upregulation of inhibitory checkpoint ligands, thereby reducing visibility to T cells. Within the CSC niche, intracellular circuits could buffer external stress, dampening responses to IFN-γ, oxidative stress, and cytotoxic attack. Beyond the niche, stromal-derived signals may contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment that excludes cytotoxic cells and supports regulatory populations, including dysfunctional DCs, ultimately silencing immune effector functions.

Multiple lines of evidence support this hypothetical model. NANOG-driven stemness programs have been shown to induce cytoplasmic WEE1 through an HSP90A/TCL1A/AKT feed-forward loop, conferring CSC-like traits and resistance to PD-1 blockade. Importantly, WEE1 inhibition restored ICB sensitivity[238]. Constitutive expression of IκBζ, a co-regulator of NF-κB, suppresses T-cell attracting chemokines (CXCL9, CXCL10, CCL5) via HDAC3/EZH2 recruitment, thereby limiting NK and CD8+ infiltration and promoting resistance to melanoma anti-PD-1 treatment [239]. Consistent with these findings, our own data demonstrate that ICB resistance in melanoma is associated with a marked enrichment of WNT3a+ CSCs, which in turn are linked to a profound depletion of CD8+ TRM cells. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of WNT-driven stemness programs reverses this phenotype, restoring TRM populations and sensitizing tumors to ICB[240].

Beyond these experimental and mechanistic insights, clinical data further support the association between CSC enrichment and immunotherapy failure. As summarized in Table 2, human studies consistently report that tumors with CSC signatures exhibit poor responses to ICB and worse patient outcomes, underscoring the translational relevance of the CSC-immunotherapy resistance axis. Across cancer types, stemness-associated transcriptional programs are consistently linked to poor response to ICB, reduced overall survival, and increased relapse rates, positioning CSC-related pathways as both predictive biomarkers and rational therapeutic targets [,139,172,239,257–272]. Considerable efforts have been devoted to developing stemness-based transcriptional signatures using bioinformatic tools to predict patient response to ICB with greater accuracy. For example, a team of researchers designed a pan-cancer stemness signature by using data derived from 17 cancer types, including breast cancer (BRCA), CRC, glioma, HNSC, HCC, nonsmall cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC), ovarian cancer (OV), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), among others. This signature showed a strong inverse correlation between stemness scores and treatment efficacy, accurately predicting ICB response [273]. Additionally, these stemness-related markers have been linked to alterations in tumor mutational burden and neoantigen presentation, mismatch repair and microsatellite status, and immune checkpoint and tolerogenic molecule expression, all of which act as critical biomarkers and modulators of immunotherapy efficacy.

Tumor mutational burden and neoantigen presentation

Historically, tumor mutational burden (TMB) has been used as a prognostic marker in various malignancies. TMB refers to the number of somatic mutations carried by tumor cells. In principle, a higher TMB is expected to confer a more favorable response to ICB because it is associated with a higher frequency of neoantigens [274]. Therefore, high TMB is often assumed to correlate with a more robust antitumor immune response and improved clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, tumors with a high stemness index appear to be exempt from this rule. In their role as repopulating entities of the tumor cell mass, CSCs can transmit acquired mutations to their progeny [275]. Because CSCs often possess relatively robust DNA repair machinery and enhanced stress response pathways, they are less susceptible to apoptosis induced by the accumulation of DNA damage. Furthermore, neoantigens generated in a stemness-enriched context are usually poorly immunogenic, due to multiple CSC-mediated immune-evasion mechanisms, including downregulation of antigen presentation in the context of MHC I class molecules, as well as over-expression of inhibitory ligands (PD-L1, CD80) and secretion of immunomodulatory molecules (TGF-B1, IL-10) [276]. As such, a paradoxical status can be generated, whereby patients with high TMB scores are unresponsive to therapy as a consequence of stemness enrichment.

Microsatellite instability

Microsatellite instability (MSI) is caused by defective DNA mismatch repair (dMMR) systems. Similar to high TMB, MSI-high tumors have traditionally been associated with more favorable response to ICB [277]. However, MSI may also generate a paradoxical state in which tumor stemness is enriched through MMR-dependent mechanisms. For example, in a model of neuroblastoma, downregulation of genes encoding dMMR enzymes, including MLH1, MSH2, and PSM2, led to a greater formation of spheroids, reflecting an increased capacity for tumor initiation and clonal expansion [278]. Furthermore, in a knockout model of MSH2, preleukemic SCs retained their hematopoietic capacity after transfer to healthy recipients, but preserved a strong tendency toward formation of thymic lymphoma [279]. This suggests that precursor SCs with dMMR defects are capable of transferring this alteration to their progeny, facilitating MSI within a relatively immune-secluded context. Moreover, EMT may also be modulated by MSI status. In a CRC model, Oh et al. [280] showed that Twist1 signaling led to Wnt-independent β-catenin activation and upregulation of CD44, a stemness molecule that binds to hyaluronic acid in MSI cells. Alternatively, microsatellite-stable cells predominantly expressed CD166, and inhibition of Twist1 in this context led to a complete inhibition of EMT. Collectively, this evidence suggests that MSI may be exploited by CSCs in order to a) facilitate tumor initiation and growth, b) transfer MSI-induced growth advantages to progeny, and c) generate an environment permissive for migration and metastasis.

Immune cell infiltration and expression of immune molecules

It has been proposed that the efficacy of ICB importantly depends on two factors: the extent and functional state of the immune infiltrate within the tumor and on the expression of target molecules by both immune effector and cancer cells. The capacity of CSCs to inhibit antitumor activity and promote immune exclusion has been extensively discussed[127, 281]. Of note, PD-L1 is often extensively expressed on CSCs, acting as a shield against antitumor immunity[282]. PD-L1 expression can be driven by various factors, including intrinsic stem signaling through Wnt, Notch and SHH; induced by EMT-promoting transcription factors, such as ZEB1 and Snai1 [283]; and upregulated in response to increased immune pressure (e.g., in response to IFN-γ). The heightened expression of PD-L1 likely contributes to the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, such as atezolizumab, in triple-negative breast cancer. Beyond PD-L1, other immune checkpoints involved in immune suppression include CTLA-4, CD80, B7-H3 and B7-H4, which may also be expressed or modulated within CSC-enriched compartments [284]. In addition, CSCs can express tolerogenic ‘don´t eat me’ molecules, such as CD47, a surface molecule that binds to SIRPa expressed by phagocytic cells to inhibit phagocytosis [281]. CSC-derived extracellular exosomes and vesicles further reinforce immune tolerance. For example, transfer of HLA-G via exosomes to DCs can abrogate their capacity to effectively present antigen, and it has also been reported to inhibit monocyte differentiation into macrophages in the periphery [285]. Together, these mechanisms enable CSCs to reshape the immune infiltrate and checkpoint landscape, ultimately blunting ICB efficacy.

Stemness-centered interventions: disrupting the root architecture of immunotherapy failure

The evidence presented throughout this review outlines a coherent mechanistic continuum linking CSC-driven immune suppression to therapy failure and clinical relapse. Rather than representing isolated features, quiescence, low immunogenicity, immune checkpoint ligand overexpression, stromal reprogramming, and exclusion of effector cells all converge to form a coordinated stemness-driven immune-evasive ecosystem that constitutes a clinically validated framework of therapeutic resistance. Importantly, each layer of this ecosystem presents distinct and potentially tractable intervention points, ranging from ligand blockade, cell signaling and transcriptional inhibition, epigenetic reprogramming, vaccination to CAR-T cell therapies targeting CSC-specific surface markers (Fig. 3b and c). Thus, the CSC program is not only a predictor of resistance but also an actionable architecture whose disruption may unlock durable responses in otherwise therapy-refractory tumors. Despite this potential, CSC-directed strategies remain underexplored, an omission that now demands urgent attention.

CSC signaling pathways and stemness regulators as therapeutic vulnerabilities

Multiple efforts have aimed to pharmacologically inhibit key stemness-related pathways. Among the first to enter clinical trials were WNT/β-catenin inhibitors, which showed limited efficacy as monotherapies and were associated with Grade III adverse events in some patients, ultimately leading to trial discontinuation [286–289]. A myriad of TGF-β pathway inhibitors have been developed, encompassing modulation of isoforms, receptors, and signaling molecules [290]. Modest clinical outcomes and variable toxicity, ranging from well-tolerated to grade III to IV adverse reactions, have limited the widespread use of these agents as monotherapy [291, 292]. Moreover, a novel therapeutic agent that simultaneously inhibits TGF-β and PD-L1 has proven efficacious in the treatment of recurring gastric cancer [293]. Newer agents targeting YAP/TAZ and SHH signaling have shown promising preliminary results. These inhibitors have demonstrated early signs of clinical response in some patients even as monotherapy [294–296], although their safety profile has not been thoroughly evaluated. These findings support continued development of refined CSC-targeting agents with improved safety and therapeutic potential.

A critical challenge remains: CSCs are often not pre-existing but emerge in response to therapeutic stress. Chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and ICB can all trigger tumor cell plasticity, leading to the enrichment of stem-like, therapy-resistant populations. In this context, the CSC self-defense program represents a dynamic adaptation rather than a fixed feature, implying that CSC inhibitors may be ineffective as standalone therapies. Instead, they are best positioned as adjuncts, deployed alongside standard regimens to counteract therapy-induced stemness. This perspective highlights the need for rationally timed combinations, where CSC inhibition overlaps with periods of heightened plasticity. Biomarker-guided administration, based on dynamic stemness signatures, may further optimize efficacy.

In addition to pathway inhibition, the distinctive molecular features of CSCs offer multiple therapeutic footholds, which include noncanonical surface antigens, metabolic enzymes, and proteostasis regulators, enabling highly selective targeting strategies. Among surface antigens, CD44 plays a key role in anchoring CSCs to the niche´s ECM via interactions with hyaluronic acid. Therapeutic strategies include either direct blockade, or indirect modulation of CD44 expression via inhibition of PI3K-AKT, NF-κB, and SHH [297]. Other surface targets under investigation are FZD, IGFBP, EphB2, LGR5, and CD133. In CRC, for example, LGR5-directed antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) carrying a tubulin inhibitor significantly reduced tumor cell viability in a preclinical study [298], while CD133 inhibition suppressed MDR1, a drug-efflux protein, reversing chemoresistance [299].

Metabolic tagging strategies exploit CSC-overexpressed enzymes, such as ALDH1A1. This enzyme can be selectively labeled using azide-containing carbohydrates that are incorporated into the plasma membrane and serve as ligands for DBCO-conjugated cytotoxicity via click chemistry, enabling highly selective killing of CSCs [300]. Additionally, CSCs exhibit heightened dependency on deubiquitylating enzymes (DUBs), particularly ubiquitin-specific processing proteases (USPs), which stabilize critical stemness factors, such as NANOG, OCT-4, SOX2, and c-MYC. DUBs inhibition leads to proteasomal clearance of them, disrupting the CSC program and resensitizing tumors to therapy [301, 302].

Targeting cell plasticity through differentiation-inducing therapies

A complementary approach involves promoting CSC differentiation into more stable, less plastic, and less tumorigenic cells. The clinical precedent for this strategy comes from acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), where therapy with all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) and arsenic trioxide overcomes the differentiation block by degrading the PML-RARA fusion protein, allowing malignant promyelocytes to mature into nonproliferative granulocytes [303]. This approach transformed APL from a fatal disease into a curable condition. A similar strategy using ATRA has been tested in solid malignancies such as BRCA and glioma, with promising results in combination with antineoplastic agents [304–306]. Tewari et al. expanded these agents to include butyrate, arsenic trioxide, and histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors to induce CSC differentiation [307]. Differentiation therapy has also been explored through modulation of other stemness-regulatory molecules such as IDH1/IDH2, MAPK, or BMP, with encouraging results [308–310].

Inhibition of stemness-related enzymes usually requires accessible catalytic sites, limiting its utility. A more recent approach involves the use of proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) [311]. For instance, PROTAC has been used in preclinical models to degrade oncogenic fusion proteins, such as EML4-ALK or BCR-ABL [312]. Thus, PROTAC-mediated degradation of stemness-associated proteins may represent a novel therapeutic route [313, 314]. However, selectivity remains a significant challenge, as many stemness regulators are also expressed in normal tissue-resident stem cells and memory T cells of the adaptive immune system, raising concerns about off-target effects and potential toxicity.

CSC-targeted vaccination

CSCs express embryonic proteins not typically found in adult somatic tissues. Despite low inherent immunogenicity, these features offer opportunities for vaccine-based interventions. Classical cancer vaccines combine tumor antigens with immunostimulatory adjuvants to enhance antigen uptake by DCs and promote CTL priming. Applied to CSCs, this strategy may bypass some challenges of antigen redundancy and self-tolerance. For instance, vaccines against ALDH1A1 or inactivated CSCs have shown efficacy in delaying tumor progression and enhancing survival in preclinical and clinical studies [315, 316]. Notably, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)-based vaccination elicited cross-protective antitumor effects in murine models [317], exploiting the shared antigenic features between iPSCs and CSCs.

Significant challenges remain. First, many embryonic antigens are poorly presented on MHC-I molecules, limiting their visibility to T cells. Second, CSCs reside in highly immunosuppressive niches, where conventional vaccine adjuvants often fail to generate robust immune activation. One emerging solution involves glyco-immunology, based on altered glycosylation patterns of surface proteins due to malignant reprogramming, producing tumor-specific glycoforms not found on normal stem cells. These glyco-epitopes may act as neoantigens or facilitate antigen presentation, thereby, improving vaccine selectivity and immunogenicity [318–322]. Altogether, CSC-targeted vaccination holds promise to expose the most resistant tumor cell population to immune attack.

CAR-T cells against stemness surface markers

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has been highly successful in hematologic malignancies and is being adapted to solid tumors. CAR-T therapy bypasses technical limitations imposed by vaccines by directly programming T cells with synthetic specificity. Early studies with engineered CAR-Ts to recognize CD44 and CD133 showed preclinical efficacy [323, 324]. However, CSC known barriers, including immune exclusion, antigen heterogeneity, and a suppressive TME, hindered durable responses.

To overcome these limitations, new-generation CAR-T constructs have been developed. Among them, Boolean logic-gated CAR-T cells, which require co-recognition of multiple antigens to trigger activation, have increased both selectivity and safety by reducing off-tumor cytotoxicity [325]. Additionally, armored CAR-T cells have been designed to express IL-7 and IL-15 upon activation, enhancing their survival and proliferation through autocrine support [326, 327]. Also, CAR-T cells transcriptionally reprogrammed to adopt a tissue-resident memory (TRM) T cell phenotype displayed enhanced infiltration, functional stability, and long-term persistence in tissue compartments, making them particularly attractive for immune-excluded, CSC-rich tumors [328]. Likewise, CAR-NK cells that are resistant to inactivation by inhibitory ligands may prove efficacious to eliminate CSCs, given the CSC lack of expression of MHC molecules[329].

Efforts are also underway to expand CAR-T targetability toward intracellular markers. Transgenic TCRs engineered to recognize CSC-associated embryonic antigens may help to bypass the limitations of surface antigen loss. Other explored nonembryonic CAR-T targets include CD338 (ABCG2), a transporter highly expressed in drug-resistant CSCs, and a glycoengineered CAR-T capable of recognizing CSC-specific glycosylation patterns. These approaches aim to further enhance the selectivity of CAR-T cells toward CSCs without harming normal stem cell populations.

Taken together, these innovative strategies reflect a shift in CAR-T cell design, from conventional cytotoxic constructs to programmable systems tailored to overcome the multifaceted barriers imposed by cancer stemness. By integrating logic gating, cytokine self-support, tissue residency, intracellular targeting, and glyco-selectivity, next-generation CAR-T cells may dismantle the resilient architecture of CSC-mediated resistance and ultimately improve clinical outcomes in patients with therapy-refractory tumors.

Conclusion

CSCs are central drivers of immune evasion and therapeutic resistance, actively reshaping the tumor-immune interface through antigenic invisibility, stromal remodeling, and immunosuppressive signaling. Yet, their unique transcriptional programs, metabolic dependencies, and surface markers offer actionable distinctions. Therapeutic strategies, from vaccines and CAR-T cells targeting stemness antigens to small molecules disrupting CSC-supportive niches, are being designed to exploit these vulnerabilities. However, the limited success of CSC monotherapies reflects a conceptual mismatch: durable responses require combinatorial regimens that simultaneously dismantle CSC biology and the bulk, differentiated/proliferating tumor cells, while also restoring effective immune surveillance. Rational combinations, such as Wnt or YAP inhibitors with ICB or more traditional cancer therapy, and interventions targeting metabolic or stromal components, may more effectively reprogram the TME.

In summary, cancer stemness is both a mechanism of resistance and a therapeutic opportunity. Leveraging this knowledge through integrated, immune-restorative strategies may be key to overcoming treatment failure and achieving long-term tumor control.
