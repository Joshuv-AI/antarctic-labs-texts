Y–shaped Generative Flows
Arip Asadulaev, Semyon Semenov, Abduragim Shtanchaev, Eric Moulines, Fakhri Karray, Martin Takac

Abstract
Modern continuous-time generative models often induce V-shaped transport: each sample travels independently along nearly straight trajectories from prior to data, overlooking shared structure. We introduce Y-shaped generative flows , which move probability mass together along shared pathways before branching to target-specific endpoints. Our formulation is based on a novel velocity-driven objective with a sublinear exponent (between zero and one); this concave dependence rewards joint and fast mass movement. Practically, we instantiate the idea in a scalable neural ODE training objective. On synthetic, image, and biology datasets, Y-flows recover hierarchy-aware structure, improve distributional metrics over strong flow-based baselines, and reach targets with fewer integration steps.

Introduction

Figure 1:
Blueprint of flow shapes
. Conceptually in a V-flow, the mass separates and moves from
x
along straight lines. In Y and T flows, the mass moves together and then splits into targets.

Recent advances in generative modeling are centered on the paradigm of continuous-time flows, where a velocity (vector) field is learned to transport mass from a simple prior distribution to a complex target distribution
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Lipman et al. 2022
)
. State-of-the-art approaches such as flow matching explicitly optimize straight-line trajectories, treating each data point as moving independently along its own path
(
Tong et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
. For instance, transporting a source point
x_{0}
to two distinct targets
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
is realized through two separate, non-interacting trajectories. This pattern corresponds to a V-shaped flow, since the objective provides no mechanism for trajectories to coordinate, merge, or share transport cost. One of the central problems that current V-shaped continues time approaches is aimed to solve is to reduce the number of steps to active the target. To generate the data a simulation need to be applied by solving Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE), which can be computationally expensive and often require many steps. To reduce the number of ODE steps, several techniques have been introduced, such as mean flows
(
Geng et al. 2025
)
, shortcut models
(
Frans et al. 2024
)
, Schrödinger bridges
(
Gushchin et al. 2024
)
, and
jumps

(
Holderrieth et al. 2024
)
.

But is the translation between simple and complex data distributions well-described by a uniform, V-shaped, flow? Is the V-shaped flow patter can be efficently
shortcuted
? We argue this statement, because this approces overlooking the fact that real-world data possesses hierarchical and taxonomic structures. A more natural formulation is to view generation through flow models that adapt their number of steps depending on the samples we aimed to generate. Samples in central modes of the target distribution can be reached in a few steps, whereas more diverse or peripheral samples may require additional ones. This flow would follow a common path for different points up to a mode or certain moment and then split toward the target. We propose to study different shapes of generative models that can allocate notion of mapping from the general to the specific, (see Figure
1
).

Building such generative models remains an open challenge that we aim to address. To this end, we draw inspiration from
branched
transportation theory, which is motivated by a wide range of natural and engineered structures as vascular systems, trees, river basins, and urban planning
(
Buttazzo & Stepanov 2003
;
Xia 2003
;
Bernot et al. 2005
)
. These structures arise from an economy of scale: transporting a large mass together is proportionally cheaper than splitting it into many smaller units
(
Santambrogio 2015
, Section 4.4)
. Consequently, efficient flows naturally exhibit branching geometry. Translating this principle into generative modeling suggests that trajectories should allow data points to merge toward well-represented modes and subsequently branch out to reach diverse targets. In contrast to conventional V-flows, we propose
Y-shaped generative flows
, where mass can aggregate and then adaptively separate, capturing the structure of the data.

Contribution.
We introduce
Y-shaped generative flows
, continuous-time generative models that favor shared transport before branching grounded in a
velocity–power
action which is concave in the speed and thus promotes concentration and subsequent branching of trajectories, while avoiding fractional powers of the density. Under standard bounded–density assumptions, we prove this velocity-based objective is equivalent (up to constants) to classical branched costs. Building on this, we develop a practical neural–ODE training objective that trades off the time-integrated branched action with a
Sinkhorn
boundary divergence to enforce endpoint matching, yielding a mesh-free and scalable procedure. Empirically, on synthetic, image, and single-cell datasets, our models learn branched trajectories and improve metrics (e.g., W
1
/W
2
/MMD) compared to strong flow-based baselines.

Background

Notation.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact and convex domain. We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Omega)
the space of probability measures on
\Omega
with a finite second moment. When a measure
\mu
admits a density relative to the Lebesgue measure, we denote its density by
\rho
(i.e.,
d\mu(x)=\rho(x)dx
). Vectors are column vectors,
\|\cdot\|
is the standard Euclidean norm, and
\nabla\!\cdot
is the divergence operator and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. The notation
d\mathcal{H}^{1}
represents the differential element with respect to the
1-dimensional Hausdorff measure
. In practical terms, for a curve or any 1-dimensional set in space, the measure
\mathcal{H}^{1}
simply calculates its length.

Continuous Normalizing Flow (CNF)
proposed by
Chen et al. 2018
are generative model that defines a probability density path through a Neural Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) to morph one probability distribution,
\mu_{0}
, into another,
\mu_{1}
:
\frac{d}{dt}x_{t}=v_{\theta}(x_{t},t)
, where
x_{t=0}\sim\mu_{0}.

Let
\Phi_{t}
be the flow map associated with this ODE, which transports a particle from its initial condition at time
0
to its location at time
t
. The pushforward density
\rho_{t}=(\Phi_{t})_{\#}\rho_{0}
evolving under this dynamics
necessarily
satisfies
continuity equation
with the parameterized velocity field
v_{\theta}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}v_{t})

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }\Omega\times(0,1),

(1)

\displaystyle\rho_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0},\quad\rho_{t=1}=\rho_{1}.

A key result is the instantaneous change of variables formula, which describes how the log density evolves along a trajectory:
\frac{d}{dt}\,\log\rho_{t}(x_{t})=-\,\nabla\!\cdot v_{\theta}(x_{t},t).
This allows for a likelihood calculation by integrating this quantity over time. Training can be done by directly maximizing likelihood.

Monge–Kantorovich Optimal Transport (OT)

seeks a way to morph one probability distribution with minimal effort, as quantified by a cost function
c(x,y)
. The Monge formulation seeks a deterministic map
T:\Omega\to\Omega
that pushes
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1}
and minimizes the total cost
\int c(x,T(x))d\mu_{0}(x)

(
Villani 2008
)
. This problem can be ill-posed, its relaxation, the Kantorovich problem, searches over
couplings
(joint distributions)
\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
with marginals
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
:
\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y).

For cost
c(x,y)=\|x-y\|^{2}
, the square root of the solution is the Wasserstein-2 distance.

Benamou–Brenier OT

frames transportation as a continuous-time problem. For flow
\Phi_{t}
, we have a pushforward density
\rho_{t}=(\Phi_{t})_{\#}\rho_{0}
evolving under the velocity field
v
.
The Benamou–Brenier theorem states that the squared Wasserstein distance equals the minimal
kinetic energy
of such a flow:

W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\;=\;\inf_{\rho_{t},\,v_{t}}\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\|v_{t}(x)\|^{2}\,\rho_{t}(x)\,dx\,dt\quad\text{s.t. Eq. \ref{eq:continuity}}.

(2)

The minimizers of this problem are constant-speed geodesics in the Wasserstein space. When an optimal transport map
T
exists for the static problem (e.g., when
\mu_{0}
is absolutely continuous), the geodesic is given by
interpolation
:
\rho_{t}=((1-t)\mathrm{Id}+tT)_{\#}\rho_{0}
. The corresponding velocity field is constant along the trajectories,
v_{t}(x_{t})=T(x_{0})-x_{0}
.

3
Flow Shape Control

What is the most natural way to control the shape of a generative flow?

To approach this question, we draw inspiration from the mathematical domain that studies the organization of branching structures in nature and human-made systems, such as rivers, blood vessels and traffic networks. Branched optimal transport serves as an umbrella term for a family of methods designed to capture such irrigation phenomena
(
Xia 2003
;
Brasco et al. 2011
)
. The core of all branched methods is a concave transport cost. Typically of the form
m^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\ell
, where
m
is the mass and
\ell
is the distance, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a branching coefficient. Since
(m_{1}+m_{2})^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}<m_{1}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}+m_{2}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
for
\alpha<1
, this cost structure incentivizes the masses to travel together along shared paths. Transporting a combined mass along a single path is cheaper than transporting a mass
m
along a few separate parallel paths
(
Santambrogio 2015
)
.

In one of the main branched transport formulations introduced by
Xia 2003
, the transport path is represented by a vector measure
\bm{u}
, which can be understood as a flow network. Physically,
\bm{u}
corresponds to the momentum of the flow at each point, encoding both the direction and the amount of mass being moved. Transport occurs along a network of one-dimensional paths, denoted by the set
M
, where
\theta
represents the volume of mass flowing along each path. The evolution of mass is governed by the continuity constraint
\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}
. The divergence,
\nabla\cdot u
, measures the net outflow from any point. Finally,
M^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\bm{u})
serves as the cost function that we seek to minimize:

M^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\bm{u})=\int_{M}\theta^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

(3)

So, this integral calculates the total mass moved multiplied by the distance it travels. However, this cost is highly nonconvex and defined over a complex space of singular, 1-dimensional vector measures
\bm{u}
. Directly minimizing this functional is computationally intractable.

Another perspective on branched optimal transport is the Benamou–Brenier formulation studied by
Brasco et al. 2011
, which is also equivalent to the formulation of
Xia 2003
. The idea is to consider the classical Benamou–Brenier optimal transport problem
2
, but with a
concave
cost function. With
\alpha\in(0,1)
being the branching parameter, we define the cost that measures the
work
of moving a mass
m
over a distance
\ell
, simply written as
m^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\ell
. If
\alpha=1
, the cost becomes linear in the mass (
m\ell
), recovering the classical OT setting
2
. Identifying the length
\ell
with the velocity
v
and the mass
m
with the time-dependent probability density
\rho_{t}
, the dynamic branched transport is:

B^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\;=\;\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho,\,v\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i\in I_{t}}\lVert v_{t,i}\rVert\,(\rho_{t,i})^{{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}}\,dt,\quad\text{s.t. Eq. }\ref{eq:continuity}.

(4)

In this formulation the cost
4
need to be finite, the measure
\rho(t,\cdot)
must be
purely atomic
for (almost every) time
t
! This means that the mass is concentrated at a countable set of points
\{x_{t,i}\}_{i\in I_{t}}
:

\rho(t,\cdot)=\sum_{i\in I_{t}}\rho_{t,i}\,\delta_{x_{t,i}},\text{where }\rho_{t,i}=\rho(t,\{x_{t,i}\}).

The associated momentum is then
\bm{u}=\rho v=\sum_{i\in I_{t}}v_{t,i}\,\rho_{t,i}\,\delta_{x_{t,i}}
, where
v_{t,i}=v(t,x_{t,i})
is the velocity of the atom
x_{t,i}
.

Remark 1:

Both the original formulation of
Xia 2003
and the branched Benamou–Brenier formulation
(
Brasco et al. 2011
)
are computationally impractical for high-dimensional problems. Incorporating a mass-dependent term in the cost function, especially with a sublinear exponent
\alpha<1
, significantly increases computational complexity and limits scalability.

This formulation is computationally intensive, which motivates our development of a more efficient relaxation.
Before presenting our main approach, it is instructive to extract the core principle from branched optimal transport that addresses the question posed at the beginning of this section.

Takeaway
: To control the shape of flows, branched optimal transport use
m^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\ell
, where
m
is the mass and
\ell
is the distance, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a branching coefficient. Since
(m_{1}+m_{2})^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}<m_{1}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}+m_{2}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
for
\alpha<1
, this cost structure incentivizes the masses to travel together along shared paths
(
Santambrogio 2015
)
. This is very useful idea, but running these methods in practice is often not feasible in the continuous case.

4
Method

To address the challenge of complex density–optimization in branched generative models, we present a simple alternative: place the nonlinearity on the
velocity
rather than on the
mass
. We start from the standard
flux
-based
\bm{u}=\rho_{t}v_{t}
branched formulation. The dynamic branched optimal transport (Benamou–Brenier style) minimizes

\inf_{\bm{u}}\;\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\lVert\bm{u}(x,t)\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dx\,dt\quad\text{s.t. Eq. }\ref{eq:continuity}

(5)

where
0<\alpha<1
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
When the density is strictly positive a.e., we write the flux in velocity form
\bm{u}=\rho\,v,v:\Omega\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d},
and the integrand in equation
5
becomes

\lVert\bm{u}\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}=(\rho\,\lVert v\rVert)^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}=\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,\lVert v\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}.

(6)

We propose to avoid fractional powers of the density by moving the sublinear power to the velocity while keeping the density linear. Concretely, our Y–shaped flow formula is:

\boxed{\mathcal{Y}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})\;=\;\inf_{\rho,v}\;\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho(x,t)\,\lVert v(x,t)\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dx\,dt\quad\text{s.t. Eq. }\ref{eq:continuity},\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}\in(0,1).}

(7)

This is our simple formulation that can produce various interesting properties that regular generative flows cannot. Below, we answer a range of questions to justify this objective choice.

Why this formulation produces branching?
For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the flux integrand equation
6
and the velocity integrand in equation
7
differ by the factor
\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}
:

\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\lVert v\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}=\big(\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\big)\cdot\big(\rho\lVert v\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\big).

If the density is uniformly bounded away from zero and above,
0<m\leq\rho(x,t)\leq M<\infty
a.e., then the two integrands are comparable up to constants; the following lemma makes this precise.

Lemma 1
(Equivalence under bounded density).

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and suppose
\rho(x,t)\in[m,M]
for a.e.
(x,t)
with
0<m\leq M<\infty
. Then

M^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho\,\lVert v\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dx\,dt\;\leq\;\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,\lVert v\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dx\,dt\;\leq\;m^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho\,\lVert v\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dx\,dt.

(8)

Under the mild assumption
m\leq\rho\leq M
, optimizing
\int\rho\lVert v\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
is equivalent, up to fixed constants depending only on
m,M,\alpha
, to optimizing
\int\lVert\bm{u}\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
. Thus our velocity-based model preserves the geometry of the branched formulation while avoiding powers of
\rho
. For proofs see Appendix
1
.

Without bounded density assumption, under the only
velocity
-based objective, instantaneous merging velocity does not by itself reduce the local integrand (unlike the pure-flux model
5
); rather, Y/T structures emerge from the
global
trade-off between path length and magnitude time-compression (presented in the next paragraph). As velocity penalizes speed sublinearly, this creates sparsity in
v
: the optimizer concentrates motion into a few high‑speed corridors and keeps
v\simeq 0
elsewhere.

For two targets, a single deterministic vector field cannot send mass in two directions at the same space-time point. A compact source must first move together along a trunk; the sublinear cost then pushes this common motion to occur as fast as allowed. After the cloud has spatially separated, the field splits into short arms to reach each target.

Why
v^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
can achieve the target in fewer steps?

The cheapest way under a sublinear power is to use
largest allowable speed on the smallest possible support
. With
v^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
, the cost can always be reduced by executing it faster. To show this formally, we provide the following time-compression lemma:

Lemma 2
(Time-compression).

Let
(\rho,v)
be feasible on
[0,1]
. For
s\in(0,1]
, define on
t\in[0,s]

\rho_{s}(t,x)=\rho(t/s,x),\qquad v_{s}(t,x)=\frac{1}{s}\,v(t/s,x).

Then
(\rho_{s},v_{s})
is feasible from
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1}
on
[0,s]
and, for
0<\alpha<1
,

\mathcal{Y}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho_{s},v_{s})=s^{\,1-{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,\mathcal{Y}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho,v).

The proof is given in Appendix
2
. When transport occurs over a fixed-short time window, the optimizer may prefer to perform rapid transport along narrow channels. Let us discretize the time interval
[0,1]
into
K
steps with step size
\Delta t
.
Along any straight corridor, let the per-step displacements be non-negative scalars
u_{1},\dots,u_{K}
summing to a fixed total
U
(representing the same net motion).
For
0<\alpha<1
, the following inequality holds:

\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{k}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\geq U^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}},
with equality if and only if all displacement occurs in a single step.
Consequently, the discrete objective
\sum_{k}\Delta t\int\rho\|v_{k}\|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}

is minimized by concentrating movement into as few steps as possible, subject to feasibility and any speed or regularity bounds.

If
\alpha>1
If does the flow reduce to V‑shaped behavior?

When
\alpha>1
, the map
s\mapsto s^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
is convex. The objective

\int\rho\,\lVert v\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
becomes convex in
v
, the time-rescaling prefactor reverses
(
\mathcal{Y}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho_{s},v_{s})=s^{1-\alpha}\mathcal{Y}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho,v)
now
increases
as
s\downarrow 0
),
and Jensen’s inequality gives the
opposite
local merging relation:

(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})^{1-\alpha}q^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\leq\rho_{1}^{1-\alpha}q_{1}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}+\rho_{2}^{1-\alpha}q_{2}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
.
Thus there is no sparsifying pressure on the velocity magnitude; optimal solutions avoid large peaks in
\lVert v\rVert
and do not gain from compressing time. Geometrically, the flow is
non-branching
(as in Benamou–Brenier with
\alpha=2
), and in the two-sources/one-sink toy problem the optimal pattern is the
V
: two straight arms that meet only at the sink. In this sense, for
\alpha>1
our method collapses to the usual non-branched OT behavior.

How stable is the resulted solution?

The potential issue is that in the limit our objective can resemble instantaneous motion along a network
jumps
, unless one adds a small temporal or spatial smoothness regularizer (e.g., a Sobolev penalty on
v
), as it is done in Modica-Mortola
20
:

\inf_{\rho,v}\;\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho(x,t)\,\lVert v(x,t)\rVert^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dx\,dt+\lambda\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho(x,t)\,\lVert\nabla v(x,t)\rVert^{2}_{F}\,dx\,dt\quad\text{s.t. Eq. }\ref{eq:continuity},\quad{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}\in(0,1).

(9)

Remark 2:

We did experiments with this formulation too. But, in practice, we did not encounter any stability issues with learning, but adding a smoothness penalty
\|\nabla\bm{v}(x,t)\|_{F}^{2}
significantly increases computation time due to the Jacobian computation. Therefore, we avoided it in our formulation.

5
Practical Implementation

We now describe a simple and scalable training scheme based on neural ODEs and a Monte Carlo (MC) approximation of the velocity–power action. Throughout this section we
fix the time horizon to
[0,1]
(to avoid time–compression pathologies; see App. C) and use the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. We parameterize the velocity field by a neural network
v_{\theta}:\Omega\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and evolve by the ODE

\frac{dx_{t}}{dt}\;=\;v_{\theta}(x_{t},t),\qquad x_{t=0}\sim\mu_{0}.

(10)

Let
\Phi_{t}
denote the flow map induced by equation
10
. The time-
t
distribution of particles is the pushforward
\rho_{t}=(\Phi_{t})_{\#}\mu_{0}
; i.e., if
x_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
then
x_{t}=\Phi_{t}(x_{0})
is a draw from
\rho_{t}
. This simple fact justifies using
equal-weight
particles to approximate expectations under
\rho_{t}
: no importance weights are introduced or needed. The (velocity–power) action we minimize is

Y^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\theta)\;=\;\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho_{t}}\!\left[\;\|v_{\theta}(x,t)\|_{2}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\right]dt\qquad\text{with }{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}\in(0,1).

(11)

Time discretization and MC estimator.

Let
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{K}=1
be a time grid with steps
\Delta t_{k}=t_{k}-t_{k-1}
(uniform case:
\Delta t_{k}\equiv\Delta t=1/K
). For a minibatch
\{x_{0}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mu_{0}
, we integrate equation
10
along the grid to obtain states
x_{t_{k}}^{(i)}=\Phi_{t_{k}}(x_{0}^{(i)})
. A consistent Riemann estimator of equation
11
is then

\widehat{Y}^{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}(\theta)\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Delta t_{k}\;\Big\|\,v_{\theta}\!\big(x_{t_{k-1}}^{(i)},\,t_{k-1}\big)\Big\|_{2}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}.

(12)

In the common uniform grid case, this equals
\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|v_{\theta}(x_{t_{k-1}}^{(i)},t_{k-1})\|_{2}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
, making the hidden
\Delta t=1/K
explicit. If an adaptive ODE solver is used, we evaluate on its (possibly non‑uniform) internal time points and accumulate the corresponding
\Delta t_{k}
in equation
12
. Under exact integration, independence of
\{x_{0}^{(i)}\}
, and mesh refinement (
N,K\to\infty
),
\widehat{J}_{\alpha}(\theta)\to Y^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\theta)
by the law of large numbers and Riemann convergence.

To enforce
\rho_{t=1}=\mu_{1}
, we add a differentiable boundary loss that compares the empirical law of final particles to
\mu_{1}
. Given mini‑batches
X_{1}=\{x_{t=1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{X}}
(pushforward samples) and
Y=\{y^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N_{Y}}\sim\mu_{1}
, we use the (bias‑corrected) entropic Sinkhorn divergence with cost
c(x,y)=\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{sinkhorn}}(\theta)\;=\;S_{\varepsilon}\!\big(\hat{\mu}_{X_{1}},\,\hat{\mu}_{Y}\big)\;:=\;\operatorname{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\mu}_{X_{1}},\hat{\mu}_{Y})-\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\mu}_{X_{1}},\hat{\mu}_{X_{1}})-\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{OT}_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\mu}_{Y},\hat{\mu}_{Y}),

(13)

where
\operatorname{OT}_{\varepsilon}
is the entropic OT cost computed by Sinkhorn iterations (regularization
\varepsilon>0
, a fixed number of iterations, and the same ground cost
c
for all three terms). Our final loss is:

Related Work

Branching transport problems were studied in discrete settings.
Classical approaches include the Euclidean Steiner Tree (ESTP), where branch points are optimized
geometrically under flow-independent costs, and early geometric constructions for single-source
branched optimal transport (BOT)
(
Buttazzo & Stepanov 2003
;
Bernot et al. 2005
;
Maddalena et al. 2003
)
. More recently,
(
Lippmann et al. 2022
)
introduced an approximate solver that decouples topology search from geometry optimization: given a tree topology, branch point positions are optimized via leaf-elimination in
O(nd)
time, while a edge-rewiring procedure explores the topology. Their method works only for
discrete
settings.

Beyond discrete graph solvers, continuous stochastic frameworks have also been proposed.
Tang et al. 2025
extend Schrödinger Bridge Matching (BSBM) to the
branched
case by learning diverging drift and growth fields that transport mass from a common source to multiple targets. This method produces continuous branched trajectories, but is not based on branched transport theory.

The ground truth continues branched transport was proposed by
Oudet & Santambrogio 2011
. This method has been shown to provide convergence to branched transport solutions in two dimensions. The formulation is a theoretically grounded approximation inspired by the Modica–Mortola framework in phase-field modeling
(
Oudet & Santambrogio 2011
)
. It replaces the singular, non-smooth branched transport problem with a sequence of regularized, elliptic energy functionals
M_{\epsilon}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
defined over the more regular space of
\mathcal{H}^{1}
vector fields. For a given
\bm{u}(x)
, the approximating functional is:

M_{\epsilon}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\bm{u})=\epsilon^{\gamma_{1}}\int_{\Omega}\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}dx+\epsilon^{\gamma_{2}}\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\bm{u}(x)\|_{F}^{2}dx,

(15)

where
\epsilon>0
is a small parameter. The exponents
\alpha
,
\gamma_{1}
, and
\gamma_{2}
are derived directly from the transport dimension
d
and the cost exponent
\alpha
. We implemented this method; For further technical details, we refer the reader to Appendix
B
. But we found it
unstable
and not possible to use for real-world data.

9
Conclusion

We introduced Y‑shaped generative flows, a continuous‑time framework that rewards mass sharing before branching by minimizing a velocity–power action.
We showed that, under bounded‑density conditions, this objective is equivalent up to constants to classical flux‑power costs, thereby importing the geometry of branched transport into the velocity domain while avoiding fractional powers of density. A time‑compression result further explains why, in practice, our flows can reach targets in fewer integration steps, as sublinear penalties prefer fast motion along narrow corridors.
Empirically, Y‑flows learn meaningful trunks and branches on synthetic mixtures, respect surface constraints on LiDAR terrains, capture lineage structure in single‑cell data, and improve distributional metrics over strong flow baselines. Also our method matching targets with significantly fewer steps in a high‑dimensional FFHQ latent translation task. Future work includes stronger comparisons with state‑of‑the‑art diffusion models on standard image metrics. We believe the ability to shape flows—from V to Y/T—opens a path toward generative models that adapt computation to structure, moving from the general to the specific along shared, interpretable pathways.

Background (Extended)

Continuous Normalizing Flow

A Continuous Normalizing Flow (CNF)
Chen et al. 2018
is a generative model that defines a probability density path through a Neural Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE):

\frac{d}{dt}x_{t}=v_{\theta}(x_{t},t),\qquad x_{t=0}\sim\mu_{0}.

(16)

Let
\Phi_{t}
be the flow map associated with this ODE, which transports a particle from its initial condition at time
0
to its location at time
t
. The pushforward density
\rho_{t}=(\Phi_{t})_{\#}\rho_{0}
evolving under this dynamics
necessarily
satisfies the
continuity equation
with the parameterized velocity field
v_{\theta}
. Where continuity equation encodes the law of mass conservation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}v_{t})

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }\Omega\times(0,1),

\displaystyle\rho_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0},\quad\rho_{t=1}=\rho_{1}.

A key result is the instantaneous change of variables formula, which describes how the log density evolves along a trajectory:

\frac{d}{dt}\,\log\rho_{t}(x_{t})=-\,\nabla\!\cdot v_{\theta}(x_{t},t).

(17)

This allows for a likelihood calculation by integrating this quantity over time. Training can be done by directly maximizing likelihood (integrating equation
17
).

Flow Matching.
The core idea of Flow Matching (FM)
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Tong et al. 2023
)
is to train a CNF by directly regressing its velocity field
v_{\theta}
toward a target vector field
u_{t}
that generates a desired probability path. The Flow Matching objective is:
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],\,x\sim\rho_{t}}\!\left[\|v_{\theta}(x,t)-u_{t}(x)\|^{2}\right]
.

A critical challenge is that sampling
x\sim\rho_{t}
from the marginal path at arbitrary times is typically intractable. Conditional Flow Matching (CFM)
(
Lipman et al. 2022
)
provides a solution by constructing the marginal path as a mixture of simpler and tractable conditional paths. Let
z
be a conditioning variable with distribution
q(z)
. We define the marginal path as follows:
\rho_{t}(x)=\int\rho_{t}(x\mid z)q(z)\,dz
, where each conditional path
\rho_{t}(x|z)
is generated by a corresponding conditional vector field
u_{t}(x\mid z)
. The marginal field
u_{t}(x)
that generates
\rho_{t}
is then given by:

u_{t}(x)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q(z\mid x)}\!\left[u_{t}(x\mid z)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q}\!\left[\frac{\rho_{t}(x\mid z)}{\rho_{t}(x)}\,u_{t}(x\mid z)\right],

(18)

where
q(z\mid x)
is the posterior. The key theorem is to minimize the following
Conditional Flow Matching
objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],\,z\sim q,\,x\sim\rho_{t}(\cdot\mid z)}\!\left[\|v_{\theta}(x,t)-u_{t}(x\mid z)\|^{2}\right]

(19)

yields the same gradient for
\theta
as minimizing the intractable
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta)
in equation
. This makes CFM a practical objective, as it only requires sampling from conditional paths
\rho_{t}(x|z)
and knowing their closed-form drifts
u_{t}(x|z)
.

The flexibility of CFM lies in the choice of conditional paths. The coupling
q(z)
is the independent joint distribution
q(x_{0})q(x_{1})
, so
z=(x_{0},x_{1})
. A common conditional path is a Gaussian bridge:
\rho_{t}(x\mid z)=\mathcal{N}(x\mid\mu_{t},\sigma^{2}I)
, where
\mu_{t}=(1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1}
is a linear interpolation. The simple constant drift that generates this path is
u_{t}(x\mid z)=x_{1}-x_{0}
. The optimal transport CFM (OT-CFM)
(
Tong et al. 2023
)
method uses an optimal coupling to define conditionals. Here,
z=(x_{0},x_{1})
is sampled from an OT plan
\pi
between
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}

2
. In practice, the OT plan
\pi
is efficiently approximated using mini-batch OT, which has been shown to work well empirically.

Appendix B
Modica-Mortola Branched Flows

Figure 6:
Modica-Mortola Discrete solver. Neural network-based field parametrization.

Oudet & Santambrogio 2011
proposed a theoretically-grounded approximation inspired by the Modica-Mortola framework in phase-field modeling. Using this, we can replace the singular problem with a sequence of regularized, elliptic energy functionals
M_{\epsilon}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
defined over the more regular space of
\mathcal{H}^{1}
vector fields. For a given vector field
\bm{u}(x)
, the approximating functional is:

M_{\epsilon}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\bm{u})=\epsilon^{\gamma_{1}}\int_{\Omega}\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}dx+\epsilon^{\gamma_{2}}\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\bm{u}(x)\|_{F}^{2}dx,

(20)

where
\epsilon>0
is a small parameter. The exponents
\alpha
,
\gamma_{1}
, and
\gamma_{2}
are derived directly from the transport dimension
d
and the cost exponent
\alpha
. The functional in Equation
20
consists of two competing terms:

1.

A concave potential term
,
\int\|\bm{u}\|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}dx
, which encourages the magnitude of the vector field to be either zero or arbitrarily large. This behavior is analogous to a double-well potential acting on zero and infinity, promoting the formation of sparse, high-intensity pathways.

2.

A Dirichlet energy term (Sobolev penalty)
,
\int\|\nabla\bm{u}\|_{F}^{2}dx
, which penalizes spatial variations in the vector field, enforcing smoothness and regularity.

The central theoretical guarantee of this approach is that, as
\epsilon\to 0
, the functionals
M_{\epsilon}^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}

\Gamma
-converge
to the original branched transport energy
M^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
. This ensures that the minimizers of the tractable, regularized problem (Eq.
20
) converge to a minimizer of the original, intractable branched transport problem. Our work leverages this variational principle, reformulating it within a modern deep learning framework.

Although theoretically elegant, solving this problem and enforcing constraint
\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}
, in a numerical simulation is highly challenging. It typically requires complex, grid-based numerical methods, such as projecting the gradient of the energy functional onto a divergence-free space at each optimization step, which is ill-suited.

For practical implementation of this method we considered the following
energy loss

\mathcal{L}_{\text{energy}}
is the time-integrated version of the functional in Equation
20
, evaluated along the particle trajectories:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{energy}}=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\mu_{0}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left(\epsilon^{\gamma_{1}}\|\bm{v}_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}+\epsilon^{\gamma_{2}}\|\nabla_{x}\bm{v}_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\|_{F}^{2}\right)dt\right].

(21)

Our objective is to find the network parameters
\theta
that minimize the total cost, which combines the time-integrated branched transport energy with a term that enforces the endpoint distribution constraint. The total loss is a Monte Carlo estimate of:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\lambda_{E}\mathcal{L}_{\text{energy}}+\lambda_{S}\mathcal{L}_{\text{sinkhorn}}.

(22)

The spatial integral is approximated by taking the expectation over mini-batches sampled from the source distribution
\mu_{0}
, while the time integral is approximated via the discrete sum over the Euler integration steps. The Jacobian term
\|\nabla_{x}\bm{v}_{\theta}\|_{F}^{2}
is computed efficiently using automatic differentiation.

The
Sinkhorn loss

\mathcal{L}_{\text{sinkhorn}}
is a differentiable measure of divergence between the distribution of transported particles,
(x_{1})_{\#}\mu_{0}
, and the target distribution
\mu_{1}
. This term enforces the boundary condition of the transport problem, analogous to the divergence constraint in the original formulation. We use a Sinkhorn divergence with entropy regularization, which provides a stable and efficient objective for matching the terminal and target distributions.

By minimizing the combined loss
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
using stochastic gradient descent, our method learns a velocity field that generates transport paths emulating the branched, tree-like structures characteristic of the problem, effectively solving the variational approximation in a mesh-free, generative setting.

Lemma 1

(Equivalence under bounded density)
.

Let the exponent
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Suppose the density

\rho(x,t)
is bounded such that for constants
m,M
:

0<m\leq\rho(x,t)\leq M<\infty

Then, the following inequality holds:

M^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{\Omega}\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dxdt\leq\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{\Omega}\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dxdt\leq m^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{\Omega}\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dxdt

Proof.

The proof relies on the properties of power functions with negative exponents. Since we are given that
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the exponent
\alpha-1
is
negative
:

0<\alpha<1\implies\alpha-1<0

For a negative exponent
p
, the function
f(y)=y^{p}
is a
decreasing function
for
y>0
.
We start with the given bounds on
\rho
:

m\leq\rho\leq M

Applying the decreasing function
y\mapsto y^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}
reverses the inequality:

M^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\leq\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\leq m^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}

The term
\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
is always non-negative, since
\rho\geq m>0
and
|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\geq 0
. Multiplying the inequality by this term preserves its direction:

M^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\cdot(\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}})\leq\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\cdot(\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}})\leq m^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\cdot(\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}})

Simplifying the middle term gives:

\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\cdot\rho^{1}|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}=\rho^{(\alpha-1)+1}|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}=\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}

This yields the pointwise inequality relating the integrands:

M^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\leq\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\leq m^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}

By the monotonicity of integration, we can integrate the entire pointwise inequality over the domain
\Omega\times[0,1]
:

\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{\Omega}M^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dxdt\leq\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{\Omega}\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dxdt\leq\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{\Omega}m^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dxdt

Finally, since
M^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}
and
m^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}
are constants, we can factor them out of the integrals to arrive at the final proposition.

M^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{\Omega}\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dxdt\leq\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{\Omega}\rho^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dxdt\leq m^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}-1}\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{\Omega}\rho|v|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dxdt

∎

Thus, under bounded density, both formulations encourage shared transport corridors with similar geometry

Lemma 2

(Time-compression)
.

Let
(\rho,v)
be any feasible pair on
[0,1]
for the continuity equation with boundary data

\rho(0,\cdot)=\mu_{0},\ \rho(1,\cdot)=\mu_{1}
.
For
s\in(0,1]
define, on the shortened horizon
[0,s]
,

\rho_{s}(t,x):=\rho(t/s,x),\qquad v_{s}(t,x):=\tfrac{1}{s}\,v(t/s,x).

Then
(\rho_{s},v_{s})
is feasible on
[0,s]
with the same boundary data, and

Y^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho_{s},v_{s})=s^{\,1-\alpha}\,Y^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho,v).

In particular, for
0<\alpha<1
the action strictly decreases under time compression
s<1
.

Proof.

Feasibility: for
t\in(0,s)
set
u=t/s
. By the chain rule and product rule,

\partial_{t}\rho_{s}(t,x)=\tfrac{1}{s}\,\partial_{u}\rho(u,x),\qquad\nabla\!\cdot(\rho_{s}v_{s})=\nabla\!\cdot\!\Big(\rho(u,x)\,\tfrac{1}{s}v(u,x)\Big)=\tfrac{1}{s}\,\nabla\!\cdot(\rho v)(u,x).

Since
(\rho,v)
solves
\partial_{u}\rho+\nabla\!\cdot(\rho v)=0
, the same holds for
(\rho_{s},v_{s})
.
Also
\rho_{s}(0,\cdot)=\rho(0,\cdot)=\mu_{0}
and
\rho_{s}(s,\cdot)=\rho(1,\cdot)=\mu_{1}
.

Action scaling:

\displaystyle Y^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho_{s},v_{s})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho(t/s,x)\,\big\|\tfrac{1}{s}v(t/s,x)\big\|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho(u,x)\,s^{-\alpha}\|v(u,x)\|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dx\,(s\,du)

\displaystyle=s^{\,1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\rho(u,x)\,\|v(u,x)\|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\,dx\,du=s^{\,1-\alpha}\,Y^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho,v).\qed

Lemma 3

(Well-posedness in the neural-ODE setting)
.

Let
v_{\theta}:\Omega\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be measurable in
t
and globally
L
-Lipschitz in
x
, uniformly in
t
.
Let
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
be absolutely continuous. Then:

(a)

For each
\theta
, the ODE
x^{\prime}(t)=v_{\theta}(x(t),t)
generates a unique flow map

\Phi^{\theta}_{t}
on
[0,1]
, and
\rho^{\theta}_{t}:=(\Phi^{\theta}_{t})_{\#}\rho_{0}
solves

\partial_{t}\rho^{\theta}+\nabla\!\cdot(\rho^{\theta}v_{\theta})=0
in the weak sense.

(b)

Suppose there exist integrable envelopes
R,G\geq 0
with

0\leq\rho^{\theta}(x,t)\leq R(x,t)
and
\|v_{\theta}(x,t)\|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}\leq G(x,t)
a.e. for all
\theta
,
and let
\theta_{n}\to\theta
be such that

\rho^{\theta_{n}}\to\rho^{\theta}
and
v_{\theta_{n}}\to v_{\theta}
a.e. Then

Y^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho^{\theta_{n}},v_{\theta_{n}})\ \longrightarrow\ Y^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho^{\theta},v_{\theta}).

Proof.

(a) Existence and uniqueness follow from Picard–Lindelöf under global
x
-Lipschitz continuity of
v_{\theta}
.
The pushforward identity and weak continuity equation are standard: for
\varphi\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega)
,

t\mapsto\int\varphi\,d\rho^{\theta}_{t}=\int\varphi(\Phi_{t}^{\theta}(x))\,d\rho_{0}(x)
is absolutely continuous with

\frac{d}{dt}\int\varphi\,d\rho^{\theta}_{t}=\int\nabla\varphi(\Phi_{t}^{\theta}(x))\cdot v_{\theta}(\Phi_{t}^{\theta}(x),t)\,d\rho_{0}(x)=\int\nabla\varphi(x)\cdot v_{\theta}(x,t)\,d\rho^{\theta}_{t}(x),

which is the weak form of
\partial_{t}\rho^{\theta}+\nabla\!\cdot(\rho^{\theta}v_{\theta})=0
.

(b) Set
f_{n}:=\rho^{\theta_{n}}\|v_{\theta_{n}}\|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
and
f:=\rho^{\theta}\|v_{\theta}\|^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}
.
By hypothesis
0\leq f_{n}\leq RG
with
RG\in L^{1}(\Omega\times[0,1])
, and
f_{n}\to f
a.e.
Dominated convergence yields
\int f_{n}\to\int f
, i.e.
Y^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho^{\theta_{n}},v_{\theta_{n}})\to Y^{{\color[rgb]{1,0.39,0.13}\alpha}}(\rho^{\theta},v_{\theta})
.
∎

D.1
LiDAR

Airborne LiDAR
Liu et al. 2023
terrain tile with ground and low-vegetation; each sample is a 3D point
(x,y,z)
with optional attributes (intensity, return number, total returns, scan angle, class). After filtering non-ground classes, removing outliers with a radius/k-NN check, and Poisson-disk (or voxel-grid) subsampling, we retain 5k points that preserve ridges, slopes, and basins. Coordinates are centered and scaled to a unit box (optionally PCA-aligned). We estimate per-point normals via local PCA and build a k-NN surface graph
(k\in[8,16])
with Euclidean edge weights as a geodesic proxy; during optimization, states are projected back to the nearest tangent patch to stay on-manifold. A single source distribution sits on a lower slope; four target clouds are disjoint regions on ridges/basins (indices provided for reproducibility).
