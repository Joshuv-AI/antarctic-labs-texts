Changes in the Epidemiology of Pediatric Olfactory Dysfunction Before and After COVID‐19 Pandemic
Authors: Yuji Kishimoto, Eri Mori, Rinko Sakurai, Hirotaka Tanaka, Monami Nagai, Rumi Sekine, Masayoshi Tei, Nobuyoshi Otori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe COVID‐19 pandemic may have altered the epidemiology and clinical features of pediatric olfactory dysfunction, but data focusing on children remain limited, particularly regarding etiological changes and qualitative symptoms such as parosmia. This study investigated temporal changes in pediatric presentations at a tertiary smell clinic.Methodology/PrincipalWe retrospectively reviewed patients younger than 18 years who visited our clinic for olfactory dysfunction between January 2009 and December 2024. Patients were divided into pre‐pandemic (January 2009–March 2020) and post‐pandemic (April 2020–December 2024) cohorts. Etiology was determined using clinical history, imaging, nasal endoscopy, and psychophysical and subjective olfactory tests.ResultsThe proportion of pediatric patients among all patients with olfactory dysfunction increased significantly from 1.5% (35/2322) in the pre‐pandemic cohort to 4.7% (48/1022) in the post‐pandemic cohort (p < 0.01), with a corresponding decrease in adults. Etiological distribution shifted significantly (p < 0.01): olfactory cleft stenosis predominated in pre‐pandemic, whereas upper respiratory tract infection became the leading cause in the post‐pandemic cohort, 76% of cases of which related to COVID‐19. Parosmia was observed exclusively in post‐COVID‐19 pediatric patients. Subjective olfactory scores were higher with post‐pandemic patients, indicating more mildly perceived dysfunction, while psychophysical test results showed no significant differences.ConclusionsAfter the COVID‐19 pandemic, pediatric olfactory dysfunction became more frequent and was predominantly associated with post‐infectious causes, particularly COVID‐19, with parosmia emerging as a characteristic feature. Greater clinical awareness and pediatric‐specific diagnostic standards will be needed.Level of EvidenceLevel 3.Keywords: COVID‐19, epidemiology, olfactory dysfunction, parosmia, pediatric

Introduction

Olfaction plays an essential role in childhood development. In the early stage of life, it contributes to flavor learning through breast milk and supports the formation of long‐term dietary preferences [1]. It also facilitates mother–infant bonding, modulates emotional responses such as stress and pain, and provides a foundation for multisensory perception, including visual development [2]. During school age and adolescence, olfaction further supports social cognition, including recognition of peers and sensitivity to body odors, underscoring its importance for quality of life and normal developmental trajectories [2].

Despite its developmental significance, pediatric olfactory dysfunction has received limited attention in rhinology practice. Its reported prevalence (1.6%–2.3%) [3, 4], is substantially lower than that of adults (22.2%) [5], and its etiological profile differs markedly. While chronic rhinosinusitis is the leading cause in adults, pediatric olfactory dysfunction is more often associated with congenital anomalies, head trauma, or adenoidal hypertrophy [4, 6]. As a result, pediatric cases have traditionally been considered rare and peripheral in specialized olfactory clinics.

Since 2020, however, the COVID‐19 pandemic fundamentally altered the clinical landscape of olfactory dysfunction. COVID‐19‐related olfactory dysfunction shows an inverse association with age [6, 7], raising the possibility that children and adolescents are particularly susceptible. In daily rhinology practice, olfactory dysfunction has become common complaints rather than incidental findings, yet epidemiological data focusing on pediatric populations—especially regarding etiological changes and qualitative symptoms such as parosmia—remain scarce.

This gap is clinically relevant. Parosmia, a hallmark symptom of post‐infectious olfactory dysfunction in adults, has received little attention in children, where it may be under‐recognized or difficult to articulate. Moreover, it remains unclear whether the pandemic has altered not only the frequency but also the clinical phenotype of pediatric olfactory dysfunction, potentially necessitating revised diagnostic awareness and follow‐up strategies in general ENT practice.

Against this background, the present study aimed to: (1) evaluate changes in the proportion of pediatric patients among all individuals presenting olfactory dysfunction at a tertiary smell clinic, (2) compare etiological distributions before and after the COVID‐19 pandemic, and (3) examine the occurrence of COVID‐19‐related olfactory dysfunction and parosmia in children. By clarifying these changes, this study seeks to reframe pediatric olfactory dysfunction as an emerging and clinically relevant entity in the post‐COVID era.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

This retrospective study was approved by the ethics committee of The Jikei University School of Medicine (approval number: 35‐279). As the study involved only a review of medical records, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. All patients or their guardians were provided with the opportunity to opt out of use of their clinical data.

Patient Selection

We retrospectively reviewed medical records of patients younger than 18 years old who visited our tertiary‐level olfactory clinic with a primary complaint of olfactory dysfunction between January 2009 and December 2024. For reference, data from all patients—regardless of age—who attended the clinic during the same period were also collected to calculate the proportion of pediatric cases. Patients were categorized into two cohorts according to the timing of their first visit: a pre‐pandemic (January 2009–March 2020) cohort and a post‐pandemic (April 2020–December 2024) cohort.

Diagnosis of Olfactory Dysfunction

Etiological diagnoses were established based on comprehensive clinical evaluation—demographic data, detailed history of both quantitative and qualitative olfactory dysfunction, and so on—laboratory findings, imaging studies (computed tomography [CT] and magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]), nasal endoscopy, and psychophysical olfactory testing. For imaging evaluation, CT was performed in all patients. In cases with no apparent abnormal findings on CT but in whom congenital or central lesions were suspected based on the medical history, additional MRI was performed.

T&T olfactometry scores were used to diagnose hyposmia and anosmia: scores of 1.2 to 5.4 were classified as hyposmia, and scores of 5.6 or higher were classified as anosmia [8]. Parosmia is defined as a distorted odor perception, typically perceived as unpleasant [9], and was assessed through structured clinical interviews conducted by board‐certified otolaryngologists. Parosmia was assessed based on the patient's response to the question, “Have you noticed any previously familiar odors that smell different from how you perceived them before?”

CT and MRI studies were used to support etiological classification, particularly for identifying olfactory cleft stenosis, sinonasal disease, and congenital anomalies. Based on the criteria outlined in the second edition of the Japanese Guideline for the Diagnosis of Olfactory Disorders [8], participants were classified into the following etiological categories: upper respiratory tract infection (URTI), head trauma, sinonasal disease, congenital anomalies, allergic rhinitis, idiopathic causes, and olfactory cleft stenosis.

Olfactory cleft stenosis was diagnosed on coronal CT images when, on at least one slice on each side of the bilateral olfactory clefts, the nasal septum and turbinate were in contact over at least one‐third of the vertical length of the olfactory cleft [10].

Olfactory Examination

T&T Olfactometry (T&T)

T&T assesses both detection and recognition thresholds using five standardized odorants, each presented in 7–8 graded concentrations from weak to strong. For each participant, detection and recognition thresholds were recorded, and the mean threshold across the five odorants was calculated. Recognition thresholds were used to classify the severity of olfactory dysfunction as normal (−2.0 to 1.0), mild (1.2 to 2.4), moderate (2.6 to 4.0), severe (4.2 to 5.4), or anosmia (5.6 to 5.8) [8]. In this test, lower threshold values indicate better olfactory function.

Open Essence (OE)

OE is a card‐type odor identification test validated in Japan [11]. When the card is opened, microcapsulated odorants are released and sniffed by patients, who then select one answer from six options: four specific odor choices, “unidentifiable,” or “odorless.” Based on our previous study, the cut‐off scores distinguishing normal from olfactory dysfunction in children are 5 for ages of 6–9 years, 6 for ages of 10–14 years, and 7 for ages of 15–18 years [12].

Questionnaire

Visual Analog Scale (VAS)

Subjective olfactory function was assessed using a VAS consisting of a 100‐mm horizontal line, with anchors labeled “no sense of smell” on the left and “normal sense of smell” on the right. Participants were asked to indicate the point that best represented their current olfactory perception. The distance in millimeters from the left end to the marked point was recorded as VAS score [13].

Self‐Administered Odor Questionnaire (SAOQ)

The SAOQ is a self‐reporting questionnaire developed in Japan to assess subjective olfactory ability in daily life. It includes items related to the recognition of common environmental odors (e.g., food, garbage, gas) and the impact of olfactory dysfunction on daily routine activities. Responses are scored on a Likert‐type scale, with higher total scores indicating better perceived olfactory function [14].

The SAOQ has been validated in Japanese clinical populations and serves as a complementary tool to objective olfactory tests such as T&T and OE.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using EZR (version 2.8‐0) [15]. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact test. Continuous variables, including age, T&T thresholds, OE scores, VAS scores, and SAOQ scores, were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Proportion of Pediatric Patients With Olfactory Dysfunction

Among all patients presenting with olfactory dysfunction during the study period, the proportion of pediatric patients—defined as the number of pediatric patients seen during the study period divided by the total number of patients seen during the same period—increased significantly from 1.5% (35/2322) in the pre‐pandemic period to 4.7% (48/1022) in the post‐pandemic period (p < 0.01). Etiology of Olfactory Dysfunction

The etiological distribution of pediatric olfactory dysfunction differed markedly between the two periods (Table 3, Figure 1). In the pre‐pandemic cohort, olfactory cleft stenosis was the most common etiology, followed by congenital disorders and URTI. In contrast, in the post‐pandemic cohort, URTI emerged as the predominant cause, with COVID‐19‐related olfactory dysfunction accounting for the majority of cases. This shift in etiological distribution was statistically significant (p < 0.01).

Discussion

In the present study, we presented three key findings: (1) a significant increase in the proportion of pediatric patients presenting with olfactory dysfunction after the COVID‐19 pandemic, (2) a notable shift in etiological patterns from olfactory cleft stenosis to URTI, particularly COVID‐19‐related infection, and (3) the exclusive occurrence of parosmia in pediatric patients with post‐COVID‐19 olfactory dysfunction. Together, these findings highlight how the pandemic has reshaped the clinical landscape of pediatric olfactory disorders in rhinology practice. Pediatric olfactory disorders should therefore no longer be regarded as rare or static conditions, but as an emerging and dynamic clinical entity that warrants heightened awareness and systematic evaluation in routine rhinology practice.

Increase in Pediatric Presentations After the COVID‐19 Pandemic

The pre‐pandemic proportion of pediatric olfactory dysfunction in our cohort (1.5%) was consistent with previously reported rates of 1.6%–2.3% [3, 4], supporting the representativeness of our baseline data. A significant increase was observed during the post‐pandemic period, particularly in the proportion of cases attributed to URTI, consistent with the findings reported by Deller et al. [16]. Although there were several differences between the studies, including differences in the study regions, potential variations in healthcare‐seeking behavior that may have influenced consultation frequency, and differences in olfactory assessment methods and diagnostic criteria, the overall findings were similar. These results suggest that the impact of COVID‐19 on pediatric olfactory dysfunction was the same globally.

Furthermore, the large number of olfactory dysfunction cases associated with COVID‐19 among URTI‐related cases was also consistent with a previous study reporting that 12.5% of pediatric patients with COVID‐19 experienced olfactory dysfunction [17].

Two main factors may account for this increase. Firstly, the fact that COVID‐19‐related olfactory dysfunction appears to affect younger individuals more frequently than other forms of post‐viral olfactory dysfunction. Eo et al. [18] demonstrate that patients with COVID‐19‐related olfactory loss were significantly younger than those with non–COVID‐19 viral infections, supporting this age‐related susceptibility. Secondly, heightened public awareness of olfactory dysfunction as a characteristic symptom of COVID‐19 is likely to influence healthcare‐seeking behavior. This shift is supported by Walker et al. [19], who reported a marked increase in Google search activity related to olfactory loss following the onset of the pandemic, suggesting increased recognition and concern among caregivers.

Shift in Etiological Patterns of Pediatric Olfactory Dysfunction

Before the pandemic, olfactory cleft stenosis was the predominant etiology in our pediatric cohort, consistent with previous reports demonstrating favorable outcomes after surgical decompression in selected cases [20]. Olfactory cleft stenosis may contribute to olfactory dysfunction by disturbing the delivery of odorants to the olfactory epithelium. However, this relationship is not absolute, as some patients retain partial or intermittent olfactory perception despite stenosis of the olfactory cleft. This may reflect incomplete obstruction, dynamic changes in the mucosa, variations in airflow, or residual function of the olfactory epithelium [21]. In contrast, URTI, particularly COVID‐19, became the leading cause after the pandemic, representing a significant etiological shift. Geographic differences in the prevalence of COVID‐19‐related olfactory dysfunction have been reported, with lower frequencies in East Asian populations compared with European cohorts [22]. Genetic variability in olfactory receptor expression has been proposed as a potential contributing factor [23].

Several mechanisms may underlie these trends. Firstly, olfactory cleft stenosis is sometimes associated with localized chronic or mild inflammation within the olfactory cleft. Prior to the pandemic, subtle or subclinical URTI may not have been clearly identified during history taking. Since the emergence of COVID‐19, however, olfactory dysfunction has become widely recognized as a hallmark symptom, potentially leading to more detailed and accurate reporting of preceding infectious symptoms. Consequently, some cases that have been previously categorized as olfactory cleft stenosis may now be more appropriately classified as post‐viral olfactory dysfunction. Secondly, with regard to sinonasal disease, the wide‐spreading of infection control measures—such as social distancing, universal masking, and enhanced hand hygiene—has been associated with a decrease in the incidence of acute rhinosinusitis [24]. A decrease in the incidence of sinonasal infections may therefore have contributed to the observed decrease in sinonasal‐related olfactory dysfunction. Finally, restrictions on outdoor activities and population movements during the pandemic were associated with fewer traffic accidents and sports‐related injuries [25], which potentially explains the decline in traumatic olfactory dysfunction.

In our study, COVID‐19 was identified as the predominant etiology of pediatric olfactory dysfunction in the post‐pandemic period. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have also reported that olfactory dysfunction may persist in children after COVID‐19 infection, with a reported prevalence of 1.7% at 12 months after infection [26]. In contrast, the corresponding prevalence in adults has been reported to be 8.4% at 12 months [27], indicating a marked difference between children and adults. Although direct comparison is difficult because of differences in study periods and geographic settings, several factors may account for the lower reported frequency in children, including children's limited ability to recognize or report olfactory symptoms and the possibility that the mechanisms of recovery differ from those in adults.

Parosmia in Pediatric Post‐COVID‐19 Olfactory Dysfunction

A particularly noteworthy finding of this study was the exclusive occurrence of parosmia in pediatric patients with post‐COVID‐19 olfactory dysfunction. Several factors may explain this observation. Firstly, children with COVID‐19‐related olfactory dysfunction may be inherently more susceptible to developing parosmia. While parosmia has been reported in approximately 4.2% of adults [28], its prevalence in affected children has been reported to reach up to 60% [29], suggesting a heightened pediatric vulnerability. Secondly, increased social and medical awareness of olfactory dysfunction after the pandemic may have improved the recognition of qualitative olfactory disorders. As olfactory symptoms gained prominence during the COVID‐19 era, caregivers and clinicians may have become more attentive to complaints of distorted or abnormal smells, leading to higher detection rates of parosmia. Thirdly, the post‐pandemic increase in relatively mild olfactory dysfunction may have contributed to the higher proportion of parosmia. Parosmia is known to be associated with residual olfactory function and the process of olfactory recovery [30]. COVID‐19‐related olfactory dysfunction has been characterized by relatively preserved olfactory function in the early stage, along with a higher frequency of parosmia [18]. Consistent with this interpretation, pediatric patients in the post‐pandemic cohort demonstrated significantly higher VAS scores, indicating milder subjective olfactory dysfunction.

Finally, it should be noted that validated tools for pediatric olfactory assessment remain limited. Establishing pediatric normative values for olfactory tests, including OE [12], is therefore an important future challenge.

Limitations

This study was conducted at a tertiary olfactory clinic and included only pediatric patients who sought specialist evaluation, which requires generalizability of the findings to the broader pediatric population. Increased public awareness of olfactory symptoms during the pandemic may also have influenced consultation behavior. In addition, the absolute number of pediatric cases remained small, limiting the statistical significance for subgroup analyses.

Conclusion

The COVID‐19 pandemic was associated with a substantial increase in pediatric presenting with olfactory dysfunction and a marked shift in etiological patterns toward URTI, particularly COVID‐19. Parosmia emerged as a distinctive qualitative olfactory symptom exclusively in pediatric post‐COVID‐19 cases, highlighting differences from pre‐pandemic and adult presentations. These findings underscore the need for the enhancement of clinical awareness of pediatric olfactory dysfunction in rhinology practice. Further efforts to establish reliable pediatric diagnostic standards and to improve age‐appropriate olfactory assessment remain an important future priority.
