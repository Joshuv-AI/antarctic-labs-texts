Gallbladder volvulus: Recognizing the rare ‘beak’ and ‘whirl’ signs
Authors: Soukayna Bourabaa, Pia Fadel, Maxime Constant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gallbladder volvulus is a rare surgical emergency caused by torsion of the gallbladder on its mesentery, leading to cystic duct and vascular obstruction with rapid progression to ischemia and gangrene, and it often mimics acute acalculous cholecystitis, making preoperative diagnosis challenging. A 80-year-old woman presented with acute right upper quadrant pain and vomiting. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a distended, horizontally displaced gallbladder with gallstones and the “whirl” and “beak” signs consistent with cystic pedicle torsion. Urgent surgery confirmed ischemic volvulus; detorsion with cholecystectomy and intraoperative cholangiography was performed, followed by an uneventful recovery. This case highlights the importance of recognizing specific radiological features to achieve early diagnosis and emphasizes that prompt surgical intervention, preferably laparoscopic, is essential to prevent severe complications and optimize outcomes.

Introduction

Gallbladder volvulus represents an exceptionally uncommon pathological entity, with a documented hospitalization incidence of approximately 1 in 365,520 [1]. The condition arises from axial rotation of the gallbladder around its mesenteric pedicle, resulting in compromise of the cystic duct as well as the cystic artery and vein. Preoperative diagnosis remains particularly challenging, as the clinical manifestations closely resemble those of acute acalculous cholecystitis. To date, only about 500 cases have been described in the published literature [2]. Owing to its nonspecific presentation, gallbladder volvulus is most frequently identified during surgical exploration, with merely 17% of cases diagnosed prior to operative intervention [3]. Early detection is critical, as delayed management may precipitate serious complications, including gangrenous changes, perforation, and septicemia, with morbidity and mortality rates reported at 16% and 6%, respectively [3]. Consequently, prompt surgical intervention—typically emergent cholecystectomy—is advocated once the diagnosis is suspected. In this report, we present a surgically confirmed case of gallbladder volvulus and provide a review of previously documented cases, accompanied by a discussion of relevant imaging characteristics and operative management strategies.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 80-year-old woman with a medical history of hypertension treated with hydrochlorothiazide, a previous pelvic fracture, and a reported but unconfirmed history of hemophilia. She presented to the emergency department with acute abdominal pain associated with a single episode of vomiting, without other accompanying symptoms. Physical examination revealed right hypochondrial tenderness. Laboratory tests showed a moderate inflammatory response (CRP 31.3 mg/L, WBC count 19,000/mm³) with normal liver function tests and normal coagulation parameters. Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrated a distended gallbladder positioned abnormally in a horizontal axis, located outside the gallbladder fossa and inferior to the liver, containing dense gallstones. A “whirl sign” involving the cystic pedicle (cystic duct and cystic artery) and a “beak sign” corresponding to tapering of the distended gallbladder at the pedicle were noted. There was also mild pericholecystic fluid. The patient was admitted to the surgical ward and started on intravenous ceftriaxone. Given her age, comorbidities, suspected advanced ischemia, and the need for rapid surgical control, an open subcostal approach was preferred over laparoscopy. Exploratory subcostal laparotomy revealed no peritonitis but confirmed gallbladder ischemia due to torsion of the cystic pedicle. A sample of reactive peritoneal fluid was collected for bacteriological analysis. Detorsion of the cystic pedicle was performed, followed by release of the gallbladder mesentery. The cystic artery was clipped using a green Hem-o-lok clip. The cystic duct was then divided, and a cholangiography catheter was inserted. Intraoperative cholangiography demonstrated adequate duodenal opacification, intact intrahepatic bile ducts, and no filling defects in the common bile duct. The cystic duct was subsequently secured with 2 Hem-o-lok clips and divided, and a cholecystectomy was completed. Peritoneal fluid cultures were negative, and histopathology confirmed necrotic changes. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with return of oral intake and bowel function on postoperative day (POD) 1. The patient was discharged on POD 2. At 1-month follow-up, she remained in good general condition and was satisfied with the surgical outcome.

Discussion

Gallbladder volvulus is an infrequent yet potentially fatal condition that predominantly affects elderly women and often presents with clinical features indistinguishable from acute cholecystitis, frequently in the context of progressive weight loss [4]. Since Wendel’s seminal description of the “floating gallbladder” in 1898 [5], numerous case reports and series have expanded the understanding of this entity. Classically, gallbladder volvulus involves rotation of the gallbladder around the axis of the cystic duct [3]; however, isolated torsion of the fundus, using the gallbladder body as a pivot point, has been documented in rare instances [6]. Rotation may occur in either a clockwise or counterclockwise direction. Complete torsion is defined as rotation of 180° or greater—with extreme cases reporting up to 1080° [7]—whereas incomplete torsion involves less than 180° and is generally not associated with gallstone disease [8]. In fact, cholelithiasis is identified in only 24%–32% of reported cases [9,10]. Among adult patients, frail elderly women are disproportionately affected, demonstrating a female-to-male ratio ranging from 3:1 to 5:1 [7]. Incidence peaks between 60 and 80 years of age, and prevalence is anticipated to increase with population aging [10].

Approximately 4% of individuals possess a gallbladder suspended by an elongated mesentery, a configuration that confers increased mobility and predisposes to torsion [1]. The mesentery may envelop both the gallbladder and cystic duct or, in some cases, only the cystic duct [7]. Age-related attenuation of supporting connective tissues and reduction in visceral fat further contribute to susceptibility [7]. When sufficiently mobile, the gallbladder may rotate around its pedicle, compromising arterial and venous flow and leading to infarction. Clinically, patients typically present with sudden-onset, intense right upper quadrant pain accompanied by nausea and vomiting [10].

Timely identification through imaging is essential, as untreated torsion can rapidly evolve into gangrene or perforation. Ultrasonography (US), often the first imaging modality employed, may reveal an enlarged, thick-walled gallbladder positioned abnormally—frequently horizontally or inferiorly in relation to the liver. Doppler studies may demonstrate diminished or absent flow within the cystic pedicle (Table 1). CT can reveal hallmark findings such as the “whirl sign,” indicative of a twisted cystic pedicle, and the “beak sign” at the gallbladder neck, reflecting abrupt tapering caused by torsion [4]. From an imaging perspective, gallbladder volvulus may closely mimic more common inflammatory conditions such as acute acalculous cholecystitis, gallbladder empyema, and emphysematous cholecystitis. However, certain imaging features help differentiate these entities. In volvulus, the most characteristic findings are abnormal gallbladder displacement—often a horizontal orientation or location outside the gallbladder fossa—and the presence of a twisted cystic pedicle producing the “whirl sign,” together with abrupt tapering at the gallbladder neck (“beak sign”). In contrast, acute cholecystitis typically demonstrates mural thickening with surrounding inflammatory fat stranding without pedicle torsion, while empyema is more commonly associated with intraluminal debris and marked luminal distension. Emphysematous cholecystitis is distinguished by the presence of intramural or intraluminal gas. Recognition of these distinguishing features is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and to ensure prompt surgical management.

Definitive management requires urgent cholecystectomy to prevent progression to gangrene and perforation. When feasible, laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the preferred approach [3,18], and robotic-assisted cholecystectomy has also been described [19]. However, the surgical approach must be individualized according to patient status, suspected ischemic severity, and operative conditions. Delays in diagnosis or conservative management strategies are associated with increased morbidity and mortality [3]. Mortality remains approximately 6% [9]. Prompt surgical intervention, however, is associated with an excellent prognosis.

Conclusion

Gallbladder volvulus should be suspected in frail, elderly, low-BMI patients presenting with acute right upper quadrant pain and atypical imaging findings. Recognition of key CT signs—particularly the “whirl sign,” “beak sign,” and abnormal horizontal displacement of the gallbladder—should prompt urgent surgical intervention. Although laparoscopy is usually preferred, the operative approach must be tailored to the clinical context, with timeliness of surgery remaining the most critical factor for favorable outcomes.
