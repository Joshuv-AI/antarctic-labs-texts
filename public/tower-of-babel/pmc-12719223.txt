Oropouche fever: Clinical characteristics and viral–bacterial coinfections in a region of the Peruvian Amazon: A cross-sectional study

Introduction

Oropouche fever is an emerging arboviral disease in Latin America and the Caribbean caused by the Oropouche virus (OROV, family Peribunyaviridae, genus Orthobunyavirus) [1]. Since its discovery, multiple outbreaks have been reported, mainly in the Amazon region of South America. For example, a large epidemic in Brazil during 1980-1981 affected approximately 100,000 people [2]. OROV is transmitted predominantly by the midge Culicoides paraensis, known locally as “jején,” which is abundant in tropical areas; other mosquito species and even sloths have also been found to carry the virus [1,3]. Clinically, Oropouche fever is typically an acute febrile syndrome characterized by high fever, severe headache, myalgias, arthralgias, and sometimes a rash [4]. Notably, about 60% of patients experience a brief recurrence of symptoms after the initial febrile phase [1]. While the illness is usually self-limited and non-fatal, there have been increasing reports of complications such as OROV-associated meningitis [5]. Indeed, as of April 2025, the Pan American Health Organization had documented five deaths directly attributed to OROV infection in Brazil and Panama [6]. Despite its expanding geographic range in Latin America, Oropouche fever remains a neglected and underdiagnosed disease in routine clinical practice [7].

Coinfection is defined as the concurrent infection of a single host by two or more different pathogens [8]. In arbovirus-endemic regions where multiple pathogens circulate simultaneously, coinfections are a frequent and clinically important phenomenon. For example, during the Zika virus epidemic in Colombia (2015-2016), a national study identified Zika-dengue or Zika-chikungunya coinfections in approximately 0.1% of all reported Zika cases, and about 20% of those coinfected patients died, suggesting a negative impact of coinfection on clinical outcome [9]. In general, the interaction of two pathogens in the same host can increase disease severity through mechanisms like antibody-dependent enhancement, or in some cases, produce viral interference that modulates and attenuates the dominant infection [8,10]. It is therefore crucial to recognize the presence of coinfections in order to guide appropriate patient therapy and to implement epidemiological surveillance that accounts for multiple circulating pathogens [11].

In the specific context of OROV, coinfections carry special clinical and epidemiological relevance. Given that OROV cocirculates with other arboviruses in tropical areas, it is not surprising that simultaneous infections in humans have been reported. A recent study from an endemic region found that approximately 10% of laboratory-confirmed OROV cases had a viral coinfection, mainly with dengue, followed by Zika and chikungunya, including some instances of triple infection (OROV + dengue + Zika) [12]. In regions where OROV is emerging, its symptoms are often mistaken for dengue or other tropical fevers; if a patient tests positive for dengue, a concomitant OROV infection could be overlooked, masking the true disease burden [13,14]. Thus, OROV infections may be underdetected when only one pathogen is tested for, highlighting the need for multipathogen surveillance strategies [11]. All these considerations point to the importance of expanding our knowledge of OROV coinfections.

In this context, the objective of the present study was to characterize viral and bacterial coinfections in OROV-positive patients, evaluating their clinical features and exploring the impact on hospital stay and antibiotic use across different infection groups.

Methods

Study design and setting

We performed an observational, cross-sectional descriptive study in a tropical region of eastern Peru during 2016. The study area has a humid tropical climate (mean annual temperature ranging from 18.7-30.5°C) [15]. Patients were enrolled from healthcare facilities in the Leoncio Prado Health Network of the Peruvian Ministry of Health and the Tingo María Contingency Hospital, which serve communities in the foothills of the Amazon rainforest (Figure 1).

Geographical representation of the study site.

Participants

Patients presenting to the healthcare facilities with acute febrile illness, defined as a fever >38°C for ≤14 days with no identifiable focal source of infection, accompanied by one or more clinical signs or symptoms of infection [16]. Additional inclusion criteria were laboratory confirmation of OROV infection and availability of complete clinical data. Patients were excluded if they had incomplete medical records, evidence of a localized infection, were pregnant, or had active cancer at any stage.

Patient grouping

For analysis, patients were classified into five groups according to their infection status at presentation:

•Only OROV: Oropouche virus infection without any coinfection (monoinfection)

•OROV + DENV-2: Coinfection with OROV and dengue virus type 2 (American/Asian [Am/As] genotype II)

OROV + DENV-2: Coinfection with OROV and dengue virus type 2 (American/Asian [Am/As] genotype II)

•OROV + Rickettsia: Coinfection with OROV and Rickettsia spp.

OROV + Rickettsia: Coinfection with OROV and Rickettsia spp.

•OROV + Leptospira: Coinfection with OROV and Leptospira spp.

OROV + Leptospira: Coinfection with OROV and Leptospira spp.

•Negative (controls): Acute febrile illness patients negative for OROV, DENV-2, Rickettsia, and Leptospira (no pathogen detected)

Negative (controls): Acute febrile illness patients negative for OROV, DENV-2, Rickettsia, and Leptospira (no pathogen detected)

Operational definitions

AFI: Temperature >38.0°C, duration ≤14 days, without evident clinical focus on admission [16].

Coinfection: Simultaneous detection (same episode) of OROV + another pathogen (viral/bacterial) by validated polymerase chain reaction (PCR) [10].

Hospitalization (yes/no): Admission ≥24 hours due to clinical indication (hemodynamic instability, risk of complications, institutional criteria).

Hospital stay (days): Difference in days (date of discharge – date of admission).

Antibiotic-etiology correspondence: Appropriate initial antibiotic treatment if the prescribed regimen has expected in vitro activity and clinical recommendation against the identified pathogen according to clinical practice guidelines:

Leptospira spp.: doxycycline / penicillin / ceftriaxone.

Rickettsia spp.: doxycycline (choice).

Inadequate if it covers common bacteria but not Leptospira/Rickettsia (e.g., cephalexin/azithromycin as empiric monotherapy), or antibiotic use when not indicated (OROV/DENV without proven bacterial infection).

Missing data: We declared that there were no missing data (no imputation of outcomes).

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Regional Hospital of Cajamarca, Peru (Approval No. 1-958-851). Administrative permission for the study was also obtained from the participating health facilities (Authorization No. 020-2016-GRHCO-HTM-UADI). This research was conducted in the context of a national public health emergency response to undifferentiated febrile illness (Peruvian Supreme Decree No. 030-2015-SA), and all procedures followed the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, the World Health Organization (WHO), and the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS). Written informed consent and assent were obtained from all patients or their guardians. Data were recorded in an anonymized format without personal identifiers to ensure confidentiality.

Sample collection and laboratory analyses

We employed consecutive census; a total of 75 acute-phase blood samples were collected from the enrolled patients. For each patient, 3 mL of whole blood was drawn and placed in a serum separator tube (Vacuette® Serum Clot Activator tube). Samples were kept refrigerated and then stored at -80°C until they were transported on dry ice to the Laboratorio de Biomedicina at Universidad Peruana de Ciencias Aplicadas (UPC) in Lima for molecular analysis.

Identification of viral and bacterial etiology

Nucleic acid extraction: Viral RNA was extracted from 200 µL of patient serum using the High Pure RNA Isolation Kit (Roche Applied Science, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. For detection of bacterial DNA (Rickettsia/Leptospira), genomic DNA was similarly extracted from patient samples; an internal control PCR targeting the human beta-globin gene was performed to check for PCR inhibitors in each sample.

OROV detection: OROV was detected by a nested reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) targeting the S segment of the OROV genome [17]. The cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 minutes; 40 cycles of 95°C for 1 minute, 55°C for 1 minute, and 72°C for 45 seconds; and a final extension at 72°C for 5 minutes. PCR products were analyzed by gel electrophoresis and SYBR Green staining, and a 300 bp band was considered positive for OROV. Amplicons were purified (SpinPrep™ Gel DNA Kit, USA) for confirmation if needed.

DENV-2 detection and genotyping: Dengue virus was detected using a one-step real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) with TaqMan probes [18]. This assay broadly detects dengue virus serotypes; in our study, all dengue-positive cases were further identified as DENV serotype 2. To confirm the genotype of DENV-2, we performed a specific real-time RT-PCR targeting the American/Asian lineage II of DENV-2 [19,20].

Rickettsia spp. detection: We detected Rickettsia DNA by a quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay targeting the PanR8 gene, a conserved gene in the Rickettsia genus [21].

Leptospira spp. detection: Leptospira DNA was detected by a TaqMan qPCR assay targeting the LipL32 gene [22].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact test (two-tailed) for categorical data, given the small sample sizes in some groups. A P-value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. We constructed a correlation matrix of clinical signs and symptoms to explore any associations or clustering of symptoms with particular coinfections. All statistical analyses were done using Minitab version 18.1 (Minitab Inc., State College, PA, USA). Graphs and figures were prepared with OriginPro software version 10.0 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA).

Bias control: To minimize selection bias, we applied prespecified inclusion/exclusion criteria for acute febrile illness and a defined temporal/spatial frame. Verification (diagnostic) bias: Only selected pathogens were tested; therefore, it is recognized that other unstudied etiologies may underestimate coinfections.

Results

A total of 75 patients with acute febrile illness were included in the study. Of these, 26 febrile patients tested negative for OROV. Thus, roughly half of the OROV infections in this sample occurred as coinfections with another pathogen (Figure 2).

Demographics

Overall, 49.3% were children or adolescents (<17 years old), 48.0% were adults 18-59 years, and only 2.7% were age 60 or above. The age distribution varied across groups: the majority of patients in the OROV monoinfection group (16/26, 61.5%) and the OROV + DENV-2 group (8/15, 53.3%) were <17 years old, whereas most patients in the OROV + Rickettsia group (3/4, 75%) and the negative group (15/26, 57.7%) were in the 18-59 year range. The OROV + Leptospira group had three of four patients (75%) who were <17. Males comprised 52% of the total cohort (39/75), and each group had a roughly similar male-to-female ratio. Overall, gender was balanced in the full sample (1.08:1, male: female) (Table 1).

Clinical manifestations: fever was the universal symptom, present in 100% of patients by study inclusion (fever was part of the entry criteria). The mean duration of fever from onset until etiologic diagnosis was approximately 4 days across all groups. Patients with OROV monoinfection had a slightly shorter mean fever duration (mean, 3.7 days; SD, 1.7) compared to those with coinfections or negatives.

Table 2 summarizes the frequency of symptoms in each group. Broadly, clinical symptoms were similar across the five groups. Nearly all patients experienced headache (88% overall) and myalgias (80% overall). Arthralgias were reported in 73% of all cases. These three symptoms (headache, myalgia, arthralgia) tended to be most frequent in the groups with bacterial coinfections and in the pathogen-negative group. For instance, 100% of patients in the OROV + Rickettsia group and 96.1% of those in the negative group had arthralgias, compared to 53.8% of OROV-only patients. A similar pattern was observed for myalgias and headache, which were reported by all four OROV + Rickettsia patients (100%) and over 90% of negatives, vs. 61.5-80.8% of OROV-only patients. In contrast, gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms were somewhat more common in the negative group: hyporexia was noted in 73% of negatives, the highest proportion among groups, and nausea/vomiting in 61.6% of negatives, whereas these GI symptoms were present in roughly 40-53.8% of patients in the other groups. Retro-ocular pain and low back pain had intermediate frequencies, with no clear predominance in any single group.

Hemorrhagic manifestations were observed in a few cases. Notably, hematemesis was recorded in one patient (1.3% overall)—this occurred in the OROV monoinfection group. Gynecological bleeding was also reported in one case (1.3% overall), also in an OROV-only infected female. Additionally, a drop in platelet count was noted in two patients (2.7% overall): one with OROV monoinfection and one with OROV + DENV coinfection. No bleeding manifestations were documented in the bacterial coinfection groups or in the negatives. Some other severe clinical signs were specifically evaluated but not observed in any patient in this series. In particular, there were no cases of hypotension, altered mental status, hemoconcentration, oliguria, jaundice, gum bleeding, syncope, hypothermia, or hemoptysis. These signs, often markers of severe disease, were absent across all study groups.

Hospitalization outcomes

A total of 10 patients (13.3%) required hospitalization for management of their acute illness. The proportion of hospitalized patients in each group ranged from 0-50%. Specifically, three of 26 OROV-only patients (11.5%) were hospitalized, three of 15 OROV + DENV-2: Am/As II-positive patients (20.0%) were hospitalized, two of four OROV + Leptospira patients (50.0%) were hospitalized, and two of 26 OROV-negative patients (7.7%) were hospitalized. The length of hospital stay averaged approximately 4 days for most groups. The longest hospitalizations were observed in the OROV + Leptospira group, with the highest mean recorded at 6.0 ± 1.4 days. In comparison, hospitalized patients in the OROV-only and OROV + DENV-2: Am/As II groups typically had lengths of stay of 3 to 4 days.

Antibiotic use and treatment correspondence

The use of antibiotics administered at initial presentation before laboratory results were known was examined in these patients. In total, seven of 75 patients (9.3%) received at least one antibiotic as part of their initial treatment. In the OROV + Leptospira group, two of four patients (50%) began empirical antibiotic treatment upon admission. In contrast, the viral infection groups (OROV monoinfection and OROV + DENV-2: Am/As II) had a lower frequency of antibiotic use: only a few patients received empirical antimicrobial treatment. In the OROV-only group, an antibiotic was initially administered in one of 26 cases (3.8%), and in the OROV + DENV-2: Am/As II group in two of 15 cases (13.3%). In the OROV + Rickettsia group, none of the four patients (0%) received antibiotic treatment at admission, whereas in the negative group, two of 26 patients (7.7%) received empirical antibiotics.

We next assessed the appropriateness (correspondence) of the initial antibiotic therapy relative to the eventual diagnostic findings. An antibiotic was considered appropriate if a bacterial pathogen was subsequently confirmed that would reasonably be covered by that antibiotic; conversely, antibiotic use was considered unnecessary or incongruent if the illness turned out to be purely viral. Of the seven patients who received empirical antibiotics, only two cases (28.6%) had an appropriate match between the administered antibiotic and the identified pathogen. The two patients with OROV + Leptospira who were started on antibiotics did indeed have leptospirosis and received an antibiotic regimen that covered Leptospira. In all other cases of antibiotic use, treatment did not align with the final diagnosis: patients with OROV-only or OROV + DENV infection who received antibiotics ultimately did not have a bacterial infection requiring such therapy. Overall, approximately 86% of initial antibiotic prescriptions were inconsistent with definitive etiologic findings (Table 3).

Discussion

Our findings illustrate that coinfections further complicate the challenges posed by Oropouche fever as an emerging disease. This study, the first to document viral–bacterial coinfections in OROV-infected patients in Peru, did not find differences in the clinical presentation between OROV monoinfected cases and those coinfected with a second virus (DENV-2: Am/As II) or with Rickettsia. In other words, patients coinfected with OROV and dengue or OROV and Rickettsia experienced a clinical illness that was largely indistinguishable from OROV infection alone. In this context, descriptive studies in endemic areas reveal a high frequency of OROV in febrile patients and coinfection rates above 20%. Among the reported coinfections, a study carried out on the northern coast of Peru detected OROV in 26.4% of patients; in this series, 9.9% presented viral coinfections, mainly with DENV (7.3% cases), followed by Zika (1.8%) and chikungunya (0.2%) [12]. In Colombia, during the last epidemiological weeks of 2024, six cases of coinfection of OROV with the four dengue serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4) were documented [23]. Similarly, Bolivia reported OROV+ DENV coinfections in patients from La Paz during 2024. In Cuba, one of the first OROV outbreaks in the Caribbean coincided with a dengue epidemic, where 16% of OROV patients presented dengue immunoglobulin M antibodies, suggesting the possibility of coinfection or recent dengue infection [23,24]. Despite these reports, the clinical relevance of coinfections has not been clarified [12].

Findings of coinfections across different countries suggest that the simultaneous circulation of OROV with other viruses is more common than reported, reflecting a complex regional epidemiologic pattern [25,26]. This reinforces the need for close surveillance in regions where OROV is emerging and for recognizing coinfection as a clinically relevant factor in the management of OROV disease. A recent meta-analysis that included 806 patients with confirmed OROV infection across 15 studies from Latin America described the most prevalent symptoms [27]. Its results partially align with our cohort, in which constitutional and musculoskeletal symptoms predominated, although GI and ocular signs were more frequent in the coinfected groups. Respiratory symptoms and rash are uncommon in OROV, which is consistent with our observation of a low frequency of rash and chest pain across all groups [27]. This broad clinical characterization of OROV underscores the complexity of the differential diagnosis in endemic areas with multiple cocirculating pathogens.

Oropouche fever has been described as self-limited and typically presents similarly to other arboviral illnesses. In OROV + DENV coinfections, clinical features likewise tend to be reported as overlapping without distinctive traits and may be considered an indistinguishable febrile syndrome. Nevertheless, in 2024, the first five deaths directly attributed to OROV were reported, all classified as monoinfections [6]. Even so, it still seems premature to draw firm conclusions, as the dynamics of coinfection during an outbreak can mask the unnoticed spread of OROV in the population, with an impact that is difficult to determine [25]. At present, there is no strong evidence that OROV + DENV or OROV + other arbovirus coinfections produce more severe clinical manifestations than single-agent infections; however, OROV monoinfection has been associated with unusual neurological complications (such as encephalitis or Guillain–Barré syndrome) [24]. Coinfection of OROV with dengue or other arboviruses complicates the clinical picture primarily because of overlapping symptomatology and potential pathogenic synergy, requiring a high index of suspicion and laboratory confirmation to distinguish the etiologies involved.

Although most coinfections described with Oropouche involve arboviruses, concurrent bacterial infections are also possible, especially in tropical settings where febrile illnesses of diverse causes coexist [28]. In OROV-endemic areas, the differential diagnosis may include bacterial diseases such as leptospirosis, rickettsioses, bartonellosis, or other causes of “undifferentiated fever” [6,28]. These conditions share clinical manifestations with OROV and can lead to misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis when two infectious agents coexist [29]. Coinfection of leptospirosis with an arbovirus can worsen the clinical course and increase the complication rate; such combinations have been documented to lead to greater severity and a higher likelihood of fatal outcomes if not recognized promptly [30]. In the case of OROV, coinfection with Leptospira or other bacteria may present with additional signs (such as jaundice, renal failure, or hemorrhagic manifestations in severe leptospirosis) on top of viral symptoms, prolonging the hospital length of stay.

Regarding management, the presence of coinfection can modify therapeutic decisions. Given diagnostic uncertainty at presentation, empiric antibiotics are often initiated to cover a potential bacterial infection until the diagnosis is confirmed [30]. This approach differs from the management of a purely viral infection, in which treatment is primarily symptomatic and supportive. The need for antibiotics in OROV + bacterial coinfections is critical to control the bacterial pathogen; disregarding this possibility can lead to significant deterioration if the coinfection is not recognized in time and appropriate therapy is not instituted, with serious complications attributable to the bacterium, such as hepatorenal failure in leptospirosis, and even death [30]. Conversely, administering antibiotics without justification is inappropriate in the context of antimicrobial stewardship, and in most cases, as reported in our study, there is suboptimal concordance between the initial antibiotic choice and the eventual diagnostic results across all groups. Although less frequently reported than viral coinfections, bacterial coinfection poses a substantial clinical challenge that can prolong hospitalization by necessitating close monitoring and combined therapies, compelling a multidisciplinary approach to manage both the emerging viral illness and the treatable bacterial infection.

Limitations

This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, a cross-sectional design was used, which precludes assessment of the clinical progression of coinfections. In addition, the absence of complementary serologic testing may have underestimated cases of recent or resolving infections, particularly in the context of arboviral diseases with transient viremia. Another limitation was the lack of evaluation for coinfections with other bacterial pathogens that may be regionally relevant. Finally, concordance between antibiotic therapy and the identified etiology was assessed retrospectively without considering the initial clinical criteria that prompted empiric prescribing, which constrains the interpretation of therapeutic appropriateness under real-world conditions.

Conclusion

We found no differences in the clinical presentation between viral coinfections, bacterial coinfections, and OROV monoinfection. However, OROV coinfection with Leptospira was associated with longer hospitalization. There is a frequent mismatch between empiric antibiotics and the final etiology.
