Abstract
. Hyperbolic polynomials are monic real-rooted polynomials. By Bronshtein’s theorem, the increasingly ordered roots of a hyperbolic polynomial of degree d d with C d − 1 , 1 C^{d-1,1} coefficients are locally Lipschitz and the solution map “coefficients-to-roots” is bounded. We prove continuity of this solution map from hyperbolic polynomials of degree d d with C d C^{d} coefficients to their increasingly ordered roots with respect to the C d C^{d} structure on the source space and the Sobolev W 1 , q W^{1,q} structure, for all 1 ≤ q < ∞ 1\leq q<\infty , on the target space. Continuity fails for q = ∞ q=\infty . As a consequence, we obtain continuity of the local surface area of the roots as well as local lower semicontinuity of the area of the zero sets of hyperbolic polynomials. We also discuss applications for the eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices and singular values.

Introduction

Determining the optimal regularity of the roots of polynomials whose coefficients depend smoothly on parameters
is a much studied problem with a long history.
It has important applications in various fields such as partial differential equations and perturbation theory.

The subject started with Rellich’s work
[
Rel37
]
on the analytic perturbation theory of linear operators.
Bronshtein
[
Bro80
]
proved Gevrey well-posedness of the hyperbolic Cauchy problem
with multiple characteristics
using his result
[
Bro79
]
on the Lipschitz continuity of the roots of hyperbolic polynomials.
Spagnolo
[
Spa00
]
,
motivated by his analysis of certain systems of pseudo-differential equations,
conjectured that the roots of smooth curves of (not necessarily hyperbolic) polynomials admit absolutely continuous parameterizations.
This conjecture was proved and the optimal Sobolev regularity of the roots was established in a series
of papers by Parusiński and Rainer
[
PR16
,
PR18
,
PR20a
]
,
after the optimal result for radicals had been obtained by
Ghisi and Gobbino
[
GG13
]
.
For a more comprehensive account of the history of the problem and its ramifications, e.g., in the perturbation theory
of linear operators, we refer to the recent survey article
[
PR25
]
.

In this paper, we focus on the class of monic
hyperbolic polynomials
for which the regularity problem has a special flavor;
the general case of monic complex polynomials is treated in
[
PR24
]
.
A monic real polynomial of degree
d
is called
hyperbolic
if all its
d
roots (counted with multiplicities) are real.
Hyperbolic polynomials appear naturally as the characteristic polynomials of Hermitian matrices for instance.

We refer by
Bronshtein’s theorem
to the statement that
any continuous system of the roots of a
C^{d-1,1}
family of hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
is
actually locally Lipschitz continuous (i.e.
C^{0,1}
). In general, this is optimal.
Bronshtein
[
Bro79
]
originally proved a version for
C^{d}
curves of hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d

(under these assumptions the roots can be represented by differentiable functions).
Bronshtein’s rather dense proof is hard to follow.
Wakabayashi
[
Wak86
]
gave a complex analytic proof
of a more general Hölder version of Bronshtein’s theorem,
which had been announced by Ohya and Tarama
[
OT86
]
;
it was later proved by Tarama
[
Tar06
]
following Bronshtein’s original approach.
Kurdyka and Păunescu
[
KP08
]
used resolution of singularities to deduce
local Lipschitz continuity of the roots of hyperbolic polynomials with real analytic coefficients.
A simple proof of Bronshtein’s theorem, based on the splitting principle,
which also established
explicit uniform bounds for the Lipschitz constants of the roots in terms of the

C^{d-1,1}
norms of the coefficients, was given by Parusiński and Rainer
[
PR15
]
.
We will recall this version in
Theorem

4.1
.

Bronshtein’s theorem gives rise to
a bounded
solution map
that takes hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
with
C^{d-1,1}
coefficients to
C^{0,1}
systems of their roots.
This will be made precise below.

The purpose of this paper is to investigate the continuity of the solution map
and thus answer a question of Antonio Lerario.

1.1.
Hyperbolic polynomials and the solution map

A monic polynomial of degree
d
,

P_{\mathsf{a}}(Z)=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}Z^{d-j}\in\mathbb{R}[Z],

is called
hyperbolic
if all its
d
roots are real.
In the following, we will identify the polynomial
P_{\mathsf{a}}
with its
coefficient vector
\mathsf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then the set of all hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
is identified with the image of
the map
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where

\sigma_{j}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=(-1)^{j}\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{j}}

is the
j
-th elementary symmetric function (up to sign).
By the Tarski–Seidenberg theorem,
\sigma(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
a closed semialgebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which we equip with the trace topology.
We denote this space by
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
and call it the
space of hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
.

For
\mathsf{a}\in\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
, let

\lambda_{1}^{\uparrow}(\mathsf{a})\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d}^{\uparrow}(\mathsf{a})
denote the increasingly ordered
roots of
P_{\mathsf{a}}
.
Then

\lambda^{\uparrow}=(\lambda_{1}^{\uparrow},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{\uparrow}):\operatorname{Hyp}(d)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

is a continuous map, see
[
AKLM98
, Lemma 4.1]
or, alternatively,
[
PR24
, Lemma 6.4]

combined with
Lemma

7.1
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open.
Let
C^{d-1,1}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
denote the space of all
C^{d-1,1}
maps
\mathsf{a}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mathsf{a}(U)\subseteq\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
.
Thus
\mathsf{a}\in C^{d-1,1}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
amounts to a hyperbolic polynomial
P_{\mathsf{a}}
of degree
d
whose coefficients are

C^{d-1,1}
functions defined on
U
.
We equip
C^{d-1,1}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
with the trace topology of the natural Fréchet topology on
C^{d-1,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Note that
C^{d-1,1}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
is a closed nonlinear subset of
C^{d-1,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Then Bronshtein’s theorem (see
Theorem

4.1
) implies that the
solution map

(1.1)

\mathcal{S}:=(\lambda^{\uparrow})_{*}:C^{d-1,1}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))\to C^{0,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\mathsf{a}\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ\mathsf{a},

is well-defined and bounded (i.e., it maps bounded sets to bounded sets).

1.2.
The main results

We will see in
Example

1.12
that the solution map

\mathcal{S}:C^{d-1,1}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))\to C^{0,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
not
continuous:
the natural topology on the target
C^{0,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is too strong.

However, the solution map
\mathcal{S}
becomes continuous if we restrict it to
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
,
carrying the trace topology of the natural Fréchet topology on
C^{d}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
and relax the topology on the target space:
for
1\leq q<\infty
, let
C^{0,1}_{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the set
C^{0,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})

equipped with the trace topology of the inclusion in Sobolev space

C^{0,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})\to W^{1,q}_{\operatorname{loc}}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
. See
Section

2
for precise definitions of the function spaces.

The following theorem, which is our main result, solves Open Problem 3.8 in
[
PR25
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open.
The solution map

\mathcal{S}:C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\mathsf{a}\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ\mathsf{a},

is continuous, for all
1\leq q<\infty
.

As a corollary, we find that the solution map on
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
is continuous
into the Hölder space
C^{0,\alpha}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, carrying its natural topology,
for all
0<\alpha<1
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open.
The solution map

\mathcal{S}:C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))\to C^{0,\alpha}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\mathsf{a}\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ\mathsf{a},

is continuous, for all
0<\alpha<1
, but not for
\alpha=1
.

The essential work for the proof of
Theorem

1.1
happens in dimension
m=1
of the parameter space.
The passage from one to several parameters is rather easy.
The following is the main technical result of the paper.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval.
Let
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(I,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
,
i.e.,
for each relatively compact open interval
I_{1}\Subset I
,

(1.2)

\|\mathsf{a}-\mathsf{a}_{n}\|_{C^{d}(\overline{I}_{1},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Then
\{\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n}):n\geq 1\}
is a bounded set in
C^{0,1}(I,\mathbb{R}^{d})
(with respect to its natural topology)
and, for each relatively compact open interval
I_{0}\Subset I
and each
1\leq q<\infty
,

(1.3)

\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})-\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})\|_{W^{1,q}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

The proof of
Theorem

1.3
is based on the dominated convergence theorem. The domination follows
from Bronshtein’s theorem which we recall in
Theorem

4.1
. We will show in
Theorem

5.1

that, for almost every
x\in I
,

\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}(x)\to\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})^{\prime}(x)\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

To this end, we will develop a version of Bronshtein’s theorem at a single point, see
Theorem

4.7
.

In
Section

8
, we prove a refinement of
Theorem

1.3
in which the assumption that

\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}
as
n\to\infty
can be weakened to convergence in
C^{p}
, where
p
is the (uniform) maximal multiplicity
of the roots of
P_{\mathsf{a}}
.

Note that by Egorov’s theorem
[
Ego11
]
we may conclude that

\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}\to\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})^{\prime}
almost uniformly on
I
as
n\to\infty
,
i.e., for each
\epsilon>0
there exists a measurable subset
E\subseteq I
with
|E|<\epsilon
such that

\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}\to\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})^{\prime}
uniformly on
I\setminus E
.
In general, the convergence is not uniform on the whole interval
I
; see
Example

1.12
.

For later reference, we state a simple consequence of
Theorem

1.3
.
Here
\|x\|_{2}
denotes the
2
-norm of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\|f\|_{L^{q}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\big\|\|f\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(I_{0})}
,
see
Section

1.6
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval and
I_{0}\Subset I
a relatively compact open subinterval.
If
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(I,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle\big\|\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})^{\prime}\|_{2}-\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(I_{0})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

and

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for all
1\leq q<\infty
.

Proof.

Let us set
\lambda:=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})
and
\lambda_{n}:=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})
. Then

\displaystyle\big|\|\lambda^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}-\|\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\big|=\big|\big\|\|\lambda^{\prime}\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(I_{0})}-\big\|\|\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(I_{0})}\big|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\big\|\|\lambda^{\prime}\|_{2}-\|\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(I_{0})}\leq\big\|\|\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(I_{0})}=\|\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}

so that the assertions follow from (
1.3
).
∎

It would be interesting to have quantitative versions of the continuity results.

Question 1.5
.

Are the continuous solution maps
\mathcal{S}
in
Theorem

1.1
and
Corollary

1.2
uniformly continuous
and, if yes, is there an effective modulus of continuity?

We have the following quantitative result in the case that all roots are simple. The monic hyperbolic polynomials
P_{\mathsf{a}}

of degree
d
with
d
simple roots are in one-to-one correspondence with the points
\mathsf{a}
in the interior
\operatorname{Hyp}^{\circ}(d)
of
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and
k\geq 1
.
The solution map

\mathcal{S}^{\circ}:C^{k}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}^{\circ}(d))\to C^{k}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\mathsf{a}\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ\mathsf{a},

is locally Lipschitz continuous:
let
U_{0}\Subset U
and
V_{0}\Subset\operatorname{Hyp}^{\circ}(d)
be relatively compact open convex sets
and
B
a bounded subset of

.
Then, for all
\mathsf{a}_{1},\mathsf{a}_{2}\in B
,

\|\mathcal{S}^{\circ}(\mathsf{a}_{1})-\mathcal{S}^{\circ}(\mathsf{a}_{2})\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U}_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\,\|\mathsf{a}_{1}-\mathsf{a}_{2}\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U}_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})},

where
C=C(d,k,B,V_{0})
.

We do not know if the continuity results in

Theorem

1.1
,
Corollary

1.2
,
Theorem

1.3
, and
Corollary

1.4

still hold for the solution map
\mathcal{S}
on
C^{d-1,1}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
(instead of
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
).

Question 1.7
.

Is the solution map
\mathcal{S}:C^{d-1,1}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
continuous, for
1\leq q<\infty
?
Is the solution map
\mathcal{S}:C^{d-1,1}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))\to C^{0,\alpha}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
continuous, for
0\leq\alpha<1
?

In the proof of
Theorem

1.3
, we need the convergence of the coefficient vectors in
C^{d}

only on the accumulation points of the preimage under
\mathsf{a}
of the discriminant locus.
If this preimage is the union of
an open set and a set of measure zero, then for (
1.3
)
it is enough that
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d-1,1}
.
Thus, for a potential counterexample
\mathsf{a}
has to meet the discriminant locus in a Cantor-like set with positive measure.

Remark 1.8
.

If the coefficients are of class
C^{d}
, as in the setting of
Theorem

1.3
, the roots of
P_{\mathsf{a}_{n}}
can be chosen as
C^{1}
functions

\lambda_{n,1},\ldots,\lambda_{n,d}:I\to\mathbb{R}
,
see
[
COP12
]
or
[
PR15
, Theorem 2.4]
.
This choice is not necessarily unique. Also, such a choice imposes an order on the roots that may change when the parameter changes.
Therefore, if the parameter space is a circle and not an interval, a consistent choice may not be possible; see e.g.
Remark

7.15
.
Moreover, in general, the roots
\lambda_{n,1},\ldots,\lambda_{n,d}
do not converge to a differentiable
system of the roots of
P_{\mathsf{a}}
(even just pointwise) as
n\to\infty
, see
Example

1.12
.

1.3.
Applications

We will give several applications of our continuity results
by highlighting, in particular, several consequences for stability under perturbations.

1.3.1.
Relation to the results for general polynomials

In
Section

7.1
,
we will interpret the results for hyperbolic polynomials as special and stronger versions of the general theorems of

[
PR24
]
.
In the general case of a complex polynomial
P_{\mathsf{a}}
of degree
d
the coefficient vector
\mathsf{a}
is an
arbitrary element of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Then
it is natural to consider the
unordered

d
-tuple of roots because there is no
canonical choice of a parameterization of the roots by continuous functions. If the parameter space has
dimension
\geq 2
, then continuous selections of the roots might not even exist.
In contrast to the hyperbolic case, the general theorems of
[
PR24
]
are only valid
in
W^{1,q}
, for
1\leq q<d/(d-1)
.

1.3.2.
Continuity of the area of the solution map

In
Section

7.2
, we will deduce from
Theorem

1.1
that, if
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))

as
n\to\infty
, where
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is open,
then the Jacobian
|J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n}))|
of
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})
converges to the Jacobian

|J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}))|
of
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})
in
L^{q}_{\operatorname{loc}}
,
for all
1\leq q<\infty
(see
Corollary

7.5
).
Combining this with the area formula, we conclude
that the surface area of the graph of each single root

\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})_{j}=\lambda^{\uparrow}_{j}\circ\mathsf{a}_{n}
, for
1\leq j\leq d
,
converges locally to the surface area of the graph of
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})_{j}
(see
Corollary

7.7
).

As a consequence, we find that the area of the zero sets of
C^{d}
families of hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d

locally has a lower semicontinuity property:

Corollary 1.9
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open.
Let
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
.
For any relatively compact open
U_{0}\Subset U
,
consider the zero sets

(1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z&=\{(x,y)\in U_{0}\times\mathbb{R}:P_{\mathsf{a}(x)}(y)=0\}\quad\text{ and }\\
Z_{n}&=\{(x,y)\in U_{0}\times\mathbb{R}:P_{\mathsf{a}_{n}(x)}(y)=0\},\quad n\geq 1.\end{split}

Then

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z_{n})\geq\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z).

Here
\mathcal{H}^{m}
denotes the
m
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.

Corollary

1.9
will be restated and proved in
Corollary

7.8
.

Without hyperbolicity, the area of the real zero set is generally not semicontinuous:
e.g., for the intersections
Z_{t}
of Whitney’s umbrella
\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x^{2}-y^{2}z=0\}

with the planes
\{z=t\}
and the cylinder
\{x^{2}+y^{2}<1\}
we have

\mathcal{H}^{1}(Z_{t})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }t<0,\\
2&\text{ if }t=0,\\
4&\text{ if }t>0.\end{cases}

1.3.3.
Approximation by hyperbolic polynomials with simple roots

In
Section

7.3
, combining our results with a lemma of Wakabayashi
[
Wak86
]
, we will
obtain the following approximation result (
Corollary

7.10
):
for each hyperbolic polynomial
P_{\mathsf{a}}
, where
\mathsf{a}\in C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
,
there exists a sequence
(\mathsf{a}_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subseteq C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
such that

•

\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
;

•

all roots of
P_{\mathsf{a}_{n}(x)}
are simple for all
x\in U
and all
n\geq 1
;

•

\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})\in C^{d}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for all
n\geq 1
, and
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})
in
C^{0,1}_{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
for all
1\leq q<\infty
, as
n\to\infty
;

•

for each relatively compact open
U_{0}\subseteq U
,
defining the zero sets
Z
and
Z_{n}
as in (
1.4
),
the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z_{n})
exists and satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z_{n})\geq\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z).

1.3.4.
Perturbation theory for Hermitian matrices

In
Section

7.4
,
we will apply our results to the eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices.
Ordering the eigenvalues increasingly,
induces a continuous map

\lambda^{\uparrow}:\operatorname{Herm}(d)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

on the real vector space
\operatorname{Herm}(d)
of complex Hermitian
d\times d
matrices.
By Weyl’s perturbation theorem (see
Proposition

7.11
), we obtain a bounded map

\mathcal{E}:=(\lambda^{\uparrow})_{*}:C^{0,1}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A.

The continuity results for hyperbolic polynomials imply the following result.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open. Then the map

\mathcal{E}:C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

is continuous, for all
1\leq q<\infty
, and the map

\mathcal{E}:C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,\alpha}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

is continuous, for all
0<\alpha<1
.

Theorem

1.10
will be proved in
Corollary

7.12
.
The map
\mathcal{E}
is not continuous with respect to the
C^{0,1}
topology on the target space,
as will be seen in
Example

7.13
which is based on
Example

1.12
.

Given that the map
\mathcal{E}
is defined and bounded on
C^{0,1}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))
,
it is natural to ask whether in
Theorem

1.10
one can replace
C^{d}
by
C^{1}
:

Question 1.11
.

Is the map
\mathcal{E}:C^{1}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
continuous, for
1\leq q<\infty
?
Is
\mathcal{E}:C^{1}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,\alpha}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
continuous, for
0<\alpha<1
?

We will prove in
Proposition

7.14
that the answer to
Question

1.11
is affirmative in the case
d=2
.

1.4.
On the optimality of the results

The following example shows that the solution map
\mathcal{S}:C^{d}(I,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))\to C^{0,1}(I,\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
where
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an open interval,
is not continuous
with respect to the
C^{0,1}
topology on the target space.

Example 1.12
.

Let
g(x):=x^{2}
and
g_{n}(x):=x^{2}+1/n^{2}
,
n\geq 1
.
Then, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and each bounded open interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,

\|g-g_{n}\|_{C^{k}(\overline{I})}=1/n^{2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
f
and
f_{n}
be the positive square roots of
g
and
g_{n}
, respectively:

f(x):=|x|
and
f_{n}(x):=\sqrt{x^{2}+1/n^{2}}
.
Then, for each bounded open interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
containing
0
,

\displaystyle|f-f_{n}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I})}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{0<x\in I}\Big|\frac{(f(x)-f_{n}(x))-(f(0)-f_{n}(0))}{x}\Big|

\displaystyle=\sup_{0<x\in I}\Big|\frac{x-\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}+\frac{1}{n}}{x}\Big|\geq\Big|\frac{\frac{1}{n}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}+\frac{1}{n}}{\frac{1}{n}}\Big|=2-\sqrt{2},

for large enough
n
.
Observe that

f_{n}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}}

tends pointwise to
f^{\prime}(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)
for all
x\neq 0
but not uniformly on any neighborhood of
0
:

f_{n}^{\prime}(\pm\tfrac{1}{n})=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

This also violates the first conclusion of
Corollary

1.4
for
q=\infty
.

Notice that this example also shows that
not every continuous (thus
C^{0,1}
) system of the roots of
g
is the limit of a continuous system of the roots of
g_{n}
:
each continuous system of the roots of
g_{n}
tends to
\pm|x|
, none to
\pm x
. See
Remark

1.8
.

In the example, the hyperbolic polynomial
Z^{2}=g(x)
with double root at
x=0
is approximated by the
hyperbolic polynomials
Z^{2}=g_{n}(x)
with simple roots for all
x
.
We will see in
Corollary

7.10
that such an approximation is always possible.

Notation

The
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{m}
.
If not stated otherwise, ‘measurable’ means ‘Lebesgue measurable’ and ‘almost everywhere’ means
‘almost everywhere with respect to Lebesgue measure’. For measurable
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we usually write

|E|=\mathcal{L}^{m}(E)
.
We will also use the
k
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{k}
.

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
\|x\|_{p}
denotes the
p
-norm of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If
f:E\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for measurable
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, is a measurable map, then we set

\|f\|_{L^{p}(E,\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\big\|\|f\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{p}(E)}.

In the following, a set is called

if it is either finite or has the cardinality of
\mathbb{N}
.

To avoid confusion,

written in
sans serif
type. For example,
the coefficient vector
\mathsf{a}_{n}=(a_{n,1},a_{n,2},\ldots,a_{n,d})
, indexed by
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

a_{n}
,
which denotes the
n
-th component of the coefficient vector
\mathsf{a}
.

We use the notation
C(d,\ldots)
to denote a constant that depends only on
d,\ldots
; its value may change
from line to line.

2.
Function spaces

Let us fix notation and recall background on the function spaces used in this paper.

2.1.
Hölder–Lipschitz spaces

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then
C^{k}(U)
is the space of
k
-times continuously differentiable real valued functions, equipped
with its natural Fréchet topology.
If
U
is bounded, then
C^{k}(\overline{U})
denotes the space of all
f\in C^{k}(U)

such that each
\partial^{\alpha}f
,
0\leq|\alpha|\leq k
,
has a continuous extension to the closure
\overline{U}
. Endowed with the norm

\|f\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U})}:=\max_{|\alpha|\leq k}\sup_{x\in U}|\partial^{\alpha}f(x)|

it is a Banach space.
For
0<\gamma\leq 1
, we consider the Hölder–Lipschitz seminorm

|f|_{C^{0,\gamma}(\overline{U})}:=\sup_{x,y\in U,\,x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\|x-y\|_{2}^{\gamma}}.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\gamma\leq 1
, we have the Banach space

C^{k,\gamma}(\overline{U}):=\{f\in C^{k}(\overline{U}):\|f\|_{C^{k,\gamma}(\overline{U})}<\infty\},

where

\|f\|_{C^{k,\gamma}(\overline{U})}:=\|f\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U})}+\max_{|\alpha|=k}|\partial^{\alpha}f|_{C^{0,\gamma}(\overline{U})}.

We write
C^{k,\gamma}(U)
for the space of
C^{k}
functions on
U
that
belong to
C^{k,\gamma}(\overline{V})
for each relatively compact open
V\Subset U
,
and endow
C^{k,\gamma}(U)
with its natural Fréchet topology.

2.2.
Lebesgue spaces

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and
1\leq p\leq\infty
.
We denote by
L^{p}(U)
the Lebesgue space with respect to the
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{m}
,
and
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(U)}
is the corresponding
L^{p}
-norm.
We will also use the space
L^{p}_{\operatorname{loc}}(U)
of measurable functions
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\|f\|_{L^{p}(K)}<\infty
for all compact subsets
K\subseteq U
.
For Lebesgue measurable sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
we also write
|E|=\mathcal{L}^{m}(E)
.
We remark that for continuous functions
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
we have (and use interchangeably)

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}=\|f\|_{C^{0}(\overline{U})}
.

2.3.
Sobolev spaces

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq q\leq\infty
,
we consider the Sobolev space

W^{k,q}(U):=\{f\in L^{q}(U):\partial^{\alpha}f\in L^{q}(U)\text{ for }|\alpha|\leq k\},

where
\partial^{\alpha}f
are distributional derivatives. Endowed with the norm

\|f\|_{W^{k,q}(U)}:=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}\|\partial^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{q}(U)}

it is a Banach space.
We will also use

W^{k,q}_{\operatorname{loc}}(U):=\{f\in L^{q}_{\operatorname{loc}}(U):\partial^{\alpha}f\in L^{q}_{\operatorname{loc}}(U)\text{ for }|\alpha|\leq k\}

and endow this space
with its natural topology.

2.4.
A result on composition

In the following proposition we use the norm

\|f\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})}:=\max_{0\leq j\leq k}\sup_{x\in U}\|d^{j}f(x)\|_{L_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})}

on the space
C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell}):=(C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}))^{\ell}
, where
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}

and
L_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
is the space of
j
-linear maps with
j
arguments in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and values in
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
be open, bounded, and convex.
Let
\psi\in C^{k+1}(\overline{V},\mathbb{R}^{p})
. Then

\psi_{*}:C^{k}(\overline{U},V)\to C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{p}),\quad\varphi\mapsto\psi\circ\varphi,

is well-defined and continuous.
More precisely, for
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
in a bounded subset
B
of
C^{k}(\overline{U},V)
,

\|\psi_{*}(\varphi_{1})-\psi_{*}(\varphi_{2})\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{p})}\leq C\,\|\psi\|_{C^{k+1}(\overline{V},\mathbb{R}^{p})}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})},

where
C=C(k,B)
.

A short proof of this result can be found in
[
PR24
, Appendix A.2]
.

3.
Hyperbolic polynomials

In this section, we recall basic facts on hyperbolic polynomials that will be used below.
The exposition follows
[
PR15
]
and
[
PR25
]
.
For the convenience of the reader and to keep the paper largely self-contained,
we include details where this does not substantially interrupt the flow.

3.1.
Tschirnhausen form

We say that a monic polynomial

P_{\mathsf{a}}(Z)=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}Z^{d-j}

is in
Tschirnhausen form
if
a_{1}=0
.
Every polynomial
P_{\mathsf{a}}
can be put in Tschirnhausen form by the substitution

P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}(Z)=P_{\mathsf{a}}(Z-\tfrac{a_{1}}{d})=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=2}^{d}\tilde{a}_{j}Z^{d-j},

which is called the
Tschirnhausen transformation
.
For clarity, we consistently equip the coefficients of polynomials in Tschirnhausen form with a ‘tilde’.
Note that

(3.1)

\tilde{a}_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{j}C_{i}a_{i}a_{1}^{j-i},\quad 2\leq j\leq d,

where
a_{0}=1
and the
C_{i}
are universal constants independent of
\mathsf{a}
.
For a polynomial
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}
in Tschirnhausen form with coefficient vector

\tilde{\mathsf{a}}=(0,\tilde{a}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{a}_{d})

we have

(3.2)

-2\tilde{a}_{2}=\lambda_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}^{2},

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
is an enumeration of the roots of
\tilde{\mathsf{a}}
.
Consequently, for a hyperbolic polynomial
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}
in Tschirnhausen form,

\tilde{a}_{2}\leq 0.

Recall that the coefficients (up to their sign) are the elementary symmetric polynomials in the roots, by Vieta’s formulas.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
PR25
, Lemma 2.4]
)
.

The coefficients of a hyperbolic polynomial
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}
in Tschirnhausen form satisfy

|\tilde{a}_{j}|^{1/j}\leq\sqrt{2}\,|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{1/2},\quad j=2,\ldots,d.

Proof.

This follows easily from the Newton identities

j\sigma_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}(-1)^{i-1}\sigma_{j-i}s_{i},\quad d\geq j\geq 1,

between the Newton polynomials
s_{k}=\lambda_{1}^{k}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}^{k}

and the elementary symmetric polynomials
\sigma_{k}
,
observing that
|s_{k}|^{1/k}\leq|s_{2}|^{1/2}
, for
2\leq k\leq d
, by a well-known relation between the
p
-norms.
∎

As a consequence,
\tilde{\mathsf{a}}=(0,\tilde{a}_{2},\tilde{a}_{3},\ldots,\tilde{a}_{d})=0
if and only if
\tilde{a}_{2}=0
.

Definition 3.2

(Spaces of hyperbolic polynomials)
.

Let
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d)
denote the space of monic hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
in Tschirnhausen form and
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)

the compact subspace of polynomials
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}
with
\tilde{a}_{2}=-1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d)

\displaystyle=\{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}\in\operatorname{Hyp}(d):\tilde{a}_{1}=0\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)

\displaystyle=\{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}\in\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d):\tilde{a}_{2}=-1\}.

3.2.
Splitting

Let us recall a simple consequence of the inverse function theorem.

Lemma 3.3

(E.g.
[
PR25
, Lemma 2.5]
)
.

Let
P_{\mathsf{a}}=P_{\mathsf{b}}P_{\mathsf{c}}
,
where
P_{\mathsf{b}}
and
P_{\mathsf{c}}
are monic real polynomials without common (complex) root.
Then
we have
P=P_{\mathsf{b}(P)}P_{\mathsf{c}(P)}
for analytic mappings
P\mapsto\mathsf{b}(P)\in\mathbb{R}^{\deg P_{\mathsf{b}}}

and
P\mapsto\mathsf{c}(P)\in\mathbb{R}^{\deg P_{\mathsf{c}}}
,
defined for
P
near
P_{\mathsf{a}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\deg P_{\mathsf{a}}}
, with the given initial values.

Proof.

The product
P_{\mathsf{a}}=P_{\mathsf{b}}P_{\mathsf{c}}
defines on the coefficients a polynomial map

\varphi
such that
\mathsf{a}=\varphi(\mathsf{b},\mathsf{c})
.
Its Jacobian determinant equals the resultant of
P_{\mathsf{b}}
and
P_{\mathsf{c}}
which is nonzero, by assumption.
Thus
\varphi
can be inverted locally, by the inverse function theorem.
∎

Let
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}\in\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d)
be such that
\tilde{\mathsf{a}}\neq 0
, equivalently,
\tilde{a}_{2}\neq 0
.
Then the polynomial

Q_{\underline{\mathsf{a}}}(Z):=|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{-d/2}P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}(|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{1/2}Z)=Z^{d}-Z^{d-2}+\sum_{j=3}^{d}|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{-j/2}\tilde{a}_{j}Z^{d-j}

belongs to
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)
.
By
Lemma

3.3
,
we have a splitting

Q_{\underline{\mathsf{a}}}=Q_{\underline{\mathsf{b}}}Q_{\underline{\mathsf{c}}},

on some open neighborhood
\underline{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
\underline{\mathsf{a}}

such that
d_{\mathsf{b}}:=\deg Q_{\underline{\mathsf{b}}}<d
,
d_{\mathsf{c}}:=\deg Q_{\underline{\mathsf{c}}}<d
, and

\underline{b}_{i}=\psi_{i}(|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{-3/2}\tilde{a}_{3},\ldots,|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{-d/2}\tilde{a}_{d}),\quad i=1,\dots,\deg Q_{\underline{\mathsf{b}}},

where
\psi_{i}
are real analytic functions; likewise for
\underline{c}_{i}
.
If
Q_{\underline{\mathsf{a}}}
is hyperbolic, then also
Q_{\underline{\mathsf{b}}}
and
Q_{\underline{\mathsf{c}}}
are hyperbolic.
If
\underline{\lambda}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\underline{\lambda}_{d}
are the roots of
Q_{\underline{\mathsf{a}}}
, then
we may assume that, on

\underline{U}\cap\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)
,

\underline{\lambda}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\underline{\lambda}_{d_{\mathsf{b}}}
are the roots of
Q_{\underline{\mathsf{b}}}
and

\underline{\lambda}_{d_{\mathsf{b}}+1}\leq\cdots\leq\underline{\lambda}_{d}
are the roots of
Q_{\underline{\mathsf{c}}}
;
this follows from continuity of the map
\lambda^{\uparrow}
and
the simple topology of
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)
(induced by the embedding in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
); cf.
[
PR25
, Theorem 8.1]
.

The splitting
Q_{\underline{\mathsf{a}}}=Q_{\underline{\mathsf{b}}}Q_{\underline{\mathsf{c}}}
induces a splitting

P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}=P_{\mathsf{b}}P_{\mathsf{c}}

on an open neighborhood
\tilde{U}
of
\tilde{\mathsf{a}}
,
where

(3.3)

b_{i}=|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{i/2}\psi_{i}(|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{-3/2}\tilde{a}_{3},\ldots,|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{-d/2}\tilde{a}_{d}),\quad i=1,\dots,\deg P_{\mathsf{b}}.

The coefficients
\tilde{b}_{i}
of
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{b}}}
, resulting from
P_{\mathsf{b}}
by the Tschirnhausen transformation,
have an analogous representation, i.e.,

(3.4)

\tilde{b}_{i}=|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{i/2}\tilde{\psi}_{i}(|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{-3/2}\tilde{a}_{3},\ldots,|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{-d/2}\tilde{a}_{d}),\quad i=1,\dots,\deg P_{\mathsf{b}}.

Shrinking
\tilde{U}
slightly, we may assume that all partial derivatives of all orders of the real analytic functions
\psi_{i}

and
\tilde{\psi}_{i}
are bounded on
\tilde{U}
.

Furthermore, since the roots of
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}
are given by
\lambda_{j}:=|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{1/2}\cdot\underline{\lambda}_{j}
,
for
1\leq j\leq d
,
we have that,
on

\tilde{U}\cap\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d)
,

\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d_{\mathsf{b}}}
are the roots of
P_{\mathsf{b}}
and

\lambda_{d_{\mathsf{b}}+1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d}
are the roots of
P_{\mathsf{c}}
.

Lemma 3.4

(
[
PR25
, Lemma 3.13]
)
.

In this situation, we have
|\tilde{b}_{2}|\leq 4\,|\tilde{a}_{2}|
.

Proof.

Using (
3.2
) and
|b_{1}|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mathsf{b}}}|\lambda_{j}|\leq\sqrt{d}_{\mathsf{b}}\,\big(\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mathsf{b}}}\lambda_{j}^{2}\big)^{1/2}
, we find

\displaystyle 2\,|\tilde{b}_{2}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mathsf{b}}}\Big(\lambda_{j}+\frac{b_{1}}{d_{\mathsf{b}}}\Big)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mathsf{b}}}\lambda_{j}^{2}+\frac{2b_{1}}{d_{\mathsf{b}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mathsf{b}}}\lambda_{j}+\frac{b_{1}^{2}}{d_{\mathsf{b}}}\leq(1+2+1)\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mathsf{b}}}\lambda_{j}^{2}\leq 8\,|\tilde{a}_{2}|.

∎

3.3.
Universal splitting

For each
d\geq 2
fix the following data.
Choose a finite cover of
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)
by open sets
\underline{U}_{1},\ldots,\underline{U}_{s}

such that on each
\underline{U}_{i}
we have a splitting

Q_{\underline{\mathsf{a}}}=Q_{\underline{\mathsf{b}}}Q_{\underline{\mathsf{c}}}
and, consequently, a splitting

P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}=P_{\mathsf{b}}P_{\mathsf{c}}
as above
together with analytic functions
\psi_{i}
and
\tilde{\psi}_{i}
,
and we fix this splitting. As seen above, we may assume that the roots
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d}
of
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}

are labelled such that
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d_{\mathsf{b}}}
are the roots of
P_{\mathsf{b}}
and

\lambda_{d_{\mathsf{b}}+1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d}
are the roots of
P_{\mathsf{c}}
.

By the Lebesgue covering lemma, there exists
\delta>0
such that each subset of
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)

of diameter less that
\delta
is contained in some
\underline{U}_{i}
.
Choose
r\in(0,\min\{\delta/2,1\})
.
Then
for each
\underline{\mathsf{p}}\in\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)

there exists
1\leq i\leq s
with

(3.5)

B(\underline{\mathsf{p}},r)\cap\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)\subseteq\underline{U}_{i}\cap\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d).

Definition 3.5

(Universal splitting)
.

We refer to this data as a
universal splitting of hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
in
Tschirnhausen form
and to
r
as the
radius of the splitting
.

4.
Bronshtein’s theorem and a variant at a single point

We recall Bronshtein’s theorem in
Theorem

4.1
.
We shall need a version at a single point with a suitable bound for the
derivative of the roots. This version is given in
Theorem

4.7
.

4.1.
Bronshtein’s theorem

The following result is a version of Bronshtein’s theorem
[
Bro79
]

with uniform bounds
due to
[
PR15
]
, see also
[
PR25
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval
and
\mathsf{a}\in C^{d-1,1}(I,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
.
Then any continuous root
\lambda\in C^{0}(I)
of
P_{\mathsf{a}}
is locally Lipschitz
and, for any pair of relatively compact open intervals
I_{0}\Subset I_{1}\Subset I
,

(4.1)

|\lambda|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I}_{0})}\leq C\,\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\|a_{j}\|^{1/j}_{C^{d-1,1}(\overline{I}_{1})},

with
C=C(d)\,\max\{\delta^{-1},1\}
, where
\delta:=\operatorname{dist}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{1})
.

A multiparameter version follows easily; see
[
PR15
]
and
[
PR25
, Theorem 3.4]
.

Note that Wakabayashi
[
Wak86
]
proved a Hölder version of
Theorem

4.1

(without uniform bounds of the type (
4.1
)),
see also Tarama
[
Tar06
]
for a different proof.

4.2.
Reclusive points

The local version of Bronshtein’s theorem,
Theorem

4.7
, holds at all points of
I
except for a countable subset of points,
which we call reclusive points. A point
x\in I
is reclusive if either all the roots of
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}(x)}
are zero and
x
is
isolated for this property, or it satisfies a similar condition for one of the local factors of
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}(x)}
,
see
Definition

4.4
for a precise formulation.

Definition 4.2

(Zero sets)
.

Let
\tilde{\mathsf{a}}:I\to\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We consider the zero set

Z_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}:=\{x\in I:\tilde{\mathsf{a}}(x)=0\}=\{x\in I:\text{ all roots of $P_{\mathsf{a}(x)}$ coincide}\}

which coincides with
Z_{\tilde{a}_{2}}=\{x\in I:\tilde{a}_{2}(x)=0\}
, by
Lemma

3.1
.
(For notational simplicity, we will generally use
Z_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}
.)
We write
\operatorname{acc}(Z_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}})
and
\operatorname{iso}(Z_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}):=Z_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}\setminus\operatorname{acc}(Z_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}})

for the sets of accumulation points and isolated points of
Z_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}
, respectively.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\tilde{\mathsf{a}}:I\to\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then

Z_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}=\{x\in I:\text{ all roots of }P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}(x)}\text{ vanish}\}.

Proof.

Since
Z_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}=Z_{\tilde{a}_{2}}
, this is immediate from (
3.2
).
∎

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval
and
\tilde{\mathsf{a}}\in C^{d-1,1}(I,\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d))
;
recall that this means
\tilde{\mathsf{a}}\in C^{d-1,1}(I,\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\tilde{\mathsf{a}}(I)\subseteq\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d)
.
Let
x_{0}\in I
be such that
\tilde{a}_{2}(x_{0})\neq 0
.
Then not all roots of
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}(x_{0})}
coincide and hence
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}
splits in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
. We may assume that it is a
full splitting
, i.e.,
if
\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}\}
are the distinct roots of
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}(x_{0})}
with multiplicities
\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\}

then

(4.2)

P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}=P_{\mathsf{b}_{1}}P_{\mathsf{b}_{2}}\cdots P_{\mathsf{b}_{k}}\quad\text{ in a neighborhood of }x_{0},

where
\deg P_{\mathsf{b}_{j}}=m_{j}
and

.

Let

\displaystyle A(\delta)

\displaystyle:=\max\big\{\delta^{-1}|\tilde{a}_{2}(x_{0})|^{1/2},|\tilde{a}_{2}^{\prime}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I}(x_{0},\delta))}^{1/2}\big\},

\displaystyle A_{2}(\delta)

The proof follows the general strategy of the proof of

]

]

Before we prove

4.4.
Local Glaeser inequality

Glaeser’s inequality
[
Gla63
]
gives Bronshtein’s theorem in the simplest nontrivial case:
for nonnegative
C^{1}
functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}
with
f^{\prime\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
we have

f^{\prime}(x)^{2}\leq 2\,f(x)\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

We need a local version.

Lemma 4.8

(
[
PR25
, Lemma 3.14]
)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open bounded interval.
Let
f\in C^{1,1}(\overline{I})
satisfy
f\geq 0
or
f\leq 0
on
I
.
Assume that
x_{0}\in I
satisfies
f(x_{0})\neq 0

and let
M>0
be such that
I_{0}:=I(x_{0},M^{-1}|f(x_{0})|^{1/2})\subseteq I
.
Then

|f^{\prime}(x_{0})|\leq(M+M^{-1}|f^{\prime}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I}_{0})})|f(x_{0})|^{1/2}.

Therefore, if additionally
|f^{\prime}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I}_{0})}\leq M^{2}
, then

|f^{\prime}(x_{0})|\leq 2M\,|f(x_{0})|^{1/2}.

It should be added that, for a function

f\in C^{1,1}(\overline{I})
satisfying
f\geq 0
or
f\leq 0
on
I
,
we have that
f(x_{0})=0
implies
f^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
, so that the conclusion of the lemma also holds
trivially at zeros
x_{0}
of
f
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f\geq 0
; otherwise consider
-f
.
Thus
f(x_{0})>0
and

0\leq f(x_{0}+h)=f(x_{0})+f^{\prime}(x_{0})h+\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(x_{0}+hs)-f^{\prime}(x_{0})\,ds\cdot h.

Setting
h:=\pm M^{-1}|f(x_{0})|^{1/2}
, implies the lemma.
∎

4.5.
Interpolation

Let us recall an interpolation inequality for intermediate derivatives.

Lemma 4.9

(
[
PR25
, Lemma 3.16]
)
.

Let
f\in C^{m,1}(\overline{I})
, where
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a bounded open interval.
Then, for
1\leq j\leq m
,

|f^{(j)}(x)|\leq C(m)\,|I|^{-j}\big(\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}+|f^{(m)}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I})}|I|^{m+1}\big),\quad x\in I.

Proof.

Fix
x\in I
. Then
[x,x+|I|/2)
or
(x-|I|/2,x]
is contained in
I
.
Let
y
be a point in the respective interval.
By Taylor’s formula,

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{j=0}^{m}\frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}(y-x)^{j}\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|f(y)-(y-x)^{m}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}(f^{(m)}(x+t(y-x))-f^{(m)}(x))\,dt\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}+|I|^{m+1}|f^{(m)}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I})}.

This implies the lemma in view of the following fact:
if a polynomial
T(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots+a_{m}x^{m}\in\mathbb{C}[x]
satisfies
|T(x)|\leq A
for
x\in[0,B]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
then

|a_{j}|\leq C(m)\,AB^{-j},\quad 0\leq j\leq m.

Indeed, for
A=B=1
this follows easily, by comparison with the
interpolation polynomial for the equidistant points

0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{m}=1
. In the general case, consider
A^{-1}T(Bx)
.
∎

5.
Proof of the main technical result

The goal of this section is the proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval.
Let
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(I,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
,
i.e.,
for each relatively compact open interval
I_{1}\Subset I
,

\|\mathsf{a}-\mathsf{a}_{n}\|_{C^{d}(\overline{I}_{1},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

It follows from
Theorem

4.1
that the set

\{\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n}):n\geq 1\}
is bounded in
C^{0,1}(I,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
We must show that, for each relatively compact open interval
I_{0}\Subset I
and each
1\leq q<\infty
,

(5.1)

\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})-\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})\|_{W^{1,q}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

5.1.
Strategy of the proof

The proof of (
5.1
) is subdivided into three steps. The first two steps are dedicated to the proof of

(5.2)

\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})^{\prime}-\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for
1\leq q<\infty
,
using the dominated convergence theorem. In the third step, we show that

(5.3)

\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})-\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Then (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) imply (
5.1
).

Step 1

We claim that the sequence of derivatives
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}
is dominated almost everywhere on
I_{0}
by a positive constant.

To see this, fix
I_{0}\Subset I_{1}\Subset I
.
By the assumption of
Theorem

1.3
,

\{\mathsf{a}_{n}|_{I_{1}}:n\geq 1\}
is a bounded subset of
C^{d-1,1}(\overline{I}_{1},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
By
Theorem

4.1
, the derivative of
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})
exists almost everywhere in
I_{0}
and
satisfies

\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\,\sup_{n\geq 1}\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\|a_{n,j}\|^{1/j}_{C^{d-1,1}(\overline{I}_{1})}=:B<\infty.

This implies the claim.

Step 2

The aim of this step is to prove the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval.
Let
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(I,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
.
Then, for almost every
x\in I
,

\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}(x)\to\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})^{\prime}(x)\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

By Step 1 and 2, the dominated convergence theorem yields that
(
5.2
) holds, for each relatively compact open interval
I_{0}\Subset I
and
each
1\leq q<\infty
.

Step 3

Now we show (
5.3
).
Fix
x_{0}\in I_{0}
. We have

(5.4)

\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})(x_{0})=\lambda^{\uparrow}(\mathsf{a}_{n}(x_{0}))\to\lambda^{\uparrow}(\mathsf{a}(x_{0}))=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})(x_{0})\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

since the map
\lambda^{\uparrow}:\operatorname{Hyp}(d)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is continuous (cf.
[
AKLM98
, Lemma 4.1]
).
For arbitrary
x\in I_{0}
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})(x)-\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})(x)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})(x_{0})-\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})(x_{0})+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})^{\prime}(t)-\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}(t)\,dt\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})(x_{0})-\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})(x_{0})\|_{2}+\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})^{\prime}-\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Thus, (
5.2
) and (
5.4
) imply (
5.3
).

Remark 5.2
.

Alternatively, (
5.3
) is a consequence of

[
PR24
, Corollary 6.5]
and
Lemma

7.1
;
for this argument
it is actually enough that
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{0}(\overline{I}_{0},\operatorname{Hyp}(d))

as
n\to\infty
.

Therefore, in order to prove
Theorem

1.3

it remains to show
Theorem

5.1
.
The rest of the section is devoted to the proof of
Theorem

5.1
.

5.2.
On the zero set of
\tilde{\mathsf{a}}

Recall that
\operatorname{acc}(Z_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}})
denotes the set of accumulation points of the zero set
Z_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}

of

. By

.

Let

∎

7.1.
Relation to the results for general polynomials

The case of general complex (not necessarily hyperbolic) polynomials is treated in
[
PR24
]

which builds on the results of
[
PR16
,
PR18
]
.
The crucial difference is that in general there is no canonical choice of a continuous ordered
d
-tuple of the complex roots.
Even worse, if the parameter space is at least two-dimensional, then a parameterization of the roots by continuous functions might not exist;
but there exist parameterizations by functions of bounded variation, see
[
PR20a
]
.
Therefore the continuity results in
[
PR24
]
are formulated in terms of the
unordered

d
-tuple of the roots.

Let us compare the results obtained in this paper with the ones of
[
PR24
]
.
To this end, we investigate the metric space
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
of unordered
d
-tuples of real numbers.
It is a simple instance of the space
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
considered in
[
Alm00
]
and
[
DLS11
]
.

For
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
[x]=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
be the corresponding unordered
d
-tuple,
i.e., the equivalence class (or orbit) of
x
with respect to the action
of the symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{d}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by permutation of the coordinates:

\sigma x:=(x_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(2)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(d)}),\quad\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d},~x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The set
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}):=\{[x]:x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
with the distance

\mathbf{d}([x],[y]):=\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\|x-\sigma y\|_{2}

is a complete metric space. If we identify the elements of
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
with formal sums

\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\llbracket x_{i}\rrbracket
, where
\llbracket x_{i}\rrbracket
denotes the Dirac mass of
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
then
\mathbf{d}
is induced by the
L^{2}
based Wasserstein metric on the space of
probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
x^{\uparrow}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the representative of the equivalence class
[x]
with increasingly ordered coordinates.
Clearly,
x^{\uparrow}
only depends on
[x]
and thus we have an injective map
(~)^{\uparrow}:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
It is a right-inverse of
[~]:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.

Lemma 7.1
.

We have

\mathbf{d}([x],[y])=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\|x^{\uparrow}-y^{\uparrow}\|_{2},\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In particular,
(~)^{\uparrow}:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
[~]:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
are Lipschitz maps.

Proof.

Evidently,

\mathbf{d}([x],[y])=\mathbf{d}([x^{\uparrow}],[y^{\uparrow}])=\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\|x^{\uparrow}-\sigma y^{\uparrow}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\|x^{\uparrow}-y^{\uparrow}\|_{2}.

Thus the assertion will follow from the claim that
\|x^{\uparrow}-y^{\uparrow}\|_{2}\leq\|x^{\uparrow}-y\|_{2}
, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For
d=2
, the claim is equivalent to

(x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+(x_{2}-y_{2})^{2}\leq(x_{1}-y_{2})^{2}+(x_{2}-y_{1})^{2}

whenever
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
and
y_{1}\leq y_{2}
.
By a simple computation, it is further equivalent to the true statement
(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1})\geq 0
.
The general case follows from the fact that any permutation is a finite composite of transpositions.
∎

By
Lemma

7.1
, the map
(~)^{\uparrow}:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies the conditions of an Almgren embedding
(as defined in
[
PR24
]
following
[
Alm00
]
and
[
DLS11
]
).
Thus
Theorem

1.3
can be interpreted as a special version of the general theorem
[
PR24
, Theorem 1.1]

with the important difference that
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})
in
W^{1,q}_{\operatorname{loc}}
as
n\to\infty
, see
(
1.3
), holds for each
1\leq q<\infty
,
while in the general result the corresponding fact is valid only for
1\leq q<d/(d-1)
.

For the next theorem, which is a stronger version of
[
PR24
, Theorem 1.3]
in the hyperbolic case,
we need to recall the notions of metric speed and
q
-energy.

Definition 7.2

(Metric speed and
q
-energy)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval and
\mathsf{a}\in C^{d}(I,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
.
Consider the Lipschitz curve
\Lambda(x):=[\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})(x)]
, for
x\in I
, in the metric space
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then (see
[
AGS08
]
) the limit

|\dot{\Lambda}|(x):=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathbf{d}(\Lambda(x+h),\Lambda(x))}{|h|}

exists for almost every
x\in I
and is called the
metric speed
of
\Lambda
at
x
.
The
q
-energy
of
\Lambda
on a subinterval
I_{0}\subseteq I
is defined by

\mathcal{E}_{q,I_{0}}(\Lambda):=\int_{I_{0}}\big(|\dot{\Lambda}|(x)\big)^{q}\,dx.

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval.
Let
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(I,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
.
Set
\Lambda:=[\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})]
and
\Lambda_{n}:=[\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})]
.
Then, for each relatively compact open interval
I_{0}\Subset I
,

(7.1)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{d}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{0})}

\displaystyle\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

(7.2)

\displaystyle\big\||\dot{\Lambda}|-|\dot{\Lambda}_{n}|\big\|_{L^{q}(I_{0})}

\displaystyle\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

(7.3)

\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{E}_{q,I_{0}}(\Lambda)-\mathcal{E}_{q,I_{0}}(\Lambda_{n})\big|

\displaystyle\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for each
1\leq q<\infty
.

Proof.

First, (
7.1
) is a consequence of (
5.3
) and
Lemma

7.1
.
By
[
PR24
, Lemma 11.1]
,

|\dot{\Lambda}|(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad|\dot{\Lambda}_{n}|(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}

for almost every
x\in I
.
Thus, (
7.2
) and (
7.3
) follow from
Corollary

1.4
.
∎

7.2.
Continuity of the area of the solution map

Let us first expand
Corollary

1.4
.

Corollary 7.4
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open.
Let
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
R\in\mathbb{R}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{dm}]
be any real polynomial in the
d\cdot m
variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{dm}
.
Set
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}):=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})
and

\lambda_{n}=(\lambda_{n,1},\ldots,\lambda_{n,d}):=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})
, for
n\geq 1
.
Then, for each relatively compact open subset
U_{0}\Subset U
and each
1\leq q<\infty
,

(7.4)

\Big\|R\Big((\partial_{i}\lambda_{j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq m\\
1\leq j\leq d\end{subarray}}\Big)-R\Big((\partial_{i}\lambda_{n,j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq m\\
1\leq j\leq d\end{subarray}}\Big)\Big\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

and consequently,

(7.5)

\Big\|R\Big((\partial_{i}\lambda_{n,j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq m\\
1\leq j\leq d\end{subarray}}\Big)\Big\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}\to\Big\|R\Big((\partial_{i}\lambda_{j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq m\\
1\leq j\leq d\end{subarray}}\Big)\Big\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

Clearly, (
7.5
) is a consequence of (
7.4
).

Let us prove (
7.4
).
It is enough to show the assertion for monomials
R
.
Let us proceed by induction on the degree
\ell
of the monomial
R
.
For
\ell=1
, the assertion follows from
Theorem

6.1
in view of

\displaystyle\|\partial_{i}\lambda_{j}-\partial_{i}\lambda_{n,j}\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}\leq\|\partial_{i}\lambda-\partial_{i}\lambda_{n}\|_{L^{q}(U_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

If
\ell\geq 2
, then, by Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|\partial_{i_{1}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{\ell}}\lambda_{j_{\ell}}-\partial_{i_{1}}\lambda_{n,j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{\ell}}\lambda_{n,j_{\ell}}\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\partial_{i_{1}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{\ell}}\lambda_{j_{\ell}}-\partial_{i_{1}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{\ell-1}}\lambda_{j_{\ell-1}}\cdot\partial_{i_{\ell}}\lambda_{n,j_{\ell}}\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\partial_{i_{1}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{\ell-1}}\lambda_{j_{\ell-1}}\cdot\partial_{i_{\ell}}\lambda_{n,j_{\ell}}-\partial_{i_{1}}\lambda_{n,j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{\ell}}\lambda_{n,j_{\ell}}\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\partial_{i_{1}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{\ell-1}}\lambda_{j_{\ell-1}}\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{0})}\|\partial_{i_{\ell}}\lambda_{j_{\ell}}-\partial_{i_{\ell}}\lambda_{n,j_{\ell}}\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\partial_{i_{1}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{\ell-1}}\lambda_{j_{\ell-1}}-\partial_{i_{1}}\lambda_{n,j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{\ell-1}}\lambda_{n,j_{\ell-1}}\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}\|\partial_{i_{\ell}}\lambda_{n,j_{\ell}}\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{0})}

which tends to zero as
n\to 0
, by the induction hypothesis, because

\|\partial_{i_{1}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{i_{\ell-1}}\lambda_{j_{\ell-1}}\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{0})}\leq C\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\partial_{i_{\ell}}\lambda_{n,j_{\ell}}\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{0})}\leq C

for a constant
C>0
independent of
n
and
i_{k},j_{k}
,
by Bronshtein’s theorem (see
Theorem

4.1
).
∎

Let
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

be a Lipschitz map,
where
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is open.
We recall that the
Jacobian

|Jf|
of
f
is the square root of the sum of the squares of the determinants of the
k\times k
minors with
k=\min\{m,d\}
of the Jacobian matrix

(\partial_{i}f_{j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq m\\
1\leq j\leq d\end{subarray}},

which exists almost everywhere, by Rademacher’s theorem.

Corollary 7.5
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open.
Let
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
.
Then, for each relatively compact open subset
U_{0}\Subset U
and each
1\leq q<\infty
,

\big\||J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}))|-|J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n}))|\big\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

and consequently,

\big\||J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n}))|\big\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}\to\big\||J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}))|\big\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

Let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{p}
and
M_{n,1},\ldots,M_{n,p}
denote the determinants of all the
k\times k
minors with
k=\min\{m,d\}

of the Jacobian matrices of
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})
and
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})
, respectively.
Fix
1\leq q<\infty
.
Then, by Hölder’s inequality,

\big\||J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}))|-|J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n}))|\big\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}\leq|U_{0}|^{1/(2q)}\,\big\||J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}))|-|J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n}))|\big\|_{L^{2q}(U_{0})}

and

\displaystyle\big\||J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}))|-|J(\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n}))|\big\|_{L^{2q}(U_{0})}^{2q}=\big\|\big(\sum_{i}M_{i}^{2}\big)^{1/2}-\big(\sum_{i}M_{n,i}^{2}\big)^{1/2}\big\|_{L^{2q}(U_{0})}^{2q}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\big|\sum_{i}M_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i}M_{n,i}^{2}\big|^{1/2}\big\|_{L^{2q}(U_{0})}^{2q}=\big\|\sum_{i}M_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i}M_{n,i}^{2}\big\|_{L^{q}(U_{0})}^{q}.

Now it suffices to apply
Corollary

7.4
.
∎

Next we will combine
Corollary

7.5
with the area and the coarea formula (see e.g.
[
EG92
]
)
which we recall for the convenience of the reader.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be Lipschitz and let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be Lebesgue measurable.
The
area formula
states that,
if
m\leq d
, then

\int_{E}|Jf|\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{H}^{0}(E\cap f^{-1}(y))\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(y).

The
coarea formula
posits that,
if
m\geq d
, then

\int_{E}|Jf|\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{H}^{m-d}(E\cap f^{-1}(y))\,dy.

Recall that
\mathcal{H}^{k}
denotes the
k
-dimensional Hausdorff measure,
in particular,
\mathcal{H}^{0}
is the counting measure.

Corollary 7.6
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open.
Let
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
.
Set
\lambda:=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})
and
\lambda_{n}:=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})
, for
n\geq 1
.

(1)

If
m\leq d
, then for each relatively compact open subset
U_{0}\Subset U
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{H}^{0}(U_{0}\cap\lambda_{n}^{-1}(y))\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(y)\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{H}^{0}(U_{0}\cap\lambda^{-1}(y))\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(y)

as
n\to\infty
.

(2)

If
m>d
, then for each relatively compact open subset
U_{0}\Subset U
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{H}^{m-d}(U_{0}\cap\lambda_{n}^{-1}(y))\,dy\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{H}^{m-d}(U_{0}\cap\lambda^{-1}(y))\,dy

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of
Corollary

7.5
(for
q=1
) and the area and coarea formula.
∎

We can also conclude that the surface area of the graphs of all the single roots
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})_{j}=\lambda^{\uparrow}_{j}\circ\mathsf{a}_{n}
, for
1\leq j\leq d
,
is locally convergent as
n\to\infty
.

Corollary 7.7
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open.
Let
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
.
For each
1\leq j\leq d
and for each relatively compact open subset
U_{0}\Subset U
,
the surface area of the graph of
\lambda_{n,j}:=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})_{j}
converges to the surface area
of the graph of
\lambda_{j}:=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})_{j}
as
n\to\infty
:
if
\overline{\lambda}_{n,j}(x):=(x,\lambda_{n,j}(x))
and
\overline{\lambda}_{j}(x):=(x,\lambda_{j}(x))
denote
the corresponding graph mappings,
then

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{n,j}(U_{0}))\to\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{j}(U_{0}))\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

We have

|J\overline{\lambda}_{j}|=\Big(1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\lambda_{j})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\quad\text{ and }\quad|J\overline{\lambda}_{n,j}|=\Big(1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\lambda_{n,j})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

As in the proof of
Corollary

7.5
, we have

\displaystyle\Big\|\Big(1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\lambda_{j})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}-\Big(1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\lambda_{n,j})^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big\|_{L^{2}(U_{0})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\lambda_{j})^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\lambda_{n,j})^{2}\Big|^{1/2}\Big\|_{L^{2}(U_{0})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\lambda_{j})^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\partial_{i}\lambda_{n,j})^{2}\Big\|_{L^{1}(U_{0})}.

So the assertion follows from
Corollary

7.4
and the area formula.
∎

It follows that the area of the zero sets of
C^{d}
families of hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d

locally has a lower semicontinuity property;
for the reader’s convenience, we restate
Corollary

1.9
:

Corollary 7.8
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open.
Let
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
.
For any relatively compact open
U_{0}\Subset U
,
consider the zero sets

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in U_{0}\times\mathbb{R}:P_{\mathsf{a}(x)}(y)=0\}\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle Z_{n}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in U_{0}\times\mathbb{R}:P_{\mathsf{a}_{n}(x)}(y)=0\},\quad n\geq 1.

Then

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z_{n})\geq\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z).

Proof.

Set
\lambda:=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})
.
For
i=2,\ldots,d
, let
E_{i}:=\{x\in U_{0}:\lambda_{i-1}(x)=\lambda_{i}(x)\}
.
Then, using the notation of
Corollary

7.7
,

(7.6)

\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z)=\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{1}(U_{0}))+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{i}(U_{0}\setminus E_{i})).

Analogously, setting
\lambda_{n}:=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})
and
E_{n,i}:=\{x\in U_{0}:\lambda_{n,i-1}(x)=\lambda_{n,i}(x)\}
, we have

(7.7)

\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z_{n})=\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{n,1}(U_{0}))+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{n,i}(U_{0}\setminus E_{n,i})).

By the continuity of
\lambda^{\uparrow}:\operatorname{Hyp}(d)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for each
i=2,\ldots,d
and each
x\in U_{0}
,

(7.8)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,i}}(x)\leq\mathbf{1}_{E_{i}}(x).

By
Theorem

5.1
(applied coordinate by coordinate), we have that
|J\overline{\lambda}_{n,i}|\to|J\overline{\lambda}_{i}|
as
n\to\infty

almost everywhere in
U_{0}
. By Bronshtein’s theorem (
Theorem

4.1
),
there is a constant
B>0
such that

\big\||J\overline{\lambda}_{n,i}|\big\|_{L^{\infty}(U_{0})}\leq B
for all
n\geq 1
.
Thus,
by the area formula and the reverse Fatou lemma,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{n,i}(E_{n,i}))

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{U_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,i}}|J\overline{\lambda}_{n,i}|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{U_{0}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big(\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,i}}|J\overline{\lambda}_{n,i}|\big)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{U_{0}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{1}_{E_{n,i}}\cdot\limsup_{n\to\infty}|J\overline{\lambda}_{n,i}|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{E_{i}}|J\overline{\lambda}_{i}|\,dx=\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{i}(E_{i})),

where we used (
7.8
) in the last inequality.

Together with (
7.6
), (
7.7
), and
Corollary

7.7
, this gives

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z_{n})

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{n,i}(U_{0}))-\sum_{i=2}^{d}\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{n,i}(E_{n,i}))\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{n,i}(U_{0}))-\sum_{i=2}^{d}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{n,i}(E_{n,i}))

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{i}(U_{0}))-\sum_{i=2}^{d}\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{i}(E_{i}))=\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z)

which ends the proof.
∎

7.3.
Approximation by hyperbolic polynomials with all roots distinct

We recall a lemma of Wakabayshi
[
Wak86
]
which extends an observation of Nuij
[
Nui68
]
.

Lemma 7.9

(
[
Wak86
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Let
P_{\mathsf{a}}\in\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
and set

(7.9)

P_{\mathsf{a},s}(Z):=(1+s\tfrac{\partial}{\partial Z})^{d-1}P_{\mathsf{a}}(Z),\quad s\in\mathbb{R}.

Then
P_{\mathsf{a},s}\in\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
and there are positive constants
c_{i}=c_{i}(d)
,
i=1,2
,
such that, if
\lambda^{\uparrow}_{1}(\mathsf{a},s)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda^{\uparrow}_{d}(\mathsf{a},s)
denote the increasingly ordered roots of
P_{\mathsf{a},s}
, then

(7.10)

\lambda^{\uparrow}_{j}(\mathsf{a},s)-\lambda^{\uparrow}_{j-1}(\mathsf{a},s)\geq c_{1}|s|,\quad\text{ for }s\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }2\leq j\leq d,

and

(7.11)

0<\pm(\lambda^{\uparrow}_{j}(\mathsf{a})-\lambda^{\uparrow}_{j}(\mathsf{a},s))\leq c_{2}|s|,\quad\text{ for }\pm s>0\text{ and }1\leq j\leq d.

In conjunction with our findings,
Lemma

7.9
leads to the following approximation result.

Corollary 7.10
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and

\mathsf{a}\in C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
. There exists a sequence
(\mathsf{a}_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subseteq C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
with the following properties:

(1)

\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
;

(2)

\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})_{1}(x)<\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})_{2}(x)<\cdots<\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})_{d}(x)
for all
x\in U
and all
n\geq 1
;

(3)

\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})\in C^{d}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
n\geq 1
;

(4)

\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})
in
C^{0,1}_{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, for all
1\leq q<\infty
, as
n\to\infty
;

(5)

for any relatively compact open
U_{0}\Subset U
,
consider the zero sets

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in U_{0}\times\mathbb{R}:P_{\mathsf{a}(x)}(y)=0\}\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle Z_{n}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in U_{0}\times\mathbb{R}:P_{\mathsf{a}_{n}(x)}(y)=0\},\quad n\geq 1.

Then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z_{n})
exists and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z_{n})\geq\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z).

Proof.

Let
(s_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be any positive sequence of reals that tends to
0
.
Consider the polynomial
P_{\mathsf{a}(x),s_{n}}
(defined in (
7.9
)), where
x\in U
, and let
\mathsf{a}_{n}(x)
be its coefficient vector.
Then, by
Lemma

7.9
,

\mathsf{a}_{n}\in C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
, for
n\geq 1
.
We will show that the sequence
(\mathsf{a}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
has the desired properties.

(1) This is clear by the definition (
7.9
) and since
s_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

(2) follows from (
7.10
) and the fact that
s_{n}>0
for all
n\geq 1
.

(3) For fixed
x\in U
,
\frac{\partial}{\partial Z}P_{\mathsf{a}_{n}(x)}(Z)
does not vanish at any root of
P_{\mathsf{a}_{n}(x)}
, by (2).
So, by the implicit function theorem, the roots of
P_{\mathsf{a}_{n}(x)}
are of class
C^{d}
in a neighborhood of
x
.
This implies (3).

(4) is a consequence of (1) and
Theorem

1.1
.

(5) Using the notation of
Corollary

7.7
, for each
n\geq 1
, the set

Z_{n}
is the union of the graphs
\overline{\lambda}_{n,j}(U_{0})
of the single roots
\lambda_{n,j}|_{U_{0}}=\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})_{j}|_{U_{0}}
, for
1\leq j\leq d
,
and these graphs are pairwise disjoint, by (2).
Thus, by (1) and
Corollary

7.7
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{n,j}(U_{0}))=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{H}^{m}(\overline{\lambda}_{j}(U_{0}))\geq\mathcal{H}^{m}(Z).

For the inequality at the end, note that the union
Z=\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}\overline{\lambda}_{j}(U_{0})
is not necessarily disjoint;
see also
Corollary

7.8
.
∎

7.4.
Perturbation theory for Hermitian matrices

Let
\operatorname{Herm}(d)
denote the real vector space of complex Hermitian
d\times d
matrices.
With
A\in\operatorname{Herm}(d)
we associate its increasingly ordered eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}^{\uparrow}(A)\leq\lambda_{2}^{\uparrow}(A)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d}^{\uparrow}(A)

and thus obtain a continuous map

(7.12)

\lambda^{\uparrow}=(\lambda_{1}^{\uparrow},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{\uparrow}):\operatorname{Herm}(d)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proposition 7.11

(Weyl’s perturbation theorem
[
Wey12
]
; see e.g.
[
Bha97
, III.2.6]
)
.

Let
A,B\in\operatorname{Herm}(d)
. Then

(7.13)

\|\lambda^{\uparrow}(A)-\lambda^{\uparrow}(B)\|_{\infty}\leq\|A-B\|,

where
\|A-B\|
denotes the operator norm of
A-B
.

In
[
Bha97
]
, the result is stated for eigenvalue vectors with decreasing eigenvalues, but reversing the order evidently
leaves the left-hand side of (
7.13
) unchanged.

As a consequence of
Proposition

7.11
,
the map (
7.12
) induces a bounded map

(7.14)

\mathcal{E}:=(\lambda^{\uparrow})_{*}:C^{0,1}(\overline{I},\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}(\overline{I},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

which takes Lipschitz curves of Hermitian matrices to Lipschitz curves of their increasingly ordered eigenvalues. The Lipschitz constants
satisfy

(7.15)

|\mathcal{E}(A)|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq|A|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I},\operatorname{Herm}(d))},

if
\operatorname{Herm}(d)
is endowed with the operator norm and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the maximum norm.
This remains true if we replace the interval
I
by a bounded open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

The following corollary includes
Theorem

1.10
.

Corollary 7.12
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open. Then:

(1)

The map

\mathcal{E}:C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

is continuous, for all
1\leq q<\infty
.

(2)

The map

\mathcal{E}:C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\to C^{0,\alpha}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

is continuous, for all
0<\alpha<1
.

(3)

If
A_{n}\to A
in
C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))
as
n\to\infty
, then over each relatively compact open subset
U_{0}\Subset U

the surface area of the graph of
\mathcal{E}(A_{n})_{j}
converges to the surface area of the graph of
\mathcal{E}(A)_{j}
as
n\to\infty
, for each
1\leq j\leq d
.

Proof.

(1) We have the commuting diagram

\textstyle{C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathcal{P}}
\scriptstyle{\mathcal{E}}
\textstyle{C^{0,1}_{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})}
\textstyle{C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathcal{S}}

where
\mathcal{P}
sends
A
to its characteristic polynomial
P_{A}
. The coefficients of
P_{A}
are given by
polynomials in the entries of
A
. Thus,
Proposition

2.1
implies that the map
\mathcal{P}
is continuous.
Consequently,
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{S}\circ\mathcal{P}
is continuous by
Theorem

1.1
.

(2) This follows similarly from the continuity of
\mathcal{P}
and
Corollary

1.2
.

(3) Use
Corollary

7.7
and the continuity of
\mathcal{P}
.
∎

The following example shows that
\mathcal{E}
is not continuous with respect to the

C^{0,1}
topology on the target space.

Example 7.13
.

The sequence
(A_{n})_{n}
of curves of symmetric
2\times 2
matrices

A_{n}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{n}&x\\
x&-\frac{1}{n}\end{pmatrix},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

converges to

A(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\
x&0\end{pmatrix},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

uniformly on every compact interval in all derivatives.
We have

\mathcal{E}(A_{n})(x)=\Big(-\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}},\sqrt{x^{2}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}\Big)

and

\mathcal{E}(A)(x)=(-|x|,|x|).

Hence,
Example

1.12
shows that the Lipschitz constant of
\mathcal{E}(A)-\mathcal{E}(A_{n})
on each bounded open interval containing
0

is bounded below by
2-\sqrt{2}

which shows that
\mathcal{E}(A_{n})\not\to\mathcal{E}(A)
in the
C^{0,1}
topology.

Given that the map
\mathcal{E}
is defined and bounded on
C^{0,1}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(d))
(see (
7.14
) and (
7.15
)),
it is natural to ask whether in
Corollary

7.12
one can replace
C^{d}
by
C^{1}
, see
Question

1.11
.

If
d=2
, this is indeed the case as evidenced in the following proposition.

Proposition 7.14
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open. Then:

(1)

The map

\mathcal{E}:C^{1}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(2))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

is continuous, for all
1\leq q<\infty
.

(2)

The map

\mathcal{E}:C^{1}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(2))\to C^{0,\alpha}(U,\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad A\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}\circ A,

is continuous, for all
0<\alpha<1
.

(3)

If
A_{n}\to A
in
C^{1}(U,\operatorname{Herm}(2))
as
n\to\infty
, then over each relatively compact open subset
U_{0}\Subset U

the surface area of the graph of
\mathcal{E}(A_{n})_{j}
converges to the surface area of the graph of
\mathcal{E}(A)_{j}
as
n\to\infty
, for
j=1,2
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove (1) in the case
m=1
. Then the multiparameter version of (1) as well as (2) and (3) follow by the arguments given in detail for hyperbolic polynomials,
if one uses
Proposition

7.11
instead of Bronshtein’s theorem
4.1
.

Let us show (1) for
m=1
.
We may assume that the trace of
A
vanishes, by replacing
A
by
A-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(A)\mathbb{I}
. Thus, we have

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b+ic\\
b-ic&-a\end{pmatrix},

where
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\lambda^{\uparrow}(A)=\big(-\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}},\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}}\big).

Let us assume that
a,b,c\in C^{1}(I,\mathbb{R})
, where
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an open interval. Then

I\ni x\mapsto\sqrt{a(x)^{2}+b(x)^{2}+c(x)^{2}}=:\mu(x)

is locally Lipschitz, differentiable almost everywhere, and

(7.16)

|\mu^{\prime}(x_{0})|\leq\sup_{x\in\overline{I}_{1}}\|A^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}=\sqrt{2}\,\sup_{x\in\overline{I}_{1}}\sqrt{a^{\prime}(x)^{2}+b^{\prime}(x)^{2}+c^{\prime}(x)^{2}},

for each relatively compact open subinterval
I_{1}\Subset I
and each
x_{0}\in I_{1}
where
\mu^{\prime}(x_{0})
exists, by (
7.15
).

Suppose that

A_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{n}&b_{n}+ic_{n}\\
b_{n}-ic_{n}&-a_{n}\end{pmatrix}\longrightarrow A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b+ic\\
b-ic&-a\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ in }C^{1}(I,\operatorname{Herm}(2))

as
n\to\infty
.
It suffices to prove that

(7.17)

\|\mu-\mu_{n}\|_{W^{1,q}(I_{1})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for each relatively compact open subinterval
I_{1}\Subset I
and all
1\leq q<\infty
, where

\mu_{n}:=\sqrt{a_{n}^{2}+b_{n}^{2}+c_{n}^{2}}.

By (
7.13
), we have

\|\mu-\mu_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{1})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for each relatively compact open subinterval
I_{1}\Subset I
.

We claim that, for almost every
x\in I
,

\mu_{n}^{\prime}(x)\to\mu^{\prime}(x)\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

This is clear on the set
\Omega:=\{x\in I:a(x)^{2}+b(x)^{2}+c(x)^{2}\neq 0\}
: for each
x_{0}\in\Omega
, the derivative
\mu^{\prime}(x_{0})
exists, and, by assumption,

a(x_{0})^{2}+b(x_{0})^{2}+c(x_{0})^{2}\neq 0
if
n
is large enough so that also
\mu_{n}^{\prime}(x_{0})
exists and
\mu_{n}^{\prime}(x_{0})\to\mu^{\prime}(x_{0})
.

Now consider
Z:=\{x\in I:a(x)^{2}+b(x)^{2}+c(x)^{2}=0\}
and the set
\operatorname{acc}(Z)
of accumulation points of
Z
.
Note that
a^{\prime}
,
b^{\prime}
, and
c^{\prime}
vanish on
\operatorname{acc}(Z)
.
Fix
x_{0}\in\operatorname{acc}(Z)
and
\epsilon>0
. By continuity, there exists
\delta>0
such that
\overline{I}(x_{0},\delta)\Subset I
and

\sup_{x\in\overline{I}(x_{0},\delta)}\sqrt{a^{\prime}(x)^{2}+b^{\prime}(x)^{2}+c^{\prime}(x)^{2}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

As
A_{n}\to A
in
C^{1}(I,\operatorname{Herm(2)})
, there is
n_{0}\geq 1
such that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\sup_{x\in\overline{I}(x_{0},\delta)}\sqrt{a_{n}^{\prime}(x)^{2}+b_{n}^{\prime}(x)^{2}+c_{n}^{\prime}(x)^{2}}\leq\epsilon.

If
\mu_{n}^{\prime}(x_{0})
exists, then we conclude, by (
7.16
), that

|\mu_{n}^{\prime}(x_{0})|\leq\sqrt{2}\,\epsilon,\quad n\geq n_{0}.

This implies the claim, since the set of accumulation points of
Z
, where all
\mu_{n}
and
\mu
are differentiable, has full measure in
Z

and
\mu^{\prime}
vanishes on this set.

Now the dominated convergence theorem implies that

\|\mu^{\prime}-\mu_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I_{1})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for each relatively compact open subinterval
I_{1}\Subset I
and all
1\leq q<\infty
,
completing the proof of (
7.17
).
∎

Remark 7.15
.

Corollary

7.12
has an evident analogue for skew-Hermitian matrices which simply follows from the fact
that a
d\times d
matrix
A
is skew-Hermitian if and only if
iA
is Hermitian. The eigenvalues of
A

and
iA
just differ by multiplication by
i
.

On the other hand, there is no consistent continuous choice of the eigenvalues of unitary
d\times d
matrices.
Consider, for example,
the curve of unitary matrices

A(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{2\pi ix}\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

with the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}(x)=\pm e^{\pi ix}
. Even though
\lambda_{\pm}:\mathbb{R}\to(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{2}
is continuous,
there is no continuous choice of the eigenvalues
\mathbb{S}^{1}\to(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{2}
of
the curve of unitary matrices induced by
A
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
(because

\lambda_{\pm}(0)=\pm 1\neq\mp 1=\lambda_{\pm}(1)
).

In this case, and more generally for normal matrices, the general continuity results of
[
PR24
]

apply. For the perturbation theory of normal matrices, see
[
Rai13
]
,
[
PR20b
]
, and the
survey
[
PR25
]
.

7.5.
Singular values

Let us consider the vector space
M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})
of complex
D\times d
matrices, where
d\leq D
.
The singular values of
A\in M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})
are the nonnegative square roots of the eigenvalues
of the Hermitian matrix
A^{*}A
, usually ordered decreasingly

\sigma_{1}(A)\geq\sigma_{2}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{d}(A)\geq 0.

This defines a map
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}):M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let us consider the real vector space
M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathbb{R}
and the homogeneous polynomial
of degree
2d
,

f(A,r):=\det(r^{2}\,\mathbb{I}-A^{*}A),\quad(A,r)\in M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathbb{R}.

Then
f
is
Gårding hyperbolic
with respect to the direction
(0,1)\in M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathbb{R}

(see
[
BGLS01
, Sec. 6]
)
which means by definition that all roots of the univariate polynomial

P_{A,r}(Z):=f((A,r)-Z\,(0,1))=\det((r-Z)^{2}\,\mathbb{I}-A^{*}A)

are real. Indeed, the roots of
P_{A,r}
(in decreasing order) are

r+\sigma_{1}(A),r+\sigma_{2}(A),\ldots,r+\sigma_{d}(A),r-\sigma_{d}(A),\ldots,r-\sigma_{1}(A).

Hence, by
Theorem

4.1
,
\sigma
induces a bounded map

\sigma_{*}:C^{2d-1,1}(U,M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to C^{0,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\sigma\circ A,

where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is open. In general, this map is not continuous, which follows from
Example

7.13
,
but we have the following result.

Corollary 7.16
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open. Then:

(1)

The map

\sigma_{*}:C^{2d}(U,M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to C^{0,1}_{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\sigma\circ A,

is continuous, for all
1\leq q<\infty
.

(2)

The map

\sigma_{*}:C^{2d}(U,M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to C^{0,\alpha}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad A\mapsto\sigma\circ A,

is continuous, for all
0<\alpha<1
.

(3)

If
A_{n}\to A
in
C^{2d}(U,M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))
as
n\to\infty
,
then over each relatively compact open subset
U_{0}\Subset U

the surface area of the graph of
\sigma_{j}(A_{n})
converges to the surface area of the graph of
\sigma_{j}(A)

as
n\to\infty
, for each
1\leq j\leq d
.

Proof.

Similarly as in the proof of
Corollary

7.12
, we have, for each
r\in\mathbb{R}
, a
continuous map
C^{2d}(U,M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to C^{2d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
,
A\mapsto P_{A,r}
,
which can be used to reduce the statements of the corollary to the corresponding one for
hyperbolic polynomials.
∎

Observing that the Hermitian matrix

\mathbf{A}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\tilde{A}\\
\tilde{A}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
\tilde{A}
is the
D\times D
matrix resulting from
A
by adding
D-d
columns consisting of zeros, has the eigenvalues

\sigma_{1}(A),\ldots,\sigma_{d}(A),0,\ldots,0,-\sigma_{d}(A),\ldots,-\sigma_{1}(A),

we conclude from (
7.13
) that, for
A,B\in M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C})
and
1\leq i\leq d
,

\displaystyle|\sigma_{i}(A)-\sigma_{i}(B)|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|\leq\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2}=|\operatorname{tr}\big((\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})^{*}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B})\big)|^{1/2}

\displaystyle=|2\,\operatorname{tr}\big((\tilde{A}-\tilde{B})^{*}(\tilde{A}-\tilde{B})\big)|^{1/2}=\sqrt{2}\,\|A-B\|_{2}.

Consequently, the map

\sigma_{*}:C^{0,1}(U,M_{D,d}(\mathbb{C}))\to C^{0,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})

is well-defined and bounded.
So, in analogy to
Question

1.11
, it is thus natural to ask whether in
Corollary

7.16
one can replace the assumption
C^{2d}
by
C^{1}
.

8.
Restricted multiplicity

In this section we prove a refinement of
Theorem

1.3
which accounts for the case that the
maximal multiplicity of the roots is smaller than the degree.

First we recall the following version of Bronshtein’s theorem.

Theorem 8.1

(
[
PR15
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval
and
\mathsf{a}\in C^{p-1,1}(I,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
, where

p
is the maximal multiplicity of the roots of
P_{\mathsf{a}(x)}
, for
x\in I
.
Then any continuous root
\lambda\in C^{0}(I)
of
P_{\mathsf{a}}
is locally Lipschitz.

If
p=d
, then we have the bound (
4.1
).

Assume
p<d
and suppose that
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}
is in Tschirnhausen form.
Let
\lambda_{1}^{\uparrow}(x)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d}^{\uparrow}(x)
be the increasingly ordered
roots of
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}(x)}
, for
x\in I
, and consider

(8.1)

\alpha(x):=\frac{|\lambda_{d}^{\uparrow}(x)-\lambda_{1}^{\uparrow}(x)|}{\min_{1\leq i\leq d-p}|\lambda_{i+p}^{\uparrow}(x)-\lambda_{i}^{\uparrow}(x)|}\quad\text{ and }\quad\alpha_{I}:=\sup_{x\in I}\alpha(x).

Then each continuous root
\lambda
of
P_{\tilde{a}}
satisfies, for any pair of relatively compact
open intervals
I_{0}\Subset I_{1}\Subset I
,

\displaystyle|\lambda|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I}_{0})}

\displaystyle\leq C(d)\,\alpha_{I_{1}}^{\frac{d-p}{p}}\,\max\Big\{\delta^{-1}\|\tilde{a}_{2}\|^{1/2}_{L^{\infty}(I_{1})},|\tilde{a}_{2}^{\prime}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I}_{1})}^{1/2},

\displaystyle\hskip 91.04872pt\max_{i\leq p}\Big(|\tilde{a}_{i}^{(p-1)}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I}_{1})}\|\tilde{a}_{2}\|^{\frac{p-i}{2}}_{L^{\infty}(I_{1})}\Big)^{1/p},

\displaystyle\hskip 91.04872pt\max_{i>p}\Big(|\tilde{a}_{i}^{(p-1)}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I}_{1})}\big(\min_{x\in\overline{I}_{0}}|\tilde{a}_{2}(x)|\big)^{\frac{p-i}{2}}\Big)^{1/p}\Big\},

where
\delta:=\operatorname{dist}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{1})
.

The next theorem generalizes
Theorem

1.3
.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval.
Let
\mathsf{a}_{n}\to\mathsf{a}
in
C^{p}(I,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))
as
n\to\infty
,
where
p
is the maximal multiplicity of the roots of
P_{\mathsf{a}(x)}
, for
x\in I
.
If
p<d
assume that, for each relatively compact open
I_{1}\Subset I
,

\alpha_{I_{1}}<\infty.

Then
\{\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n}):n\geq 1\}
is a bounded set in
C^{0,1}(I,\mathbb{R}^{d})

and, for each relatively compact open interval
I_{0}\Subset I
and each
1\leq q<\infty
,

\|\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})-\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})\|_{W^{1,q}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

In view of
Theorem

1.3
, we may assume that
p<d
. Furthermore, we may assume that all polynomials are
in Tschirnhausen form.

We first observe that, for each relatively compact open
I_{1}\Subset I
,

(8.2)

\|\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\mathsf{a}})-\mathcal{S}(\tilde{\mathsf{a}}_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{1},\mathbb{R}^{d})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

as a consequence of
[
PR24
, Corollary 6.5]
and
Lemma

7.1
.
(For this it is actually enough that
\tilde{\mathsf{a}}_{n}\to\tilde{\mathsf{a}}
in
C^{0}(\overline{I}_{1},\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d))

as
n\to\infty
.)

Fix relatively compact open subintervals
I_{0}\Subset I_{1}\Subset I
.
Then there exists
n_{0}\geq 1
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}

the maximal multiplicity of the roots of
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}_{n}}
on
I_{1}
is at most
p
.
(If not this is violated on a sequence
x_{n_{k}}
in
I_{1}
, leading to a contradiction at an
accumulation point of this sequence in
\overline{I}_{1}
, since
\lambda^{\uparrow}:\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is continuous.)

Consequently, the functions
\alpha_{n}:I_{1}\to\mathbb{R}

associated to
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}_{n}}
as in (
8.1
) are well-defined, for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
By the assumption
\alpha_{I_{1}}<\infty
and (
8.2
),

\alpha_{n}\to\alpha
uniformly on
I_{1}
as
n\to\infty
and thus the sequence

\alpha_{n,I_{1}}:=\sup_{x\in I_{1}}\alpha_{n}(x)
is bounded.

Hence, by
Theorem

8.1
, the derivative of
\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})
exists almost everywhere in
I_{0}
and
is uniformly bounded on
I_{0}
by a constant independent of
n
.

By
Lemma

3.3
and
Proposition

2.1
,
we can split
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}}
and
P_{\tilde{\mathsf{a}}_{n}}
, for large
n
,
locally in factors of degrees at most
p
in a simultaneous way. This allows us to apply
Theorem

5.1

in the case
d=p
and conclude that, for almost every
x\in I_{0}
,

\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a}_{n})^{\prime}(x)\to\mathcal{S}(\mathsf{a})^{\prime}(x)\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Now it suffice to invoke the dominated convergence theorem to finish the proof.
∎
