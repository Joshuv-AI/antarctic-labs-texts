Abstract
. This paper studies the L p L_{p} dual Minkowski problem for all q > 0 q>0 and p ∈ ℝ p\in\mathbb{R} . We establish the existence of solutions for group-invariant convex bodies (which may not be origin-symmetric), covering two major open problems as special cases: the L p L_{p} Minkowski problem ( q = n , p ∈ ℝ q=n,p\in\mathbb{R} ) and the dual Minkowski problem ( p = 0 , q > 0 p=0,q>0 ).



1.
Introduction

The central problem in convex geometry involves characterizing geometric measures of convex bodies. The classical Minkowski problem seeks necessary and sufficient conditions for a given Borel measure on the unit sphere to be the surface area measure of a convex body. This is an influential problem not only in geometric analysis but also in fully nonlinear partial differential equations.

Since the classical Minkowski problem, many extensions have been introduced and extensively studied. The
L_{p}
Minkowski problem, which characterizes the
L_{p}
surface area measure, was first introduced by Lutwak
[
34
,
35
]
. When
p=1
, the
L_{p}
Minkowski problem reduces to the classical Minkowski problem. The
L_{p}
Minkowski problem has been solved for
p\geq 1
(see
[
28
,
34
,
36
]
). However, for
p<1
, the family of
L_{p}
Minkowski problems contains many important unsolved cases, notably the logarithmic Minkowski problem (
p=0
) and the centro-affine Minkowski problem (
p=-n
), which remain significant open problems, though partial results appear in
[
4
,
11
,
26
,
37
,
54
,
55
]
. Particularly for
p<0
, little is known, with progress mainly in discrete cases (
[
55
,
56
]
) or absolutely continuous cases: results for
-n<p<0
with densities in
L_{\frac{n}{n+p}}(S^{n-1})

[
8
,
10
]
, and results under regularity assumptions for
p<-n

[
18
]

Minkowski problem, see
[

In the groundbreaking work
[
21

, Huang-LYZ introduced a new family of geometric measures called dual curvature measures, which can be viewed as differentials of dual quermassintegrals. The dual Minkowski problem, which characterizes the dual curvature measure, remains widely open. While this problem is completely solved for even cases when

The

-th dual curvature measure, introduced in

Given a nonzero finite Borel measure

When the measure

\det(\nabla_{ij}h+h\delta_{ij})=h^{p-1}\rho_{Q}^{q-n}(\nabla h)f,

We study the

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\right)
denote the
n
-dimensional Euclidean space with the standard inner product.

Let
S^{n-1}
represent the unit sphere and
B
the closed unit ball centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
B(r)=rB
be a closed ball with radius
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and centered at the origin. Let
\mathcal{B}^{n}
denote the class of Euclidean balls centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

A
convex body
is a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with non-empty interior. The set of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}^{n}
, the set of origin-symmetric convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
denotes the set of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that contain the origin in their interior.

The
support function
of a compact convex set
K
is given by

h_{K}(x)=\max_{y\in K}\langle y,x\rangle,\quad\text{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

A set
K
is
star-shaped
with respect to a point
x
if every line through
x
that meets
K
does so in a (possibly degenerate) closed line segment.
If
K
is a compact star-shaped set with respect to the origin, then for all
u\in S^{n-1}
, its
radial function

\rho_{K}(\cdot)
is defined by (see
[
16
,
48
]
)

\rho_{K}(u)=\max\{c:cu\in K\},

if the radial function of
K
is continuous, then
K
is called a
star body
. We define the set of star bodies
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{o}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose radial functions are positive and continuous. The radial function is homogeneous of degree
-1
, that is,

\displaystyle\rho_{K}(cu)=c^{-1}\rho_{K}(u),\quad c>0.

(2.1)

For an invertible linear transformation
\phi
, the support function
h_{K}
and the radial function
\rho_{K}
transform as follows:

\displaystyle h_{\phi K}(y)

\displaystyle=h_{K}(\phi^{T}y),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\rho_{\phi K}(y)

\displaystyle=\rho_{K}(\phi^{-1}y).

(2.3)

The polar body
K^{*}
of
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
is defined
by

K^{*}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,y\rangle\leq 1\quad\text{ for all }y\in K\right\}.

(2.4)

For a convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
and a Borel set
\eta\subset S^{n-1}
, define

\alpha_{K}^{*}(\eta)=\big\{u\in S^{n-1}:\rho_{K}(u)u\in\nu_{K}^{-1}(\eta)\big\},

where
\nu_{K}
is the outer unit normal vector of
K
.

The
q
-th dual mixed volume for star bodies
K,Q\in\mathcal{S}_{o}^{n}
and
q\in\mathbb{R}
is given by

\widetilde{V}_{q}(K,Q)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}\rho_{K}^{q}(u)\rho_{Q}^{n-q}(u)du.

When
Q=B
, for each
q=1,\ldots,n
,
\widetilde{V}_{q}(K,B)
coincides exactly with the
(n-q)
-th dual quermassintegral
\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(K)
:

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(K)=\frac{\omega_{n}}{\omega_{q}}\int_{G(n,q)}\mathcal{H}^{q}(K\cap\xi)\,d\xi,

where the integral is taken with respect to the Haar measure on the Grassmannian
G(n,q)
of all
q
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Here
\mathcal{H}^{q}
denotes the
q
-dimensional Hausdorff measure and
\omega_{q}
denotes the
q
-dimensional volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
.

For convex bodies
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
, the variation of the
q
-th dual mixed volume yields the
q
-th dual curvature measure with respect to
Q\in\mathcal{S}_{o}^{n}
. This Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
, introduced by Lutwak-Yang-Zhang
[
30
]
(and by Huang-LYZ
[
21
]
for

), is defined as

\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,Q,\eta)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\alpha_{K}^{*}(\eta)}\rho_{K}^{q}(u)\rho_{Q}^{n-q}(u)du

for every Borel set
\eta\subset S^{n-1}
.

The set of continuous positive functions on the sphere
S^{n-1}
will be denoted by
C^{+}(S^{n-1})
. For each
f\in C^{+}(S^{n-1})
, the Wulff shape
[f]
generated by
f
is a convex body defined by

[f]=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,v\rangle\leq f(v),\;\text{for all}\;v\in S^{n-1}\right\}.

(2.5)

The variational formula for the
q
-th dual mixed volume is established in
[
21
,
30
]
. Specifically, for
q\neq 0
,
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
,

, and a continuous function

,

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\widetilde{V}_{q}([h_{K}+tf],Q)-\widetilde{V}_{q}(K,Q)}{t}=q\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{f(v)}{h_{K}(v)}\,d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,Q,v),

From Lemma 5.1 in
[
30
]
, for

and a bounded Borel function

, we have:

\int_{S^{n-1}}f(v)\,d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,Q,v)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial K}f(\nu_{K}(x))\langle\nu_{K}(x),x\rangle\rho_{Q}^{n-q}(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).

(2.7)

The

-th dual curvature measure, introduced by Lutwak-Yang-Zhang

Let

. Then

.
If
\dim V_{i}\geq 2

3.
Classification of G-Invariant Convex Bodies

We recall some standard notation. Let
\mathcal{K}^{n}
denote the set of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
the set of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing the origin in their interior,
and
\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
the set of origin-symmetric convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
\mathcal{B}^{n}
denote the class of Euclidean balls centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The purpose of this section is to reveal connections between
G
-invariant convex bodies and these four classes of convex bodies.

We endow the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(n)
with the subspace topology induced by the inclusion
\mathrm{O}(n)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
.
Specifically, we identify each matrix
U=(a_{ij})\in\mathrm{O}(n)
with a point in
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
by considering its
n^{2}
components as coordinates.
The group
\mathrm{O}(n)
is compact in
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
since for any
U=(a_{ij})\in\mathrm{O}(n)
,
|a_{ij}|\leq 1
holds for all
i,j
.
Moreover,
\mathrm{O}(n)
is clearly closed in
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
since the condition
U^{T}U=I
defines a closed set.
Consequently, the closed subgroup
G
of
\mathrm{O}(n)
is also compact.

Given a subgroup
G
of
\mathrm{O}(n)
,
we denote by
\mathcal{K}_{G}
the collection of all
G
-invariant convex bodies, i.e.,

\mathcal{K}_{G}:=\left\{K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}:gK=K\text{ for all }g\in G\right\}.

First, it is well-known that when
G=\mathrm{O}(n)
or
G=\mathrm{SO}(n)
, the only
G
-invariant convex bodies
are Euclidean balls centered at the origin. For any subgroup
G\subset\mathrm{O}(n)
,
balls centered at the origin are always contained in the class of
G
-invariant convex bodies.
We shall prove that if balls are the only
G
-invariant convex bodies,
then
G
acts transitively on the unit sphere, where the
transitive action on
S^{n-1}
means that for any

u,v\in S^{n-1}
, there exists
g\in G
such that
gu=v
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be a closed subgroup of the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(n)
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

Every
G
-invariant convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Euclidean ball centered at the origin, i.e.,
\mathcal{K}_{G}=\mathcal{B}^{n}
.

(2)

G
acts transitively on the unit sphere
S^{n-1}
.

Proof.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1):

Assume
G
acts transitively on
S^{n-1}
. Let
K
be a
G
-invariant convex body. Fix a vector
v\in S^{n-1}
,
h_{K}(v)=r
. For any
u\in S^{n-1}
,
the transitivity of the
G
-action guarantees that there exists
g\in G

satisfying
gv=u
. By (
2.2
), the support function satisfies:

h_{K}(u)=h_{K}(gv)=h_{g^{-1}K}(v)=h_{K}(v)=r.

This implies
h_{K}
is constant on
S^{n-1}
. Thus,
K
is the Euclidean ball of radius
r
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2):

Suppose
G
does not act transitively on
S^{n-1}
, then there exist
u,v\in S^{n-1}
with
u\notin Gv=\{gv:g\in G\}
.
We construct a
G
-invariant convex body that is not a Euclidean ball.
Define
K=\operatorname{conv}\left(B(\frac{1}{2}),Gv\right)
,
where
B(r)
is the closed ball of radius
r
centered at the origin.

We have
u\notin K
. Otherwise, if
u\in K
, there exist

g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in G
,

x_{m+1},\dots,x_{k}\in B(\frac{1}{2})
,
and weights
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\geq 0
with
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1

such that:

u=\lambda_{1}g_{1}v+\cdots+\lambda_{m}g_{m}v+\lambda_{m+1}x_{m+1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}x_{k}.

We may assume
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}>0
. Since
g_{i}\in G\subset\mathrm{O}(n)
,
|g_{i}v|=|v|=1
, and
|x_{j}|\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then

\displaystyle 1=|u|

\displaystyle=|\lambda_{1}g_{1}v+\cdots+\lambda_{m}g_{m}v+\lambda_{m+1}x_{m+1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}x_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}|g_{1}v|+\cdots+\lambda_{m}|g_{m}v|+\lambda_{m+1}|x_{m+1}|+\cdots+\lambda_{k}|x_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{m}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{m+1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{m}+\lambda_{m+1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}=1.

The equality holds if and only if
g_{1}v=\cdots=g_{m}v\triangleq w\in S^{n-1}
and
\lambda_{m+1}=\cdots=\lambda_{k}=0
. Then
u=w=g_{1}v=\cdots=g_{m}v
, which contradicts the fact that
u\notin Gv
. Thus,
v\in K
but
u\notin K
. Since
|u|=|v|=1
,
K
cannot be a Euclidean ball centered at the origin.

Since
G
is compact and the group action
(g,v)\mapsto gv
is continuous, then
the orbit
Gv
is compact,
K
is a compact convex set. Furthermore,
B(\frac{1}{2})\subset K
. Hence
K
is a convex body. For any
g\in G
and any

y=\lambda_{1}g_{1}v+\cdots+\lambda_{m}g_{m}v+\lambda_{m+1}x_{m+1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}x_{k}\in K,

we have

gy=\lambda_{1}gg_{1}v+\cdots+\lambda_{m}gg_{m}v+\lambda_{m+1}gx_{m+1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}gx_{k}\in K

because
gg_{i}\in G
and
|gx_{j}|=|x_{j}|\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Therefore,
gK\subset K
. Since
g
was arbitrary, replacing
g
by
g^{-1}
gives
g^{-1}K\subset K
, which implies
K\subset gK
. Consequently,
gK=K
for all
g\in G
. Then
K
is a
G
-invariant convex body. But
K
is not a ball, which contradicts
(1)
.

∎

Therefore, if the class of
G
-invariant convex bodies coincides precisely with the set of balls, then
G
must be an infinite group when
n\geq 2
.

In the study of the
L_{p}
dual Minkowski problem, origin-symmetric convex bodies constitute an important class of research objects. When
G=\{\pm I\}
where
I
is the identity map, it is evident that the class of
G
-invariant convex bodies coincides precisely with
\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
.

This observation naturally leads to the following question: Under what conditions does the class of
G
-invariant convex bodies exactly equal
\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
, contain
\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
, or be contained in
\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
?

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(n)
.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{K}_{G}=\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
.

(2)

G=\{\pm I\}
.

Proof.

We prove both directions of the equivalence.

(2
\Rightarrow
1):

Assume
G=\{\pm I\}
.
If
K
is
G
-invariant, then for
g=-I\in G
, we have
(-I)K=-K=K
.
Thus
K
is origin-symmetric.
If
K
is origin-symmetric (
K=-K
), then

IK=K
,

(-I)K=-K=K
,
so
K
is
G
-invariant.
Hence the
G
-invariant convex bodies are precisely the origin-symmetric convex bodies.

(1
\Rightarrow
2):

Suppose that for a given
g\in G
, every vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies either
gv=v
or
gv=-v
.
Let
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis. Then we have

ge_{i}=\lambda_{i}e_{i}\quad\text{for each }i=1,\ldots,n

(3.1)

where
\lambda_{i}=\pm 1
. For
i\neq j
,

g(e_{i}+e_{j})=\lambda_{i}e_{i}+\lambda_{j}e_{j}=\mu(e_{i}+e_{j})

for
\mu=\pm 1
. Therefore,
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}
. By (
3.1
) we have
g=\pm I
.

Assume condition (1) holds. We show that
G=\{\pm I\}
.
If there exists
g\in G
with
g\neq\pm I
, we construct a convex body that is origin-symmetric but not
G
-invariant.
Since
g\neq\pm I
, there exists a unit vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
gv\neq\pm v
.
Assume
v=e_{1}
after a change of coordinates.

Consider the ellipsoid (an origin-symmetric convex body)

K=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x^{T}Ax\leq 1\},

(3.2)

where
A=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,\dots,2)
is a positive definite matrix.
Under the action of
g
, we have:

\displaystyle gK

\displaystyle=\{gx\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x^{T}Ax\leq 1\}

\displaystyle=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:(g^{-1}y)^{T}A(g^{-1}y)\leq 1\}

\displaystyle=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:y^{T}(gAg^{T})y\leq 1\}.

(3.3)

Now compute the quadratic forms:

e_{1}^{T}Ae_{1}=1,

e_{1}^{T}(g^{T}Ag)e_{1}=(ge_{1})^{T}A(ge_{1})=w^{T}Aw,\quad\text{where}\quad w=ge_{1}.

Since
g
is orthogonal,
w
is a unit vector. As
ge_{1}\neq\pm e_{1}
, we have
w\neq\pm e_{1}
.
Let
w=(w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{n})
with
w_{1}^{2}<1
and
\sum_{i=2}^{n}w_{i}^{2}=1-w_{1}^{2}>0
. Then

w^{T}Aw=w_{1}^{2}+2\sum_{i=2}^{n}w_{i}^{2}=w_{1}^{2}+2(1-w_{1}^{2})=2-w_{1}^{2}>1=e_{1}^{T}Ae_{1}.

Thus
g^{T}Ag\neq A
, since
g^{T}=g^{-1}
, so
gAg^{T}\neq A
. Then we have
gK\neq K
by (
3.2
) and (
3
), contradicting

(1)
.
Therefore, if
g\in G
,
g=I
or
g=-I
.

So we have
G\subset\{\pm I\}
.
If
G=\{I\}
, then all convex bodies (including non-origin-symmetric ones) are
G
-invariant,
contradicting
(1)
. Thus
G=\{\pm I\}
.
∎

In Theorem
3.2
, we established that for any
g\neq\pm I
, there exists an origin-symmetric convex body
K
such that
gK\neq K
. Therefore, we obtain the following lemma:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
. Then
\mathcal{K}^{n}_{e}\subset\mathcal{K}_{G}
if and only if
G=\{I\}
or
G=\{\pm I\}
.

If
-I\in G
, then the
G
-invariant convex bodies are obviously origin-symmetric. The converse, however, is not true. For example, when
n
is odd,
-I\notin\mathrm{SO}(n)
, but the
\mathrm{SO}(n)
-invariant convex bodies are only balls, which are naturally origin-symmetric. In fact, we have the following equivalence.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
G
be a closed subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{K}_{G}\subset\mathcal{K}^{n}_{e}
.

(2)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
-x\in Gx
.

Proof.

(2
\Rightarrow
1):

Assume
(2)
holds, and let
K
be a
G
-invariant convex body. To show
K=-K
, take any
x\in K
. Since
K
is
G
-invariant,
gx\in K
for all
g\in G
. By the condition (2),
-x\in Gx\subset K
. Thus,
K=-K
.

(1
\Rightarrow
2):

Assume there exists
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that
-x\notin Gx
. Using the same construction as in Theorem
3.1
, let
0<r<|x|
and define

K=\operatorname{conv}(B(r),Gx)
. Since
|-x|=|gx|
for all
g\in G
, a similar argument to Theorem
3.1
shows that

-x\notin K
. But
x\in K
, so
K\neq-K
.
Since
G
is compact,
the orbit
Gx
is compact, and thus
K
is a compact convex set. Furthermore,
B(r)\subset K
, so
K
is a convex body.
K
is clearly
G
-invariant, but
K\notin\mathcal{K}^{n}_{e}
, which contradicts
(1)
.

∎

But when
G
is finite, we have

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
G
be a finite subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
.
Then
\mathcal{K}_{G}\subset\mathcal{K}^{n}_{e}
if and only if

-I\in G
.

Proof.

A finite group
G
is naturally closed. From Theorem
3.4
, it suffices to prove that for every vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
-x\in Gx
if and only if
-I\in G
.
If
-I\in G
,
-x=-Ix
is naturally contained in
Gx
.

Conversely, if for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
-x\in Gx
, suppose
-I\notin G
. For each
g\in G
, define the subspace:

V_{g}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid gx=-x\}.

Since
g
is orthogonal and linear,
V_{g}
is indeed a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
V_{g}\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
g\in G
, because if
V_{g}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
g=-I
, contradicting our assumption that
-I\notin G
.

By the given condition, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists some
g_{x}\in G
such that
g_{x}x=-x
, meaning
x\in V_{g_{x}}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{R}^{n}=\bigcup_{g\in G}V_{g}.

Since
G
is finite, we have a finite union of proper subspaces (
\operatorname{dim}\leq n-1
). However, in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a finite union of proper subspaces cannot cover the entire space. This leads to a contradiction.
∎

Another fundamental class in convex geometry is
\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
, consisting of convex bodies containing the origin in their interior. We now examine the relationship between
G
-invariant convex bodies and this class.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{K}_{G}\subset\mathcal{K}^{n}_{o}
.

(2)

G
has no non-zero fixed points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., if
gx=x
for any
g\in G
, then
x=0
.

Proof.

(1
\Rightarrow
2):

Suppose, to the contrary, that there exists a non-zero vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

such that
gw=w
for all
g\in G
. Let

K=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:|x|\leq 1\text{ and }\langle x,w\rangle\leq 0\right\}.

Then
K
is a convex body as the intersection of the closed unit ball and a half-space.
Moreover,
K
is
G
-invariant: for any
g\in G\subset\mathrm{O}(n)
and
x\in K
, we have
|gx|=|x|\leq 1
and

\langle gx,w\rangle=\langle gx,gw\rangle=\langle x,w\rangle\leq 0,

so
gx\in K
. Thus
gK=K
. However, the origin is not in the interior of
K
,
because for any

, the point
tw
satisfies
\langle tw,w\rangle=t|w|^{2}>0
, so
tw\notin K
.
This contradicts
\mathcal{K}_{G}\subset\mathcal{K}^{n}_{o}
.

(2
\Rightarrow
1):

Assume
G
has no non-zero fixed points. Let
K\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
be a
G
-invariant convex body.
Since the centroid of a convex body is equivariant with linear transformations, the centroid of
K
is G-invariant. In other words, let
T(K)
denote the centroid of
K
, we have

gT(K)=T(gK)=T(K)

for all
g\in G
. Thus
T(K)
is a fixed point of
G
, since
G
has no non-zero fixed points,
T(K)=0
. Since the centroid of the convex body
K
is at the origin, we have
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
.
∎

For
G=\{I\}
, the
G
-invariant convex bodies constitute the entire collection of convex bodies, which naturally includes
\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
. Furthermore, we have:

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{K}^{n}_{o}\subset\mathcal{K}_{G}
.

(2)

G=\{I\}
.

Proof.

(2
\Rightarrow

If

, then

constitutes the entire collection of convex bodies.

(1
\Rightarrow

Suppose

Since

. But

, so

, so

4.
Geometric Characterization of Irreducible Closed Subgroups

A group
G\subset\mathrm{O}(n)
is irreducible if the only subspaces
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are invariant under
G
(i.e.,
gV=V
for all
g\in G
) are
\{0\}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In the previous section, we classified
G
-invariant convex bodies. We now turn to investigating the Minkowski problem. Specifically, when
G
is an irreducible closed subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
, we study the existence of solutions to the
G
-invariant
L_{p}
dual Minkowski problem. The objective of this section is to characterize the geometry of irreducible closed subgroups, which is indispensable for solving the Minkowski problem using variational methods.

The following observation is crucial for solving the Minkowski problem via variational methods.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G
be a closed subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
with
n\geq 2
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

For every
v\in S^{n-1}
, the orbit
Gv
is not contained in any closed hemisphere.

(2)

G
is irreducible (i.e.,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has no nontrivial
G
-invariant subspaces).

Proof.

(1
\Rightarrow
2):

Assume condition
(1)
holds. Suppose, for contradiction, that
G
is reducible. Then there exists a nontrivial subspace
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
0<\dim W<n
) that is
G
-invariant (
gW=W
for all
g\in G
). Since
G\subset\mathrm{O}(n)
, the orthogonal complement
W^{\perp}
is also
G
-invariant: If
u\in W^{\perp}
, for any
w\in W
, since
W
is
G
-invariant,
g^{-1}w\in W
for all
g\in G
, then
\langle gu,w\rangle=\langle u,g^{-1}w\rangle=0
. Thus,
gu\in W^{\perp}
.

Take a unit vector
v\in W
, then
Gv\subset W
by
G
-invariance. Choose a unit vector
u\in W^{\perp}
. For all
x\in Gv\subset W
, we have
\langle x,u\rangle=0
. Thus,

Gv\subset\{x\in S^{n-1}:\langle x,u\rangle\geq 0\}=H_{u},

contradicting
(1)
. Hence
G
is irreducible.

(2
\Rightarrow
1):

Assume
G
is irreducible. For any
v\in S^{n-1}
, irreducibility implies
\text{span}(Gv)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
; otherwise
\text{span}(Gv)
would be a nontrivial
G
-invariant subspace.

Suppose there exists a vector
v\in S^{n-1}
, such that the orbit
Gv
is contained in some closed hemisphere, then there exists a unit vector
u\in S^{n-1}
such that

\langle gv,u\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{ for all}\quad g\in G.

(4.1)

Since
G
is a compact group, there exists a
G
-invariant probability measure (the Haar measure)
dh
on
G
. Let
\bar{v}=\int_{G}gvdh(g)
. For any
g_{1}\in G
, we have

\displaystyle g_{1}\bar{v}=

\displaystyle\int_{G}g_{1}gvdh(g)=\int_{G}svdh(g_{1}^{-1}s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{G}svdh(s)=\bar{v}.

In other words,
\bar{v}
is a fixed point of
G
. Since
G
is irreducible,
n\geq 2
, it has no non-zero fixed points. If there exists a non-zero fixed point
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
gx_{1}=x_{1}
for all
g\in G
, then the line
\mathbb{R}x_{1}
forms a nontrivial
G
-invariant subspace, contradicting the irreducibility. Therefore,

\bar{v}=\int_{G}gvdh(g)=0.

(4.2)

By (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), consider the nonnegative continuous function
g\mapsto\langle gv,u\rangle
. Its integral satisfies:

\int_{G}\langle gv,u\rangle dh=\left\langle\int_{G}gvdh,u\right\rangle=\langle\bar{v},u\rangle=\langle 0,u\rangle=0.

Since
\langle gv,u\rangle
is nonnegative, continuous, and the integral vanishes, note that the Haar measure is positive on nonempty open sets. If
\langle g_{0}v,u\rangle>0
for some
g_{0}\in G
, then by continuity there exists a neighborhood
U
of
g_{0}
such that
\langle gv,u\rangle>0
in
U
. This implies
\int_{G}\langle gv,u\rangle dh>0
, leading to a contradiction. Thus, we must have
\langle gv,u\rangle=0
for all
g\in G
. This implies
u\perp\text{span}(Gv)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so
u=0
, contradicting
|u|=1
. Hence
Gv
is not contained in any closed hemisphere, proving
(1)
.

∎

We need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
,
v\in S^{n-1}
. Then

Gv
is not contained in any closed hemisphere if and only if
0\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{conv}(Gv))
.

Proof.

Let
H_{u}=\{x\in S^{n-1}:\langle x,u\rangle\geq 0\}
denote the closed hemisphere with normal vector
u
. If
0\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{conv}(Gv))
, for any
u\in S^{n-1}
, there exists
x\in\operatorname{conv}(Gv)
such that
\langle x,u\rangle<0
(since
B(\epsilon)\subset\operatorname{conv}(Gv)
for some
\epsilon
, take
x=-\epsilon u
). Let
x=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}g_{i}v
where
g_{i}\in G
,
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}=1
. In other words,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\langle g_{i}v,u\rangle<0
. Then there exists
g_{i}
such that
\langle g_{i}v,u\rangle<0
. Therefore,
Gv\not\subset Hu
.

Conversely, suppose
Gv\not\subset Hu
for any
u\in S^{n-1}
. If
0\notin\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}(Gv)
, then there exists
u\in S^{n-1}
such that
\operatorname{conv}(Gv)\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,u\rangle\geq 0\}
. In other words,
Gv\subset H_{u}
. This leads to a contradiction.
∎

Lemma
4.2
and Theorem
4.1
show that when
G
is an irreducible closed subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
with
n\geq 2
,
\operatorname{conv}(Gv)
is a convex body with the origin in its interior for all nonzero vectors
v
.

From Theorem
4.1
, we observe that when
G
is irreducible, a
G
-invariant measure
\mu
is not concentrated
in any closed hemisphere.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G
be an irreducible closed subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
,
n\geq 2
. If
\mu
is a
G
-invariant nonzero Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
, then
\mu
is not concentrated
in any closed hemisphere.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.1
, if
\mu(U)>0
for some Borel set
U
,
GU
is not contained in any closed hemisphere. Since
\mu
is
G
-invariant,
\mu(gU)>0
for every
g\in G
. Therefore,
\mu
is not concentrated
in any closed hemisphere.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
a>0
and
S\subset S^{n-1}
. If the vectors in
S
are not contained in any closed hemisphere, then

K=\bigcap_{u\in S}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,u\rangle\leq a\}

is a compact convex set.

Proof.

Clearly,
K
is closed and convex. If
K
is unbounded, then there exists a sequence
\{x_{k}\}\subset K
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}|x_{k}|=\infty.

Consider the sequence
y_{k}=\frac{x_{k}}{|x_{k}|}
. Since
y_{k}\in S^{n-1}
for all
k
, there exists a convergent subsequence
\{y_{k_{j}}\}
such that

\lim_{j\to\infty}y_{k_{j}}=v\quad\text{for some}\quad v\in S^{n-1}.

For each
x_{k_{j}}\in K
and every
u\in S
, the definition of
K
implies

\langle x_{k_{j}},u\rangle\leq a.

Dividing both sides by
|x_{k_{j}}|>0
, we obtain

\langle y_{k_{j}},u\rangle\leq\frac{a}{|x_{k_{j}}|}.

Taking the limit as
j\to\infty
, the left side converges to
\langle v,u\rangle
, and the right side converges to
0
(since
|x_{k_{j}}|\to\infty
). Thus, for every
u\in S
,

\langle v,u\rangle\leq 0.

This implies that
S
is contained in the closed hemisphere
\{x\in S^{n-1}:\langle v,x\rangle\leq 0\}
. This leads to a contradiction. Therefore,
K
is compact.
∎

Thus, when the group
G
is irreducible, by setting
S=Gv
, we obtain:

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
G
be an irreducible closed subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
,
n\geq 2
. For every
v\in S^{n-1}
and
a>0
, the set

K(v)=\bigcap_{u\in Gv}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,u\rangle\leq a\}

is a convex body.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.4
and Theorem
4.1
,
K(v)
is a compact convex set. Clearly,
aB\subset K(v)
, so
K(v)
is a convex body with the origin in its interior.
∎

If
G_{1}
is irreducible and
G_{1}\subset G_{2}
, then
G_{2}
is also irreducible.
Next we present several classical examples of finite irreducible groups, some of which have appeared in
[
7
]
.

(1)

The symmetry group
G_{n}
of a regular
n
-simplex centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(regular triangle, regular tetrahedron, etc.)
is isomorphic to the symmetric group
S_{n+1}
.
Since
-I\notin G_{n}
, by Theorem
3.5
the class of
G_{n}
-invariant
convex bodies is not contained in
\mathcal{K}^{n}_{e}
.

For example, when
n=2
, the symmetry group of a regular triangle consists of 6 elements: identity map,
rotation by
120^{\circ}
,
rotation by
240^{\circ}
, and the reflections across the three axes that pass through a vertex and are perpendicular to the opposite side.

(2)

The rotation symmetry group (i.e., orientation-preserving symmetries) of a regular
n
-simplex centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is isomorphic to the alternating group
A_{n+1}
.
For example, the rotation symmetry group
G
of an equilateral triangle consists of rotations by
0^{\circ}
,
120^{\circ}
, and
240^{\circ}
.

The class of
G
-invariant convex bodies is rich. For instance, we take a suitable convex arc connecting two vertices of an equilateral triangle, and rotate this arc by
120^{\circ}
and
240^{\circ}
to form the boundary of a convex body. For detailed constructions, see Construction 3.4 in
[
7
]
.

(3)

For
m\geq 3
, the dihedral group
D_{m}
is the symmetry group of a regular
m
-gon in the plane. If
m
is odd, then
-I\notin D_{m}
. From Theorem
3.5
, the class of
D_{m}
-invariant convex bodies is not a subset of
\mathcal{K}^{2}_{e}
.

(4)

The cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
is a subgroup of
D_{m}
for
m\geq 3
, and it constitutes the rotational symmetry group of a regular
m
-gon (generated by the rotation
\frac{2\pi}{m}
).

(5)

The symmetry group of the cube
[-1,1]^{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the Weyl group
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{n}\rtimes S_{n}
(order
2^{n}n!
).
Each element
(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n};\sigma)
corresponds to an
n\times n
generalized permutation matrix (having exactly one nonzero entry
\pm 1
in each row and column), where
the permutation
\sigma
determines the positions of the nonzero entries, and
the signs
\epsilon_{i}
determine whether these nonzero entries are
+1
or
-1
.
When
n\geq 3
is odd, its rotation symmetry group does not contain
-I
.

(6)

Since in
\mathbb{R}^{1}
there exist no nontrivial subspaces (the only subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{1}
are
\{0\}
and
\mathbb{R}^{1}
), any subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(1)
(namely
\{1\}
or
\{\pm 1\}
) is automatically irreducible.

For a finite irreducible group
G\subset\mathrm{O}(n)
with
-I\notin G
, Theorem
3.5
implies that for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
-x\notin Gx
. For any such
x
,
\mathrm{conv}(Gx)
is a convex body that is not origin-symmetric. Consequently,
\mathrm{conv}(Gx)+tB
forms a family of
G
-invariant convex bodies that are not origin-symmetric for any
t\geq 0
.

There have been extensive studies on the algebraic properties of irreducible representations. For example,

[
51
]
classified all irreducible subgroups of
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, and
[
15
]
investigated the irreducible representations of finite groups.
[
47
]
discussed the classification of real representations of a finite
group. In particular, for a finite group
G
acting irreducibly on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the endomorphism ring
\operatorname{End}_{G}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of
G
-equivariant linear maps is isomorphic to one of

\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
, or
\mathbb{H}
(the quaternions).

We next present two theorems characterizing the geometry of irreducible groups. They help us understand the symmetry inherent in the corresponding group-invariant convex bodies,
though not used elsewhere in the paper.

Ellipsoids are fundamental objects in convex geometry.
The
celebrated
John ellipsoid
, which was introduced by John,
is an extremely useful tool in convex geometry and Banach space
geometry (see, e.g.,
[
27
,
29
]
). Associated with a convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
the
John ellipsoid

JK
is the unique ellipsoid of
maximal volume contained in
K
. For
G
-invariant convex bodies corresponding to an irreducible group
G
, we have:

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
G
be an irreducible subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
with
n\geq 2
. Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
G
-invariant convex body. Then the John ellipsoid of
K
is a ball centered at the origin.

Proof.

Let
E
be the John ellipsoid of
K
.
Since
G\subset\mathrm{O}(n)
preserves volume and
K
is
G
-invariant, for any
g\in G\subset\mathrm{O}(n)
, we have
gK=K
. Since
E\subset K
,
gE
is an ellipsoid contained in
gK=K
with
\operatorname{vol}(gE)=\operatorname{vol}(E)
. By the uniqueness of the John ellipsoid, we have
gE=E
. Therefore
E
is
G
-invariant. Its centroid is a fixed point of
G
, and by irreducibility, must be the origin.
Consequently,
E
can be expressed as:

E=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x^{\top}Ax\leq 1\}

(4.3)

for some symmetric positive definite matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})
. And

gE=\{gx\mid x^{\top}Ax\leq 1\}=\{y\mid(g^{-1}y)^{\top}A(g^{-1}y)\leq 1\}=\{y\mid y^{\top}(gAg^{\top})y\leq 1\}.

Equating
gE=E
gives
gAg^{\top}=A
. Since
g^{-1}=g^{\top}
for orthogonal matrices, this implies

gAg^{-1}=A\quad\text{for all}\quad g\in G.

In other words,

Ag=gA\quad\text{for all}\quad g\in G.

(4.4)

Thus,
A
commutes with every element of
G
.

Since
A
is a symmetric positive definite matrix, its eigenvalues are real numbers. Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
A
, and
V_{\lambda}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
its corresponding eigenspace:

V_{\lambda}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid Av=\lambda v\}.

For any
v\in V_{\lambda}
and any
g\in G
, by (
4.4
), we have

A(gv)=g(Av)=g(\lambda v)=\lambda(gv).

This shows
gv\in V_{\lambda}
, so
V_{\lambda}
is
G
-invariant.

By the irreducibility of
G
, the only
G
-invariant subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are
\{0\}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
V_{\lambda}\neq\{0\}
(as
\lambda
is an eigenvalue), we must have
V_{\lambda}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, every vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an eigenvector of
A
with eigenvalue
\lambda
, implying
A=\lambda I
for some
\lambda>0
(since
A
is positive definite).

Substituting
A=\lambda I
into the equation (
4.3
)
for
E
yields:

E=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x^{\top}(\lambda I)x\leq 1\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\lambda|x|^{2}\leq 1\}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\bigm||x|\leq\lambda^{-1/2}\right\},

which is a ball centered at the origin with radius
\lambda^{-1/2}
. Therefore, the John ellipsoid of
K
is a ball centered at the origin.
∎

This demonstrates the symmetry of group-invariant convex bodies for irreducible groups.

For irreducible groups, we also have the following characterization:

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
with
n\geq 2
. Then the action of
G
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is irreducible if and only if the only
G
-invariant symmetric bilinear forms on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are scalar multiples of the standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

Proof.

Suppose
G
is irreducible. Let
B:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
G
-invariant symmetric bilinear form. We show
B(x,y)=c\langle x,y\rangle
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Since
B
is a symmetric bilinear form,
there exists a linear operator
A_{B}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that:

B(x,y)=\langle A_{B}x,y\rangle\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(4.5)

Since
B
is symmetric, then
B(x,y)=\langle A_{B}x,y\rangle=B(y,x)=\langle x,A_{B}y\rangle
, i.e.,
A_{B}
is self-adjoint.

Since
B
is
G
-invariant and
G\subset\mathrm{O}(n)
, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\displaystyle B(gx,gy)

\displaystyle=B(x,y)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\langle A_{B}(gx),gy\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A_{B}x,y\rangle

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\langle g^{-1}A_{B}gx,y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle A_{B}x,y\rangle\quad(\text{using }\langle gu,gv\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow g^{-1}A_{B}g

\displaystyle=A_{B}\quad\text{(i.e., }A_{B}g=gA_{B}).

Since
A_{B}
is self-adjoint, all eigenvalues of
A_{B}
are real numbers. Similar to the proof of Theorem
4.6
, we have
A_{B}=cI
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Substituting into (
4.5
), we have
B(x,y)=c\langle x,y\rangle
.

Conversely, assume that the only
G
-invariant symmetric bilinear forms on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are scalar multiples of the standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, but
G
is reducible. Then there exists a non-trivial
G
-invariant subspace
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0<\dim W<n
.
W^{\perp}
is also
G
-invariant.

Define a symmetric bilinear form:

B(x,y)=\langle\operatorname{proj}_{W}x,\operatorname{proj}_{W}y\rangle,

where
\operatorname{proj}_{W}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
W
.

For any
g\in G
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, since
gW=W
and
g\in\mathrm{O}(n)
:

B(gx,gy)=\langle\operatorname{proj}_{W}(gx),\operatorname{proj}_{W}(gy)\rangle=\langle g(\operatorname{proj}_{W}x),g(\operatorname{proj}_{W}y)\rangle=\langle\operatorname{proj}_{W}x,\operatorname{proj}_{W}y\rangle=B(x,y).

Thus
B
is
G
-invariant. However,
B
is not a scalar multiple of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
; take
v\in W\setminus\{0\}
and
u\in W^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
, then:

B(v,v)=\langle v,v\rangle>0,\quad B(u,u)=0,\quad\text{but}\quad\langle u,u\rangle>0.

Hence
B\neq c\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for any scalar
c
.

∎

5.
Existence of Solutions to the
L_{p}
Dual Minkowski Problem When
G
is Closed

To solve the Minkowski problem via variational methods, we require uniform estimates for certain geometric structures associated with irreducible closed subgroups
G\subset\mathrm{O}(n)
. From Lemma
4.2
and Theorem
4.1
, we know that for any
v\in S^{n-1}
, the convex hull of its orbit
\operatorname{conv}(Gv)
is a convex body containing the origin in its interior. Furthermore, we have:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
G
be an irreducible closed subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
with
n\geq 2
. There exists
r>0
, independent of
v
, such that for all
v\in S^{n-1}
, the inclusion

B(r)\subset\operatorname{conv}(Gv)
holds.

Proof.

Assume no such
r
exists. Then for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a unit vector
v_{k}\in S^{n-1}
such that:

B\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\not\subset\operatorname{conv}(Gv_{k}).

Thus there exists
y_{k}\in B\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
with
y_{k}\notin\operatorname{conv}(Gv_{k})
. Since
S^{n-1}
is compact,
\{v_{k}\}
has a convergent subsequence (still denoted by
\{v_{k}\}
) satisfying
v_{k}\to v
for some
v\in S^{n-1}
. For any
\epsilon>0
, if
k
is sufficiently large, we have

|v_{k}-v|<\epsilon\quad\text{and}\quad|gv_{k}-gv|=|g(v_{k}-v)|=|v_{k}-v|<\epsilon\quad\text{for all $g\in G$}.

Therefore, for any
x=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}g_{i}v_{k}\in\operatorname{conv}(Gv_{k})
where
\lambda_{i}>0
,
g_{i}\in G
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}=1
, there exists
y=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}g_{i}v\in\operatorname{conv}(Gv)
such that

\displaystyle|x-y|=

\displaystyle|\lambda_{1}(g_{1}v_{k}-g_{1}v)+\cdots+\lambda_{m}(g_{m}v_{k}-g_{m}v)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}|g_{1}v_{k}-g_{1}v|+\cdots+\lambda_{m}|g_{m}v_{k}-gv|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\epsilon=\epsilon.

Similarly, for any
y=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}g_{i}v\in\operatorname{conv}(Gv)
,
take
x=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}g_{i}v_{k}\in\operatorname{conv}(Gv_{k})
, then
|x-y|<\epsilon
.
Then the Hausdorff metric satisfies

d_{H}(\operatorname{conv}(Gv_{k}),\operatorname{conv}(Gv))\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

By irreducibility of
G
(Lemma
4.2
and Theorem
4.1
),
0\in\operatorname{int}(\operatorname{conv}(Gv))
, so there exists
\delta>0
such that:

B(\delta)\subset\operatorname{conv}(Gv).

Let
C_{k}=\operatorname{conv}(Gv_{k})
and
C=\operatorname{conv}(Gv)
. Since
d_{H}(C_{k},C)\to 0
, there exists a large
N
such that for any
k>N
, the support function

\left|h_{C_{k}}(u)-h_{C}(u)\right|<\frac{\delta}{2},\quad\text{for each $u\in S^{n-1}$}.

(5.1)

Since
B(\delta)\subset C
, we have
h_{C}(u)\geq\delta
. Then
by (
5.1
),

h_{C_{k}}(u)>h_{C}(u)-\frac{\delta}{2}\geq\delta-\frac{\delta}{2}=\frac{\delta}{2}\quad\text{for each $u\in S^{n-1}$}.

In other words,
B(\frac{\delta}{2})\subset C_{k}=\operatorname{conv}(Gv_{k})
. Let
k>\max\{N,\frac{2}{\delta}\}
, we have:

y_{k}\in B\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\subset B\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\subset\operatorname{conv}(Gv_{k}),

which implies
y_{k}\in\operatorname{conv}(Gv_{k})
, contradicting
y_{k}\notin\operatorname{conv}(Gv_{k})
. Thus the lemma holds.
∎

We have proved that
K(v)
is a convex body in Lemma
4.5
, i.e.,
K(v)
is bounded (depending on
v
). Now we establish a uniform estimate for the upper bound of
K(v)
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
G
be an irreducible closed subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
and
a>0
fixed. For every
v\in S^{n-1}
, define

K(v)=\bigcap_{u\in Gv}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,u\rangle\leq a\}.

(5.2)

There exists
R>0
(independent of
v
) such that
K(v)\subset B(R)
for all
v\in S^{n-1}
.

Proof.

Let
r>0
be the constant from Lemma
5.1
, so
B(r)\subset\operatorname{conv}(Gv)
for any
v\in S^{n-1}
. For any
0\neq x\in K(v)
, let
w=x/|x|
, we have the support function

h_{\operatorname{conv}(Gv)}(w)=\max_{u\in Gv}\langle w,u\rangle\geq r\quad\text{for all $v\in S^{n-1}$}.

Hence, there exists
u_{v}\in Gv
such that
\langle w,u_{v}\rangle\geq r
.
Since
x\in K(v)
and
u_{v}\in Gv
, from the definition of (
5.2
), we have
\langle x,u_{v}\rangle\leq a
. Thus

a\geq\langle x,u_{v}\rangle=|x|\langle w,u_{v}\rangle\geq|x|r,

which implies:

|x|\leq\frac{a}{r}.

Taking
R=a/r>0
gives the uniform bound
K(v)\subset B(R)
for all
v\in S^{n-1}
.
∎

Next, we hope to transform the
L_{p}
dual Minkowski problem into an optimization problem.

Let
G
be an irreducible closed subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
and
Q
a
G
-invariant star body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For any nonzero finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}
,
we define the set
C^{+}(S^{n-1})
as the collection of continuous functions
f:S^{n-1}\to(0,\infty)
. If
q\neq 0
,
p\neq 0
, we introduce a functional
\Phi_{p}:C^{+}(S^{n-1})\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\Phi_{p}(f)=\frac{1}{p}\log\int_{S^{n-1}}f^{p}d\mu-\frac{1}{q}\log\widetilde{V}_{q}([f],Q).

(5.3)

If
q\neq 0
,
p=0
, define

\Phi_{0}(f)=\frac{1}{|\mu|}\int_{S^{n-1}}\log fd\mu-\frac{1}{q}\log\widetilde{V}_{q}([f],Q),

(5.4)

where
[f]
is the Wulff shape generated by
f
:

[f]=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,v\rangle\leq f(v),\;\text{for all}\;v\in S^{n-1}\right\}.

(5.5)

Note that
\Phi_{p}
is homogeneous of degree
0
, i.e., for all
\lambda>0
and
f\in C^{+}(S^{n-1})
, we have

\Phi_{p}(\lambda f)=\Phi_{p}(f).

(5.6)

Lemma 5.3
.

For
q\neq 0
,
p\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f\in C^{+}(S^{n-1})
, we have

\Phi_{p}(h_{[f]})\leq\Phi_{p}(f).

(5.7)

Proof.

From the definition of the Wulff shape, we have
0<h_{[f]}\leq f
and
[h_{[f]}]=[f]
. Therefore, if
p<0
,

h_{[f]}^{p}\geq f^{p},\quad\quad\frac{1}{p}\log\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{[f]}^{p}d\mu\leq\frac{1}{p}\log\int_{S^{n-1}}f^{p}d\mu.

If
p>0
,

h_{[f]}^{p}\leq f^{p},\quad\quad\frac{1}{p}\log\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{[f]}^{p}d\mu\leq\frac{1}{p}\log\int_{S^{n-1}}f^{p}d\mu.

If

,

h_{[f]}\leq f,\quad\quad\int_{S^{n-1}}\log h_{[f]}d\mu\leq\int_{S^{n-1}}\log fd\mu.

From definitions (

) and (

), the result is proved.
∎

Define the set

as:

\mathcal{L}=\{f\in C^{+}(S^{n-1}):\text{$f$ is $G$-invariant and $\widetilde{V}_{q}([f],Q)=1$ }\}.

(5.8)

By definitions (
5.3
) and (

), for

\Phi_{p}(f)=\frac{1}{p}\log\int_{S^{n-1}}f^{p}d\mu,

(5.9)

and when

,

\Phi_{0}(f)=\frac{1}{|\mu|}\int_{S^{n-1}}\log fd\mu.

(5.10)

Due to the homogeneity of

in (

), we can restrict the optimization problem for

-invariant functions to the set

, where local extrema become global. This is a standard approach for homogeneous Minkowski problems. In contrast, for non-homogeneous cases like the Gaussian Minkowski problem, see, e.g.,
[

,

.

Note that, if

is a

-invariant function, then the Wulff shape

is clearly a

-invariant convex body. Since for any

\displaystyle g[f]=

\displaystyle\left\{gx\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,u\rangle\leq f(u),\;u\in S^{n-1}\right\}

\displaystyle\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle g^{-1}y,u\rangle\leq f(u),\;u\in S^{n-1}\right\}

Conversely, if

Next, we consider the minimization problem for the functional
\Phi_{p}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
q>0
,
p\in\mathbb{R}
.
\{K_{i}\}
is a family of
G
-invariant convex bodies. If
h_{K_{i}}\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\lim_{i\to\infty}R_{i}=+\infty
, where
R_{i}=\max\{|x|:x\in K_{i}\}
, then

\lim_{i\to\infty}\Phi_{p}(h_{K_{i}})=+\infty.

(5.11)

Proof.

From Theorem
5.2
with
a=1
, there exists
m>0
such that

K(v)=\bigcap_{u\in Gv}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,u\rangle\leq 1\}\subset mB

for every

. Then

\bigcap_{u\in Gv}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,u\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2m}\}\subset\frac{1}{2}B

(5.12)

for every
v\in S^{n-1}
. Since
\lim_{i\to\infty}R_{i}=+\infty
, there exists a large

such that for

,
R_{i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}<\frac{1}{2m}
. We choose
v_{i}\in S^{n-1}
such that
R_{i}v_{i}\in K_{i}
, by (
5.12

\bigcap_{u\in Gv_{i}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,u\rangle\leq R_{i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\}\subset\bigcap_{u\in Gv_{i}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,u\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2m}\}\subset\frac{1}{2}B.

(5.13)

Therefore, for
i>N
, from (

), if
u\in S^{n-1}
, then

u\notin\bigcap_{w\in Gv_{i}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,w\rangle\leq R_{i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\}.

In other words, for any

, there exists

such that
\langle u,w\rangle>R_{i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

is

. Hence,

h_{K_{i}}(u)\geq\langle u,R_{i}w\rangle=R_{i}\langle u,w\rangle\geq R_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\text{$\forall u\in S^{n-1}$}.

(5.14)

Therefore, by (

), if

\log\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K_{i}}^{p}d\mu\geq\log\int_{S^{n-1}}R_{i}^{\frac{p}{2}}d\mu\rightarrow+\infty,

\Phi_{p}(h_{K_{i}})=\frac{1}{p}\log\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K_{i}}^{p}d\mu\rightarrow+\infty.

(5.15)

If

\log\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K_{i}}^{p}d\mu\leq\log\int_{S^{n-1}}R_{i}^{-\frac{|p|}{2}}d\mu\rightarrow-\infty,

\Phi_{p}(h_{K_{i}})=\frac{1}{p}\log\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K_{i}}^{p}d\mu\rightarrow+\infty.

(5.16)

If

\Phi_{0}(h_{K_{i}})=\frac{1}{|\mu|}\int_{S^{n-1}}\log h_{K_{i}}d\mu\geq\frac{1}{|\mu|}\int_{S^{n-1}}\log R_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}d\mu\rightarrow+\infty.

(5.17)

Combining (
5.15
), (
5.16
) and (
5.17
), we complete the proof.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

,

Lemma 5.8

Let

Next, we will demonstrate that the minimizer

of this optimization problem solves the corresponding

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
q>0
,
p\in\mathbb{R}
,
G
be an irreducible closed subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
with
n\geq 2
, and
Q
be a
G
-invariant star body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any non-zero finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}
,
\mu
is
G
-invariant if and only if there exists a
G
-invariant convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K,Q,\cdot),\quad\text{$p\neq q$,}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu=\left(\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K}^{p}d\mu\right)\cdot\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K,Q,\cdot),\quad\text{$p=q$}.

Proof.

Let
h_{K}
be the minimizer of functional
\Phi_{p}
in Theorem
5.6
with
\widetilde{V}_{q}(K,Q)=1
.
By the homogeneity of
\Phi_{p}
in (
5.6
),
h_{K}
is the global minimizer of
\Phi_{p}
. For any continuous
G
-invariant function
f:S^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the function
\left(h_{K}+tf\right)
belongs to
C^{+}(S^{n-1})
and is
G
-invariant for sufficiently small
|t|
. Let
g(t)=\Phi_{p}(h_{K}+tf)
for sufficiently small
|t|
.
From (
5.18
), we obtain

g(t)=\Phi_{p}(h_{K}+tf)\geq\Phi_{p}(h_{K})=g(0).

(5.21)

If
p\neq 0
, by (
5.3
),

g(t)=\frac{1}{p}\log\int_{S^{n-1}}(h_{K}+tf)^{p}d\mu-\frac{1}{q}\log\widetilde{V}_{q}([h_{K}+tf],Q).

Therefore, by (
5.21
) and (
2.6
), if
p\neq 0
,

0=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}g(t)=\dfrac{\int_{S^{n-1}}fh_{K}^{p-1}d\mu}{\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K}^{p}d\mu}-\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{f}{h_{K}}d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,Q).

In other words,

\int_{S^{n-1}}fh_{K}^{p-1}d\mu=\lambda\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{f}{h_{K}}d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,Q)

(5.22)

for
\lambda=\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K}^{p}d\mu
. Therefore, for any continuous
G
-invariant function
\phi
, since
K
is
G
-invariant, the function
f=\frac{\phi}{h_{K}^{p-1}}
is also
G
-invariant. Substituting into (

), we have

\int_{S^{n-1}}\phi d\mu=\lambda\int_{S^{n-1}}\phi h_{K}^{-p}d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,Q)=\lambda\int_{S^{n-1}}\phi d\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K,Q).

(5.23)

Then by (
5.23
), Lemma
5.7
, and Lemma
5.8
, we have
\mu=\lambda\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K,Q,\cdot)
. When
p\neq q
, from the
(q-p)
-th homogeneity of
\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K,Q,\cdot)
in
K
, let
K^{\prime}=\lambda^{\frac{1}{q-p}}K
, we obtain

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K^{\prime},Q,\cdot)
.

Similarly, if
p=0
,

g(t)=\frac{1}{|\mu|}\int_{S^{n-1}}\log(h_{K}+tf)d\mu-\frac{1}{q}\log\widetilde{V}_{q}([h_{K}+tf],Q),

then we have

0=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}g(t)=\frac{1}{|\mu|}\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{f}{h_{K}}d\mu-\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{f}{h_{K}}d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,Q).

In other words,

\int_{S^{n-1}}\phi d\mu=|\mu|\int_{S^{n-1}}\phi d\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,Q)

(5.24)

for any continuous
G
-invariant function
\phi
. From Lemma
5.7
and Lemma

\mu=|\mu|\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,Q,\cdot)=\widetilde{C}_{q}(|\mu|^{\frac{1}{q}}K,Q,\cdot)
.
∎

6.
General Cases of Non-closed Group
G

In the discussion of earlier sections, we required the group
G
to be closed in certain theorems.
This section aims to remove the closed group restriction in Theorems
3.1
,
3.4
and
5.9
. First, we establish the following lemma:

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\overline{Gx}=\overline{G}x
, where
\overline{G}
denotes the closure of
G
in
\mathrm{O}(n)
.

Proof.

If
y\in\overline{Gx}
, there exists a sequence
\{g_{i}x\}
with
g_{i}\in G
such that
g_{i}x\rightarrow y
. Since
\mathrm{O}(n)
is compact, there exists a convergent subsequence of
\{g_{i}\}
, which we still denote by
\{g_{i}\}
, such that
g_{i}\rightarrow g\in\overline{G}
. Then
g_{i}x\rightarrow gx\in\overline{G}x
. As
g_{i}x\rightarrow y
, it follows that
y=gx\in\overline{G}x
.

Conversely, if
z\in\overline{G}x
,
z=gx\in\overline{G}x
for some
g\in\overline{G}
. Then there exist
g_{i}\in G
such that
g_{i}\rightarrow g
. Since
g_{i}x\in Gx
and
g_{i}x\rightarrow gx
, we have
z=gx\in\overline{Gx}
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
. The set of
G
-invariant convex bodies equals
the set of
\overline{G}
-invariant convex bodies, i.e.,
\mathcal{K}_{G}=\mathcal{K}_{\overline{G}}
.

Proof.

Since
G\subset\overline{G}
,
\overline{G}
-invariant convex bodies are automatically
G
-invariant.

Conversely, let
K
be a
G
-invariant convex body. For any
g\in\overline{G}
, there exists a sequence
g_{i}
in
G
such that
g_{i}\rightarrow g
. Since
K
is
G
-invariant, for any
x\in K
, we have
g_{i}x\in K
. Since
K
is compact,
g_{i}x\to gx\in K
. Thus
K
is
\overline{G}
-invariant.
∎

Similarly, if the star body
Q
is
G
-invariant, then
Q
is also
\overline{G}
-invariant.

When the closedness restriction is removed, theorems
3.1
and
3.4
fail to hold, since the set
K
constructed in the proof may not be a convex body. We present the following counterexample:

Example 6.3
.

Let
n=2
and
G
be the subgroup of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
generated by an irrational rotation. Specifically, fix an irrational rotation
\alpha
(
\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\notin\mathbb{Q}
) and define
G=\{R_{k\alpha}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, where
R_{\theta}
denotes the rotation matrix by angle
\theta
. Then
G
is dense in
\mathrm{SO}(2)
.

Take
x=(1,0)
. The orbit
Gx=\{(\cos(k\alpha),\sin(k\alpha))\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is dense in the unit circle, but
-x=(-1,0)\notin Gx
(since
-x
corresponds to angle
\pi
, and
k\alpha\equiv\pi\pmod{2\pi}
has no integer solution; otherwise
\alpha
would be a rational multiple of
\pi
). Therefore, the action of
G
on the circle is not transitive.

Since
G
is dense in
\mathrm{SO}(2)
, Lemma
6.2
implies that a convex body is
G
-invariant if and only if it is
\mathrm{SO}(2)
-invariant. The only
\mathrm{SO}(2)
-invariant convex bodies are disks centered at the origin. This contradicts both Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.4
.

By making slight modifications to Theorems
3.1
and
3.4
, we have:

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{K}_{G}=\mathcal{B}^{n}

(2)

For any
v\in S^{n-1}
, the orbit
Gv
is dense in
S^{n-1}
.

Proof.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1):

For any
v\in S^{n-1}
, since
\overline{Gv}=S^{n-1}
, by Lemma
6.1
,
\overline{G}v=\overline{Gv}=S^{n-1}
. Thus,
\overline{G}
acts transitively on
S^{n-1}
. From Theorem
3.1
, this implies that the only
\overline{G}
-invariant convex bodies are balls. From Lemma
6.2
,
\mathcal{K}_{G}=\mathcal{K}_{\overline{G}}=\mathcal{B}^{n}
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2):

By Lemma
6.2
, condition
(1)
implies that every
\overline{G}
-invariant
convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Euclidean ball centered at the origin. By Theorem
3.1
, for any
v\in S^{n-1}
we have
\overline{G}v=\overline{Gv}=S^{n-1}
. In other words, the orbit
Gv
is dense in
S^{n-1}
.
∎

Similarly, we have:

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{K}_{G}\subset\mathcal{K}^{n}_{e}
.

(2)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
-x\in\overline{Gx}
.

In fact, for any finite Borel measure,
G
-invariance implies
\overline{G}
-invariance:

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
, and
\mu
be a finite Borel measure on the unit sphere
S^{n-1}
. If
\mu
is
G
-invariant, then
\mu
is
\overline{G}
-invariant.

Proof.

We need to show that
\mu(hE)=\mu(E)
for all
h\in\overline{G}
and all Borel sets
E\subset S^{n-1}
.

Since
\mathrm{O}(n)
is a compact Lie group under the Euclidean topology of
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
,

\overline{G}
is a closed subgroup. The
G
-invariance means
g_{*}\mu=\mu
for all
g\in G
, where
g_{*}\mu(E)=\mu(g^{-1}E)
.

Fix
h\in\overline{G}
. Let
\{g_{k}\}\subset G
be a sequence converging to
h\in\overline{G}
. For any continuous function
f:S^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, consider:

\int_{S^{n-1}}f(x)dg_{k*}\mu(x)=\int_{S^{n-1}}f(g_{k}y)dg_{k*}\mu(g_{k}y)=\int_{S^{n-1}}f(g_{k}x)d\mu(x),

and

\int_{S^{n-1}}f(x)dh_{*}\mu(x)=\int_{S^{n-1}}f(hx)d\mu(x).

Since
g_{k}\to h
and the action is continuous,
g_{k}x\to hx
for each
x\in S^{n-1}
. By continuity of
f
, we have
f(g_{k}x)\to f(hx)
pointwise. As
f
is bounded and
\mu
is finite, the Dominated Convergence Theorem implies:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{S^{n-1}}f(g_{k}x)d\mu(x)=\int_{S^{n-1}}f(hx)d\mu(x).

Thus
g_{k*}\mu\to h_{*}\mu
weakly.

By
G
-invariance,
g_{k*}\mu=\mu
for all
k
. The constant sequence
\mu
converges weakly to
\mu
, so by uniqueness of weak limits:

h_{*}\mu=\mu\quad\text{as finite Borel measures}.

Hence
\mu(h^{-1}E)=h_{*}\mu(E)=\mu(E)
for all Borel sets
E\subset S^{n-1}
, so
\mu
is
\overline{G}
-invariant.
∎

Therefore, for general subgroups (without the closedness restriction) of
\mathrm{O}(n)
, we can still obtain solutions to the corresponding group-invariant
L_{p}
dual Minkowski problem.

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
q>0
,
p\in\mathbb{R}
,
G
be an irreducible subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
with
n\geq 2
, and
Q
be a
G
-invariant star body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any non-zero finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}
,
\mu
is
G
-invariant if and only if there exists a
G
-invariant convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K,Q,\cdot)\quad\text{when $p\neq q$},

and

\mu=\lambda\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K,Q,\cdot)\quad\text{for some $\lambda>0$ when $p=q$}.

Proof.

If
\mu
is
G
-invariant, by Lemma
6.6
,
\mu
is
\overline{G}
-invariant. Since
G
is irreducible and
G\subset\overline{G}
, the closure
\overline{G}
is also irreducible. Moreover, since

is
G
-invariant, it is also
\overline{G}
-invariant. Thus, by Theorem

, there exists a
\overline{G}
-invariant convex body
K

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K,Q,\cdot)\quad\text{when $p\neq q$},

and

\mu=\lambda\widetilde{C}_{p,q}(K,Q,\cdot)\quad\text{for some $\lambda>0$ when $p=q$}.

From Lemma
