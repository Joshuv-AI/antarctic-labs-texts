Photon-driven magnetization switching in Fe₃GeTe₂ enables programmable, multilevel magnetic memory
Authors: Wei-Qing Li, Sheng-Hsiung Hung, Jia-Xin Li, Chung-Chun Lu, Po-Liang Chen, Ching Kuo, Kun-Hung Pan, Horng-Tay Jeng, Chang-Hua Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rediscovery of van der Waals magnets has revitalized research into intrinsic two-dimensional magnetism and motivated unconventional strategies, such as electrostatic gating and strain, to switch magnetic states. Photon-driven magnetization switching, however, remains largely unexplored but holds promise for fast, high-density, and energy-efficient spintronic applications. Here, we show that the itinerant van der Waals ferromagnet Fe₃GeTe₂ exhibits tunable magnetism under optical pulsed excitation, with the response governed by photon energy, fluence, and polarization. This is evident from our magneto-optic measurements, which reveal that pulses with 2.3 eV photon energy effectively reduce the coercivity of Fe₃GeTe₂. When coercivity is sufficiently lowered by strong pulse fluence, the magnetic order becomes helicity-dependent, enabling reversible suppression and restoration of the out-of-plane magnetization. Further studies indicate that these phenomena originate from distinct semiconductor-like transitions in Fe₃GeTe₂, through which photons of opposite helicity induce distinct modifications in the electronic structure, thereby altering magnetic anisotropy and the ground state differently via optical doping effect. Building on this understanding, we further demonstrate the potential of Fe₃GeTe₂ for optically controlled nonvolatile memory, capable of multilevel information storage with reversible writing and erasing, as well as spatially defined spin states, offering promise for next-generation spintronics.

Introduction

Understanding the interplay between photons and spin systems is both of fundamental interest and a central focus of modern spintronics, as optical excitation can tune magnetic anisotropy energy (MAE) and spatially pattern spin structures1,2. Beyond these effects, magnetization can also be switched reversibly using light alone, without external magnetic fields or electric currents. This phenomenon, known as all-optical switching (AOS), offers the potential for high-speed, high-density, and energy-efficient magnetic memory3–5. So far, AOS has been predominantly demonstrated in rare-earth transition-metal ferrimagnetic alloys, such as GdFeCo and TbCo5–7, via two main approaches. One involves a single femtosecond laser pulse to drive the electronic system into a highly non-equilibrium state; during subsequent cooling, angular momentum transfer between antiferromagnetically coupled sublattices via conduction electrons determines the magnetization orientation, enabling single-shot switching between out-of-plane states (M↓/M↑)8,9. The other approach employs multi-shot excitation with helicity control, where laser pulses heat the material near its Curie point, allowing left- (σ−) and right- (σ+) circularly polarized pulses to direct the magnetization toward opposite out-of-plane orientations (i.e., helicity-dependent AOS). While the exact origins remain debated, the inverse Faraday effect, magnetic circular dichroism, and light-induced angular momentum transfer are considered possible mechanisms7,10–12. Beyond metallic systems, helicity-dependent AOS has also been demonstrated in ferromagnetic semiconductors, such as (Ga,Mn)As, but with a non-thermal origin. In this case, near-bandgap circularly polarized excitation generates spin-polarized holes that drive collective Mn spin rotation, causing magnetization reversal13. Despite these advances, practical application of AOS in spintronic devices remains challenged by the requirements for precise metal alloy deposition or complex epitaxial growth, complicating device fabrication and integration4.

In contrast, van der Waals (vdW) magnets are considered a potential alternative due to their layered structure, which allows exfoliation into atomically thin crystals, integration into two-dimensional (2D) heterostructures, and assembly on various substrates without lattice mismatch14–16. Their ultrathin nature also renders their magnetic properties susceptible to external stimuli, making it possible to reversibly tune magnetic states via strain and electrostatic gating17–20. By comparison, experimental demonstrations of AOS of magnetic states in vdW magnet remains limited, with only a few reports on few-layer CrI321,22. This material owns layered antiferromagnetism with out-of-plane spin orientation (type-A antiferromagnetic order)23, and helicity-dependent AOS has been demonstrated based on trilayer CrI3, showing switching between ↑↓↑ and ↓↑↓ two layered-antiferromagnetic states. This switching is likely driven by spin angular momentum transfer from photoexcited carriers to localized magnetic moments21. Helicity-dependent AOS has also been observed in bulk CrI3/monolayer WSe2 heterostructures. In such system, pulses trigger thermal demagnetization in CrI3, and interfacial charge transfer of photoexcited spin-polarized carriers from WSe2 to CrI3 governs the net magnetization during the subsequent cooling process22.

Beyond CrI3, Fe3GeTe2 (FGT) is a noteworthy emerging vdW magnet, known as an itinerant ferromagnet with strong perpendicular magnetic anisotropy and a high Curie temperature (~200 K in its bulk state)24,25. Although AOS has not yet been realized in FGT, prior studies have shown that its magnetism can be controlled optically. For example, femtosecond pulse excitation can transiently tune saturation magnetization, coercivity, and Curie temperature via optical-doping-induced changes in the electronic structure26. Additionally, circularly polarized CW excitation during zero-field cooling can guide magnetic domain formation, likely mediated by optically injected spin-polarized electrons27. Here, we extend these studies and demonstrate helicity-dependent AOS in FGT. Specifically, we show that pulsed excitation involving interband transitions in FGT enables modulation of the coercive field and control of the remanent magnetization. Notably, the remanent magnetization exhibits helicity-dependent, non-volatile switching: upward (downward) out-of-plane magnetization can be fully suppressed by σ− (σ+) pulses and restored by reversing the helicity, as confirmed by reflective magnetic circular dichroism (RMCD) measurements and theoretical calculations. Exploiting these features, we further demonstrate the potential of FGT for optically controlled magnetic memory.

Discussion and theoretical analysis of FGT under pulsed excitation

After presenting the phenomenological observations, we now discuss how the helicity-dependent AOS behavior in FGT differs from that in conventional metallic magnetic alloys. In these alloys, AOS is primarily associated with thermal effects induced by pulsed excitation that raise the system close to its Curie temperature, thereby making the magnetization susceptible to photon helicity1,2,7,10–12. Because their absorption spectrum is relatively flat across the commonly used pulsed-laser wavelength range (visible-near-IR), this process shows weak photon-energy dependence. FGT, on the other hand, exhibits a markedly different response, with its magnetism modulated as pulse photon energy corresponds to an electronic transition. Although this transition may induce heating, our simulations indicate that under 300 µW pulsed excitation, the peak transient temperature of FGT reaches only ~52 K (Supplementary Section 5), well below its Curie temperature (~200 K). Moreover, our power-dependent hysteresis measurements (Figs. 1f and 2e, f) reveal a two-stage response: an initial decrease in coercive field, followed by a helicity-dependent suppression of remanent magnetization as excitation power increases. This behavior differs from temperature-dependent RMCD results for FGT reported in the literature24,30 and observed in our measurements (Supplementary Section 6), where both coercivity and remanent magnetization decline as temperature rises, pointing to a nonthermal origin under pulse excitation. Another key distinction is that FGT exhibits helicity-driven transitions between fully saturated and suppressed out-of-plane magnetization, the latter likely corresponding to in-plane or type-A antiferromagnetic order. This contrasts with the discrete M↑/M↓ switching observed in typical metallic alloys2–4, implying that photon helicity plays a fundamentally different role in FGT.

To clarify the distinct AOS behavior in FGT, we perform DFT calculations (see “Methods”) to understand the impact of pulsed excitation and how photon helicity alters the magnetic ordering of FGT. Our calculations reveal that pulsed excitation induces a high density of excited holes near the Fermi level (i.e., optical doping effect; see Supplementary Section 7 for further evidence)26–28. This process depopulates electron pockets near the Fermi level at the K and H points, primarily from orbitals with magnetic quantum number m = ± 2 (Supplementary Section 8), and such depopulation reduces the MAE of FGT31. Additionally, the extent of depopulation, and hence MAE reduction, depends on the polarization of the pulses (Fig. 4a). This dependence arises because in the remanent M↑ state, σ−, σ+, and π polarized pulses selectively excite minority spin-down, majority spin-up, and holes of both spins, respectively, producing distinct modifications to the electronic structure that can even lead to distinct magnetic ground states (Fig. 4a). Specifically, as σ⁻-induced doping exceeds 0.7 hole per formula unit (f.u.), the ground state would transit from out-of-plane ferromagnetic order (Fig. 4a, b) into in-plane ferromagnetic order (Fig. 4a, c), a consequence of the complete depopulation of the electron pockets. In contrast, the same level of hole doping, but induced by σ+- or π-polarized excitation, only partially depletes the electron pockets, thereby preserving the out-of-plane ground state despite a substantial MAE reduction (Fig. 4a, d, also see Supplementary Section 8 for details on the changes in electronic structure and Section 9 for ground-state calculations). Thus, with FGT prepared in the remanent M↑ state prior to pulse excitation, it can be driven into an in-plane ferromagnetic configuration under σ− excitation and then reversibly returned to the out-of-plane configuration with σ+ excitation (Fig. 4b–d), establishing the theoretical basis for helicity-dependent AOS in FGT.Fig. 4DFT calculations of pulsed-excitation-induced changes in MAE and magnetic ground state of FGT.a Calculated changes in the MAE of FGT, defined as the energy difference between in-plane and out-of-plane magnetization, with increasing hole doping induced by σ+ (red), π (green), and σ− (blue) polarized pulsed excitation, respectively. Having established that pulse helicity governs the magnetic ground state during transient excitation, we next discuss why the pulse-induced in-plane state persists even after pulse termination, rather than recovering its out-of-plane ground state. We attribute this nonvolatile behavior to two possible intertwined mechanisms. The first is associated with the contrast between illuminated and non-illuminated regions, which induces distinct magnetic configurations and gives rise to domain walls at their boundaries. These domain walls persist after pulse excitation due to domain-wall pinning, as observed in conventional ferromagnetic thin films32–35. The second involves phonon modes activated by pulsed excitation, which can persist beyond the pulse and modify the magnetic anisotropy energy36. Our DFT calculations analyze a broad set of pulse-activated phonon modes across the Brillouin zone and find that those at the K and M points produce significant negative MAE, rendering the in-plane configuration metastable. This allows FGT initially in the M↑ state to be driven into the in-plane state during σ− pulsed excitation and to remain in the in-plane state after the pulse (see Supplementary Section 10 for further details).

The second memory scheme leverages the helicity-dependent AOS of FGT remanent states. This is demonstrated using a thin FGT flake with its remanent magnetization initially set to the fully M↓ state, as shown in the RMCD image in Fig. 6d. The M↓ state is prepared by sweeping the magnetic field from −0.3 to 0 T and holding it there throughout the experiment. A σ+-polarized pulsed laser with powers of 310, 280, and 260 μW is then sequentially applied to three distinct locations, locally modifying the magnetic state. The resulting RMCD map (Fig. 6e), acquired after the pulse excitation is switched off, exhibits well-defined spots with different degrees of M↓ suppression, as higher pulse powers suppress the out-of-plane magnetization in deeper layers. Notably, these modified regions can be restored to the initial M↓ state by scanning them with a σ−-polarized pulsed laser (310 μW), demonstrating reversible switching, as confirmed by the RMCD map acquired after completion of the σ−-polarized pulse scan (Fig. 6f). Such control enables spatially patterned spin alignment for information encoding, which, combined with magnetic-field-free switching for information resetting, offers a route to compact spintronic systems. In conclusion, we experimentally demonstrate that pulsed excitation of electronic transitions in FGT enables control over coercivity and, through helicity dependence, reversible suppression and restoration of the out-of-plane magnetization, with theoretical calculations suggesting that the suppressed state corresponds to an in-plane-favored magnetic configuration. These findings provide new insights into light–magnetism interactions in layered systems and lay the groundwork for exploring similar phenomena in other Fe-based layered magnets exhibiting near-room-temperature ferromagnetism, such as Fe₅GeTe₂, Fe₃GaTe₂, and as well as materials-engineered Fe₃GeTe₂20,40–45. Additionally, the observed switching is correlated with a photoexcitation-induced modification of the electronic structure. This process occurs on a sub-picosecond timescale, suggesting that ultrafast optical control can be achieved using femtosecond excitation. However, reducing the pulse duration from the picosecond to femtosecond regime leads to a higher transient electron temperature, potentially introducing additional thermal demagnetization effects that may influence the switching and merit further investigation. In this context, ultrafast time-resolved techniques combined with advanced nanoscale probes could further resolve the evolution of switching pathways and spin textures, as well as the role of disorder-related effects, in greater detail46–48. From a technological perspective, our work demonstrates proof-of-concept FGT-based memory devices with optical control and anticipate that this approach can drive progress in areas beyond conventional magnetic thin-film systems. For example, recent studies have integrated FGT with other 2D materials to form vdW heterostructures for spin injector, valleytronics, and magnetic tunneling junctions applications49–52. The ability to optically modulate the magnetism of FGT thus offers a practical route for dynamically tuning device electronic properties and functionalities. Moreover, these vdW heterostructures can be readily integrated with diverse on-chip photonics53,54, collectively opening new avenues for optically controlled reconfigurable logic and information processing.

Methods

Sample preparation

FGT flakes were prepared by mechanical exfoliation onto 285 nm SiO₂/Si substrates. All exfoliation and procedures were performed in a nitrogen-filled glove box, with oxygen and moisture levels maintained below 0.5 ppm to minimize oxidation. The thicknesses of flakes were initially identified based on optical contrast and subsequently confirmed by AFM. The selected samples were then loaded into a cryostat for magnetic characterization using the RMCD technique. We note the thinnest FGT samples exfoliated on SiO₂/Si substrates are ~6 layers thick. Although thinner flakes can be obtained on exfoliating on gold substrates, we find pulsed excitation in these samples leads to only a negligible reduction in coercivity (see Supplementary Section 12), likely due to electronic hybridization between FGT and gold, which modifies its photon interactions55.

Optoelectronic measurements

Exfoliated FGT flakes were mounted on the cold finger of a closed-cycle helium cryostat, where the temperature was maintained at 30 K for all measurements unless otherwise noted, and an electromagnet was used to apply an out-of-plane magnetic field. To simultaneously manipulate and probe the magnetic properties of FGT, two laser beams were collinearly focused onto a 1.5 μm spot on the flake using an objective lens. Magnetic manipulation was carried out using pulsed excitation generated by a supercontinuum white light laser (pulse duration ~20 ps, repetition rate 78 MHz) coupled with an acousto-optic tunable filter (AOTF) for wavelength selection. The pulsed beam output from the AOTF then passed through a polarizer and a quarter-wave plate to control its polarization state before being focused onto the FGT. The time-averaged pulse power was kept below 400 μW for all experiments to prevent photodamage to FGT magnetization. The magnetization properties of FGT were probed via RMCD using a linearly polarized HeNe laser beam (λ = 633 nm) modulated at 50.1 kHz between right- and left-circular polarization by a photoelastic modulator. This modulated light, with a power of 10 μW, was focused normally onto the FGT flake through the objective lens. The reflected beam then returned through the same lens and was subsequently collected by a balanced amplified photodetector to measure the differential reflectivity between the two circular polarization states24,49. The detector output was measured using a lock-in amplifier referenced to the PEM modulation frequency (50.1 kHz) to extract the RMCD signal. To improve the signal-to-noise ratio, a power-stabilized HeNe laser was used together with signal averaging, and hysteresis loops were acquired over repeated cycles to ensure reproducibility.

First-principles calculations

All first-principles calculations in this study were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)56–58 based on density functional theory (DFT). The exchange-correlation functional employed was the generalized gradient approximation of the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (GGA-PBE) type59. The cutoff energy was set to 400 eV. The 12 × 12 × 6 and 24 × 24 × 12 k-meshes sample the Brillouin zone for ground-state electronic structure calculations and for the MAE calculations using the force theorem60, respectively. The DFT-D3 method of Grimme with a zero-damping function was considered to correct the vdW interaction61. The convergence tolerances were set to 10−6 eV for the total energy and 0.01 eV/ Å for the force on each atom. The helicity-dependent hole doping is modeled by simultaneously reducing the number of total electron and constraining the total magnetic moment with respect to the undoped ferromagnetic bulk FGT. Specifically, to simulate σ−-induced spin-down hole doping of one hole per formula unit (f.u.), we remove one electron from the system and increase the total magnetic moment by 1 μB, thereby enforcing that the hole resides in the minority spin channel. For σ+-induced spin-up hole doping, we remove one electron from the system and instead decrease the total magnetic moment by 1 μB. For linearly polarized (π) excitation, one electron is removed without applying spin constraint; the total magnetic moment is determined self-consistently. Under these conditions, each system is calculated self-consistently, yielding the spin-selected doping-dependent electronic structure and MAE presented in the manuscript.
