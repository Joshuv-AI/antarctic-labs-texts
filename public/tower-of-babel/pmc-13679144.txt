A multifunctional bishydroxycoumarin conjugate exhibiting AIE characteristics and tandem Co2+/ATP detection with logic gate operations, on-site sensing and bioimaging applications
Authors: Rajendran Kishore Kumar, Suryaa Manoharan, Ekambaram Perumal, Balasubramanian Murugesapandian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An efficient coumarin-based fluorescent probe, HCPD, was synthesized via condensation of 7-diethylamino-4-hydroxycoumarin aldehyde with o-phenylenediamine. The structure of the probe was confirmed by FT-IR, NMR and HRMS studies. The probe, HCPD exhibited aggregation-induced emission (AIE) characteristics in a THF-water mixture, showing enhanced emission at 80% water fraction. The probe functions as a molecular switch for the sensitive detection of the environmentally and biologically significant metal ion Co2+ at the nanomolar level. HCPD emits green emission at 500 nm which selectively detects Co2+ ions in a DMF/Tris-HCl buffer system (9 : 1, v/v) through an emission “turn-off” response, attributed to paramagnetic quenching. In addition, Job's plot and HRMS analysis established a 1 : 1 binding stoichiometry between HCPD and Co2+ ions. The probe demonstrated remarkable sensitivity with a low detection limit of 11.7 nM and a binding constant of 3.6798 × 104 M−1. Notably, the emission of the HCPD–Co2+ ensemble was restored upon the addition of ATP over various competing anions, enabling reversible sensing behavior not previously reported in the reversibility of Co2+ ions. The regeneration capability of the probe facilitated practical on-site applications, including paper strips, cotton swabs and the construction of an IMPLICATION logic gate circuit. Furthermore, HCPD was also successfully applied in bio-imaging studies of MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell lines, highlighting its biological relevance. Overall, HCPD represents a highly sensitive and reversible dual-responsive fluorescent probe for Co2+ and ATP detection with significant environmental and biological applications.

Introduction

Accurate recognition and discrimination of small molecules in complex matrices is critically important for medical diagnostics and environmental monitoring.1 In this context, fluorescent probes have emerged as powerful analytical tools owing to their high sensitivity, rapid response and excellent temporal and spatial resolution. These probes enable real-time monitoring of biological and pathological processes without the need for sophisticated instrumentation or laborious procedures.2 Consequently, the design and development of efficient chemosensors, particularly for metal ions, have attracted considerable interest due to the pivotal roles of these species in biological, chemical, medical and environmental systems.3–5 Among various sensing platforms, fluorogenic receptors are especially appealing, as they allow analyte recognition through distinct optical changes that can be observed either spectroscopically or by the naked eye.6,7

Cobalt ions are widely distributed in minerals, soils, rocks and their concentration in industrial wastewater can reach as high as 100 mg L−1. In the human body, cobalt is present in trace amounts (approximately 1.1–1.5 mg), with significant accumulation in muscles, soft tissues and bones.8 Biologically, Co2+ is an essential element as it constitutes the core of vitamin B12 and is involved in several vital processes, including haematopoiesis, amino acid and lipid metabolism, DNA synthesis and red blood cell formation.9–12 However, an imbalance in cobalt levels can be detrimental, excessive exposure is associated with cardiomyopathy, thyroid dysfunction and other cardiovascular disorders, whereas deficiency may lead to anemia, growth retardation and neurological complications arising from impaired vitamin B12 activity.13–16 Given the dual nature of cobalt as both an essential and potentially toxic ion, its precise detection is of significant importance.

In addition, phosphate ions and phosphate-containing species such as PPi, AMP, ADP, and ATP are indispensable to living systems and are involved in numerous fundamental biological processes, including energy transduction, enzyme regulation, and amino acid metabolism. Among them, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) plays a central role as the primary cellular energy currency due to its multiple negatively charged phosphate groups.17,18 ATP is synthesized mainly in the mitochondria, drives essential cellular activities such as biosynthesis, muscle contraction, protein synthesis, DNA replication, neurotransmission, and immune responses.19–22 Beyond energy transfer, ATP also functions as an important intra- and extracellular signaling molecule and serves as an indicator of cellular health.23 The human body maintains a dynamic ATP pool (∼250 g), and disturbances in ATP homeostasis are closely associated with various pathological conditions, including metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurodegenerative disorders.24–27 Owing to its vital physiological significance, the reliable and selective detection of ATP remains of considerable importance, motivating the development of efficient ATP sensing strategies. Therefore, the development of a single fluorescent probe capable of reversibly detecting Co2+ ions and ATP are highly desirable. Such a dual-responsive system can offer deeper insight into complex biological environments by enabling sequential and reversible recognition of multiple analytes using a single platform, thereby enhancing analytical accuracy and practical applicability in biological and environmental sensing. Evaluating the performance of fluorescent probes in biologically relevant cancer cell systems is essential for validating their applicability in cellular environments. Therefore, in addition to spectroscopic characterization, the present study investigates the probe's intracellular response in MDA-MB-231 cells using live-cell fluorescence imaging, along with biological assays.28

Although numerous fluorescent probes have been developed for the individual detection of Co2+ ions11,12,29–33 and ATP,18,24,27 systems capable of reversibly and simultaneously recognizing both analytes using a single probe remain scarce. To address this limitation, we designed a coumarin-based derivative that integrate efficient metal coordination and anion recognition within a single molecular framework, enabling reversible and sequential sensing behavior. Coumarin-based derivatives occupy an important position in both natural product chemistry and synthetic organic chemistry and have been widely explored as fluorescent platforms for sensing applications.34 The coumarin scaffold is well known for its favorable photophysical characteristics, including good aqueous solubility, high fluorescence efficiency, excellent cell permeability, and structural tunability.35,36 These features enable coumarin-based probes to selectively recognize metal ions in aqueous and biologically relevant environments.37 The photophysical performance of coumarin fluorophores can be further improved by incorporating strong electron-donating substituents, such as N,N-diethylamino groups, which enhance intramolecular charge transfer and increase emission output. In addition, the diethylamino unit exhibits reversible protonation–deprotonation behavior, allowing effective modulation of the emission response under different chemical stimuli. This stimulus-responsive nature makes coumarin derivatives particularly suitable for constructing switchable and reversible fluorescence systems.38,39

Furthermore, the aggregation of molecules with donor–acceptor conjugates often lead to pronounced changes in emission behavior, providing a convenient pathway for exploiting aggregation-induced emission (AIE) phenomena in the design of advanced fluorescent chemosensors.40 In this context, understanding the emission behavior of fluorophores in the aggregated or solid state becomes particularly important for the practical application of coumarin-based probes.40,41 The performance of many conventional fluorophores in the solid or aggregated state is often limited by aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ), wherein strong emission is observed in dilute solutions but significantly diminished upon aggregation.42 In contrast to this behavior, Tang and co-workers introduced the concept of aggregation-induced emission (AIE), describing a class of luminogens that display weak or negligible fluorescence in solution while exhibiting markedly enhanced emission in the aggregated or solid state. This phenomenon is generally attributed to the restriction of intramolecular motions, which suppresses nonradiative decay pathways and promotes radiative emission upon aggregation.43,44 Owing to these unique photophysical characteristics, AIE-active materials have found widespread applications in diverse fields such as optoelectronic devices, organic light-emitting diodes, organic lasers, photovoltaic systems, information storage, fluorescent sensing, and bioimaging.45–49

In light of the above discussion and our continued interest in coumarin-based functional materials, we developed an efficient synthetic strategy that enables the transformation of simple starting materials into structurally well-defined molecules with distinct physicochemical properties (Chart S1).28,50–55 Using this approach, a coumarin-functionalized o-phenylenediamine-based fluorescent probe, HCPD 3,3′-((1E,1′E)-(1,2-phenylenebis(azaneylylidene))bis(methaneylylidene))bis(7-(diethylamino)-4-hydroxy-2H-chromen-2-one), was designed and synthesized. The probe exhibits aggregation-induced emission characteristics and enables the reversible and simultaneous detection of Co2+ ions and ATP through a “turn-off/turn-on” emission response in a DMF/Tris-HCl buffer system (9 : 1, v/v, pH 7.4), with a nanomolar detection limit. Owing to its reversible off–on emission behavior, HCPD was successfully applied in molecular logic gate operations, as well as in practical sensing formats such as test paper strips and cotton swabs, demonstrating its potential for real-world analytical applications. The probe was further explored for biological applications, including cytotoxicity assessment, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) analysis, live/dead cell viability studies and bioimaging in MDA-MB-231 cell lines. Experimental section

General procedures

Reagents including triethylamine, phosphorus oxychloride, o-phenylenediamine and various metal chloride, metal nitrate and anion salts were procured from Loba Chemie. 2,4,6-Trichlorophenol was supplied by Alfa Aesar, while malonic acid was obtained from Avra Synthesis. Analytical-grade solvents were purchased from Thomas Baker and used directly without any additional purification. ATP was sourced from Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance Neo NMR spectrometer operating at 400 MHz and 100 MHz, respectively, using CDCl3 as the solvent and tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal reference. Chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (ppm). High-resolution mass spectrometric (HRMS) analyses were carried out using a WATERS XEVO G2-XS QToF instrument in electrospray ionization (ESI) mode. Infrared spectra were obtained on a Bruker Tensor-27 spectrometer using KBr pellet techniques. Melting points were measured using a Guna melting point apparatus. UV-Visible absorption spectra were recorded on a JASCO V-630 spectrophotometer over the wavelength range of 200–800 nm, while emission spectra were collected using a JASCO FP-8300 spectrofluorometer in the 400–800 nm range with a slit width of 2.5 nm. All spectroscopic measurements were performed using a 3 mL quartz cuvette with a 1 cm path length. Fluorescence lifetime measurements were performed using an Edinburgh Instruments FLS-980 fluorescence spectrometer equipped with a 450 W ozone-free xenon arc lamp as the excitation source. The spectral measurements were carried out in the wavelength range of 200–870 nm and the obtained data were analysed using the F980 software package.

Preparation of HCPD

The precursor, 7-diethylamino-4-hydroxycoumarin aldehyde, was synthesized according to a previously reported method.56 The Schiff base ligand, HCPD was obtained via condensation reaction between o-phenylenediamine (1.8 mmol, 0.200 g) and 7-diethylamino-4-hydroxycoumarin (3.8 mmol, 1.00 g). Both the reactants were dissolved in ethanol (20 mL) and the reaction mixture was refluxed at 90 °C for 5 h. Upon completion, a yellow solid precipitate was collected and repeatedly washed with hot ethanol to eliminate unreacted starting materials. The ligand was isolated in 90% yield. Melting point: 184–186 °C. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) (Fig. S2): δ 13.94 (d, J = 8 Hz, 2H, NH, E isomer), 11.86 (d, J = 16 Hz, 1H, NH, Z isomer) 8.86 (d, J = 12 Hz, 1H, CH, Z isomer), 8.70 (d, J = 8 Hz, 2H, CH, E isomer), 7.90 (d, J = 8 Hz, 1H, Ar–H, Z isomer), 7.84 (d, J = 12 Hz, 2H, Ar–H, E isomer), 7.49 (t, J = 12 Hz, 1H, Ar–H, Z isomer), 7.43 (t, J = 12 Hz, 2H, Ar–H, E isomer), 7.36 (q, 2H, Ar–H), 6.54 (t, J = 12 Hz, 1H, Ar–H, Z isomer), 6.50 (t, J = 12 Hz, 2H, Ar–H, E isomer), 6.33 (d, J = 4 Hz, 2H, Ar–H), 3.41 (q, 8H, CH2), 1.20 (t, J = 12 Hz, 12H, CH3) ppm. 13C NMR (100 MHz, CDCl3) (Fig. S3): δ 181.02, 177.47, 165.95, 164.29, 157.36, 157.12, 156.00, 155.69, 153.76, 153.14, 131.54, 131.33, 128.30, 128.20, 128.11, 128.02, 128.85, 120.67, 108.98, 108.71, 108.57, 108.41, 100.14, 100.05, 97.29, 97.22, 44.98, 12.60 ppm. Methodology

Cell culture and maintenance

The human breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-231 (PN: 24) was procured from the National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, India. MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cells were maintained under standard incubation conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Experimental studies were carried out using MDA-MB-231 cells that had attained approximately 80% confluency. To ensure uniformity and reliability of the results, all assays were performed using cells within 30 passages. The cytotoxic effect of HCPD, HCPD–Co2+ and HCPD–Co2+–ATP were determined using MTT assay and given elaborately in the SI.

Live/dead assay

The effects of HCPD, HCPD–Co2+ and HCPD–Co2+–ATP were assessed using a Live/Dead assay kit. MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at an appropriate density and incubated overnight to allow cell attachment. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to HCPD (8 µM) either alone or together with Co2+ (15 µM). Where required, ATP (10 µM) was added following Co2+ treatment under standard cell culture conditions. After the incubation period, the cells were washed carefully with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stained with a solution containing 2 µM Calcein-AM and 4 µM Ethidium homodimer-1 (EthD-1). The staining was carried out for 30 min at room temperature in the absence of light. Following staining, excess dye was removed, and the cells were directly examined using an Olympus Fluoview FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM). Viable cells exhibited green fluorescence as a result of intracellular esterase activity converting Calcein-AM into fluorescent calcein, whereas non-viable cells with damaged membranes displayed red fluorescence due to EthD-1 uptake.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) assay

Changes in MMP were investigated using the lipophilic cationic fluorescent probe 3,3′-dihexyloxacarbocyanine iodide (DiOC6). MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and exposed to HCPD (8 µM) either alone or in the presence of Co2+ (15 µM). For selected experiments, ATP (10 µM) was administered following Co2+ treatment. Upon completion of the treatment period, the cells were rinsed with PBS and incubated with DiOC6 (100 nM) at 37 °C for 30 min under dark conditions. After staining, excess dye was removed by washing with PBS, and the cells were visualized using an Olympus Fluoview FV3000 LSCM. The fluorescence intensity of DiOC6 was used to evaluate MMP, where a decrease in fluorescence signal reflected the loss of mitochondrial polarization.

Biosensing and imaging

MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in six-well plates containing MEM and divided into four experimental groups. The first group was maintained as the control. Cells in the remaining groups were incubated with HCPD (8 µM) for 30 min. Subsequently, the third group received an additional treatment with Co2+ (15 µM) for 30 min. For the fourth group, cells treated with Co2+ (15 µM) were further exposed to ATP (10 µM). After each treatment, the cells were washed thoroughly with PBS to remove any unbound reagents. Biosensing and imaging was then performed using an Olympus Fluoview FV3000 LSCM to monitor intracellular changes in fluorescence intensity.

Statistical analysis

All data were obtained from three independent experiments and represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Differences were considered statistically significant when the p-value was less than 0.05 (p < 0.05).

Result and discussion

Design strategy and synthesis

The probe was rationally designed to incorporate a Lewis basic site at the 4th position along with an electron-rich diethylamino substituent at the 7th position, enabling efficient coordination with incoming metal ions. Solvatochromic studies

Owing to the presence of a strong electron-donating diethylamino unit and an electron-withdrawing imine moiety, HCPD is expected to exhibit optical behavior governed by an intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) process. To evaluate this behavior, UV–Vis absorption and emission studies were carried out in solvents of varying polarity, namely chloroform, acetonitrile, THF, DMF and DMSO. The UV–Vis absorption spectra of HCPD recorded in these solvents show only minor variations in band position, indicating that the ground-state electronic structure is weakly influenced by solvent polarity. A slight blue shift of approximately 20 nm was observed when moving from weakly polar chloroform to highly polar DMSO (Fig. S6A). In contrast, the emission spectra display a pronounced solvent-dependent response. Upon excitation at the respective absorption maxima, HCPD exhibits a substantial bathochromic shift in emission with increasing solvent polarity. The emission maximum gradually shifts from 473 nm in chloroform to 577 nm in DMSO, corresponding to an overall red shift of nearly 100 nm (Fig. S6B). Intermediate emission maxima were observed in acetonitrile (510 nm), THF (534 nm), and DMF (561 nm). In addition to the spectroscopic confirmation, the corresponding photographic images of HCPD solutions in the above-mentioned solvents under UV illumination are presented in Fig. S7, clearly demonstrating the visually observable solvatochromic emission changes. The emission spectra of HCPD exhibit a strong dependence on solvent polarity, whereas the absorption spectra remain largely unaffected, indicating a highly polar excited state. Upon excitation, intramolecular charge transfer occurs from the diethylamino donor to the imine acceptor unit, forming a charge-separated excited state. Increased stabilization of this state in polar solvents results in a pronounced red shift in emission, confirming ICT – driven solvatochromism in HCPD. The quantum yield of HCPD in various solvents was determined using quinine sulphate as the reference (Table S3). HCPD exhibited a higher quantum yield in non-polar solvents such as chloroform and in moderately polar solvents like THF, whereas a comparatively lower quantum yield was observed in polar aprotic solvents such as acetonitrile. Furthermore, a significantly reduced quantum yield was recorded in highly polar solvents like DMF and DMSO when compared to non-polar and moderately polar media. These observations clearly indicate the presence of positive solvatochromic behavior.

AIE properties of HCPD

Organic solvents effectively dissolve organic molecules, whereas water generally exhibits poor solubilizing ability and often promotes aggregation, which can significantly influence emission behavior in AIE-active systems.41 In this study, the aggregation-induced emission characteristics of HCPD were quantitatively investigated using a binary THF-water solvent system by recording emission spectra at varying water fractions. In pure THF, HCPD displayed weak fluorescence with an emission maximum at 534 nm. This diminished emission is attributed to the well-dispersed molecular state of HCPD, wherein free rotation about the C–N and C–C bonds and isomerization around the CN bonds are permitted. These intramolecular motions facilitate non-radiative decay pathways, thereby suppressing radiative emission. Upon gradual increase of the water fraction, the emission intensity remained nearly unchanged, showing neither significant enhancement up to approximately 70% water content compared to pure THF. Interestingly, the emission intensity of HCPD increased upon increasing the water fraction in the THF/water mixture, compared with that observed in pure THF. The maximum emission intensity was observed at 80% water fraction, corresponding to an approximately five-fold enhancement relative to HCPD in pure THF, accompanied by a blue shift of about 8 nm (Fig. 1A). This emission enhancement and blue shift are attributed to the formation of molecular aggregates, which restrict intramolecular motions and activate the AIE mechanism. Notably, this distinct change in emission behavior was readily visible to the naked eye under UV illumination (λmax = 365 nm) as illustrated in the Fig. 1C. Further increasing the water fraction from 90% to 99% resulted in a gradual decrease in emission intensity, accompanied by an overall bathochromic shift of approximately 50 nm. In the 70–80% THF – water mixtures, HCPD molecules progressively undergo self-assembly in an ordered manner, leading to the formation of emissive crystalline aggregates. With further increasing water content, the enhanced polarity of the solvent environment induces a pronounced bathochromic shift of approximately 50 nm in the emission band in the 90–99% water range. At higher water fractions, however, the aggregates undergo precipitation, resulting in a gradual decrease in emission intensity.57,58 Collectively, these observations clearly establish that HCPD exhibits prominent AIE activity at 80% water content in the THF-water solvent system. A plot of water fraction versus emission intensity was constructed from the AIE emission spectra as shown in the Fig. 1B.

(A) Emission spectra of HCPD (1 × 10−4 M) in THF upon varying water fraction (0–99%). (B) Plots of emission intensity vs. water fraction. (C) Photographic vials images of HCPD in THF–water mixtures on varying water fractions (0–99%) (λmax = 365 nm).

To better understand the morphology of aggregates with the increasing water fractions, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses were carried out. SEM images of HCPD before aggregation revealed well-defined rod-like and needle-shaped microstructures with relatively discrete distribution (Fig. S8A). Upon aggregation, the rod-like morphology was largely retained; however, a higher degree of packing, interconnection and clustering of the micro-rods was observed. The increased compactness of the aggregated assemblies suggests enhanced intermolecular interactions and restriction of molecular motions in the aggregated state (Fig. S8B). Such restriction effectively suppresses non-radiative relaxation pathways, thereby leading to the observed aggregation-induced emission enhancement. Although no significant morphological transformation was observed after aggregation, the denser packing and increased assembly of the rod-like structures indicate stronger intermolecular interactions in the aggregated state. These aggregated domains can effectively restrict intramolecular rotations and vibrations (RIR mechanism), resulting in enhanced fluorescence intensity at 80% water fraction.

The aggregate formation was further confirmed by DLS measurements. While SEM analysis provided visual evidence of the morphological changes associated with aggregation, DLS measurements offered additional confirmation of aggregate formation in the THF–water mixture. In pure THF, HCPD exhibited a negligible particle size of 4.1 nm, indicating the presence of well-dispersed molecular species. However, upon increasing the water fraction to 80%, the particle size increased significantly to 491.7 nm, confirming the formation of larger aggregated species (Fig. S9A and B). Thus, although SEM revealed only subtle changes in the morphology of the aggregates, the substantial increase in particle size observed from DLS measurements clearly confirms the formation of aggregates at an 80% water fraction, which is consistent with the observed aggregation-induced emission enhancement. To further support the AIE behaviour of HCPD, the absolute fluorescence quantum yields were determined in pure THF (0% water fraction) and in the aggregated state at 80% water fraction. The quantum yield increased from Φ = 6.19% in pure THF to Φ = 17.28% at 80% water fraction, confirming the enhanced radiative emission upon aggregation. This increase in quantum yield is consistent with the observed fluorescence enhancement and can be attributed to the restriction of intramolecular motions in the aggregated state, which suppresses non-radiative decay pathways.

HCPD as a viscochromic dye

Viscochromism refers to the viscosity-dependent modulation of emission arising primarily from the restriction of intramolecular motions in a highly viscous environment.59 To investigate the viscochromic behavior of HCPD, emission measurements were performed in THF–glycerol mixtures containing different glycerol fractions (0–90%) at a fixed HCPD concentration of 2 × 10−5 M. The corresponding emission photographs recorded under UV illumination (365 nm) and the emission spectra clearly demonstrate the viscosity-dependent changes in the optical response of HCPD (Fig. S10A and B). In pure THF (0% glycerol), HCPD exhibited weak emission and appeared as a faint green emission under UV illumination, indicating efficient non-radiative relaxation of the excited state. With increasing glycerol content, a pronounced enhancement in emission intensity was observed, accompanied by a noticeable change in emission color. Interestingly, upon increasing the glycerol fraction to 60%, the emission maximum underwent a substantial blue shift from 530 to 455 nm, accompanied by a change in the observed emission color. Further increasing the glycerol fraction to 70% resulted in a slight decrease in emission intensity, followed by a gradual red shift of the emission band at higher glycerol contents. At 80% and 90% glycerol, dual emission bands centered approximately at 490 and 535 nm were observed. At 90% glycerol, the intensity of the 535 nm band was further reduced, resulting in a modified emission profile compared with that observed at lower glycerol fractions. The observed viscosity-dependent fluorescence response can be attributed primarily to the restriction of intramolecular motions of HCPD in the increasingly viscous medium. In low-viscosity THF, the molecular framework has greater conformational freedom, particularly involving rotational and vibrational motions around the imine (CN) linkage, which facilitates non-radiative relaxation of the excited state and consequently results in weak fluorescence. As the viscosity of the medium increases with the addition of glycerol, these intramolecular motions become progressively restricted, suppressing non-radiative decay pathways and favoring radiative relaxation, thereby enhancing the emission intensity.60 The distinct changes in emission wavelength and the corresponding color variation observed in the vial photographs further demonstrate the viscosity-responsive optical behavior of HCPD. Overall, the combined changes in fluorescence intensity, emission wavelength, and visually observed emission color demonstrate the pronounced viscosity-responsive and viscochromic behavior of HCPD.

Sensing studies

The HCPD contains a fluorophoric coumarin unit along with multiple coordination sites, which motivated us to explore its metal-ion sensing capability using UV-Vis absorption and emission studies in DMF/Tris-HCl buffer (9 : 1, pH = 7.4, v/v) at room temperature. The UV-Vis absorption spectrum of HCPD (10 µM) displays two prominent absorption bands at approximately 300 nm and 385 nm. The higher-energy band at 300 nm is attributed to the π–π* transition of the conjugated aromatic system, while the lower-energy band at 385 nm arises from the n–π* transition associated with the azomethine (CN) moiety of the probe. The selectivity and the sensitivity of HCPD was examined over a range of biologically and environmentally relevant cations such as Ag+, Al3+, Cd2+, Co2+, Cr3+, Cu2+, Fe2+, Fe3+, Hg2+, Mg2+, Mn2+, Na+, Ni2+, Pb2+ and Zn2+ (10 equiv.) were added with HCPD (10 µM) under identical conditions. Upon addition of the metal ions to the probe resulted in the 10 nm bathochromic shift of the metal ions including Fe2+, Fe3+, Cu2+ and 5 nm red shift of Zn2+ ions and also 15 nm bathochromic shift of Ni2+ ion (Fig. S11). From the above observations, no significant changes were detected in the absorption spectra upon the addition of various metal ions, indicating that HCPD does not exhibit selective colorimetric sensing behavior towards any of the tested metal ions. Consistent with the spectral results, the corresponding daylight photographs (Fig. S12A) also shows no noticeable color variation of the probe solution in the presence of different metal ions, further confirming the absence of visible colorimetric selectivity for HCPD under ambient light conditions.

In the emission measurements, HCPD (10 µM) exhibits broad band at 500 nm upon excitation at λex = 385 nm in DMF/Tris-HCl buffer system (9 : 1, v/v, pH 7.4). Upon addition of 10 equiv. of different metal ions to the HCPD (10 µM), only Co2+ ion caused quenching of emission whereas the other metal ions didn't produce any negligible effect on the emission of HCPD (Fig. 2A). These observations demonstrate a highly selective emission response of HCPD towards Co2+ ions under the investigated experimental conditions, which is attributed to the favorable formation of the HCPD–Co2+ complex and the efficient quenching of HCPD emission by the coordinated paramagnetic Co2+ center. In agreement with the emission studies, a distinct fluorescence color change from bluish-green to colorless was readily visible to the naked eye under UV illumination (λmax = 365 nm), as illustrated in Fig. S12B. To gain deeper insight into the sensing behavior of HCPD towards Co2+ ions, emission titration experiments were performed in DMF/Tris-HCl buffer (9 : 1, pH = 7.4, v/v). Upon the gradual addition of increasing concentrations of Co2+ to the solution of HCPD (10 µM), a continuous decrease in the emission intensity at λem = 500 nm was observed, ultimately leading to complete quenching upon the addition of 5 equiv. of Co2+ ions which results in the complex formation of HCPD towards Co2+ ions (Fig. 2B). The quenching emission of HCPD might be due to the paramagnetic nature of Co2+ ion which leads to the Chelation enhanced quenching effect (CHEQ). The pronounced emission quenching of HCPD upon coordination with Co2+ can be associated with the paramagnetic nature of the Co2+ center, which provides an efficient pathway for excited-state quenching and promotes non-radiative deactivation of the excited HCPD–Co2+ complex. This behavior is consistent with the observed static quenching mechanism and the formation of the ground-state HCPD–Co2+ complex. A linear relationship between the emission intensity at λem = 500 nm and Co2+ concentration was obtained within the investigated range, with a high correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.9938), indicating a reliable and reproducible sensing response of HCPD towards Co2+ ions. The limit of detection was calculated using the equation LOD = 3σ/M, yielding a very low detection limit of 11.7 nM as illustrated in the Fig. S13A. The binding constant (Ka) of HCPD towards Co2+ ions was determined using the Benesi–Hildebrand method and the calculated value was found to be 3.6798 × 104 M−1 (R2 = 0.9919) (Fig. S13B). The observed selectivity is considered to originate from the favorable coordination interaction between the donor sites of HCPD and Co2+, together with the efficient emission-quenching characteristics of the resulting HCPD–Co2+ complex. HCPD contains imine nitrogen and oxygen donor atoms capable of coordinating Co2+ ions.

The stoichiometry of the HCPD–Co2+ complex was established using Job's plot analysis in DMF/Tris-HCl buffer (9 : 1, pH = 7.4, v/v), which clearly indicated the 1 : 1 complex formation (Fig. S14). This binding stoichiometry was further corroborated by ESI-MS. The isolated complex is formulated as a charge-neutral Co(ii) complex, [Co(ii)L], in which the bis-coumarin ligand exists in its dianionic form (L2−). In the positive-mode ESI-MS spectrum (Fig. S15), a major ion is observed at m/z 651.1654, which is in excellent agreement with the calculated monoisotopic mass of C34H32CoN4O6 (651.1661). Since the formally neutral [Co(ii)L] complex cannot directly cause a singly charged positive ion, the detected peak is assigned to [Co(iii)L]+, formed via one-electron oxidation of the Co(ii) complex during the electrospray ionization process.61 This assignment represents the gas-phase ion detected by ESI-MS and does not imply a Co(iii) oxidation state for the isolated solid complex. This observation suggests coordination of Co2+ with HCPD accompanied by ligand deprotonation, resulting in an overall neutral metal-ligand assembly. The observed mass spectral data confirmed the formation of mononuclear HCPD–Co2+ complex. Based on these experimental results, a plausible coordination structure for the HCPD–Co2+ complex has been proposed and is illustrated in the Fig. 3.

The proposed structure of the HCPD–Co2+ complex.

Emission quenching mechanism of HCPD towards Co2+ ions

Fluorescence quenching can occur through several pathways, including ground-state complex formation, inner filter effect (IFE), fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET), and photoinduced electron transfer.62 Upon coordination with Co2+ ions, the emission quenching of HCPD was analyzed quantitatively using the Stern–Volmer equation. The Stern–Volmer quenching constant (Ksv), obtained from the slope of the linear fit of F0/F versus Co2+ concentration, was determined to be 1.8930 × 105 dm3 mol−1 with a high correlation coefficient (R2 = 0.9963), indicating an efficient quenching interaction between HCPD and Co2+ ions (Fig. S16). To distinguish between static and dynamic quenching, TRPL studies were carried out. The calculated bimolecular quenching constant for Co2+ (1.4427 × 1014 dm3 mol−1 s−1) is significantly higher than the diffusion-controlled limit (2 × 1010 dm3 mol−1 s−1). This clearly indicates that the quenching proceeds via a static mechanism involving ground-state complex formation between HCPD and Co2+, consistent with the TRPL results.

Competitive studies

To evaluate the selectivity of HCPD towards Co2+ ions, competitive emission experiments were carried out in the presence of various potentially interfering metal ions under identical experimental conditions in DMF/Tris-HCl buffer (9 : 1, pH = 7.4, v/v). The emission spectra were recorded by adding Co2+ ions (50 µM) to the solution of HCPD (10 µM), both in the absence and in the presence of 10 equiv. of other metal ions. As depicted in the Fig. 2C, the addition of Co2+ resulted in complete quenching of the emission of HCPD at λem = 500 nm, even in the presence of competing metal ions. Notably, none of the other metal ions caused any significant interference with the Co2+ induced quenching response. These results clearly demonstrate the high selectivity of HCPD towards Co2+ ions, which can be attributed to the preferential complex formation between HCPD and Co2+ ions.

Reversible sensing of the HCPD

The development of reversible fluorescent probes based on metal-ion displacement is crucial for practical, real-world applications.63 Such systems enable dual functionality, allowing the selective detection of Co2+ ions while simultaneously facilitating probe regeneration through metal displacement. The removal of the bound metal ion restores the probe to its free form, thereby recovering its original fluorescence response. This reversible sensing behavior significantly enhances probe reusability and operational efficiency, making it highly suitable for routine and day-to-day analytical applications. To examine the reversibility of the HCPD–Co2+ complex, emission studies were carried out in DMF/Tris-HCl buffer (9 : 1, pH = 7.4, v/v) in the presence of various competing biologically and environmentally important anions, including CN−, H2PO42−, ATP, Br−, CH3COO−, S2−, P2O74− (PPi), NO3−, SCN−, ADP, F−, HCO3−, and HSO4−. Upon the addition of 10 equiv. of these anions to the in situ generated HCPD–Co2+ ensemble, a pronounced change in the emission profile was observed exclusively in the presence of ATP (Fig. 4A). Notably, ATP induced a distinct fluorescence recovery, accompanied by a visible color change of the ensemble from non-emissive to green emission under UV illumination (λmax = 365 nm), as depicted in the corresponding Fig. S17. The stepwise addition of ATP (0–10 equiv.) to the HCPD–Co2+ ensemble resulted in a gradual enhancement of fluorescence intensity at 500 nm with the slight 20 nm red shift, reaching saturation upon the addition of 10 equiv. of ATP (Fig. 4B). The emission recovery observed upon addition of ATP is attributed primarily to the competitive coordination of Co2+ by the phosphate groups of ATP, which promotes the dissociation of the HCPD–Co2+ complex and restores the emission of free HCPD. ATP contains multiple negatively charged phosphate groups that provide several coordination sites for Co2+, thereby favoring the formation of Co2+–ATP species. The resulting regeneration of uncomplexed HCPD restores its original emissive state and consequently recovers the emission. In addition to the phosphate groups, the adenine moiety of ATP may involve in the weak interactions. However, the phosphate groups are considered to be the principal contributors to Co2+ sequestration because of their high negative charge density and strong metal-binding capability.17,64 Therefore, the ATP-induced fluorescence recovery is proposed to occur predominantly through phosphate-mediated competitive coordination of Co2+, with possible additional contributions from interactions involving the adenine moiety.

(A) Emission spectra of the HCPD–Co2+ ensemble in the presence of various anions (10 equiv.) in DMF/Tris-HCl buffer (9 : 1, pH = 7.4, v/v), (B) emission titration of the HCPD–Co2+ ensemble with ATP in DMF/Tris-HCl buffer (9 : 1, pH = 7.4, v/v).

A plot of emission intensity as a function of ATP concentration exhibited good linearity over the examined range, enabling quantitative analysis. The limit of detection (LOD) was determined to be 60.2 nM with a correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.9920, as illustrated in the corresponding Fig. S18A, indicating high sensitivity of the HCPD–Co2+ ensemble towards ATP. Furthermore, the binding constant of the HCPD–Co2+ ensemble was evaluated using the Benesi–Hildebrand method and calculated to be 5.1809 × 104 M−1 (R2 = 0.9941) (Fig. S18B). Overall, these results demonstrate that the HCPD–Co2+ ensemble functions as a highly sensitive and reversible fluorescent platform for ATP detection.

The fluorescence selectivity of the HCPD–Co2+ ensemble towards ATP was systematically examined in the presence of a range of potentially interfering anions. For this study, the ensemble was exposed to 10 equiv. of ATP in combination with 15 equiv. of various competing anions in a DMF/Tris-HCl buffer system (9 : 1, v/v, pH 7.4), as depicted in Fig. S19 Remarkably, the presence of other anions did not cause any noticeable alteration in the fluorescence response, indicating that they do not interfere with ATP recognition. These findings clearly establish the high selectivity of the HCPD–Co2+ ensemble for ATP, even under competitive conditions, underscoring its potential as a reliable and interference-resistant fluorescent platform for reversible ATP detection in biologically relevant media.

The reversibility of the HCPD sensing system was further evaluated through stepwise alternated additions of Co2+ ions and ATP to a solution of the probe. As illustrated in Fig. S20A, the addition of 5 equiv. of Co2+ resulted in efficient quenching of the green emission of HCPD, whereas subsequent introduction of 10 equiv. of ATP led to a pronounced recovery of the emission signal, consistent with the release of the free probe. Repeated alternating additions of Co2+ and ATP produced successive “off-on” modulation of the emission intensity, confirming the reversible nature of the sensing process. Notably, this fluorescence switching cycle was reproducibly sustained over six consecutive iterations with only minimal signal fluctuation (Fig. S20B), indicating good stability and robustness of the system. Overall, these observations establish HCPD as an effective reversible chemosensor for selective detection of Co2+ ions and ATP in a DMF/Tris-HCl buffer medium (9 : 1, v/v, pH 7.4).

To elucidate the origin of the ATP-induced emission recovery, control emission experiments were performed for HCPD, HCPD–Co2+, Co2+, ATP and Co2+–ATP in DMF/Tris-HCl buffer medium (9 : 1, v/v, pH 7.4). The individual Co2+, ATP and Co2+–ATP systems showed no appreciable changes in their emission profiles. In contrast, the preformed HCPD–Co2+ complex exhibited a pronounced recovery of emission upon subsequent addition of ATP (Fig. S21). These results demonstrate that the emission recovery is not associated with an independent emissive response arising from Co2+–ATP interactions. Instead, the observed recovery is consistent with the ATP-induced sequestration of Co2+ from the HCPD–Co2+ complex, resulting in the regeneration of free HCPD and consequently restoring its emission.

To further investigate the behavior of HCPD under different buffer conditions, additional emission experiments were performed in DMF/PBS buffer using HCPD alone, HCPD in the presence of Co2+, and the HCPD–Co2+ system upon subsequent addition of ATP under the corresponding experimental conditions (Fig. S22). The emission profile of HCPD in DMF/PBS was comparable to that observed in Tris-HCl buffer, with no significant changes in the emission intensity or spectral profile upon addition of Co2+ and ATP. These results indicate that the sensing response of HCPD independent on the buffer environment, while its intrinsic emission remains largely unchanged in aqueous PBS.

To gain a clearer understanding of the reversible sensing mechanism of HCPD towards Co2+/ATP, time-resolved lifetime studies were performed for HCPD, HCPD–Co2+ and HCPD–Co2+–ATP (Fig. S23). The corresponding lifetime values along with their χ2 parameters are summarized in Table S4.

NMR titration experiment

To elucidate the sensing mechanism of HCPD towards Co2+ and ATP, 1H NMR titration experiments were performed at room temperature (Fig. S24). The 1H NMR spectrum of the free probe displayed two distinct NH resonances at δ 13.94 and 11.86 ppm, which are assigned to the E and Z isomeric forms of HCPD, respectively. Incremental addition of Co2+ ions resulted in a gradual attenuation of these NH signals, accompanied by noticeable broadening and shift of both aromatic and aliphatic proton resonances. At approximately 2 equiv. of Co2+, the proton signals were almost completely suppressed, consistent with paramagnetic-induced broadening and confirming coordination between HCPD and Co2+ ions. Subsequent treatment of the resulting HCPD–Co2+ complex with 5 equiv. of ATP led to the reappearance of the NH, aromatic and aliphatic proton signals at chemical shifts comparable to those of the free probe. This spectral recovery confirms the efficient displacement of Co2+ ions by ATP and the regeneration of unbound HCPD. Collectively, the 1H NMR results provide strong evidence for the reversible coordination of HCPD with Co2+ ions and ATP-induced metal displacement, thereby substantiating the proposed sensing mechanism. Practical application

The chemosensor, HCPD was rationally designed and synthesized to exhibit distinct fluorescence responses, showing efficient turn-off and turn-on emission behavior towards Co2+ and ATP, respectively. The probe demonstrates a low limit of detection and a high binding affinity, highlighting its strong sensing capability. Beyond these analytical parameters, the practical utility of a sensor is ultimately determined by its performance under real-world conditions. To assess the applicability and robustness of HCPD in practical scenarios, a series of systematic experiments were carried out to evaluate its sensing performance in relevant environments. The following studies were presented to validate the real-world applicability of HCPD and to demonstrate its effectiveness and reliability in practical analytical applications.

Logic gate function

A simulation-based molecular logic operation was constructed using the emission response of HCPD. The Co2+ ions and ATP were employed as chemical input signals, while the emission intensity at λem = 500 nm served as the output signal. In this system, emission quenching was assigned as binary value of 0, whereas fluorescence recovery was assigned a value of 1. The native emissive state of HCPD was defined as the ON state (1). Upon the addition of Co2+ ions, the emission of HCPD was efficiently quenched, corresponding to the OFF state (0). Subsequent introduction of ATP to the HCPD–Co2+ ensemble resulted in restoration of the emission intensity, which was again designated as the ON state (1). Based on these distinct emission outputs, a corresponding truth table was constructed, revealing that the system operates according to an IMPLICATION logic gate function (Fig. 6A).

(A) Truth table for the logic gate of HCPD, operation of logic gates using AND gate. (B) Both Co2+ and ATP were not added. (C) Only Co2+ was added. (D) Only ATP was added. (E) Both Co2+ and ATP were added.

As illustrated in the Fig. 6B, a green emission was observed in the absence of both Co2+ and ATP inputs. In Fig. 6C, the addition of Co2+ alone led to complete quenching of the emission and no green emission was detected. As shown in Fig. 6D, the presence of ATP alone retained the green emission of the probe. In Fig. 6E, the addition of ATP to the HCPD–Co2+ system resulted in the recovery of the green emission. This reversible and predictable fluorescence switching behavior highlights the potential of HCPD for the development of intelligent molecular logic devices and advanced chemosensing platforms capable of information processing at the molecular level.65,66

Paper strips applications

To demonstrate the practical applicability and reversibility of the HCPD based sensing system for Co2+ detection and ATP mediated fluorescence recovery, a paper-strip assay was developed. Filter paper strips were immersed in a 10−3 M solution of HCPD prepared in a DMF/Tris-HCl buffer system (9 : 1, v/v, pH 7.4) and subsequently air-dried to obtain HCPD-coated strips. As shown in the corresponding Fig. 7, the coated paper strips exhibited a bright green emission under UV irradiation (λmax = 365 nm). To evaluate Co2+ sensing on the solid support, a simple pattern was drawn on the HCPD-coated paper using a brush dipped in a Co2+ solution, followed by air drying. Under UV light, the regions exposed to Co2+ showed distinct fluorescence quenching, clearly revealing the drawn pattern on the paper strip. Subsequently, the Co2+-treated paper strip was immersed in an ATP solution and air-dried. Notably, the green emission was regenerated upon ATP treatment under UV illumination, indicating reversible fluorescence switching due to displacement of Co2+ from the HCPD–Co2+ complex. These results demonstrate that the HCPD coated paper strips enable visual, reversible, and selective detection of Co2+ ions, highlighting the potential of this system for practical and real-world sensing applications.

Visual representation of Co2+/ATP sensing by HCPD using paper strips under UV irradiation (λmax = 365 nm).

Test kit application

The feasibility of HCPD for on-site and portable sensing was further examined using cotton swabs as a solid support. The swabs were functionalized by soaking them in a 10 µM solution of HCPD prepared in a DMF/Tris-HCl buffer system (9 : 1, v/v, pH 7.4) for 1 min, followed by air drying at ambient conditions. The fluorescence response of the HCPD-coated swabs was then evaluated by exposure to various metal ions solutions (10 equiv.) and visualized under UV illumination (Fig. 8). The majority of metal ions produced no noticeable change in fluorescence and the swabs retained their characteristic green emission. In contrast, treatment with Co2+ ions resulted in pronounced emission quenching, clearly distinguishing Co2+ from the other tested metal ions. The reversibility of the sensing response on the solid surface was subsequently assessed by immersing the Co2+ treated swabs into ATP solution (10 equiv). Upon UV irradiation, a clear restoration of green emission was observed, indicating recovery of the emissive state of HCPD. These results demonstrate that HCPD maintains selective and reversible optical behavior on cotton swabs, highlighting its suitability for practical, portable, and on-site detection of Co2+/ATP ions.

Biosensing studies

The cytotoxic effect of HCPD, HCPD–Co2+, and HCPD–Co2+–ATP was assessed in MDA-MB-231 cells using the MTT assay. The cytotoxicity profiles revealed that all three systems maintained satisfactory cellular tolerance over the investigated concentration range. The corresponding IC50 values, summarized in Fig. S25, further confirmed their compatibility with biological environments and supported their suitability for intracellular imaging studies. A comparison of the IC50 values demonstrated differences in the cytotoxic behavior of the tested species. HCPD (IC50 = 7.290 ± 1.132 µM) and HCPD–Co2+–ATP (IC50 = 9.523 ± 1.461 µM) exhibited greater cytotoxicity than HCPD–Co2+ (IC50 = 14.52 ± 1.78 µM), as evidenced by their lower IC50 values. Nevertheless, the concentrations employed for cellular imaging were selected within the permissible cytocompatible range, ensuring minimal adverse effects on cell viability during fluorescence imaging experiments. These findings validate the applicability of the HCPD-based sensing platform for live-cell investigations.

Live/dead analysis

To investigate the influence of HCPD-based Co2+ sensing on cellular viability, a Live/Dead assay was performed using MDA-MB-231 cells. As shown in Fig. 9, untreated control cells exhibited intense green fluorescence with negligible red fluorescence, confirming a high proportion of viable cells. In contrast, cells incubated with HCPD displayed a noticeable increase in red fluorescence, indicating a reduction in cell viability relative to the control group. Upon treatment with the HCPD–Co2+ system, red fluorescence remained evident, reflecting the presence of compromised cells, although the extent of cell death differed from that observed for HCPD alone. Similarly, cells exposed to the HCPD–Co2+–ATP system showed detectable EthD-1 staining, suggesting that the sensing process was accompanied by measurable cellular damage under the experimental conditions.

Live/dead assay was examined to analyse the effect of HCPD, HCPD–Co2+, and HCPD–Co2+–ATP treatment in MDA-MB-231 cells using fluorescence microscopy. Live cells exhibit green fluorescence, whereas dead cells with compromised membrane integrity display red fluorescence.

Overall, the Live/Dead staining results demonstrate that exposure to HCPD and its corresponding Co2+ and ATP-associated complexes affects cell viability to varying degrees when compared with untreated cells. These observations provide valuable insight into the biological response of cells toward the HCPD-based sensing platform.

MMP analysis

To determine whether mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to the cytotoxic effects of HCPD-based treatments, MMP was assessed using DiOC6 staining. As illustrated in Fig. 10, untreated control cells exhibited intense green fluorescence, indicative of intact mitochondria with preserved membrane integrity. Likewise, cells pretreated with NAC or CsA retained comparatively high fluorescence intensity, suggesting effective maintenance of mitochondrial function. In contrast, a marked reduction in DiOC6 fluorescence was observed in cells exposed to HCPD, indicating significant mitochondrial depolarization. Similar decreases in fluorescence intensity were also detected in cells treated with the HCPD–Co2+ complex and the HCPD–Co2+–ATP system, reflecting disruption of mitochondrial membrane integrity. The loss of MMP observed in these treatment groups is consistent with mitochondrial dysfunction and may be associated with the initiation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HCPD and its corresponding Co2+ and ATP associated systems induce mitochondrial depolarization, whereas NAC-, CsA-treated and untreated control cells largely preserve the MMP. The results therefore support the involvement of mitochondria-mediated apoptosis in the cytotoxic response elicited by HCPD based treatments.

In control cells, a strong green fluorescence was observed, signifying an intact MMP, while HCPD, HCPD–Co2+ and HCPD–Co2+–ATP treated cells displayed a diminished fluorescence intensity, indicating mitochondrial depolarization. The reduction in DiOC6 fluorescence implies the initiation of mitochondrial-mediated apoptotic pathways as a result of HCPD related treatments.

HCPD-mediated Co2+/ATP sensing in MDA-MB-231 cells

The intracellular sensing capability of HCPD was investigated through confocal fluorescence imaging in MDA-MB-231 cells. As depicted in Fig. 11A, cells treated with 8 µM HCPD exhibited pronounced cyan fluorescence, demonstrating effective cellular uptake of the probe. Upon incubation with 15 µM Co2+ for 30 min, the fluorescence signal was substantially diminished, indicating successful interaction between HCPD and intracellular Co2+ ions. Subsequent exposure of the HCPD–Co2+ treated cells to 10 µM ATP for an additional 30 min resulted in a significant recovery of fluorescence intensity, similar to the cells treated with HCPD treated alone. The reversible fluorescence modulation observed during sequential Co2+ and ATP treatment confirms the ability of HCPD to function as an intracellular sensing platform. These results demonstrate that the probe readily penetrates living cells and enables fluorescence-based monitoring of Co2+ and ATP under biological conditions. The clear fluorescence switching behavior observed in MDA-MB-231 cells suggests that HCPD remains sufficiently stable and responsive within the complex intracellular environment. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of HCPD as a promising fluorescent probe for cellular sensing and analyte detection in biologically relevant systems.

Conclusion

In summary, a novel coumarin-based chemosensor, HCPD, was successfully synthesized through a one-pot condensation reaction and developed for the efficient tandem detection of Co2+ and ATP ions. The probe exhibited polarity-dependent emission in different solvents and showed clear aggregation-induced emission (AIE) behavior in a THF-water mixture. HCPD demonstrated high sensitivity towards Co2+ ions in a DMF/Tris-HCl buffer system through an emission “turn-off” response. The binding between HCPD and Co2+ occurred in 1 : 1 stoichiometric ratio, as confirmed by Job's plot and HRMS analysis. The probe displayed a remarkably low detection limit of 11.7 nM for Co2+, indicating its excellent sensing performance and selectivity. Furthermore, the HCPD–Co2+ ensemble system enabled the selective detection of ATP over various competing anions via emission “turn-on” mechanism, demonstrating reversible sensing behavior. The practical applicability of HCPD was further validated through on-site detection platforms, including IMPLICATION logic gate construction, cotton swabs and paper strips. The probe was further successfully employed for live/dead cell analysis, MMP assessment and live-cell imaging in MDA-MB-231 cells. Thus, HCPD represents a promising dual-responsive fluorescent probe for the selective and reversible monitoring of Co2+ and ATP in real-time applications.
