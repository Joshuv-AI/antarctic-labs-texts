Evaluation of the Effectiveness of Additional Risk Minimisation Measures for Tofacitinib (Xeljanz®) in Europe: A Prescriber Survey
Authors: Joanne Wu, Nana Koram, Kofi Asomaning, Lubna Merchant, Robert Massouh, Edward Nagy, Subhan Khalid, Laura Walsh, Rafia Bosan, Krystal Cantos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe evaluated the effectiveness of tofacitinib (oral Janus kinase inhibitor) additional risk minimisation measures (aRMM) in Europe via prescriber surveys. Operational challenges/methodological limitations of surveys and recommendations for future considerations were summarised.MethodsIn this post-authorisation safety study, multimodal surveys were conducted in eight European countries from 2021 to 2022 among prescribers of tofacitinib for rheumatoid arthritis and/or psoriatic arthritis (RA/PsA) or ulcerative colitis (UC) in the 12 months preceding survey administration. Prescribers’ awareness (receipt) of aRMM materials, knowledge of key risk messages and adherence to risk minimisation practices were assessed. A priori effectiveness thresholds (≥ ~80%) were set across outcomes.ResultsOf 18,764 and 12,777 prescribers invited to take the RA/PsA or UC survey, 164 and 81 completed the surveys, respectively (response proportion: 326/18,764, 1.7%; 154/12,777, 1.2%, respectively). Among completers, self-reported receipt of all initial and/or 2019 updated aRMM materials was 33.5% and 32.7% in the RA/PsA and UC surveys, respectively. In the RA/PsA and UC surveys, 39.5% and 24.5%, respectively, answered ≥ ~80% of knowledge questions correctly; 51.2% and 69.7%, respectively, provided desirable responses to ≥ ~80% of adherence questions.ConclusionsAlthough the tofacitinib aRMM materials did not achieve the desired effectiveness for awareness (receipt), knowledge or adherence to risk minimisation practices among survey completers, the study was deemed inconclusive given the low response rate. This demonstrated that unless an acceptable response rate is achieved, surveys should not be the only method for aRMM evaluation. Secondary data may supplement future surveys to increase their robustness.HMA-EMA Catalogues RegistrationEUPAS43143 (date of registration: 29/09/2021)Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40261-024-01408-5.

Key Points

Low response rates to the surveys made it difficult to conclude whether the additional risk minimisation measures (aRMM) for tofacitinib were effective among prescribers in terms of awareness, knowledge or adherence to risk minimisation practices.Given the limitations of surveys, additional data and/or methods are required to fully evaluate the effectiveness of aRMM materials.

Introduction

Risk minimisation measures (RMM), such as Summaries of Product Characteristics (SmPC), are routine activities required for all medicinal products [1, 2]. They are intended to reduce and/or prevent the occurrence of treatment-related adverse events for medicines, or lessen their impact or severity, should they occur. However, when routine RMM are considered inadequate for the safe and effective use of a medicine, additional RMM (aRMM) may be proposed [1, 2]. A variety of aRMM tools are currently utilised and can include educational programmes, direct healthcare professional (HCP) communications and controlled access and distribution programmes [1, 2]. The evaluation of the effectiveness of these measures is required per a 2010 European Commission directive [3].

Tofacitinib (Xeljanz®) is an oral Janus kinase inhibitor approved by the European Commission for the treatment of chronic immune-mediated inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA) and ulcerative colitis (UC) [4–6]. To enhance the awareness and knowledge of prescribers and patients about safety concerns of tofacitinib, the marketing authorisation holder (i.e. Pfizer) implemented aRMM for tofacitinib at the time of marketing authorisation (from April 2017 for RA, June 2018 for PsA and August 2018 for UC); these aRMM are herein termed the ‘initial aRMM materials’. The tofacitinib aRMM consist of an educational programme intended to enhance the communication of the key risk messages and risk minimisation practices of tofacitinib to patients and prescribers. The programme includes the following components: (1) tofacitinib prescriber brochure; (2) tofacitinib prescriber treatment initiation checklist; (3) tofacitinib prescriber treatment maintenance checklist; and (4) tofacitinib patient alert card (distributed to patients by the prescriber).

In November 2019, based on a recommendation from the Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee (PRAC) of the European Medicines Agency, the Committee of Human Medicinal Products concluded that patients treated with tofacitinib were at increased risk of venous thromboembolism events, particularly those patients with existing venous thromboembolism risk factors [7]. Therefore, the PRAC recommended that warnings be added to the tofacitinib SmPC. In addition, following interim results from ORAL Surveillance, a post-authorisation safety study (PASS) of tofacitinib in patients with active RA aged ≥ 50 years with one or more additional cardiovascular risk factor [8], the PRAC also concluded that there was a potential increased risk of mortality [9], and that tofacitinib should be used in patients aged > 65 years, patients who are current or past smokers and patients with other cardiovascular risk and malignancy risk factors, only if there are no other suitable treatments [10]. The initial aRMM materials were, therefore, updated to reflect the findings from the Committee of Human Medicinal Product’s re-assessment of the benefit-risk balance of tofacitinib and were distributed by mail, e-mail or Salesforce after February 2020; these aRMM are herein termed the ‘2019 updated aRMM materials’.

To assess the effectiveness of the tofacitinib aRMM programme, a comprehensive plan was proposed by the marketing authorisation holder, which consisted of the following two components: (1) process indicators (i.e. prescribers’ receipt and understanding of the aRMM materials) and (2) outcome indicators (i.e. prescribing and clinical practice behaviours with respect to recommendations). To realise the first component, a survey was conducted as it is a commonly used approach to assess the distribution, receipt, usage and knowledge of key risk messages contained in the aRMM materials [11–14]. The second component was realised through a drug utilisation study using secondary healthcare data.

In this PASS, the effectiveness of the tofacitinib aRMM was evaluated using two surveys of HCPs who prescribe tofacitinib to patients with RA and/or PsA, or UC, across Europe. The surveys primarily assessed prescribers self-reported awareness (receipt) and utilisation of the tofacitinib aRMM materials and tofacitinib prescriber website (a repository of the aRMM materials); prescribers’ self-reported awareness of the key risk messages relating to tofacitinib use described in the aRMM and if prescribers were adhering to the risk minimisation practices recommended in the aRMM materials. In addition, as part of this article, the operational and methodological challenges of using HCP surveys to evaluate the effectiveness of aRMM under PASS classification were described, and recommendations for future considerations were provided.

Methods

Study Design

Two cross-sectional non-interventional surveys were conducted among HCPs who prescribed tofacitinib to patients with RA and/or PsA (the RA/PsA survey), or to patients with UC (the UC survey); the surveys were split because of specific questions pertaining to initiation and maintenance dosing for patients with UC (the RA/PsA and UC surveys are included as Appendices 1 and 2, respectively, in the published study protocol [15]). To allow sufficient time for prescribers to familiarise themselves with the aRMM materials and tofacitinib uptake (as applicable), the surveys were conducted no earlier than 6 months after the distribution of the 2019 updated aRMM materials or tofacitinib reimbursement, respectively. Participation was voluntary and all data were collected via a structured survey questionnaire, which was conducted online or by phone interview. Invitations and phone interviews were managed by call centre operators who were specialised in health surveys and were trained on the survey methodology prior to fieldwork. Invitations, which included an overview of the rationale for the survey and a secure URL for completion (online only), were issued to prescribers primarily by e-mail (if available), or by post or phone, depending on the country, and at least three attempts were made to reach each prescriber by these means before they were deemed ‘unreachable’. Data collection was planned to last for 12 weeks but lasted for 14 weeks if the survey period spanned winter holidays (Fig. 1). Honoraria were not provided because of local regulations. The study was registered in the European Union Register of Post-Authorisation Studies (register number: EUPAS43143).

aRMM Materials and Dissemination

The distribution of the initial aRMM materials (tofacitinib prescriber brochure, treatment initiation checklist, treatment maintenance checklist and patient alert card) to prescribers in European countries began at the same time as the initial marketing authorisation: April 2017 (for RA), June 2018 (for PsA) and August 2018 (for UC). The initial aRMM materials were updated to reflect the findings from the Committee of Human Medicinal Products' re-assessment of the benefit-risk profile of tofacitinib; these were redistributed by mail, e-mail or Salesforce (depending on country) in February 2020 (Poland), March 2020 (Germany, Sweden and UK), April 2020 (France), May 2020 (Netherlands), August 2020 (Romania) and November 2020 (Spain) (2019 updated aRMM materials; Fig. 1).

The target population for the distribution of the 2019 updated aRMM was based on selection criteria agreed upon with the relevant national health authority. Generally, this population consisted of dermatologists, gastroenterologists, rheumatologists and, in some cases, additional HCPs (e.g. internists, general practitioners and specialist pharmacists). The database used to identify eligible prescribers for aRMM material distribution in each member state was obtained from the proprietary IQVIA OneKey database, which was also the same database used to identify prescribers for the surveys.

Study Population

The study population comprised prescribers from eight European countries: France, Germany, the Netherlands, Poland, Romania, Spain, Sweden and the UK. These countries were selected based on a variety of factors, including the operational feasibility to implement the survey and to provide a meaningfully large and representative sample of prescribers across Europe. Prescribers were included if they were listed in the proprietary IQVIA OneKey database (i.e. the same database as for the distribution of the aRMM materials) with ‘rheumatology’, ‘dermatology’ or ‘gastroenterology’ as their primary, secondary or tertiary speciality; had contact information in the OneKey database; were located in the selected countries; prescribed tofacitinib for RA and/or PsA, or UC, in the 12 months preceding survey administration; and agreed to participate in and completed the survey. No sampling method was used for selecting prescribers. All prescribers who met the pre-specified eligibility criteria were invited to participate in the survey. The worldwide IQVIA OneKey database is available in 73 countries and contains more than 13.7 million HCPs; it has been used in research to recruit HCPs [16, 17] and by international organisations to construct HCP census data [18]. Prescribers were excluded if they participated in pilot testing of the survey and/or were employees of Pfizer.

Survey Development and Validation

Data were collected from a multimodal survey instrument, which included closed-ended questions or statements with multiple-response choices that were written to follow the principles of health literacy and readability. The survey instrument was designed to assess: (1) prescribers’ self-reported awareness (receipt) and utilisation of aRMM materials (and tofacitinib prescriber website); (2) prescribers’ knowledge of key risk messages; and (3) prescribers’ self-reported adherence to the risk minimisation practices. Other questions included those for screening/eligibility, demographics and clinical practice characteristics, primary source of prescribers’ information on the safety and prescribing information for tofacitinib and attitudes towards the aRMM materials.

Survey questions were created using a systematic approach that corresponded to key risk messages communicated in the aRMM, including awareness (receipt) and utilisation of the aRMM materials and knowledge and adherence to risk minimisation measures. Each key risk message had at least one corresponding question in the RA/PsA and UC surveys. Of the 35 questions in the RA/PsA survey relating to key risk messages, 18 were knowledge questions and 17 were adherence questions. In the UC survey, of which there were 37 questions, 20 were knowledge questions and 17 were adherence questions. For survey questions that consisted of several response options, each option was counted as a separate question as they could have been viewed as distinct sub-messages pertaining to the key risk messages.

The survey questions were programmed to ensure that they were asked in the appropriate sequence. Prescribers were not able to return to a question once it had been answered or skip ahead. Response options were presented in a list that was randomised to minimise positional bias. Programming was reviewed by quality control and simulated users (i.e. pilot testing) prior to the implementation of the surveys. Surveys could only have been submitted once all questions had been answered. Survey items were presented as either instructions to prompt the prescriber to indicate whether a series of statements were ‘true’ or ‘false’, or if they did not know the answer (there were also questions that used ‘yes’ or ‘no’ or ‘I don’t know’ as potential response options); or statements or questions asking the prescribers to choose from a defined list of possible statements or answers.

Prior to the launch of the RA/PsA and UC surveys, the survey instrument was pilot tested by a sample of prescribers, including two rheumatologists, two dermatologists and two gastroenterologists, who could potentially prescribe tofacitinib in the UK. This qualitative testing assessed clarity and comprehension among prescribers of the words, phrases and response options to be used in the surveys, to ensure that the intended content of the questions was adequately conveyed. The feedback received from the pilot testing was incorporated into the final versions of the surveys. The survey instrument was translated from English into the local language for each survey country using a committee-based approach; this ensured accurate and comprehensive local versions of the survey by a team of translators. All translations were performed by certified translators.

Independent ethics committee/institutional review board review was not required in any of the study countries, and a waiver was received from a German independent ethics committee. All prescribers who chose to participate provided written informed consent.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse survey data, including frequencies and proportions. For categorical outcomes, weights were applied to the proportions, with weighted standard errors for the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), calculated using the normal approximation of the binomial distribution. For continuous variables, weights were applied to the calculated means, and corresponding weighted standard deviations (SDs) were reported. The data collected from the RA/PsA and UC surveys were analysed separately, and only prescribers with completed surveys were included in the main analyses. All primary analyses were conducted using pooled data across all countries; no statistical comparisons within or between countries, specialties and/or indications were conducted.

Because of the descriptive nature of the study, an a priori sample size calculation, based on the normal approximation of the binomial distribution, was used to describe the precision of the estimated response proportion. A target sample size of 300 prescribers (completed surveys) for the RA/PsA survey and 300 prescribers for the UC survey was sought, which would have achieved an overall precision of 5.7% for each survey. The target sample size was also based on the operational feasibility of achieving it. However, no sample size limit was applied, and all completed surveys received during the data collection window were included in the study.

As the distribution of prescribers of tofacitinib for RA, PsA or UC in each country (in the sample of prescribers who completed the surveys) was different from the underlying population, the results from the surveys for the overall population were weighted to reflect the proportions of prescribers who prescribe tofacitinib across the countries, and within each country to reflect the proportion of each prescriber specialty authorised to prescribe tofacitinib in that country (RA/PsA survey only). As the true numbers of prescribers of tofacitinib for RA, PsA or UC in each survey country are unknown, the numbers of rheumatologists and dermatologists (RA/PsA survey) and gastroenterologists (UC survey) identified in the OneKey database were used as proxies for the true numbers.

The effectiveness for the three main objectives of the surveys were defined as: (1) ≥ 80% of prescribers reporting that they received all aRMM materials (initial or 2019 updated aRMM materials) or reporting that they ever received all 2019 updated aRMM materials only; (2) prescribers correctly answering ≥ 14 out of 18 (≥ 78%, or approximately 80%) RA/PsA or ≥16 out of 20 (≥ 80%) UC knowledge questions; and (3) prescribers providing desirable responses for ≥14 out of 17 (≥82%, or approximately 80%) adherence questions.

In addition to a weighted analysis to determine the primary source of prescribers’ information on the safety and prescribing information for tofacitinib, an additional post hoc analysis was conducted to provide a more meaningful estimate of whether aRMM materials were an important source of information for prescribers. This unweighted analysis was restricted to prescribers who self-reported ever receiving aRMM materials.

Results

Survey Response

Of 18,764 (RA/PsA survey) and 12,777 (UC survey) prescribers invited to participate: 860 (4.6%; RA/PsA) and 485 (3.8%; UC) responded to the invitation; 339 (1.8%; RA/PsA) and 164 (1.3%; UC) agreed to participate; and 164 (0.9%; RA/PsA) and 81 (0.6%; UC) eligible prescribers completed the survey (Fig. 2). The survey response proportion (i.e. the proportion screened for participation out of those invited to participate) was 1.7% (RA/PsA survey; 326/18,764) and 1.2% (UC survey; 154/12,777). Response proportions for individual countries ranged from 0.5% (17/3,140) in Poland to 3.3% (90/2,760) in Spain (RA/PsA survey) and from 0.7% (7/948) in Poland to 1.9% (30/1,607) in France (UC survey) (Fig. 1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)).

Across surveys, a similar proportion of prescribers reported treating < 5 (41.9% [69/164] and 36.1% [29/81] for RA/PsA and UC, respectively) or 5 to ≤ 10 patients (28.5% [47/164] and 34.1% [28/81] for RA/PsA and UC, respectively) in the past 12 months; 83.0% (136/164; RA/PsA) and 93.3% (76/81; UC) of prescribers were involved in both tofacitinib treatment initiation and maintenance (Table 1). Self-reported Awareness (Receipt) and Utilisation of Tofacitinib aRMM Materials

Of the 164 prescribers who completed the RA/PsA survey, 33.5% (55/164; 95% CI 26.2–40.8) and 26.0% (43/81; 95% CI 19.2–32.7) self-reported awareness (receipt) of all aRMM materials ever (i.e. initial and/or 2019 updated aRMM materials) and all 2019 updated aRMM materials, respectively, which was below the ≥80% effectiveness threshold. Most (61.6%; 101/164) prescribers reported awareness (receipt) ever of the tofacitinib prescriber brochure and patient alert card; 49.9% (82/164) and 40.6% (67/164) reported awareness (receipt) ever of the tofacitinib prescriber treatment initiation checklist and tofacitinib prescriber treatment maintenance checklist, respectively. Prescriber self-reported awareness (receipt) of individual 2019 updated aRMM materials ranged from 32.3% (53/164; tofacitinib prescriber maintenance checklist) to 45.7% (75/164; tofacitinib patient alert card; Fig. 3a); 46.9% (77/164) reported awareness of the tofacitinib prescriber website (data not shown).

Of the 81 prescribers who completed the UC survey, less than one-third reported awareness (receipt) of all aRMM materials ever (32.7% [26/81; 95% CI 22.2–43.1]) and all 2019 updated aRMM materials (24.3% [20/81; 95% CI 14.8–33.9]); the ≥ 80% effectiveness threshold was not met. Over half of prescribers self-reported awareness (receipt) ever of the tofacitinib patient alert card (61.8%; 50/81) and the tofacitinib prescriber brochure (58.9%; 48/81); while fewer than half reported awareness (receipt) ever of the tofacitinib prescriber treatment initiation (44.8%; 36/81) and maintenance (43.7%; 35/81) checklists. Of the individual 2019 updated aRMM materials, 36.1% (29/81) of prescribers reported awareness (receipt) of the tofacitinib prescriber initiation checklist and tofacitinib prescriber maintenance checklist, while 46.0% (37/81) reported awareness (receipt) of the tofacitinib prescriber brochure (Fig. 3b); 37.0% (30/81) reported awareness of the tofacitinib prescriber website (data not shown).

Among prescribers who self-reported receipt (ever) of the materials, 97.2% (98/101 [patient alert card])–99.0% (100/101 [prescriber brochure]; RA/PsA) and 95.5% (35/36 [prescriber treatment initiation checklist])–100% (48/48 [prescriber brochure]; UC) reported utilisation of the individual aRMM materials. Among prescribers aware of the tofacitinib prescriber website, most reported utilisation of this resource (55.8% [43/77; RA/PsA] and 79.9% [24/30; UC]).

Knowledge of Tofacitinib Key Risk Messages and Self-Reported Adherence to Tofacitinib 2019 Updated aRMM Materials

Of the 18 questions related to knowledge of tofacitinib key risk messages, in the RA/PsA survey, on average, the prescribers answered 12.4 (SD 2.4) questions correctly; 39.5% (65/164; 95% CI 31.9–47.1) of prescribers answered ≥ 78% (approximately 80%) of the questions correctly. Of the 20 knowledge questions in the UC survey, 13.2 (SD 3.0) questions were answered correctly on average; 24.5% (20/81; 95% CI 14.9–34.1) of prescribers answered ≥ 80% of the questions correctly (Fig. 4). For both surveys, correct responses to the knowledge questions were below the ≥ 80% effectiveness threshold.

Of the 17 questions relating to adherence to tofacitinib risk minimisation practices, on average, the prescribers provided 13.6 (SD 1.5) and 14.4 (SD 1.4) adherent answers in the RA/PsA and UC surveys, respectively. Desirable responses for ≥ 82% (approximately 80%) of the questions were recorded for 51.2% (84/164; 95% CI 43.5–58.9; RA/PsA survey) and 69.7% (56/81; 95% CI 59.5–80.0; UC survey; Fig. 4) of prescribers. For both surveys, the proportion of desirable responses to the adherence questions was below the ≥ 80% effectiveness threshold.

Primary Source of Prescribers’ Information on Tofacitinib Safety and Prescribing Information and Perceived Usefulness of Tofacitinib aRMM Materials/Prescriber Website

Among prescribers who completed the RA/PsA survey, the most frequently reported source of tofacitinib safety/prescribing information was the SmPC (46.5% [76/164]), followed by the aRMM materials (32.2% [53/164]), websites (including ‘National Health Authority website’ and ‘National formulary website’; 16.3% [27/164]) and other sources (5.0% [9/164]; Fig. 5). For prescribers who completed the UC survey (excluding Germany), a similar proportion reported aRMM materials and the SmPC as the source of tofacitinib safety/prescribing information (31.8% [19/59] and 31.0% [18/59], respectively); 25.5% [15/59] reported websites (including ‘National Health Authority website’ and ‘National formulary website’) as their source of information; and 11.7% [8/81] reported other sources (Fig. 5). In the post hoc analyses, restricted to prescribers who reported ever receiving the aRMM materials, the SmPC (38.0% [52/137]), aRMM materials (32.1% [44/137]) and websites (including ‘National Health Authority website’ and ‘National formulary website’; 25.5% [35/137]) were the primary sources of tofacitinib information reported in the RA/PsA survey, while the SmPC and aRMM materials (both 33.3% [15/45]) and websites (including ‘National Health Authority website’ and ‘National formulary website’; 26.7% [12/45]) were the primary sources of tofacitinib information reported in the UC survey (unweighted data).

Among prescribers in the RA/PsA and UC surveys who reported ever receiving the tofacitinib aRMM materials (i.e. initial and/or 2019 updated aRMM materials), most (> 96%) indicated that they found the tofacitinib prescriber brochure, initiation/maintenance checklists and patient alert card ‘somewhat’ or ‘very useful’, as opposed to ‘not useful’. Similarly, among prescribers in the RA/PsA and UC surveys who reported ever visiting the tofacitinib prescriber website, all (100%) found the website ‘somewhat’ or ‘very useful’, as opposed to ‘not useful’ (data not shown). The proportion of prescribers who indicated that individual aRMM materials were ‘very useful’ ranged from 69.7% (70/101; patient alert card)–84.5% (34/40; prescriber treatment maintenance checklist) in the RA/PsA survey and 61.5% (14/23; prescriber treatment maintenance checklist)–74.5% (35/47; prescriber brochure) in the UC survey.

Discussion

These multinational surveys of prescribers across Europe were conducted to assess the effectiveness of the tofacitinib aRMM, which included the initial and 2019 updated aRMM materials, reflecting recommendations for use in patients with venous thromboembolism risk factors and in those > 65 years of age. Overall, among prescribers surveyed, the pre-determined threshold (≥ 80%) for effectiveness was not met across any of the domains for self-reported awareness (receipt) of all aRMM materials, knowledge of key risk messages or adherence to risk minimisation practices. However, the effectiveness of the aRMM materials cannot be meaningfully inferred from the results because of the low survey response proportions (< 2%). Low response rates are a common and major limitation in HCP surveys conducted under PASS classification [19, 20]. Other challenges with HCP surveys include limitations with self-reported data, such as social desirability bias and recall error, and the lack of a validated threshold to determine the effectiveness of aRMM materials.

The effectiveness of aRMM has been examined for other pharmaceutical products in Europe; these included: voriconazole, an anti-fungal agent; apixaban, an oral anticoagulant; and aripiprazole, an atypical antipsychotic [11–13]. Similar to this analysis, low response rates were observed with the voriconazole (1.7%), apixaban (2.0%) and aripiprazole (2.3%) aRMM effectiveness surveys [11–13]. For surveys evaluating the effectiveness of RMM and aRMM materials, it is not uncommon for the proportion of HCP responses to be below 10% [11–14], especially as participation rates in epidemiologic studies have decreased over the past 30–60 years [21, 22]. Indeed, a systematic review of 24 surveys assessing the effectiveness of RMM in Europe found an average response proportion of 5% (completers of those invited) [20]. The response rates were low in the tofacitinib aRMM surveys presented here, despite efforts made to improve the response rates. For example, the surveys were pilot tested for clarity, they were conducted in local languages, there was a multimodal recruitment approach (phone, e-mail and post), and prescribers were contacted at least three times before they were deemed ‘unreachable’. Several factors may have impacted the level of responses: participation in the survey was voluntary; honoraria were not provided because of local regulations; there was no pre-notification for prescribers of an invitation to complete the survey; there were challenges in identifying and targeting tofacitinib prescribers because of local privacy restrictions; there was low prescriber interest in responding, perhaps because of competing priorities; and there were logistical challenges (particularly with postal methods). In addition, even though 12 months had passed from the 2019 updated aRMM material distribution to survey initiation, the time of 2019 updated aRMM material distribution varied across countries (February 2020 [Poland] to November 2020 [Spain]), and market penetrance may have been low at the start of data collection. For example, tofacitinib reimbursement was most recent in Romania for PsA and UC (August 2021 for both indications) versus the other study countries, which would have resulted in a reduced number of those with experience in prescribing tofacitinib, thereby rendering them ineligible to participate. Given the low response rate, there is a possibility that survey completers differed in terms of characteristics, awareness of the aRMM materials and knowledge of the risks of tofacitinib, versus prescribers who did not respond to the survey. Thus, selection bias cannot be ruled out, and the generalisability of the study was limited.

Another limitation of the survey was the lack of baseline data and, therefore, the inability to measure the extent to which prescribers’ knowledge or behaviours could be attributed to the aRMM programme itself. For the initial aRMM materials that were distributed at the time of initial marketing authorisation, no baseline data were available for comparison, therefore an a priori threshold was used to assess the effectiveness of aRMM materials. In the systematic review of 24 European surveys, approximately one-third used a predefined threshold (e.g. 80%), as in our study [20]. As acknowledged by the authors of the voriconazole aRMM evaluation study, which utilised an effectiveness threshold of 80% [11], the selection of the threshold was subjective and was not based on established scientific criteria in the education or risk communication literature. This was partly owing to applying a single threshold across different questions for aRMM dissemination, knowledge and self-reported adherence to determine effectiveness, a limiting factor of the study. A recent review of 93 industry-sponsored PASS that evaluated the effectiveness of RMM (assessed by PRAC from 2016 to 2021) suggested that the utilisation of predefined effectiveness criteria may improve the effectiveness of RMM evaluation [23]. The European Medicines Agency guideline on Good Pharmacovigilance Practices Module XVI (Revision 3) outlined a list of factors to consider for determining thresholds, including seriousness of the indication, patient population at risk, nature and magnitude of the risk, and acceptability by regulators and society, but also recognised the need to determine the threshold on a case-by-case basis [24]. For surveys evaluating updated aRMM materials, it may be possible to collect baseline data prior to the dissemination of the updated aRMM materials as a comparator, which is more robust than using a prespecified threshold. The feasibility of collecting baseline data may depend on there being a sufficient gap between the initial and updated aRMM materials, and if the same group of HCPs can be surveyed. The latter could pose operational challenges because of the inability to identify and target HCPs because of local privacy restrictions. Currently, pre-post surveys are rarely used in evaluating the effectiveness of aRMM materials [20]. The review of 93 PASS found that PASS that led to a conclusion on RMM effectiveness more frequently used a comparator [23].

Reliance on the respondent’s recall and self-reported data, and the susceptibility to social desirability biases limited the accuracy of the surveys. Notably, in the RA/PsA survey, there was differential recall in the self-reported awareness (receipt) of individual components of the aRMM materials, despite these being distributed in one package. This is likely owing to a higher recall for the most frequently utilised materials. Interestingly, over half of prescribers reported that they did not remember whether they received the aRMM materials, supporting the limitation of human recall. Recall could be improved by shortening the time between aRMM material distribution and survey administration, while also allowing for wide dissemination of aRMM materials. However, timely launch of a survey may not always be achieved because of the stringent regulatory requirements under PASS designation, which increase the operational complexity, as has been previously noted [11, 19]. The launch of this survey was delayed across countries to an average of 20 months after the dissemination of the 2019 updated aRMM materials owing to local regulatory submissions and approvals. In addition, social desirability bias may threaten the validity of self-reported data, particularly regarding prescribing behaviours. For example, approximately 60% of prescribers answered ‘Yes’ to the question, ‘in your clinical practice, do you perform the following tests to make a decision about whether to initiate a patient on tofacitinib (Yes/No)? Check blood glucose level?’, where the desirable response was ‘No’; prescribers likely assumed that ‘Yes’ was the expected answer. Therefore, it is recommended to include negative response questions to assess the extent of social desirability bias; multiple-choice questions rather than binary response questions may also minimise such bias.

The impact of PASS designation on aRMM evaluation surveys has been discussed extensively; this includes operational challenges that arise from the varying country-specific regulations and requirements regarding local regulatory and ethics committee submissions [11, 19]. These challenges reduce response rates, introduce selection bias and limit generalisability, which are among the main concerns raised by regulators in assessing survey study reports [20]. Nevertheless, surveys remain important in the evaluation of aRMM materials as they are an effective, and sometimes the only feasible approach to assess the distribution, receipt, usage and knowledge of key risk messages contained in the aRMM materials. According to Good Pharmacovigilance Practices Module XVI (Revision 3), the PASS designation for surveys that evaluate the effectiveness of aRMM is unlikely to change [24]; therefore, changes in how surveys are conducted should occur to ensure their utility in evaluating aRMM materials. For instance, investigators should first consider conducting feasibility assessments prior to the full study to estimate response rates and introduce strategies to improve response rates, whilst remaining compliant with local regulations. Prior recommendations suggest that a response rate of ≥ 60% is preferred to ensure that non-response bias does not compromise the validity of survey results [25]. While honoraria are likely to enhance participation [26], incentivisation could instead be linked with fulfilling requirements for continuing education; an example of this was the inclusion of accredited continuing education activities as part of the risk evaluation and mitigation strategy for extended-release long-acting opioid analgesics [27]. Continuing education activities have been shown to improve prescriber behaviour, knowledge, skills, attitudes and patient health outcomes [28].

In anticipation of the operational challenges and methodological limitations in surveys, investigators may also consider a hybrid design, such as supplementing surveys with drug utilisation studies, thereby leveraging secondary data. This may allow for a more objective assessment of prescribing behaviour, for example, by assessing changes in prescribing patterns before and after specific updates to aRMM. The complementary strengths of primary and secondary data in a single PASS have the potential to be a more robust evaluation of the effectiveness of aRMM materials than either method alone [20]. In the review of 93 industry-sponsored PASS evaluating the effectiveness of RMM materials, only 7.5% utilised this hybrid design [23]. Further development of this hybrid method and the logistics of correlating survey data with secondary data is warranted.

Conclusions

Among prescribers who completed the survey, the tofacitinib aRMM materials did not reach the required levels of effectiveness for awareness (receipt), knowledge or adherence to risk minimisation practices; however, the low response rate rendered the study inconclusive. The experience of our study, as with others, demonstrates that the confinement of the current model (i.e. including PASS designation) leads to operational challenges, partly because of varying country-specific regulations and local regulatory requirements. Therefore, dissemination of surveys to all potential prescribers is not effective at achieving a high response rate, which is important to making meaningful scientific conclusions. Unless an acceptable response rate can be achieved, surveys should not be the only method for the evaluation of aRMM materials. A hybrid design leveraging both primary and secondary data in the same study may overcome some of the limitations associated with surveys and increase the robustness of aRMM evaluations; however, further development is warranted.
