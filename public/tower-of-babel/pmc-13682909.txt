Dietary Inflammation and Chronic Dizziness With Imbalance in Middle-Aged and Older Adults: A Nationally Representative Study
Authors: Seong-Hae Jeong, Eun Ji Kim, Eunjin Kwon, Eunice Hong Lim Lee, Sukyoung Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic dizziness and imbalance, which can significantly impact an individual’s quality of life and even mortality, are prevalent conditions with multifactorial etiologies, including inflammation. We examined the association between dietary inflammatory potential and chronic dizziness with imbalance.MethodsThis study included 7,562 Korean adults aged ≥ 40 years from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2019–2022. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) was calculated from 24-hour dietary recall, with higher scores indicating more pro-inflammatory diets. Chronic dizziness was categorized as isolated or accompanied by imbalance. Multinomial logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsPrevalence was 3.8% for isolated dizziness and 1.4% for dizziness with imbalance. Higher DII scores were associated with higher odds of chronic dizziness with imbalance (tertile 3 vs. tertile 1; OR, 2.89; 95% CI, 1.54–5.43; P trend = 0.016), but not with isolated dizziness. The association was stronger among participants without hearing loss (OR per 1-point increase, 1.45; 95% CI, 1.19–1.77; P interaction = 0.047) than in those with hearing loss.ConclusionA higher dietary inflammatory potential was associated with increased odds of chronic dizziness accompanied by imbalance, suggesting that dietary inflammation may be relevant to vestibular-related symptoms.

INTRODUCTION

Dizziness is associated with reduced quality of life, a higher risk of falls, and increased healthcare burden1; notably, its frequent co-occurrence with balance deficits warrants particular attention.2 From a life-course perspective, subclinical losses in balance reserve can begin in midlife (40–64 years) as age-related vestibular decline and sarcopenia unfold, while low-grade systemic inflammation—to which diet is a tractable upstream contributor—can degrade sensorimotor integration and burden central processing, thereby lowering postural reserve.345

Chronic inflammation undermines balance by disrupting the integrated functioning of the vestibular system, skeletal muscle, and central nervous system (CNS). Acting across multiple levels, it can establish a vicious cycle in which inflammation in one subsystem triggers or amplifies dysfunction in another—for example, CNS-delimited inflammatory signaling can accelerate muscle catabolism via hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA)-axis activation,6 while peripheral inflammatory states are accompanied by impaired proprioception and higher fall risk.7 Inflammatory pathways are also implicated across vestibular disorders (e.g., vestibular migraine and Ménière disease) and vestibular neuritis, supporting a mechanistic link between inflammation and balance dysfunction.8910 Coupled with age-related vestibular decline and challenges in multisensory integration, these processes deplete postural reserve and compound fall risk.3

Diet is a modifiable driver of low-grade systemic inflammation,1112 and the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) provides a useful tool for quantifying the inflammatory potential of individual dietary patterns.13 Higher DII scores have been linked to adverse health outcomes, such as frailty, sarcopenia, and cognitive decline—conditions sharing mechanistic pathways with dizziness and balance impairment.1415 Consistent with this biology, chronic dizziness and imbalance may be influenced by diet-mediated mechanisms, particularly via low-grade systemic inflammation, sarcopenia, and impaired sensorimotor integration.16 Yet whether dietary inflammatory potential relates selectively to functional imbalance—as opposed to dizziness symptoms per se—has been largely overlooked. Because falls are the leading cause of injury and injury deaths among adults aged ≥ 65 years and a major global cause of disability and mortality, public-health guidance prioritizes early, systematic identification and mitigation of modifiable fall risks, including balance deficits.1718

We therefore examined the association between the DII and chronic dizziness with imbalance in a nationally representative sample of Korean adults aged ≥ 40 years. Age strata were prespecified (40–64 vs. ≥ 65 years) to map the midlife-to-older-age continuum. Our objective was to determine whether more pro-inflammatory diets are associated with a higher risk of functional imbalance and, in turn, to identify preventive nutritional targets from midlife into older age.

METHODS

Study population

The Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) is a nationwide, population-based, cross-sectional survey administered by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA). It is designed to assess the health and nutritional status of Korean individuals who do not reside in institutions.19 The KNHANES employs a multistage, stratified probability sampling approach to ensure the representativeness of the Korean population. The survey consists of three major components: a health interview, a physical examination, and a dietary assessment. Further information about the survey methodology and data can be found on the official website (https://knhanes.kdca.go.kr/knhanes/main.do).

Among 10,429 adults aged 40 years or older who completed the chronic dizziness questionnaire, participants were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: pregnancy or lactation (n = 3); incomplete or implausible energy intakes (< 500 kcal or > 5,000 kcal) (n = 1,589); self-reported coronary heart disease or cancer (n = 792); or missing information on sociodemographic variables (n = 546). The final analytic sample included 7,562 adults (3,380 men and 4,182 women) (Fig. 1).

KNHANES = Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

Assessment of the exposure: DII

Trained dietitians conducted in-home, 24-hour dietary recall interviews one week after participants completed the health interview and examinations, obtaining detailed dietary information from survey participants. During the nutrition survey, participants provided comprehensive data on all foods and beverages consumed within the previous 24 hours, including portion sizes (by volume), meal times, and eating locations. A standardized multiple-pass method, supported by visual measuring guides, was used to enhance dietary recall accuracy. Nutrient and total energy intakes were calculated using the 10th Edition of the Korean Food Composition Table published by the Rural Development Administration20; this information is publicly accessible through the KNHANES website.

The primary exposure was the DII, which quantifies the inflammatory potential of an individual’s diet.13 The DII is a composite measure initially developed through a systematic review of 1,943 scientific publications published between 1950 and 2010. This index assigns scores to 45 dietary components—including nutrients, whole foods, and bioactive substances—based on their impact on inflammatory biomarkers such as interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (CRP). The total DII score in the original validation ranged from −8.87 to +7.98.13 In the current analysis, the DII was calculated following the standard methodology of its initial development. Of the original 45 parameters, only 28 were available in the KNHANES dataset and thus included in the calculation: energy, carbohydrate, protein, fat, fiber, vitamin A, D, E, C, beta-carotene, vitamin B1, B2, niacin, folate, magnesium, iron, zinc, n3-fatty acids, n6-fatty acids, cholesterol, saturated fat, polyunsaturated fatty acids, monounsaturated fatty acids, garlic, ginger, onion, green tea, and pepper. The remaining 17 components—such as various phytochemicals, selenium, and caffeine—were excluded due to a lack of available data.

The calculation procedure involved several steps. First, a Z-score for each dietary component was calculated by subtracting the global mean intake (from a reference database) from the individual’s intake and dividing by the global standard deviation. These Z-scores were then converted into percentiles to reduce the impact of skewed distributions and extreme values. Next, the percentiles were centered around zero by multiplying each by 2 and subtracting 1. These centered percentiles were subsequently multiplied by their respective food-specific inflammatory effect scores to generate individual DII component scores. Summing all component scores yielded the overall DII. Higher scores indicate more pro-inflammatory dietary patterns, whereas lower scores represent more anti-inflammatory dietary patterns. In the present study, DII scores ranged from −4.7 to +5.9.

Assessment of chronic dizziness and imbalance

Chronic dizziness and imbalance were assessed using responses to a structured questionnaire. Chronic dizziness was defined based on positive responses to both of the following questions: “Have you ever experienced dizziness or impaired balance in the past 12 months?” and “Have you ever felt chronically dizzy for 3 months or more in the past 12 months?”16 Chronic dizziness with imbalance was defined as chronic dizziness plus a positive response to at least one of the following questions: “Have you had difficulty maintaining a standing position for 3 months or more? (postural instability while standing),” “Have you had difficulty walking for 3 months or more? (postural instability while walking),” or “Have you repeatedly fallen down for 3 months or more? (falling).” Individuals without chronic dizziness or chronic dizziness with imbalance were classified as the robust group and served as the reference group for analysis (Fig. 2).16

Flow chart of chronic dizziness and imbalance group classification.

Assessment of covariates

Covariates included demographic variables (age, sex, and residential area), socioeconomic factors (education level, monthly household income, and marital status), lifestyle behaviors (current smoking, current drinking, regular walking, and weight training), mental health variables (perceived stress and physician-diagnosed depression), and hearing ability (tinnitus experience and hearing loss), which was assessed using an automatic audiometer (AD629; Interacoustics, Middelfart, Denmark) in a soundproof booth. Anthropometric measurements included standing height (cm) measured by a stadiometer, and body weight (kg) measured by a metric scale with participants in light clothing. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). Total daily energy intake (kcal/day) was also included as a covariate.161921 Detailed lists and definitions of covariates are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

For categorical analysis, DII scores were divided into tertiles (T), with T1 as the reference group. General characteristics of the study participants were presented as weighted means with standard errors (SEs) for continuous variables and weighted percentages with SEs for categorical variables across DII tertiles. Differences across tertiles were assessed using analysis of variance (continuous variables) and Rao-Scott χ2 tests (categorical variables).

Multinomial logistic regression models were employed to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for two outcomes: isolated chronic dizziness and chronic dizziness with imbalance, each compared to the robust group. To test linear trends across DII tertiles, the median DII value for each tertile was modeled as a continuous variable (P for trends). Continuous analyses were also performed, estimating ORs per 1-point increase in DII. Two models were fitted: 1) a model adjusted for age and sex; and 2) a fully adjusted model that further accounted for educational attainment, household income, marital status, current smoking, current drinking, physical activity (walking and weight training), perceived stress, physician-diagnosed depression, tinnitus experience, hearing loss, BMI, and total energy intake.

We also conducted stratified multivariable analyses according to key covariates. The strata were defined as follows: age (< 65 or ≥ 65 years), sex (men or women), residential area (urban or rural), education level (high school graduate: yes or no), household income level (quartiles 1, 2 or quartiles 3, 4), smoking status (current smoker: yes or no), drinking status (current drinker: yes or no), regular walking (yes or no), perceived stress (yes or no), physician-diagnosed depression (yes or no), tinnitus experience (yes or no), and hearing loss (yes or no). Interaction effects were evaluated by including cross-product terms between DII categories and each covariate in the model. As a sensitivity analysis, we further adjusted the final model for histories of hypertension, diabetes, and stroke to evaluate the potential impact of these medical conditions on the main findings.

All analyses were performed using the PROC SURVEY procedures in SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), incorporating sampling weights to account for the complex survey design of the KNHANES. Statistical significance was determined using two-sided tests, with a significance level set at 0.05.

Ethics statement

The study protocols of the KNHANES 2019–2022 were approved by the KDCA Ethics Review Board (Institutional Review Board [IRB] No. 2018-01-03-C-A, 2018-01-03-2C-A, 2018-01-03-5C-A, 2018-01-03-4C-A). All participants provided written informed consent before participation, and all survey data were anonymized before analysis. This study was deemed exempt by the IRB of the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs (IRB No. 2024-132) because only publicly available and anonymized data were used.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Overall, the prevalence of any chronic dizziness was 5.2% (n = 491), comprising 3.8% (n = 346) with isolated chronic dizziness and 1.4% (n = 145) with chronic dizziness accompanied by imbalance. Participants with higher DII scores were associated with older age, lower educational attainment and household income, current smoking, less frequent walking or weight training, and a higher prevalence of hearing loss. In terms of daily nutrient intake, participants in the highest DII tertile generally consumed higher amounts of most nutrients included in the DII, with the exception of carbohydrates (Table 1).

DII in relation to chronic dizziness and imbalance

Fig. 3 presents the cross-sectional associations between DII scores and both isolated chronic dizziness and chronic dizziness with imbalance. Participants in higher DII scores were positively associated with chronic with imbalance, whereas no significant association was observed for isolated chronic dizziness. After adjusting for age and sex, participants in the highest DII tertile had significantly higher odds of chronic dizziness with imbalance compared to those in the lowest tertile (OR, 2.60; 95% CI, 1.54–4.37; P trend = 0.001). A 1-point increase in the DII was associated with 1.3-fold higher odds of chronic dizziness with imbalance (OR per 1-point increase, 1.31; 95% CI, 1.15–1.50). This positive association remained significant in the multivariable-adjusted model, which further controlled for sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, hearing loss, and total energy intake (OR, 2.89; 95% CI, 1.54–5.43; P trend = 0.002; OR per 1-point increase, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.19–1.63). No significant association was observed between DII scores and isolated chronic dizziness.

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

Stratified analyses indicated that higher DII scores were consistently associated with increased odds of chronic dizziness with imbalance across most subgroups defined by demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle behaviors, mental health, and hearing ability variables (all P interaction > 0.05), except for hearing loss (P interaction = 0.047) (Fig. 4). A significant positive association between DII scores and chronic dizziness with imbalance was evident among participants without hearing loss (OR per 1-point increase, 1.45; 95% CI, 1.19–1.77), whereas no such association was observed among those with hearing loss (OR per 1-point increase, 1.16; 95% CI, 0.88–1.54). No significant associations were identified between DII scores and isolated chronic dizziness across the strata of other covariates (Supplementary Fig. 1). In the sensitivity analysis, the main findings remained unchanged after additional adjustment for history of hypertension, diabetes, and stroke (Supplementary Fig. 2).

DISCUSSION

In this nationally representative cross-sectional study of Korean adults, more pro-inflammatory dietary patterns, as reflected by higher DII scores, were associated with 2.6- to 2.9-fold increased odds of chronic dizziness with imbalance after adjusting for confounders. Stratified analyses indicated that these positive associations remained consistent across most subgroups, except for hearing loss status, with stronger associations observed among participants without hearing loss. No significant association was identified between dietary inflammatory potential and isolated chronic dizziness.

To our knowledge, this is the first study examining the association between dietary inflammatory potential and chronic dizziness with imbalance; therefore, direct comparisons with previous literature are limited. Nevertheless, our findings align with previously observed beneficial effects of Mediterranean diets on chronic dizziness with imbalance, as suggested by a prior Korean study.21 Specifically, a cross-sectional study involving Korean adults aged 40 years and older reported that adherence to the Mediterranean dietary pattern was associated with lower odds of experiencing chronic dizziness with imbalance, highlighting the potential role of reduced oxidative stress and the anti-inflammatory properties of individual components within the Mediterranean diet.21

Before discussing specific inflammatory mechanisms, it is important to clarify what a higher DII represents in this study. The DII does not directly measure circulating inflammatory biomarkers; rather, it reflects the inflammatory potential of habitual dietary patterns. Higher DII scores have been associated with biomarkers of chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, such as CRP,11 IL-6, and TNF-α.1322 This type of inflammation is typically persistent and has been linked to impaired balance performance and increased fall risk, rather than to acute inflammatory symptoms.23

Inflammation appears to play a significant role in vestibular disorders through multiple mechanisms. Recent research has explicitly linked inflammatory processes to various vestibular conditions, including vestibular migraine, benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, Meniere’s disease, vestibular schwannoma, and persistent postural perceptual dizziness.810242526 This connection is further exemplified by vestibular neuritis, a disorder affecting the vestibulocochlear nerve of the inner ear, a structure crucial for transmitting balance and head-position information to the brain. When inflammation affects this nerve, it disrupts normal signal transmission, causing symptoms such as vertigo, dizziness, and significant balance impairment.9

The relationship between inflammation and muscular degradation is well established in the literature.27 Research has demonstrated that CNS inflammation can trigger muscle catabolism. Specifically, CNS-delimited IL-1β signaling rapidly induces muscle atrophy.6 These findings challenge earlier assumptions that inflammatory cytokines must act directly on muscle tissue to cause degradation. The underlying mechanisms involve activation of the HPA axis. CNS inflammation activates this neuroendocrine pathway, releasing glucocorticoids that promote muscle wasting. This pathway is not merely contributory but essential for inflammation-induced muscle catabolism, as demonstrated by studies showing that adrenalectomy blocks muscle atrophy responses to systemic inflammation.6 This research reveals a critical neural-immune-endocrine interaction, whereby brain inflammation drives peripheral muscle degradation, compromising physical function and stability. The resulting muscle weakness significantly contributes to impaired mobility and increased fall risk.

Interestingly, we observed that more pro-inflammatory dietary patterns were specifically associated with chronic dizziness with imbalance, but not with isolated chronic dizziness. This null finding underscores the likelihood that inflammatory mechanisms predominantly affect vestibular or postural control mechanisms rather than subjective dizziness alone. Chronic dizziness with imbalance often indicates more advanced or multisystem involvement, including vestibular dysfunction, sarcopenia, and impaired sensory integration, all potentially more vulnerable to systemic inflammation.31628 Indeed, emerging literature identifies chronic low-grade inflammation as a key contributor not only to vestibular deficits but also to muscular degradation and CNS processing deficits, collectively exacerbating balance impairment and fall risk.

Notably, high DII scores were significantly associated with chronic imbalance, particularly among individuals without hearing loss. Auditory inputs significantly influence balance by facilitating multisensory integration and sensorimotor reweighting.29 Individuals without hearing impairment may rely more heavily on vestibular and proprioceptive signals, making them more vulnerable to inflammatory disruptions within these sensory systems. Consequently, dietary inflammation might selectively exacerbate imbalance symptoms in individuals dependent on these intact sensory modalities. From a statistical perspective, the absence of a significant association among participants with hearing loss may also be attributed to limited statistical power and subgroup heterogeneity. The proportion of participants reporting hearing loss was relatively small, comprising only 13.8% of the total sample. Within this subgroup, substantial variation likely exists in the severity and etiology of hearing loss and the use of hearing aids or cochlear implants. Such variations may dilute potential associations, reducing statistical power and hindering the detection of modest effects.3031

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study’s cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Although significant associations were observed between DII scores and chronic dizziness with imbalance, we cannot conclude causality. Longitudinal studies are necessary to confirm the directionality of these associations. Second, self-reported dizziness, imbalance, and dietary intake data may introduce recall and reporting biases, potentially affecting accuracy. Future studies incorporating objective measures of vestibular function and dietary intake could validate these findings further. Third, the DII calculation included only 28 components due to data availability, excluding 17 original components. Thus, our findings might differ if these omitted components were available, although many excluded components (e.g., thyme/oregano, rosemary, saffron) are rarely consumed in Korea. Additional studies incorporating a broader range of DII components would help clarify this issue. Fourth, the generalizability of this study is limited, as it focused exclusively on Korean adults aged 40 years and older. Fifth, we cannot completely rule out potential confounding from unknown or unmeasured factors, especially histories of other chronic diseases such as dementia, Parkinson’s disease, and vestibular disorders that may affect the observed associations. Sixth, inflammatory biomarkers such as CRP were not measured in this study; therefore, the DII should be interpreted as a proxy of dietary inflammatory potential rather than a direct measure of individual systemic inflammatory status. Lastly, although total energy intake was adjusted for in the analyses, this does not completely rule out the possibility that the observed differences may still be influenced by reduced caloric intake rather than fully reflecting a true shift in dietary inflammatory potential.

In conclusion, Korean middle-aged and older adults with more pro-inflammatory dietary patterns, as indicated by higher DII scores, exhibited greater odds of experiencing chronic dizziness with imbalance compared to those with anti-inflammatory dietary patterns. Although the cross-sectional nature limits causal interpretation, these findings suggest possible links between dietary inflammatory potential and vestibular-related symptoms. Longitudinal or prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings, clarify their biological plausibility, and strengthen the evidence base for dietary recommendations.
