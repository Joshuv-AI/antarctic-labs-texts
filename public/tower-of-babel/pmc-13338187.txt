Formal modeling and verification of GRANDPA finalization safety in polkadot
Authors: Muhammad Rashid, Nazir Ahmad Zafar, Lal khan, Mohammad Zubair Khan, Abdulrahman Alahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolkadot enhances its security and reliability through the GRANDPA(GHOST-based Recursive Ancestor Deriving Prefix Agreement) finalization protocol, which provides deterministic and irreversible block finalization via structured validator voting rounds. Finality is a fundamental component of consensus, as any failure in this process can compromise the integrity and operational continuity of the entire blockchain. Ensuring the safety and correctness of GRANDPA finalization is therefore crucial to prevent critical failures and maintain network trust. This work presents a formal verification study of the GRANDPA finalization mechanism using model checking techniques. The protocol is first analyzed in detail and represented as structured algorithms encompassing authority updates, Pre-Vote, Pre-Commit, vote aggregation, and finalization phases. A comprehensive Promela model is developed to capture the complete behavior of validators and the interactions across voting phases. Key safety properties, including no conflicting finalization and finality irreversibility, are formalized using Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) and systematically verified using the SPIN model checker. During verification, multiple performance and scalability metrics are analyzed, including total states visited, transitions explored, verification time, memory usage, search depth, and property-specific verification complexity. The analysis identifies properties that significantly influence state-space growth, providing insight into the structural complexity and computational demands of the protocol. This study demonstrates the effectiveness of formal model checking for evaluating blockchain consensus protocols and establishes a foundation for future verification efforts, including liveness properties, extended adversarial scenarios, and verification of more complex consensus configurations within the Polkadot ecosystem.Keywords: Blockchain, Polkadot, GRANDPA, Model checking, SPINSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The accelerating expansion of cryptocurrencies has generated sustained interest among both practitioners and researchers. This growth is primarily driven by blockchain technology, which provides decentralized, tamper resistant, and transparent digital infrastructures1. First introduced by Satoshi Nakamoto in 20082, blockchain established the cryptographic and mathematical foundations that enabled the Bitcoin system3. Since its inception, blockchain has progressed far beyond its initial use in digital currency and has been adopted across diverse sectors, including supply chain protection4, identity management frameworks5, healthcare information systems6, and electronic voting mechanisms7. Although blockchain technology supports a broad spectrum of applications, one of its most influential contributions remains the strengthening of financial transparency through cryptocurrencies. Well known platforms such as Bitcoin8, Ethereum9, Ripple10, and Litecoin11 illustrate the practical realization of blockchain based systems and highlight the operational viability of decentralized financial infrastructures. Polkadot represents a next generation multi chain framework designed to provide unified security across multiple interconnected blockchains. Introduced by Gavin Wood in 201612, Polkadot proposes a central relay chain responsible for coordinating a set of heterogeneous and autonomous blockchains referred to as parachains. The relay chain enforces shared security guarantees while enabling interoperable and trustless communication among these parachains13. Each parachain may be optimized for specialized use cases such as decentralized finance, digital identity, supply chain tracking, or Internet of Things applications14, while inheriting the overall security assurances of the network15. This composable architecture enhances scalability, flexibility, and cross chain communication efficiency. Consequently, Polkadot is increasingly regarded as a foundational infrastructure for scalable decentralized ecosystems16. To sustain both network liveness and secure finality, Polkadot employs two complementary consensus components: BABE12 for block production and GRANDPA17 for block finalization. Within this architectural design, GRANDPA plays a critical role in ensuring the safe and irreversible finalization of blocks18. The protocol organizes consensus into iterative voting rounds in which validators exchange prevote and precommit messages to determine which blocks should be finalized. Through this structured voting process, the system guarantees agreement, consistency, and protection against divergent chain histories17. Rooted in Byzantine fault tolerant principles and adapted to Polkadot’s heterogeneous multi chain environment12, GRANDPA achieves deterministic finality by repeatedly executing coordinated voting rounds. Validators independently issue votes, and once a supermajority threshold is satisfied, the corresponding blocks are finalized and propagated throughout the network19. When conflicting chains emerge, GRANDPA resolves forks by selecting the highest block supported by a supermajority, thereby establishing a single canonical chain and preserving global consistency20. Ensuring the safety of this finalization mechanism is essential, as incorrect or inconsistent finality decisions could compromise the integrity of the entire ecosystem.

Polkadot has grown substantially, with approximately 800 million USD currently staked within its network. The magnitude of economic value secured by the protocol highlights the importance of strong correctness guarantees and rigorous safety validation. Safeguarding participant assets requires more than empirical testing or simulation; it necessitates mathematically grounded assurance techniques capable of detecting subtle logical errors prior to deployment. Formal verification provides such guarantees by enabling systematic validation of protocol behavior and demonstrating the absence of fundamental design flaws. In particular, formal methods are well suited to verifying safety properties, such as the impossibility of conflicting finalizations or irreversible state violations, which are central to maintaining trust in consensus operations. By applying formal reasoning to the blockchain state machine, inconsistencies can be identified and eliminated, thereby reinforcing long term network reliability. Existing research on Polkadot has largely emphasized architectural exploration and empirical analysis rather than rigorous formal safety validation. For instance,21 examined message exchange mechanisms to enhance interoperability between components. While valuable for testing improvements, that work did not provide formal correctness proofs and primarily focused on application level interactions. Similarly,15 offered a descriptive assessment of Polkadot’s design and scalability features, concentrating on structural and communication aspects without performing formal safety verification. In addition,13 presented a detailed account of the protocol’s cryptographic and architectural foundations. Although theoretically informative, it did not extend to exhaustive validation or mathematically rigorous proof of consensus safety. Collectively, these contributions reveal a significant research gap: the lack of comprehensive formal safety verification applied directly to Polkadot’s GRANDPA finalization protocol.

In this work, we address this gap by conducting a formal safety verification of the GRANDPA finalization mechanism. The essential operations of GRANDPA, including prevote and precommit voting phases and supermajority driven block finalization, are formally modeled using the Promela specification language. Core safety properties are identified and expressed as Linear Temporal Logic LTL formulas to precisely capture correctness requirements such as finality irreversibility and consistency across voting rounds. The formal model and corresponding specifications are verified using the SPIN model checker, enabling exhaustive exploration of all reachable execution paths. Through model checking, we systematically evaluate whether conflicting finalizations, safety violations, or inconsistent state transitions can occur under any possible scenario. This automated and comprehensive approach provides strong assurance that the GRANDPA protocol preserves safety under the modeled assumptions.

Model checking is a formal verification technique specifically designed for finite state reactive systems, including communication protocols and hardware controllers. In this methodology, system behavior is represented as a state transition structure, while desired correctness properties are specified using temporal logic22. In recent blockchain research, model checking has emerged as a valuable tool for analyzing consensus mechanisms under adversarial or fault prone conditions23. Protocols such as Proof of Stake, BABE, and GRANDPA govern fundamental operations including block production, validator coordination, and block finalization. Even minor logical errors within these mechanisms can lead to forks, inconsistent finality, or severe financial consequences. By exhaustively exploring all reachable states, model checking enables precise verification of safety guarantees and detection of subtle design vulnerabilities. Therefore, formal verification complements conventional testing by offering stronger correctness assurances and increasing confidence in blockchain systems responsible for protecting assets of substantial economic value.

Formal analysis of distributed ledger consensus protocols

This section provides an extensive overview of prior studies addressing the formal verification of blockchain consensus protocols, together with research related to Polkadot and other comparable distributed systems. Formal verification methodologies have increasingly been adopted to rigorously establish correctness guarantees of consensus algorithms and to validate their safety, robustness, and operational reliability under adversarial and partially synchronous network conditions. A significant body of research applies deductive verification and theorem proving to blockchain consensus mechanisms. For example, the Paxos protocol is formally specified and verified using Temporal Logic of Actions in24, where correctness is demonstrated through mathematically precise invariants and behavioral specifications. In a similar vein, the Algorand protocol undergoes formal verification in25, where the Coq theorem prover is used to analyze global states and confirm essential safety guarantees. The Nakamoto-inspired Toychain protocol is verified in26, also using Coq, to prove properties including safety and ledger consistency. Furthermore, theorem proving techniques are applied in27 to verify liveness and persistence in a blockchain-based crowdsourcing protocol, ensuring protocol correctness through structured formal reasoning. Beyond theorem proving, model checking has emerged as a dominant verification technique for distributed consensus systems. In28, a Proof-of-Stake protocol is modeled in CSP# and analyzed with the Process Analysis Toolkit, enabling detailed structural verification of safety properties. The Federated Byzantine Agreement algorithm is abstracted and verified using the UPPAAL model checker in29, focusing on correctness and system resilience. Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance is examined through probabilistic model checking using PRISM in30, where reliability is evaluated within healthcare system deployments. Another Byzantine Fault Tolerance protocol is analyzed using the ByMC model checker in31, emphasizing formal guarantees of protocol dependability. Additional investigations examine specific consensus frameworks. Tendermint is formally specified in TLA+ in32, offering a structured representation of correctness properties. The Raft consensus protocol is verified using Coq in33, concentrating on safety invariants and system consistency. The Stellar Consensus Protocol is modeled and verified in34 using Ivy and Isabelle HOL, where safety and liveness are established under eventual synchrony assumptions, although verification remains limited to certain quorum configurations. The Red Belly Blockchain protocol is translated into Threshold Automata and verified through LTL properties using the ByMC tool in35, illustrating systematic model-based safety validation. Emerging blockchain architectures have also been subject to formal study. Multiple DAG-based consensus protocols, including DAG Rider, Cordial Miners, HashGraph, and Bullshark, are analyzed in36 using TLA+ and TLAPS, primarily addressing safety properties. The LiDO DAG framework introduced in37 employs Coq to verify Narwhal and Sailfish while addressing leader bottleneck challenges in Byzantine settings. The Moonshot protocol is formally verified using the Ivy framework in38, ensuring fork prevention and consistent global state evolution. The Common Knowledge Base protocol is modeled using timed automata in39, where selfish mining resistance and other security aspects are verified via UPPAAL. Broader distributed ledger verification efforts are also evident. A general distributed ledger framework is formally analyzed in40, and insights from this modeling approach are later applied to formalize HotStuff in TLA+. In41, a customizable consensus mechanism is verified against Byzantine attacks using SMT-based reasoning combined with incremental refinement techniques. Probabilistic and statistical verification techniques are explored in42, evaluating resilience against DNS-based and double-spending attacks. Similarly,43 applies probabilistic Linear Temporal Logic to a discrete-time Markov chain representation of a Hyperledger multi-party protocol. Formal methods have also been extended beyond core consensus algorithms. In44 and45, blockchain-based crowdsourcing platforms are modeled in CSP# and verified using PAT to confirm fraud resistance and operational safety. Consensus systems such as Hyperledger Sawtooth and parallel PBFT variants are analyzed through automated model checking in46,47, validating fault tolerance and correctness guarantees. Although these studies demonstrate significant advancements in formal verification of blockchain consensus, limited attention has been devoted to rigorous safety analysis of Polkadot’s native consensus components. Finality gadgets and hybrid consensus in modern blockchain systems

This subsection surveys research related to finality mechanisms, with particular emphasis on the GRANDPA finality gadget and hybrid consensus architectures combining block production and deterministic finalization layers. The initial introduction of GRANDPA is presented in18, where it is proposed as a modular finality layer capable of transforming probabilistic consensus into deterministic finality. The protocol finalizes the longest prefix endorsed by at least two-thirds of validators and allows multiple blocks to be finalized simultaneously, thereby improving efficiency compared to sequential block confirmation methods. This modular structure enables GRANDPA to operate independently of block production algorithms, increasing its adaptability within the Polkadot ecosystem. A more detailed theoretical exposition appears in48, where GRANDPA is characterized as a Byzantine Fault Tolerant protocol operating under partial synchrony. The study clarifies the impossibility of deterministic agreement in fully asynchronous networks and explains how GRANDPA separates safety from liveness. Block production mechanisms such as BABE provide optimistic progress, while GRANDPA ensures eventual deterministic finalization through structured validator voting rounds. This architectural separation underpins Polkadot’s scalability and resilience. Practical deployments of hybrid consensus systems incorporating GRANDPA are examined in49, where a Substrate-based automotive data monetization platform is implemented. The study highlights how finality layers enhance reliability and trust within consortium environments. Similarly,50 presents a hybrid automotive blockchain integrating BABE or AuRa for block production with GRANDPA for finalization. Experimental evaluation demonstrates stable fork resolution and efficient finalization across varying network scales. Applications in vehicular and data-sharing contexts further underscore the importance of hybrid consensus. The framework proposed in51 emphasizes secure data trading for connected vehicles, necessitating reliable consensus and deterministic finality. Likewise,52 discusses blockchain-based vehicular security mechanisms, where efficient finality is essential for maintaining consistency across distributed participants. Comparative analyses of consensus architectures provide additional context. The survey in53 reviews various blockchain consensus models and highlights hybrid designs that separate block generation from finalization, aligning closely with GRANDPA’s architectural philosophy. Theoretical perspectives on Byzantine Fault Tolerance are revisited in54, while adversarial capabilities in PBFT-like systems are analyzed in55, both offering insights relevant to GRANDPA’s voting and threshold assumptions. Finally, modular finality mechanisms in other ecosystems are examined in56, where Binance’s consensus architecture is evaluated with respect to safety and performance. Such studies illustrate the increasing adoption of layered finalization strategies in modern blockchain networks. Collectively, prior research confirms the growing significance of modular finality gadgets and hybrid consensus structures. Foundational works including18 and48 establish the conceptual and theoretical basis of GRANDPA, while subsequent investigations explore practical deployment scenarios and related Byzantine Fault Tolerant models. Nevertheless, comprehensive formal safety verification of GRANDPA’s internal voting rounds and finalization logic remains comparatively underexplored, thereby motivating the present study. Background

Polkadot is a next-generation blockchain platform designed to enable interoperability, scalability, and shared security among multiple blockchains, known as parachains12. Unlike traditional blockchains that operate as isolated networks, Polkadot provides a heterogeneous multi-chain framework, where distinct blockchains can communicate and exchange data seamlessly while maintaining their own governance and consensus mechanisms. The primary components of the Polkadot network include the Relay Chain, responsible for finality and shared security, and multiple parachains that perform specialized computations or support decentralized applications.

BABE: block production in polkadot

BABE (Blind Assignment for Blockchain Extension) is the slot-based block production protocol used in Polkadot to propose new blocks on the Relay Chain12. In BABE, time is divided into discrete intervals called slots, each of which may be assigned to one or more validators who are eligible to produce a block. Validators are pseudo-randomly assigned to slots based on a verifiable random function (VRF) output, ensuring fairness and unpredictability in block proposals. Each slot may contain at most one block produced by the assigned validator, though empty slots are possible if no validator is selected or fails to produce a block. BABE guarantees liveness by ensuring that, over time, blocks continue to be proposed and the chain extends consistently. Figure 3 demonstrate the the difference between BABE and GRANDPA. Epochs and slots

In BABE, time is further organized into epochs, each consisting of a fixed number of slots. An epoch represents a period during which the validator set remains constant and block production assignments are determined. At the end of each epoch, a new validator set may be elected or updated, and VRF assignments for slots are recalculated. Formally, if an epoch consists of S slots, each slot () is associated with a unique validator such that is eligible to produce a block in . Epochs allow the network to periodically update validator assignments, rebalance stake, and enhance security by preventing long-term manipulation by malicious actors. GRANDPA: finalization in polkadot

GRANDPA is the deterministic finality protocol used in Polkadot, designed to finalize blocks that are proposed by BABE. While BABE is responsible for producing blocks and extending the chain, GRANDPA ensures that a subset of blocks becomes irreversible, providing security guarantees to users and applications. Unlike traditional blockchains where finality is implied by the longest chain rule, GRANDPA allows the network to finalize large sets of blocks efficiently in a single round, even under asynchronous network conditions .

Voting mechanism

GRANDPA operates through repeated voting rounds among a set of validators. In each round, validators participate in two structured phases: Pre-Vote and Pre-Commit. During the Pre-Vote phase, validators select a candidate block based on the highest observed block height that is consistent with previously finalized blocks. The selection is performed independently by each validator according to its local view of the blockchain. In the Pre-Commit phase, validators confirm their previous vote by casting a Pre-Commit vote for the same candidate block. A block is finalized if it receives a supermajority of Pre-Commit votes (typically of total validators). This two-phase voting process guarantees both liveness and safety: no conflicting blocks can be finalized, and the chain continues to grow under honest validator participation.

Interaction between BABE and GRANDPA

BABE and GRANDPA are logically decoupled but operate concurrently. BABE continuously produces new blocks in each slot, enabling chain growth and supporting network throughput. GRANDPA, on the other hand, focuses solely on finality, coordinating validator votes to finalize blocks efficiently. This separation allows Polkadot to achieve high scalability: block production is not delayed by finalization, and finality is achieved deterministically without sacrificing network liveness. In practice, a block proposed by BABE becomes a candidate for finalization in GRANDPA only after it has been observed and agreed upon by validators during a voting round. This combination of probabilistic block production and deterministic finalization forms the foundation of Polkadot’s robust and secure consensus architecture.

Formal verification

Formal verification refers to the rigorous mathematical analysis of system models to ensure that specified correctness properties hold under all possible executions. Unlike conventional testing approaches, which examine a limited subset of behaviors through simulation or empirical experimentation, formal verification provides exhaustive guarantees by systematically analyzing the entire state space of a system. This makes it particularly suitable for distributed and safety-critical systems, where subtle concurrency errors, race conditions, or adversarial behaviors may remain undetected through traditional validation techniques. Blockchain consensus protocols, including hybrid architectures that separate block production from finalization, operate in partially synchronous and adversarial environments. Such systems must guarantee safety (no conflicting finalization), liveness (eventual progress), and fault tolerance under Byzantine assumptions. Given the complexity of validator interactions, voting rounds, threshold conditions, and fork resolution strategies, informal reasoning is insufficient to ensure correctness. Formal verification therefore plays a central role in providing mathematically sound assurances of reliability and consistency. In the context of consensus verification, formal methods typically involve constructing an abstract yet faithful model of the protocol, specifying desired properties using temporal logic, and applying automated reasoning tools to check whether those properties hold. If a property is violated, the verification tool produces a counterexample trace, which can be analyzed to identify design flaws or modeling inconsistencies. This counterexample-guided refinement process enables systematic strengthening of protocol correctness. Among the available formal techniques, model checking has emerged as one of the most effective approaches for verifying distributed and concurrent systems. The following subsections introduce the theoretical and practical foundations underlying model checking, temporal logic specification, safety properties, and the SPIN verification environment used in this study.

Model checking

Model checking is an automated formal verification technique based on systematic state space exploration. Given a finite-state model of a system and a temporal logic specification, the model checker exhaustively explores all reachable states to determine whether the specification holds. If the property is satisfied, the verification concludes with mathematical certainty within the explored model. If violated, a counterexample execution trace is generated. The core principle of model checking involves representing the system as a transition system , where S is the set of states, the set of initial states, and the transition relation. Verification consists of checking whether all possible execution paths originating from satisfy a given logical formula. This exhaustive exploration distinguishes model checking from testing-based techniques. In distributed protocols, concurrency introduces nondeterminism due to interleavings of process actions. Model checking systematically enumerates these interleavings, ensuring that no potential execution ordering is overlooked. This is particularly important in blockchain consensus mechanisms, where validator votes, message delays, and fork choices may interleave in complex ways. However, model checking faces the well-known state explosion problem. As the number of processes or system variables increases, the state space grows exponentially. Various reduction techniques such as partial order reduction, symmetry reduction, and bit-state hashing have been developed to mitigate this challenge. In the context of validator-based consensus systems, scalability analysis is essential to evaluate how verification complexity evolves as the validator set increases. The applicability of model checking to consensus protocols stems from its ability to verify both safety and liveness properties under adversarial scheduling assumptions. It enables formal reasoning about threshold voting, fork resolution, and deterministic finalization logic without relying solely on probabilistic testing. Formally, a safety property can be characterized as a prefix-closed property: if a violation occurs, it is detectable within a finite execution prefix. This makes safety properties particularly amenable to model checking, as counterexamples are finite and diagnostic. In deterministic finality gadgets, safety is paramount. Once a block is declared finalized, no conflicting alternative should ever be finalized. Violating this condition compromises system consistency and undermines trust in the ledger. Therefore, verifying safety invariants across all possible validator behaviors is essential. Safety verification typically involves invariant checking. An invariant is a condition that holds in all reachable states. For example, in a voting-based finalization mechanism, an invariant may require that at most one block per height can satisfy the supermajority threshold. Model checking ensures that this invariant is preserved under all possible message orderings and validator behaviors within the modeled assumptions. Safety properties differ from liveness properties in that they do not require reasoning about eventual progress; instead, they prevent undesirable states. In hybrid consensus systems, safety is often decoupled from liveness, allowing deterministic guarantees even when network delays temporarily prevent progress. SPIN model checker

SPIN is an explicit-state model checker designed for the formal verification of distributed and concurrent systems. It uses the Promela (Process Meta Language) modeling language to describe system behavior. Promela supports constructs for process creation, message passing, nondeterministic choices, and synchronization, making it well-suited for modeling consensus protocols. In SPIN-based verification, the system model is written in Promela, and LTL properties are specified either directly or through never claims generated from temporal logic formulas. SPIN translates the model into C code and performs exhaustive state exploration, applying reduction techniques such as partial order reduction to minimize redundant interleavings. SPIN supports both safety and liveness verification. Safety properties can be checked through assertions and invariants embedded within the model. Liveness properties are verified using Büchi automata constructed from LTL formulas. If a property is violated, SPIN produces a counterexample trace that can be replayed for debugging and analysis. A key advantage of SPIN is its ability to provide detailed statistics regarding state exploration, including the number of states visited, transitions executed, memory consumption, and maximum search depth. These metrics are particularly valuable when analyzing scalability in validator-based consensus systems. In the verification of voting-based finality mechanisms, each validator can be modeled as an independent Promela process. Communication channels represent message exchanges, while shared variables encode voting thresholds and finalized states. The nondeterministic scheduling of processes captures asynchronous interleavings and potential adversarial behaviors within the modeled assumptions. SPIN’s reduction techniques, including bit-state hashing and compression methods, allow verification of relatively large state spaces compared to naive exploration approaches. Although the state explosion problem cannot be entirely eliminated, careful abstraction and parameterization enable scalable analysis of validator sets under bounded configurations.

GRANDPA finalization

The finalization mechanism constitutes the central component of the GRANDPA protocol, delivering a secure and deterministic procedure for confirming blocks irreversibly. Within the Polkadot network, each block is uniquely characterized by its height , indicating its position in the blockchain according to the order of production and the adopted fork-choice rule. The fork-choice rule identifies the canonical branch among competing forks, ensuring that validators maintain a consistent and unified view of the ledger structure. Block production is performed concurrently by the BABE consensus protocol, which is responsible for proposing new blocks and enabling continuous chain growth. In contrast, GRANDPA is exclusively dedicated to the finalization process by coordinating validator votes over candidate chains to establish irreversible agreement. A block becomes finalized once it secures a supermajority threshold–typically at least of the total voting power. Although BABE and GRANDPA function simultaneously, their responsibilities remain logically separated: BABE governs block generation, while GRANDPA guarantees deterministic and permanent finality. This decoupled design enhances scalability, improves operational efficiency, and reinforces the overall reliability and robustness of the network.

GRANDPA achieves deterministic finality through an iterative round-based voting framework. Each round is indexed by and consists of two sequential stages: the Initial Vote stage and the Confirmation Vote stage. These stages collectively drive the system toward irreversible agreement on a specific chain prefix while preserving safety guarantees. Byzantine fault modeling implementation

In this work, Byzantine behavior is explicitly modeled by designating a subset of validators, bounded by , as faulty using a dedicated indicator array. These faulty (Byzantine) validators are allowed to exhibit fully non-deterministic behavior, meaning they can arbitrarily select blocks during the Prevote phase and may change their decisions during the Precommit phase. In particular, the model captures equivocation, where a Byzantine validator can cast conflicting votes across different stages by assigning inconsistent values to its recorded vote and local decision variables. In contrast, honest validators strictly follow the protocol and are constrained to maintain consistency between Prevote and Precommit. This separation enables the model checker to explore adversarial executions in which Byzantine nodes attempt to violate protocol correctness. The model assumes a standard Byzantine fault tolerance threshold (e.g., for , up to faulty validators), ensuring that safety properties are verified under realistic adversarial conditions consistent with GRANDPA’s design assumptions.

This LTL formula, called vote_integrity, ensures that validators do not change their vote between the prevote and precommit phases. The [] operator means that this condition must always hold throughout execution. The arrays prevote_choice and precommit_choice represent the decisions of validators in each phase. The formula checks that if a validator precommits to a block, then it must have previously prevoted for the same block. In simple terms, it enforces consistency in voting behavior and prevents equivocation across phases.

Single vote

This LTL formula, called single_vote, ensures that each validator casts only one vote per round. The [] operator enforces that this rule always holds. The formula prevents a validator from simultaneously voting for two different blocks in the same phase. In simple terms, it guarantees that a validator cannot double vote, preserving the integrity of the consensus process.

Detailed interpretation of LTL safety properties

To improve clarity and explicitly connect formal specifications with protocol behavior, each LTL safety property is further elaborated by linking it to its corresponding GRANDPA mechanism, design rationale, and safety guarantee.

1. No Conflicting Finalization (Agreement Property)

LTL Formula:

Protocol Mechanism: This property corresponds to the agreement guarantee enforced by the GRANDPA finalization protocol. It relies on the requirement that finalization can only occur when a supermajority of validators precommit to the same block.

Rationale: Since GRANDPA operates in a Byzantine fault-tolerant setting, at least validators must agree. Due to overlapping quorums, two different blocks cannot both obtain such a supermajority unless validators equivocate.

Safety Guarantee: This property ensures that no two conflicting blocks can be finalized simultaneously, thereby preserving blockchain consistency.

2. Precommit Forcing (Voting Consistency)

LTL Formula:

Protocol Mechanism: This captures the vote consistency rule between the prevote and precommit phases. Validators must only precommit to blocks they have previously prevoted.

Rationale: The two-phase voting structure of GRANDPA ensures that validators first express tentative support (prevote) and then confirm it (precommit). This prevents arbitrary switching between blocks.

Safety Guarantee: By enforcing monotonic voting behavior, this property prevents equivocation and ensures that voting decisions remain consistent across protocol phases.

3. Threshold Enforcement (Supermajority Requirement)

LTL Formula:

Protocol Mechanism: This property models the supermajority voting threshold required for finalization.

Rationale: The threshold ensures Byzantine fault tolerance by requiring agreement from a sufficient number of validators. This prevents a small subset of faulty nodes from finalizing blocks.

Safety Guarantee: It guarantees that only blocks with sufficient consensus support can be finalized, preventing premature or invalid finalization.

4. Finality Irreversibility (Persistence of Decision)

LTL Formula:

Protocol Mechanism: This reflects the immutability of finalized blocks in the GRANDPA protocol.

Rationale: Once a block is finalized, it must remain finalized in all future states to ensure ledger stability and prevent forks.

Safety Guarantee: This property ensures that finalization is permanent and cannot be revoked, maintaining long-term consistency.

5. Counter Reset Correctness (Round Integrity)

LTL Formula:

Protocol Mechanism: This corresponds to the round transition logic, where vote counters must reset when a new round begins.

Rationale: Without resetting counters, votes from previous rounds could incorrectly influence the current round, leading to incorrect finalization.

Safety Guarantee: This property ensures isolation between rounds, preventing stale or residual votes from violating protocol correctness.

The above mappings demonstrate that each LTL property is directly derived from a specific protocol rule within GRANDPA. Together, these properties collectively enforce agreement, consistency, threshold correctness, and finality persistence. This explicit correspondence between formal specifications and protocol behavior strengthens the validity and interpretability of the verification results.

Generality and scalability of LTL specifications

It is important to note that the LTL formulas presented above are written in an explicit, index-based form for a fixed number of validators and blocks (e.g., 7 validators and 4 candidate blocks) to improve readability and clarity of exposition. However, these specifications are not inherently limited to a fixed system size. In the proposed framework, all LTL properties are defined at an abstract level using indexed variables over the validator set and candidate block set . The concrete Promela models used in SPIN verification are generated programmatically, where the number of validators and blocks are parameterized. Accordingly, the corresponding LTL formulas are automatically instantiated by expanding quantified expressions (e.g., ) into conjunctions over the chosen system size.

For example, the Pre-Commit consistency property:

is automatically translated into a conjunction over all validator indices based on the configured validator count. This allows seamless scaling from small configurations (e.g., 7 validators) to larger validator sets (e.g., up to 20 validators as evaluated in this work).Similarly, threshold-based and safety properties are parameterized with respect to the number of validators and the supermajority requirement, ensuring that the correctness guarantees remain valid under different system configurations. Thus, while the LTL formulas in this section appear syntactically hard-coded, they are in practice generated systematically and support scalable verification across varying network sizes.

Why safety over liveness

In this work, our primary focus is on safety properties, specifically finalization safety and irreversibility, within the GRANDPA finalization protocol. This focus is intentional and motivated by the critical role safety plays in blockchain system, a violation of safety can lead to irreversible inconsistencies and compromise the integrity of the entire network. In contrast, liveness violations typically result in temporary halting of progress rather than permanent inconsistency. We acknowledge that safety can be trivially satisfied if the protocol ceases to make progress. However, GRANDPA is designed as a partially synchronous protocol with explicit assumptions about validator participation and network conditions, under which liveness is expected to hold. Modeling and verifying liveness properties require incorporating additional assumptions such as fairness constraints, network synchrony, and adversarial scheduling, which significantly increase model complexity and state-space size in model checking.Therefore, liveness verification is considered outside the scope of this paper and is explicitly identified as part of our future work.

Counterexample and error trail analysis

Although no safety violations were observed under the original model assumptions, we further analyzed the robustness of the protocol by introducing a controlled fault scenario allowing Byzantine equivocation during the precommit phase. Under this relaxed condition, the SPIN model checker generated an error trail demonstrating a violation of the safety property. The following execution trace illustrates the sequence of events leading to conflicting finalization:

The error trail clearly demonstrates that a Byzantine validator can equivocate by issuing conflicting precommit votes, thereby contributing to multiple branches simultaneously. As a result, two conflicting blocks (B1 and B2) both satisfy the finalization threshold.This violates the core safety property of GRANDPA, which requires that at most one block can be finalized. The counterexample confirms that the enforcement of vote consistency between prevote and precommit phases is essential to prevent conflicting finalization.It is important to note that no such counterexample was found in the original model, as strict constraints prevent equivocation. Therefore, the observed violation arises only under adversarial conditions, further validating the correctness of the protocol under honest assumptions.

Conclusion

A comprehensive formal verification of the GRANDPA finalization mechanism has been presented in this work using an automated model checking approach. The study commenced with a structured analysis of the GRANDPA voting and finalization workflow to ensure a precise understanding of its operational semantics and validator interactions. Based on this analysis, a formal system model was constructed in the Promela language, accurately capturing the essential phases of the protocol, including Pre-Vote, Pre-Commit, threshold validation, and finalization decision logic. The verification phase focused primarily on evaluating critical safety guarantees of the protocol. Fundamental safety properties, including absence of conflicting finalization, enforcement of voting thresholds, precommit forcing correctness, counter reset behavior, and finality irreversibility, were formally identified and expressed using LTL. These LTL specifications, together with the formal Promela model, were provided as inputs to the SPIN model checker to enable exhaustive state-space exploration and automated correctness verification. The verification results confirm that all specified safety properties hold under the explored validator configurations. Detailed performance metrics, including states visited, transitions explored, verification time, memory usage, and search depth, were systematically analyzed to assess scalability and computational behavior as the number of validators increased. The empirical findings demonstrate predictable state-space growth and controlled resource consumption, while also highlighting properties that induce comparatively higher complexity due to stronger synchronization constraints. This study demonstrates the effectiveness of formal model checking techniques in validating consensus finalization mechanisms within blockchain systems. The results strengthen confidence in the correctness and robustness of the GRANDPA protocol under bounded exhaustive analysis and provide a structured methodological framework for analyzing similar voting-based consensus mechanisms. While the present work primarily concentrates on safety verification and scalability evaluation, future research directions may include extended adversarial modeling, probabilistic verification techniques, safety integrated analysis, and verification under larger validator configurations. Such extensions would further enhance the formal assurance of correctness, reliability, and resilience in decentralized consensus protocols deployed within the Polkadot ecosystem.

Limitations and future work

This study performs bounded verification of the GRANDPA finalization mechanism using a Promela model and a limited number of validator configurations due to state-space explosion constraints inherent in model checking. While the model captures the essential voting and finalization semantics, certain real-world factors such as network delays, adversarial behaviors, and probabilistic message ordering were abstracted. Additionally, liveness properties were not exhaustively analyzed. These limitations indicate that the results provide strong safety assurance within the explored bounds but do not represent full-scale deployment conditions. In Future Liveness with extended parameters can be verified using model checking techniques to ensure consistency and progression of GRANDPA, and also the number of rounds and validators can be increased by increasing the hardware resources.
