Dual-Site Chemically Engineered MNAzymes for One-Pot Isothermal Detection of MicroRNAs
Authors: Yuying Huang, Can Jia, Yajuan Tang, Jun Zhang, Ruolian Zhao, Jihong Zhang, Lin Chai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sensitive detection of microRNAs (miRNAs) remains challenging due to their low abundance and sequence similarity. Conventional DNAzyme- and multicomponent nucleic acid enzyme (MNAzyme)-based assays are enzyme-free but often limited by poor catalytic turnover. Here, we present a dual-site chemically engineered MNAzyme platform that integrates xeno-nucleic acid modification within the catalytic core and locked nucleic acid substitution within the target-binding arms to synergistically enhance catalytic and recognition efficiency. The resulting hybrid, termed LXMz, exhibits an ∼760-fold improvement in catalytic rate and an ∼450-fold increase in sensitivity relative to the unmodified MNAzyme. Under isothermal, one-pot conditions, LXMz achieves the quantitative detection of miR-92a with a limit of detection of 2 pM while maintaining single-base specificity. By combining catalytic enhancement and hybridization stabilization within a unified molecular design, this study establishes a new chemical engineering principle for constructing robust, enzyme-free biosensors. The LXMz framework provides a versatile and sensitive platform for miRNA analysis and holds promise for liquid biopsy and point-of-care diagnostics.

Introduction

Circulating nucleic acids in biological fluids have emerged as valuable liquid biopsy biomarkers for diverse applications, including early cancer diagnosis and infectious disease surveillance. , Among them, miRNAs are particularly attractive due to their regulatory roles in gene expression and strong links to tumor progression. Consequently, the accurate and rapid identification of miRNAs holds significant promise for diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring. However, translation into clinical practice remains difficult because of their dynamic expression, molecular heterogeneity, and extremely low abundance in biological samples. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop ultrasensitive, specific, and user-friendly bioassays.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) remains the benchmark for nucleic acid detection due to its high sensitivity and specificity. Nevertheless, its dependence on thermal cycling, complex workflows, and stringent reaction conditions constrains its use in point-of-care testing and resource-limited settings. As alternatives, a variety of isothermal amplification methods, such as rolling circle amplification (RCA), strand displacement amplification (SDA), loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), and recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA), have been developed, enabling signal amplification under constant temperature. While these approaches achieve excellent sensitivity, their reliance on costly and delicate enzymes, as well as risks of nonspecific amplification, significantly restricts their clinical applicability. In this context, enzyme-free isothermal detection strategies that bypass target amplification and allow a one-pot, one-step operation represent a particularly promising direction for advancing molecular diagnostics.

DNAzymes, a class of single-stranded DNA molecules capable of performing catalytic reactions such as site-specific RNA cleavage, have emerged as powerful tools in biosensing. − A typical RNA-cleaving DNAzyme consists of a catalytic core flanked by two substrate-binding arms, enabling sequence-specific cleavage via Watson–Crick base pairing. DNAzymes are programmable, structurally versatile, and readily synthesized, and they have been widely engineered as allosteric biosensors by coupling their activity to modulators such as small molecules, − proteins, , or nucleic acids. − The multicomponent nucleic acid enzyme (MNAzyme) represents a particularly attractive architecture, in which separate oligonucleotide fragments assemble into an active catalytic complex only in the presence of a specific target sequence. , This modular design confers high programmability and specificity, and MNAzymes have been successfully applied in detecting diverse biomolecules, including proteins and nucleic acids. , Despite these advantages, conventional MNAzymes suffer from intrinsic limitations, including impaired catalytic efficiency, susceptibility to signal leakage, and instability in complex biological matrices. Moreover, many existing MNAzyme-based biosensors rely on multiple steps or auxiliary enzymes, hindering their integration into simple one-pot assays. Chemical modifications such as xeno-nucleic acids (XNAs), locked nucleic acids (LNAs), and phosphorothioate have separately been shown to enhance DNAzyme activity or target affinity, − yet their integrated and rational coengineering within a single MNAzyme framework has not been systematically explored.

Herein, we report a dual-engineered MNAzyme that strategically integrates XNA modification within the catalytic core and LNA substitution within the target-binding arms to improve the catalytic kinetics and hybridization fidelity. This chemically engineered hybrid, termed LXMz, achieves a synergistic enhancement, over 760-fold higher activity and ∼450-fold greater sensitivity than the unmodified counterpart, while maintaining a one-step, isothermal operation. Unlike prior studies that focused on either catalytic-core optimization or binding-arm modulation, this work establishes a unified chemical engineering principle for MNAzymes, enabling enzyme-free biosensing of miRNA with unprecedented efficiency and simplicity.

Experimental Section

Reagents and DNA Oligonucleotides

All oligonucleotides (HPLC-purified) were purchased from Sangon Biotech and used without further purification. The sequences are provided in Supporting Information Table S1. Manganese­(II) chloride hexahydrate and HEPES were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Sodium chloride and sodium hydroxide were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm) was prepared using a Milli-Q system (Millipore).

DNAzyme and MNAzyme Cleavage Assays

The substrate cleavage was monitored by Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) under multiple turnover conditions. DNAzyme reactions were conducted using 500 nM substrate and 5 nM DNAzyme. MNAzyme reactions were performed with 500 nM substrate, 50 nM MNAzyme, and 50 nM target miR-92a, unless otherwise specified. Reaction mixtures were preincubated at the indicated temperature in reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4). After 10 min, MgCl2 was added to a final concentration of 5 mM to initiate the reaction. Reactions were carried out in a black 96-well plate with a total volume of 200 μL. Fluorescence intensities were recorded with a SpectraMax iD5 plate reader (molecular devices) with an excitation at 490 nm and emission at 525 nm. The PMT gain was set at 500 V. To calculate the change in relative fluorescence units (ΔRFU), fluorescence signals from control reactions containing only the substrate and buffer were subtracted from experimental readings. The percentage of substrate cleavage (% cleavage) was calculated using the following equation: 1 %cleavage=(RFUt−RFU0)/(RFU∞−RFU0) where RFU t is the fluorescence intensity at reaction time t, RFU0 is the background signal from substrate-only controls, and RFU∞ is the fluorescence intensity at complete reaction.

The observed rate constant (k obs) was obtained by fitting experimental data to the exponential equation: 2 RFUt=RFU0+(RFU∞−RFU0)(1−e−kobst)

Analytical Performance Assessment

The sensitivity assays were conducted using 500 nM substrate, 50 nM MNAzyme, and varying concentrations of target miR-92a in reaction buffer at 30 °C. Specificity was evaluated using the LXMz platform (50 nM LXMz and 500 nM substrate). The assays were performed against 10 nM of various nontarget miRNAs, 1 nM single-nucleotide variants of miR-92a, or 1 nM miR-92a containing two, three, or four mismatches. Following incubation for 90 min, fluorescence intensities were measured, as described above. Signals were normalized by setting the fluorescence response of 1 nM miR-92a to 1. The total RNA isolated from HeLa cells was added at a final concentration of 2 μg/mL, and then the LXMz assay was performed as described above.

Results and Discussion

Enhancement of DNAzyme Activity by XNA Modification

The 10–23 DNAzyme is a widely studied RNA-cleaving catalyst and serves as a benchmark in DNAzyme research (Figure a). Prior work has shown that chemical modification of its catalytic core can produce DNAzymes with substantially improved efficiency. − However, these studies were largely restricted to RNA substrates, and the influence of such modifications on DNA substrate cleavage has remained unclear.

Catalytic performance of chemically modified DNAzymes. (a) Sequence of 10–23 DNAzyme and chemical structures of chemical modifications. PS: phosphorothioate; OMe: 2′-methyl; MOE: 2′-O-methoxyethyl. (b) Nomenclature of the unmodified DNAzyme (Wt) and six chemically modified variants (V1–V6). (c) Representative kinetic curves of Wt and V1–V6. (d) Observed reaction rates (k obs) of Wt and V1–V6.

To address this, we designed six chemically modified variants of 10–23 DNAzyme (designated V1–V6) (Figure b) and evaluated their performance against DNA substrate under multiple-turnover conditions. All variants exhibited activity significantly higher than that of the wild-type DNAzyme (Figure c). The observed rate constant (k obs) for the wild-type was 0.029 ± 0.002 min–1, whereas V1–V6 displayed values between 0.19 and 0.39 min–1, representing 6.5–13.5-fold enhancement (Figure d). Magnesium ion titration confirmed enhanced activity across 1–25 mM Mg2+, with V6 consistently outperforming the wild type (Figure S1), underscoring the robustness conferred by the chemical modification strategy. These results demonstrate that XNA modifications markedly enhance DNAzyme catalysis, extending activity from RNA to DNA substrates.

Feasibility of XMz Systems

We next constructed six XNA-modified MNAzyme variants (XMz-1 to XMz-6) derived from the corresponding XNAzymes. MiR-92a, a clinically important biomarker frequently upregulated in multiple cancers, was selected as the model target (Figure a). , At a concentration of 50 nM miR-92a, all six XMzs exhibited enhanced cleavage activity relative to that of the unmodified MNAzyme (Mz) (Figure b), indicating that segmentation of the catalytic core does not diminish the activity gains conferred by XNA modifications. Interestingly, the activity ranking of XMzs differed from that of their parental DNAzymes. While DNAzymes V4–V6 were more active than V1–V3, the opposite trend was observed in the MNAzyme series: XMz-4 to XMz-6 displayed activity lower than that of XMz-1 to XMz-3 (Figure b), implying that OMe modification at position C7 may impair MNAzyme function. Comparative analysis of signal-to-background ratios (SBRs) revealed that XMz-1 combined high catalytic reactivity with minimal background fluorescence, resulting in the most favorable performance (Figure b). Its SBR was ∼2.7-fold greater than that of unmodified Mz. These findings confirm that XNA-modified MNAzymes offer superior performance compared to unmodified systems.

Evaluation of XNA-modified MNAzymes (XMzs). (a) Schematic construction of MNAzyme. (b) Fluorescence signals, background responses, and corresponding signal-to-background ratios (SBRs) for the unmodified Mz and six XMz variants. Reactions were performed with 50 nM miR-92a, 50 nM MNAzyme, and 500 nM substrate at 30 °C for 30 min. Data represent the mean ± SD from three technical replicates. Statistical significance was assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test: **P < 0.01; ****P < 0.0001.

Synergistic Chemical Engineering of LXMz

To further augment the catalytic efficiency of XMz-1, we introduced a second, complementary modification by incorporating LNA modification into the target-binding arms, generating an LXMz system (Figure a). LNA substitutions rigidify the sugar–phosphate backbone, thereby enhancing hybridization affinity and accelerating target-induced complex assembly. ,, In contrast, the previously introduced XNA modification at the catalytic core primarily strengthens the intrinsic catalytic process by stabilizing the active conformation and improving the metal-ion coordination during phosphodiester cleavage. The combination of these two orthogonal enhancements was expected to synergistically elevate the overall MNAzyme performance.

Comparison of Mz, XMz, and locked nucleic acid-modified XMz (LXMz) platforms. (a) Schematic illustration of Mz, XMz, and LXMz. The blue dot indicates MOE modification at the G14 position within the catalytic core. Green dots denote LNA substitutions in the target-binding arms. (b) Representative kinetic curves of Mz, XMz, and LXMz. (c) Summary of k obs for the three platforms. Reactions were performed with 50 nM miR-92a, 50 nM MNAzyme, and 500 nM substrate at 30 °C.

Kinetic analysis confirmed this synergistic effect. The k obs for the unmodified Mz was 6.3 × 10–4 min–1, while XMz displayed a 6.5-fold improvement (4.1 × 10–3 min–1) (Figure c), confirming that core chemical modification alone accelerates catalysis independently of hybridization. Upon further addition of LNA modifications in LXMz, the reaction reached equilibrium within 5 min. Its k obs was 4.8 × 10–1 min–1, representing a 115-fold increase over XMz and a 760-fold enhancement relative to Mz (Figure b,c). This remarkable improvement demonstrates that the LNA-mediated acceleration of assembly and XNA-mediated acceleration of catalysis act cooperatively rather than redundantly. Moreover, the LNA modification did not elevate background fluorescence in the absence of target, indicating that enhanced reactivity arises from genuine catalytic synergy rather than nonspecific activation (Figure S2). These results establish that dual-site chemical engineering, integrating XNA at the catalytic core and LNA at the recognition arms, simultaneously optimizes both molecular recognition and catalytic turnover, yielding a robust and highly efficient MNAzyme platform suitable for one-pot, enzyme-free nucleic acid detection.

Optimization of Detection Conditions

After establishing the high catalytic efficiency of LXMz, we systematically optimized the assay conditions. The concentration dependence of LXMz was evaluated by using 10 nM miR-92a. The reaction rate increased with rising LXMz concentrations, plateauing at 50 nM with a k obs of 0.34 ± 0.01 min–1 (Figure a,b). Then, at 50 nM LXMz, the reaction rate was found to scale with increasing miR-92a concentrations (Figure c). At 1 nM miR-92a, equilibrium was reached within 90 min (Figure c), which was defined as the optimal reaction time. Finally, temperature profiling revealed that LXMz achieved the strongest fluorescence signal at 30 °C (Figure d). Based on these results, subsequent assays were performed under standardized conditions of 50 nM LXMz, 30 °C, and 90 min.

Analytical Performance of the LXMz Platform

Under optimized conditions, the sensitivity of Mz, XMz, and LXMz was compared. For Mz, a detectable signal was observed at 2 nM miR-92a; however, the fluorescence responses from the background and 1 nM target were not statistically distinguishable (Figure a). The linear detection range of Mz was 0–50 nM, and the limit of detection (LOD) was calculated as 0.9 nM based on the 3σ/k principle, where σ represents the standard deviation of the blank and k denotes the slope of the calibration curve (Figure d). In contrast, XMz enabled reliable detection at 0.2 nM with an LOD of 90 pM, representing a 10-fold improvement over Mz (Figure b,e). Remarkably, LXMz demonstrated detectable signals at 10 pM and achieved an LOD of 2 pM, corresponding to an ∼450-fold improvement relative to Mz (Figure c,f). This sensitivity is on par with those of other enzyme-free isothermal amplification strategies (Table S2). Importantly, unlike most amplification platforms that rely on cascaded reaction or assistance of nanomaterials, − LXMz attains comparable sensitivity through a single-step process. Integration of LXMz with complementary amplification methods is expected to further enhance detection performance, and such investigations are currently underway.

The detection specificity of LXMz was also systematically validated. A panel of nontarget miRNAs was tested, all of which generated fluorescence signals indistinguishable from background levels (Figure S3). Next, we assessed the detection of single-, double-, triple-, and quadruple-base mismatched targets. All variants produced signals below 20% of that generated by the perfectly matched miR-92a, with the signal intensity decreasing as the number of mismatches increased (Figure ). Moreover, single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) of miR-92a were examined. These variants yielded higher signals than double-base mutations and exhibited position-dependent differences, but all were significantly distinguishable from the wild-type target (Figure S4). Together, these results confirm that LXMz offers excellent target selectivity and enables discrimination at a single-base resolution.

Detection specificity of LXMz. Fluorescence responses of LXMz toward different targets: miR-92a, single-base mismatched target (SM), double-base mismatched target (DM), triple-base mismatched target (DM), quadruple-base mismatched target (QM), and blank control sample (BC).

Finally, the performance of the LXMz platform was evaluated in the presence of total RNA extracted from HeLa cells. The fluorescence response obtained in the presence and absence of total RNA was nearly identical (Figure ), indicating that nontarget RNA exerted minimal interference in miRNA detection. This stability likely arises from the LNA modification, which enhances hybridization affinity and specificity, thereby preventing nonspecific binding and mitigating matrix effects. Although cellular RNA could potentially perturb MNAzyme folding or compete for hybridization sites, the LXMz system maintained a high catalytic efficiency without the need for additional sample purification, demonstrating strong compatibility with complex biosamples for practical miRNA detection.

Conclusion

In this study, we reported a synergistically engineered MNAzyme platform (LXMz) that unites catalytic-core and recognition-arm modifications. Incorporation of xeno-nucleic acids (XNAs) into the catalytic center enhanced turnover efficiency, while locked nucleic acids (LNAs) at the target arms reinforced binding and accelerated assembly, yielding a >700-fold catalytic improvement and an ultralow 2 pM detection limit for miR-92a in a single-tube, enzyme-free assay. This performance surpasses conventional MNAzymes and rivals other enzyme-free isothermal amplification methods while preserving operational simplicity. Importantly, LXMz also demonstrated robust specificity, discriminating mismatched and single-base variants. Together, these findings establish chemical modification as an effective strategy to overcome intrinsic limitations of MNAzymes, offering a powerful platform for ultrasensitive and precise miRNA detection. This work lays the foundation for next-generation biosensors in cancer diagnostics and molecular medicine.
