The impact of precession and higher-order multipoles on cosmological inference
Charlie Hoy, Konstantin Leyde

Abstract
Gravitational-wave astronomy presents an exciting opportunity to provide an independent measurement of the expansion rate of the Universe. By combining inferences for the binary component masses and luminosity distances from individual observations, it is possible to infer H 0 H_{0} without direct electromagnetic counterparts or galaxy catalogs. However, this relies on theoretical gravitational-wave models, which are known to be incomplete descriptions of the full predictions of general relativity. Although the accuracy of our models are improving, they are also becoming increasingly expensive as additional phenomena are incorporated. In this work, we demonstrate that there is no significant advantage in including spin-precession and higher-order multipole moments when inferring H 0 H_{0} via the mass spectrum method for current and near-future gravitational-wave event numbers. Even when simulating a population of highly precessing and preferentially asymmetric-mass-ratio binaries, we show that the inferred H 0 H_{0} posterior changes minimally. Using a simpler, less accurate model, achieves comparable H 0 H_{0} estimates with six times less computational cost (on average). Using computationally cheaper models for single event inference may become crucial as thousands of gravitational-wave observations are expected to be detected in the near future.

Single-event parameter estimation

The source properties, described by the multi-dimensional vector

In Bayesian inference, Bayes’ theorem is exploited to calculate the

p(\boldsymbol{\lambda}|d,\mathfrak{M})=\frac{\mathcal{L}(d|\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathfrak{M})\,\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\mathfrak{M})}{\mathcal{Z}(d,\mathfrak{M})},

(1)

where

\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and model

is the probability of the vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
given the model
\mathfrak{M}
, otherwise known as the prior, and
\mathcal{Z}(d,\mathfrak{M})
is
\int\mathcal{L}(d|\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathfrak{M})\,\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\mathfrak{M})\,d\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, otherwise known as the evidence.

If the injections cover the explored binary parameter space sufficiently, the final hyperparameter posterior is independent of the reference choice
\Lambda_{\rm ref}
.

For a total number of
N_{\rm inj,tot}
of injected signals, and a probability of
p(\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\Lambda_{\rm ref})
of being drawn in a reference population/cosmology, the denominator of Eq. (
3
) can be computed via a Monte-Carlo sum:

\int\differential\boldsymbol{\lambda}\,p_{\rm det}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\,p(\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\Lambda)\approx\frac{1}{N_{\rm inj,tot}}\sum_{\nu=1}^{N_{\rm inj,sel}}\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\nu}|\Lambda)}{p(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\nu}|\Lambda_{\rm ref})}\,,

(4)

where the
\nu
index runs over the entire recovered injection set (i.e. the signals that pass the selection criterion), with
N_{\rm inj,sel}
the number of recovered signals.

We note that the Monte-Carlo sum over posterior samples (in the numerator of Eq.
3
), and the Monte-Carlo sum in the denominator of Eq.
3
, are subject to fundamental statistical uncertainties. The expected variance in our estimate of
\log p(\{\mathcal{D}_{{\rm GW}}\}|\Lambda,\mathfrak{M})
scales as
Essick:2022ojx
;
Talbot:2023pex
;
Heinzel:2025ogf

\sigma^{2}\approx\sum_{\nu=1}^{N_{\rm obs}}\sigma_{i}^{2}+N_{\rm obs}^{2}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}^{2}\,,

(5)

where
\sigma^{2}_{i}
is the variance from the individual posterior samples, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{det}}^{2}
is the variance of Eq.
4
. As the variance from Eq.
4
scales quadratically with the catalog size, it is possible that we may not be able to reliably perform inference in the future with a large catalog of observed events; see
Wolfe:2025yxu
which suggests analysing fewer but higher
SNR

III.2
Simulated population

Given that the inferred
H_{0}
shows very little dependence on precession and higher-order multipole moments for real
GW

BBHs
with significant spin-precession and higher-order multipole power. We estimate that 8% of the binaries drawn from the observed
LVK

We achieve this simulated population by assuming the mass ratio follows a power law
q^{\beta_{q}}
with index
\beta_{q}=-1.1

a_{i}=0.7
and standard deviation
\sigma=0.2

(
KAGRA:2021duu
)

\alpha=-2.3

m_{\mathrm{min}}=5\,M_{\odot}

m_{\mathrm{max}}=80\,M_{\odot}
mass. We also assume that there is a Gaussian component in the mass distribution at
m_{1,{\rm s}}=34\,M_{\odot}
, with relative weight
\lambda=0.038

\sigma_{\rm g}=5~M_{\odot}

Finally, we model the redshift distribution as a power-law relation

p(z)\propto\tfrac{\mathrm{d}V_{c}}{\mathrm{d}z}\tfrac{(1+z)^{2}}{1+z}

IV
Conclusions

In this work, we explore the impact of missing physics (higher-order multipoles and precession in
GWs
) on our ability to infer
H_{0}
with
GW
observations via the mass spectrum method.
Using full Bayesian inference for both the individual and hierarchical analyses, we show that even for a population with large, non-aligned spin (leading to spin-precession) that has a larger fraction of
BBHs
with unequal mass ratios (leading to observable higher-order multipoles), there is no advantage in using models that include physics beyond the dominant quadrupole to perform cosmological inference.
We note that this population is a conservative choice not motivated by current astrophysical data – the observed
LVK
GW population has mostly low spin magnitudes with sources being equal mass-ratio.
Statistical uncertainties in individual
GW
analyses dominate over errors from missing physics. Using simpler, less accurate models to infer
H_{0}
reduces the total computational cost by a factor of six.
Although evidence for precession and higher-order multipoles in the
GW
helps break the well known distance-inclination degeneracy, enabling more accurate estimates for the luminosity distance of the source, we argue that simpler models still obtain roughly the correct distance, although with a larger uncertainty.
As apparent in the results from this study, it seems the exact single-event posterior shape is not relevant when estimating
H_{0}
from GW data via the mass spectrum method.
Note that this no longer applies when direct electromagnetic counterparts are simultaneously detected – in this case accurate spin-precession models are important
Feeney:2020kxk
.

Our results demonstrated that neglecting higher-order multipole information is more important than neglecting precession when estimating
H_{0}
. Further work is needed to identify the exact number of observed GW events above which this becomes an important
H_{0}
systematic. However, given that we start to see minor differences in the inferred
H_{0}
for a population including
48/80
binaries with significant spin-precession and
20/80
events with significant higher-order multipole power, we predict that we will need to observe a catalog of
\sim 1500
binaries before we observe similar systematics in the observed population:
Hoy:2024wkc
demonstrated that a single event with significant evidence for precession will occur once
in every
\sim 50
detections, and a binary with significant evidence for higher-order multipoles will
occur once in every
\sim 70
observations according to current estimates for the black hole mass and spin distribution. This means that we will need to detect
\sim 1500
binaries before we observe a comparable number of binaries with significant higher order multipole power.

As part of this work, we also found that we obtained comparable sky localizations between waveform models when analysing most of the simulated signals in our fiducial population. Since accurately inferring the sky location is crucial to narrow down possible host galaxies, it is possible that our key conclusion – there is little advantage in using models that include physics beyond the dominant quadrupole to perform cosmological inference – extends to the dark siren method, which combines
GW
observations with galaxy catalogs. Although we highlight that this is not explicitly tested in this work.

This work has consequences for the future of
GW
astronomy: as we observe significantly more signals with next generation
GW
detectors
Punturo:2010zz
;
Reitze:2019iox
, we may not be able to consistently use the most accurate models for Bayesian inference as the model accuracy typically correlates with computational cost. Unless novel methods are developed for performing Bayesian inference on
GW
data in
\mathcal{O}({\rm sec})
, such as machine learning algorithms
Green:2020dnx
;
Green:2020hst
;
Dax:2021tsq
;
Dax:2024mcn
;
Raymond:2024xzj
or others
Fairhurst:2023idl
, we may be forced to use simpler models that include less physics. Thankfully, in the near future, we will still be able to reliably infer
H_{0}
with
GW
observations via the mass spectrum method.
