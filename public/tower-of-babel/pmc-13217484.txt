It takes two: A two-component system to visualize single-molecule HIV-1 infection in vivo
Authors: Amanda M Dudek
Published: 2026

Main text

Understanding HIV-1 reservoir and dynamics is critical to identifying interventions leading to eventual eradication of latently infected cells required for HIV cure.1,2 In the previous issue of Molecular Therapy Advances, the Di Nunzio lab presents an elegant in vivo model system to visualize replication competent HIV-1-infected cells after transplanting modified human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs).3 This two-component ANCH-OR-GFP system, derived from a chromosomal segregation system in bacteria,4 allows single-molecule visualization of virus infection dynamics.5 By providing an ANCH DNA target sequence within a replication competent HIV-1 (NL4-3 AD8), infection can be visualized by binding of an OR-GFP protein that is provided in trans through stable transduction of human HSPCs prior to cell transplant. The OR-GFP nucleates formation of bright puncta on the double-stranded DNA in a sequence-specific manner to allow visualization of single HIV-1 genomes after infection. The authors were able to demonstrate high lentiviral transduction of HSPCs (∼70%) and high engraftment of the modified human cells (∼20%) into the human immune system (HIS) mouse. Importantly, the ANCHOR modified HIV-1 allowed visualization of individual HIV-1 genomes within human CD4+ T cells after ANCHOR-marked HSPC engraftment, while at the same time allowing active replication in vivo. High-resolution imaging combined with single-molecule RNA-fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH) allowed discrimination of latent versus transcriptionally active provirus, highlighting intriguing differences based on the CD4+ T cells’ secondary lymphoid tissue of origin. Decreased prevalence of actively transcribing HIV in the lymph nodes recapitulates observations from people living with HIV (PLWH) and highlights the importance of visualizing these dynamics in secondary lymphoid tissue.

The mouse model

There is no perfect in vivo animal model for HIV-1 infection due to species specific restriction, but advances in humanized mouse models continue to advance their utility. Human and mouse immune development differ, and many human hematopoietic cells do not reach late developmental stages in mice, though this has been improved using transgenic models. A major limitation to mouse models is the genetic immunodeficiency which allows human hematopoietic cells to survive transplantation additionally prevents development of secondary lymphoid tissues, which are critical reservoirs of HIV-1 latency. Using the BRGS (Balb/c Rag2−/− IL2rg−/− SirpaNOD) HIS model modified to express human thymic-stromal-cell-derived lymphopoietin (TSLP),6 secondary lymphoid tissues are generated while TSLP improves reconstitution of specific T cell subsets relevant to HIV infection such as follicular T helper (Tfh) cells. These cells are thought to harbor a significant portion of the viral reservoir7 and are important for priming of B cell responses.

While this novel system is a powerful tool for interrogating reservoir dynamics in secondary lymphoid tissue, not all potentially infectable cells can be visualized with this system. Because infection of any hematopoietic cells derived from HSPCs lacking the lentiviral expression cassette for the OR-GFP cannot be visualized, there is likely a significant amount of infection that is missed. Importantly, not all cells permissible to infection are derived from the bone marrow; therefore, any susceptible cells such as tissue resident macrophages or microglia derived from the yolk sac will not be visualized with this system. However, just because everything cannot be visualized does not mean we can’t learn a lot from those infected cells we can observe.

The molecular system

It is important to recognize the value of a system that allows visualization of replication competent HIV infection without the removal of any essential or accessory proteins. Essential or accessory genes are often removed to incorporate reporters within the packaging capacity of HIV-1. Many fluorescent reporter systems insert GFP or another fluorophore in place of essential genes such as Env, requiring a fusogen to be provided in trans and rendering the virus replication incompetent. Alternatively, reporters may be incorporated in place of non-essential accessory proteins such as Nef; however these accessory proteins often have immune-modulatory and pathogenesis functions which are used to evade immune recognition in vivo. By allowing the HIV-1 genome to be marked with the ANCH-target sequence without removal of large portions of the HIV genome or accessory genes, HIV-1 replication and reservoir dynamics can be studied in the context of these factors, which contribute significantly to pathogenesis and disease progression. While incorporation of fluorescent reporters often significantly attenuates viral infectivity and replication dynamics, incorporation of the 400 bp or a naturally selected shorter 250 bp repeat ANCH sequence did not cause any decrease in viral fitness compared to unmodified virus. Importantly, the 250 bp sequence was stable over the course of the 20+ day infection in vivo and was sufficient to visualize infected human CD4+ T cells.

A major technical hurdle in the latency and reservoir field has been the difficulty distinguishing between cells containing actively replicating provirus and cells harboring latent and/or defective provirus. To address this hurdle, the authors combine single-molecule FISH (smFISH) targeting the HIV transcript to visualize viral RNA expressed in CD4+ T cells isolated from tissues of HIV-1 infected mice. Dual fluorescent, high-resolution imaging was able to distinguish between cells containing active or latent viral genomes. Specific tissue reservoir sites harboring transcriptionally repressed virus have been described in secondary lymphoid tissues like lymph nodes and gut-associated lymphoid tissue. The increased ratio of latent to active genomes in T cells isolated from lymph nodes in this mouse model mirrors high prevalence of latent virus observed in lymph nodes from PLWH. Interestingly, T cells isolated from spleen demonstrated a higher prevalence of active viral transcription as well as increased infectivity in cell culture after cell free virus was collected from these cells, suggesting not only an increase in viral transcript expression from these cells but actual increases in viral replication. These data demonstrate that this system can be used to not only visualize individual infected cells in a variety of lymphoid tissues but additionally to distinguish active versus latent infection and relative level of spread from infected T cells residing in a given tissue.

The possibilities

The latent proviral reservoir has an increased prevalence of specific integration sites as well as genomic rearrangements or deletions that alter accessory protein integrity, and this system provides a novel model to molecularly interrogate and visualize these events within unique tissue niches that cannot be recapitulated ex vivo. One could envision expanding and multiplexing the smFISH to identify and quantify relative levels of individual viral transcripts for a more detailed picture of how the anatomical location of the infected cells influences the ratio of different viral transcripts, known to signify various stages of the viral replication cycle. With the recent boom in spatial proteomics technologies, this in vivo model may also provide valuable information about interactions between infected cells and the tissue niche.

While in its current state this model does not allow visualization of microglia, advances in conditioning regimens that clear microglia from the brain8 now allow reconstitution of microglia from the bone marrow, and gene-edited cells can effectively traffic to the brain and form microglia post-transplant.9 With recent advances in embryo editing, one could envision a humanized transgenic mouse with mouse-specific restriction factors deleted and the reporter system stably edited into the germline such that infection of non-bone-marrow-derived target cells could be visualized as well. While this is still a relatively sci-fi idea, it highlights the potential for how model systems can be combined and expanded. Although there are some limitations, this system provides a valuable and important step forward in our ability to model and query the human HIV-1 infection reservoir derived from hematopoietic cells. A glimpse into the reservoir hints at many more exciting findings to come.
