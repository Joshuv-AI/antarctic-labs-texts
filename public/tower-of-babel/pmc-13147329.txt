Ionizable Polymeric Micelles Targeting Transferrin Receptor 1 Enhance Systemic mRNA Delivery to the Brain
Authors: Jumpei Norimatsu, Hayato L. Mizuno, Yuki Mochida, Shigeto Fukushima, Satoshi Uchida, Horacio Cabral, Yasutaka Anraku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
mRNA-based therapies hold immense potential for treating a plethora of diseases. However, their application to central nervous system (CNS) disorders remains limited due to biological barriers, such as rapid degradation in circulation, restricted brain access, and endosomal entrapment within cells. To address these challenges, we developed a polymeric micelle-based nanocarrier capable of systemically delivering mRNA to the brain. This system employed triphenylphosphonium (TPP) as a cationic moiety to stably complex with mRNA and prolong its blood circulation. TPP was introduced into a PEG–polyaspartamide derivative bearing diethylenetriamines, whose pH-responsive ionizable amines facilitated endosomal escape. For brain targeting, antibody fragments against transferrin receptor 1 were conjugated to the PEG shell at a controlled density via click chemistry. This rational, multifaceted design enabled robust in situ protein production in the brain following systemic administration, achieving around a 10-fold increase compared to our initial formulation, while exerting lower impact on off-target expression in other organs. Our system offers a promising platform for systemic mRNA delivery to the brain, opening new avenues for treating CNS disorders.

Introduction

Since the advent of COVID-19 vaccines, mRNA-based therapies have emerged as promising candidates for a broad spectrum of diseases, including cancer, metabolic, and genetic diseases. − Central nervous system (CNS) disorders, such as neurodegenerative diseases, represent a key area with an urgent need for effective treatments. − Building on the recent advance in protein therapeutics, , mRNA-based approaches, which can transiently and efficiently produce therapeutic proteins, offer a promising alternative, potentially improving both safety and efficacy.

To date, various synthetic nanocarriers have been designed for in vivo mRNA delivery. − With precise tuning of formulations and materials, mRNA has been successfully delivered to target organs such as the muscle, liver, spleen, or lungs. − However, mRNA nanocarriers tailored for CNS disorders remain extremely underdeveloped. , While systemic administration is desirable for the CNS due to its minimally invasive nature, biological barriers, including swift mRNA degradation, endosomal entrapment, and off-target distribution, complicate the effective application of systemic mRNA therapy. , Moreover, the blood-brain barrier (BBB) severely restricts the entry of macromolecular drugs into the brain, posing a major hurdle to effective therapy. − These challenges create a critical demand for delivery systems capable of overcoming the biological impediments to achieve systemic mRNA delivery to the brain.

Among synthetic carriers, polymeric micelles (PMs) have emerged as simple yet highly customizable nanocarriers capable of delivering diverse therapeutic modalities to target sites including hard-to-reach tissues. − However, an optimal system specifically designed for mRNA delivery to the brain has yet to be established.

Here, we rationally designed PM-based systems to address the above-mentioned challenges and allow effective delivery of mRNA into the brain. To ensure stable mRNA circulation in the bloodstream, we employed triphenylphosphonium (TPP) as a cationic moiety to stably associate with mRNA through its unique combination of hydrophobicity and phosphonium-derived cationic charge. Indeed, TPP-bearing catiomers form more stable complexes with mRNA than conventional amine-based counterparts, markedly enhancing mRNA bioavailability following systemic administration. For brain-specific delivery, antibody fragments targeting transferrin receptor 1 (anti-TfR1 Fab′) were conjugated to the PM surface in a controlled manner. , The smaller size of Fab′ fragments offers several advantages over full-length antibodies, including reduced perturbation of the nanocarrier structure upon conjugation and diminished Fc receptor–mediated immune recognition. In addition, the free thiol group at the Fab′ hinge enables site-specific and oriented coupling to maleimide-functionalized moieties, ensuring optimal antigen-binding orientation. Moreover, to facilitate endosomal escape within the target cells, we introduced ionizable diethylenetriamines moieties to the catiomers that respond to acidic endosomal pH, , allowing the disruption of endosomal membranes. These functional elements were integrated into PMs, and their potential for mRNA delivery to the brain was evaluated in vitro and in vivo.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), RPMI-1640 medium, Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (D-PBS), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin, 4-((2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid) (HEPES) (1.0 M) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). Firefly luciferase mRNA was purchased from TriLink Biotechnologies (San Diego, CA, USA). NanoLuc mRNA was in vitro transcribed from custom designed plasmid (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) using mMESSAGE mMACHINE T7 Ultra Kit (Ambion, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), followed by RNA purification with RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Primers and probe for qRT-PCR were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, Iowa, USA) and TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix was purchased from Applied Biosystems (Foster City, CA, USA). CT26 and B16F10 cells were obtained from Riken BioResource Center (Tsukuba, Japan). 8D3 antibodies (InVivoMab antimouse CD71) were purchased from BioXCell (NH, USA). DBCO-PEG4-maleimide was purchased from BroadPharm (San Diego, CA). All animal studies detailed below received approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Tokyo (Tokyo, Japan) (Approval number: A2023E005–01).

Synthesis of Block Catiomers

PEG-poly­(β-benzyl-l-aspartate) block copolymers (PEG–PBLA) were synthesized via the ring-opening polymerization of BLA-NCA, initiated with methoxy-PEG-NH2, as previously described. PEG–PAsp­(DET) polymers were then prepared through aminolysis of PBLA segments with diethylenetriamine (DET), following established procedures. The degree of polymerization (DP) of the cationic segments was calculated based on 1H NMR spectra [D2O at 80 °C, δ = 3.4–3.6 ppm (4H: −CH 2–CH 2–O−), δ = 4.6–4.9 ppm (1H: −CO–CH–NH−)]. To synthesize azide-containing block copolymers, azide-PEG-NH2 was used as an initiator, instead of methoxy-PEG-NH2.

Triphenylphosphonium (TPP) moieties were conjugated to the primary amines in PEG–PAsp­(DET) side chains through an amide coupling reaction. PEG–PAsp­(DET) (1 μmol), 4-(carboxybutyl)­triphenylphosphonium bromide (136 μmol), and DMT-MM (90 mg) were dissolved in 50 mM NaHCO3/MeOH (3 mL/9 mL), and stirred at 4 °C for 6 h. During this incubation, additional DMT-MM (90 mg) was added at 2 and 4 h. The resulting solution was dialyzed (molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of the membrane: 12–14 kDa) against methanol and pure water alternately at 4 °C for 1 week to remove unreacted reagents. The solution was then lyophilized to obtain PEG–PAsp­(DET) with TPP moieties (PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP))). The TPP introduction ratio into PEG–PAsp­(DET) side chains was determined by 1H-NMR (D2O at 80 °C): δ = 3.4–3.6 ppm (4H: −CH 2–CH 2–O−), δ = 7.6–8.0 ppm (15H: Ph3P-). Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was performed using an Extrema 4500 Model (JASCO, Tokyo, Japan) with a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column; the eluent was 10 mM PB with 500 mM NaCl at pH 7.4, temperature 28 °C, flow rate 0.5 mL/min, and detection at UV 270 nm.

Acid–Base Titration

To evaluate the ionizability of block copolymers (PEG–PLys, PEG–PLys­(TPP), PEG–PAsp­(DET), and PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP))) across different pH levels, titration experiments were performed using a 916 Ti-Touch automatic titrator (Metrohm, Herisau, Switzerland). Each polymer (35.2 μM) was dissolved in 1.25 mM HCl, and titrated with 5 mM NaOH solution. The pK a of each polymer was determined by the obtained titration curve.

Evaluation of Polymer Colloidal Behaviors

Block copolymers (9.2 μM) were dissolved in 10 mM HEPES buffer at either pH 7.4 or 5.5 and mixed with an equal volume of 10 mM HEPES with the same pH, containing various concentrations of NaCl (ranging from 0 to 3 M). The mixed polymer solutions were analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS). DLS measurements were conducted at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK). Scattered light intensity (SLI) was monitored to detect the formation of multimolecular assemblies. When an increase in SLI was observed, the hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) of the assemblies were also measured.

Preparation of mRNA-Loaded Polymeric Micelles (mRNA/PMs)

mRNA and block copolymers (PEG–PLys, PEG–PLys­(TPP), PEG–PAsp­(DET), or PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP))) were separately dissolved in 10 mM HEPES buffer without NaCl. The block copolymer solution (1 volume unit) was added to the mRNA solution (2 volume units, 50 ng/μL) with brief vortex mixing. The final molar ratio of cationic moieties (primary amine or TPP) in the block copolymers to anionic moieties (phosphate groups) in mRNA, defined as the C/A ratio, was fixed at 3. The mixture was then kept on ice for 10 min before analysis. The size distribution of the mRNA/PMs was measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK).

mRNA Stability against Serum

To evaluate stability against serum nucleases, mRNA/PMs were incubated in 50% FBS, with the final mRNA concentration set to 1.67 ng/μL. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by RNA extraction using the RNeasy Mini Kit. cDNA was then synthesized through reverse transcription using the High Capacity RNA-to-cDNA Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Finally, qPCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System using the specified primer/probe pairs: (Forward: GTGGTGTGCAGCGAGAATAG, Reverse: CGCTCGTTGTAGATGTCGTTAG, Probe: TTGCAGTTCTTCATGCCCGTGTTG)

To evaluate the structural stability of mRNA/PMs in serum, mRNA was double labeled with Cy3 and Cy5 using the Label IT Cy3 Labeling Kit and Label IT Cy5 Labeling Kit (Mirus Bio LLC, Madison, WI), followed by encapsulation into PMs. Before and after incubation in 50% FBS at 37 °C, samples were excited at 520 nm, and fluorescence emission spectra were recorded using Infinite M Nano (Tecan Group Ltd., Zürich, Switzerland). Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) was calculated as follows FRETratio=[Cy5intensity(680nm)]/[Cy3intensity(570nm)]

Cellular Uptake and Intracellular Distribution of mRNA/PMs

mRNA was labeled with Cy5 using the Label IT Cy5 Labeling Kit (Mirus Bio LLC, Madison, WI) and subsequently encapsulated in PMs as described above. CT26 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The cells (12,500 cells/well) were seeded into an 8-well chamber (IWAKI AGC Techno Glass Co., Ltd., Shizuoka, Japan). After 24 h of incubation, PMs encapsulating Cy5-labeled mRNA (300 ng mRNA/well) were added to each well and incubated for 4 h. The cells were then washed three times with D-PBS. The cell nuclei and lysosomes were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) and LysoTracker Green DND-26 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), respectively. Stained cells were observed using an LSM 880 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) with excitation wavelengths of 405 nm for Hoechst 33342, 488 nm for LysoTracker, and 633 nm for Cy5. Cy5 signals within individual cells were quantified to evaluate the internalized mRNA amounts. To assess the colocalization of mRNA and lysosomes, Pearson’s coefficient was calculated for each cell using ImageJ software.

In Vitro Translation by mRNA/PMs

B16F10 and CT26 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The cells (15,000 cells/well) were seeded onto 96-well plate. After overnight incubation, mRNA/PMs (165 ng mRNA/well) were added to the medium. 24 h later, the medium was removed, and cells were lysed using Cell Culture Lysis Reagent (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Luminescence in the lysate was measured using Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega) and GloMax 96 Microplate Luminometer (Promega).

Fab′-PEG4-DBCO Preparation

Fab′ fragments were prepared from 8D3 antibodies. The Fc region of 8D3 (10 mg) was digested using pepsin (0.5 mg) in 2 mL of 200 mM acetate buffer (pH 4.4) to generate (Fab′)2 fragments. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, the buffer was exchanged with 10 mM phosphate buffer (PB) containing 500 mM NaCl (pH 7.4) via ultrafiltration (MWCO: 30 kDa), followed by collection of the (Fab′)2 fragments using size exclusion chromatography (SEC). SEC was performed using an Extrema 4500 Model (JASCO, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column and eluted with 10 mM PB (500 mM NaCl), pH 7.4, at room temperature, with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min and UV detection at 220 nm.

Subsequently, (Fab′)2 was reduced to generate Fab′ fragments. The (Fab′)2 (2 mg) were incubated with 2 mL of 0.2 mM tris­(2-carboxyethyl)­phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) in 10 mM PB containing 500 mM NaCl (pH 7.4) at room temperature. After 2 h, DBCO-PEG4-maleimide (at a 5-fold molar excess relative to (Fab′)2) was added to the solution. Following another 2-h incubation, the solution was concentrated by ultrafiltration (MWCO: 30 kDa). The final Fab′-PEG4-DBCO product was obtained by SEC, and the buffer was exchanged to PBS using ultrafiltration (MWCO: 30 kDa).

Ligand Installation on mRNA/PMs

Azide-displaying mRNA/PMs (N3-mRNA/PMs) were prepared using azide-containing block copolymers (PEG–PLys­(TPP) or PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP))), as described in Section .

For Fab′ installation, Fab′-PEG4-DBCO (at 0.05, 0.1, or 0.2 mol equiv relative to azide groups on mRNA/PMs) was added to the N3-mRNA/PMs, followed by incubation at 4 °C. The size distribution of the resulting Fab′-mRNA/PMs was measured using DLS. The number of Fab′ molecules attached to each mRNA/PM was quantified using fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS). To this end, sulfoCy3-NHS ester (at a 4-fold molar excess relative to (Fab′)2) was added during the preparation of Fab′-PEG4-DBCO. Following overnight incubation at 4 °C, unreacted dyes were removed by ultrafiltration (MWCO: 30 kDa), after which the Fab′ fragments were reduced and conjugated with DBCO-PEG4-maleimide as described previously, yielding Cy3-labeled Fab′-PEG4-DBCO. After decoration onto mRNA/PMs, Cy3-specific parameters, such as diffusion time and count per molecule (CPM), were analyzed by FCS using an LSM 780 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) with an excitation wavelength of 543 nm. The number of Fab′ molecules per mRNA/PM was calculated using the following equation AssociationnumberofFab′=[CPMofFab′‐mRNA/PM]/[CPMoffreeFab′‐PEG4‐DBCO]

The percentage of Fab′-PEG4-DBCO associated with mRNA/PMs was quantified using the following equation after two-component model fitting of the FCS autocorrelation curves, in which the diffusion time of component 1 was fixed to that of free Fab′-PEG4-DBCO. The percentage of Fab′ associated with mRNA/PMs relative to the total input was calculated as follows Fab′‐PEG4‐DBCOassociatedwithmRNA/PMs(%ofinput)=N×F2/(F1+N×F2)×100 where N represents the number of Fab′ molecules per micelle, F 1 represents the fitted fraction of component 1 (free Fab′-PEG4-DBCO), and F 2 represents the fitted fraction of component 2 (micelle-associated Fab′-PEG4-DBCO).

Cellular Uptake of Fab′-mRNA/PMs

Cy5-labeled mRNA was encapsulated into azide-displaying PMs and varying amounts of Fab′ were installed as described above. B16F10 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. B16F10 cells (12,500 cells/well) were seeded into an 8-well chamber (IWAKI AGC Techno Glass Co. Ltd., Shizuoka, Japan). After 24 h of incubation, Fab′-decorated PMs encapsulating Cy5-labeled mRNA were added to each well (300 ng mRNA/well) and incubated for 3 h. The cells were then washed three times with D-PBS, and the nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). The stained cells were visualized using an LSM 780 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) with excitation wavelengths of 405 nm for Hoechst 33342 and 633 nm for Cy5. Cy5 fluorescence intensity in each cell was quantified using ImageJ software to assess mRNA uptake.

To evaluate TfR1-dependent uptake, B16F10 cells were preincubated with 8D3 antibodies (50 ng/well) to block TfR1. After a 30 min preincubation, Fab′-decorated PMs encapsulating Cy5-labeled mRNA were added to each well (300 ng mRNA/well) and incubated for 3 h. The cellular uptake of Cy5-labeled mRNA was then quantified as described above.

In Vitro Protein Expression by Fab′-mRNA/PMs

B16F10 cells (15,000 per well) were seeded into a 96-well plate. After overnight incubation, Fab′-installed mRNA/PMs (165 ng mRNA per well) were added to the medium. Following a 24-h incubation, the medium was removed, and the cells were lysed using Cell Culture Lysis Reagent (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Luminescence in the lysate was measured using the Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay System and a GloMax 96 Microplate Luminometer (Promega).

Blood Circulation and Biodistribution of Fab′-mRNA/PMs

BALB/c mice (female, 7 weeks old; Charles River Laboratories Japan Inc., Kanagawa, Japan) were intravenously injected with mRNA/PMs in a 10 mM HEPES buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, at a dose of 10 μg mRNA per mouse, through the jugular vein. 2 μL of blood was collected from the tail vein and was added to RLT buffer containing 1% 2-mercaptoethanol. Then, RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and qPCR were conducted to quantify the intact mRNA in the blood. After 2.5 h, the mice were euthanized, and their tissues were immediately frozen. Each tissue sample was homogenized with 10 equiv volumes of RLT buffer containing 1% 2-mercaptoethanol using a Multi Beads Shocker MBX (Yasui Kikai, Osaka, Japan). The supernatant was collected, and RNA was extracted from 100 μL of the supernatant using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Reverse transcription was performed on 0.5 μg of extracted RNA to synthesize cDNA using the High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA Kit (Applied Biosystems). qPCR was then conducted on the Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). GAPDH mRNA was utilized as an internal control to normalize mRNA levels in each tissue.

In Vivo Protein Expression by Fab′-mRNA/PMs

BALB/c mice (female, 7 weeks old) were intravenously injected with Fab′-installed mRNA/PMs in a 10 mM HEPES buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, at a dose of 10 μg mRNA per mouse, administered via the jugular vein. After 24 h, the mice were euthanized, and their tissues were collected. Each tissue was homogenized with four equivalent volumes of cell culture lysis buffer using a Multi Beads Shocker MBX (Yasui Kikai, Osaka, Japan). Luciferase expression in the homogenates was measured using the Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega) and quantified with the GloMax 96 Microplate Luminometer (Promega).

Results and Discussion

Preparation of Ionizable Block Catiomers Bearing TPP

We first synthesized PEG–PBLA via ring-opening polymerization of BLA-NCA from α-methoxy-ω-aminopropyl-PEG (PEG–NH2) of molecular weight (M n = 12,000), followed by amino-lysis of PBLA segments with diethylenetriamine (DET) to obtain PEG–PAsp­(DET) polymers. The degree of polymerization (DP) was determined to be 68 according to 1H NMR measurement. Then, triphenylphosphonium (TPP) moieties were introduced to the primary amines of PEG–PAsp­(DET) via an amide-coupling reaction to obtain PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) (Figure A and Scheme S1). The 1H NMR measurement revealed complete conversion from primary amines to TPP moieties in the side chains (Figure S1). Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) analysis further verified the polymer’s unimodal size distribution (Figure S2). As a counterpart, we also prepared PEG–PLys bearing TPP (PEG–PLys­(TPP)) following our previous report (Figure A, and Scheme S1). The DP of Lys was adjusted to 68, and the TPP introduction rate was set to 97%. Both PEG–PLys­(TPP) and PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) are TPP-bearing block catiomers, but PEG–PLys­(TPP) lacks secondary amino groups in its side chains.

Next, we evaluated the ionizability of these polymers at varying pH to assess their potential for endosomal escape. Endosome acidification occurs during its maturation, and nanocarriers ionized at pH 5.5–7.0 can enhance their endosomal escape by destabilizing the endosomal membrane. Acid–base titrations revealed that polymers without TPP, such as PEG–PLys and PEG–PAsp­(DET), exhibited buffering capacity at alkaline pH (>7.0), indicating the protonation/deprotonation behavior of primary amines (Figure B). Notably, PEG–PAsp­(DET)-based polymers, including PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) and PEG–PAsp­(DET), displayed buffering properties at acidic pH (<7.0), suggesting their ionizable potential to facilitate endosomal escape. The pK a of PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) was determined to be 5.9, which approximately matched that of PEG–PAsp­(DET). In contrast, PEG–PLys-based polymers, including PEG–PLys and PEG–PLys­(TPP), exhibited negligible buffering at acidic pH. These results showcase that secondary amines in the side chains of PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) contribute to its ionization at acidic pH.

Before adapting these polymers to mRNA encapsulation, we assessed their colloidal behaviors in aqueous solutions. Our previous study showed that TPP incorporation into PEG–PLys polymers induced their NaCl-responsive self-assembly. While TPP-derived cationic repulsion maintained the dispersion of PEG–PLys­(TPP) polymers in aqueous solutions, NaCl addition suppressed this repulsion, triggering self-assembly via TPP-derived hydrophobicity. Similar to PEG–PLys­(TPP), PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) also exhibited NaCl-responsive self-assembly (Figure C,D), while TPP-deficient polymers, PEG–PLys and PEG–PAsp­(DET), did not (Figure S3). Although PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) was well-dispersed in aqueous solutions, as indicated by low light scattering in DLS measurements, the scattered light intensity (SLI) increased significantly after NaCl addition. This increase corresponded to the formation of highly monodispersed nanoparticles around 60 nm, suggesting amphiphilic micelle formation driven by hydrophobic interactions. Moreover, pH also influenced the self-assembly of PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)). PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) required a higher NaCl concentration to self-assemble at pH 5.5 compared to pH 7.4 (Figure D). This is likely due to the increased cationic charge from the secondary amines protonated at acidic pH, necessitating more NaCl to overcome cationic repulsion. In contrast, PEG–PLys­(TPP)’s self-assembly behavior remained unaffected by pH, plausibly due to consistent protonation states at pH 7.4 and 5.5 (Figure C).

Overall, we successfully synthesized PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) polymers, which self-assembled under high ionic strength, presumably due to suppression of cationic repulsion through Cl– ion binding. Since mRNA is anionic, its binding may also promote polymer self-assembly, leading to stable complexation with mRNA. Additionally, PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP))’s ionizability at acidic pH suggests significant potential for facilitating the endosomal escape of nanocarriers.

Development of mRNA-Loaded Polymeric Micelles (mRNA/PMs)

We next prepared mRNA/PMs by mixing block catiomers with mRNA in 10 mM HEPES solution without NaCl. The molar ratio of cationic moieties (primary amines or TPP in polymers) to anionic moieties (phosphates in mRNA) was fixed at 3 across all formulations, following our previous studies. , All polymers successfully formed monodispersed nanoparticles with diameters of 50–60 nm, indicating the successful creation of mRNA/PMs (Figure A and Table ). When encapsulating Cy3/Cy5 double labeled mRNA, all PMs exhibited higher FRET signals than free mRNA, indicating dense packaging of mRNA within the PM structures (Figure B). Notably, PMs formed with TPP-bearing polymers exhibited even higher FRET signals, likely due to stronger mRNA–polymer and polymer–polymer interactions.

Enzymatic degradation is a major obstacle for mRNA-based therapeutics, even when encapsulated in nanoparticles. To verify their mRNA protection in physiological environments, we incubated mRNA/PMs in 50% serum. Then, we quantified the remaining intact mRNA (Figure C). While PEG–PLys or PEG–PAsp­(DET) allowed significant mRNA degradation, TPP incorporation into these polymers substantially improved mRNA stability against serum. We further evaluated the structural stability of mRNA/PMs using a FRET-based assay (Figure D). Upon incubation in 50% serum, mRNA/PMs formulated with PEG–PAsp­(DET), followed by PEG–PLys, showed a rapid decrease in FRET signal toward the level of free mRNA, indicating mRNA release due to structural disruption. In contrast, mRNA/PMs formulated with PEG–PLys­(TPP) and PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) maintained higher FRET signals over time, demonstrating greater resistance to serum-induced destabilization. TPP likely enhances mRNA protection through favorable ionic interactions and additional interpolymer hydrophobic interactions, effectively stabilizing the mRNA complex against both structural and enzymatic degradation. This elevated stability facilitated mRNA internalization in cells. PEG–PLys­(TPP) and PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) polymers achieved comparable levels of mRNA cellular uptake (Figure S4). In contrast, TPP-deficient polymers, PEG–PLys and PEG–PAsp­(DET), showed poor mRNA uptake, possibly due to insufficient stability.

Once internalized, mRNA must reach the cytosol to initiate protein translation. Thus, we evaluated the intracellular distribution of mRNA delivered by PEG–PLys­(TPP) and PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)). Despite similar cellular uptake levels, mRNA/PMs with ionizable PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) exhibited significantly lower colocalization with late endo/lysosomes compared to those with nonionizable PEG–PLys­(TPP), indicating more efficient escape from lysosomal degradation (Figure E). Consequently, PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) encapsulating luciferase mRNA resulted in significantly higher luciferase expression within cells, underscoring its potential for cytosolic mRNA delivery (Figure F). To further examine the role of endosomal escape in mRNA delivery, we evaluated protein expression in the presence of the endosomal escape enhancer, l-leucyl-l-leucine methyl ester (LLOMe). LLOMe treatment increased luciferase expression for both PEG–PLys­(TPP) and PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) formulations (Figure S5), supporting the importance of endosomal escape in this system. The enhancement was more pronounced for PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)), suggesting a synergistic effect between LLOMe-induced disruption and the intrinsic endosomal escape capability of this polymer. Although PEG–PAsp­(DET) also demonstrated ionizability at acidic pH, potentially facilitating endosome escape (Figure S6), its insufficient stability against RNase may hinder mRNA entry into cells, leading to negligible protein expression (Figure S7).

Taken together, biologically stable and ionizable mRNA/PMs were developed using PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) polymers. While TPP moieties stabilized mRNA to enable cellular uptake, secondary amines in the side chains boosted endosomal escape in response to endosomal pH. These properties synergistically facilitated cytosolic mRNA delivery for efficient protein production.

Ligand Installation onto mRNA/PMs

For brain delivery, mRNA/PMs were decorated with antibody fragments (Fab′) targeting transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1), due to its overexpression on brain endothelial cells. , Copper-free click chemistry between azide and DBCO was employed for Fab′ decoration on the mRNA/PM surface (Figure A). ,− To this end, block copolymers (PEG–PLys­(TPP) and PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP))) with azide moieties were synthesized as described above, using azide-PEG-NH2 as an initiator. These polymers were then mixed with mRNA at a cation/anion ratio of 3 to generate azide-displaying mRNA/PMs (N3-mRNA/PMs). Fab′-PEG4-DBCO was prepared from antimouse TfR1 antibodies (8D3) (Figure S8). The Fc regions of 8D3 were digested with pepsin to produce (Fab′)2, followed by cleavage of intramolecular disulfide bonds. The resulting Fab′ with thiol moieties was reacted with maleimide-PEG4-DBCO to obtain Fab′-PEG4-DBCO. DBCO conjugation to Fab′ was confirmed by UV absorbance around 310 nm (Figure S8), and the Fab′ retained its binding capability to TfR1 after DBCO modification. (Figure S9)

We then incubated Fab′-PEG4-DBCO with N3-mRNA/PMs consisting of PEG–PLys­(TPP) or PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) at varying molar ratios (0.05, 0.1, or 0.2 equiv to azide groups). Fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) analysis showed that the diffusion time of Fab′ became comparable to that of mRNA/PMs after incubation, confirming successful Fab′ conjugation at all tested ratios (Figures B and S10). The Fab′ association number on each mRNA/PM was calculated from the count per molecule (CPM) in FCS measurements. Regardless of whether mRNA/PMs were formulated with PEG–PLys­(TPP) or PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)), the Fab′ association number similarly increased with the feeding amount of Fab′-PEG4-DBCO, indicating that Fab′ decoration could be precisely controlled in a dose-dependent manner (Figure C). FCS analysis revealed that the fraction of unbound Fab′ was minimal in all formulations (Table ), indicating a negligible impact on mRNA delivery (Figure S11). According to DLS measurements, all mRNA/PMs maintained their unimodal size distributions around 60 nm, though Fab′ installation slightly increased their size (Table ). Additionally, zeta potential slightly shifted toward neutral after Fab′ installation, but all mRNA/PMs maintained weak positive charges.

Altogether, varying amounts of Fab′ were successfully installed onto mRNA/PMs in a well-controlled manner, while minimally impacting the physicochemical properties of the original mRNA/PMs.

In Vitro Performance of Ligand-Installed mRNA/PMs

We next investigated the TfR1-targeting capabilities of Fab′-installed mRNA/PMs (Fab′- mRNA/PMs). Cy5-labeled mRNA was encapsulated into PMs and incubated with B16F10 cells, a model of murine TfR1-positive cells. After 3 h of incubation, Fab′-mRNA/PMs formulated with either PEG–PLys­(TPP) or PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) exhibited stronger Cy5 signals within the cells compared to those without Fab′, indicating enhanced mRNA uptake due to Fab′ installation (Figure A,B). Furthermore, the uptake of Fab′-mRNA/PMs was significantly inhibited in the presence of 8D3 antibodies blocking TfR1, while no detectable changes were observed for mRNA/PMs without Fab′. These results highlight that Fab′-mRNA/PMs can selectively target TfR1-positive cells via ligand–receptor interactions.

Protein expression by Fab′-mRNA/PMs was further evaluated after 24 h of incubation with B16F10 cells (Figure C). As anticipated, Fab′ installation enhanced protein expression by mRNA/PMs due to increased cellular uptake via TfR1-targeting. Notably, Fab′-mRNA/PMs formulated with ionizable PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) demonstrated significantly higher expression than those with nonionizable PEG–PLys­(TPP), despite having a comparable number of Fab′ ligands (Figure C) and showing comparable cellular uptake efficiency (Figure A,B). This pronounced protein expression is likely due to efficient endosomal escape facilitated by PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) (Figure E), which promotes mRNA localization in the cytosol for protein translation.

In Vivo Performance of Ligand-Installed mRNA/PMs

Lastly, Fab′-mRNA/PMs were intravenously injected into mice to assess their potential for in vivo mRNA delivery. All formulations exhibited similar blood circulation profiles, remarkably improving the bioavailability of injected mRNA (Figure A,B). Fab′ installation did not ruin the circulation of mRNA/PM; rather, it appeared to improve it. The reduction in cationic charges following Fab′ installation may have suppressed nonspecific interactions or entrapment in vivo (Table ), potentially prolonging the blood circulation of mRNA/PMs. − However, this interpretation requires further investigation using different Fab′ fragments, including nonbinding controls, to assess the generality of this effect.

Subsequently, intact mRNA accumulation in each organ was evaluated using qRT-PCR. Regardless of whether mRNA/PMs were formulated with PEG–PLys­(TPP) or PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)), intact mRNA primarily accumulated in the lung, liver, and spleen (Figure C,D). Fab′ installation showed a trend toward reduced lung accumulation, possibly due to the decreased cationic charges following Fab′ attachment. Notably, mRNA/PMs with PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) demonstrated higher mRNA distribution across major organs than those with PEG–PLys­(TPP). This increased mRNA detection might be linked to the efficient endosomal escape facilitated by ionizable PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) (Figures B and E), potentially preserving mRNA integrity postinternalization. Although endosomal retention has been proposed to prolong intracellular persistence for chemically stabilized oligonucleotides such as siRNA or ASOs, this mechanism may not necessarily preserve intact mRNA because trafficking toward lysosomes typically exposes nucleic acids to degradative enzymes. , Consistently, mRNA/PMs formulated with PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) exhibited slower intracellular mRNA decay than those formulated with PEG–PLys­(TPP) (Figure S12), suggesting that efficient endosomal escape may contribute to preserving intact mRNA after cellular uptake. For brain delivery, Fab′ installation successfully amplified mRNA accumulation for both polymer compositions, confirming the effectiveness of the TfR1-targeting strategy. Importantly, 0.05 equiv of Fab′ installation yielded the highest mRNA accumulation, highlighting the moderate ligand density as optimal for brain delivery. Higher ligand densities might result in excessive avidity to TfR1, potentially leading to off-target binding to cells or tissues with different TfR1 expression levels.

Encouraged by the elevated mRNA accumulation in the brain, we finally assessed the protein expression efficiency in vivo. mRNA/PMs, with or without 0.05 equiv of Fab′, were intravenously injected into mice, and luciferase expression in each organ was evaluated 24 h postinjection (Figure E,F). Consistent with the mRNA biodistribution, the lung, spleen, and liver exhibited the highest protein expression irrespective of mRNA/PM formulation. Within the brain, however, mRNA/PMs formulated with nonionizable PEG–PLys­(TPP) showed negligible expression, even after Fab′ installation. In stark contrast, mRNA/PMs with ionizable PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) induced higher brain expression, which was further amplified by 0.05 equiv of Fab′ installation (Figure E). Then, brain selectivity was determined by calculating the ratio of expression levels in the brain to those in the other organs. 0.05 equiv Fab′-mRNA/PMs with PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) could also increase the brain selectivity (Figure G). Notably, extrahepatic delivery remains a major challenge in RNA therapeutics. − Although hepatic luciferase expression was still higher than that in the brain, the brain-to-liver ratio reached approximately 0.26, which surpasses values reported for other brain-directed mRNA delivery strategies. , This finding highlights the unique potential of our delivery platform to improve extrahepatic mRNA delivery to the brain.

Overall, the moderate Fab′ installation enhanced active brain targeting, while the ionization of PEG–PAsp­(DET­(TPP)) promoted endosomal escape. These factors likely facilitated systemic mRNA delivery to the brain, leading to enhanced protein production in a brain-selective manner.

Conclusions

This study presents multifunctional polymeric nanocarriers capable of delivering mRNA to the brain via systemic injection. Specifically, TPP introduction into the polymers for highly stable mRNA complexation, ionizable side chains for potent endosomal escape, and precisely controlled Fab′ installation for TfR1-guided brain targeting represent a uniquely synergistic design, enabling selective enhancement of protein production in the brain. To our knowledge, this is the first report of systemic mRNA delivery to the brain using polymer-based nanocarriers. Although recent studies have achieved brain-targeted mRNA delivery with lipid-based systems, expanding the diversity of delivery platforms will be essential for advancing mRNA therapeutics targeting a broad range of neurological disorders. Although a large portion of the injected mRNA still accumulated in off-target organs, including the lung, investigating the mechanism should provide insights for further optimizing nanocarrier design to improve brain delivery efficacy, which is currently under investigation. Ultimately, given the customizable nature of mRNA sequences to encode proteins of interest, our system holds promise as a low-invasive yet robust platform for addressing various challenging-to-treat CNS disorders through in situ production of therapeutic proteins within the brain.
