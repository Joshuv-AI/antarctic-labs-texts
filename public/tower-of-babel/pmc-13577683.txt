Hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1a (HIF1a) as a context‐dependent integrator of metabolic stress in skeletal muscle
Authors: Nicole Welch, Pugazhendhi Kannan, Seshu Vardhan Pothabathula, Annette Bellar, Abhishek A. Chakraborty, Saurabh Mishra, Srinivasan Dasarathy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkeletal muscle experiences large fluctuations in ATP demand and redox state during contraction, ischaemia and chronic disease, requiring rapid adaptations in substrate selection, mitochondrial workload, vascular coupling, regeneration and protein homeostasis. Hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1a (HIF1a) is classically viewed as an oxygen‐responsive transcription factor that mediates rapid adaptation to hypoxia. Accumulating evidence across tissues, including skeletal muscle, indicates that HIF1a is also responsive to physiological and pathological inputs, such as exercise, circadian timing, redox perturbations and endogenous/exogenous cytotoxins, even when tissue hypoxia is not detectable. During hypoxia, including transient mismatches between oxygen demand and supply during muscle contraction, HIF1a activation shifts metabolism from oxidative to non‐oxidative energy production, suppresses non‐essential energy‐consuming processes, including protein homeostasis, and promotes vascular responses that improve oxygen delivery. Under normoxic conditions, persistent HIF1a activation promotes maladaptive responses, including impaired mitochondrial remodelling, reduced anabolic responsiveness, defective regeneration, fibrosis, and atrophy‐ and senescence‐associated reprogramming. Current evidence shows that this shift from adaptive to maladaptive signalling is determined in part by post‐translational mechanisms that regulate signalling duration and target gene engagement, as well as by fibre type, circadian state and the nature of the upstream stressor. Unlike the robust responses in muscle tissue observed in preclinical models, human muscle biopsies often show modest or transient HIF1a accumulation, yet transcriptional responses indicate meaningful pathway activation, suggesting that biologically relevant signalling occurs even when total protein levels appear low. Genetic models, multiomics, and human studies support HIF1a as a context‐dependent regulator of metabolic reprogramming that balances short‐term adaptation with long‐term energetic cost.

Introduction

Hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1a (HIF1a) is the oxygen‐responsive subunit of the HIF1 transcriptional complex, originally defined as a basic helix‐loop‐helix PAS heterodimer composed of HIF1a and HIF1b/aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT) (Wang et al., 1995). Under normoxic conditions, canonical degradation of HIF1a is initiated by EGLN/prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD)‐dependent dioxygenases, which require oxygen, iron and 2‐oxoglutarate to hydroxylate HIF subunits and promote proteasomal degradation through the von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) pathway (Epstein et al., 2001). When oxygen availability declines, reduced PHD activity results in HIF1a stabilization, nuclear accumulation, heterodimerization with HIF1b/ARNT, and transcriptional activation of genes involved in glycolysis, angiogenesis, erythropoiesis and cell survival (Epstein et al., 2001; Wang et al., 1995).

HIF2a, encoded by EPAS1, is regulated by overlapping oxygen‐dependent degradation mechanisms but has distinct transcriptional outputs. Comparative studies show that HIF1a preferentially activates glycolytic genes, whereas HIF2a regulates a broader subset of hypoxia‐responsive genes in a cell‐ and tissue‐dependent manner (Hu et al., 2003). Human genetic studies also implicate EPAS1 in adaptation to chronic hypoxemia, including high‐altitude adaptation (Beall et al., 2010). In skeletal muscle, HIF1a is especially relevant to acute metabolic reprogramming, glycolytic control and oxygen independent stress responses, whereas HIF2a may contribute to isoform‐specific or compensatory signalling in selected contexts.

In addition to oxygen availability, metabolic perturbations can impact HIF1a stability and transcriptional output. Because PHD activity requires Fe2+ and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediate α‐ketoglutarate (aKG), changes in mitochondrial metabolism, redox state and TCA cycle intermediates can modify HIF1a degradation without a sustained decrease in systemic oxygenation (Epstein et al., 2001; O'Hagan et al., 2009; Welch et al., 2025). Although, HIF biology was first defined in models of sustained hypoxia, ischaemia and oxygen‐sensitive gene regulation, HIF1a signalling in skeletal muscle has not been completely evaluated. During contraction, skeletal muscle undergoes rapid changes in ATP demand, blood flow, oxygen utilization, mitochondrial respiration and redox state, often without sustained systemic hypoxemia (Poole & Musch, 2023). Human studies show that HIF1a can be activated in skeletal muscle after physiological exercise, with increased HIF1a protein, nuclear localization, DNA‐binding activity and induction of HIF target genes, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and erythropoietin (EPO) (Ameln et al., 2005). These observations raise a fundamental question: how does HIF1a function in skeletal muscle, an organ that is highly dependent on oxidative metabolism but repeatedly exposed to transient increases in energetic demand?

Evidence over the past two decades indicates that HIF1a signalling in skeletal muscle is frequently engaged during cellular stress without sustained systemic hypoxia. Muscle‐specific [muscle creatine kinase (MCK)‐Cre] Hif1a deletion decreases exercise‐induced glycolytic gene responses, reduces lactate production and shifts muscle metabolism toward oxidative pathways, demonstrating that HIF1a contributes directly to metabolic control during energetic stress (Mason et al., 2004). More recent studies show that HIF1a also functions during physiological oxygenation, where postnatal muscle‐specific human skeletal actin (HSA)‐Cre driven Hif1a deletion increases mitochondrial oxidative function, ATP content and TCA cycle intermediates at the same time as reducing mitochondrial free radical generation and senescence‐associated features (Welch et al., 2024). Thus, a key question is how skeletal muscle regulates HIF1a under hypoxic, physiological and oxygen independent stress conditions, and how the duration of signalling determines adaptive vs. maladaptive outcomes. This review integrates genetic, human and systems‐level evidence to define HIF1a as a regulator of mitochondrial workload, substrate use, vascular remodelling and proteostasis across physiological and disease settings (Table 1).

Core mechanisms regulating HIF1a signalling

HIF1a signalling is regulated not only by protein stabilization, but also by nuclear accumulation, coactivator recruitment, DNA binding and post‐translational modification‐dependent transcriptional activity (Ameln et al., 2005; Hewitson et al., 2002). Factor inhibiting HIF (FIH) provides one mechanism that separates HIF1a protein abundance from transcriptional output. FIH hydroxylates an asparagine residue in the C‐terminal transactivation domain of HIF1a, limiting p300/CBP recruitment and suppressing transcriptional activation even when HIF1a protein is present (Hewitson et al., 2002). Thus, total HIF1a abundance, nuclear localization, DNA binding and HIF target gene expression are related but not interchangeable readouts.

HIF1a can also be stabilized by oxygen independent mechanisms that modify the canonical degradation pathway. Acidosis sequesters VHL in the nucleolus and allows HIF to evade oxygen‐dependent degradation, including in differentiated myotubes, indicating that pH can regulate HIF stability without a primary decrease in oxygen tension (Mekhail et al., 2004). Redox state provides another route for non‐hypoxic pathway activation. In pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells, thrombin and cobalt increased reactive oxygen species, HIF1a protein, VEGF mRNA and HIF‐dependent reporter activity, whereas vitamin C, glutathione peroxidase and catalase limited these responses (BelAiba et al., 2004). Mitochondrial metabolism may also generate a pseudohypoxic state in which redox signalling, substrate selection and HIF1a activation occur despite preserved oxygen availability (Archer et al., 2008; Bell et al., 2005). Because hypoxia and HIF1a activation can redirect TCA cycle carbon flux and alpha‐ketoglutarate metabolism, metabolite availability may further modify HIF1a regulation through effects on dioxygenase‐dependent hydroxylation and cellular redox balance (Wise et al., 2011).

Among post‐translational modifications, the metabolic and redox states can regulate HIF1a via lysine acetylation, independent of oxygen availability. The functional consequence of HIF1a acetylation is site‐specific rather than uniformly activating or destabilizing (Brahimi‐Horn et al., 2005; Minisini et al., 2023). Sirtuin (SIRT)1 deacetylates HIF1a at Lys674, reduces p300 recruitment and represses HIF target gene expression, linking NAD+‐dependent deacetylation to HIF1a transcriptional activity (Lim et al., 2010). By contrast, p300‐mediated acetylation at Lys709 increases HIF1a stability and transcriptional activity by reducing polyubiquitination (Geng et al., 2012). These findings indicate that acetylation regulates HIF1a signalling duration and transcriptional potency by modulating both protein stability and coactivator recruitment.

This redox–acetylation biology is relevant to skeletal muscle because mitochondrial metabolism and NAD+/NADH balance are highly dynamic during physiological and disease‐associated stress. In alcohol‐related liver disease models, ethanol stabilizes HIF1a in murine C2C12 and human‐induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived myotubes (hiPSCm), and in mouse skeletal muscle under ambient normoxia, without detectable cellular hypoxia as assessed by a hypoxia‐sensitive dye. Ethanol also reduces the NAD+/NADH ratio, decreases SIRT3 expression, increases protein acetylation, suppresses electron transport chain components, and induces a senescence‐associated molecular phenotype in myotubes and skeletal muscle (i.e. p16, p21, p‐P53, beta‐galactosidase activity). Correction of redox imbalance with mitochondrial‐targeted Lactobacillus brevis NADH oxidase (MitoLbNOX) reduces HIF1a stabilization, acetylation and myostatin expression (Welch et al., 2025). These data support a redox‐acetylation axis that sustains HIF1a stabilization under normoxia and suggest that increased acetylation may contribute to persistent HIF1a activity in skeletal muscle.

In parallel, phosphorylation of HIF1a can regulate localization, stability and degradation through oxygen independent mechanisms, linking kinase signalling to HIF1a transcriptional output (Minisini et al., 2023). Extracellular signal regulated kinase 1/2‐dependent phosphorylation at Ser641/Ser643 has been reported to enhance transcriptional activity by limiting chromosomal maintenance‐1/exportin‐1‐dependent nuclear export, whereas other phosphorylation events more directly affect HIF1a stability (Minisini et al., 2023). Serine/threonine kinase‐1 (PIM1)‐dependent phosphorylation can also stabilize HIF1a during normoxia by disrupting PHD‐dependent regulation (Casillas et al., 2021). Phosphorylation‐dependent control also varies with the temporal pattern of oxygen stress: in endothelial cells exposed to intermittent hypoxia, repeated hypoxia‐reoxygenation cycles progressively increased HIF1a phosphorylation, and protein kinase A inhibition reduced this phosphorylation pattern and the associated transcriptional responses (Toffoli et al., 2007). Although these studies are not skeletal muscle‐specific, they show that kinase signalling can modify HIF1a stability and transcriptional activity without sustained hypoxia. These data support the concept that, in skeletal muscle, HIF1a protein abundance alone is insufficient to define pathway activity. HIF1a abundance/transcriptional target responses reflect the combined effects of oxygen availability, PHD activity, FIH‐dependent coactivator restriction, pH, mitochondrial redox state, acetylation, phosphorylation, and ubiquitin‐mediated degradation (Fig. 1). These findings help explain how HIF1a can support transient adaptation during energetic stress but contribute to maladaptive remodelling when oxygen independent signalling persists.

Oxygen tension in skeletal muscle: Physiological gradients and diffusion constraints

In skeletal muscle, oxygen availability at the myofibre level is determined by microvascular partial pressure of oxygen (Po2), capillary‐myofibre diffusion, intracellular oxygen transport and mitochondrial oxygen consumption rather than arterial oxygen tension alone. Oxygen tension is therefore lower and more heterogeneous within skeletal muscle than in arterial blood. Modelling studies of myoglobin‐containing fibres show that intracellular oxygen gradients depend on sarcolemmal oxygen tension, myoglobin concentration, oxygen diffusivity and fibre/myobundle geometry (Federspiel, 1986). Human in vivo measurements further show that resting intracellular Po2 is lower than arterial Po2 and falls during ambient hypoxia, whereas exercise studies show much lower intracellular Po2 during contraction (Richardson et al., 2006). Contemporary models of capillary‐mitochondrial oxygen transport emphasize dynamic matching of oxygen delivery to oxygen utilization through changes in red blood cell flux, capillary haematocrit, longitudinal recruitment of the exchange surface, interstitial oxygen transport and mitochondrial demand (Poole & Musch, 2023).

During contraction, ATP turnover and mitochondrial respiration increase rapidly, creating transient mismatches between oxygen demand and local oxygen availability. Microvascular Po2 reflects the balance between oxygen delivery and muscle oxygen uptake, and recovery studies show that oxygen delivery and utilization are dynamically co‐ordinated after contractions (Behnke et al., 2009). These local gradients may be sufficient to reduce prolyl hydroxylase activity in selected regions despite preserved systemic oxygenation. Thus, arterial oxygen saturation, microvascular Po2, intracellular Po2 and mitochondrial oxygen flux are related but non‐equivalent measures of oxygen stress.

HIF1a activation in skeletal muscle should therefore be interpreted as evidence of oxygen‐sensitive or stress‐responsive signalling, not as direct proof of severe tissue hypoxia unless oxygen tension is measured at the relevant compartment. Increased mitochondrial oxygen consumption can lower intracellular oxygen availability and stabilize HIF1a under physiological oxygen concentrations in skeletal muscle cells (O'Hagan et al., 2009). Redox state can also regulate HIF signalling independently of hypoxia because thrombin‐ and cobalt‐induced HIF1a protein, VEGF mRNA and HIF‐dependent reporter activity are limited by antioxidant interventions in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (BelAiba et al., 2004). In skeletal muscle models of alcohol‐related liver disease, ethanol stabilizes HIF1a during ambient normoxia without detectable cellular hypoxia, whereas correction of redox imbalance reduces HIF1a stabilization and acetylation (Welch et al., 2025).

These distinctions separate physiological oxygen gradients from pathological hypoxia. During acute contraction, transient oxygen gradients may engage HIF1a as part of reversible adaptation to energetic demand. During chronic disease, prolonged diffusion limitation, repeated hypoxia‐reoxygenation, impaired oxygen delivery or persistent redox stress may sustain HIF1a signalling even when systemic hypoxemia is absent. Prolonged intermittent hypoxia in myotubes and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) muscle provides an example in which repeated hypoxia–normoxia cycling produces adaptive exhaustion, mitochondrial oxidative dysfunction, lower protein synthesis and incomplete restoration of homeostasis during normoxia (Attaway et al., 2023). Defining the severity, duration and compartment of oxygen limitation is therefore necessary for determining whether HIF1a signalling reflects adaptive oxygen sensing, metabolic demand or maladaptive oxygen independent pathway activation in skeletal muscle. These occur across systemic hypoxia, local diffusion limitation, normoxic physiological activation and oxygen independent stress states (Table 2).

Physiological HIF1a activation in skeletal muscle

Exercise‐associated HIF1a stabilization

Acute exercise causes a rapid rise in ATP turnover and can transiently increase diffusion limitation at the capillary–myofibre interface. Exercise‐associated HIF1a activation in human skeletal muscle occurs after a single bout of exercise, but the magnitude and readout depend on exercise modality, intensity, training status and biopsy timing (Ameln et al., 2005; Aragon‐Vela & Casuso, 2025). In healthy men performing 45 min of one‐legged knee‐extension exercise, HIF1a staining became more prevalent, HIF1a DNA‐binding activity increased and HIF target genes, including VEGF and EPO, were induced without a parallel increase in HIF1a mRNA (Ameln et al., 2005). VEGF mRNA increased further when blood flow to the exercising leg was restricted, supporting a model in which local oxygen delivery and metabolic demand regulate HIF1a‐linked angiogenic signalling during contraction (Ameln et al., 2005). Systematic evaluation of exercised human skeletal muscle further shows that HIF1a mRNA and protein increase after dynamic exercise, but VEGF mRNA does not correlate consistently with HIF1a mRNA, indicating that transcriptional measures alone do not fully define HIF1a activity or angiogenic output (Aragon‐Vela & Casuso, 2025). These findings help explain why exercise studies can show HIF1a pathway engagement without uniform or sustained VEGF‐mediated angiogenic remodelling.

Genetic studies support a direct role for HIF1a in regulating the metabolic response to exercise. Skeletal muscle‐specific (CKM‐Cre) HIF1a deletion decreases exercise‐induced expression of genes involved in glucose transport and glycolysis, including GLUT4, PFK‐M, PGK and LDH‐A, and reduces postexercise serum lactate (Mason et al., 2004). HIF1a‐deficient muscle compensates by increasing oxidative enzyme activity and fatty acid oxidation, preserving ATP during acute exertion and initially increasing exercise time (Mason et al., 2004). However, repeated exercise causes muscle damage and reduces performance in Hif1a‐deficient mice, indicating that HIF1a supports acute glycolytic adaptation and stress tolerance even though it limits oxidative metabolism (Mason et al., 2004). Thus, exercise‐associated HIF1a activation should be interpreted as a graded physiological response that helps match substrate use to acute energetic demand.

Training changes the relationship between exercise and HIF1a signalling. In human hypoxic training studies, 6 weeks of cycling under simulated altitude conditions increased HIF1a mRNA after both high‐ and low‐intensity hypoxic training, whereas myoglobin and VEGF mRNA increased only after high‐intensity hypoxic training (Vogt et al., 2001). These findings indicate that hypoxic exposure can engage HIF1a‐related transcriptional responses, but downstream oxygen transport and angiogenic genes depend on training intensity. In mice, endurance training improved running capacity in both wild‐type and skeletal muscle HIF1a‐deleted mice. However, untrained Hif1a‐deleted mice already showed features of an endurance‐trained oxidative phenotype, including lower respiratory exchange ratio during low‐intensity exercise, increased capillary‐to‐fibre ratio, higher oxidative enzyme activity, decreased phosphoinositide‐dependent kinase 1 (PDK1) expression and constitutive AMP‐activated protein kinase activation (Mason et al., 2007). These data suggest that reduced HIF1a activity favours oxidative remodelling, whereas HIF1a activity restrains oxidative metabolism in the untrained state. Additional evidence from high‐intensity interval training supports a role for HIF1a in exercise‐associated metabolic remodelling. In rat gastrocnemius muscle, high‐intensity interval training increased basal HIF1a protein abundance together with glycolytic enzyme activity, and pharmacological HIF1a stabilization during normoxia increased expression of genes involved in glycolysis, glycogenesis and lactate transport (Abe et al., 2015).

Consistent with this interpretation, skeletal muscle from elite endurance athletes and from individuals after 6 weeks of endurance training shows increased expression of negative regulators of HIF signalling, including PHD2, FIH and SIRT6, with reduced PDK1 expression (Lindholm et al., 2014). In a 3‐week high‐intensity interval training study, 83% of genes regulated after the first exercise bout were regulated in the same direction after the ninth bout, but the overall response was attenuated to ∼70% of the first bout, with especially strong attenuation in glycolysis‐related pathways (Norrbom et al., 2022). These findings indicate that training reduces selected HIF1a‐linked transcriptional responses as skeletal muscle becomes more oxidative and metabolically adapted. Exercise‐associated HIF1a activation should therefore be interpreted as graded and heterogeneous, reflecting the combined effects of local oxygen limitation, metabolic flux, training status, exercise modality, biopsy timing and post‐translational regulation.

Circadian timing and physiological HIF1a regulation

The magnitude and metabolic consequences of HIF1a activation also vary by time of day. In mice, acute exercise performed at different circadian phases produces distinct skeletal muscle transcriptomic and metabolomic responses, with early active‐phase exercise producing stronger activation of HIF1a‐linked pathways, glycolysis, lipid metabolism, branched‐chain amino acid breakdown and systemic energy expenditure (Sato et al., 2019). More recent work using skeletal muscle‐specific HIF1a knockout (KO) mice showed that HIF1a is required for time‐of‐day‐dependent metabolic responses to exercise. Loss of HIF1a reduced exercise‐induced lactate production, decreased glucose oxidation after exercise and shifted substrate handling toward oxidative pathways, especially during early rest‐phase exercise (Ehrlich et al., 2025). These findings indicate that HIF1a integrates exercise timing with substrate preference and glucose utilization.

Circadian regulation of HIF1a is also linked to the skeletal muscle clock. In diet‐induced obesity, skeletal muscle‐specific (HSA‐Cre) Bmal1 deletion worsens glucose tolerance and reduces HIF1a target gene expression, whereas genetic HIF1a stabilization restores glucose tolerance and a subset of dysregulated glycolytic genes (Chaikin et al., 2025). Although this model is not an exercise intervention, it supports a BMAL1–HIF axis through which circadian state can modify muscle glucose metabolism and HIF1a transcriptional output. Thus, physiological HIF1a activation in skeletal muscle is not uniform. Acute exercise can activate HIF1a protein, DNA binding and target gene expression; hypoxic training amplifies HIF1a‐related signals in an intensity‐dependent manner; training attenuates selected HIF1a‐linked transcriptional responses at the same time as favouring oxidative remodelling; and circadian timing shapes the metabolic consequences of pathway activation.

Metabolic reprogramming, redox signalling and mitochondrial quality control

HIF1a stabilization initiates co‐ordinated metabolic reprogramming in skeletal muscle. As previously described, skeletal muscle (MCK‐Cre) HIF1a deletion shifts substrate use away from glycolytic metabolism and toward oxidative and fatty acid‐dependent metabolism during energetic stress (Mason et al., 2004; Mason et al., 2007). Beyond substrate selection during exercise, basal HIF1a activity during normoxia appears to lower mitochondrial oxidative efficiency. Hif1a deletion in myotubes and skeletal muscle (HSA‐Cre) increases mitochondrial oxidative function, ATP content and TCA cycle intermediates at the same time as decreasing mitochondrial free radical generation and promoting a more fused mitochondrial network, suggesting greater oxidative efficiency (Welch et al., 2024).

Mitochondrial state can also regulate HIF1a through oxygen demand, redox signalling and metabolite availability. Peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma coactivator 1 alpha (PGC‐1a)‐driven mitochondrial biogenesis can stabilize HIF1a in skeletal muscle by increasing oxygen consumption to lower intracellular oxygen availability and activate HIF1a‐dependent gene expression (O'Hagan et al., 2009). Mitochondrial redox signalling can link oxygen sensing to HIF1a activation and metabolic remodelling, providing a mechanism by which mitochondrial function can modify HIF1a target gene responses even when ambient oxygen is unchanged (Archer et al., 2008). In parallel, hypoxia and HIF1a activation can redirect carbon flux through the TCA cycle. Studies in normal and cancerous cells show that hypoxia promotes reductive carboxylation of glutamine‐derived aKG into citrate and lipids (Wise et al., 2011). Although this pathway has not been fully defined in skeletal muscle, this metabolic route illustrates how oxygen‐sensitive signalling can redirect aKG and citrate flux with potential consequences for dioxygenase activity, redox balance and HIF1a regulation.

HIF1a‐dependent metabolic reprogramming also varies by time of day. In skeletal muscle, HIF1a mediates phase‐dependent metabolic responses by promoting glycolysis and glucose utilization at the same time as limiting reliance on oxidative pathways (Ehrlich et al., 2025). Muscle‐specific deletion (HSA‐Cre) of Hif1a shifts skeletal muscle toward oxidative metabolism, reduces exercise‐associated lactate production and alters glucose fate, indicating impaired switching between glucose and lipid use across the circadian cycle (Ehrlich et al., 2025). These data identify HIF1a as a regulator of substrate preference and metabolic flexibility during both acute stress and predictable metabolic states.

By contrast to its physiological role in co‐ordinating substrate use across acute and circadian metabolic states, HIF1a‐directed signalling becomes maladaptive under chronic non‐hypoxic stress. For example, in alcohol‐related liver disease models, ethanol stabilizes HIF1a during ambient normoxia at the same time as reducing the NAD+/NADH ratio, decreasing Sirt3 expression, suppressing electron transport chain components, increasing protein acetylation and inducing senescence‐associated molecular phenotype. Correction of redox imbalance with mitochondrial‐targeted NADH oxidase reduces HIF1a stabilization, acetylation and myostatin expression (Welch et al., 2025). These findings suggest that, when mitochondrial redox imbalance persists, HIF1a‐linked substrate switching no longer functions as a transient adaptive response but contributes to impaired oxidative phosphorylation, proteostatic stress and maladaptive remodelling.

Angiogenesis and oxygen delivery

Skeletal muscle vascular architecture is highly adaptable and tightly coupled to energetic (i.e. ATP) demand. HIF1a contributes to this plasticity primarily through transcriptional activation of VEGF, linking oxygen‐sensitive and workload‐associated signalling to angiogenic gene expression. However, VEGF mRNA is a signalling readout rather than a structural end‐point. Exercise‐induced VEGF induction should therefore not be interpreted as durable angiogenesis unless capillary structure, perfusion remodelling or related vascular end‐points are measured. Consistent with this distinction, exercise‐induced angiogenesis in skeletal muscle is regulated by the transcriptional coactivator PGC‐1a, supporting the concept that vascular remodelling reflects integrated metabolic and contractile signalling rather than HIF1a activation alone (Chinsomboon et al., 2009). Although HIF2a can also regulate vascular and hypoxia‐responsive transcriptional programs, HIF1a and HIF2a have distinct target‐gene preferences, and the human exercise literature in skeletal muscle has most directly linked acute VEGF induction to HIF1‐related readouts (Ameln et al., 2005; Gustafsson et al., 1999; Hu et al., 2003). In a 45 min, one‐legged knee‐extension model, dynamic exercise increased VEGF mRNA in human skeletal muscle and increased HIF1b mRNA, whereas HIF1a mRNA and FGF‐2 mRNA did not significantly increase (Gustafsson et al., 1999). VEGF mRNA changes correlated with changes in HIF1a and HIF1b mRNA and with femoral venous lactate, supporting a relationship between angiogenic gene expression and metabolic stress (Gustafsson et al., 1999). In a related human exercise study, HIF1a protein, nuclear localization, HIF DNA‐binding activity and HIF target genes including VEGF and EPO increased after acute exercise, and VEGF mRNA increased further when blood flow to the exercising leg was restricted (Ameln et al., 2005). These findings indicate that acute contraction can induce HIF1a‐linked angiogenic signalling, but, by themselves, they do not establish sustained capillary remodelling.

Training and blood flow restriction studies further show that hypoxia‐related signalling depends on workload and local metabolic stress. Six weeks of cycling under simulated altitude increased HIF1a mRNA after both high‐ and low‐intensity hypoxic training, whereas VEGF and myoglobin mRNA increased only after high‐intensity hypoxic training (Vogt et al., 2001). Similarly, low‐load resistance exercise with blood flow restriction increased HIF1a, HIF1b, VEGF, neural nitric oxide synthase and inducible nitric oxide synthase gene expression after 8 weeks, with greater HIF1a and HIF1b responses than low‐load and high‐load training without blood flow restriction (Volga Fernandes et al., 2022). However, this study did not measure capillary‐to‐fibre ratio, capillary contacts or other direct structural angiogenesis end‐points (Volga Fernandes et al., 2022). Systematic evaluation of exercised human skeletal muscle also shows that VEGF responses are heterogeneous and do not consistently correlate with HIF1a mRNA, indicating that HIF1a transcript levels alone do not reliably define angiogenic output (Aragon‐Vela & Casuso, 2025). Thus, hypoxia or HIF1a activation alone is insufficient to infer durable angiogenesis. In skeletal muscle, angiogenic remodelling is most probably when oxygen‐sensitive signalling occurs together with contraction, metabolic flux, perfusion changes and repeated workload‐associated stimuli.

Fibre‐type and contractile phenotype

The influence of HIF1a on contractile phenotype is less clear than its effects on metabolic programming. Skeletal muscle‐specific (MCK‐Cre) HIf1a deletion produces an endurance‐like metabolic phenotype with enhanced oxidative capacity, but this does not establish that HIF1a is a primary determinant of stable fibre‐type identity (Mason et al., 2004). This distinction is important because HIF‐dependent changes in oxidative metabolism, glycolytic gene expression and fatigue resistance can occur without a parallel or durable shift in myosin heavy chain composition. Basal HIF1a expression differs across fibre types, with relatively higher levels observed in fast oxidative–glycolytic fibres compared to slow oxidative fibres (Mason et al., 2004). HIF1a overexpression promotes a shift toward fast myosin heavy chain expression, whereas skeletal muscle–specific (MCK‐Cre) Hif1a deletion induces an endurance‐like metabolic phenotype characterized by enhanced oxidative capacity without major changes in fibre‐type composition at rest (Mason et al., 2004). Discrepancies among models probably reflect differences in developmental timing, injury context, hypoxia severity and the relative contributions of HIF1a and HIF2a. Overall, HIF1a appears to regulate myofibre metabolic phenotype more consistently than fixed contractile identity. This distinction is important for interpreting regeneration studies, where HIF‐dependent effects may reflect satellite‐cell state, local hypoxia, inflammatory signalling, vascular disruption or energetic demand rather than a stable fibre‐type program.

Satellite cells and regeneration

Skeletal muscle regeneration occurs within a low‐oxygen microenvironment that influences satellite cell fate. Moderate, time‐limited hypoxic signalling can support satellite cell activation and maintenance of stem‐like features, whereas severe or prolonged hypoxia impairs differentiation and regeneration (Nguyen et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2017). Available data also suggest isoform‐specific effects, with HIF2a linked more strongly to quiescent stemness and HIF1a to activated progenitor states (Xie et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2017). HIF1a and HIF2a are dispensable for embryonic muscle development under normal conditions, but postnatal satellite cell‐specific deletion (Pax7‐Cre) of HIF1a/HIF2a delays injury‐induced muscle repair and reduces satellite‐cell self‐renewal under hypoxic conditions (Yang et al., 2017). Consistent with this isoform‐specific model, quiescent satellite cells reside in an intrinsically hypoxic niche and preferentially express HIF2a rather than HIF1a; HIF2a maintains quiescence and self‐renewal, whereas transient HIF2a inhibition increases satellite‐cell proliferation and accelerates regeneration (Xie et al., 2018).

Recent data from ageing muscle extend this concept by linking HIF1a to satellite‐cell epigenetic state. In aged mouse tibialis anterior satellite cells, HIF1a protein abundance was reduced, whereas pharmacological reactivation with roxadustat restored HIF signalling, increased intracellular lactate, increased global histone lactylation, reduced p16INK4a, increased Pax7 and promoted formation of hypertrophic myotubes after differentiation (Piccoli et al., 2026). These findings suggest that HIF signalling can support regenerative competence in satellite cells when activation is appropriately timed and linked to a quiescent or repair‐permissive state.

HIF1a also contributes to muscle resilience during repeated stress. Skeletal muscle‐specific (MCK‐Cre) Hif1a deletion does not prevent baseline muscle development, but repeated exercise causes muscle damage and reduced performance in Hif1a‐deficient mice, indicating impaired adaptation to recurrent contractile injury (Mason et al., 2004). Mitochondrial control of HIF1a also regulates later stages of myofibre maturation. In regenerating skeletal muscle, Mfn2 is induced during muscle stem cell activation and is required for growth and adult fate specification of new myofibres; loss of Mfn2 causes sustained HIF1a activity, loss of H3K27me3 repression at the neonatal Myh8 locus and arrest at the neonatal‐to‐adult fibre transition (Wang et al., 2022). Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of HIF1a releases this maturation arrest and accelerates regeneration, indicating that HIF1a functions as a regenerative checkpoint when pathway activation persists after injury (Wang et al., 2022). Thus, efficient regeneration probably requires time‐limited co‐ordinated HIF1a and HIF2a signalling that supports satellite‐cell activation, metabolic adaptation and stress resolution. By contrast, prolonged hypoxia, repeated hypoxia‐reoxygenation or persistent oxygen independent HIF1a stabilization may reduce regenerative efficiency by impairing mitochondrial function, protein homeostasis, and cellular stress resolution.

Oxygen independent and disease‐associated HIF1a activation

In addition to reduced oxygen availability, HIF1a can be stabilized or functionally activated by pH changes, redox imbalance, mitochondrial stress, altered metabolite availability, ammonia, ethanol and repeated hypoxia‐reoxygenation. These mechanisms are relevant to skeletal muscle because energetic demand, mitochondrial oxygen consumption, NAD+/NADH balance and protein homeostasis change rapidly during contraction and are chronically perturbed in disease. Acidosis is one mechanism by which HIF1a can be stabilized without a primary decrease in oxygen tension. Low extracellular pH sequesters VHL in the nucleolus and permits HIF1a to evade oxygen‐dependent degradation, including in differentiated myotubes (Mekhail et al., 2004). Redox stress provides another oxygen independent route. In pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells, thrombin and cobalt increase reactive oxygen species, HIF1a protein, VEGF mRNA and HIF‐dependent reporter activity, whereas antioxidant interventions reduce these responses (BelAiba et al., 2004). Mitochondrial redox signalling may therefore generate a pseudohypoxic state in which HIF1a activation reflects altered electron transport, reactive oxygen species or redox buffering rather than true hypoxia (Archer et al., 2008).

Metabolic intermediates can also modify HIF1a regulation. Because PHD enzymes require oxygen, iron and aKG, shifts in mitochondrial metabolism or aKG availability can alter HIF1a degradation. Hyperammonaemia stress (HAS) is relevant in chronic liver disease because ammonia metabolism intersects with aKG, glutamate, mitochondrial function and proteostasis. In cancer stem‐like cells, HIF1a contributes to cellular adaptation during HAS, supporting the broader concept that nitrogen stress can engage HIF1a‐linked survival programs (Kitajima et al., 2017). In skeletal muscle and myotubes exposed to hyperammonaemia, integrated multiomics analyses identify mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired proteostasis and metabolic pathway remodelling, consistent with a disease state in which nitrogen stress and mitochondrial metabolism converge on HIF1a‐linked pathways (Welch et al., 2021). Exercise‐associated phosphoproteomic responses also overlap with hyperammonaemia‐associated signalling, suggesting that pathways engaged during physiological adaptation can become maladaptive during persistent ammonia exposure (Welch et al., 2022).

Repeated hypoxia‐reoxygenation is distinct from sustained hypoxia. Prolonged intermittent hypoxia in myotubes and COPD skeletal muscle causes adaptive exhaustion, mitochondrial oxidative dysfunction, reduced protein synthesis and incomplete restoration of homeostasis during normoxic recovery (Attaway et al., 2023). This pattern indicates that repeated cycling between low oxygen and reoxygenation can impair skeletal muscle homeostasis through combined oxygen‐sensitive and redox‐dependent mechanisms. Intermittent hypoxia should therefore be considered separately from sustained hypoxia because the reoxygenation phase can amplify oxidative stress and prolong HIF1a‐linked remodelling.

Ethanol is an example of oxygen independent stabilization of HIF1a in skeletal muscle. In alcohol‐related liver disease models, ethanol stabilizes HIF1a in C2C12 myotubes, hiPSCm and mouse skeletal muscle during ambient normoxia, without detectable cellular hypoxia using a hypoxic adduct‐sensitive dye. Ethanol also reduces the NAD+/NADH ratio, decreases SIRT3 expression, suppresses electron transport chain components, increases protein acetylation and induces senescence‐associated molecular features (i.e. Correction of mitochondrial redox imbalance with MitoLbNOX reduces HIF1a stabilization, acetylation and myostatin expression (Welch et al., 2025). These findings support a model in which ethanol‐induced redox imbalance sustains HIF1a through oxygen independent mechanisms and links HIF1a stabilization to impaired mitochondrial function, proteostatic stress and senescence‐associated remodelling.

Physiological interventions may modify these disease‐associated pathways. Voluntary activity wheel running improves hyperammonaemia‐induced skeletal muscle molecular and metabolic perturbations in mice, suggesting that contraction‐associated adaptation can oppose selected metabolic stress responses (Bellar et al., 2025). These data do not establish exercise as a direct HIF1a‐targeted therapy, but they support the broader concept that skeletal muscle stress pathways are reversible when the initiating metabolic disturbance is reduced or counterbalanced. Together, these studies show that HIF1a activation in skeletal muscle can arise from true hypoxia, intermittent hypoxia‐reoxygenation, pH shifts, mitochondrial redox imbalance, ammonia, ethanol and altered metabolite availability, with adaptive or maladaptive consequences determined by context, duration, reversibility and metabolic state (Table 3). These inputs are not interchangeable and their consequences also depend on the compartment in which HIF1a signalling occurs. This distinction is central to interpreting HIF1a in skeletal muscle pathology: HIF1a is not only a marker of oxygen deficiency, but also a sensor and amplifier of metabolic stress when oxygen independent signalling persists.

Therapeutic opportunities and unresolved questions

Pharmacological manipulation of oxygen‐sensing pathways requires caution. Prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors activate HIF‐mediated transcription and are used clinically to increase endogenous EPO production in selected anaemia contexts (Gao et al., 2022). However, systemic HIF stabilization may also influence skeletal muscle metabolism, mitochondrial function, angiogenic signalling and protein turnover. Consistently, repeated pharmacological HIF1a stabilization in diaphragm muscle increases extracellular matrix proteins and profibrotic mediators, including collagen, fibronectin and CTGF/CCN2, indicating that chronic HIF1a activation can promote fibrotic remodelling rather than adaptive repair (Valle‐Tenney et al., 2020). Because HIF1a and HIF2a share oxygen‐dependent regulatory mechanisms but have distinct transcriptional outputs, the tissue‐level effects of HIF‐directed therapy may depend on isoform balance, dose, treatment duration, baseline oxygenation and the metabolic state of skeletal muscle (Hu et al., 2003). At the same time, recent satellite‐cell studies suggest that PHD inhibition may restore selected HIF1a‐linked regenerative programs during ageing, indicating that therapeutic effects may differ between aged progenitor cells and mature myofibres (Piccoli et al., 2026).

In selected tissues, controlled HIF activation can be protective, including in liver injury models, but the same intervention may not have similar effects in skeletal muscle (Gao et al., 2022). In skeletal muscle, exercise provides a physiological means to repeatedly engage HIF1a without sustained activation. However, the response to exercise depends on oxygen availability, timing, disease state and recovery. Hypoxic exposure can reduce the transcriptional plasticity normally elicited by exercise, and prolonged intermittent hypoxia is associated with adaptive exhaustion, mitochondrial oxidative dysfunction, reduced protein synthesis and incomplete restoration of homeostasis during normoxic recovery (Attaway et al., 2023; Lundby et al., 2009; Norrbom et al., 2022). Strategies that limit recurrent systemic hypoxemia may help preserve muscle remodelling capacity and protein homeostasis in chronic disease.

Several mechanistic questions remain unresolved and have direct therapeutic implications for skeletal muscle. First, what determines whether HIF1a signalling remains transient vs. prolonged enough to drive muscle dysfunction? Redox‐driven HIF1a stabilization and co‐ordinated acetylome remodelling in ethanol‐associated muscle dysfunction indicate that oxygen independent mechanisms can sustain HIF1a activity, providing potential upstream therapeutic targets (Welch et al., 2025). Muscle regeneration studies demonstrate temporal effects of HIF1a: inhibition of sustained HIF1a signalling can accelerate myofibre maturation after injury, whereas HIF1a reactivation may support regenerative programs in aged satellite cells depending on cellular state and timing (Piccoli et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2022). Second, do HIF1a post‐translational modifications predict transcriptional output better than total HIF1a abundance? Future studies should integrate HIF1a protein abundance with measures of nuclear localization, hydroxylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, coactivator recruitment and target‐gene expression. Third, how do fibre type, recruitment pattern, circadian timing, sex, age and disease state modify HIF1a output during exercise and recovery (Aragon‐Vela & Casuso, 2025; Chaikin et al., 2025; Ehrlich et al., 2025)? Finally, functional overlap and divergence between HIF1a and HIF2a complicate therapeutic targeting and isoform‐specific analyses are needed to distinguish compensatory from disease‐promoting signalling (Hu et al., 2003). Because HIF1a transcriptional output is determined by nuclear localization, hydroxylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, coactivator recruitment and target‐gene selectivity, future studies should evaluate pathway activity rather than relying on total HIF1a abundance alone. Overall, therapeutic targeting of HIF1a in skeletal muscle should focus less on simple activation or inhibition and more on correcting the upstream condition that maintains HIF signalling.

Conclusions

HIF1a in skeletal muscle is best considered a context‐dependent regulator of metabolic stress rather than a binary response to hypoxia. Oxygen availability, mitochondrial redox state, energetic demand and circadian timing determine how HIF1a functions across physiological oxygen levels to co‐ordinate signalling in response to fluctuating cellular needs. The consequences of HIF1a activation depend primarily on duration: transient activation (exercise or diffusion‐limited hypoxia) for adaptive responses, whereas prolonged activation (repeated hypoxemia or oxygen independent redox and acetylation pathways) impairs mitochondrial plasticity, suppresses anabolic signalling, and promotes fibrosis and senescence‐associated remodelling.

Human studies support this interpretation, showing that hypoxia blunts the transcriptional response to exercise and that functionally important HIF1a signalling can occur despite minimal changes in steady‐state protein levels, emphasizing the importance of temporal and spatial regulation. Defining conditions that maintain adaptive HIF1a signalling and identifying mechanisms that drive persistent maladaptive responses will help develop strategies to preserve muscle plasticity without causing chronic dysfunction.
