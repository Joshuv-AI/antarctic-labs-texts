Boson peak as a phenomenon participated by the vast majority of particles
Cunyuan Jiang

Abstract
The origin of the excess vibrational density of states (DOS) beyond Debye’s theory in amorphous solids (often referred to as the Boson peak) has been attributed to the presence of quasi-localized vibrational modes in recent years. However, by dispersing the total DOS onto each degree of freedom (DOF), the results of this report provide evidence that 99.9 % 99.9\% of DOFs, and hence almost all particles, contribute to the Boson peak (BP). These results challenge the prevailing opinion that BP is contributed by a minority of particles and highlight its long-neglected global and collective origin.



The nature of the anomalous vibrational density of states (DOS) in amorphous solids remains an ongoing debate since the seminal work by Zeller
et al.
in 1971
Zeller and Pohl 1971
. In crystals, due to discrete translational symmetry, the DOS in the low-frequency region can be well described by Debye’s theory
Debye 1912
, which explains the dimensionality dependence of the scaling law
g(\omega)\propto\omega^{d-1}
, where
\omega
is the frequency,
g(\omega)
is the DOS as a function of frequency, and
d
is the spatial dimensionality of the crystal. For amorphous solids, whose microscopic structure is random yet compact, the violation of Debye’s law in the low-frequency region—commonly referred to as the Boson peak (BP) in the literature because it appears as a peak in the DOS reduced by Debye’s law,
g(\omega)/\omega
—has been widely observed in various systems
Zeller and Pohl 1971
;
Pérez-Castañeda et al. 2014
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Tan et al. 2012
;
Rainone et al. 2020
;
González-Jiménez et al. 2023
. After half a century of exploration, the nature of BP is often attributed to a type of excitation termed “(quasi)-localized modes,” as mentioned, for example, in Refs.
Wang et al. 2019
;
Tan et al. 2012
;
Rainone et al. 2020
. Although the morphology and properties of such localized modes remain quite controversial, the consensus in this scenario can be summarized as follows: only a minority of particles participate in localized modes at low frequencies and thereby contribute to BP. This view is quantitatively supported by the unusually low participation ratio
Richard et al. 2020
;
Lerner et al. 2016
;
Hu and Tanaka 2022
(the fraction of particles participating in the vibrational modes) at low frequencies. However, as a decisive piece of evidence for microscopic origin of BP in amorphous solids, the descriptions of localized modes, soft spots, vibrational defects, and the definition of participation ratio remain rather phenomenological and lack multi-validation to support the claim that BP is contributed by only a minority of particles.

To determine what percentage of particles contribute to BP, we combine dynamical simulation with the Green’s function method and disperse the total DOS (including BP) onto each degree of freedom (DOF) (totaling
8192
DOFs) of a two-dimensional glass containing
4096
particles. The results show that
8184/8192\sim 99.9\%
of the local DOS of the DOFs exhibit the trend of BP. This conclusion does not support the opinion that BP is contributed by a minority of particles and inspires the development and consideration of new mechanisms that match its global and collective origin.

As a mature method to obtain the structure of amorphous solids, we annealed
4096
bidispersed particles with a mixture ratio of
1:1
and a size ratio of
1.4:1
interacting through the harmonic potential. At the configuration with minimum total potential energy
U
, the Hessian matrix can be obtained by computing the second derivatives with respect to each DOF
x_{i(j)}
:
H_{ij}=\partial^{2}H/\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}
. Here, the DOFs
x_{i(j)}
include both the
x
and
y
coordinates of all particles. The dynamical response properties can be obtained using the Green’s function (
G
) method once the Hessian matrix
H
of the system is known. Their relation is

G(\omega)=[(\omega^{2}+i\eta)I-H]^{-1},

(1)

with
\eta=0^{+}
a small number ensuring causality,
[]^{-1}
denoting the inverse of a matrix, and
I
the identity matrix. The total DOS can be easily obtained using the Green’s function method as
g(\omega)=-\omega\mathrm{Im}[\mathrm{Tr}[G(\omega)]]

\mathrm{Tr}[]

Baggioli and Zaccone 2019

i

g_{i}(\omega)=-\omega\mathrm{Im}[G_{ii}(\omega)]

g(\omega)/\omega=-\mathrm{Im}[\mathrm{Tr}[G(\omega)]]
and
g_{i}(\omega)/\omega=-\mathrm{Im}[G_{ii}(\omega)]

-\dfrac{\partial^{2}\mathrm{Im}[G_{ii}(\omega)]}{\partial\omega^{2}}\Bigg|_{\omega\to 0}.

(2)

If the second derivative coefficient at low frequency is positive, it means that the curve of the normalized local DOS has an upward trend, violating Debye’s law and hence contributing to BP. Fig.
1
-c shows the distribution of the second derivative coefficient at low frequency. The vast majority—
8184/8192
curves—have a positive coefficient. That the vast majority of DOFs contribute to BP is the main message of this report.

Another point worthy of discussion is that some DOFs (
182/8192
, with a maximum higher than
0.1
as mentioned above) have normalized local DOS with a strong peak in the BP region. Do these “highly-excited” DOFs contribute the most to BP? We have two reasons to deny this consideration. On one hand, the height of BP is a measure of the violation of Debye’s law, which aligns with the meaning of the second derivative coefficient discussed above. The distribution of the second derivative coefficient is rather concentrated, as shown in Fig.
1
-c, indicating that the violation of Debye’s law is similar for all DOFs. On the other hand, the percentage of DOFs with a strong peak in the BP region is small. The height weighted by the number of DOFs yields the opposite conclusion:

0.1\times 182<0.01\times(8192-182).

(3)

Here, the height of the strong peak is assumed to average
0.1
and that of the weak peak
0.01
, according to Fig.
1
-b. While the minority contributes strongly on an individual basis, the collective contribution of the majority dominates due to their sheer number. This discussion strengthens the main message that the vast majority of DOFs contribute to BP.

In the mainstream literature, BP is attributed to a minority of particles in the scenario of localized modes and vibrational defects. The main message of this report questions that opinion and suggests that BP is a phenomenon participated in by the vast majority of particles. Although the conclusion is based on simulation data of a two-dimensional glass, its representativeness is universal, and the analysis can be easily applied to other systems. The results also encourage the development of a new scenario to interpret BP beyond localized modes, in which the long-neglected global and collective mechanisms should be properly considered.
