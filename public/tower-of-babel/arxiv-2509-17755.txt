Learning Neural Antiderivatives
Submission 1007, F. Rubab 1,2, N. E. Nsampi 1, M. Balint 1, F. Mujkanovic 1, H.-P. Seidel 1, T. Ritschel 3 and T. Leimkühler 1 1 Max-Planck-Institut für Informatik 2 Michigan State University 3 University College London

Abstract
Neural fields offer continuous, learnable representations that extend beyond traditional discrete formats in visual computing. We study the problem of learning neural representations of repeated antiderivatives directly from a function, a continuous analogue of summed-area tables. Although widely used in discrete domains, such cumulative schemes rely on grids, which prevents their applicability in continuous neural contexts. We introduce and analyze a range of neural methods for repeated integration, including both adaptations of prior work and novel designs. Our evaluation spans multiple input dimensionalities and integration orders, assessing both reconstruction quality and performance in downstream tasks such as filtering and rendering. These results enable integrating classical cumulative operators into modern neural systems and offer insights into learning tasks involving differential and integral operators.

Introduction and Related Work

Neural representations are playing an increasingly prominent role in modeling core primitives in visual computing.
Moving beyond traditional, specialized formats – such as pixel grids for images or triangle meshes for surface geometry – neural fields
[
XTS*22
]
offer a unified and continuous representation by mapping spatial coordinates to function values
[
DKE*22
]
.
These representations are not hand-crafted, but learned from data via gradient-based optimization.
Especially important are cases where the available data originate in a different domain than the one being reconstructed, with the two connected by a differentiable forward model, enabling the solution of inverse problems.
Popular examples of this paradigm include learning a scene’s appearance from image observations
[
MST*20
]
, where the forward model is volume rendering, and learning a signed distance function (SDF) from depth maps
[
LZP*20
]
, where the forward model is sphere tracing.

In this work, we consider a specific, yet highly fundamental, inverse problem to be addressed using neural fields: learning repeated antiderivatives.
Specifically, given, and given only, training samples from a function with one or more input variables, our goal is to obtain a representation that encodes the result of integration of that function – possibly multiple times and along multiple dimensions. Unlike a single integration which only yields box filtering, repeated integration enables weighted aggregation through higher-order splines, as we show in applications.

In the context of discrete representations, this problem has been the subject of extensive research in computer graphics, computer vision, and adjacent fields.
Summed-area tables (SAT)
[
Cro84
]
– also known as prefix sums or integral images – are regular grids that store the (repeated) cumulative sum of an underlying tabulated function, and constitute a fundamental data structure.

While SATs work in the pixel basis, pre-integration of other basis functions is possible, such as for (moving) least squares
[
Lev98
]
(Example 2), for splines
[
ANW67
]
or subdivision surfaces
[
CC78
]
.
We would not be aware of a general way to do this for neural representations.

Over the decades, SATs have supported a wide range of applications, including
filtering
[
Hec86
,
BR07
]
,
pattern matching
[
VJ01
,
Vek03
,
FLM14
,
BTV06
,
Por05
,
WJWX24
]
,
rendering
[
HSC*05
,
HSSL07
,
Lau07
,
LZW*21
]
,
media streaming
[
LDB*21
]
, neural architecture design
[
BMT*23
,
BTD*23
,
YJR25
]
,
and multiscale modeling
[
ZHB*24
,
LFV23
,
AWKK21
]
.
The efficient computation of cumulative sums has emerged as a productive research area in its own right
[
Ble89
,
SHZO07
,
NMLH11
,
KNI14
,
CRP*15
,
UBD13
]
.
However, the inherent dependence on regular grids renders all these methods unsuitable for continuous neural representations.

Motivated by recent advances in the signal-processing capabilities of neural fields
[
YBHK21
,
XWJ*22
,
NDS*23
,
MNT*24
]
, as well as their success in solving inverse problems involving differential equations
[
RPK19
,
CG22
]
, we investigate the neural, continuous analogue of summed-area tables: learning repeated integrals from sampled data.
After reviewing the necessary mathematical preliminaries (Sec.
2
), we define the problem setting in Sec.
3
.
Although analytic solutions exist for obtaining single-integral functions using a highly restricted class of shallow neural architectures
[
Sub21
,
LIA20
]
, scalable and broadly applicable approaches require dedicated learning strategies.
To this end, we provide a hierarchical taxonomy of methods (Sec.
4
, Fig.
1
) – some adapted from existing techniques originally developed in different contexts, and others representing novel contributions to the design space.
We evaluate each class of methods across modalities with varying input dimensionalities and integration orders, employing a range of computational tools – including assessments of antiderivative quality on synthetic and real data, as well as performance evaluation in downstream tasks such as filtering and rendering (Sec.
5
).
We conclude with concrete recommendations (Sec.
6
).

In summary, our contributions are:

•

A taxonomy of methods for learning repeated antiderivatives from function samples, including novel method designs.

•

An evaluation of these methods across signal modalities, input dimensionalities, and integration orders.

•

A demonstration of the practical impact of method choice in downstream tasks such as filtering and rendering.

All source code and trained models are available at
https://neural-antiderivatives.mpi-inf.mpg.de/
.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we review mathematical concepts relevant to our task and its solutions.
Specifically, we recall techniques for reducing repeated integrals (Sec.
2.1
) and provide a discussion of gradient bias in stochastic estimators (Sec.
2.2
).

2.1
Integral Reduction

Repeated integrals of a function

f(x):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

of the form

\int^{(n)}\!\!f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x^{(n)}\coloneq\underbrace{\int\!\!\ldots\!\int}_{n\text{ times}}f(x)\,\,\underbrace{\vphantom{\int}\mathrm{d}x\ldots\mathrm{d}x}_{n\text{ times}}

(1)

can be reduced to equivalent formulations involving only single integrals.
We recall two techniques in this context:
The classical formula of Cauchy
[
Cau23
]

\int^{(n)}\!\!f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x^{(n)}=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\int_{-\infty}^{x}(x-x^{\prime})^{n-1}f(x^{\prime})\,\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

(2)

and a recent formulation by Haddad
[
Had21
]

\int^{(n)}\!\!f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x^{(n)}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{n,l}\,\,x^{l-1}\int x^{l}f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x,

(3)

which uses scalar coefficients

\alpha_{n,l}=\frac{(-1)^{n-l}}{(l-1)!(n-l)!}.

There are two fundamental differences between these reduction formulas.
First, Eq.
2
collapses the repeated integral into a single integral, whereas Eq.
3
rewrites it as a sum of
n
separate single integrals.
Second, the individual summands in Eq.
3
are genuine antiderivatives, whereas the integrand in Eq.
2
depends on the upper integration limit – i.e., it is an explicit function of the evaluation coordinate
x
.
In other words, the reduction in Equation Eq.
2
yields a spatially varying single integral, whereas the reduction in Equation Eq.
3
results in a sum of stationary antiderivatives.

2.2
Gradient Bias of Stochastic Estimators

Consider the stochastic computation

f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\xi)
,
where
\xi
denotes a random input to a deterministic model
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, which is parameterized by trainable parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
Our objective is to learn
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
via gradient descent, using a loss function
\mathcal{L}
, i.e., we are interested in computing

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\xi)\right]\right).

(4)

In practice, computing the expectation

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\xi)\right]

is infeasible and therefore approximated using a Monte Carlo estimator
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
Unfortunately, the resulting expected gradient

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\right]

(5)

is generally biased with respect to the true gradient defined in Eq.
4
, even when
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is itself an unbiased estimator, for two reasons.
First, the interchange of the loss gradient and the expectation in the transition from Eq.
4
to Eq.
5
is valid only when the loss gradient is a linear function of its argument.
This condition is naturally satisfied only by the squared error (L2) loss
[
NRN*23
]
; any other loss function leads to biased gradient estimates.
The second source of bias becomes apparent upon applying the chain rule to expand Eq.
5
:

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}\left(\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)}{\partial\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right].

(6)

Both the outer and inner derivatives involve the same random variable
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
Since the expectation of a product equals the product of the expectations only when the random variables are independent, this dependence introduces a bias
[
ALKN19
]
, whose magnitude scales with the variance of
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

[
DLW*22
]
.

In practice, gradient bias in stochastic models can be mitigated using two countermeasures:
First, by employing the L2 loss; and second, by explicitly computing the product in Eq.
6
using estimators
\hat{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with different random inputs
\xi
for each factor.

3
Setting

We consider the setting where a given signal

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{o}}

can be sampled, but is otherwise unknown.
Sampling is possible at any arbitrary position, hence we do not solve a (scattered) data interpolation problem
[
Lev98
]
, neither do we extrapolate, but purely seek to represent a signal and its antiderivative.
We assume that
f
defined on the unit hyper-cube

\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
and leave analysis on other domains, e.g., 3D surface manifolds to future work.
The samples are denoted by

\mathcal{S}=\left\{\left(x_{1,i},\dots,x_{d,i},f_{i}\right)\right\}_{i},

(7)

where

f_{i}\coloneq f(x_{1,i},\dots,x_{d,i})
.
Our goal is to obtain the continuous, repeated antiderivative

F^{(n)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{o}},

i.e., the result of applying
n
-fold integration to
f
along each of the
d
input dimensions, resulting in a total of

d\times n

integration steps:

F^{(n)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\coloneq\int^{(n)}\!\!\!\!\ldots\!\int^{(n)}\!\!f(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\,\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{(n)}\ldots\mathrm{d}x_{d}^{(n)}.

(8)

To support notational clarity and a consistent evaluation structure, we assume that all
d
input dimensions are integrated the same number of times,
n
.
This cases arises most frequently in practical applications.
However, all formulations and analysis results naturally extend to the more general case where the number of integrations may vary across dimensions.
In our setting, we further ignore the (higher-order) constants of integration – a single integral is defined up to a constant, a double integral up to a constant and a linear term, and so on – as these terms cancel out exactly in practical applications of
F^{(n)}
.

While numerical integration would, in principle, allow the computation of
F^{(n)}
at any specific coordinate
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
in isolation
[
Caf98
]
, our objective is to learn the full continuous antiderivative as a unified model

(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\approx F^{(n)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}),

parameterized by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
,
which enables efficient evaluation at arbitrary coordinates.
To this end, we employ a neural field
[
XTS*22
]
to represent , due to its flexibility in modeling diverse modalities and dimensionalities.

In the remainder of this work, we explore different methods for the non-trivial task of learning from
\mathcal{S}
.
In this context, we largely disregard the influence of specific neural architectures, as their impact proved to be minor compared to that of the learning strategy.
After a reasonably comprehensive pilot architecture search, we adopted a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with Swish activation functions
[
RZL17
]
and axis-aligned positional encoding
[
MST*20
,
TSM*20
]
for all our experiments – an architecture that has also been identified as effective in a related integration setting
[
LMW21
]
.
The network has four hidden layers with 256 neurons each and is trained using the Adam
[
KB15
]
optimizer. We use the default Adam parameters with a learning rate of
10^{-3}
.
To isolate the effect of different supervision structures, we refrain from varying the loss function except where strictly necessary (Sec.
2.2
).
A pilot study indicated that the Huber loss yields the most consistent performance across integration orders, input dimensionalities, and signal modalities. Training is run for
100{,}000
iterations for methods based on automatic differentiation and direct integral supervision, and for
200{,}000
iterations when numerical differentiation is employed. Batch sizes vary depending on the integration order, input dimensionality, and method, due to time and memory considerations.

.

for the taxonomy tree and Fig.

for graphical illustrations.

4.1
Integral Supervision

In this method, the samples
\mathcal{S}
are used to numerically estimate the target antiderivative
F^{(n)}
at specific coordinates, and the model is trained to match this estimate (Fig.
2
, left).
Since our objective is to compute a
repeated
integral, this naturally gives rise to a nested Monte Carlo estimator
[
RCY*18
]
.
However, we observe that such an estimator fails to converge to reasonable antiderivative estimates under practical compute constraints, due to the nesting induced by repeated integration.
We therefore turn to integral reduction.
Although both formulas in Sec.
2.1
are compatible with Monte Carlo sampling, Cauchy’s reduction (Eq.
2
) involves only a single integration.
Consequently, we adopt this formulation for our task:

\begin{split}&F^{(n)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\approx\hat{F}^{(n)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\\
\coloneq&\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}}{N(n-1)!}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(x_{j}-x_{j,k}\right)^{n-1}f(x_{1,k},\dots,x_{d,k}),\end{split}

(9)

where
N
denotes the number of Monte Carlo samples, and

x_{j,k}\sim\mathcal{U}\left[0,x_{j}\right]
,
i.e., we draw samples uniformly from the
d
-dimensional, axis-aligned hyperrectangle defined by the origin and the current coordinate
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
.
In practice, convergence is improved by using
d
-dimensional Sobol sequences
[
Sob67
]
to generate the sample locations.
The estimate
\hat{F}^{(n)}
can then be used to directly train the model via the loss

\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\Omega}\left[\left\|(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})-\hat{F}^{(n)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\right\|\right].

(10)

4.2
Differential Supervision

At their core, differential supervision strategies are grounded in the fundamental theorem of calculus
[
Ste12
]
, which, when applied to our setting, takes the form

\frac{\partial^{dn}}{\partial x_{1}^{n}\dots\partial x_{d}^{n}}F^{(n)}=f,

(11)

i.e., integration and differentiation act as inverse operations.
This motivates a class of training strategies whose loss takes the general form

\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\Omega}\left[\left\|\mathcal{D}\,(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})-\mathcal{B}\,f(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\right\|\right],

(12)

where the operator

\mathcal{D}\approx\frac{\partial^{dn}}{\partial x_{1}^{n}\dots\partial x_{d}^{n}}

(13)

approximates the repeated differentiation described in Eq.
11
, and
\mathcal{B}
is a method-specific operator introduced to compensate for errors introduced by the approximation in
\mathcal{D}
(Fig.
2
, right).
An appealing property of this class of methods is that it bypasses the need for
pre-integration
using samples
\mathcal{S}
, as required in Sec.
4.1
, and instead makes use of
\mathcal{S}
in a more direct supervision setting involving the
differentiated model
.
Since all operations involved are local, this approach typically offers substantial efficiency gains compared to the global estimators required for numerical integration.

To understand why the compensation operator
\mathcal{B}
is necessary, consider the common case where
\mathcal{D}
does not compute the exact repeated derivative, but instead produces a blurred approximation.
Fig.
3
illustrates the effect of training with the loss from Eq.
12
, when
\mathcal{B}
is set to the identity operator.
We observe that fails to converge to an accurate approximation of the true antiderivative, instead exhibiting pronounced ringing artifacts (Fig.
3
b).
The artifacts can be eliminated by applying the blur introduced by
\mathcal{D}
to the solution as well, effectively canceling the ringing.
To avoid such a costly post-processing step, we instead incorporate the blur into
f
via the compensation operator
\mathcal{B}
in Eq.
12
, resulting in a more faithful solution (Fig.
3
c).
In practice, this smoothing is estimated using a Monte Carlo convolution
[
HRV*18
]
.
Note that this approach can yield slightly blurred antiderivatives, as both the model output and the supervision signal are smoothed – providing little to no incentive for the model to capture high-frequency details.
Nevertheless, a mildly blurred solution, as shown in Fig.
3
c, is typically preferable to the more severe artifacts observed in Fig.
3
b.

Figure 3:

An example illustrating the effect of the compensation operator
\mathcal{B}
in Eq.
12
, when using a differential operator
\mathcal{D}
that introduces blurring.
(
a
) The function
f
, accessible through samples, is a 1D step function.
(
b
) Training the first antiderivative without compensating for the blur introduced by
\mathcal{D}
results in ringing artifacts that contaminate the solution.
(
c
) Applying an appropriate compensation operator
\mathcal{B}
eliminates the artifacts and yields a faithful solution.

Furthermore, note that losses of the form Eq.
12
admit infinitely many minimizers, corresponding to models that differ by (higher-order) constants of integration, which vanish under differentiation -- an invariance with no practical impact.

In the remainder of this section, we present specific realizations of Eq.
12
, corresponding to particular choices of
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{B}
.

4.2.1
Automatic Differentiation

The most straightforward way to apply the repeated differential operator
\mathcal{D}
to a neural model is through automatic differentiation
[
Lin70
,
RHW*85
]
, which is natively supported by all major machine learning frameworks
[
PGC*17
,
BFH*18
,
ABC*16
]
.
Automatic differentiation of the model output with respect to its input constructs or augments a computational graph that evaluates the derivative directly, using either forward-mode or reverse-mode variants.
This derivative graph shares the trainable parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with the original model and can be used for training
[
TNVG19
,
LMW21
]
.
Notably, this approach yields an exact equality in Eq.
13
, up to numerical inaccuracies.
As a result, the compensation operator
\mathcal{B}
can be safely set to the identity.
Following Lindell
et al.

[
LMW21
]
, we use a normalized positional encoding to improve training stability.

While implementations of higher-order automatic differentiation vary, the memory and computational complexity of the resulting derivative graphs typically scale superlinearly with the order of differentiation.
Consequently, performing
d\times n
differentiations sequentially and backpropagating gradients through the resulting graphs becomes increasingly demanding as the order increases.

Integral Reduction

Integral reduction (Sec.
2.1
) offers a way to curb the explosive growth of the computational graph caused by repeated applications of automatic differentiation.
Accordingly, we adopt Haddad’s formulation (Eq.
3
) – Cauchy’s reduction (Eq.
2
) does not yield antiderivatives, which are required for differential supervision.
Letting

\left\{\Phi_{l}\right\}

denote the first-order antiderivatives in Eq.
3
, we write

F^{(n)}(x)=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\alpha_{n,l}\,\,x^{l-1}\Phi_{l}(x).

(14)

The key advantage of this formulation is that, instead of applying
higher
-order automatic differentiation to a
single
model , we apply
first
-order automatic differentiation to
n
models

\left\{\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},l}\right\}
using the loss

\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{x\in\Omega}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},l}(x)-x^{l}f(x)\right\|\right].

(15)

After training, the models

\left\{\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},l}\right\}

are combined according to Eq.
14
.
Extension to multiple dimensions is achieved by applying Eq.
14
recursively, resulting in
n^{d}
models, each requiring only
d
automatic differentiations during training.

Since the individual models
\left\{\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},l}\right\}
are highly correlated – their derivatives differ only by powers of the input coordinates – we found that training a single model with
n^{d}
outputs performs comparably to training separate models.
Evidently, this approach requires minor architectural modifications to the neural field, specifically in the form of a wider output layer.

4.2.2
Numerical Differentiation

Unlike automatic differentiation, which leverages the structure of a model’s computational graph, numerical differentiation estimates derivatives by evaluating the model at perturbed input coordinates
[
BF15
]
.
Crucially, it requires only forward passes through the model, and is therefore often significantly more efficient – particularly for larger
d
and
n
.
However, replacing computations of instantaneous rates of change with finite coordinate perturbations to approximate
\mathcal{D}
naturally introduces a smoothing effect, which can be addressed through an appropriate choice of
\mathcal{B}
.
Note that integral reduction (Sec.
4.2.1
) could, in principle, also be used in combination with numerical differentiation; however, unlike in the case of automatic differentiation, it provides no efficiency advantage.

In the following, we present the two commonly used classes of techniques for learning with numerical differentiation.
Our exposition focuses on the 1D case, as the extension to higher-dimensional operators can be readily achieved using outer products of 1D coordinate perturbation offsets.

Finite Differences

A widely used approach for estimating the derivative is the scheme

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}g(x)\approx\frac{g(x+\epsilon)-g(x-\epsilon)}{2\epsilon},

(16)

where
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
denotes a small, fixed coordinate perturbation.
This central difference operation can be interpreted as the convolution of
g
with a pair of Dirac delta functions.
It is the the natural and minimal approach for numerically estimating derivatives in continuous representations that support sampling at arbitrary coordinates
[
LME*23
,
NDS*23
]
– unlike in discrete representations, where it is common to choose among different additional schemes (e.g., forward and backward differences) to balance trade-offs such as bias and numerical stability.

Higher-order derivatives can be estimated by recursively applying Eq.
16
, resulting in the sampling schemes illustrated in the first row of Fig.
4
.
Since sampling locations coincide across recursive steps, the number of required samples grows linearly rather than exponentially:
computing a derivative of order
n
requires
n+1
samples.
The compensation operation
\mathcal{B}
corresponding to this scheme is a convolution with a piecewise polynomial blur kernel
[
Hec86
,
NDS*23
]
, as illustrated in the second row of Fig.
4
.

Figure 4:

Convolution kernels for numerical differentiation operators
\mathcal{D}
and the corresponding compensation operators
\mathcal{B}
, shown across different orders of differentiation
n
.
In simple finite differencing, the kernel is a set of Dirac delta functions (first row).
This introduces smoothing via piecewise polynomial kernels (second row; dashed lines indicate the boundaries of the segments).
Smooth estimators use derivatives of the Gaussian distribution (third row), which inherently introduce smoothing through the undifferentiated Gaussian kernel (last row).

Smooth Estimators

The scheme in Eq.
16
, along with its higher-order extensions, suffers from a key limitation:
the coordinate perturbation
\epsilon
must be predetermined and remains fixed.
This is of only minor concern in discrete representations, where
\epsilon
typically corresponds to the signal’s sample spacing, thereby ensuring that little to no information is lost.
However, continuous neural representations tend to allocate spatial resolution adaptively.
Therefore, a fixed perturbation magnitude may result in an aliased estimate of the derivative
[
Sha49
]
.

A solution to this problem is to replace the convolution with Dirac delta functions with a convolution using the (higher-order) derivative of a smooth, non-negative kernel
\kappa

[
FR23
,
FR24
]
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}g(x)\approx\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{n}}\kappa(\tau)\,g(x-\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

(17)

A natural choice for
\kappa
is the Gaussian distribution, which admits analytic expressions for its (higher-order) derivatives (third row in Fig.
4
).
In practice, Eq.
17
is computed using the Monte Carlo estimate

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}g(x)\approx\frac{1}{N_{\kappa}}\sum_{\tau_{i}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{n}}\kappa(\tau_{i})\,g(x-\tau_{i}),

(18)

using
N_{\kappa}
random samples
\tau_{i}
.
Kernel derivatives

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{n}}\kappa

with odd
n
yield odd functions (first and third column in Fig.
4
), for which antithetic sampling
[
HM56
]
is highly effective in reducing the variance of the estimate.
Furthermore, low-discrepancy sampling using Sobol sequences
[
Sob67
]
has a significant positive effect on convergence.
Regardless of
n
, the compensation operator
\mathcal{B}
associated with this scheme corresponds to a convolution with
\kappa
itself (last row in Fig.
4
).

A variation of this scheme involves using stochastic, Gaussian-weighted sampling to fit a local
n
-th-order polynomial to
g
, from which derivative values can be read off directly
[
CYW*25
]
.
Eq.
18
is a stochastic estimator of the form given in Eq.
5
.
In practice, we observe that a naïve implementation results in substantial gradient bias, to the point where the results become practically unusable.
Therefore, we apply the de-biasing techniques discussed in Sec.
2.2
.

5
Evaluation

In this section, we present a systematic evaluation of all methods introduced above.
We begin by describing our evaluation protocol (Sec.
5.1
), followed by a presentation of the experimental results (Sec.
5.2
).

5.1
Protocol

5.1.1
Data

We select a representative set of signals
f
, spanning input dimensionalities
d
from one to three, organized into two general categories.
First, we include isolated function classes composed of synthetic signals.
For each
d
, we use
a random mixture of Gaussians to represent smooth behavior,
a random mixture of hyper-rectangles to model discontinuities,
and the Ackley function
[
Ack87
]
to capture oscillatory characteristics.

Second, for each
d
, we include a set of representative real-world signals relevant to visual computing.
As a 1D representative, we use 20 motion-capture sequences, each consisting of 23 3D joint positions (
d_{o}=69
) capturing full-body human motion over 3.3 minutes.
As a 2D representative, we use 20 natural RGB images (
d_{o}=3
) with a resolution of
1640\times 1640
, randomly sampled from the DIV2K dataset
[
AT17
]
.
As a 3D representative, we use 10 signed distance functions (SDFs) (
d_{o}=1
) representing surface geometry, randomly drawn from multiple standard datasets
[
CL96
,
BRLB14
,
CFG*15
,
ZJ16
,
KMJ*19
]
.
All signals were padded to ensure proper boundary handling.
For the rendering task (Sec.
5.1.2
), we use three HDR environment maps at a resolution of
1024\times 512
sourced from an online repository
[
Pol24
]
, each used to illuminate three meshes.
Fig.
5
shows data samples.

Figure 5:

Example inputs from the set of test signals used in our evaluation, spanning multiple input dimensionalities.

5.1.2
Tasks and Metrics

We learn the
n
-fold antiderivative of the
d
-dimensional data, with
n
ranging from one to three for
d=1
, and from one to two for
d>1
.
We assess performance using two complementary approaches.
The first approach assesses antiderivative accuracy via reconstruction.
To this end, we apply (higher-order) automatic differentiation to the predicted antiderivatives and compare the result to the original data, since (higher-order) constants of integration may differ between solutions.
The second approach evaluates performance across different integration orders on two downstream tasks: filtering and rendering.
For the filtering task, we use the antiderivative representations of all real-world signals to perform convolution with a piecewise polynomial approximation of a
d
-dimensional Gaussian filter at varying bandwidths
[
Hec86
,
NDS*23
]
.
The results are compared against converged Monte Carlo-based convolutions applied to the original data.
For the rendering task, we use antiderivative representations of environment maps to compute glossy reflections of distant illumination
[
HSC*05
,
HSSL07
]
.
Renderings from three different viewpoints are compared against corresponding results produced using the original environment maps.

We quantify errors using the mean squared error (MSE) across all signals.
For image outputs, we additionally report DSSIM
[
WBSS04
]
and LPIPS
[
ZIE*18
]
scores, which incorporate structural similarity.
For all metrics, lower values indicate higher output quality.

5.1.3
Methods

Our comparative evaluation includes all methods described in Sec.
4
:

•

Integral
refers to the integral supervision technique based on integral reduction (Sec.
4.1
).

•

AD-Naïve
denotes differential supervision via automatic differentiation (Sec.
4.2.1
).

•

AD-Reduc
denotes automatic differentiation combined with integral reduction (Sec.
4.2.1
).

•

Num-FD
and
Num-FD-
\mathcal{B}

refer to numerical differentiation using finite differences, without and with the compensation operator
\mathcal{B}
, respectively (Sec.
4.2.2
).

•

Num-Sm
and
Num-Sm-
\mathcal{B}

refer to numerical differentiation using smooth estimators, without and with the compensation operator
\mathcal{B}
, respectively (Sec.
4.2.2
).

Outcome

The results of our quantitative evaluation are reported in
Tab.

for the synthetic functions,
in Tab.

for the real-world signals,
in Tab.

for the filtering task, and
in Tab.

for the rendering task.
Qualitative results are shown in Fig.

Consistent with expectations, our first observation is that increasing the product
d\times n
– and thus the number of integrations – makes the task more difficult for all methods.
Indeed, some methods fail to succeed across all tasks.
We do not report numbers for methods that completely fail on a specific sub-task – either by failing to converge during training or by producing numerical results more than 100
\times
worse in MSE than those of the best-performing method – and mark these results with a
\lightning
instead.

We begin by examining the antiderivative reconstruction results.
We observe that
Integral
performs well only on the simplest task, where
d=n=1
, whereas in all other cases, training fails to converge reliably or produces high errors due to excessive variance in the training signal.

AD-Naïve
consistently achieves the best performance across tasks and integration orders.
In fact, it is the only method that does not fail on any of the tasks.
However, this level of quality comes at the cost of significantly longer training times, often exceeding those of all competing methods by orders of magnitude.
In contrast,
AD-Reduc
performs reasonably well on a small selection of functions but completely fails on nearly all others.
We observe that training the individual sub-integrals

\left\{\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},l}\right\}

converges reliably and efficiently.
However, their combination introduces interference patterns caused by slight misalignments between the sub-integrals.
Numerical differentiation in
Num-FD
and
Num-FD-
\mathcal{B}

yields reasonable results with moderate training times. However,
Num-FD
can exhibit ringing artifacts due to the oscillatory frequency response of the finite-difference operator. The blur compensation operator
\mathcal{B}
suppresses these artifacts, resulting in
Num-FD-
\mathcal{B}

significantly outperforming
Num-FD
.
The smooth operators in
Num-Sm
and
Num-Sm-
\mathcal{B}

frequently failed to converge meaningfully, except in low-dimensional settings.

In the filtering task,
Integral
yields decent results for
n=1
, but becomes unreliable for higher-order integration, with quality degrading significantly as the filter kernel size increases.

AD-Naïve
again performs best overall, although its error increases with filter kernel size.

AD-Reduc
performs well for larger kernels and higher integration orders, occasionally even outperforming
AD-Naïve
in the higher-order regime.

Num-FD-
\mathcal{B}

performs significantly better than
Num-FD
, reaching quality close to
AD-Naïve
.
Again,
Num-Sm
and
Num-Sm-
\mathcal{B}

fail to converge reliably.

The results of the rendering task reveal a similar overall pattern.

AD-Naïve
consistently outperforms all other methods across glossiness levels and integration orders.

AD-Reduc
performs decently but deteriorates rapidly as the glossiness level increases.

Num-FD
offers a middle ground in terms of quality, while
Num-FD-
\mathcal{B}

performs surprisingly poorly in the HDR setting compared to its performance in the equivalent LDR image filtering scenario discussed previously.
We observe that the compensation operator
\mathcal{B}
struggles to handle high-intensity highlights in the environment map when operating under a limited sample budget.

Overall, a good number of combinations is marked as to not have converged, and one could ask why this is.
Often, some minima are also poor, and our criteria to call something ‘not converged’ or just report a high error, is arbitrary.
Unfortunately, no better differentiation might be available.
We experimented with several strategies to improve convergence, such as different network architectures, loss functions with additional constraints, and gradient debiasing, but none improved it significantly. Our experimental design cannot differentiate if this is because the gradients are ‘wrong‘, if this is because they are noisy, if this particular optimizer cannot find the minimum of that cost function in general or a combination of all the above.
This is not a property of learning of antiderivatives, but a property of learning itself.

6
Summary, Recommendations, and Conclusion

We have introduced a hierarchical taxonomy of supervision structures for learning repeated antiderivatives from function samples.
In particular, we contrasted direct supervision based on antiderivative estimates with several method classes that rely on repeated differentiation, including both automatic and numerical approaches.
As part of this framework, we explored integral reduction techniques as a means to mitigate the computational overhead associated with repeated integration or differentiation.

Our systematic experimental analysis of all presented methods across a diverse set of modalities reveals that differential supervision via naïve automatic differentiation generally outperforms all competing approaches in terms of result quality.
However, this performance comes at the cost of substantial training time, particularly for higher-order integration.
A practical alternative in many scenarios is numerical differentiation via finite differences, which often achieves a reasonable trade-off between quality and computational cost.
Nevertheless, it is important to incorporate a compensation operator to correct for the signal smoothing inherent in this differentiation scheme.
Our findings suggest directions for future research, such as investigating progressive supervision paradigms that leverage different supervisory signals throughout the learning process.

Interestingly, our analysis reveals that antiderivative quality correlates only weakly with downstream task performance.
Cumulative schemes often excel at efficiently computing highly non-local aggregates, where local inaccuracies tend to cancel out.
Certain combinations of signal modality and integration order therefore benefit from more sophisticated integral reduction schemes.

We believe our work provides a foundation for integrating classical cumulative operators into modern neural pipelines, and we hope the insights gained will carry over to a broader set of learning tasks involving differential and integral operators.
