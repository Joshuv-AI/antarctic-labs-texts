Universality in peristaltic locomotion: Linking biology, theory, and simulation
Authors: Roberta Santoriello, Francesco Viola, Vincenzo Citro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inertial jet-propelled swimmers, such as squids, jellyfishes, and salps, swim through rhythmic body contractions, offering an optimized propulsion mechanism that enables sustained locomotion at low energetic cost. Among the different strategies, the peristaltic propulsion is poorly understood, and a unified view accounting for experimental observations is still missing. This study aims to unveil the fundamental principle behind this type of locomotion, proposing a model swimmer, inspired by salps, that mimics the muscle contractions of real aquatic organisms. Numerical simulations reveal the universality of peristaltic locomotion and allow us to explain experimental data and reconcile biological observations. On the basis of these findings, we develop a simple theoretical framework to predict the system dynamics across different geometric configurations. Beyond its biological relevance, this work shows how the principles of fluid-structure interaction can be translated into predictive models, bridging the gap between the foundations of aquatic jet propulsion and physical laws.

INTRODUCTION

Locomotion in aquatic environments relies on a series of physical principles that nature has adapted to different morphologies (1). Oscillating fins (2), undulating bodies (3), and pulsating jets (4) are different mechanisms that have been refined over eons of evolutionary development to achieve efficient propulsion across marine species (5). Among these swimming strategies, jet propulsion is a common swimming mode used by a diverse array of aquatic taxa from chordates to cnidarians. This type of locomotion entails the compression of a flexible cavity, resulting in the ejection of pulsed jets that generate effective thrust. Despite the shared underlying principle, this mechanism varies among species. In some organisms, such as jellyfish (6), water is drawn in and expelled through the same orifice (Fig. 1B), while in others, distinct refill and expelling orifices are used. For instance, squids (7) draw water via openings on either side of their head and then expel it under high pressure through a funnel (Fig. 1A). Alternatively, water can be drawn in at the front of the animal and expelled at the rear, as seen in salps (8). Shaped like hollow tubes (see the schematic in Fig. 1C), salps propel themselves by opening their oral siphon to fill their hollow body and radially compressing their bodies using muscle fibers arranged in the circumferential direction (9). This motion forces water out of their rear opening (the atrial siphon), by which they generate forward thrust. When the body muscles relax, water enters again through the oral siphon to fill the body chamber. Characterized by a sequence of temporally coordinated contractions, this mechanism is analogous to peristaltic pumping (10) adapted for aquatic locomotion (8, 11).

Much of our knowledge about the locomotion of salps has been derived from experimental observations. Harbison and Campenot (12) identified a negative correlation between pulsation frequency and body length in hand-collected salps, while specimens gathered with trawl nets did not exhibit this trend due to stress and behavioral alterations induced during collection. As a result, this frequency-length correlation became a reliable metric to assess specimen health (12). The experimental findings of Sutherland and Madin (13) further validated this inverse relationship across different species, suggesting the existence of a universal principle governing salp locomotion.

To date, most theoretical analyses of peristaltic-driven swimming have focused on Stokes regimes (14), in which swimming speed exhibits a monotonic dependence on pulsation frequency, regardless of body length. Therefore, inertial peristaltic swimmers lack a comprehensive framework that reconciles biological observations, across size scales, with physical laws. Moreover, existing bio-inspired robotic prototypes (15) are unable to explain the rich dynamics observed in natural swimmers. These platforms are typically characterized by fixed geometries, with a primary focus on performance optimization.

Here, we aim to fill this gap by combining three-dimensional direct numerical simulations with biohybrid (16) design principles to develop a salp-inspired swimmer, which serves as a platform to unveil the principles governing the peristaltic locomotion in the inertial regime. Taking into account the coupled fluid-structure-electro interaction underlying locomotion, our multiphysics computational tool (17) enables the performance characterization of the model swimmer through high-fidelity numerical simulations. The computational model lies on three pillars: a fluid solver for the hydrodynamics, an electrophysiology solver for the description of the electro-mechanical properties of the muscle tissue, and a structural solver for the simulation of the dynamics of the system. A brief overview of the multiphysics computational model is provided in Materials and Methods. Guided by the numerical results, we introduce a minimal theoretical model, built only on first principles and free of adjustable parameters, which accurately predicts the optimal swimming dynamics across scales. While reconciling with and explaining the empirical observations on salps, the insights gained from the locomotion of our model swimmer could be translated into key guidelines for the design of bio-inspired and biohybrid swimming vehicles.

RESULTS

Model swimmer

The model swimmer consists of a tubular structure featuring circumferential muscle bands, whose orientation allows for periodic radial compressions that drive the swimmer’s peristaltic locomotion (Fig. 1D). In line with common biohybrid practices for swimming platforms (18–21), we selected cardiac muscle tissue (the working myocardium) for its capability to sustain continuous, rhythmic activity and for its contractile properties. This tissue is made of striated cells (cardiomyocytes), which share electrical signals via “gap junctions” to operate collectively as a “functional syncytium” (22, 23). Within this cellular network, an electrical stimulus induces a local cell depolarization, which propagates to adjacent cells. This process generates active stress aligned with the myocyte axis, resulting in coordinated contraction and relaxation cycles (23).

The propulsion cycle begins from a resting state in a quiescent fluid, driven by the electrical activation of a pacemaker that induces muscle contraction. As shown in Fig. 1D, the model swimmer is actuated at its leading edge through a pacemaker ring which delivers a localized electrical current and regulates the pulsation frequency (the excitation pulse waveform is illustrated in Fig. 1E). At the macroscopic level, the synchronized contractions of muscle cells produce a radial compression of the cylindrical body, thereby reducing its volume. This movement displaces the surrounding fluid, generating a closed loop of vorticity at its leading edge, termed a vortex ring (Fig. 1F) (24). As the leading edge tissue relaxes back to its original configuration, the peristaltic wave propagates downstream, progressively compressing the remaining part of the cylindrical body. Simultaneously, the fluid propelled backward by the propagating contraction results in another vortex at the trailing edge. Once the traveling wave reaches the end of the body, this vortex is expelled, propelling the swimmer forward (i.e., counter to wave propagation) (see Fig. 1G). Then, the cylindrical body returns to its initial resting shape and, regulated by the actuation frequency, the traveling pattern repeats, generating a cyclic peristaltic motion that sustains continuous swimming.

Overall, the propulsion emerges from the interplay between the surrounding fluid, the flexible structure, and the electrophysiology system. Electrical signals propagating through the biological tissue trigger body contractions, which, in turn, transfers momentum to the fluid and gives rise to coherent flow structures such as vortex rings. The latter are a defining characteristic of pulse-jet propulsion. Previous studies have suggested that these structures enhance swimming efficiency in biological systems (25). The improvement in performance is attributed to the increased fluid entrainment generated in the wake during locomotion. In particular, the circulation of a vortex ring causes additional water to be entrained from the surrounding fluid, increasing the amount of fluid that is driven backward in the wake of the jet.

Figure 1 (H and I) highlights the analogy between a real salp (Fig. 1H) and our system (Fig. 1I). In both cases, muscle contraction has reached the end of the cylindrical body, and fluid is expelled through a toroidal vortex. Rather than imitating the exact mechanism found in nature, our goal is to abstract the fundamental characteristics of locomotion to unveil the principles of inertial peristaltic-driven swimming. The model swimmer will serve as a platform for identifying a universal principle of locomotion, translating biological observations into key design guidelines for advancing robotic swimming platforms.

Reconciling with experimental observations

To align the model with experimental observations (12, 13), we begin by assessing its robustness across a range of flow regimes characterized by different Reynolds numbers (Re). This parameter quantifies the ratio of inertial to viscous forces and is defined as Re=U∗D∗/ν∗. Here, U∗ denotes a reference velocity, held constant across all investigated configurations, D∗ is the resting diameter of the swimmers, and ν∗ is the fluid kinematic viscosity. The analysis is performed through a pulsation frequency sweep on a fixed geometric configuration. The insights gained from this analysis are then exploited to guide the investigation of swimmers with different body lengths. The findings presented hereafter are reported in dimensionless form; all quantities have been nondimensionalized using the characteristic length (D∗) and velocity (U∗) and the fluid density (ρf∗).

To quantify the swimming performance, we track the position of the center of mass of the swimmer (Fig. 2A) and compute the corresponding speed (Fig. 2B). Starting from rest, the swimmer accelerates and, after a transient phase, the acceleration (averaged over one period) vanishes and the speed (Uˆ) reaches a plateau (steady regime) (Fig. 2B). The inset in Fig. 2B shows in detail the swimming mechanism and its corresponding phases of contraction. During each excitation cycle, the swimming process consists of periodic oscillations of the instantaneous speed (Uˆ) around its average value (U). The minimum of the waveform (blue marker in the inset of Fig. 2B) is achieved when the contraction has just occurred on the leading edge, while the maximum (red marker in the inset of Fig. 2B) corresponds to the peak of thrust, occurring when the contraction reaches the end of the body and fluid is expelled through a toroidal vortex (see also Fig. 1G).

The initial acceleration (A), computed as the initial slope of the mean swimming speed (as indicated in Fig. 2B), has been chosen as a performance indicator of the transient phase of the swimming dynamics. On the other hand, to characterize the steady regime, the mean value of the terminal swimming speed (U) has been considered (as indicated in the inset of Fig. 2B). Henceforth, we refer to these quantities simply as acceleration and speed.

Figure 3 (A and B) illustrates the results of the pulsation frequency (f) sweep, in terms of acceleration (Fig. 3A) and speed (Fig. 3B), in the intermediate Reynolds number regime, Re=[20,100,200,350]. Acceleration and speed show similar trends: They increase monotonically at low frequencies, attain a maximum value, and subsequently decline for higher frequencies. As expected, higher Reynolds numbers correspond to higher accelerations and speeds. We observe that the optimal pulsation frequency range (fopt), which drives the system to its maximum acceleration or speed, does not depend on the Reynolds number. Peak performance is always achieved within the range fopt=[1 to 1.25]. Unlike speed, the peaks of the acceleration curves are sharper. In the following, we refer to these peaks as “optimal” conditions, as they occur within the optimal pulsation frequency range. In both graphs, the maxima of the curves are highlighted with white markers outlined in red. In instances where the maxima of speed and acceleration do not perfectly align, the highlighted marker for the speed curve (which exhibits a smoothed peak) indicates the frequency at which the maximum acceleration is recorded.

The Reynolds number independence of the optimal pulsation frequency demonstrates the robustness of the locomotion strategy across distinct flow regimes. Despite variations in flow topology [see Fig. 3 (E to H)], the same pulsation frequency guarantees the attainment of peak initial acceleration or terminal swimming speed. Furthermore, as detailed in the Supplementary Materials (see fig. S1), this optimal condition (i.e., reaching peak acceleration and terminal speed when actuated at fopt) remains insensitive to variations in the peristaltic wave amplitude (Aw).

In light of the scenario just described, it is natural to examine how the optimal acceleration and speed correlate with the Reynolds number. The graphs in Fig. 3 (C and D) show, respectively, the optimal initial acceleration (Aopt) and terminal speed (Uopt) versus Reynolds number at fopt=1.167. At lower Reynolds numbers, acceleration and speed increase almost linearly, reflecting a regime in which viscous forces dominate the flow dynamics. However, beyond a threshold of about Re=200, the acceleration and speed reach a plateau at Aopt≈0.11 and Uopt≈3.32, respectively. The saturation of both parameters suggests that when the Reynolds number exceeds a certain threshold, further increases in inertial forces do not lead to substantial improvements in swimming performance.

Figure 3 (I to K) illustrates the peristaltic patterns for the optimal case (denoted by a white circular marker outlined in red in Fig. 3 (A and B), at Re=100) and for selected sub-optimal configurations [see white markers outlined in blue and green in Fig. 3 (A and B)]. When the system is stimulated at f=fopt, only one wave propagates along its length. This is evident in Fig. 3J, where a single red arrow (indicating the direction of the peristaltic wave) is visible at any given time along the swimmer’s body. In this optimal configuration, a new propulsive cycle is triggered the moment the previous wave arrives at the end of the body. Below this optimum (f<fopt), the propulsion becomes intermittent due to resting phases between successive pulsations (see Fig. 3I). On the other hand, operating above the optimal pulsation frequency (f>fopt) causes the subsequent cycle to begin before the previous wave has reached the trailing edge, resulting in a spatial overlap of multiple waves. This is shown in Fig. 3K, where two red arrows appear along the length of the swimmer at the same time.

These observations suggest that the propagation of one traveling wave along the body plays a key role in the propulsion mechanism. To test this hypothesis and to provide an explanation for the negative correlation between body length and pulsation frequency, as experimentally observed in the literature (12), we investigated swimmers of different body lengths. Previously, the body length was set to L=4, whereas we now investigate the following range of lengths L=[2,3,4,6] at Re=100. The graphs in Fig. 4 (A and B) illustrate, respectively, the relation between initial acceleration (Fig. 4A) and terminal swimming speed (Fig. 4B) versus pulsation frequency, parametrized by the aforementioned body lengths. To ensure a fair comparison with the data reported in the literature (12, 13, 26), the swimming performance is normalized by body length (L). As displayed in Fig. 4 (A and B), regardless of body length, both kinematic metrics are characterized by a monotonic increase toward a peak, followed by a subsequent decrease. Therefore, each curve features an optimal pulsation frequency (denoted by a red circular marker) that maximizes acceleration and speed. Across the range of investigated body lengths, the lowest initial accelerations and terminal swimming speeds are associated with the longest swimmer (L=6), while the highest values are recorded for the shortest one (L=2). The swimmer with L=2 exhibits a slight misalignment between the two peaks: While acceleration is maximized at f=1.667, a slightly higher speed is attained at a lower frequency of f=1.5. Thus, for the L=2 case, the red marker in Fig. 4B refers to the frequency of maximum acceleration, where speed remains close to its maximum value.

Considering the optimal speed of the swimmers analyzed in this study, each characterized by different body lengths (i.e., the data highlighted with red circular markers in Fig. 4B), we observe a linear increase of the optimal speed with the corresponding optimal pulsation frequency. A similar trend was observed for salps, as illustrated in Fig. 4B, where we have reported with gray square markers the experimental measurements and the corresponding regression curve (black dashed line) of Madin (26).

A close inspection of Fig. 4 (A and B) reveals a clear correlation between pulsation frequency and body length: Longer swimmers reach peak performance at lower frequencies, whereas shorter ones require higher actuation rates. The optimal frequencies for the L=6, L=4, L=3, and L=2 cases are fopt=0.834, fopt=1.167, fopt=1.334, and fopt=1.667, respectively [which correspond to the red circular markers in Fig. 4 (A and B)]. As illustrated by the body dynamics and vorticity fields in Fig. 4 (D to G), these optimal conditions share a common mechanism, as in each case, only one peristaltic wave travels along the length of the cylinder, without multiple-wave interference or resting phases. The moment this wave reaches the trailing edge, a new pulse is triggered at the leading edge.

Given that the wave propagation speed remains invariant across all configurations (since it is a property defined by the electrophysiological model), the transit time required for the wave to traverse the body scales linearly with its length. This leads longer swimmers to operate at lower excitation frequencies and shorter ones to require higher pulsation frequencies, due to the greater or shorter distance (body length) the wave must travel before the next excitation cycle begins, thereby preventing inactive phases or overlapping between pulses.

This trend not only aligns with but also explains the experimental data reported in the literature about the swimming of salps (12, 13). The pioneering work of Harbison and Campenot (12) originally documented a negative correlation between pulsation frequency and body length. By providing empirical regression curves for salp species such as Salpa cylindrica, Salpa maxima, Salpa fusiformis, Salpa aspera, and Pegea confederata, the authors were led to hypothesize that this correlation “probably holds for all salps” (12). Empirical data from Sutherland and Madin (13) further corroborate this relationship. As documented in their study, shorter salp species (e.g., Weelia cylindrica) swim at pulse frequencies of f≈2, whereas longer ones are characterized by lower pulsation rates of f≈1.5 and f≈0.8, recorded for P. confederata and Cyclosalpa affinis, respectively.

In Fig. 4C, we superimpose our predictions onto the (L,fopt) parameter plane to compare our numerical findings with the experimental measurements of (12). A close agreement is evident between the two datasets. Our results recover the biological trend, showing a linear decrease in pulse frequency as body length scales from L=2 to L=6. The alignment between our findings and the biological data reinforces the hypothesis that the inverse correlation between frequency and body length represents a universal feature among salp species. Our framework provides a physical explanation for this empirically observed trend, suggesting that the single-wave propagation along the body length is the fundamental mechanism governing the specific swimming frequencies of salps. Therefore, under inertial flow regimes, increasing the pulsation frequency leads to an increase in swimming speed until the wave transit time matches the actuation period. This linear increase in swimming speed as the pulsation frequency increases is linked to a progressive reduction in the inactive phases between pulses, which minimizes thrust intermittency. Speed achieves its maximum when the propagation of one wave along the body length allows for full muscular recovery immediately before the subsequent excitation. Beyond this optimum, kinematic performance decreases linearly, as a consequence of energy losses due to the interference emerging from overlapping waves. In contrast to the Stokes regime, where theoretical models indicate that swimming speed increases monotonically with the pulsation frequency (14), our results for the inertial regime show that higher optimal pulse frequencies drive an increase in swimming speed (see Fig. 4B), which is directly associated with a decrease in body length (see Fig. 4C).

Energetic optimality of peristaltic swimmers

This section investigates whether the kinematic optimum, defined by peak initial acceleration (Aopt) and terminal swimming speed (Uopt), aligns with the maximal energetic efficiency. To this end, we calculate the time-averaged mechanical power (P¯) spent by the swimmer during steady-state regime (27): P¯=∫[(σ⋅n)⋅u]dS¯, where σ represents the fluid stress tensor acting upon the swimmer’s surface S, n is the unit normal vector to the surface, and u is the velocity field encompassing both the velocity of radial body compressions and the resulting swimming velocity. The overbar denotes the time average obtained over several swimming cycles once the system reaches a steady state.

Following established literature conventions (27–29), we quantify the energetics of locomotion strategy through the mechanical cost of transport, which defines the rate of mechanical energy expenditure required to move a given mass at a specific velocity. To facilitate comparisons across different geometrical configurations and flow regimes, the cost of transport is scaled by the characteristic acceleration ac=U2/V1/3 (29), where V is the body volumeCoT=P¯mUac(1)

In Eq. 1, m denotes the mass of the swimmer, while U represents its terminal swimming speed. According to this formulation (Eq. 1), lower CoT values indicate a more energetically convenient swimming strategy. Furthermore, assuming that the available muscle power of a given swimmer is proportional to V2/3 (29), the inverse of CoT can be interpreted as a dimensionless efficiency, E=1/CoT=(m/V)U3V2/3/P¯, measuring the ratio of the available power to the actual power spent (29).

Figure 5A illustrates the CoT as a function of the pulsation frequency (f) at Re=100. We observe that the minimum CoT falls within the optimal frequency range previously identified, fopt=[1 to 1.25], confirming that the kinematic optimum (defined by Aopt and Uopt) aligns with the energetic optimum (i.e., the lowest CoT, denoted as CoTopt). Thus, the optimal pulsation frequency, which ensures single-wave propagation along the swimmer’s length—mirroring experimental observations (12, 13)—not only maximizes kinematic performance but also minimizes the cost of transport.

In the following, we evaluate the key forces governing propulsion: thrust and drag.

Thrust modeling

Guided by numerical results, our model for the thrust force assumes that the system maximizes its performance by sustaining one wave at a time, with the pacemaker triggering a new pulse as the previous wave exits the trailing edge of the cylinder. This behavior implies that the speed of the traveling wave uw can be computed as the product between the optimal excitation frequency (fopt) and the length (L) of the swimmer as uw=foptL. Using a mechanical analogy, we can think of peristaltic propulsion as a “propagating block.” In other words, we represent the contraction as a holed piston of external radius R=D/2 (recalling that the resting diameter of the swimmers D∗ is the characteristic length scale, making the dimensionless diameter D=1) and internal radius Ri=Di/2=R−Aw (denoting with Di the internal diameter of the piston and with Aw the amplitude of the traveling wave), which pushes the fluid at a speed uw (see the sketch in Fig. 6A). Thus, computing the mass flow rate pushed by the piston as m˙=uwSc, with Sc the circular cross-sectional area of the holed piston given by Sc=π(R2−Ri2), the force exerted by the piston on the fluid could be determined from the momentum transfer as FT,max=m˙uw=uw2Sc.

Universality of peristaltic locomotion

In the previous paragraphs, we observed that the optimal values of both terminal swimming speed and initial acceleration curves shift depending on the body length when plotted against the pulsation frequency [see Fig. 4 (A and B)]. This trend is linked to the optimal propulsion condition, which requires the presence of only one wave along the body length, ensuring full muscular relaxation before the subsequent excitation and eliminating inactive periods. This mechanism explains why longer and shorter swimmers require different pulsation frequencies, causing the peaks of the curves (Fig. 4, A and B) to shift toward lower or higher frequencies, respectively.

To rationalize this behavior and capture the underlying physics, we introduce the wave propagation number (Nw) as the ratio between the wave transit time (Tw=L/uw) and the actuation period (T=1/f): Nw=Tw/T=fL/uw. As shown in Fig. 7, we rescaled the initial acceleration (A) and the terminal swimming speed (U) by their respective optimal values (Aopt and Uopt), and we plotted the rescaled data against Nw. This scaling successfully collapses the data, revealing a universal principle that governs locomotion across different body lengths and flow regimes. In particular, Nw gathers the characteristic variables of peristaltic locomotion: the pulsation frequency (f), the body length (L), and the wave velocity (uw), and allows us to define the optimal condition (Nw≈1) as a state of synchronization between the wave transit time and the actuation period (Tw≈T), ensuring continuous wave propagation without overlap. Deviations from this synchronization point correspond either to inactive phases (Nw<1, where T>Tw) or to wave interferences (Nw>1, where T<Tw).

Scaled (A) initial acceleration and (B) terminal swimming speed as a function of the wave propagation number Nw=Tw/T=fL/uw.

Notably, this universal scaling holds despite changes in flow topology that, as reported in Fig. 3 (E to H), varies considerably with Reynolds number. At Re=20 (see Fig. 3E), the flow field is laminar without turbulent fluctuations. As the Reynolds number increases [from Re=100 to Re=200 and Re=350; see Fig. 3 (F to H), respectively], the flow field gradually transitions to a turbulent state. Despite the distinct flow topologies and body lengths (ranging from L=2 to L=6), the scaling links the initial acceleration (Fig. 7A) or the terminal swimming speed (Fig. 7B) to body kinematics, which is dictated by the propagation of the traveling wave, that is the key of propulsion. These findings suggest that any inertial peristaltic swimmer, whose motion is based on the propagation of a contraction wave, could benefit from the key principle reported here, making the observed scaling a universal characteristic of this locomotion strategy.

DISCUSSION

This study introduces a unified framework for peristaltic-driven swimming, bridging experimental observations with high-fidelity numerical simulations and predictive theory. As documented in the literature, salps exhibit specific swimming frequencies that correlate with their body length. Our simplified model swimmer confirms and explains this trend, showing that both kinematic and energetic optima are achieved when the wave transit time matches the actuation period, ensuring peristaltic wave propagation without overlap or inactive phases.

Building on the principle of single-wave propagation, the theoretical model successfully mirrors the trend observed in both numerical simulations and experimental data. This alignment strengthens the hypothesis that the propagation of a single wave is a fundamental mechanism of peristaltic-driven swimming in the inertial regime. The key aspects of this locomotion mode are captured through a universal scaling that links swimming performance to body kinematics, that is dictated by the traveling peristaltic wave. This scaling allows the prediction of both transient and steady-state swimming performance across different body lengths and in distinct flow regimes.

While this work has abstracted the fundamental characteristics of locomotion to unveil the key principles of inertial peristaltic-driven swimming, future developments of the proposed framework could incorporate species-specific anatomical details, such as the diverse appendages observed in various salp genera (e.g., the passive processes of C. affinis and Thetys vagina or the anterior muscular processes of Brooksia rostrata), whose hydrodynamic roles remain to be fully elucidated (26), as well as the active vectoring of the jet efflux through asymmetric muscular contractions (9). Therefore, the proposed framework not only advances our understanding of salp locomotion but also provides a robust foundation for investigating other specific, yet now unresolved, aspects of biological swimming, yielding key principles that can inform the realization of bio-inspired and biohybrid platforms.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Methods

The computational model relies on a fluid solver for the hydrodynamics, an electrophysiology solver for the propagation of the action potential through the myocardium, and a structural solver for the simulation of the dynamics of the system. The numerical tool reproduces the swimming dynamics without prescribing any motion to the tissue of the swimmer, whose kinematics will be instead part of the model solution. The solver is accelerated using graphics processing units (GPU) (31) using Compute Unified Device Architecture (CUDA) Fortran, and it relies on an immersed boundary (IB) method and a moving least squares approach (32). Here, we provide a brief overview of each model, along with the related equations and their coupling. Details of the algorithms and thorough validations can be found in (17).

Here, χ, Cm, M¯¯, and Iion(s) denote, respectively, the surface-to-volume ratio of cells, the cell membrane capacitance per unit area, the conductivity tensor (34), and the ionic current per unit area derived from the state vector (s) of the cell model (i.e., the last of Eq. 10). Here, we have adopted the cell model proposed by Ten Tusscher and Panfilov (35) for the working myocardium. However, the model swimmer was also successfully tested using different cell models, such as Purkinje fibers (36) which are characterized by higher conduction velocities than the working myocardium (23). While these cells lead to increased acceleration and swimming speed, the underlying propulsion mechanism remains robustly tied to single-wave propagation for optimal performance. The term Is, in Eq. 10, denotes the input current required to start the electrical propagation through the muscle tissue; this signal is nonzero only at the designated pacemaker site. The action potential propagation and the consequent tissue depolarization are quite insensitive to the time duration and amplitude of Is (17). Equation 10 is solved numerically using an in-house finite volume library (37), which yields the action potential distribution at each time step. Following Nash and Panfilov (38), we calculate the internal active tension (σnact), at each structural node (n) as follows, dσnact/dt=ψ(vn)[kσ vn−σnact]. In this formulation, kσ controls the active stress amplitude, while ψ is a smoothed Heaviside function (increasing monotonically from 0.1 to 1, during muscular contraction and relaxation, respectively) which sets the delay of the active stress with respect to the action potential. The nodal active tension translates into a force vector directed along the local muscle fibers as follows, Fnact=σnactSneˆf (17). In this expression, eˆf represents the unit vector of the fiber orientation, while Sn is the local tissue cross sectional area.

Structural solver

The structural dynamics relies on a spring network model (32) based on the interaction potential approach (39), modeling the swimmer as a surface triangulation, with mass lumped at the mesh nodes interconnected by elastic springs. Within this framework, the membrane total energy accounts for both in-plane stretching/compression and out-of-plane bending contributions. In-plane deformations are described by the elastic potential We=1/2 kex2, where ke is the spring stiffness (40) defined on the mesh edges, x=l−l0 is the mesh edge deformation relative to the stress-free length, with l and l0 denoting the spring length for the stretched and stress-free configurations, respectively. By computing the gradient of this potential (Fint=∇We), we derive the nodal elastic forces acting on two generic connected nodes i and j. On the other hand, bending effects between two adjacent triangles sharing an edge are modeled by means of a bending spring. Following (32), we define the bending energy relative to a flat rest configuration as Wb=kb(1−ni⋅nj)=kb[1−cos(θ)]. In this expression, θ identifies the angle between the unit normals ni and nj of the elements sharing the edge, and kb is the bending constant linked to the average bending modulus B. This leads to the determination of the bending nodal forces (Fnb,int). Thus, the total internal load is the sum of bending and elastic contributions, Fnint=Fnb,int+Fne,int. This deformed configuration serves as the input for the multiphysics solvers to update active and hydrodynamic loads.
