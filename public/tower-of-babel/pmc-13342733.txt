Evaluation of the efficacy of hybrid arch bar versus conventional arch bar in the management of facial fractures: A pilot study
Authors: Subhajit Mitra, Nitesh Mishra, Flora Kalita, Nimish Agarwal, Akhilesh K. Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Intermaxillary fixation (IMF) has been utilized to stabilize maxilla/mandibular fractures and orthognathic procedures. In the literature, many techniques for maxillomandibular fixation are reported, including the use of arch bars or bone screws. Except for using bone screws, all other treatments run the danger of damaging the lips and periodontal tissues, taking longer duration, and exposing the surgeon or assistant to different infections through glove punctures and subsequent wire prick wounds. A redesigned hybrid arch bar is being utilized to solve these issues. This research compares the typical arch bar for MMF with a hybrid arch bar (with screws).Material and Methods:Twenty patients with facial fractures needing IMF were included in the study. Ten patients were treated with Erich’s arch bars, and 10 patients were treated with hybrid arch bars (with screws). Time consumed for the placement of arch bar, glove perforation, and pre- and postoperative periodontal condition and vitality was assessed and compared.Results:Results of the study showed the increase in mean plaque index to be statistically significant in the conventional arch bars group, suggestive of poor oral hygiene. Also, the time duration for the modified arch bar placement was lower than that of conventional arch bars (P < 0.001).Conclusion:Patients treated with the hybrid arch bars showed better periodontal status post-IMF than patients treated with the conventional arch bar. The technique of placing hybrid arch bar was easier and less time-consuming, with fewer wire prick injuries while placing the modified arch bars.

INTRODUCTION

Facial fractures make up a major fraction of maxillofacial injuries, and despite advances in terms of imaging technology and fixation techniques, evaluating, diagnosing, and managing these fractures remains difficult.[1] The significance of reduction and immobilization has been emphasized increasingly as maxilla/mandibular fracture management has advanced over time. “Bridal wire,” still relevant, was used by Hippocrates to explain the significance of reduction and immobilization.[2] Then came the idea of maxillomandibular fixation (MMF).[3]

In maxillofacial trauma management, MMF is essential as it provides a solid foundation on which facial form and function can be restored, and it serves as a cornerstone in maxillofacial trauma management.[4] It aids in the diminution and stabilization of both simple and complex facial fractures, restoring the patient’s premorbid occlusion. When used alone without having plate fixation, most mandibular fractures require 4 to 6 weeks of MMF. However, some fractures will require 8 weeks, depending on the pattern, location, severity, and comorbidities. In contemporary practice, a variety of MMF techniques are available, such as arch bars, MMF screws, various wiring techniques (Risdon wiring, ivy loop wiring, Gilmer wiring, etc.), and wires for skeletal suspension. It is of utmost importance that closed reduction should only be applied to patients with stable occlusion and minimal-to-undisplaced favorable fractures. Arch bars, the standard MMF workhorse, maintain the intra-arch contour as well as provide a tension band in the alveolar region.[5] Traditional arch bars have several disadvantages, including greater surgical time for insertion and removal, risk of penetrating injuries to surgeons, periodontal damage, and poor oral hygiene. The self-drilling/tapping MMF screws, which were recently introduced, have resolved some arch bar-related issues, but screw loosening, screw shearing, and aspiration are a few of the inherent risks and limitations highlighted in recent reports.[6] A hybrid arch bar with slots for screws is being introduced to counter these issues. A hybrid arch bar is believed to reduce trauma to periodontal tissues, ensure the surgeon’s safety, and speed up the procedure. It is also hypothesized that this redesigned hybrid arch bar combines the stability of conventional arch bars with the convenience and ease of installation of intermaxillary fixation (IMF) screws.

In our study, we are comparing the hybrid arch bar and conventional Erich arch bar on parameters of tooth vitality, glove perforation, plaque index, and time taken in minutes. We aim to check the efficacy and advantages of the hybrid arch bar over a conventional arch bar.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

In this study, 20 patients were selected from the outpatient department of oral and maxillofacial surgery and then randomized by simple random sampling and divided into two groups (A and B). Both groups A and B consisted of 10 patients each, who underwent fracture reduction using conventional arch and hybrid arch bars, respectively. The inclusion criteria were all maxillomandibular fractures with minimal displacement, patients with good systemic health and an age group between 18 and 50 years and the exclusion criteria were multiple comminuted fractures and fractures more than 2 weeks old or infected fractures of the maxilla or mandible. Permission from the ethical committee of the university was obtained for this study Ethical clearance was obtained from Institutional ethical comittee with ref no. TMDCRC/IEC/20-21/OMFS5 dated 15.02.2021.

After the routine clinical and radiological examination protocol, the diagnosis was made, and routine blood investigations were performed [Figure 1]. All patients gave their consent to participate in this study. Group A patients received the conventional Erich arch bar that is secured with the help of 24-gauge stainless steel wire to achieve proper occlusion [Figure 2], while the patients of group B received the hybrid arch bar that is applied with the help of screws with a 2 mm diameter and 8 mm length [Figure 3]. The patients of both groups were followed up at postoperative intervals of 1 week, 3 weeks, and 6 weeks on clinical and radiological evidence based on parameters of tooth vitality, glove perforation, plaque index and time taken to complete MMF [Figure 4].

Statistical analysis

SPSS (statistical package for social sciences) version 25.0 and MedCalc software were used for the statistical analysis. Statistical tests applied were the unpaired or independent t-test. The Chi-square test is used to analyze whether categorical variable distributions vary from each other. The P value was considered important when less than 0.05 (P < 0.05), and 95 percent confidence interval was taken.

DISCUSSION

A MMF is an important tool for properly reducing fractures in facial bone fractures. Among oral and maxillofacial surgeons, the most common MMF technique employs arch bars and wiring; However, it has drawbacks such as prolonged recovery time, discomfort, and soft tissue damage.[7] This study set out to find a more effective approach to achieving MMF. In particular, the efficacy of each MMF method was evaluated, as was the occlusal reproducibility and the gingival health of patients treated with conventional and hybrid arch bars. Arch bars or interdental eyelet wiring have traditionally been utilized to accomplish IMF. There are inherent drawbacks to these methods.[8] IMF with bone screws was described as a technique. In the treatment of mandibular fractures, intraoral cortical bone screws can be used instead of arch bars to fix the intermaxillary space. The most significant issue with this procedure is iatrogenic root damage, which can be minimized by an experienced surgeon.

Coburn et al.’s[9] previous research on mandibular fractures reported that 142 patients with mandibular fractures were fitted with IMF screws. Five patients (4% of the total) experienced complications, including a fracture of the screws when they were inserted, iatrogenic damage to the teeth that resulted in tooth loss, and bony sequestra around the screw placement area. Roccia and others[10] noted that none of the screws were broken, 1.9 percent were lost, or 4.9% were covered by oral mucosa. One patient (1.6 percent) had malocclusion and a displaced mandibular bone fracture that did not heal.

This study sought to contrast IMF with hybrid arch bars secured with stainless steel screws in the maxilla and mandible with Erich’s arch bars secured with circumdental wires. To circumvent issues that arise when placing individual arch bars, this modified version combines the advantages of IMF screws and Erich’s arch bars. The expectation was that there would be similar rates of complications, but that the latter would take less time to apply, result in fewer glove perforations, improve oral hygiene, and cause less damage to the tooth root.

The oral hygiene status of patients who were treated with hybrid arch bars was better than in patients with conventional arch bars. The plaque index at pretreatment level was higher in patients treated with hybrid arch bars than in patients treated with conventional arch bars at the 1st week, 3rd week, and 6th week. In the conventional group, the mean plaque score was 2.06, whereas in the hybrid group, the plaque score at the pretreatment level was 2.02.

The intragroup increase in the plaque score (from pretreatment level) at the 1st week, 3rd week, and 6th week was statistically significant in both the conventional and hybrid groups. Tabrizi and team, Arch bars have been shown to raise the plaque index, but how the periodontium reacts to them is still up for debate.[11] There has not been a study that looked at how the jaws’ pocket depths changed after the arch bar was released at different locations. Akshat Mediratta[12] conducted a study comparing patients’ mean plaque index scores at 6 weeks, three months, and six months, respectively, from the baseline. Ngassapa et al.[13] in their animal model study found that the degree of inflammation in the gingiva did not just depend on the amount of plaque. They also suggested that arch bars acting as a constant irritant and direct trauma to the marginal gingiva caused inflammation.

The technique of placing the hybrid arch bar (with screw) was easier than placing the conventional arch bar, and the time taken for the procedure (64.22 min for the modified arch bar and 135.50 min for the conventional arch bar) was significantly lower (and statistically significant). Preoperative orthopantomogram or cone beam computed tomography was required to plan the position of screw placement. According to Rai et al.,[14] patients with screws have better oral hygiene maintenance and employing screws for MMF is a method that reduces operating time.

In a research published by Brett J. King and Christensen,[15] glove penetrations occurred much more frequently during application in the Erich Arch Bar group than in the hybrid group. As per our findings, the average number of glove perforations in the traditional group was 2.40, whereas in the hybrid group, it was 0.55. The traditional group had considerably greater mean glove perforation than the hybrid group. In a study by S Hamid and Bede,[16] 7 out of 10 of the patients treated in the control group had their gloves perforated by an operator or assistant, whereas there was no gloves perforation during a procedure in the study group. All patients in both groups had stable, acceptable occlusion after the 6-week follow-up, and there were no late complications.

The vitality of teeth was tested using an electronic pulp tester both preoperatively and postoperatively at the time of splint removal in our study. In the conventional group, the mean number of nonvital teeth at the pretreatment level was 1.20. At the pretreatment level, the average number of nonvital teeth in the hybrid group was 0.55. The intergroup increase in the number of nonvital teeth (from pretreatment level) at 1st week, 3rd week, and 6th week was statistically significant in both the conventional and hybrid groups.

Asscherick et al.[17] revealed that the periodontal structure—such as the cementum, periodontal ligament, and bone—had been nearly completely repaired. Although there was contact between the root and the screw, there was no clinically significant damage. Roccia et al.,[10] according to their study, iatrogenic damage to dental roots was the most serious complication (1.5%).

When the perioperative criteria were used to evaluate the ease of the hybrid arch bar’s placement, it was found that placing intraoral vestibular incisions was challenging when the arch bars were positioned low into the vestibule.[6] Additionally, careful planning for the depth of the incision was deemed necessary if a hybrid arch bar was used in conjunction with the intraoral incision. Even then, the operating surgeon had to be very careful when closing the vestibular incision. In addition, screw tightening was required at follow-up visits in four cases due to the screws gradually loosening. In addition, 1.5 × 6 mm screws were substituted for 2.0 × 6 mm screws in a few instances due to loosening.[18]

During the treatment of mandibular fractures, this hybrid arch bar was found to have a tension band effect at the upper border that only IMF screws cannot provide. As a result, the current study’s findings suggest that the hybrid arch bar (with screws) can be used in place of conventional arch bars or other IMF methods and achieves results comparable to those of conventional arch bars in terms of oral hygiene and ease of placement.

CONCLUSION

The hybrid arch bar has advantages over the conventional Erich arch bar: Reduced harm to the periodontium, improved oral hygiene, less time needed to place the modified arch bar, no sharp pricks or needles, and better patient compliance. However, there were also a few other restrictions on how the hybrid arch bar could be used. Such as it is not cost-effective. When the lower arch bar is positioned lower in the vestibule, it can be difficult to make a vestibular incision. Endodontic treatment may be required if the root is damaged. Where necessary, the requirement to replace or tighten the screws.

However, the use of a hybrid arch bar (with screws) is a better alternative than the use of a conventional arch bar due to the shorter amount of time required for its placement, lower risks of operator injuries, improved periodontal health, and increased patient compliance. However, the placement of the modified arch bar is technique sensitive. As a result, the modified arch bar ought to be included in the inventory of the maxillofacial surgeon.

The major limitations of this study are that it is unable to account for variations between the two treatments, such as fracture patterns, due to the nature of sample selection. Furthermore, the period of follow-up in this trial may have been insufficient to detect endodontic problems. Since there is a small sample size, there is a risk of biased conclusions and potential for data contamination, so future research with larger and more diverse populations is warranted to validate the findings of this study.
