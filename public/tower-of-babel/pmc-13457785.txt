Validation of Self-Reported Disease History in Korea: A National Health Insurance Cohort Study
Authors: Yeon Woo Oh, Eun-Cheol Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-reported disease history is widely used in epidemiological studies but is susceptible to misclassification. This study aimed to evaluate the accuracy of self-reported history of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and stroke in Korea and to identify factors associated with reporting accuracy.MethodsWe analyzed data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS)-National Sample Cohort (2002–2019) for participants aged ≥ 40 years who underwent health check-ups (n = 312,367 for hypertension, 291,356 for diabetes, 289,596 for dyslipidemia, and 278,296 for stroke). Self-reported disease history was compared with medical claims records, which served as the reference standard for diagnosis history. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and observed agreement were calculated. Logistic regression models identified factors associated with false negative responses.ResultsSelf-reported prevalence was substantially lower than claims-based prevalence across all conditions: hypertension (33.9% vs. 47.9%), diabetes (15.0% vs. 36.1%), dyslipidemia (14.7% vs. 66.8%), and stroke (2.0% vs. 7.7%). Sensitivity was highest for hypertension (69.9%), followed by diabetes (40.7%), dyslipidemia (21.6%), and stroke (21.8%). Specificities exceeded 99% for all conditions. Factors reflecting greater healthcare engagement—advanced age and higher comorbidity burden—were associated with better reporting accuracy. Longer duration since initial diagnosis improved sensitivity for chronic conditions, while longer time since last hospital visit decreased sensitivity for hypertension and dyslipidemia.ConclusionSelf-reported disease history in the NHIS health check-up demonstrates high specificity but variable sensitivity across conditions, with underreporting being the primary concern. Temporal factors and healthcare engagement patterns significantly influence reporting accuracy. Our disease-specific validation parameters provide essential benchmarks for bias correction in epidemiological research and clinical practice in Korea, particularly for conditions with lower reporting sensitivities.Keywords: Self Report, Medical History Taking, Validation Study, National Health Insurance Service, Korea

INTRODUCTION

Misclassification in epidemiological studies can significantly bias results and reduce the power of statistical inferences.1,2 When exposures are misclassified, identification of risk factors may be compromised.3,4 Outcome misclassifications can adversely affect disease burden estimation and clinical relevance.5,6 The direction of bias tends toward-the-null in nondifferential misclassification, while remaining indeterminate in differential misclassification.1,4,7

Disease history data collected through self-reporting is particularly susceptible to misclassification.7,8,9,10 Various biases, including recall bias,11 interviewer bias12 and response bias,1 can influence the information-gathering process. Consequently, the validity of studies based on self-reported data may be questionable.13 In clinical settings, misclassification of patients’ significant medical histories may lead to inappropriate or potentially harmful interventions.

The accuracy of self-reported disease history has been assessed in several studies. Previous research has evaluated the accuracy of self-reported AIDS,14 chronic diseases,15 peptic ulcer16 and spondyloarthritis.17 The findings varied considerably depending on the type of disease and target population. For example, a meta-analysis on the sensitivity of self-reported hypertension history18 revealed that sensitivity ranged from 13.1%19 to 94.9%20 across different settings. In South Korea, studies have reported on the accuracy of self-reported stroke,21 myocardial infarction,21 cancer history,22 and history of hospitalization and outpatient visit23,24 among various subpopulations.

However, validation studies of self-reported prevalent chronic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and stroke, in Korea have been limited in scope and generalizability. Previous Korean studies have either used cross-sectional designs comparing self-reports with concurrent clinical measurements rather than longitudinal diagnosis records,25 or focused on highly specific populations such as atomic bomb survivors.26 No comprehensive longitudinal validation study has examined the accuracy of self-reported chronic diseases using medical claims data as the reference standard in a large-scale Korean population.

The Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) general health check-up provides an ideal setting for such validation research. This comprehensive nationwide program, provided biennially to adults aged 20 years and older, represents one of the largest health screening initiatives globally with over 17 million participants in 2022. The program routinely collects self-reported disease history for hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and stroke, which can be linked with participants' medical claims records for validation purposes.

The present study has 2 primary objectives. First, we aim to evaluate the accuracy of self-reported disease history of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and stroke in Korea using medical claims data as the reference standard. Second, we seek to explore patterns of misclassification according to socioeconomic and temporal factors, and identify factors that influence the accuracy of self-reporting. This study is expected to contribute to a better understanding of misclassification in self-reported disease history, with implications for both research methodology and clinical practice.

METHODS

Study population

We conducted this investigation using version 2.2 of the National Health Insurance Service-National Sample Cohort (NHIS-NSC),27 a nationally representative dataset from Korea. Established in 2002, this cohort initially enrolled 1,025,340 individuals, representing 2.2% of the 46,605,433 people registered in the National Health Information Database, with continuous enrollment of newborns annually thereafter. Participant selection utilized stratified random sampling based on total annual healthcare expenditures. The cohort provides integrated data encompassing insurance enrollment status, healthcare utilization, provider characteristics, and routine health screening results. Further details regarding cohort construction and composition are available in prior publications.27

For our analysis, we selected participants who underwent at least 1 general health check-up between 2018 and 2019, and were aged 40 years or older at the time of examination. Specifically, we focused on those who reported their history of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and stroke. After excluding participants with missing covariates, the final analytic sample comprised 312,367 participants for hypertension, 291,356 for diabetes, 289,596 for dyslipidemia, and 278,296 for stroke.

Assessment of self-reported and actual diagnosis history

Participants were classified as having a positive self-report for each disease if they indicated so at their most recent general health check-up between 2018 and 2019. Actual diagnosis history was determined by the presence of at least 1 claim record with the relevant diagnosis from 2002 to the check-up date. Disease identification was based on Korean Standard Classification of Diseases (KCD) codes: hypertension (I10–I15), diabetes (E10–E14), dyslipidemia (E78), and stroke (I60–I64).

Sociodemographic and temporal factors

We assessed the accuracy of self-reported disease history across various sociodemographic factors. The covariates included sex, age (categorized as 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years), insurance percentile (stratified as medical aid beneficiaries, 0–30%, 31–70%, and 71–100%), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI; categorized as 0, 1, 2, and ≥ 3) based on a claim data of a recent year,28 body weight (dichotomized as overweight: body mass index [BMI] ≥ 23 kg/m2 vs. not overweight: BMI < 23 kg/m2), alcohol consumption (current drinker or not), smoking (current smoker or not), disability status (registered as disabled or not), and place of residence (Greater Seoul, metropolitan city, or other).

To evaluate the temporal effect on self-report accuracy, we included 2 additional variables: time elapsed since last hospital visit (< 1 month, 1–6 months, 7–12 months, and ≥ 13 months) and duration since first diagnosis of each disease (never diagnosed, ≤ 5 years, 6–10 years, 11–15 years, and ≥ 16 years). Different cutoffs for duration since first diagnosis (never diagnosed, ≤ 1 year, 2–10 years, and ≥ 11 years) were also applied to check robustness.

Statistical analysis

First, we characterized participants at the time of their health check-up according to their self-reported status for each disease of interest. As the target populations differed for the 4 diseases under investigation, these characteristics are presented separately.

Second, we evaluated the accuracy of self-reported disease history using medical claim data as the reference standard. We calculated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and observed agreement as measures of accuracy. Sensitivity was defined as the proportion of participants with claims records who correctly self-reported having the disease. Specificity was defined as the proportion of participants without claims records who correctly self-reported not having the disease. PPV represented the proportion of participants who self-reported having a disease and actually had claims records for that disease, while NPV represented the proportion of participants who self-reported not having a disease and actually had no claims records for that disease. Observed agreement was calculated as the proportion of participants whose self-reported disease status matched their claims records among all participants. The prevalence of each disease within analytic sample is also reported, as PPV and NPV are directly influenced by disease prevalence. We stratified these accuracy metrics by demographic and temporal subgroups for exploratory analysis of potential variations in reporting accuracy. To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted two sensitivity analyses using more stringent case definitions. First, we required participants to have at least 2 claim records with the relevant diagnosis within 1 year prior to their health checkup. Second, for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, we required both at least 1 claim record with the corresponding diagnosis code and at least 1 prescription claim for disease-specific medications (antihypertensives, antidiabetics, or lipid-lowering agents, respectively) during the entire observation period from 2002 to the checkup date. For each alternative definition, we recalculated overall accuracy metrics for all diseases.

Third, among participants with a confirmed diagnosis based on claims data, we explored factors associated with false negative misclassification (i.e., failure to self-report a diagnosed condition). We constructed logistic regression models for each disease, using self-report status as a binary outcome variable (true positive [0] or false negative [1]), to evaluate associations with demographic and temporal factors. 95% confidence intervals of odds ratio are presented for reference. All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Ethics statement

The study follows the ethical considerations of the Helsinki Declaration. All protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Yonsei University Health System, Severance Hospital (IRB No. 2024-1413-001). Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Among the study participants, self-reported prevalence was 33.9% for hypertension, 15.0% for diabetes, 14.7% for dyslipidemia, and 2.0% for stroke, while claims-based prevalence was substantially higher at 47.9% for hypertension, 36.1% for diabetes, 66.8% for dyslipidemia, and 7.7% for stroke (Table 1). This pattern of underreporting was consistent across all four conditions, with dyslipidemia showing the largest discrepancy (52.1 percentage points difference). For both self-reported and claims-based prevalence, the proportion of positive cases increased with advancing age, higher CCI scores, overweight, and medical aid beneficiary status for all 4 conditions. Participants who had visited a hospital within a month were more likely to report having been diagnosed with each of the four conditions. Additionally, the likelihood of self-reporting a diagnosis increased with longer time elapsed since first diagnosis for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, but not stroke.

Accuracy of the self-reported disease history

The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and agreement of self-reports were calculated for each disease, along with the prevalence (Table 2). Dyslipidemia exhibited the highest prevalence (66.8%), followed by hypertension (47.8%), diabetes (36.1%), and stroke (7.7%). Self-reports demonstrated notably high sensitivity for hypertension (69.9%), compared to diabetes (40.7%), dyslipidemia (21.6%), and stroke (21.8%). Specificities were consistently high across all conditions, exceeding 99.0%. Distinct patterns in PPV and NPV emerged between chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia) and the acute condition (stroke). The chronic diseases demonstrated very high PPV, with values of at least 97.7% for each condition, while stroke exhibited relatively lower PPV of 82.6%. Conversely, NPV was highest for stroke, whereas chronic diseases showed NPV below 80.0%. Notably, dyslipidemia had the lowest NPV at only 38.6%. Observed agreement was highest in stroke (93.6%) and lowest in dyslipidemia (47.4%).

Temporal factors showed strong associations with sensitivity among chronic diseases. Recent hospital visits dramatically improved reporting accuracy: hypertension sensitivity declined from 72.8% for visits < 1 month to 16.3% for visits ≥ 13 months prior, with similar patterns for diabetes and dyslipidemia. Conversely, longer disease duration enhanced sensitivity for chronic conditions, with hypertension improving from 56.6% at ≤ 5 years to 83.1% at ≥ 16 years, and diabetes from 27.6% to 61.9%. These temporal patterns were not observed for stroke. All results were persistent with different disease duration cutoffs (never diagnosed, ≤ 1 year, 2–10 years, and ≥ 11 years, Supplementary Table 1).

When we applied two more stringent case definitions, distinct patterns emerged (Table 2). Under the first definition requiring at least 2 claim records within 1 year of health checkup, sensitivity increased substantially across all diseases, with dramatic improvements observed for diabetes (from 40.7% to 83.6%) and stroke (from 21.8% to 50.3%). The second definition, requiring at least 1 claim record and corresponding drug prescription during the entire observation period, yielded similarly elevated sensitivities for the three chronic conditions: hypertension (75.5%), diabetes (84.2%), and dyslipidemia (33.0%). Specificities remained consistently high (> 97%) across all conditions under all case definitions. As expected with more restrictive criteria, disease prevalence decreased substantially for all conditions, resulting in lower PPVs and higher NPVs. The most pronounced changes were observed in the first sensitivity analysis, where stroke showed the greatest PPV decrease (from 82.6% to 55.5%), while dyslipidemia demonstrated the largest NPV increase (from 38.6% to 79.4%).

Factors associated with false negative response

We fitted four logistic regression models among participants with each disease, considering false negative response as the “event” (Table 4). Among sociodemographic factors, older age, overweight, current drinker, and current smoker were associated with lower odds of false negative response for hypertension. For diabetes, male sex, younger age, CCI score ≥ 1, overweight, and current smoker were associated with lower odds. Female sex, older age, CCI score ≥ 1, and overweight were associated with lower odds of false negative response among those with dyslipidemia. DISCUSSION

In this analysis of the NHIS-NSC data, we conducted the first comprehensive longitudinal validation study of self-reported chronic disease history in Korea using medical claims data as the reference standard. Our findings revealed three key patterns in reporting accuracy. First, sensitivity varied dramatically by disease type, with hypertension showing the highest accuracy (69.9%) and dyslipidemia the lowest (21.6%), while specificity remained consistently high (> 99%) across all conditions. This pattern indicates that underreporting rather than overreporting is the primary concern for self-reported disease history. Second, temporal factors emerged as critical determinants of reporting accuracy, with longer time since diagnosis improving recall for chronic diseases, while longer intervals since healthcare visits decreased accuracy. Third, factors reflecting greater healthcare engagement—advanced age and higher CCI scores—were associated with substantially better disease recall across conditions.

Two studies have evaluated the sensitivity of self-reported history of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and stroke among the Korean population. A cross-sectional study using a nationally representative sample of Koreans aged 50 and older reported higher sensitivities for hypertension (73.0%), diabetes (79.3%), and hypercholesterolemia (46.7%) compared to our findings (69.9%, 40.7% and 21.6%).25 This discrepancy likely stems from differences in study design: the previous study used current values of clinical measurements to determine disease status rather than diagnosis records, thus effectively assessing disease awareness rather than recall of diagnosed conditions. Another study examining an atomic bomb survivor cohort compared self-reported hypertension and diabetes history with NHIS claim data and found 83% sensitivity for both diseases,26 but this highly specific population limits generalizability. Our study fills this gap by providing validation parameters for a large, diverse Korean cohort using longitudinal claims data.

Temporal factors significantly influence self-reporting accuracy. A Finnish longitudinal study comparing self-reported disease history between prevalent and incident cases found substantially lower sensitivities for incident cases of hypertension (55% vs. 86%) and diabetes (63% vs. 91%).8 These findings align with our results showing increased sensitivity for chronic diseases with longer time since initial diagnosis. Additionally, an English longitudinal study demonstrated that participants with at least 2 hospital visits in the preceding 2 years exhibited higher sensitivity of self-reporting for cancer, stroke, and heart attack compared to those with at least 1 visit.29 Our study confirms this relationship and extends it to chronic diseases, establishing a clear connection between healthcare utilization patterns and reporting accuracy.

It is important to distinguish between two different temporal effects observed in our study. First, regarding time since first diagnosis, we found that patients with longer disease duration (≥ 16 years since first diagnosis) had higher sensitivity compared to those recently diagnosed (≤ 5 years). This reflects improved disease awareness and recall that develops over time with ongoing management of chronic conditions. Second, regarding time since last hospital visit, we observed that patients who had recent hospital visits (< 1 month) showed much higher sensitivity than those with distant visits (≥ 13 months). This reflects the recency effect where recent clinical encounters enhance recall of medical information. Therefore, the optimal reporting accuracy occurs among patients with both established diagnoses and recent healthcare engagement, while the poorest accuracy is seen in newly diagnosed patients who have not had recent medical contact.

This distinction has important implications for study design. Cross-sectional studies may systematically miss recently diagnosed patients while potentially overrepresenting patients with recent healthcare visits. Conversely, longitudinal studies using repeated self-reports can leverage the finding as reporting accuracy improves with disease duration, potentially leading to better case ascertainment in later follow-up periods compared to early study phases.

From a clinical perspective, our findings provide guidance for primary care physicians taking patient histories. When patients report having chronic diseases like hypertension, diabetes, or dyslipidemia, the very high PPVs (> 97%) indicate physicians can be confident that reported diagnoses are accurate. However, the relatively low NPVs, particularly for dyslipidemia (38.6%), suggest that negative responses to chronic diseases cannot rule out disease. For instance, when a patient denies a history of dyslipidemia but presents with risk factors (e.g., obesity, family history, or other metabolic conditions), clinicians should proceed with confirmatory testing such as a lipid profile rather than relying solely on the patient’s self-report. Similarly, for patients who deny diabetes but exhibit clinical indicators such as elevated BMI or symptoms of hyperglycemia, obtaining fasting glucose or hemoglobin A1c measurements would be prudent. Conversely, for stroke, positive reports (PPV = 82.6%), rather than negative reports (NPV = 93.8%), should be interpreted more cautiously. Clinicians should consider verifying positive stroke reports through objective documentation such as hospital discharge records, imaging results, or specialist consultation notes when making clinical decisions that depend on stroke history.

Our sensitivity analysis provides important insights into the interpretation of self-reported disease history. The substantial improvement in sensitivity observed when applying the case definition requiring at least 2 claim records within 1 year can be interpreted from two complementary perspectives. First, the more stringent definition may improve accuracy by excluding transient or rule-out diagnoses that do not represent established disease. This interpretation is supported by the substantially lower sensitivities observed among participants with only a single claim record within 1 year of health checkup compared to those with multiple claims (Supplementary Table 2). Second, the improved sensitivity suggests that self-reported disease history primarily captures recent healthcare encounters rather than comprehensive lifetime disease burden. This recency effect has important clinical implications: rather than relying on patients’ spontaneous recall, clinicians should explicitly probe for historical diagnoses by asking targeted questions such as “Have you ever been diagnosed with diabetes, even if it was years ago?” or “Have you ever been told you have high cholesterol, regardless of when you last saw a doctor for it?”

Several approaches exist to address misclassification bias in epidemiological research using our validation parameters. For case-control studies, a simple method for calculating bias-adjusted odds ratios using known sensitivity and specificity values are available.7 A simple illustration of correction approach, including a worked example using our data is provided in the supplementary material (Supplementary Data 1).6 More sophisticated techniques for correcting odds ratio in logistic regression when either exposure30 or outcome6 measurements contain uncertainty have been developed. Alternative approaches include using probabilistic values for binary variables,31,32 or implementing specialized study designs such as counter-matched nested case-control studies to minimize exposure misclassification effects.33 When misclassification is a concern, appropriate additional analyses are strongly recommended to maintain the validity of research findings.

Our study offers several unique contributions to the literature on self-reported disease history. First, this is the first investigation evaluating the accuracy of self-reported disease history in the Korean national general health check-up program. Second, our disease-specific sensitivity and specificity measurements across various conditions provide essential parameters for researchers applying bias-correction methodologies, as many techniques for addressing misclassification bias require these values. Finally, our examination of temporal factors can be useful for designing longitudinal studies, implementing targeted health education interventions, and developing more effective screening questionnaires that account for time-dependent variations in disease recall. By identifying when and for whom self-reported disease history is most reliable, healthcare systems can better calibrate screening tools and risk assessment protocols.

This study warrants several limitations. First, we used medical claim data as the reference standard for disease diagnosis, but the NHIS-NSC data only dates back to 2002. Consequently, individuals diagnosed before 2002 who had no subsequent claims would be misclassified as having no diagnosis history in our analysis. Although this likely affects a minimal number of participants given our 2018–2019 data collection timeframe, it may impact assessment of long-standing conditions. Second, we relied on KCD codes from medical claims to establish disease status, which may introduce additional misclassification. Claims data primarily serves administrative and reimbursement purposes rather than research, potentially containing coding errors, upcoding, or diagnostic inconsistencies. Third, our study population consisted exclusively of individuals who voluntarily participated in general health check-ups, who typically have different characteristics compared to non-participants. This selection bias limits the generalizability of our findings to the broader Korean population, particularly to those with limited healthcare access or health literacy. Fourth, our study design did not distinguish between participants who misreported due to genuinely not knowing their diagnosis versus those who knew but failed to report it for other reasons. This distinction is crucial for developing targeted interventions to improve reporting accuracy. Qualitative follow-up with participants demonstrating discordant reporting would provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying misclassification. Lastly, while sensitivity and specificity are considered intrinsic test characteristics that remain stable across different populations,34,35 some studies suggest this assumption may not always hold true.36,37 Therefore, our sensitivity and specificity values should be interpreted within the context of the specific disease prevalence observed in this Korean health screening population. Future validation studies in populations with different disease prevalence rates would help confirm the generalizability of these accuracy parameters.

Our study demonstrates that the accuracy and interpretation of self-reported disease history depend on the complex interplay among 3 key dimensions: case definition, temporal dynamics, and disease characteristics. The case definition determines whether self-reports capture transient diagnostic encounters or established clinical conditions, while temporal factors–both disease duration and healthcare recency–modulate recall accuracy through distinct mechanisms. These elements jointly shape reporting patterns: chronic conditions show improving sensitivity with longer disease duration but declining accuracy with distant healthcare encounters, whereas acute conditions like stroke exhibit different trajectories.

This integrated framework has practical implications for both research and clinical practice. Researchers must select case definitions aligned with their research questions and apply appropriate bias-correction methods informed by disease-specific validation parameters. Clinicians should actively elicit historical diagnoses rather than relying on spontaneous recall, while recognizing that positive self-reports of chronic diseases are highly reliable. Understanding self-reported disease history as the product of clinical reality, healthcare engagement patterns, and time-dependent recall processes—rather than simple disease presence or absence—enables more sophisticated interpretation and appropriate application in both epidemiological research and clinical care.
