Influence of heat-treatment temperature on the optical behavior and luminescence characteristics of Sm₂O₃-doped lithium borate glasses
Authors: A Ratep, I Kashif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the effect of heat treatment on light emission within the thermal equilibrium region between the glass transition temperature (Tg) and the crystallization temperatures (Tc). Samples were prepared and analyzed in the glassy state and thermal analysis was used to determine the thermal stability zone. Heat treatments were performed at 450, 500, 550, 600, and 675 °C. Most samples containing lithium oxide up to 50 mol% exhibit a strong absorption peak at 450 °C, which decreases with increasing heat treatment temperature. In contrast, samples containing more than 50% lithium oxide show a higher peak intensity at 500 °C. Optical transmittance decreases as the annealing temperature approaches the glass transition temperature (Tg) and increases as it approaches the crystallization temperature. The X-ray diffraction patterns of the studied samples indicate the presence of either two phases or one phase of Li2B4O7, LiBO2, and LiB3O5 deposited in the heat-treated sample at 700 °C, with crystal sizes in the nanoscale up to 18 nm. This resulted in a decrease in decay time and a change in the emission color to white or blue. The optical band gap energy of the heat-treated samples increases by up to 10%. The Judd-Ofelt coefficients follow the order Ω2 > Ω6 > Ω4, consistent with the typical behavior of glass. The excitation and emission spectra show that the main excitation peak shifts from 393 nm in the glass state to 403 nm (heat-treated glass), while the dominant emission peak shifts from 596 to 600 nm. The intensity of both excitation and emission increases with increasing heat treatment temperature. Correlational color temperature CCT values for heat-treated samples below 4000 K indicate their suitability for laser and red-orange light emission applications, while some samples heat-treated at temperatures at 650 °C or higher exhibit CCT values ​​above 4000 K, approaching the bluish-white region.Keywords: Heat treatment temperature; XRD; Lithium borate glass; Judd-Ofelt; Absorption, and emission properties; CIE; CCT; Color puritySubject terms: Materials science, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

The diverse applications of rare-earth element-doped glass allow for the modification and study of the effects of the host material, modifiers, and heat treatment on the prepared glass. Glass’s ability to be doped with various rare-earth elements1 makes it suitable for a wide range of applications as solid-state lasers, laser cooling, optical communications, storage, optical devices for three-dimensional color displays, and upconverting optical devices2 depend on the emission color intensity and the color chromaticity. Glass offers superior optical capabilities compared to phosphor-based LED lamps. The presence of phosphorus3 increases light scattering, which reduces the intensity of the light and raises the temperature. Furthermore, the phosphor coating reduces light transmission, thereby decreasing luminous efficiency3. Using borate as a host material offers several advantages4–7 of high transparency, high thermal stability, low melting temperature high solubility of RE, high thermal expansion coefficient, high refractive index. Lithium borate glass is considered a solid electrolyte that can produce various structural units, including boroxel, pentaborate, tetraborare and diborate1. The previous references8–14 demonstrate that different rare earth (RE) ions emit different colors. Additionally, variations in color emission and purity make these ions suitable for various applications. Among the different RE ions, the choice to use samarium ions (Sm3+) doped into glass materials is based on their high quantum efficiency5, broad stimulated emission cross-section, and strong luminous intensity in the red-orange spectrum. These characteristics make Sm3+ ions ideal for use in memory devices and laser production4 and important for next-generation nuclear fusion and gain media15. The wide range between 4G5/2 and the next lower level reduces the multi-phonon transition and is unaffected by high phonon in the host material. It has a high energy level emission resulting in visible and near IR emission4. Glass porcelain is a solid material composed of a crystalline phase and a glassy phase. Traditional glass porcelain is made by heating a sample to a temperature approximately 10 K above its glass transition temperature (Tg). As the temperature rises, crystal nucleation begins, and with further increases, crystal growth occurs. The crystal nuclei cluster together increasing the critical radius of the nuclei16. Glass porcelain has been used since ancient times as decorative glass17, and with continued research, it has attracted the attention of many fields, such as the study of its thermal properties. Glass porcelain is used as a filling material to bond ceramics and metals, reaching temperatures exceeding 800 °C18. It is also used in dental prosthetics due to its various optical properties and the availability of different colors to suit the patient’s needs. It is also used in diverse applications, such as cooktop panels and telescope mirror components. The other advantage of glass porcelain lies in its ability to integrate highly efficient crystals within a glass matrix. The curvature of the glass containing nanocrystals is controlled by altering the refractive index within the material. This allows for the fabrication of optical fibres with nonlinear and thermal properties19. Furthermore, the rearrangement of nanocrystals modulates absorption and radiation, also reducing phonon energy and increasing lifetime. Glasses can be easily modified for specific functions by changing their chemical composition and heat treatment temperature. Glasses’ adaptable structural units enable modifications in crystalline phases and changing functional characteristics according to planned uses.

Lithium borate is a favored host due to its transparency, low melting point, and thermal stability. When Sm2O3 is added, these glasses are suitable for orange-red phosphors, lasers, and high-density optical storage. Accordingly, research has examined heat treatment within the thermal equilibrium range and the impact of the lithium-to-boron ratio on optical properties. However, there is little research on how heat treatment affects the optical properties of oxide glass. This study aims to address that gap and analyze the resulting changes.

The work focuses on how heat treatment at temperatures above the glass transition temperature (Tg) and up to the crystallization temperature affects structural changes through the Judd-Ofelt process, as well as how it influences luminescence properties and color emissions.

Results and discussion

The XRD patterns were recorded for the heat-treated lithium borate glass samples doped with samarium at 450, 500, 550, 600, 675, and 700 °C.

Based on the composition of 80% B2O3 and 20% Li2O and heat treated at 700 °C (S1HT700), observing a mixed-phase devitrification. The glass has shifted from an amorphous network into a complex crystalline “glass-ceramic” containing at least two distinct lithium borate structures. Firstly, lithium tetraborate (Li2B4O7), specifically the tetragonal crystal system (Space Group I4₁cd). With peaks at 22.4, 23.3, 26.16, 27.96, 34.9, 35.2, 40.86, and 44.7. The second lithium triborate (LiB3O5) peaks at 22.4°, 23.3°, and 26.16° are the primary fingerprints for the orthorhombic phase (JCPDS 30–0749). And third, lithium metaborate (LiBO2) exhibits a peak near 18.5° or 28.4°, which may suggest a secondary phase of lithium metaborate (JCPDS 32–0546). The peaks of XRD patterns of heat-treated glass composition 66.7B2O3−33.3Li2O (S2HT700) are characteristic of the crystallisation of the lithium diborate phase. While some peaks overlap with the tetraborate phase (Li2B4O7) seen in previous sample, the shift in relative intensities and specific positions confirms a change in the dominant crystalline structure. For the 50B2O3 − 50Li2O (mol%) (S3HT700) composition, the peaks observed align very well with the monoclinic phase of LiBO2 (typically JCPDS card #32–0592). With a composition of 33.3 B2O3 – 66.7 Li2O (S4HT700), the experimental peaks are a strong match for the monoclinic phase of Li3BO3 (often associated with JCPDS cards #18–0717 or #74–1085). All heat-treated samples up to 675 °C have a glassy nature44–46 as shown in Fig. 2. The crystal size of the phases formed because of heat treatment at 700 °C was determined using the Scherrer equation. The results confirmed that the crystal sizes were in the nanoscale, ranging from 6 to 18 nanometers.

1. Structural Transformation:

The Li₂O-B₂O₃ glass lattice exhibits a stochastic arrangement of BO₃ and BO₄ units. Crystallisation is induced to produce composite material.

Glass State: Homogeneous and isotropic. Sm³⁺ ions occupy slightly varied positions (heterogeneous expansion).

Ceramic Glass State: Comprises crystalline grains (such as Li₂B₄O₇ or LiBO₂ or LiB3O5) embedded within a residual glass matrix. This leads to the creation of “granular boundaries” and internal interfaces.

2. Optical Properties:

Light traverses glass with minimal dispersion due to its uniform density. In ceramics, when crystalline grains exceed approximately 18 nanometers in size, the disparity in refractive index between the crystal and the glass results in light scattering.

The increase in transmission can generally be attributed to homogeneity52. Compared with previous work49, this study found that a closer annealing temperature to the crystallization point increases transmission in contrast, when the annealing temperature is closer to the glass transition temperature (Tg), transmission decreases.

This results from the structural relaxation of the glass lattice and the elimination of internal defects during the thermal treatment process. Within lithium borate systems, during annealing close to the glass transition temperature (Tg), the thermal energy is insufficient for the complete alignment of the structural units. These stresses reduce the amount of light that can traverse the sample, resulting in a decrease in transmittance. Annealing near the crystallization point (Tc) occurs within the “relaxation” zone, where elevated temperatures enable the borate lattice to relax. During this process, internal stresses decrease, resulting in more structurally homogeneous glass. As a result of the reduced number of dispersion centers and the lower internal stress, optical transparency increases. While transmittance increases as the critical temperature (Tc) approaches, exceeding it or maintaining a temperature too close to it for an extended period will have the opposite effect, causing a sharp decrease in transmittance and the glass becoming opaque.

The optical band gap energy Eg depends on several factors, including bond linkage and number of non-bridging oxygen. Compared to the previous studies, and contrary to the data in Table 2, an increase in Eg values was observed compared to the base glass25. This increase is attributed to a reduction in defects, specifically, a decrease in oxygen vacancies, resulting in fewer non-bridging oxygens (NBOs) upon heat treatment. The change in the network structure and atomic arrangement improves bond strength, which requires more energy for electron transition. Furthermore, heat application can enhance the potential for crystallization, which explains the higher Eg values compared to the amorphous state due to the reduction in impurities53,54.

The date obtained from the oscillator strength can be used to identify the hypersensitive transitions, indicated by high intensity associated with ligand effects around rare earth (RE) elements. This hypersensitive transition can be identified through the oscillator strength data, particularly where a high intensity is observed during the transition 6H5/2→6F7/2 in all the heat-treated glass samples. This observation aligns with the selection rules ΔJ ≤ 2, ΔL ≤ 2, and ΔS = 0.

J-O coefficients provide detailed information about the energy levels of rare-earth elements. Specifically, Ω2 represents short-range characteristics, such as asymmetry and covalent bonding between the rare-earth element and its surrounding bond. In contrast, Ω4 and Ω6 reflect long-range characteristics, such as the hardness and viscosity of heat-treated glass. The J-O coefficients are used for two comparisons: first, to assess the effect of increasing the annealing temperature, and second, to compare the results with the pre-prepared glass samples.

Figure 5 illustrates the relationship between the obtained J-O coefficients and the increase in annealing temperature. It was observed that the value of Ω2 was lower than that of Ω4 and Ω6, indicating a reduction in asymmetric properties and a decrease in covalent bonding around rare earth elements. The lower value of Ω2 is attributed to the incorporation of rare earth elements into nanocrystals, as explained by Jlassi et al. (2012)55. Figure 5 shows that the high values of Ω4 and Ω6 in all heat-treated glass samples indicate an enhancement in the long-range properties, such as viscosity and hardness of the annealed glass. Additionally, when comparing the results to previously published data, the Ω2 value in the prepared glass was found to be higher suggesting an increase in bond order and improved bonding around rare earth elements. The J-O (Ω₂) properties are of great importance. Higher Ω₂ values ​​indicate a high degree of structural irregularity, low symmetry, and a stronger covalent bond between the metal and the bonding agent. A lower Ω₂ value suggests a more centrally symmetric and ionic environment. Studies of heat-treated samples show that Ω₂ values ​​are higher than those of phosphates, tellurates, and some borate groups. In these samples, the observed trend is Ω₂ > Ω₆ > Ω₄, indicating that Ω₆ is greater than Ω₄. This trend indicates that the samples studied exhibit high efficiency in generating lasers from the 4G5/2 to 6H7/2 transition in samarium ions.

In most types of glass, Sm3+ emits a “red-orange” color due to three main transitions: from 4G5/2 to 6H5/2 (green-yellow, ~ 560 nm), from 4G5/2 to 6H7/2 (orange, ~ 600 nm), and from 4G5/2 to 6H9/2 (red, ~ 645 nm) the latter being typically the strongest in glass.

When glass samples containing varying proportions of lithium and boron are heat-treated from 450 °C to 675 °C, red light emission is observed. At these temperatures, Sm3+ ions are embedded in an amorphous and irregular borate lattice. The local site around the Sm3+ ion lacks a center of symmetry. This enhances the electric dipole transitions, particularly the red emission at around 645 nm. The high phonon energy of the B-O lattice (around 1400 cm⁻¹) sometimes quenches higher-energy transitions, making the red emission dominant.

Upon heat treatment at 700 °C, crystalline phases such as Li₂B₄O₇ (JCPDS 18–0717) or LiBO₂ (JCPDS 32–0592) are formed. As the glass crystallizes, Sm³⁺ ions may occupy multiple, distinct locations some in the new crystal lattice and some remaining in the residual glass phase. The crystalline environment alters the relative intensity of the Sm³⁺ transitions. If the intensity of yellowish green (around 560 nm) and orange (600 nm) is greater than that of red, the visible combination of these wavelengths appears white to the human eye. Crystallization at 700 °C can also induce structural defects, also known as “color centers,” in the borate lattice, which emit light in the blue to cyan range. This light mixes with the red/orange light of samarium trivalent ions (Sm3+) to produce a cool white light.

In the treated sample containing 66.7% Li2O, the blue light emitted by the orthoborate crystalline phase of sample Li3BO3 (JCPDS 18–0717) is due to the formation of isolated BO3 units. The Li3BO3 crystal itself, or its defects, emits blue light that overpowers the Sm3+ signal.

Conclusion

The influence of heat-treatment temperature on the thermal-equilibrium range of the prepared samples was examined, along with its impact on the optical and photonic behavior of lithium borate glass doped with samarium ions (Sm³⁺). The characteristic Sm³⁺ ion transitions were identified in both the optical absorption and emission spectra across the ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared regions. While the emission wavelengths remained unchanged compared to previously measured untreated samples, a clear variation in emission intensity was observed. The spectra feature of the prepared glasses closely resembles those reported of Sm³⁺ ions in other borate-based hosts. Measurements of absorption and emission bands revealed that the Sm³⁺-doped glasses exhibit strong optical properties in both the visible and near-infrared regions. The heat-treated samples produced emissions in a range of color including green, orange, reddish orange and bright red, with an intensity that depends on the processing condition. Notably, the sample containing 20 mol% Li₂O generated white-light emission with a purity 20%. The presence of specific phases, such as Li₂B₄O₇, LiBO₂, and LiB₃O₅, which were thermally precipitated in samples treated at 700 °C, in a nanocrystalline size (≈ 18 nm). This decrease in decay time and a shift in emission color to white or blue, with a color temperature ranging from 6000 to 7000 K. These findings demonstrate that this class of glasses possesses considerable potential for use in advanced luminescent and photonic devices.
