Insurance IQ: Demystifying health insurance for young people with inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Laura Gilligan, Jeannie S Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMany adolescents and young adults with inflammatory bowel disease (AYA‐IBD) lack essential health insurance knowledge. We evaluated an electronic health record (EHR)–based educational intervention to improve insurance literacy. From January to August 2025, AYA‐IBD who had completed a health insurance knowledge assessment and had an upcoming clinic visit received a patient portal message containing a brief educational video and infographic. Health insurance knowledge was reassessed at the subsequent visit. Among 140 participants, 49 (35%) viewed the message. Education intervention engagement was associated with significantly greater improvement in health insurance knowledge scores (p = 0.03), with between‐group differences in scores evident by age (p = 0.04), ethnicity (p = 0.01), and insurance coverage (p < 0.01, government vs. private). EHR‐delivered health insurance education improved health insurance knowledge in AYA‐IBD and represents a scalable approach to support health literacy and engagement during transition‐focused care.Keywords: electronic health record, health literacy, transition

INTRODUCTION

Navigating health insurance is a complex yet essential skill for adults in the United States, particularly for individuals with chronic medical conditions. Developing health insurance literacy should begin in adolescence for patients with conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease that require lifelong care and successful transition from pediatric‐ to adult‐centered systems. The American Academy of Pediatrics identifies understanding health insurance as a core transition‐readiness competency. 1 Despite this, only a minority of adolescents (7.4%–20.4%) report discussing health insurance coverage changes with their pediatric healthcare provider. 2 , 3

A prior study by our group in 2023 identified low health insurance literacy among adolescents and young adults with IBD (AYA‐IBD). 4 The cohort included 50 AYA‐IBD with similar demographic characteristics, predominantly non‐Hispanic males with Crohn's disease. Using a validated assessment, participants achieved a mean score of only 40% correct, indicating substantial gaps in understanding. Similarly, studies in adult populations using the same standardized insurance literacy instrument have identified lower knowledge among vulnerable groups, including uninsured individuals, those with lower educational attainment, and young adults. 5 Together, these findings highlight the need for targeted educational interventions addressing health insurance knowledge during adolescence.

Electronic‐based interventions have demonstrated effectiveness in educating adolescents and young adults across a range of health topics, including nutrition, physical activity, sexual health, and mental health, utilizing modalities such as mobile applications, videos, social media, and video games. 6 , 7 More recently, a 2024 study showed that a virtual health insurance navigation intervention improved insurance literacy among young adults with cancer 8 ; however, participants were limited to ages 18–39 years.

While poor health insurance knowledge among adolescents has been documented, few studies have evaluated interventions to address this gap. Accordingly, we developed an electronic health record (EHR)‐based educational intervention to improve health insurance understanding among AYA‐IBD.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was reviewed by the University of California San Diego institutional review board and classified as exempt research, with a waiver of informed consent granted due to the minimal risk and anonymous nature of the data collection.

Study protocol

We evaluated an EHR‐based health insurance literacy intervention for AYA‐IBD at a freestanding children's hospital.

Participants were identified between January and August of 2025. Eligible patients were 15 years or older, had previously completed a health insurance knowledge assessment, and had an upcoming IBD clinic visit. One week prior to the visit, the patient and/or their guardian (depending on portal account activations) received a portal account message containing an infographic outlining fundamental health insurance concepts, including key terminology, methods to obtain insurance, differences between health maintenance organization (HMO) and preferred provider organization (PPO) plans, and applied example scenarios (Figure 1). The message also contained links to two brief educational videos about health insurance. At our institution, adolescent EHR portal accounts are available beginning at age 12 years, with limited proxy access for parents or guardians until age 18 years when the young adult assumes sole access to their EHR portal account.

Health insurance infographic distributed to participants via MyChart. HMO, health maintenance organization; PPO, preferred provider organization.

At their subsequent clinic visit, participants repeated the 10‐item health insurance knowledge assessment. Pre‐ and post‐intervention scores were compared, and changes were analyzed based on whether the participant viewed the portal communication prior to completing the post‐intervention assessment. Message engagement and timing were verified through EHR audit functionality.

The 10‐item health insurance knowledge assessment was developed by the Kaiser Family Foundation to evaluate American adults' understanding of U.S. health insurance concepts and terminology. 5 It consists of six multiple‐choice questions, two true‐false questions, and two computational items. Although designed for adults and written above a 6th‐grade reading level, we elected to apply adult standards given the relevance of insurance literacy to transition readiness. When validated in 2014 among more than 1200 adults, the instrument demonstrated lower performance among young adults (ages 18–29) and uninsured individuals, underscoring its applicability to vulnerable populations.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was change in health insurance knowledge assessment score from baseline to post‐intervention. Participants were categorized based on engagement with the portal education communication. Prespecified subgroup analyses examined ethnicity (Hispanic/Latino vs. non‐Hispanic/Latino), insurance type (government vs. private), and duration of disease (<5 vs. ≥5 years).

Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical characteristics, including IBD subtype, age, sex, ethnicity, preferred language, insurance coverage, and disease duration. Group comparisons by exposure to the education intervention were performed using the Van der Waerden test, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (Table 1).

To assess whether changes in health insurance knowledge differed by exposure to the portal education communication, we conducted a multivariate repeated‐measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with time (pre vs. post) as the within‐subject factor and portal communication engagement (exposed vs. not) as the between‐subject factor. Ethnicity, disease duration (<5 or ≥5 years), and insurance status (government vs. private) were included as additional between‐subject variables. The three‐way interactions time × intervention × ethnicity, time × intervention × disease duration, and time × intervention × insurance were also tested to determine whether the intervention's temporal effect differed across ethnicity, disease duration, and insurance status.

Given the non‐normal distribution of knowledge scores, model effects were evaluated using F‐tests with Type III sums of squares, which remain robust under moderate non‐normality. 9 As the dataset contained no missing data, all participants were included. Statistical significance was defined as two‐sided α = 0.05. All analyses were conducted using JMP version 18 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC).

RESULTS

Between January and August of 2025, 140 AYA‐IBD participated in the intervention. Participants were 56.4% male with a median (interquartile range [IQR]) age of 18 (16–19) years; 71.4% identified as non‐Hispanic/Latino. Most (55.7%) had Crohn's Disease, with 33.6% having Ulcerative Colitis and 10.7% having Indeterminate Colitis. Median disease duration was 3.9 (2.5–6.7) years, calculated from the date of diagnostic endoscopy to the date of the post‐intervention health insurance knowledge assessment. Government insurance covered 32.9% of participants. The mean interval between baseline and post‐intervention health insurance knowledge assessments was 307 days.

Forty‐nine participants (35%) viewed the health insurance message in their portal account prior to completing the post‐intervention health insurance knowledge assessment. Among the remaining 91 participants, the message was not viewed, viewed only by a proxy, or viewed after completion of the assessment. Participants who viewed the health insurance education message were older (median [IQR]: 19 [18, 19] years) compared with those who did not view the message (17 [16–19] years, p = 0.001). In contrast, other demographic and clinical characteristics—including sex, ethnicity, disease duration, IBD subtype, and insurance status—were similar between groups.

Participants who engaged with the portal education message demonstrated significantly greater improvement in health insurance knowledge scores compared with those who did not (Table 1, p = 0.03). Among message viewers, the median score change was +1 (IQR 0–2), whereas non‐viewers had a median score change of 0 (IQR −1 to 2).

Subgroup analyses (Table 1) revealed significant improvements in health insurance knowledge scores among participants with government insurance (N = 46; p = 0.02) and those with disease duration <5 years (N = 90; p = 0.03). Hispanic participants demonstrated higher post‐intervention health insurance knowledge scores among message viewers compared with non‐viewers (p = 0.04); however, the change from baseline did not significantly differ significantly between groups (p = 0.11).

A multivariate repeated‐measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant time × portal education exposure interaction, F(1, 134) = 4.88, p = 0.03, ηp 2 ≈ 0.04, indicating that changes in knowledge from pre‐ to post‐intervention differed between participants exposed to portal education messages and those not exposed. Specifically, participants who engaged with portal education messages demonstrated increased health insurance knowledge scores from baseline, whereas those without exposure exhibited minimal change. Among the between‐subject factors, insurance status (F(1, 134) = 10.25, p < 0.01, ηp 2 ≈ 0.08) and ethnicity (F[1, 134] = 6.45, p = 0.01, ηp 2 ≈ 0.05) and age (F[1, 134] = 4.25, p = 0.04, ηp 2 ≈ 0.03) were significantly associated with health insurance knowledge scores. In contrast, disease duration was not a statistically significant between‐subject factor. Three‐way interaction terms (evaluating time × portal education exposure × insurance status/ethnicity/disease duration) were also nonsignificant. Together, these findings indicate that exposure to the portal‐based education communication contributed to improved health insurance knowledge over time, while insurance status, ethnicity, and age—but not disease duration—were associated with differences in health insurance knowledge levels.

DISCUSSION

We demonstrate the successful implementation of an EHR‐based health insurance education intervention for AYA‐IBD, a population previously shown to have low health insurance literacy. Participants who engaged with the educational content delivered via their portal account demonstrated significantly greater improvement in health insurance knowledge scores compared to those who did not. Notably, health insurance knowledge differed by government insurance and ethnicity.

The EHR represents a practical and effective platform for delivering health education to AYA, given its accessibility and adolescents' familiarity with and aptitude using digital technologies. Our group has previously shown that transition services for AYA‐IBD can be successfully delivered via the EHR, 11 and the current study extends this work by demonstrating the feasibility of delivering foundational health insurance education electronically. EHR‐based education allows information to be reviewed asynchronously outside of clinic visits, complementing in‐person counseling. This approach also enables providers to efficiently identify and address individualized gaps in understanding using existing clinical infrastructure and workflows.

Participants with government insurance demonstrated different overall health insurance knowledge scores compared with those with private insurance, although improvements over time did not differ by insurance group. This pattern aligns with national data showing that individuals covered by Medicaid, Medicare, or lacking insurance often report lower health and insurance literacy than those with private, military, or employer‐sponsored coverage. 12 , 13 The increase in health insurance knowledge scores observed among participants exposed to the portal‐based educational intervention suggests that accessible insurance education may help improve knowledge broadly, but the intervention did not eliminate baseline differences by insurance type. Further research is needed to understand the factors contributing to persistent disparities in insurance literacy across coverage groups.

Prior research has consistently demonstrated limited health insurance knowledge among AYA across diverse populations, including individuals with chronic illness, cancer survivors, and high school and college students. 4 , 10 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 However, most studies evaluated the literacy of young adults over the age of 18 years. Researchers have hypothesized that the gap in knowledge can be attributed to the complexity of the United States healthcare system and the lack of dedicated education in the school system or in healthcare settings. 16 Further, these studies have primarily described knowledge gaps rather than tested interventions. Our study addresses this gap by demonstrating the feasibility and impact of a scalable, EHR‐based intervention implemented in a real‐world clinical setting.

This study has several limitations. It was conducted at a single institution, and most participants were non‐Hispanic and English‐speaking, which may limit generalizability. Materials were delivered only in English, potentially reducing accessibility for some participants. Engagement was defined by whether the portal message was opened and does not distinguish passive viewing from comprehension or retention. Only 35% of participants opened the message, raising concern for selection bias, as those who engaged may have differed in motivation, health literacy, or other unmeasured characteristics. Participants who opened the message were older than those who did not, suggesting differential engagement by age and highlighting the need for strategies to increase uptake among younger AYA. Finally, the long interval between assessments (307 days) may have allowed participants to acquire knowledge through external sources rather than the intervention alone. Future studies should evaluate retention, behavior change, and insurance‐related outcomes, and incorporate more robust measures of engagement.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, EHR‐based educational interventions can effectively improve health insurance knowledge among AYA with chronic diseases. In AYA‐IBD, engagement with a brief EHR‐delivered intervention was associated with improved understanding of core insurance concepts, with particularly strong effects among vulnerable subgroups. This scalable approach may be adapted to address other transition‐related knowledge gaps and reduce disparities in health literacy during the transition to adult care.
