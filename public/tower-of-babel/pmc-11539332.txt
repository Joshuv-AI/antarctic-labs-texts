Alterations in subgingival microbiome and advanced glycation end-products levels in periodontitis with and without type 1 diabetes mellitus: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jialu Chen, Hong Wang, ShouShan Bu, Xiaofan Cheng, Xiaoya Hu, Min Shen, Hai Zhuang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExisting studies predominantly focused on the relationship between periodontitis and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), with limited data on the association between periodontitis and type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM). This study aimed to examine the impact of T1DM and periodontitis on the subgingival microbiome and levels of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs).MethodsSamples were collected from four groups: T1DM, periodontitis (P), T1DM with periodontitis (DP), and periodontally and systemically healthy controls (Control). Subgingival microbiome composition and AGE levels were assessed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), respectively. Correlations between clinical indexes, microbiome composition, and AGEs were analyzed using Spearman correlation coefficient.ResultsAlpha and beta diversity analyses revealed significant differences in bacterial diversity between the DP group and other groups. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis identified specific bacteria influencing each group: Acinetobacter, Leptotrichia, Raoultibacter, and Veillonella in the Control group; Tannerella, Porphyromonas, Filifactor, and Treponema in the P group; and Lactobacillales in T1DM individuals. Prevotella and Selenomonas were notably influential in the DP group. PICRUSt2 analysis showed pathways alterations were concentrated in cell motility, translation, cell growth and death and metabolism in the DP and P groups. Spearman correlation analysis indicated a positive correlation between AGEs and periodontitis or diabetes-related parameters and AGEs were positively correlated with Haemophilus and Arachnia.ConclusionsThe findings suggested that the composition and function of the subgingival microbiome in the P group with or without T1DM were significantly different. Additionally, AGEs were involved in the development of periodontitis even in absence of hyperglycemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-05089-6.Keywords: Type 1 diabetes mellitus, Periodontitis, Advanced glycation end products, Subgingival microbiome, 16S rRNA gene sequencing

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) encompasses a group of metabolic disorders primarily classified into type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). T1DM, also known as insulin-dependent diabetes or juvenile diabetes, is characterized by the destruction of pancreatic β-cells due to idiopathic or autoimmune causes [1–4]. Although T1DM can manifest at any age, it predominantly presents in children, adolescents, and young adults [4]. T2DM is characterized by a progressive deterioration in the adequate secretion of insulin by β-cells, coupled with the manifestation of insulin resistance [5].Both types may lead to long-term complications, including retinopathy, nephropathy, peripheral neuropathy, atherosclerotic cardiovascular, peripheral arterial, and cerebrovascular diseases [6, 7]. The etiology of diabetic complications resulting from chronic hyperglycemia is multifactorial. Notably, intermittent fluctuations in blood glucose levels contribute to the formation of predominantly irreversible advanced glycation end-products (AGEs). AGEs as a heterogeneous group of compounds formed through a non-enzymatic process involving the reaction between sugars and amine groups on proteins, lipids, or nucleic acids were found to be associated with severity of periodontal degeneration [8, 9].

Periodontitis, a chronic inflammatory condition marked by the progressive destruction of periodontal tissues that support the teeth, ranks as the sixth most prevalent complication among individuals diagnosed with diabetes. Metabolites derived from subgingival microbiota may significantly influence the interplay between diabetes and periodontitis. Factors such as microbial antigens, toxins, metabolites, and enzymes associated with periodontal disease can collectively contribute to localized inflammation and damage to periodontal tissues. When these substances infiltrate deeper tissue regions, they have the potential to elicit both a host immune response and a systemic inflammatory reaction [10, 11].

Although the etiology of diabetes differs significantly between T1DM and T2DM, individuals diagnosed with T1DM or T2DM exhibit a common heightened susceptibility to periodontal disease, especially in cases of suboptimal metabolic control or the presence of diabetic complications [12]. The composition of the subgingival microbiome varies among individuals with different types of diabetes compared to healthy subjects [13]. The strongest correlation was detected between the bacteria Fusobacterium nucleatum and Capnocytophaga species, HbA1c and clinical parameters of periodontal pathology in individuals with T1DM [14]. Numerous studies have demonstrated a robust correlation between specific oral microbiome and T2DM. For instance, the presence of Porphyromonas gingivalis with type II fimbriae within periodontal pockets has been shown to affect glycemic control in individuals with T2DM [15–17]. Additionally, patients with periodontitis complicated by T2DM were demonstrated to have a significantly reduced relative abundance of Streptococcaceae/Pasteurellaceae and an elevated presence of Leptotrichiaceae in comparison to periodontitis individuals [10]. The increased severity of periodontal disease in individuals with T2DM may be attributed to the altered pathogenic capabilities of oral bacteria and/or modified characteristics of the host’s inflammatory response, potentially disrupting periodontal homeostasis [18]. Therefore, investigating the oral microbiome, including bacteria associated with periodontal disease, as a potential contributor to the pathogenesis of diabetes mellitus is warranted. However, despite extensive research on the relationship between periodontitis (P) and T2DM, the lower prevalence of T1DM (5–10%) [19, 20] poses challenges for recruiting sufficient numbers of patients for study purposes. Consequently, our comprehension of the correlation between T1DM and P remains constrained. The precise mechanisms through which various forms of diabetes influence periodontitis require further exploration.

The primary objective of this study was to compare the subgingival microbial composition between patients with T1DM and controls, both with and without periodontitis, using next-generation amplicon sequencing of the hypervariable V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene. Additionally, correlations between diabetes/periodontitis-related parameters, subgingival microbiome, and AGEs were analyzed.

Methods

Study participants

A total of 73 subjects participated in the study, divided into four groups: 18 healthy controls (Control), 19 patients with periodontitis (P), 18 patients with Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), and 18 patients with both T1DM and periodontitis (DP). This study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University and received approval from Ethical Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 2019-SR-121). The present study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and prior to sample collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All diabetic patients were diagnosed by trained clinicians, were currently using insulin, and had been monitored for glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and fasting blood glucose (FBG) in an outpatient setting for more than 2 years [21]. The diagnosis of periodontitis was established in accordance with the 2018 World Workshop classification for periodontal diseases [22]. Inclusion criteria for patients with periodontitis in the study included Stages III and IV, Grades B and C periodontitis with at least 6 sites having a pocket depth (PD) and clinical attachment loss (CAL) ≥ 5 mm, and alveolar bone resorption in at least 30% of sites [23]. Exclusion criteria were as follows: need for prophylactic antimicrobial coverage, scaling and root planning within the previous 6 months, use of antibiotics or anti-inflammatory drugs within 6 weeks of recruitment, systemic conditions (other than diabetes) that could affect the progression of periodontitis, long-term use of medication that could interfere with periodontal response, pregnancy or lactation, smoking, having fewer than 20 teeth, and inability to provide consent.

Clinical assessment and sample collection

For each participant, body mass index (BMI), age, and gender were recorded. Metabolic control was assessed based on HbA1c and FBG. Periodontal clinical parameters, including CAL, PD, bleeding on probing (BOP), and plaque index (PLI), were measured at six sites per tooth (mesiobuccal, buccal, distobuccal, distolingual, lingual, and mesiolingual) according to the Community Periodontal Index [24].

Subgingival plaque samples and gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) were collected from the most severe sites using sterile, medium-sized paper strips. The strips were gently inserted for at least 30 s and collected twice from each participant [25]. The samples were then placed in 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and immediately frozen at -80 °C until analysis.

Biochemical measurements

The GCF samples stored at -80 °C were thawed and supplemented with 300 µL of sample diluent. The samples were agitated at room temperature for 60 min, vortexed for 10 min, and then centrifuged at 4 °C at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. After centrifugation, the paper strips were discarded.

The levels of AGEs in the samples were quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), following the manufacturer’s protocols (Cusabio life science, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The concentrations of AGEs were calculated in picograms per milliliter (pg/mL) based on standard curves generated for each assay.

DNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from the samples using the cetyl trimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. DNA purity and concentration were assessed through agarose gel electrophoresis. Quantification was performed using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and the Qubit® dsDNA BR Assay Kit (Life Technologies, Eugene, OR, USA). PCR amplification targeted the V4 variable region of the 16S rRNA gene, using diluted genomic DNA as a template and specific primers incorporating barcodes. The forward primer sequence was 515 F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGCTAA-3′), and the reverse primer sequence was 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTTWTCTAAT-3′). PCR products were purified using AMPure XT beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, Danvers, MA, USA). Library construction was performed using the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Library quality was assessed with the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and sequenced on the HiSeq2500 PE250 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Biological and statistical analysis

Clinical parameters were analyzed using SPSS 25 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk normality test was employed to assess the normality assumption. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA, and differences between groups were compared using the LSD test. For non-homogeneous variances, the Tamhane post-hoc test was used. Raw sequencing data were filtered to generate high-quality clean data. FLASH (v1.2.11) was used for sequence splicing, assembling paired-end reads with overlap into sequences. Sequences with ≥ 97% similarity were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using USEARCH (v7.0.1090). Then, OTU representative sequences were compared with the RDP database using RDP classifier (v2.2) software for species annotation. Subsequently, the annotation results were filtered, leaving only the OTUs that could be used for the final analysis. Alpha and beta diversity analyses were conducted using R software (v3.2.1) and QIIME software (v1.8.0). Differentially abundant microbial taxa between groups were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) and linear discriminant analysis (LDA), with significant features defined by p < 0.05 and LDA score > 4. The nonparametric factorial Kruskal-Wallis rank‐sum test was used in LEfSe analysis to detect characteristics between different groups. The effect sizes of the identified characteristics were then analyzed with an LDA model. Predicted microbial functions were analyzed using PICRUSt2, and differences between groups were compared by Wilcox test or Kruskal-Wallis test through R software (v3.2.1). Correlations between clinical indices, AGEs, and subgingival microbiome were examined using Spearman correlation analysis. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 73 participants were enrolled in this study based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The demographic characteristics, as well as clinical characteristics of the patients, are presented in Table 1. There were no significant differences among the groups regarding age or body mass index (BMI). However, patients with DP had significantly higher levels of fasting blood glucose (FBG) and HbA1c compared to those with P (p < 0.05). Patients with periodontitis, including those in the P and DP groups, exhibited significantly higher probing depth (PD), bleeding on probing (BOP), and plaque index (PLI) values compared to patients in the T1DM and Control groups (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in PD, clinical attachment level (CAL), PLI, and BOP between patients with P and DP (p > 0.05).

Operational Taxonomic Unit (OTU) statistics

The Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencing platform was utilized to sequence the V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene in subgingival plaque samples from the Control, P, T1DM, and DP groups. The Venn diagram illustrated that 481 OTUs overlapped in all four sample sets (Fig. 1a). Specifically, the P group exhibited 586 OTUs, the T1DM group had 769 OTUs, the DP group contained 654 OTUs, and the Control group harbored 665 OTUs. Preliminary findings indicated that species richness was highest in the T1DM group, followed by the Control group, with the P group showing the lowest richness, as depicted in Fig. 1a. Additionally, within these groups, there were 8 unique OTUs in the P group, 89 unique OTUs in the T1DM group, 9 unique OTUs in the DP group, and 23 unique OTUs in the Control group. The species accumulation curve demonstrated an initial rapid ascent followed by saturation as the number of sequenced samples increased (Fig. 1b), suggesting that the sequenced samples adequately reflected subgingival microbial abundance.

Each circle in the figure represents a distinct group, wherein the numbers within the overlapping regions of the circles and their intersections denote the count of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) shared among corresponding groups, while the numbers outside these overlapping parts indicate the unique OTUs specific to each individual group. (b) Species accumulation curve. The x-axis represents the number of samples, while the y-axis denotes the count of OTUs

Microbial diversity analysis

Alpha diversity, as indicated by the Shannon and Simpson indices, revealed a significant increase in diversity in the DP group compared to the P, T1DM, and Control groups (p < 0.05) and no significant difference was observed among the P, T1DM, and Control groups (Fig. 2a, b, p > 0.05), indicating that the diversity of subgingival microbiomes was influenced by both periodontal health status and blood glucose levels. Beta diversity among the four groups is depicted in Fig. 2c. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the weighted UniFrac distance revealed closer proximity between samples in the Control and T1DM groups (R2 = 0.0174, p > 0.05), suggesting similar bacterial evolution and community structure within these groups. Conversely, the bacterial community structure between the P group and the Control group (R2 = 0.2798, p < 0.05), the DP group and the T1DM group (R2 = 0.2988, p < 0.05), the DP group and the P group (R2 = 0.1602, p < 0.05) and the DP group and the Control group (R2 = 0.2823, p < 0.05) were statistically significant.

Diversity analysis plot. (a) Shannon index. (b) Simpson index. (c) The samples belonging to the same group are depicted using identical colors, with each point representing an individual sample. The spatial separation between these points reflects the extent of dissimilarity. * p < 0.05

The microbial composition at different taxonomic levels is illustrated in Fig. 3. At the phylum level (Fig. 3a), the dominant bacteria (relative abundant > 1%) in each group mainly included Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Fusobacteriota, Pseudomonadota, Spirochaetota and Campylobacterota. At the genus level (Fig. 3b), 17 dominant bacteria (Leptotrichia, Acinetobacter and Capnocytophaga, etc.) in the Control group, 15 dominant bacteria (Fusobacterium, Treponema and Prevotella, etc.) in the P group, 20 dominant bacteria (Segatella, Acinetobacter and Filifactor, etc.) in the T1DM group and 19 dominant bacteria (Selenomonas, Porphyromonas and Alloprevotella, etc.) in the DP group were found.

Microbial analysis in different disease states

To further identify the differential taxa among these groups, LEfSe analysis was conducted. Taxonomic groups with varying abundance, meeting the criteria of an LDA score > 4 and a significance threshold of p < 0.05, are depicted in Fig. 4a. The LDA bar chart demonstrated that at the level of genus, Acinetobacter, Leptotrichia, Raoultibacter and Veillonella in the Control group, Tannerella, Porphyromonas, Filifactor and Treponema in the P group, Prevotella and Selenomonas in the DP group were found to have significantly high impact on the difference among these four groups. At the level of order, Lactobacillales in T1DM was found to have significantly high impact. We have also generated cladograms using the LEfSe analysis to visually depict the phylogenetic distribution of these samples, spanning from class to genus level (Fig. 4b). Functional analysis of subgingival microbiome

The aforementioned analysis aims to elucidate the composition, differences and similarities of subgingival plaque microbial species under various disease conditions. However, it does not mention the functions of these bacterial species. In this analysis, the potential biological functions of the microbiota in the four groups were illustrated using PICRUSt2. This analysis provided possible third-level KEGG pathways and abundance values in the sequencing samples (excluding unknown functions). At level 1 (see Additional file 1: Fig. S1), cellular processes, human diseases, genetic information processing, and organismal systems were higher in the P group than in the Control group (p < 0.05), while there were no significant differences between the Control group and the T1DM group (p > 0.05). The DP group exhibited significantly higher functions of cellular processes and organismal systems than the Control group and the T1DM group (p < 0.05). At level 2 (see Additional file 1: Fig. S2) and level 3 (Fig. 5), the P group showed significantly enriched glycan biosynthesis and metabolism (e.g., GPI-anchor biosynthesis, N-Glycan biosynthesis, peptidoglycan biosynthesis), cell motility (e.g., flagellar assembly, bacterial chemotaxis), cell growth and death (e.g., cell cycle-caulobacter), metabolism of cofactors and vitamins (e.g., thiamine metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, vitamin B6 metabolism), environmental adaptation, transcription (e.g., RNA polymerase), translation (e.g., aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, Ribosome), immune system, replication and repair (e.g., homologous recombination), nucleotide metabolism, infectious diseases: bacterial (e.g., bacterial invasion of epithelial cells), methane metabolism, protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides (e.g., terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, zeatin biosynthesis), and other functions compared with healthy subjects (p < 0.05). The T1DM group showed significantly enriched polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon degradation and glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism compared with healthy subjects (p < 0.05). The DP group exhibited significantly enriched cell motility (e.g., flagellar assembly, bacterial chemotaxis), amino acid metabolism (e.g., lysine biosynthesis, alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism), metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides (e.g., biosynthesis of ansamycins), immune system, and other functions compared with healthy subjects (p < 0.05).

Correlations between clinical indexes and subgingival microbiome or AGEs

To confirm the role of AGEs in T1DM or P individuals, the levels of AGEs in GCF from T1DM patients and healthy controls with or without periodontitis were measured. The Spearman analysis was employed to examine the association between periodontitis/diabetes-related parameters and the levels of AGEs (Table 2). The findings indicated that AGEs had positive correlations with CAL, PD, PLI and BOP and diabetes-related parameters such as HbAc1 and FBG (p < 0.05). Significant higher levels of AGEs were found in the disease groups compared to the Control group (Fig. 6a, p < 0.05). Furthermore, the levels of AGEs in the DP group were significantly higher compared to the T1DM or P group (p < 0.05). Periodontitis-related parameters were positively correlated with Porphyromonas, Streptococcus, Actinomyces and others. HbA1c had a positive correlation with Leptotrichiaceae and AGEs were positively correlated with Haemophilus and Arachnia (Fig. 6b, r ≥ 0.4, p < 0.05).

Discussion

Periodontitis is caused by a synergistic interplay between bacterial infection and an imbalance in the periodontal microenvironment, which is intricately associated with dysbiosis within the microbial community residing within the periodontal pocket [26]. Emerging evidences suggest a bidirectional association between diabetes mellitus and periodontal diseases, in which diabetes increases the susceptibility to periodontitis while periodontal inflammation adversely affects glycemic control [27, 28]. Despite extensive research on the association between periodontitis (P) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), investigations into the association between P and type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) have been comparatively limited. The lack of insulin synthesis in type 1 diabetes distinguishes significantly from type 2 diabetes [4]. Evidences suggested that an imbalanced microbiome played a crucial role in the development of both T1DM and T2DM and most of them focused on the impact of gut microbial communities [29]. It was reported that the gut microbiome of individuals with T1DM was correlated with an elevated Bacteroides: Firmicutes ratio [30]. The oral cavity served as a reservoir for gut microbial strains has received more and more attention, and oral-fecal transmission played a pivotal role in shaping the gastrointestinal microbiome in both health and disease [31]. This study sought to analyze the subgingival microbiota in individuals using 16S rRNA sequencing to elucidate how the presence or absence of T1DM influences the subgingival microbiota of periodontitis patients and reveal the composition and related functions of the subgingival microbiome associated with the risk/progression of the disease.

Similar to previous studies on the association between T2DM and P [32–34], our results also indicated comparable subgingival bacterial diversity between T1DM patients and healthy individuals. This outcome may be attributed to the relatively small sample size of the T1DM group, potentially introducing bias. The impact of T1DM on subgingival microbial composition might be significant only when coexisting with periodontitis, necessitating further studies with larger T1DM cohorts to validate this assertion. Consequently, we inferred that periodontitis-related parameters predominantly dictated the composition of subgingival microbes, with blood sugar status exerting a notable influence on subgingival microbial composition in patients with periodontitis.

The LEfSe analysis, conducted at the genus level, identified that Acinetobacter, Leptotrichia, Raoultibacter, and Veillonella were markedly prominent in the Control group, while Tannerella, Porphyromonas, Filifactor, and Treponema exhibited heightened abundance in the P group. Prevotella and Selenomonas were notably enriched in the DP group, indicating their potential role in taxonomic distinctions. The presence of Porphyromonas, Treponema, and Tannerella, collectively known as the red complex bacteria, was widely acknowledged to strongly correlate with periodontitis [35]. Additionally, Filifactor, a Gram-positive anaerobic rod belonging to the Firmicutes phylum, has emerged as a potential etiological agent in oral infections, particularly periodontitis, demonstrating positive correlations with relevant clinical parameters [36, 37]. At the order level, Lactobacillales emerged as a significant factor, particularly in association with diabetes, with its abundance positively correlated with HbA1c levels [38]. Extant evidences have suggested the beneficial role of Lactobacillales in oral health maintenance, with certain species capable of inhibiting periodontal pathogen growth and reducing gingival inflammation [39–41]. Moreover, a positive correlation between Lactobacillales prevalence and elevated T-helper cell levels underscored its association with the host’s adaptive immune response [42]. Our findings were consistent with this pattern, as lower abundance of Lactobacillales in the DP group compared to the T1DM group was observed, indicative of its potential role in modulating oral microbial communities in diabetic contexts. Selenomonas exhibited significantly higher relative abundance in the DP group, suggesting its association with blood sugar status. Despite limited literature discussions, Selenomonas species, particularly Selenomonas sputigena, has been implicated in biofilm formation and generalized aggressive periodontitis through coaggregation with diverse species [43]. Prevotella, predominant gram-negative obligate anaerobes, was notably abundant in the P group, with diabetes exacerbating the expression of Prevotella intermedia in saliva [44]. In the present study, Prevotella abundance was positively correlated with fasting blood sugar (FBG) levels, possibly attributed to lipopolysaccharide-induced local immune responses and ensuing low-grade systemic inflammation, predisposing individuals to insulin resistance and diabetes progression [45]. This study underscored the susceptibility of T1DM patients to oral flora dysbiosis, heightening the risk and severity of periodontitis.

KEGG analysis uncovered potential metabolic dysregulation induced by inflammatory mediators released during periodontitis progression, particularly impacting individuals with T1DM. Elevated levels of cell motility such as bacterial chemotaxis and flagellar assembly observed in the P and DP groups suggested an enhanced capacity for pathobiont invasion, thereby implicating their role in the pathogenesis of the disease. Remarkably, DP group exhibited higher amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism and membrane transport than in the P group. Although the precise mechanism underlying the relationship between amino acids and diabetes and periodontitis remained unclear, previous studies have demonstrated an association between amino acid metabolism (such as phenylalanine, tryptophan, and tyrosine) and the risk of developing both diabetes and periodontitis [46, 47]. The differences in amino acid expression may be due to the induction of innate immune system overactivation through the oral-gut axis by endotoxin [48]. Certain tryptophan metabolites played crucial roles in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and were implicated in the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease, as well as influencing the development of T2DM [49]. This suggested that the subgingival microbiome may exert a systemic impact on overall health through its interactions with the intestine.

Studies have indicated that inflamed periodontal tissues and periodontal pathogens contribute to elevated levels of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) in both normoglycemic and hyperglycemic individuals [50–53]. In our investigation, significantly higher levels of AGEs in the disease groups compared to the Control group were observed, with the DP group exhibiting the highest expression. Spearman analysis further revealed positive correlations between AGEs and clinical parameters associated with periodontitis and diabetes. Evidence showed that the interaction between periodontal pathogens and host receptors initiated an immune response, leading to the release of various reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protease enzymes. ROS entered the systemic circulation and resulted in the formation of AGEs through mitochondrial induction [54]. Meanwhile, the elevation of AGE levels led to heightened systemic oxidative stress, resulting in an escalation of glycemic load and the generation of AGEs in a continuous cycle [55]. Individuals diagnosed with diabetes exhibited an increased prevalence of AGEs, which significantly heightened the risk of both microvascular and macrovascular complications due to the accumulation of AGEs [9]. It was crucial to acknowledge the existence of a detrimental cycle that intertwines periodontitis, diabetes, and AGEs. The findings may provide a non-invasive approach to detect the levels of AGEs expression in the GCF and by targeted intervention to diminish periodontal pathogens such as Tannerella forsythia and Porphyromonas gingivalis and curtail the production of AGEs can improve blood glucose levels and mitigate the occurrence of diabetes-related complications.

Our study identified several genera, including Porphyromonas, Streptococcus, Actinomyces, and others, exhibiting positive correlations with periodontitis-related clinical indexes such as BOP, PD, CAL, and PLI. Furthermore, HbA1c showed a positive correlation with Leptotrichiaceae, while AGEs exhibited positive correlations with Haemophilus and Arachnia. It was reported that some nonbutyrate bacteria, such as Haemophilus parainfluenzae, were associated with T2DM. However, the association was not very strong. Therefore, larger samples with more statistical power may be required to detect true associations [56]. This study was the first to identify bacteria genera associated with AGEs, which might be a potential marker of AGEs. However, it is essential to acknowledge the limitations of our study, particularly its focus on examining differences in the subgingival microbiome among the four groups without exploring the causal relationship between T1DM and microbial composition alterations. Thus, further longitudinal studies are warranted to validate and substantiate these findings.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings indicated that the subgingival microbiome compositions of the DP group and other three groups were distinct, with periodontitis emerging as a primary factor influencing microbial composition. Furthermore, LEfSe analysis further revealed dominant microbial taxa in each group: Acinetobacter, Leptotrichia, Raoultibacter, and Veillonella in the Control group; Tannerella, Porphyromonas, Filifactor, and Treponema in the P group; Lactobacillales in the T1DM group and Prevotella and Selenomonas in the DP group. Functions concentrated in cell motility, translation, cell growth and death and metabolism were detected to be enriched both in the DP and P groups compared with the Control group. AGEs had positive correlations with periodontitis/diabetes-related parameters and were involved in the development of periodontitis even in absence of hyperglycemia. Furthermore, several genera, including Porphyromonas, Streptococcus, Actinomyces, and others exhibited positive correlations with periodontitis-related clinical indexes while AGEs exhibited positive correlations with Haemophilus and Arachnia. These findings may inform the development of novel therapeutic targets for preventing, controlling, or treating periodontitis, T1DM, and their coexistence. Future studies should be advisable to increase the sample size and perform metagenome sequencing in order to yield more comprehensive results.
