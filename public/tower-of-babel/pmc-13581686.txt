Towards precision medicine: Application of the structural causal model in psychiatry
Authors: Wei Chen, Liya Sun, Fanyuan Zhang, Jijun Wang, Chunbo Li, Shengying Qin, Chunling Wan, Fangyu Chen, Chen Zhang, Ruichu Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract LEARNING POINTS The structural causal model (SCM) is a next‐generation artificial intelligence method. It can transparently reveal the causal graph of a system, make predictions based on causal mechanisms, enable individualised inference and facilitate virtual intervention experiments.Each SCM method has its own advantages and limitations that should be considered in practice. As the field develops rapidly, further breakthroughs in both SCM methods and precision medicine are anticipated.Currently, there are only a few applications of SCM in psychiatry research. We encourage the use of it as a tool for advancing precision medicine in psychiatry.Keywords: causal inference, multiomics, precision medicine, psychiatry, structural causal model

Recent advances in personal omics studies have led to the rapid generation of molecular and imaging data at individual level. 1 Precision medicine, which aims to predict disease risk and guide treatment based on an individual's disease‐driving characteristics, has emerged as a promising direction for future development in psychiatry. It requires an integration of causal understanding, predictive modelling, treatment stratification and ultimately, personalisation. However, due to the complicated aetiology of psychiatric diseases, though a number of differentiated or disease‐associated molecular signatures and traits have been identified, only a few have been established as causal factors that can be therapeutically targeted, let alone translated into clinical applications.

This challenge also affects current diagnostic and predictive models. Machine‐learning models developed using large numbers of noncausal parameters may overfit training datasets, potentially resulting in unstable performance in test samples and reduced generalisability. Moreover, although traditional randomised controlled trials (RCTs) remain the gold standard for establishing causal relationships, they primarily provide evidence at the population level rather than for individual patients. They also have practical limitations, including high cost and ethical constraints. For instance, it would be unethical to withhold standard care from patients simply to evaluate the effect of an investigational treatment. On the other hand, psychiatric disorders are characterised by substantial heterogeneity, even among those with the same diagnosis. Although subtype analysis is commonly used to capture this heterogeneity, only a limited number of subgroups can usually be identified, whereas the true spectrum of patient variation is far more continuous and individual‐specific.

The structural causal model (SCM), a causal inference framework within artificial intelligence, provides a way to represent causal relationships within complex systems, generates predictions based on underlying mechanisms, supports individualised inference and enables virtual intervention experiments. SCMs may therefore offer a promising approach to overcome current limitations and advance precision medicine in psychiatry, although their clinical utility requires further empirical validation.

METHODS

The SCM was first proposed by Judea Pearl in the mid‐1990s. 2 It unifies structural equation models (SEMs), graphical models and potential outcomes models (POMs), enabling causal reasoning across observational, interventional and counterfactual settings. Unlike traditional correlation‐based methods, SCMs explicitly represent and reason about causal relationships between variables by encoding them in a causal graph, that is, a directed acyclic graph (DAG), which is generated through data‐driven mechanisms. Each node in a DAG corresponds to a variable, whereas each directed edge denotes causal influence. This graphical representation makes it easier to intuitively grasp the causal pathways of a system and understand how changes in one part of the system may propagate through and affect other parts. This capacity makes them particularly valuable for tasks that require moving beyond mere classification towards understanding individualised mechanisms that drive observed data, and even simulating interventions or answering ‘what‐if’ questions through counterfactual computation. Such capabilities can be crucial for decision‐making across various fields.

Typically, a stage termed ‘causal discovery’ is first performed on observational data from a given cohort (preferably more than 200 samples). A group of candidate variables is first screened using experts' knowledge, from which a DAG structure is then constructed 3 through (1) constraint‐based algorithms, (2) score‐based algorithms and (3) functional causal model‐based algorithms. In particular, constraint‐based algorithms identify causal dependencies between variables by exploiting the constraints derived from conditional independence tests (such as the Peter–Clark [PC] algorithm 4 ). Score‐based algorithms use a scoring function to identify the causal model that best fits the data. Typical scoring criteria include the Bayesian Information Criterion, the Minimum Description Length and the Akaike Information Criterion. These criteria balance model fit and complexity when selecting the most appropriate SCM structure. Functional causal model‐based methods, however, identify the causal direction between variables by seeking asymmetries in their causal relationships, under assumptions about the data generation mechanism. This is followed by the ‘causal inference’ stage. The weights of the causal edges (i.e., causal effects) can be estimated using Bayesian networks for categorical variables or SEMs for continuous variables. Methods based on functional causal models enable simultaneous causal discovery and causal inference, as they fundamentally stem from modelling the inherent data generation mechanism. Deep learning or reinforcement learning methods can also be incorporated into these algorithms, enhancing the performance of resulting SCM models and enabling prediction or diagnosis for new samples, as well as causal inference (e.g., do‐calculus).

How to choose and conduct an SCM method

In psychiatric precision medicine, data vary in type (discrete [binary or categorical], continuous or mixed), in whether the independently‐and‐identically‐distributed (i.i.d.) assumption holds and in whether they contain temporal information (Supplementary Table 1). For SCM estimation, (conditional) independence tests are used, which assess whether two variables are dependent (conditioning on other variables): Chi‐square or G‐square 5 for discrete data; partial correlation for continuous data when their relationships are linear and the noise is Gaussian; kernel methods (Hilbert–Schmidt Independence Criterion, 6 Kernel Conditional Independence 7 ) for mixed data. Under the i.i.d. assumption, these tests are embedded into the PC algorithm (no latent variables) or the Fast Causal Inference (FCI) algorithm (with latent variables) for high‐dimensional causal discovery.

When constructing an SCM with continuous data, it is also typical to make specific assumptions about the functional relationship between variables and their data distributions. These assumptions are critical to the identifiability of SCMs. Such relationships may be linear or nonlinear. The Linear Non‐Gaussian Acyclic Model (LiNGAM) 8 is a representative example of an SCM. If the relationship between variables is linear and the data follow nonGaussian distributions, LiNGAM can be estimated. 9 LiNGAM has also been extended to address special cases such as latent variables, 10 , 11 temporal data 12 and measurement error, 13 among others. For the nonlinear relationships, the additive noise model 14 and the postnonlinear model 15 are representative models capable of distinguishing cause from effect. To adapt these models to the high‐dimensional setting, methods based on generative models or incorporating skeleton search strategies have been proposed.

For data that incorporate temporal information (longitudinal/time series data), several survey papers have summarised relevant methods. 16 , 17 Traditional causal discovery often assumes causal sufficiency (i.e., no hidden confounders). Constraint‐based methods, such as PC algorithm with Momentary Conditional Independence (PCMCI) 18 and PCMCI+, 19 can handle continuous or discrete data by using conditional independence tests to identify both instantaneous and time‐lagged causal relationships. PCMCI+ leverages autocorrelation to improve performance, but these methods are computationally intensive and difficult to scale to high‐dimensional data. DYNOTEARS 20 estimates both instantaneous and time‐lagged relationships by minimising a penalised loss, ensuring that the resulting causal graph is a DAG. Vector‑Autoregressive Linear Non‑Gaussian Acyclic Model 12 extends LiNGAM to time series by incorporating vector autoregressive principles to handle nonGaussian noise, enabling the identification of linear causal structures. For nonlinear problems, the additive nonlinear time series causal model 21 combines additive regression with graphical algorithms such as FCI to handle instantaneous causation, lagged causation and latent variables. Time Series Models with Independent Noise 22 is based on independent noise and nonlinear SCMs, identifying lagged and instantaneous causes by testing the independence of regression residuals. When latent variables are present, latent PCMCI 23 explicitly accounts for latent parents in the conditioning sets, improving the robustness of detecting direct and indirect causal relationships in autocorrelated time series.

Several implementations of the aforementioned SCM methods are available in R or Python. They are summarised, and detailed usage is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Two main approaches address missing data in causal discovery. Imputation methods (e.g., multiple imputation) fill in missing values before applying standard causal discovery: they are simple but rely on correctly specified imputation models. 24 , 25 Missing‐mechanism modelling explicitly characterises missingness by introducing indicators and proxies: 26 some approaches further correct spurious relations using weighting or adjusted independence tests 27 or treat missing data as additive noise in causal models. 28 For sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of causal conclusions to violations of SCM assumptions, experiments can be performed on simulated data. Through simulation, various scenarios and assumptions can be modelled on real‐world settings to observe model performance under these different assumptions, thereby assessing the robustness of causal conclusions. The simulated scenarios and assumptions can include varying sample sizes, introducing measurement errors and potential hidden confounders and adjusting the strength of causal relationships, to observe whether these changes affect the causal conclusions. Further model validation using longitudinal real‐world datasets or interventional experiments is also necessary.

APPLICATIONS

To better illustrate the entire process from data handling to SCM analysis, here we present an example based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) data, 29 demonstrating how to identify factors influencing depression levels and further estimate the causal effects at the individual level. Assuming a linear causal relationship, LiNGAM is used to model the data and recover the causal graph. The data are split into a training set and a test set, preserving the original class distribution. DISCUSSION

Comparison with relevant methods

To position SCM clearly and demonstrate how it complements or challenges existing approaches, a concise comparison of six methods applicable to uncovering specific mechanisms in psychiatry is presented in Supplementary Table 4. The methods are organised in a hierarchical progression based on their mathematical foundations—moving from methods focused on association detection towards those capable of causal inference. In brief, network analysis 30 maps symptom networks via correlations but cannot yield causal conclusions. Genome‐wide association analysis identifies statistically associated genetic variants (single‐nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs]) but requires biological validation to confirm causal genes linked to the identified SNPs. Bayesian networks build on conditional probabilistic dependencies but may produce spurious associations. The POM estimates intervention effects by comparing factual and counterfactual outcomes but does not support individual‐level estimates. RCTs remain the gold standard for causality despite the practical limitations discussed earlier. SCM is the most robust causal framework: it integrates prior knowledge with data, uncovers mechanisms in complex systems and supports individual‐level counterfactual reasoning, offering considerable potential for precision psychiatry.

Challenges of SCM in applications for psychiatry and current solutions

Challenge of hidden confounding factors

In psychiatric research, many key confounding factors are difficult to fully measure or observe. For example, the severity of early traumatic experiences, specific manifestations of genetic susceptibility and other factors that may significantly affect treatment outcomes and disease progression are often impossible to completely capture through standardised scales. In SCM research, some methods relax the no unmeasured confounders assumption, providing new possibilities for handling hidden confounding factors. For instance, the latent variables LiNGAM (LvLiNGAM) 11 method extends the classical LiNGAM by employing over‐complete independent component analysis (ICA) techniques to discover relationships between variable noise and convert them into a causal relationship matrix between observed and latent variables. However, LvLiNGAM uses optimisation approaches to search for target solutions, which may fall into local optima. Building on LvLiNGAM, Cai et al. 31 used higher‐order cumulants to capture information about hidden confounding factors, proposing analytical solutions for causal coefficients between hidden confounders and observed variables, and further developing causal discovery methods that include latent variables. When hidden confounders cannot be determined in advance, one can apply the Generalised Independence Noise 32 condition or multiple latent confounders LiNGAM 10 to identify the presence of hidden confounders and further learn the true causal relationships between variables. All these methods are suitable for analysing complex psychiatric data.

Challenge of high‐dimensional data

Psychiatric research frequently encounters high‐dimensional data. Researchers need to consider numerous variables or features (such as multiple symptom dimensions, biomarkers and cognitive function indicators); however, the available sample sizes are typically limited. When the number of observed variables approaches or exceeds the sample size, traditional statistical inference methods face severe challenges, and SCM methods that do not consider high‐dimensional scenarios also suffer significant decreases in accuracy. Current SCM research has proposed various strategies to address these challenges. Classical strategies involve using principal component analysis, generative models and other methods to reduce data dimensionality, or employing regularisation methods to first select features before applying SCM. From the perspective of algorithm optimisation, some methods employ a divide‐and‐conquer approach to break down complex causal structures into smaller, manageable subproblems 33 or partition the graph structure using edge‐cutting techniques to enhance the identification of causal relationships while reducing computational complexity. 34 When analysing causal relationships between brain regions of interest in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data, Cai et al. 35 first used canonical correlation analysis to identify brain regions, then applied SCM to analyse causal relationships between different brain regions, avoiding the dimensionality issues caused by directly processing thousands of voxels. This method can be used for subsequent causal relationship analysis between brain regions and cognitive states.

Challenge of interpretability of results

As noted earlier, SCMs provide an inherent advantage in interpretability by explicitly modelling causal relationships rather than black‐box associations, which facilitates adoption by clinicians. In other words, SCMs explicitly reason about causal relationships between variables by encoding them in a DAG to grasp the causal network of a system and to understand how changes in one variable may propagate through and affect other variables. In principle, therefore, a personalised SCM graph could be generated for each patient. By setting the occurrence of a given psychiatric disorder as the child node, its upstream nodes (e.g., parent nodes) can then be traced to identify candidate biological targets.

In practice, SCM provides data‐based statistical causal evidence, but the biological mechanisms of psychiatric disorders are often complex and poorly understood. This gap between ‘statistical causality’ and ‘biological causality’ might complicate the interpretation of results. One possible solution is to first validate SCM methods through simulation experiments, then apply them to real data to discover unknown mechanisms and finally verify whether the causal relationships learnt by SCM are accurate through biological intervention experiments. Another solution is to collaborate with domain experts for model refinement. The neurobiological evidence can be formalised in advance as constraints and incorporated into the SCM, so that the solution process is guided by these constraints. This reduces the complexity of recovering the SCM while allowing for precise identification of the causal relationships between biological variables.

Future development of SCM methods for psychiatry research

Causal representation learning for extraction of important factors

Psychiatric data are multimodal, high‐dimensional and contain substantial noise. Causal representation learning (CRL) 36 extracts low‐dimensional latent causal variables with DAG‐based dependencies, enabling interventions and counterfactuals. Recent work embeds domain knowledge into encoders. 37 , 38 Classical CRL methods impose SCM constraints and alternative approaches use ICA 39 with additional information for identifiability. 40 , 41 Recent ICA‐based models employ cumulants to extract causal representations from continuous 42 or discrete data, and a closed‐form solution for causal influence coefficients exists. 31 Thus, CRL can compress psychiatric data into sparse latent variables for structural causal modelling.

Individual structural causal models for precision medicine

Precision medicine demands patient‐specific treatment plans using multimodal data. 43 Individual‐level SCMs encode a person‐specific causal graph (nodes: latent disease drivers; edges: intervention effects). Directly applying SCMs to individual data is limited by small sample sizes and latent variables. Existing methods leverage multisource information: Group Iterative Multiple Model Estimation 44 reveals individual/group time‐lagged causal relationships (no theoretical guarantees); Specific and Shared Causal Model 45 learns shared/specific structures and clusters mechanisms; Additive Noise Model Mixture Model 46 models nonlinear relations with discrete parameter distributions; Individual Linear Acyclic Model 47 uses a shared environmental latent variable to jointly infer individual causal graphs, outperforming separate modelling in fMRI under small samples. Despite these advances, challenges such as data sparsity, latent variables and selection bias remain.

Last but not least, although SCM offers promising data‐driven solutions, an algorithmically optimised clinical recommendation alone is not sufficient to improve clinical practice in psychiatry. Barriers related to dissemination and implementation involve clinical and practical factors, technical aspects, ethical considerations and fundamental contextual issues. 48 Ethical considerations, for example, raise critical concerns, including protecting personal data privacy, eliminating algorithmic bias and social inequities and maintaining a trustworthy, transparent workflow. As for SCM itself, potential risks such as biased decision‐making warrant attention, and SCM‐based models should be further developed to address problems such as measurement error and assumption violation, and should be deployed with fairness‐aware constraints, uncertainty quantification and human‐in‐the‐loop validation.
