Bisphosphonate-related ocular adverse events: a pharmacovigilance study based on the FAERS database
Authors: Yue Cui, Zhihui Song, Xinglong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This retrospective study aimed to identify and characterize signals of ocular adverse events (AEs) related to bisphosphonates (BPs) using FDA adverse event reporting system (FAERS) data (Q1 2004 to Q3 2024) to inform future safety investigations. Disproportionality analysis was conducted utilizing the reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN) methods to identify BP-related ocular AE signals. In addition, a modified ROR method was utilized to examine differences across gender and age. Among 6,965 ocular AE reports for five BPs (alendronate, zoledronate, pamidronate, risedronate, and ibandronate), 136 positive signals were identified, predominantly unlisted in drug labels. Notable risks included ocular inflammatory AEs (especially zoledronate) and novel risk signals such as cataract, glaucoma, and macular degeneration. Standardized MedDRA queries (SMQ) linked BPs to 11 eye disorders, with scleral disorders common to all five BPs and pamidronate involving the broadest SMQ categories. Ocular AEs for alendronate, zoledronate, and pamidronate exhibited age-related differences, while those for alendronate and zoledronate showed gender differences. This study identifies high-risk and novel ocular AEs related to BPs. These findings warrant further validation in future studies.

Introduction

Bisphosphonates (BPs) are widely utilized as primary therapeutic agents for osteoporosis, as well as for managing hypercalcemia of malignancy and bone diseases associated with cancer (1). BPs are analogs of pyrophosphate, and their diverse mechanisms of action are dictated by their molecular structure. The first-generation BPs, such as etidronate, clodronate, and tiludronate, do not contain nitrogen. These compounds accumulate within the bone matrix and are phagocytosed by osteoclasts, subsequently inducing osteoclast apoptosis (2). In contrast, second and third-generation BPs, including pamidronate, alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronate, contain nitrogen. These agents inhibit farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase within the mevalonate pathway, resulting in the accumulation of isopentenyl diphosphate, which is cytotoxic to osteoclasts (3). In oncological applications, BPs exert their effects by modulating the tumor microenvironment or by directly targeting cancer cells (2).

In addition to the therapeutic benefits associated with BPs, an increasing body of the literature highlights the potential ocular adverse events (AEs) linked to their use (4). Although ocular AEs are less prevalent than other types of AEs related to BPs, the serious risks they pose, such as eye pain and potential blindness, necessitate heightened vigilance. Commonly reported ocular AEs related to BPs include conjunctivitis, uveitis, and scleritis (5).

The majority of existing studies on ocular AEs related to BPs consist of case reports, and there is a paucity of research comparing the safety profiles of different BPs. Furthermore, no studies have systematically compared the ocular AEs across multiple BPs. This paper utilizes the FDA adverse event reporting system (FAERS) database and employs disproportionality analysis to identify signals related to eye-related AEs linked to BPs. We analyze the various types of ocular AEs and compare the safety of five BPs: alendronate, zoledronate, pamidronate, risedronate, and ibandronate, thereby providing valuable insights for the pharmaceutical care of patients undergoing treatment with BPs.

Methods

Data source

FAERS is recognized as one of the databases that can be used for post-marketing safety surveillance of drugs. This database encompasses AEs reported to the FDA for a wide array of drug and biologic products. AEs are encoded with preferred terms (PTs) in the medical dictionary for regulatory activities (MedDRA) version 27.1. PTs can also be classified into system organ classes (SOCs) or aggregated with standardized MedDRA queries (SMQs). The dataset spanning from Q1 2004 to Q3 2024 was downloaded at https://fis.fda.gov/extensions/FPD-QDE-FAERS/FPD-QDE-FAERS.html.

Data processing procedure

The data were analyzed using SAS software version 9.4. Deduplication was conducted in accordance with the method recommended by the FDA. Specifically, for reports sharing an identical CASEID (a unique case identifier), we retained the single report with the most recent FDA_DT (the date the FDA received the report) and the highest PRIMARYID (a unique identifier for each report submission). Furthermore, reports were also considered duplicates if they shared identical key characteristics, including gender, age, event onset date, suspected drug, indication, and AEs. AEs were filtered based on the generic and brand names of five BPs (alendronate, zoledronate, pamidronate, risedronate, and ibandronate), and we focused on BPs with a ROLE_COD (role code) of primary suspect (PS).

Statistical analysis

Disproportionality analysis was used to identify potential safety signals, and the algorithm was shown in Supplementary Table S1 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article). Reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN) methods were applied to signal detection, with formulas and thresholds in Supplementary Table S2. A signal was generated when all three method thresholds were met, as defined by the following criteria: the number of AE reports was at least three, the lower limit of the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the ROR exceeded 1, the PRR was greater than or equal to 2 with a chi-square value of at least 4, and the lower limit of the 95% CI for the information component (IC), denoted as IC025, was greater than 0. The strength of these detected signals was further stratified based on the IC025 value as follows: strong (IC025 > 3), moderate (IC025 > 1.5 and ≤3), and weak (IC025 > 0 and ≤1.5). We examined and analyzed the AEs listed under the SOC of eye disorders at the PT level. We classified the AEs into SMQs and focused on eleven eye-related SMQ categories to investigate the disease manifestations of BP-related ocular AEs.

In order to mine gender and age differences, a modified ROR method with a 2 × 2 contingency table was used (6). If ROR >1 and P < 0.05 (chi-square test), females or those under 65 years of age were likelier to report a specific AE than males or those aged 65 and older. Conversely, if ROR <1 and P < 0.05, males or those aged 65 and older were likelier to report a specific AE than females or those under 65. The statistical analysis of the data was processed using SAS 9.4, GraphPad Prism 9.5, and Microsoft Excel software.

Results

Descriptive analysis of cases with bisphosphonate-related ocular AEs

A total of 54,336,884 AE reports were retrieved from the FAERS database, spanning from Q1 2004 to Q3 2024, following the removal of duplicates. Among these, 6,965 cases were identified as ocular AEs related to BPs, including alendronate (2,741 cases), zoledronate (2,848 cases), pamidronate (398 cases), risedronate (389 cases), and ibandronate (589 cases) (Table 1). The majority of these cases involved female patients. Except for those not specified, the age distribution of cases was predominantly concentrated among people over 45 years. Reports of alendronate were submitted most by physicians, whereas the remaining four BPs were reported most by consumers. The region of occurrence was mainly in North America and unspecified regions. Regarding the outcomes of AEs, hospitalization-initial or prolonged was the main outcome for alendronate, zoledronate, risedronate, and ibandronate, followed by disability. As for pamidronate, the primary outcome was hospitalization – initial or prolonged, followed by death. Zoledronate exhibited the shortest median onset time (median: 1 day), whereas pamidronate had the longest (median: 468 days).

PT analysis

There were 136 signals in total detected at the PT level, and all of them were compared with instructions. Both zoledronate and pamidronate had 38 positive signals, more than alendronate (29), risedronate (23), and ibandronate (8). All detected signals were presented in Supplemental Table S3, with AEs not reported in the product labels annotated with an asterisk (*). The top 20 PT signals of five BPs ranked by frequency were shown in Table 2. If the number of signals was less than 20, all PT signals were displayed. For alendronate and pamidronate, cataract ranked first, with 931 cases and 131 cases, respectively. There were only eight PT signals of ibandronate, and macular degeneration (26 cases), not mentioned in the instructions, was the most. It was worth noting that cataract and glaucoma ranked high in alendronate and pamidronate, and both the two PTs were not mentioned in the instructions. In zoledronate and risedronate, eye pain was reported most frequently and was also not in the instructions.

To mine for ocular AE signals that were more relevant to BPs, PTs with moderate and strong signals (IC025 > 1.5) were selected to explore (Fig. 1). For alendronate, presbyopia (IC025:2.64), and cataract nuclear (IC025:2.63) were noteworthy. Zoledronate had 15 moderate and strong PT signals, and parophthalmia (IC025:4.84), idiopathic orbital inflammation (IC025:4.23), iridocyclitis (IC025:3.46), and scleritis (IC025:3.12) had strong signals. Pamidronate showed more correlation with presbyopia (IC025:3.53), cataract nuclear (IC025:2.81), and astigmatism (IC025:2.66). Compared with other BPs, pamidronate had a weak signal with scleritis (IC025:0.46). In addition, risedronate was clearly linked to scleritis (IC025:2.55), parophthalmia (IC025:2.40), conjunctival edema (IC025:2.29), and episcleritis (IC025:1.76). Remarkably, presbyopia and cataract nuclear were closely related to alendronate, zoledronate, and pamidronate.

According to the prescribing information, ocular AEs related to BPs are relatively uncommon. However, our study detected a substantial number of signals for such events. Furthermore, a large proportion of these identified ocular AE signals were novel. Specifically, the proportions of novel ocular signals varied across different BPs: 96.55% for alendronate, 68.42% for zoledronate, 81.58% for pamidronate, 78.26% for risedronate, and 62.50% for ibandronate.

Notably, we identified novel signals that ranked among the top ten in both ROR ranking and signal frequency. For alendronate, these signals included presbyopia and nuclear cataract; for zoledronate, the signal was iridocyclitis; for pamidronate, the signals were presbyopia, astigmatism, and nuclear cataract; for risedronate, the signals were scleritis and parophthalmia; and for ibandronate, the signals included macular degeneration, blepharitis, iridocyclitis, episcleritis, eyelid pain, and eye opacity. These novel signals may be particularly worthy of further investigation, as they supplement the current safety information of BPs.

SMQ analysis

Eleven SMQ categories were employed to classify eye-related AEs of BPs. A signal was detected for an SMQ category only when it met the criteria in all three disproportionality methods, and the signal strength was represented by the IC025 value. As illustrated in Fig. 2, nine SMQ categories were identified as signals (IC025 > 0), including lens disorders, retinal disorders, conjunctival disorders, glaucoma, ocular infections, corneal disorders, ocular motility disorders, optic nerve disorders, and scleral disorders. Scleral disorders were related to all five BPs: risedronate (IC025:2.58), zoledronate (IC025:2.13), ibandronate (IC025:1.49), pamidronate (IC025:0.84), and alendronate (IC025:0.64). In addition, alendronate was correlated with lens disorders (IC025:1.86) and glaucoma (IC025:0.60), while zoledronate was concerned with lens disorders (IC025:0.26) and ocular motility disorders (IC025:0.02). Pamidronate was involved in eight eye-related SMQ categories, the most among the five BPs, encompassing a broad spectrum of ocular diseases. Risedronate presented a weak signal related to conjunctival disorders (IC025:0.20) and ocular motility disorders (IC025:0.13).

Gender and age difference analysis

To identify gender and age differences in ocular AEs related to BPs, the ROR method was used to identify the positive PT signals. As for alendronate, males were more likely to experience conjunctivitis allergic. With zoledronate, conjunctival edema and presbyopia tended to occur in males, while females were more prone to iridocyclitis, eye pain, scleritis, and uveitis (Table 3). The other three BPs did not show any gender differences. In terms of age, patients aged 65 and above were more susceptible to cataract, macular degeneration, and glaucoma with alendronate; idiopathic orbital inflammation and iris adhesions with zoledronate; and cataract with pamidronate (Table 4).

Discussion

Through an analysis of eye-related AEs linked to BPs in the FAERS database, our study has identified that ocular AEs linked to BPs are predominantly characterized by ocular inflammation. Beyond the adverse reactions documented in drug instructions, such as uveitis, scleritis, iritis, and orbital inflammation, our findings suggest the emergence of additional risks for ocular disorders, including cataract, glaucoma, presbyopia, and macular degeneration. Furthermore, distinct BPs exhibit varying risk profiles for ocular events, and the reporting patterns for the same bisphosphonate (BP) differ based on gender and age. Early identification of ocular AE risks and timely intervention are crucial for mitigating the risk of vision loss.

The relationship between BPs and ocular inflammation, including conditions such as scleritis, uveitis, and orbital inflammation, has been extensively documented in the literature, aligning with our research findings (7, 8). Ocular inflammation is a rare adverse effect of BPs, reported in only 0.4% of cases (9). In our study, scleritis was consistently linked to all five BPs examined. Scleritis is mentioned in the package inserts of four BPs, except for risedronate. This omission may be attributed to risedronate’s later market entry and its limited application. A retrospective cohort study indicated that the incidence of uveitis (0.29%) and scleritis (0.63%) associated with oral BPs was higher compared to non-users (0.20 and 0.36%, respectively) (10). Scleritis can result in severe symptoms, including eye pain and decreased vision. Although these AEs are rare, physicians should inform patients about the potential ocular AEs when prescribing BPs and advise them to seek medical attention promptly should such symptoms arise.

Based on the frequency and intensity of PT analysis, our findings suggested a more pronounced signal for ocular inflammation with zoledronate. Zoledronate exhibited a higher number of moderate and strong PT signals, as well as reports of ocular inflammation. Previous research has documented the incidence of acute anterior uveitis associated with zoledronate at 0.8% (11). A recent retrospective study further elucidated the clinical characteristics of zoledronate-induced uveitis, noting that this condition, predominantly anterior uveitis, typically manifests within 72 h following the initial dose (12). All patients experienced improvement or recovery following the discontinuation of zoledronate (97.6%) and the administration of topical steroids (97.7%), systemic steroids (15.9%), and cycloplegics (72.7%). Intravenous BPs, such as zoledronate and pamidronate, were more likely to induce ocular inflammation with a higher frequency and more rapid onset, often occurring within a few days post-infusion. In contrast, oral BPs, such as alendronate and risedronate, tended to present these AEs with less predictable and more variable timing (4). The elevated incidence of ocular inflammation risk signal for zoledronate may be attributed to the high doses and intravenous administration employed in cancer treatment.

The ocular inflammation related to BPs may be attributed to the activation of γδT cells, which subsequently stimulated the production of interleukin-17 (IL-17) by Th17 cells (13). Uveitis was more likely to manifest in patients with thyroid diseases or autoimmune disorders (14). A study has demonstrated that alendronate (100 μM) significantly enhanced the expression of IL-8 (P = 0.02) in human retinal pigment epithelial cells in vitro (15). However, the precise mechanisms underlying these observations remain to be fully elucidated.

Cataract was identified as a risk signal for alendronate, pamidronate, and zoledronate, marking a novel finding not previously documented in their labels. Alendronate exhibited the highest number of reported cataract cases, totaling 970 across all cataract types, and demonstrated high signal strength. Pamidronate presented the strongest risk signal for cataract nuclear (ROR: 29.56, 95% CI: 18.24–47.91). The literature contains limited reports on BP-related cataract, potentially due to insufficient attention to this issue. However, a cohort study involving patients with osteoporosis indicated that beginning denosumab therapy was related to a modest reduction in the risk of cataract surgery compared to zoledronate (16). Furthermore, a randomized controlled trial involving postmenopausal breast cancer patients revealed a similar incidence of cataract between the denosumab and placebo groups (0.9 vs 1.7%) (17). These findings suggested that BPs presented a stronger risk signal for cataract than RANKL inhibitors. Our study found that the risk of cataract related to alendronate and pamidronate was elevated in patients over the age of 65, which may be attributed to the increased prevalence of cataract with advancing age, particularly after 60 years (18). Our results indicated that cataract represents a risk signal, potentially due to the partial overlap between the onset ages of cataract and the ages of patients using BPs.

Regarding the risk signal between BPs and cataract, it is hypothesized that this may be linked to calcium regulation. Research has indicated that the disruption of calcium homeostasis in the lens tissue of diabetic patients constitutes a significant risk factor for cataract development (19). The maintenance of normal lens transparency necessitates a physiological range of calcium concentration. Deviations from this calcium balance can initiate processes that alter the molecular structure of the lens, resulting in light scattering (20). BPs are known to reduce calcium levels by inhibiting osteoclast activity (21), suggesting that they may influence cataract development through the modulation of calcium balance in lens tissue. However, existing studies have provided insufficient evidence to establish a definitive correlation between BPs and cataract. The potential relationship between them requires further research to be elucidated.

In our study, alendronate and pamidronate were related to the risk of glaucoma. Alendronate exhibited a higher number of reported glaucoma cases and greater signal intensity (ROR: 2.75, 95% CI: 2.44–3.10), with the most pronounced signal intensity observed in cases of borderline glaucoma (ROR: 13.19, 95% CI: 7.33–23.73). A case–control study involving osteoporosis patients indicated that users of BPs had a higher likelihood of developing acute angle-closure glaucoma compared to non-users (adjusted IRR = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.05–3.01), with risedronate users being more susceptible than users of other BPs. This study found that BPs posed no risk for open-angle glaucoma (22). Our study and the case–control study suggested that glaucoma may be a noteworthy AE for BPs. However, we did not identify angle-closure glaucoma as a positive signal when employing three different disproportionality analysis methods. This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in study methodologies and the lack of specific differentiation between glaucoma types in some AE reports. In addition, a case report documented the use of alendronate as a potential cause of bilateral anterior uveitis and choroidal effusions with secondary angle closure in an old woman (23). However, the occurrence of glaucoma in this case may be related to uveitis, and further research is needed to elucidate its relationship with BP usage.

In vitro experiments have shown that zoledronate upregulated the expression of matrix metalloproteinases MMP-9 and MMP-13 in rat vascular smooth muscle cells by inducing oxidative stress and activating the NF-κB pathway, thereby influencing tissue remodeling (24). This mechanism of tissue remodeling may also impact the contraction and relaxation functions of ocular smooth muscles, potentially affecting aqueous humor circulation and contributing to the development of glaucoma (22).

A study has indicated that the risk of developing wet age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is elevated with prolonged use of oral BPs, such as alendronate, risedronate, and ibandronate (25). Our findings are consistent with this potential link. Specifically, we identified that pamidronate exhibited the strongest risk signal for AMD (ROR: 7.91, 95% CI: 4.37–14.31) among the five BPs examined. Alendronate had the highest number of macular degeneration reports, while risedronate demonstrated the strongest correlation with neovascular AMD (ROR: 6.35, 95% CI: 2.38–16.94). In contrast, zoledronate did not present any indication of macular degeneration. Another study has suggested that BPs possess anti-angiogenic properties and potential benefits for choroidal neovascularization in AMD patients (26). A randomized controlled pilot study evaluated the feasibility of using zoledronate as an adjunctive therapy for neovascular AMD. This study observed a potential visual benefit in the zoledronate group compared to the control group, with no evidence of an impact on treatment-related outcomes (27). These findings are consistent with our results, in which no significant signal for macular degeneration was identified for zoledronate.

AMD arises through multiple mechanisms, involving inflammatory processes and impairment of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) as contributing factors (28, 29). Proinflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor-α, interferon-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-17, have been linked to the pathogenesis of AMD (30). In individuals with AMD, increased levels of IL-8 were found in intraocular fluid (31). Alendronate has been shown to increase IL-8 expression in human RPE, indicating the pro-inflammatory properties of BPs, which may have a part in the development of AMD (15). While the anti-angiogenic properties of BPs may inhibit pathological neovascularization in wet AMD, their pro-inflammatory properties could potentially elevate the risk of AMD. Current research suggests that the type, dosage, duration of BP use, and the observation period may influence outcomes. Further investigation is necessary to elucidate the impact of BPs on AMD.

Limitations

This study evaluated and compared AEs related to BPs using data from the FAERS. First, the disproportionality analysis employed serves to generate hypotheses and identify potential AEs for further investigation; however, it does not quantify risks, establish causal associations, or independently confirm drug safety. Second, the analysis was conducted without adjusting for reporter type, which represents a limitation, as reports submitted by individuals without a healthcare professional background carry the potential for inaccuracy. Third, additional constraints inherent to spontaneous reporting systems include the inability to control for confounding factors, such as underlying comorbidities and concomitant medications, due to the absence of detailed clinical data. Fourth, the classification of ocular conditions, including glaucoma and macular degeneration, lacks consistent precision; distinctions such as open-angle versus closed-angle glaucoma or dry versus wet macular degeneration are not always clearly delineated. Therefore, more granular studies are warranted to investigate a potential link between BPs and specific ocular disease subtypes. While the findings of this pharmacovigilance study are insufficient to definitively establish safety or directly inform regulatory actions, they provide valuable insights and direction for future research into the ocular safety profile of BPs, underscoring the need for further investigation.

Conclusion

The ocular AEs related to BPs are varied. During BP therapy, it is important to monitor for ocular inflammation, such as scleritis. Notably, ocular inflammation related to zoledronate occurs more frequently than with other BPs. Importantly, AEs not listed in the instructions, such as cataract, glaucoma, and macular degeneration, should also be monitored. As a hypothesis-generating study, the identified signals require validation in rigorously designed, large-scale prospective studies.
