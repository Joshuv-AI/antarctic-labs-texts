Nursing students’ experiences using photovoice with team-based learning in pediatric nursing education
Authors: Fatemeh Ebrahimpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Iran, traditional teacher-centered methods remain the dominant approach in nursing education; however, these methods are often perceived as unsatisfactory by nursing students. There is a growing need for innovative, student-centered teaching strategies to enhance learning outcomes. Photovoice, a participatory method that combines photography and reflection, has been proposed as a potential approach; however, its application in Iranian nursing education, particularly in pediatric nursing, has not yet been explored.MethodsThis study employed a qualitative descriptive design supported by quantitative descriptive data and involved 36 undergraduate nursing students enrolled in a pediatric nursing course at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran, in 2024. The students were instructed to use Photovoice to document community-level factors influencing child health and participated in group discussions using a team-based learning approach. Data were collected through a researcher-developed questionnaire assessing motivation, engagement, understanding of child health determinants, teamwork, and critical thinking, as well as semi-structured interviews with 10 nursing students. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, and qualitative data were analyzed through content analysis.ResultsOver 90% of the nursing students agreed that the Photovoice combined with a team-based learning approach enhanced their motivation, active participation, teamwork, and overall learning experience. Qualitative analysis identified five main themes: (1) perceived novelty and pedagogical creativity; (2) enhanced engagement through active and experiential learning; (3) increased critical awareness of environmental factors influencing child health; (4) deeper learning through visual contextualization and peer discussion; and (5) practical challenges related to photography in public settings and group collaboration. These findings underscore the pedagogical value of Photovoice while highlighting areas that require further support.ConclusionPhotovoice combined with team-based learning represents a creative and effective pedagogical strategy in pediatric nursing education, fostering active learning, collaboration, and critical thinking. To ensure optimal implementation, ethics training, structured teamwork facilitation, and standardized consent procedures for photographing minors are recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08347-5.

Introduction

The pediatric nursing course is a mandatory component of undergraduate nursing education that prepares students to provide safe, competent, and family-centered care for children, a highly vulnerable population [1]. Effective teaching strategies in pediatric nursing education are therefore essential for developing critical thinking, empathy, and communication skills among future nurses [2]. In Iran, lecture-based instruction remains the dominant teaching method in nursing education. However, evidence suggests that traditional lectures often fail to engage students or achieve meaningful learning outcomes actively. For example, a study involving 840 students at an Iranian university of medical sciences found that only 36% regarded lectures as desirable, even when modern teaching aids were used [3]. In contrast, several Iranian studies have demonstrated that student-centered teaching approaches can enhance both satisfaction and learning outcomes among nursing students compared to traditional methods [4–6]. There is a global shift in nursing education toward student-centered pedagogies grounded in constructivist and active learning theories. These theories emphasize that learners construct knowledge by connecting new information to their prior understanding through reflection, collaboration, and interaction [7–11]. Within this framework, participatory and experiential learning methods such as Photovoice have gained increasing attention in medical and nursing education [12–15]. Photovoice enables students to express their experiences and perceptions through photography, fostering reflection, dialogue, and a deeper understanding of complex health and social issues [16–18]. Prior studies have shown that Photovoice enhances students’ critical awareness and empathy, supporting learning in areas such as community health, cultural competence, social determinants of health, and patient-centered care [19–24].

Despite these promising findings, the application of Photovoice in pediatric nursing education remains limited. Existing research has primarily focused on using Photovoice with pediatric nurses or with children and their families rather than as a teaching tool for nursing students [25–27]. In Iran, although Photovoice has been used in qualitative research, no study to date has examined its use as a pedagogical strategy in undergraduate nursing education, particularly within pediatric nursing courses [28]. Addressing this gap is important because nursing education in Iran continues to rely heavily on teacher-centered lectures, which restrict students’ engagement and creativity. Exploring the use of Photovoice combined with team-based learning may offer an innovative and culturally relevant approach to enhance students’ motivation, engagement, and critical understanding of child health issues. This study contributes new evidence by integrating Photovoice and collaborative learning within the context of pediatric nursing in Iran, an area previously unexplored in the literature. Constructivist learning theory provided an interpretive lens for understanding how students constructed knowledge through reflection, collaboration, and engagement during Photovoice activities, rather than serving as a prescriptive theoretical framework guiding the study design.

In summary, while international studies have demonstrated the pedagogical value of Photovoice in nursing education, its use in Iranian pediatric nursing education has not been investigated. Therefore, this study examines the experiences of Iranian nursing students in using Photovoice, combined with team-based learning, as an innovative, student-centered approach to pediatric nursing education. This study aimed to address the following research questions:RQ1: What are the perceived effects of this approach on students’ motivation, engagement, critical thinking, and understanding of child health determinants?RQ2: How do undergraduate nursing students perceive the use of Photovoice combined with team-based learning in a pediatric nursing course?

Methods

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design supported by quantitative descriptive data. This study was designed as a single-group intervention study to evaluate the effectiveness of the Photovoice-based teaching method on undergraduate nursing students’ satisfaction and learning experiences. All students received the intervention, and outcomes were assessed using a quantitative questionnaire and a complementary qualitative component via semi-structured interviews. Multiple data collection methods were used to provide deeper insights into students’ experiences and perceptions of the teaching method.

This study was conducted in 2024 at the School of Nursing and Midwifery, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran. Inclusion criteria included enrollment in the course and willingness to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria consisted of withdrawal from the course or incomplete submission of the Photovoice assignment. All fifth-semester undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the pediatric nursing course (n = 36) were invited to participate.

A detailed 17-session lesson plan was developed in accordance with the national nursing curriculum. The Photovoice activity was implemented during the third session, focused on “Social Determinants of Child Health.” The intervention consisted of two stages. In first stage, the students were introduced to the Photovoice method in the second session of class. They were asked to photograph three community-level factors affecting child health using a mobile phone, camera, or tablet. They reflected on their photos using the SHOWeD technique by Wang and Burris (1997) [29], which includes the questions: “What do you see here?” What is really happening? How does this relate to a child’s life?” Why does this situation exist?” What can be done about it? Students documented their photos and reflections using a structured Word template: (1) Student info and assignment instructions; (2) Photo location frame; (3) SHOWeD questions with answer space. The instructor provided guidance throughout the assignment period via email or in person. All students had to bring a hard copy of their completion file to the third session.

In the second stage, during the following class session, students formed groups of four to six members to share and discuss their Photovoice assignments. Each group received a printed Word template that included member names, the group leader, and the discussion summary. The group leader selected by group members was responsible for completing the form, managing time, and facilitating the discussion. Within each group, members were instructed to first share their completed Photovoice assignment and provide a brief explanation of their work to the group members. They had to classify and discuss the photos according to child health-related factors (Fig. 1). Finally, each group leader recorded a summary of the key discussion, which was later presented to the entire class. This phase lasted approximately 40 min. During this time, the instructor facilitated discussions, provided feedback, and highlighted key learning concepts.

Following the group work, each group leader presented a summary (lasting 7–10 min) of the key issues discussed in their group, based on the selected photographs. These presentations were shared with the entire class, and the instructor contributed by offering feedback, reinforcing key learning points, and summarizing the most important concepts.

A researcher-developed questionnaire was administered to all participants at the end of the Photovoice session to assess nursing students’ perceptions and experiences of this teaching method. Since no standardized instrument existed for evaluating nursing students’ perspectives on Photovoice, the questionnaire was designed specifically for this study, based on a review of the literature on active learning strategies and pediatric nursing education. The questionnaire was not previously published and is available as a supplementary file. The questionnaire consisted of 10 items designed to measure the perceived impact of the Photovoice and team-based learning approach on nursing students’ motivation to participate and learn, engagement in class activities, understanding of child health determinants, teamwork and collaboration, and critical thinking. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” The questionnaire was completed in written form by students at the end of the Photovoice session. Content validity was evaluated by four faculty members experienced in pediatric nursing education, who reviewed each item for clarity, relevance, and alignment with learning objectives. Minor revisions were made based on their feedback. Reliability was assessed through Pilot testing and Internal consistency. The questionnaire was administered to a small group of nursing students (n = 5) who were not participating in the main study to assess clarity, comprehension, and response variability. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.76, which is considered acceptable for newly developed educational instruments [30].

It has adopted a content analysis approach, as described by Hsieh and Shannon, to explore nursing students’ experiences with an innovative teaching method that combines Photovoice with team-based learning in a pediatric nursing course [31]. Reporting of methodological procedures followed best practice guidelines [32]. Participants were undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the pediatric nursing course. Inclusion criteria were: (a) attendance in the course that used the Photovoice and team-based learning method, (b) willingness to share personal learning experiences, and (c) ability to communicate in Persian. Students who were absent from the class session or declined participation were excluded. Of the 36 students in the course, 10 students were selected for interviews, regardless of their questionnaire responses, using purposive sampling with maximum variation (based on gender, academic performance, and class participation) to capture diverse perspectives. This sample size was determined to be sufficient when data saturation was determined based on the point at which no new themes or subthemes emerged during the final interviews, indicating thematic saturation.

Semi-structured, individual interviews were conducted between October and November/2024. Interviews took place in a quiet, private classroom on campus to ensure confidentiality and comfort. Each interview lasted 30–40 min and was conducted in Persian. All interviews were conducted by the first author (PhD in nursing education, with training and prior experience in qualitative research and interviewing). The interviewer, who also taught the course, was familiar with the participants; however, reflexivity and transparency were maintained through the use of memo-writing and peer debriefing. Additionally, questionnaires were collected anonymously, and students were assured that their responses would not influence their course grades, minimizing potential instructor–researcher bias. To minimize social desirability and novelty effects, students were assured that negative feedback was valued, and qualitative interviews were used to complement the questionnaire and capture more authentic responses.

Interviews were audio-recorded with informed consent, and field notes were taken to capture non-verbal cues and contextual details. An interview guide was used to ensure consistency, including questions such as (1) Please describe your overall experience of participating in the class that used Photovoice combined with team-based learning. (2) In what ways, if any, did this teaching method influence your learning in the pediatric nursing course? (3) What aspects of this method were most helpful or enjoyable for you? Which aspects were less helpful or challenging? (4) How did you feel during the activities such as taking photos, participating in group discussions, and working in teams? (5) If you compare this method with traditional teaching approaches, what differences stand out to you?

Interviews were transcribed verbatim in Persian by the first author within 48 h of data collection. No transcription software was used. The qualitative data were analyzed using the content analysis approach proposed by Elo and Kyngäs [33]. The analysis included reading the transcripts repeatedly to gain a sense of the whole, identifying meaning units, generating initial codes, grouping codes into subcategories, and developing categories and overarching themes. The trustworthiness of the findings was evaluated using the five criteria proposed by Guba and Lincoln (1989) [34]. Credibility was supported through prolonged engagement with participants, peer debriefing, and member checks with selected students. Dependability was enhanced by maintaining an audit trail of coding and analytic decisions. Confirmability was addressed through reflexivity, member checking, and peer feedback. Transferability was supported by providing rich descriptions of the research context and participants. Authenticity was pursued by representing diverse perspectives in the findings. It was reported using the COREQ 32-item checklist (supplementary material).

Results

The average age of the nursing students was 21 years (SD = 1.3), and 52% (n = 19) of the 36 participants were male. Figure 2 shows the percentages derived from the quantitative component of the study, based on students’ responses to the questionnaire. A significant proportion of students expressed positive perceptions of the photovoice and group discussion-based teaching method. Specifically, they had high levels of agreement that Photovoice improved several factors, including group work, understanding of health determinants, class participation, and motivation.

Collectively, students submitted 108 color photographs using mobile phone cameras, capturing various community-based factors affecting child health, including play and recreation, injury and safety, child labor, air pollution, and nutrition. For example, a nursing student took a picture of a sign on the subway and wrote, “This is the subway. This sign shows where the mother and baby room is in the subway. This shows that social services in public places for child welfare can be beneficial for children’s health. In this room, the mother can breastfeed her baby or get the necessary health care for her baby” (Participant 3). Qualitative analysis of semi-structured interviews conducted with ten students (six females and four males) led to the identification of five overarching themes (Table 1).

The main themes, sub-themes, and participant quotes

Theme 1: perceived novelty and pedagogical creativity

Students unanimously described the use of photographs in teaching as a novel and creative approach compared to traditional lecture-based instruction. Many viewed this deviation from routine pedagogy as refreshing and motivating. For example, one participant noted, “This teaching method was creative and different from what other instructors usually use” (Participant 3). At the same time, another said, “Using pictures as part of the lesson was new to us and broke the monotony of traditional learning” (Participant 5).

Theme 2: enhanced engagement through active and experiential learning

Students highlighted the Photovoice method as a source of dynamic and enjoyable learning. Taking photographs in the community and engaging in class discussions were described as stimulating and interactive. One student shared, “Photographing children’s lives in the community was a very interesting experience for me” (Participant 9), and another remarked, “The photography made the topic more enjoyable, and I completed the assignment with enthusiasm” (Participant 6). Group discussions further enriched the learning experience by promoting exchange of ideas, enhancing classroom engagement, and fostering a student-centered learning environment.

Theme 3: critical awareness of environmental determinants of child health

The Photovoice method prompted students to develop a more critical perspective on children’s health issues in their communities. Many acknowledged an increased awareness of issues they had previously overlooked. One participant stated, “My perspective on children changed. I became more attuned to issues I had ignored before” (Participant 8). Another shared, “This assignment made me think more deeply about why certain problems exist and their implications for child health” (Participant 2).

Theme 4: deeper learning through visual contextualization and peer sharing

Students reported that the use of real-life images and shared experiences enhanced their understanding of course concepts. By connecting theoretical content with tangible examples, they achieved deeper comprehension and improved retention. One student remarked, “Discussing and analyzing the photos with classmates helped us learn better and remember more” (Participant 8), while another added, “Visualizing the concepts through photos and group discussion deepened my understanding” (Participant 5).

Theme 5: practical barriers to visual data collection and collaborative work

Despite the overall positive experience, students encountered several challenges. Photographing in public spaces, especially those involving children, occasionally caused discomfort or hesitation. One student noted, “I was nervous that people would question me while taking photos in places like supermarkets” (Participant 1), and another added, “It was hard to take pictures of children because they kept moving, and it was difficult to communicate with their parents” (Participant 7). Group work also presented some difficulties, particularly in terms of time management and unequal participation. “Two members of our group didn’t contribute much, so others had to take on their tasks” (Participant 2), and “We spent a lot of time trying to delegate tasks; if everyone had better teamwork skills, we could have used our time more efficiently” (Participant 5).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that nursing students perceived the Photovoice and team-based learning (TBL) methods as creative, motivating, enjoyable, collaborative, and engaging approaches to pediatric nursing education. The quantitative findings indicated high levels of agreement regarding increased motivation, engagement, and teamwork, providing supportive evidence that closely aligns with the qualitative themes identified in the interviews. Specifically, the quantitative results complemented the qualitative findings of enhanced engagement through active and experiential learning, as well as deeper learning through visual contextualization and peer sharing. This convergence suggests that students’ self-reported perceptions were consistent with their in-depth reflections and narratives, indicating a coherent pattern of positive learning experiences across both data sources.

From a constructivist perspective, these findings illustrate that Photovoice combined with TBL can foster active knowledge construction rather than passive reception of information. According to constructivist learning theory, students build understanding through experience, reflection, and collaboration. In this study, active engagement reflected the process of experiential learning, as students participated in real-life, context-based photographic activities that deepened their conceptual understanding of pediatric nursing. Similarly, peer collaboration aligned with the constructivist notion of knowledge co-construction, as students worked collectively to interpret visual data and connect theory to practice. Furthermore, the reflective learning observed in the interviews illustrated the process of scaffolding, whereby guided discussions and feedback supported students in progressing from surface-level observation to higher-order analysis and synthesis. Thus, Photovoice with TBL provided a learning environment consistent with constructivist principles facilitating the co-creation of meaning, promoting self-directed inquiry, and supporting the gradual internalization of professional knowledge.

These findings are consistent with prior research indicating that student-centered pedagogies grounded in constructivism enhance engagement, autonomy, and critical reflection in nursing education. For instance, Andina-Díaz et al. found that combining Photovoice with reflective storytelling promoted critical thinking and deeper comprehension of social determinants of health [35], while Lickiewicz et al. reported similar improvements in active learning and professional awareness among early-career nurses [36]. Moreover, Andina-Díaz et al. emphasized that over 70% of students considered Photovoice an engaging and valuable educational tool for nursing practice. Students reported that it not only encouraged reflection on social and health inequities but also fostered positive emotional and affective responses [23]. This consistency across studies reinforces the validity and generalizability of Photovoice as an effective pedagogical strategy in nursing education, highlighting its potential to promote both cognitive and affective learning outcomes [37].

Although Photovoice was originally developed as a participatory data collection method at the community level [38], it can be effectively adapted for healthcare higher education, particularly in specialized fields such as pediatric nursing, offering opportunities to enhance students’ critical thinking, reflection, and collaborative skills. In nursing education, it functions as a novel and student-centered teaching strategy that provides multiple pedagogical benefits, and existing evidence indicates that it is well accepted by learners [21, 39].

Nevertheless, despite these pedagogical advantages, challenges remain in implementing Photovoice effectively. Randall et al. reported that methodological variability limits reproducibility and generalizability in nursing education, recommending standardized guidelines to ensure consistency. Careful planning and supervision are therefore essential to maximize its educational value [21]. In this regard, our study also revealed that, based on nursing students’ experiences, implementing the Photovoice teaching method was associated with certain limitations and challenges. Participants identified issues such as fear of criticism from the public or parents, and children’s limited cooperation during photo sessions, all of which underscore the ethical complexities inherent in photography. Ethical considerations have consistently been a critical aspect of Photovoice projects, particularly when involving vulnerable populations such as children [40, 41].

Ethical issues including consent, privacy, and potential exploitation of minors have been well documented in prior research [42, 43]. In a study by Hannes et al., students reported facing ethical challenges when taking photographs for a university project, largely due to personal and cultural factors. Feelings of shyness, fear of interacting with strangers, discomfort in sensitive settings, difficulties obtaining consent, and cultural beliefs led many to avoid photographing people. Instead, they preferred capturing images of objects or, in some cases, proceeded without formal consent [40]. Since photography is central to the Photovoice approach, nursing students need to be equipped to handle related ethical challenges. Providing preparatory sessions on ethical photography, clear guidance on consent, and institutional support is essential both to protect participants and to enhance the educational value of Photovoice.

In this study, group work challenges including unequal participation and time management difficulties also emerged as limitations from nursing students’ experiences using Photovoice with TBL. These findings echo previous research showing that while nursing students recognize the importance of teamwork, many lack practical training in collaboration and conflict resolution [44]. A study conducted in Iran found that first-year nursing students, while aware of teamwork principles, struggled with practical collaboration, whereas senior students, despite greater experience, still encountered difficulties coordinating and sustaining group efforts [45]. This highlights the need for structured scaffolding of collaborative learning to ensure that team-based approaches not only promote engagement but also cultivate essential interpersonal and professional competencies.

Conclusion

This study supports Photovoice as a creative and engaging pedagogical strategy in pediatric nursing education, while also identifying ethical and teamwork-related challenges that must be addressed to ensure its effective implementation. To enhance the application of Photovoice in educational settings, several practical recommendations are proposed: (1) integrate ethics training prior to photography assignments; (2) scaffold teamwork skills through structured preparatory activities; (3) provide standardized consent templates for photographing minors; and (4) explicitly align Photovoice projects with defined learning outcomes and assessment rubrics.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. Methodological: The small sample size and absence of a control group limit the generalizability of the findings and preclude causal inference. Instrument-related: The self-developed questionnaire underwent limited psychometric validation, which may have affected its reliability. Researcher bias: Because the course instructor also conducted the interviews, potential biases such as social desirability and instructor–researcher bias may have influenced participants’ responses. These biases were mitigated through reflexive journaling, peer debriefing, and member checking to enhance the study’s credibility. Contextual: The study was conducted at a single university in Iran, which may limit the transferability of findings to other settings and cultural contexts. Moreover, practical and ethical challenges associated with the Photovoice method, such as photographing children in public spaces and managing group collaboration, may have influenced student participation and responses.

Future research should employ larger, multi-site samples; incorporate control groups through randomized controlled trials or mixed-method designs; utilize standardized and psychometrically validated measurement tools; and adopt strategies to minimize potential biases. Additionally, further studies should continue to explore the ethical and cultural factors influencing the implementation of Photovoice in nursing education.
