Genetic and clinical features of microcephaly in a prenatal cohort
Authors: Yi Zhang, Huibing Liu, Ailan Yin, Xin Jiang, Lijun Tang, Siping Liu, Xia Qin, Mei Zhong, Chantao Huang, Bei Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFetal microcephaly, defined as a small head circumference in utero or at birth, is a rare but clinically significant condition that may be associated with an increased risk of neurodevelopmental delay and impairment. However, the comprehensive analysis of multiple genetic testing methods for fetal microcephaly remains limited. This study aimed to summarize the clinical characteristics, ultrasound phenotypes, and genetic etiologies of prenatally diagnosed microcephaly.MethodsA single-center retrospective study was performed on fetuses with microcephaly. A total of 197 fetuses with microcephaly undergoing invasive prenatal diagnosis were recruited. Cytogenetic and monogenic abnormalities were investigated using karyotyping, chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), copy number variant sequencing (CNV-seq) and whole exome sequencing (WES) were further analyzed.ResultsAmong the 197 fetuses, 48.7% (96/197) had isolated microcephaly, while 51.3% (101/197) had non-isolated microcephaly. Within the non-isolated group, 24.9% (49/197) presented soft markers and 10.2% (20/197) exhibited structural defects. The incidences of chromosomal abnormalities (12.9%, 13/101) and pathogenic CNVs (6.9%, 7/101) were both higher in nonisolated fetuses than those in isolated fetuses. Significant differences were observed in the rate of chromosomal abnormalities across the borderline, moderate and severe microcephaly subgroups, and pCNVs were detected more commonly in the severe subgroup than in the borderline or moderate subgroup. Trio-WES, performed in 24 cases, revealed single gene variants including the POGZ gene, which was associated with the phenotype of microcephaly. The overall rate of adverse pregnancy outcomes was 33.1% (57/172), excluding ongoing pregnancies and cases lost to follow-up.ConclusionsFetal microcephaly represents a genotypically and phenotypically heterogeneous disorder. The comprehensive application of multiple genetic testing approaches provides an effective and essential strategy for the prenatal diagnosis of the diverse etiologies underlying fetal microcephaly.Keywords: Fetal microcephaly, Prenatal diagnosis, Karyotyping, Chromosomal microarray analysis, Copy number variant sequencing, Whole exome sequencing

Background

Fetal microcephaly, defined as a small head circumference (HC) detected in utero, is a rare condition associated with an increased risk of neurodevelopmental and intellectual delay, cerebral palsy, epilepsy, and visual or hearing problems [1]. The prevalence of prenatal microcephaly is estimated at 1.53 per 10 000 in Europe, 2.3 per 10 000 in India, and approximately 6 per 10 000 in the United States [2]. Despite this, there remains a lack of consensus regarding the diagnostic criteria for fetal microcephaly.

Microcephaly is typically categorized into borderline, moderate, and severe forms based on head circumference measurements [3]. Etiologies of microcephaly are various, diverse, and can include genetic disorders, environmental factors, and still unknown etiologies [1]. Previous studies have reported that various genetic disorders can present with microcephaly, including aneuploidies (trisomy 13, 18, 21), microdeletion and duplication syndromes (e.g., Miller-Dieker lissencephaly syndrome), and monogenic disorders (e.g., Seckel syndrome, Galloway-Mowat syndrome, Smith-Lemli-Opitz syndrome) [1, 4].

Prenatal diagnosis and clinical counseling for fetuses with microcephaly remain challenging due to variations across ethnicities and populations, uncertain gestational age and potential unknown causes. In addition to conventional G-banding karyotyping and chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), copy number variant sequencing (CNV-seq) and whole-exome sequencing (WES) have been introduced for prenatal clinical use [5–8]. Recent studies have shown that CNV-seq and WES can provide diagnostic yields ranging from 9.1% to 32.3% in fetuses with structural defects [8–10]. However, comprehensive analyses integrating multiple genetic testing methods for fetal microcephaly are still limited. In this single-center retrospective cohort, 197 fetuses with microcephaly were enrolled and categorized into isolated and non-isolated group based on their ultrasound findings. Isolated microcephaly refers to cases in which only fetal microcephaly was identified, wheras non-isolated microcephaly refers to microcephaly combined with additional ultrasound anomalies. We reviewed the rates and types of genetic findings detected by karyotyping, CMA, CNV-seq and/or WES and investigated their clinical characteristics, ultrasound phenotypes, genetic etiology and pregnancy outcomes. Although no treatment is currently available, early diagnosis facilitates timely investigation, supports informed genetic counseling, and aids in planning long-term management strategies.

Methods

Patient selection

This single-center, retrospective study enrolled 197 singleton pregnant women who received an invasive prenatal diagnosis for fetal microcephaly at the Fetal Medicine Center of Nanfang Hospital between January 2007 and July 2023. The gestational week was confirmed based on the last menstrual period and the ultrasound scan at 11–13+ 6 gestational weeks. Inclusion criteria comprised fetuses with a head circumference (HC) measuring less than − 2 standard deviations (SDs) of given reference ranges [11, 12]. And fetuses were classified into three subgroups: borderline microcephaly (− 2SD ≥ HC > − 3SD), those with moderate microcephaly (−3SD ≥ HC > − 5SD), and those with severe microcephaly (HC ≤ − 5SD). Parental occipitofrontal diameter was measured and recorded in all cases. All parents were given comprehensive pretest genetic counseling, covering the benefits and disadvantages of different genetic assays, the testing process, possible results and limitations. Finally, 114 cases underwent karyotyping, 125 cases underwent CMA, 28 cases underwent CNV-seq, 24 cases underwent WES and 39 TORCH assay. Some cases underwent multiple genetic testing methods.

We reviewed the clinical characteristics of these 197 fetal cases with microcephaly, ultrasonographic presentations, maternal serum screening results, noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) results, results of invasive prenatal diagnosis, pregnancy outcomes, and postnatal follow-up data. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital and and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (NFEC-2021-159). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Invasive prenatal diagnosis

Depending on the gestational age, fetal samples from the amniotic fluid or cord blood were obtained by ultrasound-guided transabdominal amniocentesis (16–24 weeks) or cordocentesis (> 24 weeks). Parental peripheral blood samples were also collected concurrently.

Cells were cultured and prepared for G-banding karyotyping using standard protocols as previously reported [13]. Genomic DNA was extracted from the uncultured samples, and then CMA was carried out by using a whole genome-wide Illumina Infinium™ OmniZhongHua-8 v1.4 BeadChip (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s operating procedures. The data were analyzed by Illumina KaryoStudio Analysis Software (Illumina Inc.) and annotated with genome version GRCh37/hg19.

For CNV-seq and WES, genomic DNA was extracted from trio samples. Then, the DNA was treated and input to generate a library for sequencing on the NextSeq CN500 platform (Berry Genomics). Concurrently, initial genomic DNA was whole-exome captured, library prepared and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The Burrows–Wheeler Alignment tool (version 0.7.5a) was used for comparison with the reference sequence of GRCh38/hg38 and GRCh37/hg19. Annotated variants and CNVs were prioritized and filtered according to phenotype relativity, inheritance mode, allele frequency, read depth, literature, and in silico prediction. Genomic variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing using the 3730xl system (ABI). Ambiguous CNVs were confirmed through Infinium Global Screening Array SNP-array (Illumina Inc.) and Single Molecule Real-Time sequencing (PacBio) [8, 14].

Variant interpretation

Copy number variants (CNVs) and gene mutation variants were interpreted as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variants of uncertain significance (VUSs), likely benign and benign according to the guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) [15].

The significance of CNVs and gene variants results was interpreted by referring to online databases, including PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [16], Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, https://www.omim.org) [17], Database of Genomic Variants (DGV, http://dgv.tcag.ca/dgv/app/home) [18], DECIPHER (http://decipher.sanger.ac.uk/) [19], ClinGen resource (https://www.clinicalgenome.org/) [20], ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) [21], and ISCA (https://www.Iscaconsortium.org/) [22].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Student’s t test, the chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test using SPSS software, version 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics

From January 2007 to July 2023, a total of 197 pregnancies with fetal microcephaly screened by ultrasound were analyzed. The cohort’s diagnostic workflow is summarized in Fig. 1. Of these, 114 cases underwent karyotyping, 125 underwent CMA, 28 underwent CNV-seq, 24 underwent trio-WES and 39 underwent TORCH assays (Fig. 1). The median gestational age for undergoing invasive prenatal diagnosis was 29 weeks (range 18–36+ 5 weeks). The number of pregnant women who underwent amniocentesis after 18 weeks was 158 cases, while 39 cases underwent umbilical vascular puncture. The indications for invasive prenatal microcephaly diagnosis for the 197 fetuses are shown in Table 1. Among the 197 fetuses, 96 (48.7%) had isolated microcephaly, while 101 (51.3%) were classified as non-isolated, such as structural malformations, soft markers or abnormal amniotic fluid volume. In addition to prenatal ultrasound results, other indications, including advanced maternal age, high risk of maternal serum screening or NIPT, adverse pregnancy history and parental thalassemia or hemophilia, are also listed.

Detailed information on the incidence of different genetic abnormalities is displayed in Fig. 1. In total, the detection rates for chromosomal abnormalities, (pathogenic + likely pathogenic) CNVs and (likely pathogenic) single gene variants were 8.1% (16/197), 4.6% (9/197) and 2.5% (5/197), respectively. Of 96 isolated fetuses, chromosomal abnormalities were detected in 3 fetuses (3.1%, 3/96), while 13 chromosomal abnormalities were detected in nonisolated fetuses (12.9%, 13/101). Among nonisolated cases, 7 fetuses had 8 pathogenic CNVs (pCNVs) (6.9%, 7/101), one fetus had 2 lpCNVs (1.0%, 1/101), and two fetuses had 5 regions with loss of heterozygosity (2.0%, 2/101). The incidences of chromosomal abnormalities (12.9%) and pCNVs (6.9%) in nonisolated fetuses were both higher than those in isolated fetuses (chromosome abnormalities: χ2 = 6.265, p = 0.017 < 0.05. pCNVs: χ2 = 6.899, p = 0.009 < 0.01).

Subgroup analysis according to the degree of microcephaly

Fetuses with microcephaly were classified into three subgroups according to the degree of head circumference (HC): those with borderline microcephaly (− 2SD ≥ HC > − 3SD), those with moderate microcephaly (−3SD ≥ HC > − 5SD), and those with severe microcephaly (HC ≤ − 5SD). The distribution of these three subgroups is displayed in Fig. 2. The rates of chromosomal abnormalities in the borderline, moderate and severe groups were 5.2% (8/154), 18.9% (7/37) and 16.7% (1/6), respectively. Significant differences were observed in the rate of chromosomal abnormalities among these three subgroups (χ2 = 8.135, p = 0.017). The incidence of pCNVs in the borderline, moderate and severe groups was 3.3% (5/154), 0% (0/37) and 33.3% (2/6), respectively, and pCNVs were detected more commonly in the severe subgroup than in the borderline or moderate subgroup (χ2 = 16.932, p = 0.000).

Variants detected by trio-WES

Prenatal trio-WES was performed in 24 cases. The results showed that 5 heterozygous variants were found in 5 fetuses, including 1 isolated and 4 nonisolated fetuses (Fig. 1). The clinical features and likely pathogenic variants are shown in Table 5. A likely pathogenic variant c.5749G > A in the NOTCH2 gene (de novo) was identified in fetus with isolated microcephaly. Among nonisolated cases, four likely pathogenic heterozygous variants were detected. Case 165 was a fetus presenting with microcephaly, bilateral renal dysplasia and shortened long bones. A likely pathogenic heterozygous point variation c.149G > A in the PAX2 gene was detected by WES and that was associated with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis 7 and papillorenal syndrome. The same loci in the PAX2 gene (c.149G > A) were also identified in its father and grandfather. The inheritance pattern is autosomal dominant inheritance. In addition, this father was diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome in 2012, and renal biopsy confirmed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. This grandfather was diagnosed with uremia in 1996 and received kidney transplant in 1997, and his mother and a brother both died of uremia. In case 154, a de novo variant c.3410T > C in the MYH9 gene was found in fetus with hydrops fetalis and microcephaly. That was associated with Deafness, autosomal dominant 17 and May-Hegglin anomaly. In case 167, another de novo variant c.2540dup in the POGZ gene was identified in fetus with microcephaly, persistent left superior vena cava and shortened long bones, that associating with autosomal dominant inherited White-Sutton syndrome. In case 190, a variant c.1030G > A in the ACTA2 gene was found in fetus with microcephaly and agenesis of corpus callosum, which was inherited from its father and related with Aortic aneurysm, familial thoracic 6 and Multisystemic smooth muscle dysfunction syndrome.

Outcomes and follow-ups

The outcomes of the 197 fetal cases with microcephaly were as follows: 115 term births, 37 elected terminations of pregnancy (TOP), 4 intrauterine fetal deaths (IUFD), 16 preterm births, 9 ongoing pregnancies, and 16 cases were lost to follow-up. Except for cases of ongoing pregnancies and loss to follow-up, the overall rate of adverse pregnancy outcomes was 33.1% (57/172). Of the 115 successful term births, 2 chromosomal abnormalities and 1 pathogenic CNV were detected by karyotype and CNV-seq (cases 67, 155 and 194), and 1 likely pathogenic variants were detected by WES (cases 157). In case 67 with nonisolated microcephaly and echogenic intracardiac focus diagnosed by prenatal ultrasound, the karyotype result was 46,X, t(X;7) (p21.1;p15.3), and the neonate only presented low weight after birth. In case 155 with isolated microcephaly, a mosaicism that combined X monosomy and XX was found by CNV-seq, while a VUS variant (KIF11, het) was detected by WES. In case 194 with nonisolated microcephaly, a pathogenic 3.846 Mb deletion in chromosome 1 was found by CNV-seq. The neonates from cases 155 and 194 both showed normal birth weight and appearance. One likely pathogenic variant (cases 157) was found in isolated microcephaly cases, while the neonate showed normal birth weight. All 16 cases lost to follow-up had negative results from the genetic tests they underwent (karyotyping, n = 12; CMA, n = 9; TORCH, n = 3).

Discussion

The application of advanced genetic testing technologies such as chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) and whole-exome sequencing (WES) has improved the diagnostic yield for fetuses with structural anomalies; however, the genotype-phenotype correlation for specific conditions like fetal microcephaly often remains unclear. This poses a significant challenge in providing precise genetic counseling. This study summarized the clinical characteristics and genetic findings from invasive prenatal diagnosis in a cohort of 197 fetuses with microcephaly.

We first evaluated the overall diagnostic yield of genetic abnormalities. The detection rates for chromosomal abnormalities, pathogenic/likely pathogenic (p/lp) CNVs, and likely pathogenic (lp) single-gene variants were 8.1%, 4.6%, and 2.5%, respectively. Wang et al. reported that the diagnosis rate was 3.7% for CMA and 19.1% for trio-WES [4]. The lower detection rate of WES in our data could be attributed to the fewer cases applied. The rate of pathogenic findings was significantly higher in non-isolated cases than in isolated ones for both chromosomal abnormalities and pCNVs. This finding aligns with previous studies, reinforcing that the presence of additional structural anomalies or soft markers increases the likelihood of an underlying genetic etiology [23, 24].

The severity of microcephaly is another critical factor potentially influencing prognosis. Currently, different countries have different criteria for the cutoff value of fetal microcephaly, and most countries use HC ≤ −3 SD as the cutoff of microcephaly [25]. We divided the patients into borderline, moderate and severe subgroups according to the degree of HC in the present study. The incidence of chromosomal abnormalities and pCNVs seemed more commonly in the severe subgroup, suggesting a potential correlation between genetic abnormality and the degree of microcephaly. Previous research indicated that 11.1% of fetuses with microcephaly had abnormal karyotypes, 67.9% of which were aneuploid [4]. However, there are no definite data for exploring the relationship between aneuploidy and the degree of microcephaly. Hemming IA et al. reported a case of a complex 1q43-q44 CNV in a 14-year-old boy with severe seizures and prenatal-onset microcephaly (HC was − 2.7 SD below the mean at 36 weeks gestation) [26]. Barua S et al. also reported another case of 3q27.1q27.2 deletion with prenatal microcephaly, in which HC was 3 SD below the mean at 24 weeks gestation [27]. Nonetheless, we still may not easily conclude that the incidence of genetic abnormalities was positively correlated with the degree of microcephaly due to the small sample sizes of our study.

Among the 24 cases that underwent trio-WES, we identified likely pathogenic variants in five fetuses, including one with isolated and four with non-isolated microcephaly. Notably, a POGZ variant was associated with the phenotype of microcephaly. This finding matched the genetics of microcephaly described in previous studies [28, 29]. Given that microcephaly is a non-specific phenotype, a single genetic test is often insufficient for a comprehensive diagnosis. Trio-WES can uncover variants in genes not primarily associated with microcephaly. As in our case 165, we identified a PAX2 variant linked to renal disease in a fetus presenting with renal dysplasia and microcephaly. Therefore, greater caution should be used in prenatal counseling when we identify irrelevant variants in fetuses with microcephaly. This uncertainty between the phenotype and variants detected may increase parental anxiety and still requires continuous ultrasound monitoring during pregnancy and detailed postnatal follow-up to clarify the clinical significance of these genetic findings.

This study had several limitations. First, the number of cases undergoing trio-WES was relatively small, limiting the power to define a precise diagnostic rate or establish a definitive spectrum of single-gene causes. Second, incomplete long-term postnatal follow-up resulted in a loss of critical phenotypic information necessary for genotype-phenotype correlation. Third, prenatal infection screening (e.g., for cytomegalovirus, toxoplasmosis, Zika virus) was not performed uniformly, so a contribution from intrauterine infection cannot be conclusively ruled out in all cases.

In conclusion, we investigated the occurrence of genetic etiology and prenatal clinical features of fetuses with microcephaly. Fetal microcephaly is a rare condition with genotypically and phenotypically heterogeneous causes. First of all, it is important to inquire about the family history and measure family members’ head circumference. Next, it is necessary to perform continuous ultrasound monitoring and fetal brain MRI to monitor the growth trend of the head circumference and find whether there were complicated with structural malformations. The last is to undergo invasive prenatal diagnosis to explore for potential genetic and infection etiology due to the developmental outcome of prenatal microcephaly depends upon the underlying etiology and associated abnormalities. Given this complexity and the current limitations of prenatal phenotype interpretation, providing rational and cautious counseling is paramount to support families in making informed decisions regarding the pregnancy.
