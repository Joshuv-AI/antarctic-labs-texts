Deciphering context-specific Axitinib escape pathways via multi-omics and explainable machine learning
Authors: Samriddhi Gupta, Khyati Patni, Simarpreet Kaur, Jaspreet Kaur Dhanjal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResistance remains a major barrier to targeted cancer therapies. Axitinib, a VEGF receptor inhibitor with anti-angiogenic activity, is effective in several cancers but shows heterogeneous patient responses, reflecting context-specific molecular adaptations. A comprehensive multi-omics approach is needed to define these mechanisms and uncover compensatory survival pathways limiting Axitinib efficacy.MethodologyWe conducted a high-throughput analysis of ~ 1000 pan-cancer cell lines treated with 44 FDA-approved targeted drugs. Basal transcriptomic (~ 36,000 transcripts) and proteomic (~ 9000 proteins) profiles were integrated to predict cell-line-specific drug response using a multi-classifier machine learning framework. Multiple models, including ensemble, linear, and kernel-based classifiers, were trained per drug and evaluated via fivefold cross-validation. Axitinib, the best-predictive drug, was further analyzed using explainable AI (LIME) to identify resistance-driving features for each cell line. Resistant cell lines were clustered using agglomerative hierarchical clustering based on LIME-identified features and highly correlated partners. Optimal clusters were determined via silhouette scoring. Enrichment analysis, pathway annotation, and literature mining were used to uncover cluster-specific resistance mechanisms.ResultsAxitinib achieved the highest predictive accuracy across all 44 drugs. The machine learning pipeline reliably classified cell lines as resistant or sensitive from basal transcriptomic and proteomic data. LIME identified key resistance-driving features at the individual cell line level. Clustering based on these features revealed two resistance subtypes shaped by tissue origin and survival constraints. In blood-derived cancers, resistance involves purine metabolism rewiring and alternative growth factor signaling to sustain proliferation. In solid tumors, resistance reflected adaptation to hypoxia, including ECM remodeling, mechanosensing, EMT, immune evasion, and senescence-induced paracrine signaling.ConclusionAxitinib resistance emerges through tissue- and context-specific adaptations. Background

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, posing a persistent challenge to global health. According to the World Health Organization (2024), around 20% of individuals are expected to develop cancer during their lifetime, with 1 in 9 men and 1 in 12 women dying from the disease. Although therapeutic modalities have progressed from conventional interventions such as surgery and chemotherapy to sophisticated approaches including immunotherapy and targeted therapies, sustained disease remission remains a significant challenge in numerous cases. Among these approaches, targeted therapies have brought a paradigm shift by aiming at specific molecular abnormalities that drive tumor growth and survival. These therapies, including monoclonal antibodies and small-molecule inhibitors, are designed to interfere with critical cellular processes such as proliferation, angiogenesis, metastasis, and immune evasion. Over the years, the repertoire of targeted therapies has expanded beyond kinase inhibitors and immunomodulators [1–7] to include proteasome inhibitors, metabolic modulators, apoptosis inducers, and epigenetic modifiers [8–11]. While these treatments have shown promise, their clinical success is frequently challenged by the emergence of resistance [12–16].Notably, resistance is not solely acquired over time; many tumors exhibit inherent or pre-existing resistance mechanisms that render therapies ineffective from the outset.

Intrinsic resistance, arising from the baseline molecular features of tumor cells, represents a significant barrier to the efficacy of targeted therapies. These mechanisms can involve pathway redundancy, mutations near the drug’s target site, activation of compensatory signaling pathways, and alterations in cellular trafficking or metabolism. Tumor heterogeneity further adds to this complexity by enabling parallel resistance strategies within subpopulations of tumor cells [17]. Some of these subclones may carry genetic alterations that inherently confer resistance, while others may promote genomic instability, leading to the emergence of mutations that support survival and proliferation despite treatment [18–21]. Thus, a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms that confer resistance, especially those present prior to treatment, is essential for designing more durable and personalized therapeutic interventions.

Receptor Tyrosine Kinases (RTKs) are a well-established class of therapeutic targets in cancer, given their central roles in regulating cell proliferation, angiogenesis, differentiation, and migration [5, 22]. Dysregulation of RTK signaling is a hallmark of many cancers, driving malignant transformation and tumor progression. The RTK family comprises 58 members across 20 subfamilies, many of which are active during development and become aberrantly activated in tumors. Their pathological relevance has led to the development and FDA approval of multiple RTK inhibitors aimed at disrupting tumor-promoting signals. Axitinib is a highly potent RTK inhibitor that selectively targets VEGFR1, VEGFR2, and VEGFR3, with minimal off-target activity against other kinases such as KIT and PDGFR [23]. Axitinib stands out as a potent VEGFR inhibitor with half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) for VEGF family receptors that are ten times lower than those of other TKIs. It has also been shown to bind to the BCR-ABL fusion protein (albeit in a different conformation from VEGF binding), thereby selectively inhibiting the drug-resistant T315I mutant isoform [24]. Despite these promising attributes and clinical benefits observed in select patient populations, resistance to Axitinib remains a significant challenge. Therefore, a deeper understanding of these mechanisms is essential for improving the efficacy of Axitinib and similar RTK-targeted therapies.

In this study, we have employed explainable AI algorithms in tandem with supervised machine learning classifiers on transcriptomic and proteomic data from cancer cell lines with known sensitivity or resistance to Axitinib. By identifying key molecular signatures and signaling pathways associated with resistance, our work uncovered various diverse and often pre-existing strategies that tumor cells deploy to evade targeted inhibition.

Methodology

Data sourcing and dataset preparation

Drug response data The drug response data comprising cell lines, the administered drugs, and the corresponding IC50 values, were obtained from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database [25]. As of January 2023, the GDSC hosted IC50 values for 580 drugs across 1000 human cancer cell lines. The database consists of two versions, GDSC1 and GDSC2, with GDSC2 being the more recently ingested. We used all available drug response data from GDSC2. For drugs absent in GDSC2, we supplemented the data with corresponding entries from GDSC1. In cases where duplicate datasets for the same drug existed within GDSC2, we selected the version with the larger number of profiled cell lines to maximize statistical power and minimize sampling bias.

Each drug was subsequently categorized into two response classes: Sensitive and Resistant. For a given drug, cell lines with IC50 values exceeding the maximum concentration or dose administered were classified as Resistant, while those with IC50 values below the maximum dosage were classified as Sensitive. To enhance clinical relevance, the dataset was further filtered to retain only FDA-approved targeted therapies [26]. Additionally, drugs with highly imbalanced class labels (i.e., where either the Sensitive or Resistant class accounted for less than 5% of total samples) were excluded as a quality control measure to ensure balanced representation and robust downstream modeling.

Omics datasets The pan-cancer cell lines were described using molecular features: transcriptomic and proteomic data, available in the Cell Model Passport [27]. Proteomic profiles were obtained as protein intensity values from mass spectrometry and log2-transformed to minimize skewness. Similarly, RNA-seq data expressed as transcripts per million (TPM) were extracted and log2-transformed to normalize distributions across cell lines, improving feature comparability. Further, the mutation data of the pan-cancer cell lines were also taken from Cell Model Passports.

Automated feature engineering and model training

An automated feature engineering and machine learning pipeline was developed to identify the targeted drugs whose response profiles (resistance or sensitivity) could be accurately predicted using supervised classifiers, as described below:

Feature reduction The biological datasets, comprising transcriptomic and proteomic features, were inherently high-dimensional. Dimensionality reduction was therefore a crucial step to reduce noise while retaining interpretability. We applied Boruta, a wrapper-based technique built on the random forest classifier [28]. Boruta demonstrated strong performance in identifying features most relevant in distinguishing resistant and sensitive cell lines, thereby achieving substantial dimensionality reduction while maintaining high predictive accuracy and contributing in optimizing model performance.

Model training Following dimensionality reduction, several supervised machine learning classifiers, including Logistic Regression, Linear Support Vector Classifier, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, AdaBoost, Decision Tree, K-Nearest Neighbors, XG Boost, Gaussian Naive Bayes, and Multi-layer Perceptron, were applied to each drug-specific transcriptomics and proteomics dataset. To ensure balanced label representation and mitigate overfitting, stratified fivefold cross-validation was employed. Model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics: Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC), precision, recall, accuracy, and F1 score. The average performance across these metrics was used to identify drugs for which resistance could be most reliably predicted.

Explainability of machine learning predictions

To interpret the model predictions and identify the key molecular drivers of resistance, the Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) algorithm [29] was implemented. LIME provides instance-specific explanations by estimating the influence of each feature on a model’s prediction, indicating whether a feature supports resistance or sensitivity in a binary classification context. As an instance-based explainer, LIME generates perturbed samples around each data point and fits a sparse linear model to the corresponding predicted responses [29]. This enables interpretation of model decisions by approximating local decision boundaries using interpretable, linear surrogates. LIME explanations were applied to the best-performing classifier for both the transcriptomic and proteomic datasets, where the classifier was trained and evaluated using five-fold stratified cross-validation to ensure robustness and generalizability. For each of the five folds, the LimeTabularExplainer was initialized independently and applied to all instances (i.e., cell lines) using the training data for a specific drug. For each instance, LIME identified the top ten features, transcripts and proteins that most significantly contributed to the classification decision (sensitive or resistant) in transcriptome and proteome dataset respectively. To ensure a comprehensive view of feature relevance, LIME explanations were generated for both the training and test datasets.

To focus the interpretation specifically on resistance-associated features, only the genes identified in resistant cell lines were pooled across all folds and used for subsequent analysis. This step was essential to ensure that the LIME results reflect molecular signatures driving resistance, thereby providing biologically meaningful insights into the resistant phenotype.

Correlation-based feature augmentation to capture biologically relevant signals

Feature selection, applied prior to model training, eliminates covarying features to improve model performance and interpretability. However, transcripts or proteins involved in the same biological process or pathway often exhibit coordinated changes in expression to propagate signals to downstream effectors. In light of this, and to enhance biological interpretation, the LIME-derived gene sets were expanded by including transcripts and proteins from the original datasets that showed strong expression correlation (absolute Pearson correlation coefficient >|0.8|) with the LIME-identified features.

To ensure the biological relevance of these correlated features in determining resistance, a Levene’s test was applied to compare expression variance between Sensitive and Resistant cell lines. Only those correlated features that demonstrated a statistically significant difference (p value < 0.05) between the two groups were retained. These selected correlated transcripts or proteins, together with the original LIME-derived features, constituted the final feature set used for downstream analyses.

Feature-driven stratification of resistant cell lines

LIME-based interpretability analyses resulted in a heterogeneous set of contributing features from different cell types for classification into the resistant phenotype. This observation hints at the existence of multiple strategies to deploy resistance against Axitinib. Therefore, clustering was performed to stratify the cell lines based on the derived resistant features. Agglomerative hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distance and average linkage was applied to all resistant cell lines, utilizing the final set of resistance-contributing features. The optimal number of resistant clusters in both transcriptome and proteome datasets was determined using the silhouette index.

Elucidating the different mechanisms of resistance

Following clustering of the resistant cell lines based on the final contributing feature sets of transcripts and proteins, two distinct groups were obtained. Downstream analyses focused on resistant cell lines common to the clusters derived from both the transcriptomics and proteomics datasets. To identify features (transcripts and proteins) with consistent expression within each cluster, a non-parametric Levene’s test was performed to detect features with minimal variability in expression, despite differences in tissue origin, suggesting their critical role in promoting resistance. Simultaneously, a non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was conducted to assess differences in mean expression levels, identifying features that were significantly upregulated in specific clusters and thus likely contributing to resistance within that group. Transcripts and proteins that showed both low variance and high mean expression within a cluster were prioritized.

Resistance-associated features demonstrating both stability (low variance) and elevated expression (high mean) were subsequently submitted to KEGG Mapper [30] for pathway enrichment analysis. In addition, features that did not exhibit significant differences in mean or variance between clusters were also analyzed using KEGG [31], as they may contribute equally to both clusters. Pathways uniquely enriched within individual clusters were identified, along with those that were commonly enriched across both clusters.

To determine whether resistance to Axitinib arises from impaired drug binding to VEGF receptors or from ineffective VEGF ligand-receptor interactions that could disrupt angiogenesis, the mutation profiles of key VEGF ligands (VEGFA, VEGFB, VEGFC, VEGFD) and receptors (FLT1, KDR, FLT4) were retrieved from Cell Model Passports and further systematically evaluated. The variants were categorized based on their predicted functional consequences: essential splice site, frameshift, nonsense, and splice-region variants were considered likely loss-of-function; intronic, upstream, and downstream variants were classified as likely neutral, though regulatory potential was considered. Missense and in-frame insertion/deletion mutations were categorized as of uncertain functional impact. Special emphasis was placed on KDR missense mutations, which were mapped to amino acid positions and annotated with reference to UniProt functional domains to assess potential effects on Axitinib binding and resistance. Comparative mutation frequencies were calculated for resistant versus sensitive cell lines to determine whether specific mutations were enriched in resistance.

Feature engineering and model implementation automation

Boruta is known to be well-suited for high-dimensional omics data, where correlated variables and noise can otherwise inflate importance scores, and hence became the method of choice [32]. As expected, it demonstrated superior performance by significantly reducing data dimensionality while preserving both predictive accuracy and feature interpretability. For instance, in the Axitinib transcriptomics dataset, 954 cell lines with ~ 36,000 transcripts were analyzed, resulting in a 99.62% reduction in feature dimensionality using the Boruta algorithm. Of these, 625 cell lines were classified as resistant and 329 as sensitive. A similar trend was observed in the Axitinib proteomics dataset, which included 937 cell lines described by ~ 9000 proteins and underwent a 98.68% reduction in features. Here, 617 cell lines exhibited resistance, while 320 were sensitive. The prevalence of resistant samples in both datasets is a notable pattern that influences classification performance metrics.

A total of ten machine learning classifiers were implemented on the omics datasets: Logistic Regression, Linear Support Vector Classifier, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, AdaBoost, Decision Tree, K-Nearest Neighbors, XGBoost, Gaussian Naive Bayes, and Multi-layer Perceptron Classifier. Among these, XGBoost and Gradient Boosting consistently outperformed others across multiple evaluation metrics. Detailed results for all 35 drugs, evaluated using these classifiers on the transcriptomic and proteomic datasets, are provided in the Supplementary Sheet 2 and 3, respectively.

The average metric score, computed across Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), AUC-ROC, precision (resistance and sensitivity), recall (resistance and sensitivity), accuracy, and F1 score, was used to assess classifier performance. In the transcriptomic dataset, 23 drugs had at least one classifier with an average metric score above 0.7. Notably, Axitinib, Bosutinib, Crizotinib, Niraparib, Sorafenib, and Venetoclax surpassed this threshold with 9 out of 10 classifiers (Fig. 2B). In the proteomic dataset, 17 drugs achieved this benchmark. Specifically, Axitinib, Crizotinib, Sorafenib, and Venetoclax again stood out, each achieving high scores with 9 out of 10 classifiers (Fig. 2B). Notably, when only 2–3 classifiers show strong performance for a given feature set, it may indicate overfitting and raise concerns about generalizability. In contrast, in our analysis, the consistent high performance across 9 classifiers suggests the robustness and generalizability of the selected features and feature engineering approach.

We chose Axitinib (Fig. 2C) to demonstrate the effectiveness of the framework in elucidating the resistance mechanisms that make cell lines unresponsive to Axitinib. All ML metric scores across five fold cross-validation for Axitinib on transcriptome and proteome datasets are shown in Fig. 2D.

The inherent label imbalance in both transcriptomic and proteomic datasets was reflected in the precision scores. For the transcriptomic dataset, Precision (Resistance) reached 0.85 and Precision (Sensitive) 0.79 with Gradient Boosting. In the proteomic dataset, XGBoost yielded Precision (Resistance) of 0.84 and Precision (Sensitive) of 0.80. Recall values were also high: 0.90 (Resistance) and 0.69 (Sensitive) for transcriptomics, and 0.91 (Resistance) and 0.67 (Sensitive) for proteomics. Although the class imbalance can lower the balanced MCC, the strong precision and recall scores confirm the models’ effectiveness in identifying resistant cases.

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves for the top-performing models—Gradient Boosting for transcriptomic and XGBoost for proteomics are presented in Fig. 2E. Both models were evaluated via fivefold cross-validation. The AUC scores for the transcriptomic dataset ranged from 0.77 to 0.81, with specific fold results: 0.80 (cv1), 0.80 (cv2), 0.81 (cv3), 0.77 (cv4), and 0.78 (cv5). The proteomic dataset showed similar performance with AUCs of 0.78 (cv1), 0.80 (cv2), 0.77 (cv3), 0.81 (cv4), and 0.79 (cv5). Despite slight variations across folds, both classifiers demonstrated consistent discriminatory power, indicating a strong ability to predict resistance patterns from transcriptomic and proteomic profiles.

Augmenting LIME-derived features with correlated transcripts and proteins

To deepen the biological insight into resistance mechanisms in Axitinib-treated cell lines, additional features highly correlated with the LIME-identified transcripts and proteins were incorporated. Specifically, transcripts and proteins exhibiting Pearson correlation coefficients greater than |0.8| with LIME-derived features were selected. To further refine this expanded set, a Levene test was applied to identify features with significantly different expression variances between resistant and sensitive groups.

Only the correlated features that also showed statistically significant expression differences (p value < 0.05) between the two groups were retained (Fig. 3C and D). These features, together with the original LIME-identified resistance mediators, were designated as the final feature set for downstream biological analyses (Supplementary Sheet 7).

Resistance Stratification via Transcriptomic and Proteomic Clustering

Hierarchical agglomerative clustering was performed independently on transcriptomic and proteomic datasets using the LIME-identified features combined with their highly correlated counterparts. The optimal number of clusters was determined to be two, based on silhouette index values of 0.50 for transcriptomics and 0.37 for proteomics (Fig. 4A and B). Reinforced evidence for cluster-specific resistance features via statistical analyses

To identify transcripts and proteins contributing to resistance within each cluster, two non-parametric statistical tests were applied: the Levene test to assess intra-cluster variance and the Mann–Whitney U test to evaluate differences in mean expression levels between clusters.

This analysis identified 140 cluster-specific features associated with resistance in Cluster 1 and 207 in Cluster 2, with an additional 68 features shared across both clusters (Fig. 5D and E, Supplementary Sheet 9). The larger number of discriminative features in Cluster 2 supports the hypothesis of greater molecular heterogeneity and diverse resistance mechanisms, consistent with the broader tissue origins of these cell lines. In contrast, the relatively smaller and more cohesive Cluster 1 highlights a tissue-specific and potentially more uniform resistance profile, particularly in blood-derived malignancies.

Discussion

This study presents a robust and interpretable machine learning framework to identify key molecular drivers of drug resistance in cancer cell lines, with a particular focus on Axitinib. Given the comparable mutational landscapes across the resistant and sensitive cell lines, including similar mutation types and burdens, resistance could not be attributed to genomic alterations alone and instead appears to be driven by differential gene and protein expression. By leveraging transcriptomic and proteomic datasets curated from the Cell Model Passports in tandem to the drug response data derived from GDSC and employing the Boruta algorithm for feature selection, we achieved substantial dimensionality reduction without compromising predictive performance. Consistently high classifier scores across multiple evaluation metrics, particularly from XGBoost and Gradient Boosting classifiers, affirmed the robustness of the selected features. The use of LIME explainable AI enabled instance-level interpretation, revealing transcripts and proteins most predictive of resistance. These were further enriched by incorporating highly correlated features that also exhibited significant expression differences between resistant and sensitive lines. Clustering analyses based on these refined features stratified resistant cell lines into two distinct groups across both omics layers, reinforcing the coherence of the feature selection pipeline. Notably, these clusters revealed tissue-specific resistance patterns, with blood-derived lines forming a uniform group distinct from the more heterogeneous solid tumors. As detailed below, this integrative approach highlighted biologically meaningful mediators of resistance, which were used to elucidate possible resistance mechanisms driven by these features, offering both reassurance of methodological rigor and a foundation for future experimental validation and therapeutic exploration.

Examining the role of VEGFR mutations and expression in Axitinib resistance

The vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling axis is central to angiogenesis, involving a family of ligands: VEGF-A, VEGF-B, VEGF-C and VEGF-D [33] with three primary receptor tyrosine kinases: VEGFR-1 (FLT-1), VEGFR-2 (KDR/FLK-1), and VEGFR-3 (FLT-4) [34]. These receptors are predominantly expressed on endothelial cells and share a conserved structure comprising seven extracellular immunoglobulin-like domains, a single transmembrane domain, and an intracellular split tyrosine kinase domain [35]. VEGF-A binds both FLT-1 and KDR, with KDR mediating most of the angiogenic responses, including endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and survival [35]. VEGFR-1, despite its higher affinity for VEGF-A, exhibits weaker kinase activity and often functions as a decoy receptor, modulating KDR signaling [36–38]. VEGF-B exclusively binds FLT-1, influencing monocyte migration and other processes [35]. VEGF-C and VEGF-D primarily interact with FLT-4 [35], promoting lymphangiogenesis [39], but can also activate VEGFR-2 under certain conditions [35], implying KDR to be the critical VEGFR receptor for mediating angiogenesis. Axitinib is a selective inhibitor of FLT-1, KDR, and FLT-4, with half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC₅₀) of 0.1 nM, 0.2 nM, and 0.2 nM, respectively [23].

To better understand potential mechanisms behind Axitinib resistance, we examined mutation profiles of VEGF ligands and receptors in resistant and sensitive cell lines to identify any significant alteration that could affect Axitinib binding and its inhibitory effects. We also assessed the baseline expression levels of the receptors and their natural ligands in the resistant and sensitive cell lines to determine whether there is an inherent consistent difference in the expression level that contributes to the resistance towards Axitinib.

All observed mutations in VEGF ligands and receptors across the analyzed cell lines were systematically categorized based on their predicted functional consequences. Essential splice site, frameshift, nonsense, and splice-region mutations were classified as likely loss-of-function variants due to their potential to truncate the encoded protein or disrupt critical RNA processing mechanisms. In contrast, mutations located in downstream, upstream, and intronic regions were predominantly considered neutral, as they are less likely to directly affect protein-coding sequences. However, potential regulatory impacts of these non-coding mutations on gene expression or splicing efficiency cannot be entirely excluded. Variants within the 3′ and 5′ untranslated regions (UTRs) were categorized as having either neutral or loss-of-function effects, given their roles in modulating mRNA stability, localization, and translational efficiency. In-frame insertions/deletions and missense mutations were categorized as having uncertain consequences, as their impact depends on the specific amino acid changes and the structural and functional context within the affected protein domains. Comparative analysis of the mutational profiles of VEGF receptors and ligands, as summarized in Fig. 6, revealed no consistent pattern or recurrent mutations that could clearly explain the differences in resistance or sensitivity among the cell lines. Notably, the majority of receptor mutations (~ 40%) were intronic and predicted to have no direct functional consequence. Less than 2% of mutations affected splice sites in VEGF receptors across both resistant and sensitive cell lines. Approximately 10% of Axitinib-resistant cell lines exhibited missense mutations in VEGF receptors. For further analysis, particular focus was placed on KDR, given that its downstream signaling pathways are central to angiogenesis. Structural studies have elucidated the binding interactions of Axitinib with the receptors. In KDR, Axitinib binds within the ATP-binding cleft of the kinase domain [40], stabilizing the receptor in an inactive conformation, thus highlighting the importance of the kinase domain in Axitinib’s mechanism of action. Notably, several mutations observed in the kinase domain of KDR, such as p.I915V, p.I915T, p.S925F, p.K939R, p.G942E, p.R944Q, p.R946H, p.D958Y, p.E979G, p.P992S, p.H1004N, p.R1032Q, p.S1037L, p.V1041L, p.A1065T, p.R1066C, p.R1118Q, p.R1126S, p.G1145R, and p.L1156F, are located in close proximity to or within the ATP-binding pocket and activation loop regions critical for Axitinib binding. Mutations in these areas could potentially alter the conformation of the kinase domain, affecting Axitinib’s binding affinity and leading to reduced drug efficacy or resistance in the ~ 10% of the resistant cell lines.

Interestingly, transcript expression analyses of VEGF ligands and receptors revealed statistically significant differences (p value < 0.05) in multiple transcripts between resistant and sensitive cell lines. Elevated expression of KDR was observed in Axitinib-resistant lines, while the expression of certain ligands, such as VEGFA and VEGFC, also demonstrated significant upregulation. This suggests that alterations in the direct target of Axitinib or its ligands are unlikely to be the primary contributors to resistance, and therefore, resistance may be driven by other mechanisms beyond simple alterations in these key genes.

Dissecting diverse mechanisms of resistance in Axitinib-resistant cell lines

Resistance-associated features identified through the machine learning framework were further examined for their distribution across the two transcriptomic-proteomic clusters. Some features showed consistent expression across both clusters, suggesting a shared role in mediating resistance, while others exhibited low variance and high mean expression in only one cluster, indicating cluster-specific relevance.

Functional enrichment of these features using KEGG pathway mapping revealed their involvement across diverse biological categories, including metabolism, cellular maintenance, signal transduction, cancer pathways, systemic diseases, viral and microbial infections, physiology, hormone-regulated functions, and addiction-related pathways. This broad distribution underscores the multifaceted nature of resistance mechanisms.

Resistance frequently arises through perturbations in intracellular signaling cascades, which in turn modulate transcription factor activity. These transcriptional changes lead to altered expression of downstream effector proteins, reprogramming cellular metabolism and maintenance to enhance survival under drug pressure. Intriguingly, some viral proteins share sequence homology with endogenous growth factors; their aberrant expression during infection can mimic growth factor signaling, potentially enhancing tumorigenic signaling and contributing to resistance.

Together, these findings illuminate the complex, context-dependent interplay of signaling disruption, metabolic adaptation, and potentially viral mimicry that underlies diverse resistance strategies in Axitinib-resistant cancer cell lines.

Mechanosensing and ECM stiffness

The tumor microenvironment (TME) exerts a significant influence on drug resistance through mechanical stress and ECM remodeling. During tumor progression, mechanical alterations in the TME activate mechano-transduction programs that drive tumor proliferation and therapeutic resistance.

Cells sense these mechanical cues, such as stiffness, strain, and topography, through specialized proteins, including proteoglycans, glycoproteins, and adhesion molecules [41]. Our analysis identified several key mechanosensors and ECM components significantly upregulated in both clusters, particularly in Cluster 2. These include – (i) Collagens: COL6A2, COL6A3, (ii) Laminins, Fibrinogen, Fibronectin, Vitronectin: LAMB3, F5, FNDC3B, VTN (iii) Adhesion Molecules: CAV1, CAV2, SDC4, NECTIN4, (iv) Integrins and GPCRs: ITGA3, ITGB1, ITGB5, ITGAV, GPRC5A, GNG12, LIF, TGFBI, (v) Mechano-transduction regulators: YAP, TAZ.

These mechanosensors regulate the activity of growth factor receptors (like EGFR, TGFβ and MET) that activate downstream signaling pathways such as PI3K-AKT, RAS-MAPK, FAK/Src/β-catenin, JAK-STAT, Wnt, FoxO, TNF, ErbB, and Hippo [42]. All of which were present in KEGG analysis. These cascades are further supported by KEGG pathway enrichment involving actin cytoskeleton reorganization, motor proteins, focal adhesion, and intercellular junctions. Activation of these signaling routes leads to enhanced cytoskeletal dynamics via ROCK/Rho and Myosin II (upregulated), promoting nuclear translocation of key transcription factors [43]. These events drive stemness, EMT, and survival in hypoxic environments, ultimately leading to resistance through four main mechanisms: (i) Enhanced drug efflux, (ii) Increased DNA repair and mutation tolerance, (iii) Sustained cell cycle progression, (iv) Evasion of apoptosis.

Mechanosensors have been directly implicated in resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., Axitinib) and other cancer therapeutics. For instance, Glioblastoma cells grown in HA-based stiff hydrogels were resistant to EGFR inhibitor erlotinib [44]; Lung cancer cells exposed to collagen-mediated integrin signaling showed resistance to multiple EGFR inhibitors [45]; Colorectal liver metastases exhibited reduced response to bevacizumab under increased ECM stiffness [46]; Breast cancer cells developed resistance to sorafenib and lapatinib via β1-integrin/JNK and YAP/TAZ signaling [47]; Melanoma cells showed BRAF inhibitor resistance via ECM-induced YAP/TAZ activation [48]. Specifically, for Axitinib, one study highlighted that inhibition of YAP1/TAZ in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), when combined with VEGF blockade, synergistically suppressed tumor angiogenesis, suggesting YAP1 as a major VEGF-independent pro-angiogenic regulator [49].

Apoptosis evasion

Apoptosis is tightly regulated by the balance between proapoptotic and antiapoptotic proteins, with a higher ratio of the former favoring cell death. Key signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt govern the expression of these apoptotic regulators [50]. Notably, increased expression of AKT (identified in our gene set) has been linked to resistance against chemotherapeutic agents including paclitaxel and cisplatin in ovarian cancer, and doxorubicin in gastric cancer, underscoring its role in suppressing chemotherapy-induced apoptosis. Furthermore, elevated levels of the antiapoptotic protein BCL2L1 (also present in our dataset) have been associated with poorer survival outcomes in ovarian cancer patients [51], reinforcing its contribution to therapy resistance through apoptosis evasion.

Metabolic manipulation to maintain cellular reductive agents and production of energy currency, ATP and GTP, independent of oxygen

Cluster 1 cell lines adapt to VEGF pathway inhibition by rewiring their metabolism. They boost de novo purine biosynthesis, which produces the essential nucleotides ATP and GTP, the energy currency of the cells. This shift allows the cells to maintain energy production without relying on the oxygen-dependent mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), helping them survive under hypoxic and oxidative stress conditions. This is evidenced by the observed over expression of PAICS (phosphoribosylaminoimidazole carboxylase and phosphoribosylaminoimidazolesuccinocarboxamide synthase) in cluster 1 cell lines, which catalyzes critical steps (steps 6 and 7) in purine biosynthesis [52], supporting this compensatory mechanism. Further, it has been previously demonstrated that Purine biosynthesis is upregulated when ETC members are inhibited [53]. However, this reliance on purine biosynthesis introduces redox stress due to an imbalance between nucleotide synthesis and salvage pathways [54], potentially increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and leading to DNA damage [55]. In response, cluster 1 cell lines show increased expression of genes involved in critical genome maintenance processes, as evidenced by enrichment in KEGG pathways such as DNA replication, homologous recombination, Fanconi anemia pathway, ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, Polycomb repressive complex, nucleotide excision repair, mismatch repair, and nonhomologous end-joining [Supplementary Sheet 11]. These pathways collectively contribute to maintaining genomic stability under stress conditions. Bypassing VEGF dependency via alternative growth receptor signaling

We also observed the upregulation of alternative growth-promoting receptors in Cluster 1, suggesting a potential bypass of VEGF dependency through lineage-specific signaling mechanisms.

Several genes highly expressed in these blood-derived cell lines are known to support immune cell development and proliferation: (i) TRAF3IP3, promotes the growth of B-cell and T-cell [58, 59], (ii) SPN (Sialophorin, also known as CD43), facilitates B-cell proliferation [60]; also implicated in angiogenesis in lung cancer [61], (iii) CD53, enhances B-cell growth through IL-7 signaling [62]. The elevated expression of these receptors indicates that Cluster 1 cells may compensate for VEGF pathway inhibition by engaging alternate growth signaling routes, ensuring continued proliferation via hematopoietic lineage-specific pathways.

Resistance mechanisms unique to Cluster 2

Analysis revealed 207 genes contributing to resistance in Cluster 2 cell lines. These resistance-associated genes were mapped to 218 KEGG pathways, of which 122 pathways were exclusive to Cluster 2 [Supplementary Sheet 12], reflecting the cluster’s broad tissue diversity.

Fatty acid and lipid signaling in proliferation, immune evasion, and inflammation

Features from Cluster 2 cell lines were enriched in several phospholipid and fatty acid metabolism pathways: including glycerophospholipid metabolism, fatty acid degradation, ether lipid metabolism, and arachidonic, linoleic, and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism.

A key gene across these pathways is PLAAT3 (phospholipase A and acyltransferase 3), identified as a critical resistance mediator in Cluster 2. This multifunctional enzyme exhibits phospholipase A1/A2, O-acyltransferase, and N-acyltransferase activities, contributing to membrane lipid remodeling. It plays a pivotal role in the N-acylphosphatidylethanolamine (NAPE) metabolic process, catalyzing the calcium-independent hydrolysis of fatty acids from glycerophospholipids and facilitating the formation of NAPE, a precursor of bioactive N-acylethanolamines (NAEs) [63–67]. These enzymatic activities promote the breakdown of membrane lipids and release fatty acids such as linoleic, alpha-linolenic, and arachidonic acid, which act as signaling molecules involved in immune modulation and inflammation.

Linoleic acid, for instance, suppresses anti-tumor T-cell responses by inhibiting TNF-α production and promoting apoptosis. Naïve T cells express E-FABP (epidermal fatty acid-binding protein), which mediates mitochondrial uptake of linoleic acid, triggering lipid peroxidation in peroxisomes and generating ROS, a process associated with chemical carcinogenesis [68]. These ROS can induce DNA damage and apoptosis in T cells, thus impairing immune surveillance [69]. Arachidonic acid, another hydrolyzed product, fuels inflammation by serving as a substrate for COX-2, leading to the production of PGE2 (prostaglandin E2) [70]. PGE2 not only promotes tumor-associated inflammation and progression but also acts as a ligand for TRP channels, particularly TRPV1, which modulates calcium signaling [71, 72]. Dysregulated calcium signaling is a hallmark of tumor growth and survival [73]. This pathway is further amplified by the overexpression of ITPR3 (inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor type 3), which facilitates calcium release into the endoplasmic reticulum and activates the phosphatidylinositol signaling pathway. This signaling cascade produces lipid messengers like phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate, which in turn activates the PI3K-Akt pathway, central to cell survival and proliferation [74, 75].

Metabolic and Signaling Changes Promoting EMT (Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition)

Inhibition of the VEGF-driven angiogenic pathway by Axitinib leads to oxygen deficiency, triggering a shift from O-linked to N-linked glycosylation in post-translational modifications. This metabolic adaptation not only reduces oxygen demand but also serves as a cellular signal for hypoxia, promoting physiological changes to survive under low-oxygen stress [76]. A key target of this altered glycosylation is E-cadherin, an epithelial marker crucial for maintaining cell–cell and cell–ECM adhesion [77]. In tumor cells, N-glycosylation of E-cadherin leads to the formation of complex branched glycans that promote its internalization into the cytoplasm, disrupting extracellular adhesion and weakening epithelial integrity [78]. This detachment from the ECM further amplifies hypoxia-induced signaling pathways, such as Hippo, HIF-1, and Hedgehog, thereby facilitating EMT and enhancing tumor invasiveness [79].

Cancer-associated cellular aging via hypoxia-linked glucose dysregulation

Several pathways upregulated in Cluster 2, including Glucagon signaling, Carbohydrate digestion and absorption, Insulin secretion, AMPK signaling, Longevity regulation (human and multispecies), and AGE-RAGE signaling in diabetic complications, suggest a hypoxia-driven metabolic shift resembling diabetic conditions. Hypoxia disrupts glucose homeostasis by stimulating gluconeogenesis via glucagon signaling and impairing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, which increases the AMP/ATP ratio and activates the AMPK pathway through LKB1 [80–84]. AMPK activation promotes insulin secretion, but prolonged activation can lead to insulin resistance [85]. Chronic hyperglycemia contributes to cellular aging and senescence across multiple tissues, as supported by the enrichment of longevity and AGE-RAGE-related pathways [86]. Senescent cells, a known hallmark of cancer, actively remodel the tumor microenvironment by secreting pro-growth factors and cytokines that stimulate neighboring cells to re-enter the cell cycle. This is further supported by the activation of Apelin, cAMP, and Thyroid hormone signaling pathways observed in Cluster 2.

This section highlights the multifaceted and cluster-specific mechanisms underlying Axitinib resistance in cancer cell lines. Shared resistance features converged on EMT, ECM remodeling, and mechano-transduction pathways, reinforcing their central role in therapy evasion. Cluster 1 displayed adaptations favoring redox balance, DNA repair, and hematopoietic lineage signaling, whereas Cluster 2 uniquely exploited lipid signaling and calcium-mediated immune modulation. Together, these findings underscore the heterogeneity of resistance strategies and lay the groundwork for context-specific therapeutic interventions.

Conclusion

Our multi-omics machine learning framework reveals that resistance to Axitinib, an anti-angiogenic drug, is not a uniform response but an adaptive, lineage-specific strategy. Surprisingly, blood-derived cancers, expected to be less reliant on angiogenesis, show the greatest sensitivity, driven by VEGF’s role as a growth factor rather than an oxygen supplier. In contrast, solid tumors resist via EMT, matrix stiffening, immune evasion, and senescence. This difference in the constraint perception by the pan-cancer cell lines was demonstrated by the Unsupervised clustering, the divergent strategies brought about by the resistant mediators, uncovered through explainable AI and classifier accuracy. The multi-omics ML-informed pipeline highlights how cancer cells evolve under distinct constraint pressures. By exposing resistance pathways like purine metabolism in hematologic malignancies and integrin-YAP signaling in solid tumors, our study not only decodes Axitinib resistance but also offers a scalable blueprint for understanding therapeutic failure across cancers.

While these findings provide a robust framework, they should be interpreted in the context of certain boundaries. Cell lines, though diverse and standardized, intrinsically cannot fully capture the complexity of patient tumors or the cross-talk with stromal and immune components. However, the use of a diverse pan-cancer cell line dataset (~ 1000 pan-cancer lines) helps mitigate this limitation by spanning multiple tissues and lineages, allowing us to observe secreted factors, immune-modulatory signals, and senescence-associated programs that echo microenvironmental pressures. Moreover, our analyses are computational; that converges with clinically established resistance mechanisms. However, further experimental validation, through perturbations, co-culture models, or patient-derived systems, will help us better understand and translate these insights into actionable therapeutic strategies.
