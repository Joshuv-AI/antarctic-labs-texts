Plasma cell‐free transcriptome profiling in blood plasma from chronic liver disease patients

ABSTRACT
Chronic liver disease (CLD) encompasses a wide range of hepatic disorders that progressively compromise liver function. To support the development of non-invasive diagnostic tools and improve patient stratification, we generated a high-throughput extracellular RNA sequencing dataset from blood plasma samples collected from patients at various stages of liver disease. Samples were obtained from two independent cohorts of 112 (cohort 1) and 489 (cohort 2) patients, and profiled using total RNA sequencing, resulting in 112 and 820 extracellular transcriptomic profiles for cohort 1 and 2, respectively. A median of 9,853 genes with counts >10 was detected in cohort 1, whereas for cohort 2 the median ranged from 5,889 to 6,998 across five sequencing runs. This dataset is provided with full documentation of the preprocessing analysis pipeline and accompanying metadata, enabling its use as an independent validation cohort for biomarker discovery and as a complementary resource for integrative multi-omics studies. The publicly available resource is intended to facilitate reproducibility and foster further research in liver disease diagnostics.

Background & Summary

Chronic liver disease (CLD) refers to a broad category of liver disorders characterized by progressive deterioration of liver function. It involves continuous cycles of inflammation, damage and regeneration of liver tissue over months or years, leading to fibrosis and cirrhosis1. The healing response to the inflammatory damage generates fibrotic tissue and therefore progressive loss of the functional liver architecture. Liver cirrhosis, the final stage of CLD, disrupts this architecture, by forming nodules and extracellular matrix deposits, which can lead to complications like hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and liver failure, often necessitating transplantation2. CLD is a major cause of morbidity and mortality globally3.

A 2019 review reported that cirrhosis accounted for 2.4% of all global deaths4,5. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the seventh most common cancer worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-related death, with approximately 832,000 deaths reported in 20206,7. Additionally, data from the European Cancer Information System show that in 2022, liver cancer was the thirteenth most frequently diagnosed cancer and the sixth leading cause of cancer mortality in the EU8. Apart from viral infections such as hepatitis B and C and long-term alcohol abuse, CLD may also be caused by metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)1,9. MASLD represents the predominant hepatic pathology in industrialized nations, contributing significantly to increased morbidity and mortality rates. Precise prevalence figures remain elusive due to the absence of specific and sensitive diagnostic tests10. In the general European population reported prevalence of MASLD ranges from 2% to 44%, whereas among individuals with type 2 diabetes estimates vary between 42.6% and 69.5%5. Characterized by an excessive hepatic accumulation of triglycerides and reactive lipid species11, MASLD is a progressive condition. It is often viewed as the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome, which impacts various organs, including the pancreas, kidneys, adipose tissue, and gut10. The prevalence of a sedentary lifestyle and a diet rich in fats has driven a global obesity epidemic, consequently leading to a rapid increase in MASLD prevalence. MASLD is categorized into ‘simple’ steatosis (MASL) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), formerly known as non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), the latter characterized by necro-inflammation and hepatocyte ballooning. MASLD is currently considered a dynamic disease, fluctuating between MASL and MASH over time12. However, if MASH persists, it leads to an increase in fibrosis and consequently cirrhosis11. Liver fibrosis is commonly classified into four stages of increasing severity (Brunt criteria13), where stage 4 is classified as cirrhosis and is strongly associated with liver-associated morbidity and mortality. Progressive cirrhosis leads to hepatic decompensation symptoms, including portal hypertension-related ascites, varices and hepatorenal syndrome, as well as complications related to liver dysfunction, such as jaundice, coagulopathy and encephalopathy, increasing the risk of HCC up to 40-fold and requiring liver transplantation14,15.

In light of the obesity epidemic there is an urgent need to develop methods for early diagnosis of liver disease. Therefore, efforts to find biomarkers for early diagnosis are crucial, as they could enable timely intervention, improve patient outcomes, and potentially reduce the burden of liver-related morbidity and mortality. The diagnosis and prognosis of MASLD largely depend on invasive procedures such as liver biopsy. Though, despite being considered the gold standard for evaluating disease severity, it presents notable challenges, including high costs, risks of complications, sampling errors, and limited accessibility16–18. Non-invasive imaging methods, such as ultrasound, CT, MRI, and transient elastography, have been introduced to reduce reliance on biopsies19–21. However, these techniques often require expensive equipment, are operator-dependent, and may still suffer from variability in results22. Blood-based biomarkers offer a promising alternative, providing a non-invasive, cost-effective, and easily accessible solution23. While panels like the NAFLD Fibrosis Score, which is based on 6 variables (age, BMI, IFG/diabetes, AST/ALT ratio, platelet count and albumin) are widely used, their accuracy is limited, often leaving a significant number of patients in an indeterminate diagnostic grey zone24,25. There is a growing need to refine these blood-based methods by identifying more specific and sensitive biomarkers, particularly through the analysis of circulating molecules like cell-free nucleic acids26. The presence of cell-free DNA and RNA in human blood has long been recognized27. These analytes, originating passively from apoptotic and necrotic cells and actively via vesicle-mediated cellular export, provide a unique opportunity to assess the health status of solid organs through circulating biomarkers28. Circulating cell-free RNA offers a powerful window into organ-specific phenotypic information, enabling global measurements of RNA species across multiple organs29. While blood plasma cell-free RNA has been extensively studied in contexts such as cancer and pregnancy, research on transcriptomic changes in MASLD is limited. Most MASLD transcriptomic studies have relied on microarray-based methods, which lack the depth provided by RNA sequencing and have been confined to small patient cohorts that do not fully represent the spectrum of disease progression—from healthy liver, through MASL and MASH, to advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis11. In context of the VIB Grand Challenges Liver Disease Project, aiming to overcome the main current diagnostic challenges in hepatology practice, we performed high-throughput cell-free RNA sequencing on blood plasma samples from two large patient cohorts of 112 and 489 individuals, capturing a broader range of transcriptomic profiles across different stages of liver disease in a more diverse and extensive population. This dataset can identify circulating biomarkers that could be more easily integrated into clinical practice, offering the potential for earlier diagnosis and more accessible monitoring of disease progression. Furthermore, transcriptomics data can be combined with other omics approaches—such as proteomics and metabolomics—to enhance the predictive power and accuracy of biomarker discovery.

Throughout this Background & Summary section we adopt the updated terminology metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), which replace the historical terms non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH). However, our cohorts and clinical diagnoses were originally recorded using the NAFLD/NASH framework, and these labels are therefore retained throughout the remainder of the manuscript when describing patient groups and clinical variables. Readers should interpret NAFLD and NASH in the cohort descriptions and figures as historical labels that broadly correspond to MASLD and MASH within the current nomenclature, acknowledging that the updated framework also introduces a distinct MetALD category (MASLD with increased alcohol intake), which was not retroactively distinguished in this dataset9.

Methods

Patient cohorts

Two independent cohorts of patients diagnosed with liver disease were sampled to collect blood plasma samples. Informed consent was obtained from each patient, and the study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Ghent University Hospital (2019/0442), the Ethics Committee Research UZ/KU Leuven (S62675) and the Committee for Medical Ethics UZA-UA (19/13/167). Cohort 1 (112 patients, 112 plasma samples) was collected retrospectively at the Antwerp University Hospital (Antwerp, Belgium). Cohort 2 includes 489 patients, 820 plasma samples and 821 transcriptome profiles, as one sample was sequenced twice. This cohort was prospectively collected at the Antwerp University Hospital (84 patients, 139 plasma samples), the Leuven University Hospital (145 patients, 208 plasma samples, Leuven, Belgium) and the Ghent University Hospital (260 patients, 473 plasma samples, Ghent, Belgium). Note that patients of cohort 1 were sampled at a single (baseline) time point, while for some patients of cohort 2, both baseline and follow-up blood samples were collected. The baseline time point refers to the first blood sample specifically collected for the VIB Grand Challenges Liver Disease Project. Follow-up samples were collected at least 6 weeks after the baseline time point collection. In summary, in cohort 2, 252 subjects provided only a baseline sample, and 229 subjects provided a baseline and at least one follow-up sample. More specifically, 149 subjects provided a single follow-up sample (PLA00277G was sequenced twice), 62 subjects provided 2 follow-up samples, 16 subjects provided 3 follow-up samples, and 2 subjects provided 4 follow-up samples. Moreover, among the 489 subjects of cohort 2, 8 individuals only had data from follow-up samples available, as baseline plasma samples or data were lost during the workflow. Of these, 2 subjects each provided 2 follow-up samples, while the remaining 6 subjects provided one follow-up sample each. Among the 229 subjects who provided at least one follow-up sample, the interval between the baseline and the first follow-up blood collection ranged from 58 to 805 days (median: 364 days, IQR: 194–456 days). Distribution: 0.6% < 6 weeks, 15.5% at 3–6 months, 38.5% at 6–12 months and 45.5% > 12 months.

Blood collection and liquid biopsy preparation

For cohort 1, blood was sampled in K2EDTA tubes and processed within 5 hours upon blood collection. Blood collection tubes were spun for 10 min at 3000 g at room temperature. The resulting plasma was stored at −80 °C. For cohort 2, blood was collected by applying a tourniquet and venipuncture in an elbow vein using a 21 gauge bore needle. A discard tube was used to collect the first 1 to 2 mL of blood, followed by removal of the tourniquet. Blood was drawn sequentially into 1 serum tube (BD Vacutainer SST II Advance tube), followed by 3 plasma tubes (BD Vacutainer K2EDTA tubes). All blood collection tubes were completely filled. The serum tube was used to prepare liquid biopsies for glycomics and metabolomics analyses of the Grand Challenge Liver Disease Project, the plasma tubes for proteomics, epigenomics and transcriptomics analyses of this project. Since this data descriptor only includes the transcriptomics data, only processing of the plasma tubes is described. Immediately after blood draw, tubes were gently inverted 10 times to mix the blood with the anticoagulant and stored at 4 °C if not processed immediately. Blood processing was proceeded within 4 hours after blood collection. Blood collection tubes were inverted 5 times before centrifugation using a swinging bucket centrifuge. In a first centrifugation step, blood collection tubes were spun for 10 min at 1,900 g at room temperature without applying a brake function. Starting from the top of the liquid, the obtained plasma from all 3 blood collection tubes was carefully transferred to a 50 mL polypropylene tube, leaving ±0.5 cm plasma above the buffy coat and not touching the buffy coat with the pipette tip. Plasma collection tubes were inverted 5 times and again centrifuged for 10 min at 1,900 g at room temperature without applying a brake function. Double-spun plasma was transferred to a new tube, leaving ±0.5 cm plasma above the platelet pellet and not touching the pellet with the pipette tip. Double-spun plasma was homogenized by inverting the tube 10 times, and aliquoted (4 mL) in Eppendorf DNA LoBind Tubes. Plasma aliquots were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen or isopentane, and stored at −80 °C.

RNA purification

For cohort 1, RNA purifications were performed using the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (Qiagen) and 200 µL plasma input per sample. Plasma was thawed on ice and 1,000 µL QIAzol Lysis Reagent was added to each sample. Samples were vortexed and incubated for 5 min at room temperature, followed by the addition of Sequin control spike-ins (2 µL of a 3,000-fold dilution of mix A (Garvan Institute of Medical Research)) and RC RNA purification Control (RC) spike-ins (2 µL of a 191 fM dilution; Integrated DNA Technologies)30–33. Subsequently, samples were vortexed and 200 µL chloroform was added, followed by vortexing of the lysates for 15 s. After a 2 min incubation at room temperature, samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 g at 4 °C. Next, 600 µL of the upper aqueous phase was transferred to a new collection tube on ice, to which 900 µL ethanol was pipetted. Samples were mixed by pipetting up and down, loaded (up to 700 µL) on a RNeasy MinElute spin column and centrifuged for 15 s at 10,000 g at room temperature. The flowthrough was discarded and loading and centrifugation repeated using the remainder of the samples. Afterwards, 700 µL RWT buffer was added to the column and samples were centrifuged for 15 s at 10,000 g. Flowthroughs were discarded and 500 µL RPE buffer was pipetted onto the columns, followed by centrifugation for 15 s at 10,000 g. Again, flowthroughs were discarded, and 500 µL 80% ethanol was loaded onto the columns. Samples were centrifuged for 2 min at 10,000 g, and afterwards, the columns were placed into a new collection tube (with open lid) and dried for 5 min at full speed (16,900 g). Finally, the columns were placed in a new collection tube, 14 µL RNase-free water (Sigma) was added to the centre of the column membrane, and RNA was eluted by centrifugation for 1 min at full speed. Eluates were stored at −80 °C. For cohort 2, RNA purifications were performed using the iCatcher Circulating cfDNA/cfRNA 2000 kit (CatchGene) and 2,000 µL plasma input per sample, in combination with the iCatcher 12 Automated Nucleic Acid Purifcation System (CatchGene). For samples with insufficient plasma volume, the plasma input was diluted using 1x phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a total volume of 2,000 µL. The manufacturer’s protocol was followed with minor modifications; the sample pretreatment described in the manual was not performed and samples were thawed on ice or at room temperature. For each sample, a total of 8.47 µL Sequin control spike-ins (of a 3,000-fold dilution of mix B) was added to the lysis buffer (2,500 µL) in well 2 of the cartridge, of which 2000 µL was transferred to the sample tube during RNA purification. Samples were eluted in 30 µL. RNA eluates were stored at −80 °C.

DNase treatment

gDNA removal of RNA samples was performed using HL-dsDNase (ArcticZymes, 70800-202) and Heat & Run 10X Reaction Buffer (ArcticZymes, 66001). For cohort 1, 2 µL External RNA Control Consortium (ERCC) spike-in controls (of a 1/25 000 dilution; ThermoFisher Scientific), 2 µL Library Prep Control (LP) spike-in controls (of a 34 fM dilution; IDT)33,34, 1 µL HL-dsDNase and 1.6 µL reaction buffer were added to 12 µL RNA eluate, and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by 5 min at 55 °C. For cohort 2, 0.2 µL External RNA Control Consortium (ERCC) spike-in controls (of a 1/30,000 dilution), 1 µL HL-dsDNase and 0.8 µL reaction buffer were added to 8 µL RNA eluate, and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by 5 min at 55 °C.

Total RNA library preparation and sequencing

Total RNA libraries were prepared starting from 8 µL (for cohort 1) or 4 µL (for cohort 2) DNase-treated RNA using the SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 - Pico Input Mammalian (Takara Bio), according to the manufacturer’s manual with minor modifications. Prior to first strand cDNA synthesis, RNA was fragmented for 2 min at 94 °C. AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences) were used for purification of the RNA sequencing libraries. During final library amplification, 16 PCR cycles were performed. In the final RNA sequencing library purification step, 20 µL Tris Buffer was used to resuspend the beads. Library size distributions were determined using Fragment Analyzer (Agilent Technologies) smear analysis in the 200 to 1,000 bp range. Library quantification was performed using the KAPA Library quantification Kit (Kapa Biosystems) and libraries were pooled equimolarly. Final pools were quantified using Qubit. For cohort 1, the library was paired-end sequenced (2 × 100 nucleotides) on a NovaSeq 6000 instrument using a NovaSeq S2 kit (Illumina), 0.65 nM library loading concentration and 1% PhiX. For cohort 2, 5 library pools were prepared and paired-end sequenced (2 × 100 nucleotides) on a NovaSeq 6000 instrument using a NovaSeq S2 or S1 kit (Illumina), 0.65 nM library loading concentration and 2% PhiX. Raw sequencing data is available in the European Genome-Phenome archive34.

Data processing of total RNA sequencing data

Sequencing data were pre-processed using an in-house pipeline specifically designed for this type of libraries (see Code Availability)35. It starts with 3′-end trimming of adaptors and low-quality bases, together with filtering of reads based on length, discarding reads shorter than 20 nt using Cutadapt (v.3.5). Next, UMIs are extracted and trimmed from the reads using UMI-tools (v.1.0.1). Paired fastq files were then mapped against the reference genome GRCh38 using STAR (v.2.7.6), followed by the assessment of strand information using the RSeQC package (v.4.0.0) on the aligned bam files. Subsequently, UMI duplicates were removed using UMI-tools. Finally, quantification was done with HTSeq (v.0.11.3). Quality control checks were conducted after each processing step with FastQC (v.0.11.9).

Quality control of total RNA sequencing data

Several metrics were computed to evaluate the quality of the data generated. These included (1) the mean sequencing quality per base (after trimming) and the total number of reads remaining after trimming of low-quality bases and filtering of too-short reads, (2) strandedness, i.e. the percentage of reads aligned to the correct strand derived from RSeQC analysis, (3) mapping rate, i.e. the percentage of uniquely mapped reads derived from STAR alignment, (4) duplication, defined as the proportion of uniquely mapped reads remaining after the deduplication based on UMIs, (5) gene counts, representing the counts of protein coding genes, (6) ratio of endogenous counts and Sequin spike counts (Endo/Sequin), representing the relative RNA concentration in plasma, and (7) the ratio of endogenous counts and ERCC spike (Endo/ERCC) counts, representing the relative RNA concentration in the eluate. Outliers were defined as samples exhibiting values greater than 3 standards deviations from the mean for at least one metric and were subsequently removed for differential abundance analysis (z scores were calculated individually for both cohorts). At the gene level, quality control was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) to detect batch effects related to the sequencing run in cohort 2 samples. In short, gene counts were normalized and transformed using the variance stabilizing transformation implemented in the R package DESeq 2 (v.1.34) before conducting PCA. Finally, PCA plots were generated using the built-in function of this package.

Differential gene abundance analyses

For cohort 1 and baseline samples of cohort 2, the R package DESeq. 2 was utilized for normalization and differential gene abundance analysis. All differential abundance models included sex as a covariate (Cohort 1: ~Cirrhosis + Gender; Cohort 2: ~Risk + Sex). Technical variation across sequencing runs and collection sites was addressed through Sequin spike-in normalization and run-stratified outlier removal rather than model inclusion, as the spike-in size factors are designed to capture the dominant sources of inter-run technical variability. Age, BMI, and metabolic covariates were not included in the primary models as these variables co-vary with the clinical phenotypes under study; their inclusion is left to users depending on the research question. Subsequently, genes were ranked according to their differential abundance (log2 fold-change) to identify gene sets significantly differing between groups, employing the fgsea R package (v.1.20). Two collections from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) were used: the hallmark gene sets and the c5 ontology gene sets36. For feature selection, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied to the filtered and normalized gene counts using the LassoCV function from the Scikit-learn Python library (v.1.2.2, Python 3.10). A 5-fold cross-validation was performed to select the optimal parameters. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) area under the curve (AUC) score and accuracy were calculated on a separate test set, distinct from the training set.

RNA concentration calculation

Blood plasma RNA concentration was estimated as follows. First, the mass of the top abundant spike-in was calculated (R2_65 Sequin) based on the input concentration and volume of spike-in mix added to the sample. Then, the RNA concentration is calculated as the product of the R2_65 spike mass with total endogenous reads divided by the number of reads mapped to the R2_65 Sequin spike. The detailed calculation has been previously described31,37 and is available via GitHub38.

Gene abundance deconvolution analysis

Deconvolution of cohort 2 samples was conducted exclusively for baseline samples belonging to patients diagnosed with NAFLD or NASH, utilizing the method established by Vorperian et al.39. This approach employs support vector regression to decompose bulk tissue transcriptomes into fractional cell type contributions, demonstrating efficacy in deconvolving cell types of origin within the plasma cell-free transcriptome. The regression was performed using the same published basis matrix derived from Tabula Sapiens version 1.0, with gene counts adjusted to counts per million (CPM) and normalized to the volume of plasma input. The hepatocyte signature score was calculated, first, as the aggregated sum of log-transformed counts per million and trimmed mean of M values (CPM-TMM) normalized hepatocyte-specific gene counts; and second using the single-sample gene signature scoring method included in the R package singscore (v.3.19). For both scores the same list of hepatocyte-specific genes identified in Vorperian et al.39 were used. Genes with less than 300 counts across samples were removed prior to the analysis.

Analysis of circRNAs in total RNA capture sequencing data

Raw sequencing data from plasma samples of cohort 1 were used to identify circRNAs based on back-splice junctions using the tool find_circ (v.1.2). Reads were trimmed with Cutadapt (v.1.18), clipping off the first three bases of R2 reads. Low-quality reads were removed with a custom script, retaining only reads where 80% of the read has a Phred quality score of at least 20 and duplicates were removed with Clumpify BBMap (v.38.26). find_circ functions were used according to the author’s instructions. Briefly, prior to run the unmapped2anchors.py script, Bowtie2 (v.2.3.4) and Samtools (v.1.7) were used to map reads against the reference genome, keeping unmapped reads contiguously to look for splice-junctions. Finally, the script find_circ.py was used to screen for spliced reads, from linear or head-to-tail (circular) splicing40 (see Code Availability). DESeq 2 was used to perform differential abundance analysis, analogously to what was done for total RNA data.

Technical Validation

Again, readers should be aware that NAFLD and NASH terms broadly correspond to MASLD and MASH under current nomenclature. In addition, note that cohort 1 and cohort 2 differ in plasma input volume (200 µL vs 2,000 µL) and RNA extraction protocol (miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit vs iCatcher Circulating cfDNA/cfRNA 2000 Kit), resulting in cohort 1 libraries showing higher complexity and lower duplication, whereas cohort 2 libraries have lower complexity and higher duplication. These are technical rather than biological differences33 and are largely handled by spike-in–based normalization, but they imply that joint analyses should treat the cohorts as two independent data sources.

Quality Control of total RNA sequencing data

Mean sequencing quality and number of reads

The mean sequencing quality per base (after trimming) for total RNA plasma samples exceeded 34 for all samples in both cohorts (Fig. 1a,b), indicating high data quality. In cohort 1, the median number of trimmed reads per sample was 27.29 million with a coefficient of variation (% cv) of 18.34%, with most libraries (111/112) yielding between approximately 14 and 37 million reads after trimming and quality filtering. However, one notable outlier, sample RNA015864, yielded only about 4.38 million reads, considerably lower than the typical range. In cohort 2, the trimmed read counts exhibited some variability across the five sequencing runs: run 581 had a median of 20.11 million reads (% cv 11.43%), run 582 a median of 18.73 million reads (% cv 43.56%), run 588 a median of 19.42 million reads (% cv 22.76%), run 590 a median of 20.01 million reads (% cv 10.10%), and run 598 a median of 24.00 million reads (% cv 11.92%). Although the majority of cohort 2 libraries (819/821) produced between 11 and 30 million trimmed reads, two extreme outliers were observed, sample RNA022990 with 129.8 million reads and sample RNA023230 with 71.6 million reads (Fig. 2a). Strandedness

In cohort 1, the median strandedness was 89.24% with a coefficient of variation (% cv) of 5.02%, underscoring consistent strand specificity across the dataset. Although the individual strandedness values in this group spanned from 51.07% to 95.75%, nearly all samples (106/112) exceeded 85%, with the sole exception of RNA015864 (51.07%), which represents a notable low-end outlier. In contrast, cohort 2 samples, evaluated as separate runs, exhibited median strandedness values of 86.29% (run 581, % cv 8.89%), 88.26% (run 582, % cv 6.61%), 88.50% (run 588, % cv 6.32%), 88.54% (run 590, % cv 8.75%), and 87.21% (run 598, % cv 8.43%). Despite a few isolated deviations, including RNA023122 from run 581 (28.91%) and RNA023498 from run 590 (35.41%), the vast majority of cohort 2 samples (711/821) maintained strandedness above 80%, attesting to the robust technical reliability of the overall workflow (Fig. 2b)42.

Mapping rate

In cohort 1, the median uniquely mapped read percentage was 31.97% with a coefficient of variation (% cv) of 25.29%, indicating moderate variability across the dataset. Notably, two samples, RNA015864 and RNA015855, deviated markedly from this central tendency, exhibiting strikingly low mapping rates of approximately 2.5% and 4.9%, respectively. In contrast, cohort 2 samples demonstrated considerably higher mapping consistency. Specifically, the median percentages for the five sequencing runs were 59.00% (% cv 16.41%) for run 581, 58.25% (% cv 22.31%) for run 582, 58.66% (% cv 15.89%) for run 588, 59.00% (% cv 13.82%) for run 590, and 58.34% (% cv 16.16%) for run 598. Despite a few isolated lower mapping instances, such as RNA023034 from run 581 with a mapping rate of 6.4% and RNA022859 from run 582 with 8.4%, the overall results from cohort 2 underscore the robustness and high technical consistency of the alignment process (Fig. 2c)42.

Duplication

Due to the inherently low exRNA input, high duplication levels are expected, resulting in a low percentage of uniquely mapped reads remaining after UMI‐based deduplication. In cohort 1 samples, the median percentage of reads retained was 64.65% with a % cv of 11.74%, with the vast majority (105/112) falling between approximately 55% and 83% (overall range: 23.76%–83.00%). Cohort 2 samples exhibited a broader range of retained reads (Fig. 2d), with run 581 at 27.88% (% cv 44.29%), run 582 at 25.13% (% cv 40.50%), run 588 at 18.47% (% cv 47.06%), run 590 at 17.14% (% cv 44.00%), and run 598 at 19.06% (% cv 41.29%) (Fig. 2d)42. These findings, underscore the robustness of our approach despite the challenges posed by minimal exRNA input.

Gene count

The number of protein‐coding genes with unique (non-duplicated) counts above 10 is a key indicator of library complexity and overall data quality. In the cohort 1 samples, gene detection was robust, with a median of 9,853 genes and a % cv of 12.28%. Aside from one marked outlier (RNA015864, 692 genes), most libraries (108/112) captured between approximately 8,000 and 11,000 genes. In contrast, cohort 2 samples showed a broader range across sequencing runs (Fig. 2e). Specifically, run 581 had a median of 6,953 genes (% cv 22.91%), run 582 a median of 6,998 (% cv 21.40%), run 588 a median of 5,889 (% cv 31.85%), run 590 a median of 5,999 (% cv 26.40%), and run 598 a median of 6,679 (% cv 24.07%). Notably, apart from three samples from run 588 (RNA023283, RNA023263, and RNA023313) that had fewer than 300 detected genes, the rest of cohort 2 samples (818/821) typically fell within a range from approximately 1,200 to 10,000 genes (Fig. 2e)42. These findings, despite a few exceptions, affirm that the libraries possess satisfactory complexity and overall high data quality.

Endogenous-to-sequin ratio

For cohort 1, the endogenous-to-sequin ratio exhibited a median of 197.26 with a coefficient of variation (% cv) of 228.45%, reflecting notable variability across samples. Ratios ranged from approximately 17 to 7,212.25, with sample RNA015864 reaching the highest value. Aside from this and a few other outliers, most samples (76/112) displayed ratios between 100 to 800 that indicate reproducible RNA extraction and quantification (Fig. 2f). In cohort 2, the distribution of endogenous-to-sequin ratios varied across the five sequencing runs: run 581 had a median of 8.26 (% cv 1,364.86%), run 582 a median of 9.05 (% cv 80.75%), run 588 a median of 7.70 (% cv 82.48%), run 590 a median of 5.70 (% cv 102.99%), and run 598 a median of 8.99 (% cv 60.54%). Notable exceptions included an extraordinarily high ratio in run 581 (sample RNA023139, approximately 987,873) and an elevated value in the same run (RNA023122, approximately 147.5), which contributed to the observed variability.

Endogenous-to-ERCC ratio

In cohort 1, the ratio of endogenous counts to ERCC spike counts demonstrated a median value of 367.77 with a % cv of 137.45%, with ratios spanning from around 32.73 to 6,808.24, sample RNA015863 exhibited the highest value. Aside from a few outliers, most of the samples (99/112) displayed ratios between 100 to 1,800, suggesting that RNA extraction and quantification were largely reproducible (Fig. 2f,g). In cohort 2, the Endo/ERCC ratios varied by sequencing run, with medians of 8,827.68 (% cv 160.97%) in run 581, 11,842.03 (% cv 67.71%) in run 582, 7,644.19 (% cv 69.38%) in run 588, 7,241.90 (% cv 112.20%) in run 590, and 9166.67 (% cv 84.68%) in run 598. A few outliers skewed the distribution, such as RNA023122 in run 581 (≈243,139) and RNA023460 in run 590 (≈102,118), while others, notably RNA023431 (≈519) and RNA023032 (≈823), showed abnormally low ratios. These deviations show the potential influence of technical or biological variability and underscore the importance of removing outliers prior to perform statistical analysis (Fig. 2g).

For cohort 2, the statistically significant differences in Endo/Sequin and Endo/ERCC ratios observed across sequencing runs (Endo/Sequin: H = 46.93, p-value = 1.6 × 10−9; Endo/ERCC: H = 69.54, p-value = 2.8 × 10−14) and across hospitals of plasma collection (Endo/Sequin: H = 89.97, p-value = 2.9 × 10−20; Endo/ERCC: H = 134.72, p-value = 5.6 × 10−30) reflect pre-analytical differences in plasma handling between collection sites, not differences in the RNA extraction protocol, which was performed centrally in a single laboratory for all cohort 2 samples. Stratification by pre-analytical metadata identified the RNA isolation instrument (two iCatcher units; Endo/ERCC: H = 41.95, p-value = 9.4 × 10−11), plasma defrosting temperature (Endo/ERCC: H = 33.22, p-value = 8.2 × 10−9), and plasma input volume (Endo/Sequin: H = 29.47, p-value = 1.8 × 10−6) as significant contributors. Because each hospital contributed samples predominantly processed under a specific combination of these conditions, run and hospital effects are partially confounded. Importantly, these factors are unrelated to sample disease status, and the spike-in–based normalization applied here was designed to absorb differences in absolute RNA yield arising from such pre-analytical sources. For integrative analyses, the full pre-analytical metadata, including hospital, sequencing run, RNA isolation batch, instrument, and defrosting temperature are available in Zenodo42 to enable covariate modelling or batch correction.

Outliers were defined as samples with a z-score exceeding ±3 for at least one of the seven QC metrics described above, with z-scores computed independently per cohort (and per sequencing run for cohort 2). Based on this criterion, 5 samples (4.5%) from cohort 1 and 41 samples (5%) from cohort 2 were removed prior to differential abundance analysis. In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) using vst-normalized gene counts of cohort 2 samples showed no batch effect related to the sequencing run (Fig. 2h). Overall, even though cohort 2 exhibited run-specific variability in metrics, the quality control analysis demonstrated high-quality sequencing data for the great majority of the samples in both cohorts.

Finally, it should be noted that the two cohorts are not technically equivalent and should be treated as independent datasets. The differences in plasma input volume (200 µL vs 2,000 µL), RNA extraction kit (miRNeasy vs iCatcher), and resulting library complexity (median ~9,853 genes in cohort 1 vs ~5,889–6,998 genes in cohort 2) mean that direct cross-cohort comparison of gene-level counts is not appropriate without explicit batch correction. Each cohort was therefore analysed independently throughout this study. Cross-cohort analyses are possible but require the use of the provided metadata covariates (extraction kit, sequencing run, hospital) in an appropriate batch-correction framework.

Total RNA in plasma

To evaluate the technical validity of the plasma RNA measurements, we performed differential gene abundance and gene set enrichment analyses across predefined clinical groups in both cohorts, including comparisons by NAFLD status, presence versus absence of cirrhosis, and fibrosis stage. These analyses were used solely to assess whether the datasets recover known liver disease–related molecular patterns, rather than to draw new biological conclusions.

Across these contrasts, we consistently detected sets of differentially abundant genes and enriched pathways that have been previously reported in association with NAFLD, liver fibrosis and cirrhosis. The signals included genes and pathways related to hepatocyte injury, inflammation, coagulation and other established features of liver disease. The recurrence of such previously described liver disease–associated signatures across independent comparisons indicates that the plasma RNA profiles capture expected disease-related patterns and supports the validity of the datasets for downstream reuse.

Finally, we examined whether differences in global plasma RNA concentration could introduce technical bias. The skew observed in the volcano plots was consistent with measured differences in plasma total RNA concentration between contrasted groups. However, plasma total RNA concentration alone showed only modest discrimination between groups (with AUC values in the ~0.6–0.7 range across contrasts), indicating that group separation is not driven solely by bulk RNA yield.

All these technical validation analyses are summarized in the differential abundance volcano plots across all clinical contrasts (Fig. 3a–d), which illustrate that the plasma RNA datasets contain structured signal rather than noise in each comparison. Taken together, these technical validation analyses demonstrate that the datasets are consistent, reproduce liver disease-related patterns at gene, pathway and cell type levels, and therefore provide a reliable resource for downstream use by other researchers.

Usage Notes

All raw sequencing data are available through the European Genome-Phenome Archive (EGA) under accession number EGAD5000000077536. Data access requests should be submitted to the Data Access Committee (DAC) via the EGA study page (https://ega-archive.org/studies/EGAS50000000545). Prior to access, applicants must review and sign the Data Usage Agreement (DUA), which is publicly available through the associated Zenodo repository42. The DUA defines the conditions for secure handling and use of the controlled-access human data, including protection of participant confidentiality, restrictions on re-identification, and data security requirements.

For requesting institutions located outside the European Union/European Economic Area (EU/EEA), Ghent University additionally requires completion of a separate Data Transfer Agreement (DTA) and, where applicable, additional GDPR-related contractual clauses before access can be granted. Detailed access instructions and all required documentation are provided in the Zenodo repository42.

Independent validation cohort

This dataset serves as a valuable independent resource for validating biomarker signatures and predictive models generated in other studies, enhancing reproducibility and generalizability across patient populations. While both cohorts profile plasma cell-free RNA from chronic liver disease patients, they differ substantially in pre-analytical protocol, library complexity, and study design (retrospective single time-point vs. prospective longitudinal multi-center). They are therefore best regarded as complementary but not interchangeable resources: cohort 1 offers higher library depth and simpler batch structure, making it well-suited for exploratory discovery analyses; cohort 2 offers larger sample size, multi-center breadth, and longitudinal data, making it better suited for validation and biomarker robustness testing. Researchers performing integrative analyses should account for these technical differences using the metadata provided in Zenodo42.

Opportunities for longitudinal analysis

While our analyses are focused on baseline samples, the dataset includes follow-up samples for 80 patients within cohort 2, providing an opportunity for researchers to analyse temporal changes in plasma gene abundance. These data can be leveraged for studies on disease progression and biomarker discovery. In our analyses, we have concentrated on patients with NAFLD without HCC. The dataset has not been explored for identifying early biomarkers of HCC.

circRNA detection and tool comparison

Our analysis highlights the potential for circRNA detection using a single tool. However, it is well-established that circRNA detection varies significantly across tools, with each predicting different sets of circRNAs43. This dataset allows for comparative analysis using multiple circRNA detection algorithms to assess consistency and expand the catalogue of detected circRNAs. Additionally, this analysis was performed only on cohort 1 samples, leaving cohort 2 yet to be analysed.

Potential for advanced feature selection

In our analysis, we applied a single feature selection approach with basic hyperparameter optimization. However, alternative methods could yield more accurate models. Previous research44 has shown that combining feature selection with model selection strategies enhances the identification of optimal models and associated features.

Multi-omics integration

This dataset offers significant utility for integrative multi‐omics analyses in cases where complete omics profiles are not available. Recent methodological advances—such as the Integrate Any Omics framework45—demonstrate that deep learning frameworks can successfully integrate heterogeneous, and often incomplete, multi‐omics data. Such approaches leverage graph-based and embedding techniques to generate unified patient representations, even in the presence of missing modalities. Consequently, our dataset not only enables independent validation, but also can be seamlessly integrated into multi‐omics pipelines that address incomplete data, ultimately supporting more comprehensive analyses and improved patient stratification in precision medicine.

Biochemical and biopsy data availability

In addition to the transcriptomics data, matching biochemical data are available, providing further context and enabling a more detailed exploration of the molecular landscape. Among various metrics, the data include the variables required to calculate the fibrosis score. Additionally, matching biopsy data for some samples is available upon request.
