Lycopene ameliorates acrylamide-induced reproductive toxicity through enhancement in testicular antioxidant status and upregulation of androgen receptor expression in male Wistar rats
Authors: Great Oluwamayokun Adebodun, Tirenimioluwa Blessings Obabolujo, Praise Ebunoluwa Adesope, Emmanuella Onyeacholem, Yinusa Raji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcrylamide (ACR), a food contaminant, has been reported to exert adverse effects on the male reproductive system through increased oxidative stress and hormonal imbalance. This study aimed to investigate the ameliorative potentials of lycopene on reproductive parameters, oxidative stress, and expression of androgen receptors in acrylamide-induced reproductive toxicity in male Wistar rats.MethodsThirty-five male Wistar rats were randomly divided into seven groups (n = 5). Groups I-VII were orally administered distilled water, olive oil, acrylamide (10 mg/kg), lycopene (5 mg/kg), lycopene (20 mg/kg), acrylamide+lycopene (5 mg/kg), and acrylamide+lycopene (20 mg/kg), respectively, for 8 weeks. Following the end of the experiment, the animals were fasted overnight, weighed, and euthanized. Blood was collected, centrifuged, and serum obtained. The testes were collected and used for sperm, biochemical, and immunohistochemical analyses.ResultsThe results revealed that acrylamide administration decreased sperm quality, resulted in oxidative stress, hormonal imbalance, and reduced testicular androgen expression. However, lycopene significantly increased sperm count, viability, motility, serum testosterone, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and luteinizing hormone levels. Also, reduced testicular oxidative stress markers, decreased malondialdehyde (MDA), and enhanced antioxidant enzymes, increased superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase activities were observed following lycopene administration. Treatment with lycopene upregulated the expression of testicular androgen receptors.ConclusionThese findings demonstrated the ameliorative potential of lycopene in acrylamide-induced reproductive toxicity by improving testicular antioxidant status, hormonal balance, spermatogenesis, and testicular androgen receptor expression in male Wistar rats.

Introduction

Acrylamide (ACR) is a chemical compound formed in plant-based foods cooked at temperatures usually above 120℃, and also in industrial products; however, it is not naturally present in foods. Major sources of ACR include potato chips, coffee, bread, French fries, crackers, and cigarette smoke [1]. In overcooked foods, ACR is formed when asparagine, an amino acid, reacts with reducing sugars such as fructose and glucose in a reaction known as the Maillard reaction. Accumulation of ACR occurs more in foods cooked longer or at higher temperatures [2]. In adults, the average daily ACR exposure is estimated to be 0.3–1.9 µg/kg, while children have a more increased ACR intake [3]. It is metabolized either through oxidation to glycidamide by cytochrome P450 2E1 or conjugation with glutathione via glutathione S-transferase [4]. Various experimental studies have reported the neurotoxic, genotoxic, hepatotoxic, and nephrotoxic effects of ACR [5–7]. Acrylamide affects male fertility by negatively affecting sperm chromatin quality, sperm motility, morphology, and count [8]. It has also been shown to damage the Leydig cells and seminiferous tubules, thus reducing testosterone production and spermatogenesis [9]. Exposure to acrylamide also disrupts the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, thereby affecting the male reproductive hormones, and alterations in the hormonal profile can cause impaired spermatogenesis and diminished fertility [10]. The hallmark of ACR toxicity is oxidative stress resulting from an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and reduced antioxidant activity [11]. Due to the minimal levels of antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase, superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) in ACR toxicity, spermatozoa are vulnerable to oxidative stress [12]. Therefore, affecting the motility, acrosomal reaction, and fertilization ability. Although the level of ACR used in this study is higher than what is consumed by humans, reports show that accumulation and long-exposure to ACR still have toxic effects [13]. Administration of 10 mg/kg of ACR in rats for eight weeks resulted in decreased sperm count and motility, abnormal morphology, reduced testosterone levels, structural aberrations in the testes [14, 15].

Lycopene (LYP) is a naturally occurring carotenoid mostly found in red pigment vegetables and fruits, and is abundant in tomatoes and tomato products [16]. It is a strong antioxidant with a polyunsaturated double bond that alleviates oxidative stress through its free radical scavenging activities and the ability to prevent damage to vital cellular components [17, 18]. Lycopene has anti-apoptotic, anti-lipidemic, chemopreventive, nephroprotective, and anti-inflammatory properties [19, 20]. Several studies in various experimental models have reported the protective effect of LYP on testicular damage [21, 22].

Reports from animal studies suggest that LYP may maintain the antioxidant profile of testicular tissue exposed to organic pollutants, mycotoxins, or medications [23, 24]. However, the effect of lycopene on sperm parameters, expression of testicular androgen receptors, hormonal levels, oxidative stress, and antioxidant enzyme activities in reproductive toxicity caused by acrylamide administration has not been explored. Therefore, this study was designed to evaluate whether lycopene can ameliorate acrylamide-induced reproductive toxicity in male Wistar rats.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Acrylamide (Product Code: 11250) was obtained from Molychem, India, while Lycopene (Product Code: Lc22500) was obtained from Loba Chemie, India.

Animals and experimental design

Thirty-five (35) male Wistar rats weighing 150 ± 20 g were procured from the animal breeding facility at the University of Ibadan. The rats were housed in the animal house of the Department of Physiology in well-ventilated plastic cages. Unrestricted access to feed and water was given to the animals. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Ibadan Animal Care and Use Research Ethics Committee with assigned number UI-ACUREC/112–0824/15, and the animal handling and experimental protocol were reported according to ARRIVE guidelines. All attempts were made to reduce animal suffering through the use of humane anaesthesia and euthanasia procedures. Following two weeks of acclimatization, the animals were randomly divided into seven groups, each containing five rats:

Lycopene and acrylamide were dissolved in olive oil and distilled water, respectively. The animals were administered once daily for 8 weeks. The doses of acrylamide [14] and lycopene [16] were chosen based on the previously published studies in rats.

At the end of the 8-week administration period, the animals were anesthetized by injecting ketamine (30 mg/kg) and xylazine (3 mg/kg) intraperitoneally to minimize pain and distress and this was confirmed by absence of pedal withdrawal and corneal reflexes before sample collection. Blood samples were then obtained through cardiac puncture. Post-blood collection, the animals were euthanized by decapitation and the testicular tissues were excised. Relative testis and epididymis weight were calculated as the testis weight (g) divided by the final body weight (g), and expressed as g per 100 g body weight.

Sperm analysis

The testes were carefully exposed, and one was removed together with its epididymis. The caudal epididymis was immediately dissected out, the epididymal fluid was collected from the caudal part, and the following parameters were determined: sperm motility, viability (live/dead ratio), and sperm count. Immediately, motility of sperm was assessed by dropping the sperm suspension on a microscope slide that had been preheated to 37℃. The average percentage of motile sperms in ten randomly selected fields under 40X magnification was used to calculate the total motility [25]. Sperm count was done with the aid of a Neubauer’s counting chamber and expressed as millions/mL of suspension. Sperm viability was carried out microscopically using a procedure by Raji & Bolarinwa [26], with the Eosin-Negrosin stain. Penetration of stain into the sperm cells occurred due to damaged membrane, and stained sperm were considered as non-viable while unstained sperm cells are considered to be viable. Sperm morphology was assessed by making a thin layer of the sample on a sterile slide, fixing it in ethanol (95%), and air drying. Sequentially, immersion of the fixed slides was done for one minute in varying ethanol concentrations, Harris haematoxylin, G-6 orange stain, and EA-50 green stain. Microscopically, with 100X magnification, slides were assessed, and 200 sperm cells were examined on the slide [27]. Normal sperm cells were those with a hook-shaped head and no visible defects, whereas abnormal sperm cells were those with head or tail deformities (deformed head, detached head, bent, double, or coiled tail).

Determination of reproductive hormone levels

The blood samples obtained were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min to obtain the serum. The serum was used to assay for testosterone (Monobind Inc. U.S.A., Product code: 3725 − 300), FSH (Cat. No.: FS232F), and LH (Cat. No.: LH231F) (Calbiotech Inc. U.S.A) using appropriate ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Evaluation of lipid profile

Serum levels of total cholesterol (Cat. No.: 464228), triglycerides (Cat. No.: 622899), and HDL (Cat. No.: 269477) were measured using standard kits (Randox Laboratories Ltd, U.K.), while LDL was calculated using Friedwald’s equation [28].

Tissue preparation

The testes were homogenized in ice-cold phosphate buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and the homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min at 4℃ to obtain the supernatant.

Determination of Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) activity

The testicular activity of LDH was determined using standard kits (Biorex Diagnostic Ltd. U.K Product Code: BXC0243) following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Measurement of lipid peroxidation and antioxidant enzyme activities

The levels of MDA(Cat No.: E-BK-K025-S), SOD (Cat. No.: E-BK-K019-S), and CAT (Cat. No.: E-BK-K031-S) activities in testis supernatants were quantified using assay kits (Elabscience Biotechnology Inc. U.S.A). For the determination of GSH levels, this was determined according to the procedure by Beutler et al. [29]. GSH content was measured by its reaction with 5,5’-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), producing a yellow-colored product and the absorbance was recorded at 412 nm.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Following tissue processing, the slides were rehydrated for five minutes each using a succession of decreasing ethanol concentrations, after being placed in xylene for fifteen minutes to remove the wax. Retrieval of antigen was done using the Tinto antigen retriever system. The sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide. After blocking, the primary antibody, anti-androgen receptor (Cat.No.: PTE 66747-1-IG-20UL), was applied to the tissue sections and incubated at 20–25℃ incubated for 2 h. The sections were rinsed in PBS after the primary antibody was incubated, then biotinylated secondary antibody was added to the sections and incubated for forty-five minutes. For detection, sections were subsequently incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP), then DAB (3,3’-diaminobenzidine) was applied, which reacted with the enzyme to produce a brown precipitate at the site of the expression. The slides were counterstained with haematoxylin to provide contrast and for easy visualisation. For quantification, positive staining for androgen receptor was analysed using ImageJ software. The relative area covered by positive staining was measured for each section. Statistical comparisons between the groups was possible through the quantification.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. The data are expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (mean ± SEM). Data analysis was done using GraphPad Prism version 7.0. Significant differences between groups were analysed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post-hoc Tukey test for multiple comparison. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

There was a significant reduction in the body weight of the ACR group compared with the control and vehicle. There was a significant increase in the body weight of the LYP (5 mg/kg), LYP (20 mg/kg) & ACR + LYP (5 mg/kg) groups compared with the ACR group. There was no significant difference in the relative testes and epididymis weights of the ACR group compared with the control, vehicle, LYP groups, and ACR + LYP groups (Table 1).

Discussion

This current study investigated the ameliorative effect of lycopene against acrylamide-induced reproductive toxicity in male Wistar rats. In this study, there was a significant decrease in the body weight of the ACR group. This could be due to the fact that ACR can cause a reduction in appetite desire or a slower rate of gaining weight [30] and this finding is consistent with results by Radad et al. [31]. The significant increase in body weight following lycopene treatment could be attributed to its ability to alleviate oxidative stress [32].

Acrylamide (ACR) has been reported to decrease sperm viability, motility, and count, and increase abnormalities in sperm morphology, thereby impairing sperm quality. Acrylamide primarily targets the testis, the primary male organ for spermatogenesis and steroidogenesis, especially the Leydig and Sertoli cells [18, 33]. In this study, significantly decreased sperm count, motility, viability with increased total sperm abnormality were observed in the ACR group. This result conforms with studies by Moradi et al. [12], Abd Al Haleem et al. [14]. Following lycopene administration, sperm count, motility, viability, and sperm morphology were significantly improved and this is similar to studies by Zhao et al. [34], Qu et al. [22]. However, lycopene administration in a study by Bahrami et al. [35] did not improve sperm count and motility in di-(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate-damaged testes.

The proper functioning of the testes is under the regulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular (HPT) axis [2]. In this study, levels of LH, FSH and testosterone reduced significantly in ACR group. This aligns with findings by Gül et al. [36], Ajibare et al. [15], Baraka et al. [37]. However, Abd Al Haleem et al. [14] reported significantly increased FSH and LH levels following administration with 10 mg/kg ACR. ACR has been discovered to impair the Leydig cells’ viability, leading to reduced serum testosterone and rate of spermatogenesis [8, 9]. Following the administration of lycopene, the levels of testosterone, LH, and FSH increased significantly compared with the ACR group. This LYP’s effect on reproductive hormones is supported by Obukohwo et al. [38], Vakili et al. [39]. This protective role could be due to the presence of active components such as carotenoids in lycopene which have direct or indirect scavenging effects on cellular and organ oxidative damage [40].

The balance in lipid metabolism is essential for proper spermatogenesis, male testicular function and ultimately fertility [41, 42]. In this study, administration of ACR resulted in significantly increased triglycerides, serum total cholesterol, LDL, and reduced HDL and this is similar with results by Faridi et al. [43]. Disruption of lipid homeostasis can affect the synthesis and secretion of FSH, LH and largely testosterone. Karam et al. [44] reported elevated cholesterol and triglyceride levels; decreased sperm motility and serum testosterone levels and this is consistent with the results in this study. Following lycopene administration, the lipid profile was improved and this is consistent with the study by Greish et al. [45] where lycopene supplementation significantly reduced cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL levels and increased HDL level with significant improvement in sperm parameters and reproductive hormone levels.

Male fertility is affected by the binding of testosterone to its androgen receptors, which are present throughout the male reproductive system. In human testes, ARs are present in Leydig, myoid, pericyte, and Sertoli cells, and a lack of AR in Sertoli cells leads to reduced testosterone production and impaired spermatogenesis [46]. The expression of AR in the Sertoli cells is important in creating an effective blood-testis barrier necessary for successful spermatogenesis [47, 48]. Defects in AR may result in male infertility, as lower sperm quality has been linked to reduced AR expression [49]. There was a reduction in AR expression in the testes in the ACR group, and this conforms with the study by Gezer et al. [50]. However, treatment with lycopene increased AR expression in the testicular tissues.

Cellular oxidative stress and damage to DNA following acrylamide administration are caused by ROS generation, which consists of superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals [51]. In this study, MDA levels increased significantly, while activities of SOD and CAT and GSH levels decreased significantly in ACR group. This also was reported by Fiedan et al. [52]; Saleh et al. [53]. Lycopene administration significantly reduced testicular MDA levels while significantly increasing the testicular GSH levels, CAT and SOD activities. The ability of LYP to reduce MDA and increase CAT and SOD activities was also reported by Obukohwo et al. [38], where lycopene was administered to rats exposed to polystyrene microplastics.

Lactate dehydrogenase is released when tissue is damaged, and functions as a marker for common diseases and injuries [54]. Changes in the activity of LDH have been associated with Sertoli and spermatogenic germ cells damage and impaired testicular functions [14, 55]. Testicular activity of LDH increased significantly in the ACR group, suggesting testicular dysfunction. This increase in LDH following ACR administration was also reported by Mostafa-Hedeab et al. [30]. Lycopene significantly reduced the LDH levels, signifying its protective role against free radicals.

A limitation of this study is the sample size of five animals per group, therefore the use of a larger sample size would be recommended to confirm the observations and strengthen the conclusions drawn from the study. Further molecular investigations on inflammatory and apoptotic pathways to explore other mechanisms of action of lycopene in acrylamide-induced reproductive toxicity are essential. Also, studies investigating the expression of steroidogenic genes are needed to explore additional mechanisms.

Conclusion

In this study, abnormal spermatogenesis, reduced levels of reproductive hormones, disrupted lipid profile, decreased antioxidant enzyme activities and expressions of testicular androgen receptors were observed following acrylamide administration. Lycopene, however, exerted an ameliorative effect on ACR-induced reproductive toxicity in male rats by improving the sperm parameters, reproductive hormone levels, reducing testicular oxidative stress and upregulating expression of testicular androgen receptors. Further research can be carried out in diverse models and clinical studies to have a better understanding of the efficacy of lycopene in reproductive toxicity induced by acrylamide.
