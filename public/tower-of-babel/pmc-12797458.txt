Integrated proteomic and transcriptomic analyses of Citrus australasica in response to Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus infection
Authors: Xiaoxiao Wu, Chuanwu Chen, Haimeng Fang, Xuefeng Wang, Chongling Deng, Jing Feng, Qiuling Pang, Yang Han, Feng Zhang, Hongbing Li, Binghai Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCandidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas) is the most prevalent pathogen causing the globally prevalent citrus Huanglongbing (HLB). The citrus relative Citrus australasica F. Muell. shows tolerance to HLB. In this study, we grafted HLB-tolerant Ca (C. australasica) and HLB-susceptible Cs (C. sinensis) Osbeck branches onto healthy C. reticulate Blanco ‘Shatangju’ (SH) and CLas-infected (SI) rootstocks.ResultsTo understand HLB tolerance mechanisms in C. australasica, a comprehensive analysis of transcriptomic and proteomic data of leaves from CaSH, CaSI, CsSH, and CsSI was conducted. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between CaSI and CaSH were enriched in acridone alkaloid biosynthesis. In contrast, some DEGs between CsSI and CsSH were enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, and MAPK signaling pathway. Several differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between CaSI and CaSH were enriched in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, whereas several DEPs between CsSI and CsSH were enriched in amino acids biosynthesis. In response to CLas infection, several genes involved in amino acid metabolism pathway and citrate cycle pathway showed opposite trends in C. australasica and C. sinensis. Among these genes with opposite expression trends, it is noteworthy that in response to HLB, arogenate/prephenate dehydratase and phenylalanine-4-hydroxylase in phenylalanine and tyrosine biosynthesis remained stable in C. australasica. In addition, glutamate dehydrogenase and amino acid N-acetyltransferase, which are involved in arginine metabolism, were up-regulated in CLas-infected C. australasica. The expression levels of isocitrate dehydrogenase, malate dehydrogenase, and aconitate hydratase, which are involved in the citrate cycle, were higher in CaSI than in CsSI.ConclusionsThese data suggest that the stabilization of phenylalanine biosynthesis and the up-regulation of arginine biosynthesis contribute to HLB-tolerance of C. australasica, and that the changes in enzymes in the citrate cycle pathway and flow of carbon sources to arginine biosynthesis reduce the energy supply for HLB pathogens. These results provide new insights into the mechanism of HLB-tolerance in C. australasica, and provide a theoretical molecular basis for breeding HLB-tolerant citrus varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07783-1.Keywords: High-throughput sequencing, Huanglongbing, Citrus reticulate blanco ‘Shatangju’, Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus, Phenylalanine, Arginine

Introduction

Citrus fruits constitute the largest category of fruits in the world and have become China’s largest fruit industry in recent years. In China, citrus is cultivated in over 20 provinces with an area of approximately 2.5 million ha. Citrus production in China has increased yearly, reaching a new high in 2018 with 41.38 million tons [1–3]. Citrus Huanglongbing (HLB) or citrus greening is a devastating citrus disease worldwide [4]. It is caused by the vector-transmitted phloem-colonizing bacteria Candidatus Liberibacter spp.; Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas) is the most prevalent of the species causing HLB [4, 5]. HLB impacts citrus tree health, productivity and fruit quality [6]. Citrus plants suffering from HLB may exhibit symptoms such as leaf mottling, yellowing, zinc-deficient vein withering, dead branches, growth retardation, and inhibition of new root growth [7–9]. Owing to the HLB epidemic, >50 million citrus trees have been cut in Ganzhou City, Jiangxi Province [10]. The Asian citrus psyllid (ACP, Diaphorina citri Kuwayama) is the main transmission vector for HLB. Current management strategies to curb the spread and harm of HLB include using insecticides to control ACP and antibiotics application to suppress C. Liberibacter spp [11]. However, these approaches show limited efficacy and have raised concerns about environmental safety and pesticide resistance development.

Using innate immunity-related genes from existing tolerant or disease-resistant varieties of plants for plant protection is one of the most effective and environmentally friendly strategies against pathogenic infections [5]. Omics analysis has been widely used to analyze defense responses in host plants during pathogen-plant interactions. Orf et al. [12] analyzed the changes in the transcripts and compounds from the primary and secondary metabolism of Arabidopsis thaliana accessions Col-0 and C24 after infection with Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (Pst). Their results showed that the mechanisms of salicylic acid (SA)-dependent systemic acquired resistance are involved in the difference in Pst-resistance between Col-0 and C24, and arginine metabolism may contribute to the resistance. In A. thaliana, ~ 10% of defense-related SA is produced by the phenylalanine ammonia lyase pathway [12]. Arginine serves as a nitrogen storage and precursor of NO and polyamines, which play a role in defense mechanisms [13].

Although all commercially important citrus varieties are susceptible to HLB, some hybrids (e.g., US-942 and Sydney hybrid 72, Citrus virgata) show HLB tolerance [5, 14]. C. virgata is derived from C. australis × C. australasica. The C. australasica belongs to the Rutaceae family and is primarily grown in tropical rainforest areas. It is a compact shrub with dense foliage that flowers and fruits multiple times annually, making it suitable for cultivation in containers and valuable as a breeding parent for new cultivars [15]. As its strong drought tolerance, it can be used as a rootstock for breeding dwarf plants with drought tolerance [16]. In addition, the scions of C. australasica grafted onto ‘Rangpur’ lime (Citrus limonia Osbeck) have partial resistance to CLas [17].

Huang et al. [5] identified a novel stable antimicrobial peptide (SAMP) in C. australiasica, with antimicrobial and priming activity. SAMP induces systemic citrus defense responses to effectively inhibit infections of CLas in plants. SAMP injection suppresses CLas titer in HLB-susceptible C. macrophylla, C. sinensis, and C. limon trees, and can cause trees with declining health to recover [5]. At the genomic level, Nakandala et al. [18] analyzed the reference genome of C. australasica and identified genes related to disease resistance. In contrast, Liu et al. [19] analyzed the genomic complement of the HLB resistance gene in five citrus species, including C. australasica, revealing the evolutionary characteristics of C. australasica and potentially important hybrid breeding sites. To understand CLas tolerance mechanisms in C. australasica, Weber et al. [20] conducted a comprehensive analysis of transcriptomic data following a non-choice infection assay. In the study, HLB-free budwood of C. australasica clone and ‘Valencia’ sweet orange clone were grafted onto Swingle citrumelo rootstock, and one-year-old budded trees were grafted with CaLas-infected ‘Valencia’ sweet orange scions. Compared to CLas-infected HLB-susceptible ‘Valencia’ sweet orange, several differentially expressed genes (DEGs) belonging to cell wall, β-glucanase, proteolysis, R genes, signaling, redox state, peroxidases, glutathione-S-transferase, secondary metabolites, and pathogenesis-related proteins categories were identified in CLas-infected C. australasica. Therefore, C. australasica has evolved specific redox control systems to mitigate the reactive oxygen species and modulate plant defense responses. In addition, compared to noninfected and infected ‘Valencia’ sweet orange, some candidate genes responsible for the production of Cys-rich secretory proteins and Pathogenesis-related 1 (PR1-like) proteins that were highly upregulated in C. australasica [20]. Mahmoud et al. [21] found that transcript levels of PR1-like gene CaCAP2 increased by over 300-fold in C. australasica compared to ‘Valencia’ sweet orange upon infection with CLas. Another transcriptome analysis revealed that many of the DEGs between two CLas-infected pummelo × C. australasica (pummelo ×finger lime, PFL) hybrid siblings (PFL 1–11 and PFL 2–61) were associated with cell wall structure, redox homeostasis, and biotic stress responses. Compared with the hybrid PFL 1–11, key genes involved in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and plant hormones showed increased expression levels in the HLB-tolerant hybrid PFL 2–61 [22]. However, transcriptomic results vary depending on factors such as tissue type, genetic background, method of CLas inoculation, cultivation conditions, and time post-inoculation. The molecular mechanism of the systemic immune response in C. australiasica against CLas infection is poorly understood, especially the mechanism of tolerance to HLB when used as scions in other citrus fruits.

‘Shatangju’ mandarin (Citrus reticulate Blanco ‘Shatangju’) is a citrus variety in southern China. ‘Shatangju’ fruit is favored for its delicious taste, however, its plant is susceptible to HLB. Recently, HLB has seriously threatened the Shatangju industry [23]. The present study elucidated the potential mechanism underlying HLB tolerance in C. australasica. Here, we grafted branches of C. australasica and HLB-susceptible C. sinensis Osbeck to Shatangju (healthy and CLas-infected) and examined the transcriptome and proteome of scion leaves of healthy and infected CLas in grafted plants. The rootstocks and the graft materials used in this study were sourced from plant populations with relatively consistent and stable genetic backgrounds. The plant materials used for grafting were in a relatively consistent state, and a consistent grafting method was employed. When phenotypic differences began to appear on the C. sinensis and C. australasica grafted branches of the infected rootstocks, the proteomes and transcriptomes of scion leaves were detected. DEGs and differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between different groups were obtained, and key differential metabolic pathways between C. australasica and C. sinensis were identified by comparative analysis. These results can enhance our understanding of the tolerance mechanism of C. australasica to HLB, and provide a theoretical basis for cultivating HLB-tolerant ‘Shatangju’ mandarin using the HLB-tolerant C. australasica resources.

Results

Phenotypes of Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus inoculated with different materials

In the early stage of infection with citrus HLB, C. australasica and C. sinensis grew normally (Fig. 1A, D). Gradually, the phenotypes of C. australasica and C. sinensis have differed significantly. After 6 months, C. sinensis began to show symptoms of HLB, however, C. australasica greened normally and did not show HLB symptoms. Six months after infection, C. sinensis developed typical symptoms of citrus HLB, including yellowing of leaves and thinning and narrowing of the leaf structures (Fig. 1E, F). In contrast, C. australasica continued to exhibit normal growth without displaying any symptoms of HLB (Fig. 1B, C), which is consistent with the growth of the healthy control group. Transcriptome analysis of C. australasica tolerance to HLB

To obtain the gene expression profile of M. australasica resistance to HLB, we performed RNA-seq analysis of five biological replicate leaf samples from the four types of Citrus reticulata, and the samples were labeled as CaSI (C. australasica infected with CLas), CsSI (C. sinensis infected with CLas), CaSH (uninfected C. australasica), and CsSH (uninfected C. sinensis). The total raw and clean data (bp) for each sample ranged from 11,502,534,300 to 19,370,240,700 and 11,443,691,359 to 19,144,440,018, respectively (Table S1). More than 94.09% of the sequenced bases had a quality score of Q30 or higher. The total mapping rate of all samples to the reference genome (NCBI accession No. GCA_018105775.1) ranged between 71.82% and 94.73% (Table S1), indicating that the data were usable for subsequent analysis. DEGs between the comparison groups were analyzed with |log2FC|≥1 and FDR < 0.05. The results showed that the number of DEGs between the different grafting materials was generally higher than that in the rootstocks infect CLas or not (Fig. 2). Compared to grafting C. australasica onto rootstock infected with CLas, when healthy rootstock material was used for the grafting of C. australasica, the number of upregulated genes (1,843) was greater than that of downregulated genes (1,103; Fig. 2A, B, Table S2). However, the opposite situation occurs in the grafting experiment with C. sinensis (Fig. 2A, C, Table S3). Compared with grafting C. australasica on healthy rootstocks, grafting C. sinensis induced the most DEGs, with 10,847 genes, and the number of downregulated genes (6,462) was greater than the number of upregulated genes (4,385; Fig. 2A, D, Table S4); grafting on rootstocks infected with CLas showed a similar trend in DEGs (2,441 upregulated and 4,337 downregulated; Fig. 2A, E, Table S5).

Proteomics analysis of C. australasica tolerance to HLB

Proteomics could reflect the effects of post-transcriptional regulation. Therefore, we performed proteomics analysis using next generation label-free quantitative proteomics technology to obtain the protein profile in four groups and totally identified 8992 proteins, with approximately 6000–7500 proteins per sample (Table S6). The identified proteins were enriched in three GO categories: Molecular Function (activity and binding), Cellular Components (cell, cell parts, membranes, and organelles), and Biological Processes (cellular and metabolic processes) (Fig. S1A). In addition, they were most enriched in two KEGG categories, including Genetic Information Processing, and Metabolism (Fig. S1B). The two pathways with the most enriched proteins were metabolic pathways (ko01100) and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ko01110), which belong to the global and overview maps (Fig. S1B).

Overall, we identified, using threshold values of absolute log2 FC ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Fig. 4A), 2115 DEPs when comparing CaSH and CsSH (Table S7), 412 DEPs when comparing CaSI and CaSH (Table S8), 1752 DEPs when comparing CaSI and CsSI (Table S9), and 843 DEPs when comparing CsSI and CsSH samples (Table S10). DEP analysis using the KEGG databases showed that C. australasica grafted onto the uninfected and infected rootstock were significantly enriched phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), cyanoamino acid metabolism (ko00460), and pentose and glucuronate interconversions (ko00040) (Fig. 4B), whereas those of the grafted C. sinensis were enriched 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism (ko01210), protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (ko04141), phagosome (ko04145), biosynthesis of amino acids (ko01230), carotenoid biosynthesis (ko00906), valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis (ko00290) (Fig. 4C, p-value < 0.05). Overall, the DEPs in the different grafting treatments were significantly enriched in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ko01110), metabolic pathways (ko01100), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism (ko00630) (Fig. 4D, E; p < 0.05).

Proteomics and transcriptome co-expression analysis

To identify the key genes and pathways involved in disease tolerance in C. australasica, we conducted an intersection analysis using the DEGs and DEPs. First, we selected the genes or proteins that were differentially expressed between C. australasica and C. sinensis, so that the DEGs or DEPs identified in CsSH vs. CsSH-vs-CaSH or/and CsSI vs. CsSI-vs-CaSI were included. These selected DEGs and DEPs have different response patterns between the two varieties. Finally, 4411 DEGs and 763 DEPs were used for KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, indicating that all the DEGs and DEPs enriched the metabolism (Fig. 5C, D). Additionally, the HLB-tolerant DEGs in C. australasica were mainly focused on the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ko01110) and metabolic pathways (ko01100) (Fig. 5C), while the DEPs were significantly exhibited in metabolic pathways (ko01100), phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis (ko00400), alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism (ko00250) (Fig. 5D).

Differential regulation of amino acid metabolism between the tolerant and susceptible materials

Among the enriched pathways of DEGs and DEPs, amino acid metabolism was screened in both the transcriptome and proteome. In phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis pathway (ko00400), phenylalanine-4-hydroxylase (phhA), tyrosine aminotransferase, phenylalanine dehydrogenase, and cyclohexadieny/prephenate dehydrogenase had higher expression or abundance in C. australasica (CaSH and CaSI) than that in C. sinensis (CsSH and CsSI) (Fig. 6). Additionally, 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase (HPD) and gentisate 1,2-dioxygenase (E1.13.11.4) in tyrosine metabolism (ko00350) had a similar trend. Meanwhile, arogenate/prephenate dehydratase (ADT), chorismate, and pretyrosine dehydrogenase (TYRAAT) in phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis (ko00400), glutamate dehydrogenas (GLUD1_2) in arginine biosynthesis (ko00220), and phenylpyruvate tautomerase (MIF) in tyrosine metabolism (ko00350) had the highest expression and abundance level in non-CLas-infected C. sinensis (CsSH) (Fig. 6). The key enzymes glutamate dehydrogenase (gudB) and amino acid N-acetyltransferase (argJ) in arginine biosynthesis (ko00220) showed the lowest expression and abundance in CLas-infected with C. sinensis (CsSI) (Fig. 6). Finally, in the citrate cycle (ko00020), the expression of isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH3) was inhibited by HLB and had a higher expression and abundance level in C. australasica than that in C. sinensis; malate dehydrogenase (MDH1) showed a completely opposite trend; and aconitate hydratase (ACO) expression and abundance levels were inhibited by HLB in C. sinensis, and promoted in C. australasica (Fig. 6). The expression of key genes in this pathway was validated by qRT-PCR (Fig. S2), which showed high consistency between the transcriptome and qRT-PCR testing. These results indicated that different regulatory mechanisms may exist during grafting.

Discussion

HLB is a devastating citrus disease [5]. The present study found that C. reticulata plants grafted with C. australasica showed tolerance to HLB. C. australasica grafted onto CLas-infected C. reticulata Shatangju grew normally, whereas C. sinensis grafted onto CLas-infected C. reticulata ‘Shatangju’ showed patchy yellow leaves six months after grafting.

The possible genetic mechanisms of HLB symptoms in citrus trees have been discussed by several groups. Disorders in carbohydrate metabolism due to starch accumulation in leaves, response of plant hormones to stress, and activation of detoxification proteins are three potential mechanisms during CLas infection [20, 24]. The DEGs of C. sinensis grafted onto uninfected and infected rootstocks were enriched in processes that were previously widely assumed to be a response to CLas infection, including plant hormone signal transduction. In addition, DEGs were enriched in important immune pathways, plant-pathogen interaction, and MAPK signaling pathway-plant. Pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP)-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity are two layers of the immune system that have evolved in plants. PTI is mediated by pattern-recognition receptors that recognize PAMPs, whereas effector-triggered immunity is mediated by resistance proteins that recognize pathogenic effectors [25, 26]. During PTI, the MAPK cascade activates WRKY-type transcription factors and other key regulators of plant immunity. The MAPK cascade regulates plant immunity by activating defense-related genes [27, 28]. In response to CLas infection, the MAPK genes MPK3 and MKK9 were upregulated in rough lemons (C. jambhiri), whereas only MPK3 was upregulated in C. sinensis [29]. Upon CLas infection, WRKY transcription factors showed increased expression levels in the HLB-tolerant hybrid PFL 2–61 compared to that of the HLB-susceptible hybrid PFL 1–11 [22]. Although these immune responses were altered in C. sinensis following HLB treatment (Fig. 3B), no evidence was found that these immune-related pathways were activated. In the leaves of CsSI compared to that of CsSH, the MAPK genes MPK3/6, MKK4/5, and MKK9 were down regulated, whereas MPK8 was upregulated (Table S2-5). The effects of these changes in the immune-related pathways on HLB tolerance in C. sinensis require further investigation.

The DEGs of C. australasica grafted onto CLas-infected and uninfected rootstock were only enriched in acridone alkaloid biosynthesis (Fig. 3A). Acridone alkaloids are large cyclic conjugated compounds containing nitrogen that exhibit strong fluorescent properties. Acridone alkaloids from citrus plants and their derivatives exhibit various biological activities, such as anti-tumor, anti-viral, anti-allergic, and analgesic properties [30, 31]. However, the role of acridone alkaloid biosynthesis in plant immunity remains unknown, and its role in the response to HLB infection requires further study.

In the differential gene enrichment pathway, both DEGs and DEPs of C. australasica (CaSH and CaSI) and C. sinensis (CsSH and CsSI) were enriched in in amino acid metabolism and the citrate cycle (Fig. 6). However, some differential expression factors in these pathways had higher expression level in C. australasica. In contrast, others were relatively abundant in C. sinensis. Therefore, it was difficult to compare the overall status of these pathways in the two types of scions. In several HLB tolerant varieties, such as Carrizo citrange (× Citroncirus webberi) and Severinia buxifolia, the levels of most amino acids, especially those implicated in plant defense against pathogens, such as phenylalanine and tyrosine, were higher [32]. Phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan are products of the shikimate pathway [33]. Phenylalanine, the first precursor of the phenylpropanoid cycle, is involved in plant defense, structural support, and survival [34]. The phenylpropanoid pathway produces many important secondary metabolites involved in plant defense such as flavonoids, lignin, lignans, and hydroxycinnamic acid amides [35].

Both phenylalanine and tyrosine have been implicated in synthesizing plant phenolics [36]. Some tolerant Citrus spp. with high levels of phenolics exhibit high antioxidant capacity [37]. The phenolic synthesis pathway is also related to the formation of lignin and cell wall metabolism, enhancing structural defense mechanisms against pathogens [38]. Liao et al. [38] analyzed the transcriptional level responses of Ponkan Mandarin and Punctate Wampee to CLas infection, and determined Ponkan Mandarin primarily relies on pathways such as lignin synthesis and cell wall modification to defend against HLB. Upon CLas infection, the metabolic pathways of the phenolic substances phenylpropanoids and flavonoids were significantly upregulated in Ponkan Mandarin [38]. ADT catalyzes the last and rate-limiting step of the main phenylalanine biosynthesis pathway, whereas phhA is involved in the conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine [39, 40]. ADT expression in C. australasica was lower than that in healthy C. sinensis, whereas phhA expression in C. australasica was higher than that in C. sinensis (Fig. 6). However, compared to the grafted branches of the same species from healthy rootstocks, the expression levels of ADT and phhA remained relatively stable in CLas-infected C. australasica. In contrast, ADT was down-regulated and phhA was up-regulated in CLas-infected C. sinensis (Fig. 6). These data suggest a decreasing phenylalanine trend in C. sinensis following CLas infection. Conversely, the phenylalanine of C. australasica may be relatively stable, which is important for maintaining the HLB-tolerance of C. australasica (Fig. 7).

The arginine metabolism pathway in CLas-infected C. australasica showed an up-regulated trend. Compared with grafted branches of the same species from healthy rootstocks, the expression of several genes, including glnA, gudB, and argJ, was higher in C. australasica, whereas only glnA was higher in C. sinensis. Furthermore, the expression levels of gudB and argJ were higher in CLas-infected C. australasica than that in CLas-infected C. sinensis (Fig. 6). Tomatoes treated with arginine develop resistance to Botrytis cinerea. Nitric oxide (NO) is produced by L-arginine. The concentration of endogenous NO was positively correlated with the induction of key enzymes involved in biotic stress responses, such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, chitinase, and polyphenoloxidase [41]. Martinelli et al. [42] reported that arginine induces key gene regulations that would benefit systemic acquired resistance responses. In CLas-infected C. australasica, the expression of the key enzymes gudB and argJ in the arginine biosynthesis pathway was significantly higher than that in healthy C. australasica and CLas-infected C. sinensis, suggesting that the arginine metabolism pathway is instrumental in promoting HLB tolerance in C. australasica (Fig. 7).

The citrate cycle is an important energy metabolic pathway in plants. Liberibacter crescens was the closest cultured relative to the uncultured CLas. Cruz-Munoz et al. [43] found that citrate is the preferred source of carbon and energy for L. crescens growth, and genome comparisons of L. crescens and CLas showed that they are metabolically very similar. Alves et al. [44] reported that after plants were exposed to CLas-positive psyllids, HLB-susceptible C. sinensis young flushes have a higher citrate cycle gene expression than that of CLas-immune Bergera koenigii, reflecting a higher activity of this metabolic pathway in susceptible C. sinensis. The expression of several enzymes involved in the citrate cycle, including IDH3, MDH1, and ACO, was affected by HLB and showed higher expression and abundance levels in CLas-infected C. australasica than that in CLas-infected C. sinensis (Fig. 6). In CLas-infected C. australasica, changes in the citrate cycle may alter the energy supply to CLas and inhibit its diffusion. However, the preference of CLas for different substances in the citrate cycle remains unclear and requires further investigation. In CLas-infected C. australasica, changes in amino acid metabolic pathways related to the citrate cycle, especially the up-regulation of the arginine synthesis pathway, may also reduce the carbon supply of CLas from the citrate cycle (Fig. 7).

Conclusion

We comprehensively analyzed the transcriptomes and proteomes of HLB-tolerant C. australasica and HLB-susceptible C. sinensis branches grafted onto healthy and CLas-infected C. reticulata ‘Shatangju’. Our results showed that HLB affected the expression of many genes and proteins in C. australasica and C. sinensis. Several genes involved in phenylalanine synthesis, arginine synthesis, and the citrate cycle of C. australasica and C. sinensis were found to be affected by HLB, and the expression of most of these genes and influence of HLB on them were different in the two grafts. The expression of ADT and phhA in CLas-infected C. australasica remained stable, maintaining the phenylalanine homeostasis. The up-regulation and high expression of important arginine biosynthesis genes, gudB and argJ, are beneficial for arginine accumulation. Phenylalanine and arginine are amino acids associated with plant defense. Therefore, we proposed that stabilizing phenylalanine content and up-regulating arginine biosynthesis contribute to HLB tolerance in C. australasica. In addition, when CLas infects C. australasica, the carbon source tends to flow into the amino acid metabolic pathways, especially arginine biosynthesis, rather than citrate cycle. Changes in the enzymes in the citrate cycle pathway and reduction of carbon sources in this cycle may reduce the energy supply for CLas. These results provide a theoretical basis for breeding HLB tolerant citrus varieties.

Methods

Plant material and sampling

The rootstock used in the present study was C. reticulata ‘Shatangju’, the rootstocks infected with CLas or not were provided by the Guangxi Academy of Specialty Crops. The graft materials were C. australasica and C. sinensis provided by the Guangxi Academy of Specialty Crops, all the plants were grown in a greenhouse at 26℃. A high grafting method (single-bud grafting) was used for grafting: select branches with a diameter of ≥ 0.5 cm from the tall rootstock, and make a longitudinal cut downwards along the boundary between the bark and xylem at the smooth point of the bark, exposing the cambium layer. The section length was 2.0–4.0 cm. The buds were cut from the branches of the germplasm material to be identified, inserted into the incision of the anvil branch, and tightly tied. Each rootstock was grafted with eight grafts, and each experiment was repeated five times.

After 6 months, two leaves were sampled for RNA-Seq analysis. All samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80℃ until use. Individual samples were labeled as CaSI (C. australasica infected with CLas), CsSI (C. sinensis infected with CLas), CaSH (uninfected C. australasica), and CsSH (uninfected C. sinensis). One sample from each repeat experiment was used for subsequent experiments, and five samples were used for RNA and protein extraction.

DNA extraction and Real-time TaqMan PCR detection of CLas

Five to twenty-three months after the grafting operation, the titers of CLas in the veins of leaves were measured monthly. Genomic DNA was extracted using the modified CTAB extraction protocol, and quantitative real-time TaqMan PCR with specific primer-probe sets (Table S11) was performed to detect CLas [45, 46]. All reactions were conducted with three repetitions. In each run, the laboratory-stored samples of CLas were used as the positive control, and the healthy leaves were used as the negative control. Plasmids containing the CLas-specific 16 S rDNA fragments were serially diluted at a 10-fold gradient, and the standard curve was obtained by performing Real-time PCR using each dilution as the template in triplicate. The CLas titers were calculated according to the standard curve [46].

RNA extraction and RNA-seq analysis

For RNA extraction and library preparation, RNA was prepared using a Qiagen RNeasy Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Concentration and quality were determined using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. The librariy was prepared following Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation (San Diego, CA, USA) using 1 µg of total RNA. Briefly, messenger RNA was isolated according to the poly (A) selection method using oligo(dT) beads, then fragmented using fragmentation buffer. Next, double-stranded cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers. According to the library construction protocol, the synthesized cDNA was subjected to end repair, phosphorylation, and adapter addition. Approximately 200 bp of cDNA was screened using AMPure XP beads and amplified using PCR. The PCR products were purified using AMPure XP beads to obtain the library. cDNA libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Raw data were preprocessed using SOAPnuke (v1.6.5) with parameters “–n 0.01 -l 15 -q 0.4 -G,” and clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome (NCBI accession No. GCA_018105775.1) with HISAT2 (v2.2.1) [47]. Gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM (v1.3.1). DESeq2 (v1.22.1) was used to analyze differential gene expression (DEG) between groups. Genes with |log2 fold change [FC]| ≥ 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were identified as significantly DEG. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed for the DEG using phyper (Q-value ≤ 0.05).

Protein extraction and trypsin digestion

Following manufacturer’s protocol, total protein was extracted from each group using a plant protein isolation kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The purity and concentration of the isolated proteins were assessed using Bradford protein assay [48]. Afterward, 0.1 mg of protein samples were digested using mass spectroscopy grade trypsin (1:50 trypsin: protein, mass ratio) for 4 h at 37 ℃. After digestion, the peptides were desalted using a Strata X column for urea removal, and dried by vacuum centrifugation.

High-pH reversed-phase separation

An equal number of peptides were collected from each sample, diluted with mobile phase A (5% Acetonitrile [ACN], pH 9.8), and the samples were separated in liquid phase using a Shimadzu LC-20AD liquid phase system with a 5 μm 4.6 × 250 mm Gemini C18 column. Separation was performed at a flow rate of 1 mL/min: 10% mobile phase B (95% ACN, pH 9.8) for 10 min, 5–35% mobile phase B for 40 min, 35–95% mobile phase B for 1 min, mobile phase B for 3 min, and equilibrium of 5% mobile phase B for 10 min. The peaks of the eluted samples were monitored at 214 nm. The fractions were collected once every minute, and the peaks were combined to obtain 10 fractions. The samples were combined to obtain 10 fractions, which were then freeze-dried.

Data-independent acquisition quantitative detection

Freeze-dried samples were reconstituted with 0.1% formic acid (FA) and centrifuged at 20,000 x g for 10 min to obtain the supernatant. The separation was performed using an UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). The samples were first enriched and desalted on a trap column, followed by separation on a self-loaded C18 column (150 μm inner diameter, 1.8 μm column feed particle size, ~ 35 cm column length) in series at a flow rate of 500 nL/min through the following effective gradient: 0–5 min, 5% mobile phase B (98% ACN, 0.1% FA); 5–90 min, the mobile phase B was linearly increased from 5% to 25%; 90–100 min, mobile phase B from 25% to 35%; 100–108 min, mobile phase B from 35% to 80%; 108–113 min, 80% mobile phase B; 113.5–120 min, 5% mobile phase B.

Following liquid-phase separation, the peptides were ionized using a nanoelectrospray ionization source, then entered into a tandem mass spectrometer (Orbitrap Exploris 480, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for detection in the data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode. The main parameters were set as follows: the ion source voltage was set to 1.9 kV, primary mass spectrometry scanning range was 400–1,250 m/z, resolution was set to 120,000, maximum ion injection time (MIT) was 90 ms, and 400–1,250 m/z was divided into 50 windows for continuous window fragmentation and signal acquisition. The ion fragmentation mode was the higher energy dissociation mode, fragmentation energy was set to normalized collision energy 30, MIT was selected as automatic mode, the fragmented ions were detected in Orbitrap, the resolution was set to 30,000, and AGC was set to 300% for the first stage and 1000% for the second stage.

Proteomic analysis

DIA data were corrected for retention time using iRT peptides. Based on the target-decoy model applicable to SWATH-MS, a false positive control was performed with an FDR of 1%, and significant quantitative results were obtained. The bioinformatics analysis of the proteomic data was performed with MSstats [49]. The thresholds of a log2 fold change (FC) value ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 were used to identify DEPs. Functional annotation of all identified proteins was performed using GO (http://geneontology.org/) and KEGG pathway analyses (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). The DEPs were further subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

qRT-PCR was performed to verify the expression of pivotal genes. RNA extraction from the four groups was transcribed into cDNA utilizing the MonScript™ RTIII All-in-One Mix with dsDNase (Monad, Shanghai, China). qRT-PCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System with specific primers (Table S12) using a QuantiNova SYBR Green PCR Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA). The relative mRNA levels of detected genes were calculated according to the 2(−ΔΔCt) algorithm, and the β-Actin gene was an internal control [50].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0). Differences among groups were calculated using one-way analysis of variance, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test. Pearson’s correlation coefficients between samples were calculated using the cor function in R for both qRT-PCR and RNA-seq data, with p < 0.05 considered significant. Five biological replicates were used in all experiments.
