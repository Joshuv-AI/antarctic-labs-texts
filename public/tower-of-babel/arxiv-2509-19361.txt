Geometric Constructions through Ordered Sets
Liudmyla Morozova

Abstract
This work discusses an approach to solving geometric construction problems in which the given figure is included in a set ordered by a certain characteristic. The approach is based on constructing geometric sequences, or chains of figures, in which each element follows logically from its predecessors, thereby making the construction logically justified and opening the way to the desired solution.



Keywords:
geometric constructions; ordered sets; proportional segments; Gothic pattern; proportional angles; geometric sequences; chains of figures

Introduction

This work discusses an approach to solving geometric construction problems in which the given figure is included in a set ordered by a certain characteristic. The core idea is to construct geometric sequences, or chains of figures, where each element follows logically from its predecessors. In this way the construction becomes logically justified and opens the way to the desired solution.

For demonstration of the proposed approach, three problems of geometric construction are considered:

•

Proportional segments, which are usually solved using Thales’s theorem. Here, an alternative solution is presented to illustrate the proposed approach;

•

A Gothic pattern — a figure that cannot be constructed using standard approaches. However, by considering the corresponding set and generalizing the problem, we obtain a set of solutions and can carry out the required construction;

•

Proportional angles. By constructing a sequence of multiple angles, we form an ordered set, which allows us to match angles of the required multiplicity and transition from angle multiplication to its division.

1
Proportional Segments

This chapter will present both the traditional and alternative approaches to
dividing a segment into proportional parts. It will be shown that the apparent
triviality of this problem is deceptive, as it contains a profound meaning.

1.1
Construction using Thales’ theorem

To relate the constructed segment to the given one, we apply Thales’ theorem (Figure
1
a). However, the reliance on parallel lines complicates the construction, and using a triangle makes the reasoning less rigorous. But most importantly, the procedure does not logically follow from the problem statement.

1.2
Inclusion of a segment in an ordered set

An alternative approach is based on the use of internally tangent circles Figure
1
b.
The ratios of their radii correspond to the given proportion. In this construction, the required segment is embedded in an ordered set determined by the lengths of its elements. Each circle divides any of these segments according to the specified proportion—without recourse to parallel lines. This gives the construction both logical completeness and internal justification.

The approach of tangent circles thus provides a constructive solution to the problem of proportional segments without direct reliance on Thales’ theorem. Embedding the segment into an ordered set of circles highlights the proportional relations as a natural consequence of order. Moreover, each resulting segment may itself generate a similar chain of circles, giving rise to internal sequences that will play a role in the further development of the subject.

Figure 1:
Two ways of dividing a segment in a given ratio.

Summary

The division of a segment into equal parts shows how even an elementary task already contains the idea of ordered sequences. Thales’ construction marks a decisive step: instead of working directly with the given segment, it introduces an external figure of arbitrary size. Only afterwards is the result transferred back to the original segment by means of similar triangles. What later came to be seen as a trivial device was in fact a breakthrough: the use of an external sequence that carries the idea of the solution and then connects back to the initial figure. In this sense, Thales’ construction may be regarded as a precursor of the idea that will find its continuation in the present work.

2
Gothic Pattern

It is possible to reduce any problem of geometric construction to a problem of algebra. We cannot present here the general theory of such reductions, but the following example illustrates the idea. A triangular region is bounded by a straight line
AB

AC

This problem, originating from Pólya’s

2.1
Algebraic approach

The desired configuration, shown in Figure

ABC

Summary

The problem of inscribing a circle into a curvilinear triangle, originating from Pólya, illustrates how algebraic and geometric approaches diverge. What appears as a single configuration turns out to be a gateway into an ordered set, where discrete cases merge into continuity. This ordered perspective not only provides a constructive solution by classical means but also reveals hidden transitions between special and generalized cases. In particular, the inclusion of neighboring circles in Figure
2

3
Proportional Angles

We illustrate the approach of ordered sets on the problem of dividing an angle into equal parts. Instead of attempting the division directly, we embed the given configuration into an ordered set and then compare its members.

3.1
A sequence of multiple angles

Let
\alpha>0
be a small initial angle with vertex at
C_{1}
and rays highlighted in red (see Figure
3
a).
Construct a chain of equal circles of radius
R
with centers
C_{i}
alternating on the two rays of the angle.
Joining successive centers yields a broken line

\langle C_{1}C_{2}\cdots C_{n}\rangle,

and we obtain the ordered set of multiples

\angle(\alpha),\ \angle(2\alpha),\ \angle(3\alpha),\ \dots,\ \angle(n\alpha).

The interior angles between consecutive segments of the polyline are

\displaystyle\angle C_{1}C_{2}C_{3}

\displaystyle=180^{\circ}-2\alpha,

\displaystyle\angle C_{2}C_{3}C_{4}

\displaystyle=180^{\circ}-4\alpha,

\displaystyle\angle C_{3}C_{4}C_{5}

\displaystyle=180^{\circ}-6\alpha,

\displaystyle\ \ \vdots

\displaystyle\angle C_{n-1}C_{n}C_{n+1}

\displaystyle=180^{\circ}-2(n-1)\alpha.

Requiring the angles in the above formulas to be nonnegative yields the basic constraint

180^{\circ}-2(n-1)\alpha\geq 0\ \Longrightarrow\ \alpha\leq\frac{90^{\circ}}{\,n-1\,}.

(1)

Thus
\alpha
and
n
are inversely related.

For accuracy we draw two red dashed support lines through the pairwise intersections of adjacent circles; these lines also make explicit that the configuration naturally involves three copies of the angle
\alpha
(the two original rays together with the dashed line between them).

Outcome 1
.

Passing from a single angle to the ordered chain
\angle(k\alpha)
avoids any direct partition: each angle (from the second onward) is already synthesized from pieces carried by other links of the chain.

3.2
Doubling within the chain

We now show how to double an angle on a given link while preserving multiplicity. For concreteness, consider the fourth link and the angle
\angle C_{4}C_{3}D_{4}
(Figure
3
b). Halving the radius doubles this angle while preserving multiplicity along the chain:

\angle C_{4}C_{3}D_{4}:\angle E_{3}C_{2}E_{3}\ =\ \angle C_{4}C_{3}A_{4}:\angle C_{3}C_{2}A_{3}\ =\ 3:2.

Further halving would move outside the region bounded by the red dashed lines; hence the above doubling is the limiting admissible case within the visible chain.

Summary

This chapter shows how the approach of ordered sets extends from linear and curvilinear proportions to multiplicities in the angular domain. The construction of proportional angles reveals that such multiplicities are not reached by direct division but by embedding the given angle into an ordered set. Doubling, in turn, becomes the gateway to inner sequences, through which the construction unfolds into a two–dimensional ordered structure.

Conclusion

Embedding a figure into an ordered set reveals the logic of its development, a logic that cannot be traced otherwise.
In the case of success, the ordered set yields not a single construction but a package of solutions.
The sequence of figures thus becomes a flow of information.

This flow is generated by two components.
The first, external, carries the abstract idea of the problem, is abstracted from any specific instance
(for example, “inscribe a circle into a curvilinear triangle” or “divide an angle into equal parts”).
Such an idea may unfold discretely, as in the chain of multiple angles.
The second, internal, is activated by each external link: it implements the idea in a continuous manner,
selecting the required element and producing the actual construction.
Thus the flow is at once discrete and continuous.

Another logical step arises if we return to Figure 3a.
Point
C_{2}
may be placed on either ray of the initial angle
\alpha
,
and we have no objective reason to prefer one to the other.
At this stage there is freedom of choice.
Once the choice is made, however, the polyline
\overline{C_{1}C_{2}C_{3}\ldots}
ties both rays together,
transforming freedom into completeness.
Moreover, the figure bounded by the red dashed lines encloses a set of three angles
\alpha
,
representing all possible placements of the rays of the initial angle.
Thus local freedom evolves into completeness.

We should also note that an arbitrarily chosen value of
\alpha
entails the emergence of inner sequences.
Here too the transition takes place from freedom of choice to completeness,
where completeness is understood as the set of all equiprobable variants.

Bringing together the notions of informational flow and completeness of choice
leads to a higher level of generalization.
What appears discrete is complemented by continuity,
and what begins as freedom resolves into completeness.
Together these observations may be interpreted as a mechanism that supports
a conservation principle of information within geometric construction.

Nota bene.
We note that this approach of geometric construction, like the
approaches based on loci or on similarity, has its limits of applicability.
Famous problems such as the quadrature of the circle and the duplication
of the cube lie beyond these boundaries. For clarity, one may begin with a
one–dimensional analogue: the construction of a segment whose length equals
the circumference of a circle. This demonstrates the transition within the
one–dimensional case (from the length of a segment to the circumference), and
further to the two–dimensional case (area), and then to the three–dimensional
case (volume). In this perspective, the classical impossible problems can be
viewed as a natural extension of the sequence of constructions: 1d → 2d → 3d.
It is likely that in such problems the conservation principle operates through
relations of magnitudes and manifests itself in transcendental numbers (such
as
\pi
,
e
,
\varphi
, etc.).
