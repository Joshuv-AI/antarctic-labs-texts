A large language model-driven multidisciplinary AI agent system predicts delirium in emergency critically ill patients
Authors: Wen Shang, Tongyue Shi, Qingbian Ma, Guilan Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDelirium occurs frequently in emergency departments and is associated with poor outcomes and increased burden. Early delirium risk prediction is crucial for timely prevention and intervention in emergency care, but most existing models focus on intensive care unit (ICU) populations and offer limited interpretability and interactivity. We propose DeLiriuMAgents, a large language model (LLM)-driven multi-agent system for predicting delirium risk in emergency critically ill patients. It simulates multidisciplinary clinical consultation by integrating data-driven, machine learning-based risk prediction; LLM-based virtual specialist reasoning in emergency medicine, neurology, and psychiatry; and medical evidence via retrieval-augmented generation to reach a final decision. In model development, Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV is used for model derivation and internal validation; a multicenter Peking University (PKU) cohort from two hospitals in China and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) cohort are used for external validation. It achieves accuracy/sensitivity/specificity of 0.749/0.762/0.747, 0.731/0.708/0.736, and 0.670/0.708/0.665 on MIMIC-IV, PKU, and eICU-CRD validation sets, respectively. Chart review and clinician evaluation verify the interpretability and usefulness of its reports.Keywords: delirium, risk prediction, emergency department, intensive care unit, machine learning, large language model, multi-agent system

Introduction

Delirium is an acute neuropsychiatric syndrome characterized by sudden and fluctuating impairments in attention, consciousness, and cognition.1,2,3 It poses a serious threat to patient safety and recovery, particularly among vulnerable populations such as critically ill patients and older adults.1,2 The prevalence of delirium is estimated at approximately 15% in emergency departments (EDs) and increases to around 32% among patients in intensive care units (ICUs).4,5 Delirium is associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including prolonged hospitalization, accelerated cognitive and functional decline, increased risk of institutionalization, and higher mortality.3,6,7,8 Delirium often goes undetected due to subtle clinical signs, contributing to delayed diagnosis and missed opportunities for intervention.1 In the US, the annual economic burden of delirium is estimated to exceed $150 billion, largely due to increased care needs, rehospitalization, and long-term disability.2,3 In high-risk environments such as ED-based ICUs,9 patients often present with rapidly evolving conditions, multiple organ dysfunction, and exposure to sedatives or invasive procedures, all of which further elevate the risk of delirium.10

However, early identification or detection of delirium remains difficult. Existing predictive models are not well suited for the time-sensitive, data-limited, and chaotic emergency critical care.11,12 There is an urgent need for more effective and context-specific approaches to enable early prediction of delirium in emergency critical care. Although numerous delirium prediction models, such as PRE-DELIRIC13 and E-PRE-DELIRIC,14,15 have been developed, these models were designed for ICU settings and are not suited to critically ill patients in emergency settings. PRE-DELIRIC requires 24-h data and is evaluated post-ICU admission, making it impractical for application at an early stage in the ED. E-PRE-DELIRIC, which improves timeliness, is primarily trained on elective surgical ICU cohorts and lacks validation in high-risk emergency populations. In particular, existing ED-specific delirium models are scarce and primarily target elderly patients, with limited external validation.16 Few delirium prediction models have been developed and externally validated specifically for the emergency critically ill population, where rapid and reliable delirium risk stratification is urgently needed.

Traditional machine learning (ML) and deep learning approaches have been increasingly applied to delirium prediction in ICU settings, leveraging structured electronic health record (EHR) data and algorithms such as logistic regression, random forests, and gradient boosting machines.17,18,19,20 These models often demonstrate improved predictive performance compared with conventional clinical risk scores by incorporating multidimensional features such as demographics, vital signs, and laboratory test data. However, their applicability in emergency settings remains limited. Emergency cases often involve rapidly evolving conditions, incomplete records, and heterogeneous interventions, which are difficult for ML models trained on static snapshots to capture. These limitations underscore the need for more adaptable and interpretable prediction frameworks that can reason over complex, dynamic, and multimodal patient data in real-world, rapidly changing clinical environments such as ED-based ICUs.9

With the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs), natural language processing has gained significant attention in clinical applications.21 LLM applications include medical record summarization, question answering, and diagnostic reasoning. LLMs excel at processing complex, unstructured clinical data and can generate coherent, human-like explanations.22,23 This capability of LLMs offers a promising path to bridge the gap between structured medical data and clinical narratives, potentially improving interpretability and interactivity in AI models. However, LLMs have their limitations. These models are prone to generating hallucinations, rely on static knowledge that may become outdated, and cannot handle structured time-series data, such as real-time physiological data monitored in the ICU. In addition, most existing LLM-based systems operate as single-model architectures, which limits their ability to reflect the collaborative nature of clinical decision-making. To overcome these limitations, LLM-driven multi-agent systems (MASs) have emerged as a paradigm in medical AI.24,25 MAS frameworks assign distinct roles to specialized agents that work together to collect patient information, reason over complex data, and assess clinical risk.26,27,28 This multi-agent structure mimics real-world multidisciplinary collaboration in clinical practice. Combining LLMs with MASs has the potential to enhance predictive performance, interpretability, and human-AI interaction for AI models in medicine.22,29,30

Despite the above-mentioned benefits, LLM-driven MASs remain insufficiently explored for delirium prediction in emergency critically ill patients. To bridge this gap, we propose DeLiriuMAgents, an LLM-driven MAS that simulates multidisciplinary team (MDT) consultations for delirium prediction. We design DeLiriuMAgents as comprising the following agents: EHRPromptAgent, ModelPhysicianAgent, EmergencyPhysicianAgent, NeurologyPhysicianAgent, PsychiatryPhysicianAgent, MedEvidenceAgent, and DeliriumDecisionAgent. The system coordinates multiple AI agents to provide explainable delirium risk prediction and clinician-oriented communication. It is worth noting that the ML model called by the ModelPhysicianAgent was trained on structured EHR data of critically ill patients transferred to the ICU from ED in the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV database.31 The MedEvidenceAgent was implemented using a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) approach. This RAG procedure enables the real-time incorporation of authoritative medical evidence, retrieved from clinical guidelines, textbooks, and recent literature, into the reasoning process, helping reduce hallucinations and enhance medical credibility. Finally, the developed DeLiriuMAgents was externally validated using a cohort collected from two grade A tertiary hospitals in China and a cohort from the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD),32 a multicenter critical care database in the US. The findings of this study support the feasibility and cross-dataset robustness of DeLiriuMAgents for early delirium prediction in emergency critical care settings.

Results

Data characteristics of study cohorts

A total of 447,712 ED admissions were initially extracted from the MIMIC-IV-ED module. After excluding patients who were not transferred to the ICU within 24 h of ED admission (n = 421,325) or were younger than 18 years (n = 0), 26,387 patients who had been initially admitted to the ED and then transferred to the ICU were identified. Further exclusions included patients with primary diagnoses of cerebrovascular disease, traumatic brain injury (TBI), psychiatric disorders, or pregnancy (n = 17,292), with a length of stay in the ICU < 24 h (n = 1,356), with unrecorded delirium outcome (n = 2,304), and those who had delirium onset within 24 h of admission in the ICU (n = 1,006). After applying the above patient selection criteria, 4,429 patients from MIMIC-IV were included in the final retrospective cohort, which was randomly split into a model-derivation cohort (n = 3,100) and an internal-validation cohort (n = 1,329). After applying the same patient inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 1,054 patients formed the Peking University (PKU) external validation cohort (928 from Peking University Third Hospital and 126 from Peking University First Hospital). The eICU external validation cohort contained 5,151 eligible patients, including 646 delirium-positive and 4,505 delirium-negative cases. The incidence of delirium was 13.6% (422/3,100) in the model derivation cohort, 13.6% (181/1,329) in the internal validation cohort, 17.6% (185/1,054) in the external PKU cohort, and 12.5% (646/5,151) in the external eICU cohort. Figure 1 illustrates the process for cohort construction. Baseline characteristics of the study cohorts are summarized in Table 1. The excluded 1,006 cases with delirium onset within the first 24 h of ICU admission had a median age of 72.19 years, and 575 (57.2%) patients are male. Common presenting symptoms included dyspnea (19.9%) and altered consciousness (15.4%), and common comorbidities included hypertension (65.9%), heart failure (38.9%), diabetes (38.3%), renal disease (31.1%), coronary heart disease (28.9%), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD; 27.8%). Construction and predictive performance of DeLiriuMAgents

The overall framework of DeLiriuMAgents is illustrated in Figure 2, and Figure 3 shows the detailed RAG-based workflow of MedEvidenceAgent. DeLiriuMAgents was developed using the model derivation cohort and tested on the internal and external validation cohorts. DeLiriuMAgents followed the transparent reporting of a multivariable prediction model for individual prognosis or diagnosis using LLM (TRIPOD-LLM) statement.33 To employ the best-performing ML tool that can be used by ModelPhysicianAgent in the DeLiriuMAgents framework, seven supervised ML models were developed and evaluated on the model derivation cohort. Figure S1 shows the overlap among features selected by the four data-driven feature selection methods and the final set of 25 selected variables. The 25 variables identified by all four methods were used as predictors in the final ML model development for ModelPhysicianAgent. The performance of all the developed ML models on the validation cohorts is shown in Table S2. Among the seven ML models, CatBoost showed the most favorable overall performance profile across the validation cohorts, combining strong discrimination with consistently balanced threshold-based metrics. Therefore, CatBoost was selected as the best-performing ML model for ModelPhysicianAgent. The calibration curve of CatBoost demonstrated good alignment with the ideal diagonal, yielding Brier scores of 0.101 (internal validation), 0.142 (PKU external validation), and 0.102 (eICU external validation). The decision curve analysis (DCA) indicated net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. Figure S2 illustrates the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) of the ML models on the internal and external validation cohorts. Figure S3 illustrates the model calibration and DCA of the CatBoost model on the internal and external validation cohorts. The predictive performance of the three approaches (LLM Directly, Best ML Model, and full DeLiriuMAgents) across the internal validation cohort and the two external validation cohorts is summarized in Table 2. We also performed a comparison with E-PRE-DELIRIC in the PKU cohort. DeLiriuMAgents showed a better performance than E-PRE-DELIRIC. Detailed results are provided in Table S3.

Model performance comparison

To investigate the contribution of individual agents within the DeLiriuMAgents framework, we conducted an ablation study. As summarized in Table S4, the fully integrated DeLiriuMAgents consistently demonstrated better overall predictive performance compared with the other three ablation configurations. Removal of any component (ModelPhysicianAgent, Multidisciplinary Agents, or MedEvidenceAgent) led to performance degradation. Here, “Multidisciplinary Agents” represents a virtual clinical MDT team, including EmergencyPhysicianAgent, NeurologyPhysicianAgent, and PsychiatryPhysicianAgent. To assess the system’s sensitivity to backbone LLM choice, we also implemented DeLiriuMAgents, using another open-source LLM, Qwen3.5, on the PKU cohort. The DeepSeek-R1-based DeLiriuMAgents showed higher overall performance than the Qwen3.5-based MAS. Importantly, the Qwen3.5-based DeLiriuMAgents also outperformed the best ML model on the same cohort. The detailed comparison results for the two LLM-based MASs on the PKU cohort are provided in Table S5. In the analysis of reproducibility of the LLM-based DeLiriuMAgents, the final binary prediction labels showed high agreement across repeated runs, with Cohen’s κ of 0.950 for all three run comparisons. At the patient level, 97 of 100 patients received the same binary label in all three runs. For the top key features, 96.5% of report pairs shared at least two key factors, and 72.7% shared at least three. These findings suggest that repeated runs generally produced consistent binary predictions and identified similar top key features.

The execution environment and runtime characteristics of DeLiriuMAgents are as follows: the system was built and tested on a MacBook Pro with an Apple M5 chip and 16 GB RAM; the LLMs used in the system were run through API services provided by VolcEngine and Alibaba Cloud; the optimized DeLiriuMAgents workflow required approximately 2.5 min per case; the main time-consuming steps were ModelPhysicianAgent generation (approximately 28 s); the three specialist agents running in parallel (approximately 45 s); DeliriumDecisionAgent question generation with parallel MedEvidenceAgent retrieval and response generation (approximately 45 s); and final report generation by DeliriumDecisionAgent (approximately 35 s). EHRPromptAgent required less than 1 s. Because the specialist agents and MedEvidenceAgent evidence-query responses can be executed in parallel, the workflow can run asynchronously in the background while clinicians continue routine assessment or documentation. In the token-cost analysis, the workflow required an average of 10 LLM API calls, 28,751 input tokens, 9,106 output tokens, and 37,857 total tokens per case, corresponding to an estimated inference cost of approximately US$ 0.038 per case. For demonstration purposes, we developed a publicly accessible web-based interactive demo of DeLiriuMAgents, available at www.deliriumagents.site. The demo allows users to follow the inference process and view the intermediate inference outputs of the main agents and the final delirium risk report.

Clinical interpretation

SHAP-based interpretation of ML-based prediction model in ModelPhysicianAgent

To improve the interpretability of the developed ML-based delirium prediction model, we conducted a comprehensive SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis for the best-performing CatBoost model. All 25 input variables were included in the SHAP calculation, and the top 15 variables ranked by the mean absolute SHAP value were selected for visualization and clinical interpretation. The SHAP summary plot illustrates the overall contribution and directionality of each of the top 15 features to the model output, with color gradients representing actual feature values (Figure 4A). Features including mechanical ventilation use, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) verbal response, GCS eye response, invasive procedures, antibiotic use, age, sedative use, SpO2, white blood cell (WBC) count, hypertension, platelet count, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), modified early warning score (MEWS), creatinine, and calcium were among the top 15 features and had the greatest impact on prediction results. A higher SHAP value indicates a greater contribution to the predicted outcome. The bar plot of mean SHAP values stratified by outcome further demonstrates that most of these top variables exerted a more substantial influence on the prediction result in the delirium-positive group than in the negative group, indicating their discriminatory roles (Figure 4B). To better understand the effect of each feature, SHAP dependence plots were drawn, showing how feature values correlate with SHAP values across individual samples (Figure 4C). Finally, to assess group-level variability, we visualized the SHAP values for the top 15 features in the model-derivation cohort, using heatmaps in Figure S4. Separate SHAP heatmaps were generated for all patients and for patients with and without delirium. The visualization of SHAP values highlights the distinct importance of features across different outcome subgroups. Collectively, these results provide clinical insights into how the ML model makes predictions based on patients’ clinical characteristics, offering interpretability of predictors in downstream decision-making.

Interpretation of DeLiriuMAgents prediction

In DeLiriuMAgents, the input is the tabular EHR data of one patient in an ED-based ICU. The EHRPromptAgent first synthesizes an EHR note, as shown in Figure S5A, that reports each clinical item value with its measurement unit and an explicit comparison with the reference range. This natural-language summary provides a standardized, LLM-readable context to support downstream agent reasoning and decision-making. Next, the ModelPhysicianAgent provides the ML-based prediction based on the EHR data. Then, its predicted results are fed into three clinical specialist agents and the DeliriumDecisionAgent. Finally, the DeliriumDecisionAgent generates the final prediction report based on the EHR note, the reports of ModelPhysicianAgent and three clinical specialist agents, and the MedEvidenceAgent. This multi-source synthesis has the potential to rectify prediction errors made by the CatBoost classifier. The knowledge-enhanced capability of the coordinating DeliriumDecisionAgent in DeLiriuMAgents is helpful for borderline patients whose risk could not be recognized by the standalone ML model.

An example risk prediction report by DeLiriuMAgents is displayed in Figure S5B. The DeLiriuMAgents-generated prediction report includes the following: (1) final risk assessment: the consolidated risk label with an explicit note on whether the decision agrees with or overrides the ML-based prediction; (2) key variables: a concise list of EHR features that most strongly supported the decision, each shown with its unit and deviation from the normal range; (3) reasoning chain with evidence: a stepwise summary linking clinical findings to the mechanism or epidemiological evidence retrieved by MedEvidenceAgent; evidence is cited at the statement level to show how it influenced the decision; (4) factors needing monitoring: the most concerning clinical features or rapidly evolving risk factors that may lead to delirium, and variables that require dynamic monitoring over the following hours; (5) initial intervention suggestions: non-pharmacologic and pharmacologic considerations aligned with current clinical guidelines and patients’ physiological status.

We further evaluated the clinical relevance of retrieval-supported question-response outputs from MedEvidenceAgent in 100 PKU cases. We invited an experienced clinician to randomly select and review one question-response output for each patient, using a predefined 3-level relevance scale. Overall, 86 of 100 outputs were rated as highly relevant, 7 as partially relevant, and 7 as irrelevant. In other words, 93% of the reviewed outputs were judged clinically relevant. These results suggest that most MedEvidenceAgent outputs were clinically related to the corresponding queries and delirium risk assessment.

The DeLiriuMAgents system reconciles multi-specialist judgments. For correctly predicted cases, we examined agreement across the three clinical specialist agents. DeLiriuMAgents coordinated these clinical specialists to reach a concordant, clinically plausible verdict. To quantify inter-agent agreement, we analyzed the binary labels generated by the “emergency,” “neurology,” and “psychiatry” agents in the correctly predicted cases in the PKU cohort. Among the 770 cases correctly predicted by DeLiriuMAgents, pairwise Cohen’s κ was 0.775 for “emergency” versus “neurology,” 0.743 for “emergency” versus “psychiatry,” and 0.915 for “neurology” versus “psychiatry.” Full agreement among all three specialist agents occurred in 712 (92.5%) cases, while 2-of-3 agreement occurred in 58 (7.5%) cases. Among these 58 cases without full agreement, the “neurology” agent most often matched the ground truth (70.7%), followed by the “psychiatry” agent (62.1%) and the “emergency” agent (41.4%). Additionally, DeLiriuMAgents grounds decisions using external authoritative domain knowledge, including clinical guidelines, consensus, and published literature, by automatically invoking the MedEvidenceAgent component. In cases that were initially misjudged by the ModelPhysicianAgent and later corrected by three clinical specialist agents, the system issued an average of 6 natural-language queries per patient to retrieve guideline or literature snippets. The evidence fragments provided by the MedEvidenceAgent filled knowledge gaps not covered by structured EHR data or expert judgment and affected the final risk prediction results.

Chart review analysis

In the chart review analysis of 100 randomly selected PKU cohort patients, unaided physician judgments achieved an accuracy of 0.620, a sensitivity of 0.647, a specificity of 0.614, a positive predictive value (PPV) of 0.256, an negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.895, and an F1 score of 0.367. The AI reports achieved an accuracy of 0.740, a sensitivity of 0.765, a specificity of 0.735, a PPV of 0.371, an NPV of 0.938, and an F1 score of 0.500. After being assisted by the AI reports, physician judgments were improved to an accuracy of 0.650, a sensitivity of 0.706, a specificity of 0.639, a PPV of 0.286, an NPV of 0.914, and an F1 score of 0.407. Evaluation of AI reports

In the evaluation, 90% of AI reports were rated as “very accurate” or “fairly accurate” for medical interpretation. For reasoning quality, 90% were rated as “very clear” or “fairly clear,” and 91% were rated as having “sufficient” or “fairly sufficient” evidence support. In addition, 91% of responses rated the reports as “very helpful” or “fairly helpful” for improving clinician efficiency, 87% considered the report structure “very reasonable” or “fairly reasonable,” and 96% expressed willingness to use DeLiriuMAgents as a decision-support tool in real-world clinical practice. In 93% of evaluations, clinicians considered that the full DeLiriuMAgents report was more helpful than an SHAP-only explanation for their understanding and decision-making. The structured ratings of report quality and usefulness are summarized in Figure 5B.

Clinicians changed their original judgments in 23 of 100 cases (23.0%) after reviewing the AI reports. Among these changed cases, the most common reason was “AI report provided more evidence-based reasoning and analysis” (14/23, 60.9%), followed by “AI report highlighted information that had not been sufficiently noticed previously” (7/23, 30.4%), and “AI report reached a different judgment prompting to reconsider” (3/23, 13.0%). The most frequently selected factors influencing judgment change were metabolic disturbances such as electrolyte, glucose, or renal abnormalities (11/23, 47.8%), altered or fluctuating mental status (9/23, 39.1%), hypoxia/respiratory failure (6/23, 26.1%), and infection/inflammation/sepsis (5/23, 21.7%). The main reasons for judgment change and the clinical factors influencing these changes are presented in Figures 5C and 5D.

In the structured hallucination analysis, all 26 reviewed reports had at least one factual or reasoning problem. No report was judged to have information that directly conflicted with the EHR. However, 9 reports “added clinical details that were not supported by the EHR,” and 14 reports “described numerical values, units, or evidence incorrectly.” Overall, 22 reports “overestimated the patient’s delirium risk by over-interpreting some abnormal variables”, and 4 reports “underestimated the patient’s delirium risk by overlooking some important warning signs in the EHR.” In the 22 false-positive reports, a common pattern was that AI paid more attention to a small number of abnormal findings, especially infection or inflammation, metabolic abnormalities, and mental status changes and, therefore, overestimated the patients’ delirium risk. In the 4 false-negative reports, AI often relied too heavily on normal findings, such as normal GCS scores, absence of sedative use, absence of mechanical ventilation, or younger age, while overlooking some clinically important risk signals, such as metabolic disturbance, hemodynamic instability, or infection.

Discussion

Delirium remains a frequent and severe complication in critically ill patients admitted through the ED. Early and accurate prediction of delirium risk is crucial for timely and appropriate clinical interventions. However, existing ML-based delirium prediction models, predominantly developed in ICU settings, lack prediction interpretability and user interactivity for emergency critically ill patients.11,34 LLM-driven MASs have recently been applied in clinical domains. Typical examples are MedAgents,24 Agent Hospital,35 ColaCare,36 and AKI-Detector,28 which employ collaborative reasoning among AI agents for various medical tasks, such as clinical diagnosis, prognosis prediction, and treatment recommendation. However, the existing MASs mostly target general clinical scenarios or chronic conditions, rather than acute settings. To address these gaps, we propose and develop an LLM-driven multidisciplinary MAS, DeLiriuMAgents, specifically designed for early prediction of delirium among critically ill patients in the emergency setting. DeLiriuMAgents integrates an ML-based prediction tool trained on structured EHR data, three simulated specialist physician agents from three delirium-related disciplines, and an external medical evidence retrieval agent using a RAG framework. This LLM-driven DeLiriuMAgents was developed and internally validated using a retrospective cohort from the MIMIC-IV database and further externally validated using prospectively collected patient data from the ED at two grade A tertiary hospitals in China (Peking University First Hospital and Peking University Third Hospital) and an eICU-CRD cohort in the US.

The DeLiriuMAgents framework generally delivered better overall predictive performance than the standalone ML model and the reduced versions of the framework in both internal and external validation cohorts, although the magnitude of improvement over the standalone ML baseline was moderate rather than dramatic. In developing an ML-based prediction model, we evaluated seven models to select the best-performing one for implementing ModelPhysicianAgent for delirium prediction. Among these ML models, CatBoost consistently demonstrated the best performance and, thus, was selected for implementing ModelPhysicianAgent. DeLiriuMAgents builds on a strong ML-based baseline model, ModelPhysicianAgent, and shows further improved performance through multidisciplinary agents (EmergencyPhysicianAgent, NeurologyPhysicianAgent, and PsychiatryPhysicianAgent) and RAG (MedEvidenceAgent). Particularly, removing the ML-based prediction module (ModelPhysicianAgent), the three LLM-simulated specialist physician agents, or the medical evidence retrieval module (MedEvidenceAgent) would lower its predictive performance. In this sense, the value of DeLiriuMAgents lies not only in improved prediction metrics but also in its ability to provide a more interpretable and interactive decision-support process than a standalone ML model.

For the developed DeLiriuMAgents, we evaluated its performance using sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, F1 score, and the Youden index. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) was not reported for the MAS because its output is a binary delirium outcome with a narrative report rather than a continuous probability score. Thresholding is applied only at the level of the underlying CatBoost in ModelPhysicianAgent, for which AUROC and calibration curves are reported separately. This is also consistent with recent LLM-based MASs, which primarily evaluate the agentic pipeline using metrics such as sensitivity, PPV, F1 score, accuracy, and false alarms per patient-hour, rather than reporting an AUROC for the MAS itself.29,37 The Brier score was close in the internal validation cohort and the eICU external validation cohort but higher in the PKU external validation cohort, suggesting relatively poor probabilistic calibration of the CatBoost model in the PKU cohort. These findings suggest that, before deployment in different institutions, the ML component may require local recalibration and threshold customization to match the expected trade-off between sensitivity and specificity in local clinical settings. In clinical practice, too many false high-risk alerts in a busy ED-ICU workflow may lead to alert fatigue. Although the DeLiriuMAgents has shown improved sensitivity and overall performance in delirium prediction, it is also likely to make false predictions. We suggest using it as a delirium monitoring trigger within the ED-ICU workflow. Specifically, when the system outputs a high-risk prediction, its primary role is to prompt the clinical team to conduct delirium-related observation and assessment. In other words, the clinical value of DeLiriuMAgents lies mainly in assisting early risk detection, rather than replacing clinician judgments.

The interpretability of DeLiriuMAgents’ predictions is demonstrated from three perspectives: model-level feature contributions, multidisciplinary specialist reasoning, and domain-knowledge-based evidence guidance. First, in ModelPhysicianAgent, SHAP-based feature importance analysis highlights top contributors to the ML prediction. Many of these variables are well-recognized delirium risk factors in critical care, reflecting underlying mechanisms such as neuroinflammation, hypoxia, metabolic disturbances, and sedative exposure.7,11,18,38,39 Second, the collaboration of LLM-based multidisciplinary specialists can mimic real-world MDT consultation. These specialist agents independently interpret patients’ EHR data, apply their specialty-specific reasoning heuristics, and produce structured, narrative assessments. Such a multidimensional reasoning process ensures that DeLiriuMAgents makes decisions by relying not only on the predictions provided by the ML-based model but also on the judgments generated by three specialist agents from multidisciplinary perspectives. Third, during the final prediction, the DeliriumDecisionAgent invokes the MedEvidenceAgent, which formulates a series of queries to the medical knowledge base to retrieve evidence supporting a more informed delirium risk prediction. The returned medical evidence snippets from the domain knowledge base filled the gaps that neither structured data nor human heuristic reasoning could bridge. The sequential hand-off from data-based prediction to multidisciplinary specialist reasoning and evidence-grounded decision-making transforms a numerical probability into an explainable narrative. Together, these aspects enhance prediction explainability, improve clinician trust, and provide a replicable template of LLM-driven MASs for clinical decision support in medicine.

To examine whether these explanations were clinically meaningful, we further evaluated the generated reports through clinician chart review and structured report assessment. In the chart review analysis, physician judgments were improved after reviewing the AI reports compared with unaided chart review. The structured hallucination analysis showed that the main problem was not the usual fabrication of facts from the EHR, but rather the misinterpretation of existing clinical information. In false-positive cases, the AI tended to pay more attention to a small number of abnormal findings and, therefore, overestimated the delirium risk. In false-negative cases, it more often relied too heavily on normal findings while missing some important warning signs. This suggests that, in tasks such as delirium prediction, the main challenge is about how much each finding should matter in the final decision.

DeLiriuMAgents facilitates smooth and natural language-based interaction between users and the system. It supports intuitive clinician-AI interaction by translating complex-structured EHR data into more understandable natural-language narratives. This capability helps physicians quickly assess patients’ condition, reducing their cognitive burden and improving clinical efficiency. Moreover, unlike traditional ML models, which are constrained by fixed feature sets and often require imputation for missing data, DeLiriuMAgents can flexibly integrate the available structured clinical information into the reasoning workflow, including cases in which real-world data are incomplete or irregular. This system design aligns more closely with real-world clinical workflows, where data completeness is often variable. Moreover, the external validation results across the PKU and eICU cohorts further support the robustness and transportability of the framework across datasets and healthcare settings. These features support the translational potential of DeLiriuMAgents for clinician-oriented decision support across different emergency critical care settings.

Beyond interpretability and workflow usability, the stability of an LLM-driven MAS is another important consideration for clinical decision support. We, therefore, examined DeLiriuMAgents from three complementary perspectives: repeated-run reproducibility, backbone-model sensitivity, and inter-agent agreement. Across repeated runs, the final binary prediction labels were highly reproducible, and the identified key features also showed high consistency. The trivial variability among top key features across repeated runs may be due to LLM reasoning, that is, the LLM may emphasize slightly different clinical features when generating the explanation. However, these differences mainly occurred at the explanation level and did not affect the final binary prediction. In future clinical deployment, repeated inference with an ensemble or majority-vote scheme can be considered. The backbone-swap experiment further showed that the backbone LLM affects DeLiriuMAgents’ performance and that the system’s improvement over the standalone ML model is also attributable to the MAS architecture. DeepSeek-R1 was selected as the backbone LLM for constructing DeLiriuMAgents because it is a powerful open-source LLM that demonstrates strong general-domain capabilities and has shown competitive performance in many tasks.40 Notably, the deployment of closed-source LLMs in medical institutions is severely limited by privacy and regulatory constraints, as such models may require cloud-based access and may be difficult to integrate into hospital information systems due to strict regulations on the use of patient data. In contrast, DeepSeek-R1 can be locally deployed and fine-tuned on institutional infrastructure, preserving data privacy and local customization. Recent studies have also highlighted that open-source models like DeepSeek can achieve performance comparable to, or even exceeding, that of commercial LLMs in different clinical reasoning tasks.41,42 Furthermore, in certain regions, access to certain closed-source LLMs may be limited by infrastructure, policy, or cost considerations, which hinder their widespread clinical adoption. Nevertheless, this backbone-swap experiment remains limited, and future studies should evaluate additional LLM backbones and larger sample sizes to explore the effects of model architecture and backbone choice. In addition, the inter-agent agreement analysis showed that, among correctly predicted cases, the three specialist agents usually reached the same binary judgment. In the small subset of cases without full agreement, agents showed varying levels of alignment with the ground truth, with the “neurology” agent showing the highest alignment. However, this does not indicate that the final prediction was solely driven by a single specialist agent. The final DeLiriuMAgents decision was generated by synthesizing evidence from EHRPromptAgent, ModelPhysicianAgent, EmergencyPhysicianAgent, NeurologyPhysicianAgent, PsychiatryPhysicianAgent, and MedEvidenceAgent.

Another important design consideration is the input data format. DeLiriuMAgents was developed using structured EHR data, without integrating raw unstructured clinical notes for the following reasons. First, emergency and critical care clinical notes are highly heterogeneous across institutions in terms of the format, narrative style, language, and completeness. In MIMIC-IV, clinical notes are available in the MIMIC-IV-Note module, which contains only discharge summaries and radiology reports. In eICU-CRD, clinical notes recorded in the note table are from different clinical processes. However, raw free-text notes were removed during the data de-identification process, and only highly structured text selected from the EHR system dropdown menus was retained, while the original unstructured clinical notes were not available in eICU-CRD. In addition, in the structured EHR data used for MAS development in this study, many variables used for clinical reasoning were originally extracted from clinical documentation, including chief complaints, comorbidities, physical examination, and parts of treatment-related information. Particularly, in the PKU cohort, the study dataset was first extracted from the admission records, progress notes, laboratory and examination reports, and nursing documentation, and then converted into a structured variable set for analysis. As a result, the most relevant clinical information contained in clinical notes has been used in the PKU external validation cohort.

Several issues with data and prediction tasks should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, EHR data may have some limitations. On the one hand, some variables in the EHR system may be missing or incompletely recorded, making some information unavailable for prediction modeling, and this might have affected model performance. On the other hand, the EHR data collected across different institutions may differ in terms of measurement units, recording frequency, variable definitions, and documentation practices, which may affect the transportability of a model derived from one institution’s EHR data to another, especially its external validation performance.43 Beyond these EHR data limitations, the temporal definition of the prediction task also requires careful interpretation. Patients with delirium onset within the first 24 h after ICU admission were excluded from the present prediction task, as these patients may already have delirium or be in the rapid-evolution process at admission. For these patients, a diagnosis of delirium is more pragmatic than early detection. However, they may also represent a different clinical scenario from the one addressed by the current DeLiriuMAgents model. Directly applying our model at ICU or ED-ICU admission to predict first-24-h delirium would be inappropriate because the patient data collected at admission may be incomplete or less comprehensive than the data collected during the first 24 h after admission. If the DeLiriuMAgents framework needs to be adapted to an ICU or ED-ICU admission-time risk-screening task, it would require redefining the prediction task as cross-sectional and rebuilding the model using data collected before delirium onset for positive cases and data collected at admission for negative cases. Although delirium labels in this study were all based on the confusion assessment method for the ICU (CAM-ICU)-related assessments recorded in the EHR, delirium was identified in different ways across the study cohorts. In the PKU cohort, delirium was identified from the standardized Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS)44 and CAM-ICU45 assessments performed by trained nurses. In the MIMIC cohort, delirium was identified via the “delirium assessment” item recorded in the “chartevents” table. In the eICU cohort, delirium was identified from the “nurseCharting” table where the “delirium scale” item was recorded as CAM-ICU, and the “delirium score” item indicated delirium outcome. However, medication records or ICD codes were not used to define the label because there are no specific medications that can be used exclusively to manage delirium, and the ICD codes are mainly recorded at admission or discharge without considering the time of delirium onset. Therefore, some heterogeneity in input EHR data, prediction timing, and outcome ascertainment remains across cohorts and may need to be considered when interpreting external validation results.

In conclusion, we developed DeLiriuMAgents, an LLM-driven MAS that integrates data-driven ML-based predictions, structured EHR information transformed into natural-language clinical narratives, LLM-based virtual clinical specialists, and external medical evidence retrieved through RAG, to support explainable and evidence-grounded prediction of delirium for emergency critically ill patients. External validation across the PKU and eICU cohorts demonstrated acceptable performance and potential transportability. DeLiriuMAgents targets critically ill patients in the ED. Rather than assisting ED physicians in responding after delirium becomes clinically apparent, DeLiriuMAgents provides early warning of delirium risk by leveraging limited, ED-specific data. This shift from post-onset management to early risk identification in the ED enables earlier delirium prevention and targeted intervention. Therefore, the LLM-driven DeLiriuMAgents has the potential to aid ED physicians in identifying patients with delirium risk at an early stage and, thus, may help improve patient outcomes in emergency critical care.

Limitations of the study

Despite these promising findings, this study has its limitations. First, DeLiriuMAgents focuses solely on early prediction of delirium risk, without considering delirium prognosis or outcomes. Future work should expand the predictive tasks to include other clinically meaningful patient outcomes. Second, although the employed LLMs provide substantial reasoning capabilities, risks of hallucination and the generation of plausible but medically inaccurate information remain an ongoing concern. More stringent controls and fine-tuning of LLMs with domain-specific medical knowledge can help maintain the reliability of predictions and ensure clinical safety. Third, the employed EHR data may affect model performance because they are not collected primarily for research purposes and are, therefore, subject to incompleteness and institutional heterogeneity. Finally, model validation across MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD, and datasets from two tertiary-care hospitals in China indicates the model’s transportability across different datasets; however, further validation is still needed in a broader range of real-world settings, such as community hospitals or low-resource settings with different EHR infrastructures.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study cohorts

MIMIC-IV,31 a publicly available, de-identified critical care dataset developed and maintained by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, was used as one of the data sources for this study. It includes detailed EHRs from 73,141 ICU admissions at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) between 2008 and 2019. MIMIC-IV-ED includes ED admissions between 2011 and 2019, which contain over 400,000 ED stays. Emergency critically ill patients were defined as adults who presented at the ED and required ICU-level organ-supportive treatment or continuous critical-care monitoring. In the MIMIC-IV, patients admitted to the ED and then transferred to the ICU within 24 h were considered to have characteristics similar to those of patients in ED-based ICU,9 all of whom required ICU-level care after being admitted to the ED. Adult patients admitted to the ED and then transferred to the ICU within 24 h were included in the retrospective cohort (MIMIC cohort). It was randomly split into a model-derivation cohort (70%) and an internal-validation cohort (30%).

To evaluate the generalizability of the developed DeLiriuMAgents, we constructed a multi-center PKU external validation cohort containing patients who presented to the emergency resuscitation room in the ED from May to November 2024 at two Grade-A tertiary hospitals in China (Peking University First Hospital and Peking University Third Hospital). In Chinese hospitals, the emergency resuscitation room can provide ICU-level care within the ED and is similar to an ED-based ICU.9 Accordingly, the PKU cohort included critically ill patients who entered the emergency resuscitation room within 24 h of their initial ED visit, either directly after triage upon ED arrival or after transfer from other ED areas, such as the observation area, thereby aligning with the ED-to-ICU definition used in MIMIC-IV. The eICU-CRD, a publicly available, de-identified multicenter critical care database from the US, was included as an additional eICU external validation dataset. Because eICU-CRD lacks an ED module comparable to MIMIC-IV-ED, an ED-to-ICU cohort was defined as comprising adult patients admitted to the ICU via the ED and transferred within 24 h of ED admission.

Patient inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) MIMIC cohort: transferred to ICU within 24 h of ED admission. PKU cohort: transferred to the emergency resuscitation room (ED-based ICU) after ED admission. eICU cohort: transferred to the ICU from ED within 24 h of admission. Patient exclusion criteria included: (1) length of stay in ICU or ED-based ICU < 24 h; (2) delirium onset within 24 h of admission to the ICU or ED-based ICU; (3) diagnoses of cerebrovascular disease, traumatic brain injury, psychiatric conditions, or pregnancy; and (4) data on delirium assessment were not recorded.

In clinical practice, delirium is defined using a standardized two-step procedure. Firstly, sedation levels are assessed using the RASS. If RASS is −4 or −5, the patient will be reassessed later. If RASS ≥ −3, the CAM-ICU is applied. If the CAM-ICU assessment is positive, delirium is present. In the PKU cohort, trained nurses conducted standardized RASS and CAM-ICU assessments twice a day for each patient throughout their stay in the ED-based ICU. In the MIMIC cohort, delirium was identified via the “Delirium assessment” item recorded in the chartevents table. In the eICU cohort, delirium was identified from the nurseCharting table where the “Delirium Scale” item was recorded as CAM-ICU and the “Delirium Score” item indicated delirium outcome.

Method details

Study design

This multicenter study was designed to combine retrospective model development with multi-center external validation to develop and evaluate an LLM-driven MAS, DeLiriuMAgents, for predicting delirium risk among critically ill patients in emergency settings. The proposed system, DeLiriuMAgents, was designed to simulate multidisciplinary collaboration among virtual multidisciplinary clinicians and generate interpretable delirium risk assessments. This study followed the TRIPOD-LLM statement for reporting clinical prediction models incorporating LLMs,33 and the completed checklist is provided in Table S7.

The overall framework of DeLiriuMAgents consists of five components: (1) EHRPromptAgent: transforming structured EHR tabular data into clinically coherent textual narratives to facilitate downstream language model processing. (2) ModelPhysicianAgent: containing a pre-trained ML-based prediction model to obtain baseline delirium risk estimates, providing key contributing features and model confidence scores about the prediction. (3) Multidisciplinary specialist agents (EmergencyPhysicianAgent, NeurologyPhysicianAgent, and PsychiatryPhysicianAgent): each receiving the structured EHR summary and the ML model-generated prediction report, and producing independent risk assessments based on its domain expertise. (4) MedEvidenceAgent: using a RAG approach to search a medical knowledge base, including clinical guidelines, textbooks, and recent publications, and then providing relevant evidence to support the final decision. (5) DeliriumDecisionAgent: acting as the attending physician, synthesizing the reports from all three virtual specialists and the ML model-based agent to reach a decision. At the same time, it consulted the MedEvidenceAgent to retrieve external medical knowledge for evidence. Finally, it reconciles all agents’ outputs and generates an interpretable report on delirium risk for clinicians.

Study variables and data preprocessing

After consulting with clinical experts and considering data availability, we defined an initial set of 71 variables spanning demographics, chief complaints, comorbidities, physical examinations, laboratory tests, and treatment-related information. This variable set was used for two purposes in the DeLiriuMAgents: (1) to construct comprehensive textual descriptions of patients’ EHR data for LLM-based agents, and (2) to serve as the initial variable set for feature selection in ML model development for ModelPhysicianAgent. These variables are clinically relevant and commonly available in practice. Demographic variables included age, sex, and race. Chief complaints were recorded at the time of ED presentation and encompassed symptoms such as chest pain, abdominal pain, dyspnea, fever, syncope, fatigue, palpitation, hematemesis, gastrointestinal bleeding, altered consciousness, headache, vomiting, trauma, cough, sputum production, hemoptysis, and diarrhea. Comorbidities included cerebral infarction, epilepsy, Parkinson’s disease, coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, heart failure, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, liver cirrhosis, renal disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and malignant cancer. The physical examination data included vital signs, such as heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2). The level of consciousness was assessed using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), including eye, verbal, and motor responses, as well as the Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS). Laboratory tests included white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin concentration, hematocrit, platelet count, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin, total bilirubin, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, glucose, pH, partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), lactate level, and quantitative D-dimer. Treatment-related variables included the use of sedatives, antibiotics, corticosteroids, invasive procedures, and respiratory support. Respiratory support included low-flow oxygen therapy (nasal cannula), high-flow oxygen therapy (high-flow nasal cannula and oxygen mask), and mechanical ventilation (non-invasive and invasive).

To ensure temporal alignment with the prediction task, variable data were extracted as follows. For demographics, chief complaints, comorbidities, physical examinations, and laboratory tests, the first available values on ICU admission were used. For treatment-related variables, we adopted a dynamic windowing strategy. For patients who developed delirium during the ICU stay, data were collected within the 24 h preceding the first documented delirium. For patients who did not develop delirium, treatment data were extracted from the first 24 h of ICU admission. Variables with a data missing rate of more than 30% were excluded from the analysis. For categorical variables, missing data were imputed by the mode. For continuous variables, we applied Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) to preserve potential multivariate relationships and avoid bias introduced by complete-case analysis.

LLM-driven multi-agent system DeLiriuMAgents

EHRPromptAgent

EHRPromptAgent bridges the gap between structured data and natural language-based narratives. It translates structured EHR tabular data into clinically contextualized natural-language text that downstream agents can interpret. It integrates demographic and clinical variables, along with their reference ranges, measurement units, and directions of deviation (above, below, or within normal limits).

Input: Patient i’s structured EHR data Di.

Output: EHR summary in natural language: EHRTexti = EHRPromptAgent(Di).

ModelPhysicianAgent

ModelPhysicianAgent (MPA) acts as a model-augmented agent that interfaces with a pre-trained ML model for delirium prediction. A best-performing ML-based prediction model was developed as a decision-support tool, enabling the ModelPhysicianAgent to assess risk, generate structured predictions, and provide interpretable explanations through feature attribution. Seven ML algorithms, including Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), were employed to develop the data-driven prediction models.

Before model construction, four techniques were used to select model predictors from the initial 71 variables: (1) Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), selecting features with non-zero coefficients; (2) Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) based on model performance; (3) CatBoost-based feature importance ranking; and (4) SHAP values computed from the CatBoost model. For RFE, CatBoost, and SHAP methods, the top 40 ranked variables were retained. Only variables selected by all four methods were retained for ML model development. Model training was conducted exclusively on the model derivation cohort. Grid search was used for hyperparameter tuning. For external validation in the eICU cohort, samples with missing values in any of the variables used in the final model were excluded to ensure data quality. Model performance was evaluated on both the internal and external validation cohorts using AUROC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, Youden Index, and F1-score. For each performance metric, we generated 95% confidence intervals via 1,000 bootstrap resamples. To prioritize sensitivity in the early prediction of delirium in the ED-ICU, we selected an optimal threshold based on the model’s performance on the derivation cohort. Candidate thresholds were identified using grid search. That is, for each threshold, sensitivity, specificity, and the Youden index were calculated. Thresholds yielding sensitivity > 0.75 were retained, and the threshold with the highest Youden index was selected. The final model selection was based on a comprehensive assessment across all metrics. To assess the reliability and clinical applicability of the best-performing ML model, we also conducted model calibration, DCA, and reported the Brier score. The best-performing model was finally selected and configured to output the predicted probability, binary prediction label, and a ranked list of key contributing variables based on SHAP values. These outputs were fed into the ModelPhysicianAgent module and served as structured inputs for downstream specialist agents, supporting collaborative reasoning and clinical decision-making.

Specifically, the ML model provides the following prediction outputs: Delirium Risk Probability (DRP) as the estimated risk of developing delirium; Delirium Prediction Result (DPR), a binary label indicating whether delirium is predicted to occur or not; Model Confidence Score (MCS), approximated by the global discrimination value (AUROC), representing the model’s overall confidence in the predicted results; Key Feature Importance (KFI), a ranked list of top contributing variables determined via SHAP analysis, highlighting which features most influenced the prediction. It then wraps these outputs and generates a structured, natural language-based report that bridges quantitative prediction and qualitative clinical interpretation, making it usable in subsequent multi-agent reasoning.

Input: EHRTexti, and ML model outputs MLModel(Di)={DRPi,DPRi,MCSi,KFIi}.

Output: ReportMPA,i = LLM(roleMPA, promptMPA,EHRTexti, DRPi, DPRi, MCSi, KFIi).

Multidisciplinary specialist agents (EmergencyPhysicianAgent, NeurologyPhysicianAgent, and PsychiatryPhysicianAgent)

EmergencyPhysicianAgent (EPA), NeurologyPhysicianAgent (NPA), and PsychiatryPhysicianAgent (PPA) emulate the clinical reasoning processes of emergency, neurological, and psychiatric physicians, respectively. Built on LLMs with medical domain alignment, each agent receives both the patient’s EHR data and the structured interpretive report generated by the ModelPhysicianAgent. Rather than merely imitating role-based responses, these agents are designed to simulate discipline-specific diagnostic heuristics, internalize clinical guidelines, and reproduce human-like decision-making patterns rooted in their respective specialties. Drawing on different reasoning templates, such as syndrome pattern recognition in emergency care, neurocognitive trajectory assessment in neurology, and mental status formulation in psychiatry, the agents can make context-aware, structured predictions about delirium risk. Each agent independently synthesizes the available information, balances known risk and protective factors, interprets key features through a specialty-specific lens, and generates a detailed diagnostic report. These outputs are designed to reflect the diverse perspectives observed in real-world MDT consultations and serve as crucial components in the system’s collaborative deliberation process.

Input: EHRTexti, and ReportMPA,i.

Output: ReportEPA/NPA/PPA,i = LLM(roleEPA/NPA/PPA,promptEPA/NPA/PPA,EHRTexti, ReportMPA,i).

MedEvidenceAgent

MedEvidenceAgent (MEA) serves as a RAG module that dynamically queries a curated domain-specific knowledge base for authoritative medical evidence. This process enables the system to provide more objective medical evidence for reference in making final prediction decisions.

The RAG pipeline consists of three key stages: indexing, retrieval, and generation. (1) Indexing stage: A domain-specific medical knowledge base consisting of clinical guidelines, standard medical textbooks, and peer-reviewed articles was constructed by MedEvidenceAgent. These raw documents were first segmented into overlapping text chunks using a fixed-length sliding window, resulting in a corpus: D={d1,d2,…,dm}, m is the number of text chunks. Each chunk dj was encoded into a high-dimensional semantic embedding vector via the embedding model: vdj=Embed(dj),j=1,2,…,m. All document vectors {vdj} were stored in a local vector database for fast semantic retrieval. (2) Retrieval stage: The MedEvidenceAgent gets the list of structured queries from DeliriumDecisionAgent: QueryList = {q1,q2, …,qn}, n represents the number of queries. Each query qk is embedded using the same model: vqk=Embed(qk),k=1,2,…,n. Semantic similarity between each query vector and all document vectors is computed using cosine similarity: sim(vqk,vdj)=vqk·vdj|vqk|·|vdj|. The top-K most relevant document chunks are selected for each query based on similarity scores: Dqktop=TopK({sim(vqk,vdj)∣j=1,2,…,m}). (3) Generation stage: Each query qk and its retrieved evidence context Dqktop are concatenated and input into the LLM. The model synthesizes a concise, clinically coherent, evidence-grounded response: RAGResponseqk=LLM(qk,Dqktop). The full response set for all queries is: RAGResponsesi={RAGResponseqi1,RAGResponseqi2,…,RAGResponseqin}. These structured evidence-backed outputs are then forwarded to the MDT coordinator to support the final clinical decision-making and report generation.

The MedEvidenceAgent knowledge base was curated with input from clinicians and consisted of clinical guidelines, standard medical textbook content, and peer-reviewed literature. Detailed source information for the knowledge base is provided in Table S8. Texts were segmented using the tokenizer with a maximum chunk length of 600 tokens and a 150-token overlap. Each chunk was embedded using Zhipu-Embedding-3, generating 2048-dimensional vectors, and stored in a local vector store. During retrieval, each query was embedded using the same model, cosine similarity was calculated between the query vector and document vectors, and the top five most similar chunks were retrieved as evidence context. The retrieved context and query were then provided to DeepSeek-R1 to generate an evidence-grounded response.

Input: Question list formulated by the DeliriumDecisionAgent: QueryListi.

Output: RAGResponsei = LLM(roleMEA, promptMEA,QueryListi).

DeliriumDecisionAgent

DeliriumDecisionAgent (DDA) functions as the attending physician. It first reviews the reports of the three specialist agents to identify consistency or conflict in the coordination stage and in the generation of RAG queries. Based on this, it generates targeted medical queries to retrieve evidence from the knowledge base.

Input: ReportEPA,i, ReportNPA,i, ReportPPA,i.

Output: QueryListi = LLM(roleDDA-1,promptDDA-1, ReportEPA,i, ReportNPA,i, ReportPPA,i).

Finally, DeliriumDecisionAgent integrates the retrieved evidence with prior agent-generated prediction reports to produce a structured, evidence-based delirium risk assessment report during the stages of obtaining the RAG responses and generating final decisions.

Input: EHRTexti, AllReportsi(ReportMPA,i, ReportEPA,i, ReportNPA,i, ReportPPA,i), RAGResponsei.

Output: FinalReporti=LLM(roleDDA-2,promptDDA-2,EHRTexti,AllReportsi,RAGResponsei).

The detailed prompts of each agent are provided in Table S9. In the DeLiriuMAgents, we employed DeepSeek-R140 as the underlying LLM to support clinical reasoning and report generation across multiple agents and Zhipu-Embedding-3 as the embedding model in RAG. DeLiriuMAgents consists of seven individual agents that operate in a coordinated pipeline, each responsible for a distinct function in the overall reasoning process. A brief experiment on sensitivity to LLM choice was further conducted on the PKU cohort by implementing DeLiriuMAgents with an alternative open-source backbone LLM, Qwen3.5, and its performance was compared with that of the DeepSeek-R1-based DeLiriuMAgents under the same settings.

Ablation study

To assess the relative contribution of each component in the DeLiriuMAgents framework, we performed the ablation study. Four configurations were compared in the ablation study: (1) the full DeLiriuMAgents; (2) DeLiriuMAgents without the ModelPhysicianAgent; (3) DeLiriuMAgents without the MDT team, including three specialist agents: EmergencyPhysicianAgent, NeurologyPhysicianAgent, and PsychiatryPhysicianAgent; and (4) DeLiriuMAgents without MedEvidenceAgent, the external knowledge RAG module. This ablation study enabled a focused investigation into the synergistic effects of combining ML-based prediction, LLM-simulated multidisciplinary clinical specialist reasoning, and external medical evidence from domain knowledge to support explainable and interactive delirium risk prediction. The detailed experimental settings of the ablation study are provided in Table S10.

Chart review analysis and evaluation of AI reports

We conducted a chart review analysis and a structured evaluation of the AI reports generated by the DeLiriuMAgents based on 100 de-identified patients randomly selected from the PKU cohort. Five clinicians from Peking University Third Hospital with experience in emergency critical care were invited to independently assess these patients’ delirium risk, with each assessing 20 cases. This clinician assessment was conducted through a three-step procedure. In Step 1, clinicians reviewed one patient’s EHR and estimated the patient’s risk of developing delirium. In Step 2, based on the first step, clinicians reviewed the patient’s AI report generated by DeLiriuMAgents, and re-estimated the patient’s delirium risk. In Step 3, clinicians provided a structured evaluation of the AI report, covering clinical accuracy, reasoning quality, evidence traceability, usefulness for improving efficiency, report structure and readability, willingness to use in practice, and hallucination-related issues. We compared the results of three types of decision-making outputs: (1) unaided clinician judgments based on chart review alone, (2) the AI reports generated by DeLiriuMAgents, and (3) clinician judgments after reviewing the EHR and AI reports. The questionnaire used for the chart review analysis and AI report evaluation is provided in Table S11.

To evaluate the AI reports generated by DeLiriuMAgents, clinicians completed a structured questionnaire covering clinical accuracy, reasoning clarity, evidence traceability, usefulness for improving efficiency, report structure and readability, and willingness to use in real practice. Whether clinicians changed their judgments after reviewing the AI reports, the reasons for such changes, and the key items in the reports that mostly influenced their decisions were all recorded. In addition, an item asking clinicians whether the full DeLiriuMAgents report or an SHAP-only explanation would be more helpful for their understanding and decision-making was included in the survey.

To further evaluate hallucination-related problems, we selected 26 cases in which the final AI prediction was inconsistent with the observed delirium outcome, including 22 false-positive and 4 false-negative cases from the 100 de-identified cases used in the chart review analysis and evaluation of AI reports. During this analysis, we examined whether the AI reports: (1) contained information that directly conflicted with the EHR; (2) added clinical details that were not supported by the EHR; (3) described numerical values, units, or evidence incorrectly; (4) overestimated the patient’s delirium risk by over-interpreting some abnormal variables; or (5) underestimated the patient’s delirium risk by overlooking some important warning signs in the EHR.

Quantification and statistical analysis

In the data characteristics analysis, continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. McNemar’s exact test was used to compare paired correctness between models. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and F1-score. For the standalone ML model, AUROC was also calculated because it produced patient-level predicted probabilities. 95% confidence intervals for performance metrics were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. Calibration performance was assessed using calibration analysis and the Brier score, and potential clinical utility was evaluated using DCA.

To compare with established ICU delirium risk scores, we assessed the feasibility of using PRE-DELIRIC and E-PRE-DELIRIC to do risk assessment. The PRE-DELIRIC-based assessment was inapplicable due to data unavailability, and the E-PRE-DELIRIC model was evaluated using binary classification metrics at two predefined thresholds: 24.5% and 35%. The 24.5% threshold was selected because it has been reported in the literature as a cutoff for positive and negative outcomes, with relatively balanced sensitivity and specificity.14,15 The 35% threshold was selected because it corresponds to the high-risk boundary in the original E-PRE-DELIRIC risk stratification, where patients with predicted risk > 35% were classified as high risk.14

To assess the reproducibility of the LLM pipeline in DeLiriuMAgents, we randomly selected 100 patients from the PKU cohort and independently ran the full DeLiriuMAgents three times for each patient using the same input data. Run-to-run agreement of the final binary prediction label was assessed using Cohen’s κ across the three run comparisons. For the top key features, we extracted them from the “Key Variables” section of each run’s final report, and the overlap of key factors between repeated runs was calculated for the same patient.

Inter-agent agreement among the Emergency, Neurology, and Psychiatry agents was assessed in the correctly predicted cases in the PKU cohort. Pairwise agreement among specialist agents was assessed using Cohen’s κ. Three-agent agreement patterns were categorized as full agreement and 2-of-3 agreement. In 2-of-3 disagreement cases, we further calculated the proportion of cases in which each specialist agent matched the ground truth.

Runtime was measured for the optimized DeLiriuMAgents workflow. We recorded the approximate execution time of each major step, including EHRPromptAgent, ModelPhysicianAgent, the parallel specialist-agent stage, MedEvidenceAgent retrieval and response generation, and final DeliriumDecisionAgent report generation. Token usage was computed from API usage records. The cost estimate included all LLM calls in ModelPhysicianAgent, the three specialist agents, DeliriumDecisionAgent, and MedEvidenceAgent RAG generation. All data preprocessing, statistical analysis, and figure generation were conducted using Python version 3.10 and R version 4.3.0.
