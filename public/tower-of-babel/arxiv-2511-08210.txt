Forgetting Alternation and Blossoms: A New Framework for Fast Matching Augmentation and Its Applications to Sequential/Distributed/Streaming Computation
Taisuke Izumi, Naoki Kitamura, Yutaro Yamaguchi

Abstract
Finding a maximum cardinality matching in a graph is one of the most fundamental problems in the field of combinatorial optimization and graph algorithms. An algorithm proposed by Micali and Vazirani (1980) is well-known to solve the problem in O ⁡ ( m ​ n ) O(m\sqrt{n}) time, where n n and m m are the numbers of vertices and of edges, respectively, which is still one of the fastest algorithms in general. While the MV algorithm itself is not so complicated and is indeed convincing, its correctness proof is extremely challenging, which can be seen from the history: after the first algorithm paper had appeared in 1980, Vazirani has made several attempts to give a complete proof for more than 40 years. It seems, roughly speaking, caused by the nice but highly complex structure of the shortest alternating paths in general graphs that are deeply intertwined with the so-called (nested) blossoms. In this paper, we propose a new structure theorem on the shortest alternating paths in general graphs without taking into the details of blossoms. The high-level idea is to forget the alternation (of matching and non-matching edges) as early as possible. A key ingredient is a notion of alternating base trees (ABTs) introduced and utilized by the recent work of Izumi, Kitamura, and Yamaguchi (2024) to develop a nearly linear-time algorithm under one of the standard distributed computation models. Our structure theorem refines the properties of ABTs exploited in their algorithm, and we also give simpler alternative proofs for them. Based on our structure theorem, we propose a new algorithm under the standard sequential computation model, which is slightly slower but more implementable and much easier to confirm its correctness than the MV algorithm. As applications of our framework, we also present new ( 1 − ϵ ) (1-\epsilon) -approximation maximum matching algorithms for general graphs in the distributed and semi-streaming settings. In the CONGEST model, which is one of the standard distributed computation models, the proposed deterministic algorithm runs in O ~ ​ ( ϵ − 4 ) \tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-4}) rounds. The semi-streaming algorithm uses O ⁡ ( ϵ − 4 ) O(\epsilon^{-4}) passes and O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) -bit memory. Both algorithms are deterministic, and substantially improve the best known upper bounds. The algorithms are built on the top of a novel framework of amplifying approximation factors of given matchings, which is of independent interest.

Introduction

1.1
Background

Matching
is often recognized as one of the central topics in graph theory and algorithms, and particularly finding a maximum cardinality matching (MCM) is a very fundamental problem in that context.
In this paper, we consider the MCM problem for general graphs. While the problem is known to be polynomial-time solvable, we still lack the clear understanding and exposition of efficient MCM algorithms.
Looking back to the history of sequential algorithms, the seminal blossom algorithm by Edmonds
[
22
]
opened the door to polynomial-time algorithms for this problem.
After a few follow-up improvements
[
12
,
28
,
25
]
, the algorithm by Micali and Vazliani (MV algorithm) has been proposed in 1980
[
45
]
, which runs in
O(m\sqrt{n})
time (where
m
and
n
are the number of edges and vertices of the input graph).
This still achieves one of the best provable running time bound of deterministic computation of MCMs in general graphs, while a slightly better bound (by at most
\log n
factor) for dense graphs is known
[
32
]
.
However, the correctness proof of this algorithm is also known to be very complex: Since the original paper
[
45
]
lacks its full correctness proof, several follow-up arguments of “dispelling” its complication are attempted by one of the authors
[
54
,
55
,
56
]
, and the state-of-the-art complete proof results in the paper of almost 40 pages
[
56
]
.
There are a few attempts of exploring simpler algorithms
[
14
,
30
,
29
]
, but some of them seems incomplete (cf.
[
55
]
) and some is via the weighted problem, which is interesting but somewhat hides the essence of tractable feature of MCM itself.

We briefly review the approach of the MV algorithm, which is the most purely graph-theoretic algorithm attaining
O(m\sqrt{n})
time and related to our result.
The high-level strategy is to find a maximal set of disjoint
shortest augmenting paths
, resorting to the seminal analysis by Hopcroft and Karp
[
36
]
.
More precisely, they prove that
O(\sqrt{n})
iterations of matching augmentation by maximal sets of disjoint shortest augmenting paths finally reaches an MCM.
The MV algorithm computes such a set in
O(m)
time for general graphs, which is the most complicated part.

The difficulty lying behind this complication is threefold:
First, shortest alternating paths in general (i.e., non-bipartite) graphs does not satisfy the property of
BFS-honesty
: given a shortest alternating path, its subpath is not necessarily the shortest among the alternating paths of the same parity.
This obstacle prevents us from utilizing the standard tree-growth approach like as BFS, although it works
for bipartite matching case.
If we do not need a shortest augmenting path, this obstacle is addressed by the approach based on
blossoms
, which is originally introduced by Edmonds
[
22
]
.
In that approach, the algorithm makes a BFS-like forest rooted by free vertices (i.e., vertices without incident matching edges), and if the process encounters a blossom, the algorithm shrinks its odd cycle part into a single vertex and recurses.
This operation preserves the existence of augmenting paths, and thus finally the algorithm finds it in the shrunk graph (if any).
That path might contain a vertex associated with a shrunk odd cycle, and then the corresponding augmenting path in the original instance is obtained by expanding such vertices one by one.

The fundamental idea of Edmonds’ algorithm is clean and easy to follow, but there is a critical shortcoming that it does not necessarily provide a
shortest
augmenting path, mainly due to the existence of
nested
blossoms.
This causes the second difficulty, because it is crucial to find shortest augmenting paths to apply the analysis by Hopcroft and Karp
[
36
]
.
The operation of shrinking an odd cycle obscures the structure of shortest augmenting paths, and thus the MV algorithm handles blossoms carefully by introducing a notion of blossom size (called the
tenacity
).
Speaking very informally, the MV algorithm tries to manage the two (shortest) alternating paths terminating with a matching edge and a non-matching edge, which respectively referred to as an
\mathrm{even}
-alternating path and an
\mathrm{odd}
-alternating path.
The notion of blossom size is utilized in the MV algorithm for identifying a
smallest
blossom containing each vertex
t
, which also deduces the shortest
\mathrm{even}
- and
\mathrm{odd}
-alternating paths to
t
: intuitively, given a vertex
t
and a blossom
B
containing
t
strictly in its odd cycle,
B
contains both
\mathrm{even}
- and
\mathrm{odd}
-alternating paths. Under some appropriate definitions of blossoms and their sizes, those paths are guaranteed to be the shortest if
B
is the smallest.

However, as mentioned in
[
56
]
, this approach faces a “chicken-and-egg” problem, because we want to identify smallest blossoms to identify the shortest alternating paths, but we need the information of the length of shortest alternating paths to each vertex to identify the smallest size blossoms.
Furthermore, the situation is more complicated when trying to take a maximal set of shortest augmenting paths.
Consider the situation that a shortest augmenting path
P
is found.
To establish a maximal set of disjoint shortest augmenting paths, we further need to find another shortest augmenting path in the graph after the removal of
P
.
However, if a vertex
v
(not in
P
) is not contained in any shortest augmenting paths after the removal of
P
,
v
must be also removed for attaining
O(m)
running time (leaving it will waste the search cost).
In other words, the algorithm needs to know quickly which vertices still have a chance of organizing a shortest augmenting path.
To overcome these issues, the correctness proof of the MV algorithm is required to introduce a bunch of extra notions and needs a heavy inductive argument
[
56
]
.

1.2
Our Results

The key contribution of this paper is to present a new simple structure theorem for a maximal set of disjoint shortest augmenting paths, based on the approach recently proposed by Izumi, Kitamura, and Yamaguchi
[
39
]
.
Different from the traditional approaches stated above (Edmonds and MV), it does not maintain an
alternating forest
(with blossoms), which is a collection of trees rooted by a free vertex such that the path from roots to each vertex is a (shortest) alternating path.
Instead, it maintains yet another tree structure called
alternating base tree (ABT)
1
1

1

To be precise, this should be called an alternating base forest in an analogous way to an alternating forest, but throughout this paper we consider a single unified tree containing all components of such a forest by adding a super root
f
to the original graph. Hence we use terminology tree instead of forest.
.
Intuitively, an alternating base tree is a collection of the edges in the BFS-like tree growth part of Edmonds’ algorithm.
In other words, an ABT is a tree such that the edge from a vertex
u
to its parent is the last edge of a shortest alternating path from the free root vertex.
The concept of ABTs is originally presented by Kitamura and Izumi
[
40
]
, and its structural property is extended to find a
single
shortest
augmenting path
[
39
]
.
In this paper, we further generalize it so that one can utilize ABTs for computing a maximal set of disjoint shortest augmenting paths by extending the tree structure into the DAGs, as well as a shorter and concise proof of the structure theorem in
[
39
]
.
For explaining its impact, we informally state our extended structure theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(informal)
.

Let
U
be the set of all free nodes in the current matching system
(G,M)
, where
G=(V(G),E(G))
is the input graph and
M\subseteq E(G)
is the current matching.
Define an
alternating base DAG (ABD)

H
as the DAG such that the vertex set is
V(G)
and an edge from
u
to
v
is in
H
if and only if
v
is the immediate predecessor of
u
in a shortest
U
–
u
alternating path.
2
2

2

For the reader familiar with the MV algorithm, this
H
can be explained as the DAG consisting of the props (the edges giving the minlevel of one of the endpoints), in which the procedure DDFS (double depth first search) runs in the base case (before shrinking any blossoms). The important difference is that, in the MV algorithm, after shrinking some blossom, the DAG in which DDFS runs should be updated by changing the heads of some edges to skip the shrunk blossom later, but we do not need to care about such things in this paper.

Let
E^{\ast}\subseteq E(G)
be some appropriate set of edges not contained in
H
(which is formally defined later).
3
3

3

For the reader familiar with the MV algorithm, again, this
E^{*}
can be explained as the set of bridges for which DDFS results in a shortest augmenting path. We give a different characterization for such edges in terms of ABTs.

A
double path
is two vertex-disjoint paths to
U
staring from the two endpoints of an edge in
E^{\ast}
.
Given a maximal set
\mathcal{D}
of vertex-disjoint double paths in
H
, it can be efficiently transformed into a maximal set of vertex-disjoint shortest augmenting paths in
(G,M)
by a very simple algorithm.

Following the theorem above, we actually provide yet another MCM algorithm running in
O(m\sqrt{n}\log n)
time.
It is slightly worse than the MV algorithm, but more structured and admits a shorter and self-contained correctness proof.
A key distinguished advantage of our algorithm is higher modularity: With the aid of our structure theorem, the construction of a maximal set of disjoint shortest augmenting paths is decomposed into almost independent two phases.
The first phase is the construction of
H
.
In this phase, we only need to know the last edges of shortest
U
–
u
alternating paths to each vertex
u
, which can be easily identified only from the information of the lengths of shortest
\mathrm{odd}
- and
\mathrm{even}
-alternating paths from
U
to each vertex (cf. Lemma
3.1
).
Interestingly, this is only the part where we have to pay attention to alternation of paths.
In the second phase, one can completely forget alternating paths, where the task is reduced to a slight variant of the standard maximal disjoint path construction.
This simplification allows us to start the “plain” DDFS in the obtained DAGs without caring path alternation or blossoms (cf.
[
56
, Sections 2, 5.3, and 8.3.1]
), which mitigates the complication the MV algorithm faces.

As other applications of our framework, we also provide new
(1-\epsilon)
-approximation maximum matching algorithms
working in the distributed and semi-streaming settings. We obtain the following theorem:

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists:

•

a
(1-\epsilon)
-approximation maximum matching algorithm for a given graph
G
which runs in the CONGEST model
with
O(\epsilon^{-4}\mbox{\rm{MM}}(\epsilon^{-2}n))
rounds, where
\mbox{\rm{MM}}(N)
means the time complexity of computing
a maximal matching in graphs on
N
vertices;
the algorithm is deterministic except for the part of computing maximal matchings.

•

a deterministic
(1-\epsilon)
-approximation maximum matching algorithm for a given graph
G
which runs in the semi-streaming model
with
O(\epsilon^{-4})
passes and
O(n\log n)
-bit memory.

Using the known best algorithm,
\mbox{\rm{MM}}(N)
becomes
O(\log N)
in the randomized case, and
O(\log^{3/2}N)

in the deterministic case
[
31
]
. Our first result in the CONGEST model substantially improves the known best bound of

\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-10}\mbox{\rm{MM}}(n))
rounds
[
46
]
. In the semi-streaming model, there are two perspectives
on pass complexity — complexity depending on
n
, and independent of
n
. Our main focus is the latter one. Along that line,
the known best complexity is
O(\epsilon^{-6})
passes and
O(\epsilon^{-1}n\log n)
-bit space
[
47
]
, and our second result
also attains the substantial improvement.

As utilized in many approximation algorithms, our algorithm relies on the seminal analysis by Hopcroft and
Karp
[
36
]
, which states that if a matching
M
does not admit shortest augmenting paths
of length less than
\lceil 2\epsilon^{-1}\rceil
, one can conclude that
M
is a
(1-\epsilon)
-approximate
matching. Since augmentation by a maximal set of (vertex-)disjoint shortest augmenting paths increases the length of
shortest augmenting paths at least by two,
O(\epsilon^{-1})
iterations of the maximal set augmentation
yields the desired algorithm. It should be emphasized that deducing these algorithms is not straightforward even
utilizing our framework. In more details we face two technical difficulties for obtaining those: complication arising
from alternating paths in general graphs, and simultaneous construction of multiple augmenting paths with
guaranteeing maximality. While the first difficulty is substantially mitigated by our framework, the second one
is still challenging in distributed and semi-streaming settings. For coping with that, we present a new common framework
of amplifying approximation factors of given matchings, which is of independent interest.

Related Works

Sequential Algorithms

There is another line of designing fast algorithms for the MCM problem with the aid of matrix formulations.
Initiated by Mulmuley, Vazirani, and Vazirani
[
49

[

O(n^{\omega})

2.371339

[
5

Thus, for relatively dense graphs, randomization currently achieves an essential speeding-up for finding an MCM.
This approach is also extended to more general problems such as the so-called exact matching problem and the linear matroid parity problem
[

19

51

For the MCM problem for bipartite graphs, the Hopcroft–Karp algorithm
[
36
]
has been one of the fastest combinatorial (deterministic) algorithms for a long while (almost 50 years).
Very recently, on the top of the so-called Laplacian paradigm,
m^{1+o(1)}
-time flow algorithms have been developed
[
17
,
53
]

This immediately leads to an algorithm for finding an MCM in a bipartite graph with the same running time bound, which beats the Hopcroft–Karp bound in general.
More recently, Chuzhoy and Kahnna
[

21
]
also proposed more direct, combinatorial algorithms, which run in

time (deterministic) and

These breakthroughs raise a natural question: for computation of MCMs in general graphs, is there any analogous breakthrough beating the

Technical Outline

2.1
Notations and Terminologies

Throughout this paper, we only consider simple undirected graphs as the inputs.
An edge connecting two vertices
u
and
v
is denoted by
\{u,v\}
. We denote the vertex set and edge set of a given undirected graph
G
by
V(G)
and
E(G)
, respectively.
The terminology “path” is used for referring to a simple path. While a path is formally defined as an alternating sequence of vertices and edges as usual, it is often regarded as a subgraph of
G
. For a path
P=v_{0},e_{1},v_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{\ell},v_{\ell}
of
G
and an edge

e=\{v_{\ell},u\}\in E(G)
satisfying
u\not\in V(P)
, we denote by

P\circ e
or
P\circ u
the path obtained by adding
e
and
u
to the tail of
P
. Similarly, for another path
P^{\prime}
starting at
v_{\ell}
with
V(P)\cap V(P^{\prime})=\{v_{\ell}\}
, we denote by
P\circ P^{\prime}
the path obtained by concatenating
P^{\prime}
to the tail of
P
(without duplication of
v_{\ell}
). The inversion of the path
P

(i.e., the path
v_{\ell},e_{\ell},v_{\ell-1},e_{\ell-1},\dots,e_{1},v_{0}
) is denoted by
\overline{P}
. Given a path
P
containing two vertices
u
and
v
such
that
u
precedes
v
, we denote by
P[u,v]
the subpath of
P
starting from
u
and terminating with
v
.

For a graph
G
, a
matching

M\subseteq E(G)
is
a set of edges that do not share endpoints. We denote by
\mu(G)
the maximum size of a matching of a graph
G
. A vertex
v
is called

if
v
has no incident edge in
M
.
Given a pair
\mathcal{I}=(G,M)
, which we refer to as a
matching system
, an
alternating path
of
\mathcal{I}
is a path
P=v_{0},e_{1},v_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{\ell},v_{\ell}
such that exactly one of

and

is in
M
for every
1<i\leq\ell
.
An
augmenting path
of
\mathcal{I}
is an alternating path connecting two different free
vertices.
Throughout this paper, we introduce a super free vertex
f

that is connected with each free vertex by a length-two alternating path (where the edge incident to
f
is a non-matching edge).
While the graph after adding
f
only contains a single free vertex
f
, we use terminology
“free vertex” for referring to free vertices in the original system. The set of all free

. Since
|U_{\mathcal{I}}|\leq 1
implies that

is a maximum matching, we usually assume
|U_{\mathcal{I}}|\geq 2
.
Similarly, an “augmenting path” is also defined as an alternating path connecting two vertices in

We denote the length of a shortest augmenting path in
\mathcal{I}
by
2\ell_{\mathcal{I}}+1
; thus,
\ell_{\mathcal{I}}
is the number of matching edges contained in a shortest augmenting path.

An alternating path of odd (resp. even) length is called an
odd-alternating path

(resp.
even-alternating path
).
We denote the length of the shortest odd (resp. even)-alternating path from
f
to a vertex

in
\mathcal{I}
by

(resp.
\mathrm{dist}^{\mathrm{even}}_{\mathcal{I}}(u)
). If

Refinement of the Izumi–Kitamura–Yamaguchi Theorem

Our new theorem is bulit on the top of the structure theorem by Izumi, Kitamura, and

Definition 2.1

(Alternating Base Tree)

Note that ABTs do

A trivial but important observation holding for any alternating base tree

to

using an edge not contained in

]

For any edge

We then define the

5

This is slightly modified from the definition in

The IKY theorem shows that picking up any

The formal statement of the IKY theorem is below:

Theorem 2.3

(Izumi, Kitamura, and Yamaguchi

Let

Since the proof of Theorem

2.3
Extension to a Maximal Set of Disjoint Shortest Augmenting Paths

Let
T
be any ABT, and recall that
\ell_{\mathcal{I}}
denotes the number of matching edges contained in a shortest augmenting path of
\mathcal{I}=(G,M)
.
We denote by
F_{T}
the forest obtained by removing
f
and its neighbors (equivalently,
the set of subtrees
T(u)
for all
u\in U_{\mathcal{I}}
) and by
\mathsf{r}_{T}(v)
the root of the tree in
F_{T}

containing
v\in V(G)
.
Considering simultaneous construction of multiple shortest augmenting paths following the IKY theorem, it
has a shortcoming that the prefix of the path
P[f,y]
is “not localized”, which means that
P[f,y]

may cross many components in
F_{T}
. This can be an obstacle to establish a collection of disjoint augmenting
paths. The key observation toward Theorem
1.1
is that
such a case never occurs if
e^{\ast}
taken in Theorem
2.3
is critical (i.e.,

e^{\ast}\not\in\mathsf{EP}_{\mathcal{I}}
). In such a case, the augmenting path found by the approach of
Theorem
2.3
necessarily lies in the two components in
F_{T}
bridged by
e^{\ast}
.
Even better, if
e^{\ast}
is critical, it is not necessary to take a minimum-level one, but suffices
to take a minimum-volume one, which is equal to
2\ell_{\mathcal{I}}+5
by a simple argument
(cf. Lemma
3.8
).

This observation straightforwardly yields the double-path argument
in Theorem
1.1
. We take all the critical edges of volume
2\ell_{\mathcal{I}}+5
which
can cross two subtrees in
F_{T}
for some
T
as the set
E^{\ast}
, and consider the
construction of a double path
(P,Q)
for
e=\{y,z\}\in E^{\ast}
in the ABD
H
(for the formal definitions of double paths and ABDs, see Section
3.2
).
Since all the edges in the double path are contained in
\mathsf{EP}_{\mathcal{I}}
, one can construct an ABT
T

respecting

(P,Q)
, i.e., an ABT containing
P
and
Q
as its paths. Then
e
is a non-tree edge of
T

bridging two components
T(\mathsf{r}_{T}(y))
and
T(\mathsf{r}_{T}(z))
in
F_{T}
(
\mathsf{r}_{T}(y)\neq\mathsf{r}_{T}(z)
).
The application of the augmenting-path construction algorithm by Theorem
2.3

yields the transformation of a double path
(P,Q)
into a shortest augmenting path from

\mathsf{r}_{T}(y)
to
\mathsf{r}_{T}(z)
lying
within
T(\mathsf{r}_{T}(y))
and
T(\mathsf{r}_{T}(z))
(cf. Algorithm
2
).
Importantly, this construction can be applied simultaneously to
the set
\mathcal{D}
of vertex-disjoint double paths: One can construct an ABT
T
respecting
all the double paths in
\mathcal{D}
(which is obviously possible because
\mathcal{D}
is vertex-disjoint),
and transform
\mathcal{D}
into a set of shortest augmenting paths simultaneously. They all lie at
the insides of distinct subtrees, and thus are disjoint. Theorem
1.1

is proved by showing that this transformation preserves the maximality.

2.4
An Overview of a New MCM Algorithm

2.4.1
Whole Structure

We present a new MCM algorithm based on our structure theorem, which runs in

O(m\sqrt{n}\log n)
time. As the MV algorithm, it iterates the construction of a maximal
set of disjoint shortest augmenting paths. The seminal analysis by Hopcroft and Karp
[
36
]
guarantees
that
O(\sqrt{n})
iterations suffices to reach an MCM.
Hence our aim is constructing a maximal set of disjoint shortest augmenting paths in
O(m\log n)
time.
By Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to construct a maximal set of disjoint double paths.

1.

Compute
\mathrm{dist}^{\theta}(u)
for all
u\in V(G)
and
\theta\in\{\mathrm{even},\mathrm{odd}\}
, and identify
the edge set
\mathsf{EP}_{\mathcal{I}}
(and
H
), which includes the identifiaction of value
2\ell+5
(by
Corollary
3.9
).

2.

Following the information on
H
, compute a maximal set

\mathcal{D}
of disjoint double paths. If
\mathcal{D}
is empty, the algorithm terminates.

3.

Construct an ABT
T
respecting all double paths in
\mathcal{D}
, and find any MIE of
T(u)
for all

u\in V(H)
.

4.

Augment the current matching by the set
\mathsf{Aug}(\mathcal{D})
of disjoint shortest augmenting paths obtained from
\mathcal{D}
.

We have already explained that the last step is implemented with
O(m)
time. Hence we see
the technical outline of other steps.

2.4.2
Step 1: Computing Orthodox/Unorthodox Distance

The hardest part in computing distance information is the computation of
\mathrm{dist}^{\beta}(u)
. Our algorithm
computes it through an observation derived from Theorem
2.3
, which states that for any
v\in V(G)

admitting unorthodox alternating path from
f
to
v
, any ABT
T
, and an MIE
e^{\ast}
of
T(v)
,
\mathrm{dist}^{\beta}(v)=\mathsf{vlevel}(e^{\ast})-\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(v)
holds (cf. Corollary
3.9
). That is, the identification of an
MIE of
T(v)
implies the computation of
\mathrm{dist}^{\beta}(v)
. Our algorithm grows up an ABT
T
in
the round-by-round manner. At round
r
, it identifies all the pairs
(v,\theta)
satisfying
\mathrm{dist}^{\theta}(v)=r

and all the edges
e
of height
r
. The main technical challenge lies at the point that we need to conduct it concurrently with the growth of
T
, because the value of
\mathrm{dist}^{\beta}(v)
is also necessary to identify the distances of its children for computing
\mathsf{vlevel}(e^{\ast})
. A straightforward idea of implementing this approach is
as follows: when the volume of a non-tree edge
e
is determined, the algorithm checks all the vertices
t

such that
e
can be an MIE of
T(t)

by upward traversal of
T
from the two endpoints of

e
to
f
. However, this idea is not efficient because the upward search might check a vertex
t^{\prime}

which has already decided
\mathrm{dist}^{\beta}(t^{\prime})
again. To avoid such a wasting search, our algorithm adopts a
simple strategy of contracting an edge
\{t,\mathsf{p}_{T}(t)\}
, i.e., shrinking the two vertices into a single vertex, when
\mathrm{dist}^{\beta}(t)
is decided. Not only
for saving search cost, this strategy also provides a simpler mechanism for identifying MIEs:
By repeating tree edge contraction, any MIE
e^{\ast}
of
T(t)
eventually becomes an edge incident to

t
earlier than round
\mathrm{dist}^{\beta}(t)
(cf. Lemma
4.3
). Hence
\mathrm{dist}^{\beta}(t)
is
correctly decided only by checking the minimum-level edge incident to
t
when the set of incident edges and
their volumes are modified (where self-loops incident to
t
are excluded, because they are the edges
incident to a vertex in
T(t)

but not incoming edges of
T(t)
).
This contraction strategy is implemented by two data structures:
The contraction operation naturally induces a partition of
V(G)
, where each subset is a vertex set shrunk into the same vertex.
It is managed by the standard union-find data structure.
The set of incident non-tree edges for each (shrunk) vertex is managed by any meldable heap supporting
find-min
,
merge
,
add
, and
delete-min
with
O(\log n)
-amortized time.

Note on CONGEST/semi-streaming Implementation

Our distance computation algorithm is naturally transformed into the ones for CONGEST
and semi-streaming settings. Basically, the algorithm performs the two tasks of
expanding
T
and aggregation of the volume information of MIEs along
T
, which are
conducted in the round-by-round manner. Hence the aforementioned models can easily emulate one round of the sequential implementation with a bundle of rounds/passes (cf. Section
5.2
). It provides CONGEST/semi-streaming implementations
of bounded-length distance computation, which outputs all orthodox/unorthodox distances up to
2\ell+5
. The running time of the CONGEST implementation is
O(\ell^{2})
rounds, and the number of passes taken by the semi-streaming
implementation is
O(\ell)
. Note that in the CONGEST model we need an extra
O(\ell)

factor due to the simulation of shrunk vertices, each of which correspond to
a subgraph of diameter
O(\ell)
.

2.4.3
Step 2: Computing a Maximal Set of Disjoint Double Paths

This part is almost the same as construction of a maximal set of disjoint shortest augmenting paths by the
double DFS (DDFS)
in
[
45
,
56
]
.
The only difference is that they start it in DAGs involving nested blossoms already shrunk during the computation of
\mathrm{dist}^{\theta}(v)
(which are called the oddlevel/evenlevel of
v
in
[
45
,
56
]
), but we first do it simply in ABDs (and then, in the later step, transform the obtained double paths into shortest augmenting paths in the original graph).
Our algorithm iteratively grows a set of disjoint double paths until no further disjoint double path is found.
More precisely, for each critical edge
e=\{y,z\}
of volume
2\ell+5
(which is not necessarily crossable), we run the DDFS on
H
with starting vertices
y
and
z
.
Here we explain its properties our algorithm utilizes, without stating the implementation details:
It outputs a double path
D=(P,Q,y,z)
or a
bottleneck

v
, and an
omissible set

W\subseteq V(H)
, which will be removed if
D
is found and shrunk into
v
otherwise.
Intuitively,
W
is a set of vertices
w
such that any
w
–
U
path necessarily intersects some vertex already removed,
V(D)
, or
v
.
Thus, if
D
is found,
V(D)\subseteq W
and we just remove
W
.
Otherwise,
W
contains the vertices reachable from
y
or
z
except for
v
and we shrink
W
into
v
.
For the resulting graph
H
after deletion/shrinking, the next try of DDFS is performed for another critical edge
e^{\prime}
of volume
2\ell+5
(if at least one of the endpoints has been removed or both of them have been shrunk into the same vertex, it is simply skipped).
The running time of the DDFS algorithm is
O(|E_{W}|)
, where
E_{W}
is the set of edges incident to a vertex in
W
.
Since the vertices in
W
do not join the subsequent runs of DDFS, whole running time (up to the construction of a maximal set of disjoint double paths) is
O(m)
.

2.4.4
Step 3: Identification of MIEs

The strategy of Step 3 is almost the same as Step 1: Let
T
be any given ABT, and each vertex
v\in V(T)
manages the
set of incident non-tree edges by an efficient meldable heap. The contraction of each tree edge from the leaf side
obviously admits the identification of an MIE for each subtree
T(v)
(
v\in V(T)
). That is, the minimum element
in the heap at any leaf vertex
v
is an MIE of
T(v)
. This step is implemented within
O(m\log n)
time.

2.5
Overview of Approximate MCM

This section sketches how our framework is utilized for leading an efficient algorithm for
(1-\epsilon)
-approximation of a maximum matching in distributed and semi-streaming settings.
For simplicity, we assume that
\epsilon
is represented as
\epsilon=2^{-x}
for some integer
x>0
.

Following the Hopcroft–Karp analysis, our
(1-\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm aims
to obtaining a matching not admitting an augmenting path of length shorter than
2\epsilon^{-1}
,
which is accomplished by iteratively computing a maximal set of shortest augmenting paths. However,
as mentioned in the introduction, it is not so easy to take a maximal set of disjoint paths in
distributed or streaming settings. While many known algorithms
[
44
,
27
,
46
]
in this context
try to find a substantially large number of short augmenting paths not limited to shortest ones for
circumventing maximality requirement, our framework helps only for finding shortest augmenting paths,
and thus one cannot adopt such an approach. Instead, our algorithm outputs
a
hitting set

B\subseteq V(G)
, which a vertex subset any shortest augmenting path in
(G,M)
intersects,
in addition to a set
\mathcal{Q}
of disjoint shortest augmenting paths.
More precisely, one can construct an algorithm
\textsc{AugAndHit}_{\ell}(G,M)
of
outputting a set
\mathcal{Q}
of shortest augmenting paths of length at most
2\ell+1
and
a hitting set
B
of size at most
16|\mathcal{Q}|(\ell+1)^{2}+|M|/(4(\ell+1))
(if the length of
shortest augmenting paths is larger than
2\ell+1
, it returns empty
\mathcal{Q}
). This
algorithm is implemented in the CONGEST model with
O(\ell^{2}\mbox{\rm{MM}}(n))

rounds, where
\mbox{\rm{MM}}(n)
is the running time of computing a maximal
matching of graphs on
n
vertices, and implemented
in the semi-streaming model with
O(n\log n)
-bit space and
O(\ell^{2})
paths.
The formal description is given in Lemma
5.3
, and its proof and implementation details are shown in Section
5.4
.

Using
\textsc{AugAndHit}_{\ell}(G,M)
, we construct an algorithm
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
, which takes a parameter
\alpha>0
(represented as a power of two) and any matching
M
such that
|M|=(1-\alpha^{\prime})\mu(G)
holds for some
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
, and outputs a matching
M^{\prime}
of
G
whose approximation factor is at least

\min\{(1-\alpha^{\prime}+\Theta(\alpha^{2})),(1-\alpha/2)\}
(Lemma
5.4
).
Iteratively applying
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}

O(\alpha^{-1})
times, we obtain a
(1-\alpha/2)
-approximate matching.
Starting with any maximal matching (which is a
1/2
-approximate matching) and calling that iteration process
O(\log\epsilon^{-1})
times with
\alpha=1/2,1/4,1/8,\dots 1/\epsilon^{-1}
, we finally obtain a
(1-\epsilon)
-approximate matching.

We sketch the outline of
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
(see Section
5.3
for the details).
The execution of
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
consists of repetition of
K=4\alpha^{-1}

phases
with modification of the input graph.
Let
(G_{i},M_{i})
be the matching system at the beginning of the
i
-th phase.
The behavior of the
i
-th phase consists of the following three steps:

1.

Path finding
: Call
\textsc{AugAndHit}_{K}(G_{i},M_{i})
and find a set of shortest augmenting paths
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
and a hitting set
B_{i}
.

2.

Length stretch
: For each vertex
v\in B_{i}
, if
v
is not free, the algorithm subdivides the matching edge incident to
v
into a length-three alternating path such that the middle edge is a non-matching edge. Otherwise, we rename
v
into
v^{\prime}
, and add a length-two alternating path to
v^{\prime}
where the edge incident to
v^{\prime}
is a matching edge and the endpoint of the path other than
v^{\prime}
becomes the new
v
. The resultant graph and matching is denoted by
(G_{i+1},M^{\prime}_{i})
.

3.

Augmentation
: Obtain a matching
M_{i+1}
in
G_{i+1}
by augmenting
M^{\prime}_{i}
along augmenting paths corresponding to
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
.

After the
K
phases, the algorithm finally executes the
recovery
step.
In this step, the algorithm iteratively restores each subdivided (or extended) part, and finally outputs an augmented matching
\hat{M}_{1}
in the original graph
G_{1}
.

Since almost all parts of
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
is to execute
\textsc{AugAndHit}_{\ell}
, its
CONGEST/semi-streaming implementations are straightforwardly deduced from Lemma
5.3
.
By a simple complexity analysis, we obtain Theorem
1.2
.

In the following argument,

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
u\in V(T)\setminus\{f\}
, the following properties hold:

(P1)

For any
u^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}(u)
,
\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(u)>\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(u^{\prime})
holds, which also implies that the construction in the definition of alternating base trees certainly provides a spanning tree. It also implies

\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(u)<\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(u^{\prime\prime})
for any descendant
u^{\prime\prime}
of
u
in any ABT
T
.

(P2)

For any neighbor
u^{\prime}
of
u
,
\mathrm{dist}^{\overline{\alpha(u)}}(u^{\prime})+1=\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(u)
holds if and only if
u^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}(u)
holds.

Proof.

Proof of (P1)
: By the definition of
\mathsf{EP}(u)
, there exists an alternating path
P
from
f
to
u
of length
\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha(u)}(u)
whose last two vertices are
u^{\prime}
and
u
. Hence
P[f,u^{\prime}]
is an alternating path from
f
to
u^{\prime}
of length less than
\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha(u)}(u)
. It implies
\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha(u^{\prime})}(u^{\prime})<\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha(u)}(u)
.

Proof of (P2)
: We first prove the direction
\Rightarrow
. Let
P
be a shortest
\overline{\alpha(u)}
-alternating path from
f
to
u^{\prime}
. For any vertex
v\in V(P)
,
\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(v)\leq|P|<\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(u)
holds.
Thus,
P
does not contain
u
, and
P\circ\{u^{\prime},u\}
is an
\alpha(u)
-alternating path from
f
to
u
of length
\mathrm{dist}^{\overline{\alpha(u)}}(u^{\prime})+1=\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(u)
, i.e., it is a shortest orthodox alternating path and thus
u^{\prime}\in\mathsf{P}(u)
holds.

Next, we prove the direction
\Leftarrow
. Let
P
be any
\alpha(u)
-alternating path from
f
to
u
terminating with edge
\{u^{\prime},u\}
.
Then the prefix
P[f,u^{\prime}]
must be a shortest
\overline{\alpha(u)}
-alternating path from
f
to
u^{\prime}
as follows.
Suppose to the contrary that there exists a shorter
\overline{\alpha(u)}
-alternating path
P^{\prime}
from
f
to
u^{\prime}
.
Then,
P^{\prime}
does not contain
u
as observed in the proof of
\Rightarrow
, and hence one can obtain a shorter
\alpha(u)
-alternating path from
f
to
u
than
P
by replacing
P[f,u^{\prime}]
with

, a contradiction.
Thus,
\mathrm{dist}^{\overline{\alpha(u)}}(u^{\prime})+1=\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(u)
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For any edge
e=\{u,v\}
, the following properties hold:

(P1)

\mathrm{dist}^{\rho(e)}(u)+1\geq\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(v)
holds.

(P2)

If

,
\mathrm{dist}^{\rho(e)}(u)+1>\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(v)

Proof.

Proof of (P1)
: Let

be any shortest
\rho(e)
-alternating path from

to

Since

\mathrm{dist}^{\rho(e)}(u)+1\geq\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(v)

Lemma 3.3

For any

\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(t)+\mathrm{dist}^{\beta}(t)\geq\mathsf{vlevel}(e^{\ast}_{T}(t))

Proof.

Let

be any shortest unorthodox alternating path from

By the definition of
\mathsf{EP}

.

Let

. The proof follows the induction on
\mathrm{dist}^{\theta}(t)
.

Basis:

Consider the case when
\mathrm{dist}^{\theta}(t)=1
.
In this case,
t
is a neighbor of
f
and
\theta=\alpha(t)
.
The shortest alternating path from
f
to

consists of a single tree edge

, whose level is zero by definition.
Hence the theorem obviously holds.

Induction step:

Suppose as the induction hypothesis that the theorem holds for any
(t^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})
such that
\mathrm{dist}^{\theta^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})<k
, and consider
(t,\theta)
such that
\mathrm{dist}^{\theta}(t)=k
.

(Case 1)
When
\theta=\alpha(t)
:
Let

be the maximal upward alternating path from
t
in

, and

be the upper endpoint of

(Fig.
5
).
The maximality of

implies that both

and the last edge
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
of

are non-matching edges.
By the definition of
\mathsf{P}
and Lemma
3.1
(P2),
\overline{Q}
must be the suffix of a shortest alternating path

from
f
to

.
Since the length of

is positive,

|P[f,v]|<|P|=k
holds. Then one can apply the theorem for

by the induction hypothesis, which results
in a shortest
\mathrm{even}
-alternating path

from

to

of level at most
\mathsf{level}(e^{\prime})
, where

is any edge in

By applying Lemma

, and thus the volume of

is less than
\mathsf{vlevel}(e^{\ast})

(Case 2)
When

\mathrm{dist}^{\beta}(t)>\mathsf{hlevel}(e^{\ast})\geq\mathrm{dist}^{\rho(e^{\ast})}(y)

\rho(e^{\ast})
-alternating path

to

\mathsf{level}(e^{\ast}_{T}(y))

is smaller than
\mathsf{level}(e^{\ast})
. Otherwise, we have

.
Thus, in any case, we have

.
Similarly, one can also obtain

Theorem 3.14

Let

3.3

As explained in Section

.

Let

be a vertex with a crossable edge in

Let

.
By Corollary

, it suffices to show that there exists an ABT

Now we are ready to prove (the formal version of) Theorem

Theorem
1.1

(formal)
.

For any maximal set
\mathcal{D}
of vertex-disjoint double paths,

\mathsf{Aug}(\mathcal{D})
is a maximal set of disjoint shortest augmenting paths.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
\mathsf{Aug}(\mathcal{D})
is not maximal. Then, there exists a shortest augmenting path
P
such that
\{P\}\cup\mathsf{Aug}(\mathcal{D})
is still a set of
vertex-disjoint shortest augmenting paths. Let
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
be the first and last vertices of
P
.
We consider the following two cases:

(Case 1)
When
P
does not intersect any double paths in
\mathcal{D}
:
By Lemma
3.16
,
P
contains a unique edge in
E(P)\cap E^{\ast}
,
which is denoted by
\{y,z\}
. Let
T
be any ABT respecting

\mathcal{D}
, and
T^{\prime}
be any ABT containing all the edges of volume
2\ell+5
in

\mathsf{EP}\cap E(P)
, which is constructed from
T
by Corollary
3.17
.
The paths
Y
from
y
to
u_{0}
and
Z
from
z
to
u_{1}
in

forms a double path

D=(Y,Z,y,z)
in

. By Corollary
3.17
, the construction of
T^{\prime}

changes only the parents of the vertices in
P
. Hence
T^{\prime}
still respects
\mathcal{D}
. That is,

\{D\}\cup\mathcal{D}
is a set of disjoint double paths, but it contradicts the maximality of

\mathcal{D}
.

(Case 2)
When
P
intersects a double path in
\mathcal{D}
: Letting
e
be the unique edge in
E(P)\cap E^{\ast}
,
we represent
P
as

, where
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
do not contain any edge in
E^{\ast}
.
By symmetry, we assume that
P_{1}
intersects a double path in
\mathcal{D}
, and define

P_{1}
as
u_{0}=v_{0},e_{1},v_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{k},v_{k}
.
Let
T
be any ABT respecting
\mathcal{D}
.
We obtain an ABT
T^{\prime}
from
T
, which still respects
\mathcal{D}
and
respects
P_{1}
“as much as possible”: for any
v_{i}\in V(P_{1})\setminus\{v_{0},v_{k}\}
,
if
v_{i}
is not a vertex in
V(\mathcal{D})
and
v_{i+1}\in\mathsf{P}(v_{i})
(resp.
v_{i-1}\in\mathsf{P}(v_{i})
) holds,
v_{i}
chooses
v_{i+1}
(resp.
v_{i-1}
) as
its parent. Let
\{v_{j},v_{j+1}\}
be the first edge in
\tilde{E}_{T^{\prime}}(u_{0})\cap E(P_{1})
, so that

is contained in
T^{\prime}(u_{0})
.
Since
\mathsf{r}_{T^{\prime}}(v_{j+1})\neq\mathsf{r}_{T^{\prime}}(v_{j})=u_{0}
,
\{v_{j},v_{j+1}\}
is a crossable edge (by Lemma
3.8
and Corollary
3.9
).
As
\{v_{j},v_{j+1}\}\not\in E^{\ast}
, we have
\{v_{j},v_{j+1}\}\in\mathsf{EP}
by definition.
Then, by the construction of
T^{\prime}
, there exists a double path
D=(Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}
hitting
v_{j}
or
v_{j+1}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
Y^{\prime}
is the path hitting
v_{j}
or
v_{j+1}
.

Suppose that
Y^{\prime}
hits
v_{j}
. Since
T^{\prime}
respects
D
,
Y^{\prime}
is a path in
T^{\prime}
as well as in

, and thus

\mathsf{r}_{T}(y^{\prime})=\mathsf{r}_{T^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})=u_{0}
holds. It contradicts that
P
is disjoint from
\mathsf{Aug}(\mathcal{D})
.
Otherwise,
Y^{\prime}
hits
v_{j+1}
.
If
v_{j+1}\in\mathsf{P}(v_{j})

\mathsf{p}_{T^{\prime}}(v_{j})=v_{j+1}
(as
Y^{\prime}
does not hit
v_{j}
), contradicting
\mathsf{r}_{T^{\prime}}(v_{j})\neq\mathsf{r}_{T^{\prime}}(v_{j+1})
.
Thus we obtain

and then
\mathsf{vlevel}(e^{\ast}_{T^{\prime}}(v_{j+1}))=2\ell+5
by Lemma
3.18
.
In the construction by
DoubleToAug
,

is transformed into an alternating path

from

to

. Hence

leaves

through the edge

must contain

is disjoint from
\mathsf{Aug}(\mathcal{D})
, again.

4.2
Sequential Algorithm for Step 2

The DDFS algorithm we utilize is the same as the one proposed in
[
45
,
56
]
.
We explain here its outline for self-containedness. Let
H
be the input DAG, and
\phi\colon V(H)\to\mathbb{N}

be any function such that
(v_{1},v_{2})\in E(H)
implies
\phi(v_{1})>\phi(v_{2})
. In our use, the value of

\mathrm{dist}^{\alpha}(\cdot)
can be used as the function of
\phi
.
The DDFS on
H
starting from vertices
y
and
z
executes two parallel DFSs with root
y
and
z

in a coordinated manner. We denote by
T_{y}
and
T_{z}
the constructed DFS trees respectively rooted by
y
and
z
,
and
h_{y}
and
h_{z}
be their search heads. The coordination strategy is summarized as follows:

•

At each step, the algorithm proceeds the tree growth of
T_{x}
for
x\in\{y,z\}
such that
x=\argmax_{x\in\{y,z\}}\phi(h_{x})
holds. The tree growth process follows the standard DFS procedure:
The algorithm finds a neighbor of
h_{x}
not occupied by any of
T_{y}
and
T_{z}
.
If it is found,
T_{x}
is expanded. Otherwise, it performs backtrack.

•

Suppose that
\phi(h_{z})<\phi(h_{y})
holds. Then the construction of
T_{y}
continues.
If
h_{y}
failed to find a path to
U
,
h_{y}
finally goes back to
y
. At this timing, it is hasty to conclude that
\{y,z\}
does not admit any double path, because an
x
–
U
path is found by the backtrack of
h_{z}
and renouncing the vertex
v
at which
h_{z}
stays might provide a
y
–
U
path.
Hence if
h_{y}
goes back to
y
, the tree
T_{y}
steals the vertex
v
, with backtracking of
h_{z}
.
Under the condition that we appropriately process an omissible set in each of the previous iterations so that for each remaining vertex
v^{\prime}
there exists at least one
v^{\prime}
–
U
path in
H
disjoint from any double paths already found,
T_{y}
necessarily has a neighbor of
v
; thus adding
v
to
T_{y}
is seen as an expansion of
T_{y}
following the standard DFS procedure.
The intuitive explanation of this fact is as follows:
Let
B
be the set of the immediate predecessors of
v
in
H
.
Obviously,
T_{z}
contains a vertex in
B
.
If
T_{y}
does not contain any vertex in
B
,
T_{z}
cannot grow up for containing
v
, because
h_{y}
searches only the predecessors of
B
in
H
and thus
\phi(h_{y})>\phi(h_{z})
holds as long as
h_{z}
is within
B
.

After
v
is taken by
T_{y}
,
h_{z}
tries to find a path to
U
not containing
v
.
If it fails and
h_{z}
is backtracked to
z
, one can conclude that both
T_{y}
and
T_{z}
need to contain
v
for their further expansion.
Hence
v
is the bottleneck, and we shrink the associated omissible set
W=(V(T_{y})\cup V(T_{z}))\setminus\{v\}
into

.
Otherwise, the DDFS search continues. In the subsequent execution,
v
behaves as the root of

, for avoiding that
h_{y}
backtracks the predecessor of
v
.
When both
h_{y}
and
h_{z}
reach
U
, a double path
(P,Q,y,z)
is found.
Due to the fundamental property of the standard DFS, any vertex
v^{\prime}
backtracked does not have a
v^{\prime}
–

path not intersecting vertices already visited.
Hence
V(T_{y})\cup V(T_{z})
should be included in the associated omissible set

.
Furthermore, while there exists a vertex

such that for every edge
(v^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime})
the head
v^{\prime\prime}
is in
W
or has been removed in the previous iterations, we add such a

to

; this procedure guarantees that each remaining vertex has at least one path to

in

after removing

(cf.

in
[

]
).
After that, we remove

from

.

The precise correctness of the DDFS algorithm above and its running time bound follow
the prior literature

5
Approximate MCM in CONGEST and Semi-Streaming Settings

This section presents how our framework is utilized for leading an efficient algorithm to compute an approximate maximum matching in distributed and semi-streaming settings.
Our aim is to obtain a matching of size at least
(1-\epsilon)\mu(G)
.
For simplicity, we assume that
\epsilon
is represented as
\epsilon=2^{-x}
for some integer
x>0
.

5.1
Models

CONGEST Model

The CONGEST model is one of the standard models in
designing distributed graph algorithms. The system is modeled by a simple connected undirected graph

G
of
n
vertices and
m
edges. Vertices and edges are uniquely identified by
O(\log n)
-bit integer values, and
the computation follows round-based synchronous message passing. In one round, each vertex
v
sends and receives

O(\log n)
-bit messages through each of its incident edges, and executes local computation following its internal
state, local random bits, and all the received messages. A vertex can send different messages to different neighbors
in one round, and all the messages sent in a round are necessarily delivered to their
destinations within the same round.

Semi-Streaming Model

The semi-streaming model is a popular one in the graph-stream algorithm, where the algorithm can use the space of the vertex-size
order (i.e.,
\tilde{O}(n)
space), and the edge set arrives as a data stream. Intuitively, the model can be seen as the
one which is sufficiently rich to store the output of the algorithm, but not enough to store whole graph information. Our study
considers its multi-path variant, where the algorithm can read the input stream multiple times. In this settings, the efficiency
of the algorithm is measured by the number of
passes
(i.e., the number of times the input stream is read). The model our
algorithm assumes on input streams is the weakest, where the order of edges in the input stream can be arbitrary, and even can
be different among different passes.

5.2

We first present the CONGEST/semi-streaming implementations of our framework.

5.3
Algorithm
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}

In this section, we prove our main result on approximation (Theorem
1.2

satisfying Lemma

.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(G,M)
be any matching system. There exists an algorithm
\textsc{AugAndHit}_{\ell}(G,M)
which outputs a set
\mathcal{Q}

of disjoint shortest augmenting paths in
(G,M)
and a hitting set
B
of size at most
16|\mathcal{Q}|(\ell+1)^{2}+\frac{|M|}{4(\ell+1)}
for
(G,M)
if
the length of shortest augmenting paths in
(G,M)
is not larger than
2\ell+1
(otherwise, it outputs
\mathcal{Q}=\emptyset
).
It is implemented in both CONGEST and semi-streaming models, which respectively attains the following properties:

•

In the CONGEST model, each vertex initially knows the edge of
M
incident to itself (if any), and outputs the incident edges in
E(\mathcal{Q})
and the one-bit flag of indicating
v\in B
or not. The running time is
O(\ell^{2}\mbox{\rm{MM}}(n))
rounds, where
\mbox{\rm{MM}}(n)
is the running time
of computing a maximal matching of graphs on
n
vertices. The algorithm is deterministic
except for the part of computing maximal matchings in CONGEST.

•

In the semi-streaming model, the algorithm is deterministic, uses
O(n\log n)
-bit space, and run with
O(\ell^{2})
passes. Initially the information on
V(G)
and
M
is stored in the memory, and outputs the set
\mathcal{Q}
and
B
.

The key building block of our algorithm is
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
.
We recall that it takes a parameter
\alpha>0
(represented as a power of two) and any matching
M
such that
|M|=(1-\alpha^{\prime})\mu(G)
holds for some
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
, and outputs a matching
M^{\prime}
of
G
whose approximation factor is at least
\min\{(1-\alpha^{\prime}+\Theta(\alpha^{2})),(1-\alpha/2)\}
(Lemma
5.4
).
Iteratively applying
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}

O(\alpha^{-1})
times, we obtain a
(1-\alpha/2)
-approximate matching.
Starting with any maximal matching (which is a
1/2
-approximate matching) and calling that iteration process
O(\log\epsilon^{-1})
times with
\alpha=1/2,1/4,1/8,\dots 1/\epsilon^{-1}
, we finally obtain a
(1-\epsilon)
-approximate matching.

The execution of
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
consists of repetition of
K=4\alpha^{-1}

phases
with modification of the input graph. Let
(G_{i},M_{i})
be the matching system at the beginning of
the
i
-th phase. The behavior of the
i
-th phase consists of the following three steps:

1.

Path finding
: Call
\textsc{AugAndHit}_{K}(G_{i},M_{i})
and find a set of shortest augmenting paths
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
and a hitting set
B_{i}
.

2.

Length stretch
: For each vertex
v\in B_{i}
, if
v
is not free, the algorithm subdivides the matching edge incident to
v
into a length-three alternating path such that the middle edge is a non-matching edge. Otherwise, we rename
v
into
v^{\prime}
, and add a length-two alternating path to
v^{\prime}
where the edge incident to
v^{\prime}
is a matching edge and the endpoint of the path other than
v^{\prime}
becomes the new
v
. The resultant graph and matching is denoted by
(G_{i+1},M^{\prime}_{i})
. We refer to this transformation as
\sigma_{i}
, i.e.,

\sigma_{i}(G_{i},M_{i})=(G_{i+1},M^{\prime}_{i})
. In addition, we abuse
\sigma_{i}
for transforming any alternating path
P
in
(G_{i},M_{i})
. That is, if
P
does not contain any vertex in
B_{i}
,
\sigma_{i}(P)
returns
P
itself. Otherwise,
\sigma_{i}(P)
returns the transformed path in
(G_{i+1},M^{\prime}_{i})
obtained by appropriately subdividing matching edges in
P
.

3.

Augmentation
: Augment
\sigma_{i}(Q)
for all
Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{i}
. The matching after the augmentation becomes
M_{i+1}
.

After the
K
phases, the algorithm finally executes the
recovery
step. We first define the reverse transformation
\hat{\sigma}_{i}
. The transformation
\hat{\sigma}_{i}(G_{i+1},M^{\prime})
returns the matching system

(G_{i},M)
constructively defined as follows: any path
v_{1},e_{1},v_{2},e_{2},v_{3},e_{3},v_{4}
of length three in
G_{i+1}
subdivided by

\sigma_{i}
is restored back to a single edge
e
in
G_{i}
. If
e_{1}
and
e_{3}
are non-matching edge in
M^{\prime}
,
e
becomes a non-matching edge in

(G_{i},M)
. Otherwise (i.e.,
e_{1}
and
e_{3}
are matching edges),
e
becomes a matching edge in
(G_{i},M)
. Note that
it never happens that only one of
e_{1}
and
e_{3}
is a matching edge because the degrees of
v_{2}
and
v_{3}
are two and
thus any augmenting path contains both
e_{1}
and
e_{3}
or neither
e_{1}
nor
e_{3}
. In addition, any path
v_{1},e_{1},v_{2},e_{2},v_{3}
of length two
added to a free vertex in
G_{i}
with
\sigma_{i}
is deleted.
Let us define
(\hat{G}_{K+1},\hat{M}_{K+1})=(G_{K+1},M_{K+1})
and
(\hat{G}_{i},\hat{M}_{i})=\hat{\sigma}_{i}(\hat{G}_{i+1},\hat{M}_{i+1})
.
Finally, the procedure
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
outputs
\hat{M}_{1}
as a matching of
\hat{G}_{1}=G_{1}
.

The following lemma claims the correctness of Algorithm
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that if
|M_{1}|=(1-\alpha^{\prime})\mu(G_{1})
holds for some
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
,
then
|\hat{M}_{1}|\geq\min\{(1-\alpha^{\prime}+c\alpha^{2}),(1-\alpha/2)\}\mu(G_{1})
holds.

Proof.

Let
c=1/648
.
Since the output matching size is not smaller than the input size, the lemma obviously holds if
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha/2
. Hence in the following argument we assume
\alpha/2<\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
.
Let
\overline{\mu}(G,M)=\mu(G)-|M|
. Given a matching system
(G,M)
and any maximum matching
M^{\max}
of
G
, the symmetric difference of
M^{\max}
and
M
induces a subgraph containing
\overline{\mu}(G,M)

disjoint augmenting paths in
(G,M)
.
Since
\sigma_{i}
(as a mapping over all paths) is a bijective mapping between the set of all augmenting paths in
(G_{i},M_{i})
and that in
(G_{i+1},M^{\prime}_{i})
, we have

\overline{\mu}(G_{i+1},M^{\prime}_{i})=\overline{\mu}(G_{i},M_{i})
, which implies
\overline{\mu}(G_{i},M_{i})-|\mathcal{Q}_{i}|=\overline{\mu}(G_{i+1},M_{i+1})
.
Similarly, we also have
\overline{\mu}(\hat{G}_{i},\hat{M}_{i})=\overline{\mu}(\hat{G}_{i+1},\hat{M}_{i+1})
. Let
q=\sum_{1\leq i\leq K}|\mathcal{Q}_{i}|
. By the definition, we obtain
\overline{\mu}(G_{1},\hat{M}_{1})=\overline{\mu}(G_{1},M_{1})-q
. If
q\geq c\alpha^{2}\mu(G_{1})
holds, we have

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(G_{1},\hat{M}_{1})=\overline{\mu}(G_{1},M_{1})-q\leq\left(\alpha^{\prime}-c\alpha^{2}\right)\mu(G_{1}).

It implies
\hat{M}_{1}
is a
(1-\alpha^{\prime}+c\alpha^{2})
-approximate matching of
G_{1}
.
Hence we assume
q<c\alpha^{2}\mu(G_{1})
.

Due to the hitting set property, any shortest augmenting path
P
in
(G_{i},M_{i})
contains a vertex

v
in
B_{i}
. If
v
is not free,
P
must contain the matching edge incident to
v
, which is subdivided into a length-three alternating path in
G_{i+1}
. Otherwise,
\sigma_{i}(P)
must contain a length-two alternating path added to
v
. In any case, the length of
\sigma_{i}(P)
increases by at least two. Then
(G_{K+1},M_{K+1})
does not admit an augmenting path of length at most
2K-1=8\alpha^{-1}-1
, and thus
M_{K+1}
is a
(1-\alpha/4)
-approximate matching of
G_{K+1}
by the Hopcroft–Karp analysis.
That is,
\overline{\mu}(G_{K+1},M_{K+1})\leq\alpha\mu(G_{K+1})/4
. If
\mu(G_{K+1})\leq 2\mu(G_{1})
holds, we have
\overline{\mu}(\hat{G}_{1},\hat{M}_{1})=\overline{\mu}(G_{K+1},M_{K+1})\leq\alpha\mu(G_{1})/2
, and thus
\hat{M}_{1}
is a
(1-\alpha/2)
-approximate matching of
\hat{G}_{1}=G_{1}
.
Hence the remaining issue is to show
\mu(G_{K+1})\leq 2\mu(G_{1})
.

We prove
\mu(G_{j})\leq 2\mu(G_{1})
for any
1\leq j\leq K+1

by the induction on
j
. Since the base case is obvious, we focus on the inductive step. Suppose as the
induction hypothsis that the statement above holds for all
1\leq i\leq j\leq K
and consider the case of
j+1
.
We have

\displaystyle\mu(G_{j+1})

\displaystyle=\mu(G_{1})+\sum_{1\leq i\leq j}|B_{i}|

\displaystyle\leq\mu(G_{1})+\sum_{1\leq i\leq j}\left(16(K+1)^{2}|\mathcal{Q}_{i}|+\frac{|M_{i}|}{4(K+1)}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{Lemma~\ref{lma:AugAndHit}})

\displaystyle\leq\mu(G_{1})+16(K+1)^{2}\cdot q+j\cdot\frac{\mu(G_{1})}{2(K+1)}

\displaystyle(|M_{i}|\leq\mu(G_{i})\leq 2\mu(G_{1}))

\displaystyle<\mu(G_{1})+16(4\alpha^{-1}+1)^{2}\cdot c\alpha^{2}\mu(G_{1})+\frac{\mu(G_{1})}{2}

\displaystyle(K=4\alpha^{-1},\ j\leq K)

\displaystyle=\mu(G_{1})+256c\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{4}\right)^{2}\mu(G_{1})+\frac{\mu(G_{1})}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mu(G_{1})+324c\mu(G_{1})+\frac{\mu(G_{1})}{2}

\displaystyle\left(\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu(G_{1})+\frac{\mu(G_{1})}{2}+\frac{\mu(G_{1})}{2}

\displaystyle\left(c=\frac{1}{648}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mu(G_{1}).

The Lemma is proved.

.

There exists:

•

a
(1-\epsilon)
-approximation maximum matching algorithm for a given graph
G
which runs in the CONGEST model
with
O(\epsilon^{-4}\mbox{\rm{MM}}(\epsilon^{-2}n))
rounds, where
\mbox{\rm{MM}}(N)
means the time complexity of computing
a maximal matching in graphs on
N

the algorithm is deterministic except for the part of computing maximal matchings.

a deterministic
(1-\epsilon)
-approximation maximum matching algorithm for a given graph
G

with
O(\epsilon^{-4})

O(n\log n)
-bit memory.

Proof.

We first focus on the CONGEST implementation. Obviously, the length-stretch step and the augmentation step are locally processed.
Note that the simulation of
G_{i}
is straightforward:
each subdivided path is managed by an arbitrary one of its endpoints without overhead. For the first
invocation of
\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
with
\alpha=1/2

1/2
-approximate matching as its input. Since any maximal matching is a

is dominated by the cost for running
\textsc{AugAndHit}_{K}

holds and the total number
of augmenting paths found in the execution of each phase never exceeds
n
, for any
(G_{i},M_{i})
appearing in the run of

\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
,
|V(G_{i})|\leq\epsilon^{-2}n
by Lemma
5.3
. It implies that any invocation of
\textsc{AugAndHit}_{K}(G_{i},M_{i})
takes

O(K^{2}\textsf{MM}(\epsilon^{-2}n))
rounds by Lemma
5.3
, which is also the cost of one phase in the invocation of

\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
. Hence the running time of
\textsc{Amplifier}{\alpha}
is

O(K^{3}\textsf{MM}(\epsilon^{-2}n))=O(\alpha^{-3}\textsf{MM}(\epsilon^{-2}n))
rounds. The invocation of

\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
with
\alpha=1/2^{j}
is repeated
O(\alpha^{-1})
times.
Summing up the cost for all invocations, the total running time is bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j\leq\log\epsilon^{-1}}O\left(\left(\frac{1}{2^{j}}\right)^{-4}\mbox{\rm{MM}}(\epsilon^{-2}n)\right)=O(\epsilon^{-4}\mbox{\rm{MM}}(\epsilon^{-2}n)).

Next, we consider the semi-streaming implementation. The algorithm keeps the information of
(V(G_{i}),M_{i})
for the current matching system
(G_{i},M_{i})
.
The pass complexity follows the round complexity of the CONGEST implementation, and can obtain the same bound of
O(\epsilon^{-4})
passes.
On the space complexity, the algorithm

\textsc{Amplifier}_{\alpha}
modifies the input graph, and thus we might need
\omega(n\log n)
-bit space if
(V(G_{i}),M_{i})
is
explicitly stored in the memory. However, if
q_{j}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq j}|\mathcal{Q}_{i}|<\alpha^{2}\mu(G_{1})/128
holds,
the argument in the proof of Lemma
5.4
guarantees
\mu(G_{j+1})=O(\mu(G_{1}))
, which implies
|V(G_{j+1})|=O(n)
and
thus
O(n\log n)
-bit space suffices. To care the case of
q_{j}>c\alpha^{2}\mu(G_{1})
, we additionally introduce the early-stopping mechanism
of omitting subsequent phases when
q_{j}>c\alpha^{2}\mu(G_{1})
happens at the end of the phase
j
. The proof of Lemma
5.4

ensures that the output of the algorithm attains the desired approximation factor even if such a mechanism is
installed. Consequently,
O(n\log n)
-bit memory is enough to implement whole algorithm.
∎

5.4
Algorithm
AugAndHit

The goal of this section is to prove Lemma
5.3
.

Consider the matching system
(G,M)
where the length of shortest augmenting paths
is bounded by
2\ell+1
(and thus any double path contains at most
2(\ell+1)
vertices).
We denote the ABD for
(G,M)
up to depth
\ell
by
H
and the set of all crossable and critical edges in
H
by
E^{\ast}
.

Parallel DFS

The implementation of
\textsc{AugAndHit}(H,E^{\ast})
utilizes the parallel DFS technique by Goldberg, Plotkin, and Vaidya
[
33
]
.
We perform the parallel DFS algorithm rooted by
U
(the set of all free vertices) in the reverse graph of
H
, i.e., it manages a disjoint set
\mathcal{T}
of (DFS) in-trees rooted by each vertex in
U
.
Each tree in
\mathcal{T}
rooted by
u\in U
is referred to as
T_{u}
, and we denote the search head of
T_{u}
by
h_{u}
.
Each vertex is assigned with one of the three states:
idle
,
active
, and
dead
.
Initially, all vertices in
V(H)\setminus U
have state
idle
, and vertices in

U
are assigned with
active
. When the search head
h_{u}
visits a vertex
v
, the state of
v
is
changed to
active
, and when
h_{u}
performs backtrack at
v
, it is changed to
dead
. In one step of the algorithm, all the heads try to expand their own trees in parallel: Letting
X
be the set of the locations of all search heads and
Y
be all
idle
vertices, construct the auxiliary bipartite graph of vertex set
X\cup Y
where
v\in X
and
v^{\prime}\in Y
are connected by an undirected edge if and only if
(v^{\prime},v)\in E(H)
holds.
Then the algorithm computes a maximal matching in it. If
h_{u}\in X
finds a matching partner
y\in Y
,
h_{u}
moves to
y
. Otherwise it performs backtrack. When
h_{u}
performs backtrack at
u
, the search from
u
terminates with changing its state to
dead
. The algorithm iterates this step
L=16(\ell+1)^{2}
times.

The key technical properties of this algorithm is stated as follows:

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
D
,
A
, and
I
be the sets of vertices with states
dead
,
active
, and
idle
, respectively, at the end of the algorithm. The following three
properties hold:

(P1)

Any path
P
in
H
ending at a vertex in
U
satisfies either
V(P)\subseteq D
or
V(P)\cap A\neq\emptyset
.

(P2)

The size of
A
is at most
\frac{|V(H)\setminus U|}{8(\ell+1)}
.

(P3)

For each search tree
T_{u}
, the size of
V(T_{u})
is at most
L=16(\ell+1)^{2}
.

Proof.

Proof of (P1)
: The DFS guarantees that there exists no edge
(v,v^{\prime})\in E(H)
such that
v
is
idle
and
v^{\prime}
is
dead
, because if such an edge exists the search head visiting
v^{\prime}
also visits
v
before the backtrack at
v^{\prime}
.
Since all the vertices in
U
can be
dead
or
active
, this implies
V(P)\subseteq D
or
V(P)\cap A\neq\emptyset
.

Proof of (P2)
: We say that the search from
u
is active at the
i
-th iteration if it does not terminate at the end of the

i
-th iteration. Any search active at the
i
-th iteration changes the state of one vertex in
V(H)\setminus U
during the
i
-th iteration.
Since the states of vertices in
V(H)\setminus U
can change at most
2|V(H)\setminus U|
times in total, at most
2|V(H)\setminus U|/L
searches are still active at the end of the algorithm (after
L=16(\ell+1)^{2}
iterations).
In each search tree
T_{u}
, only the vertices in the path from
u
to
h_{u}
are
active
, whose number is bounded by
\ell+1
, and thus
the total number of active vertices at the end of the algorithm is at most
\frac{|V(H)\setminus U|}{8(\ell+1)}
.

Proof of (P3)
: It is obvious because at most one node is added to each tree in one iteration.
∎

Main Algorithm

We present the outline of the behavior of Algorithm
\textsc{AugAndHit}(G,M)
more precisely:

•

First, the algorithm constructs
H
and
E^{\ast}
and runs the above-stated parallel DFS from
U
in the reverse of
H
.
It provides a set of in-trees
T_{u}
of
H
rooted by each vertex
u\in U
. We refer to the vertex set
R_{u}
spanned by each in-tree
T_{u}
as
the
region
of
u
. All
active
vertices at the end of the parallel DFS are added to a hitting set
B
.

•

A vertex
v\in R_{u}
is called
double reachable
if there exists a partial double path
(P,Q,y,z)
to
v
such that both
P
and
Q
lie in
R_{u}
, i.e.,
P
is a
y
–
u
path,
Q
is a
z
–
v
path,
V(P)\cap V(Q)=\emptyset
, and
V(P)\cup V(Q)\subseteq V(R_{u})
.
For each region
R_{u}
, the algorithm identifies the set
D_{u}\subseteq R_{u}
of all double reachable vertices.

•

We say that two different regions
R_{u_{1}}
and
R_{u_{2}}
are
mergeable
if there exists an edge
(v,v^{\prime})\in E(H)
from
R_{u_{1}}
and
R_{u_{2}}
such that
v

is double reachable, or an edge
\{v,v^{\prime}\}\in E^{\ast}
bridging
R_{u_{1}}
and
R_{u_{2}}
.
Note that the merged region
R_{u_{1}}\cup R_{u_{2}}
necessarily contains a double path. If two mergeable pair of regions
R_{u_{1}}
and
R_{u_{2}}

are actually merged, the algorithm finds a double path in the merged region, and stores it into
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
. Then
all vertices in the merged region is added to
B
. The pairs of regions to be merged are decided by parallel maximal matching: First,
the algorithm constructs the auxiliary graph where the vertex set is the set of all regions and two vertices are connected by an edge if and only
if they are mergeable. Computing a maximal matching in that graph, all matched pairs are merged. Finally, the algorithm outputs
\mathcal{Q}=\{\mathsf{Aug}(Q)\mid Q\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\}
and
B
.

The first step is easily implemented by both CONGEST and semi-streaming settings.
The construction of
H
and
E^{\ast}
follows the algorithms of Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
. In the parallel DFS,
each vertex
v\in V(H)
manages its state and the parent of the DFS tree it joins.
The update of the search head locations is implemented by a single invocation of any maximal matching algorithm.
Thus, the time complexity of the first step in CONGEST is
O(\ell^{2}\textsc{MM}(n))
rounds, and the pass complexity is
O(\ell^{2})
in the semi-streaming model.
In the second step, our algorithm first executes
\textsc{ComputeDist}_{\ell}
in the subgraph
G[R_{u}]
of
G

induced by each region
R_{u}
(with adding the super vertex
f
).
Let
\mathcal{I}=(G,M)
,
M[R_{u}]=M\cap E(G[R_{u}])
, and
\mathcal{I}[R_{u}]=(G[R_{u}],M[R_{u}])
. The following lemma holds:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
v\in R_{u}
be any vertex with an outgoing edge
(v,v^{\prime})\in E(H)
reaching to another region.
Then
v
is double reachable if and only if
\mathrm{dist}^{\mathrm{even}}_{\mathcal{I}}(v)=\mathrm{dist}^{\mathrm{even}}_{\mathcal{I}[R_{u}]}(v)
holds.

Proof.

Proof of direction
\Rightarrow
: Since
v
admits a partial double path within
R_{u}
, there exists a
shortest
\mathrm{even}
-alternating path
X
from
f
to
v
for
\mathcal{I}
which lying in
V(T_{u})
by Lemma
3.20
.
It is also an
\mathrm{even}
-alternating path for
\mathcal{I}[R_{u}]
, and thus we have
\mathrm{dist}^{\mathrm{even}}_{\mathcal{I}}(v)=\mathrm{dist}^{\mathrm{even}}_{\mathcal{I}[R_{u}]}(v)
.

Proof of direction
\Leftarrow
:
Let
X
be any shortest
\mathrm{even}
-alternating path from
u
to
v
in
G[R_{u}]
. By Lemma
3.21
, that path is transformed into a partial double path lying in
R_{u}
. That is,
v
is double reachable.
∎

The lemma above suffices to implement the second step. The main task of the third step is to find a
shortest augmenting path in the merged region
R_{u_{1}}\cup R_{u_{2}}
. This is regarded as the task of
finding a single augmenting path between
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
in the subgraph induced by that region.
It can be done with
O(\ell^{2})
rounds in CONGEST, and
O(\ell^{2})
passes in the
semi-streaming model using our framework and the subroutines in Section
5.2
.
Hence the total running time is
dominated by the first step, i.e., it takes
O(\ell^{2}\textsf{MM}(n))
rounds in CONGEST, and takes

O(\ell^{2})
passes in the semi-streaming model.

The correctness of the algorithm (Lemma
5.3
) is shown by the following key technical lemma.

Lemma 5.7
.

The set
B\subseteq V(H)
at the end of the algorithm is a hitting set for all shortest augmenting paths in
H
, and its size is at most

16|\mathcal{Q}|(\ell+1)^{2}+\frac{|M|}{4(\ell+1)}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.5
(P2) and (P3), the size of
B
is bounded by
16|\mathcal{Q}|(\ell+1)^{2}+\frac{|V(H)\setminus U|}{8(\ell+1)}
. Since
2|M|=|V(H)\setminus U|
holds, we obtain the stated bound.
Hence it suffices to show that
B
is certainly a hitting set for all shortest augmenting paths.

We first show that
B
is a hitting set for all double paths.
Suppose for contradiction that a double path
(P,Q,y,z)
in
H
does not intersect
B
.
By Lemma
5.5
(P1), both
P
and
Q
lie in the subgraph induced by
dead
vertices.
If
y
and
z
respectively belong to different regions

R_{u_{1}}
and
R_{u_{2}}
, at least one of
R_{u_{1}}
and
R_{u_{2}}
is merged with some region (otherwise,
R_{u_{1}}
and
R_{u_{2}}
must be
merged into one with edge
\{y,z\}
), which implies
y\in B
or
z\in B
. If both
y
and
z
belong to a common region
R_{u}
, we
consider the following two cases:

(Case 1)
Either
P
or
Q
is contained in
R_{u}
: Without loss of generality, we assume
V(P)\subseteq R_{u}
.
Then
P
terminates with
u
, and
Q
necessarily goes out from
R_{u}
. Let
(v_{q},v^{\prime}_{q})
be the first edge in
Q
which goes out from

R_{u}
. Due to the existence of paths
P
(from
y
to
u
) and
Q[z,v_{q}]
(from
z
to
v_{q}
), one can conclude
that
v_{q}
is double reachable.
Hence at least one of
R_{u}
and the region containing
v^{\prime}_{q}
must be merged, which implies
Q
intersects
B
.
It is a contradiction.

(Case 2)
None of
P
and
Q
is contained in
R_{u}
: Let
(v_{p},v^{\prime}_{p})
(resp.
(v_{q},v^{\prime}_{q})
) be the
first edge in
P
(resp.
Q
) going out from
R_{u}
.
If either
v_{p}
or
v_{q}
is double reachable, one can lead a contradiction
with the same argument as Case 1. Otherwise, any path
P^{\prime}
from
v_{p}
to
u
intersects
Q[z,v_{q}]
,
because if it does not intersect,
v_{q}
must be double reachable due to the existence of disjoint paths
P[y,v_{p}]\circ P^{\prime}
and

Q[z,v_{q}]
. Similarly, any path
Q^{\prime}
from
v_{q}
to
u
intersects
P[y,v_{p}]
. Then, for any vertex

x_{1}\in V(P^{\prime})\cap V(Q[z,v_{q}])
and
x_{2}\in V(Q^{\prime})\cap V(P[y,v_{p}])
,
P^{\prime}[v_{p},x_{1}]\circ Q[x_{1},v_{q}]\circ Q^{\prime}[v_{q},x_{2}]\circ P[x_{2},v_{p}]

forms a cycle, contradicting that
H
is a DAG.

Finally, we show that
B
is a hitting set of all shortest augmenting paths.
Suppose for contradiction that there exists a shortest augmenting path
X

from
u_{1}
to
u_{2}
with edge
\{y,z\}\in E^{\ast}
.
From the construction,
B
consists of in-trees rooted by vertices in
U
(some of them may be paths formed by active vertices), which are all mutually disjoint.
By Lemma
3.19
, there exists two disjoint paths in
H
respectively from
y
to
u_{1}

and from
z
to
u_{2}
which do not intersect
B
. It contradicts the fact that
B
is a hitting set for all double paths.
The lemma is proved.
∎

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we presented a new structure theorem on shortest alternating paths in general graphs, which bypasses
most of the complication caused by the blossom argument. A key technical ingredient is the notion of alternating
base trees
[
40
,
39
]
. Our new structure theorem extends it so that the framework can address
the construction of a maximal set of disjoint shortest augmenting paths, whereas the original theorem focuses on
finding a single (shortest) augmenting path in general graphs to obtain efficient distributed message-passing
algorithms for MCM in general graphs. It includes a short and concise proof of the original theorem in
[
39
]
. Following our new structure theorem, we presented a new framework of finding a maximal set of disjoint shortest augmenting paths, which yields a new MCM algorithm under the standard sequential computation model.
The proposed algorithm is slightly slower but more implementable and much easier to confirm
its correctness than the currently known fastest algorithm (i.e., the MV algorithm). We also presented two

(1-\epsilon)
-approximation matching algorithms in the CONGEST and semi-streaming models, which substantially
improve the best known upper bounds for those models.

The authors believe that our framework can yield new improved MCM algorithms in more various
settings such as PRAM, MPC, dynamic algorithms, weighted graphs, and so on. The application and/or extension of
our framework for those scenarios is a natural open question to be addressed. Another intriguing open
question is to refine the framework for bringing more shorter and concise algorithms and proofs.
Obviously, it is also a highly challenging open problem if one can beat the
O(m\sqrt{n})
time bound
for sequential MCM computation in general graphs resorting to our framework.
