cyberDMR: accurate and robust identification of differentially methylated regions from WGS-derived methylomes
Authors: Yang Li, Yadong Liu, Xian Chen, Weize Kong, Yadong Wang, Tao Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferentially methylated regions (DMRs) are essential regulatory features for characterizing epigenetic variation, understanding population-level differences, and interpreting haplotype-specific methylation patterns. However, existing DMR detection methods often show unstable performance under biologically challenging scenarios such as sparse CpG regions, low sequencing depth, small sample sizes, extreme region lengths, and subtle methylation differences. These limitations highlight the need for a more robust and versatile approach capable of achieving accurate and consistent DMR detection across diverse genomic contexts. To address these challenges, we developed cyberDMR, a robust and noise-resilient DMR detection framework that integrates coverage-adaptive smoothing, seed-growing clustering, and weighted beta regression to achieve stable performance under whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and varying sequencing conditions.ResultscyberDMR consistently achieved the highest F1-scores on real WGBS prostate cancer datasets, outperforming other strong-performing methods by 2–18%. This advantage was further supported by extensive simulations spanning diverse genomic and experimental scenarios, in which cyberDMR maintained stable and leading performance, achieving a mean F1-score of 99.7% (± 0.3%) and consistently outperforming competing methods across diverse simulated settings. Background

DMRs are coordinated genomic segments that exhibit significant and biologically meaningful changes in DNA methylation, and they play critical roles in interpreting population-level epigenetic diversity, imprinting regulation, and genetic control of methylation through meQTLs [1–3]. To support genome-wide DMR discovery, multiple computational frameworks have been developed for whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) and related methylation datasets. DSS employs a beta-binomial regression framework to model methylation variability and enhance statistical power through dispersion shrinkage [4, 5]; Metilene utilizes binary segmentation combined with a two-dimensional nonparametric statistical test to delineate sharp methylation boundaries [6]; BSmooth employs local smoothing across CpG sites followed by t-statistic modeling to detect broader and more gradual regional changes [7]; HOME adopts a machine-learning paradigm based on support vector regression to distinguish true biological signals from noise in heterogeneous samples [8]; MethyLasso incorporates Lasso-based penalized regression to perform robust DMR segmentation at large genomic scales [9]; and DiffMethylTools, one of the most recent toolkits, integrates flexible workflows for DMR inference under diverse experimental designs [10]. Collectively, these methods form the foundation of current genome-wide epigenomic analyses.

Although these methods generally achieve a reasonable balance between detection accuracy and analytical sensitivity under conventional CpG distribution and sequencing conditions, variation in inter-group methylation differences and inconsistent intra-group methylation levels under more extreme scenarios can cause substantial fluctuations in their performance. For example, when sample sizes are very small—particularly in the extreme case of having only one sample per group—many statistical methods become invalid or fail to operate reliably [11]. Low-coverage regions, which frequently arise in structurally complex genomic loci and are increasingly common in cost-effective population-scale studies such as low-pass whole-genome sequencing (WGS) or shallow WGBS [12–14], further challenge algorithmic stability. In addition, sparse CpG regions constitute nearly 90% of the human genome, which greatly increases the spacing between CpG sites and leads to a more fragmented CpG distribution [15, 16]. Finally, extreme cases—including very long or very short DMRs, or regions exhibiting unusually high or subtle methylation changes—tend to generate additional noise and unstable test statistics [17].

Herein, we propose cyberDMR, an accurate and robust approach for genome-wide DMR detection. cyberDMR integrates several complementary strategies to enhance detection across diverse genomic contexts. First, a coverage-adaptive smoothing procedure stabilizes CpG-level variability within each sample and improves signal detectability in low-coverage regions [18]. Second, a seed-growing clustering (SGC) algorithm combined with sliding windows models local methylation coherence and reduces noise arising from inter-group inconsistency [19]. Third, statistical inference based on weighted beta regression (WBR), paired with likelihood ratio testing (LRT) and an F-like statistic (F-type statistic comparing inter-group effect size to intra-group variability) filter, effectively suppresses CpG sites with excessive intra-group variability [20]. To enable systematic benchmarking across heterogeneous biological scenarios, we further developed a versatile DMR simulation module capable of generating regions with diverse CpG distribution landscapes. By effectively capturing consistent inter-group methylation differences and maintaining intra-group consistency, cyberDMR provides a powerful solution for high-throughput epigenomic studies aiming to uncover functional regulatory variation across the epigenome.

Results

Overview of cyberDMR

cyberDMR takes genome-wide methylation summaries in BED format—including genomic coordinates, sequencing coverage, and CpG-level methylation values—as input to robustly and accurately identify DMRs from WGS-derived DNA methylation data. The key advantage of cyberDMR lies in its ability to robustly and accurately identify DMRs across highly diverse CpG distribution scenarios throughout the genome, across both short-read (e.g., WGBS) and long-read (e.g., ONT) sequencing platforms. The framework is specifically designed to address three major challenges in genome-wide DMR detection.

First, due to variable sequencing depth and uneven genomic CpG distribution, low-coverage CpG sites frequently occur in complex genomic regions, which interferes with accurate DMR identification [25, 26]. To mitigate this issue, in Step 1, cyberDMR applies a weighted smoothing strategy that integrates methylation signals from neighboring CpG sites based on genomic distance and local methylation levels, thereby producing more reliable estimates for low-coverage sites.

Second, CpG sites exhibiting locally consistent methylation patterns across groups can obscure local methylation shifts and confound the delineation of DMR boundaries [27]. To address this problem, in Step 2, cyberDMR segments CpG sites into preliminary blocks by grouping sites with consistent methylation differences, and in Step 3, applies a SGC strategy that extends from the maximal ΔM site while ensuring that the retained segments exhibit sufficiently large inter-group differences to maintain block coherence and achieve precise boundary detection.

Third, intra-group methylation inconsistency introduces noise that may mask true differential patterns. In some DMRs, although the overall methylation shift is substantial, large within-group fluctuations can cause the region to become insignificant as sample size increases [28]. To overcome this issue, cyberDMR computes an F-like statistic to pre-filter unstable CpG sites and subsequently applies WBR followed by LRT to enhance robustness against intra-group noise and retain only biologically consistent and statistically reliable DMRs.

These designs enable cyberDMR to maintain accurate and robust performance across diverse genomic and experimental scenarios. The effectiveness of this framework has been consistently demonstrated across multiple datasets.

Benchmarks of baseline performance on prostate cancer datasets

To evaluate the DMR detection performance of cyberDMR, we first analyzed the WGBS prostate cancer dataset GSE158927, which includes seven lethal prostate tumors and four matched adjacent normal tissues [29, 30] (Additional file 2: Table S1). Using this dataset, cyberDMR identified 11,063 DMRs with an average length of 741 bp, a mean ΔM of 0.348 ± 0.102, and a median of 28 CpG sites per region, exceeding those detected by other methods by an average of 7–21 CpG sites per region (Additional file 2: Table S2; Additional file 1: Fig. S2). This advantage is largely attributable to its weighted smoothing strategy for low-coverage (< 5 ×) CpG sites, which enabled the recovery of 38.9% of sites that would otherwise be lost. Given the substantial variation in the total number of DMRs reported by existing tools (e.g., 115,432 for BSmooth vs. 6,530 for DiffMethylTools), we selected the top 5,000 most significant DMRs from each tool for a fair comparison (see Methods). We then annotated the detected DMRs using prostate cancer–related regulatory regions curated in MethMarkerDB to assess their biological relevance [31, 32]. As shown in Fig. 2A, cyberDMR exhibited the strongest functional enrichment, identifying 5,051 regulatory elements previously reported to be functionally relevant, with the highest proportion (75.3%) located within exon and promoter regions. These results indicate that the low-coverage smoothing strategy implemented in cyberDMR substantially enhances its detection sensitivity, enabling the accurate identification of biologically meaningful regulatory regions.

We further evaluated the consistency and accuracy of DMRs detected by different tools. Because real datasets do not provide an absolute ground truth for DMR detection, we defined consensus truth sets as regions detected by different numbers of tools (C = 3, 4, and 5). As shown in Fig. 2B (see also Additional file 2: Table S3), cyberDMR achieved the highest F1-like scores (a precision–recall-based F1-score variant for DMR concordance) under both consensus settings (70.9% for C = 3, 71.1% for C = 4, and 70.0% for C = 5) and exhibited stability (SD = 0.01, standard deviation), outperforming all other tools. All seven tools jointly identified 1,223 shared DMRs, among which Figs. 2C and D (sample-level view in Additional file 1: Fig. S3A) illustrates a representative example (chr1:968,226–969,914). In this region, an inconsistent CpG site appears near position chr1:969,042, indicating that the correct DMR boundary should begin at chr1:969,063 (Fig. 2E). Similarly, another inconsistency at chr1:969,922 suggests that the DMR should terminate at the preceding CpG, chr1:969,914 (Fig. 2F). Between these boundaries (chr1:969,063–969,914), the methylation differences remain consistently aligned, supporting neither premature termination nor excessive extension of the region. This example further demonstrates that cyberDMR’s segmentation strategy effectively filters out locally inconsistent CpG sites, yielding clearer DMR boundaries.

For the DMRs uniquely identified by cyberDMR (n = 92 DMRs), we observed that these regions typically exhibit low sequencing coverage. As illustrated in Fig. 2G (sample-level view in Additional file 1: Fig. S3B), 43 out of 90 CpG sites within the example region have sample coverage below 5 × . After applying cyberDMR’s low-coverage smoothing, the ΔM of this region was 0.134, showing a clearly enhanced inter-group methylation difference. In contrast, for the DMRs not detected by cyberDMR but jointly identified by the other six tools (n = 5 DMRs), we found that these regions generally exhibit substantial intra-group variability. As shown in Fig. 2H (sample-level view in Additional file 1: Fig. S3C), within the DMR located at chr1:41,349,859–41,350,292, the intra-group median standard deviation reached 0.213. Moreover, an inconsistent CpG at position chr1:41,350,014 prompted cyberDMR to split the region into two segments: DMR#1 (SD = 0.215) and DMR#2 (SD = 0.232). Due to their pronounced intra-group fluctuations, both segments were classified as notable-DMRs. These results show that cyberDMR restores stable signals in low-coverage regions and suppresses unreliable sites in high-noise regions via its WBR framework.

In addition to detection characteristics, we evaluated the computational performance of all methods under a unified environment. All analyses were conducted on a Linux server equipped with an Intel Xeon Gold 5220 CPU (2.20 GHz, 18 cores, 36 threads) and 128 GB RAM. Although cyberDMR exhibited a substantially longer single-threaded runtime than DiffMethylTools (349 s), Metilene (716 s), BSmooth (2,616 s), and MethyLasso (1,901s), it demonstrated substantial scalability with multithreading, reducing runtime from 295 min (1 thread) to only 22 min using 24 threads (Additional file 2: Table S2). In terms of memory usage, Metilene required only 0.03 GB, which was markedly lower than all other tools (cyberDMR: 32 GB, DSS: 32 GB, HOME: 8 GB, BSmooth: 58 GB, MethyLasso: 31 GB, DiffMethylTools: 16 GB). Overall, cyberDMR provides strong computational scalability while maintaining high detection performance.

Benchmarking DMR detection performance across diverse simulation scenarios

Although WGBS prostate datasets capture a broad spectrum of CpG distributions across the genome, they do not allow controlled, scenario-specific evaluation of different CpG distributions and, more importantly, lack ground-truth labels. Therefore, simulated data are indispensable for systematically assessing the stability of different tools across varying sample sizes, sequencing depths, CpG densities, DMR lengths, and methylation differences (Additional file 2: Table S4). In our simulation framework, five classes of regions were generated. Among these, good-DMRs and sub-DMRs (featuring slightly diffuse boundaries) were defined as true positives, whereas non-DMRs, inconsistent-DMRs (containing CpG sites with opposing inter-group effects), and notable-DMRs (characterized by substantial intra-group variability) served as noise-containing controls (Additional file 1: Fig. S4).

To assess the impact of sample size on DMR detection, we simulated five settings with n = 1, 5, 10, 25, and 50 samples per group (Fig. 3A; Additional file 2: Table S5). Under moderate sample sizes (n = 10), cyberDMR achieved an F1-score of 99.8%, correctly recovering all 250 good-DMRs and all 50 sub-DMRs, with only one notable-DMR false positive (1/20) and no inconsistent-DMR errors (0/30). Under extreme low-sample conditions (n = 1), several tools (BSmooth, DiffMethylTools) failed to produce valid results. Metilene showed a marked decline to F1-score = 83.9%, while cyberDMR and HOME achieved the highest F1-scores (99.5%), followed by MethyLasso (94.4%) and DSS (91.5%). In large-cohort scenarios (n ≥ 25), cyberDMR maintained near-perfect F1-scores (99.5%). Meanwhile, other tools showed moderate to substantial performance loss; for example, Metilene decreased to F1-scores = 93.5% at n = 25 and 93.3% at n = 50, and MethyLasso dropped to ~ 94%. Importantly, cyberDMR produced the lowest false-positive noise across all sample sizes—typically yielding only 0 inconsistent-DMR and 1 notable-DMR—while maintaining exceptionally stable performance, with its F1-score fluctuating by only ~ 0.1% across all tested conditions. Using 5–50 sample-size groupings and retrospective boundary-deviation evaluation, cyberDMR further demonstrated the most accurate localization of DMR boundaries, with median deviations of ~ 33 bp, followed by Metilene (~ 45 bp), both substantially outperforming other methods (Additional file 1: Fig. S5).

To evaluate the impact of CpG density on DMR detection, we simulated three representative genomic contexts: dense, moderate, and sparse regions (Fig. 3B; Additional file 2: Table S6). In dense and moderate regions, all tools achieved generally strong performance, with cyberDMR consistently delivering the highest accuracy, reaching F1-scores of 99.2% and 99.7%. In sparse regions, cyberDMR still maintained high accuracy and stability (F1-score = 99.8%), whereas other tools (excluding BSmooth) showed substantially larger performance fluctuations of 1–7%. We next evaluated the impact of sequencing coverage using simulated datasets ranging from 5 × to 80 × . Under higher coverage conditions (≥ 25 ×), most tools achieved F1-scores above 90%, with cyberDMR maintaining near-perfect accuracy (F1-score ≈ 100%), whereas other high-performing methods exhibited moderate fluctuations, with DSS ranging from ~ 97 to 99%, Metilene from ~ 94 to 97%, MethyLasso from ~ 94 to 96%, and HOME from ~ 92 to 96% (Fig. 3C and Additional file 2: Table S7). To further examine DMR characteristics, we simulated regions spanning 100–2,000 bp and ΔM values between 0.11 and 0.45 (Figs. 3D, E and Additional file 2: Tables S8, S9). cyberDMR exhibited the highest accuracy and stability across both DMR lengths and ΔM levels, yielding median F1-scores of 99.6% (SD = 0.6%) and 99.6% (SD = 0.3%), respectively. Although Metilene, DSS, and MethyLasso achieved median F1-scores above 90% in most settings, their stability was notably lower than that of cyberDMR, exhibiting SD values of 1–10% across varying DMR lengths and 1–6% across ΔM gradients.

Finally, using four mixed-distribution simulation settings generated with an independent simulation framework provided by Metilene, we assessed tool performance under complex genome-wide CpG distribution backgrounds (Fig. 3F and Additional file 2: Table S10). Under the high-contrast beta distribution (α=40, β=3), cyberDMR consistently achieved perfect accuracy (F1-score = 100.0%) across all mixture configurations, with MethyLasso showing similarly strong performance (F1-score = 100.0%). When the methylation contrast decreased (α=15, β=5), performance differences among tools became more evident: cyberDMR still maintained F1-scores of 100% across all configurations and outperformed competing methods by approximately 1–33%, depending on the parameter combination. Together, these findings demonstrate that cyberDMR delivers strong and stable detection performance under mixed genomic backgrounds across the entire genome.

cyberDMR reveals functionally relevant population- and haplotype-specific DMRs

The comprehensive evaluations on both real and simulated datasets highlight the strong performance of cyberDMR. To further investigate its discovery power on real biological samples, we applied cyberDMR to the GIAB samples HG002–HG007 to identify pDMRs [33] (Additional file 2: Table S1). We first identified 1,343 pDMRs between Ashkenazi Jewish individuals (HG002–HG004) and Chinese Han individuals (HG005–HG007), including 588 hypomethylated and 755 hypermethylated regions (Fig. 4A). Genomic annotation of these pDMRs revealed broad coverage across gene-associated, repetitive, and CpG-related features, notably overlapping key functional regions such as gene bodies (47.5%), CpG islands (CGIs, 30.2%), and SINE elements (34.0%) (Fig. 4B). To assess their potential genetic regulation, we cross-referenced pDMR coordinates with the GoDMC database and found that 103 pDMRs harbored an average of two putative meQTL-associated CpG sites [34]. Among these, 15 pDMRs contained ≥ 20% meQTL-associated CpG sites, suggesting that methylation divergence in these regions is strongly influenced by underlying cis-acting genetic variation [35] (Additional file 2: Table S11). After excluding the 84 pDMRs that overlapped with putative meQTLs to focus on non-genetically driven methylation changes, a total of 475 pDMR-associated genes were identified. Functional enrichment analysis revealed significantly over-represented pathways such as homophilic cell adhesion via plasma membrane adhesion molecules (P < 1 × 10−20), embryonic organ development (P < 1 × 10−7), and brain development (P < 1 × 10−9), which are primarily involved in developmental and tissue-specific regulatory processes [36, 37] (Fig. 4C and Additional file 2: Table S12). These pathways are likely subject to regulation by the methylation differences observed in the pDMRs.

We next applied cyberDMR to identify hDMRs using the phased HG002-HG004 trio genomes. Across individuals, an average of 3,008 hDMRs were detected, each comprising ≥ 10 CpG sites and showing substantial allelic divergence (ΔM > 0.5). To obtain robust and reproducible signals, we constructed high-confidence consensus hDMRs, yielding a total of 92 regions, among which 49 overlapped previously reported human imprinting control regions (ICRs) from the imprintome study [38] (Additional file 2: Table S13). Consistent with this observation, annotation using the GENEIMPRINT database further revealed that 27 consensus hDMRs overlapped well-established imprinting-associated loci, encompassing 18 known imprinted genes, including canonical regulators such as DIRAS3, PEG10, and MEST [39–41] (Fig. 4D). Among the remaining hDMRs outside known ICRs, we identified 16 putative novel imprinting candidate genes. Notably, two of these—ZNF714 (33 CpG sites, ΔM = 0.965) and NINJ2 (20 CpG sites, ΔM = 0.857)—exhibited strong haplotype-specific methylation imbalance (ΔM > 0.8) and harbored ≥ 20 CpG sites, suggesting that they may represent previously uncharacterized candidate ICRs with potential allele-specific regulatory activity (Fig. 4E).

Discussion

With the expansion of large-scale sequencing projects, obtaining high-resolution, genome-wide DNA methylation profiles has become increasingly feasible [42–45]. However, accurately identifying DMRs remains challenging, particularly under complex CpG distribution landscapes, variable sequencing depths, and heterogeneous population backgrounds [46]. Here, we introduce cyberDMR, an accurate and robust DMR detection framework. By incorporating a weighted CpG smoothing strategy, unified CpG aggregation across samples, a SGC algorithm, and a combined WBR with LRT approach, cyberDMR effectively mitigates the instability commonly observed in traditional statistical approaches. This framework substantially improves genome-wide methylation assessments across diverse sequencing and biological contexts, enabling applications such as pDMR discovery, hDMR identification, and deeper investigations into epigenetic regulatory mechanisms. To better facilitate the practical use of cyberDMR, its advantages and limitations are further discussed in detail below.

In genome-wide methylation analysis based on WGS, accurately identifying DMRs is challenged by both inter-group noise and intra-group noise. The high accuracy and robustness of cyberDMR arise from two complementary design principles.

First, cyberDMR effectively reduces inter-group noise, which commonly arises from low sequencing depth, uneven CpG distribution, and the presence of CpG sites exhibiting inconsistent directional changes between groups. To address these challenges, cyberDMR introduces a weighted CpG smoothing strategy that leverages neighboring CpG information to improve the reliability of methylation estimates at low-coverage sites. In parallel, cyberDMR performs unified CpG aggregation across samples and employs a segmentation procedure that groups CpG sites with coherent inter-group differences into the same candidate region. These designs collectively suppress the impact of inconsistent CpG sites, enhance boundary definition, and enable more accurate distinction between true differential methylation signals and local background noise.

Second, cyberDMR addresses intra-group variability, which often produces regions with strong mean methylation differences but substantial within-group fluctuations—features characteristic of notable-DMRs and a major source of false-positive detections. To mitigate this effect, cyberDMR incorporates a multi-layer statistical framework that first applies an F-like statistic to quantify regional consistency and exclude candidates with excessive intra-group noise. It then models methylation proportions using WBR and evaluates statistical significance through a LRT. By integrating these components, cyberDMR achieves highly accurate detection of true DMRs while effectively filtering notable-DMR artifacts, ensuring robust performance across complex and noisy biological contexts.

Across both real datasets and diverse simulation scenarios, cyberDMR consistently demonstrated stable and reliable DMR identification. Moreover, under both large-cohort and large-region conditions, cyberDMR exhibited stable near-linear O(n) computational scaling (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). In practical applications, cyberDMR successfully uncovered a series of pDMRs and hDMRs from the GIAB long-read methylation data. These regions map to genes, regulatory elements, and imprinting-associated loci, and highlight potential biological relevance in population-specific epigenetic variation and allele-specific regulatory mechanisms.

Despite its strong overall performance, cyberDMR still has several limitations that warrant further improvement. First, in extremely low-coverage regions where neighboring CpG sites are also low depth and no high-coverage boundary sites are available, the smoothing process becomes difficult. In such cases, cyberDMR can only rely on the overall trend of nearby CpG sites for limited smoothing. Second, although multithreading substantially accelerates runtime, the overall resource consumption remains relatively high due to the computational cost of the WBR and LRT statistical framework, making it comparatively less lightweight than methods such as Metilene and MethyLasso. In addition, unlike DSS, the current model does not incorporate biological covariates (e.g., age or sex). Third, the current design of cyberDMR is primarily optimized for the human genome and pairwise group comparisons, whereas other tools such as BSmooth and Metilene have demonstrated broader applicability across multiple species, and HOME supports simultaneous comparisons across multiple groups. Finally, cyberDMR does not yet provide integrated visualization modules, in contrast to tools such as MethyLasso and DiffMethylTools, which offer diverse and convenient visualization functionalities for downstream interpretation. We will continue to address these issues in future work to further improve the practical utility of cyberDMR [47–49].

Conclusions

In this study, we introduce cyberDMR, a robust and high-resolution framework for identifying DMRs from WGS-derived DNA methylation data. Through a combination of CpG-level smoothing, unified segmentation, and SGC, cyberDMR effectively suppresses inter-group noise and enhances the consistency of regional methylation patterns. Furthermore, the integration of an F-like statistic with WBR and LRT enables accurate characterization of true DMRs while rigorously filtering noise-driven or unstable regions. These algorithmic designs collectively contribute to the reliable detection of DMRs across a wide spectrum of genomic contexts. Compared with existing state-of-the-art methods, cyberDMR demonstrates stable and reliable performance across both WGBS prostate cancer datasets and diverse simulation scenarios. Its application to long-read methylation data further highlights its ability to uncover biologically meaningful differential regions, including 103 pDMRs enriched for meQTL-associated CpG sites, suggesting that methylation divergence in these regions may be influenced by underlying cis-acting genetic variation, and 92 hDMRs that may represent candidate ICRs, thereby underscoring its strong practical utility. We believe that cyberDMR will play an important role in future studies involving methylation landscape construction, population epigenetics, allele-specific methylation analysis, and multi-omics integration.

CpG segmentation

After CpG smoothing, cyberDMR merges per-sample methylation profiles into a unified CpG coordinate space across all individuals. For each CpG site, we compute the group-wise aggregated methylation level (mean methylation) for the experimental group (g1) and control group (g2), and derive the site-level methylation difference (ΔMi) used for downstream segmentation.

cyberDMR then partitions the continuous CpG space into candidate CpG blocks based on both genomic proximity and concordance of methylation changes. Consecutive CpG sites are assigned to the same block if the genomic distance between adjacent sites is ≤ 500 bp and their methylation differences share the same directional change. To ensure robust statistical inference, cyberDMR additionally requires that each CpG site within a block Bk is supported by at least one sample with non-missing methylation measurements in both groups and that each block contains a minimum of five CpG sites.

Block clustering

After defining the initial CpG blocks Bk, cyberDMR further refines each block using a SGC strategy to capture coherent methylation patterns at a finer resolution. Within each block, CpG sites are first ranked by the magnitude of their methylation differences ΔMi. The CpG site with the largest ΔMi is selected as the seed of a new sub-block Bk,j.

Starting from this seed, cyberDMR applies a two-pointer sliding-window procedure along the genomic coordinate to iteratively expand the sub-block toward neighboring CpG sites. The sub-block is extended toward the side with the higher score, and the expansion continues until no additional sites satisfy the criteria (i.e., the absolute methylation difference of the sub-block exceeds the preset threshold, |ΔM(Bk,j)|≥0.1). The remaining unassigned CpG sites are then processed iteratively, each time selecting the CpG site with the largest ΔMi as the seed of the next sub-block. To ensure statistical robustness, cyberDMR retains only those sub-blocks that contain at least five CpG sites and exhibit a mean ΔM of at least 0.1 (Additional file 1: Fig. S1 for details).

DMR testing

cyberDMR performs statistical testing at the sub-block level to determine whether methylation levels differ significantly between groups. For each CpG site, the group-wise methylation levels are modeled using a beta distribution, and parameters are estimated using either a weakly informative prior for low-coverage sites or the method of moments for well-supported sites (detailed in Additional file 1: Supplementary Note S1).

In multi-sample comparisons, ΔBk,j and SEBk,j are derived using Welch-type inverse-variance weights.

For each sub-block Bk,j, cyberDMR fits a WBR model, in which the logit-transformed mean methylation level of each CpG site serves as the response variable and the group indicator serves as the predictor. The model is fitted across all CpG sites within the sub-block, with site-specific weights reflecting variance, effect size, and sample support. By incorporating site-specific weights, the WBR framework gives greater influence to CpG sites with lower variance, stronger inter-group differences, and higher sample support, while down-weighting noisy or poorly supported sites. This strategy improves the robustness of parameter estimation and reduces the impact of unstable CpG sites. Model parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood estimation, and statistical significance is assessed using a likelihood ratio test (LRT). The LRT provides a principled comparison between models, enabling reliable region-level significance assessment directly from the sub-block model, rather than aggregating single-CpG tests, and improving overall DMR detection accuracy under heterogeneous genomic conditions. All resulting P values are adjusted for multiple testing using the BH procedure.

Simulated DMRs with cyberDMR

We simulated methylation data for control and treatment groups using a two-step framework. First, baseline CpG methylation levels for the control group were generated from a bimodal mixture distribution of methylation levels, with the low and high methylation peaks contributing 25% and 75%, respectively [50]. Treatment-group methylation values were then obtained by adding a normally distributed shift to the control methylation values. In parallel, we simulated four genomic context types—CGI, shore, shelf, and open sea—by assigning CpG spacing ranges of 3–15 bp, 15–50 bp, 50–100 bp, and 100–500 bp, respectively, with spacing lengths sampled from lognormal distributions within the specified ranges [51] (detailed in Additional file 1: Supplementary Note S2).

Based on this simulated background, we constructed five categories of regions to evaluate the DMR detection performance: good-DMRs (≥ 50 bp in length, ≥ 5 CpG sites, consistent effect direction, and ΔM ≥ 0.1), sub-DMRs (meeting the same criteria as good-DMRs but with more diffuse boundaries), inconsistent-DMRs (regions containing multiple significant CpG sites with conflicting effect directions), notable-DMRs (regions showing elevated within-group variance and unstable methylation patterns), and non-DMRs (background regions without systematic differences, defined as those with < 5 CpG sites or ΔM < 0.1).

Simulated DMRs with Metilene

To benchmark the performance of cyberDMR, we generated synthetic methylation WGBS datasets using the simulation framework provided by Metilene [6]. First, we employed the simulate_background.R script with parameters α = 40 and β = 3 to construct 10 treatment samples and 10 control samples based on the CpG background distribution of chromosome 10. Using this simulated background, we then applied the simulate_DMRs_WGBS.R script to introduce DMRs under four parameter settings: (40,3,1), (40,3,0.87), (15,5,1), and (15,5,0.87). The first two parameters (α and β) are shape parameters of the beta distribution that define the methylation level distribution of CpG sites within DMRs, while the third parameter (c) is a mixture factor that specifies the effect size.

Data evaluation

We systematically benchmarked our method against six representative DMR detection tools with distinct methodological strategies, including DSS (v2.54.0), Metilene (v0.2.8), BSmooth (v1.42.0), HOME (v1.0.0), MethyLasso (v1.0.0), and DiffMethylTools (v1.0.0). For all methods, detected DMRs were required to meet the following criteria: region length ≥ 50 bp, ≥ 5 CpG sites, ΔM ≥ 0.1, and adjusted P value < 0.05. To evaluate the concordance between each tool and the gold-standard reference regions, we computed genomic overlaps using BEDtools (v2.31.0) [52]. A DMR was considered a true positive (TP) if the reciprocal overlap with the ground truth exceeded 50%; otherwise, it was classified as a false positive (FP) or false negative (FN). In simulated datasets, predefined true DMR regions were treated as the gold standard, and method performance was assessed using precision, recall, and F1-score (definitions provided in the Additional file 1: Supplementary Note S2). Detailed command-line instructions for all DMR detection tools used in this study are available in the Additional file 1: Supplementary Note S3.

WGBS cancer data processing

We benchmarked DMR detection performance using the publicly available WGBS dataset GSE158927, which profiles prostate tumor tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues [29]. The data were generated on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform using the TruSeq® Rapid SBS Kit–HS (50-cycle) and the TruSeq® Rapid PE Cluster Kit–HS, with samples multiplexed across two lanes. For evaluation, we selected seven lethal prostate tumor samples and four matched adjacent normal tissues. Only autosomal CpG sites (chr1–22), excluding sex chromosomes, were retained for downstream DMR detection. We used the prostate cancer DMR annotation from MethMarkerDB, which compiles experimentally supported and functionally annotated DMRs across multiple prostate cancer studies. Intergenic annotations were excluded, and the remaining DMRs with lengths not exceeding 2,000 bp were converted to the hg19 reference genome assembly to match the coordinate system used in downstream analyses.

Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing data processing

We analyzed a set of high-quality long-read sequencing datasets generated from the Genome in a Bottle (GIAB) benchmark panel, including samples HG002–HG007. HG002–HG004 represent individuals of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry, whereas HG005–HG007 correspond to individuals of Han Chinese origin [53]. All samples were derived from Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)–transformed lymphoblastoid cell lines. Whole-genome long-read sequencing was performed on the Oxford Nanopore PromethION instrument using R10.4.1 flow cells (FLO-PRO114) in combination with the SQK-LSK114 ligation kit (v14). Each GIAB sample was sequenced across two independent flow cells.

Raw nanopore signals were processed using Dorado (v0.9.0) [54] with the dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps model to perform integrated basecalling and modified-base detection (5mC/5hmC). Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome using the alignment module in Dorado, and resulting BAM files were further sorted and indexed with Samtools (v1.15.1) [55]. Only reads with mapping quality ≥ 20 were retained. CpG methylation calls were extracted in bedMethyl format using Modkit (v0.5.0) [56] and used for downstream analyses. Single-nucleotide variants were identified using Clair3 (v1.0.4) [57] and haplotype phasing was performed with WhatsHap (v2.3) [58]. Haplotype-resolved methylation profiles were generated by integrating phased SNPs and CpG calls using NanoMethPhase (v1.2.0) [59].

Functional validation

Genomic annotations were obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser, including gene structural elements (exons, introns, and 2 kb upstream promoter regions) and CpG-related features (CpG islands, shores, and shelves). For annotation resources available only in GRCh37, genomic coordinates were converted to GRCh38 using liftOver [60]. Intersections between identified hDMRs and known imprinting control regions were computed using BEDtools, based on curated loci from the GENEIMPRINT database [61]. To assess genetic regulation underlying population-level methylation differences, we intersected our pDMRs with meQTLs reported by the GoDMC Consortium and quantified the number of overlapping regulatory variants [34]. Only cis-acting meQTLs located within 100 kb of the corresponding pDMRs were retained. For haplotype-specific analysis, we selected hDMRs consistently shared by HG002–HG004 individuals, merged overlapping intervals into maximal consensus regions, and calculated the mean ΔM and average CpG methylation levels for each region. These consensus hDMRs were further annotated using previously reported human ICR datasets [38]. For downstream biological interpretation, functional enrichment analyses—including GO terms and KEGG pathways—were performed using Metascape (v3.5.20250701) [62] with default settings (minimum overlap ≥ 3 genes, P value < 0.01, enrichment factor ≥ 1.5).
