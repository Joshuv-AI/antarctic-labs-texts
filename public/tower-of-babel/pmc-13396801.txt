Time-stratified daily walking speed measurement via smartphone and its predictive utility for mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Nobuhiro Fujiyama, Ayuto Kodama, Marco M Z Sharkawi, Kazuo Mishima, Hidetaka Ota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the utility of a smartphone-based walking-speed application for early mild cognitive impairment (MCI) screening under every day, real-world conditions. Community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 65 years were recruited from dementia prevention activities in Yokote City, Akita Prefecture, (entry period: October 2022–January 2023; assessment period: October 2022–May 2023). A walking-speed app was installed on each participant’s personal smartphone, and outdoor, GPS-enabled daily walking speed was continuously recorded. At baseline, we collected clinical and physical measures, including infrared-measured 5-meter usual walking speed (UWS), the National Center for Geriatrics and Gerontology–Functional Assessment Tool (NCGG-FAT), and the Touch Panel-type Dementia Assessment Scale (TDAS). Of 123 enrollees, evaluable walking-speed data were obtained from 115. To reduce noise in the raw dataset, segments meeting predefined criteria for walking distance, walking time, walking speed, steps per unit time, and step length were extracted and defined as unconscious walking speed (UcWS). In time-stratified analyses evaluating its potential as a proxy for UWS, UcWS showed a positive correlation with UWS (maximum r = 0.47). Compared with clinical measures, UcWS was significantly slower in MCI positive participants (median comparison p = 0.018; mean comparison p = 0.011), supporting UWS substitutability. Using walking-duration stratification, we identified a foundational UcWS analytic framework that may serve as a practical proxy for UWS using readily obtainable smartphone-based GPS and accelerometer data. Introduction

Early detection of mild cognitive impairment (MCI), which is considered a condition preceding dementia, is important for medical and social interventions in an aging societies1. In previous risk factor analyses for MCI positive cases, lack of exercise, cerebrovascular factors, and anxiety/neuroticism are reported to be important factors2–5.

Particularly, motor function assessment has recently gained attention as an indirect approach for evaluating cognitive decline. Commonly used methods include a 30-second sit-to-stand test6, knee extension strength7, 6-minute walk test8,9, and face-to-face 5-meter walking speed test10–12. However, these all are collective or face-to-face assessments that are conducted under conscious awareness through structured test environments under supervision, which can introduce several biases13,14. Participants may perform differently when consciously observed (performance bias), only health- conscious individuals may participate in group (face-to-face) cognitive function assessments (selection bias), and measurements are restricted to infrequent assessment opportunities (e.g., once every few years) (time-sampling bias). Collectively, these limitations reduce the ecological validity of traditional motor assessments. On the other hand, evidence on whether reductions of walking speed in daily life are associated with MCI has been scarce15.

Recent advances in smartphone- and sensor-based technologies have accelerated the development of digital gait biomarkers capable of passively capturing walking behavior in real-world environments. Prior studies in neurological populations, including Parkinson’s disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis16,17, have demonstrated that smartphone-derived gait parameters can provide clinically meaningful information regarding motor and neurological status, supporting their potential utility as scalable and nonintrusive health-monitoring tools18,19. However, despite growing interest in digital biomarkers, evidence specifically evaluating passive daily-life walking speed for early MCI screening remains limited20.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate walking speed in daily life using the smartphone-based GPS sensors in order to resolve issues in face-to-face group assessments. The walking speed in life activity was defined as unconscious walking speed (UcWS) to distinguish with UWS. Furthermore, to evaluate the effects of UcWS, we conducted an evaluative prospective study within the framework of dementia prevention activities that Akita University had been jointly promoting with Yokote City in Akita Prefecture, one of the highest aging rates regions in Japan. By establishing a reproducible framework for extracting reliable gait-speed features from passively collected smartphone data, this study provides a practical and scalable methodological contribution to MCI research. The proposed UcWS approach offers a foundational methodological contribution to real-world MCI screening research and may support broader future investigations of scalable digital biomarkers for cognitive decline.

Methods and design

Study organization

This study was conducted with approval from the Ethics Committee of Akita University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2874), as an add-on to the previously conducted longitudinal prospective Yokote cohort study (Approval No.2001). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The Yokote City government provided personal support through public health nurses and access to spaces for dementia prevention activities.

Subject recruitment

Participants were recruited by letters and phone calls from Yokote city government in Akita Prefecture based on the previous study21; some eligibility criteria were added as follows; (i) age 65 and older, (ii) non-dementia, (iii) living in Yokote City. In this study, some eligible criteria were added as below, (i) user of smartphone, (ii) provision of written informed consent, (iii) completion of the smartphone application download for walking speed measurement (UcWS). The entry period was from October 2022 to January 2023 and the UcWS measurement period was from October 2022 to May 2023.

Smartphone application for walking speed measurement (UcWS)

The Walking Speed Measurement Application is a smartphone application developed by InfoDeliver Inc22. The smartphone’s built-in GPS and accelerometer calculate distance moved and walking time. The smartphone’s acceleration sensor data determines the user’s walking status, and a moved distance. Also, the time is measured when the user enters a stable gait, and ends acquiring data when the unstable gait is detected. Measurement start time (st), measurement end time (et), number of steps (sc), distance (d), time (time), and walking speed (speed) were collected as walking speed measurement data. Measurements were continued throughout the study period unless the app is uninstalled or the GPS function is turned off. All collected data were securely stored in Amazon Web Service (AWS). The daily data were used solely to calculate walking distance, and no location information was recorded.

Because passively collected smartphone gait data inherently include substantial environmental and behavioral noise, this foundational study prioritized extraction of highly reliable walking segments over maximal data inclusion. Filtering thresholds were empirically optimized based on real-world data characteristics to maximize stable gait capture while minimizing ambiguous or non-walking segments, informed by prior smartphone-based gait validation frameworks emphasizing data quality optimization in free-living conditions16,18.

Clinical assessment data

Clinical assessment data were collected, including patient background characteristics; performance on the National Center for Geriatrics and Gerontology–Functional Assessment Tool (NCGG-FAT)23; scores on the Touch Panel-type Dementia Assessment Scale (TDAS)24; and face-to-face 5-meter usual walking speed (UWS). NCGG-FAT is a tablet-based neuropsychological battery that assesses four cognitive domains—memory, attention, executive function, and processing speed, through using tasks such as word list memory, trail-making–type tests, and digit symbol substitution–type tasks. In accordance with the original development study, domain-specific raw scores are converted to age and education-adjusted standardized scores and further expressed as a 5-point rating (1–5), where lower ratings indicate poorer performance. In the present study, consistent with previous reports, a rating of 1 in any domain was treated as evidence of clear impairment on NCGG-FAT. The TDAS is a validated touch panel–based cognitive scale derived from the Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale–cognitive subscale. It comprises nine subtests that assess orientation, memory, visuospatial/executive, and language functions.

The total TDAS score ranges from 0 to 101, with higher scores indicating more severe cognitive impairment. Although prior studies have sometimes subdivided TDAS into normal, MCI, and dementia ranges, in the present study we adopted a single threshold of TDAS ≥ 7 to indicate MCI-suspected cognitive impairment. According to a previous report, the presence of MCI on screening was determined using a combined algorithm based on NCGG-FAT and TDAS (Kume et al.). Briefly, participants were classified as MCI-positive if either of the following criteria was met: (i) on NCGG-FAT, at least one domain (memory, attention, executive function, or processing speed) received a rating of 1; or (ii) on TDAS, the total score was ≥ 7. This combined framework was used for epidemiological MCI screening purposes rather than formal clinical diagnosis. For UWS, participants walked along a 7-meter walkway, and infrared sensors measured walking speed over the central 5-meter section.

Statistical analysis

GPS-derived UcWS data were stratified by walking-duration intervals, and intra-individual median and mean walking speeds were calculated for each interval. Group comparisons between MCI-positive and MCI-negative participants were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations between UcWS and continuous clinical variables, including UWS. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

UcWS data extraction conditions for analysis

Of the 123 persons who provided consent, 119 were able to successfully install the gait speed measurement application and had usable walking speed data collected. In total, approximately 210,000 measurements were obtained.

Continuous UcWS recording via smartphones inevitably generates substantial noise, making it impossible to verify across the entire dataset whether each segment truly represents walking. To address this, we retained only data segments that could be identified as walking with high probability. Segments deemed non-walking based on acceleration patterns, as well as ambiguous segments that could not be reliably classified, were excluded. These criteria were empirically optimized to prioritize stable walking episodes and minimize misclassification from noisy passive smartphone recordings. Seasonal influence on UcWS

We defined winter as December–February and non-winter as March–November to evaluate seasonal effects on UcWS. For 21–60 s UcWS in winter, the correlation with UWS was R2 = 0.1556 and R2 = 0.1597 for median and mean values, respectively (Fig. 4, left). In non-winter periods, the correlations were R2 = 0.1834 and R2 = 0.1826 for median and mean values, respectively (Fig. 4, right). Discussions

GPS- and acceleration-based measurements of walking or movement speed are widely available through open-source software25. However, a gait-speed assessment optimized specifically for medical and long-term care use has yet to be established. Clinical application remains at the technical validation stage26,27. In this study, we (i) extracted valid information from the full set of UcWS records and (ii) examined whether smartphone-app–based UcWS serve as a practical proxy for UWS.

We defined valid data strata and extraction conditions for passively collected UcWS, therefore establishing a foundational methodological framework for constructing UcWS datasets. Specifically, UcWS measured after at least 20 s of walking time correlated with UWS, indicating that UcWS obtained from community-dwelling older adults can feasibly serve as a substitute for UWS. Continuous UcWS measurement via smartphones inevitably generates substantial noise. Regardless of how segments are cut, it is impossible to verify across the entire dataset whether each segment truly represents walking. Therefore, our analytic concept was to retain only data that could be identified as walking with high probability: acceleration patterns clearly inconsistent with walking were excluded, and segments whose time or distance characteristics did not convincingly indicate walking were also removed. In other words, we analyzed only “white” data that could be regarded as walking with near certainty. With this preprocessing and the resulting extraction criteria—which form the backbone of UcWS data construction—we obtained a dataset of practical value. Specifically, UcWS measured after at least 20 s of walking time correlated with UWS, indicating that UcWS obtained from community-dwelling older adults can feasibly substitute for conventional face-to-face 5-meter UWS testing. In addition, in the present study, we evaluated the predictive performance of UcWS for MCI and found that UcWS not only correlated with UWS but also showed a good correlation with MCI status. MCI was defined using both the NCGG-FAT (domain ratings) and TDAS; correlations with each component measure were modest, consistent with prior reports21,28.

UcWS can, in principle, be captured with IoT or wearable devices, our measurements were obtained exclusively via smartphones. Wearables such as smartwatches are unlikely to achieve high adherence outside of habitual users and may introduce reactivity, making the assessment more conscious and intrusive29,30. Moreover, in the absence of incentives, participant withdrawals occur frequently, rendering the approach unsuitable for long-term follow-up. By contrast, smartphones are already used by roughly 80% of the population (Urban areas in developed countries exceed 90%) (Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications announcement), allow app installation in everyday use, and therefore minimize device-related selection bias. Because smartphone users are numerous, participant selection bias is reduced, and nearly all participants can be evaluated without interference in their daily lives31.

Furthermore, although several dementia assessment tools have been developed, most require the individual’s voluntary participation. Among people with cognitive impairment, this reliance can lead to variability in test performance and frequency, potentially narrowing the evaluable population30,32. Our approach requires minimal dependence on participant characteristics and can sensitively capture longitudinal change simply by having individuals carry their phone while walking, suggesting the potential to evolve into a highly efficient and cost-effective tool. Importantly, it enables automatic tracking of changes in MCI screening status from natural, everyday walking without any active tasks or intrusive prompts. In addition, real-time data collection enables continuous, remote measurement in everyday life, providing a marked advantage by allowing assessment without the need for a clinical setting. From both patient-centered and societal-burden perspectives, there is a growing need for scalable approaches to dementia prevention and early MCI prediction. In this context, the accurate feature construction based on daily walking speed demonstrated in the present study is expected to have strong potential for further development and broad applicability in future device-based strategies aimed at population-level dementia prevention. Although the present study focused on mean walking speed as an initial clinically translatable metric, previous studies have shown that gait variability and temporal gait dynamics may represent particularly sensitive markers of neurological dysfunction and cognitive decline17,18,20. In neurological populations, digital gait variability metrics have demonstrated important clinical relevance for disease monitoring and fall-risk prediction16,19. Future analyses incorporating variability-related parameters into broader UcWS frameworks may further enhance sensitivity for early MCI screening.

This study has several limitations. Participants were restricted to residents of Yokote City, and data were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may limit generalizability to other regions and time periods. Second, only individuals who owned and used smartphones were eligible, which may introduce selection bias. Third, the application was not validated across all smartphone brands and operating systems worldwide, and subtle differences in hardware or software could affect measurement accuracy; further technical validation studies are warranted.

In conclusions, we identified a UcWS analytic method based on simple by walking-duration stratification that may serve as a practical proxy for UWS. Because UcWS represents a ubiquitous behavioral signal that may be captured during very early, potentially preclinical stages of cognitive decline, our findings suggest that passive smartphone-derived walking speed may support scalable MCI screening research without requiring structured clinical testing. More broadly, the current study findings should be interpreted as a foundational methodological step within the expanding field of real-world digital biomarker development. As smartphone-based monitoring technologies continue to evolve, integration of passive gait measures with broader clinical, frailty-related, and longitudinal datasets may support increasingly sophisticated predictive frameworks for cognitive and neurological health18,20.
