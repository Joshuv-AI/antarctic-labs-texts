Enhanced antimicrobial and anticancer properties of penicillin G–adapted Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG against colorectal cancer cells
Authors: Fardin Soltani, Seyedeh Zahra Bahojb Mahdavi, Morteza Eskandani, Mohammad Yousef Memar, Omid Pourbagherian, Amir Ali Mokhtarzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent cancers globally, ranking third in women and fourth in men, and accounts for approximately 10% of cancer-related deaths. Antibiotic therapy and chemotherapy are common treatments for intestinal diseases and CRC, but they can affect the gut microbiota and cause dysbiosis. Probiotics are vital for maintaining a healthy gut microbiota and preventing dysbiosis. Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (L. rhamnosus) is well-known for its antimicrobial and anticancer properties.MethodsIn the present study, we aimed to investigate the effects of long-term penicillin G treatment on the antimicrobial and anticancer properties of L. rhamnosus. Initially, an antibiotic-resistant L. rhamnosus was created by the adaptation process. The acid tolerance of both antibiotic-resistant and normal strains was assessed. The antimicrobial properties of the cell-free supernatants (CFS) from both untreated and penicillin G-treated strains (Pen(G)-CFS) on different species of pathogenic bacteria, including Staphylococcus epidermidis (CoNS), Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus faecalis, Salmonella typhi, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were investigated. To assess the anticancer effects of these supernatants on HT-29 CRC cells, various cellular and molecular assays, including MTT, apoptosis (Annexin V/PI staining), cell cycle, scratch, and colony formation assays, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), and Western blot were performed.ResultsOur results demonstrated no significant difference in proliferation between the two strains in acidic conditions. Interestingly, Pen(G)-CFS exhibited a significantly stronger antimicrobial effect against pathogenic bacteria compared to the CFS. The results of cellular experiments showed that treating HT-29 cells with Pen(G)-CFS compared to CFS could significantly inhibit proliferation. Additionally, this treatment increased the rate of apoptosis, arrested the cell cycle in the sub-G1 phase, and suppressed cell migration and colony formation ability. These findings were confirmed using qRT-PCR and western blot analysis, which revealed Pen(G)-CFS could considerably modulate the expression of genes and proteins (BAX, BCL2, CASP3, CASP9, MMP3, MMP9) compared with CFS.ConclusionsOur results suggest that antibiotic-resistant L. rhamnosus not only retains probiotic properties but also exhibits enhanced antimicrobial and anticancer activities compared to the normal strain. This approach holds promise for reducing the adverse effects of antibiotics and chemotherapy on the gut microbiota and may offer a novel strategy for improving patient outcomes in CRC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42840-1.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Probiotic, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus, Penicillin GSubject terms: Cancer, Microbiology

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a globally significant problem and is the third most widespread and second most common cause of cancer deaths worldwide. CRC is influenced by economic and social development, lifestyle changes, and various other factors that affect the overall outcome of the disease1. The survival rates for CRC vary for a variety of reasons. The 5-year survival rate is 58% globally, but there are significant differences across racial and gender groups. On the whole, men are more likely to get the disease (60%) than women (57%). To comprehend CRC, it is crucial to discern its heterogeneity, which is particularly manifested in different clinical presentations, molecular profiles, and therapeutic effects. This heterogeneity arises from the several diverse types of CRC2. Generally, the traditional means of treating such a complex disease are surgery, chemotherapy, and, at times, radiation therapy. These can be used individually or in combination to treat the patient. However, due to their nonspecific nature, they are associated with numerous adverse effects linked to their cytotoxicity, affecting all cells, including normal cells3.

The gut microbiota is a collection of various species of microorganisms, such as prokaryotes, viruses, fungi, and other unicellular organisms that inhabit in the digestive tract. Through several mechanisms, this ecosystem becomes an important part of health or a source of disease. Via the microbiota, the gut can digest food that the human gastrointestinal system is unable to act on, such as fibers and sugars. The bacterial community that resides in the gastrointestinal system creates a lining over the intestinal surface making the immunity and physical wall between the gut and the blood stronger. A normal gut microbiota ensures there is no leak of pathogenic bacteria as well as toxins from the intestines to circulation which may be the main factor of chronic inflammation hence the development of chronic diseases4.

The microbiome exhibits a dual role through both direct and indirect alterations in CRC, which can either drive or inhibit tumor growth and the course of the disease. These processes include inflammation modulation, improving host defense surveillance, and immune homeostasis. The most recent research conducted shows that some bacterial strains and their metabolites can majorly impact the effectiveness of cancer therapy5. For instance, Mager et al. (2020) demonstrated that gut commensals (Lactobacillus johnsonii, Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, and Olsenella spp.) enhance checkpoint-inhibitor immunotherapy through the production of inosine, a metabolite that can activate anti-tumor T cells during intestinal transit. The efficacy of inosine depends on signaling and stimulation of the adenosine A2A receptor (A2AR) expressed by T cells, improving responses in melanoma, bladder, and colorectal cancer models. These findings suggest a potential microbial-immune metabolite axis for adjuvant cancer therapies6. Probiotics are live microorganisms that benefit the host by regulating the intestinal microbiota. They are broadly applied to prevent gastrointestinal disorders, improve immune functions, and boost nutrient absorption. Probiotics play essential roles in inhibiting pathogenic bacterial colonization, improving intestinal barrier function, and relieving conditions such as inflammation and dysbiosis. Fermented foods, including yogurt and kimchi, are natural reservoirs of probiotics7,8. Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (L. rhamnosus) is one of the most popular and widely used probiotics known for its ability to survive the low pH environment of the stomach, adhere to the intestinal mucosa, and produce protective biofilms. L. rhamnosus improves intestinal health by enhancing epithelial integrity, decreasing apoptosis, and defending against pathogenic bacteria such as Salmonella. Furthermore, it supports the immune response by stimulating type 1 immune responses, boosting the production of IL-10, IL-12, and TNF-α in macrophages, and mitigating inflammatory markers9. Studies in animal models show that oral administration of L. rhamnosus strengthens anti-PD-1 immunotherapy and by enhancing the recruitment of cytotoxic CD8 + T cells and dendritic cells, positioning it as a promising approach for improving cancer treatment outcomes9,10. Antibiotic treatment and chemotherapy profoundly affect the gut microbiome. They often cause dysbiosis, characterized by diminished microbial diversity, loss of beneficial bacterial populations, and proliferation of harmful pathogens. This disorder can aggravate gastrointestinal side effects like diarrhea, mucositis, and systemic inflammation and considerably affect the patient’s quality of life. Probiotics, such as Lactobacillus species, are effective in reducing these adverse effects. They help restore microbial balance, strengthen the integrity of the intestinal barrier, and diminish inflammation. For example, probiotics can decrease chemotherapy-induced mucosal damage by modulating immune responses and suppressing the growth of harmful bacteria11–13.

Penicillin G (benzylpenicillin) is a β-lactam antibiotic that is mainly effective against Gram-positive bacteria, such as Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Clostridium species. It acts by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis through binding to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), which leads to cell lysis. Despite its broad utility, the emergence of β-lactamase production and altered PBPs has limited its effectiveness14. There is evidence that the administration of penicillin G can alter the composition of the gut microbiota and cause dysbiosis15,16.

Considering the side effects of various treatments on the intestinal microbiota and the potential benefits of probiotic species, this study investigated whether antibiotic-resistant L. rhamnosus (resistant to penicillin G) retains the same probiotic effects as the natural strain. The anti-cancer properties of antibiotic-resistant L. rhamnosus metabolite were evaluated in comparison with the reference strain. The antimicrobial potential of the metabolites was initially assessed through microbiological assay against different pathogenic bacterial strains. Various techniques were used to evaluate anticancer effects, such as MTT assay, Annexin V-FITC staining, cell cycle assay, colony formation assay, etc. In addition, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and western blot assay were used to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which metabolites exert their effects on cancer cells.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and culture

The L. rhamnosus strain (PTCC 1637) was grown in MRS Broth medium at 37 °C for a period of 18 h to achieve an optical density (OD) value ranging between 0.7 and 0.8 at 600 nm wavelength.

Adaptation process

In this step, we aimed to induce the adaptation of L. rhamnosus to elevated concentrations of penicillin G. Initially, L. rhamnosus was cultivated in an MRS broth medium and then treated with penicillin G until a density of 109 CFU/mL was achieved at an OD600 of 0.8. The development of resistance was induced by gradually increasing the concentration of penicillin G (0.001 to 0.02 IU/mL). The bacteria were repeatedly cultured for 48 h in various concentrations of penicillin G until the colony count reached 109 CFU/mL. The resistant strain that tolerated the highest dose of penicillin G (0.015 IU/mL) was selected. Both resistant and normal strains were subjected to centrifugation (4000×g, 5 min) to separate bacterium sediments, which were washed twice in PBS (pH 7.4) and subsequently resuspended in an antibiotic-free RPMI-1640 media and incubated for 24 h. Following incubation, cell-free supernatants (CFS) from penicillin G-treated and normal cultures were harvested, centrifuged, and filtered (0.2 μm pore-size). These were labeled Pen(G)-CFS and CFS, respectively, with pH adjusted to 7.4. The sterilized supernatants were employed in cellular bioassays.

Acid tolerance assay

To evaluate the performance of antibiotic-resistant L. rhamnosus compared to the normal strain under acidic stomach-like conditions, an acid tolerance assay was conducted. Briefly, MRS broth was prepared and adjusted to a pH of 3.0 using HCl. Both strains were cultured in the prepared medium and incubated for 0, 1, 2, and 3 h. Antimicrobial effects

To study the antimicrobial activity, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of supernatants was assessed utilizing the microbroth dilution technique on various bacterial species (Table 1). First, serial dilutions of both Pen(G)-CFS and CFS were made in 96-well microplates with Mueller-Hinton broth. The pathogenic bacteria were subsequently inoculated into the wells of the plates and cultured at 37 °C for 16 to 20 h. After incubation, bacterial growth was evaluated by monitoring turbidity in the wells. The MIC was determined as the lowest concentration of supernatants which prevented bacterial growth. This process was performed in triplicate.

Biofilm formation

The inhibitory effects of Pen(G)-CFS and CFS supernatants on biofilm formation of pathogenic bacteria (listed in Table 1) were assessed using the standard microtiter plate assay. Bacterial suspensions were first adjusted to 0.5 McFarland and then mixed with sub-MIC concentrations of the supernatants in microplate wells, followed by a 1:10 dilution in TSB medium. A total of 200 µL of each mixture was incubated in 96-well plates at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the wells were carefully emptied and washed with PBS, then air-dried before staining with 200 µL of 0.9% crystal violet solution for 15 min. The unbound dye was removed by washing with PBS, and the attached dye was solubilized in 95% ethanol. Biofilm biomass was quantified by measuring the optical density at 450–630 nm using a microtiter plate reader. Control wells contained bacterial suspensions in TSB without supernatant supplementation17.

Mammalian cell culture

HT-29 cell line, a human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line, was provided by the Pasteur Institute of Iran and grown in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS from Gibco, and 1% antibiotic mix comprising penicillin (100 U/ml) and streptomycin (100 µg/ml). The cells were seeded in a 25 cm² culture flask at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO₂ and 95% relative humidity.

MTT assay

To evaluate the impact of bacterial supernatants on cell proliferation, the MTT test was performed. Briefly, 1 × 104 cells per well were dispensed into 96-well plates using a hemocytometer, and cell viability was determined to be > 95% by trypan blue exclusion assay. Subsequently, the media was exchanged with RPMI-1640 containing various concentrations of supernatants (37.5% (v/v) to 100% (v/v)), followed by 24 h of incubation. Next, 50 µL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL) and 100 µL of complete media were dispensed into each well and maintained at 37 °C for 4 hours. Each well received 100 µL of DMSO to dissolve the produced formazan crystals. Absorbance was calculated at 570–620 nm employing an ELISA reader (Sunrise, Tecan, Switzerland). The IC₅₀ values were calculated using nonlinear regression analysis in GraphPad Prism software (version 9).

Apoptosis analysis using Annexin V/PI staining

Apoptosis in HT-29 cells was analyzed utilizing the FITC Annexin V/PI assay kit (Exbio, Czech Republic) following the kit instructions. Initially, 2.5 × 105 HT-29 cells were seeded into each well of the 6-well plates. The cells were grown to approximately 70–80% confluence and then exposed to a concentration slightly less than IC50 of Pen(G)-CFS (65% (v/v)) for a duration of 24 h. After incubation, the cells were harvested, rinsed twice with chilled PBS, and stained with 5 µl of FITC Annexin V and 5 µl of PI (as provided in the kit) in 100 µl of 1X binding buffer, resulting in final concentrations as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The staining was performed in the dark at room temperature for 15–20 min. Flow cytometric analysis was carried out using a MACS Quant flow cytometer, and the obtained data were analyzed employing FlowJo software.

Cell cycle analysis

Flow cytometry was utilized to evaluate the progression of the cell cycle, with DAPI staining performed according to the standard protocol supplied by the manufacturer. Initially, 2.5 × 105 HT-29 cells were seeded into each well of the 6-well plates. Briefly, cells were exposed to the Pen(G)-CFS and CFS at a concentration of 65% (v/v) for 24 h. Afterward, the cells were harvested, washed using PBS, and fixed in 75% ethanol (1 mL) for storage at -20 °C overnight. After fixation, the cells were rewashed with PBS and treated with PBS containing 5µL of RNase A for 30 min. A staining solution containing DAPI and 0.1% Triton X-100 was added to the cells, and the sample was kept at 4 °C for 30 min to incubate. The distribution of cells across the cell cycle phases was evaluated through flow cytometry, and data interpretation was carried out using FlowJo software.

Scratch wound healing assay

To investigate how bacterial supernatants affect cell migration, a scratch assay was conducted. A suspension of cells was distributed into 24-well plates and incubated for 24 h. A linear scratch was created utilizing a sterile yellow pipette tip, and the culture medium was replaced with a fresh medium containing Pen(G)-CFS and CFS at their respective concentrations (65% (v/v). To assess wound closure, the plate was incubated for 72 h at 37 °C and images were taken at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after treatment with an OPTIKA inverted light microscope. Wound healing was assessed qualitatively by direct visual comparison of the images at each time point (0, 24, 48, and 72 h). The extent of wound closure and cell migration into the scratch area was evaluated and compared between the treatment groups and the untreated control. This visual assessment provided a clear and consistent observation of the inhibitory effects of the bacterial supernatants on cell migration.

Colony formation assay

A colony formation assay was performed to evaluate the influence of bacterial supernatants on the growth of HT-29 cell colonies. Approximately 1000 cells were distributed in each cavity of the six-well plate, where 2 mL of culture medium was added. After an initial incubation period of 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, the cells were treated with bacterial supernatants at the respective concentrations (65% (v/v). Following treatment, the cells were incubated for an additional seven days. After incubation, the colonies were rinsed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 60 min, stained with 0.01% crystal violet solution for 30 min, and washed again with PBS. The colonies were manually enumerated using an OPTIKA light microscope.

Extraction of RNA and qRT-PCR analysis

Cells were cultured and treated with bacterial supernatants (65% (v/v)concentrations) in a six-well plate. Using the TRIzol™ Reagent kit, 1 mL of TRIzol reagent was added to each well to lyse cells and transferred to microcentrifuge tubes. Total RNA was extracted following the protocol. The concentration and purity of RNA were verified by measuring the 260/280 nm absorbance ratio using a NanoDrop instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Using the Biofact™ RT Kit, the purified RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA. Target gene expression analysis (BAX, BCL2, CASP3, CASP9, MMP3, MMP9) was performed via qRT-PCR employing BioFACT™ 2X Master Mix (Korea). Reactions were performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems, USA), with GAPDH serving as the internal normalization gene. Western blot

Cells were cultured and treated with supernatants (65% (v/v) concentrations), and Proteins were extracted from cells (1 × 10⁶ cells) using 500 µL of lysis buffer containing Tris-HCl (pH 8), NaCl, EDTA, sodium deoxycholate, SDS, NP-40 (1%), and a protease inhibitor cocktail. The samples were homogenized and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected as the total protein extracts and stored at − 20 °C until use. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was used to generate a standard curve with serial dilutions, and absorbance was measured at 630 nm using an ELISA reader. Protein concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve using Microsoft Excel software. Equalized protein samples were mixed with 3× sample buffer containing Tris (pH 6.8), glycerol, β-mercaptoethanol, SDS, and bromophenol blue, followed by boiling at 100 °C for 5–10 min. Proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE using a 10% separating gel and a 5% stacking gel. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane using a wet transfer system at 120 V for 1.5 h. The PVDF membrane was pre-activated in methanol prior to transfer. Following transfer, the membrane was blocked with 2% non-fat dry milk in TBS-T for 1 h and 15 min at room temperature to prevent non-specific binding. The membrane was then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution overnight (16–18 h) at 4 °C. GAPDH was used as the internal loading control. After primary antibody incubation, the membrane was washed three times with TBS-T, each for 15 min, and subsequently incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody diluted at 1:1000 for 1 h and 15 min at room temperature with gentle shaking. The membrane was then washed again three times with TBS-T for 15 min each. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents. Reagents A and B were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and applied to the PVDF membrane (0.1 mL per cm²). The membrane was wrapped in plastic film and exposed to X-ray film in a darkroom. Exposure times ranged from 10 to 80 s depending on the target protein. Developed films were analyzed using ImageJ software for densitometric quantification. Protein expression levels were normalized to GAPDH.

Statistical analysis

Results were reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical evaluations were carried out using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA, www.graphpad.com). The significant difference between groups was assessed using Student’s t-test for pairwise comparisons and one-way ANOVA for multiple group analyses. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Adaptation of L. rhamnosus with penicillin G

In this study, we investigated the anticancer properties of antibiotic-resistant L. rhamnosus compared to normal L. rhamnosus as a reference strain. For this purpose, a strain of L. rhamnosus that tolerates high concentrations of penicillin G was developed. In the first step, the bacteria were exposed to gradually increasing doses of penicillin G (0.001 to 0.02 IU/mL). At the beginning of penicillin G treatment, the bacteria experienced a significant decrease in growth, but after repeated subcultures, their growth rate returned to normal levels (Fig. 1). For each dose of penicillin G, the bacterial cultivation process was repeated until the L. rhamnosus population reached the specified CFU level of normal bacteria. Following approximately 170 days of repeated cultivation, it was determined that L. rhamnosus failed to adapt to 0.02 IU/mL of antibiotic, and its normal growth was maintained only at concentrations up to 0.015 IU/mL.

The process of L. rhamnosus adaptation to penicillin G. The graph demonstrates that over 170 days of repeated subculturing, the bacteria were able to tolerate a maximum concentration of 0.015 IU/mL penicillin G.

Acid tolerance assay

One of the key characteristics of probiotics is their resistance to acidic conditions, which allows them to survive the stomach’s harsh conditions and colonize the intestine. The acid tolerance assay was performed to evaluate whether antibiotic-resistant L. rhamnosus could tolerate acidic conditions similar to those of the normal strain. As shown in Table 3, no significant change was observed between the two strains (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that long-term treatment with penicillin G does not affect the acid-resistance properties of L. rhamnosus.

Antimicrobial effects of CFS and Pen(G)-CFS

Results from the antimicrobial assay revealed that Pen(G)-CFS significantly (p < 0.05) decreased the MIC values across all bacterial strains (Table 1) relative to CFS (Fig. 3). The findings showed that the antimicrobial activity of L. rhamnosus increased after adaptation to penicillin G. This improvement allowed the strain to suppress pathogenic bacteria at lower supernatant concentrations. Microbroth dilution results showed that the MIC of coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, S. aureus and E. faecalis in the Penicillin G treated bacterial cell free supernatant (Pen (G) CFS) groups was lower than that in the untreated bacterial cell free supernatant (CFS), so Pen(G)-CFS has a greater antimicrobial effect than CFS, the difference between these two groups is significant (p < 0.05).

Antibiofilm effect of CFS and Pen(G)-CFS

The antibiofilm assessment indicated that while the CFS suppressed biofilm formation across most bacterial species, it exhibited no significant inhibitory effect against E. coli. Notably, Pen(G)-CFS demonstrated comparable or superior biofilm inhibition relative to CFS, which showed effectiveness against all tested strains, including E. coli. In other words, the antibiofilm effect of the Pen(G)-CFS and CFS groups was significantly greater than the control group in Gram positive bacteria. The effects of CFS and Pen(G)-CFS on biofilm formation in Gram-negative bacteria (Salmonella typhi, P. aeruginosa, and E. coli) were investigated. The antibiofilm inhibition effect of the Pen (G)-CFS and CFS groups was significant compared to the control group, except in E. coli, in which CFS had no significant effect. Also, the antibiofilm inhibition effect of Pen(G)-CFS is greater than CFS against P. aeruginosa, and E. coli, this difference was not significant only in Salmonella typhi bacteria (p > 0.05). MTT assay

To determine the cytotoxicity of penicillin G-treated bacterial CFS (Pen(G)-CFS) and untreated bacterial CFS on HT-29 cells, an MTT assay was performed. Findings indicated that both Pen(G)-CFS and CFS showed a dose-dependent cytotoxicity toward cancer cells. At doses of 37.5%, 50%, 87.5%, and 100% (v/v), the cytotoxic effects of the two supernatants were similar. Notably, at doses of 62.5% and 75% (v/v), Pen(G)-CFS demonstrated a significantly greater cytotoxic effect compared to CFS (p < 0.0001). Furthermore, the IC50 values were calculated as 70.55% (v/v) for Pen(G)-CFS and 87.35% (v/v) for CFS (Fig. 5).

Effect of Pen(G)-CFS and CFS on cell apoptosis

The Annexin V/PI staining assay assessed the impact of Pen(G)-CFS and CFS on apoptosis induction in HT-29 cells. As depicted in Fig. 6, the apoptosis rate was significantly elevated in cells treated with Pen(G)-CFS (40.3%) and CFS (14.38%) compared with the control. It is noteworthy that Pen(G)-CFS showed a greater apoptotic effect than CFS (p < 0.0001). To evaluate the molecular mechanisms more deeply, qRT-PCR was performed to study the expression of apoptosis-related genes. As represented in Fig. 7, treatment with Pen(G)-CFS significantly upregulated pro-apoptotic genes, including Bax (p < 0.001), Caspase-3 (p < 0.001), and Caspase-9 (p < 0.01), while downregulating the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2 (p < 0.001). In contrast, CFS treatment significantly raised the expression levels of Bax and Caspase-3, but the changes in Bcl-2 and Caspase-9 expression were not statistically significant (ns). These gene expression changes were more pronounced in Pen(G)-CFS-treated cells, highlighting its superior pro-apoptotic potential.

Effect of CFS and Pen(G)-CFS on cell cycle status

To investigate the mechanism behind the effect of supernatant on cell proliferation, flow cytometric analysis was employed to evaluate cell cycle arrest. As shown in Fig. 8, treatment with both CFS and Pen(G)-CFS resulted in a significant increase in Sub-G1 phase arrest compared to the control group (p < 0.0001). The CFS treatment elevated the Sub-G1 cell population from 1.23% in the control group to 8.20%. In comparison, Pen(G)-CFS treatment considerably increased the Sub-G1 cell population to 45.1%. The data show that Pen(G)-CFS induces a significant cell cycle arrest in HT-29 cells at the Sub-G1 phase compared to CFS (p < 0.0001).

Effect of Pen(G)-CFS and CFS on cell migration

Since cell migration is an essential early phase of metastasis, a wound-healing assay was applied to determine the migration capacity of HT-29 cells. According to the scratch assay result, both CFS and Pen(G)-CFS treatments remarkably reduced cell migration, with Pen(G)-CFS offering a more potent inhibitory effect (Fig. 9). To achieve a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanism involved in malignant behavior, the qRT-PCR technique was employed to assess the expression levels of metastasis-related genes such as MMP-3 and MMP-9. As depicted in Fig. 10, in CFS-treated cells the levels of MMP-3 and MMP-9 expression did not significantly differ. However, Pen(G)-CFS treatment remarkably reduced the expression of these genes (p < 0.01), (p < 0.001).

To verify the suppressive effects of CFS and Pen(G)-CFS on the stemness characteristics of cancer cells, a colony formation assay was conducted. Western blot

To evaluate the effects of CFS and Pen(G)-CFS on protein expression, western blot analysis was performed. The results demonstrated that both treatments significantly modulated the expression of Bax and Bcl-2 (Figs. 12 and 13). Based on the results, Bax/Bcl-2 ratio was significantly higher in Pen(G)-CFS treated group compared to control and CFS treated group (Fig. 14). Furthermore, treatment with CFS and Pen(G)-CFS markedly raised the levels of Cleaved-Caspase-3 and Cleaved-Caspase-9, while reducing their pro-forms (Pro-Caspase-3/9). The Cleaved-to-Pro-Caspase ratio was significantly higher in Pen(G)-CFS-treated groups (Figs. 12 and 13), which confirmed its superior efficacy. Additionally, both treatments significantly suppressed MMP-3 and MMP-9 expression. Notably, Pen(G)-CFS showed a stronger inhibitory effect (Figs. 12 and 14).

Discussion

A distinctive feature of CRC is its close relationship to the gut microbiota, which plays an important role in shaping the tumor microenvironment. Over the recent years, studies have highlighted a connection between gut microbial dysbiosis, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses, and the development of CRC, suggesting a potential role in tumorigenesis. Mechanistic studies have revealed the capacity of the gut microbiota to engage with the colonic epithelium and host immune cells by releasing a wide range of proteins, metabolites, and macromolecules that influence the progression of CRC. Preclinical studies, coupled with specific clinical observations, highlight the role of the gut microbiota in influencing the treatment outcomes of CRC patients undergoing chemotherapy and immunotherapy23.

Treatment with antibiotics and chemotherapy agents causes serious problems for the intestines. These factors have many downstream consequences on the gut microbiome, causing dysbiosis and affecting drug responses. Various studies have shown that preventive administration of probiotics leads to an improvement in the gut microbial community after treatment24,25.

L. rhamnosus was among the earliest probiotics studied for its role in cancer and is now included in experimental research. By producing anti-stress proteins, the bacteria demonstrate resistance against stomach acid and bile salts, allowing them to survive the journey through the intestines after being consumed. Evidence indicates that administering L. rhamnosus can reduce the risk of CRC via modulation of the gut microbiota and reduction of pro-inflammatory compounds. L. rhamnosus is widely recognized for its numerous benefits, including anti-inflammatory properties, prevention of nosocomial infections, and positive impact on dysbiosis26,27. Currently, various methods are used to improve the probiotic effects of bacteria or increase their tolerance to various stresses, such as long-term exposure to antibiotics, which can lead to increased production of bioactive compounds without compromising the main probiotic properties28.

Gradual exposure to increasing sublethal concentrationsis a well-established laboratory evolution strategy that induces physiological adaptation in lactobacilli through overexpression of stress-response genes, enhanced efflux pump activity, and modifications in cell wall/membrane composition without necessitating horizontal transfer of resistance determinants29–31. Based on the previous publications, L. rhamnosus achieves penicillin G resistance through two mechanisms: low-affinity penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) with altered amino acid sequences that prevent penicillin binding while maintaining normal catalytic function, and or it can be due to chromosomal efflux pumps (lmr genes) that actively remove penicillin from cells32. The antibiotic resistance mechanisms in L. rhamnosus are chromosomally encoded rather than plasmid-mediated due to multiple lines of definitive evidence. Direct genome sequencing of L. rhamnosus Pen revealed a single circular 2,884,966 bp chromosome with 46.8% GC content and 2,907 open reading frames, with no plasmids detected. This means that all resistance genes may be chromosomally located33. However, in this study, we did not investigate the mechanisms by which L. rhamnosus developed resistance to penicillin G. This should be investigated further in future studies.

In this study, a novel antibiotic-resistant L. rhamnosus GG strain was developed by prolonged exposure to penicillin G for 170 days to induce tolerance at 0.015 IU/mL. This innovation fills an important gap in the literature is that it demonstrates that the resistance not only preserves but even surpasses the wild-type strain regarding antimicrobial and anticancer activities. This can be particularly beneficial in CRC patients undergoing antibiotic-induced dysbiosis caused by chemotherapy, allowing the probiotic to survive and become active in antibiotic-exposed environments.

An acid tolerance assay was initially performed to evaluate whether long-term treatment of L. rhamnosus with penicillin G affects the bacterium’s capacity to withstand acidic conditions. The results indicated no significant difference in acid tolerance between antibiotic-resistant and normal strains. These findings suggest that the performance of antibiotic-resistant L. rhamnosus under gastric conditions is similar to that of the normal strain. An antimicrobial assay was conducted to evaluate the antibacterial properties of the supernatants against various pathogenic bacteria. Our results indicated that Pen(G)-CFS significantly diminished the MIC rates in all pathogenic bacterial strains compared to CFS. Furthermore, the inhibitory effects of Pen(G)-CFS and CFS on biofilm formation were evaluated. Antibiofilm assays showed that CFS inhibited biofilm formation in most species but was ineffective against E. coli. In contrast, Pen(G)-CFS showed greater efficacy against all strains tested, including E. coli. The antimicrobial activity of probiotics against different types of pathogenic bacteria has been well-documented. Numerous studies have highlighted the antimicrobial potential of Lactobacillaceae strains. In line with our conclusions, Keersmaecker et al. investigated the antimicrobial activity of L. rhamnosus against S. typhimurium. Their findings demonstrated that L. rhamnosus produced a bactericidal compound with low molecular weight and non-proteinaceous properties, that is heat-stable and active at acidic pH against various bacterial species34. In another study, Coman et al. studied the antimicrobial activity of L. rhamnosus against fifteen pathogenic bacterial strains. The results demonstrated that the tested probiotic strain significantly inhibited the growth of most pathogenic bacteria compared to the control group35.

Cellular assays were performed to continue this study. According to the MTT assay results, CFS and Pen(G)-CFS demonstrated dose-dependent cytotoxicity on the cells. At four tested doses, Pen(G)-CFS exhibited similar cytotoxicity to CFS. However, at concentrations of 62.5% (v/v) and 75% (v/v), Pen(G)-CFS showed significantly greater cytotoxic effects compared to CFS. Moreover, the IC50 value of Pen(G)-CFS was considerably lower than that of CFS, signifying higher potency. Annexin V/PI staining revealed that treatment with Pen(G)-CFS and CFS significantly increased the apoptosis rate in HT-29 cells compared to the control group. Furthermore, comparing the two treatment groups indicated that Pen(G)-CFS induced apoptosis more effectively than CFS.

Consistent with our observations, the study by Gholipour et al. showed that long-term treatment of L. lactis with crocin can improve the properties of probiotics. The results showed that CFS of treated L. lactis (cro-CFS) had a stronger cytotoxic effect on HCT-116 cells compared to the wild-type strain. Furthermore, cro-CFS could induce apoptosis significantly more than CFS36. In another study, L. rhamnosus was exposed to propyl gallate and butylhydroquinone for a long time, and its probiotic properties against HT-29 cells were investigated. The results indicated that the CFS of treated L. rhamnosus had a high potential in inducing cytotoxicity and apoptosis compared to the CFS of wild-type bacteria37.

To investigate the molecular effects of Pen(G)-CFS and CFS on cancer cells, qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses were performed to assess the expression levels of key apoptosis-associated genes. These analyses focused on both pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic marker genes to provide a clearer insight into treatments’ effect on apoptotic pathways.

BCL2 family members are key regulators of the intrinsic apoptosis pathway, displaying both pro- and anti-apoptotic activities. This pathway causes mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP). In fact, they can cause cells to irreversibly progress towards cell death or, contrariwise, allow cells to always escape apoptosis and convert themselves into malignant clones. The BCL2 family, characterized by conserved BCL2 homology (BH) domains, is divided into three subgroups: (a) anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., BCL2, BCL-xL) have BH1–BH4 domains that inhibit apoptosis; (b) pro-apoptotic effector proteins (e.g., BAX, BAK) possess BH1–BH3 domains that promote MOMP; and (c) BH3-only pro-apoptotic proteins (e.g., BID, BAD) are characterized by a single BH3 domain38,39. In the intrinsic apoptosis pathway, CASP3 and CASP9 play crucial roles. CASP9 initiates the cascade by activating CASP3, which leads to cell death by cleaving cellular proteins. Together, CASP3 and CASP9 participate as key players in mitochondria-driven apoptosis40.

Our qRT-PCR results showed that treatment of HT-29 cells with CFS notably increased the expression of BAX and CASP3 mRNA, but had no significant effect on the expression of BCL2 and CASP9 mRNA. On the other hand, treatment of cells with Pen(G)-CFS significantly affected the expression of all four genes, causing suppression of BCL2 and increase of BAX, CASP3, and CASP9 mRNA. Western blot analysis further demonstrated that both treatments significantly downregulated BCL2 protein while upregulating BAX and CASP3 (and to a lesser extent CASP9) protein expression. Notably, Pen(G)-CFS exhibited remarkably stronger effects on these apoptotic markers compared to CFS alone. These results highlight the superior pro-apoptotic potential of Pen(G)-CFS. Consistent with our findings, a recent study by Amin et al. showed that treatment of Caco-2 cells with L. rhamnosus GG-derived extract significantly reduced viability in a time- and dose-dependent manner. Furthermore, their results demonstrated that the extract induced apoptosis through increased CASP3 and CASP9 activation accompanied by downregulation of BCL2 and BCL-xL proteins41. In another study, Erfanian and colleagues investigated the anticancer effects of postbiotics derived from L. rhamnosus GG on the HT-29 cell line. Their results revealed that these postbiotics exerted anti-proliferative and anti-migratory effects and induced apoptosis through upregulation of BAX and CASP3 and downregulation of BCL2 at both mRNA and protein levels42.

Inducing cell cycle arrest is a fundamental strategy in cancer treatment, as it prevents cancer cells from proliferating. This mechanism not only reduces tumor growth but also improves the effectiveness of simultaneous treatments. It is especially crucial for designing targeted therapies that use specific cell cycle checkpoints to selectively eradicate cancer cells43. In the present study, we evaluated the therapeutic effects of Pen(G)-CFS and CFS on cell cycle status. Our results revealed that both supernatants could significantly increase the cell population at the sub-G1 phase compared to the control group. Additionally, a comparison of the two groups displayed that cell cycle arrest was remarkably higher in the Pen(G)-CFS group compared to the CFS group. In agreement with our findings, Dehghani et al. also conducted a similar project and investigated the effects of L. rhamnosus supernatant on HT-29 cells. They reported that treating cells with the supernatant significantly increased cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 phase. Furthermore, the supernatant could induce apoptosis and suppress cell proliferation44.

The ability of malignant cells to migrate has a vital role in tumor development. It enables tumor cells to infiltrate surrounding tissues and spread metastases in remote organs. Complex mechanisms, such as the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) drive this process and enhance the motility and invasiveness of cancer cells. Furthermore, degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) by enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) promotes tumor cell invasion45. MMP-3 and MMP-9 play a key role in cancer metastasis. MMP-3 contributes to the creation of a favorable tumor microenvironment for metastasis by remodeling the ECM and inducing inflammation, while MMP-9 enhances tumor cell migration and promotes angiogenesis. Targeting these enzymes may provide therapeutic potential to limit cancer progression46,47.

In the present study, the anti-migratory potential of the supernatants was evaluated using the wound-healing (scratch) assay. The results revealed that both CFS and Pen(G)-CFS significantly inhibited HT-29 cell migration, with Pen(G)-CFS exhibiting markedly stronger suppression compared to CFS alone. To explore the underlying molecular mechanisms, qRT-PCR was performed. Treatment with CFS did not significantly alter MMP3 or MMP9 mRNA levels, whereas Pen(G)-CFS dramatically downregulated both MMP3 and MMP9 mRNA expression. Consistent with the transcriptional changes, Western blot analysis demonstrated that both treatments reduced MMP3 and MMP9 protein levels, with Pen(G)-CFS showing a significantly greater inhibitory effect than CFS. These findings indicate that long-term adaptation of L. rhamnosus GG to penicillin G not only preserves but substantially enhances its anti-migratory and anti-metastatic properties. In agreement with our results, Lee et al. reported that L. rhamnosus GG-derived metabolites strongly suppressed migration of malignant melanoma cells by reversing EMT through upregulation of CDH1 (E-cadherin), downregulation of CDH2 (N-cadherin) and VIM (vimentin), and suppression of MYC48. Similarly, Maghsood et al. showed that L. rhamnosus GG CFS reduced MMP9 expression in THP-1 cells by downregulating BSG (CD147) and upregulating TIMP149.

The clonogenic assay is a basic laboratory technique used to assess the reproductive capacity of individual cancer cells after treatment. This method explores the ability of a single cell to proliferate indefinitely, and form a colony and provides valuable insights into its capacity to endure cytotoxic treatments like radiation or chemotherapy50. In the current research, we employed the colony formation assay to investigate the impacts of supernatants on the reproductive viability of HT-29 cells. Our findings revealed that both supernatants markedly reduced the colony formation ability of the cancer cells; however, Pen(G)-CFS led to a more potent inhibitory effect than CFS. In another similar study, Sugimura et al. investigated the anticancer effects of L. gallinarum on CRC cells. They reported that treatment with the supernatant markedly reduced proliferation and colony formation51. We summarized the anticancer effects of Pen(G)-CFS in HT-29 colorectal cancer cells (Fig. 15).

Proposed mechanism of action of Pen(G)-CFS on HT-29 colorectal cancer cells. Pen(G)-CFS derived from Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG adapted to penicillin G exerts multi-target anticancer effects in HT-29 cells. Pen(G)-CFS induces intrinsic apoptosis through upregulation of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX and downregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2. The expression of caspase-9 and caspase-3 increases, ultimately resulting in apoptotic cell death. Furthermore, Pen(G)-CFS causes cell cycle arrest with accumulation of cells in the sub-G1 phase. Additionally, Pen(G)-CFS inhibits cancer cell migration and invasion by downregulating matrix metalloproteinases MMP3 and MMP9. Collectively, these molecular events lead to reduced cell viability, decreased colony formation, and overall inhibition of colorectal cancer progression. Green arrows indicate activation or upregulation, while red T-bars indicate inhibition or downregulation.

Overall, the present study demonstrates that long-term adaptation of L. rhamnosus GG to sublethal concentrations of penicillin G yields a robust derivative strain that fully retains its probiotic properties (acid tolerance) while displaying significantly enhanced antimicrobial and anticancer activities. These improvements are likely attributable to stress-induced overproduction or modification of bioactive metabolites present in the cell-free supernatant. However, several limitations must be acknowledged: the findings are based exclusively on in vitro models that cannot fully recapitulate the complex gut microenvironment, only a single probiotic strain was investigated, and the precise identity and structure of the bioactive compounds responsible for the observed effects remain uncharacterized. Future studies are therefore warranted to1 validate these results in appropriate in vivo and ex vivo models of the intestinal ecosystem 2, elucidate the genetic and metabolic mechanisms underlying the enhanced bioactivity3, identify and characterize the specific metabolites conferring superior antimicrobial and anticancer properties4, explore potential synergistic or antagonistic interactions with conventional antibiotics, and5 assess the safety, efficacy, and clinical translational potential of this adapted strain for preventing or alleviating chemotherapy- and antibiotic-associated gut dysbiosis in patients with colorectal cancer.

Conclusion

The present study aimed to compare the antimicrobial and anticancer properties of a penicillin G-adapted L. rhamnosus GG strain (Pen(G)-L. rhamnosus GG) with its parental strain. Acid tolerance assays revealed no significant differences between the two strains, confirming that long-term antibiotic exposure preserved key probiotic characteristics. Antimicrobial assays demonstrated that Pen(G)-CFS exhibited significantly lower MIC values against all tested pathogenic bacteria compared to CFS from the parental strain. In vitro anticancer assays further revealed markedly enhanced activity of Pen(G)-CFS. Compared with CFS-treated HT-29 cells, Pen(G)-CFS more potently inhibited cell proliferation, induced apoptosis, and altered the expression of key apoptosis-regulatory genes, including upregulation of BAX, CASP3, and CASP9 and downregulation of BCL2. Flow cytometry analysis showed a prominent increase in the sub-G1 population following Pen(G)-CFS treatment, indicative of enhanced apoptotic cell death. Moreover, wound-healing and colony-formation assays confirmed superior anti-migratory and anti-clonogenic effects of Pen(G)-CFS over CFS, which correlated with stronger downregulation of MMP3 and MMP9 mRNA and protein levels. Collectively, these results demonstrate that prolonged adaptation of L. rhamnosus GG to sublethal concentrations of penicillin G generates a robust derivative strain that retains full probiotic traits while displaying significantly improved antimicrobial and anticancer activities. These findings open new avenues for the development of next-generation probiotics capable of simultaneously combating pathogenic bacteria and supporting anticancer therapy, thereby mitigating antibiotic- and chemotherapy-induced gut dysbiosis. Future studies are warranted to fully elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and to evaluate the in vivo efficacy and safety of this adapted strain in preclinical and clinical settings.
