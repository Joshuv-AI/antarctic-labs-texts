Whole-genome sequencing identifies HOXD13 variants in syndactyly pedigrees
Authors: Yi-feng Xu, Jing Zhang, Tian-ying Wei, Man-li Zhang, Jia-en Liu, Kai Yang, Ya-ping Tian, Hua-ying Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSyndactyly demonstrates high genetic heterogeneity, with many cases lacking molecular diagnosis despite known HOXD13 involvement, suggesting conventional methods may miss a sub-class of variants.ResultsIntegrated whole-exome sequencing (WES) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) analyses identified three novel HOXD13 variants: one 2-bp heterozygous deletion c.314_315del, p.(Lys105ArgfsTer131), and two heterozygous polyalanine expansions (PAE): c.186_212dup, p.(Ala63_Ala71dup) and c.203_204insAGCAGCGGCGGCTGCGGCGGCGGC, p.(Ala64_Ala71dup). WGS successfully identified cryptic variants undetectable by WES technology.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate the utility of WGS in identifying HOXD13 variants and support the genotype-phenotype correlation of polyalanine expansions in limb malformations, providing new insights for molecular diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00870-2.

Introduction

Syndactyly ranks among the most prevalent congenital limb deformities. A notable osseous subtype is synpolydactyly (SPD), which follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern and typically presents bilaterally in the hands and feet [10]. The phenotypic hallmark of SPD is a combination of syndactyly and polydactyly, most commonly manifesting as partial or complete digit duplication within a syndactylous web between the third/fourth fingers or the fourth/fifth toes [6]. Notably, the management of SPD poses significant challenges in differential diagnosis, genetic counseling, and risk stratification, this is primarily attributable to the condition’s incomplete penetrance and pronounced heterogeneity, both clinically and genetically [9].

Located on chromosome 2q31, the homeobox D13 gene (HOXD13) is a key regulator of limb development and a major locus for SPD [2]. Its protein product functions as a transcription factor in embryonic patterning and features two key domains: an N-terminal polyalanine tract, translated from a polymorphic GCN repeat, and a C-terminal homeodomain that mediates DNA binding [3]. Pathogenic variants cluster within these domains, while expansions of the polyalanine tract represent the most frequent cause of SPD1, deletions, nonsense, and missense substitutions are also reported and are associated with diverse classical or atypical phenotypes. A notable feature of HOXD13-related SPD is its variable expressivity and occasional asymmetry, underscoring the current limitations in defining clear genotype-phenotype correlations.

Current molecular diagnostics for digit malformations primarily use Sanger and next-generation sequencing (NGS). However, Sanger sequencing is inefficient for large-scale screening due to its cost and turnaround time. Although NGS methods like targeted panels and whole-exome sequencing (WES) are widely used, their accuracy in detecting HOXD13 polyalanine tract expansions—key mutations in SPD1—remains limited due to the challenges short-read platforms face with repetitive sequences. While new bioinformatic tools are improving repeat variant detection and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) is becoming more affordable, its cost-effectiveness for diagnosing Mendelian disorders is still debated.

This study details the molecular diagnostic process for three SPD1-type syndactyly pedigrees. Notably, in two pedigrees where WES failed to identify pathogenic mutations, WGS combined with Sanger sequencing validation successfully detected HOXD13 polyalanine repeat expansions. These findings not only highlight the unique capability of WGS in identifying complex structural variants but also provide valuable information for optimizing molecular diagnostic pathways for syndactyly.

Methods

Patient recruitment and sample collection

We examined three unrelated kindreds with affected members presenting limb malformations, collecting detailed pedigrees including family and reproductive histories. Three probands exhibiting characteristic SPD manifestations were selected for comprehensive clinical evaluation, with photographic and radiographic documentation obtained. Peripheral blood samples were collected from probands and available family members, with genomic DNA extracted using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, CA 69506, Germany) for sequencing analyses.

This study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Jiaen Hospital (Approval No.: BJH-2024007).

Whole-exome sequencing and whole-genome sequencing

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on probands from three independent pedigrees, with additional whole-genome sequencing (WGS) conducted for probands in Family 1 and Family 2. The whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on the Illumina sequencing platform (NovaSeqTM 6000) utilizing IDT’s xGen Exome Research Panel v2.0 for target enrichment and library preparation, with a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) sequencing strategy. For whole-genome sequencing (WGS), libraries were constructed using IDT’s 42.5 M/WGS capture system. Raw sequencing data underwent quality assessment using FastQC (v0.11.9) and were aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome via Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA-MEM v0.7.17). Variant calling for single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (Indels) was performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v4.2.6.1) following Best Practices guidelines.

Next-generation sequencing data analysis

The analytic pipeline for variant annotation and prioritization was implemented as previously described [7]. Following quality control, variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) >1% in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v2.1.1) were excluded. Candidate variants were selected based on the following criteria: (1) splice-site variants affecting canonical ± 1–2 bp regions; (2) frameshift variants disrupting protein reading frames; (3) nonsense variants introducing premature termination codons; and (4) missense variants predicted as deleterious by composite computational models (SIFT v6.2.1 [cutoff < 0.05], PolyPhen-2 v2.2.2 [probably damaging score >0.453], GERP++v1.0 [score >2.0], and CADD v1.6 [Phred-scaled score >15]). Given the familial segregation patterns, rare heterozygous variants demonstrating complete cosegregation with the phenotype under an autosomal dominant inheritance model were prioritized. These variants were present in all affected individuals but absent in unaffected family members and 1000 Genomes Project controls.

Verification by Sanger sequencing

PCR amplification of the HOXD13 gene (NM_000523.4) exon 1 region (GRCh37/hg19: Chr2: 176,945,322–76,945,501) was performed using a GC-rich optimized system with the primers 5′-GAGAGAGGGCTAGAGGAAGAGG-3′ (forward) and 5′-GGCAGTTGAAATCAGAGGAGGAAT-3′ (reverse). Reactions contained 1× GC buffer, 200 µM dNTPs, 0.2 µM of each primer, 1 U FastStart High Fidelity DNA polymerase, and 50 ng of genomic DNA. Thermal cycling included an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 1 min/kb; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were purified (QIAquick PCR Purification Kit, QIAGEN, Cat# 28104) and subjected to bidirectional sequencing using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 4337455) with M13 universal primers (forward: 5′-TGTAAAACGACGGCCAGT-3′; reverse: 5′-CAGGAAACAGCTATGACC-3′). Purified sequencing reactions (ethanol/EDTA precipitation) were electrophoresed on an Applied Biosystems 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting electropherograms were analyzed with Mutation Surveyor (v5.1.2, SoftGenetics).

Results

Clinical presentation

Family-1 (Fig. 1A): The phenotype segregated through four consecutive generations, with 7 affected individuals among 28 family members. The proband (IV-12) presented with osseous fusion of the middle and distal phalanges in the right third and fourth digits, accompanied by preaxial polydactyly (Fig. 1D, left). Bilateral syndactyly of the fifth and sixth toes with supernumerary phalanges was observed. The proband’s mother (III-11) displayed angular deformities of the middle and distal phalanges in the third and fourth digits bilaterally (Fig. 1D, right), while the maternal uncle (III-3) exhibited comparable phenotypic features.

Family-2 (Fig. 1B): The trait spanned three generations, affecting 3 of 8 family members. The proband (III-2) demonstrated osseous syndactyly at the proximal phalanges of the third and fourth digits, combined with angular deformity of the middle and distal phalanges in the fifth digit (Fig. 1B). Radiographic analysis revealed postaxial polydactyly with intercalary phalanges between the fourth and fifth toes (Fig. 1E).

Family-3 (Fig. 1C): Transmission through four generations was documented, with 8 affected individuals among 18 family members. The proband (IV-3) exhibited cutaneous syndactyly of the right third and fourth fingers and bilateral postaxial polydactyly (Fig. 1F).

Whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing data analysis

WES analysis identified a frameshift mutation c.314_315del, p.(Lys105ArgfsTer131) in the HOXD13 in the Family-3 proband (Fig. 2C), which introduces a premature termination codon at position 105. However, WES failed to reveal definitive pathogenic variants in the probands of Family-1 and Family-2. Recognizing the limitations of WES in detecting structural variations (SVs) and repeat expansions, we subsequently conducted high-depth (> 30×) whole-genome sequencing (WGS) for these two probands. This integrated approach enabled comprehensive identification of variants potentially missed by WES, including non-coding variants, complex genomic rearrangements, and dynamic mutations.

The standard variant detection pipeline for Illumina whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data successfully identified pathogenic polyalanine repeat expansions in the HOXD13 gene among probands from two families. In Family-1’s proband, we detected a heterozygous 27-base pair (bp) duplication c.186_212dup resulting in an in-frame repeat of alanine residues at positions 63–71. The Family-2 proband carried a 24-bp tandem repeat insertion c.203_204insAGCAGCGGCGGCTGCGGCGGCGGC causing duplication of alanine residues 64–71. Sanger sequencing confirmed these variants and their co-segregation with the phenotype in all pedigrees. The mutations were heterozygous in affected individuals and absent in unaffected controls (Fig. 2A and B, right). Maternal inheritance was observed in Family-1 and Family-2, whereas the Family-3 variant was of paternal origin with the mother confirmed as a non-carrier.

All three variants were located in exon 1 of HOXD13 (Supplemental Fig. S1A). In Family-1, the variant resulted in a 9-alanine repeat expansion p.Ala63_Ala71dup, classified as a likely pathogenic variant; in Family-2, the variant caused an 8-alanine repeat expansion p.Ala64_Ala71dup (Supplemental Fig. S1B), also deemed likely pathogenic. The Family-3 variant led to a lysine-to-arginine substitution at position 105 accompanied by a frameshift mutation, inducing premature translational termination, and was also classified as likely pathogenic.

Integrating segregation analysis with prior pathogenicity evidence, we concluded that polyalanine repeat expansions in HOXD13 were the underlying cause in Family-1 and Family-2, whereas the frameshift deletion in HOXD13 was responsible for the disease in Family-3. The integration of clinical and molecular evidence confirmed autosomal dominant syndactyly-polydactyly type 1 (MIM#186000), ending the diagnostic odyssey for these three families.

Discussion

Although molecular genetic testing has advanced considerably, standard approaches like WES often fail to provide a diagnosis for many suspected rare genetic conditions. A key limitation of WES is its restricted ability to reliably identify small copy-number variations, indels, and complex structural variants. WGS offers a more comprehensive solution, demonstrating enhanced capability to detect single-nucleotide variants and indels due to superior coverage uniformity. It also performs effectively in GC-rich genomic areas that are problematic for WES [8]. In this study, WGS successfully identified causal HOXD13 variants in two syndactyly pedigrees that were missed by WES, highlighting its value as a diagnostic tool when conventional diagnostics fail.

HOXD13 polyalanine repeat expansions are the predominant genetic alterations identified in SPD pedigrees, where a longer repeat length predicts greater clinical severity. This spectrum is evidenced by shorter expansions (≤ 7 alanines), which show incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, contrasting with longer ones (≥ 10 alanines) that typically result in high penetrance and pronounced phenotypes [5]. Our identification of 9-alanine and 8-alanine expansions in two Chinese SPD families aligns with this spectrum. All affected individuals exhibited classic syndactyly-polydactyly manifestations, with the 9-alanine proband showing more severe phenotypic expression, thereby reinforcing the correlation between repeat length and clinical severity.

HOXD13 gene mutations are recognized contributors to SPD, yet the functional mechanisms are not fully elucidated. Although these mutant proteins often maintain their ability to bind DNA, they can interfere with crucial N-terminal interactions, resulting in dysregulated expression of target genes. One central pathological process entails the expansion of polyalanine tracts (≥ 7 residues). Such expansions promote protein aggregation, hinder nuclear translocation, and lead to dosage-sensitive misregulation of downstream genes [4], which aligns with the genotype-phenotype correlations observed in our study. Additionally, we identified a frameshift mutation (p.Lys105Argfs*) predicted to cause a premature termination. Truncated HOXD13 proteins typically fail to bind DNA and can act in a dominant-negative manner, thereby interfering with the normal function of the wild-type allele and disrupting limb development [1].

While our findings highlight the potential of WGS for detecting variants in limb malformations, several limitations should be considered. First, the analysis is based on a relatively small number of pedigrees, which may not fully represent the HOXD13 genotype-phenotype spectrum. Additionally, the apparent superior detection rate of WGS over WES could be influenced by bioinformatic differences, preventing a definitive quantitative comparison. Finally, while the variants’ pathogenicity is supported primarily by their co-segregation with the disease in pedigrees and consistency with established literature, it lacks functional experimental validation. Consequently, our findings, while highlighting the promise of WGS, should be confirmed through larger prospective studies with standardized methodologies.

Conclusions

Our analysis of three HOXD13-mutated pedigrees systematically delineates genotype-phenotype relationships and inheritance patterns, reinforcing HOXD13’s key role in SPD pathogenesis. Crucially, within the confines of our analytical workflow, WGS successfully identified two pathogenic variants that were previously missed by standard WES. This observation, while potentially influenced by bioinformatic factors, underscores the potential diagnostic value of WGS. Consequently, our findings suggest that WGS could be a valuable diagnostic tool in the molecular investigation of congenital limb malformations, though further studies in larger cohorts are warranted to confirm its utility.
