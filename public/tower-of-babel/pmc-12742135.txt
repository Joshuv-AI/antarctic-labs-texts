Potential benefits of group cognitive–behavioural therapy for insomnia in outpatients with coronary heart disease versus usual care: an early economic evaluation
Authors: André Pekkola Pacheco, Toril Dammen, John Munkhaugen, Costas Papageorgiou, Kari Jorunn Kvaerner, Linn Nathalie Støme
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveInsomnia afflicts about half of outpatients with coronary heart disease (CHD) and worsens prognosis. We aimed to simulate the potential socioeconomic savings of integrating group cognitive–behavioural therapy for insomnia (gCBT-I) into cardiac outpatient care for patients with CHD compared with usual care.DesignEarly health technology assessment using prospective and cross-sectional cohort data.SettingSecondary care for outpatients with CHD in Norway in a non-university hospital setting.ParticipantsA hypothetical cohort of 100 outpatients with CHD and insomnia.InterventionsWeekly sessions of gCBT-I over 5 weeks versus usual care.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPotential savings related to possible effects on revascularisations and hospitalisations due to a recurrent CHD event, and reduced sick leave. Representative data on healthcare consumption and sick leave rates were collected from the NORwegian CORonary cohort study (n=528). Costs of productivity loss were drawn from public sources and calculated using a conservative human capital approach. Estimates of therapeutic effectiveness in conveying insomnia remission by gCBT-I were informed by results from randomised trials and meta-analyses.ResultsRates of healthcare consumption and sick leave were higher in outpatients with CHD and insomnia compared with those without insomnia. Our model indicated that treating 100 patients with gCBT-I would cost €2406. If patients treated with gCBT-I show similar rates of revascularisations and hospitalisations as those without insomnia, there is a maximum potential saving of €117,221 per 100 patients tied to healthcare consumption. Similarly, the maximum potential savings related to reduced productivity loss from sick leave range from €53,244 to €692,172.ConclusionTreating insomnia among outpatients with CHD has the potential for substantial reduction of socioeconomic costs related to healthcare consumption and sick leave. Implementation into routine outpatient care seems relevant, but randomised controlled trials investigating the effects of gCBT-I in this population are warranted.Keywords: Coronary heart disease, Insomnia, SLEEP MEDICINE, Health Services, PREVENTIVE MEDICINE, HEALTH ECONOMICS

Introduction

Sleep is recently recognised as a crucial psychosocial and behavioural component of cardiovascular health.1 Alarmingly, almost half of coronary heart disease (CHD) outpatients screen positive for chronic insomnia disorder,2 a condition characterised by at least 1 month of difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, or waking up too early, and suffering from daytime dysfunction (eg, lack of energy and poor concentration) as a consequence.3 In the general population, chronic insomnia is associated with an increased risk of sick leave4 and incurs significant costs for society.5 6 Concurrent with CHD, insomnia is associated with a range of cardiovascular, metabolic and psychosocial risk factors, including subclinical inflammation.2 Besides being linked to poor quality of life,7 recent data show that patients with CHD and comorbid insomnia have a 40% increased risk of experiencing recurrent cardiovascular events compared with those without insomnia, even after adjusting for somatic comorbidity, and symptoms of anxiety and depression.8

Cognitive–behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the recommended first-line treatment9 10 and involves multiple components aiming to improve sleep (eg, restricting time in bed to eliminate prolonged middle of the night awakenings and addressing dysfunctional beliefs about sleep).9 The effects of CBT-I are comparative among patients with comorbid somatic and psychiatric conditions that might affect sleep as in those without.911,13 Trials that have investigated the effect of CBT-I in patients with CVD have shown promise in reducing insomnia symptoms.14,16 However, the availability of CBT-I is limited by factors such as lack of trained therapists and cost of treatment,17 creating a crucial need for innovation of treatment to address this modifiable risk factor in patients with CHD. CBT-I is a safe and efficacious treatment with 70%–80% of patients showing symptom reduction in response to treatment,17 the effects of which can last for up to 10 years.18 Up to 35%–48% of those offered treatment overcome their insomnia.17

CBT-I is cost-effective in the general population in outpatients with comorbid depression and schizophrenia, particularly when assessed over longer time periods.19 However, studies on cost-effectiveness in patients with significant medical comorbidities such as CHD are scarce. In patients with stable heart failure (HF), there seems to be no difference in resource use 3 months and 12 months after treatment.20 Nevertheless, the effects and cost-effectiveness of group CBT-I (gCBT-I) in patients with CHD without HF remain to be ascertained in a randomised controlled trial (RCT). Thus, we have designed an RCT to investigate these aspects in patients with CHD. The intervention resembles a brief course and will be delivered by a nurse and lasts for 5 weeks. It has been developed and validated in patients with insomnia within primary care and oncological care,21,23 and was recently adapted and translated to a Norwegian context with permission from the author of the CBT-I treatment manual.

Implementation into outpatient care nonetheless requires proof of actual and potential cost-effectiveness. Some socioeconomic savings might require years to emerge, such as a reduction of hospitalisations due to CHD. Pending results, potential savings can be estimated using early health technology assessment (early HTA), a method used to evaluate innovations under development to explore potential value prior to broader implementation24 and regulatory approval.25 To our knowledge, this has not been done before. Hence, we aimed to simulate potential socioeconomic benefits of CBT-I in patients with CHD and insomnia, related to savings from reduced hospital admissions for CHD and revascularisation procedures, and reduced sick leave.

Methods

This economic simulation is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards checklist.26 In our model, we compare gCBT-I with the treatment-as-usual condition. Part of the early HTA method included combining input from expert opinion, case reports, RCTs and meta-analyses, a CBT-I treatment manual, a planned future RCT and follow-up data from the NORwegian CORonary (NORCOR) cohort study were used to identify cost and gains of the intervention to build the model.1112 21,23 27

Study population

In order to obtain representative data for this group of patients, we extracted descriptive sociodemographic, clinical and hospital admission data from the NORCOR study, which includes a comprehensive dataset on clinical and psychosocial factors, described in detail elsewhere.27 In brief, patients hospitalised with a CHD event (n=528) aged 18–62 (mean 53.5) years old at two hospitals provided informed consent and attended a cross-sectional examination in 2014–2015, after 2–36 months after the index event.27 Only data on patients <62 years old at study participation are used in the present study because these individuals have at least 5 years or more left in the workforce until Norwegian ordinary retirement age of 67 years. Data on recurrent major adverse cardiovascular events were collected from hospital medical records by two study cardiologists after a median 4.2-year follow-up. The catchment area is representative of the Norwegian population regarding demography, socioeconomic status, morbidity and mortality rates.27 The study received ethical approval from the Regional Ethical Committee (REK 2013/1885 & 678911).

Statistical methods

The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS V.20 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R V.4.3.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria), using the epiR package.28 Figures were created using the ggplot2 package29 in R, with a code template provided by ChatGPT-4 (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA), coupled to the count data and generated in R. Count data for the variables of interest were analysed using the χ2 test with the significance level set at 0.05.

Simulation model

For simulation purposes, we use a 1-year time horizon for the cost of the intervention, while the time horizon for potential savings is based on the follow-up period in the NORCOR study. The time horizon for the cost of the intervention was based on a minimum of one cycle of providing the group-based 5-week therapy to 100 patients, which will ideally take 20 weeks with 5 patients in each group. Hence, we set the discount rate to 0%.

Setting

The simulation is set within an outpatient specialist care setting with cardiac nurses providing the intervention. Norway is a well-developed, tax-funded welfare state that covers most of the cost of hospital care with patients paying a nominal fee to receive healthcare. Social benefits provided during sick leave are covered by the state.

Selection and measurement of outcomes

The primary outcome for the simulation model is remission from fulfilling diagnostic criteria for chronic insomnia disorder (insomnia vs no insomnia) for patients with CHD and chronic insomnia (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition, criteria) receiving gCBT-I or usual care and its associated effects on direct healthcare usage (herein defined as cardiac readmission to hospital and subsequent treatment). The secondary outcome was the effect of gCBT-I on sick leave. The simulation model was constructed based on input from JM (specialist in cardiology) and TD (specialist in psychiatry) related to potential costs and resources required for implementing the intervention and potential effects of treatment. This was supplemented with observations from the literature on response and remission rate1112 17 21,23 combined with empirical evidence indicating reduced rates of sick leave on successful insomnia treatment.30 Therefore, we chose two outcome scenarios, selected to simulate the potential benefits of reducing the proportion of CHD patients with insomnia, with a one-way sensitivity analysis with potential savings related to differential remission rates after treatment.

First, we simulated the effect on direct healthcare usage related to CHD as hospital admissions due to coronary causes requiring percutaneous coronary revascularisation. We assumed an average time in hospital of 3 days, that is, patient alive after 4 days and discharged according to the diagnosis-related group (DRG) cost (table 1). Second, we simulated the effect on rates of sick leave on the short-term (2 weeks) and long-term (26 weeks) following nurse-delivered, protocol-driven gCBT-I compared with treatment as usual (no treatment and no change), comparing the cost of patients with and without insomnia as an ideal outcome scenario, including a one-way sensitivity analysis with differential rates of remission. The treatment-as-usual represents no change in our model. When chronic insomnia is addressed therapeutically and non-pharmacologically in standard care, it is often through sleep hygiene advice, commonly not considered an active component of CBT-I when delivered alone.9 When chronic insomnia is addressed pharmacologically, it is often with compounds that are at odds with current recommendations for treating chronic insomnia, with limited success.9 Nevertheless, only 24% of patients with CHD and insomnia in the NORCOR study take sleep medication.2 The treatment-as-usual condition mirrors cardiac outpatient care in Norway, with low participation rates and a limited recognition of chronic insomnia as a problem for cardiac prognosis or outcomes.31 32

For the primary outcome for this analysis, we used the difference in hospitalisation with CHD with insomnia and the group without insomnia as an exemplar of complete insomnia remission following intervention. The secondary outcome was based on inducing a similar pattern of sick leave in the group with insomnia as the one without. Potential benefits in terms of reduced healthcare consumption were based on an average hospitalisation of 3 days. Length of stay was based on expert opinion (input from JM) as the available dataset only included hospitalisation rates, not length of stay. We estimated the potential benefits in terms of reduced sick leave for short-term (2 weeks) and long-term (26 weeks). As the dataset did not include length of sick leave, we stipulated two scenarios representing consequences of both short-term (2 weeks) and long-term (26 weeks) sick leave. To capture the potential benefits of reducing hospitalisation, we used DRG tariffs as a measure of the average cost per hospitalisation with a cardiac reason requiring revascularisation. The average cost of sick leave was based on a survey of members of the association for companies of the Confederation of Norwegian Enterprise, containing costs associated with productivity loss, expenses for substitutes and overtime when an employee is on sick leave.33 Although first issued in 2011, the report was updated in 2022.34

Financing

Costs of the intervention were derived from a healthcare perspective. Potential benefits of the intervention were estimated for two outcome measures: direct healthcare consumption (hospitalisation and percutaneous coronary intervention) and sick leave, and their indirect expenditures for the public budget. Specialist health services in Norwegian public healthcare are financed through activity-based and basic financing. For 2024, activity-based funding for somatic specialist healthcare was set around 40% and basic funding around 60%.35 Activity-based funding is a system whereby healthcare providers are reimbursed based on the actual treatments or services they deliver to patients, with a predetermined price per performed treatment or service. The purpose of activity-based funding is to stimulate efficient resource use and prioritisation of patient care.36 Reimbursements are calculated in a system based on the diagnosis and treatment needs of patients using DRG tariffs.37 Each DRG has a predetermined price reflecting the expected costs associated with treating patients within the specific group. The DRG system helps standardise and streamline payment for healthcare services and may be used as a top-down method to estimate average costs of health services in health economics.

Implementation cost

We derived the cost of implementing the intervention from a Norwegian health sector perspective and drew estimates of direct costs associated with the intervention from the perspective of the healthcare services, which include hospital charges and professional fees. Estimates about costs were informed from expert opinion on the design and delivery of the intervention (average time needed to train a therapist/treatment provider, administrative costs and the duration of treatment), from the ongoing RCT (hours spent on the intervention) and hourly salary from the Norwegian national statistics agency, Statistics Norway. The estimation of the duration of the intervention and associated required resources was conducted on 13 March 2024. We used the currency conversion rate from Norwegian Kronor to Euro, of 18 March 2024 (0.087 Kr/€). We then derived figures of average salary from Statistics Norway, with values from 2023.

Rationale and description of model

The economic model simulates effects in terms of direct costs of intervention for the hospital as well as the direct and indirect effects of the intervention. In this model, which can be made available on reasonable request, we included both direct and indirect costs in the cost-of-illness, which may potentially be reduced after intervention. Direct costs in this model were estimated in terms of effects on hospitalisation and calculated as a direct cost for the hospital. Furthermore, we included indirect productivity loss costs from morbidity in terms of the effect of intervention on sick leave. To account for the considerable amount of uncertainty in this simulation model, both in the descriptive data and in the cost and potential savings estimates, we considered different scenarios related to remission from insomnia of 25%, 50% and 75% in addition to full remission, for effects on sick leave to illustrate how uncertain values in the cost estimates affect the final output of the analysis, and to provide a range of potential benefits in terms of indirect costs saved due to the intervention.

Patient and public involvement

The NORCOR study user group comprising user group representatives from the Norwegian Health Association (n=1), the Norwegian Heart and Lung Association, in Buskerud and Vestfold (n=2), cardiac patients (n=1), general practitioners (n=5), cardiac nurses (n=3) and clinical cardiologists (n=2) from the hospitals of Drammen and Vestfold have provided feedback in the NORCOR cohort and CBT-I RCT study design, the self-reported study questions, patient information letters and the data collection tools. The group will also play an important role in disseminating the study results.

Discussion

To our knowledge, no previous study has conducted an early HTA of gCBT-I in outpatients with CHD. We simulated the potential socioeconomic benefits of providing nurse-delivered, protocol-driven gCBT-I to outpatients with CHD and insomnia compared with treatment as usual using data from the NORCOR study, public sources and estimates on resources required to provide treatment within a future RCT. In line with previous data from the same cohort with a wider age range,8 we found that the hospital admission rate due to recurrent cardiovascular events was higher in patients with chronic insomnia compared with those without among patients with CHD aged 18–62 years. Correspondingly, sick leave was seen more often in individuals with insomnia, in line with results from the general population.4

In line with a meta-analysis that found that CBT-I is cost-effective compared with pharmacotherapy or no treatment in the general population,19 we found that treating insomnia in patients with CHD is likely to be cost-effective. Our estimate indicated that providing treatment to 100 patients in groups of 5 will cost €2406. As the reimbursement for one acute revascularisation is more than twofold this number, a net saving ensues if only one acute CHD event can be prevented per 200 patients. Even conservative assumptions of remission after treatment indicate that there are significant savings to be made.

Stakeholders should also take note of the finding that insomnia was associated with an increased risk of sick leave in this group, as even costlier productivity loss in the form of disability retirement is more likely after revascularisation, particularly in individuals on long-term sick leave.38 If the empirical evidence of remission,17 with 30%–50% remission rate after treatment, can be extrapolated to a corresponding reduction of the 26-week sick leave, the potential savings range from €207,652 to €346,086. Our data also indicate that the cost per working day is €296. With a mean reduction of 1.5 days for those offered treatment as observed in other cognitive–behavioural interventions for return to work,39 a more modest saving of €444 per participant becomes possible.

Our findings related to sick leave are promising since a recent trial showed that sleep restriction therapy (SRT),40 a core behavioural component of CBT-I, had several beneficial effects.30 Delivered by nurses to primary care patients with insomnia, SRT led to a significant reduction of absence from work and a reduction of impaired performance at work in parallel to improvement of sleep quality and quality of life.30 This intervention had a high probability of being cost-effective.30 However, compared with behavioural therapy alone, the largest therapeutic effects are likely found in the full treatment package of CBT-I,41 which offers a diverse set of tools that can be used by patients to overcome insomnia.9 This motivates offering CBT-I to patients with CHD and insomnia to ease suffering and potentially improve cardiovascular prognosis.

Findings from the present study can facilitate implementation of CBT-I as an additional preventive treatment and a tool to optimise care of cardiac outpatients. Shortening the time to implementation could benefit a large number of individuals. For instance, during 2021, 11,500 individuals were diagnosed with myocardial infarction in Norway.42 Hence, only in Norway, a considerable number of individuals would be eligible for the therapeutic intervention, with a potentially serious condition that is frequently overlooked. Based on the preliminary findings of this study, the potential of economic benefits from facilitating insomnia treatment for these patients seems promising if the results of the future RCT indicate effective insomnia remission after CBT-I. The estimates provided herein can thus serve as a point of reference that will be updated in an economic analysis when there is available data from a future RCT, with values of net benefit and incremental cost-effectiveness ratio. The preliminary results can provide support for a more rapid implementation, when the potential benefits are calculated in parallel with the future RCT.

Strengths and limitations

The present study has a comprehensive dataset that provided the base for this simulation, combined with publicly available data on the salary and the standardised cost of hospitalisations and interventions for cardiovascular disorders. Moreover, we assumed a conservative stance in the model for possible savings as we did not consider disability pension, more frequent among individuals with insomnia,2 43 nor the effects on impaired performance at work. Omitting possible effects on performance of employees increases the probability of additional savings that might be conveyed after CBT-I treatment. The simulation model presents results that are readily available to stakeholders, which might lower the threshold for implementation once robust evidence for effectiveness of treatment in this patient group becomes available. Furthermore, our estimates related to the economic burden of an acute coronary event and its treatment did not include the period of 1–2 weeks of sick leave that is recommended in Norway after acute coronary syndrome,44 with a median time of about 18.8 days in a recent sample.45 Integrating this (approximately €1479/week/patient/event) in the model would have added further potential socioeconomic savings. Moreover, given the potential negative synergistic action between comorbid insomnia and obstructive sleep apnoea (COMISA) on cardiovascular disease risk as well as potential for greater impairments in sleep, mood and quality of life, and that some recent larger RCTs suggest that CBT-I in COMISA patients not only improves insomnia symptoms but may also improve subsequent PAP adherence,46,48 there might be additional potential cost-saving benefits among patients with CHD that were not accounted for.

Some limitations warrant consideration when interpreting the results of this study. First, the treatment outcomes in the 100 hypothetical patients who were given gCBT-I or treatment as usual are merely early estimates, based on prospective data regarding coronary admissions, and cross-sectional data on sick leave from participants in the NORCOR cohort study. The observational nature of the data limits the extent to which a causal connection can be drawn between insomnia and these outcomes. Hence, further conclusions regarding the efficacy and effectiveness require the results from RCTs investigating the effects of CBT-I administered to patients with CHD. Nevertheless, uncertainty regarding treatment outcomes is mitigated by our combination of the input patient data with findings from the literature.2 30

Second, we recognise that there is some dispute on how to quantify loss of economic productivity,49 and that the costs may be lower using a friction cost approach.50 To account for this possible limitation, we opted to use a conservative human capital approach to calculate the cost of productivity loss. Therefore, in this model, we allow for the costs of sick leave to be reduced through compensating interventions such as transferring tasks and overtime. Moreover, our productivity loss calculation did not include the effect of the disease on impaired work performance at work. Hence, we regard the estimates provided by our model to represent a conservative approach, since we did not account for other effects of treatment on productivity.

Finally, even though gCBT-I has been found effective,21,23 there is limited evidence for the effectiveness of gCBT-I in patients with CHD and without HF, although a recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported CBT-I to be effective in patients with cardiovascular disease.51 Thus, opting for gCBT-I in a simulation analysis without empirical data supporting its use in this population carries some uncertainty. For instance, gCBT-I generally tends to produce poorer outcomes for sleep onset insomnia compared with individual CBT-I.17 This may be a consideration when determining what type of CBT-I to select for patients with CHD depending on what type of insomnia they have, for example, whether the patient predominantly has sleep onset or sleep maintenance insomnia.

Conclusion

Our study indicates that treating chronic insomnia in patients with CHD could potentially convey significant socioeconomic savings, due to the large costs associated with the disorder. With this in mind, further investigation is needed on the benefits of offering gCBT-I to outpatients with CHD, compared with usual treatment or sleep hygiene advice. The present simulation study provides stakeholders with preliminary data on the potential value of incorporating gCBT-I into CHD outpatient care at an early stage. Ultimately, this can facilitate sustainable implementation and improve health outcomes in this group of patients.
