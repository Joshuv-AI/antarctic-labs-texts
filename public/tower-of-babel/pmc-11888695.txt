Colchicine in the Management of Oral Submucous Fibrosis – A Systematic Review and GRADE Assessment
Authors: Naveenaa Chellapandian, Vandana Sekizhar, Pavithranand Ammayappan, Kumar Chandan Srivastava, Ahmed Ismail Abdelrahman Nagy, Shivashankar Kengadaran, Artak Heboyan, Deepti Shrivastava
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Objectives:Oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF) is a chronic oral potentially malignant disorder affecting the oral mucosa. The habit of areca nut chewing is established to be the causative factor. Various treatment modalities are available for managing OSMF. Colchicine obtained from plants of the Colchicum genus can downregulate collagen synthesis and upregulate collagenolytic activity, thereby establishing its antifibrotic property. Hence, the objective of the current study is to assess the efficacy of colchicine in the treatment of OSMF.Methods:A comprehensive search was conducted in MEDLINE via PubMed, Cochrane, ProQuest, Ovid, and Google Scholar, and the clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of oral colchicine for the treatment of OSMF were included. The Cochrane risk of bias tool and GRADE were utilized for assessing the quality.Results:Three clinical trials (one randomized and two non-randomized trials) comprising 110 OSMF patients followed the eligibility criteria. The follow-up time for the patients was between 3 and 6 months. Mouth opening and burning sensation were the primary outcomes of interest. The risk of bias was found to be high in the studies included. GRADE quality assessment showed moderate quality for one study and two studies to be of low-quality evidence.Conclusion:This review suggests that oral colchicine is efficacious in the management of OSMF and more distinctly in improving mouth opening. Considering the significant side effects from conventional treatment for OSMF, colchicine can be an effective modality in improving burning sensation and mouth opening in patients with OSMF.

INTRODUCTION

Schwartz in 1952 described oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF) as “Atropica idiopthica.” OSMF, an oral potentially malignant disorder (OPMD), is known by other names also, which include idiopathic scleroderma of the mouth, Asian sideropenic dysphagia, juxta epithelial fibrosis, idiopathic palatal fibrosis, sclerosing stomatitis, and diffuse OSMF.[1] Later in 1966, Pindborg described it as an undetectable, long-term illness that can strike any area of the mouth and occasionally the throat. Though it is sometimes preceded or linked to vesicle formation, it is invariably linked to a juxta-epithelial inflammatory response, which is then followed by fibroelastic lamina propria alteration and epithelial atrophy, which causes the oral mucosa to stiffen and results in trismus and impaired eating ability.[234] More et al.[5] in 2019 opined that chronic chewing of areca nut and its commercial preparations can cause a debilitating, progressive, irreversible collagen metabolic disorder that affects the oral mucosa, pharynx, and esophagus, occasionally causing mucosal stiffness and functional morbidity with a potential risk of malignant transformation. Clinical features of OSMF include ulcers, blanching, burning sensation, restricted mouth opening, and shrunken uvula in severe cases.[6]

It is established that OSMF has a complex etiology. Autoimmunity, vitamin B and C deficiencies, iron deficiencies, chewing areca nuts, and genetic mutations are some of the reported contributing factors. However, epidemiological investigations have demonstrated that the primary cause of OSMF is the chewing of areca nut in any formulation.[7] Arecoline in the areca nut is primarily responsible for the initiation of fibrosis.[891011]

Since Paymaster first identified it as a potential precancerous lesion, its occurrence has gradually increased, with a malignant transition rate between 7% and 13%.[121314] Teh et al.[15] was the one who provided the earliest and most convincing evidence that genomic instability correlates with various grades of OSMF and is a key factor in its progression and malignancy. Mechanical trauma, alcohol, smoking, the presence of immunological disorders, aging, and close association with other OPMDs (leukoplakia, oral lichen planus) are additional risk factors for malignant transformation.[161718]

Staging the condition with the clinical presentation decides the management. Numerous treatments, including therapeutic intervention, surgical intervention, and physiotherapy, have been proposed as effective treatment modalities for managing this chronic inflammatory condition. Antifibrotic, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant medications have been used for the treatment of OSMF.[19] Various treatment modalities are emerging every single day. There has been a shift toward complementary medicine, which has gained attention in recent times as they are believed to have less or no side effects compared to conventional medical treatment modalities. There is no single drug that has proven effective in the management of OSMF and hence a combination of available treatment modalities has been used so far. Consequently, efforts to find a successful therapeutic strategy are in progress.

The chemical name for colchicine is colchicinum N-(5,5,7,9-Tetrahydro-1,2,3,10-tetramethoxy-9oxobenzo[alpha]heptalen-7-yl] acetamide), and it is derived from plants of the Colchicum genus (autumn crocus).[2021] Its antifibrotic and anti-inflammatory activities are mediated via its influence on polymorphonuclear leukocytes, monocyte chemotaxis, leukocyte adhesiveness, and reduction of leukocyte activity through its effect on prostaglandin E.[22]

Studies have looked at its application in OSMF considering its well-known antifibrotic and anti-inflammatory capabilities. However, this systematic review was intended to examine the effectiveness of colchicine as a monotherapy in the management of OSMF.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Protocol and registration

The protocol was structured in accordance with PRISMA 2009 and has been registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023431588).

Focused question

The addressed research question was: “Is colchicine effective in the treatment of OSMF?”

Eligibility criteria

Population [P] – Subjects with OSMF. No history of previous treatment for OSMF. Intervention [I] – Colchicine (Oral, topical, and in other forms). Comparison [C] – All medical interventions, including placebo and standard treatment. Outcomes [O] – Mouth opening and burning sensation. Study design [S] – Experimental study. Subjects suffering from systemic disorders, who have limited mouth opening as a result of other conditions (pericoronitis, space infection, abscess, fractures), pregnant and lactating mothers, and the presence of additional oral potentially malignant lesions such as oral leukoplakia and oral lichen planus were excluded. Language – English. Case reports, case series, reviews, and animal studies were excluded.

Search strategy

Databases, including Medline via PubMed, Cochrane, ProQuest, Ovid, and Google Scholar search engines, were searched for all relevant studies published in the English language. The following keywords were used in combination: “colchicine,” “oral submucous fibrosis,” “submucous fibrosis,” and “goutnil.” Two reviewers screened the title and abstract of the shortlisted articles independently to get the eligible full-text articles in English by excluding the irrelevant studies [Table 1].

Study selection

Three reviewers independently looked over the titles and abstracts of included studies, excluding any research that was not relevant. Three reviewers thoroughly evaluated all eligible full-text studies to determine their inclusion.

Data collection

The following information was collected by utilizing a data extraction table (customized) containing the study’s title, the author’s name, the year it was published, the study’s setting, its design, its population, the type of randomization method used (if present), the intervention type, the comparator types, the age, gender of the subjects, the period of measurement, the primary and secondary outcome, and its resultant remarks.

Quality assessment

Two independent reviewers carried out the quality assessment using RoB 2 (randomized trials) and ROBINS I (non-randomized). Any disagreements arising were discussed with the third reviewer to be resolved. GRADE assessment for the quality of included studies was carried out.[23]

Statistical-analysis

Considering the heterogeneity among the studies, meta-analysis was not carried out.

RESULTS

Literature search and selection of studies

Studies were selected based on following the PRISMA standards. The total number of articles obtained through the literature search yielded 267 articles (the first 100 articles from Google Scholar were included) that were screened. After the removal of duplicates and irrelevant studies, 21 articles were left. After reviewing the title and abstract of 21 articles, 11 were excluded, eight of which were review articles and three of which were systematic reviews. Seven of the 10 full-text articles were excluded after being put through inclusion screening (no comparator for one study, another drug (anti-helminthic) was used in one study, and five studies used colchicine as an adjuvant in combination with intralesional steroids). Three articles were finally included in the review [Figure 1].

Characteristics of Studies

Formulation – Intervention and control groups

All the studies used oral colchicine tablets with a dosage of 0.5 mg for a period of 3 months. All the included studies compared colchicine with intralesional steroids – 2 mL of dexamethasone with 1500 IU of hyaluronidase and 2 mL lignocaine.

Clinical parameters

The primary outcome analyzed in all three studies was mouth opening and burning sensation. The secondary outcomes taken into consideration were cheek flexibility and tongue protrusion. One out of the three studies utilized a vernier caliper to measure mouth opening. One more study used a scale for measuring mouth opening and another study did not mention about the tool of measurement. Two of the three studies utilized visual analog scale (VAS) to record the burning sensation.

Outcome measures

Among the three studies that were included, the study by Neupane et al.[24] revealed statistically significant results in mouth opening but not for burning sensation. Two other studies did not show any statistical significance for mouth opening and burning sensation.[2526] None of the three studies showed any side effects with regard to colchicine. Only one study reported mild pain at the intralesional injected sites.[24]

Quality assessment

For evaluating the quality of three studies, the Cochrane tool RoB2 was utilized for randomized controlled trials, and ROBINS I was utilized for non-randomized controlled studies. One randomized controlled trial[26] showed a high risk of bias, having performance and detection bias [Figure 2]. Two non-randomized trials[2425] also showed serious risk of bias resulting from confounding bias and bias in the measurement of outcome [Figure 3].

DISCUSSION

OSMF, a chronic OPMD, is common among South and Southeast Asian populations.[27] The prevalence of OSMF ranges between 0.2% and 0.5%. The higher prevalence is ascribed to the commercial advertising of areca nut. The consumption of areca nut products worldwide is thought to be between 10% and 20%.[2] The disorder has its presentation in the age group between 11 and 60 years. Cases have been reported even in children between the ages of 2.5 and 10 years.[2829]

The primary etiologic factor of OSMF is chewing areca nut. Numerous investigations have established that OSMF is caused by dysregulation of collagen, specifically by increased production and decreased clearance. Defective collagen homeostasis in OSMF involves inflammatory cytokines release and growth factors. Betel nut contains alkaloids, namely arecoline, arecaidine, guvacaine, and guvacoline, which are responsible for stimulating collagen production from fibroblasts. Arecoline is responsible for forming cross-linkage among collagen peptides, which makes them resistant to degradation. These adjustments in collagen metabolism are caused in part by areca nuts. In addition to alkaloids, flavonoids and copper are found in betel nut. All of these disrupt the oral tissue’s extracellular matrix equilibrium. A significant percentage of people who chew betel nuts also use tobacco or alcohol. Studies have shown that alcohol and tobacco have an additive influence on OSMF pathogenesis.[7]

Both homemade and commercially produced areca nut products, such as gutkha, mawa, and paan masala, are widely accessible in India. They are freeze-dried. Areca nut, betel leaf, calcium hydroxide, and catechu (without tobacco) are ingredients in paan masala. While gutkha is a combination of dried areca nut, tobacco, and additional chemical flavorings, mawa is a simple combination of areca nut, lime, and tobacco. The concentration of areca nut is higher in commercially accessible goods because of their higher dry-weight concentration. Products created from scratch typically include fewer areca nuts overall and have a lower concentration of areca nuts. When compared to homemade versions, commercial areca nut products are linked to an accelerated development of OSMF. This could be because homemade preparations have a lower amount of areca nut and also usage with betel leaf. The antioxidant beta-carotene found in betel leaf is thought to have antimutagenic qualities and can eliminate free radicals from the areca nut.[12]

The symptoms of OSMF patients include oral ulcers, reduced mouth opening, and a burning sensation of the oral mucosa.[30] Prodromal symptoms include dry mouth, excessive salivation, impaired gustatory perception, ulcerations or recurrent generalized inflammation of the oral mucosa, blisters that appear especially on the palate, and a burning sensation in the mouth while eating spicy food. The buccal fibrous bands run vertically, and their density ranges from a faint white patch on the soft palate to thick fibrosis that causes the uvula and soft palate to fixate, shorten, or even deviate. The fibrous tissue in the faucial pillars can range from a sparse buildup of submucosal tissue in both pillars to a thick fibrosis that extends deeply into the pillars and causes tonsil strangulation. These symptoms can cause decreased mouth opening and difficulty in chewing, thereby affecting the quality of life.

The severity of OSMF is assessed by several grading systems, and the treatment differs accordingly.[31] Grading OSMF depends on one or more of the following criteria, such as inter-incisal mouth opening, burning sensation, blanching of oral mucosa, histological features, functional component, treatment, and prognosis. The treatment of trismus and the burning sensation in the oral mucosa is the primary issue in this condition. Numerous therapeutic approaches, both non-surgical and surgical, have been tested. The prevention strategies should involve breaking the habit, which can be promoted via habit cessation counseling and education.

Management for OSMF includes the use of lycopene, spirulina, curcumin, pentoxifylline, aloe vera, oxitard, salvianolic acid, tulsi, tea pigments, iron supplements, immunomodulators, hyperbaric oxygen, and mouth exercising.[32333435363738394041424344] Surgical procedures such as myotomy, coronoidectomy, or the removal of fibrous bands can be used to treat fibrosis. Other treatments that can be tried include physiotherapy, local heat therapy, and mouth-opening exercises. Amid all the available treatment modalities, intralesional steroids have been the mainstay of treatment. Though they have been proven to be effective, they have their own side effects such as adrenal insufficiency and osteoporosis.

There are several systematic reviews aimed at providing an effective treatment in the management of OSMF, but they have failed to provide a single treatment regimen. Among the various systematic reviews carried out till now, no single treatment has established its effectiveness in treating the condition. In recent years, there has been a shift toward the use of alternate medicine as people strongly believe that interventions made from natural sources have minimal side effects compared to conventional treatment and are economical and easily adaptable. Every disease condition in the human possesses its own kind of alternative therapy. However, the use of such treatments is encouraged only after they have been proven effective through human clinical trials.

Colchicine is one such alkaloid obtained from plants of the Colchicum genus (autumn crocus), which is known for its antifibrotic and anti-inflammatory properties.[1920] Its ability to break neutrophil microtubules and prevent them from migrating in the direction of chemotactic factors has been linked to its anti-inflammatory effects.[45] Colchicine also has inhibitory effects on the mitotic phase of the cell cycle by inhibiting the formation of mitotic spindle and sol-gel formation. Colchicine gets metabolized majorly in the gastrointestinal tract by CYP3A4. It is available in tablet form (0.6 mg) and also in topical form.[28] Colchicine exhibiting both antifibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects will be beneficial in the long-term treatment of OSMF.

Colchicine is the primary drug of choice for gout. It has also been used in other conditions such as acute and recurrent pericarditis, and Paget’s disease of the bone. It is known to have side effects when given in higher doses, and monitoring is needed during the course of treatment. In dentistry, apart from OSMF, colchicine has its role in recurrent aphthous ulcers, oral lichen planus, and prophylactic potential in periodontal tissue destruction through anti-inflammatory, antiapoptotic, antioxidant, and bone protective effects.[4647484950] The proposed mechanism of action of colchicine in OSMF is attributed to its anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic properties. Colchicine is known to exert both antifibrotic and anti-inflammatory action on OSMF. It suppresses procollagen secretion as well as the conversion of procollagen to collagen, thereby inhibiting collagen synthesis. It binds with tubulin to disrupt the microtubule by inhibiting its polymerization. Colchicine also has destabilizing action on microtubules. It reduces the number of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) receptors on the surface of macrophages and endothelial cells and suppresses the TNF-α induced activation of macrophages. It influences mast cell degranulation, thereby inhibiting the release of inflammatory mediators.

The lack of systematic reviews analyzing the effectiveness of colchicine to be used as a monotherapy has led to the present review with the focused research question “Is colchicine effective in the treatment of OSMF?” Following a comprehensive literature search, three clinical trials were included, of which all three studies showed colchicine to be effective in managing OSMF. The primary outcomes assessed in this review were burning sensation and mouth opening. All the studies compared colchicine with intralesional steroids and measured their efficacy in reducing the burning sensation and improving mouth opening. One of the three studies used a vernier caliper for measuring mouth opening. One more study used a scale for measuring mouth opening, and another study did not mention about the tool of measurement. Two of the three studies used VAS for burning sensation.

With regard to dosage for OSMF, all the included studies used colchicine systemically at a dosage of 0.5 mg two times daily for 3 months (12 weeks). All three included studies concluded that colchicine has an effective role in managing OSMF. These studies compared colchicine with conventional treatment of intralesional steroid injection with hyaluronidase.

A study by Neupane et al.[24] compared colchicine with intralesional steroids – dexamethasone (4 mg/mL) plus hyaluronidase 1500 IU – and noted a highly statistically significant result in mouth opening.

A study by Sidana et al.[25] compared colchicine with 2 mL of dexamethasone and 1500 IU of hyaluronidase administered intralesional with 2 mL of lignocaine HCL, and the results suggested a significant decrease in the intensity of burning sensation in the colchicine group, whereas mouth opening and tongue protrusion were found to be better among the other group. The colchicine group showed a more rapid decrease in the severity of the burning sensation and a continued increase in mouth opening even after the course of treatment had been stopped.

A study by Somishetty et al.[26] compared colchicine with 2 mL of dexamethasone and 1500 IU of hyaluronidase administered intralesional with 2 mL of lignocaine. Rapid reduction in the intensity of the burning sensation was accompanied by a persistent improvement in mouth opening regardless of the cessation of the treatment, which was comparable to other studies in the review. The results of all these studies were in accordance with earlier studies by Krishnamoorthy et al.,[51] where colchicine was utilized in conjunction with intralesional therapy. This study was excluded as colchicine was used in conjunction with intralesional steroids. Four other clinical trials had also been excluded for the same reason.[5253545556] However, these studies also proved colchicine to be an effective adjuvant.

Recently, a systematic review and meta-analysis on colchicine in the management of OSMF included studies that used colchicine in conjunction with another modality in treating OSMF.[57] It could not be inferred from the study that colchicine is an effective adjuvant as some other medication used in conjunction could have caused the effect. The difference here is we have included the studies that used 0.5 mg of oral colchicine alone in the management of OSMF.

All three included studies reported colchicine to be an effective treatment in managing OSMF. None of the included studies reported any side effects pertaining to colchicine. Thus, it can be inferred that colchicine can be used as an effective adjuvant in improving mouth opening and reducing burning sensation in patients with OSMF.

The quality of studies is the main decisive factor for the quality of systematic review, which resulted in a high risk of bias and the reason being lack of randomization and blinding. Only one of the three included studies[26] showed better methodology compared to the other two.

Limitations and future implications

While this systematic review implies the positive efficacy of colchicine in the treatment of OSMF, a number of limitations of the review had to be considered. The main limitations of the review are the relative paucity of studies on the role of colchicine in OSMF management, no data on the efficacy of colchicine on the specific stage of OSMF, smaller number of samples, high risk of bias, and low quality of evidence from GRADE assessment out of the shortlisted studies.

In addition, there were no single-cell culture studies to assess the individual effects of colchicine and hyaluronidase. More randomized clinical trials with robust methodology and larger sample sizes are needed in the future to prove colchicine as an effective monotherapy in the treatment of OSMF.

CONCLUSIONS

When accounting for the dominance of conventional medicine and its side effects, OSMF being more common among the Southeast Asian population, the use of an alternative medication with insignificant side effects is highly recommended. Colchicine obtained from natural sources can serve as an effective modality in treating OSMF patients with minimal side effects.

Therefore, to conclude colchicine as a monotherapy for treating OSMF, a greater number of standard clinical trials on a larger population is needed. In view of these limitations, this review attempted to sum up the existing evidence on the management of OSMF using colchicine as a monotherapy.
