Environmental Enrichment May Alleviate Neural Damage by Reducing Ferroptosis Through Tlr4
Authors: Kai Li, Xingyu Zhang, Jichun Wu, Qian Li, Jianu Ni, Xia Bi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ischemic stroke, particularly cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury, represents a significant global health challenge, leading to high rates of mortality and disability. This study investigates the neuroprotective effects of environmental enrichment (EE) following middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) in Sprague–Dawley rats, aiming to elucidate underlying molecular mechanisms. Employing a controlled experimental design, we observed that EE significantly reduced neuronal damage and improved functional recovery, as demonstrated through Nissl staining and Longa scale assessments. Gene expression analysis revealed 2003 upregulated and 1056 downregulated genes in the EE group compared to the MCAO group, with significant associations identified in pathways including the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways, crucial for cell survival and apoptosis regulation. Additionally, histopathological analysis indicated enhanced neuronal integrity in the EE group. Notably, Tlr4 emerged as a key gene with differential expression, implicating its role in mediating ferroptosis. These findings underscore the potential of EE as a non-invasive therapeutic strategy for neuroprotection in ischemic stroke rehabilitation, highlighting the need for further investigation into its application in clinical settings. Future research should focus on the functional roles of identified differentially expressed genes and the development of targeted interventions that can mimic the beneficial effects of enriched environments.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke is characterized by a sudden loss of blood flow to the brain and is a major cause of death and long-term disability worldwide. The underlying mechanisms of ischemic stroke involve complex pathophysiological processes, including energy expenditure, excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammation, ultimately leading to neuronal cell death and cerebral infarction [1, 2]. Although there are currently treatment methods such as thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy, these methods are limited by the narrow time window for effective intervention and potential adverse reactions. Therefore, there is an urgent need for other treatment strategies [3, 4].

Previous studies have shown that environmental factors play a crucial role in neuroprotection and recovery after ischemic events. Environmental enrichment (EE) provides enhanced sensory, social, and cognitive stimuli and has been proven in various animal models to improve cognitive function and promote neuroprotection [5, 6]. Our research aims to explore the effect of EE on the recovery of neurological function after middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) in rats. We hope to clarify the neuroprotective effect of EE on ischemic injury through gene expression analysis and determine its potential molecular mechanism.

The main purpose of this study is to evaluate the neuroprotective effect of EE on ischemic injury and its potential to promote functional recovery after MCAO. By analyzing differentially expressed genes and related biological pathways, our goal is to gain an in-depth understanding of the molecular basis of EE-induced neuroprotection. This research not only helps us understand the role of environmental factors in the rehabilitation of ischemic stroke, but also has the potential to develop new treatment strategies, which can improve the prognosis of stroke rehabilitation patients.

Methods

Animals

In this investigation, a cohort of 54 adult male Sprague–Dawley rats, aged 8 weeks and weighing between 220 and 250 g, was procured from the Shanghai Experimental Animal Center affiliated with the Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The subjects were maintained in a controlled environment characterized by a temperature of 23 ± 1 °C, humidity levels from 45 to 60%, and a 12-h-light/dark cycle, specifically from 08:00 to 20:00. Throughout the study period, all animals had unrestricted access to food and water.

The experimental protocols adhered to the guidelines outlined in the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals,” as established by the National Institutes of Health (NIH Publications No. 80–23, Rev. 1996), and were sanctioned by the Animal Ethics Committee at the Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Ethical reference numbers: PZSHUTCM210305004).

Rats were randomly divided into three groups: sham surgery with prior rearing in standard conditions (sham + SE); Mcao surgery with prior rearing in standard conditions (Mcao + SE); Mcao surgery with prior rearing in EE (Mcao + EE). Each group has 18 rats.

Transient Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion/Reperfusion (Mcao/R)

After 3 weeks of intervention treatment, the rats were subjected to Mcao/R or sham surgery to establish a cerebral ischemia model. During the surgery, 1.5% isoflurane was used for continuous anesthesia of the rats, and their body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using an incubator. The rats were placed in a supine position, and the skin along the midline of the neck was incised. The left common carotid artery (CCA), internal carotid artery (ICA), external carotid artery (ECA), and vagus nerve were separated bluntly, and they were separated with cotton swabs. The distal end of the ECA was ligated, and a slipknot was tied at the proximal end of the ECA. Temporary arterial clamps were placed on the CCA and ICA. A 4-0 surgical nylon monofilament was inserted into the ECA and then passed through the CCA into the ICA. When the traction line entered the CCA, the standby movable connector was tightened to prevent vascular elastic recoil from causing the traction line to loosen, and the two arterial clamps were released. In the sham surgery group, only the blunt separation was performed. The entire blocking period lasts for 90 min. After the anesthesia wore off, rats with Longa scores between 1 and 3 were included in the experiment. Among these, all 18 sham-operated rats survived; however, six rats that underwent Mcao/R were euthanized. Two of these rats succumbed to intraoperative hemorrhage resulting from an unsuccessful tethered wire insertion. Furthermore, four rats died within 2 h post-MCAO/R, all attributed to hemorrhagic complications at the base of the skull following cranial opening. Only ten out of the MCAO + EE samples met the experimental requirements. Thus, ten rats were selected for each group for the subsequent research.

The sham + SE and Mcao + SE groups were housed in standard environmental cages, whereas the Mcao + EE group was accommodated in enriched environmental cages subsequent to the surgical intervention. A total of 54 rats underwent the MCAO/R treatment over a period of 6 days. Brain tissue samples were collected after a 72-h ischemia–reperfusion treatment. The rats were initially exposed to 100% CO2 for a duration of 2 min with active ventilation, followed by a minimum of 3 min of passive exposure before being sacrificed via the cervical vertebrae whitening technique. Accordingly, three rats were designated for high-throughput sequencing, four for quantitative PCR (qPCR), and three for Nissl staining and hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining.

Enriched Environment Training Protocol and Bioinformatics Analysis Processes

Rats randomly assigned to the EE group were housed in EE cages (40 cm W × 100 cm H × 60 cm L) for 4 weeks. The EE cages provided water, food, and necessary nesting materials. They also included running wheels, wooden climbing ladders, jumping boards attached to the walls, swings, colorful balls, long moldable ladders, and various colorful blocks. Additionally, a suspended space structure was included. HE staining is essential for identifying different tissue types and the morphological changes that underpin modern diagnosis. This staining has not changed over the years because it works well with various fixatives and shows a wide range of cytoplasmic, nuclear, and extracellular matrix characteristics (Fischer et al. 2008). The brain tissue was dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5-μm-thick sections. Slices were moved onto glass slides, dried, and dewaxed. HE staining was performed according to standard protocols. The results were examined under a light microscope (Vectra 3, PerkinElmer, USA).

Next-Generation Sequencing of mRNA (RNA-seq)

Infarcted brain tissue was collected from three rats in each group for RNA-seq analysis. The statistical power of this experimental design, calculated in RNASeq Power, is 0.87. RNA concentrations and purities were measured using the Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA).

For the preparation of each RNA sample, 1 μg of RNA was utilized as the input material. Sequencing libraries were constructed in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol using the NEBNext UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA), and index codes were incorporated to ensure the correct assignment of sequences to their respective samples. In summary, poly-A mRNA was isolated from total RNA employing magnetic beads conjugated with poly-T oligonucleotides. The fragmentation of RNA was conducted at an elevated temperature in the presence of divalent cations within the NEBNext First Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer (5 ×). The first strand of complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized employing a random hexamer primer along with M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase. Subsequently, the synthesis of the second strand of cDNA was carried out using DNA polymerase I and RNase H. Any residual overhangs were converted into blunt ends through the action of exonucleases and polymerases. After performing adenylation at the 3′ ends of the DNA fragments, NEBNext Adaptors featuring a hairpin loop structure were ligated to promote hybridization. To isolate cDNA fragments with a preferential length of 240 base pairs, the library fragments underwent purification using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). Following this, 3 μl of USER Enzyme (NEB, USA) was applied to the size-selected, adaptor-ligated cDNA at 37 °C for 15 min, and subsequently heated to 95 °C for 5 min prior to polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The PCR amplification was conducted using Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, Universal PCR primers, and the Index (X) Primer. Ultimately, the PCR products were purified utilizing the AMPure XP system, and the quality of the libraries was evaluated on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system.

The samples of the brain and cortex were compared between MCAO and sham operation. The blood samples were compared between the time of 0 h before MCAO and 24 h after MCAO. Gene Ontology (GO) Terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Pathways of Analysis of Degs

We used the online tool DIVID 6.8 (https://david.ncifcrf.gov) to analyze the functional enrichment of the DEGs. GO consists of three categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). These categories can be used to annotate genes and predict protein functions. KEGG pathways help identify possible biological pathways. To better understand gene expression, we categorized the DEGs into two groups: upregulated and downregulated genes. GO terms and KEGG pathways were considered significant with a P-value < 0.05. Finally, we downloaded the data from DIVID for further research.

Statistical Analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± SD using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. In this study, we divided the subjects into three groups, and only one factor influencing the level of brain injury (results of Nissl staining and HE staining) was analyzed by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Longa scale with two factors which were the treatment and time factors was analyzed by two-way ANOVA. Data were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05. The rank sum test was adopted for those that did not conform to the normal distribution and homogeneity of variance.

After the model was made, the mice could also recover to a certain extent over time. However, considering the interaction between the treatment method factor and time, there was no statistical difference, further indicating that the treatment method factor played an independent role in neuroprotection.

Discussion

The prevalence of ischemic stroke, especially in the context of MCAO, is a considerable public health challenge, resulting in high morbidity and mortality rates worldwide. This situation not only leads to acute neurological deficits but also causes long-term disability, seriously affecting the quality of life of survivors. Furthermore, due to the need for extensive rehabilitation and long-term care for affected individuals, the economic burden on the healthcare system is huge [9].

Although current treatment methods, such as thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy, are used to restore blood flow, they are often limited by strict efficacy time windows and potential complications, including bleeding transformation. Therefore, there is an urgent need for innovative strategies that can address the acute and chronic consequences of ischemic stroke, emphasizing the importance of exploring neuroprotection and rehabilitation intervention measures, such as EE, to enhance the recovery of stroke models [10, 11].

This study investigates the neuroprotective effects of EE on neurological recovery after MCAO in a rat model. Previous research has suggested that EE can enhance cognitive functions and promote neuroprotection in various animal models, indicating its potential application in stroke rehabilitation [6, 12]. EE enhanced the expression of the antioxidant enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), reduced iron deposition and lipid peroxide (MDA) levels, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis. Additionally, EE decreased the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) in brain tissue, alleviated the neuroinflammatory response, and further promoted the repair of brain tissue. EE also reduced hepcidin expression, which led to decreased DMT1 expression and increased FPN1 expression in neurons, resulting in lower neuronal iron levels and alleviated ferroptosis [13, 14]. Our previous research also found that EE inhibits neuroinflammation and oxidative stress by activating the Nrf2-ARE pathway and endoplasmic reticulum stress-dependent autophagy and apoptosis [15, 16].

This study aims to reveal the further correlation between EE and autophagy and ferroptosis through gene expression analysis, thereby facilitating a deeper understanding of the neuroprotective effect of EE in ischemic injury situations [17, 18].

The protein encoded by Tlr4 is a member of the Toll-like receptor (TLR) family and plays an important role in pathogen recognition and innate immune activation. From fruit flies to humans, TLRs are highly conserved and have structural and functional similarities. They recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns expressed on infection factors and mediate the production of cytokines necessary for effective immune development [19]. Tlr4 can inhibit neuroinflammatory responses and oxidative stress through the Nlrp3 signaling pathway, thereby exerting a protective effect on cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury in rats [20].

Our research also found that EE may upregulate Tlr4 to reduce ferroptosis and achieve the effect of protecting nerves. The involved pathways were the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, lipid and atherosclerosis, phagosome, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and NF-kappa B signaling pathway. The TLR4-related signaling pathway is associated with alleviating inflammatory injury in rat brain tissue, improving neurological function impairment, and downregulating the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines [21]. According to the results of PPI, Tlr4 can also interact with genes such as Atf3, Acta2, Axl, Cd38, Cd44, Ctsb, Cybb, and Tp53 to produce corresponding functions. The study further emphasizes the importance of understanding the underlying molecular mechanisms driving the neuroprotective effects of EE. The identification of DEGs associated with EE offers a critical pathway for future research. Notably, the upregulation of genes linked to neuroprotection and recovery suggests potential biomarker candidates that could be targeted in subsequent studies aimed at enhancing therapeutic strategies for ischemic stroke [18].

The limitations of this study include the absence of in vivo clinical validation, which restricts the translation of findings to human applications. Additionally, the relatively small sample size may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the results. Potential batch effects arising from the use of multiple datasets for biological analysis also warrant consideration, as they could confound the observed gene expression patterns. Furthermore, while the study elucidates the neuroprotective effects of environmental enrichment, it does not explore the long-term outcomes of such interventions or their applicability in diverse patient populations. Future research should aim to address these limitations by incorporating larger cohorts and validating findings in clinical settings to better understand the therapeutic potential of environmental enrichment in stroke rehabilitation.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that environmental enrichment significantly enhances neuroprotection and functional recovery following ischemic injury, underscoring its potential as a non-invasive therapeutic strategy. The identification of differentially expressed genes and associated signaling pathways provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underpinning the neuroprotective effects of environmental enrichment. These findings pave the way for future research aimed at developing targeted interventions that harness the benefits of enriched environments, ultimately contributing to improved patient outcomes in stroke rehabilitation.
