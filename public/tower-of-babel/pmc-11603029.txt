Utilization of Motor Imagery Training for Improvement of Balance of Ataxic Children after Medulloblastoma Resection
Authors: Reham Saeed Alsakhawi, Mohamed Ali Elshafey, Mohamed Nader Alkhouli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of training using motor imagery on balance, gait parameters, and ataxia severity in children after they underwent medulloblastoma tumour resection. Fifty participated children, aged seven–nine years and diagnosed with cerebellar ataxia after medulloblastoma resection were selected from the Tumor Hospital of Cairo University. Two groups of patients were randomly divided: the study group and the control group. The control group received a physical therapy program, whereas the study group received training in motor imagery along with a traditional physical therapy program. Each group was assessed using the Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA), Pediatric Berg Balance Scale (PBBS), and kinematic gait analysis using the Kinovea software. Significant improvements were noted in balance, ataxia, and spatial and temporal gait parameters in both groups, which favoured the study group (P > 0.05). Introduction

Medulloblastoma (MB) accounts for approximately 20% of all childhood brain tumours and is the most common type of posterior fossa tumours (PFTs) (30-40%)1. Loss of balance and gait disturbances are common symptoms of obstructive hydrocephalus and cerebellar dysfunction2. The MB is a rapidly growing malignant solid tumour that occurs in childhood and is treated with surgical resection, craniospinal irradiation, and chemotherapy3.

After MB resection, children suffer from axial hypotonia, ataxia, dysarthria, dysphagia 4, abducens or facial nerve palsies, and pseudobulbar cognitive deficits5. The severity of this syndrome ranges from mild to completely disabling 6. Cerebellar damage is characterised by variable ataxic gait 7.

Ataxia is the predominant and most disabling clinical sign in children with PFTs prior to and after intervention, and is becoming increasingly prevalent in children with MBs and midline tumours. This sign is observed in between 58% to 90% of children with PFTs. In MB, worsening preoperative motor ataxia is usually observed 8.

Children’s ataxia is one of the primary clinical indicators of a variety of disorders and should be discussed based on its temporal course. Cerebral tumours, particularly MBs, cerebellar astrocytomas, brainstem gliomas, and ependymomas, are among the most common causes of progressive ataxia in children 9.

Cerebellar ataxia results from damage to the cerebellum after resection of PFTs10. Numerous studies have divided MB patients into "high-risk," "average," or “standard risk” groups based on factors including age, post-operative residual illness, pathologic variation, and staging. Long-term MB survivors need sophisticated medical treatment from subspecialists11. ]. Long-term sequelae after PFT resection include apraxia, ataxia, and linguistic, memory, and behavioural disabilities 12. Ataxia results in cognitive and motor deficits, including a loss of coordination, dysmetria, hypotonia, decreased balance, and impaired gait 13. Children with ataxia are characterised by impaired coordination and balance and a deficiency in muscle control with physical activity14.

Cerebellar ataxic gait is characterised by an abnormal pattern of gait, decreased cadence, speed of gait, stride length, shorter steps, swing phase, increased step width 15, double limb support time augmented stance phase 16, and variability in timing 17. These parameters can increase the risk of falling and decrease the walking18.

The clinical signs of cerebellar ataxia are associated with impaired localisation. Waddling, nystagmus, and truncal imbalance are the outcomes of focal cerebellar vermis dysfunction. A high steppage stride, asymmetry of the ipsilateral limbs, and a veering toward the afflicted side are all symptoms of cerebellar hemisphere impairment. Stepping gait and sensory impairment of the extremities are signs of sensory and afferent ataxia involvement7.

People who have ataxia have problems with balance and coordination, which causes them to sway uncontrollably. Patients often stop performing actions that need a lot of coordination and steer clear of these uncontrollable motions. Patients lose coordination, response, and gait safety to a greater degree when their range of motions is more limited to movements with little variety. Physical therapy serves as the main therapeutic option 19.

Action observation and motor imaging are two different methods of representing movement that may be used alone to improve certain physical attributes or in conjunction with active movement and sensory stimulation20. Motor imagery is a dynamic state in which the brain represents an action without actual motor execution 21.

Motor imagery is the mental image of movements that includes the internal simulation of motor activity and motor planning. It is a compound cognitive operation, specifically self-generated using perceptual and sensory processes, allowing the reactivation of exact motor actions within the working memory22. Motor imagery is a useful technique for enhancing motor function in rehabilitation programs, despite the fact that 25% of brain neurons are mirror neurons that activate via thoughts. It involves thinking about a motor task and executing it to enhance motor cortex areas. They do not impose any physical load on patients23,24.

Through motor imagery training, brain regions involved in voluntary movement planning, execution, automation, and adjustment may be activated in a manner that mimics the action’s actual occurrence. Neurophysiological activity was quantitatively lower during movement representation approaches than during the actual execution of the motion, despite the fact that it was qualitatively same25. Motor imagery training significantly improved hemiplegic children according to the mental chronometry, Movement Imagery Questionnaire for Children (MIQ-C), resting muscle activation, and functional mobility 26.

This research studied the effects of training using motor imagery on balance, gait parameters, and ataxia severity in children after MB tumour resection.

Materials and methods

Study design

This randomised controlled trial was conducted between October 2023 and January 2024. Informed consent was collected from the caregivers of all participants. All ethical considerations were met prior to conducting the study. In the Faculty of Physical Therapy, Cairo University, the Institutional Review Board gave its approval to the experimental protocol (P.T.REC/ 012/004454). Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05992207, 08-07-2023).

Participants

Fifty children diagnosed with cerebellar ataxia during the follow-up period after MB resection were chosen from the Tumor Hospital of Cairo University within two months after surgical MB tumour resection. Their ages ranged from seven to nine years, and they underwent radiotherapy and chemotherapy sessions. The inclusion criteria were as follows: ataxia signs and poor balance, ability to comprehend and follow test instructions, good compliance, cognition, medical stability, and ability to stand and walk with minimal support. The exclusion criteria included children that were medically unstable or had increased intracranial pressure; visual impairment; hydrocephalus; any other neuromuscular diseases; other types of PFTs such as pilocytic astrocytoma, ependymoma, and hemangioblastoma; peripheral neuropathy due to chemotherapy; or immunity isolated during rehabilitation.

Sample size

Utilizing the G*power software version 3.1.9 (G power program version 3.1; Heinrich-Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany), the sample size for this study was calculated. Calculation of sample size using t-tests (Means: Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test; two groups), Type I error (α) = 0.05, power (1-β error probability) = 0.80, and effect size f2 (V) = 0.9354697 with 2 independent groups comparison for balance as a major variable outcome. For the current study, a sample size of 40 patients (20 patients each group) was suitable.

Randomization and allocation

Children who met the eligibility requirements were blinded and selected at random to one of the two groups. The randomization was carried out by a separate researcher who was not involved in this study. A simple method of randomization was used. Each participant received a unique, randomly generated code from a computer vis the GraphPad software (GraphPad®, QuikCalcs, La Jolla, California, USA). Before the researchers applied the designated interventions, the allocation was hidden in opaque, sealed envelopes, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Measures

Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA) quantified the severity of ataxia. It assesses deficits in ataxic children such as rhythmic movements, imbalance, dysmetria, speech, and tremors27. High inter-rater reliability was shown by the SARA for both the overall score (intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC = 0.97) and the walking, standing, sitting, alternating hand motions, and heel sliding items. However, the nose-finger test, language, and finger tracking items were less satisfactory (ICC ≤ 0.80) 28.

Procedures

1—For evaluation

Both groups were evaluated before and at the end of the three-month of intervention using the SARA, PBBS, and kinematic gait analysis using Kinovea software by a blindfolded external examiner.

Three meters away from the walkway, the therapist set up a tripod with a fixed video camera. 2—For intervention

For three consecutive months, the control group received one hour of physical therapy program, three times a week. The program included exercises to strengthen the trunk and upper and lower limbs, gait training activities to correct gait patterns, and activities to enhance balance and protective reactions when standing on one leg, shifting weight from a standing position, and rising from a squat. The study group received a 30-minute motor imagery program with 30- minute of physical therapy program, as described above34.

Tasks for the first month included sitting at a table and reaching forward in multiple directions, standing up from a seated position (starting with arm assistance and ending without arm assistance, and starting from a high and ending with a low surface), rising on toes (starting from supporting the upper limbs and ending without support, and starting from both sides rises and ending with one side rises), heel raises while walking a short distance and returning to the chair, bridging from the supine position on both legs (progressing to one leg only), standing, and standing up from a chair.

Tasks for the second month included standing with the feet together and reaching for objects includes standing against a wall, falling backward with the trunk straight (moving further away from the wall), standing on a balancing disk, half squats, reaching down to the floor, and sudden stops and turns while walking.

Tasks for the third month included repeatedly stepping forward and sideways onto a step (ending with reducing arm support and at a higher step), kicking a ball with each foot, walking, standing on one leg, and climbing stairs.

Statistical analysis

The obtained data were statistically analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26. Means and standard deviations were calculated for demographic characteristics including age, weight, body mass index (BMI), and sex. Paired t-tests were used for within-group comparisons before and after intervention. The demographic information and baseline characteristics were compared between the groups using an independent t-test, in addition to the pre- and post-treatment mean differences in all measured variables. The statistical significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Results

General characteristics of the participants

No significant difference was noted between the groups in the base line characteristics; the mean age of the control and the study group were 7.8 ± 0.5 and 8 ± 0.7, respectively and P value was 0.11 and the mean BMI for the control and the study group were 22.9 ± 1.8 and 23.5 ± 1.3, respectively and P value was 0.27. The mean height for the control and the study group were 126.5 cm and 127 cm respectively and P value was 0.52. In terms of gender, no significant difference between both groups was noted (P = 0.42; Chi-squared value = 0.21).

Discussion

The purpose of this research was to study the effects of training using motor imagery on balance, ataxia severity, and gait parameters in children after MB tumour resection. The results showed significant improvements in balance, ataxia, and spatial and temporal gait parameters in both groups, favouring the study group.

After MB resection, children experience ataxia and loss of balance due to damage to the superior cerebellar peduncle and functional disorders of the cerebrocerebellar circuitry and cerebral hemispheres35. However, limited research has been conducted on the effects of training using motor imagery in children after MB resection. Nakano et al. (2018) noted the need of assessing participants’ ability to focus on imagining movements, due to the quality of imagination in motor imagery training may vary across persons36.

The selected children ranged in age from seven to nine years and were capable of following the instructions of the motor imagery protocol. The ability to form a mental image of one’s own movements appears in children age seven years37. Children aged 5 years cannot engage in motor imagery, and half of the children aged 6 years can perform motor imagery38.

The outcomes of this study showed that there was a significant improvement in both groups in the PBSS, severity of ataxia according to the SARA, and kinematic gait parameters such as step length, step width, foot angle, cadence, and gait velocity. Significant improvements were recorded in all variables in the motor imagery program group compared to the control group.

Children who received motor imagery training showed significant improvements in functional performance, as evidenced by improved balance, reduced severity of ataxia, and gait parameters. Motor imagery is a cognitive process with no limitations such as physical training, which may lead to physical exertion. Therefore, it is widely applied in physical rehabilitation protocols because motor imagery is a backdoor to the motor system and the neural images of movement.

This finding agrees with that of Nicholson et al. (2019), which shown that motor imagery training may serve as an alternate means for enhancing balance and gait 39. According to Oh et al. (2021), task-oriented training and motor imagery have a favorable impact on elderly persons’ static and dynamic balance, ambulation, and fall efficacy. Functional abilities were improved more successfully by the combination of task-oriented training and motor imagery training than by either intervention alone40. The gait and balance of chronic stroke patients are much improved when gait training and motor imagery are combined41.

Our findings support those of Simonsmeier et al., who reported that imagery training improves performance and promotes motor learning42 and Sarah et al., who concluded that direct motor instruction had limited effectiveness, whereas motor imagery training improved motor performance43. Multicomponent tasks involving dynamic balance and mobility function are better improved by motor-cognitive treatments, as measured by the Timed Up and Go test 44, Moreover, Wilson et al. reported that motor imagery training improved motor skill development in children with developmental coordination disorders45.

Motor imagery is the rebuilding of motor strategies for movement patterns in the mind. Caeyenberghs et al.46 argued that movement imagery explains internal modelling processes, providing the basis for goal-directed adaptive movements and supporting the application of motor imagery protocols in children with coordination disorders. Mental imagery is a technique used to improve physical skills and modify physiological behaviours47. Furthermore, motor imagery improves the learning process of motor function and creates a cognitive image for a skill that adjusts the motion generation used as the accuracy criterion of movement to determine errors in performing a skill43.

Motor imagery training activates brain areas associated with planning, adjustment, automation, and execution of voluntary movements, such as movement performance, balance, and kinematic gait parameters. This agrees with Di Rienzo et al., who concluded that neurophysiological activities are lower in actual action execution than motor imagery performance, with no qualitative difference between them20. This is also in agreement with Johnson et al., who illustrated that motor imagery involves the same neural mechanisms as those activated during actual movement execution, thus improving balance and decreasing the severity48.

Therefore, motor imagery presents a common neurophysiological foundation established in the mirror neuron system. It stimulates the primary sensory-motor cortex, premotor and parietal cortices, subcortical regions (cerebellum and basal ganglia), and corticospinal pathways. In addition, motor imagery can induce neuroplasticity comparable to active interventions49.

A meta-analysis of neural structures after motor imagery showed that the inferotemporal, superior parietal, and frontal cortices were activated50. Studies have also shown the involvement of the sparietal cortical areas, supplementary motor cortex, primary motor cortex, and premotor cortex using functional magnetic resonance imaging and positron emission tomography51. Transcranial magnetic stimulation studies have shown that simulation of the corticomotor pathway is controlled by the same spatial and temporal characteristics throughout motor imagery as during actual motions52.

A few limitations applied to this research. First, the sample size was small, which might have limited the generalisability of the results. Second, immediately after a three-month intervention, the impact of the interventions was assessed. Therefore, the results of this research do not show that the gains shown after therapy are sustainable.

In conclusion, motor imagery training is an efficient rehabilitation approach for treating children with cerebellar mutism and may be effective in conjunction with an appropriate physical therapy program.
