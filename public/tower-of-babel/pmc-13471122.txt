Accuracy, safety, and usability of a real‐time continuous glucose monitoring system in pediatric diabetes management
Authors: Bingyan Cao, Zhe Su, Yi Wang, Yanhui Chen, Zhigang Hu, Mingsong Han, Chunxiu Gong, Yifei Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe purpose of the study was to evaluate the accuracy, safety, and usability of a real‐time Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) system compared with venous blood glucose (vBG) in children and adolescents.MethodsThe study enrolled 81 participants aged from 3 to 17 at three sites in China. Two sensors (GS1, SiBionics, Shenzhen) were inserted on the back of each upper arm for up to 14 days for each participant. A variety of analyses were conducted for evaluating accuracy by comparing the CGM readings and vBG values, including 20%/20 agreement rate, the mean absolute relative difference (MARD), the Clarke error grid, and consensus error grid analyses. Safety was measured by adverse event (AE) monitoring. AEs were documented, and the incidence rate was calculated. Usability was evaluated by self‐reported questionnaires.ResultsData from 80 participants were analyzed. Our results demonstrated high accuracy of the real‐time CGM system. The 20%/20 agreement rate across all glycemic ranges was 93.9%. The MARD value was 8.7%. The results of the Clarke and consensus error grid analyses in zone A + B were 99.6% and 100%, respectively. The average score of usability was 95.3 ± 7.6, reflecting high satisfaction. The CGM also showed high safety, given that only 3 device‐related AEs in 2 participants were reported , and no serious adverse events (SAEs) were reported.ConclusionThe real‐time CGM system demonstrated high accuracy, safety, and usability in the glycemic monitoring of children and adolescents.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major global public health challenge, posing significant risks to individual health and societal well‐being. In 2021, the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) estimated that 537 million people worldwide were living with diabetes, with projections indicating this number rising to 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045 1 . Among children and adolescents, over 1.2 million aged 0–19 years were living with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) in 2021 1 , with an annual incidence of approximately 22.9 new cases per 100,000 juveniles under 15 2 . These trends underscore the profound impact of diabetes on children and adolescents, including substantial economic costs and a reduced life expectancy.

Effective glycemic control is crucial to prevent diabetes‐related complications. However, traditional methods such as self‐monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) have notable limitations. HbA1c fails to capture daily glucose fluctuations and episodes of hypoglycemia, whereas SMBG provides only intermittent data, often missing critical variations 3 , 4 . Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems offer real‐time data on glucose trends, fluctuations, and alarms, making them particularly useful for detecting asymptomatic hypoglycemia 5 , 6 . As a result, CGM is increasingly recommended by international academic organizations, such as the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes (ISPAD) to monitor glucose and guide treatment decisions for children and adolescents with diabetes 7 .

The GS1 CGM system, a real‐time CGM system, was introduced in 2021 by Shenzhen SiBionics Co. Ltd,. The GS1 CGM system includes several advanced features such as factory calibration, direct connectivity to mobile apps via Bluetooth, 14‐day real‐time monitoring, and alarms for both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia. In clinical trials among adult population, the GS1 CGM demonstrated an average mean absolute relative difference (MARD) of 8.83% 8 . The system has been widely used in China since 2022, and its performance has shown consistency, stability, repeatability, and usability, making it suitable for adult clinical applications. However, its effectiveness in pediatric population has yet to be fully evaluated.

Therefore, this study was conducted aiming to assess the accuracy, usability, and safety of the GS1 CGM system in children and adolescents aged 3 to 17 years by comparing CGM readings with venous blood glucose (vBG) measurements. By addressing these objectives, the study aims to facilitate broader adoption of CGM systems in pediatric diabetes management, ultimately improving clinical outcomes for younger participants.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This prospective, single‐arm, multicenter clinical study was conducted at three Chinese hospitals: Beijing Children's Hospital, Capital Medical University, Shenzhen Children's Hospital, and Fujian Medical University Union Hospital. The study took place from 11 July to 17 November 2023. The study protocol and informed consent forms were approved by the Ethics Committees of all three hospitals. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice (GCP), and local regulatory requirements. The study was registered with Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300073025).

Participants

Participants were enrolled in the study based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were individuals aged 3 to 17 years, with a clinical diagnosis of DM, and willing to wear the study device and comply with the study protocol. Participants were excluded if they met the exclusion criteria: with a history of severe hypoglycemia within the past 6 months, significant skin conditions at sensor application sites, abnormal coagulation function, anemia, pregnancy, concurrent participation in another clinical trial, and expected MRI or CT scans during the study. Participants were free to withdraw from the study at any time, with reasons documented. The guardians were required to sign the informed consent forms. For participants ≥16 years and supporting themselves through labor who were considered of full civil capacity according to the Civil Code of the People's Republic of China, they were allowed to sign the informed consent forms on their own. All participants or their guardians provided written informed consent before enrollment in the study.

Procedures

Study participants or caregivers/researchers were instructed to insert and wear the CGM sensors (GS1, SiBionics, Shenzhen) on the back of each upper arm (two sensors in total). Each sensor can be worn for up to 14 days. The first sensor worn by the participant was designated as the primary sensor, and only the primary sensor CGM values were included in the final analysis for comparison with venous reference values. The second applied sensor was used for the assessment of precision.

Participants were randomly divided into five groups according to different stages of the wearing period: Early (Day 1), Early‐Middle (Days 2–5), Middle (Days 6–9), Middle‐Late (Days 10–13), and Late (Day 14). There was one scheduled in‐clinic visit during the 14‐day sensor wear period after the participant was allocated to the designated wearing period. During the in‐clinic visit, venous blood sample was drawn and measured immediately as the reference glucose values by using a blood glucose lactic acid analyzer (EKF BIOSEN C‐line series, Germany), employing the glucose‐oxidase method after centrifugation (hereafter referred to as the venous blood glucose [vBG] value). CGM readings were paired with vBG value by using the closest reading in time to the corresponding venous blood draw, but no more than 5 min before or after the venous blood draw.

The frequency of venous blood sampling varied by age: for children under 6 years, venous blood was collected every 30 min for 4 h; for children aged 6–12 years, blood was drawn every 20 min for 6 h; and for adolescents aged 13–17 years, venous blood was collected every 15 min over a 7‐h period. The total volume of venous blood sampling was below the requirements suggested by the guideline of POCT05 on the Performance Metrics for Continuous Interstitial Glucose Monitoring from Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute 9 , that total blood draw volume should be below 50 mL or 3 mL/kg body weight (whichever is lower) for children and pregnant women within an 8‐week period.

Accuracy and safety assessment

Primary endpoints for accuracy measurements included the MARD and 20%/20. MARD was calculated as the absolute value of the average percent difference between the paired CGM and reference vBG values. The 20%/20 agreement rate is defined as the proportion of CGM values within ±20% difference from the reference vBG values when blood glucose levels >4.4 mmol/L, or within ±1.1 mmol/L (20 mg/dL) difference when reference vBG ≤4.4 mmol/L. These metrics were evaluated across all paired glucose values, in different glucose ranges (<4.4 mmol/L, 4.4–11.1 mmol/L, >11.1 mmol/L), and across different wearing periods. Clinical accuracy was assessed using the Clarke error grid and consensus error grid analyses 10 . Safety assessments included adverse events (AEs), which were categorized by severity and their relationship to the device. The incidence and management of these events were analyzed to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the device's safety profile.

Alarms

The system triggers alarms based on different thresholds. In this study, the alarm will be triggered when blood glucose is below 4.4 mmol/L or greater than 11.1 mmol/L. The true alarm rate quantifies the proportion of hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia alarms corroborated by vBG values at/below (hypoglycemia) or at/above (hyperglycemia) the threshold setting within −15 min or +30 min after the alarm. The true detection rate quantifies the proportion of hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia events (defined by the vBG values) that were associated with a CGM alarm within −15 min or +30 min of the event. The false alarm rate and missed detection rates were calculated as (100% − true alarm rate) and (100% − true detection rate), respectively.

Usability

A detailed usability survey with 20 items was completed by the guardians and/or the participants to evaluate device's comfort and ease of use. Guardians were required to fill the questionnaire when the participants were below 8 years, and the guardian (mandatory) and the participant were allowed to fill in the questionnaire together if the participant was 8–17 years. In rare cases where the participant is ≥16 years and supporting themselves through labor, the participant is allowed to fill in the questionnaire on their own. As no adolescents aged ≥16 years who were self‐supporting through work participated in this study, all the informed consent formss and questionnaires were completed by the guardians (mandatory) and participants. The questionnaire contains sections regarding the user guide, the application and removal of the sensor, the mobile phone App, and the usability of the product over glycemic control. A 5‐point scale was used for each question: “Very Satisfied,” “Satisfied,” “Neutral,” “Dissatisfied,” and “Very Dissatisfied,” with a full score range of 0–100 points. Higher scores represent higher level of satisfaction.

Statistical analysis

The demographics were summarized as mean and standard deviation (SD) for parametric variables, and median and interquartile range (IQR) for nonparametric variables. The count and percentages of age groups (<6 years, 6–12 years, and 13–17 years), sex, ethnicity, duration of diabetes, and treatment were presented. Accuracy was demonstrated by using 20%/20 agreement rate, MARD, mean absolute difference (MAD), Clarke error grid, and consensus error grid. Mean values and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of 20%/20 agreement rate, MARD, and MAD were reported by vBG levels, age groups, and wearing periods. The true/false alarm rates and true/missed detection rates were calculated by comparing the CGM readings with vBG. Precision was measured by paired absolute relative difference (PARD), which is calculated as the average of the absolute relative differences between the two sensors across all data pairs. Usability was represented by the mean score of the questionnaires. All other analyses were conducted with SAS 9.4. P‐value of <0.05 (two‐tailed) was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 81 participants were enrolled across three clinical centers. One participant was excluded from the accuracy assessments due to the absence of paired sensor and blood glucose data, as the sensor detached before venous blood collection. Consequently, 80 pediatric participants with paired reference measurements were included in the analysis.

The cohort exhibited a well‐balanced age distribution, encompassing individuals from various age groups, including a notable representation of younger children. Additionally, the study population included 72 participants on multiple daily injection (MDI) and 8 users with oral hypoglycemic agents.

Primary endpoints

A total of 1,457 valid data pairs were included in the final analysis. Primary endpoints for accuracy measurements included the 20%/20 agreement rate, MARD, the Clarke error grid, and consensus error grid analysis.

The 20%/20 agreement rate and MARD

Overall, the CGM system demonstrated that 93.9% of sensor readings were within 20% or 1.1 mmol/L (20 mg/dL) of the vBG values. Furthermore, the MARD across all study participants was 8.7% (95%CI [7.8–9.6]), indicating a high level of overall accuracy.

Table 2 presents the 20%/20 agreement rate and MARD results across different glucose ranges (<4.4 mmol/L, 4.4–11.1 mmol/L, >11.1 mmol/L) and age groups. When performance was assessed by categorized glucose ranges, the 20%/20 agreement rates were 97.4%, 91.3%, and 97.1%, respectively. Similarly, when analyzed by pediatric age groups, the 20%/20 agreement rates were 93.5%, 94.0%, and 93.8% for participants aged <6 years, 6–12 years, and 13–17 years, respectively.

To further assess accuracy in the hypoglycemia range (<4.4 mmol/L), the mean absolute difference (MAD) was used to measure the mean of the absolute differences between sensor readings and vBG results. For the system, MAD in the hypoglycemic range were categorized into two subranges. In the 2.2–3.3 mmol/L range, with 33 paired measurements, the MAD was 0.26 mmol/L. In the 3.3–4.4 mmol/L range, with 160 paired measurements, the MAD was 0.46 mmol/L. These results highlight the system maintains high accuracy even at low glucose levels.

The Clarke error grid and consensus error grid analyses

The error grid analyses is used to estimate clinical accuracy. Zone A indicated perfectly accurate results; Zone B represented clinically acceptable results; and Zones C, D, and E denoted inaccurate results with increasing degrees of deviation. Our results showed high accuracy of the GS1 CGM. The Clarke error grid analysis showed 92.9% of the data in the A zone and 99.6% of the data in the A + B zone of the error grid (Figure 1). The consensus error grid analysis showed 92.4% of the data in the A zone and 100% of the data in the A + B zone of the error grid (Figure 2).

Secondary endpoints

Accuracy at different wearing periods

The results of 20%/20 agreement rate and MARD between CGM readings and vBG measurements throughout the wearing duration were assessed and showed high consistency across the wearing periods (Table 3). The percentage of 20%/20 agreement rate was above 90% from Day 2 (range: 92.7%–95.7%). The MARD remained consistently low throughout the wearing periods, with the MARD of 7.4% and 7.5% in the middle and middle‐late stages, respectively (Table 3). These findings indicate that the GS1 CGM maintained stable and reliable performance throughout the wearing period.

Alarms performance

The GS1 CGM system demonstrated strong performance in detecting both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia events. For the hypoglycemic alarm (set as 4.4 mmol/L), the true detection rate was 97.6%, and missed detection rate was 2.4%, with true alarm rate of 87.3% and false alarm rate of 12.7%. For hyperglycemic alarm (set as 11.1 mmol/L), the true detection rate and missed detection rate were 89.8% and 10.2%, respectively, and true alarm rate and false alarm rate were 97.0% and 3.0%, respectively.

PARD

The mean PARD of GS1 CGM was 7.1% (SD: 4.5%) yielded from 1,355 data pairs.

Sensor failure

Sensor failure was defined as premature removal or functional failure during the 14‐day wear period. A total of 15 out of 160 sensors (9.4%) were deactivated, with the distribution across the study days as follows: Day 1 (2 sensors), Day 4 (1 sensor), Day 6 (2 sensors), Day 9 (2 sensors), Day 10 (4 sensors), Day 11 (1 sensor), and Day 12 (1 sensor), and Day 14 (2 sensors). Among the 15 sensor failure issues, 1 (0.6%) occurred in a child under 6 years old, 11 (6.9%) in children of 6–12 years, and 3 (1.9%) in adolescents of 13–17 years. Sensor detachment due to movement of physical activity was the main reason for sensor failure (n = 10, 6.3%), followed by self‐removal of the sensor (n = 3, 1.9%) and detachment during dressing/undressing (n = 2, 1.3%). One participant withdrew early without any reported device‐related reliability issues.

Usability

The average score was 95.3 ± 7.6 out of full score of 100, whereas no “Dissatisfied” or “Very Dissatisfied” responses were reported by the participants, indicating high user satisfaction for the GS1 CGM. 82.5% of participants reported being “Very Satisfied” with easily wearing the sensor and 80% with easy removal. For application usability, 76.3% participants were “Very Satisfied” with reading the glucose profile, and 83.8% with alarm performance, highlighting a positive user experience across participants.

Safety Measurement

During the trial, 12 AEs were reported among 10 participants, resulting in an overall incidence of 12.5%. Of these, 3 events in 2 participants were device‐related; one is the skin irritation at the right arm of a 9‐year‐old boy (mild erythema at the application site), and the other 2 were skin irritations at each arm from a 5‐year‐old girl (sensor removal after 14 days, rashes at the application site, treated with Mucopolysaccharide Polysulfate Cream). All device‐related AEs were resolved after sensor removal, and no long‐term effects lasted. Non device‐related AEs, including respiratory infections and influenza, were assessed as unrelated to the device or trial procedures. No serious adverse events (SAEs) or withdrawals due to AEs occurred. Overall, the safety profile of the GS1 CGM system was favorable, with no major complications identified.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates the robust performance and usability of the GS1 CGM system in pediatric participants aged 3 to 17 years. The findings reveal strong agreement between the system's sensor readings and vBG reference values, with the system achieving a MARD of 8.7% and a 20%/20 agreement rate of 93.9%. The results also underscore the reliability of the GS1 CGM across a wide range of glycemic levels. Furthermore, 99.6% of measurements fell within Clarke error grid zones A and B, and 100% of readings were classified within consensus error grid zones A and B, demonstrating the system's high clinical accuracy. Notably, the GS1 also exhibited solid performance in detecting critical glycemic events, with detection rate of 97.6% and true alarm rate of 87.3% for hypoglycemia. These findings highlight the system's potential as a valuable tool in managing pediatric diabetes.

CGM has become a cornerstone in the management of T1DM in children and adolescents. A landmark randomized clinical trial demonstrated that 26 weeks of CGM use resulted in a significant reduction in HbA1c levels (7.92%) compared to conventional treatment (8.35%) 11 . Additional studies have highlighted the positive impact of early and frequent CGM use on glycemic control in pediatric populations 12 , 13 , 14 . Effective blood glucose monitoring not only helps maintain glucose levels within recommended targets but also contributes to delaying the onset of diabetes‐related complications 15 , 16 . Therefore, the use of CGM is recommended by several prominent international diabetes associations. The ADA recommends that children and adolescents with T1DM monitor their blood glucose levels up to 10 times daily, with CGM emerging as an effective tool for frequent testing 17 . For young participants on insulin therapy, real‐time CGM is prioritized for glucose management at diagnosis 17 . Similarly, the ISPAD advocates for the use of CGM in all youth with T1DM, emphasizing the role of CGM in reducing HbA1c levels and minimizing glucose variability 18 .

Despite its benefits, many CGM systems have not undergone clinical trials specifically targeting pediatric populations. Nevertheless, certain systems, including the Dexcom G7 and Abbott FreeStyle Libre 2, have received approval for use in children. The GS1 CGM system's performance closely aligns with that of other established CGM technologies, such as the FreeStyle Libre 19 and Dexcom systems 12 , demonstrating comparable accuracy and reliability in glucose monitoring. With a MARD of 8.7%, the GS1 showed high accuracy, with values comparable to those of the FreeStyle Libre 2 (9.7%) 19 and Dexcom G7 (8.1%) 12 . The 20%/20 agreement rate of our study is 93.9%, also demonstrating high accuracy. The 20%/20 rates of Dexcom G7 have been reported to be 95.3% 12 and FreeStyle Libre 2 of 92.1% 19 . Notably, the study designs and population varied from studies, thus direct comparison may not lead to a conclusion on the accuracy of each product. Glucose manipulation was adopted in the studies examining the accuracy of FreeSytle Libre 2 and Dexcom G7 to simulate dysglycemic situations, while no glucose manipulation was used in our study. Besides, the participants of our study were Chinese, while the above‐mentioned studies were conducted in the United States with a multi‐ethnic cohort.

Hypoglycemia poses significant acute and chronic risks for participants with diabetes. MAD is prioritized for hypoglycemic ranges because the MARD can amplify errors due to small denominator values. The MAD of our study is 0.26 mmol/L in the range of 2.2–3.3 and 0.46 mmol/L in the range of 3.3–4.4 mmol/L. Despite the different study designs, comparison in the accuracy‐related metrics across products should be cautious; the MADs of our study are still comparable to other studies. The MADs of Dexcom G7 were 0.63 mmol/L for the range of 2.2–3.3 and 0.36 mmol/L for the range of 3.3–4.4 mmol/L 12 , and the MADs of Abbott FreeStyle Libre Flash Glucose Monitoring System for 2.2–2.8 and 2.8–4.4 mmol/L were 0.49 mmol/L and 0.38 mmol/L 19 , respectively.

In addition to accuracy, safety remains a critical consideration for CGM systems designed for pediatric use. The GS1 CGM exhibited a favorable safety profile, with an overall AE incidence of 12.5% and no SAEs. Device‐related AEs, such as mild skin irritation, were reported in 2.5% of cases and resolved spontaneously. Skin reaction is a commonly reported AE caused by CGM adhesives. Younger age may be associated with more frequent skin reactions 20 , 21 . The future development direction of CGM involves smaller and thinner next‐generation models with hypoallergenic adhesives free of sensitizer, such as isobornyl acrylate (IBOA, an adhesive used in medical devices), which may reduce the contact area and lead to fewer skin‐related complications, making it more suitable for pediatric use and improving the sensor survival rate.

Usability is another key factor influencing the adoption of CGM systems in pediatric populations. The GS1 CGM received high satisfaction ratings, with an average score of 95.3 ± 7.6, reflecting its intuitive design and ease of use. This is crucial for promoting adherence among pediatric participants and their caregivers, as engagement and convenience are central to sustained use. Younger children, who may face cognitive or motor challenges, particularly benefit from the device's ergonomic design and straightforward instructions. Enhanced usability correlates directly with improved adherence, ensuring consistent use and maximizing clinical benefits 22 . Noteworthy features of the GS1 system, such as its ability to store data directly on the sensor (ensuring no loss of information even during Bluetooth disconnection), make it particularly suitable for pediatric care. Furthermore, the system supports data sharing with family members and healthcare providers, fostering collaborative care. For children without smartphones, a dedicated smartwatch functions as a reader replacement, further enhancing usability. While this feature is a standard component of the GS1 CGM system, all participants in this study only utilized smartphones with the dedicated App to monitor their glucose.

This study has several limitations. First, the demographic scope was limited to Chinese participants, which may not fully capture the diversity of global pediatric populations. Ethnic and racial differences could potentially influence CGM performance due to physiological variations (e.g. skin structure, interstitial fluid dynamics) 23 , 24 . However, current evidence has not established a definitive link between ethnicity and CGM accuracy. Further studies should investigate and compare the accuracy performance of CGM systems across different ethnic groups to enhance the global applicability and clinical reliability. Second, the study did not include high or low glucose manipulations as recommended by the expert consensus on CGM evaluations 25 , primarily due to ethical and safety considerations in pediatric trials. Additionally, the GS1 CGM system currently relies on threshold‐based alarms rather than predictive alerts, limiting its ability to proactively warn users of impending glycemic events. Future iterations of the GS1 CGM will focus on integrating advanced algorithms for predictive alerts, enhancing comfort for younger children, and reducing the device's size for improved usability. Moreover, additional longitudinal studies and real‐world evidence are needed to better understand the long‐term impact of the GS1 CGM system on glycemic control and quality of life in young children. Finally, comparative evaluations with other CGM systems under standardized clinical conditions, including assessments of accuracy during rapid glucose fluctuations and high and low glucose manipulations, are essential for further validating the clinical utility of the GS1 CGM system.

CONCLUSION

The real‐time CGM provides an accurate, reliable, and user‐friendly solution for pediatric glycemic monitoring. Its ability to record continuous glucose data and detect critical glycemic events can significantly improve diabetes management, leading to better clinical outcomes and enhanced quality of life for young participants. These findings strongly advocate for the integration of the real‐time CGM into routine pediatric diabetes care.
