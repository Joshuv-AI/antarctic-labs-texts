Caffeine Consumption Pattern and Associated Side Effects and Withdrawal Syndromes Among University Students of Bangladesh
Authors: Israth Jahan Rimu, Md Tazul Islam, Humayra Asima Mim, Nadima Sultana, Effat Ara Jahan, Akibul Islam Chowdhury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDue to tight academic activity and poor time management, consumption of caffeine among university are increased day by day. Despite the stimulating effects, the effects of caffeine on health remain unexplored in Bangladesh. This study aimed to investigate caffeine consumption patterns among university students in Dhaka, Bangladesh, focusing on associated side effects and withdrawal symptoms.MethodsA quantitative cross‐sectional survey was conducted from June to September 2024, involving simple random sampling of students aged 18 and above (mean ± SD = 23.15 ± 2.79) across various universities. Data were collected using a validated questionnaire covering demographic profiles, caffeine consumption patterns, and side effects. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS, employing descriptive statistics and logistic regression models to identify associations between caffeine use and demographic factors.ResultsAmong the surveyed students, about 34.0% drinking caffeine once daily, and 34.8% consuming caffeine two to three times daily. Tea (82.6%) emerged as the preferred source, followed by coffee (35.8%). Notably, female students reported higher consumption rates than males (AOR: 1.2, 95% CI: 0.82–1.81, p < 0.001). Students from non‐nutrition background have higher caffeine intake than nutrition background student (AOR: 2.53, 95% CI: 1.65–3.86, p < 0.001). Withdrawal symptoms reported by students were common, particularly headaches, which were significantly correlated with higher consumption of caffeine (p < 0.001). Additionally, muscle pain and sleep disturbances were linked to increased caffeine intake, highlighting the potential for physical dependence and health issues. Students often reported using caffeine to enhance focus and manage academic stress, with 90.2% indicating habitual consumption.ConclusionThese findings highlight the widespread caffeine consumption among students, particularly among females and those from non‐nutrition backgrounds, raising concerns about potential dependence and associated health issues. Addressing caffeine use can enhance student well‐being and academic performance, emphasizing the need for awareness and education on its effects. Longitudinal studies are also need to be carried out for evidence‐based intervention.Keywords: caffeine consumption, health risks, university students, withdrawal symptoms

Introduction

Caffeine (1,3,7‐trimethylxanthine) is a familiar companion in our daily routines, found in common beverages like coffee, tea, and energy drinks. It is also an addictive substance that can lead to tolerance and withdrawal symptoms [1, 2]. In Bangladesh, where coffee and tea are integral to social and cultural practices, understanding the consumption patterns of caffeine among university students is particularly crucial.

Students in Bangladesh often gather in cafes, clutching cups of steaming tea or energy drinks in the vibrant atmosphere of a university campus. These beverages become a part of life during late‐night study sessions, providing the energy boost needed to tackle assignments or prepare for exams [3]. Many young adults believe that caffeine enhances their focus and academic performance, leading them to rely on it more than they might realize [4]. Research shows that a significant number of college students up to 58.99% use caffeine as a coping mechanism to deal with the stresses of university life [5, 6, 7].

In the United States, about 80% of people like coffee or tea, with nearly 20% consuming caffeine in amounts that could be harmful [8]. A study conducted in Noakhali of Bangladesh revealed that 90% of males and 83% of females regularly drink tea, and many also consume coffee. For males, reasons for drinking caffeine‐related beverages include refreshment, habit, and enjoyment. Females, on the other hand, often cite enjoyment, refreshment, and the desire to overcome fatigue. Interestingly, withdrawal symptoms like headaches and low mood are reported more frequently among males [1]. In southeastern Bangladesh, another study highlighted that about 83.7% of university students preferred tea over coffee, especially during exam periods when the pressure mounts [9]. This suggests that tea as a beverage serves comfort during stressful times.

While caffeine is widely regarded for its ability to enhance wakefulness and cognitive function, it is essential to consider the potential risks associated with excessive consumption. High caffeine intake can lead to increased blood pressure, sleep disturbances, and even dependency [7, 10, 11]. For university students, the pressure to excel academically may drive them to consume larger quantities of caffeine, often overlooking the possible health implications. Withdrawal symptoms such as fatigue, irritability, and anxiety can create additional challenges during their academic journey [12].

The effects of caffeine can vary widely from person to person, influenced by factors such as dosage, gender, and overall health. While moderate caffeine consumption can enhance focus and cognitive function, too much can impair performance and exacerbate anxiety [4, 6]. Students with anxiety disorders may be especially sensitive to caffeine, experiencing heightened negative reactions, which underscores the importance of understanding how caffeine affects their lives [13, 14].

To our best knowledge, there are several studies that evaluate the consumption patterns of caffeine in Bangladesh among university students, but there is a limitation in research about the effects of caffeine consumption, including side‐effects and withdrawal syndrome. Given the unique cultural context of Bangladesh and the rising trend of caffeine consumption among university students, this study aimed to investigate the caffeine consumption patterns among university students and also evaluate how caffeine‐related consumption patterns are associated with demographic characteristics and self‐reported withdrawal syndromes and side‐effects.

Methods

Study Design and Settings

A quantitative cross‐sectional study was carried out during June 2024 to September 2024. Data were collected from different universities of Dhaka city of Bangladesh. Dhaka has been selected due to its status as a central hub of education in Bangladesh, where coffee shops and tea stalls are readily available, particularly in proximity to academic institutions.

Study Population and Sampling Method

Data were collected following a simple random technique from students of different universities. Participants who were 18 years old and above and gave consent to the study were selected as a sample of this study. Participants were excluded if they don't consume caffeine or related beverages.

Study Tools

Data were collected using a validated questionnaire, which was previously used in a study of UAE and confirmed the validity and reliability of that questionnaire [15]. In the study of UAE, the validity and reliability were measured by using content validity of the questionnaire and Cronbach's α test [15]. In Bangladesh cultural context, we also do reliability test among 65 students where the Cronbach's α value is 0.86 which indicates that the questionnaire is reliable for data collection. The questionnaire was adopted in English language without any further modification. This questionnaire was a close‐ended questionnaire having four sections including demographic profiles, patterns of caffeine consumption, caffeine and social expectations, and caffeine intake purposes. Patterns of caffeine consumption included questions related to knowledge about caffeine and patterns of consumption along with a regular diet.

The expectations of caffeine consumption were measured using Likert 5‐point scale [16]. Some questions about caffeine addiction, withdrawal syndrome, and side effects were also included. Addiction to caffeine is defined as consumption of caffeine at least 100 mg per day which is equal to 1 cup of coffee [15]. Using Likert scale, point 1–5 was given to the answer of questions related to caffeine consumption to determine the addiction level to caffeine. Social habits and reasons behind the caffeine consumption from tea, coffee or other caffeinated beverages were also included in the questionnaire.

Sample Size

Sample size was calculated using the Raosoft sample size calculator [17], which is used in many previous literatures. According to the calculator, a minimum sample size of 415, including 10% non‐response rate is required for the study. However, we have collected 500 sample from different university of Bangladesh.

Data Collection

Students were administered in this study through face‐to‐face interview and online survey form. Students from different disciplines were included in this study. Details about the study were discussed with each student before collecting data.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) software version 25.0. Descriptive statistics were used to describe the demographic profile of respondents. χ 2 test and Fisher's exact test were used appropriately to find out the relationship between dependent and independent variables. Multiple logistic regression models were used to determine the association of caffeine consumption patterns with demographic variables. All statistical analyses were considered as significant at 5% level of significance.

Discussion

In this study, university students in Dhaka, Bangladesh, were asked about how they use caffeine, the side effects they feel, and withdrawal syndromes. The results showed that many students drink caffeinated drinks multiple times a day, meaning caffeine use is very common among students. The results show that, the habitual nature of caffeine use, that students often believe caffeine helps them feel more energetic and alert. These results align with previous studies conducted in university settings in other countries, such as the UAE, the USA, and Malaysia, where caffeine intake is notably high among students due to academic demands, stress, and social habits [15, 18, 19].

The most common sources of caffeine were tea (82.6%) and coffee (35.8%). This shows a cultural preference in Bangladesh, where people of all ages and backgrounds often drink tea. However, the consumption of soft or energy drinks among the students was not negligible. Female students were found to use caffeine more than male students (p < 0.001). This may be due to differences in behavior with things like social pressure, sleep problems, or study stress. Similar results were seen in studies from the UAE and USA, where women were more likely to use caffeine to fight tiredness and stay focused while studying [15, 19]. Another study also shows that females who may experience greater stress, hormonal sensitivity, and stronger effects from caffeine compared to males [6].

There was also a clear link between a student's field of study and their caffeine use. Students who did not study nutrition drank more caffeine than those who did (p < 0.001). This finding aligns with another study, which also found that students in non‐nutrition fields tend to consume higher amounts of caffeine [20]. This might be because nutrition students are more aware of the health risks of having too much caffeine. Students from health/nutrition background are well aware about the consumption pattern of caffeine and its side effects which lower the caffeine consumption than non‐health/nutrition background [6].

The most common withdrawal symptom was a headache, which was strongly linked to how often students consumed caffeine (p < 0.001). This supports previous researches showing that headaches are a common sign of caffeine withdrawal, especially when regular users reduce or delay their caffeine intake [21, 22, 23]. There were also significant links between higher caffeine consumption and symptoms like muscle soreness (p = 0.043) and trouble sleeping (p = 0.013). This highlights the physical dependence that can develop with regular caffeine use. Some studies showed that caffeine is used as a painkillers [24, 25], whereas some studies showed that excessive intake of caffeine may increase chronic pain [26]. A recent review described that caffeine can affect the perception of pain by biochemical effects, that is, GABA A receptor blocking, calcium ion release, and so forth. It also inhibits the adenosine A2A receptors which reduces tiredness and increases the alertness [24]. However, symptoms like nausea, fatigue, or drowsiness did not show strong links to caffeine use, likely due to individual differences in tolerance and metabolism. A similar study also found that while muscle soreness and sleep problems were linked to higher caffeine intake, symptoms like nausea and fatigue had weaker connections, probably for the same reasons [23].

Student's expectations from caffeine, as measured by a 5‐point Likert scale, reflected a neutral to moderately positive attitude. Most students said they use caffeine regularly (90.2%) because they believe it improves their mood (26.6%) and alertness (34.4%), even though there are negative effects. In line with this, a similar study found that 83.4% of participants perceived caffeine as making them feel more alert, while 79.8% believed it enhanced their mood [27]. Many students were unsure if caffeine helps with thinking or reduces tiredness. The mixed responses may suggest that people have different experiences with caffeine or don't fully understand how it affects them. The reason behind this might be not aware much about caffeine. Another study looked at regular caffeine consumption, the results showed mixed views on its effects on thinking and tiredness, which could reflect individual differences [28].

On the other hand, side effects like difficulty falling asleep and restlessness were more commonly reported among those with higher consumption rates. These effects are well‐documented in studies linking caffeine intake to sleep disturbances, which matches research showing 80.7% of college students reported poor sleep quality, with 83.3% of those consuming excessive caffeine experiencing this issue [29]. Students who drank coffee in the evening may have been most affected. Notably, many students continued using caffeine despite experiencing withdrawal symptoms and negative effects, suggesting possible psychological or physical dependence. A similar study also found that students often keep consuming caffeine even with withdrawal symptoms [30].

The study shows that tea (82.6%) and coffee (35.8%) are the most common caffeine sources because of cultural preferences rather than caffeinated soft drink (11.4%). Caffeinated soft drinks are less popular among Bangladeshi students because tea and coffee are culturally preferred. Most students are well aware of the refreshing effects of tea and coffee, which are more accessible, affordable compared to soft drinks and seen as healthier options to manage academic stress and improve focus. Similar findings were also drawn in a previous study [31].

The strength of this study is it provides valuable information about caffeine use, side effects, and withdrawal symptoms among university students in Dhaka, Bangladesh. The results are more trustworthy because a comprehensive questionnaire was utilized to collect information from a wide range of pupils. We can better understand how caffeine impacts students' daily lives, particularly withdrawal symptoms, by examining both caffeine habits and associated negative effects. Caffeine use is associated with variables such as gender and field of study, according to the report. The study is helpful for developing health initiatives in the area because it focuses on the local culture. This study offers useful insights into caffeine use and perceptions among Bangladeshi students, but has a few limitations. Since it's a cross‐sectional study, it can only show the prevalence caffeine use is but can't prove a cause‐and‐effect link between caffeine and its negative effects. The use of self‐reported data may also lead to recall or social desirability biases, affecting the accuracy of the results. Additionally, there was no biochemical confirmation of caffeine use or withdrawal symptoms, which could further reduce the reliability of the findings. Another limitation is that the caffeine content in local beverages can vary based on how they are made and served, which was not measured in this study. This study did not consider other factors like academic workload, part‐time jobs, sleep, diet, or stress, which might affect both caffeine uses and the symptoms observed. Lastly, since the study was conducted in universities in Dhaka, the results may not apply to all students in Bangladesh.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on the caffeine consumption patterns among university students in Dhaka, Bangladesh, revealing that many rely on caffeinated beverages like tea and coffee to enhance alertness and manage academic pressures. The findings highlight a concerning link between high caffeine intake and withdrawal symptoms, particularly headaches, emphasizing the need for awareness about potential health risks. To address these issues, universities should implement educational campaigns, including awareness programs, to inform students about the effects of caffeine and promote moderation. Screening of mental health issues also needed to find out the relations between caffeine consumption with mental health problems. Establishing support systems for those experiencing dependency or withdrawal can also be beneficial. Additionally, encouraging healthier alternatives for managing stress, such as proper hydration and physical activity, can help students reduce reliance on caffeine. By fostering a healthier campus environment, universities may provide better support students' overall well‐being. Expanding research to include various demographics will further enhance understanding and inform strategies tailored to the unique cultural context of Bangladesh. Different studies including longitudinal studies or biochemical effect of caffeine use are recommended to, understand the side effect and other syndromes of caffeine on human.
