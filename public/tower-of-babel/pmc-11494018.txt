Green synthesis of anethole-loaded zinc oxide nanoparticles enhances antibacterial strategies against pathogenic bacteria
Authors: Muhammad Waqas Mazhar, Muhammad Ishtiaq, Mehwish Maqbool, Anila Arshad, Mohammed Ali Alshehri, Seham Sater Alhelaify, Ohud Muslat Alharthy, Mustafa Shukry, Samy M Sayed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe threat of antibiotic resistance is escalating, diminishing the effectiveness of numerous antibiotics due to the rapid development of resistant bacteria. In response, the use of green-synthesized nanoparticle, alone or combined with antimicrobial agents, appears promising. This study explores the effectiveness of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) synthesized using Loranthus cordifolius leaf extracts and subsequently coated with anethole. The fabrication of these nanoparticles was confirmed via UV-Vis, FTIR and TEM analyses, ensuring the nanoparticles were produced as intended. Utilizing a nanoprecipitation process that excludes evaporation and drying, a high drug loading capacity of 16.59% was accomplished. The encapsulation efficiency for anethole was recorded at 88.23 ± 4.98%. Antibacterial efficacy was assessed by com paring the green-synthesized ZnONPs (average size: 14.47 nm), anethole-loaded ZnONPs (average size: 14,75 nm), and commercially sourced ZnONPs. The ZnONPs with anethole demonstrated superior inhibition against all tested bacterial strains, including Gram-negative species like Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Escherichia coli, and Gram-positive species like Bacillus subtilis and Staphylococcus aureus, outperforming the commercially available ZnONPs. Additionally, anethole-coated ZnONPs showed the greatest inhibition of Gyr-B activity (IC50 = 0.78 ± 0.2 M), better than both green-synthesized and commercially available ZnONPs. These findings emphasize the enhanced antimicrobial properties of ZnONPs, particularly when combined with green synthesis and anethole loading, highlighting their potential in various biomedical applications.Keywords: ZnONPs, Green synthesis, Anethole loading, Antibacterial efficacy, Loranthus Cordifolius, NanoprecipitationSubject terms: Drug discovery, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Although the discovery of antibiotics and, subsequently their utilization in treating microbial diseases is perhaps the biggest achievement of chemotherapy, the broad spectrum of antibiotic usage has led to a serious issue in biomedical research more commonly known as antimicrobial resistance1. Several drugs used to treat microbial diseases eventually developed resistant strains2. These superbugs are posing great challenges to biomedical researchers across the world, and there is an increasing need to develop novel drugs and protocols. In the United States, CDC’s 2019 Antibiotic Resistance Threats Report3indicates that annually, there are over 2.8 million infections caused by antimicrobial-resistant pathogens, leading to more than 35,000 fatalities3.

Advances in material sciences have led to the application of matter at the nanoscale, more commonly known as nanotechnology4. The nanoscale material particles usually do not penetrate the bacterial cell; rather, they interact with the bacterial cell envelope, and their targeted degradation leads to cell death. Nanoparticles are, therefore, interesting antimicrobial candidates since the chance for antimicrobial resistance becomes less likely due to their mode of action. Researchers across the globe have reported the use of nanoparticles (NPs) for efficient drug delivery. These NPs include liposomal NPs, solid lipid NPs, metal oxide NPs, etc. Due to their nanoscale geometry, the NPs are potential nano-drug carriers and highly suitable for antimicrobial purposes5.

The NPs are being modified in their surface structure to enhance their antimicrobial efficacy and stability6–8. The surface modification of NPs with drugs synergistically enhances their antimicrobial potential, and thus, they become increasingly better9. For example, surface modification of silver NPs with ubiquicidin significantly improved their antimicrobial efficacy10.

Green synthesis is the most cost-effective, environmentally friendly, and bio-rational approach to nanomaterial synthesis11–13. The Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) have been synthesized by various researchers worldwide and have been investigated for their antimicrobial abilities. Various studies have confirmed the antimicrobial properties of ZnONPs, even at smaller concentrations14–17. ZnONPs are now being used for a variety of purposes, including priming agents, preservatives, and antimicrobial candidates, due to their high surface-to-volume ratio18. ZnONPs are effective against a broad species of gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, including food pathogens, highlighting their role as food preservatives.

Furthermore, the ZnONPs are being used commercially in various antimicrobial ointments and applications13. Additionally, the ZnONPs are being used commercially in surface cleaners. The ZnONPs interact with and perform surface abrasiveness on the bacterial cell envelope, cause DNA replication inhibition, promote the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and are potentially antimicrobial in terms of their usage as nanodrugs19.

Anethole is a natural compound derived from anise oil, making it a more appealing choice for applications where toxicity and biocompatibility are concerns20. Anethole has shown effective antimicrobial and antifungal activity, making it a valuable asset for wound healing, skincare, and combating antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Anethole has demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects, which can be beneficial for various applications, including pain management and skin care. Loading anethole onto ZnONPs can improve its bioavailability and stability21, allowing for more effective delivery and absorption. ZnONPs can be designed for targeted release, ensuring anethole is delivered precisely where needed, reducing potential side effects. Furthermore, the ZnONPs can enhance the solubility of anethole, making it more suitable for various applications. Therefore, a combination of anethole and ZnONPs may exhibit synergistic effects, enhancing their individual properties and potential applications20–22. An essential ingredient in anise, star anise, and fennel oils, anethole is hydrophobic by nature. It has been noted that anethole possesses antibacterial properties. Hydrophobic antimicrobial substances that have been nano-encapsulated have a great deal of promise to increase the effectiveness and efficiency of delivery in food and medication systems23.

Nanoparticles have a number of benefits. They can enter extracellular and intracellular spaces that may not be accessible to another drug-delivery system because of their small size. Nanoparticles shield a medicine against deterioration and lessen its adverse effects5,6. ZnONPs as a commercially available product, green synthesized product, and surface-loaded ZnONPs were studied for antimicrobial efficacy in the current study, with the assumption that anethole-loaded ZnONPs could be a potential alternative to antibiotics. The objectives of the study include the development of a novel drug that might be potentially beneficial in the context of antibiotic resistance.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Samples of Loranthus cordifolius (Family: Loranthaceae) were collected from Quercus dilatata (Family: Fagaceae) in Mirpur, AJK, Pakistan, in August 2022. Formal permission was obtained from the Department of Forests, and the purpose for collection was communicated for a Ph.D. scholar’s thesis research. Two healthy, disease-free specimens of each plant were collected. The samples were dried, and herbarium specimens were prepared and identified by Muhammad Ishtiaq. The voucher specimens were assigned MUH-2885 for L. cordifolius and MUH-2886 for Quercus dilatataand deposited in the Herbarium of the Department of Botany, MUST, for future reference and further studies. The collection complied with the guidelines of MUST, Mirpur AJK, Pakistan. Plant materials were selected and washed thoroughly with distilled water to remove impurities, then dried and crushed into a fine powder to increase surface area. To prevent contamination, all equipment and glassware were thoroughly cleaned and sterilized, gloves and lab coats were worn, work surfaces were covered, and samples were prepared in a laminar flow hood. After synthesis, nanoparticles were washed, centrifuged, dried, and stored in a clean environment to minimize exposure to contaminants, ensuring high-quality nanoparticles are produced4.

Green Synthesis and characterization of ZnONPs

The present research utilized three types of nanoparticles: commercially available zinc oxide nanoparticles (Sigma-Aldrich product: 544906), zinc oxide nanoparticles synthesized using leaf extracts from L. cordifolius, and these green-synthesized nanoparticles further coated with anethole as a third treatment. For preparing leaf extract for the green synthesis, fresh leaves of L. cordifolius were thoroughly washed thrice with tap water, and then distilled water (dH2O) was also used greatly in order to remove the contamination. These leaves were air dried for 7 days at 37 °C. A pestle and mortar ground each 5 g from the leaves to a fine powder.

The obtained powder was added to 0.5 L of distilled water and heated at 70 °C for 30 min. The mixture was filtrated using Whatman filter paper (No. 1). The obtained leaf extract was pale yellow. A 5 ml of this extract was used for the ZnONPs synthesis. Zinc acetate dehydrate (0.01 M) was dissolved in distilled water. In a 0.25 L flask, 95 mL of this solution was mixed with 5 mL of leaf extract. The mixture was incubated at 70 °C for 60 min with shaking at 150 rpm using a laboratory shaker incubator. The supernatants were decanted, and the powdery residues were transferred to centrifuge tubes of 1.5 mL. Each sample was subjected to washing with dH2O by centrifugation for 30 min at 3000 rpm. The washing step was repeated thrice to ensure the removal of impurities24. The characterization of NPs was carried out using UV visible spectroscopy, fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Model: Agilent Cary 630 FTIR), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Model: FEI Tecnai 12). Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectral analysis was carried out using the spectrophotometer (ELICO SL-159) with a range from 350 to 470 nm. TEM images were investigated for each sample using a FEI Tecnai 12 equipped with a low-dose digital camera (Gatan)4. The FTIR spectra were collected in the range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹. For each sample, 32 scans were averaged to obtain a clear spectrum. The characteristic absorption peaks were identified and compared to standard reference spectra to determine the functional groups present in the ZnONPs and the changes that occurred upon coating with anethole.

Although, UV-Vis spectroscopy is limited in providing information about particle size, shape, and distribution, and may not detect particles without chromophores. Similarly, the TEM is limited by its high vacuum conditions, and requires extensive sample preparation, which can introduce artifacts. However, UV-Vis spectroscopy provides information on the optical properties and size distribution, FTIR identifies functional groups and confirms surface modifications, and TEM offers high-resolution imaging to analyze the size, shape, and structural details of nanoparticles6–8.

Coating of Anethole on ZnONPs

In the nanoprecipitation method, a diffusing phase was achieved by dissolving green synthesized ZnONPs (0.1 g of ZnONPs in 10 mL of ethanol ), and 0.2 g of anethole (in 10 mL of acetone). Then, this phase was injected into a non-solvent amount of water through a syringe equipped with a 20-G angio catheter positioned by gentle mixing with the needle directly in the medium. The obtained formed NPs were centrifuged and washed with dH2O23.

Efficiency determination of drug loading and entrapment

Anethole entrapment was investigated with UV analysis at 284 nm. After dissolving 10 mg from the nanoparticles in acetonitrile (10 mL), 20 mL of methanol was added in order to precipitate the polymer. Each sample was detected with UV monitoring. To determine the encapsulation efficiency indirectly, 1 mL of the supernatant from the first centrifugation was mixed with acetonitrile (50 mL) and analyzed23. The methods for calculating drug loading percentage and encapsulation efficiency are crucial in the development and evaluation of drug delivery systems, such as nanoparticles, liposomes, and microspheres. The drug loading percentage, quantifies the amount of drug present in a given weight of the delivery system. This measure ensures that the correct amount of drug is incorporated, which is essential for consistency, quality control, and the therapeutic efficacy of the formulation. On the other hand, encapsulation efficiency, assesses the effectiveness of the encapsulation process by comparing the actual drug content to the intended amount. High encapsulation efficiency indicates optimal use of the drug and effective formulation processes, which is important for cost-efficiency and minimizing waste. Together, these methods help researchers and manufacturers optimize formulation processes, ensure regulatory compliance, and develop effective and reliable drug delivery23.

In vitro anethole release from ZnONPs

Drug release from ZnONPs was investigated using a dialysis technique. The nanoparticles of 3 mg were placed in a dialysis bag. This bag was soaked in 40 mL of phosphate-buffered saline solution (pH 7.4) and maintained with 100 rpm shaking at 37 °C and in a shaker. At predetermined time intervals, each sample was taken, and the medium was replaced with fresh phosphate-buffered saline solution (40 mL). The amount of drug release was determined by UV or HPLC23. The choice of using 3 mg of nanoparticles in our protocol for investigating in vitro anethole release from ZnONPs ensures that the released amount of anethole can be accurately quantified using UV spectroscopy or HPLC, given the sensitivity of these methods to detect trace amounts of substances. Additionally, this amount is sufficient to provide adequate surface area for controlled release of anethole into the phosphate-buffered saline solution (pH 7.4) over the experimental timeframe at 37 °C with agitation. These conditions mimic physiological environments and are conducive to studying the release kinetics of anethole from ZnONPs effectively.

Antimicrobial analysis

Tested microorganisms

Four bacterial strains or taxa were tested in order to explore the antimicrobial activity of selected materials. Two gram-negative bacteria, i.e., Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 10145), Escherichia coli (ATCC 10799) and two Gram-positive, i.e., Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 11774) and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 29213).

For the investigation of antimicrobial activity, the protocol of Ajaib et al25. was used. The zone of inhibition (ZI) was measured to evaluate the antimicrobial activity. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) were investigated to determine the effectiveness of the treatments. The activity index (AI) was calculated based on these measurements.

For the preparation of nutrient agar medium, 7 g of agar powder, 4 g of nutrient broth, and 0.5 L of distilled water were combined in a flask or beaker. The mixture was thoroughly dissolved in distilled water and heated until it reached a semi-solid state. Sterilization was conducted using a digital autoclave at 121 °C and 15 psi for 15 min.

The MIC was determined using the method described by Mitscher et al26., which involves assessing the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial agent that inhibits visible bacterial growth. The MBC was calculated by visual inspection under a binocular microscope or with a colony counter, identifying the concentration of extracts that eradicated all or nearly 99.5% of the bacterial population. This was compared to the positive control, ciprofloxacin, to evaluate the efficacy of the extracts.

Discussion

The physical processes used to create nanoparticles are not economical, and the requisite control of particle size is frequently not achieved. More control over particle size and dimension is made possible by the chemical processes used in the creation of nanomaterials. Yet, the technique is expensive and demands a lot of talent. Under the biological synthesis model, green synthesis is far more advantageous financially. Although it takes time and effort, green synthesis is very cost-effective and eco-friendly. In the current work, the method of green synthesis was used to create the ZnONPs27, and further, we performed coatings on these green synthesized nanoparticles with the drug anethole. ZnONPs have been observed to have a characteristic wide absorption peak from 330 to 460 nm28. Green synthesis processes involving plant extracts typically occur under ambient conditions (e.g., room temperature, atmospheric pressure), reducing energy consumption compared to traditional chemical methods that often require high temperatures, pressure, and energy-intensive processes like refluxing or microwave heating. In contrast, traditional chemical methods for nanoparticle synthesis often involve hazardous chemicals, solvents, and reagents that can generate toxic by-products and waste6–8. These processes typically require high energy inputs and extensive purification steps, contributing to higher energy consumption and increased waste production. On the other hand, green synthesis with plant extracts minimizes hazardous waste generation and reduces the overall environmental footprint by utilizing natural, biodegradable materials. Furthermore, the simplicity of green synthesis methods can potentially lower production costs and improve scalability, making them economically feasible for large-scale nanoparticle production without compromising environmental sustainability14.

Leaf extract of L. cordifoliuscan successfully produce zinc nanoparticles, as shown by the optical absorption band peaking at 374 nm and 402 nm. It may be possible to develop a straightforward method for producing nanoparticles using this quick extracellular precipitation process4. Additionally, surface coatings on these ZnONPs might be done successfully. The wavelength absorbed by nanoparticles depends on their size and the material they are made of. The anethole-coated nanoparticles recorded their absorbance in a longer wavelength (402 nm). In general, larger nanoparticles tend to absorb longer wavelengths of light, while smaller nanoparticles tend to absorb shorter wavelengths. This phenomenon is due to the fact that the size of the nanoparticle affects its electronic structure and the way it interacts with light. As the size of the nanoparticle increases, the energy levels of its electrons become more closely spaced, and this results in the absorption of longer wavelengths of light. The results regarding TEM imaging of ZnONPs are comparable with those of Mashrai et al29. and demonstrate that the shape of particles is almost quasi-spherical with just a minor fluctuation in the ZnO TEM images after loading with anethole.

Furthermore, the TEM histogram showed that synthesized NPs were uniformly distributed. This is important information for understanding the properties of the nanoparticles and how they will behave in different applications. For example, nanoparticles with a narrow size distribution (like those in this sample) tend to have better optical and electrical properties than those with a wider size distribution4. This is because the particles are more likely to be uniform in shape and composition, which can lead to more predictable and consistent results. The size of nanoparticles can also affect their solubility and stability. Nanoparticles that are too small can be easily aggregated, while those that are too large can be difficult to disperse. The size distribution of the nanoparticles in a sample can, therefore, be important for determining how they will interact with other molecules and materials. The FTIR spectra confirmed the successful capping of ZnO nanoparticles with anethole. The characteristic peaks of both ZnO and anethole were present in the spectrum of anethole-capped ZnO NPs, indicating the retention of the core structure and the surface modification by anethole. The presence of anethole peaks in the blue spectrum confirmed the successful capping of ZnO nanoparticles with anethole6–8. The peaks associated with ZnO were visible in the blue spectrum, which further supports that the core ZnO structure is retained after capping. The slight shifts or changes in the intensity of peaks (compared to pure anethole or ZnO NPs) suggest interactions between anethole and the ZnO surface, possibly through physical adsorption or chemical bonding6–8.Antibacterial activities of ZnONPs against the tested bacteria in this study; E. coli, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and B. subtiliswere reported in many previous investigations30–34. Moreover, several studies revealed that ZnONPs had antibacterial and antifungal effects35,36.

The green synthesized ZnONPs were better in terms of their efficacy as antimicrobial agents. The lowest activity index of commercially available ZnONPs was observed with the gram-negative bacteria P. aeruginosa.Our results are in accordance with the investigations of Perera et al37. and Mohd et al38., where lower efficacy of ZnONPs was reported against gram-negative bacteria. These findings may be due to the safe and non-pathogenic approach followed in green synthesis, where plant extract acts as a reducing and capping agent39. The main reason why green-synthesized ZnONPs exhibit antimicrobial activity is their high surface area, the ratio of the volume, and their small size. This high surface area maintains the interaction of a large number of active sites with the microbial cells, leading to disruption of their membrane integrity and subsequent cell death. Additionally, the small size of the NPs enables them to penetrate the microbial cells and interact with their intracellular components, such as DNA, RNA, and proteins, further contributing to their antimicrobial activity. Furthermore, the surface of ZnONPs can generate ROS under UV or visible light irradiation, which have potent antimicrobial effects. These ROS can induce oxidative stress in the microbial cells, leading to damage to their cellular components and eventual cell death40. ZnONPs release Zn ions into the surrounding environment. These ions can penetrate bacterial cells and interfere with cellular processes such as enzymatic reactions and metabolic pathways. The disruption of these essential processes can impair bacterial growth and survival. Additionally, ZnONPs have been shown to interact with bacterial DNA, leading to inhibition of DNA replication and transcription. This interference can result in genetic mutations, impairing bacterial viability and reproduction. Overall, the combination of small size, high surface area, and ROS generation makes green-synthesized ZnO NPs an effective antimicrobial agent against a wide range of bacteria, fungi, and viruses. Anethole loading increased the antibacterial activities of ZnONPs even more. This increase in activity may be due to anethole. The role of anethole as an antimicrobial agent has been reported by different investigations40,41. There are various mechanisms by which anethole acts as an antimicrobial agent. One of the main mechanisms is by disrupting the cell membrane of the microorganisms. Anethole has been shown to increase the permeability of the cell membrane, leading to leakage of cellular components and, eventually, cell death. Another mechanism by which anethole acts as an antimicrobial agent is by inhibiting the activity of microbial enzymes. Anethole can inhibit the activity of several important microbial enzymes, such as peroxidase, catalase, and superoxide dismutase, leading to a reduction in the ability of the microorganisms to defend themselves against oxidative stress. Additionally, anethole has been shown to disrupt the energy metabolism of microorganisms by inhibiting the production of ATP, which is an essential energy source for the survival and growth of microorganisms. In all cases, however, the ZnONPs restricted the bacterial growth and killed them (Table 2). The ZnONPs disturb the bacterial DNA replication and significantly alter the expression of virulence-causing genes in the bacteria, thereby limiting their pathogenic role42 .

Plants contain flavonoids, which are the main reducing and capping agents in the green synthesis approach. These flavonoids have been reported to cause negative supercoiling of bacterial DNA through inhibiting action on the activity of DNA gyrase7. In the present study, we found ZnONPs effective in inhibiting Gyr-B activity. The structural dynamics of Gyr-B suggested that the enzyme is composed of alpha and beta subunits. Antibiotics target the alpha subunit to cause disturbances in the amplification of bacterial DNA43. Anethole has been shown to inhibit the activity of DNA gyrase, which is an important enzyme for DNA replication and repair. This enzyme is a type II topoisomerase and introduces negative supercoils into DNA. Therefore, it is an important target for many antibiotics. Studies have shown that anethole can inhibit the activity of DNA gyrase by binding to its subunit B (GyrB) and preventing its interaction with subunit A (GyrA). This inhibition leads to the accumulation of double-stranded DNA breaks and the cessation of DNA replication and transcription, ultimately resulting in bacterial cell death. The inhibition of DNA gyrase activity by anethole has been demonstrated in several bacterial species, including E. coli and S. aureus. In addition, anethole has been shown to potentiate the activity of certain antibiotics that target DNA gyrase, such as ciprofloxacin and norfloxacin, leading to synergistic effects against bacterial growth. Singh et al44. reported that genes such as those encoding gyrase and single-stranded binding protein genes are upregulated upon application of ZnONPs, thus interfering with bacterial DNA replication and promoting bacterial death and static growth. These findings are a testament to our present results. Furthermore, Slade and Radman45 reported that genes involved in the mechanisms of DNA repair, such as mismatch repair and base excision, are down-regulated by ZnONPs. Gyr-B inhibition was also observed in the green synthesized ZnONPs. In this study, the leaf extracts of mistletoe L. cordifolius were used. The antimicrobial activities of mistletoe have also been documented in the scientific literature.

Following the green synthesis of ZnONPs, the drug loading, and entrapment efficiencies were recorded. The nature of the drug being loaded highly correlates with the type of protocol being used for drug loading. Generally, hydrophobic drugs are better encapsulated by the nanoprecipitation method followed in the current experiment23 .

Excellent drug-loading behavior of ZnONPs has been reported by Perera et al37.. Anethole can be entrapped within nanoparticles to improve its stability, bioavailability, and controlled release. Several types of NPs, such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and solid lipid NPs, have been used for the encapsulation of anethole. The entrapment of anetholes within nanoparticles can protect them from degradation by environmental factors, such as heat, light, and oxygen, and can also prevent their rapid metabolism and elimination in the body23,24,40. Moreover, nanoparticles can enhance the solubility of anethole in aqueous solutions and facilitate its delivery to target cells or tissues. The preparation of anethole-entrapped nanoparticles typically involves the use of biocompatible and biodegradable polymers, lipids, or surfactants as encapsulating materials. These materials can form stable and homogeneous nanoparticles through various techniques, such as emulsification, solvent evaporation, and coacervation24. Once prepared, anethole-entrapped NPs can be administered through different routes, such as topical, oral, and intravenous, depending on the intended application. The release of anethole from the nanoparticles can be controlled by the type and composition of the encapsulating materials, as well as pH and temperature.

The in vitro release of anethole from nanoparticles promised encouraging results. In vitro release can be studied by conducting a release kinetics study4. In general, the release of anethole from nanoparticles can be classified into two types: burst release and sustained release. Burst release occurs during the initial phase of the release kinetics study, where a significant amount of anethole is rapidly released from the nanoparticles due to the presence of anethole on the surface or in the outer layers of the NPs. Sustained release, on the other hand, occurs over a longer period28, where the anethole is released from the inner layers of the NPs due to diffusion or erosion of the encapsulating materials. The rate and extent of anethole release from NPs can be influenced by various factors, such as the type and composition of the encapsulating materials, surface area and the size of the nanoparticles, and the pH and temperature of the release medium37–39. These factors can be optimized to achieve the desired release profile for the intended application of the anethole-entrapped nanoparticles. Furthermore, the data presented in the Figure shows that after the initial burst, the behavior of nanoparticle release became constant, possibly due to matrix erosion mechanisms and drug diffusion. Furthermore, the nature of nanoparticles (in our case, ZnONPs) is largely responsible for the initial burst detected in the current experiment.

Translating in vitro findings of anethole-coated ZnONPs and green-synthesized ZnONPs to in vivo applications holds substantial implications for clinical settings. In vitro studies demonstrating the antibacterial efficacy of anethole-coated ZnONPs provide a foundational understanding of their potential in treating bacterial infections. These findings suggest that these nanoparticles can effectively inhibit bacterial growth through mechanisms such as membrane disruption and ROS generation. However, transitioning from controlled laboratory conditions to complex in vivo environments requires addressing several challenges. One key consideration is the biodistribution and pharmacokinetics of anethole-coated ZnONPs in living organisms. Understanding how these nanoparticles behave in biological fluids, their absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion profiles are crucial for determining optimal dosages and administration routes in clinical settings46. Techniques such as pharmacokinetic studies and biodistribution analyses in animal models can provide essential data for this purpose. Moreover, assessing the safety profile of anethole-coated ZnONPs is paramount. Clinical translation also necessitates the development of suitable formulations and delivery systems for anethole-coated ZnONPs. This includes considerations such as stabilizing agents, nanoparticle size, surface modification for targeting specific tissues or pathogens, and methods for scalable production that meet regulatory standards46.

To minimise the toxicity issues and hazardous impacts of nanoparticles, the green synthesis approach is currently preferred. The green synthesis approach ensures the targeted and controlled delivery of nano-medicine and is being encouraged globally in the scientific community. Furthermore, fewer side effects are being reported on the design of the nanoparticles following the green synthesis route47. Natural botanicals and bioactive compounds present in the plants provide a controlled release of the nano-medicine. Additionally, the bioavailability of the nanoparticles becomes easier. Alam et al48. reported that the thymoquinone-loaded lipid nanoparticles provided around 6-fold better bioavailability than free thymoquinone. All of these findings provide a testament to our results.

Conclusions

This study sheds light on the escalating concern of antibiotic resistance and introduces a promising avenue for combating this global threat. The investigation into green-synthesized zinc oxide nanoparticles, incorporating the bioactive agent anethole, has unveiled a novel approach to bolstering antimicrobial effectiveness. The successful synthesis of ZnONPs from L. cordifolius extracts and the subsequent loading with anethole has been validated through analytical techniques. The demonstrated enhanced antibacterial activity of anethole-loaded ZnONPs, as evidenced by the notable zone of inhibition and potent inhibition of Gyr-B activity, presents a compelling case for their efficacy in addressing antibiotic-resistant strains. The comparative analysis against commercially available counterparts further underscores the advantages of this innovative approach. The findings of this study contribute to the expanding knowledge base in the realm of nano-medicine and antimicrobial research. The implications extend beyond theoretical constructs, as the green synthesis method and the incorporation of anethole into ZnONPs pave the way for potential biomedical applications. By addressing antibiotic resistance through a multifaceted approach involving nanotechnology and bioactive compounds, this research sets a foundation for future investigations and innovations in the field. Furthermore, exploring potential applications beyond antibacterial therapy is intriguing. For instance, the combination of anethole-coated ZnONPs with other therapeutic agents could be explored for synergistic effects in treating viral infections or even cancers, leveraging their unique properties in targeted delivery and enhanced cellular uptake. In conclusion, future research should focus on harnessing the potential of anethole-coated ZnONPs for advanced drug delivery systems, conducting mechanistic studies to deepen understanding, advancing into preclinical and clinical trials, and exploring broader applications in biomedical fields beyond antibacterial therapy. These efforts hold promise for developing innovative nanotechnological solutions with significant clinical impact.
