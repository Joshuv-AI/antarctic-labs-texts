Analogue black string in a quantum harmonic oscillator
Matheus E. Pereira 1,2 , and Alexandre G. M. Schmidt 1,2

Abstract
For a scalar particle without self-interaction or backreaction from the space-time background, the dynamics are governed by the Klein-Gordon equation. In this work, we write the exact solution of this equation in the background of a chargeless, static black string in terms of the biconfluent Heun function. In this curious system, we are able to explore what happens if we have negative values for the masses. The eigenvalue problem provides complex energy values for the particle, which may indicate the presence of quasinormal modes. We show a simple quantum system that can imitate the particle in the black string background, whose solutions are also applications of the biconfluent Heun function.

Introduction

The most general asymptotically flat and stationary solution of the Einstein–Maxwell equations is the Kerr–Newman solution, which, according to Hawking’s topology theorem, also manifests spherical symmetry at the horizon
[
1
,
2
]
. Solutions of Einstein’s equations whose event horizon surface does not possess spherical symmetry are thus called
[
3
]
topological black holes, like the black membrane, black string, and toroidal black holes.

Topological black holes are often found in higher–dimensional or non-asymptotically flat spacetimes, like an AdS spacetime
[
4
]
, and are unstable
[
5
]
under scalar perturbations, which does not make them any less interesting from the theoretical point of view. Indeed, there have been reports on rotating black strings
[
6
,
7
]
, hairy black strings
[
8
]
, and even a type of stable black string
[
4
]
.

Unfortunately, there seems to exist little progress in the analogue gravity programme
[
9
]
related to the black string, except for a connection in Obukhov’s work relating a property of classical confinement of cylindrically symmetric solutions for SU(
2
) Yang–Mills gauge theory and an analogue property of black strings
[
10
,
11
]
. The importance of constructing analogue models of gravity has been thoroughly reviewed by
[
9
]
, and the mission of the present work is to start filling this gap in the literature.

Zel’dovich proposed the first analogue model for a gravitational system. It was an experiment
[
12
]
involving wave scattering by a cylindrical object that would act similarly to a black hole. Zel’dovich also predicted a new effect using this analogue system. Later on, Unruh
[
13
]
investigating acoustic waves found equations equivalent to those of a scalar field in a pseudo-Riemannian space-time. Recently, there have been investigations into quantum analogue models for the Kerr–de Sitter
[
14
]
and the Kerr–Sen
[
15
]
black holes, using a particle on a spheroid and on a sphere immersed in an electromagnetic field, respectively. Analogue models allow us to visualize hidden features, to investigate inaccessible domains of physical quantities (e.g., negative masses, magnetic monopoles
[
16
,
17
]
and dyons
[
18
]
) and even unphysical quantities
[
19
]
(e.g., charge conjugation and time reversal) of target systems.

To add to the analogue gravity programme, in this work we present the exact solutions of the wave equation in the background of a non-rotating, chargeless black string in a static universe in terms (or as an application) of the biconfluent Heun functions. The black string solution allows both the test particle and the black hole masses to be negative, and we present plots for the normalized probability density of the wavefunction in these cases. Further, we propose a damped quantum harmonic oscillator that can mimic the behavior of the test particle, and we solve the Schrödinger equation also using the biconfluent Heun function.

The structure of this work is divided as follows: in section
II
, we dive in search of the exact solutions for the Klein–Gordon equation in the background of our black string. Next, section
III
shows our suggestion for an analog black string, using a damped quantum harmonic oscillator. The problem is formulated using a one-dimensional system of coupled springs. A table is shown, where we present the mapping of the two systems into each other. We conclude in section
IV
with our final remarks and future perspectives.

\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\;\partial_{\alpha}\left(g^{\alpha\beta}\sqrt{-g}\;\partial_{\beta}\right)\Psi-\mu_{0}^{2}\Psi=0,

(2)

(3)

\Psi(t,r,\rho,\phi)=\exp\left(i\sigma t+i\nu\phi\right)R(r)S(\rho),

(4)

\nu

R

\frac{d^{2}S}{d\rho^{2}}+\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dS}{d\rho}+\left(2M\lambda-\frac{\nu^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)S=0,

(5)

S_{\nu}(\rho)=c_{\nu}J_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{2M\lambda}\rho\right)+d_{\nu}H_{\nu}^{(1)}\left(\sqrt{2M\lambda}\rho\right),

(6)

\frac{d^{2}g}{dx^{2}}+\left(\frac{\lambda\kappa}{x}+\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}\kappa^{3}x}{2M}+\frac{1}{4x^{2}}-x^{2}\right)g=0.

(8)

By means of the transformation
R(r)=r^{A}e^{Br}e^{Cr^{2}}f(r)

7

\frac{d^{2}f}{dr^{2}}+\left(\frac{1}{r}+\frac{i\mu_{0}^{2}}{\sigma}+\frac{i\sigma r}{M}\right)\frac{df}{dr}+\frac{1}{r}\left[\left(\frac{i\sigma}{M}-\frac{\mu_{0}^{4}}{4\sigma^{2}}\right)r+\lambda+\frac{i\mu_{0}^{2}}{2\sigma}\right]f(r)=0,

III
Analogue black string

We can use the previous results to investigate a very interesting mapping between two distinct systems: the one we studied in the
II
and an one-dimensional system studied by Kamath
[
25
]
, namely, the two coupled oscillators interacting harmonically plus a classical gravitational potential. The Schrödinger equation for this problem reads,

\left[\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}-2\mu lr^{2}+4a\mu lr+\frac{2G\mu m^{2}}{r}+2\mu\left(E_{n}-4a^{2}l\right)\right]\psi(r)=0,

(12)

where
m
is the oscillating particles mass,
l=3k/4
,
\mu
is the system’s reduced mass,
a
is the system’s equilibrium position,
G
is Newton’s constant,
r
is the distance,
k
the spring coupling constant, and
E_{n}
is the energy eigenvalue. Introducing an external potential
V_{ext}(r)
we obtain,

\left[\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}-2\mu lr^{2}+4a\mu lr+\frac{2G\mu m^{2}}{r}+2\mu\left(E_{n}-V_{ext}(r)-4a^{2}l\right)\right]\psi(r)=0.

(13)

Let us call it
laboratory equation
, noticing that comparing it with the canonical biconfluent Heun equation, it is written in the normal form.

Now turn to our equation (
8
) and let us call it
target equation
. Both laboratory and target equations, i.e., (
13
) and (
8
) can be mapped to each other if the external potential could be adjusted to,

V_{ext}(r)=-\frac{1}{8\mu r^{2}}-4a^{2}l+E_{n},

(14)

namely, to an energy dependent constant potential plus an attractive inverse square potential. The change of variable
r=\xi x
, where
\xi=(2\mu l)^{-1/4}
, makes it straightforward to relate the laboratory parameters

and
V_{0}
to the target parameters
\lambda,\sigma,\mu_{0},
and
M
, since
\xi
and
\kappa
are inversely proportional. These relations are listed in table

the laboratory equation (

8

m
is a mass,

is a length and

IV
Conclusions

We studied the Klein-Gordon equation for a massive particle interacting with the gravitational field of a kind of topological black hole called black string, holding a mass
M
. The equation is separable, and we managed to solve it exactly. We presented the wavefunction as a product of complex exponentials
\exp(i\sigma t+i\nu\phi)
times cylindrical functions — Bessel and first kind Hankel functions of
\rho
— times a biconfluent Heun’s function, which depends on
r
. The probability densities are shown in figure
1
, and they illustrate that there are no normal modes for the particle in this background. We contribute to the analogue gravity initiative suggesting that a damped quantum harmonic oscillator as an analogue for the particle in the chargeless, non-rotating black string background in a static universe.

As we suggest in this work, the emergence of imaginary eigenvalues may indicate the presence of quasinormal modes and other effects when we consider charge — whether electric, magnetic, or dyonic — and the cosmological constant.

Appendix A
The Biconfluent Heun Differential Equation

The biconfluent Heun differential equation (BHE) can be achieved as follows
[
23
]
. Starting from the confluent Heun equation (CHE)

y^{\prime\prime}+\left(\frac{\gamma}{z}+\frac{\delta}{z-1}+\varepsilon\right)y^{\prime}+\left[\frac{\alpha z-q}{z(z-1)}\right]y(z)=0,

(16)

we shift the singular point
z=1
to
z=\frac{1}{b}
through
z=bz^{\prime}
and
y(z(z^{\prime}))=f(z^{\prime})
. Then, imposing
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}/b^{2}
,
q=q^{\prime}/b
, and
\varepsilon=-\varepsilon^{\prime}/b^{2}
, together with
\delta=-\delta^{\prime}/b-\varepsilon^{\prime}/b^{2}
, we take the limit
b\rightarrow\infty
and drop the primes, acquiring

f^{\prime\prime}+\left(\frac{\gamma}{z}+\delta+\varepsilon z\right)f^{\prime}+\left(\frac{\alpha z-q}{z}\right)f(z)=0,

(17)

which we refer as the canonical form of the BHE. This form is somewhat in agreement with
[
22
]
, although our BHE is more general, and it has a
+
instead of a
-
sign in the second term, contrasting with the NIST Handbook
[
24
]
. This notation is also in agreement with
[
25
]
and it can make use of the Mathematica standard notation for its solution,
\text{HeunB}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\varepsilon,r)
.

To solve (
17
), we introduce the ansatz

f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n+s},

(18)

from which, upon substitution in (
17
), results in the three-term recurrence relation

\displaystyle a_{0}s(s+\gamma-1)=0,

(19)

\displaystyle a_{0}(s\delta-q)+a_{1}(s+1)(s+\gamma)=0,

(20)

\displaystyle a_{n+1}(n+1)(n+\gamma)+a_{n}(n\delta-q)+a_{n-1}(\gamma+(n-1)\varepsilon)=0

(21)

From equation (
19
), we have the values of s, namely,
s={0,1-\gamma}
. From (
20
), results

a_{1}=\frac{q-\delta s}{(s+1)(s+\gamma)}a_{0},

which means that we must impose the restriction
\gamma\neq 0,-1,-2,...
. To proceed further, we massage equation (
21
) into the shape

a_{n+1}=\underbrace{\frac{(q-n\delta)}{(n+1)(n+\gamma)}}_{A_{n}}a_{n}\overbrace{-\frac{(\gamma+(n-1)\varepsilon)}{(n+1)(n+\gamma)}}^{B_{n}}a_{n-1}=A_{n}a_{n}+B_{n}a_{n-1}.

(22)

We are now prepared to tackle this three-term recurrence relation with the methodology laid down by Kristensson
[
27
]
, in which we rewrite

a_{1}=\frac{q-\delta s}{(s+1)(s+\gamma)}a_{0}=A_{0}a_{0},

and, as Kristensson concludes, we are allowed to consider

a_{n}=D_{n}a_{0},

(23)

with
D_{n+1}
being the determinant

D_{n+1}=\begin{vmatrix}A_{0}&-1&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
B_{1}&A_{1}&-1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&B_{2}&A_{2}&-1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&B_{n-1}&A_{n-1}&-1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&B_{n}&A_{n}\end{vmatrix}

(24)

The power series is convergent so long as the limit

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}D_{n}=0

(25)

is satisfied. This equation can be understood as an eigenvalue equation, that gives us a condition on
q
to unsure the convergence of (
18
). The convergence can be determined by observing the limit

\lambda=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}+B_{n}\frac{a_{n-1}}{a_{n}}=A+\frac{B}{\lambda},

that exists according to the Poincaré–Perron theorem, where
A=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}A_{n}
and
B=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}B_{n}
. We see that

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\delta}{n}+\frac{\delta(\gamma+1)+q}{n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\epsilon}{n}+\frac{\epsilon\gamma+2\epsilon-\gamma}{n^{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right).

Consequently, we find
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=0
, meaning the radius of convergence is infinite.
Another possibility lies in the polynomial solutions of BHE. To achieve these solutions, we must turn back to (
22
) and set
a_{n}=B_{n}=0
. To do so, we notice that if
\gamma/\varepsilon=1-N
, where
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{n}=D_{n}=0
, we have
a_{n+1}=a_{n}=0
, thus truncating the series (
18
).
