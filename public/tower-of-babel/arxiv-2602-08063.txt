Abstract
. The Wasserstein distance has emerged as a key metric to quantify distances between probability distributions, with applications in various fields, including machine learning, control theory, decision theory, and biological systems. Consequently, learning an unknown distribution with non-asymptotic and easy-to-compute error bounds in Wasserstein distance has become a fundamental problem in many fields. In this paper, we devise a novel algorithmic and theoretical framework to approximate an unknown probability distribution ℙ \mathbb{P} from a finite set of samples by an approximate discrete distribution ℙ ^ \widehat{\mathbb{P}} while bounding the Wasserstein distance between ℙ \mathbb{P} and ℙ ^ \widehat{\mathbb{P}} . Our framework leverages optimal transport, nonlinear optimization, and concentration inequalities. In particular, we show that, even if ℙ \mathbb{P} is unknown, the Wasserstein distance between ℙ \mathbb{P} and ℙ ^ \widehat{\mathbb{P}} can be efficiently bounded with high confidence by solving a tractable optimization problem (a mixed integer linear program) of a size that only depends on the size of the support of ℙ ^ \widehat{\mathbb{P}} . This enables us to develop intelligent clustering algorithms to optimally find the support of ℙ ^ \widehat{\mathbb{P}} while minimizing the Wasserstein distance error. On a set of benchmarks, we demonstrate that our approach outperforms state-of-the-art comparable methods by generally returning approximating distributions with substantially smaller support and tighter error bounds.

2.1.
Notation

For a vector

, we denote by
x^{(i)}
its
i

\bar{\mathbbm{1}}=(1,\dots,1)^{\top}
. The set of indexes
\{1,\dots,N\}
is denoted as
[N]
. For a given set

, we denote
\left\lVert C\right\rVert=\sup_{x,y\in C}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert
as the diameter of

. We represent
{\mathbbm{1}}_{C}(x)=1\,\text{ if }x\in C;0\,\text{ otherwise}
as the indicator function for the set
C
. For
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\big\{C_{1},...,C_{M}\big\}
is a partition of
{\mathcal{X}}
in
M

regions
if
C_{i}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}C_{i}={\mathcal{X}}
, and
C_{i}\cap C_{j}=\emptyset\;\forall i\neq j
.
Given a Borel measurable space
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we denote by
\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{X}})
the Borel sigma algebra over
{\mathcal{X}}
and by
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
the set of probability distributions on
{\mathcal{X}}
.
For a random variable
{{x}}
taking values in
{\mathcal{X}}
,
{{x}}\sim\mathbb{P}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
represents the probability measure associated to
x
.
For
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Delta^{N}:=\{\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}_{\geq 0}\;:\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}{{{\omega}}^{(i)}}=1\}
is the
N
-probability simplex. We denote with
\Delta_{[a,b]}^{N}:=\{\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}_{\geq 0}\;:\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}{{{\omega}}^{(i)}}=1,\;a^{(i)}\leq{{{\omega}}^{(i)}}\leq b^{(i)}\}
the box-constrained probability simplex. A discrete probability distribution
\mathbb{D}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
is defined as
\mathbb{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{{{\pi}}^{(i)}}\delta_{{{c}}_{i}}
, where
\delta_{{c}}
is the Dirac delta function centered at location
{{c}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
\pi\in\Delta^{N}
, and

. Finally, given

each defined in a probability space

, where

N

2.2.
Wasserstein Distance

Let
\rho\geq 1
, and
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
,
\left\lVert.\right\rVert
represent the
L_{m}
-norm. Further, define
{\mathcal{P}}_{\rho}({\mathcal{X}})
as the set of probability distributions with finite
\rho
-th moments, i.e. all
\mathbb{P}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
are such that
\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{\rho}d\mathbb{P}(x)<\infty
. Then, for
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\rho}({\mathcal{X}})
the
\rho
-Wasserstein distance
{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}
between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
is defined as

{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})={\mathcal{T}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})^{\frac{1}{\rho}},

(1)

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}\int_{{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}}}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{\rho}d\gamma(x,y),

(2)

and
\Gamma(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\subset{\mathcal{P}}_{\rho}({\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{X}})
represents the set of joint probability distributions with given marginals
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
(also known as
couplings
between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
), i.e., for all
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
,

\displaystyle\gamma(A\times{\mathcal{X}})=\mathbb{P}(A),\;\gamma({\mathcal{X}}\times A)=\mathbb{Q}(A)\qquad\forall A\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{X}}).

While computing
{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
is generally infeasible, when both distributions are discrete,
{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
can be computed as the solution of a finite linear program (LP)
[
31
]
. In particular, for
\mathbb{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}{{{\omega}}^{(i)}}\delta_{c_{i}}
and
\mathbb{Q}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}{{{\pi}}^{(i)}}\delta_{z_{i}}
, we have that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\gamma\geq 0}\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}\left\lVert c_{i}-z_{j}\right\rVert^{\rho}{{{\gamma}}^{(i,j)}}

(3)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{s.t. }\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\gamma\bar{\mathbbm{1}}=\omega\\
\gamma^{\top}\bar{\mathbbm{1}}=\pi\end{array}\right.

which is a linear program in
M^{2}
variables.

3.
Theoretical Results

We start by introducing the theoretical results enabling our framework to efficiently bound the Wasserstein distance between an unknown distribution and one built from data. Then, in Section
4
, we demonstrate how these results can be combined with intelligent clustering algorithms to develop an algorithmic framework for efficiently learning a distribution from data with bounds on the Wasserstein distance.

3.1.
A-Posteriori Probability Bounds

We consider a vector of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples
\mathcal{D}_{N}=({{x}}_{1},...,{{x}}_{N})
of size
N
from an unknown distribution
\mathbb{P}
with support contained in a bounded set
{\mathcal{X}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Our goal is to employ the samples to approximate
\mathbb{P}
with a discrete distribution
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
with error bounds on the
\rho
-Wasserstein distance. To do so, given a partition of
{\mathcal{X}}
into
M
sets
\big\{C_{1},...,C_{M}\big\}
and representative points
\{{{c}}_{1},\ldots,{{c}}_{M}\}
with
{{c}}_{i}\in C_{i}
, we define the following clusterized empirical distribution, where each of the
N
samples is assigned to its corresponding region, that is, for
\pi^{(i)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}}_{C_{i}}({{x}}_{n})
we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{P}}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\pi^{(i)}\delta_{c_{i}},

(5)

where
\delta_{c_{i}}
is the Dirac delta distribution centered in
c_{i}
and we call
\pi=(\pi^{(1)},...,\pi^{(M)})
. Intuitively,
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
is a generalization of the standard empirical distribution
[
17
]
, where samples are clustered per region.
We should already stress that our results remain valid independently of
M
and the choice of sets
\big\{C_{1},...,C_{M}\big\}
and points
\{{{c}}_{1},\ldots,{{c}}_{M}\}
. Nevertheless, in Section
4
, we will devise an algorithmic framework that relies on the results of this Section to automatically select regions
\{C_{1},...,C_{M}\}
and representative points
\{{{c}}_{1},\ldots,{{c}}_{M}\}
to minimize
{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
.

Existing results to bound
{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
with high confidence rely on worst-case reasoning to derive uniform (tight) bounds that only depend on
N
,
d,
and
\mathcal{X}
(or alternatively on some assumptions on the moments of
\mathbb{P}
)
[
17
,
41
]
. In what follows, we propose an alternative approach: we bound
{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
with a high confidence that depends on the collected data. That is, we trade the fact that the confidence can only be computed after the data have been observed, to obtain tighter bounds. Our reasoning is based on the fact that
the error to quantize a distribution
\mathbb{P}
into a discrete distribution is highly dependent on
\mathbb{P}
itself
[
12
]
. For instance, a distribution close to a delta Dirac intuitively requires less data to be approximated with a certain error than a uniform distribution over a large support, and, while we cannot generally make this assumption a priori, we can rely on data to automatically rule out worst-case distributions that would need to instead be taken into account without additional information. Theorem
3.1
below formalizes this reasoning by combining recent advances in optimal transport and stochastic optimization with Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals built from data.

Theorem 3.1
.

Given a confidence
\beta>0
and a partition
\big\{C_{1},...,C_{M}\big\}
with representative points
\{{{c}}_{1},\ldots,{{c}}_{M}\}
, associate to each set
C_{i}
parameters
{p_{\ell}^{(i)}},{p_{u}^{(i)}}
, where
{p_{\ell}^{(i)}}=0
if
\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}}_{C_{i}}({{x}}_{n})=0
, otherwise
{p_{\ell}^{(i)}}
is such that

\frac{\beta}{2M}=\sum_{j=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}}_{C_{i}}(x_{n})}^{N}\binom{N}{j}({p_{\ell}^{(i)}})^{j}(1-{p_{\ell}^{(i)}})^{N-j},

(6)

and
{p_{u}^{(i)}}=1
if
\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}}_{C_{i}}(x_{n})=1
, otherwise
{p_{u}^{(i)}}
is such that

\frac{\beta}{2M}=\sum_{j=0}^{\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbbm{1}}_{C_{i}}({{x}}_{n})}\binom{N}{j}({p_{u}^{(i)}})^{j}(1-{p_{u}^{(i)}})^{N-j}.

(7)

Then, it holds that
{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})\leq\epsilon(\mathcal{D}_{N})
with probability at least
1-\beta
,
where
\epsilon(\mathcal{D}_{N})
is the solution of the following mixed integer linear problem (MILP):

\displaystyle\epsilon(\mathcal{D}_{N})^{\rho}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\Delta_{[p_{\ell},p_{u}]}^{M},\\
\gamma\in\Delta^{M\times M}\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\max_{x\in C_{i}}\left\lVert x-c_{j}\right\rVert^{\rho}{{{\gamma}}^{(i,j)}}

(8)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{s.t. }\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\gamma\bar{\mathbbm{1}}=\omega\\
\gamma^{\top}\bar{\mathbbm{1}}=\pi\\
{{{\gamma}}^{(i,i)}}\geq\min\bigl\{{{{\omega}}^{(i)}},{{{\pi}}^{(i)}}\bigr\},\quad\forall i\in[M]\end{array}\right.

In Theorem
3.1
, we first use the data to restrict with confidence
1-\beta
to the distributions that assign mass within
[{p_{\ell}^{(i)}},{p_{u}^{(i)}}]
to
C_{i}
for any
i\in\{1,...,M\}
. Then, within this set of distributions, we seek the one that maximizes the Wasserstein distance from
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
. Unfortunately, as
\mathbb{P}
is unknown, computing the optimal transport plan that would lead to true Wasserstein distance is infeasible. Furthermore, even if
\mathbb{P}
were known, in general, computing exactly
{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
would lead to an infinite-dimensional optimization problem. To obtain a tractable problem (a MILP) that can be efficiently solved with existing tools, e.g., Gurobi
[
22
]
, instead, we relax the transport plan to a sub-optimal one that is constrained to keep as much mass as possible in the same region (i.e., condition
{{{\gamma}}^{(i,i)}}\geq\min\{{{{\omega}}^{(i)}},{{{\pi}}^{(i)}}\}
), where the transport cost is minimized. We should also stress that the confidence in Theorem
3.1
is with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{N}
, the product measure of
\mathbb{P}

N
times, that is, the probability induced by the sample vector
\mathcal{D}_{N}
. A detailed proof can be found in Section
7.6
and for further details, as well as an explicit proof of the measurability of the event
\{{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})\leq\epsilon(\mathcal{D}_{N})\}
, we refer the interested reader to Section
7.6.3
.

Note that, as detailed in the proof of Proposition
7.1
in the Appendix, the MILP in (
8
) arises as a relaxation of maximin linear program and by fixing a transport plan that is optimal only in the diagonal elements (i.e., in the
{{{\gamma}}^{(i,i)}}
). In practice, one could try to fix a transport plan also for the non-diagonal elements of
\gamma,
instead than considering a worst-case approach as in Theorem
3.1
. However, because of the supremum over
\omega
, this would generally require non-linear normalization coefficients that would greatly increase the computational complexity of the resulting problem. Another alternative could be to restrict
\{C_{1},...,C_{M}\}
to be a dyadic partition and then rely on existing results for this setting (e.g., Proposition 1 in
41
). However, as we will discuss in more detail in Section
6
, this restriction would limit the flexibility of the resulting framework.

Remark 1

(Comparison with existing literature)
.

We would like to remark that Theorem
3.1
should not be considered as a replacement for the convergence rates provided in
[
17
]
or
[
41
]
, which have been shown to be tight. In fact, our bound serves a different purpose: by providing error bounds that are a function of the data, Theorem
3.1
aims to obtain tighter non-asymptotic bounds for a specific given problem. This is substantially different from the approach and problem taken in
[
17
,
41
]
, where the resulting convergence rates are valid for any distribution with a specific support size. Consequently, it is natural that by focusing on any possible distribution, the resulting bounds (on average) will be worse than those in Theorem
3.1
, where the information given by the specific data is exploited to narrow the set of distributions that must be considered in the bounds by solving a set of optimization problems. In Section
5
, we will present an empirical comparison of these results with our framework.

3.2.
Computational Complexity and Further Relaxations

At first glance, the reader may find Theorem
3.1
computationally expensive due to the need to solve a MILP to compute
\epsilon(\mathcal{D}_{N})
. However, we would like to remark that the MILP in (
8
) has a complexity that only depends on
M
, the support of
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
, which is generally much smaller than
N
. In practice, even if solving a MILP has a worst case theoretical complexity that is exponential in the number of integer variables, due to the presence of various heuristics for this class of problems, solving (
8
) using state-of-the-art tools such as Gurobi
[
22
]
and a common machine can be generally performed in a few seconds for
M<200
(a value of
M
that generally suffices for obtaining relatively small errors with our framework, as we will show in Section
5
).
To further improve scalability and remove the worst case exponential complexity in
M
, in Proposition

3.2
below we show that (
8
) can be relaxed into a MILP with only one integer independently of
M
and
N
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Consider the setting in Theorem
3.1
. Let
v=\arg\min_{z\in\text{Vert}\big(\Delta_{[p_{\ell},p_{u}]}^{M}\big)}\left\lVert z-\pi\right\rVert
be the closest vertex of set
\Delta_{[p_{\ell},p_{u}]}^{M}
to
\pi
.
Denote
I,J\subset[M]
as the sets of indexes such that
{{{v}}^{(i)}}={p_{\ell}^{(i)}}
for
i\in I
, and
{{{v}}^{(j)}}={p_{u}^{(j)}}
for
j\in J
. Then, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\sum_{i=1}^{M}{{{v}}^{(i)}}\delta_{c_{i}},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})+\xi(\mathcal{D}_{N}),

(11)

with probability at least
1-\beta
, where
\xi(\mathcal{D}_{N})
is the solution to the following MILP with one integer variable:

\displaystyle\xi(\mathcal{D}_{N})^{\rho}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\in\Delta_{[p_{\ell},p_{u}]}^{M},\\
\gamma\in\Delta^{M\times M}\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\max_{x\in C_{i}}\left\lVert x-c_{j}\right\rVert^{\rho}{{{\gamma}}^{(i,j)}}

(12)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{s.t. }\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\gamma\bar{\mathbbm{1}}=\omega\\
\gamma^{\top}\bar{\mathbbm{1}}=v\\
{{{\gamma}}^{(i,i)}}\geq{{{v}}^{(i)}},\quad\forall i\in I\\
{{{\gamma}}^{(j,j)}}\geq{{{\omega}}^{(j)}},\quad\forall j\in J\\
{{{\gamma}}^{(f,f)}}\geq\min\bigl\{{{{\omega}}^{(f)}},{{{v}}^{(f)}}\bigr\}\text{ for the free index }f=[M]\setminus(I\cup J)\end{array}\right.

A detailed proof of Proposition
3.2
can be found in Section
7.6
, where we rely on the triangle inequality with the distribution
\sum_{i=1}^{M}{{{v}}^{(i)}}\delta_{c_{i}}
to be able to reduce the number of integer variables in the resulting optimization problem for
\xi(\mathcal{D}_{N})
, while limiting the conservativeness introduced. In fact, Proposition
3.2
allows one to relax the MILP in (
8
) into a LP with
M^{2}
variables (needed to compute
{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\sum_{i=1}^{M}{{{v}}^{(i)}}\delta_{c_{i}},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
, see Section
2.2
) and an MILP with
1
integer variable and
M^{2}+M
continuous variables. As we will show in Section
5
, this has the effect of greatly improve the computational efficiency at the cost of slightly more conservative bounds.

Remark 2

(Finding the vertex
v
)
.

Proposition
3.2
is stated for a specific
v\in\text{Vert}\big(\Delta_{[p_{\ell},p_{u}]}^{M}\big)
, the one that minimizes the distance to
\pi
. However, we should stress that Proposition
3.2
would hold for any other vertex of
\Delta_{[p_{\ell},p_{u}]}^{M}
. Yet, choosing
v=\arg\min_{z\in\text{Vert}\big(\Delta_{[p_{\ell},p_{u}]}^{M}\big)}\left\lVert z-\pi\right\rVert
yields a good performance in practice. In fact, not only having
v
close to
\pi
helps reducing the conservativeness of the problem, but finding
v=\arg\min_{z\in\text{Vert}\big(\Delta_{[p_{\ell},p_{u}]}^{M}\big)}\left\lVert z-\pi\right\rVert
can be done particularly efficiently via a sorting algorithm. In fact, as minimizing
\left\lVert z-\pi\right\rVert
is equivalent to minimizing
\left\lVert z-\pi\right\rVert^{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}|{{{z}}^{(i)}}-{{{\pi}}^{(i)}}|^{m}
, we can first start choosing
{{{v}}^{(i)}}={p_{\ell}^{(i)}}
, sort indexes by the gap
|{{{\pi}}^{(i)}}-{p_{u}^{(i)}}|^{m}-|{{{\pi}}^{(i)}}-{p_{\ell}^{(i)}}|^{m}
ascendingly, and then progressively increase
{{{v}}^{(i)}}
(capping at
{p_{\ell}^{(i)}}
) for the sorted indexes until the condition
\sum_{i=1}^{M}{{{v}}^{(i)}}=1
is met. Intuitively, this algorithm fills up the probability mass in the coordinates where the distance to the upper bound is smaller than to the lower bound.

5.2.1.
On the Effect of the Variance

We consider
\mathbb{P}
to be an isotropic Gaussian distribution and analyze the effect of the variance of the underlying distribution
\mathbb{P}
on the resulting bounds. This provides a controlled setting to isolate how the concentration of probability mass influences the performance of the proposed data-driven bounds.
Notably, the Fournier bounds are agnostic to
\mathbb{P}
and depend only on the number of samples
N
and the ambient dimension, whereas our approach explicitly adapts to the empirical distribution of the data.
As a consequence, our method particularly benefits from low-variance settings, where most of the probability mass is concentrated in a small region of the support. In this regime, the partitioning strategy yields regions with small radii, leading to substantially less conservative bounds.
This behavior is illustrated in Figure
4

In particular, the bound decreases rapidly in the low-

, which results from the shrinking confidence intervals associated with each partition element, is analyzed in detail in Appendix

3.3
, to benefit from a larger support

5.2.2.
On the Effect of the Dimensionality

We next investigate the effect of the dimension on the relative performance of the proposed bounds and the Fournier bounds. Figure
5

Despite the unfavorable comparison setup (the

Furthermore, we should stress that to expose the relative dimensional trend, we consider Gaussian distributions with a non-small standard deviation. For smaller standard deviations, as illustrated in the previous section, the ratio would be even smaller already for small

We note that the ratio between the proposed bounds and the Fournier bounds remains below

\mathbb{P}

6.
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a data-driven framework for learning probability distributions from a finite amount of data. Given i.i.d. observations from an underlying distribution
\mathbb{P}
, our approach relies on clustering algorithm and optimal transport to construct a discrete approximation
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
whose
\rho
-Wasserstein distance to
\mathbb{P}
can be bounded with high confidence by solving a finite mixed-integer linear optimization problem. Our results show that our approach generally yields substantially less conservative bounds than state-of-the-art methods, while returning distributions of substantially more compact support.

A central objective of our approach was not only to limit the total number of data used, but also to control the support size of the resulting
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
. In fact, this is often critical to guarantee the tractability of downstream computations, such as when the resulting bounds are used in distributionally robust optimization frameworks
[
28
]
or to propagate uncertainty through non-linear functions
[
16
]
. To this end, we employed a modified version of Lloyd’s algorithm to find our partition of
{\mathcal{X}}
. An alternative would have been to instead rely on a dyadic partition of a set containing most of the probability mass of
\mathbb{P}
(for instance, the complement of set
C_{M}
introduced in Section
4
). Such a strategy would allow us to leverage known Wasserstein distance bounds for dyadic optimal transport (see, e.g., Proposition 1 in
41
). However, this would also require the construction of a hierarchical partition in cells of decreasing diameter, typically resulting in substantially more than
M
regions for the error levels considered in our experiments. Nevertheless, we see combining our results with existing results for hierarchical optimal transport as an exciting research direction.

This work also opens several additional promising research directions. First, the bound in Theorem
3.1
may be significantly tightened by directly solving the min-max optimization problem appearing in (
25
), rather than the relaxation considered in this paper. While this may increase the resulting computational complexity, tailored approaches to solve directly this problem may be beneficial. Second, while in this work we assumed a bounded support; it remains an interesting future work to extend our results for unbounded (or completely unknown) support. Furthermore, while our approach is tailored for Wasserstein distances, it can also be in principle extended to other optimal transport distances, such as Sinkhorn Divergences
[
19
]
. We believe it is an interesting research direction to explore how our data-driven framework can be combined with existing algorithms for these distances. Nevertheless, note that in many safety-critical applications, a bound in Wasserstein distance is often preferred, e.g., to bound worst-case probabilities and expected values of functions
[
28

7.2.1.
Approximating the true support of the unknown distribution
\mathbb{P}

As discussed in Section
4
, minimizing
{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
corresponds to choosing regions
\{{\mathcal{C}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}}_{M-1}\}
that form a (Voronoi) partition of the true support of
\mathbb{P}
.
Since the true support is unknown, Algorithm
1
approximates it as the union of
L_{m}
-balls centered at the representative points
\{{{c}}_{1},\ldots,{{c}}_{M-1}\}
, with associated radii
\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{M-1}\}
, denoted by
{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{approx}}
.
The radii are constructed according to the heuristic described in lines 3-5 of Algorithm
1
, where the hyperparameter value
0.05
used in line 4 is selected via a grid search over a range of settings and fixed a priori for all experiments reported in this work.
To assess whether this approximation adequately covers the true support, we measure the fraction of
N
samples drawn independently of those used to construct
{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{approx}}
that are contained in the uncovered region
{\mathcal{X}}\setminus{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{approx}}
.
Figures
2(a)
show that, for both the Gaussian mixture and Uniform settings, no samples are observed in the uncovered region across all considered partition sizes
M
and number of samples
N
.
This observation indicates that the radii
\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{M-1}\}
are chosen sufficiently large to cover the true support of
\mathbb{P}
with high probability.

While coverage of the true support could trivially be guaranteed by setting each
r_{i}
equal to the diameter of the assumed support
{\mathcal{X}}
, yielding
{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{approx}}={\mathcal{X}}
, this choice would be overly conservative. Our objective instead is to learn an approximation of the true support of
\mathbb{P}
, which may be strictly contained in
{\mathcal{X}}
, as illustrated by the 2D Uniform setting in Figure
A1
. A conservative approximation of the support leads to looser upper bounds on
{\mathbb{W}}_{\rho}(\mathbb{P},\widehat{\mathbb{P}})
in Theorem
3.1
, since these bounds depend on terms of the form
\max_{{{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}}\|{{x}}-{{c}}_{j}\|
.
From Figure
A1
, we observe that in the uniform setting
{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{approx}}
tightly encloses the true support of
\mathbb{P}
, with the approximation becoming increasingly tight as
M
grows. For the Gaussian mixture setting shown in Figure
2
, where the true support coincides with the assumed support
{\mathcal{X}}
,
{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{approx}}
instead concentrates on regions of high probability mass. Since any uncovered region is explicitly accounted for through the additional region
{\mathcal{C}}_{M}
, this behavior does not affect correctness and is in fact desirable, as it avoids unnecessarily enlarging the regions
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
in areas with negligible probability mass. Figures
2(c)
quantify these observations by showing that the average radius of the regions
\{{\mathcal{C}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}}_{M-1}\}
decreases as the support size
M
increases.

7.2.2.
Radii of the partition regions

Recall that the regions
\{{\mathcal{C}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{C}}_{M-1}\}
are defined as the Voronoi partition induced by the representative points
\{{{c}}_{1},\ldots,{{c}}_{M-1}\}
on
{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{approx}}
.
Equivalently, these regions can be interpreted as Voronoi cells on
{\mathcal{X}}
, each truncated by an
L_{m}
-ball of radius
r_{i}
centered at
{{c}}_{i}
.
By construction, if all neighboring representative points are closer to
{{c}}_{i}
than
r_{i}
, the truncation is inactive, and the radius of the smallest
\ell_{p}
-ball enclosing
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}

\max_{{{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}}\|{{x}}-{{c}}_{i}\|
, can be strictly smaller than
r_{i}
.
Interestingly, Figures
2(c)
show that, in practice, the values
r_{i}
closely approximate
\max_{{{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}}\|{{x}}-{{c}}_{i}\|
across regions.
We exploit this observation to efficiently bound the cost terms
\max_{{{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}}\|{{x}}-{{c}}_{j}\|
appearing in Theorem
3.1
, whose exact computation becomes prohibitive for large
M
and high dimensions. In particular, by a single application of the triangle inequality,

\max_{{{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}}\|{{x}}-{{c}}_{j}\|\leq\max_{{{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}}\|{{x}}-{{c}}_{i}\|+\|{{c}}_{i}-{{c}}_{j}\|\leq r_{i}+\|{{c}}_{i}-{{c}}_{j}\|.

As suggested by Figures
2(c)

Moreover, the pairwise distances
\|{{c}}_{i}-{{c}}_{j}\|
are obtained directly as a byproduct of the
(M-1)
-means clustering step in Algorithm

Figures
2(d)
further show that, as
M

\|{{c}}_{i}-{{c}}_{j}\|
remain relatively stable, so that our bounds on the terms
\max_{{{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}_{i}}\|{{x}}-{{c}}_{j}\|
diminish due to decreasing radii
r_{i}

.

, while the others via

and

, as in (

) and (
