Major hand replantation at the carpometacarpal level: A case report
Authors: José Gerardo Solano Vázquez, Jaaziel Azareel Aparicio Muñoz, José Maria Zepeda Torres, Yuri Jiménez Caprielova, Kenia Selene Avilés Palomares, Juan Carlos Hernández Torón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor hand replantation at the carpometacarpal level is rare and technically demanding. We report the case of a 69-year-old right-hand-dominant man who sustained traumatic amputation of the right hand while cleaning a metal-cutting guillotine. On admission, he was hemodynamically stable, and physical examination showed complete amputation at the carpometacarpal level with exposed bone and tendons. Radiography confirmed the level of injury. The patient underwent urgent replantation under general anesthesia with Kirschner-wire fixation from the metacarpals to the carpus, arterial anastomosis of the ulnar artery and a radial arterial branch, repair of multiple flexor and extensor tendons, and neurorrhaphy of the median and ulnar nerves. Postoperatively, the replanted hand remained viable, with preserved distal perfusion. The patient developed acute anemia and multifactorial acute kidney injury with concern for ischemia-reperfusion injury, which improved with medical treatment and monitoring. At discharge, the wound was well approximated, distal filling was preserved in all digits, and limited but present motion and partial sensory recovery were noted. Follow-up demonstrated maintained perfusion, stable fixation, and no evidence of infection. This case underscores that carpometacarpal-level hand replantation can achieve successful limb salvage in selected patients when timely multidisciplinary microsurgical management is available.Keywords: Hand replantation, Carpometacarpal level, Traumatic amputation, Limb salvage, Microsurgery

Introduction

Major hand amputation at or just distal to the wrist or carpometacarpal (CMC) level is an uncommon but devastating injury. Epidemiologic data specific to CMC-level amputations are limited, and most available reports group these injuries within hand/wrist or major upper-extremity amputations. In the United States National Trauma Data Bank, 1386 major traumatic upper-extremity amputations were identified from 2009 to 2012, representing 46 per 100,000 trauma admissions; only 14% occurred at the hand level [1]. In Germany, national quality reports identified 329 major traumatic upper-extremity amputations and 87 replantations from 2014 to 2018, corresponding to an overall replantation rate of 26%, with most injuries involving the wrist and forearm [2]. Likewise, in a national analysis of traumatic hand and wrist amputations in the United States, patients were predominantly young men, only 21% underwent replantation, and these procedures were concentrated in urban teaching hospitals [3].

The goal of replantation is not merely survival of the amputated part, but recovery of the greatest possible useful function for the individual patient. Accordingly, decisions at or near the CMC level must integrate hemodynamic stability, mechanism of injury, ischemia time, extent of soft-tissue and neurovascular destruction, contamination, comorbidities, expected rehabilitation burden, and patient priorities [4,5]. In major upper-extremity trauma, preservation of life takes precedence over limb salvage, and primary amputation is favored in the setting of uncontrollable hemodynamic instability, prolonged ischemia with nonviable tissue, or overwhelming combined destruction of bone, vessels, nerves, muscle, and soft tissue [4,5].

Comparative literature suggests that, in severe upper-extremity trauma, limb salvage and amputation may yield broadly comparable functional and psychological outcomes overall, emphasizing that treatment selection should be individualized and aligned with realistic expectations and patient goals [5,6]. Nevertheless, more distal major upper-extremity replants generally achieve better functional recovery and return-to-work rates than more proximal injuries [6]. For this reason, well-documented cases at the wrist/CMC/transmetacarpal level remain clinically relevant, particularly when they illustrate the decision-making, technical demands, and imaging assessment involved in major hand salvage.

Case report

A 69-year-old right-hand-dominant man with no relevant medical history presented approximately 25 minutes after traumatic amputation of the right hand while cleaning a metal-cutting guillotine at home. On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable and fully oriented. Examination showed complete amputation at the carpometacarpal level with active bleeding and exposed bone and tendons (Fig. 1), and anteroposterior radiography confirmed the level of injury (Fig. 2). He subsequently underwent urgent replantation under general anesthesia. The recorded operative time was 6 hours and 50 minutes. Intraoperative preparation included dissection and identification of the relevant structures in the amputated segment and proximal stump (Fig. 3). Reconstruction included skeletal fixation with retrograde Kirschner wires from the first through fifth metacarpals to the carpus (Fig. 4), arterial anastomosis of the ulnar artery and a radial arterial branch, repair of multiple flexor and extensor tendons, and neurorrhaphy of the median and ulnar nerves. Primary repair of the anatomical snuffbox structures was deferred to avoid compression of the radial arterial anastomosis, and secondary reconstruction was performed at a later stage. No intraoperative complications were documented.

During the early postoperative period, the replanted hand remained viable, with preserved color and distal capillary refill. On the first postoperative day, laboratory abnormalities suggested acute blood loss and multifactorial acute kidney injury, with concern for ischemia-reperfusion injury; these findings improved with medical management and monitoring. Postoperative management included close vascular monitoring, serial assessment of capillary refill, maintenance of the hand elevated above heart level, splint protection, and local wound care. At discharge, the patient was instructed to continue splint use, perform daily wound cleansing, maintain the extremity elevated, and attend scheduled outpatient follow-up. At discharge, the wound was well approximated, without infection or dehiscence, and all digits had preserved distal perfusion. Limited but present flexion and extension and partial sensory recovery were noted. No standardized functional outcome measure was available in the medical record; functional assessment was limited to serial clinical examination. At 27-day outpatient follow-up, the hand remained perfused, with preserved distal filling, limited active motion, and a dorsal distal flap eschar in demarcation without evidence of infection (Fig. 5). Follow-up radiography demonstrated maintained alignment and stable Kirschner-wire fixation (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This case illustrates the distinctive challenges of major hand replantation at the CMC level. Mechanism of injury remains one of the strongest predictors of outcome, as sharp guillotine-type amputations generally provide better survival and functional recovery than crush or avulsion injuries because they involve less endothelial damage, less soft-tissue devitalization, and shorter zones of vascular injury [4,7,8]. Ischemia time is similarly critical. Unlike distal digital amputations, major hand amputations carry a greater muscle burden and therefore a narrower margin for delayed reperfusion, increasing the risks of tissue necrosis, reperfusion injury, and systemic complications when revascularization is delayed [4,8,9].

From a technical standpoint, replantation at the CMC/transmetacarpal level is among the most demanding forms of upper-limb salvage. Successful reconstruction requires coordinated restoration of skeletal stability, arterial inflow, venous outflow, neural continuity, tendon balance, and soft-tissue coverage, often during a single prolonged operation [[7], [8], [9]]. Stable fixation provides the framework for vascular and tendon repair, whereas meticulous debridement and anastomosis outside the zone of injury are essential for durable revascularization. Recent technical reports have emphasized the value of tourniquet-assisted microsurgery, aggressive vessel debridement, and liberal use of vein grafts when required [8]. In complex mutilating hand injuries, the reconstructive objective should be a stable, sensate, and functionally useful hand rather than purely anatomic replacement of every injured structure without regard to global function [9].

Expected outcomes after major upper-extremity replantation should be framed in terms of meaningful function rather than simple tissue survival. Distal major replants, including those at the wrist and transmetacarpal level, generally achieve better functional scores and higher return-to-work rates than arm- or elbow-level replantations [6]. However, these benefits are rarely immediate and often depend on prolonged rehabilitation, staged reconstruction, and strong patient adherence to therapy [5,6,10]. In the present case, no validated functional scoring system was available, and early functional assessment was limited to clinical findings, including preserved distal perfusion, limited but present active flexion and extension, and partial sensory recovery. Detailed formal hand therapy records were not available; therefore, rehabilitation data in this report are limited to documented postoperative protection, elevation, wound care, vascular monitoring, and outpatient follow-up instructions. Contemporary discussions of hand salvage have also emphasized that the decision to pursue replantation must account for the burden of secondary procedures, recovery time, and the patient’s expectations for useful hand function [5,10].

The broader significance of this case is also systemic. Major hand replantation requires more than microsurgical expertise alone; it depends on coordinated trauma triage, prompt transport, imaging assessment, operative readiness, postoperative monitoring, and structured rehabilitation. In this context, successful CMC-level salvage reflects both surgical capability and the availability of a multidisciplinary infrastructure capable of supporting time-sensitive complex extremity reconstruction [2,5,10]. Because published evidence at this level remains limited largely to reviews, case reports, and small series, each well-documented case contributes meaningful information regarding patient selection, operative priorities, and the practical boundaries of salvage in major hand trauma [2,6,7].

Conclusion

Major hand replantation at the carpometacarpal level is uncommon, technically demanding, and highly dependent on careful patient and injury selection. When the patient is physiologically stable, ischemia time is acceptable, and the pattern of tissue injury remains reconstructible, replantation may provide substantial limb salvage potential and meaningful long-term function, particularly in distal major injuries managed by experienced microsurgical teams. Nevertheless, the decision to replant must remain individualized and should be based on mechanism of injury, limb viability, anticipated functional recovery, patient goals, and the expected burden of rehabilitation and secondary surgery.
