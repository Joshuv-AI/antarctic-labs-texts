Early budget impact analysis of magnetic balloon technology for facilitating the completion of difficult colonoscopies
Authors: Carla Rognoni, Ludovico Cavallaro, Alessandro Repici, Cesare Hassan, Gaia Pellegatta, Oriana Ciani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColonoscopy is one of the most frequently performed medical procedures worldwide, yet incomplete examinations remain common, leading to higher costs and delay in diagnosis and therapy. When a colonoscopy is not finished, a second procedure is recommended to complete the exploration of the large bowel such as another optical colonoscopy or virtual colonoscopy. Endorail® is a medical device accessory for colonoscopes, based on magnetic balloon technology, developed to facilitate colonoscopy completion. This study reports an early budget impact analysis to quantify the short-term financial impact on hospital and National Healthcare System (NHS) budgets considering the introduction of this innovative technology in the routine clinical practice.MethodsWe estimated the budget impact of using Endorail for the management of colonoscopies that would be declared incomplete according to the standard of care (SoC), in the clinical practice in Italy over the first five years of commercialization. The hospital perspective considered a micro-costing analysis, while the NHS perspective considered the national reimbursement rates for healthcare services and the costs due to possible diagnostic delays in patients with colon cancer.ResultsDiagnostic pathway per-patient costs for Endorail strategy (hospital perspective 252.97 €, NHS perspective 132.75 €) showed slight savings compared to SoC (hospital perspective 255.89 €, NHS perspective 138.63 €). Increasing the use of the device from 2% to 10% of incomplete colonoscopies in five years could lead to savings at national level of 633,449 € and 856,286 €, respectively in the hospital and NHS perspective. Savings occur already during the first year and are mainly due to avoided second procedures. The sensitivity analyses, by varying the model parameters, showed savings at national level up to 1,691,686 €.ConclusionsThe early budget impact analysis indicates that Endorail can be considered a cost-neutral strategy for both hospital and NHS perspectives in Italy. Per-patient differences slightly favor Endorail, and a gradual increase in its use yields modest national savings that begin in the first year. In view of the model’s limitations, further research is required to validate the results and evaluate additional economic, organizational, and environmental effects. Moreover, evaluating patient perspectives and preferences may reveal further benefits of the technology, which is designed to improve both patient outcomes and experience by shortening procedure time, reducing discomfort, and minimizing the need for repeat examinations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-025-00676-y.Keywords: Incomplete colonoscopy, Early budget impact analysis, Magnetic balloon technology, Medical device, Early economic evaluation

Introduction

Colonoscopy is one of the most common medical examinations worldwide, with approximately 50 million procedures performed annually [1]. Although it allows to timely diagnose and treat many clinical conditions, it remains a complex exam whose success is affected by various factors [2], and with significant rates (4%-25%) of incomplete colonoscopies [3]. A colonoscopy can be defined as incomplete when the endoscopist fails to reach the last segment of the colon, called the caecum. Specifically, this occurs when the endoscopist feels that it is no longer useful to continue the procedure, and to make further efforts [4]. The US Task Force on Colorectal Cancer, the American Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy and the American College of Gastroenterology have recommended as an adequate performance indicator in colonoscopy the achievement of a caecum in 90% of cases in diagnostic colonoscopy and 95% in screening colonoscopies [5].

Since neoplasms or other pathologies can develop in any part of the colon, it is essential to complete the colonoscopy and explore all segments. If the caecum is not reached, it is always recommended to complete the examination with a second procedure [6]. Completion is usually performed with virtual colonoscopy or colonoscopy under deep sedation, possibly with a more experienced endoscopist (21,22). Virtual colonoscopy is a non-invasive radiological diagnostic technique, which generates three-dimensional images of the colon, conducted using computed tomography and the introduction of a small rectal probe. Scheduling and performing a complementary examination increases the overall costs for the healthcare system and the patient [7–10].

Therefore, whilst effective as diagnostic and therapeutic tool, colonoscopy remains a complex procedure that requires a long learning curve. In 10–20% of the cases mentioned above, i.e., with difficult caecal intubation, colonoscopy is problematic even for an experienced operator and the risk of not reaching the caecum increases significantly. Most of the technical challenges encountered during colonoscopy are related to loops formation in the sigma and transverse colon due to the presence of a mobile mesentery [11].

Endorail® (CE Mark 2022 – FDA Clearence 2024) is an on-demand accessory for colonoscopes developed to facilitate their progression in the large intestine, thus enabling completion of the procedure. The device comprises a balloon catheter inserted through the colonoscope’s working channel, which is filled with ferromagnetic fluid and then magnetically anchored externally via a handheld device. This system acts as a stabilizing rail, helping straighten loops and guide the scope more effectively through the colon. In 2023 Endorail has been clinically tested in 38 patients with difficult colonoscopies, demonstrating the absence of adverse events and a completion rate of 100% [12, 13].

This medical device is in the early phase of market introduction in Italy, a stage in which generating robust evidence on its budgetary impact is crucial to support evidence-based decisions by healthcare providers, payers, and policymakers, and to help the manufacturer refine its value proposition and market access strategy. This study therefore presents a budget impact analysis (BIA) evaluating the financial implications of using Endorail to complete difficult colonoscopies arising from loop formation, that would be declared incomplete according to the current clinical practice in Italy, vis-à-vis the standard of care (SoC), which is the optical colonoscopy.

The analysis was performed from both the hospital and the National Health Service (NHS) perspectives in Italy to capture the financial implications for providers and payers. The Italian NHS is a tax-funded system that provides universal coverage and is characterized by regional decentralization in financing and service delivery [14]. This means that the central government provides overall stewardship, defines the national benefits package, and allocates resources to the regions, which in turn finance, plan, and deliver services locally. This decentralization creates variation in procurement, provider payment, and service availability across regions. As hospitals and regional payers operate under distinct budgetary frameworks and incentives, adopting both perspectives allows a more comprehensive assessment of the potential budgetary impact of Endorail across different levels of the healthcare system.

Methodology

A BIA was conducted through the design of a research protocol aimed at collecting data on the consumption of healthcare resources by patients undergoing colonoscopy for the two diagnostic strategies described, i.e. Endorail and SoC [15].

The BIA model was developed in Microsoft Excel to estimate the budgetary impact of introducing Endorail, compared with the SoC, from the hospital and NHS perspectives over a five-year time horizon in Italy. The analysis considered the diagnostic pathway of adult patients undergoing colonoscopy in both outpatient and inpatient settings.

From the NHS perspective, costs reflected public-payer outlays. For outpatient visits and diagnostics, national fee schedules were considered, while inpatient stays were valued using national DRG (Diagnosis-Related Group) tariffs, which serve as the reference rates adopted throughout Italy and underpin interregional reimbursement flows. These national tariffs are representative of all regions and ensure standardization in a national NHS perspective. Given their representativeness and the assumption that regional adjustments are largely proportional, region-specific analyses on tariff levels were not performed. Potential heterogeneity unrelated to tariffs (e.g., local procurement discounts) was explored in dedicated scenario analyses. This approach maintains national policy relevance while avoiding unnecessary complexity and preserving interpretability.

From the hospital perspective, costs captured provider resource use via micro-costing analysis, including healthcare personnel time, consumables, drug costs, maintenance, amortization and allocated overheads.

To enable direct comparison, we reported per-patient costs considering the diagnostic pathway for both strategies (Endorail and SoC) under both perspectives (hospital and NHS). To aid interpretation of the budget-impact results, we also modelled small- and large-hospital scenarios, assuming 100 and 1,000 colonoscopies per year, respectively. In both scenarios, we retained the base-case outpatient–inpatient mix of colonoscopies.

Published data report that the second procedure is a deep sedation colonoscopy in 85% of cases, while in the remaining cases it is a virtual colonoscopy [22, 23]. According to a study of 1,545 screening colonoscopies, only 30% of second procedures are performed within the year [24]. In contrast, another study conducted on a smaller sample of patients (n = 100) reports that 71.8% of them repeated the examination within one year of the incomplete procedure [25]. Therefore, the model considered a percentage of 30% in the base scenario and 71.8% in a scenario analysis.

The scenario for Endorail was developed to address difficult colonoscopies, arising from loop formation in the colon, that would be declared incomplete according to the SoC. It is assumed that when used, Endorail always allows the incomplete colonoscopy to be completed, thereby negating the necessity for a second diagnostic procedure. However, this applies exclusively when the primary cause of the incomplete procedure is attributed to loop formation. In cases where inadequate bowel cleansing is deemed the primary cause, Endorail cannot be definitive, mandating the need for a secondary diagnostic procedure. Consequently, in contrast to the earlier percentage of incomplete colonoscopies (7%), this was adjusted to account for the proportion of incomplete procedures stemming from inadequate bowel cleansing, i.e. approximately 2.9% of total colonoscopies [3, 26, 27]. Therefore, Endorail was considered suitable for use in only 4.1% of the total procedures.

For the two diagnostic strategies, the utilisation of healthcare resources was identified for the first procedure (standard colonoscopy or Endorail-assisted colonoscopy) and for the second procedure in terms of time spent by healthcare personnel, materials and drugs used, management, depreciation, and overhead costs.

The use of healthcare resources then concerned the management of possible adverse events following conventional colonoscopies. Their frequencies were obtained from a scientific literature search. The Italian study by Benazzato et al. [28] reports a bleeding frequency of 0.238%, a perforation frequency of 0.068% for the first colonoscopy and 0.369% for the second colonoscopy. In addition, Larsen et al. [29] report a frequency of 0.265% for intestinal infections. For colonoscopies performed with the aid of Endorail, the same complication frequencies of standard colonoscopies were assumed. Finally, the study by Iafrate et al. [30] shows a risk of bowel perforation in the case of virtual colonoscopy of 0.02%, which can occur both at the time of probe introduction and during insufflation. Therefore, the use of healthcare resources then concerned also the management of possible adverse events following conventional colonoscopies. Complication rates are reported in Table 3.

From the hospital perspective, the possible delay in patient discharge caused by incomplete colonoscopy was also considered for the inpatient setting. From the NHS perspective, it was impossible to estimate the additional reimbursement for the same setting because the extension of one day of hospitalisation would only lead to additional reimbursement if the threshold number of days were exceeded (which is difficult to estimate).

A further aspect considered in the BIA concerned the consequences of possible delays in colonoscopy in the case of screening for colon cancer prevention. The National Screening Observatory reports a detection frequency of this type of cancer following colonoscopy of 1.1 cases per 1,000 [21]. As mentioned above, only 30% of second diagnostic procedures following an incomplete colonoscopy are performed in the same year. An Italian study by Ricciardiello et al. [31] showed that delays in screening beyond 4–6 months would significantly increase advanced colon cancer cases and mortality if delays lasted longer than 12 months. Indeed, in the case of a situation without delays, the stage of disease at diagnosis would show 74% of patients as grade I/II and the remaining 26% as grade III/IV, whereas in the case of delays in diagnosis beyond 12 months, the distribution of patients in the two groups would be 67% and 33%, respectively (Table 2). A diagnostic delay in patients with colon cancer may cause a shift of patients to higher severity classes, leading to higher management costs from the NHS perspective.

Cost analysis

Cost of diagnostic procedures – hospital perspective

The consumption of hospital resources for optical colonoscopy or SoC was quantified through a literature search, mainly through the 2006 revision of the tariffs of the endoscopic services by the SIED (Società Italiana Endoscopia Digestiva - Italian Society for Digestive Endoscopy) [32]. The cost determined by the study, through micro-costing analysis, is 148.21 € and includes the following cost items: personnel (77.49 €), materials (16.35 €), maintenance (20.24 €), depreciation (9.42 €) and overheads (24.70 €). This data, updated to the current year, corresponds to a cost of 195.34 €. The latter was also assumed to be the cost of incomplete colonoscopies (see Table 4).

Cost of diagnostic procedures – NHS perspective

From the NHS perspective, the reimbursement tariffs (national values) for outpatient services were considered for the diagnostic procedures: colonoscopy 95.90 € (code 45.23), virtual colonoscopy 158 € (code 88.01.9) (see Table 4). It should be noted that the fee for colonoscopy does not differentiate between using Endorail during a standard colonoscopy and colonoscopy without Endorail.

Management of complications

The model considers the management of severe complications such as bleeding, perforation and intestinal infection following standard colonoscopy and intestinal perforation following virtual colonoscopy. The costs of the management of these complications were derived from the relevant DRG reimbursement rates: the DRGs 452 and 453 for bleeding reported rates of 3,283 € and 1,733 € (average 2,508 €); DRGs 188, 189, 567 and 569 for intestinal perforation, which show rates of 3,215 €, 855 €, 13,712 €, and 13,793 €, respectively (average 7,894 €), and DRG 572 for an intestinal infection, which corresponds to a rate of 3,484 € (see Table 4). Because complication-specific hospital cost data were unavailable, these costs were used for both the hospital and NHS perspectives. The costs of managing complications were weighted according to the frequency of occurrence of these events.

Finally, we inferred from the study by Dimitriou et al. [33] that an incomplete colonoscopy performed in hospitalised patients causes an increase of one day in the hospital stay. The cost of such a day was quantified at 876 € (updated to current year) (see Table 4) [34].

Diagnostic delays

Concerning diagnostic delay in colon cancer, a shift of patients to higher severity classes leads to higher management costs for the NHS. The study by Francisci et al. [35] reports for stage IV patients a cost nearly 70% higher than for stage I and II patients (patients 70–79 years, annual cost stage I/II about 9,600 €, annual cost stage III about 13,500 €, annual cost stage IV about 16,600 €) (see Table 4). In the BIA model, for both perspectives, we assumed a cost for the advanced stage calculated as the average of the costs related to managing stage III and stage IV patients (equal to 15,050 €).

Definition of BIA scenarios

For the BIA, 100% SoC utilisation was assumed in the current scenario as Endoral is a quite new technology. For future scenarios of 1 to 5 years, increasing Endorail utilisation rates of 2%, 4%, 6%, 8% and 10% were considered for incomplete colonoscopies in Italy, as foreseen by the manufacturer. The remaining share not adopting Endorail continues to perform colonoscopies according to the SoC.

Base-case scenario and sensitivity analyses

The BIA results enabled a comparison of the current scenario with future scenarios to assess the impact of increased use of Endorail in clinical practice on the national hospital and NHS budgets.

Results

Hospital perspective

Per-patient costs for the diagnostic pathway (Table 5) were slightly higher for SoC (255.89 €) compared to Endorail strategy (252.97 €). The cost of the first procedure with Endorail is offset by the higher costs for SoC for the management of second procedures, with related complications management and delayed hospitalization discharge.

Discussion

In the dynamic landscape of healthcare, the integration of new technologies into hospital settings requires healthcare facility administrators to make evidence-based decisions that align with their institution’s goals and budgetary constraints. Early health technology assessment (HTA) and early economic evaluations offer a holistic perspective on the potential benefits and challenges associated with new technology adoption. These methodologies aid in identifying the most promising technologies poised to elevate patient care quality, optimize operational workflows, and fortify the long-term viability of the healthcare establishment.

Furthermore, these assessments extend their impact beyond the confines of the healthcare facility. They offer valuable insights to manufacturers, informing their strategic decisions related to R&D allocation, pricing strategies, and effective management of uncertainties.

There have been many published early real-world HTA and economic studies along the innovation process [37–39], but they focused on different innovation stages and contexts than the one presented in this work. Additionally, although early health economic evaluation has been advised for more than 20 years, this is still an area that needs further research [40–42]. This work adds to the literature by demonstrating evidence generation’s value in the early phases of medical device development.

Per-patient costs for the diagnostic pathway for Endorail and SoC showed to be very similar, with only slightly savings for the former. For hospitals, higher index-episode costs (device, set-up, overhead) are partly offset by shorter procedures and fewer repeat colonoscopies. For the NHS, the index tariff is unchanged, and any impact comes from downstream use (re-procedures, management of complications), yielding at most modest savings.

At national level, the study showed that an increased utilization of Endorail in the clinical practice from 2% to 10% in the next five years may lead to possible savings in the budget from the hospital perspective in the range 633,449-1,424,358 €; considering the NHS perspective the possible savings may be in the range 856,286-1,691,686 €. It can be seen that the savings from increased Endorail use are due to the second procedures avoided. Moreover, the higher the rate of incomplete colonoscopies, the better the cost-saving profile for Endorail use.

This study has some limitations that need to be recognized. Endorail has been investigated in clinical trials and no adverse event has been recorded [12, 13]. However, a potential reduction in the frequency of complications related to the procedure has not been considered in this study.

The potential impact of Endorail on waiting lists and hospitalisation times has also been hampered by the absence of specific evidence up to this point. Indeed, waiting lists should be decreased due to fewer additional procedures and shortened turnaround times for difficult colonoscopies. This may lead to an improved diagnostic pathway with likely better patients’ health outcomes.

Moreover, the model did not capture the entire patients care pathway. For example, the management of additional identified polyps has not been considered in the analyses. Indeed, in case of virtual colonoscopy, polyp removal necessitates an additional procedure, whereas it can be performed during the same session when using traditional colonoscopy in conjunction with Endorail. This difference in the management approaches to polyps removal was not considered in the model, anyway this aspect would have improved the cost profile for the innovative procedure.

The model did not differentiate the healthcare personnel costs for complete and incomplete colonoscopies due to the lack of precise information on the duration of the procedures. The duration is highly dependent on the confidence and expertise of the healthcare professionals performing the colonoscopy, therefore it is highly susceptible to variance. A continuous data collection on clinical outcomes and healthcare resource use, including healthcare personnel, will be able to provide more accurate results.

Another important aspect of the limitations section relates to the Italian healthcare system, in which the purchase of devices is decentralised at the level of individual hospitals and local health authorities, or is delegated to regional single purchasing centres. If, on one hand, the model provides results at national level, on the other, it can be customized in order to reflect any regional context to provide appropriate information to the different stakeholders and policymakers.

The study highlights the value of evidence generation and may show useful aspects to inform the design of future clinical trials. In particular, the model provides information on the cost drivers and the possible savings that hospitals and the NHS could obtain by using this innovative technology. It would be interesting in the future to include the patient’s perspective and the measurement of their preferences in order to pursue the value-based healthcare paradigm [43, 44]. This would probably reveal even more favourable results for the technology, as it is designed to guarantee patients shorter and less painful difficult colonoscopies other than avoiding repeating the exam in case the first is incomplete.

Conclusions

Evaluating medical devices is more arduous than pharmaceuticals due to a less developed regulatory evidence framework and potential long-term incremental efficacy improvements [45]. In this sense, the new EU regulatory environment for MDs, disrupted by the MDR and the latest HTA Regulation [46, 47], offers an excellent opportunity to improve the quality of clinical evidence throughout the device’s lifecycle. However, there should also be a continuum of evidence generation for the other dimensions of the HTA spectrum, such as the organisational, and ethical ones. Lastly, this analysis could inform the public about the potential benefits of the newly developed medical devices, as well as the industry and other critical stakeholders like payers and healthcare providers.

Endorail provides significant clinical [13] benefits by improving colonoscopy completion rates, especially for patients at high risk of incomplete procedures or adverse events. Based on this early economic evaluation, Endorail is best characterized as cost-neutral from both hospital and NHS perspectives. Per-patient costs for the diagnostic pathway slightly favor Endorail, and phased uptake is associated with modest, system-level savings beginning in the first year. These findings suggest that Endorail could be adopted with minimal budget impact and may support more efficient use of resources.

Once again, it is of fundamental importance to recognize the multiple limitations of the analytical method, to create room for future research to provide an increasingly more solid evidence basis. In the future, it would be interesting to investigate the cost-effectiveness profile of Endorail toward the SoC by comparing costs to clinical benefits. Additionally, analyses of the organisational, ethical and environmental implications may be carried out. While we prioritized data from Italy in our analysis, it is important to note that clinical care pathways in other countries may differ, potentially requiring adjustments to the model structure and inputs. Applying this framework with country-specific calibration can support informed market access and early adoption decisions for innovative medical devices internationally.
