Sarcopenic obesity definitions and their association with cardiovascular disease and mortality
Authors: Qian Xu, Rong-Fang Shi, Xiu-Juan Yu, Fan Bu, Kuan Li, Zi-Tong Song, Chen Fang, Hui Li, Yu-Fang Pei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcopenic obesity (SO) is a prevalent geriatric syndrome with significant implications for cardiovascular health and survival. However, the absence of a standardised diagnostic consensus hinders risk stratification and targeted management in older adults. We evaluated SO prevalence using the ESPEN-EASO, FNIH, and EWGSOP2 criteria and assessed associations with cardiovascular disease (CVD) and all-cause mortality.MethodsWe analysed data from 121,111 UK Biobank participants aged ≥ 60 years. SO was operationally defined by ESPEN-EASO, FNIH, and EWGSOP2 criteria. We compared the prevalence and agreement among the three definitions. Cox-proportional hazard models were used to investigate the association between the combined categories of sarcopenia and obesity and the incidence of CVD and all-cause mortality.ResultsSO prevalence varied significantly: 2.28% (ESPEN-EASO), 0.28% (FNIH), and 0.01% (EWGSOP2), with poor inter-criteria agreement (kappa < 0.20). Among men, ESPEN-EASO-defined SO showed the strongest associations with incident CVD and all-cause mortality (HR 1.61, 95% CI 1.43–1.81; and HR 1.66, 95% CI 1.45–1.91, respectively). Among women, ESPEN-EASO-defined SO showed the strongest association with all-cause mortality (HR 1.59, 95% CI 1.37–1.83), whereas FNIH-defined SO showed the highest point estimate for incident CVD (HR 1.99, 95% CI 1.40–2.82). The EWGSOP2 criteria identified too few SO cases for robust prognostic assessment.ConclusionSO prevalence and prognostic performance differed substantially across consensus definitions. ESPEN-EASO identified more SO cases and showed more consistent associations with incident CVD and all-cause mortality in older adults. Regardless of definition, SO was associated with increased risks of incident CVD and all-cause mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07433-7.Keywords: Sarcopenic obesity, Diagnostic criteria, Cardiovascular disease, Mortality

Introduction

Sarcopenic obesity (SO), the co-existence of sarcopenia and obesity, is an emerging geriatric syndrome associated with heightened risks of disability, metabolic complications, and mortality [1, 2]. However, the lack of a standardised diagnostic framework for SO hinders its clinical identification, epidemiological comparison, and the development of targeted interventions. A major barrier is the inconsistency in defining its core component, sarcopenia, across major consensus guidelines [3]. A systematic review showed that the prevalence of SO in different studies varies greatly and lacks comparability, which may affect the formulation of prevention and treatment strategies [4].

Recently, the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism and the European Association for the Study of Obesity (ESPEN-EASO) published a consensus statement proposing a diagnostic pathway for SO that recommends weight-adjusted skeletal muscle mass (SMM/W) for assessing low muscle mass in this context [5]. However, other widely used sarcopenia definitions, including those from the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2019 (EWGSOP2) and the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health Biomarkers Consortium Sarcopenia Project (FNIH) Sarcopenia Project, employ different metrics (e.g., appendicular lean mass [ALM]/height2, ALM/body mass index [BMI]) and cut-offs for low muscle mass, and crucially, lack obesity-specific thresholds for older adults [6, 7]. Consequently, it remains uncertain which definition of sarcopenia, when combined with an obesity criterion, yields the most clinically relevant SO classification in older populations.

Although SO is consistently associated with adverse outcomes such as CVD and mortality in older adults [8–10], its prognostic performance likely depends on the diagnostic definition applied. A critical, unanswered question is which operational definition of SO—based on differing sarcopenia consensuses (e.g., ESPEN-EASO, EWGSOP2, FNIH) combined with obesity measures—most accurately identifies older adults at the highest risk for major clinical endpoints. To address this gap, we conducted a comparative study. We hypothesised that SO definitions incorporating adiposity-adjusted or body composition–derived muscle indices (e.g., SMM/W or ALM/BMI) would show stronger prognostic performance for incident CVD and all-cause mortality than definitions based on height-adjusted muscle mass (ALM/height²) in older adults.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank (UKB) is a large prospective cohort study that recruited approximately 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years from 22 centers in England, Wales, and Scotland between 2006 and 2010. At baseline, these participants completed touch-screen questionnaires and physical measurements and provided biological samples, as described elsewhere [11]. The UKB study was ethically approved by the North West Centre for Research Ethics Committee (11/NW/0382), and all participants provided informed consent. This study (application number 41542) was covered by general ethical approval for the UKB cohort.

In this study, after excluding participants of non-European ancestry (n = 46,616), those with missing information on sarcopenia or obesity (n = 11,151), those with missing covariate data (n = 87,969), those aged < 60 years (n = 201,522), and those with prevalent CVD (n = 16,311) or cancer (n = 17,690) at baseline, 121,111 participants were included in the analyses. The detailed flow chart is shown in Figure S1.

Body composition and muscle strength measurements

We measured fat mass percent (FM%) and fat-free mass (FFM) via bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) using a Tanita BC418MA body composition analyzer (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/ukb/ukb/docs/body_composition.pdf). We used BIA because it is a practical, non‑invasive, and cost‑effective method for estimating body composition in large‑scale epidemiological studies such as the UKB, where imaging techniques like dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry (DXA) are not feasible for all participants [12, 13]. We calculated ALM using the equation developed by Dodds et al. al. [14] based on UKB participants (n = 4,350) with total-body DXA scans. The equation estimates the ALM from the appendicular FFM (sum of arms and legs): ALM (kg) = (0.958 × [appendicular FFM (kg)])-(0.166 × G)-0.308, with G taking the value 0 if female and 1 if male. We calculated skeletal muscle mass (SMM) using Janssen’s equation [12]. Handgrip strength (HGS) was measured using a Jamar J00105 hydraulic dynamometer. We used the mean of both hands forsubsequent analysis.

Ascertainment of sarcopenia and obesity

We defined obesity using the ESPEN-EASO recommendation of high FM% (> 31% in men and > 43% in women) [15]. We defined sarcopenia according to three consensus criteria (ESPEN-EASO, FNIH, and EWGSOP2), using the combination of low muscle mass and low muscle strength. For the ESPEN-EASO definition, a two-step diagnostic procedure for SO was followed: initial screening using BMI (≥ 30 kg/m²) or waist circumference (WC; ≥90 cm in men and ≥ 85 cm in women), followed by the application of diagnostic cut-offs only to those who screened positive. In contrast, the EWGSOP2 and FNIH consensus was used to assess muscle mass and function directly in all participants without a screening step. Table 1 provides cut-offs for sarcopenia and SO under each criterion. We then combined the parameters of obesity and sarcopenia to classify all participants into SO, sarcopenia-only, obesity-only, and normal groups.

Ascertainment of covariates and outcomes

We used sociodemographic factors (age, sex, and Townsend Deprivation Index) and lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol intake, and physical activity) collected by standardised questionnaires at baseline as covariates. In brief, the Townsend Deprivation Index is a proxy measure of socioeconomic status based on a combination of the percentage of unemployed people, households without a car, households without home ownership, and households with overcrowding in an area. It is derived from the residential postcode of the participants, with positive values representing lower socioeconomic status [16]. Smoking status was categorized as never, former, and current. Alcohol intake was categorized as daily or almost daily, 1–4 times a week, 1–3 times a month, and special occasions only or never. Physical activity was measured as the sum of metabolic equivalent task (MET) minutes per week for all activities.

The outcomes in this study were incident CVD and all-cause mortality. CVD events were defined by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), including coronary heart disease (I20-I25), heart failure (I50), and stroke (I60-I64). The date and cause of death were obtained from death certificates held by the National Health Service (NHS) Information Centre (England and Wales) and the NHS Central Register of Scotland (Scotland). The date of the first incident of CVD was determined by record linkage to the Health Event Statistics (HES) (England and Wales) and the Scottish Morbidity Record (SMR 01) (Scotland). Detailed information on the linkage can be found at http://content.digital.nhs.uk/services. Mortality data were available until November 30, 2021, and follow-up for death was censored at that date or the date of death recording. Data on CVD events were available until October 31, 2022, and CVD follow-up was censored on that date or the date of the first CVD event, whichever came first.

Statistical analyses

The descriptive characteristics of the baseline population are presented as the means (standard deviations [SDs]) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test (for continuous variables) and the Chi-square test (for categorical variables) were used to compare the differences in characteristics across multiple groups. We used a Venn diagram to show the overlap among different diagnostic criteria and compare the prevalence of SO as defined by different consensuses. Additionally, we used the Kappa statistic to evaluate the level of concordance between these consensus definitions.

The associations between SO and CVD incidence and all-cause mortality were examined using Cox proportional hazards models. Individuals with neither sarcopenia nor obesity (normal) were used as the reference group. Two models were established for each outcome. Specifically, Model 1 was unadjusted, and Model 2 was adjusted for sociodemographic variables (age, TDI) and lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol intake, and physical activity). The results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Schoenfeld residual tests indicated a violation of the proportional hazards assumption for sex. Given this evidence of non-proportional hazards and the well-recognised sex differences in body composition and muscle strength (with sex-specific cut-offs used in the SO definitions), we conducted sex-stratified analyses to obtain clinically interpretable, more robust risk estimates. In addition, the small sample size of SO (n = 15) defined by the EWGSOP2 criteria may have resulted in insufficient statistical power, compromising the reliability of effect estimates, so associations with CVD incidence and all-cause mortality were not assessed.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing), and two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Prevalence and agreement of SO based on different criteria

The three diagnostic criteria yielded markedly different estimates of SO prevalence. The ESPEN-EASO criteria identified the highest prevalence (2.28%), followed by the FNIH criteria (0.28%). The EWGSOP2 criteria resulted in the lowest prevalence (0.012%), identifying a very small subgroup. This extremely low frequency limits the stability and interpretability of EWGSOP2-based risk estimates. Notably, sex-specific patterns differed by criteria: FNIH and EWGSOP2 identified higher prevalence in men, while ESPEN-EASO criteria showed a higher prevalence in women (Table 3). The overlap between SO subjects defined by these three criteria is shown in Figure S2, with less overlap between these three criteria. In addition, we used Cohen’s Kappa to assess the agreement of the different criteria. The agreement between EWGSOP2 and the other two criteria was low (kappa < 0.02) (Table 4). However, given the very small number of EWGSOP2-defined SO cases, these kappa estimates primarily reflect limited overlap driven by sparse classification rather than substantive clinical discordance.

Associations of sarcopenia, obesity, and sarcopenic obesity with CVD

During a median follow-up of 13.60 years (interquartile range: 12.90-14.31), 18,169 (15%) participants developed CVD, including 10,428 (8.61%) with coronary heart disease, 4,671 (3.86%) with heart failure, and 3,070 (2.53%) with stroke. Across most diagnostic definitions, SO, obesity only, and sarcopenia only were each associated with elevated CVD risk compared with the normal group (Supplementary Table 4). In men, SO defined by the ESPEN-EASO criteria was associated with a higher CVD risk (HR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.43–1.81) than either obesity-only or sarcopenia-only. When defined by FNIH criteria, sarcopenia only showed a numerically higher relative risk (HR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.14–2.17) than SO, although confidence intervals were wider (Fig. 1). In women, SO defined by both ESPEN-EASO (HR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.71–2.14) and FNIH (HR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.40–2.82) criteria was associated with increased CVD risk, with point estimates generally exceeding those of obesity-only. FNIH-defined sarcopenia-only in women showed no statistically significant CVD risk association (P = 0.73), likely due to insufficient statistical power from a small sample size (n = 27) (Fig. 2).

Associations of sarcopenia, obesity, and sarcopenic obesity with all-cause mortality

During a median follow-up of 12.8 years, 11,770 participants (9.7%) died. All sarcopenia and obesity combinations were associated with increased all-cause mortality risk relative to the normal group, with notable variability in risk by criterion and sex (Supplementary Table 5). Specifically, only SO defined by ESPEN‑EASO was consistently associated with higher all‑cause mortality in both men (HR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.45–1.91) and women (HR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.37–1.83). FNIH-defined SO showed no significant all-cause mortality risk in either sex. For sarcopenia-only, ESPEN-EASO-defined cases in men had elevated all-cause mortality risk (HR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.27–1.53), while FNIH-defined sarcopenia-only in men showed no significant association (P = 0.17). Among women, sarcopenia-only defined by the ESPEN-EASO criteria was modestly associated with increased mortality risk. Although the EWGSOP2-defined sarcopenia-only group showed a relatively large point estimate, the confidence interval was wide, reflecting limited case numbers; therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

In this large population‑based cohort of older adults, we compared three consensus-based definitions of sarcopenic obesity (SO) and evaluated their associations with incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) and all-cause mortality. We observed substantial heterogeneity in SO prevalence and low agreement across criteria. Importantly, SO defined by the ESPEN-EASO criteria was associated with higher risks of incident CVD and all-cause mortality compared with obesity-alone or sarcopenia-alone.

Our findings indicate that SO, particularly when defined by the ESPEN-EASO criteria, is associated with the highest risk for both incident CVD and all-cause mortality in older adults. One plausible explanation for the stronger associations observed for the ESPEN-EASO definition is its use of SMM/W, which better captures the metabolic imbalance between skeletal muscle and adipose tissue. In obesity, a higher proportion of muscle mass may be required to maintain functional and metabolic equilibrium against excess fat [5]. Skeletal muscle is the principal site of insulin‑mediated glucose disposal, and a relative deficit in muscle mass in the presence of excess adiposity may aggravate systemic insulin resistance [17]. Obesity is also associated with intramuscular lipid accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic low‑grade inflammation, all of which contribute to endothelial impairment and atherosclerosis development [18, 19]. By normalizing muscle mass to total body weight, the ESPEN‑EASO framework may better capture the disproportion between fat and muscle that underlies cardiometabolic vulnerability in older adults.

To date, several studies have applied the ESPEN-EASO consensus to patients with cancer [20], stroke [21], and community-dwelling older adults [22, 23], and the prevalence of SO in these studies ranges from 4% to 9.6%. The prevalence of SO in our study was lower than that in other studies, and this difference may be related to the different ages of the participants, comorbidities, methods of measuring body composition, and cut-offs used to define sarcopenia and obesity in the different studies. The substantial variation in SO prevalence and the low diagnostic agreement between current criteria underscore a critical challenge in geriatric clinical practice: misclassification. Applying criteria with low sensitivity (e.g., EWGSOP2 in obesity) could result in missed opportunities for preventive interventions in individuals at high cardiometabolic risk. Conversely, overly inclusive criteria might lead to unnecessary concern or overtreatment. This diagnostic uncertainty complicates epidemiological comparisons, hampers the development of standardised clinical pathways, and ultimately may delay the establishment of effective, evidence-based management strategies for SO as a key geriatric syndrome.

In our analyses, EWGSOP2 yielded the lowest SO prevalence, which is consistent with prior observations in populations with obesity [23, 24]. This may reflect, at least in part, ALM/ height ², which may not adequately capture pathological muscle loss in the context of high adiposity. Therefore, the EWGSOP2 criteria may have lower sensitivity for identifying SO among older adults with obesity, which may contribute to the failure to identify a sufficient number of high-risk individuals in clinical geriatric practice. Nevertheless, EWGSOP2 was primarily developed to define sarcopenia in broader older populations and remains a widely validated framework for that purpose [25]. Its application in the specific context of SO diagnosis, as highlighted by our and others’ findings, may require careful consideration of its inherent limitations regarding adiposity-adjusted muscle assessment.

This study has several limitations. First, participants were restricted to White European individuals in the UK Biobank, limiting generalizability to other ethnic groups. Second, body composition was assessed via BIA, which, while practical for large-scale studies, is generally considered less precise for quantifying skeletal muscle mass compared to imaging modalities like DXA, computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). This measurement imprecision may contribute to some degree of misclassification. Third, the stratification of analyses by sex, combined with the low prevalence of SO according to certain criteria (particularly FNIH and EWGSOP2), resulted in reduced statistical power in some subgroups. Negative results and comparisons across criteria should be interpreted with caution, and replication in larger studies with an adequate sample size should be emphasized.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated significant heterogeneity among different SO diagnostic criteria, with clear differences in prevalence and risk prediction ability. The ESPEN-EASO criteria based on weight-adjusted muscle mass can more effectively identify SO patients with the highest risk of cardiovascular disease incidence and all-cause mortality. In clinical practice, it is of great value to adopt a more sensitive diagnostic framework for the syndrome, such as ESPEN-EASO, for early screening, risk stratification, and implementation of targeted combined intervention strategies in older adults.
