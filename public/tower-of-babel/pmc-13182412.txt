Video-supported telephone triage in emergency primary healthcare: an observational study from Norway
Authors: Nathalie Sandal, Ingrid Hjulstad Johansen, Magnus Hjortdahl, Valborg Baste, Stine Ness, Erik Zakariassen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo describe how video support is used in telephone triage at Norwegian Local Emergency Medical Communication Centres (LEMCs), including frequency, call duration, patterns of video use and associations with urgency and response assessments.DesignAn observational study using national-level operational data and prospectively collected triage data from the Watchtower Project.SettingNorwegian LEMCs, which provide 24/7 nurse-managed telephone triage within the municipal emergency primary healthcare system, handling a broad range of medical inquiries.Participants79 LEMCs using the Norwegian Air Ambulance video solution in 2024 and six LEMCs participating in the Watchtower Project in 2022–2023.Main outcome measuresFrequency of video use and call duration with and without video support. Background

The pilot introduction of video in 2020 denoted a shift in how triage assessments are conducted within Norway’s Local Emergency Medical Communication Centres (LEMCs), providing visual input to supplement verbal information.1 Telephone contact is often the first point of interaction between the public and prehospital emergency medical services, and structured telephone triage is an integral part of many healthcare systems worldwide to ensure timely access and patient safety.2 3 In Norway, telephone triage is performed both in LEMCs and in regional Emergency Medical Communications Centres (EMCCs). In the LEMCs, telephone triage is most commonly done by registered nurses, but in some LEMCs, it is also done by paramedics, licensed medical students or biomedical scientists4 who assess and prioritise inquiries to determine the appropriate level of care. Until the introduction of video, triage decisions were constrained by the limitations of ‘faceless communication’, in which the absence of visual cues could challenge clinical interpretation.5

When implemented correctly, telephone triage is considered both safe and efficient for managing patient flow, although vigilance is needed for high-risk cases.6 It may contribute to improved resource utilisation and reduce unnecessary visits to primary care clinics and hospitals.2 3 By facilitating timely access and appropriate redirection of patients, telephone triage can also help mitigate overcrowding in acute care settings, an established threat to patient safety due to delays, resource strain, and increased demand for emergency services.7,10

Symptom descriptions and communication can vary widely across age groups, languages, and cultural contexts.5 Such variation adds complexity to clinical assessment and requires operators to adapt their communication strategies to ensure mutual understanding. Several decision-support tools are used in Norwegian LEMCs, most commonly the Norwegian Legevaktindeks (79% of all LEMCs) and the Norwegian Index for Medical Emergency Assistance, which provide guideline-based support for symptom assessment and prioritisation.4 However, their use does not replace clinical judgement, and operators rely largely on their clinical experience and communication skills to navigate uncertainty.11

Operators report difficulties interpreting symptoms based solely on verbal descriptions, particularly in children12 and cases involving skin conditions.13 Video communication may supplement verbal information with visual cues, thereby supporting clinical assessments and decision-making14, reducing uncertainty, and enhancing reassurance among both callers and operators.15

Despite increasing implementation of video in prehospital telephone triage, research remains limited on which symptoms prompt its use and how it affects triage outcomes. Studies from a Danish out-of-hours system suggest that video is typically used in situations where visual assessment is expected to add clinical value.13 16 When the video service within this system was temporarily unavailable, the proportion of patients referred to in-person consultations increased substantially, suggesting that access to video may help reduce unnecessary clinic visits and improve triage efficiency.17

Video use has also raised operational concerns. The Emergency Medical Regulation in Norway requires LEMCs to answer 80% of calls within 2 min,18 yet most LEMCs struggle to meet this target.19 The concern is that video use may further lengthen call durations, thereby increasing the need for resources to maintain response capacity at the LEMCs.20 However, recent evidence suggests that longer calls do not necessarily imply inefficiency. A randomised trial in Denmark found that video transmission added time but improved assessment quality and first-aid performance.21 Likewise, operator feedback from Norwegian LEMCs indicates that video could clarify the clinical situation more efficiently, potentially offsetting the added time.20

The use of video support may have implications for the health services and quality of care, as well as for patient safety. There is a need to provide a foundation of knowledge and understanding of how use of video support influences triage processes, patient outcomes, and resource allocation. The aim of this study is to describe current video use in Norwegian LEMCs, including the frequency and patterns of use, its impact on call duration, and its association with triage decisions.

Methods

This observational study used two data sources: national-level operational data from 79 LEMCs and data from six LEMCs participating in The Watchtower Project.22

Study setting

In Norway, prehospital emergency medical services operate through a two-tiered system comprising LEMCs and EMCCs, both available 24/7/365. In principle, LEMCs should handle urgent but non-life-threatening cases as a part of the municipal emergency primary healthcare service, while EMCCs, operated by regional health authorities, should manage life-threatening emergencies. In practice, both services receive and handle a broad range of calls, including cases outside their primary scope.18 The public can reach the LEMCs by dialling the national number ‘116 117’. Calls are primarily handled by registered nurses, who assess urgency, provide advice, and initiate an appropriate response.18 They refer calls to the EMCC when necessary. In 2024, there were 94 LEMCs covering all of Norway’s 357 municipalities.23

In 2020, video communication was introduced in both LEMCs and EMCCs. Operators initiate a one-way livestream from the caller’s smartphone after obtaining verbal consent. The video is not recorded or stored. No national guidelines regulate video use, and it is applied at the operator’s discretion, based on clinical judgement and local protocols. Most LEMCs have local procedures that discourage video use in high-urgency (acute) cases.

Data sources, collection and variables

National-level data

Aggregated national-level operational data were obtained from Helsetjenestens Driftsorganisasjon for Nødnett HF (HDO), the public service provider responsible for the operation and management of control room solutions connected to the Norwegian critical communication network.24

The dataset included all calls made to 116 117 in 2024 from 79 LEMCs that actively used the video solution developed by The Norwegian Air Ambulance Foundation (NAAF) in collaboration with the Norwegian Directorate of Health.25 Although most LEMCs had access to the NAAF video solution, not all used it during the study period. LEMCs using other video solutions, lacking video functionality or co-located with the EMCCs where LEMC-specific data could not be separated were therefore not included in the dataset.

The data were generated automatically from incoming calls and delivered in an anonymised, aggregated format. The original dataset contained 2 767 894 calls. To ensure inclusion of valid, answered calls with consistent time data, several cleaning steps were applied. Technical duplicates (n=33 063) and unanswered calls (n=475 762) were excluded. Unanswered calls represent calls that were terminated before being answered by an operator, for example, when callers hang up during the waiting time. Calls registered on 31 March and 27 October 2024 (n=16 311) were removed due to inconsistencies in time calculations related to daylight saving time transitions. Finally, 236 calls with negative call durations were excluded due to likely technical logging errors. This resulted in a final dataset of 2 242 522 answered calls.

The dataset included timestamps for call start, answer and end, as well as video start and end if applicable, enabling calculation of total call duration and video duration. All answered calls were included regardless of recorded duration. Each call was identified using a unique call ID. For calls with video, the dataset provided one video start timestamp and one video end timestamp per call.

Watchtower data

The Watchtower Project is a sentinel network established in 2006 to monitor activity in Norwegian emergency primary healthcare services.22 Data relevant to this study were collected over 2 years, from 1 January 2022 to 31 December 2023. During this period, additional registration fields related to video use were added to the routine reporting.

The network comprises seven Watchtowers. One Watchtower (Arendal) was excluded as it had not implemented video functionality during the study period. The six included Watchtowers were Alta, Austevoll, Kvam, Nes, Solør and Tromsø. As of 1 January 2023, the six included Watchtowers served a combined population of 158 907 inhabitants, corresponding to approximately 2.9% of Norway’s total population.26

Following each call, operators registered anonymised data in a secure online database as part of routine Watchtower reporting. Registered variables included video use (yes/no), caller, patient age, gender, time of contact, urgency level and the initiated response. For contacts involving video, additional variables included reason for contact, the initially intended response, and whether video use led to changes in urgency level or response. A detailed description of the variable categories is provided in table 1. Urgency levels follow the triage categories used in Norwegian LEMCs: non-urgent (green), urgent (yellow), and acute (red). ‘Acute’ refers to potentially life-threatening conditions requiring immediate response, whereas ‘urgent’ indicates conditions requiring prompt medical assessment but not immediate emergency response.

The eight most frequent contact reasons were retained as separate categories, and less common reasons were grouped as ‘other’. Response categories were grouped according to resource level: ‘advice/consultation nurse’ (‘operator advice’ + ‘consultation nurse/other’), ‘consultation physician’ (‘consultation physician’ + ‘telephone physician’ + ‘home visit’) and ‘ambulance’ (‘ambulance’ + ‘ambulance and physician’). For analyses of how video influenced triage decisions, two outcome variables were created: change in urgency level and change in response, both coded as binary (change/no change).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in Stata/SE V.18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

National-level data

National-level HDO data are presented with numbers and percentages of video use. Video use was coded as a binary variable (yes/no). The total call duration and video duration were calculated from the timestamps. Skewed data are presented as medians with IQRs. Group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The proportion of call time spent on video was derived by dividing the total video duration by the total call duration.

Watchtower data

Categorical variables were presented using frequencies. Age was presented as a continuous variable (median and IQR) and categorised into predefined groups for the regression analyses. Contacts categorised as ‘not relevant’ or with missing values were excluded.

Associations between contact characteristics and video use were analysed using generalised linear models (GLMs) with a binomial distribution and log link. Changes in urgency level and response after video use were described according to reason for contact. Associations between time of day, age group, gender, caller type and reason for contact and the two binary outcome variables (change in urgency level and change in response) were assessed using GLMs. Results are reported as risk ratios (RR) with 95% CIs and p values.

Results

National-level data

Of 2 242 522 answered calls in 2024, 110 214 (4.9%) involved video support. The median total call duration for all calls was 4:06 min (IQR: 2:22–6:30; range 0:00–81:05). Median call duration with video support was 7:13 min (IQR: 5:09–10:02) compared to those without, which had a median of 3:58 min, (IQR: 2:18–6:16) (p < 0.001).

In calls with video support, the video transmission accounted for 26% of the total call time. Video duration ranged from 0:00 to 77:51 min. The median time from the call was answered to the video link being sent was 2:55 min (IQR: 1:57–4:33). The video started with a median of 15 s (IQR: 0:10–0:26) after the video link was sent. The median video duration was 1:47 min (IQR: 1:05–3:04). Median time from ended video transmission to end of call was 1:20 min (IQR: 0:05–3:05).

Watchtower data

Of the 109 281 telephone contacts during the 2-year study period (2022–2023), 1422 (1.3%) involved video support.

Contact characteristics

The majority of calls with video support took place in the evening (62%). compared with daytime, the likelihood of video use was higher in the evening (RR 2.54, 95% CI 2.24 to 2.89) and at night (RR 1.69, 95% CI 1.42 to 2.02) (table 2).

The age range among the patients was 0 to 109 years. The median age was 33 years (IQR 18–60) for non-video contacts and 17 years (IQR 4–42) for video contacts. Among video contacts, 47% involved children aged 0–15 years. The youngest group (0–4 years) alone accounted for 26% of total video contacts. Compared to those aged 30–59 years, the likelihood of video use was higher among children aged 0–4 and 5–15 years, and lower among older adults aged 60 years and above (table 2).

In contacts without video, patients were the most frequent callers, accounting for 49%, followed by next of kin at 32%. In video contacts, patients accounted for 38%, whereas next of kin accounted for 58% (table 2). The likelihood of video use was higher in calls made by next of kin than by patients.

Most contacts were assessed as non-urgent at the end of the call, independent of video use (table 2). Acute urgency was rare overall and particularly uncommon among video contacts (7% vs 0.7%).

In contacts without video, the most frequently initiated response was ‘consultation physician’ (48%), whereas ‘advice/consultation nurse’ was most common when video support was used (58%) (table 2). Ambulance responses were rare overall and less frequent among video contacts (5% vs 0.5%). Compared with cases ending with ‘consultation physician’, video use was more likely in calls resulting in ‘advice/consultation nurse’.

Video support was mostly used for skin-related issues (65%), including rash/swelling/redness, cuts/wounds/bleeding, and burns/frost/etching (table 3). Injured extremity was the second most frequent reason for contact (10%).

Changes in urgency level after video use

Assessed urgency level changed after video support, with 22% downgrades and 14% upgrades (table 3). Most downgraded cases were reclassified to non-urgent (n=280), while most upgrades resulted in an urgent level (n=144). Among unchanged cases, the majority remained non-urgent (n=507).

Among the eight most frequent reasons for contact, injured extremity and burns/frost/etching showed the highest proportions of upgrades (20% and 19%, respectively) (table 3). Respiration and fever/infection/sepsis had the highest proportions of downgrades (37% and 34%).

Respiration, injured extremity, and cuts/wounds/bleeding had a higher likelihood of changes in urgency level after video support, when compared with rash/swelling/redness (table 4).

Changes in response after video use

A change of response was registered in 31% of video contacts (table 3). Among contacts where ‘advice/consultation nurse’ was the intended response, 24% were instead managed by ‘consultation physician’ after video use (figure 1). Conversely, 34% of those initially assessed as requiring ‘consultation physician’ were subsequently managed with ‘advice/consultation nurse’.

Compared with rash/swelling/redness, injured extremity had a higher likelihood of a change in response (table 5).

Discussion

Summary of results

On a national level, video was used in 4.9% of answered calls and was associated with longer call durations. Video-supported calls lasted nearly twice as long as non-video calls. In the Watchtower data, video was most frequently used when the caller was next of kin, in the evening and for younger patients, particularly children. Video support was also most common when the final urgency level was non-urgent and when the initiated response was advice or consultation by a nurse. The most frequent reasons for contact in video-supported calls were skin-related conditions and injuries. Following video support, operators adjusted the urgency level in 37% of calls and the response in 31%. Changes occurred in both directions, including upgrades and downgrades in urgency and shifts between nurse- and physician-managed responses. The likelihood of changes in urgency and response varied by reason for contact, with some categories showing higher proportions of change.

Strengths and limitations

Combining national and sentinel datasets provided both breadth and detail. The national data offered a comprehensive overview of video use and call duration across most Norwegian LEMCs, although the information was limited to technical variables without demographic or clinical information. All answered calls were included regardless of duration, as even short calls may represent genuine contacts. A sensitivity analysis excluding calls shorter than 30 seconds (3.1% of all calls) showed marginal differences in the results. Very long calls were rare and occurred predominantly during night shifts. As the national dataset contained no information about call content, it was not possible to determine whether these represented complex calls or technical artefacts in the logging system.

Likewise, all recorded video durations were retained. In 3.9% of video contacts, video time exceeded total call time, likely reflecting technical artefacts from sessions not properly terminated. Very short durations may reflect immediate visual confirmation, technical disruption (eg, failed connections or network instability) or user-related issues (eg, difficulties handling the device). As the dataset contained only one video start and one video end timestamp per call, any repeated connection attempts or multiple video sessions were captured as a single continuous interval. Although these artefacts were infrequent and unlikely to influence overall patterns, they illustrate challenges in measuring video activity accurately.

The Watchtower data provided prospectively collected information on contact characteristics, urgency and response, enabling analyses of how video was associated with triage adjustments. These data reflect routine clinical work, reducing the risk of recall bias. The Watchtower dataset represents a subset of Norwegian LEMCs, and the lower proportion of video use observed (1.3% compared with 4.9% nationally) likely reflects differences in data coverage and local context rather than systematic selection or registration bias. We have no reason to suspect that missing registrations or local variation introduced systematic bias.

Triage changes were based on operator-reported assessments rather than objective outcomes, and pre-video assessments were reported retrospectively, which limits the ability to conclude regarding accuracy or clinical safety. However, these data provide insight into how operators reassess cases when visual information becomes available. The option to classify a case as ‘not relevant’ when no pre-video urgency or intended response had been considered reduced the sample size but likely improved validity by avoiding artificially assumed pre-video values.

As some subgroups were small, we limited the regression models of urgency and response to binary outcomes. This approach prioritised robustness over granularity and reduced the risk of overinterpreting sparse data.

Patterns and frequency of video use

Video use in the LEMCs remained limited overall. A Norwegian report from 2022 showed substantial variation in video use between LEMCs, from negligible use to 13% of calls20, suggesting discretionary rather than standardised implementation. Operator preferences, local routines and technical infrastructure are likely to influence use. There are currently no national guidelines in Norway specifying when or how video should be used, and operators rely on clinical judgement. Feasibility also depends on caller consent, smartphone access and network stability.

Despite broad availability, video appears to be applied selectively, mainly when visual information was expected to add clinical value. Nearly two-thirds of video contacts involved skin conditions, and almost half concerned children, which aligns with previous research indicating that visual input is particularly useful in paediatric and dermatological cases where verbal descriptions can be difficult or unreliable.13 27 28

The predominance of child-related contacts likely reflects both clinical uncertainty and parental reassurance needs. Parents and bystanders might also be more willing to accept video and assist in showing symptoms,12 13 27 as reflected by the high proportion of next-of-kin callers in video contacts. Callers often describe video as increasing their sense of safety and making it easier to convey concerns29, indicating that video use not only depends on clinical indication but also on caller characteristics and situational factors. Conversely, the lower use of video among older adults could reflect differences in digital access or familiarity with smartphone technology. In addition, contacts concerning older patients may more often involve institutional caregivers, which may influence the feasibility or perceived relevance of video use.

Video use was more frequent during the evening than the daytime, despite similar call volumes across shifts. This likely reflects the organisation of Norwegian out-of-hours services, which handle a broader range of acute conditions when the surgeries of regular general practitioners are closed.30 31 A Danish study reported a similar pattern but at a higher overall level, with video used in 9.5% of out-of-hours primary care contacts.16 Part of this difference could reflect organisational variation, as the Danish model in that study involves physician-based triage. Even so, the consistency in patterns across systems, using video selectively rather than routinely, suggests that video is applied when operators think it can offer a clear clinical benefit.17

Operational and practical considerations

In our data, video-supported calls were considerably longer than calls without video, which could reduce the LEMCs’ ability to meet the national requirement that 80% of calls should be answered within 2 min.18 Most LEMCs already struggle to reach this target, and any increase in call duration could add to existing capacity pressure. In systems where unanswered calls already represent a recognised operational challenge, increased call duration may also have implications for waiting times and call handling capacity.

Longer duration, however, is not inherently problematic. Prior research suggests that video can both prolong and streamline communication, depending on context.20 21 Video-supported calls may also improve triage accuracy,21 enhance decision-making and resource allocation by accelerating mutual understanding and reducing repeated questioning.28 32 In our study, the median time from the video link being sent to the video starting was only 15 seconds, suggesting that the technical setup itself added little to the overall duration. In some cases, longer duration may also relate to concurrent assessment with another operator or physician, which is not documented in the available data.

Longer calls may reflect case complexity or provision of advice. Video was most often used in low-urgency contacts requiring reassurance, detailed guidance or interpretation of symptoms. More than half of video contacts ended with nurse-managed advice or consultation, whereas physician involvement was more common in non-video calls. Calls ending with telephone advice are often time-consuming even without video.11 33 34 A study from the Danish medical helpline ‘1813’ similarly found that video was perceived as particularly useful in ambiguous cases.27

Overall, these patterns suggest that longer video-supported calls likely reflect case complexity and the types of situations in which video is chosen, rather than inefficiency or technical delay.

Clinical decision-making and the impact of video on triage

Telephone triage in Norwegian LEMCs requires rapid decision-making under uncertainty. Operators must assess a wide range of unsorted problems, from minor ailments to life-threatening emergencies, often with limited information and high workload.11 30 Previous research has shown that uncertainty is particularly pronounced when symptoms are non-specific or difficult to describe verbally, such as in children or skin-related conditions.35,37 These were also the contexts in which video was most frequently used in our study.

Earlier studies have shown that video could enable earlier detection of urgent conditions,38 improve clinical assessment and decision-making39, allow for verification or reconsideration of earlier assumptions39 40, and reduce the need for consultations.17 While visual input does not eliminate uncertainty, it can support more confident and nuanced assessments and facilitate clearer communication with callers.39 41

In our study, video use was followed by changes in urgency in 37% of calls and changes in intended response in 31%. The changes were bidirectional and indicate a recalibration of clinical judgement. Reason for contact was the only factor associated with changes in both urgency and response, suggesting that case characteristics influence how video affects triage decisions. Downgrades were most common in contacts involving fever and respiratory symptoms, whereas upgrades were most common in injuries and burns, indicating that video may reduce reliance on ‘worst-case’ assumptions in ambiguous cases and facilitate escalation when visual findings indicate greater severity.14 21 42

These patterns align with previous qualitative research, in which operators describe video as allowing them to ‘see enough to be sure’, increasing confidence in uncertain cases.14 27 39

Implications and future research

This study provides new insight into how video is currently used in telephone triage in Norwegian LEMCs and its potential role as a clinical tool. The selective nature of video use raises questions for policy and practice. Standardised criteria could promote more consistent use, but overly rigid protocols might reduce flexibility and clinical autonomy. Implementation strategies need to account for capacity, workflow and technical proficiency to ensure sustainable use.

Although video support was associated with substantial changes in urgency and response, this study cannot determine whether these adjustments improved clinical safety or efficiency. Future research should therefore examine the impact of video on patient safety, repeated contacts and service utilisation, ideally through linkage of call data with outcome measures to clarify when video adds value.

The transferability of these findings will depend on differences in triage systems, professional roles and organisational structures. Internationally, telephone triage ranges from non-clinical, protocol-driven models to nurse- or physician-managed systems,43,45 which may shape both the feasibility and the impact of video. Future studies comparing video use across different organisational models could clarify how system design influences when and how video adds clinical value.

Conclusion

Video-supported calls in telephone triage at Norwegian LEMCs were longer than non-video calls, and video was used selectively. Video appeared to influence clinical decision-making, with changes in urgency and response in a substantial share of calls. Future research should clarify when video meaningfully supports triage, including its implications for patient safety, resource use and cost-efficiency.
