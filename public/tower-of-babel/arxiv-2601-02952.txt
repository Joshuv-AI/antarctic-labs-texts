The left-to-right minima basis of the group algebra of the symmetric group (updated version)
Darij Grinberg \addressmark 1, Ekaterina A. Vassilieva \addressmark 2

Abstract
We introduce a new basis of the group algebra of the symmetric group, built using the left-to-right minima sets of permutations. We show that on this basis, the descent algebra acts by triangular operators, thus making it an analogue of a cellular basis. The proof involves Dynkin elements (nested commutators) of the free algebra and their interactions with the 𝐁 \mathbf{B} -basis.

Introduction

For a nonnegative integer

denote the symmetric group on

[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}

\mathbf{k}

over

\mathbf{k}

For every subset

define the element

\mathbf{B}_{I}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in S_{n};\\
\operatorname{Des}(w)\subseteq I\end{subarray}}w\in\mathcal{A}.

The span of the elements

is the classical

introduced by Solomon in
[
7

. We call the family

the

-basis
of
\Sigma_{n}
.
The aim of this paper is to construct and study a new basis

(\beta_{w})_{w\in S_{n}}
of the whole algebra
\mathcal{A}
which is
compatible with the right-action of the descent algebra in the following
sense: there should exist a partial order
\preceq
on
S_{n}
such that
for every
w\in S_{n}
and every

one has

\mathbf{B}_{I}\cdot\beta_{w}\;\in\;\operatorname{span}\{\,\beta_{u}\mid u\preceq w\,\}.

In other words, the action of
\Sigma_{n}
on
\mathcal{A}
should
be represented by triangular matrices on this matrix.
Our basis will furthermore be compatible with a
natural family of right ideals of
\mathcal{A}
generated by the

\mathbf{B}
-basis. In the sequel we introduce a
poset-indexed filtration of
\mathcal{A}
built from the right ideals

\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}\mathcal{A}
(reindexing
\mathbf{B}_{I}
by
compositions), construct our candidate basis, and prove that it is a
basis adapted to the filtration.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2

define the filtration, recall the necessary combinatorics of integer

\mathbf{B}
-basis
action. Section
3
introduces the left-to-right-minima basis

The remaining sections are devoted to the proof and to examples.

A poset-indexed filtration of the group algebra

2.1
Compositions and refinement

A

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{p})
of a nonnegative

Let

Two compositions
\alpha

(of each other) if they satisfy

\widetilde{\alpha}=\widetilde{\beta}
.
Let
\operatorname{Comp}_{n}
be the set of compositions of

A well-known bijection between compositions of
n
and subsets of

[n-1]
sends each composition
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{p})\in\operatorname{Comp}_{n}

\operatorname{Set}(\alpha):=\{\alpha_{1},\ \alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\ \dots,\ \alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{p-1}\}\subseteq[n-1],

and its inverse map sends each subset

to

\operatorname{Comp}(I)=(i_{1}-i_{0},i_{2}-i_{1},\dots,i_{p}-i_{p-1})\in\operatorname{Comp}_{n}
,
where we set
i_{0}:=0
and
i_{p}:=n
.
Accordingly we reindex the
\mathbf{B}
-basis by compositions by
setting

\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}:=\mathbf{B}_{\operatorname{Set}(\alpha)}
for

\alpha\in\operatorname{Comp}_{n}
.

Compositions are partially ordered by refinement:

Definition 1

(refinement)
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
and

\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{p})
be compositions of the same integer

. We say that
\alpha

refines

\beta

\alpha\preceq\beta
, if
\alpha
can be partitioned into

\alpha^{(1)},\dots,\alpha^{(p)}
with

for all

Partitions are also partially ordered by refinement, but of a different kind:

Definition 2

Let

be partitions of

is a

\lambda\preceq_{\pi}\mu

if some anagram of

\mu

(as a composition).

Equivalently, this relation

can be defined as follows:
Two partitions

if and only if there exists a map

\mu_{j}=\sum\lambda_{i}
, where the sum

.

Both relations

to be the
k
subsets

Action of the

If

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}\mathbf{B}_{\beta}=\sum_{M\in\mathbb{N}^{\alpha,\beta}}\mathbf{B}_{\operatorname{read}(M)}.

In particular every composition

If

If

The family

Proposition 1
.

If
\beta,\gamma\in\operatorname{Comp}_{n}
and
\widetilde{\beta}\preceq_{\pi}\widetilde{\gamma}

\mathcal{R}_{\beta}\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}
.

Proof.

Lemma

does not change if we permute

Hence

where

.
Furthermore, these right ideals are stable under the

\Sigma_{n}

Proposition 2
.

For every
\beta\in\operatorname{Comp}_{n}
, the subspace
\mathcal{R}_{\beta}
is a

Right

\alpha\in\operatorname{Comp}_{n}
the product
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}\mathbf{B}_{\beta}
is a

\mathbf{B}_{\gamma}

\mathcal{R}_{\beta}/\sum_{\alpha\prec\beta}\mathcal{R}_{\alpha},

where the sum runs over compositions
\alpha
strictly finer than

\beta
, or, equivalently, over compositions
\alpha
satisfying

(the latter sum has more addends than the former, but

shows that these extra summands

acts on these

Theorem 1
.

Fix
\alpha,\beta\in\operatorname{Comp}_{n}
and let
\eta_{\beta}(\alpha)
be the number

such that for every

Then left multiplication by
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}
acts on the quotient

as

\eta_{\beta}(\alpha)

Proof.

This is an easy corollary of Solomon’s formula (

\eta_{\beta}(\alpha)
are exactly those contributing the

in
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}\mathbf{B}_{\beta}

\mathbf{B}_{\alpha}\mathbf{B}_{\beta}=\eta_{\beta}(\alpha)\mathbf{B}_{\beta}+\left(\text{a sum of }\mathbf{B}_{\alpha^{\prime}}\text{ with }\alpha^{\prime}\prec\beta\right)

3
The left-to-right minima basis of
\mathcal{A}

3.1
Left-to-right minima of a permutation

We begin by recalling a classical statistic on permutations.

Definition 4

(Left-to-right minima)
.

Let
w\in S_{n}
.
The
left-to-right minima
(short:
LRMs
)
of
w
are the entries

\operatorname{LRM}(w):=\Bigl\{\,i\in[n]\ \big|\ \forall\,k<w^{-1}(i),\;\;w(k)>i\Bigr\}.

Thus
i
belongs to
\operatorname{LRM}(w)
if it is smaller than every entry appearing to its left in the one-line notation of
w
.
Clearly,
\operatorname{LRM}(w)
always contains
1
and
w(1)
.
We also define the shifted set

\operatorname{LRM}^{\prime}(w):=\{\,\ell-1\mid\ell\in\operatorname{LRM}(w),\;\ell>1\}\subseteq[n-1],

and finally the associated composition
(see §
2.1
for notations)

\operatorname{cLRM}^{\prime}(w):=\operatorname{Comp}\bigl(\operatorname{LRM}^{\prime}(w)\bigr).

The following easy fact relates the LRMs of a permutation to those of its inverse.

Remark 2
.

For every
w\in S_{n}
, one has

\operatorname{LRM}(w)=w\bigl(\operatorname{LRM}(w^{-1})\bigr)
.

Example 1
.

Lemma
2
says that the LRMs of
w^{-1}
occur at the
LRM positions
of
w
.
For example, if
w=\mathbf{6}7\mathbf{2}49\mathbf{1}853
,
then
\operatorname{LRM}(w)=\{6,2,1\}
,
whereas

w^{-1}=\mathbf{63}948\mathbf{1}275

and
\operatorname{LRM}(w^{-1})=\{6,3,1\}
,
which matches the fact that the LRMs of
w
appear at positions
6,3,1
.

3.2
A new basis of the group algebra

For each permutation
w\in S_{n}
we consider the element

\mathbf{B}_{\operatorname{LRM}^{\prime}(w)}\,w\;\in\;\mathcal{A}.

We shall prove that these vectors form a basis of
\mathcal{A}

The key property is the following triangularity statement.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\leq_{\mathrm{lex}}
denote the lexicographic order relation on permutations seen as their one-line notation. Let

\operatorname{Des}(u)\subseteq\operatorname{LRM}^{\prime}(w)

Proof.

Assume

(otherwise the claim is trivial).
Choose the smallest

(this exists since

).
Then,

Let

be the

\operatorname{LRM}(w)
such that
u(\ell)<\ell
.
We show that

(3)

Suppose on the contrary that

for some

maximal.
Then all

Among these

Because

Corollary 1

For every

\mathbf{B}_{\operatorname{LRM}^{\prime}(w)}\,w=w+\Bigl(\text{a $\mathbf{k}$-linear combination of lexicographically smaller permutations}\Bigr).

Proof.

Expand:

\operatorname{Des}(u)\subseteq\operatorname{LRM}^{\prime}(w)\end{subarray}}u,\qquad\mathbf{B}_{\operatorname{LRM}^{\prime}(w)}\,w=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S_{n};\\
\operatorname{Des}(u)\subseteq\operatorname{LRM}^{\prime}(w)\end{subarray}}uw.

By Lemma

, every summand

∎

Corollary 2
.

The family

\bigl\{\mathbf{B}_{\operatorname{LRM}^{\prime}(w)}\,w\;\big|\;w\in S_{n}\bigr\}

is a basis of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Corollary
1
shows that this family expands unitriangularly in the standard basis
\{w\}_{w\in S_{n}}
.
∎

transformation. Our innovation is
constructing such a basis explicitly, for all commutative rings
\mathbf{k}
,
and obtain a wholly new proof of Corollary
3
.

4
The free algebra and the Dynkin elements

4.1
Definitions

We now introduce the main ingredients in the proof of
Theorem
2
.

Let
W_{n}
be the monoid of all
words
(i.e., finite tuples) with
letters (i.e., entries) in
\left[n\right]
; its multiplication is
concatenation. We denote its generators – i.e., the letters – by
\underline{1},\underline{2},\ldots,\underline{n}
, so as to distinguish them from the
respective numbers. The monoid algebra
F_{n}:=\mathbf{k}\left[W_{n}\right]
of

W_{n}
is the ring of
noncommutative polynomials
(aka the
free
algebra
) in
n
indeterminates

\underline{1},\underline{2},\ldots,\underline{n}
over
\mathbf{k}
.
It is graded by degree (i.e., word length),
and we let
F_{n,k}
denote its
k
-th graded component, with basis given by
the set
W_{n,k}
of length-
k
words. The symmetric group
S_{k}
acts on

W_{n,k}
from the right by permuting the letters:

\left(\underline{w_{1}}\ \underline{w_{2}}\ \cdots\ \underline{w_{k}}\right)\cdot\sigma=\underline{w_{\sigma\left(1\right)}}\ \underline{w_{\sigma\left(2\right)}}\ \cdots\ \underline{w_{\sigma\left(k\right)}}\qquad\text{for }w_{i}\in\left[n\right]\text{ and
}\sigma\in S_{k}.

Extending this action linearly, we obtain a right
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{k}\right]
-action on
\mathbf{k}\left[W_{n,k}\right]=F_{n,k}
.
Two words in

W_{n,k}
are said to be
anagrams
(of each other) if they belong to the
same
S_{k}
-orbit.

There is an injective map
S_{n}\rightarrow W_{n,n}
, sending each permutation

\sigma\in S_{n}
to its one-line notation
\underline{\sigma}:=\underline{\sigma\left(1\right)}\ \underline{\sigma\left(2\right)}\ \cdots\ \underline{\sigma\left(n\right)}
. By linearity, it extends to
an injective
\mathbf{k}
-linear map
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]\rightarrow\mathbf{k}\left[W_{n,n}\right]=F_{n,n}
, which we also write as

a\mapsto\underline{a}
. This map is not a ring morphism, but it respects the
right
\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
-actions, i.e., satisfies

\displaystyle\underline{a}\cdot b=\underline{ab}\qquad\text{for each }a,b\in\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]\text{.}

(4)

A word
w=\left(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}\right)
is said to have

V-shape
if it satisfies

w_{1}>w_{2}>\cdots>w_{i}<w_{i+1}<w_{i+2}<\cdots<w_{k}\qquad\text{ for some }i\in\left[k\right]

(that is, it first decreases, then increases). In this case, the
i
is unique
and is denoted
\operatorname*{val}w
. A permutation
v\in S_{n}
is said to
have
V-shape
on an interval
[i,j]=\left\{i,i+1,\ldots,j\right\}
if the
word
\left(v\left(i\right),v\left(i+1\right),\ldots,v\left(j\right)\right)
has V-shape.

When
a,b\in F_{n}
, we write
\left[a,b\right]
for the
commutator

ab-ba
.

The crucial notion to us is the following:

Definition 5
.

For any nonempty subset
S
of
\left[n\right]
, we
define the
Dynkin element
(or
nested commutator
)

V^{S}:=\left[\left[\cdots\left[\left[\underline{s_{1}},\ \underline{s_{2}}\right],\ \underline{s_{3}}\right],\ \ldots\right],\ \underline{s_{k}}\right],

where
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k}
are the elements of
S
in increasing order.
(If
k=1
, then this means just
\underline{s_{1}}
.)

Example 2
.

We have

\displaystyle V^{\left\{2,6,7\right\}}

\displaystyle=\left[\left[\underline{2},\ \underline{6}\right],\ \underline{7}\right]=\underline{2}\ \underline{6}\ \underline{7}-\underline{6}\ \underline{2}\ \underline{7}-\underline{7}\ \underline{2}\ \underline{6}+\underline{7}\ \underline{6}\ \underline{2};

\displaystyle V^{\left\{1,4\right\}}

\displaystyle=\left[\underline{1},\ \underline{4}\right]=\underline{1}\ \underline{4}-\underline{4}\ \underline{1};\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ V^{\left\{3\right\}}=\underline{3}.

The letter
V
is chosen because of the following simple formula:

Proposition 3
.

Let
S
be a nonempty subset of
\left[n\right]
.
Then,

V^{S}=\sum\left(-1\right)^{\operatorname*{val}w-1}w,

where the sum ranges over all words
w
that contain each element of
S
exactly
once (and no other letters) and have V-shape.

Proof.

Easy induction on
\left|S\right|
, since
V^{S}=\left[V^{S\setminus\left\{\max S\right\}},\ \underline{\max S}\right]
for

\left|S\right|>1
.
∎

Dynkin elements are fundamental to the study of the free Lie algebra; see,
e.g.,
[
5
, (8.4.2)]
. We shall have more use for their
products in
F_{n}
.

Definition 6
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{p})\in\operatorname{Comp}(n)
be a composition of
n
. Then, we set

\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}=V^{\operatorname{Set}(\alpha)_{1}}V^{\operatorname{Set}(\alpha)_{2}}\cdots V^{\operatorname{Set}(\alpha)_{p}}.

Example 3
.

For the compositions
(2,1)
and
(2,2)
we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{(2,1)}

\displaystyle=V^{\left\{1,2\right\}}V^{\left\{3\right\}}=\left(\underline{1}\ \underline{2}-\underline{2}\ \underline{1}\right)\ \underline{3}=\underline{1}\ \underline{2}\ \underline{3}-\underline{2}\ \underline{1}\ \underline{3};

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{(2,2)}

\displaystyle=V^{\left\{1,2\right\}}V^{\left\{3,4\right\}}=\left(\underline{1}\ \underline{2}-\underline{2}\ \underline{1}\right)\left(\underline{3}\ \underline{4}-\underline{4}\ \underline{3}\right)=\underline{1}\ \underline{2}\ \underline{3}\ \underline{4}-\underline{1}\ \underline{2}\ \underline{4}\ \underline{3}-\underline{2}\ \underline{1}\ \underline{3}\ \underline{4}+\underline{2}\ \underline{1}\ \underline{4}\ \underline{3}.

4.2
Action on the
\mathbf{B}
-basis

The Dynkin elements
\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}
are themselves images of certain
elements of the descent algebra
\Sigma_{n}
under the injection

\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]\rightarrow\mathbf{k}\left[W_{n,n}\right]=F_{n,n},\ a\mapsto\underline{a}
, namely of the “
power sums of
the first kind
”
\Psi_{\alpha}
studied in
[
3
, §5]
. However, we will use another of their properties
– namely, a formula
for the action of
\mathbf{B}_{\beta}\in\mathbf{k}\left[S_{n}\right]
on
the
\mathbf{V}_{\alpha}
, which appears as Lemma 9.33 in

[
5
]
:

Theorem 3

(Lemma 9.33 of
[
5
]
)
.

Let
\beta=\left(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{p}\right)

and
\gamma=\left(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{q}\right)

be two compositions of
n
.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{\beta,\gamma}
be the set of all ordered set partitions

T=(T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{q})
of the index set
[p]=\left\{1,2,\ldots,p\right\}
into
q
blocks that satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in T_{j}}\beta_{i}=\gamma_{j}\quad\text{for all }j.

(5)

For each block
T_{j}
of

T
, let
V^{!T_{j}}
denote the product
of the Dynkin elements
V^{\operatorname{Set}(\beta)_{i}}
with
i\in T_{j}

(in the order of increasing
i
). Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\beta}\,\mathbf{B}_{\gamma}=\sum_{T=\left(T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{q}\right)\in\mathcal{L}_{\beta,\gamma}}V^{!T_{1}}V^{!T_{2}}\cdots V^{!T_{q}}.

(6)

Remark 4
.

Reutenauer’s
[
5
, Lemma 9.33]

is even more general: He views
F_{n}
as the tensor algebra
T\left(V\right)

of the free
\mathbf{k}
-module
V=\mathbf{k}^{n}
with basis
\left(\underline{1},\underline{2},\ldots,\underline{n}\right)
.
This equips
F_{n}
with a Hopf algebra structure: that of the tensor
Hopf algebra
T\left(V\right)
.
All Dynkin elements
V^{S}
are primitive elements of
T\left(V\right)
,
as can be easily proved by induction on
\left|S\right|
.

Let
\gamma=\left(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{q}\right)
be a composition of
n
, and
let
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{k}
be
k
homogeneous primitive
elements of
T\left(V\right)
with total degree

\deg P_{1}+\deg P_{2}+\cdots+\deg P_{k}=n
.

Set
P_{W}:=P_{w_{1}}P_{w_{2}}\cdots P_{w_{m}}
for any subset
W=\left\{w_{1}<w_{2}<\cdots<w_{m}\right\}
of
[k]
.

Then, the right action of the symmetric group
S_{n}
on
F_{n,n}

satisfies

\left(P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}\right)\mathbf{B}_{\gamma}=\sum P_{T_{1}}P_{T_{2}}\cdots P_{T_{q}},

where the sum ranges over all ordered set partitions
\left(T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{q}\right)
of
[k]
such that
\deg P_{T_{i}}=\gamma_{i}
for
each
i
.

This can be proved slickly using Hopf algebra theory; here is an outline
of the proof:

1.

First, observe that the action
of
\mathbf{B}_{\gamma}
on the
n
-th degree component of
T\left(V\right)
is the Hopf-algebraic operator
m^{[q]}\circ P_{\gamma}\circ\Delta^{[q]}

(where
\Delta^{[q]}:T\left(V\right)\to T\left(V\right)^{\otimes q}

is the
q
-fold comultiplication,

m^{[q]}:T\left(V\right)^{\otimes q}\to T\left(V\right)
is the

q
-fold multiplication, and
P_{\gamma}
is the projection

T\left(V\right)^{\otimes q}\to T\left(V\right)^{\otimes q}
onto the

\left(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{q}\right)
-th multigraded component),
since
\Delta^{[q]}
sends each “word”
v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{n}\in T\left(V\right)

(with
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\in V
) to the tensor

\sum_{W_{1}\sqcup W_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup W_{q}=[n]}v_{W_{1}}\otimes v_{W_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{W_{q}}\in T\left(V\right)^{\otimes q}
,
where
v_{W}
for a subset
W=\left\{w_{1}<w_{2}<\cdots<w_{m}\right\}\subseteq[n]

is defined to be
v_{w_{1}}v_{w_{2}}\cdots v_{w_{m}}\in T\left(V\right)
.

2.

Then, show that the latter
operator
m^{[q]}\circ P_{\gamma}\circ\Delta^{[q]}

sends
P_{1}P_{2}\cdots P_{k}
to
\sum P_{T_{1}}P_{T_{2}}\cdots P_{T_{q}}
.
The easiest way to do so is by observing that this holds when

P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{k}
are themselves among the generators of
the free algebra
T\left(V\right)
(that is, belong to a basis of
V
),
but the general case then follows easily
(since the primitivity of
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{k}
ensures that
there is a bialgebra morphism from a free algebra to
T\left(V\right)

that sends its generators to
P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{k}
).

Remark 5
.

The products
V^{!T_{1}}V^{!T_{2}}\cdots V^{!T_{q}}
in Theorem
3

are – in general – not of the form
\mathbf{V}_{\gamma}
. For instance,

V^{\{3\}}V^{\{1,2\}}\;=\;\underline{3}(\underline{1}\ \underline{2}-\underline{2}\ \underline{1})\;=\;\underline{3}\ \underline{1}\ \underline{2}-\underline{3}\ \underline{2}\ \underline{1},

a summand in
\mathbf{V}_{(2,1)}\mathbf{B}_{(1,2)}
,
differs from both

\mathbf{V}_{(2,1)}\;=\;V^{\{1,2\}}V^{\{3\}}\;=\;(\underline{1}\ \underline{2}-\underline{2}\ \underline{1})\underline{3}\;=\;\underline{1}\ \underline{2}\ \underline{3}-\underline{2}\ \underline{1}\ \underline{3}

and

\mathbf{V}_{(1,2)}\;=\;\underline{1}(\underline{2}\ \underline{3}-\underline{3}\ \underline{2})\;=\;\underline{1}\ \underline{2}\ \underline{3}-\underline{1}\ \underline{3}\ \underline{2}.

.

Consider
\alpha=(2,2)
and
\beta=(3,1)
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{(2,2)}

\displaystyle=(\underline{1}\underline{2}-\underline{2}\underline{1})(\underline{3}\underline{4}-\underline{4}\underline{3})=\underline{1}\underline{2}\underline{3}\underline{4}-\underline{2}\underline{1}\underline{3}\underline{4}-\underline{1}\underline{2}\underline{4}\underline{3}+\underline{2}\underline{1}\underline{4}\underline{3},

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{(3,1)}

\displaystyle=1234+1243+1342+2341.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{(2,2)}\mathbf{B}_{(3,1)}

\displaystyle=(\underline{1}\underline{2}\underline{3}\underline{4}-\underline{2}\underline{1}\underline{3}\underline{4}-\underline{1}\underline{2}\underline{4}\underline{3}+\underline{2}\underline{1}\underline{4}\underline{3})(1234+1243+1342+2341)

\displaystyle=\underline{1}\underline{2}\underline{3}\underline{4}-\underline{2}\underline{1}\underline{3}\underline{4}-\underline{1}\underline{2}\underline{4}\underline{3}+\underline{2}\underline{1}\underline{4}\underline{3}+\underline{1}\underline{2}\underline{4}\underline{3}-\underline{2}\underline{1}\underline{4}\underline{3}-\underline{1}\underline{2}\underline{3}\underline{4}+\underline{2}\underline{1}\underline{3}\underline{4}

On the other hand, for

,

Proof of the main theorem

A direct consequence of Theorem
3
is the following vanishing
criterion:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\beta}\npreceq_{\pi}\widetilde{\gamma}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbf{V}_{\beta}\,\mathbf{B}_{\gamma}=0

(7)

(since
\mathcal{L}_{\beta,\gamma}=\varnothing
in this case).
A slightly less obvious particular case is the following:

Lemma 4
.

Let
\beta=\left(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{p}\right)
and
\gamma=\left(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{p}\right)
be two compositions of
n
having the same length. Then,

\mathbf{V}_{\beta}\,\mathbf{B}_{\gamma}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\in S_{p};\\
\beta_{\chi\left(i\right)}=\gamma_{i}\text{ for each }i\end{subarray}}V^{\operatorname{Set}(\beta)_{\chi(1)}}V^{\operatorname{Set}(\beta)_{\chi(2)}}\cdots V^{\operatorname{Set}(\beta)_{\chi(p)}}.

Proof.

Set
q=p
, and apply Theorem
3
.
Observe that any ordered set partition
T\in\mathcal{L}_{\beta,\gamma}

must consist entirely of
1
-element blocks (since it partitions a

p
-element set into
p
blocks), and thus has the form

\left(\left\{\chi\left(1\right)\right\},\left\{\chi\left(2\right)\right\},\ldots,\left\{\chi\left(p\right)\right\}\right)
for a unique permutation
\chi\in S_{p}
.
Moreover, this latter permutation
\chi
must satisfy

\beta_{\chi\left(i\right)}=\gamma_{i}
for all
i\in[p]
,
in order for
T
to satisfy (
5
).
This is necessary and sufficient for
T\in\mathcal{L}_{\beta,\gamma}
.
Thus, (
6
) rewrites as

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\beta}\,\mathbf{B}_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\in S_{p};\\
\beta_{\chi\left(i\right)}=\gamma_{i}\text{ for each }i\end{subarray}}V^{!\left\{\chi\left(1\right)\right\}}\cdots V^{!\left\{\chi\left(p\right)\right\}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\in S_{p};\\
\beta_{\chi\left(i\right)}=\gamma_{i}\text{ for each }i\end{subarray}}V^{\operatorname{Set}(\beta)_{\chi(1)}}\cdots V^{\operatorname{Set}(\beta)_{\chi(p)}}.

This proves Lemma
4
.
∎

We shall now study the products

V^{\operatorname{Set}(\beta)_{\chi(1)}}V^{\operatorname{Set}(\beta)_{\chi(2)}}\cdots V^{\operatorname{Set}(\beta)_{\chi(p)}}

on the right hand side of Lemma
4
more closely.
These products are multilinear in the generators

\underline{1},\underline{2},\ldots,\underline{n}
of
F_{n}
,
so they
are linear combinations of words

\underline{v}
with
v\in S_{n}
.
The next lemma reveals some insights about the
v
’s that
appear in these combinations.

Lemma 5
.

Let
w,u\in S_{n}
. Assume that the compositions
\beta=\left(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{p}\right):=\operatorname{cLRM}^{\prime}(w)
and
\gamma=\left(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{p}\right):=\operatorname{cLRM}^{\prime}(u)
are anagrams of one another, and let

\chi\in S_{p}
be a permutation satisfying

\beta_{\chi\left(i\right)}=\gamma_{i}\qquad\text{for each }i\in\left[p\right].

(8)

Let
v\in S_{n}
be a permutation such that the word
\underline{v}\in W_{n,n}
appears in the expansion of the product
V^{\operatorname{Set}(\beta)_{\chi(1)}}\cdots V^{\operatorname{Set}(\beta)_{\chi(p)}}
.
Equivalently, let

v\in S_{n}
be a permutation that sends each
\operatorname*{Set}\left(\gamma\right)_{i}
to
\operatorname*{Set}\left(\beta\right)_{\chi\left(i\right)}
and has V-shape on each
\operatorname*{Set}\left(\gamma\right)_{i}

(this is equivalent by Proposition
3
and by
(
8
)). Assume that

vu=w.

Then,
\chi=\operatorname*{id}
and
v=\operatorname*{id}
and
\gamma=\beta
.

Proof.

For each
i\in\left[p\right]
, we define a claim
\mathcal{C}\left(i\right)

as follows:

Claim
\mathcal{C}\left(i\right)
:

We have
\chi\left(i\right)=i

and
\gamma_{i}=\beta_{i}

and
\operatorname{Set}\left(\beta\right)_{i}=\operatorname{Set}\left(\gamma\right)_{i}

and
\left.v\mid_{\operatorname{Set}\left(\beta\right)_{i}}\right.=\operatorname{id}
.

It clearly suffices to show that
\mathcal{C}\left(i\right)
holds for all

i\in\left[p\right]
. We will prove this by strong descending induction
on
i
. That is, we fix
i\in\left[p\right]
, and we assume that
all the “higher” claims

\mathcal{C}\left(i+1\right),\mathcal{C}\left(i+2\right),\ldots,\mathcal{C}\left(p\right)
hold. We must then prove
\mathcal{C}\left(i\right)
.

By (
1
),
we have
\operatorname*{Set}\left(\beta\right)_{i}=\left[\beta_{\leq i-1}+1,\ \beta_{\leq i}\right]

and
\operatorname*{Set}\left(\gamma\right)_{i}=\left[\gamma_{\leq i-1}+1,\ \gamma_{\leq i}\right]
.

If
\sigma\in S_{n}
is a permutation and

is a positive integer, then we let
\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{j}\sigma
denote the
j
-th largest LRM of
\sigma
. We extend this
to
j=0
by setting
\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{0}\sigma:=n+1
. We note
that the LRMs of a permutation
\sigma
appear in decreasing order when
reading
\sigma
from left to right, so that
\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{j}\sigma
is the
j
-th LRM of
\sigma
encountered in such a read.
Thus, it is easy to see that each
\sigma\in S_{n}
and each positive

satisfy

\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{j}\sigma=\sigma\left(\min\left(\sigma^{-1}\left(\left[\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{j-1}\sigma-1\right]\right)\right)\right).

(9)

(Indeed, this is just saying that if we remove all numbers larger than

\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{j-1}\sigma-1
from the one-line notation of

\sigma
, then the first of the remaining numbers will be
\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{j}\sigma
; but this is clear from the definition of an LRM.)

But
\beta=\operatorname{cLRM}^{\prime}\left(w\right)
shows that

\beta_{\leq i-1}+1
is the
i
-th smallest LRM of
w
, and hence is the

\left(p+1-i\right)
-th largest LRM of
w
(since
w

\ell\left(\operatorname{cLRM}^{\prime}(w)\right)=\ell\left(\beta\right)=p

many LRMs in total). That is,

\beta_{\leq i-1}+1=\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{p+1-i}w
. Likewise,

\beta_{\leq i}+1=\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{p-i}w
. But
(
9
) (applied to
\sigma=w
and

) yields

\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{p+1-i}w=w\left(\min\left(w^{-1}\left(\left[\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{p-i}w-1\right]\right)\right)\right).

Using
\beta_{\leq i-1}+1=\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{p+1-i}w
and

\beta_{\leq i}+1=\operatorname*{lrm}\nolimits_{p-i}w
, this rewrites as

\beta_{\leq i-1}+1=w\left(\min\left(w^{-1}\left(\left[\beta_{\leq i}\right]\right)\right)\right).

(10)

Similarly,

\gamma_{\leq i-1}+1=u\left(\min\left(u^{-1}\left(\left[\gamma_{\leq i}\right]\right)\right)\right).

(11)

However, our induction hypothesis yields
\beta_{j}=\gamma_{j}
for all
j>i
,
and thus easily
\beta_{\leq i}=\gamma_{\leq i}
(since
\left|\beta\right|=n=\left|\gamma\right|
). Furthermore, it shows
that
\left.v\mid_{\operatorname*{Set}\left(\beta\right)_{j}}\right.=\operatorname*{id}
for all

, and thus

\left.v\mid_{\left[n\right]-\left[\beta_{\leq i}\right]}\right.=\operatorname*{id}
,

v\left(\left[n\right]-\left[\beta_{\leq i}\right]\right)=\left[n\right]-\left[\beta_{\leq i}\right]
.
By taking complements, we obtain

,
thus

v^{-1}\left(\left[\beta_{\leq i}\right]\right)=\left[\beta_{\leq i}\right]
.
But

, so that

and therefore

w^{-1}\left(\left[\beta_{\leq i}\right]\right)=u^{-1}\left(v^{-1}\left(\left[\beta_{\leq i}\right]\right)\right)=u^{-1}\left(\left[\beta_{\leq i}\right]\right)
,
because

v^{-1}\left(\left[\beta_{\leq i}\right]\right)=\left[\beta_{\leq i}\right]
.

Now, applying
v
to (
11
), we find

\displaystyle v\left(\gamma_{\leq i-1}+1\right)
