Partial replacement of the tricuspid valve using cryopreserved tricuspid homograft: 20-year outcomes
Authors: Samad Raza, Bishwo Shrestha, Fiona Doig, Peter Pohlner, Homayoun Jalali, Rishendran Naidoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOBJECTIVESThere is limited consensus on the optimal strategy for surgical treatment of severe tricuspid valve pathology. At our institution, we have implemented a unique technique of using a tricuspid homograft with its supporting apparatus for partial replacement of the native tricuspid valve. We now present the long-term outcomes of these patients.METHODSWe analysed a cohort of patients who underwent partial tricuspid valve replacement using tricuspid homograft. Yearly clinical and echocardiographic follow-up was performed. Fine–Gray methods were used to estimate freedom from death and reoperation and reverse Kaplan–Meier methods were used to calculate follow-up.RESULTSFourteen patients were included (age range 15 days to 73 years). Indications included congenital anomalies (n = 9, 64%) and infective endocarditis (n = 5, 36%). The median follow-up was 17 years (95% confidence interval (CI) 10–21 years). Two patients (14%) died due to causes unrelated to the primary tricuspid valve surgery, and three (21%) underwent redo tricuspid valve operations. In the remaining cohort, seven (50%) were asymptomatic and two (14%) reported class II dyspnoea, while none had severe tricuspid regurgitation on echocardiogram. Estimated freedom from death was 93% at 10 years and 83% at 15 and 20 years, while estimated freedom from reoperation was 77% at 10, 15 and 20 years.CONCLUSIONSPartial replacement of the tricuspid valve using tricuspid homograft tissue effectively restores the anatomical conformity of the native tricuspid valve and has durable long-term survival and freedom from severe tricuspid regurgitation in patients with congenital anomalies and infective endocarditis.

INTRODUCTION

Tricuspid valve replacement has several limitations. Biological valves have limited durability and growth potential, while mechanical valves have numerous issues such as the requirement for anticoagulation and high rate of conduction system injury [1–3]. There is therefore a need to explore alternative management strategies for severe tricuspid valve disease. A novel technique of partial replacement of the tricuspid valve using homograft tissue for severely deformed or damaged native tricuspid valves was applied by two surgeons at our institution between 1997 and 2010. While the use of mitral valve homograft tissue for tricuspid valve replacement has been described in several studies [4–9]. The use of tricuspid homograft tissue has been limited to sporadic cases [10–12]. Our cohort of 14 cases is the largest case series reported so far. This paper presents the surgical technique of tricuspid homograft implantation and its long-term results with over 20 years of follow-up, serving as a complement to the mid-term outcomes published in 2009 [13]. Long-term follow-up of these patients included assessment of clinical status, valve function and requirement for reintervention. We hypothesized that partial replacement of the tricuspid valve with tricuspid homograft tissue would result in long-term preservation of valvular function while limiting the rate of adverse complications such as pacemaker insertion and reinfection.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design

This study was a cohort study involving a prospectively collected database which was retrospectively reviewed for patients who underwent partial tricuspid valve replacement using tricuspid valve homograft tissue between 1992 and 2010 at The Prince Charles Hospital in Brisbane, Australia. Follow-up was closed on 19 February 2024.

Surgical technique

Tricuspid homograft tissue (Fig. 1) is available from donor hearts harvested for aortic or pulmonary homografts. Compared to mitral homografts, tricuspid homografts have three leaflets that correspond closely to the tissue characteristics of the native recipient valve, along with subvalvular chordae and papillary muscles that provide intrinsic leaflet support. Although the harvesting and cryopreservation technique is not significantly different from that of aortic and mitral homografts, valve bank staff require extra training to process tricuspid homograft tissue.

Data collection and statistical methods

Patients’ medical records were reviewed, and yearly clinical and echocardiographic follow-up was performed. Outcomes analysed included New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional status, echocardiographic valve function, reoperation, and mortality. Fine–Gray competing risk analyses were used to estimate freedom from reoperation and overall survival, while reverse Kaplan–Meier methods were used to calculate median follow-up in the entire cohort. There was no missing data at baseline.

Ethics

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee on 29 August 2008 (approval number EC2873), prior to data collection for the mid-term outcomes, and utilized anonymized data that was stored in a secure database. Individual informed consent was waived.

RESULTS

Fourteen patients have undergone partial replacement of the tricuspid valve using tricuspid homograft at TPCH. Since the publication of our series of mid-term outcomes of the technique in 2009 [13], one further patient underwent this procedure. This 20-year-old female had a history of Ebstein malformation and pulmonary stenosis, having previously undergone pulmonary valve replacement with a pulmonary homograft, tricuspid valve repair and closure of an atrial septal defect as a child. She re-presented with severe tricuspid and pulmonary regurgitation and underwent a partial tricuspid valve replacement utilizing tricuspid homograft tissue and a 36 mm Physio annuloplasty ring and right ventricular outflow tract reconstruction with pulmonary homograft (Table 1).

The indications for surgery were as follows: five patients (36%) with infective endocarditis, five (36%) with Ebstein’s anomaly, two (14%) with AV septal defect, one (7%) with Tetralogy of Fallot and one (7%) with pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum. There were six females (43%) and eight males (57%) in our series. There were five paediatric patients (36%), age range from 2 weeks to 7 years (mean 22.3 months) and nine adult patients (64%), age ranged from 20 to 67 years (mean 45.8 years).

Median follow-up was 17 years (95% confidence interval (CI) 10–21 years, maximum 23 years) and three patients (21%) were lost to follow-up, one in the endocarditis group at 2 years and two in the Ebstein group at 1 and 11 years. Of the 11 patients who completed follow-up, there were two deaths (18%). One patient (patient 2) died at 10 years due to aspiration pneumonia while the other (patient 4) died from multiorgan failure due to infective endocarditis of the concurrently implanted mitral valve prosthesis, not involving the tricuspid valve (Tables 2 and 3). Ten patients (91%) were in NYHA class I. Recurrence of greater than moderate tricuspid regurgitation occurred in two patients (18%): one with endocarditis (patient 3) and the other with Ebstein’s anomaly (patient 7). Both patients tolerated this well with NYHA class I and II symptoms, respectively. Patient 3 had been treated with Lisinopril 20 mg daily and patient 7 with Bisoprolol 5 mg daily.

Reoperations were required in three patients (27%). These occurred exclusively in patients with congenital pathology and the decision to re-operate on the tricuspid valve was influenced by the presence of pulmonary incompetence in two of these patients. Dehiscence of the implanted homograft occurred in one patient (patient 6), 2 years following the primary surgery. At reoperation, the previously inserted septal homograft leaflet had become detached at the base. Repair of the defect was attempted with bovine pericardium but competence of the valve could not be restored, and therefore the valve was replaced with a 29 mm Mosaic bioprosthesis. The excised homograft tissue in this patient demonstrated surface fibrin, fibrosis and neovascularization on histological analysis (Fig. 3). Aside from one patient requiring reoperation for mixed tricuspid stenosis and regurgitation (patient 12), no other patient had significant tricuspid stenosis on latest echocardiographic follow-up. None of the patients in our series required permanent pacemaker insertion.

Three-dimensional echocardiography was performed to assess the tricuspid valve on one patient at 21 years of follow-up (Supplementary Videos 1 and 2). This patient had severe staphylococcal endocarditis involving the anterior and posterior leaflets and subvalvular apparatus. These were excised and replaced with tricuspid homograft tissue, while the native septal leaflet was preserved. Echocardiography showed mild tricuspid regurgitation. While there was mild tethering of the posterior leaflet, there was no calcification or tricuspid stenosis, with a mean transvalvular gradient of 1 mmHg.

DISCUSSION

The optimal surgical approach to tricuspid valve pathology remains controversial. While tricuspid valve replacement is less technically demanding than surgical repair, it has numerous disadvantages. Both mechanical and biological prostheses perform poorly in adults, with a 15-year reoperation-free survival of 62% and 53%, respectively [14], due to complications such as valve thrombosis and degeneration. Furthermore, valve prostheses are susceptible to reinfection, particularly in intravenous drug users [3], and pose a high risk of conduction system injury leading to heart block and permanent pacemaker insertion, which can be as high as 12.5% [15]. Given their lack of growth potential, tricuspid prostheses are poorly tolerated in the paediatric population, with an average time to redo surgery of 2.7 ± 2.6 years [16].

While simple tricuspid valvular pathologies can be repaired with primary techniques or reconstruction with pericardial patches [1], in complex valvular pathologies such as endocarditis resulting in total leaflet destruction or significant leaflet deficiency due to congenital malformation, traditional repair techniques may not restore the anatomical conformity of the valve. Our study has demonstrated that the use of homograft tissue is a valuable alternative in such patients. The long-term freedom from reoperation in our cohort is comparable to published literature on pericardial patch repair techniques [17].

Unlike pericardial patches, homograft tissue has superior tissue characteristics and is less susceptible to foreign body response and degenerative calcification [18–20]. It is particularly useful in young patients who are poor candidates for valve replacement due to the risk of long-term prosthesis-related complications. Furthermore, in comparison to pericardial patches, the presence of intrinsic subvalvular support enables the restoration of native valve geometry and optimizes haemodynamic performance by maintaining annuloventricular continuity. Additionally, homograft tissue is less thrombogenic and less susceptible to infection than prosthetic valves [21, 22], and there is no requirement for anticoagulation. The lack of postoperative heart block in our cohort is another advantage of this technique—a rim of native leaflet tissue adjacent to the triangle of Koch is preserved, enabling safe suturing of the homograft and avoiding direct instrumentation of the annulus.

Most of the pre-existing literature on the use of homograft tissue for tricuspid valve replacement involves the use of mitral homografts. The technique was pioneered by Pomar et al. in 1993 in a series of three patients [4]. Later studies have demonstrated good functional outcomes and long-term survival; however, the follow-up duration has been limited in most studies [5–8], with only one case report citing follow-up >10 years [9]. There have only been three other reported cases on the use of tricuspid homograft [10–12], with a maximum follow-up of 9 months [10]. We have previously reported the largest series in the literature on the use of tricuspid homograft tissue rather than mitral homografts [13]. In our experience, the use of tricuspid valve leaflets, which are thinner and more pliable than mitral valve leaflets, enables us to more closely replicate the structure and function of the native valve.

Since the publication of our mid-term results in 2010 [13], two patients in the cohort have died, due to causes unrelated to the tricuspid valve. In the patients requiring reintervention, we identified a technical failure resulting in dehiscence of homograft tissue (in an Ebstein’s anomaly case) and the presence of associated congenital anomalies and pulmonary regurgitation as potential contributors (TOF and PAIVS). This highlights a possible limitation of this technique in patients with associated pulmonary valve pathology. In our series, we noted the best results were achieved in Ebstein’s disease and infective endocarditis.

An alternative technique for tricuspid valve restoration involves the use of pericardial and extracellular matrix cylinders to reconstruct the valve [18, 23]. While these techniques avoid the use of prosthetic material and are more easily accessible than homograft tissue, current data suggests a high rate of complications. In a series of 19 patients who underwent reconstruction with CorMatrix ECM cylinder, three patients experienced early mechanical dehiscence of the papillary muscle attachments or ECM cylinder seam [18]. In another series of seven patients who underwent reconstruction with a pericardial cylinder, three patients experienced degeneration and stenosis of the cylinder within six months of the index operation [23].

Our study has some limitations. We lost three patients (21.4% of the cohort) to follow-up in an otherwise small series. Additionally, this cohort study did not enrol a control group of patients undergoing repair of tricuspid valves using standard techniques or replacement with prosthetic valves.

The uptake of tricuspid homograft replacement has been limited by several factors, including the small case numbers in previously published reports, limited availability of tricuspid homograft tissue in valve banks, and the technical complexity of the operation, with different techniques published by different authors [4–8]. Overall, the technique requires a high degree of surgical expertise and must be individualized to the patient’s pathology. It was only performed by two surgeons in our institution, each with several years of specialist congenital cardiac surgical experience and would require significant training to reproduce. Since 2010 the valve bank in our institution moved to a different location and the paediatric cardiac surgical department moved to a different hospital, limiting further use of tricuspid homografts at our institution.

CONCLUSION

The long-term results of partial tricuspid valve replacement with tricuspid homograft, while limited to a small number of patients, indicate that this is a viable technique in complex valvular pathology, especially infective endocarditis and Ebstein’s anomaly, and demonstrates good long-term survival and freedom from reoperation, comparable with standard repair techniques.
