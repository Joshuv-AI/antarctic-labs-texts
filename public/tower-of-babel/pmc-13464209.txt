Race/ethnicity and outcomes of venous ablation procedures
Authors: Pavel Kibrik, Jenna Kwon, Nikita Singh, Hason Khan, Ali Basil Ali, Tyler Santos, Michael Arustamyan, Leana Shugol, A. Natalie Ascher, Enrico Ascher, Anil Hingorani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness and safety of endovenous ablation across racial and ethnic groups by comparing rates of endovenous heat-induced thrombosis (EHIT) and recanalization.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 13,335 endothermal ablation procedures (radiofrequency or laser) performed from 2012 to 2022 at a single outpatient center. Patient demographics, including self-identified race/ethnicity (such as Asian, Black, Hispanic, and White), were recorded. Postprocedural duplex ultrasound study was conducted at 3 to 7 days, 3 to 6 months, and then every 6 to 12 months. EHIT (classes 1-4) and recanalization of the treated vein were identified on follow-up imaging. Outcomes were compared across racial/ethnic groups using univariable and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsAmong 13,335 ablations (8187 radiofrequency and 5148 laser) in 3218 patients, 67.1% were performed in women. Mean follow-up was 25.8 ± 12.9 months (range, 3-72 months). EHIT incidence was highest in Black patients (3.0%), followed by Asian (2.6%), White (1.7%), and Hispanic (1.4%) patients; the difference between Black and White patients was significant (P < .001), whereas other pairwise comparisons were not. Procedural success (no EHIT or recanalization) was high and comparable across groups: 96.1% in Asians, 95.2% in Blacks, 96.1% in Hispanics, and 96.2% in Whites. On multivariable analysis, Black race was associated with higher EHIT risk (odds ratio, 1.92; P < .001), and Asian race with higher recanalization risk (odds ratio, 1.65; P = .045). Additional predictors of recanalization included advanced disease (Clinical, Etiologic, Anatomic, and Pathophysiologic class 6; P < .05), whereas the ablation of the great, anterior, or small saphenous veins was protective compared with perforator veins (all P < .001). Predictors of EHIT included older age (P < .0001) and previous recanalization (P < .0001), whereas laser ablation was associated with a significantly lower EHIT risk compared with radiofrequency (P < .0001). Baseline disease severity also varied: Black patients were more likely to present with advanced disease (Clinical, Etiologic, Anatomic, and Pathophysiologic class, 5-6) than Hispanic and Asian patients (P < .0001).ConclusionsEndovenous ablation is a safe and effective treatment for chronic venous insufficiency across different racial/ethnic groups, with overall success rates of 95% to 96%. However, racial differences were observed in complication rates and baseline severity: Black patients had higher EHIT risk and more advanced disease at presentation, whereas Asian patients had a slightly higher risk of recanalization. These findings highlight the need for tailored postablation surveillance and improved early access to care to address differences in minority populations.

Health care disparities, defined by the Institute of Medicine as differences in the quality of health outcomes among groups with equal access to care, remain a persistent challenge in the United States.1 These differences arise from a complex interplay of socioeconomic, behavioral, and systemic factors, including income, health behaviors, cultural beliefs, geographic location, education, and race. According to the 2010 National Healthcare Disparities Report, 33% of the US population identified as belonging to a racial or ethnic minority group, a proportion projected to rise to 50% by 2050.2 This demographic shift underscores the urgency of understanding how health disparities manifest across diverse populations, beginning with careful identification and characterization of observed differences.

A growing body of research suggests that race and ethnicity are associated with differences in health outcomes across a range of medical and surgical conditions.3,4 In chronic venous disease (CVD), many studies report demographic characteristics, including race and ethnicity; however, relatively few are specifically designed or powered to evaluate differences in disease presentation or procedural outcomes across diverse populations. As patient populations continue to diversify, further characterization of these differences may help inform future, more targeted investigations.

In this context, we conducted a single-center retrospective study to evaluate differences in outcomes following endothermal ablation for chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) across racial and ethnic groups. We focused on two endpoints, endothermal heat-induced thrombosis (EHIT) and recanalization, to describe whether differences in procedural outcomes are observed.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective review of all endovenous thermal ablation procedures performed for CVI at a single outpatient vascular center between 2012 and 2022. Data collection ended in 2022 due to the implementation of updated procedural protocols. Both radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and endovenous laser ablation (EVLA) cases were included.

Patient demographic and clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical record, including self-reported race and ethnicity recorded at registration (Table I). Although minor updates to the registration interface occurred during the study period, the core race and ethnicity categories remained consistent and aligned with the Office of Management and Budget classification framework. For analysis, race and ethnicity were treated as distinct variables and mapped to standardized categories (race: Asian, Black/African American, White, others; ethnicity: Hispanic/Latino vs non-Hispanic/Latino). Patients who identified as “other race” were retained as a separate category in descriptive analyses; however, due to small sample size and heterogeneity, they were not included as a primary comparator in multivariable models to avoid unstable estimates. Missing demographic data were handled using a complete-case approach for analyses involving those variables. Patients with missing race or ethnicity data were excluded only from analyses requiring those fields but were retained in the overall cohort and in analyses not dependent on these variables. The proportion of missing data was low, and no imputation was performed.

Patients presenting with signs or symptoms of CVI [Clinical, Etiologic, Anatomic, and Pathophysiologic (CEAP) class ≥ C2] underwent a thorough history and physical examination, supplemented by duplex ultrasound study of the lower extremities when indicated. Saphenous reflux was defined as >500 ms of retrograde flow in a superficial vein or >350 ms in an incompetent perforator vein. Concomitant procedures were not performed at the time of index ablation, to maintain consistency in procedural assessment. Patients meeting these criteria were initially managed conservatively with compression therapy and leg elevation for at least 3 months. In accordance with the Society for Vascular Surgery and American Venous Forum clinical practice guidelines, those with persistent or significant superficial venous reflux were offered one or more endovenous ablation procedures based on the anatomic distribution of reflux.

The choice between RFA and EVLA was primarily determined by insurance authorization. In the absence of insurer preference, RFA was generally favored for patients younger than 65 years based on institutional observations of reduced postprocedural pain in this group. A similar difference in postprocedural pain was not observed in older patients, and EVLA was therefore used more variably in this population depending on coverage and other considerations. All incompetent perforator veins were treated using RFA. EVLA procedures were performed with the VenaCure 1470 nm laser catheter (AngioDynamics). RFA of saphenous veins used the ClosureFast catheter (Medtronic), and perforator vein ablations were performed with the ClosureRFS stylet (Medtronic).

Postprocedural duplex ultrasound study was routinely performed at 3 to 7 days, 3 to 6 months, and at 6 to 12 month intervals thereafter to evaluate for complications or treatment failure. Patients were not excluded for incomplete follow-up during these periods, and the median follow-up duration was measured. EHIT was defined as thrombus propagation from the treated vein and classified according to standard EHIT grading (classes I-IV), recognizing that these classification systems are well established for truncal vein interventions but lack a widely accepted standard for perforator vein treatments. Recanalization was defined as the reappearance of both flow and pathologic reflux within a previously ablated vein segment; reappearance of flow without reflux was not considered treatment failure. Procedural success was defined as the absence of both EHIT and recanalization during follow-up. These endpoints were selected as objective measures consistently captured across the study period; patient-reported outcomes, including quality-of-life (QoL) measures, were not uniformly available and were therefore not included.

Comparative analyses were performed across racial and ethnic groups. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and compared using t-tests or analysis of variance, whereas categorical variables were analyzed using χ2 tests in JMP 14 (SAS Institute). Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of EHIT and recanalization, with covariates including race/ethnicity, age, sex, treated vein, ablation modality (RFA vs EVLA), CEAP classification, and history of previous venous interventions or complications. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Vascular Institute of New York and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Because the data were retrospective, deidentified, and posed minimal risk, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

These procedures reflect multiple interventions per patient over the study period, including treatment of different venous segments and clinically indicated reinterventions for persistent or recurrent disease. The cohort was predominantly female (67.1%). EHIT outcomes

The overall incidence of EHIT was low at 1.8%. Rates were highest in Black patients (3.0%), followed by Asian (2.6%), White (1.7%), and Hispanic patients (1.4%) (P < .01 for Black vs White/Hispanic). In absolute numbers, EHIT occurred in 47 Black, 125 White patients, 34 Hispanic patients, and 3 Asian patients, reflecting differences in procedural volume across groups. Most cases (approximately 85%) were class 1 or class 2, with thrombus limited to the superficial deep junction or <50% extension into the common femoral vein. Management followed standard protocols: class 1 EHITs were treated with antiplatelet therapy, whereas classes 2 to 4 were managed with anticoagulation and serial duplex ultrasound surveillance. Class 4 EHIT was managed as deep venous thrombosis per American College of Chest Physicians (CHEST) guidelines. Classes 2 and 3 EHIT were treated with therapeutic anticoagulation until thrombus retraction/resolution on follow-up imaging, and antiplatelet therapy use was individualized. No patients developed pulmonary embolism or other adverse sequelae.

Recanalization outcomes

Symptomatic recanalization rates were low across all groups, with no statistically significant difference overall by racial and ethnic groups (P = .08). Rates were 3.4% among both Asian and Black patients, compared with 2.4% in Hispanic patients and 2.0% in White patients (P = .08 overall; pairwise P = .04 for Asian vs White). Most recanalizations occurred within the first year of follow-up, and many were partial, responding well to repeat ablation or sclerotherapy when symptomatic.

On multivariable analysis, Asian patients were independently associated with higher odds of recanalization (adjusted OR ≈ 1.6; 95% CI, 1.0-2.6; P = .045), whereas Black patients were not statistically significant. Additional predictors included a history of EHIT (OR ≈ 11.54; P < .0001) and CEAP class 6 at presentation (OR ≈ 1.66; P < .05). Discussion

The racial and ethnic composition of the United States is changing rapidly, with minorities projected to comprise 50% of the population by 2050.2 This demographic shift highlights the importance of addressing disparities in health care, including in CVD. Previous studies have shown worse outcomes in nonwhite populations, but race-specific data on the safety and efficacy of venous ablation remain sparse.1,3, 4, 5, 6 By analyzing more than 13,000 ablation procedures across diverse patient groups, this study contributes insights to this gap.

Overall, our results are encouraging. Endovenous ablation was highly effective across all populations, with procedural success rates exceeding 95% and low recanalization rates. These findings suggest that when quality care is delivered equitably, outcomes are consistent regardless of race or ethnicity. Outcomes were largely comparable across racial and ethnic groups, supporting that when care is delivered within a consistent clinical setting, treatment effectiveness appears similar.

Despite overall comparable outcomes, modest differences in EHIT rates were observed across groups. Black patients had a higher incidence of EHIT (3.0%), compared with Asian (2.6%), White (1.7%), and Hispanic (1.4%) patients. This aligns with broader vascular literature showing a higher thrombotic risk among Black individuals, potentially related to genetic, inflammatory, or socioeconomic factors. Epidemiologic studies have documented higher rates of venous thromboembolism in Black populations compared to White and Asian groups.7,8 Although the mechanisms remain unclear, all EHIT events in our cohort were managed promptly with anticoagulation and surveillance, and no patient developed pulmonary embolism or lasting sequelae.

However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The magnitude of these differences was modest, and the study was not designed to evaluate underlying causes. Importantly, symptomatic recanalization was determined based on clinical documentation rather than standardized measures such as Venous Clinical Severity Score or validated QoL instruments, which were not consistently collected during the study period. As such, these outcomes reflect clinically documented events rather than comprehensive patient-centered measures of success.

In addition, several known clinical and behavioral factors associated with venous disease outcomes were not available for inclusion in the analysis. Variables such as body mass index, comorbid conditions, previous thrombotic history, activity level, and socioeconomic factors may contribute to both EHIT and recanalization risk. Although multivariable models included available covariates such as age, sex, CEAP classification, and treated vein, residual confounding is likely. Therefore, the observed associations with race and ethnicity should be interpreted as descriptive and not as independent causal relationships.

Consistent with this, recanalization rates did not differ significantly across groups in unadjusted analyses, suggesting that the overall durability of ablation is similar when procedures are performed using standardized techniques. Factors such as disease severity and vein characteristics were more strongly associated with recanalization risk, aligning with previous literature demonstrating that anatomic and clinical features are primary drivers of outcomes.

Similarly, although EHIT rates differed across groups, overall incidence was low, and events were predominantly minor and managed effectively without long-term complications. These findings further support the overall safety of endovenous ablation across diverse patient populations.

Multivariate analysis revealed that anatomical and clinical factors were key predictors of recanalization risk. Patients with the most advanced venous disease (CEAP class 6) had higher recanalization rates, whereas ablation of larger truncal veins, such as the great saphenous vein, was associated with a lower risk of recanalization than smaller accessory or perforator veins. These findings are consistent with previous work showing that disease severity and vein characteristics drive recanalization risk.9 Taken together, our results suggest that race is not inherently predictive of long-term procedural failure once disease burden and anatomy are accounted for.

Other well-recognized risk factors for complications were reaffirmed. Increasing age was associated with a higher odds of EHIT, consistent with established venous thromboembolism trends in older populations.10,11 Age-related changes in the venous system—including reduced elasticity of vein walls, slower ambulation and calf muscle pump function, and a greater burden of prothrombotic comorbidities—likely contribute to this elevated risk.12 Previous studies focusing on elderly patients undergoing vein ablation have indeed reported higher rates of EHIT and recanalization in older cohorts, though still within an acceptable range, and noted that most of these complications are minor and manageable.12 A history of EHIT strongly predicted symptomatic recurrence, suggesting underlying patient-level vulnerabilities.

Importantly, our data demonstrated that EVLA was associated with a lower incidence of EHIT than RFA. This may reflect differences in technique, patient selection, or other unmeasured factors rather than intrinsic differences between modalities. Given the single-center, retrospective design, these results are not intended to establish comparative effectiveness but rather to inform future investigation. This aligns with previous reports from our institution and suggests that EVLA may induce less injury at the superficial-deep venous junction than RFA.13 Another study similarly reported higher EHIT rates with RFA, specifically with the ClosureFast catheter, and proposed that differences in heat dispersion and catheter pull-back technique might contribute to this disparity.13 Clinically, this distinction may be relevant in tailoring procedural choice, with EVLA potentially preferred for patients at elevated thrombotic risk, such as those with a history of EHIT or hypercoagulable states.

We also found differences in disease severity at presentation. Black and White patients were more likely to present with advanced disease (CEAP class 5-6), whereas Hispanic and Asian patients more often presented at earlier stages (CEAP class 3-4; P < .0001). These findings mirror patterns in other vascular conditions, where minority groups often present at more advanced stages, influenced by access barriers, differences in health-seeking behavior, and referral patterns. Consistent with previous work by Pappas et al,6 Black patients with varicose vein disease presented with more severe symptoms despite younger age, suggesting delays in recognition or intervention. Such trends highlight the need for earlier diagnosis and targeted outreach in high-risk communities. Notably, despite presenting with more advanced disease, Black patients in our cohort achieved comparable outcomes following treatment, apart from the higher EHIT risk.

Finally, these findings highlight areas for further investigation. Given the observational design and limited covariate availability, the factors contributing to these differences cannot be determined. Future prospective studies incorporating comprehensive clinical, behavioral, and socioeconomic variables, as well as standardized patient-reported outcomes, are needed to better understand variation in outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. As a retrospective, single-center analysis, its findings may not be generalizable to other populations or practice settings. Variability in follow-up duration and potential inconsistencies in data collection may introduce bias that is not mitigated by sample size alone. Differences in follow-up patterns across groups may also affect the detection of outcomes, particularly late complications.

The racial and ethnic distribution of the cohort was uneven, with smaller representation of certain groups, which may limit statistical power for subgroup analyses. In addition, important potential confounding factors were not captured, including QoL, socioeconomic status, access to care, health behaviors, comorbid conditions (ie, smoking and malignancy), and procedural or anatomic variation. These unmeasured variables may contribute to the observed differences in outcomes and cannot be accounted for in this analysis.

These limitations restrict the ability to determine whether observed differences are attributable to underlying patient, disease, or care-related factors. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than causal. Prospective studies with standardized data collection and more comprehensive variable capture are needed to better evaluate these relationships.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that endovenous ablation is a safe and effective treatment for CVD across diverse racial and ethnic groups, with similarly high rates of procedural success and durable symptom relief. Race/ethnicity was not associated with differences in long-term outcomes such as recanalization, indicating that equitable care delivery can yield outstanding results regardless of race/ethnicity.

However, significant differences in the incidence of postablation complications and disease severity at presentation were identified. Black patients had a higher incidence of EHIT and, along with White patients, were more likely to present with advanced disease (CEAP class 5-6). Asian patients showed a modest increase in recanalization risk. These differences point to factors that require proactive measures: enhanced surveillance for postprocedure complications and earlier intervention for minority populations at risk of delayed presentation. Ultimately, recognizing and appropriately contextualizing these differences and determining whether they reflect true disparities is essential to ensuring equitable outcomes in modern venous therapies.
