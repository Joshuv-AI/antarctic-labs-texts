Do Temporary Workers Face Higher Wage Markdowns? Evidence from India’s Automotive Sector
Davide Luparello

Abstract
Are temporary workers subject to different wage markdowns than permanent workers? This paper examines productivity, output markups, and wage markdowns in India’s automotive sector during 2000–2020. I develop a model integrating CES production, nested logit labor supply, and differentiated labor market conduct: Nash-Bertrand wage setting for temporary workers versus Nash bargaining for unionized permanent workers. Results reveal declining output markups as marginal costs outpace prices through productivity deceleration. Rising labor-augmenting productivity cannot offset declining Hicks-neutral productivity, reducing overall TFP. Labor market power substantially compresses worker compensation: wage markdowns persist at 40% for temporary workers and 10% for permanent workers. Theoretical Framework for Automotive Plant Production

This section develops a plant production behavioral model capturing four defining characteristics of India’s automotive industrial structure: contractual dualism in labor markets, multimodal technical change, and union bargaining power for permanent workers. The production framework employs nested CES technology, incorporating dual productivity channels—Hicks-neutral and labor-augmenting technical progress. For blue-collar labor supply, nested logit specifications distinguish temporary from permanent worker categories. Wage determination follows distinct mechanisms: Bertrand-Nash wage setting for temporary workers, Nash bargaining between plants and unions for permanent workers.

Empirical Framework

This section develops an empirical strategy to estimate the model’s parameters and latent variables. The analysis proceeds through five stages. First, the estimation recovers substitution elasticities across production nests and labor-augmenting productivity. Second, the methodology identifies capital allocation distortions, which fully characterizes the CES share parameters, and extracts a residual composition of Hicks-neutral productivity and measurement error. Third, a state-space approach decomposes Hicks-neutral productivity from measurement error. Fourth, the framework derives establishment-level output markups and labor market friction measures. Finally, the procedure estimates blue-collar labor supply elasticities and the plant’s bargaining power parameter
\theta

Concluding Remarks

To conclude, this paper analyzes the Indian automotive industry and its dynamic patterns of productivity, output prices, and wage markdowns over the past two decades. I develop a production and labor supply model featuring vector-valued productivity (Hicks-neutral and labor-augmenting), upward-sloping labor supply curves for blue-collar permanent and temporary workers derived from a discrete choice-nested logit framework, and heterogeneous conduct in the blue-collar labor market: Nash-Bertrand for temporary workers and Nash bargaining between plants and unions for permanent workers.

Findings demonstrate declining output price markups—the Lerner index decreased from 60% to 40%, reflecting broader profitability reductions. This results from marginal costs rising faster than output prices amid productivity deceleration: though labor-augmenting productivity increased, declining Hicks-neutral productivity diminished overall TFP. Blue-collar wage markdowns persist despite wage growth: 40% for temporary workers and 10% under Nash bargaining for permanent workers. Labor share exhibits significantly higher sensitivity (4× for temporary, 8× for permanent workers) to labor market power increases than output market power. These findings illuminate the underlying mechanisms driving industry performance over two decades and establish an analytical foundation for projecting future trajectories of India’s foremost manufacturing sector.

Finally, this paper faces two significant limitations. First, it analyzes labor market power through a static framework, disregarding potential interactions with dynamic labor adjustment costs.
Bertrand, Hsieh, and Tsivanidis 2021
and
Chatterjee et al. 2024
demonstrate that substantial hiring and firing costs constrain establishment growth. Extending this paper’s framework into a dynamic model where adjustment costs influence workers’ MRPL would advance our understanding and measurement of labor market power. Second, the current approach overlooks double marginalization for temporary contract workers. Temporary worker expenditures in the data represent payments potentially made to contracting agencies, which likely extract additional rent from workers’ MRPL. This circumstance renders the markdown estimates for temporary workers a lower bound from the workers’ perspective. Both extensions offer valuable directions for future research.

Appendix C
Normalization via Geometric Means

The analysis follows the CES normalization approach of
Grieco, Li, and Zhang 2016
and
Hong and Luparello 2024
, scaling variables by their geometric means to eliminate unit-of-measurement effects in estimation. This normalization reduces factor share parameters to data-driven functions containing a single unknown parameter. At the baseline point
\bar{Z}
, each variable equals its geometric mean:
\bar{X}=\left(\prod_{n=1}^{N}X_{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}
. Zero-mean measurement error
\varepsilon_{jt}
ensures that geometric means of observed and planned output are identical:
\bar{Q}=\bar{\tilde{Q}}
.

The selection of the baseline value defines a family of CES functions characterized by the following system of equations in partial equilibrium:

(A1)

Appendix F
Kalman Procedures

This appendix details the Kalman filtering procedure used to compute the likelihood function for the state-space model in equations (
25
) and (
26
). Recall that the state-space representation of the estimating equation in Step 3 is:

(A55)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\omega}_{jt}=H\pi_{jt}+AY_{jt}+\hat{\ddot{\iota}}_{H,t},

(A56)

\displaystyle\pi_{jt}=F\pi_{jt-1}+Gu_{jt},

For each plant
j
, the Kalman filtering steps proceed as follows (
Hamilton 1994a
):

\displaystyle\pi_{j,0|0}=\mathbb{E}(\pi_{j0})=\vec{0}_{3\times 1},

\displaystyle P_{j,0|0}=\mathbb{E}(\pi_{j,0|0}\pi_{j,0|0}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\pi_{j,t+1|t}=F\pi_{j,t|t},

\displaystyle P_{j,t+1|t}=FP_{j,t|t}F^{\prime}+G\Sigma G^{\prime},

\displaystyle\nu_{jt+1}=\widetilde{\omega}_{jt+1}-H\pi_{j,t+1|t}-AY_{jt+1}-\hat{\ddot{\iota}}_{H,t+1},

\displaystyle N_{j,t+1}=HP_{j,t+1|t}H^{\prime},

\displaystyle O_{j,t+1}=P_{j,t+1|t}H^{\prime}N_{j,t+1}^{-1},

\displaystyle\pi_{j,t+1|t+1}=\pi_{j,t+1|t}-O_{j,t+1}\nu_{jt+1},

\displaystyle P_{j,t+1|t+1}=(I-O_{j,t+1}H)P_{j,t+1|t}.

At each iteration, we compute the log-likelihood of observing
\widetilde{\omega}_{jt+1}
using
\nu_{jt+1}
and
N_{j,t+1}
:

\displaystyle\log{f(\widetilde{\omega}_{jt+1}|I_{t})}=-\log{(\text{det}(N_{j,t+1}))}-\nu_{jt+1}^{\prime}N_{j,t+1}^{-1}\nu_{jt+1}.

The contribution of plant
j
to the overall likelihood is then:

\displaystyle L_{j}(\sigma_{H}^{2}|D)=-\sum_{t=0}^{T_{j}}\log{(\text{det}(N_{j,t}))}-\sum_{t=0}^{T_{j}}\nu_{jt}^{\prime}N_{j,t}^{-1}\nu_{jt},

and the total likelihood function is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\sigma_{H}^{2}|D)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}L_{j}(\sigma_{H}^{2}|D).

Upon estimating
\sigma_{H}^{2}
via MLE, I recover the estimates of
\xi_{jt}^{H}
and
\varepsilon_{jt}
using the Kalman smoother
(
Hamilton 1994b
)
, based on all available information over the sample period. This retrospective improvement yields more precise estimates of
\pi_{jt}
, denoted by
\pi_{j,t|T_{j}}
. Specifically, given the Kalman-filtered variables
(\pi_{j,t|t},P_{j,t+1|t+1},\pi_{j,t|t+1},P_{j,t+1|t})
, I recursively apply the Kalman smoother from
T_{j}
back to
t=1
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}_{j,T_{j}|T_{j}}=\pi_{j,T_{j}|T_{j}},

\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{j,T_{j}|T_{j}}=P_{j,T_{j}|T_{j}},

\displaystyle C_{jt}=P_{j,t|t}F^{\prime}P_{j,t+1|t}^{-1},

\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{j,t|T_{j}}=P_{j,t|t}+C_{jt}(\widehat{P}_{j,t+1|T_{j}}-P_{j,t+1|t})C_{jt}^{\prime}.

and
\hat{\varepsilon}_{jt}
correspond to the first and second elements of
