Unexpected retention of a spinal needle in a morbidly obese patient: a case report and technical reflection
Authors: Mustapha Nahle, Mohamad Ghandour, Fahda Ali, Baboyan Vanik, Mohammed Zaatari, Mayyas Msheik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal anesthesia is considered a safe and effective anesthetic technique. However, some complications may be encountered in clinical settings such as needle fracture or retention, which is a rare but serious complication. Morbid obesity is a recognized risk factor for such technical difficulties due to increased tissue depth and altered anatomical landmarks. A 19-year-old male (BMI > 40) presented for elective umbilical hernia repair. Spinal anesthesia was attempted using a 25 G Vygon needle (120 mm). During the successful puncture, the patient suddenly hyperextended his back. On attempted needle withdrawal and redirection, only the introducer was retrieved; the spinal needle was retained subcutaneously. Surgical removal via a 10 mm incision at 6 cm depth was successful. Multiple factors contributed: morbid obesity, sudden patient movement, weak contact between introducer and needle, and potentially defective equipment. This case underscores the need for vigilance, appropriate equipment, and immediate multidisciplinary management.

Introduction

Spinal anesthesia is a technique widely used for lower abdominal and limb surgeries. While having a generally safe profile and a rapid onset, different complications may be encountered, from nausea/vomiting and post-dural puncture headaches to more severe ones like needle trauma, infections, and spinal cord injuries [1]. Needle breakage or retention is exceptionally rare but can occur under certain mechanical and patient-related factors; its exact incidence is still not well reported [2].

Risk factors that increase the probability of facing complications in spinal anesthesia may include needle characteristics, operator inexperience, and patient anatomy. Particularly, obese patients may present additional difficulties, such as needle insertion depth, tissue resistance and altered spinal landmarks [3].

In this report, we describe the case of a retained spinal needle in a morbidly obese patient that occurred during attempted spinal anesthesia. Additionally, a technical reflection on considerations for prevention is provided for better future practice.

Case presentation

A 19-year-old male (weight 147 kg, BMI ∼50) presented for elective umbilical hernia repair under spinal anesthesia. In the operating room, the patient was placed in a sitting position. A 25 G × 120 mm Vygon spinal needle was used (Figure 1).

Vygon Whitacre spinal needle (25 G, 120 mm with INTRODUCER).

The first spinal attempt by the assigned anesthesiologist failed to obtain cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). The attending anesthesiologist then performed the second attempt. CSF was seen, but before injection, the patient suddenly hyperextended his back. During attempted withdrawal and redirection, only the introducer was removed, while the spinal needle remained inside.

The patient and family were immediately informed. Orthopedic surgery was consulted. Under local anesthesia and after proning, a 10 mm skin incision was made. The needle was located at 6 cm depth and was successfully extracted without complication (Figure 2). This movement was later attributed to a miscommunication: a staff member instructed the patient to recline slightly, but it was misunderstood by the patient as a cue to hyperextend the back, leading to a sudden and forceful movement.

The removed spinal needle (25 G, 120 mm), showing two distinct bends, likely resulting from patient hyperextension and poor introducer-needle integration. No fracture or breakage was noted.

Discussion

Although spinal anesthesia is considered a safe technique, serious complications such as spinal needle retention can arise. These cases are still not well reported, but the literature regarding these incidences has been piling up, especially in difficult cases such as obesity [3]. Multiple key contributors to this incident were identified.

Morbid obesity

Morbid obesity has been established as a major and well-known risk of needle retention and breakage, and has been reported in many incidents [3]. Altered anatomical landmarks and excess adipose tissue make it difficult to accurately identify the needle entry point [4]. In our case, the estimated skin-to-CSF depth of ∼6 cm exceeds the standard length of many spinal needles, exceeding the average length of the spinal needle, which can necessitate multiple attempts to properly insert it, leading to bending and deflection from intended trajectories [5].

Equipment and technical consideration

Poor positioning has been reported as one of the factors that contribute to spinal anesthesia failure [6]. In our case, the patient’s sudden hyperextension likely led to torque forces on the needle while it was in situ. Additionally, attempting to redirect the needle using excessive force increases the risk of needle fracture and bending [7]. In our case, the spinal needle was bended at multiple angles as seen in Figure 2, highlighting not only the use of excessive force in redirection, but also suggesting that the needle may have been structurally compromised (e.g., metal fatigue or prior damage during storage). The redirection of the needle without simultaneous redirection of the introducer was another suggested factor for needle insertion failure [3]. In our case, the separation of the introducer from the spinal needle under stress (Figure 2) implies possible slippage or faulty locking between the two components, leading to incoherent needle redirection.

It also worth noting that prior to the procedure, the spinal needle was visually inspected and appeared intact without any detectable deformities. Following the incident, examination of the broken fragment revealed that the fracture occurred near the junction between the distal end of the needle and the introducer exit, which is a known area of mechanical stress. The sharp and relatively rigid edge of the introducer tip may create a weak point at that interface. This is specially the case when torque or bending forces are applied during insertion.

The event was indeed reported to the manufacturer for further evaluation.

From this perspective, it remains important to note that pre-procedural inspection of spinal needles and introducers should not be overlooked. It should also be coupled with gentle handling during insertion, and awareness of this potential weak point. These steps may help prevent similar occurrences. We should also not report such incidents to manufacturers as it contributes to quality improvement and device refinement.

Prevention strategies

Incidents related to spinal anesthesia, such as needle retention and fracture, could be avoided through multiple measures. Ultrasound-guided spinal anesthesia has been suggested to identify depth and anatomical landmarks, and thus improve first-pass success and reduce the need to repeat the procedure multiple times [8,9]. Additionally, the use of larger needles, ensuring proper patient positioning, and withdrawing the spinal needle into the intruder before attempting to redirect it were proposed to limit needle bending and fracture in the future [10].

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare but important complication of spinal anesthesia in morbidly obese patients. Prompt identification, transparent communication, and surgical support led to safe resolution. Technical, patient-related, and equipment-related causes must be anticipated in similar scenarios.
