Asthma during pregnancy: how obstetricians regard asthmatic patients’ treatment
Authors: Ilkay Keskinel, Müzeyyen Eryılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsthma is the most common pulmonary condition encountered during pregnancy. Based on our clinical experience as a pulmonologist and an internal medicine specialist, we frequently manage pregnant women with asthma, often without prior referral from an obstetrician. This study aimed to evaluate obstetricians’ clinical approaches to asthma management during pregnancy using a structured questionnaire.MethodsA 12-item questionnaire was developed and distributed electronically to obstetricians nationwide. Participation was voluntary, and respondents were assured of full anonymity. The survey was administered via e-mail through a secure online platform.ResultsAmong the participants, 26 (61.9%) were specialists, while 16 (38.1%) held academic titles as associate professors or professors. Regarding consultation practices, 23 obstetricians (54.8%) reported consistently consulting a pulmonologist from the early stages of pregnancy, 10 (23.8%) did so frequently, 8 (19.0%) occasionally, and 1 (2.4%) stated that they never sought consultation. In terms of delivery preferences for asthmatic patients, 32 respondents (76.2%) favored vaginal delivery, while 10 (23.8%) preferred cesarean section. Among those who opted for cesarean delivery, 41 (97.6%) reported a preference for regional anesthesia, and only 1 (2.4%) indicated the use of general anesthesia.ConclusionAsthmatic pregnant patients should be reassured both by obstetricians and by the pulmonologists or internists involved in their care that the majority of medications used in asthma management are classified as Category B and are generally considered safe during pregnancy. Physicians should remain aware of their dual responsibility, both ethical and legal, when making therapeutic decisions during this sensitive period.Trial registrationThis study was registered by the scientific board of Fatih Sultan Mehmet Education and Research Hospital (FSM EAH-KAEK 2023/71).Keywords: Asthma, Pregnancy, Pregnant, Pulmonologist, Internal medicine, Survey, Inhalers, Vaccines, Steroids, Fetal health

Background

Bronchial asthma is one of the most common chronic illnesses, affecting approximately 300 million people worldwide [1]. In individuals with asthma, chronic inflammation of the airways leads to symptoms such as wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and coughing. The global prevalence of asthma is estimated to range between 1% and 18% [1]. Studies conducted in Turkey have reported prevalence rates between 1% and 16.8% [2–21]. While asthma is more common in males during early life, it tends to become more prevalent in females in later years [22].

Asthma is the most common pulmonary disease encountered during pregnancy [23]. Its prevalence among pregnant women worldwide is estimated to be between 3% and 12% [24]. However, not all cases of dyspnea during pregnancy should be attributed to asthma. Shortness of breath may also occur in non-asthmatic pregnant women, often due to hormonal changes—particularly the effects of progesterone—or mechanical factors. A key distinction between pregnancy-related dyspnea and asthma is the absence of accompanying symptoms such as cough and wheezing. Gestational dyspnea, which is not uncommon, has been reported in up to 70% of pregnant women [23].

Regarding the course of asthma during pregnancy, approximately one-third of patients show no significant change in clinical symptoms, while another third experience worsening symptoms, and the remaining third report symptom improvement [1, 23–25]. Although asthma exacerbations can occur at any stage of pregnancy, they are more frequently observed during the latter part of the second trimester. Fortunately, acute asthma attacks during labor are rare, likely due to the influence of endogenous corticosteroids. As a result, up to 90% of pregnant women with asthma give birth without experiencing asthma-related complications [23].

There is currently insufficient evidence to suggest that commonly used asthma medications—such as inhaled corticosteroids, beta2-agonists, montelukast, or even theophylline—increase the risk of fetal anomalies. As a general principle, pregnant women with asthma should be managed in the same way as non-pregnant asthmatic patients [26, 27]. Notably, long-acting beta2-agonists should never be used as monotherapy and must be prescribed in combination with inhaled corticosteroids [23, 28].

In our clinical practice as a pulmonologist and an internal medicine specialist, we frequently encounter pregnant women with asthma, many of whom are not referred by their obstetricians. It is not uncommon for obstetricians to manage pregnant women with mild asthma or those without active symptoms without seeking pulmonary consultation. In this study, we aimed to assess obstetricians’ approaches to the management of asthma during pregnancy through a structured questionnaire.

Methods

The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Fatih Sultan Mehmet Training and Research Hospital on April 27, 2023, under the protocol number FSMEAH-KAEK 2023/55, and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

A 12-item questionnaire, developed based on relevant literature, was used to explore obstetricians’ perspectives on the necessity of consulting pulmonology and internal medicine departments for pregnant women with asthma, with a specific focus on their views regarding the use of asthma medications during pregnancy. This observational, cross-sectional study involved the distribution of survey forms via email to 528 obstetricians across the country. Participation was voluntary, and respondents completed and returned the survey anonymously via email. The data collection began in May 2023, and the evaluation of responses was completed by the end of June 2023, within a one-month assessment period.

The questionnaire included demographic items related to participants’ professional titles and workplace settings. Questions 4 and 10 allowed for multiple responses. The full set of survey items is presented in Fig. 1.

The data were entered by the investigators into a pre-structured Excel datasheet. To ensure accuracy and plausibility, two authors independently verified the data at random during the descriptive analysis phase. Survey results were presented as frequencies (n, %).

Results

Unfortunately, the response rate was low: only 45 out of 528 obstetricians responded to the survey (8.5%). Of these, three declined to complete the questionnaire, citing that they do not provide care for pregnant women. As a result, the final analysis includes responses from 42 obstetricians (7.9%).

Among the respondents, 26 (61.9%) were specialists, while 16 (38.1%) were associate professors or professors. Regarding workplace distribution, 16 obstetricians (38.1%) worked in university hospitals, 13 (31%) in private practice, 7 (16.7%) in state hospitals, 3 (7.1%) in specialized gynecology/obstetrics hospitals, and 3 (7.1%) in polyclinics. None of the respondents reported working in family health centers.

When asked about collaboration with pulmonologists, 23 obstetricians (54.8%) reported that they always consult a pulmonologist from the early stages of pregnancy. Ten (23.8%) consult in most cases, 8 (19%) rarely do so, and 1 (2.4%) stated they never consult a pulmonologist for pregnant asthmatic patients.

Regarding asthma medication use in the first trimester, participants responded to the statement: “I do not recommend the use of asthma medications during the first trimester in pregnant women with asthma.” Among them, 20 obstetricians (47.6%) selected “always,” indicating that they consistently do not recommend the use of such medications in this period. Ten (23.8%) chose “mostly,” and another 10 (23.8%) selected “rarely,” reflecting a more flexible approach. Only 2 respondents (4.8%) answered “never,” meaning they always recommend asthma medication use during the first trimester.

In terms of modifying treatments initiated by pulmonologists, 29 obstetricians (69%) indicated that they do not interfere with asthma management during pregnancy. However, 5 (11.9%) reported that they mostly, and 8 (19%) that they rarely, discontinue asthma medications in the first trimester, even if prescribed by a pulmonologist.

Fourteen obstetricians (33.3%) believe that asthma treatment in pregnant patients does not differ from that in non-pregnant asthmatics, whereas 28 (66.7%) disagree with this view.

Regarding the preferred mode of delivery in asthmatic pregnant women, 32 obstetricians (76.2%) reported a preference for vaginal delivery, while 10 (23.8%) favored cesarean section. When cesarean delivery is performed, 41 obstetricians (97.6%) preferred regional anesthesia, and only 1 (2.4%) reported a preference for general anesthesia.

In terms of pulmonology follow-up, 22 obstetricians (52.4%) stated that asthmatic pregnant patients should see a pulmonologist monthly, while 14 (33.3%) believed that a consultation every three months is sufficient. Six respondents (14.3%) indicated that such patients should only consult a pulmonologist in the event of an acute asthma exacerbation.

All 42 obstetricians (100%) expressed confidence that asthmatic patients can complete pregnancy without complications, provided appropriate follow-up is ensured.

With respect to vaccination safety during pregnancy (Fig. 3), 38 obstetricians (90.5%) considered the influenza vaccine to be safe, 31 (73.8%) viewed mRNA COVID-19 vaccines as safe, and 23 (54.8%) believed that pneumococcal vaccines can be safely administered during pregnancy.

Discussion

The primary objective in asthma management is achieving ‘asthma control’, which encompasses not only the regulation of current symptoms but also the prevention of potential future risks [1]. Our study highlights a number of key trends in obstetricians’ attitudes toward asthma management in pregnancy, with important implications for clinical practice.

In clinical practice, it is frequently observed that expectant mothers tend to discontinue their regular asthma medications during pregnancy due to concerns about potential harm to the fetus [29]. While this behavior is understandable, as many fear that anti-asthmatic drugs might affect fetal development, it is not always evidence-based. In some cases, obstetricians may also act with excessive caution, reducing or discontinuing medications without prior consultation with a pulmonologist. Although well-intentioned, such decisions may increase the risk of asthma exacerbations and negatively impact both maternal and fetal outcomes.

To better understand these prescribing patterns and safety perceptions, it is useful to consider how medication safety in pregnancy has traditionally been classified in the literature. In this discussion, reference will occasionally be made to the pregnancy categories previously proposed by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), as they are cited in the literature we have consulted. It is important to note, however, that the FDA has eliminated the use of categories A, B, C, D, and X through the implementation of the “Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule” (PLLR or final rule), having determined that such categorizations often led to misinterpretation and inappropriate clinical decisions [30]. With this regulatory context in mind, we now turn to our findings regarding obstetricians’ views on specific asthma medications during pregnancy.

Thirty-three obstetricians (78.6%) participating in our survey consider inhaled beta2-mimetics to be safe for use during pregnancy. To contextualize this finding, we reviewed current recommendations on beta2-agonist use in pregnancy. Terbutaline is recommended as the first-line option among short-acting beta2-agonists in pregnant patients [28]. The use of long-acting beta2-mimetics, such as salmeterol and formoterol, as monotherapy should be avoided during the first trimester due to insufficient safety data [1, 28]. However, when used in combination with inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting beta2-mimetics are considered safe during pregnancy and may be continued when clinically indicated, as supported by current guidelines [1, 28]. It is also essential to note that long-acting beta2-mimetics should never be prescribed alone, and must always be combined with inhaled corticosteroids to ensure both efficacy and safety. It has been demonstrated that the combination of a low-dose inhaled corticosteroid with a long-acting beta2-agonist in the treatment of pregnant women with asthma does not present a higher risk of major congenital malformations compared to the use of high-dose inhaled corticosteroids alone [31]. Budesonide, a category B inhaled corticosteroid, is considered safe for use in asthmatic patients during pregnancy [32]. Although systemic corticosteroids are generally regarded as non-teratogenic, dexamethasone and betamethasone are known to cross the placenta to a greater extent than prednisone, prednisolone, and methylprednisolone [23, 33].

Twenty-six (61.9%) of the physicians who completed our questionnaire believe that oral antihistamines are safe during pregnancy, while 21 (50%) consider intranasal antihistamines to be safe.

This reflects a generally cautious but partially aligned approach with current guidelines. According to the literature, certain intranasal antihistamines such as azelastine, as well as first-generation oral antihistamines including brompheniramine and hydroxyzine, should be avoided during pregnancy [23, 32, 34]. Desloratadine, fexofenadine, and azelastine fall under category C antihistamines. In contrast, cetirizine, levocetirizine, and loratadine—often referred to as ‘second-generation’ antihistamines—are generally considered safe for use during pregnancy [23, 32, 34]. Cetirizine, a carboxylic acid metabolite of hydroxyzine [35], has demonstrated no association with adverse outcomes, even when used during the first trimester [36].

Some leukotriene receptor antagonists, specifically montelukast and zafirlukast, are classified as category B drugs for use in pregnancy [23], whereas zileuton is contraindicated [32]. A cross-sectional epidemiological study conducted in Denmark found no increased risk of major congenital anomalies in the infants of asthmatic pregnant women treated with montelukast [37]. Similarly, a Japanese research group reported no rise in major congenital anomalies associated with montelukast use, including when administered during the first trimester [38].

These findings support the relative safety of montelukast during pregnancy. However, this evidence appears to be underrecognized in clinical practice: only 13 obstetricians (31%) participating in our study considered these drugs to be safe for use in pregnancy.

There is limited data regarding the safety of biologic agents such as omalizumab in pregnant women with asthma [39]. Although omalizumab, classified as a category B drug during pregnancy, has not been associated with an increased risk of major congenital anomalies in infants born to asthmatic mothers, the decision to initiate or continue biologic therapy during pregnancy should be individualized [23, 40–42]. Patients should be informed about the potential benefits and side effects of the treatment, and made aware of the risks that severe, uncontrolled asthma may pose to the fetus.

Despite growing evidence supporting the relative safety of omalizumab, particularly when clinically justified, this option remains underutilized in obstetric practice: only 4 (9.5%) of all respondents in our study considered this treatment suitable for pregnant patients.

Regarding allergen-specific immunotherapy during pregnancy, although no fetal complications have been reported to date, existing evidence predominantly involves cases where treatment was initiated before conception. For this reason, it is generally advised not to begin or escalate allergen immunotherapy during pregnancy; however, continuing a previously established regimen is considered acceptable [23, 43]. Despite this, the obstetricians surveyed in our study exhibited an even more conservative stance: all but 2 respondents (4.8%) indicated that allergen-specific immunotherapy should be avoided entirely during pregnancy. This suggests a significant level of caution that may reflect concerns about medicolegal risk or a lack of familiarity with current recommendations.

In cases where asthma is accompanied by allergic rhinitis, current guidelines recommend intranasal budesonide as the preferred treatment option during pregnancy. This recommendation is based on its more favorable safety profile compared to other intranasal corticosteroids, most of which—including triamcinolone—are classified as category C drugs [23]. Although intranasal triamcinolone has not been definitively associated with an increased risk of major congenital malformations or spontaneous abortion, some reports suggest a higher incidence of respiratory tract anomalies, such as choanal atresia, in infants exposed to this agent in utero [44].

Although some clinical guidelines support the use of oral corticosteroids for the treatment of severe asthma exacerbations during pregnancy, their use, particularly in the first trimester, should be approached with caution. This is due to a possible, albeit limited, association with adverse fetal outcomes [34]. The concern is especially pronounced for corticosteroids such as dexamethasone and betamethasone, which exhibit higher placental transfer compared to alternatives like prednisone or methylprednisolone [23, 33]. As such, in early pregnancy, the decision to initiate oral corticosteroids should be based on a careful assessment of the potential maternal and fetal risks versus the anticipated therapeutic benefits.

Twelve obstetricians (28.6%) in our study indicated that they consider herbal cough syrups to be safe during pregnancy. One of the most frequently used herbal formulations in this context contains Hedera helix (English ivy) leaf extract. Although one review suggests that this compound may be safe for short-term use during pregnancy [45], the European Union Herbal Monograph advises against its use due to a lack of sufficient safety data [46]. Other commonly included ingredients in these syrups—such as Thymus serpyllum (wild thyme) and Althea officinalis (marshmallow)—are similarly limited by the absence of robust safety data regarding their effects in pregnant populations [47, 48]. These findings underscore the cautious stance taken by most obstetricians in our sample and reflect the broader uncertainty in the literature concerning the safety of herbal preparations during pregnancy.

Another frequently used component in herbal cough syrups is Glycyrrhiza glabra (licorice). Licorice is known to cause hypokalemia and hypertension, and excessive use may lead to preterm birth [49]. In a study by Räikkönen et al., children born to women who consumed 500 mg or more of glycyrrhiza extract per week during pregnancy were more likely to develop cognitive or psychiatric problems by the age of 8.1 years, compared to those whose mothers consumed little or none [50]. An over-the-counter herbal cough syrup available in our country contains 825 mg of licorice extract per 30 mL and is recommended at a dosage of 10 mL three times daily. This results in a daily intake well above the threshold linked to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in the study by Räikkönen et al. Given this potential risk, it is crucial that obstetricians inquire about herbal product use and provide clear counseling regarding the safety of such over-the-counter remedies during pregnancy.

Twenty-eight obstetricians (66.7%) in our study reported the belief that asthma treatment should differ in pregnant women compared to non-pregnant individuals. This perception may reflect heightened concerns regarding fetal safety or uncertainty about the teratogenic risks of asthma medications. However, current international guidelines emphasize that the core principles of asthma management remain the same during pregnancy as in the general population, underscoring the importance of maintaining disease control to prevent adverse maternal and fetal outcomes [26, 27, 51].

Beyond pharmacologic considerations, other aspects of obstetric management in asthmatic patients, such as mode of delivery, also warrant attention. Thirty-two obstetricians (76.2%) in our study reported a preference for vaginal delivery over cesarean section in pregnant women with asthma. This finding is consistent with national recommendations, which state that vaginal delivery is appropriate for asthmatic patients—particularly those with mild to moderate and well-controlled disease. The 2020 Update of the Asthma Diagnosis and Treatment Guide by the Turkish Thoracic Society and the Turkish National Allergy and Clinical Immunology Association emphasizes that cesarean section should not be routinely preferred in such cases. Furthermore, both epidural and spinal anesthesia are considered safe and suitable options for labor and delivery in this patient group [23].

In addition to delivery planning, the frequency and structure of pulmonary follow-up during pregnancy is another important aspect of asthma management. Regarding pulmonary follow-up, 22 obstetricians (52.4%) believe that pregnant asthmatic patients should visit a pulmonologist monthly, 14 (33.3%) support visits every three months, and 6 (14.3%) recommend pulmonology evaluation only during exacerbations. In alignment with the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) recommendations, we advise that asthmatic pregnant patients undergo monthly pulmonary evaluation throughout pregnancy [1].

In fact, when asthma is well controlled, it is not expected to pose significant risks to either the mother or the fetus [1]. Supporting this, all participating obstetricians in our study agreed that controlled asthma does not adversely affect pregnancy outcomes.

In addition to pharmacologic and clinical management, preventive strategies such as vaccination play a critical role in optimizing maternal and fetal outcomes. Expectant mothers have been routinely vaccinated for tetanus for years, and most express no concern regarding this vaccine. All vaccines other than live vaccines are considered safe during pregnancy [52]. Live vaccines should only be administered when there is a significant risk of morbidity or mortality to the mother or fetus. When administered, the antibodies produced can cross the placenta and offer protection to the newborn.

Influenza complications are known to be more severe in pregnant women than in non-pregnant females [53]. The inactivated influenza vaccine is widely accepted as safe [52], and 38 obstetricians (90.5%) in our study endorsed its use. Similarly, both the polysaccharide and conjugate pneumococcal vaccines are considered safe during pregnancy [52–54].

Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumococcus) is a gram-positive, encapsulated bacterium that remains a major cause of respiratory tract infections, including pneumonia, sinusitis, and otitis media. It was also responsible for a substantial number of fatal pneumonia cases during the 1918 influenza pandemic [55, 56]. In pregnant women with underlying medical conditions where pneumococcal vaccination is indicated, the 13-valent conjugate vaccine should be administered before the polysaccharide vaccine. Notably, only 23 obstetricians (54.8%) in our study considered pneumococcal vaccination to be safe during pregnancy.

Pneumococcus remains one of the most prevalent respiratory pathogens, particularly in individuals with underlying pulmonary conditions. It is estimated to cause 20–50% of all pneumonia cases [57]. Additionally, it can lead to a range of other infections, including upper and lower respiratory tract infections, otitis media, sinusitis, meningitis, and sepsis [58, 59].

A systematic review by Fu et al. found no evidence of significant adverse effects associated with COVID-19 vaccination in either pregnant women or their fetuses [60]. These findings are corroborated by a separate review conducted by Badell et al., which further supports the safety of COVID-19 vaccination during pregnancy [61].

Limitations

This study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small, with only 42 obstetricians completing the survey. Although the questionnaire was distributed to a large number of physicians across the country, the response rate was low. This may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of obstetricians.

Second, participation in the study was voluntary, which may have introduced selection bias; it is possible that physicians with a greater interest or experience in managing asthma during pregnancy were more inclined to respond. Additionally, as this was a cross-sectional survey based on self-reported data, the responses may not fully reflect actual clinical practices.

Lastly, the study was conducted within a single country, and institutional differences in medical guidelines or healthcare systems across regions may influence how asthma is managed in pregnant patients elsewhere. Despite these limitations, the study provides important preliminary insights into how obstetricians perceive and manage asthma during pregnancy, and highlights areas where interdisciplinary collaboration can be strengthened.

Conclusions

Both the obstetrician and the pulmonologist or internist responsible for the care of a pregnant woman with asthma should clearly communicate to the patient that the majority of drugs currently used in asthma treatment are classified as “Category B” medications, and are considered safe during pregnancy. Physicians carry not only legal but also ethical responsibility for their patients, and this must be reflected in their guidance. It is essential to emphasize that maternal hypoxia can have direct adverse effects on the fetus.

Raising awareness in asthmatic patients should begin in the pre-pregnancy period. This includes encouraging smoking cessation if applicable, educating patients on how to avoid asthma triggers, and advising them to seek medical attention promptly in the event of an exacerbation.

Our study highlights the need for pulmonologists and internists to maintain closer collaboration with obstetricians and to reinforce the understanding that the management of asthma during pregnancy does not fundamentally differ from the approach used in non-pregnant patients.

In addition to its clinical implications, this study offers a novel contribution to the literature by being, to our knowledge, the first structured investigation focusing specifically on obstetricians’ practices and attitudes regarding asthma management during pregnancy. While existing studies have primarily reflected the views of pulmonologists or examined patient outcomes, our findings provide direct insight into how obstetricians approach this issue in real-world settings. These results are relevant not only within our country, but also have broader significance in the context of global maternal healthcare, where interdisciplinary cooperation remains a key factor in optimizing outcomes for both mother and fetus.
