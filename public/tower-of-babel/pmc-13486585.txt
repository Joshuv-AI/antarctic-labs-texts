DNA-bound avian R2 non-LTR retrotransposon protein recruits a second R2 protein for genome-protective second-strand nicking
Authors: Adedeji M. Aderounmu, Connor A. Horton, Briana Van Treeck, Anthony Rodríguez-Vargas, Kathleen Collins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceRetrotransposons encode a protein with nicking endonuclease and reverse transcriptase activities. This protein binds specific RNA transcripts and positions bound RNA for complementary DNA synthesis at the nicked genomic target-site. How the “first strand” of complementary DNA gains a “second strand” to become stably embedded in the genome is not understood. Using an avian R2 retrotransposon protein to insert genes into the human genome, we show that the second strand of target-site DNA is nicked by a second R2 protein recruited to the DNA-bound first subunit and that this mechanism is important for making minimally disruptive genome insertions. Our findings have broad implications for mechanisms of retrotransposon mobility and for optimizing the therapeutic insertion of transgenes into the human genome.

Selfishly propagating retrotransposons have had widespread success in their colonization of eukaryotic genomes (1). This mobility occurred despite evidence that retrotransposon machinery induces innate immune responses and that new genome insertions inactivate gene function and cause genome instability (2, 3). Animal genomes have predominantly non-long-terminal-repeat (non-LTR) retrotransposons, which encode a protein with nicking endonuclease (EN) and reverse transcriptase (RT) activities (4). Coordination of EN and RT activities supports the target-primed reverse transcription (TPRT) mechanism for new genome insertions (5). In TPRT, nicking of one strand of target-site DNA (the “first strand”) generates a 3′OH that is transferred to the RT active site to prime cDNA synthesis using the protein-positioned RNA template (Fig. 1 A, Left). After TPRT, stable embedding of the first-strand cDNA in the genome requires a nick on the opposite strand (the “second strand”) and second-strand synthesis (Fig. 1 A, Right).

The most streamlined non-LTR retrotransposons encode a single protein with a “restriction-like” EN domain. Retrotransposon proteins with this EN domain generally have sequence-specific target-site recognition, which in evolutionarily successful non-LTR retrotransposons is for a target site in a multicopy noncoding RNA gene (7). The model system for this type of retrotransposon is R2, with particular depth of investigation for R2 from the silk moth Bombyx mori (8). Across animals, R2 retrotransposons have high selectivity for insertion to the coding region of 28S ribosomal RNA, in the 100s of tandemly repeated gene units transcribed by RNA Polymerase I (rDNA loci) (9, 10). In native context, after RNA Polymerase I transcription, the retrotransposon transcript 3′UTR folds to bind R2p and the 5′UTR is formed by a ribozyme self-cleavage that retains 0 to 36 nucleotides (nt) of upstream rRNA on R2 RNA (8, 11). The ORF-encoded protein (R2p) has N-terminal sequence-specific DNA-binding domains, a central RT domain, and a C-terminal EN (Fig. 1 B, Top). Two early-branching lineages of R2p predominate: Lineage D R2p, including R2p from B. mori, typically have a single N-terminal zinc-finger (ZnF) and adjacent Myb as their sequence-specific DNA-binding domains, whereas lineage A R2p have three N-terminal ZnF and adjacent Myb (10). R2p across lineages share the ability to bind and bend up to 40 bp of upstream target site and to melt an ~10 bp region centered on the first-strand nick (6, 12, 13) (Fig. 1C).

In previous work, we used avian lineage A R2p to develop a human genome supplementation approach termed precise RNA-mediated insertion of transgenes (PRINT) (14, 15). In this approach, autonomously expressed transgenes are synthesized into rDNA loci after codelivery of two RNAs: an mRNA encoding an avian R2p, such as from Taeniopygia guttata (hereafter TaGu) or Zonotrichia albicollis, and a template RNA encoding the transgene payload flanked by compact 3′ and 5′ modules. The 3′ module includes an avian R2 3′UTR pseudoknot (PK)-hinge-hairpin motif for R2p binding (Fig. 1 B, Bottom) followed by a 3′ tail of 4 nt that base-pairs with the target-site primer. The optional 5′ module supports formation of ~20 to 30 bp between the cDNA 3′ end and upstream target site (final step in Fig. 1A). This annealing is the favored pathway for making the transgene 5′ junction, but other DNA repair pathways support cDNA joining to upstream target site without homology (16, 17). Because truncated cDNAs are not able to anneal to the upstream target-site, suppression of the homology-independent repair pathways results in only full-length transgene insertions (17, 18).

Among the mysteries about mechanisms that support non-LTR retrotransposon mobility, it remains to be established whether R2p makes the second-strand nick in cells, and if it does, whether this R2p activity is critical for new genome insertions. The few R2p that have been assayed as purified proteins make a second-strand nick at a conserved position 2 bp upstream from the first-strand nick, stimulated by TPRT (8, 19). This activity is inefficient relative to first-strand nicking for some R2p, but the nick position mapped using purified R2p is consistent with frequent 2 bp deletion at B. mori R2 junctions with upstream rDNA in the organism (8, 20). Possibly unique to B. mori R2, second-strand nicking is stimulated by an RNA motif at the 5′ end of the ORF that binds R2p in a mutually exclusive manner with 3′UTR RNA (21, 22). However, the 5′ RNA is not necessary for precise genome insertions (23). Only limited experimental evidence supports the hypothesis that R2p second-strand nicking is important in cells. First, PRINT using an R2p with reduced EN activity produced genome insertions with increased heterogeneity of rDNA position at transgene 5′ junctions (14). Second, deletion of the N-terminal ZnF or two ZnFs (ZnF3-2) from Z. albicollis R2p also increased the heterogeneity of rDNA positions joined to transgene 5′ ends, correlated with a biochemically demonstrated defect in second-strand nicking (19). At TPRT initiation, ZnF3-2 is positioned on upstream DNA at its split into single strands (Fig. 1C), implying that ZnF3-2 deletion could alter the positioning of the intact second strand prior to nicking. Many other hypotheses for the role of ZnF3-2 are also plausible, including altered recruitment of a cellular EN.

Here, we demonstrate a mechanism for second-strand nicking and establish its significance for new genome insertions. Persistent DNA binding by the R2p that launched TPRT (the “first” subunit) recruits another R2p to make the second-strand nick (the “second” subunit). This recruitment requires displacement or disruption of the PK in bound template RNA. It does not require the second subunit to have sequence-specific DNA-binding domains, consistent with the persistent grip of the first-subunit R2p on the upstream target site. Screening numerous purified R2p sequence variants allowed us to generate a double-mutant R2p with nearly eliminated second-strand nicking activity yet uncompromised nicking of the first strand. PRINT assays demonstrate that compromised second-strand nicking reduces transgene insertion efficiency and results in a shift from a predominantly precise junction of rDNA with the transgene 5′ end to heterogeneous target-site duplications of ~1 kbp that oblige more target-site DNA repair. These phenotypes from reduced second-strand nicking can be ameliorated by coexpression of an N-terminally truncated R2p with second-strand but not first-strand nicking activity. Overall, our insights delineate a second-strand nicking mechanism that minimizes genome perturbation upon non-LTR retrotransposon insertion. This mechanism adds an additional layer of complexity to non-LTR retrotransposon protein function. We demonstrate the importance of efficient R2p-mediated second-strand nicking for PRINT, and knowledge of its mechanism enables additional PRINT optimization for therapeutic transgene insertion to the human genome.

Results

Separate Subunits Make the First and Second Nicks.

We investigated whether second-strand nicking by purified avian R2p was catalyzed by the same DNA-bound R2p that did first-strand nicking. This mechanism has precedent in studies of the monomeric restriction EN BcnI (24), the monomeric homing EN I-BmoI (25), and Cas12 ENs (26), all demonstrating that the same active site makes first-strand and second-strand nicks without monomer exchange from DNA. Instead, structural rearrangements of protein and/or DNA after first-strand nicking place the second strand in the first-strand nick active site with the same strand polarity. As an R2p system, we used bacterially expressed and highly purified TaGu (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A), motivated by its strong second-strand nicking activity, robust PRINT, and near-atomic resolution structure for the protein-DNA-RNA ternary complex at TPRT initiation (6, 14). To examine whether a second subunit was required for second-strand nicking, we developed a two-step incubation assay. In the first incubation, TaGu EN-dead (END, with active-site D1067N mutation) was bound to 64 bp target-site DNA prenicked on the first strand (the bottom strand in illustrations here). If template RNA was added, we used a compact motif for R2p binding from Geospiza fortis R2 (14, 15) annotated here as Gf_68 (Fig. 1 B and C). Protein in the first incubation was in 10-fold molar excess of target site (100 nM vs. 10 nM) to promote saturation of target-site DNA. Subsequently, in a second incubation, wild-type (WT) TaGu or a variant TaGu protein was added in twofold excess of first-step protein, with or without dNTPs to support TPRT. We designed an N-terminally truncated version of TaGu lacking all of the DNA-binding domains, designated TaGuC, paralleling the N-terminally truncated B. mori R2p we optimized as an RT for RNA sequencing library production (27). We expected TaGuC would not support first-strand nicking or TPRT due to lack of upstream target-site binding, but it retains an active EN and could therefore support second-strand nicking. Reaction products were resolved by denaturing PAGE and imaged to detect the fluorophore at the second-strand 5′ end. In Fig. 1 D–G the fluorophore is represented by a green square, and in Fig. 1H both strands are labeled with distinct fluorophores represented as yellow and green squares; these symbols are consistent in subsequent figure panels.

The two-step incubation assays revealed that the protein added second performed second-strand nicking (Fig. 1D). If RNA was included in the first incubation of TaGu-END with prenicked target site, robust second-strand nicking required addition of dNTPs, such that the RT domain of TaGu-END could perform TPRT (Fig. 1D, compare lanes 6–7 to 8–9). This result matches expectation based on many previous experiments documenting an inhibitory influence of bound 3′UTR RNA on second-strand nicking. Omission of Gf_68 was not as effective as the combination of Gf_68 and dNTPs for stimulation of second-strand nicking (Fig. 1D, compare lanes 1–4 to 8–9), paralleling observations for first-strand nicking (28) that linked RNA binding to stabilization of active R2p conformation. Curiously, second-step incubation with TaGu-WT or truncated TaGuC supported second-strand nicking, albeit with less efficiency using TaGuC. The TaGuC deficit could reflect perturbation of protein folding or solubility as a monomer, since higher concentrations of TaGuC increased second-strand nicking efficiency (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Therefore, the second-strand nick made by avian R2p does not require sequence-specific DNA recognition.

We next performed two-step incubations with the first incubation containing TaGu or TaGu-END and Gf_68 and the second incubation containing dNTPs and either TaGu or TaGuC that was WT or END. As initial controls, we performed single-step incubations with TaGu or TaGuC. TaGuC alone did not support second-strand nicking even on prenicked target site (Fig. 1E, lane 3), suggesting a requirement for DNA-bound R2p to present the second strand for nicking. Comparing TaGu-END vs. TaGu-WT in the first incubation, results were generally parallel with a slight increase in second-strand nicking efficiency using TaGu-WT in the first incubation (Fig. 1E, compare lanes 4–7 to 8–11), likely because the first-step incubation protein was in excess of target site. A Y-shaped substrate with RNA fully base-paired to TPRT cDNA, potentially mimicking post-TPRT nucleic acid structure, also supported second-strand nicking (Fig. 1F, see substrate schematic at right of the gel). The Y-shaped substrate was favorable, allowing excess TaGu-WT from the first incubation to nick the second strand independent of the second-incubation protein added (Fig. 1F, compare lanes 4–7 to 8–11). It is not known in cells whether RNA has been removed from cDNA prior to second-strand nicking, so both prenicked DNA and Y-shaped substrates are potentially physiological.

To investigate the requirement for first-strand nicking prior to second-strand nicking, we used intact target-site duplex. We first surveyed second-strand nicking in reactions with TaGu bound to either Gf_68 or a version of Gf_68 with the PK disrupted by sequence substitution (Gf_scr, SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). Like Gf_68, Gf_scr stimulates first-strand nicking (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D, compare lanes 3 and 4); however Gf_scr did not support TPRT (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D, compare lanes 5 and 6). As expected, Gf_68 blocked second-strand nicking without dNTPs, but this block was released by TPRT with added dNTPs (Fig. 1G, lanes 3 and 5). TaGu bound to Gf_scr had an intermediate level of second-strand nicking that was independent of dNTP addition (Fig. 1G, lanes 4 and 6). This is consistent with second-strand nicking results for an RNA truncation series assayed using a different recombinant version of TaGu (13). Since Gf_scr reduced second-strand nicking compared to Gf_68 + dNTPs, even PK-disrupted RNA is somewhat inhibitory for second-strand nicking. Nonetheless, Gf_scr provides a conformational stabilization of TaGu that stimulates EN activity without introducing a requirement for RNA release by TPRT.

We exploited these features of Gf_scr in two-step incubation assays. In the first incubation, intact target site was bound by TaGu-END with Gf_68 or Gf_scr, followed by second-incubation addition of TaGu or TaGuC. No first-strand nicking would occur if first-incubation TaGu-END saturated every target-site duplex and remained bound to the target site during second-step incubation, because TaGu-END dissociation would be required to allow a different R2p to perform the first-strand nick. First-strand nicking product was detected for first-incubation TaGu-END with Gf_68 and second-incubation TaGu-WT (Fig. 1 H, Top panel set, lanes 4 and 6) and at lower level when Gf_scr replaced Gf_68 (lanes 9 and 11). This profile was not predictive for second-strand nicking: Robust second-strand nicking was observed when Gf_scr replaced Gf_68 under any second-subunit incubation condition, or if Gf_68 binding to first-incubation TaGu-END was released by dNTP addition for TPRT (Fig. 1 H, Bottom panel set, lanes 6–7 and 9–12). These results establish that first-strand nicking is not inherently a prerequisite for second-strand nicking. Overall, we conclude that the second-strand nick is made by a second R2p recruited to the initial R2p–DNA complex.

Uncoupling of First-strand and Second-strand Nicking Efficiency.

At TPRT initiation, the second strand is threaded across the back side of the EN domain, the adjacent zinc knuckle (ZnK), and ZnF3-2 (Fig. 2A, with the second strand in green; see also Fig. 1B for R2p motifs and Fig. 1C for overall context). From the upstream target-site perspective, strand melting around the nick sites places the second-nick position 4 nt into the single-stranded sequence TTAA^GGTAGC. The adenosine immediately 5′ of where the second nick will occur is hydrogen-bonded to the first base-pair of the template RNA PK, which is sandwiched by contacts with ZnF3-2 and NTE-1 (Fig. 2A, nick site is indicated with a blue arrow; see Fig. 1B for R2p motifs). When the template RNA PK is displaced from ZnF3-2 and NTE-1 by TPRT, the single-stranded part of upstream second-strand DNA can shift closer to these formerly RNA-bound motifs (6) (Fig. 2B). Even after second-strand nicking, the extended contact surface between upstream duplex DNA and R2p is preserved (6). This structural context suggests that the inhibition of second-strand nicking imposed by deletion of Z. albicollis ZnF3 or ZnF3-2 (19) could arise from altered second-strand positioning on the DNA-bound R2p. We used the two-step incubation assay with prenicked target site to investigate whether the contribution of ZnF3-2 is from the DNA-bound R2p and/or the second-subunit R2p. If first-step incubation used TaGu-∆ZnF3-2-END instead of TaGu-END, subsequent addition of TaGu-WT, TaGu-∆ZnF3-2, or TaGuC resulted in less second-strand nicking (Fig. 2C, compare lanes 4–6 to lanes 10–12). As controls, no second-strand nicking was detected if the second incubation was with an END version of the proteins (Fig. 2C, lanes 1–3 and 7–9). With TaGu-END as the DNA-bound R2p, second-strand nicking by TaGu-WT or TaGu-∆ZnF3-2 was similar (Fig. 2C, lanes 4 and 5). Parallel results were observed in assays using the Y-shaped substrate (Fig. 2D). We conclude that DNA-bound TaGu-∆ZnF3-2 supports less second-strand nicking than DNA-bound TaGu-WT.

To investigate whether ZnF3-2 could directly recruit the second-subunit R2p, we used AlphaFold3 modeling (29) to evaluate potential protein–protein interaction. AlphaFold3 predictions for docking a second R2p to the initial R2p–DNA complex place the second-subunit EN domain proximal to the first-subunit ZnF3-2 (Fig. 2E). This positioning sterically clashes with RNA in the TPRT initiation complex (Fig. 2 E, Left). After TPRT and second-strand nicking, the upstream second-strand end is closer to NTE-1 (6). If the intact second strand moves into this position after TPRT, it would be well positioned for nicking by a second R2p recruited by protein–protein interaction (Fig. 2 E, Right). We suggest that dimerization does not situate the second-subunit R2p for second-strand synthesis, in part because the first-subunit R2p retains contact with the target site (Discussion).

Because ZnF3-2 has many functional roles, we sought sequence changes less drastic than ZnF3-2 deletion that would selectively cripple second-strand nicking with no or limited effect on first-strand nicking. We performed a screen of side-chain substitutions in the TaGu EN domain, each assayed as bacterially expressed, highly purified protein. These TaGu variants were designed based on side-chain proximity to the EN active site or melted target-site single strands, R2p sequence conservation, and lack of structural disruption judged by AlphaFold3 modeling. Initial screening eliminated from further consideration any variants with strongly compromised first-strand nicking or no discernable nicking deficiency. Two variants had the sought-after selective crippling of second-strand nicking relative to first-strand nicking: E1040Q and K1052A. The side-chains of these two amino acids extend from a loop that has conserved sequence across lineage A and D R2p (Fig. 3 A and B). TaGu-WT and variants E1040Q, K1052A, and their combination (hereafter EK/QA), as purified proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), all retained first-strand nicking activity on intact target-site duplex (Fig. 3C). Using prenicked target site to assay second-strand nicking, each of the variants had reduced activity relative to TaGu-WT, especially TaGu-EK/QA (Fig. 3D). The K1052A substitution but not the E1040Q substitution had a strong negative impact on second-strand nicking of the Y-shaped substrate, but again TaGu-EK/QA was the most compromised (Fig. 3E). Similar results were observed in single-step reactions with intact target-site DNA under TPRT conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C; compare ratio of second-nick product to TPRT cDNA).

We supported the findings above using two-step incubations to assay second-strand nicking. Using prenicked target site, we compared first-step incubation with TaGu-END or TaGu-EQ/KA, each bound to Gf_68, followed by second-step incubation with TaGu-END, TaGu-WT, TaGu-EQ/KA, or TaGuC. The second incubation contained dNTPs to support TPRT by first-incubation TaGu-END or TaGu-EQ/KA. There was little difference in second-strand nicking comparing first-incubation TaGu-END vs. TaGu-EQ/KA, but the second-incubation R2p strongly influenced second-strand nicking: TaGu-EK/QA was severely inhibited relative to TaGu-WT or TaGuC (Fig. 3F, compare lanes 3–5 to 8–10). These observations suggest that DNA-bound TaGu-EQ/KA retains function in presenting the second strand for recognition by another R2p, but it is compromised in function as the second-subunit R2p. Additional two-step incubation assays compared second-strand nicking by TaGu-WT, E1040Q, K1052A, and EQ/KA on prenicked target site and Y-shaped substrate (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D and E), with results paralleling the respective single-step reactions (Fig. 3 D and E). We conclude that there are distinct EN side-chain requirements for nicking of the target-site first strand vs. second strand. Overall, we conclude that TaGu-EK/QA has selectively crippled second-strand nicking activity.

Second-strand Nicking Contributes to PRINT Efficiency.

The TaGu E1040Q, K1052A, and EK/QA variants provided a long-sought opportunity to extend our previous investigation of whether a lineage A R2p does second-strand nicking in cells (19), and if so, its physiological significance. Analysis of new PRINT insertions provides a convenient system. While transgene insertions disrupt rDNA loci, the high rDNA copy number is permissive for several stably inserted transgenes per cell (14). We cotransfected human hTERT RPE-1 cells with a TaGu mRNA and a template RNA encoding a GFP expression cassette with strong promoter, GFP ORF, and polyadenylation signal (Fig. 4A). The fraction of GFP-positive cells (%GFP+) was quantified by flow cytometry 1 d after transfection. We also quantified median fluorescence intensity of GFP+ cells (MFI), which is a proxy for relative number of transgene insertions per cell (14). PRINT %GFP+ and MFI both decreased when using a TaGu variant compromised for second-strand nicking relative to first-strand nicking (Fig. 4B).

We next investigated whether coexpression of TaGuC could boost the PRINT efficiency of TaGu variants with compromised second-strand nicking. Because TaGuC does not support first-strand nicking or TPRT, its influence would be by complementation of a second-strand nicking defect in trans (Fig. 4C). We tested whether TaGuC-WT, or versions of TaGuC lacking EN activity (END) or RT activity (RT-dead, RTD), could boost the PRINT efficiency of TaGu-EK/QA. PRINT mRNA was combined with a TaGuC mRNA and template RNA at a molar ratio of 1:1:5 (i.e., with equimolar amounts of each mRNA and excess template RNA). Coexpression of TaGuC-RTD or TaGuC-WT improved PRINT by TaGu-EK/QA, but coexpression of TaGuC-END reduced PRINT efficiency (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A; the negative control sample had luciferase mRNA in place of TaGuC mRNA). We then compared the influence of coexpression of TaGuC-RTD vs. TaGuC-END on PRINT by each TPRT-competent TaGu variant compromised for second-strand nicking. Remarkably, each amino acid substitution variant of TaGu that showed compromised second-strand nicking had increased PRINT efficiency when coexpressed with EN-active TaGuC-RTD relative to TaGuC-END, a difference particularly striking for TaGu-EK/QA (Fig. 4D). If TaGu-∆ZnF3-2 was coexpressed with TaGuC-RTD or TaGuC-END, differential “rescue” by TaGuC-RTD vs. TaGuC-END was blunted (Fig. 4 D, Right and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), consistent with DNA-bound TaGu ZnF3-2 participation in second-subunit recruitment (Fig. 2). However, interpreting this outcome has the caveat that TaGu-∆ZnF3-2 was severely inhibited for PRINT, more so than the Z. albicollis equivalent assayed previously (19).

As a complementary assay, we used ddPCR to quantify transgene 3′-end copy number, which is inclusive for 5′-truncated as well as full-length transgenes. The ddPCR readout of total transgene signal uses genomic DNA fragmented by restriction digest to leave the amplicon intact (Fig. 4E, MluI and BamHI). We discriminated the fraction of transgene insertions that were fully repaired duplex DNA by their sensitivity to restriction digest conditions that destroy amplicon integrity (Fig. 4E, MluI and AseI), because the ddPCR signal that remains after restriction digest derives from unrepaired TPRT cDNA in single-stranded state or base-paired to template RNA (17). The ddPCR readout of total transgene copy number generally matched PRINT %GFP readout of functional transgenes, with rank order WT > E1040Q > K1052A > EK/QA (Fig. 4F, left axis; blue bars are total ddPCR signal). A major difference comparing coexpression of TaGuC-RTD vs. TaGuC-END was the decrease in percentage of unrepaired TPRT product when TaGuC-RTD was coexpressed with a TaGu variant compromised for second-strand nicking (Fig. 4F; left-axis orange bars are absolute amount of unrepaired TPRT product, and right-axis horizontal lines are %unrepaired). This was especially striking for TaGu-EK/QA, which had ~90% unrepaired TPRT intermediates when coexpressed with TaGuC-END but only ~60% when coexpressed with TaGuC-RTD. In comparison, TaGu-WT and TaGu-∆ZnF3-2 PRINT had little if any difference in ddPCR product profile comparing coexpression of TaGuC-RTD with TaGuC-END. Altogether, these results implicate R2p-mediated second-strand nicking as a determinant of functional transgene addition to the human genome. The decrease in %unrepaired achieved by coexpression of TaGu-EK/QA with EN-active TaGuC-RTD suggests that second-strand nicking promotes second-strand synthesis.

Compromised Second-Strand Nicking Induces an Extra Burden of DNA Repair.

Insight into potential second-strand nick positions in cells can be inferred from the rDNA side of transgene 5′ junctions using two metrics (19). The first is the fraction of transgene 5′ junctions that occur by annealing of the cDNA 3′ end to upstream rDNA (“Anneal” junctions, see Fig. 5 A, Top Right). Because the template RNA 5′ module has only 28 nt of rDNA sequence, annealing obliges the presence of a second-strand nick close to the first-strand nick. The second metric considers transgene 5′ junctions created by end-joining of cDNA to upstream rDNA (“Join” junctions, Fig. 5A), which can occur via two pathways, with or without microhomology (17). These junctions typically occur with limited (<10 bp) to no deletion of upstream rDNA, indicative of a second-strand nick near the first-strand nick position.

To increase the depth of transgene 5′ junction profiling relative to our previous use of whole genome sequencing, we developed a generalizable long-read approach for insertion profiling that we term RIPPLE-seq (Readout of Insertions by Phage Polymerase Linear Enrichment). For this method, the PRINT template RNA contains a 75 bp amplification module with a phage SP6 promoter and UMI (Fig. 5B). Fragmented genomic DNA from PRINTed cells is used as input for linear amplification by SP6 RNA polymerase transcription, generating a pool of multi-kb RNAs that are converted to duplex DNA without PCR (Fig. 5C). Related methods using T7 RNA polymerase for linear amplification have been previously described (for example, refs. 30 and 31), but RIPPLE-seq use of SP6 RNA polymerase was required due to the use of T7 RNA polymerase for PRINT RNA transcription.

We applied RIPPLE-seq to profile PRINT transgene 5′ junctions for TaGu-WT and its E1040Q, K1052A, and EK/QA variants. Anneal junction formation was strongly compromised in PRINT by TaGu-EK/QA, as was the percentage of full-length transgene insertions (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B–F). Transgene 5′ Join junctions were analyzed to plot upstream rDNA position at the junction, and results were quantified by percentage of junctions near the first-strand nick (Proximal join) or instead with >30 bp target-site deletion (Upstream join) or >5 bp target-site duplication (Downstream join; see schematics in Fig. 5E). Since human rDNA units are ~45 kbp, junction positions are plotted with linear scale close to the first-strand nick position (set as zero) and log scale farther away (Fig. 5F). As expected based on prior analysis using whole genome sequencing reads (17), the RIPPLE-seq profile of rDNA positions for transgene 5′ Join junction formation in TaGu-WT PRINT was overwhelmingly within ~10 bp upstream of the first-strand nick (Fig. 5 F, Top). In comparison, all of the TaGu variants with compromised second-strand nicking showed a shift in distribution of rDNA positions at the transgene 5′ junction, which was especially severe for TaGu-EK/QA (Fig. 5 F and G). A new junction signature emerged with reduced R2p-mediated second-strand nicking: target-site duplications on the order of 1,000 bp (Fig. 5F). The rDNA zone that supports junction formation by target-site duplication is within the region transcribed by RNA Polymerase I (SI Appendix, Fig. S3G). We speculate that as transcription approaches the termination region, the rDNA sense strand (the R2p second strand) is prone to single-strand breaks that can be used for transgene 5′ junction formation.

We then investigated whether coexpression of TaGuC-RTD but not TaGuC-END could rescue the 5′-junction defects of TaGu variants compromised for second-strand nicking. Remarkably, coexpression of TaGu-EK/QA with TaGuC-RTD shifted the new signature of large target-site duplications back toward the TaGu-WT profile of small deletions (Fig. 5 H and I). TaGu-E1040Q and TaGu-K1052A were similarly but much less dramatically responsive to coexpression of TaGuC-RTD vs. TaGuC-END (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–C). In parallel, the proportion of transgene 5′ junctions formed by the Anneal pathway increased if TaGu-EK/QA was coexpressed with TaGuC-RTD (Fig. 5J and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 D and E), as did the percentage of transgenes that were full-length (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F–I).

PRINT Engineering.

The findings above support a second-subunit mechanism for the second-strand nick (Fig. 6A), with implications for PRINT optimization. Previously we found that an order of magnitude range in ratio of PRINT mRNA to template RNA, centered around a 1:3 molar ratio of R2p mRNA to template RNA, gave roughly comparable PRINT efficiencies (14). A potential hypothesis for this previously puzzling observation is that template RNA availability limits RNP assembly and TPRT but R2p level limits second-strand nicking. To investigate this interpretation, we performed PRINT with molar ratios of mRNA to template RNA ranging from 3:1 to 1:200, across roughly three orders of magnitude, while maintaining a constant total amount of RNA. PRINT efficiency remained high across this range (Fig. 6B), and by ddPCR the change in total transgene 3′ copy number was less than threefold (Fig. 6C). RIPPLE-seq profiling of the rDNA positions of transgene 5′ junction formation revealed that reduction in the mRNA amount decreased precise transgene 5′ junction formation while increasing the target-site duplication hallmark of a second-strand nicking deficiency (Fig. 6 D and E). These results are consistent with reduced dimerization at lower R2p concentration in cells. Full-length transgene insertion was similar across the range of ratios but slightly lower at the highest mRNA level (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–E). Anneal junction formation was optimal at the 1:2 and 1:20 ratios (SI Appendix, Fig. S5F). Because mRNA translation efficiency varies across cell types and cell states, we suggest that to optimally balance transgene insertion efficiency with minimization of required DNA repair, PRINT RNA ratio should be optimized by molecular profiling of insertions for each cell type and RNA delivery method used.

Discussion

Non-LTR retrotransposon mobility has been rampant in animal genomes despite only rarely proposed benefit to the organism (1, 32). One strategy that R2 and many other non-LTR retrotransposons independently finessed was the minimization of consequences for the host cell by site-specific insertion to rDNA (8–10). Self-targeting to rDNA provides an especially favorable haven due to the hundreds of gene copies that buffer loss of function from disruption of individual units (7). A second finesse is the TPRT mechanism. Because the same protein coordinately makes a genome nick and uses that nick to prime cDNA synthesis, DNA repair factors cannot compete for access to the nick. On the other hand, second-strand synthesis relies on DNA repair machinery and is therefore vulnerable to interference (17). In this work, we delineate a third finesse of R2 mechanism achieved by cooperation of two R2p subunits. A second-subunit mechanism for second-strand nicking allows the initially bound R2p to continue its protection of upstream DNA even after the second nick, cloaking upstream target-site identity as a single-ended DNA break. If second-strand nicking is compromised, new genome insertions require more complex DNA repair. The large target-site duplications that result may increase the likelihood of transgene deletion by homologous recombination.

A second-subunit mechanism for generating the second-strand nick was first proposed for B. mori R2 (21, 33). We suggest the hypothesis that variations of the same second-strand nicking mechanism are shared by R2p across lineages with conserved reliance on second-strand positioning by the initial DNA-bound R2p. We speculate that protein–protein association is also conserved as a major contribution to recruiting and properly positioning the second R2p. In support of this model, the second-strand nick position 2 bp upstream of the first-strand nick is shared by purified B. mori R2p from lineage D, flour beetle R2p from lineage A, and avian R2p from lineage A (8, 19). Nonetheless, some R2p and R2 RNA features that stimulate second-strand nicking appear unique to a subset of R2 RNPs. For example, B. mori R2 RNA has a structure 5′ of the essential ORF that stimulates second-strand nicking, and B. mori R2p Myb domain gained preference for sequence-specific recognition of downstream duplex (19, 22, 34, 35). Also, avian R2p ZnF3-2, unique to lineage A R2p, contributes to second-strand nicking. Findings from this work will advance structural studies of additional stages of R2p-mediated gene insertion. One of the remaining open questions is whether R2p second-strand nicking is coupled to second-strand synthesis. We suggest that this is unlikely, because R2p recruitment for second-strand nicking is not primarily DNA-mediated, implying that it does not have the same anchor on substrate DNA as the R2p that nicked the first strand. Any second-strand synthesis observed in reconstituted assays of B. mori R2p (36, 37) could be performed by R2p exchange, which appears to have been excluded in recent modeling due to assumption that both nicking reactions are catalyzed by a single R2p without dissociation (37).

In broader perspective, whether mobility of any non-LTR retrotransposon other than R2 shares an R2p-like second-strand nicking mechanism is an open question. It will be of interest to investigate second-strand nicking by the R2-derived R8 and R9 retrotransposon proteins that switched target-site specificity (38, 39), especially because R9 insertion is associated with an 126 bp target-site duplication (39). Non-LTR retrotransposons typically generate a short target-site duplication (7). A few non-LTR retrotransposons may evade the need for a second-strand nick altogether, for example if first-strand nicking is favored adjacent to a DNA gap (40). To date, large-scale sampling of target sites for new non-LTR retrotransposon insertions has been limited. RIPPLE-seq could be transformative for survey and quantification of insertion profiling.

Our findings demonstrate that PRINT is more efficient when R2p contributes second-strand nicking. This insight opens opportunities for PRINT optimization. For example, to maximize conversion of TPRT intermediates into stable, full-length transgenes, there may be a favorable timing for the second-strand nick relative to the first-strand nick or relative to completion of cDNA synthesis. Second-nick timing could be toggled by amount of R2p expression, R2p engineering, and/or fusion or coexpression of R2p with a protein dedicated to making the second nick. Separable tuning of the first-strand vs. second-strand nick could also optimize for a combination of efficient PRINT and low collateral genome damage. PRINT with an EN-tuned R2p allowed transgene-containing cells to proliferate without growth penalty, but it also reduced the percentage of cells containing a transgene (14). We suggest that this trade-off can be adjusted by engineering and/or by characterizing more of the large number of avian R2s in recently sequenced genomes (41). These research avenues have high promise for advancing the efficiency, safety, and broad applicability of TPRT-based methods for transgene supplementation of the human genome.

Materials and Methods

Constructs.

Plasmids, RNAs, and oligonucleotides generated in this work are listed in Dataset S1.

Protein, RNA, and DNA Purification for Biochemical Assays.

Bacterial protein expression and purification used previously described methods (6); proteins retained the fusion tags previously shown to be noninterfering: N-terminal 14xHis–MBP–bdSUMO and C-terminal TwinStrep. After purification, aliquots were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Protein concentrations were calculated by densitometric analysis of Coomassie-stained SDS PAGE gels with bovine serum albumin as standard. RNA expression and purification used previously described methods (6). In brief, in vitro transcription used the HiScribe T7 High Yield Synthesis Kit (NEB, E2040S). Gel-purified RNA was aliquoted, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. DNA oligonucleotides were chemically synthesized (Integrated DNA Technologies) to include a 3′ phosphate when relevant to block unwanted cDNA synthesis. Fluorescent dyes were placed at oligonucleotide 5′ ends: generally IR800CWN for the first strand and/or Cy5 for the second strand, except the second strand of a prenicked duplex or Y-shaped substrate had IR800CWN. Gel-purified annealed target-site DNAs were stored at −20 °C.

Biochemical Assays.

Reactions were performed at 37 °C. TPRT or one-step nicking reactions were performed in TPRT reaction buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM DTT, 2 % w/v PEG-6000) with final concentrations of 50 nM R2p, 400 nM 3′UTR RNA, 10 nM target DNA, and 500 μM dNTPs where applicable, in 20 μL final volume. Components were assembled on ice with R2p incubated with RNA for 10 min to facilitate RNP formation before addition of target-site DNA to initiate reactions.

For two-step incubations, the first incubation was performed by assembling reaction components at final concentrations of 100 nM R2p, 10 nM target DNA or Y-shaped substrate, and 400 nM 3′UTR RNA where applicable, at 20 μL final volume. Target-site DNA was added last after 10 min incubation on ice and the reaction was moved to 37 °C for 30 min. Second-incubation cocktail was assembled to contain 200 nM R2p, and 500 μM dNTPs where applicable, in TPRT reaction buffer. Equal volumes of first- and second-incubation mixes were combined to give final concentrations of 150 nM total R2p (50 nM first subunit and 100 nM second subunit), 5 nM target-site DNA or Y-shaped substrate, 200 nM RNA and 250 μM dNTPs, where applicable, and incubated on ice for 5 min before incubation at 37 °C for 30 min.

For all assays, reactions were stopped after 30 min with equal volume formamide loading buffer (95% v/v deionized formamide, 10 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 0.025% w/v bromophenol blue), incubated with Proteinase K at 37 °C for 30 min, and transferred to 95 °C for 15 min. Samples were placed on ice before loading on a prerun 9% denaturing PAGE gel (19:1 acrylamide/bisacrylamide, 7 M urea, and 0.6× tris-borate EDTA). Electrophoresed gels were visualized on a Typhoon 5 Imager (Cytiva) and signals quantified with ImageQuant.

Structure Prediction by AlphaFold3.

Two subunits of full-length TaGu and the Y-shaped target DNA, excluding the RNA, were submitted to the AlphaFold3 online web server (29) to generate a predicted ternary complex structure. Dimeric TaGu in complex with target DNA was visualized and annotated in ChimeraX to display predicted local distance different test (pLDDT), a per-residue confidence metric. The predicted placement of the second-subunit EN domain is shown in Fig. 2E, with the AlphaFold3 first-subunit TaGu replaced using maximum alignment with the previously determined cryoEM structures of TaGu at TPRT initiation and R2p from the big-headed turtle P. megacephalum after second-strand cleavage (6). AF3/ChimeraX session files are available upon request.

Cell Culture, PRINT, and Flow Cytometry.

hTERT RPE-1 cells were grown in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Seradigm) and 100 μg/mL Primocin (InvivoGen). Luciferase mRNA was purchased from TriLink [CleanCap Renilla Luc mRNA (5moU), L-7204]. Cells were seeded the day before PRINT at either 100,000 cells per well of a 12-well plate (Fig. 4B) or 300,000 cells per well of a six-well plate (Figs. 4D and 6). Cells were transfected with mRNA(s) and template RNA using Lipofectamine MessengerMAX (Thermo Fisher) at 1/2 mass/volume ratio per the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA per transfection was 0.5 μg using a 12-well plate at a 1:3 molar ratio (mRNA:template RNA), and 1.5 μg using a six-well plate at a 1:1:5 molar ratio (mRNA1:mRNA2:template RNA); ratio of mRNA:template RNA was varied in Fig. 6 while keeping total mass of transfected RNA constant. Cells were collected 20 to 24 h following transfection for flow cytometry analysis on a BD FACSymphony A1 using FSC = 300 V, SSC = 193 V, and BB515 (GFP) = 300 V. Data analysis was performed in FlowJo (v.10.8.1) with template RNA alone as the negative control. The %GFP+ cells was calculated by subtracting minimal if any %GFP from template-only transfection. Median GFP intensity was determined using only GFP+ cells. Each set of PRINT results was validated with at least one biological replicate.

Genomic DNA Purification, ddPCR, and RIPPLE-seq.

Genomic DNA purification for ddPCR and RIPPLE-seq was done as previously described, as was ddPCR quantification including use of RPP30 as a reference gene to calculate transgene copy number per cell (14, 17). Primer sequences are included in Dataset S1. With the exception of Figs. 4F and 6D, each set of results was validated with at least one biological replicate. For RIPPLE-seq, genomic DNA was digested with EcoRI, XmaI, and AvrII (NEB) prior to in vitro transcription with the HiScribe SP6 RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB). In brief, purified RNA was polyadenylated, reverse transcribed with Induro RT (NEB), and converted to duplex DNA using LongAmp Taq 2× Master Mix (NEB). Sequencing adaptors were ligated and used for long-read sequencing (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). Sequencing was performed on MinION r10.4.1 flow cells on an Mk1D device.
