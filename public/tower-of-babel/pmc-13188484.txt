Changes of medicinal food plant uses in the Gaoligongshan biocultural diversity center, China
Authors: Zhuo Cheng, Xiangxiang Lou, Ren-ai Xu, Fangfang Xia, Chunlin Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Gaoligongshan area, located in western Yunnan, China, is the richest biocultural diversity hotspot with unique geological history and ecological environment. Local people such as the Lisu, Nu, Dulong and Han have developed rich traditional knowledge systems associated with medicinal food plants (MFPs). These MFPs play a vital role in local communities by contributing to health, the economy, and cultural heritage. However, MFPs are at risk of disappearing, and there is a lack of comprehensive and systematic studies on their earlier transformation and current use in Gaoligongshan area. This study synthesizes earlier literature and recent market data to reveal specific shifts in MFP use and practices in this hotspot.MethodsEthnobotanical methods, including literature reviews and market surveys were employed to collect information on MFPs used by different linguistic groups in the Gaoligongshan area across two periods, the earlier time and a recent market-survey stage. Information was recorded on plant families, medicinal parts, processing methods, and medicinal efficacy. Comparisons were made between earlier and current data to evaluate the adaptation and transformation of traditional knowledge associated with MFPs.Results117 MFPs (56 families) were documented in the earlier stage and 178 MFPs (89 families) were documented in the current period. The used parts shifted from roots being dominant in the earlier time (43.59%) to fruits being most commonly used in the recent period (22.47%), with roots decreasing to 8.43%. Processing methods also changed, with stir-frying increasing (from 36.75% to 48.31%) and boiling decreasing (from 34.19% to 25.84%). The findings suggest continuity and adaptation in the selection of MFPs by local people, highlighting the dynamic interaction between cultural practices and environmental changes. The research identified 39 MFPs consistently used over time, reflecting their ongoing cultural and medicinal importance, and compiled an MFP resource pool of 244 candidate species that were not recorded in either the earlier or current datasets, indicating opportunities for future exploration and sustainable development.ConclusionThis study documents cross-period changes and continuity in medicinal food plants and provides descriptive evidence relevant to ethnobotany and the food–medicine continuum. We highlight three key insights: a relative decrease in root use and increase in fruit use across periods, the persistence of 39 core species, and the compilation of a regional MFP resource pool that may inform future verification. Developing sustainable utilization and conservation strategies is important for preserving biodiversity and traditional culture in the Gaoligongshan area.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-026-00892-7.

Background

The plant-derived food or edible plants (food plants) showed great diversity, of which many species possess obvious pharmacological or biological activities, and have health-promoting functions. They are called medicinal food plants (MFPs) in the present paper. Traditional knowledge, especially those associated with MFPs, reflects the local community’s understanding and wisdom in preventing and curing diseases [1]. MFPs serve the dual functions of food and medicine, providing multiple benefits in nutritional support, disease prevention and health promotion [2–4]. The adaptation and transformation of traditional knowledge of MFPs in different time reflect how cultural practices have evolved in response to changing environmental, social and economic conditions [5]. However, with the acceleration of modernization and the disappearance of traditional lifestyles, this knowledge has changed considerably. There is an urgent need for a systematic analysis of how traditional knowledge has adapted and changed over time [6].

The Gaoligongshan area, or Gaoligong Mountains, located in northwestern Yunnan, China, has a unique geological history and ecological environment [7]. It has been recognized as the richest area in biodiversity of China and is listed as one of the world’s 25 biodiversity hotspots [8, 9]. Up to now, 5139 species of seed plants have been recorded in the Gaoligong Mountains. The richness of endemic species is higher than most areas in the country [10]. At the same time, the Gaoligongshan is also home to multi-ethnic communities, including the Lisu, Nu and Dulong, which have developed a rich body of traditional knowledge closely related to MFPs, which is reflected in their daily lives, dietary habits and medical practices [11].

In recent years, some ethnobotanical studies have documented the traditional utilization of numerous medicinal food plants in the Gaoligongshan area [7]. These studies have revealed how different ethnic groups have utilized wild plants for food and medicine, such as Caryota obtusa, Toxicodendron vernicifluum, and Angiopteris esculenta [12–15]. Nevertheless, most of the existing studies have focused on species documentation and lack a comprehensive understanding of the dynamics of these plants in terms of changes over time and contemporary use. More specifically, the existing literature is typically confined to single-period surveys, does not integrate earlier records with contemporary market/community data within a comparable framework, and seldom quantifies temporal changes in plant families, parts used and processing methods; moreover, evidence regarding family-level or phylogenetic selectivity remains limited.

Adaptation and change in traditional knowledge of MFPs in the Gaoligongshan area reflect the continuous interaction between cultural traditions and environmental changes [9]. Over time, local communities have adapted their use of medicinal food plants in response to ecological conditions, socioeconomic pressures, and the introduction of agricultural and market practices. For example, some traditional plants that were once widely used have been gradually forgotten as a result of habitat loss, overcollection or changes in cultural preferences, while others have gained prominence as their medicinal and nutritional values have been rediscovered or validated by modern science [16–18].

Although there have been a large number of studies on the diversity of MFPs in the Gaoligongshan area, there are still significant gaps in the understanding of the variation over time and current status of these plants. In addition, there is still insufficient research on how traditional knowledge systems are responding to contemporary challenges such as biodiversity loss, climate change and globalization. An in-depth understanding of these changes is important for the conservation of biological and cultural diversity. Against this backdrop, comparing the earlier and current sets with the resource pool provides a management-relevant rationale: overlaps between earlier and current sets identify culturally persistent “core species” that can be prioritized for conservation and intergenerational transmission; divergences in parts used and processing methods inform non-destructive harvesting practices and biocultural resilience under modernization; and the resource pool offers a practical pipeline for future nutritional and pharmacological evaluation while helping avoid overexploitation.

The research objectives of this study were to: (1) identify the specific changes in species composition, parts used, and processing methods of MFPs utilized by residents in the Gaoligongshan area between the past and the present; (2) determine whether differences in the phylogenetic relationships of MFPs used in different periods indicate selectivity in plant taxa by local communities; (3) conduct cross-regional and cross-cultural comparisons.

Methods

Study area

The Gaoligong Mountains (24°40′ to 28°30′ N, 97°30′ to 99°30′ E) are located at the junction of southwestern Yunnan and northern Myanmar [9]. As a narrow mountain range connecting the Tibetan Plateau and the Central South Peninsula, the Gaoligongshan area is high in the north and low in the south, with a total length of more than 600 km, a difference of 4,918 m between the north and south elevations, and a complex and diverse topography, which creates a unique three-dimensional climate and ecological environment [10]. Gaoligongshan area is one of the 25 biodiversity hotspots in the world, and also one of the richest areas in China in terms of species and cultural diversity, inhabited by Lisu, Nu, Dulong and other hereditary ethnic groups [19]. Although the Gaoligongshan area is one of the poorest areas in Yunnan, the local people of various linguistic groups have very rich traditional knowledge associated with biodiversity. Such a variety of life forms and national traditional cultures in the Gaoligong Mountains have created its world-renowned biocultural diversity. Until the early 2010s, many villages in this mountainous region remained difficulty to access, with limited road infrastructure and weak market integration, whereas in the last decade road construction has markedly improved connectivity to towns and regional markets.

Literature research

The data of MFPs for the earlier time were obtained from ethnobotanical literature research, many monographs and documents were collected from 1999 to 2013, and MFPs in Gaoligongshan area were classified and organized (Table S1).

The data of MFPs in current periods were obtained through market surveys. The information of species names, distribution, edible and medicinal parts, and processing methods of MFPs was recorded using the free listing method and semi-structured interviews [9] (Table S2).

The data of MFP resource pool was obtained by integrating public data. Based on the List of Medicinal Plants in Gaoligongshan area and China Useful Plant Resource Database, the plants with medicinal and food values were screened [20, 21] (Table S3).

Field survey

A total of 13 representative local farmers’ markets close to different linguistic groups were selected in the area from 2021 to 2023. Market surveys included semi-structured interviews, key informant interviews and participatory observation. During the market investigation, A total of 45 stallholders were invited to list all medicinal dietary plants that are still regularly used. The interviews consisted of two parts, the first part was about the basic situation of the informants, and the other part contained questions related to recording detailed information on MFPs, including part used, medicinal effects, and other uses. The primary content of interview consisted of “5 W + H” questions [22]: (1). What kind of MFPs do you collect?; What’s its name in your language?; (2) Which part of the MFPs do you collect?; (3) Where and when do you collect MFPs? How often do you collect it?; (4) How do you consume MFPs? What kind of process is needed?; (5) Why do you collect MFPs? What kind of medicinal effect does it have?

Before collecting indigenous knowledge, all informants were informed about the purpose of the research and its benefits, clearly underlining the fact that the results will be used for academic purposes and that no commercial interest will be attached to it. It was carried out following the ethical code of the International Society of Ethnobiology [23]. The categories suggested for diseases were classified according to International Classification of Primary Care (ICPC) (https://www.who.int/classifications/icd/en/), system for intercultural comparisons, with minor modifications [24]. The voucher specimen of each medicinal food plant was collected, and deposited in the herbarium at Minzu University of China. For the identification of plants, the voucher specimens were studied and compared with reference books (Flora Republicae Popularis Sinicae, and Flora of China) and electronic online resources (http://www.iplant.cn/, and https://powo.science.kew.org).

Data analysis

In this study, the collected data were recorded and initially statistically analyzed through Excel 2020. In order to explore the species of MFPs and their distribution in different periods, several data analysis methods were applied. In order to visualize the overlap and uniqueness of dual-use plant species in different data sources, we used a Venn diagram for visualization and analysis (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). We constructed the phylogenetic tree by using the package “V.PhyloMaker2” in R language. For family-level summaries, all families that actually occurred in our datasets were included; no additional subjective inclusion/exclusion or merging of families was performed. We then imported the generated phylogenetic tree into the website software iTOL v. 5 to beautify and add markers [25]. In iTOL, each species tip was annotated with a categorical marker indicating its presence in earlier time only, at present only, or in both periods.

Results

Diversity of traditional knowledge associated with MFPs in the Gaoligongshan area

Through literature collection, we found 117 MFPs belonging to 56 families used by various ethnic groups in Gaoligongshan area in the earlier time. Through the market survey, we found 178 MFPs belonging to 89 families used by various ethnic groups in the current period.

Diversity of plant families

The most frequently used families were Gingeraceae (8 species), Solanaceae and Labiatae (7 species each), followed by Leguminosae and Wujiaceae (6 species), and Oleaceae (5 species) in the earlier time. MFPs have increased to 178 species in the current period. The most frequently used families were Leguminosae (11 species), Asteraceae (10 species) and Umbelliferae (9 species), Labiatae and Cucurbitaceae (8 species each), Rosaceae (6 species). Comparing the earlier and current lists, MFPs in the Gingeraceae, Solanaceae and Labiatae families were widely used in both stages, reflecting the continuation of their food and medicinal values locally. In the current list, Leguminosae and Zingiberaceae still occupy an important position, but the significant increase in the number of Oleaceae and Cucurbitaceae species may stem from the emerging market demand for them or their newfound value (Fig. 1). In the Venn diagram (Fig. 1E), the overlap between the earlier and current sets indicates continuity across periods, while the non-overlapping sectors indicate species recorded only in one period. The central overlap among earlier, current and resource pool marks taxa that appear in all three sources, suggesting culturally persistent use recognized across datasets.

The used parts of MFPs in the earlier time were mainly roots, accounting for 43.59%, while the whole plant and leaves accounted for 25.64% and 23.93%, respectively. Fruits, stems and bark were used relatively less, accounting for 19.66%, 18.80%, and was used less, accounting for 9.40%. In contrast, currently, fruits became the most commonly used part with 22.47%, while root use decreased significantly with only 8.43% (Fig. 1). The extensive use of roots is associated with traditional medicine preferences as they are believed to contain more medicinal components [26]. In modern times, the frequency of medicinal use of fruits has increased due to the development of food processing techniques, easier preservation of fruits, and consumer concerns about nutrition and diversity [27]. At the same time, ecological sustainability considerations and market supply and demand have reduced reliance on roots and increased the use of fruits and whole herbs [28].

Diversity of processing methods

Earlier time data showed that stir-fried and boiled were the most common processing modes, accounting for 36.75% and 34.19%, respectively, followed by making tea (18.80%), eaten fresh (16.24%), and cold and dressed with sauce (11.11%). Current period data showed that stir-fried (48.31%) and boiled (25.84%) were still the main processing methods. Emerging methods such as frying eggs, cold mixing, pickling, soaking in wine and processing into starch accounted for a relatively low percentage. Traditional methods of stir-fried and boiled reflect their deep-rooted place in local culture, but modern emphasis on health and nutrition has pushed for nutrient-preserving methods such as eating raw and soaking in water. Technological advances and economic development have led to more diverse processing methods, and emerging food processing technologies have increased market demand for convenience foods [29].

Diversity of efficacy

In the earlier time, the category with the highest FIC was the circulatory system.

The most common efficacies were clearing heat and detoxifying and reducing swelling and pain, involving 85 and 81 species, accounting for 72.65% and 69.23% of the total, respectively, reflecting the high dependence of local residents on detoxifying and analgesic plants, which may be related to the local environment and living habits [7]. Current period data show that the most common medicinal effects are still clearing heat and removing toxins and relieving swelling and pain, involving 64 and 60 plant species, or 35.96% and 33.71%, respectively. These data indicate that the demand for clearing heat and removing toxins and relieving swelling and pain has remained high from earlier to modern times, probably due to the local climatic conditions that predispose to the induction of inflammatory diseases and trauma associated with daily physical labor, which led to the popularity of these plants as common herbs [7].

Comparison of MFPs in Gaoligongshan area in different periods

Comparison of MFPs in earlier time and at present

Overlapping analysis of the MFPs in the earlier and current periods based on Venn diagrams (Fig. 1) showed that there were 39 MFPs recorded in the earlier and current periods (Table 1), suggesting that these plants have been widely used in the past and remain in circulation in the marketplace today. MFPs such as Canna indica and Foeniculum vulgare, which have been used in the past and up to the present (Fig. 2), suggesting the enduring nature of their medicinal value. The medicinal value of these plants is mostly focused on treating indigestion, relieving inflammation and improving respiratory diseases. Consistent use may be due to their remarkable medicinal properties, easy accessibility or specific significance and use in the local culture. For example, Canna indica is important in both traditional and modern medicine because of the medicinal value and food safety of its roots and flowers [30]. Foeniculum vulgare is not only an important ingredient in flavored foods, but is also used in herbal remedies to help relieve digestive problems [31].

There were 61 MFPs that appeared only in the earlier time. Medicinal plants such as Amomum maximum that were used in the past and are now eliminated may be due to environmental changes, scarcity of resources, emergence of substitutes, or changes in people’s habits. A. maximum may have become less prevalent due to changes in its specific growing environment or over-collection, reflecting the evolution of local socio-economic and ecological systems [32].

The 101 MFPs that appeared only in the current periods may reflect new market demand or newly discovered medicinal resources. Medicinal plants that were not used in the past and are now being used such as Colocasia esculenta and Juglans regia, which are now available in the market but were not widely utilized in the past, may have medicinal values that are related to modern lifestyles and health needs. C. esculenta is valued for its excellent nutritional value and potential to be used as a staple food [33]. J. regia is becoming increasingly popular for their rich omega-3 fatty acids and antioxidant properties [34]. These plants may be adapted to the needs of modern people due to their specific medicinal properties, such as antioxidant and lipid regulation. This reflects the changing understanding and use of different medicinal plants as society develops and medical research advances.

MFP resource pool in the Gaoligong mountains

The resource pool (n = 244) indicates that these plants are considered valuable, but have not been widely documented or used in the literature or market surveys. MFPs such as Embelia undulata and Grona heterocarpos, which were not used in the past or present but existed in the resource pool, suggesting that they may have under-explored medicinal or edible potential. Their specific efficacy and safety can be explored through scientific research in the future, and these plants may contain unknown active ingredients that may become new sources of medicines or nutritional supplements through scientific research in the future.

Local people’s choice of MFPs

Local people’s choice of MFPs families

By comparing the phylogenetic tree of the MFPs from different periods in Gaoligongshan (Fig. 3), it can be seen that the local people’s selection of MFPs families demonstrated a certain continuity and adaptability. In the circular phylogenies (Figs. 3 and 4), species names in purple denote taxa that occur in both the earlier and current lists, and red-highlighted branches mark lineage segments with multiple listed species. Comparing Figs. 3 and 4, stable clusters are retained in legume- and ginger-rich lineages, while clusters expand in fruit/vegetable-rich lineages such as Asteraceae and Cucurbitaceae, indicating lineage-level continuity alongside adaptive diversification. The high frequency of occurrence of Leguminosae, Cucurbitaceae, Asteraceae and Zingiberaceae is maintained in both period lists, which may relate to the wide range of medicinal and edible values of plants in these families, as well as the long-term knowledge and use of them by local people [35].

Leguminosae was the family with the highest number of species occurring in both periods, reflecting the diversity of its plant species and their fixed nutritional and medicinal values. Legumes are usually rich in protein and other key nutrients, and many species also have medicinal properties, such as purgative and anti-inflammatory properties [36]. Cucurbitaceae species maintained a high occurrence in both periods. This may be related to the frequent therapeutic use of this family, many of which such as Cucurbita moschata and Momordica charantia are part of the daily diet locally, as well as being utilized for their medicinal properties [37]. Amaranthaceae, Pentacostaceae, Polygonaceae and Rosaceae have been added to the current list or have increased in number, which may be related to the expansion of their distribution in local ecosystems or to an increased awareness of the medicinal properties of these plants.

These changes may be linked to advances in scientific research, the evolution of people’s concepts of health, and changes in economic market conditions. New discoveries resulting from pharmacological research on plants may have led to an increase in the importance of certain plant families, while the growing interest in health and natural products may have influenced the choice of medicinal plants. In addition, international trade and the expansion of markets may have provided larger markets for certain plants, thus changing the frequency of their local use.

Selectivity of local people for overlapping MFPs in different periods

In the Gaoligongshan area, the selection of MFPs in different periods showed some continuity, especially in some specific families. There are 39 MFPs repeated in different periods (Fig. 4), among which the Umbelliferae has the highest frequency of occurrence, with five occurrences. Umbelliferae plants are widely used not only as ingredients in cooking due to their unique aroma and medicinal properties, but also occupy an important position in traditional medicine, such as Centella asiatica and Coriandrum sativum. Cucurbitaceae, Labiatae, Pentacarpaceae and Asteraceae followed with 3 occurrences each. Cucurbita moschata, Gynostemma pentaphyllum and Momordica charantia are vegetables with high nutritional value and can be used in a variety of culinary styles, and have been favored by the local people at different times [37]. Culturally, many of these persistent species function as everyday aromatics or staple vegetables, which may contribute to their repeated recording in cuisine and household remedies; ecologically, their dispersion across families and niches aligns with the region’s heterogeneity and helps buffer practices against environmental fluctuation; pharmacologically, their long-standing traditional medicinal properties reinforce continued inclusion in local health practices. Overall, the distribution of these 39 repetitive plant families is relatively decentralized. This decentralized distribution may be related to ecological diversity and biological adaptability. The unique ecosystem of the Gaoligongshan supports diverse plant growth, and different families of plants are adapted to different ecological niches in the region. This ecological and biological diversity has led to the wide distribution of the MFPs families. The purple-labelled species in Figs. 3 and 4 correspond to the cross-period taxa described here, making persistence visible on the phylogenies.

MFPs with potential for future research and sustainable use

MFPs that may be available for future exploitation include those 39 medicinal plants that have been used both in earlier time and recent periods, and whose continued use reflects the transmission of traditional knowledge and culture by local communities. These plants have long played an important role in the dietary and medicinal practices of local communities and are part of people’s lifestyle and cultural identity. At the same time, the long history of use has provided a basis for scientific research that has helped to explore the wider medical and nutritional potential of these plants, leading to the development of new products and applications.

In addition, there are 244 MFPs that are considered valuable but have not yet been documented or used in earlier and current investigations. These plants could be explored in the future through scientific research for specific efficacy and safety, and may be a new source of medicines or nutritional supplements. Of these, Asteraceae is the largest family, with 21 plants. Many Asteraceae plants are used in traditional medicine to treat a variety of ailments, for example, Glebionis coronaria is commonly cultivated in several parts of Asia, and its leaves, which are rich in vitamins and minerals and part of a healthy diet, may contain bioactive compounds with medicinal value that could be further developed for research in the future [38]. Perilla frutescens var. purpurascens of the Labiatae family is widely used in Asian cuisine, especially in Korea and Japan, and its leaves and seeds are edible and rich in many vitamins and minerals. From a medicinal point of view, perilla has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and possibly anti-allergic properties. Considering its nutritional value and medicinal properties, wild perilla may be further developed into health food or medicine [39].

Comparative analysis with regional ethnobotanical datasets

We compared the medicinal dietary taxa recorded in the Gaoligongshan market survey with two published Yunnan ethnobotanical inventories on medicinal dietary plants, namely the Naxi dataset from Lijiang (northwestern Yunnan) and the Yi dataset from Mile City (central Yunnan) [40, 41]. The Venn diagram (Fig. 5) shows a limited shared core and substantial dataset-specific components: only 6 taxa are shared by all three datasets, whereas Gaoligongshan shares 9 taxa with the Naxi list and 36 taxa with the Yi list, and the Naxi and Yi lists share 9 taxa. In contrast, 139 taxa occur only in the Gaoligongshan market dataset and are not reported in either reference study.

The small overlap among datasets likely reflects a combination of factors, including (i) differences in regional floristic pools and elevational and ecological contexts that shape what is locally available for medicinal food use, (ii) cultural preferences and knowledge transmission within different ethnolinguistic groups that influence which taxa are considered suitable as food medicine, and (iii) market-mediated selection, whereby commercial availability, consumer demand, and trade across borders or within the province can amplify certain taxa while excluding others. Notably, the larger overlap between Gaoligongshan and the Yi inventory than between Gaoligongshan and the Naxi inventory may be consistent with closer regional exchange networks or more similar culinary and medicinal practice in the compared localities, whereas the very small three-way intersection suggests that only a narrow set of medicinal dietary taxa is broadly shared across distinct areas of Yunnan.

Discussion

This study examines the changes in the utilization of medicinal food plants in the Gaoligongshan area over time and discusses plausible factors that may be associated with these patterns. The rich biodiversity and complex geoclimatic conditions of the Gaoligongshan have created a special environment in which medicinal food plants play an important role. These plants not only support the food and medicinal needs of local communities, but also reflect the rich ethnic culture and traditional knowledge.

Our findings support the shift from predominantly roots and rhizomes in the earlier period to higher relative use of fruits and leaves in the current period, alongside more diverse processing methods. The expanded taxonomic and lineage coverage and wider phylogenetic spread of the current‑period medicinal and food plant assemblage are consistent with the expectation of broader phylogenetic representation at present. The cross‑period “core species” appear to cluster within several recurrent lineages and families, in line with phylogenetic patterning and cultural selectivity in the food–medicine continuum. The following factors may explain the adaptation and change of medicinal and food plants in the Gaoligongshan area across different periods.

Impacts of environmental changes and biodiversity: ecological changes in the Gaoligongshan area, especially the reduction of biodiversity, may influence the availability and utilization of medicinal plants, and some traditionally used medicinal plant species are facing a decreasing trend due to factors such as habitat destruction and climate change. For example, over-collection and habitat loss have led to some medicinal plant resources becoming scarce, thus limiting their availability and traditional utilization. In our dataset, the proportion of roots used declines from 43.59% (earlier) to 8.43% (current), while fruits become the most used part (22.47%); this shift is consistent with evidence that, when properly managed, harvesting fruits and other above-ground parts is generally less destructive than root or bark extraction and is commonly recommended in non-timber forest products (NTFP) sustainability practice [42–44]. Comparable diachronic studies in the Italian Alps and Central Europe likewise report the co-occurrence of long-term persistence with reconfiguration of uses, which provides an external frame for our pattern of continuity (39 cross-period taxa) alongside a shift away from destructive parts [45, 46].

Driven by economic development and market demand: With economic development and changes in market demand, the utilization patterns of medicinal plants in the Gaoligongshan area have been transformed. Some traditional MFPs have gained wider utilization due to their increased economic value and market demand, while others have been marginalized due to changes in market preferences. For example, increased market demand for certain MFPs with specific health benefits may encourage the commercial cultivation and utilization of these plants. Consistent with our finding that fruit/vegetable-rich lineages (e.g., Asteraceae, Cucurbitaceae) are more prominent in the current list and phylogenies, studies from Yunnan and broader NTFP systems show that commercialization and market integration can reshape species portfolios and management intensity through changes in value-chain requirements and demand conditions [47–49]. Mediterranean comparisons also note high present–past overlap in key medicinal families (e.g., Asteraceae, Lamiaceae) together with shifting prominence of everyday food–medicine taxa, aligning with our family-level redistribution [45]. Beyond local markets, globalization and contemporary nutrition discourses, including the marketing of “superfoods,” influence consumer preferences and upstream value chains, favoring antioxidant- and phytonutrient-rich fruits and leaves rather than destructive root or rhizome extraction [50–52]. This broader demand environment may be consistent with our observed increase in fruit use and the greater prominence of families dominated by fruit and vegetable taxa in the current period.

Decline and transmission of traditional knowledge: The decline of traditional knowledge may be an important factor influencing changes in the utilization pattern of medicinal plants. With the acceleration of modernization, the transmission of traditional knowledge of MFPs among the younger generation has encountered challenges. Despite the wealth of knowledge about these plants among the older generation, the transmission of this knowledge is not always guaranteed, which may lead to the gradual fading out of certain traditional knowledge and methods of MFPs. Our results show both continuity (39 species recorded across periods) and turnover; empirical studies from China and the region document intergenerational gaps and erosion of traditional ecological knowledge, which can weaken continuity and contribute to shifts in species use [53–55]. Comparable cross-border and Himalayan research emphasizes that temporal dynamics of wild food/medicinal plant knowledge include persistence, decline and revitalization pathways, underscoring the importance of safeguarding transmission for biocultural resilience and contextualizing our “continuity-with-turnover” pattern [56, 57].

Beyond documenting the present inventory, our market-based dataset has practical implications for future environmental management and public-health work in the Gaoligongshan region. Environmentally, repeatedly traded taxa and newly recorded market taxa together define a priority set for follow-up monitoring, as market demand can concentrate harvest pressure and shift sourcing across space and time. The transition from root-dominated use toward greater reliance on fruits and leaves may suggest less destructive practices, but it also calls for verification of whether rising demand is being met through sustainable wild harvesting, local cultivation, or expanded external supply. From a public-health perspective, medicinal food plants are embedded in everyday diets and household self-care, yet commercialization and longer supply chains can raise safety concerns related to species identity, processing consistency, and basic quality control. Overall, our results provide an evidence base for integrated follow-up work that links biodiversity monitoring with consumer-safety considerations, supporting a food–medicine continuum that remains both ecologically sustainable and safe.

Based on the cross-period patterns described above, we suggest potential measures to support ecological protection and sustainable use in the Gaoligongshan area. (i) Consider non-destructive harvesting: where culturally acceptable, favor the use of fruits, leaves or flowers instead of roots/rhizomes; when root use is necessary, adopt rotational harvesting, retain a proportion of mature individuals, and avoid uprooting entire clumps to allow natural regeneration. (ii) Safeguard cross-period persistent species in situ: identify sites with high occurrence of the 39 persistently used species, apply seasonal closures where needed, and encourage household cultivation for high-demand taxa to shift pressure away from the wild. (iii) Link processing practices with resource efficiency: promote preparation methods that do not require destructive parts and may reduce waste of raw biomass, while maintaining local culinary norms. And (iv) Community-based monitoring and knowledge transmission: engage local harvesters to track availability and phenology, and document intergenerational guidelines on harvest timing and minimal take, so that customary rules are aligned with ecological renewal.

There are some limitations in this study. The differences in data sources across periods may affect the reliability of longitudinal comparisons. The potential market coverage bias could weaken the external validity of the research findings. Most critically, all reported medicinal uses have not undergone rigorous experimental validation, thus their efficacy and safety cannot be confirmed. Future studies may cover these issues as mentioned above.

Conclusion

In this paper, we conducted an in-depth study of medicinal food plants in the Gaoligongshan region through database searches, literature collection, and market surveys. Our cross-period comparison demonstrates continuity, with 39 species used both in earlier time and at present, and transformation, including a shift in plant parts used from roots toward fruits and a diversification of processing methods. These patterns may inform for conservation and biocultural resilience: prioritizing non-destructive use (e.g., fruits) can reduce pressure on perennial taxa while sustaining local knowledge and practice. In addition, the resource pool of 244 species highlights taxa for future screening and verification. This study underscores the need for deeper exploration of the biological activities and pharmacological effects of MFPs, integrating traditional knowledge with modern scientific research. Developing sustainable utilization and conservation strategies is important for preserving biodiversity and traditional culture in the Gaoligongshan area.
