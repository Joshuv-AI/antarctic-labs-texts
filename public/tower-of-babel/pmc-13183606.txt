Reconstructing cerebral lymphatic clearance: an emerging target in the Alzheimer's disease therapeutic pipeline
Authors: Jiheng Zhang, Jiren Zhang, Jian Ding, Xian Zhang, Yuhua Wang, Wei Wei, Yan Chai, Shu Zhang, Xin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurotoxic protein accumulation is widely recognized as a key feature of Alzheimer's disease (AD), and increasing evidence indicates that impaired brain clearance associated with dysfunction of the meningeal lymphatic and glymphatic may contribute to this process. Disruption of these pathways may weaken homeostatic mechanisms and facilitate amyloid‑β and tau buildup. This review considers lymphatic reconstruction as a potential therapeutic direction aimed at modestly improving clearance efficiency. We summarize current findings from pharmacological approaches targeting aquaporin‐4 or vascular endothelial growth factor C signaling, non‐invasive methods such as photobiomodulation and focused ultrasound, and emerging surgical or lifestyle interventions designed to enhance drainage. Early clinical attempts, including deep cervical lymphovenous anastomosis (dCLVA), provide preliminary proof of concept but require careful validation. Progress in this field also depends on developing sensitive, non‐invasive imaging markers and defining appropriate intervention windows. By situating lymphatic modulation within the broader AD drug‐development landscape, we highlight its possible role within multimodal therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, aquaporin‐4, glymphatic, meningeal lymphatic system, neurodegenerative diseases

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD), the most widespread neurodegenerative condition, significantly affects the life quality of those aged over 65. Its pathological features consist of extracellular amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau aggregates. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 The recent US Food and Drug Administration approvals of amyloid‐targeting monoclonal antibodies, such as lecanemab and donanemab, have marked a transformative shift in the AD therapeutic landscape. However, while these therapies demonstrate the feasibility of plaque reduction, their clinical efficacy is often constrained by modest cognitive benefits and safety risks, including amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities. 5 , 6 , 7 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel strategies and explore new therapeutic targets, particularly those addressing metabolic waste clearance impairments and neuroinflammation, which are key drivers of the pathogenesis of AD.

Emerging evidence highlights the cerebral lymphatic system as a pivotal regulator of brain homeostasis. 8 , 9 , 10 Comprising the glymphatic system and meningeal lymphatic vessels (mLVs), this network facilitates cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)–interstitial fluid (ISF) exchange, clears neurotoxic proteins (e.g., Aβ, tau), and modulates neuroimmune responses. 11 , 12 , 13 Crucially, mLV dysfunction exacerbates AD progression by impairing Aβ drainage and perpetuating neuroinflammation. Activated microglia and astrocytes, triggered by Aβ accumulation, release pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin‐1 beta [IL‐1β], tumor necrosis factor‐alpha [TNF‐α]), which further damage mLVs and create a vicious cycle of inflammation and neurodegeneration. 14 These findings position lymphatic restoration as a dual‐target strategy to mitigate both proteinopathy and neuroinflammation in AD.

Recent innovations targeting lymphatic clearance show preliminary promise in preclinical models. 15 , 16 Natural compounds and herbal formulations enhance meningeal lymphatic expansion, 17 while non‐invasive neuromodulation techniques augment glymphatic drainage efficiency. 18 Surgical approaches such as cranial bone maneuvers (CBMs) have also demonstrated improved waste clearance in animal studies. 19 Notably, a pilot clinical study reported cognitive improvement in one AD patient following deep cervical lymphovenous anastomosis (dCLVA), 20 though broader validation is essential. Nevertheless, translational challenges persist, including heavy reliance on transgenic animal models (which may not fully recapitulate human lymphatic anatomy), limited dynamic imaging tools for human lymphatic function assessment, and unresolved questions regarding intervention timing relative to disease stages.

This review evaluates and clarifies the role of cerebral lymphatic modulation in AD and its therapeutic potential. We systematically synthesize evidence from molecular mechanisms to clinical interventions, with emphasis on the feasibility of pharmacological, non‐invasive, and microsurgical lymphatic‐modulation strategies as adjunctive AD therapies. It should be noted that most current treatment ideas about the brain lymphatic fluid clearance mechanism come from animal experiments, and direct clinical evidence in humans is still limited and mostly comes from single‐center and single‐arm studies. We also identified the key challenges encountered in the clinical application process, aiming to provide insights into interventions targeting the brain lymphatic systemx.

CORE PATHOLOGICAL MECHANISMS OF AD AND THEIR CASCADING EFFECTS

Core pathological features: the Aβ–tau axis

The core neuropathological features of AD are extracellular Aβ plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 The predominant hypothesis posits that the abnormal accumulation of Aβ pathology is a pivotal initiating event in the disease process, ultimately driving tau pathology. 25 , 26 This interplay forms the fundamental cascade axis of AD pathogenesis.

Molecular mechanisms underlying Aβ‐driven tau pathology

The disease process is initiated by an imbalance in Aβ peptide metabolism. 27 Sequential cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP) by β‐and γ‐secretases generates Aβ peptides, 28 among which aggregation‐prone Aβ oligomers have been demonstrated to exhibit significant neurotoxicity. These oligomers disrupt the basis of memory by directly impairing synaptic plasticity and inhibiting long‐term potentiation (LTP). 29 , 30 Concurrently, they induce oxidative stress, compromise mitochondrial function, and activate microglia and astrocytes, thereby triggering neuroinflammation. 31

The abnormal aggregation of Aβ creates a permissive environment for tau pathology. The underlying mechanisms include triggering neuroinflammation and vascular dysfunction (e.g., synergizing with advanced glycation end products to cause endothelial impairment) and disrupting proteostasis, which exacerbates and is exacerbated by impaired autophagy and mitochondrial dysfunction. 25 , 26 , 32 Critically, Aβ can directly drive tau hyperphosphorylation by activating kinases such as glycogen synthase kinase‐3β (GSK‐3β), establishing an Aβ‐tau feedforward cascade. Furthermore, biomarker studies support this temporal relationship: Both CSF and neuroimaging data indicate that abnormal Aβ deposition precedes the widespread propagation of tau pathology. High‐sensitivity detection techniques (e.g., assays for Aβ42, Aβ40, and phosphorylated tau‐217) have further elucidated the dynamics of this pathological cascade, providing a basis for disease staging and the timing of interventions. 33

Tau pathology as an independent driver of neurodegeneration

Once engaged, tau pathology becomes the principal driver of neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. Under physiological conditions, tau protein stabilizes microtubules and maintains neuronal structure. 34 In the pathological state, hyperphosphorylated tau dissociates from microtubules and aggregates into tangles, disrupting the cytoskeleton and impeding axonal transport, ultimately leading to neuronal death. 35 Neuroimaging evidence demonstrates that both baseline cortical tau accumulation levels and the rate of its accumulation can independently predict subsequent accelerated atrophy in white matter regions, and this relationship is unidirectional. This highlights the central role of tau‐induced axonal degeneration in disease progression. 33 , 36

Neuroinflammation and glial cell activation

Aβ deposits and tau pathology together act as persistent inflammatory stimuli, driving chronic overactivation of microglia and astrocytes. 37 Activated microglia release substantial amounts of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and neurotoxic substances. Notably, TNF‐α and IL‐1β can reciprocally promote Aβ production and aggregation, creating a self‐reinforcing pathological cycle. 38 Concurrently, the functional state of reactive astrocytes also underwent a transformation. They secrete pro‐inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby directly exacerbating neuronal damage. 39 , 40 This chronic, glia‐mediated neuroinflammation operates as an independent pathological driver. It forms a positive feedback loop with Aβ/tau pathology, synergistically accelerating synaptic loss and neuronal death. 41 Consequently, targeting inflammatory pathways (e.g., NLRP3 inflammasome) represents a major therapeutic focus.

The activity of neutrophils also triggers a series of detrimental cascades within the brain, worsening the pathological advancement of AD. 42 , 43 Aβ is key in recruiting neutrophils. It activates brain microvascular endothelial cells, upregulating the expression of adhesion molecules such as intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM‐1), vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM‐), E‐selectin, and P‐selectin, thereby promoting neutrophil adhesion to the vascular endothelium. 43 , 44 Substances released by neutrophils, such as matrix metalloproteinase‐9 (MMP‐9) and myeloperoxidase, degrade tight junction scaffold proteins and the endothelial basement membrane, leading to blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction and damage, increasing vascular permeability. 42 , 45 , 46 , 47 Furthermore, during the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), the release of ROS, pro‐inflammatory cytokines, and granular components initiates and amplifies chronic inflammation. 48 Ultimately, these processes promote neurodegeneration and cognitive dysfunction.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic derangements

Mitochondrial impairment emerges early in AD, characterized by deficient ATP synthesis and excessive ROS production. 49 This energy shortage may be aggravated by mutations in mitochondrial DNA. Furthermore, central insulin resistance, by impairing PI3K/Akt signaling, exerts a triple pathological impact: reducing Aβ clearance efficiency, promoting tau phosphorylation, and enhancing neuroinflammatory responses. This creates a metabolically driven mechanism accelerating neurodegeneration. 50 Importantly, peripheral metabolic syndrome, often stemming from chronic high‐fat/high‐sugar diets, is a significant risk factor for developing central insulin resistance.

Neurotransmitter system dysfunction

Cholinergic system degeneration is fundamental to cognitive deficits in AD. Progressive loss of cholinergic neurons in the basal forebrain leads to decreased activity of choline acetyltransferase (the key enzyme for acetylcholine [ACh] synthesis), impaired choline uptake, and significantly reduced ACh release. 51 The concomitant dysfunction of cholinergic receptors further compromises neural signaling critical for learning and memory. 52 Simultaneously, the glutamatergic system, mediating excitatory synaptic transmission and plasticity, suffers significant disruption from both Aβ and tau pathology. Structural abnormalities in the postsynaptic density and functional inhibition of glutamate receptors collectively impair synaptic plasticity, directly contributing to cognitive decline. 53

Environmental and lifestyle factors: modifiable risks

Environmental factors significantly modulate AD progression by influencing core pathological pathways. Diet and metabolism play a key role; chronic consumption of high‐fat/high‐sugar diets promotes insulin resistance, which in turn drives pathology by hindering Aβ clearance, facilitating tau phosphorylation, and exacerbating inflammation. 50 , 54 Sleep disturbances interfere with the glymphatic system's crucial function of clearing metabolic waste, including Aβ, leading to its accumulation. Sleep deprivation also directly induces tau hyperphosphorylation, substantially increasing the risk of NFT formation. 55 , 56 Among environmental exposures, heavy metals and chemicals are undoubtedly the most prominent. Some studies have found that aluminum can bind to tau protein and promote its abnormal phosphorylation and aggregation into neurofibril tangles. Epidemiological studies have found that the incidence of AD is relatively high in some aluminum‐contaminated areas. 57 Particularly significant is the protective effect of cognitive reserve: Higher educational attainment and sustained cognitive engagement throughout life build neural resilience. This enhanced cognitive reserve allows for functional compensation in the presence of AD pathology, delaying the onset of clinical symptoms, with the degree of protection correlating positively with reserve levels. 58 , 59

Overall, these elements converge to drive neurodegenerative mechanisms that result in the cognitive deterioration typical of AD (Figure 1).

THE CENTRAL ROLE OF THE CEREBRAL LYMPHATIC SYSTEM IN AD

The brain's waste clearance network: a structural and functional hub

The brain's lymphatic system, a complex network, has been defined in the past 10 years as crucial for upholding brain homeostasis. It comprises several interconnected components: the glymphatic system, mLVs, the nasopharyngeal lymphatic plexus, and deep cervical lymph nodes (dCLNs). 60 , 61 , 62 This network functions in conjunction to enable the crucial removal of metabolic waste and govern neuroimmune monitoring, forming a brain‐wide “sewage” infrastructure.

The glymphatic pathway: intraparenchymal fluid exchange and clearance

CSF, as a central mediator, plays a pivotal role in mechanical protection, metabolic balance, and the removal of interstitial solutes. 63 , 64 Through the glymphatic pathway, CSF enters the brain parenchyma via periarterial spaces, mixes with ISF, and facilitates the clearance of solutes and proteins like Aβ. 65 , 66 This bulk flow is powerfully driven by arterial pulsation and is critically dependent on the polarized localization of AQP4 water channels on astrocytic endfeet. 65 , 66 , 67 Facilitated by AQP4, the glymphatic system enables efficient mixing of CSF with ISF and directs waste solutes toward clearance pathways along venous perivascular spaces. 68 This mechanism underscores the importance of sleep in metabolic clearance. Conversely, sleep deprivation has been shown to reduce Aβ clearance, thereby elevating the risk of neurodegenerative disorders. 69 The efficiency of this CSF–ISF exchange is markedly enhanced during sleep, underscoring a fundamental link between sleep–wake cycles and brain metabolic health.

mLVs: the central nervous system (CNS)‐periphery conduit

As early as the 18th century, Paolo Mascagni (1787) described lymphatic vessels in the meninges in his work Vasorum lymphaticorum corporis humani historia et ichonographia. This early observation was later supported by anatomical studies, including the work of Li et al. in 1996. 70 , 71 In 2015, advances in imaging and molecular techniques enabled the detailed characterization of mLVs, which renewed scientific interest in this system. 11 , 13 These vessels, lined by lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs), absorb CSF, ISF, and their cargo of metabolic waste and immune cells from the subarachnoid space, ultimately draining them to the dCLNs for processing. 72 , 73 , 74 While anatomically distinct, the glymphatic system and mLVs are functionally integrated, forming a comprehensive clearance pathway conceptualized as the CSF–ISF–mLV–dCLN axis, which is indispensable for the removal of neurotoxic aggregates implicated in AD. 11 , 13 , 75 , 76 , 77 The ability to non‐invasively visualize these vessels using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has significantly advanced our understanding of their physiology and pathology (Figure 2). 12 , 78

Functions of meningeal lymphatic vessels (mLVs) and glymphatic system in AD. Functions of meningeal lymphatic vessels (mLVs) and the glymphatic system in AD. Under healthy conditions, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) enters the brain parenchyma through perivascular spaces via aquaporin‐4 (AQP4) water channels expressed by astrocytes, where it exchanges with interstitial fluid (ISF). This exchange facilitates the clearance of metabolic waste products, including amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau protein. The waste‐laden ISF is subsequently drained back into the subarachnoid space along venous compartments and further transported by meningeal lymphatic vessels (mLVs) located within the dura mater to the deep cervical lymph nodes (dCLNs). Dysfunction of this clearance system leads to the accumulation of pathological proteins (Aβ plaques and phosphorylated tau) within the brain parenchyma. This accumulation triggers neuroinflammation, activating microglia and reactive astrocytes to release pro‐inflammatory cytokines. Crucially, these cytokines can further damage lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) and compromise the integrity of mLVs, thereby establishing a feedforward mechanism. This exacerbates the failure of waste clearance, accelerating the ultimate pathogenesis of AD.

Structural and functional decline of the lymphatic vasculature

Age and genetically driven clearance failure

In the healthy brain, the cerebral lymphatic system ensures the continuous drainage of pathological protein like Aβ and tau. However, the decline in its function creates a favorable environment for pathological development. The initial damage often stems from impaired clearance. With advancing age or due to genetic risk factors like the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 genotype, the structure and function of mLVs degenerate‐vessels constrict, valve distribution is altered, and wall permeability increases. 79 Concurrently, glymphatic influx is dampened by a loss of AQP4 polarization and reduced arterial pulsatility, often associated with comorbid conditions like arteriosclerosis and hypertension. 80 , 81 This collective decline in clearance capacity leads to the accumulation of Aβ and phosphorylated tau in the brain parenchyma. 4 , 82

Protein accumulation and neuroinflammatory sabotage

The accumulated pathological proteins then trigger chronic neuroinflammation, which in turn causes further damage to the lymphatic system. Activated microglia and astrocytes release pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐1β and TNF‐α, which can directly damage LECs and disrupt the integrity and function of mLVs. 14 , 83 This forms a feedforward loop The interaction between proteinopathies and neuroinflammation, which is promoted by the failure of the lymphatic system function, is now regarded as the core driving factor of the pathogenesis of AD.

The critical impact of aging on cerebral clearance

Aging is the single greatest risk factor for AD and is concomitantly associated with a profound decline in cerebral lymphatic function. This age‐related impairment is multifactorial. 84 , 85 Structurally, mLVs in aged individuals exhibit a reduction in diameter and complexity, alongside alterations in lymphatic valves, which hinder efficient fluid transport. 14 Functionally, the supporting “drivers” of glymphatic flow wane; arterial pulsatility diminishes, and perivascular AQP4 localization becomes depolarized. 84 , 85 Some studies found that factors such as arteriosclerosis and hypertension may further reduce the efficiency of the glymphatic system by reducing the amplitude of arterial pulsation and further affect APQ4 channels, which may accelerate the accumulation of Aβ and tau proteins. 86 Beyond central brain changes, aging also compromises the efferent limb of clearance. A recent study by Du et al. has provided direct evidence that aging reduces the contractile frequency and flow rate of cervical lymphatic vessels and that enhancing this contractility can rescue CSF drainage and CNS clearance in aged mice. 87

The role of sleep alterations and impaired lymphatic motility

Furthermore, age‐related changes in sleep architecture, particularly the reduction in slow‐wave sleep, directly blunt the nocturnal surge of glymphatic activity, which is crucial for the clearance of daily metabolic waste. 88 Recent clinical neuroimaging evidence further supports this association. A study in chronic insomnia patients found that whole‑brain cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) was reduced and declined with insomnia severity, while the Diffusion Tensor Image‐Analysis along the Perivascular Space (DTI‐ALPS) index, a non‑invasive marker of glymphatic function, was also lower; a strong positive correlation emerged between the two (r = 0.720), indicating a direct link between impaired vascular reactivity and glymphatic dysfunction. This identifies sleep quality as a key determinant of cerebral clearance efficiency, independent of the broader structural declines associated with aging. 89

Measures targeting cerebral lymphatic function improving prognosis in neurological disorders

Increasing evidence suggests that dysfunction of the cerebral lymphatic system is a common pathological feature across many neurological disorders. Beyond AD, impaired brain waste clearance is also a hallmark of other proteinopathies. In Parkinson's disease (PD), patients show markedly reduced mLV flow and delayed perfusion of the dCLNs. 90 These changes are associated with increased deposition of α‐synuclein (α‐syn) and accelerated disease progression. Molecular research further indicates that the degradation of β‐dystroglycan (β‐DG) mediated by MMP‐9 may lead to depolarization of AQP4, thereby disrupting lymphatic transport. 91 Notably, DTI‐ALPS index has emerged as a robust non‐invasive imaging marker of glymphatic function. Reduced DTI‐ALPS values have been reported in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD), and these reductions correlate with disease severity. 92 , 93

Overall, impairment of the glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic systems is not limited to neurodegenerative diseases. Similar abnormalities have also been observed in epilepsy, stroke, brain tumors, and traumatic brain injury (TBI). Similarly, in intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and subdural hematoma (SDH), mLVs play a crucial role in hematoma resolution. Panax notoginseng saponins promote lymphangiogenesis and enhance drainage, reducing hematoma volume and improving neurological function. 97 Ligation of cervical lymphatics significantly impedes this clearance process. 98

Following TBI, persistent loss of AQP4 polarity contributes to chronic glymphatic dysfunction. 99 , 100 Cannabidiol (CBD) has been proven to enhance cognitive and motor functions. The mechanism may lie in the fact that CBD reduces the accumulation of pathological proteins, regulates the polarization state of AQP4, and clears waste from the lymphatic nodes of cells. Notably, the therapeutic benefits of CBD are eliminated upon surgical disruption of cervical lymphatic pathways, underscoring the system's indispensability. 101

In idiopathic PD (iPD), patients exhibit reduced mLV flow and delayed dCLN perfusion, which impedes the clearance of αsyn from the CSF and accelerates disease progression. 90 In A53T α‐syn transgenic models, oligomeric α‐syn in the CSF triggers endoplasmic reticulum stress in dCLN macrophages, promoting peripheral inflammation and highlighting a key immune‐lymphatic axis in PD pathogenesis. 102

In summary, cerebral lymphatic dysfunction is a common pathological feature across multiple neurological disorders. Therapeutic strategies that enhance mLV function, either through pharmacological agents or by modulating specific signaling pathways, may reduce neuroinflammation, facilitate clearance of pathological proteins, and support neurological recovery, thereby offering promising therapeutic avenues.

MULTIMODAL STRATEGIES FOR RECONSTRUCTING AND ENHANCING CEREBRAL LYMPHATIC CLEARANCE

The clearance of Aβ and tau via the mLVs and the glymphatic system is increasingly recognized as a vital component of brain proteostasis. Emerging data suggest that the progressive failure of these interconnected pathways may facilitate the accumulation of neurotoxic metabolites, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of AD. These observations have stimulated a growing interest in the functional reconstruction of cerebral drainage pathways, a therapeutic framework that now integrates pharmacological and physical modulations alongside specialized microsurgical interventions. Intervention strategies. These can be categorized into four types: (1) Pharmacological interventions (e.g., melatonin, borneol), employed to regulate lymphatic structure and clearance kinetics. (2) Non‐invasive physical modulation (e.g., photobiomodulation, PBM; transcranial magnetic/focused ultrasound, TMS/FUS), utilized to regulate fluid dynamics. (3) Molecularly targeted interventions, such as those targeting VEGF‐C or AQP4. (4) Surgical interventions (e.g., dCLVA or CBM) to directly enhance anatomical drainage. These therapies synergistically promote AQP4 channel hyperpolarization, restore CSF‐ISF exchange efficiency, return microglia and astrocytes to quiescent states to mitigate neuroinflammation, and repair damaged mLV, thereby enhancing waste clearance. The ultimate outcome is reduced neurodegeneration and improved cognitive function.

Pharmacological restoration of lymphatic infrastructure and clearance kinetics

Several studies in AD mouse models have demonstrated that pharmacological agents enhance brain waste clearance through diverse molecular pathways, targeting both the glymphatic system and mLVs. These interventions range from natural hormones and isolated compounds to complex herbal formulations, each with distinct mechanisms and translational considerations.

Natural hormones and isolated compounds

Melatonin, a hormone primarily secreted by the pineal gland, is synthesized from tryptophan via serotonin and is tightly regulated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus. Its secretion follows a circadian rhythm and is strongly inhibited by environmental light. 103 , 104 Increasing evidence suggests a functional interaction between melatonin and dopaminergic signaling within circadian regulatory networks. 105 , 106 Melatonin signaling deficiency and circadian rhythm disruption are common clinical manifestations in neurodegenerative diseases, including AD and PD. 107 Similarly, dopamine, which is also regulated by photoperiod and circadian rhythms, is significantly reduced in the brains of AD patients. 108 , 109 , 110 Given that dopamine has been reported to facilitate Aβ degradation through neprilysin activation, the melatonin–dopamine axis may represent a neurochemical interface linking circadian regulation, neurotransmitter dynamics, and amyloid homeostasis. 111 It is worth noting that supplementation with melatonin has been proven to promote the clearance of Aβ through the cervical lymphatic system, increase soluble Aβ40 in the lymph nodes, and reduce Aβ oligomers in the brain. Although its high safety profile is advantageous, the optimal dosing regimen and its direct effects on cognition require further behavioral validation. 112 Additionally, melatonin may also inhibit Aβ generation by downregulating pathogenic secretases, though the link between this neuroprotective effect and lymphatic clearance remains unclear and warrants investigation. 113

Other natural molecules also demonstrate targeted therapeutic effects. Borneol, for instance, activates the Forkhead Box C2 (FOXC2)/Akt axis, improving lymphatic endothelial tight junctions (e.g., VE‐cadherin) and valve function, thereby enhancing CSF Aβ efflux in APP/PS1 mice. While its ability to cross the BBB permeability is beneficial, high doses carry a risk of aberrant vascular permeability. 114 The clinically approved molecule L‐3‐n‐butylphthalide (NBP) enhances both CSF influx and ISF outflow in wild‐type mice and promotes Aβ clearance in APP/PS1 models, acting on both fluid dynamics and molecular targets; however, its cognitive benefits and long‐term efficacy need further confirmation. 115

Systemic regulation by traditional Chinese medicine formulas

Xueshuantong (XST) partially rescues meningeal lymphatic defects in APP/PS1 mice, promoting Aβ clearance and reducing inflammation, though its active components and precise molecular pathways require elucidation. 116 Similarly, Jiawei Xionggui Tang (JWXG) inhibits the COX‐2/PGE2 pathway, reducing lymphatic endothelial inflammation and restoring AQP4 polarity, 17 while Yuanzhi San modulates the transforming growth factor‐β1/Smad3 pathway to enhance collagen remodeling and meningeal lymphatic coverage. 117 A systematic review highlighted that various traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formulations promote Aβ clearance via AQP4 polarization and perivascular space modulation, yet mechanistic depth and standardization are often lacking. 118

Novel agents and delivery routes

Novel compounds and delivery routes are also under investigation. Kinsenoside, a compound similar to ginsenosides, can enhance the cognitive abilities of mice. It can expand microvascular lymphatic vessels, inhibit interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ) signal transduction, and improve lymph circulation. While these findings are supported by network pharmacology, human pharmacokinetics remain unexplored. 119 Intranasal oxytocin represents a non‐invasive strategy, facilitating Aβ transit from the brain parenchyma to the dCLNs via the CSF and improving cognition in APP/PS1 mice. This approach benefits from bypassing the BBB, though delivery efficiency can be variable, and its effects on human cerebral blood flow and AQP4 function require further investigation. 120

Non‐invasive physical modulation of glymphatic and lymphatic fluid dynamics

Non‐invasive physical techniques represent a promising therapeutic strategy for enhancing brain waste clearance by modulating glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic fluid dynamics. These approaches can be broadly categorized into several mechanistic classes, as summarized below.

Optical and optical‐hybrid approaches

Several methods utilize light to stimulate clearance pathways. Among them, photobiomodulation (PBM) has attracted increasing attention because of its ability to modulate extracellular fluid dynamics and cellular activity without invasive intervention. Experimental evidence suggests that PBM can facilitate the removal of metabolic waste from the extracellular space (ECS) and is associated with improved cognitive performance in AD models. For example, red light at 755 nm has been reported to accelerate the clearance of inflammatory mediators in the rat ECS by enhancing ISF flow and CSF influx, a process that may involve restoration of astrocytic AQP4 polarization. 121 In parallel, PBM may influence amyloid pathology through direct molecular effects. Exposure to 630 nm red light has been shown to disrupt the secondary structure of Aβ and suppress its aggregation in vitro. 122 Vascular dynamics may represent another contributing mechanism. Clinical observations have shown that irradiation with 627 nm red light (70 mW/cm2, 10 J/cm2) increases the contraction and relaxation rates of the human middle cerebral artery by approximately 25% and 30%, respectively, suggesting a potential improvement in cerebrovascular perfusion. 123

Near‐infrared (NIR) wavelengths further expand the therapeutic reach of PBM because of their deeper tissue penetration. 124 In AD mouse models, transcranial illumination at 808 nm has been reported to restore mLV architecture and improve lymphatic drainage efficiency, accompanied by improved cognitive performance in both 5xFAD and APP/PS1 mice. 16 These effects may be linked to enhanced mitochondrial metabolism and preservation of lymphatic endothelial integrity. It has been shown that 1267 nm NIR stimulation promotes CSF drainage through dorsal mLVs in 5xFAD mice. 125 In addition, 1070 nm NIR irradiation (4 J/cm2, 10 Hz) has been reported to improve cognitive function in 5xFAD mice by promoting microglial polarization toward an anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype and reducing Aβ burden. 126 Transcranial PBM (tPBM) has been shown to reduce Aβ plaques and improve cognition, with tracer studies suggesting involvement of glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic pathways, though precise light parameters and penetration depth remain constraints. 127 Nighttime photostimulation (PS) aligns with circadian rhythms, proving more effective than daytime application in promoting Aβ transport to dCLNs and improving recognition memory, yet potential sleep disruption and limitations of rodent models exist. 120 The combined approach of PBM (635 nm) + 5‐aminolevulinic acid (5‐ALA) restores lymphatic clearance during deep sleep, capitalizing on physiological rhythms non‐invasively, but parameter control and reliance on rodent data are current limitations. 128 While AQP4 is the predominant aquaporin regulating glymphatic fluid exchange in the CNS, PBM has also been shown to modulate other aquaporin subtypes in peripheral tissues. For instance, NIR PBM can upregulate AQP5 expression in salivary gland epithelial cells and improve fluid secretion in models of Sjogren's syndrome, suggesting that PBM may influence aquaporin‐mediated water transport across different physiological systems. 129

Electrical, magnetic, and acoustic stimulation

Modalities employing electrical, magnetic, or sound energy offer targeted neuromodulation. Electroacupuncture (EA) at specific acupoints enhances paravascular inflow, suppresses astrogliosis, reduces Aβ deposition, and improves cognition, leveraging a historically safe approach; however, parameter standardization and quantification of effects are needed. 130 High‐frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) enhances glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic clearance efficiency, reduces Aβ deposition, and improves memory, building on existing clinical applications, but it requires assessment of magnetic field depth and long‐term impacts. 131 Very low‐intensity ultrasound (1 MHz) activates the astrocytic transient receptor potential vanilloid‐4 (TRPV4)‐AQP4 pathway to boost CSF tracer influx, presenting a safe, repeatable method; however, its Aβ clearance efficacy and human brain penetration parameters require optimization. 132 MRI‐guided focused ultrasound (FUS) has confirmed glymphatic‐lymphatic efflux pathways in human AD and ALS patients, validating its relevance for therapeutic delivery, though device costs and BBB stability upon repeated use are concerns. 133

Rhythmic and multisensory neuromodulation

Stimulation at specific frequencies can entrain brain networks to facilitate clearance. Gamma entrainment at 40 Hz via visual stimulation restores AQP4 polarization, enhances CSF–ISF exchange and lymphatic drainage, and improves neural/behavioral outcomes, offering high compliance and potential for synergy with immunotherapies. Challenges include limited penetration efficiency and the need for long‐term visual safety data. 134 Similarly, multisensory gamma stimulation (40 Hz) promotes CSF influx/ISF outflow alongside AQP4 polarization and mLV expansion, activating endogenous clearance non‐invasively, but requires sustained application and faces uncertainty in late‐stage AD. 134 Continuous theta‐burst stimulation restores perivascular AQP4 polarization, enhances CSF–ISF exchange, reduces Aβ burden, and improves spatial memory, offering localized, rapid effects, yet parameter personalization and long‐term electrophysiological impacts need further study. 135

Aerobic and highintensity exercise

Preclinical studies demonstrate that both sustained aerobic exercise and high‐intensity interval training (HIIT) enhance glymphatic clearance and reduce AD pathology. These benefits are mediated through the improvement of astrocytic AQP4 polarization, which facilitates efficient CSF–ISF exchange. 136 , 137 Notably, HIIT has been shown to promote a shift in astrocytes from a neurotoxic (A1 type) to a neuroprotective (A2 type) phenotype, with the A2 phenotype being strongly associated with enhanced AQP4 polarization. This seems to provide a mechanistic basis for the induced changes in astrocytes through exercise and the increased clearance of CSF. 137

These neuroprotective mechanisms are strongly supported by clinical evidence. A recent longitudinal neuroimaging study in healthy volunteers revealed that 12 weeks of structured aerobic exercise concurrently enhanced both putative glymphatic influx in the brain parenchyma and the flow dynamics of mLVs. 138 This enhancement was linked to a beneficial modulation of inflammatory plasma proteins (e.g., S100A8/S100A9), implicating systemic immunomodulation in the underlying mechanism and solidifying exercise as a key non‐pharmacological strategy for brain clearance. 138

Hypertonic plasma treatment

Systemic hypertonic intervention enhances glymphatic activity without disrupting the BBB. Plog et al. demonstrated that elevated plasma osmolality boosted glymphatic influx by approximately three‑fold, reversing its suppression in AD models and improving anti‐Aβ antibody delivery to plaques. 139 Lilius et al. further clarified this mechanism using dynamic single‐photon emission computed tomography‐computed tomography, showing that hypertonic saline significantly increased parenchymal penetration of CSF‐administered tracers (∼80%) but had minimal effect on direct brain clearance. 140 Thus, this approach primarily enhances therapeutic delivery via the glymphatic pathway, offering a strategic window for improving intrathecal drug efficacy, though risks such as dehydration require careful management.

Surgical anatomical reconstruction and functional augmentation of drainage pathways

Surgical strategies aim to restore brain waste clearance by directly reconstructing or augmenting anatomical drainage pathways. These interventions range from validating mechanistic pathways to developing potential therapies. Surgical impairment of natural drainage pathways has been instrumental in validating their functional significance. In APP/PS1 mice, ligation of the dCLNs exacerbates Aβ accumulation, disrupt AQP4 polarization, and aggravate cognitive deficits. This highlights the importance of the intact cervical drainage pathway. While this approach is primarily used for mechanistic validation rather than therapy, it provides essential proof of concept for lymphatic‐targeting strategies. 141

Lymphatic regeneration and functional restoration

A key surgical strategy involves promoting the regeneration of damaged lymphatic networks. Following meningeal lymphatic ablation, administration of VEGF‐C can restore lymphatic function, thereby mitigating the adverse effects associated with anti‐Aβ immunotherapy and synergistically improving Aβ clearance and cognition. 142 In aged and craniosynostosis models, the Piezo1 agonist Yoda1 has been shown to restore meningeal lymphangiogenesis and CSF perfusion, demonstrating a reversible, mechano‐lymphatic therapeutic avenue. 143 In aged AD rats, implanting a collagen scaffold that bridges the dura to the submandibular lymph nodes guides lymphatic regeneration, significantly improves novel object recognition, and correlates with neolymphangiogenesis. This approach offers a targeted, minimally invasive strategy. However, its long‐term biocompatibility and the effects of scaffold degradation require further investigation. 144 Beyond pharmacological and structural interventions, photonic strategies have emerged as a promising non‐invasive modality to support functional restoration during the regenerative process. Unlike purely anatomical reconstruction, PBM may help compensate for cellular functional insufficiency by optimizing mitochondrial bioenergetics and mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction. 124 As highlighted by Chamkouri et al., NIR PBM has been associated with enhanced Aβ clearance and improved cognitive performance in experimental models, potentially through modulation of mLVs and immune responses. In addition to facilitating metabolic waste removal, PBM has been reported to promote neuroprotective and pro‐regenerative cellular responses. 145 The integration of light‐mediated functional enhancement with anatomical lymphatic regeneration may therefore represent a complementary strategy for restoring cerebral clearance capacity in AD.

Surgical modulation of drainage and drug delivery

Surgical techniques can also enhance the efficiency of existing pathways or local drug delivery. A long‐acting lyotropic liquid crystalline implant, designed for co‐delivery of cilostazol and donepezil near the dCLNs, enhances brain drug delivery, promotes macromolecule efflux via mLVs, and restores cognition in aged mice. 146 The CBM represents another innovative approach, demonstrating sustained cognitive benefits, reduced amyloid plaques, and promotion of meningeal lymphangiogenesis in AD models. Crucially, these benefits are abolished by mLV ablation, definitively establishing mLVs as central mediators of CBM's therapeutic effect. 19

Deep cervical lymphovenous anastomosis: a novel surgical avenue

The dCLNs have been proposed as a potential peripheral tissue correlate of brain waste clearance capacity. Several studies have reported elevated levels of AD‐related biomarkers such as Aβ and tau phosphorylated at threonine 181 (pTau‐181) in human cervical lymph nodes. This makes them a distinct portal to understanding brain pathology. 147 Therefore, microsurgical anastomosis of the cervical lymphatic vessels with veins, known as dCLVA, has gradually attracted attention as a new treatment approach. It aims to reduce lymphatic pressure and accelerate the circulation of CSF–ISF. 9 , 20 , 148 Still, its role in AD treatment remains exploratory.

The available clinical evidence is preliminary. In one case report, a 70‐year‐old patient with AD underwent dCLVA to enhance cerebral lymphatic drainage, and some short‐term clinical improvement was observed within 5 weeks. The Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score increased from 5 to 7, the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes score improved from 10 to 8, and the Geriatric Depression Scale score decreased from 9 to 0. Better sleep and daytime alertness were also reported. 20 A prospective cohort study of 26 AD patients reported that about 60% of caregivers observed symptomatic improvement 1 month after surgery, with increases in MMSE scores and reductions in neuropsychiatric symptoms. 149 More recent single‐arm studies also described postoperative changes after dCLVA. Tang et al. reported early improvements in cognitive and functional status in patients with advanced AD, although the authors emphasized that the absence of a control group limited causal interpretation. 150 Another prospective study in a larger cohort of severe AD patients reported modest cognitive improvement during 6 months of follow‐up, together with decreased CSF Aβ and phosphorylated tau and corresponding increases in plasma levels. 151 Overall, these clinical observations are of great significance and support further research. At the same time, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Although dCLVA has received increasing clinical attention in China and has been applied in multiple medical centers, its role in the treatment of AD is still in the research stage. 152 , 153

Possible mechanisms have mainly been inferred from animal experimental work. Some studies have shown that brain‐derived solutes can reach the dCLNs through meningeal lymphatic vessels, together with CSF and ISF circulation. 154 , 155 , 156 These studies support the idea that dysfunction of meningeal and cervical lymphatic drainage may impair waste removal from the brain. In AD, this process may contribute to the retention of toxic proteins such as Aβ and tau. 157 , 158 , 159 , 160 , 161 On this basis, dCLVA has been suggested to improve lymphatic drainage by diverting lymph into the venous system and lowering local lymphatic pressure. 162 , 163 This may help fluid exchange and metabolite clearance. However, direct evidence that dCLVA enhances Aβ or tau clearance in human AD patients is still lacking.

Inflammation may also be involved. Neuroinflammation is a key feature of AD, and inflammatory factors such as IFN‐γ and IL‐17A may further impair meningeal lymphatic function. 164 , 165 , 166 , 167 , 168 This raises the possibility that better lymphatic drainage could also affect inflammatory signaling. Some support for this idea comes from studies of lymphaticovenous anastomosis in limb lymphedema. For example, Imai et al. reported postoperative reductions in IFN‐γ, IL‐17A, and markers of T‐cell exhaustion such as PD‐1 and Tim‐3. 169 These findings suggest that lymphatic reconstruction may influence immune status. Still, whether similar effects occur after dCLVA in AD patients remains unknown. Therefore, any immunological benefit in this setting should be regarded as hypothetical.

At the same time, dCLVA is a technically demanding microsurgical procedure and may lead to postoperative complications. Short‐term risks include lymphatic leakage, which may result from high local pressure or imperfect anastomosis and can cause local inflammation and edema. 170 , 171 , 172 , 173 Lymphangitis may also occur after surgery and may present with pain, redness, and fever, which requires timely treatment. 174 , 175 Thrombosis or scar formation at the anastomotic site may block drainage and reduce patency. 176 , 177 Long‐term complications warrant particular vigilance. Lymphatic backflow, where lymph flows retrogradely from veins into lymphatics, can reduce efficacy and cause problems; contributing factors include suboptimal anastomosis design (e.g., angulation, tension) or pathological vessel changes. 178 , 179 Preventive measures involve precise surgical planning, tension‐free anastomosis, careful suturing, and postoperative rehabilitation (e.g., exercise, compression therapy). 180 Venous hypertension is another concern and may result from venous injury, stenosis, outflow obstruction, or abnormal hemodynamics at the anastomotic site. 181 , 182 , 183 , 184 Other possible complications include infection, bleeding, lymphatic fistula, and nerve injury. 185 , 186 , 187 , 188 , 189 , 190 These risks should be fully considered when this procedure is discussed for patients with AD.

In summary, dCLVA may be an emerging approach. Early clinical observations have generated interest in this strategy, but the current human evidence remains limited and largely exploratory. The link between cervical lymphatic dysfunction and AD in humans has not yet been firmly established, and the long‐term clinical benefits of dCLVA remain unproven. In addition, the proposed mechanisms are mainly inferred from animal studies. Future multicenter studies with larger patient cohorts, longer follow‐up, and robust clinical and biomarker endpoints will be required to determine its safety, clarify its mechanisms, and evaluate its true therapeutic value in AD.

Precision molecular strategies for reconstructing specific clearance pathways

In contrast to surgical approaches, molecular strategies offer precise, mechanism‐driven modulation of the brain's clearance systems, targeting specific proteins and pathways.

Targeting the glymphatic system: AQP4 modulation

AQP4 modulation is a core strategy, given its critical role in glymphatic fluid flux at astrocytic endfeet; its dysregulation in AD makes it a prime target, though systemic effects necessitate brain‐specific delivery strategies. 191 AQP4 modulators, such as the enhancer TGN‐073 significantly improve glymphatic fluid dynamics and Aβ clearance in mouse models, offering precise and reversible channel targeting, though safety and efficacy in humans are unknown. 192

Promoting mLV function

Enhancing the structure and function of mLVs is another key strategy. VEGF‐C therapy, which induces mLV growth and enhances drainage, has been shown to reverse age‐related lymphatic decline and improve cognition. 193 Similarly, intranasal administration of oxytocin can increase the branching of microlymphatic vessels, improve the polarization of AQP4, and enhance Aβ clearance throughout the brain. Novel targets and immunomodulation

Emerging targets offer new avenues for intervention. AAV1‐mediated overexpression of DSCR1 increases dorsal mLV coverage, promotes Aβ clearance, and improves cognition in 5xFAD mice. 194 Heparanase inhibition represents another novel strategy, as its overexpression impedes perivascular Aβ clearance in human AD brain. 195 Furthermore, given that neuroinflammation impairs meningeal lymphatic function, immunomodulatory approaches like IFN‐γ‐neutralizing antibodies have shown promise in restoring aged lymphatic drainage to youthful levels, thus representing a strategy against age‐related lymphatic decline. 196

TRANSLATION CHALLENGES AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

The clinical translation of brain lymphatic‐targeted therapies faces three primary challenges. Current heavy reliance on transgenic AD mouse models (e.g., 5xFAD) remains problematic, as their Aβ overexpression patterns significantly differ from those in human sporadic AD. Substantial anatomical disparities exist between rodent and human glymphatic/lymphatic systems; notably, humans possess a more intricate meningeal lymphatic network, further limiting model relevance. Present lymphatic function assessment still largely depends on invasive tracers like fluorescein isothiocyanate‐dextran (FITC‐dextran) or terminal histology, creating an urgent need for non‐invasive, real‐time imaging techniques such as high‐resolution magnetic resonance lymphography. Critically, lymphatic dysfunction may precede overt Aβ deposition by 15 to 20 years in the preclinical AD stage. However, existing clinical trials focus primarily on mild to moderate patients, resulting in understudied preventive strategies and scarce data. Future research must prioritize establishing humanized lymphatic endothelial organoid models to better recapitulate the AD microenvironment, developing dual‐modality PET‐MRI probes for dynamic in vivo quantification of lymphatic drainage, and exploring synergistic modulation of lymphatic‐immune crosstalk targets, including the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis. Preliminary basic and clinical studies demonstrate that reducing Aβ and tau accumulation in CSF significantly enhances cognitive function and improves quality of life in AD patients. 197 Consequently, targeting the glymphatic system holds substantial therapeutic potential. Validation of dCLVA specifically requires broader, longer‐term studies (Figure 4A). Further research should also elucidate its mechanisms, refine surgical techniques, evaluate long‐term efficacy and safety, and explore combinations with other therapeutic strategies to optimize outcomes.

Beyond advancing brain lymphatic‐targeting technologies, future research should explore their integration with other emerging modalities to further enhance treatment efficacy for attention and cognitive impairments. Melatonin, secreted by the pineal gland, not only regulates sleep but also exhibits antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties (Figure 4B). Antibody‐based immunotherapies represent another promising direction. Passive immunization via intravenous monoclonal antibodies (e.g., lecanemab, donanemab) has demonstrated efficacy in clearing cerebral Aβ plaques (Figure 4C). 198 , 199 , 200 , 201 , 202 Alternatively, FUS+MB transiently disrupts the BBB, potentially enhancing the delivery and efficacy of AD therapeutics 203 , 204 , 205 ; microbubbles serving as contrast agents improve ultrasound penetration, rendering FUS+MB non‐invasive, safe, and relatively rapid. When combined, these technologies may synergistically promote waste clearance and improve intracranial homeostasis (Figure 4D). Combining lymphatic‐targeted agents with memory‐enhancing interventions like rTMS is also feasible. Non‐invasive high‐frequency stimulation of relevant cortical regions via rTMS can enhance neuroplasticity and improve short‐term memory and executive function. 206 , 207 Furthermore, AI‐driven personalized cognitive training programs show significant potential. These systems dynamically adapt memory, language, and spatial cognition exercises based on a patient's real‐time performance, improving adherence and therapeutic effects (Figure 4E). 208 , 209 In AD patients, melatonin improves sleep quality and alleviates behavioral disturbances. Its coadministration may amplify the benefits of other interventions by optimizing sleep and neural states. 113 Collectively, these novel approaches and adjunctive therapies offer considerable promise for slowing cognitive decline and improving quality of life in AD. Integrating cutting‐edge technologies with personalized treatment paradigms may pave the way for more comprehensive and effective AD management.

CONCLUSION

Reconstruction of the cerebral lymphatic system has been proposed as a potential therapeutic direction for AD, with the aim of addressing impaired brain clearance and drainage dysfunction. Strategies targeting lymphatic pathways, including pharmacological treatments, physical interventions, and surgical approaches, may help improve the removal of metabolic waste and therefore represent a potentially promising direction. However, current mechanistic understanding is derived largely from animal models, and direct clinical evidence in humans remains limited. In particular, a causal relationship between cervical lymphatic dysfunction and AD progression has not yet been firmly established. Future research should focus on determining whether the modulation of lymphatic drainage can produce measurable clinical benefits. This will require well‐designed clinical studies, improved non‐invasive imaging methods to assess lymphatic function, and clearer identification of therapeutic windows. At present, lymphatic‐targeted strategies should be considered exploratory. Further validation through rigorous clinical studies is needed before such approaches can be integrated into clinical treatment frameworks for AD.
