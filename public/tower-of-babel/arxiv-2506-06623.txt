Neural Operators for Forward and Inverse Potential–Density Mappings in Classical Density Functional Theory Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Runtong Pan, Xinyi Fang, Kamyar Azizzadenesheli, Miguel Liu-Schiaffini, Mengyang Gu, Jianzhong Wu ∗

Abstract
Neural operators are capable of capturing nonlinear mappings between infinite-dimensional functional spaces, offering a data-driven approach to modeling complex functional relationships in classical density functional theory (cDFT). In this work, we evaluate the performance of several neural operator architectures in learning the functional relationships between the one-body density profile ρ ⁡ ( x ) \rho(x) , the one-body direct correlation function c 1 ​ ( x ) {c_{1}}(x) , and the external potential V e ​ x ​ t ​ ( x ) V_{ext}(x) of inhomogeneous one-dimensional (1D) hard-rod fluids, using training data generated from analytical solutions of the underlying statistical-mechanical model. Several variants of the Deep Operator Network (DeepONet) and the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) were considered, each incorporating different machine-learning (ML) architectures, activation functions, and training strategies. These operator learning methods are benchmarked against a fully connected dense neural network (DNN), which serves as a baseline. We compared their performance in terms of the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss in establishing the functional relationships as well as in predicting the excess free energy across two test sets: (1) a group test set generated via random cross-validation (CV) to assess interpolation capability, and (2) a newly constructed dataset for leave-one-group CV to evaluate extrapolation performance. Our results show that FNO achieves the most accurate predictions of the excess free energy, with the squared ReLU activation function outperforming other activation choices. Among the DeepONet variants, the Residual Multiscale Convolutional Neural Network (RMSCNN) combined with a trainable Gaussian derivative kernel (GK-RMSCNN-DeepONet) demonstrates the best performance. Additionally, we applied the trained models to solve for the density profiles at various external potentials and compared the results with those obtained from the direct mapping V e ​ x ​ t ↦ ρ V_{ext}\mapsto\rho with neural operators, as well as with Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) combined with Active Learning by Error Control (ALEC), which has shown strong performance in previous studies. While the direct mapping from V e ​ x ​ t ↦ ρ V_{ext}\mapsto\rho suffers from high extrapolation error and proves inefficient for out-of-distribution predictions, the neural-operator mapping ρ ↦ c 1 \rho\mapsto{c_{1}} can effectively be used to solve the density profile via the Euler-Lagrange equation or be integrated with other surrogate methods. Moreover, neural operators offer additional flexibility through specialized operations, such as significance-based predictions on uneven grids (as in GK-CNN-DeepONet) and adaptive grid resolution adjustment (as in FNO), both of which can enhance prediction accuracy.

I
Introduction

Classical density functional theory (cDFT)
is a powerful tool in statistical mechanics to establish structure-property relationships for inhomogeneous thermodynamic systems.
Evans 1979
;
Jeanmairet et al. 2014
;
Wu 2017
The variational approach relies on formulation of the grand potential as a functional of the one-body density profile(s). For an equilibrium state specified by temperature, system volume, the external potentials, and chemical potentials of individual species, minimization of the grand potential leads to a set of self-consistent equations to predict both structural and thermodynamic properties, enabling a seamless connection between microscopic variables and the macroscopic behavior. Although the theoretical procedure is formally rigorous, the practical applications of
cDFT
are often hindered by challenges in deriving a reliable free-energy functional and in numerical complexity for solving the density profiles.
Wu 2006
;
Wu and Li 2007
;
de Morais Sermoud et al. 2024
These limitations have sparked considerable interest in using
machine learning (ML)
methods to develop reliable free-energy functionals and/or to solve the computationally demanding
Euler-Lagrange (EL)
equation.
Sammüller, Hermann, and Schmidt 2024
;
Simon and Oettel 2024
;
Wu and Gu 2023

ML
methods can be utilized to establish functional relationships in
cDFT
for the free energy (or the excess free energy) of an entire thermodynamic system, the local thermodynamic potential, or the external potential. For example, Oettel and coworkers explored the feasibility of using neural networks to construct free-energy functionals for one-dimensional (1D)
hard-rod (HR)
and
Lennard–Jones (LJ)
fluids.
Lin and Oettel 2019
;
Lin, Martius, and Oettel 2020
;
Dillon et al. 2017
With the free-energy functionals expressed in terms of artificial neural networks, they found that the
ML
functionals can reproduce the density profiles at conditions beyond the training region. Kelley
et al.
formulated a universal
ML
framework and training protocol to learn nonlocal functionals, combining a
Convolution Neural Network (CNN)
with weighted-density approximation for the free energy and a smooth convolution kernel. The
ML
functionals achieve excellent accuracy for a diverse set of systems, including the 1D
HR
fluids, liquid water, and the Ising model, as well as the exchange energy and the kinetic energy of inhomogeneous electrons.
Kelley et al. 2024
Yatsyshin
et al.
introduced a Bayesian method for predicting the external potential of classical particles from the density profiles generated through
Monte Carlo (MC)
simulation.
Malpica-Morales et al. 2023
;
Yatsyshin, Kalliadasis, and Duncan 2022
The
ML
regression predicts the grand potential and density profile of 1D
HR
systems in good agreement with exact results. Cats
et al.
combined the
cDFT
formalism with a stochastic optimization method to regress the free-energy functional of
LJ
fluids.
Cats et al. 2021
They expressed the non-mean-field component of the free energy in terms of the density profile in quadratic and cubic forms with the kernels obtained by fitting with numerical results obtained from grand-canonical
MC
simulation. The
ML
functional provides good predictions of both the thermodynamic properties of inhomogeneous systems and the pair correlation functions of bulk
LJ
fluids. Dijkman
et al.
proposed training the free-energy functional of
LJ
fluids exclusively on the
radial distribution functions (RDF)
of bulk systems, circumventing the need to sample costly heterogeneous density profiles in a wide variety of external potentials.
Dijkman et al. 2025
;
Sammüller and Schmidt 2024
Sammüller
et al.
applied a fully connected
Dense Neural Network (DNN)
to map the local value of the one-body direct correlation function as a functional of the one-body density profile.
Sammüller et al. 2023
In comparison to alternative methods, learning the one-body direct correlation function has advantages because it is typically short-ranged, allowing for quasi-local approximation to reduce the length scale of input space. The neural functional outperforms the state-of-the-art
cDFT
methods for hard-sphere fluids and is able to capture a broad spectrum of physical behavior associated with liquid-gas phase coexistence in bulk and at interfaces.
Sammüller, Schmidt, and Evans 2025
Bui and Cox incorporated the neural functional approach for one-body direct correlation functions with the local molecular field theory to achieve an accurate description of the structure and thermodynamics of electrolyte solutions and ionic liquids near various charged surfaces.
Bui and Cox 2025
Yang
et al.
demonstrated that an optimized operator learning method can be established to represent the functional relation between the density profile and the one-body direct correlation function for polyatomic systems.
Yang et al. 0

In the realm of
cDFT
, artificial neural networks can also be used as surrogates for the iterative solution of the
Euler-Lagrange (EL)
equation.
Santos-Silva et al. 2014
Furthermore,
ML
methods can be used to establish operator relationships among the one-body external potential, the one-body density profile, and the one-body direct correlation function. For example, Fang
et al.
utilized a
parallel partial Gaussian process (PP-GP)
emulator to predict the one-body density profile from the one-body external potential for 1D
HR
fluids.
Fang, Gu, and Wu 2022
The infinite-dimensional mapping is applicable to high-fidelity predictions with a controlled predictive error and is more computationally scalable than direct
cDFT
calculations.

While neural networks can achieve high accuracy in learning functional relationships, they typically require extensive training data and do not provide reliable error estimates when applied outside the training distribution.
Li et al. 2020a
Moreover, neural functionals trained on a specific class of external potentials may not generalize well to other classes due to distributional shifts between the training and testing inputs, limiting their applicability to realistic molecular systems.
Wang et al. 2023
In contrast, operator learning offers a more fundamental and flexible approach by learning relationships between functions—i.e., mathematical operators—directly in function spaces. Compared to traditional functional learning methods, which map functions to scalar outputs, operator learning naturally accommodates functional inputs, is invariant to discretization, and provides improved generalization and computational efficiency for inference at variable resolutions.
Kovachki et al. 2023

In this work, we investigate the performance of several neural operators in learning the mappings from the one-body density profile
\rho(x)
to the one-body direct correlation function
{c_{1}}(x)
and that from the external potential
V_{ext}(x)
to
\rho(x)
. Reference data for training and testing the
ML
models are generated from the analytical equations for 1D
HR
fluids.
Vanderlick, Scriven, and Davis 1986
;
Fang, Gu, and Wu 2022
This prototypical model enables fast generation of the training data and easy construction of interpolation and extrapolation data sets for calibrating various
ML
methods.
Muckley et al. 2023
Several variants of the
Deep Operator Networks (DeepONet)
and
Fourier neural operators (FNO)
models are considered, which incorporate different network architectures, activation functions, and training strategies. These operator learning methods are evaluated against
DNN
as the baseline. We demonstrate that operator learning methods can be used to effectively learn the functional relationship between the density profile and the one-body direct correlation function, and that between the external potential and the density profile. Analysis based on the Chinchilla scaling law,
Kaplan et al. 2020
which describes how model performance scales with compute, model size, and data size, shows that operator learning methods generalize better than traditional discrete
ML
approaches.

II
Data Preparation and ML Methods

II.1
cDFT

To illustrate the basic features of
cDFT
, consider a thermodynamic system of classical particles specified by absolute temperature
T
and chemical potential
\mu
. The training data for establishing the functional relation between the one-body density profile
\rho(x)
and the one-body direct correlation function
{c_{1}}(x)
can be generated from molecular simulation (or analytical methods) by systematically varying the external potential
V_{ext}(x)
. These real-valued functions are defined in the same spatial domain
\mathbb{D}
that defines the system volume, and they are interconnected through the
EL
equation:

\rho(x)\Lambda^{D}=\exp\left[-\beta V_{loc}(x)+{c_{1}}(x)\right]

(II.1)

where
V_{loc}(x)\equiv V_{ext}(x)-\mu
represents the background potential,
\Lambda
denotes the thermal wavelength, and
D
stands for the dimensionality of spatial domain
\mathbb{D}
. As usual,
\beta=1/(k_{B}T)
and
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant. The exact form of
\mathbb{D}
is system-specific, depending on the space accessible to the classical particles and their configuration.

cDFT
asserts that both the external potential
V_{ext}(x)
and the correlation function
{c_{1}}(x)
are uniquely determined by the density profile
\rho(x)
, and that all thermodynamic properties can be derived from these functional relationships.
Hohenberg and Kohn 1964
;
Mermin 1965
;
Evans 1979
By learning the functional relationship between
{c_{1}}(x)
and
\rho(x)
using
ML
methods, it is, in principle, possible to predict the microscopic structure and thermodynamic properties under arbitrary external potentials—without explicitly sampling the system’s microstates. This data-driven approach eliminates the need for approximations often required in constructing the free-energy functional. Furthermore, learning the mapping from
V_{loc}(x)
to
\rho(x)
allows us to bypass the computationally intensive task of minimizing the grand potential or solving the
EL
equation.

. The performance of various neural operators for predicting

II.2
Operator learning models

DeepONet
and
FNO
are two powerful
ML
models that have been commonly used to solve partial differential equations and learn operator mappings between function spaces.
Lu et al. 2022a
While
DeepONet
provides a general approach for learning operators across diverse domains,
FNO
leverages Fourier transforms to capture global spatial correlations.
Li et al. 2021a
The spectral method is known for efficiency in structured problems, especially in handling smooth functions and structured grids. However, its performance may deteriorate greatly for systems with complex geometries. In both
DeepONet
and
FNO
models, the input and output functions are discretized and evaluated at sets of distinct positions.

II.2.1
Deep Operator Networks (DeepONet)

As illustrated in Figure
3
,
DeepONet
consists of two subsidiary neural networks, known as the branch net and the trunk net. The branch net processes the discretized input function
\textit{u}(x)
as a multidimensional vector by evaluating it at a set of positions
\mathbf{x}_{I}=(x_{I,1},x_{I,2},\cdots,x_{I,n_{GI}})
:

\mathbf{u}=[u(x_{I,1}),u(x_{I,2}),\cdots,u(x_{I,n_{GI}})],

(II.2)

where
n_{GI}
denotes the number of input grid points. The trunk net, in contrast, takes the output sensor positions
\mathbf{x}_{O}
as its input,
\mathbf{x}_{O}=(x_{O,1},x_{O,2},\cdots,x_{O,n_{GO}})
, where
n_{GO}
is the number of grid points for the output function. While the grid points for the input and output functions need not be the same, for any given sample, the output layers of the branch net,
{\mathbf{b}}=[b_{1},\ldots,b_{p}]
, and the trunk net,
{\bm{\tau}}=[\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{p}]
, are both one-dimensional vectors of the same length
p
. In our implementation of
DeepONet
, the output size of both networks is fixed at
p=512
, commensurate with the domain size
\mathbb{D}=[0,L]
. The final output function
v(x)
is computed as the inner product of
{\mathbf{b}}
and
{\bm{\tau}}
, plus a bias term
b_{0}
, and is represented as another multidimensional vector:

{\mathbf{v}}=[v(x_{O,1}),v(x_{O,2}),\cdots,v(x_{O,n_{GO}})],

(II.3)

where

\textit{v}(x)=\mathcal{G}(\textit{u})(x)={\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{u})\cdot{\bm{\tau}}(x)+b_{0}.

(II.4)

DeepONet
is not tied to any specific architecture for its branch or trunk networks. Instead, it adopts
DNN
s as the default implementation. In this work, we refer to this architecture as
DNN
-
DeepONet
, and use it as one of the benchmarks for comparing various
ML
methods. As illustrated in
Appendix

D
(Figure
S2
),
DNN
-
DeepONet
consists of two hidden layers with 512 nodes each in the branch
DNN
net, and a single hidden layer with 512 nodes in the trunk
DNN
net.

Figure 3:
Schematic of the general
DeepONet
architecture. The branch net consists of
p
unstacked sub-networks, each processing components of the discretized input function. The trunk net is a single stacked network that produces
p
outputs corresponding to the sensor locations of the output function.

The performance and training efficiency of
DeepONet
can be enhanced by optimizing the structures of both the trunk and branch networks.
Diab and Al Kobaisi 2024
;
Kontolati et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2021
In our case, the input to the trunk net is simply a Cartesian grid. Prior studies have shown that for such structured sensor inputs, the choice of trunk net architecture has minimal impact on training outcomes due to the limited information content.
Lu et al. 2022b
Therefore, we adopt the default architecture for the trunk net without further optimization.

In contrast, the branch net encodes rich information from the input function, and its architecture plays a critical role in determining the performance. To optimize the network structure, we explore two advanced architectures for the branch net: a standard
CNN
and a
Residual Multi Scale CNN (RMSCNN)
(multi-scale
CNN
with
Residual neural network (ResNet)
). These network designs have been widely applied to computer vision tasks such as image detection and restoration.
Diakogiannis et al. 2020
;
Gao et al. 2021
;
Sosnovik, Moskalev, and Smeulders 2021
The architectural flowcharts of these branch networks are presented in
Appendix

D
(Figures
S3
and
S4
).

We consider
CNN
in our investigation due to its ability to generate translation-equivariant responses, which is especially suitable for image-like functional inputs. In the context of
cDFT
, the translational symmetry applies both to the entire system and to any non-interacting subsystems. Pooling, a fundamental operation in
CNN
, is used to reduce the spatial resolution of feature maps while retaining essential information. This approach improves computational efficiency and enhances robustness to variations in the input function.

RMSCNN
is also considered due to its deeper architecture and use of smaller convolutional kernels compared to standard
CNN
s. This design enables the extraction of long-range dependencies in deeper layers. Additionally, the skip-connections in
ResNet
help preserve high-resolution features that might otherwise be lost due to pooling operations.

Kelley
et al.
Dillon et al. 2017
demonstrated that Gaussian functions used as convolution kernels yield the best performance among a wide class of smooth functions in
CNN
-based models for
DFT
problems. Following their findings, we adopt the same Gaussian convolution kernels in this work, defined as

G_{\mathrm{gau}}(\sigma)(x)=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})(x)*(C_{0}+C_{1}x),

where
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})(x)
denotes a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and variance
\sigma^{2}
, and
*
represents convolution. These kernels are referred to as
Gaussian Kernel (GK)
when used within the
CNN
-
DeepONet
framework. For benchmarking purposes, the performance of the
GK
kernels is compared with that of the discrete kernels.

II.2.2
Fourier neural operators (FNO)

FNO
leverages Fourier transforms to capture global spatial correlations between input and output functions. In contrast to
DeepONet
, it requires that both input and output functions,
u(x)
and
v(x)
, be defined over the same domain,
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{D}_{in}=\mathbb{D}_{out}
. For generating the training data, these functions are discretized on a uniform grid:

{\mathbf{x}}_{u}={\mathbf{x}}_{v}={\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{N_{G}})

(II.5)

where the grid spacing
\Delta x=x_{i+1}-x_{i}
is constant. In the application of
FNO
to
cDFT
, all local properties, including the density profile
\rho(x)
, the reduced background potential
\beta V_{loc}(x)
, and the one-body direct correlation function
{c_{1}}(x,[\rho(x)])
, are defined on a real-space domain
x\in\mathbb{D}
with a uniform grid spacing. As a result, these functions naturally satisfy the input format requirements of the
FNO
framework.

To establish the operator mapping between functions
u(x)
and
v(x)
within
FNO
, the input network
\mathcal{P}
and output network
\mathcal{Q}
are realized through linear transformations that expand or reduce the data dimensionality:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}:[{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}_{0}(\mathbf{x})]_{i}=u(\mathbf{x})

(II.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}:v(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{v}}w_{i}\cdot[{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}_{L}(\mathbf{x})]_{i}

where
i\in(1,d_{v})
,
d_{v}
denotes the number of hidden channels, and
L
is the number of Fourier layers. The intermediate representation
{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times N_{G}}
is the output of the
j
-th Fourier layer evaluated on the
N_{G}
uniform grid points. As shown in Figure
4
,
{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}_{0}
is the lifted input matrix, and
{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}_{L}
is the final hidden representation passed to the output net. The
i
-th channel of
{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}_{0}
and
{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}_{L}
is denoted by
[{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}_{0}]_{i}
and
[{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}_{L}]_{i}
, respectively. The output net
\mathcal{Q}
computes the predicted function
v(x)
by performing a weighted summation across the
d_{v}
channels of
{\hat{\mathbf{z}}}_{L}
, using the learned weights
\mathbf{w}=[w_{1},\cdots,w_{d_{v}}]
.

II.2.3
Dense Neural Network (DNN)

For comparison between conventional functional learning and the operator learning methods, we also trained a full-scale
DNN
, meaning it learns the full-scale mapping, and quasi-local
DNN
as implemented in Neural-DFT
Sammüller, Hermann, and Schmidt 2024
which learns the quasi-local mapping of
\rho\mapsto c_{1}
. These functional networks take
\rho(x)
as the input and
{c_{1}}(x)
as the output. Both functions are measured at the sensor
\mathbf{x}_{sen}

III.3
Neural scaling law analysis

To better understand the dependence of model performance on key training parameters, we analyzed the mean squared error (
MSE
) loss using the Chinchilla scaling law. This empirical law was originally developed by researchers at OpenAI and Google.
Kaplan et al. 2020
;
Bahri et al. 2024
It offers a unified framework for assessing the performance of various
ML
models. While the Chinchilla scaling law has been successfully applied to tasks such as transfer learning and neural machine translation, to our knowledge, this approach has not yet been extended to
ML
models in physical problems including
DFT
.

The Chinchilla scaling law describes the log-transformed regression loss,
\log(\text{MSE})
, which is proportional to
Gaussian negative log-likelihood (GNLL)
with constant error, as a power-law function of model size (
D_{model}
) and training dataset size (
N_{sample}
):

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\infty}+\frac{A}{N_{sample}^{\alpha}}+\frac{B}{D_{model}^{\beta}}

(III.2)

where
A
,
B
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
are positive-valued scaling parameters to be inferred from data, and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
represents the irreducible loss—i.e., the theoretical minimum loss achievable by an ideal model trained on an infinite dataset with infinite model capacity. This asymptotic limit serves as a fundamental measure of the generalization capability intrinsic to the class of
ML
models under consideration, particularly in the extrapolation regime where both data and model resources are abundant.

A complementary quantity of interest in this analysis is the total training cost (
C_{t}
), given by

C_{t}=C_{s}\times N_{sample}

(III.3)

where
C_{s}
denotes the number of floating-point operations (
FLOPs
) required to train model parameters. For instance, it has been empirically determined that
C_{s}=6D_{model}
for
LLM
s. As anticipated, the training cost scales linearly with the size of the dataset for most
ML
methods. For parameter tuning, the computational cost also scales linearly with the number of model parameters. When some
D_{model}
-irrelevant costly steps are included in model training, we have
C_{s}=C_{0}+C_{1}D_{model}
, with
C_{0}
being the cost of those operations.
This provides a practical constraint for model selection and optimization under limited computational budgets.

We investigated the scaling behavior of both interpolation and extrapolation performance to evaluate and quantify the generalization capabilities of the various
ML
methods. The results are shown in Figure
8
. The fitted scaling parameters are summarized in Table
2
. The high
R^{2}
values confirm that the Chinchilla scaling law provides an excellent fit across all cases, with the exception of the extrapolation performance of the quasi-local
DNN
, whose results fall entirely within a plateau region and thus do not exhibit meaningful scaling behavior. Among the methods studied, the
FNO
achieves the lowest extrapolation
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
, indicating superior generalization capability in the asymptotic limit. In contrast, discrete functional learning models—such as the full-scale
DNN
and the quasi-local
DNN
—show markedly higher irreducible loss, reflecting significantly weaker extrapolation performance.

When training cost is taken into account, these findings offer important insights into the efficiency of each
ML
method. The training cost,
C_{t}
, which is assumed to be proportional to the training time, increases linearly with training data size and model size. For
DNN
,
DNN
-
DeepONet
,
DK
-
CNN
-
DeepONet
,
C_{0}\simeq 0
, since most of their calculation steps are
D_{model}
-dependent such as forward and backward propagation. Kernel operations in
GK
and
FFT
operations in
FNO
are
D_{model}
-irrelevant, resulting in a measurable
C_{0}
for them.
Despite the higher computational demands of
FNO
, its favorable scaling behavior implies that it reaches superior generalization performance more efficiently in the high-resource regime. Conversely, the plateauing behavior observed in the quasi-local
DNN
suggests that additional computational investment yields diminishing returns, making it less cost-effective for extrapolation tasks. Overall, these results underscore the importance of considering both performance scaling and computational cost when evaluating and selecting
ML
models for predictive tasks beyond the training domain.

III.5
Choice of activation function

Activation functions play a critical role in the training and performance of operator learning models, as they directly influence the model’s expressiveness, stability, and convergence behavior. For both
DeepONet
and
FNO
, the choice of activation function determines their ability to capture complex, nonlinear input–output mappings across function spaces. Unlike traditional pointwise regression tasks, operator learning requires handling high-dimensional and often nonlocal dependencies, placing additional demands on the smoothness and differentiability properties of the activation function. Moreover, certain activation functions can significantly affect the conditioning of gradients during training, impacting both the speed and robustness of optimization. As such, a careful evaluation of activation functions is essential for balancing model accuracy, training efficiency, and generalization performance, particularly when extending models to out-of-distribution or extrapolation regimes.

III.6
cDFT solvers

As mentioned above, neural operators can also be trained to predict
\rho(x)
from a given external potential
V_{\text{loc}}(x)
. In this case, the density operator
\mathcal{G}_{\rho}:V_{\text{loc}}\mapsto\rho
acts as a surrogate for solving the
EL
equation or direct minimization of the grand potential
\Omega
. This
ML
-based strategy can be implemented using the excess free energy functional or the one-body direct correlation function, derived either analytically or through functional learning.

Picard’s iteration remains the state-of-the-art method for most current applications of
cDFT
. This method is thus used as the baseline in this study. In the operator learning framework, we consider two strategies: (1) solving the
EL
equation using Picard’s iteration, and (2) minimizing the grand potential
\Omega
using the
Limited-memory Broyden, Fletcher, Goldfarb, and Shanno (L-BFGS)
algorithm. Both approaches are driven by the learned functional
\mathcal{G}_{c_{1}}:\rho\mapsto c_{1}
. This approach is referred to as operator-
DFT
, with the variant implemented via
FNO
denoted as
FNO
-
DFT
, which achieves the best overall performance.
ML
-
DFT
via quasi-local
DNN
is referred to as “Neural-DFT,” following the implementation by Sammüller et al.
Sammüller, Hermann, and Schmidt 2024
. In contrast, for direct learning of the operator
\mathcal{G}_{\rho}:V_{\text{loc}}\mapsto\rho
, the
FNO
-based model again yields the most accurate results and is presented as the representative example of direct density-based operator learning.

In a previous study,
Fang, Gu, and Wu 2022
we demonstrated that the
Active Learning by Error Control (ALEC)
framework provides an effective surrogate method for solving the
EL
equation. Here we use
ALEC
with
Gaussian process regression (GPR)
for learning the operator
\mathcal{G}_{\rho}
as a point of comparison. As a sampling-based active learning strategy,
ALEC
requires a sample generator to provide training data of
V_{loc}\mapsto\rho
.
ML
-based
DFT
methods, such as Neural-DFT using Quasi-local
DNN
and operator-

III.7
Transferability of operator mapping

Operator learning methods aim to predict an infinite-dimensional output function
v
from a given input function
u
, offering flexibility that is often unattainable with discrete functional learning methods. In comparison to functional learning methods, a key advantage of operator learning lies in its compatibility with newly positioned sensors for output measurements, thereby enabling model migration across different sensing configurations.

For instance, in the case of
DeepONet
, the output function can be evaluated at arbitrary sensor locations, provided the input function is sampled at consistent positions. This flexibility is further enhanced in smooth-kernel
CNN
-based
DeepONet
architectures, such as
GK
-
CNN
-
DeepONet
and
GK
-
RMSCNN
-
DeepONet
, which can tolerate changes in the input sensor positions as long as the sampling resolution satisfies
\Delta x\leq x_{conv,cut}=0.1
. In contrast,
FNO
requires uniform sampling for both input and output functions, which limits its ability to focus on regions of physical significance. Nevertheless,
FNO
supports model transfer across different sensor resolutions within the same spatial domain.

This capacity for flexible sampling is particularly relevant to
cDFT
problems, where spatial significance often varies across the domain. Regions near phase interfaces or solid boundaries exhibit rapid variations in density
\rho
and other physical properties, necessitating higher sampling frequencies to capture local behavior accurately. Conversely, bulk-like regions with nearly uniform external potentials tend to exhibit lower variability and can be adequately described with coarser sampling.

IV
Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the application of various neural-operator architectures—including multiple variants of
DeepONet
and
FNO
—for learning the mapping from the density profile
\rho(x)
to the direct correlation function
{c_{1}}(x)
within the
cDFT
framework. The results are compared against baseline models based on discrete functional mappings using
DNN
, trained with either full-range data or quasi-local data (Neural-DFT). We analyzed and discussed in detail the influence of the selected
ML
architectures and activation functions on the model training and performance.

For numerical efficiency, we used the training and testing data from analytical solutions of the one-dimensional
HR
fluid subjected to various external potentials. To evaluate both interpolation and extrapolation capabilities of the
ML
models, we constructed five distinct groups of external potentials, with the training samples randomly selected from four distinctive data groups (I-IV). The in-group test set is generated within the same data distribution as the training set. An additional test set, referred to as the ‘new data’ test set, is drawn from data group V to assess the extrapolation performance of the models. In addition to evaluating the mean squared error (
MSE
) of the predicted output function
{c_{1}}
, we further assess model performance based on the calculated excess free energy,
F_{ex}
, given a specified
\rho(x)
, and the predicted
\rho(x)
under a new background potential
V_{\mathrm{loc}}(x)
.

The results show that the
sRelu
activation function outperforms all other activation functions across the different
ML
models.
FNO
is the best neural operator model in every metric of model evaluation, including on
MSE
loss for In-group and New Data test loss, as well as the prediction of
{F_{ex}}
. Among the DeepONet variants tested in this work, the model using a Gaussian Kernel Multi-scale Residual Convolutional Neural Network as its branch net (
GK
-
RMSCNN
-
DeepONet
) delivers the best performance.
Neural scaling law analysis is used to estimate the generalization ability of ideal models with infinite trainable parameters
D_{model}
and infinite training samples
N_{sample}
. All neural operators closely follow the Chinchilla scaling law, with
FNO
exhibiting the strongest ideal generalization performance.

We also demonstrate that neural operators can be used to compute density profiles by solving the self-consistent
EL
equation. However, they struggle to directly learn the mapping
V_{\text{ext}}\mapsto\rho
, exhibiting poor extrapolation performance. Among the surrogate methods tested,
ALEC
-
GPR
proves to be the most effective alternative to solving the
EL
equation. Because active learning requires an efficient sample generation strategy, operator-DFT offers a promising surrogate for
NS
in this context.
In contrast to standard neural networks, a key advantage of neural operators is their ability to perform operator-based computations. These models learn infinite-dimensional function mappings, enabling evaluation at arbitrary positions—a capability inherently supported by
DeepONet
architectures. Furthermore, scalable convolution layers that use smooth kernels, such as those in Gaussian Kernel CNNs (
GK
-
CNN
), allow flexibility in the input sensor grid of the first convolution layer. This feature enables models like
GK
-
RMSCNN
-DeepONet (and
GK
-
CNN
-DeepONet) to make predictions on uneven grids while maintaining relevance to the underlying functional structure. Unlike
DeepONet
, the
FNO
architecture restricts both input and output functions to the same domain with uniform discretization. As a result, it does not support evaluation at arbitrary positions but excels in transferring across sensor resolutions—making it suitable for tasks involving model migration. These operator-based capabilities also extend to other
cDFT
computations, such as predicting
\rho(x)
under new external potentials
V_{\text{loc}}(x)
.

Although in this work we evaluated the performance of neural operators for
cDFT
using data from analytical solutions of the 1D
HR
fluid, these
ML
methods can be readily extended to more complex systems lacking satisfactory analytical density functional approximations, such as multi-component systems and those with many-body interactions.
Yang et al. 0
The operator learning models are inherently designed for multiple-input, multiple-output mappings, facilitating straightforward application to multi-component systems.
Tan and Chen 2022
;
Jiang, Zhu, and Lu 2024
.

Finally, the full-scale mapping employed in this study restricts the spatial domain of the trained model to
\mathbb{D}
, limiting the applicability of the operator learning methods. In
ML
-enhanced
cDFT
calculations, we may approximate the dependence of
{c_{1}}(x,\rho)
on
\rho(x)
via the quasi-local mapping:

{\rho^{(k)}(x^{\prime})}_{x-x_{cf}}^{x+x_{cf}}\mapsto{c_{1}}^{(k)}(x)

(IV.1)

where
x_{cf}
is a cutoff distance. In that case, only
\rho(x^{\prime})
within the position window centered at
x
with length
2*x_{cf}
impacts the
c_{1}
value at position
x
. While neural-DFT models the local value of
c_{1}
with a segment of
\rho
as the input,
Sammüller and Schmidt 2024
;
Sammüller et al. 2023
the quasi-local operator learning treats
c_{1}
as a function rather than as discrete data points, thereby preserving the operator relationship.
Yang et al. 0
Besides,
FNO
and its theoretical foundation, the graph neural operator (GNO), can effectively utilize training data at varying resolutions. By combining low-resolution data with physically informed neural operators (PINO), it becomes possible to accurately predict solutions at higher resolutions for solving partial differential equations.
Li et al. 2021b
;
Goswami et al. 2023
A major limitation in
ML
-based approach is the high cost of generating high-quality, high-resolution simulation data. PINO models trained on low-resolution data but guided by high-resolution physical constraints offer a promising approach to efficiently develop accurate
ML
correlation functions, particularly for complex molecular systems involving many-body interactions.

\displaystyle l(x_{0})\exp\left[\int_{x_{0}}^{x}h(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\right]

(A.4)

\displaystyle-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\exp\left[-\int_{x_{0}}^{x^{\prime}}h(x^{\prime\prime})dx^{\prime\prime}\right]l(x^{\prime}+d_{hr})h(x^{\prime}+d_{hr})dx^{\prime}

where
x_{0}\in[0,L]
is an arbitrary reference position, and
V_{loc}(x)=V_{ext}(x)-\mu
is the background potential in the domain
x\in[0,L]
. Eq.(
A.3
) can be solved successively over the intervals
kd_{hr}<x<(k+1)d_{hr},k=0,1,2,\cdots
, beginning with
0<x<d_{hr}
,

h_{0}(x)=\frac{e^{-\beta V_{loc}(x)}}{1+\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\beta V_{loc}(x^{\prime})}dx^{\prime}}

(A.5)

Similarly, Eq.(
A.4
) can be solved successively over intervals
L-(k+1)d_{hr}<x<L-kd_{hr},k=0,1,2,\cdots
, beginning with
L-d_{hr}<x<L
,

l_{0}(x)=\exp\left[-\int_{x}^{L}h(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\right].

(A.6)

In programming,
h(x)
and
l(x)
can be too small with 32-bit floating number, leading to high round-off errors in the integration steps.
\ln h(x)
and
\ln l(x)
are used to control round-off errors.
The default
\Delta x
would cause high truncation errors in the numerical integration of
h(x)
and
l(x)
with small
|x-x_{0}|
in
eq.

A.3
and
eq.

A.4
, which is controlled by increasing the sampling resolution 4 times.

Appendix B
External potential and chemical potential sets

The density profiles
\rho(x)
and one-body correlation functions
c_{1}(x)
investigated in this work were generated from five parametric forms of the external potential, defined over the domain
0\leq x\leq L
, with the reduced chemical potential in the range
\beta\mu\in(0,4)
. In dimensionless form, these external potentials are given by:

Group I
: Simple hard walls

\beta V_{ext}(x)=0

(B.1)

Group II
: Van der Waals-like attractive walls

\beta V_{ext}(x)=-\epsilon\left[\left(\frac{d_{hr}}{x}\right)^{3}+\left(\frac{d_{hr}}{L-x}\right)^{3}\right]

(B.2)

with
\epsilon\in(0.1,2.2)
.

Group III
: Linear potentials (Constant Force)

\beta V_{ext}(x)=m_{g}x

(B.3)

with
m_{g}\in(0.1,3)

Group IV
: Power-law potentials

\beta V_{ext}(x)=u_{0}\left|\frac{x-L/2}{x_{0}}\right|^{a_{0}}

(B.4)

with
u_{o}\in(1,3),x_{0}\in(1,3),a_{0}\in(2,5)
.

Group V
: Linear combination of groups 2 and 3 potentials

\beta V_{ext}(x)=m_{g}x-\epsilon\left[\left(\frac{d_{hr}}{x}\right)^{3}+\left(\frac{d_{hr}}{L-x}\right)^{3}\right]

(B.5)

with
\epsilon\in(0.1,2.2),m_{g}\in(0.1,3)
.

Appendix C
Activation functions

The following four activation functions were tested for training different
ML
models:
I. Rectified linear unit (
Relu
):

\mathcal{\sigma}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\leq 0\\
x,&x>0\end{cases}

(C.1)

II. Scaled exponential linear unit (
Selu
) function:

\mathcal{\sigma}(x)=\begin{cases}\lambda*x,&x>0\\
\lambda*\alpha*(e^{x}-1),&x\leq 0\end{cases}

(C.2)

where
\lambda=1.67326
and
\alpha=1.05070
are fixed constants of
Selu
, allowing auto internal normalization.

III. Logistic function:

\mathcal{\sigma}(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}

(C.3)

IV. Squared Relu (
sRelu
):

\mathcal{\sigma}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\leq 0\text{ or }x\geq 2\\
x(2-x),&0<x<2\end{cases}

(C.4)
