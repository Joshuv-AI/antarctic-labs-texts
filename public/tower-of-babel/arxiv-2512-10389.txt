The k k -flip Ising game
Aleksandr Kovalenko (a) , Andrey Leonidov (a,b)

Abstract
A partially parallel dynamical noisy binary choice (Ising) game in discrete time of N N players on complete graphs with k k players having a possibility of changing their strategies at each time moment called k k -flip Ising game is considered. Analytical calculation of the transition matrix of game as well as the first two moments of the distribution of φ = N + / N \varphi=N^{+}/N , where N + N^{+} is a number of players adhering to one of the two strategies, is presented. First two moments of the first hitting time distribution for sample trajectories corresponding to transition from a metastable and unstable states to a stable one are considered. A nontrivial dependence of these moments on k k for the decay of a metastable state is discussed. A presence of the minima at certain k ∗ k^{*} is attributed to a competition between k k -dependent diffusion and restoring forces.



Introduction

The dynamics of Ising games on graphs was previously studied in the single-flip approximation using an explicit Markov chain type description of system trajectory
[
3
]
as well as by considering the corresponding Langevin
[
4
]

In all these cases the arising description of a game was given in terms of a "free utility" (an analogue of the free energy in statistical physics) dependent, in the case of the Ising game on complete graph, on a single collective variable and combining the myopic utility of current configuration and its entropy.

An important problem which can naturally be analysed using the free utility description is that of a decay of a metastable state. This situation arises, in particular, in the Ising game in an external field where single-flip dynamics leads to an exponentially long decay
[
4
]
. Let us note that this decay can be accelerated in a self-excited version of the game considered in
[
1
,
2
]
.

In game theory simultaneous decisions of agents are rather a norm than an exception. In this context a single-flip assumption in describing dynamical properties of noisy discrete choice games looks somewhat unnatural and simply borrowed from Glauber dynamics
[
7
,
18
]
in spin systems where indeed simultaneous flipping of spin clusters is naturally considered as having low probability. In statistical physics such cluster algorithms
[
20
,
16
,
21
]
are used to accelerate Monte Carlo simulations. In particular, the Swendesen-Wang algorithm
[
20
]
is based on using the Fortuin-Kasteleyn cluster decomposition
[
9
,
6
]
developed for efficient description of the structure of partition function in the Potts model. Two-spin parallel dynamics was also considered in the framework of dynamical message passing approach to studying dynamical properties of Ising model on graphs in
[
15
]
.

The choice between sequential and parallel dynamics plays an important role in construction of models of (associative) memory with neural networks. In particular, the papers
[
14
,
12
,
13
]
analyse the possible role of parallel dynamics in reaching long-lived states of a neural network, while in
[
19
]
it was assumed that at each time step a Poisson-distributed random number of neurons could switch their states. Finally, in the foundational paper
[
8
]
a Glauber-type sequential dynamics was assumed. The models discussed in all of these papers have close parallels with studies of spin system dynamics and Ising games on graphs.

It is therefore of considerable interest to analyse dynamics of multi-agent noisy discrete choice games allowing for simultaneous decisions of a randomly chosen group of agents. In this paper we consider a simplest setting of a noisy binary choice (Ising) game of
N
agents located in the vertices of a complete graph played in discrete time in which at each time moment
k
randomly chosen agents,
k\in[1,N]
, are allowed to reconsider their current strategies. It is to be expected that a transition from single-flip to "multi-flip" dynamics involving randomly formed clusters of
k
agents should directly affect evolution pace by accelerating it. A quantitative assessment of this acceleration can be provided by studying, for given initial and final configurations of a system, a
k
-dependence of a probability distribution of time interval (number of steps in the discrete time dynamics) characterising trajectories connecting these configurations. In the literature one usually considers the first moment of this probability distribution, the mean time interval. For Markovian dynamics a general way of computing it is described in the literature on Markov chains, see e.g.
[
10
,
5
]
. Another popular strategy of addressing this issue is to use the Langevin
[
4
]
or Fokker-Planck equation formalism
[
17
]
, the latter employed, in particular, in the analysis of metastable state decay rate in the self-excited Ising game in

1.2
Transition matrix

At any time step decisions of

2.1
General remarks

In general a transition from sequential
1
-flip game to a partially parallel
k
- flip one does significantly affect all its aspects including the very character of dynamical evolution. In the present paper we concentrate on one specific aspect of distinction between the two games, a difference in time required to complete a transition between two given states of a system. Naively one would expect that in the (partially) parallel game the evolution is faster and so is this transition. We will see that this naive expectation is not supported by the analysis presented below and that the picture is more nuanced.

2.2
Transition times in Markov chain formalism

The main object studied in this paper is a first hitting time characterising a trajectory

, i.e. a number of steps
\tau_{ij}
required for the system to reach the state

starting from the state

For trajectories

generated by the random walk evolution of a

is stochastic and thus characterised by some probability distribution

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{ij}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\cdot\Omega_{ij}+\sum_{n\neq j}\Omega_{in}\langle(\tau_{nj}+1)\rangle=1+\sum_{n\neq j}\Omega_{in}\langle\tau_{nj}\rangle

\displaystyle\langle(\tau_{ij})^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\cdot\Omega_{ij}+\sum_{n\neq j}\Omega_{in}\langle(\tau_{nj}+1)^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{n\neq j}\Omega_{in}\langle(\tau_{nj})^{2}\rangle+2\sum_{n\neq j}\Omega_{in}\langle\tau_{nj}\rangle.

To explain the presence of minimum of
r_{\tau}(\beta,\gamma,k,N)
let us notice that any deviation from the stationary (or quasi-stationary) state induces a restoring force (
23
), which acts to shift the step-length distribution back towards the stationary (or quasi-stationary) state. The magnitude of this restoring force increases linearly with
k
. Considering the plots
9

26
) as a function of
k
, it is evident that its growth rate decelerates substantially with increasing
k
, i.e. its growth rate is significantly higher for small values of
k
as compared to larger ones. This leads to the conclusion that the emergence of a minimum in the transition time can be attributed to the competition between two opposing mechanisms. The first mechanism is the rapid increase in the width of the step-length distribution, corresponding to an enhancement of diffusion within the system. This factor acts as a mechanism that accelerates the escape from a metastable state. The second mechanism is associated with the mean displacement, or the restoring force. This mechanism, in contrast, provides an increasing confining effect as
k
grows. For small
k
, the diffusion is the dominant factor, enabling the system to escape the metastable state more rapidly. However, at a specific point
k=k_{\rm min}
(or
\rho=\rho_{\rm min}
), the influence of these two factors becomes comparable. Beyond this point, the confining effect of the restoring force surpasses the accelerating effect of diffusion.

Conclusion

The paper analyses the effects of simultaneous decision making in a (partially) parallel
k
-flip Ising game on a complete graph on transition times on sample trajectories corresponding to decays of metastable and unstable states into a stable one. The presented analysis is based on the analytical calculation of the transition matrix of a game allowing to calculate the moments of the first hitting time distribution and of the first two moments of the variable
\varphi=N^{+}/N
enabling to give an explanation for the observed behaviour of the moments of first hitting time distribution for the transition starting in the metastable state in terms of the competition between restoring and diffusive forces.

Appendix A

For arbitrary
k
, the mean of
\langle\Delta\varphi\rangle
is

\displaystyle\langle\Delta\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{x\}}\frac{k!}{x_{1}!\ x_{2}!\ x_{3}!\ x_{4}!}\ p_{+}^{x_{1}+x_{3}}(1-p_{+})^{x_{2}+x_{4}}\ \frac{N^{-}!}{(N^{-}-x_{1}-x_{2})!}

\displaystyle\times\frac{N^{+}!}{(N^{+}-x_{3}-x_{4})!}\frac{(N-k)!}{N!}\ \frac{1}{N}(x_{1}-x_{4}),

(50)

where
x_{1}
is the number of outcomes when a player with
s=1
is selected and changes strategy to
s=-1
, i.e.
+1\rightarrow-1
, then
x_{2}
is the number of outcomes
+1\rightarrow+1
(i.e. does not change strategy),
x_{3}
:
-1\rightarrow-1
and
x_{4}
:
-1\rightarrow+1
.

Let us fix
x_{1}+x_{3}
,
x_{2}+x_{4}
as

x_{1}+x_{3}=n,

(51)

and

x_{2}+x_{4}=k-n,

(52)

Then (
50
) will be rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1},x_{4},n}\frac{k!}{x_{1}!\ (k-n-x_{4})!\ (n-x_{1})!\ x_{4}!}\ p_{+}^{n}(1-p_{+})^{k-n}

\displaystyle\times\frac{N^{-}!}{(N^{-}-x_{1}-k+n+x_{4})!}\frac{N^{+}!}{(N^{+}-n+x_{1}-x_{4})!}\frac{(N-k)!}{N!}\ \frac{1}{N}(x_{1}-x_{4}).

(53)

Let us first consider the sum over
x_{1}
and
x_{4}
. The factor
p_{+}^{n}(1-p_{+})^{k-n}/N
is irrelevant to this sum and can be omitted. We can explicitly separate the sums:

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1}=0}^{n}\sum_{x_{4}=0}^{k-n}\frac{k!}{x_{1}!\ (k-n-x_{4})!\ (n-x_{1})!\ x_{4}!}\ \frac{N^{-}!}{(N^{-}-x_{1}-k+n+x_{4})!}

\displaystyle\times\frac{N^{+}!}{(N^{+}-n+x_{1}-x_{4})!}\frac{(N-k)!}{N!}(x_{1}-x_{4})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1}=0}^{n}\sum_{x_{4}=0}^{k-n}\frac{k!}{x_{1}!\ (k-n-x_{4})!\ (n-x_{1})!\ x_{4}!}

\displaystyle\times\frac{(N-k)!}{(N^{-}-x_{1}-k+n+x_{4})!(N^{+}-n+x_{1}-x_{4})!}\ \frac{N^{+}!N^{-}!}{N!}(x_{1}-x_{4}).

(54)

One can rewrite everything in terms of binomial coefficients and also consider only the term multiplied by
x_{1}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1}=0}^{n}\sum_{x_{4}=0}^{k-n}\binom{k}{n}\binom{n}{x_{1}}\binom{k-n}{x_{4}}\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-k}{N^{-}-x_{1}-k+n+x_{4}}\Bigg(\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}}{N^{-}}\Bigg)^{-1}\ x_{1}.

(55)

For binomial coefficients we use the Vandermonde convolution

\sum_{k}\binom{r}{m+k}\binom{s}{n-k}=\binom{r+s}{m+n}.

(56)

Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{4}=0}^{k-n}\binom{k-n}{x_{4}}\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-k}{N^{-}-x_{1}-k+n+x_{4}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{4}=0}^{k-n}\binom{k-n}{k-n-x_{4}}\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-k}{N^{-}-x_{1}-k+n+x_{4}}

\displaystyle=\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-n}{N^{-}-x_{1}}.

(57)

It follows from the property of binomial coefficients that

\binom{n}{x_{1}}\ x_{1}=n\binom{n-1}{x_{1}-1}.

(58)

Taking into account the above expressions , we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1}=0}^{n}\binom{k}{n}n\binom{n-1}{x_{1}-1}\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-n}{N^{-}-x_{1}}\Bigg(\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}}{N^{-}}\Bigg)^{-1}.

(59)

In (
59
) we see a similar Vandermonde convolution:

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1}=0}^{n}\binom{n-1}{x_{1}-1}\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-n}{N^{-}-x_{1}}=\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-1}{N^{-}-1}.

(60)

Then for the sum with
x_{1}
we have

\displaystyle\binom{k}{n}n\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-1}{N^{-}-1}\Bigg(\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}}{N^{-}}\Bigg)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{n}n\frac{(N^{-}+N^{+}-1)!}{N^{+}!(N^{-}-1)!}\frac{N^{+}!N^{-}!}{(N^{-}+N^{+})!}

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{n}n\frac{N^{-}}{N^{-}+N^{+}}=\binom{k}{n}n\frac{N^{-}}{N}.

(61)

Similarly, we can obtain for the term with
x_{4}
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1}=0}^{n}\sum_{x_{4}=0}^{k-n}\binom{k}{n}\binom{n}{x_{1}}\binom{k-n}{x_{4}}\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-k}{N^{-}-x_{1}-k+n+x_{4}}\Bigg(\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}}{N^{-}}\Bigg)^{-1}\ x_{4}

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{n}(k-n)\frac{N^{+}}{N}.

(62)

The final expression for
\langle\Delta\varphi\rangle
is given by:

\displaystyle\langle\Delta\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\Bigg[\binom{k}{n}n\frac{N^{-}}{N}-\binom{k}{n}(k-n)\frac{N^{+}}{N}\Bigg]\ p_{+}^{n}(1-p_{+})^{k-n}\frac{1}{N}=\frac{k}{N}(p_{+}-\varphi).

(63)

Appendix B

The expression for the second moment
\langle(\Delta\varphi^{2})\rangle

\displaystyle\langle(\Delta\varphi)^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1},x_{4},n}\frac{k!}{x_{1}!\ (k-n-x_{4})!\ (n-x_{1})!\ x_{4}!}\ p_{+}^{n}(1-p_{+})^{k-n}

\displaystyle\times\frac{N^{-}!}{(N^{-}-x_{1}-k+n+x_{4})!}\frac{N^{+}!}{(N^{+}-n+x_{1}-x_{4})!}\frac{(N-k)!}{N!}\ \frac{1}{N^{2}}(x_{1}-x_{4})^{2}.

(64)

One can expand
(x_{1}-x_{4})^{2}=(x_{1}^{2}-2x_{1}x_{4}+x_{4}^{2})
. Let’s factor out the sum over
n
and consider the sum for the first term
x_{1}^{2}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1}=0}^{n}\sum_{x_{4}=0}^{k-n}\binom{k}{n}\binom{n}{x_{1}}\binom{k-n}{x_{4}}\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-k}{N^{-}-x_{1}-k+n+x_{4}}\Bigg(\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}}{N^{-}}\Bigg)^{-1}\ \frac{1}{N^{2}}x_{1}^{2}.

(65)

Using the Vandermonde convolution, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{4}=0}^{k-n}\binom{k-n}{x_{4}}\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-k}{N^{-}-x_{1}-k+n+x_{4}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{4}=0}^{k-n}\binom{k-n}{k-n-x_{4}}\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-k}{N^{-}-x_{1}-k+n+x_{4}}

\displaystyle=\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-n}{N^{-}-x_{1}}.

(66)

Then (
65
) reads

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{1}=0}^{n}\binom{k}{n}\binom{n}{x_{1}}\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}-n}{N^{-}-x_{1}}\Bigg(\binom{N^{-}+N^{+}}{N^{-}}\Bigg)^{-1}\ \frac{1}{N^{2}}x_{1}^{2}.

(67)

Make the following transformation

\displaystyle\binom{n}{x_{1}}x_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=n\binom{n-1}{x_{1}-1}x_{1}=n\binom{n-1}{x_{1}-1}(x_{1}-1+1)

\displaystyle=n(n-1)\binom{n-2}{x_{1}-2}+n\binom{n-1}{x_{1}-1}.
