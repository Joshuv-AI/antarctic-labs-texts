Rare Case of Co‐Existing Hydatid Cyst Along With Pulmonary Tuberculosis; Both Being Evident on Chest X‐Ray
Authors: Thasniya Ummer, Salman Qayum, Harshana Bandara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 45‐year‐old male immigrant builder presented with haemoptysis and a dry cough. Imaging revealed a right apical cavitating lesion and an incidental calcified hepatic cyst. Sputum confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis , while CT identified a ce5‐stage hydatid cyst. He received anti‐TB therapy, highlighting concurrent infections and the value of comprehensive radiologic evaluation.Keywords: calcified hepatic opacity, cavitation, co‐existing infection, hydatid cyst, tuberculosis

A 45‐year‐old male builder immigrant, residing in the UK since 2013, presented with a two‐week history of persistent dry cough and new‐onset haemoptysis. He denied symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or shortness of breath. His medical history included heavy smoking, prior alcohol excess, and a contact history of tuberculosis (TB). Initial chest x‐ray showed a right apical cavitating lesion and incidentally revealed a calcified hepatic opacity suggestive of a hydatid cyst (Figure 1a). Sputum testing confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis , and TB treatment was initiated with a two‐week isolation period.

(a) Chest radiograph demonstrating right upper lobe cavitary consolidations (black arrowheads) consistent with post‐primary tuberculosis and a well‐defined cystic lesion in the right subdiaphragmatic region (black arrows) compatible with a hydatid cyst. (b) Axial CT image of the thorax showing cavitary and consolidative changes in the right upper lobe (black asterisk), corresponding to the lesion seen on the chest radiograph. (c) Axial CT image at a lower level demonstrating bilateral tree‐in‐bud appearance (white arrows), indicative of endobronchial spread of infection.

A contrast‐enhanced computed‐tomography (CT) scan confirmed a 3 cm cavitating lesion with extensive ‘tree‐in‐bud’ nodularity, consistent with active pulmonary TB. The CT also demonstrated a large calcified hepatic lesion as suspected from the x‐ray, consistent with a CE5‐stage hydatid cyst (Echinococcus granulosus), with negative hydatid serology (Figure 1b,c; Figure 2). Following the multidisciplinary team meeting, it was concluded that no treatment was required for the liver lesion, and follow‐up imaging and outpatient surveillance were planned.

Axial contrast‐enhanced CT image of the upper abdomen showing a well‐defined cystic lesion in the right hepatic lobe with internal serpiginous, detached membranes (black arrows), consistent with the ‘water lily sign’, characteristic of a hydatid cyst. The cyst demonstrates peripheral wall calcification and internal daughter cysts, typical of hepatic echinococcosis.

Liver function tests were normal, and immunology showed polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia. HIV testing was negative, and no immune deficiency was identified. The patient tolerated anti‐TB therapy well and was discharged with follow‐up for TB management, liver monitoring, and family screening for latent TB.

This case highlights classic pulmonary tuberculosis with haemoptysis and cavitation in a migrant from an endemic area [1], alongside an incidental inactive hydatid liver cyst. It underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis, multidisciplinary management, and vigilance for coexisting infections, particularly in migrants from high‐incidence regions [2], even in the absence of typical symptoms. Furthermore, it emphasises the need for meticulous review of chest x‐rays beyond pulmonary findings, as incidental extrapulmonary abnormalities, such as the subdiaphragmatic cyst in this patient, can be easily overlooked without careful assessment.
