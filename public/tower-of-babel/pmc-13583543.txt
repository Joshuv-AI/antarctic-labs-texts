Nd-induced defect engineering in Ba3Fe2WO9 triple perovskite: controlling phase competition, magnetic crossover, and visible-light photocatalysis
Authors: Aaqib Rashid, Showket Ahmad Bhat, Aarif Ul Islam Shah, Khalid Sultan, Edson L. Meyer, Mohd Ikram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hexagonal triple-perovskites offer a defect-sensitive platform in which magnetism and photoactivity are tuned by the interplay among local coordination, strain, and oxygen non-stoichiometry. Here, we report the synthesis of Nd-substituted Ba3Fe2WO9via solid-state reaction and the structure–property correlations using diffraction, microscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, magnetometry, and visible-light photocatalysis. Rietveld analysis confirms the hexagonal host phase as the main phase in the whole substitution series with a small amount of tungstate secondary phase only. The incorporation of Nd systematically improves the microstructure and enhances lattice distortion. Spectroscopies at near-surface levels reveal a reduced contribution of Fe(ii)-like chemical states in agreement with an oxygen-defect equilibria shift driven by substitution. The magnetisation measurements reveal a strong suppression of the ferromagnetic-like hysteresis to an antiferromagnetic-dominated, weakly canted response. The degradation of crystal violet is better under visible irradiation than the parent compound, and optimum at intermediate substitution. These results demonstrate that A-site rare-earth substitution can be a useful tool for controlling the defects and microstructure of Ba3Fe2WO9 based oxides, while simultaneously tuning their magnetic and photocatalytic properties.

Introduction

Rare earth metal doped perovskite oxides offer a canonical framework in which small, chemically accessible perturbations (cation substitution, oxygen non-stoichiometry, and microstructural control can reorganise the electronic structure and, with it, the magnetic and catalytic responses. This sensitivity is particularly apparent in the hexagonal polytypes, where the connectivity of the BO6 network deviates from the simple corner-sharing motif of cubic perovskites, leading to short metal–metal distances, constrained bond angles, and competing exchange pathways. In such systems, magnetic order and transport are often governed by defect equilibria and mesoscale morphology in addition to the nominal composition, which together set the distribution of local coordination environments. The same defect that determines exchange interactions can also influence optical absorption and charge–carrier recombination, making defect and microstructure engineering an attractive route to couple magnetic and visible-light photocatalytic behaviour within a single chemically stable oxide platform. Pervoskites are oxides of the general formula ABO3, where A and B are the cations or anions, and O is the oxygen atom.1 The properties of single perovskites are interesting, but the focus is on further improving the properties of the perovskite material by introducing different cations or anions. Thus, the ideal Pervoskites ABO3 can be modified by changing the cation and anion position by doubling and tripling of cation and anion position, leading to the formation of more complex Pervoskites known as Double or Triple Pervoskites of the general formula A2B′BO6, A2B2O6, A3B2B′O9, etc.2–6 Examples of this linked structure-defect property landscape include the space group P63/mmc hexagonal 6H “triple perovskites” with the formula A3B2B′O9. Their lattice is composed of alternating face-sharing and corner-sharing octahedral motifs, which permits the effective magnetic Hamiltonian to host a variety of superexchange geometries. Depending on the cation order and oxygen content, other weak ferromagnetic components may also exist. The dominant exchange couplings in insulating oxides are determined by the orbital occupancy and the bond geometry in the Goodenough–Kanamori–Anderson picture.7–10 Different types of linkages in 6H lattices allow small variations in bond angles, local distortions or the change in valence upon the presence of defects to modify the balance between antiferromagnetic and ferromagnetic contributions without any change in the crystal symmetry. This makes the 6H family a natural platform for testing how controlled chemical pressure and defect biasing translate into magnetic crossover phenomena that are robust at room temperature.

As a concrete, well-characterised reference system, we use the triple perovskite Ba3Fe2WO9. Neutron diffraction experiments have established that Ba3Fe2WO9 has a layered magnetic structure with Fe layers that are ferromagnetically ordered and coupled antiferromagnetically along the hexagonal axis.11 Importantly, Ba3Fe2WO9 is also highly defect sensitive; oxygen nonstoichiometry can induce structural changes and strongly affect the magnetic response.12 Independent syntheses by soft-chemistry methods have further emphasised the ability of nanoscale disorder and microstructure to produce large hysteresis and coercivity in nanocrystalline Ba3Fe2WO9 and related compounds.13 This agrees with the general expectation that finite size, strain and defect distributions can introduce uncompensated moments, and enhance weak ferromagnetic components in complex oxides. These results together establish Ba3Fe2WO9 as a system in which the intrinsic competition of exchange pathways is symmetry-allowed and experimentally accessible via defect and microstructural control, and the magnetic structure of the parent compound provides a stringent baseline for interpreting perturbations.

Substitution at the A-site offers a conceptually clean route towards such control. The replacement of a rare earth ion for the Ba site is mainly responsible for the change in lattice metrics and local strain fields. Since the rare earth ion is typically trivalent, e.g., Nd3+ for Ba2+, charge neutrality must be achieved by modifications of oxygen content, cation vacancies and/or the Fe valence distribution. Each of these compensation channels can contribute to magnetism through tuning the covalency of Fe–O bonds and the effective superexchange geometry, and to the optical response through generating or suppressing defect-related states near the band edges. A-site substitution in Ba3Fe2WO9 is thus doubly attractive: it perturbs the exchange network indirectly, without replacing the magnetic Fe sublattice, and introduces a controlled bias on defect equilibria in a host already known to be very sensitive to oxygen content. Given these motivations, the literature on Ba3Fe2WO9 is dominated by either the determination of the crystallographic and magnetic structure of the parent compound and its oxygen sensitivity, or the investigation of microstructure-driven magnetic effects in nanocrystalline variants. There are relatively few studies that systematically correlate A-site rare-earth substitution with a quantitative, multi-probe characterisation of phase constitution, defects, magnetism and optical/photocatalytic function within a single controlled series.

Also, visible-light photocatalysis continues to drive the development of defect and band-structure engineering in oxide semiconductors. Since the discovery of photoelectrochemical water splitting on TiO2 and other tungsten-based oxides,14–17 much work has clarified how optical absorption, surface reaction kinetics, and charge–carrier recombination combine to determine activity in heterogeneous photocatalysis.18 The apparent first-order kinetics of dye degradation and related reactions in dilute aqueous solutions are often analysed within the framework of a Langmuir–Hinshelwood model. However, it is also well known that different microscopic mechanisms can lead to the same macroscopic rate laws, and that adsorption, scattering, and recombination effects must be treated with care.19 These constraints underscore the importance of integrated structure-defect property correlations.

The present work addresses this gap with a controlled A-site substitution study of Ba3−xNdxFe2WO9 (0 ≤ x ≤ 0.3), explicitly designed to link crystallography, microstructure, defect-sensitive spectroscopy, magnetism, and visible-light photocatalysis within a single experimental framework. Polycrystalline samples are prepared by a scalable solid-state route, and phase constitution and lattice evolution are characterised by powder X-ray diffraction and Rietveld refinement,20,21 thereby enabling a conservative assessment of phase competition in this tungsten-containing chemistry. Microstructural refinement and strain are analysed using line-broadening methods22 and correlated with morphology. The defect and valence trends are studied by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy with assignments based on standard reference compilations and databases.20,21 The photocatalytic activity under visible irradiation is evaluated by kinetic analysis, a method that is emphasised in the modern photocatalysis literature.23 The magnetic interpretation is based on the known layered motif of the parent compound12 and its oxygen sensitivity.13 The goal of the study is to confirm Nd substitution as a deterministic control parameter for defect and microstructure engineering in Ba3Fe2WO9, and to clarify how these controlled perturbations mediate the observed crossover in room-temperature magnetic response and concomitant changes in visible-light photocatalytic performance.

Experimental part

Polycrystalline Ba3−xNdxFe2WO9 was synthesised for nominal compositions x = 0.0, 0.1, and 0.3 by a conventional solid-state route adapted to the 6H triple-perovskite chemistry of the Ba3Fe2WO9 family.12 Commercial oxide and carbonate precursors with stated purities of at least 99.9% were employed as BaCO3, Fe2O3, WO3, and Nd2O3. To minimise uncontrolled A-site and oxygen compensation arising from hydrated or carbonated rare-earth oxides, Nd2O3 was pre-dried in air prior to weighing. All powders were stored and handled under low-humidity conditions. Stoichiometric batches were calculated on a molar basis from the nominal formula Ba3−xNdxFe2WO9; the oxygen content is not directly fixed by weighing and is instead controlled indirectly through the oxygen chemical potential during the thermal schedule. Because tungsten-containing systems can exhibit phase competition under solid-state reaction conditions, care was taken to ensure homogeneous mixing at the powder stage and to maintain identical processing history across compositions to avoid mixing kinetic variability with substitution trends. The mixed powders were homogenised by extended grinding to reduce diffusion length scales and to mitigate local stoichiometric gradients that can seed secondary phases. The homogenised mixtures were then calcined in air to decompose BaCO3 and promote initial solid-state reaction. In the present work, calcination was carried out in alumina crucibles under static laboratory air with a controlled heating and cooling rate to an intermediate temperature in the 900 °C dwell of order 10–12 h, and furnace cooling, after which the calcined powder was reground to re-homogenise partially reacted regions and to break up agglomerates. The reground powders were pelletized under uniaxial pressure to improve interparticle contact and reduce porosity during sintering. A small amount of volatile-free organic binder was used when required to enhance green strength, with complete burnout verified by a low-temperature pre-anneal prior to high-temperature sintering. Final sintering was performed in air at a temperature selected to stabilise the 6H host phase while suppressing extensive grain coarsening. A typical schedule is 1150 °C with a 24 h dwell time, followed by furnace cooling. All compositions were processed with the same calcination and sintering schedule and cooled under identical conditions to bias oxygen stoichiometry toward a common equilibrium manifold. The furnace-cooled pellets were lightly ground to obtain powders for diffraction, spectroscopy, and photocatalysis. To minimise contamination and uncontrolled hydration, all powders were stored in sealed containers and handled with minimal exposure time prior to surface-sensitive spectroscopic analysis. Powder X-ray diffraction acquisition and quantitative structural refinement

Room-temperature powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired using a laboratory diffractometer operating in Bragg–Brentano geometry with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), as shown in Fig. 2. Data were collected over a 2θ range of 10–90° with a step size of 0.02° using a fixed divergence configuration, which is suitable for quantitative refinement. Powders were mounted on low-background sample holders and gently pressed to produce a flat surface; to minimise preferred orientation in the layered hexagonal polytype, the powders were sideloaded where feasible and rotated during acquisition when the instrument permitted. Instrumental line broadening and zero shift were monitored using standard reference materials measured under the same optical configuration, and residual specimen-displacement effects were treated within the refinement model when required. Fig. 2(b) shows the stacked powder XRD patterns of Ba3−xNdxFe2WO9 (x = 0.0, 0.1 and 0.3), and Fig. 2(c) shows the peak shift due to Nd doping. The dominant reflection set is indexed to the 6H triple-perovskite host phase, and the peak positions systematically shift to higher 2θ with increasing Nd content, indicating a contraction of the average lattice parameter. A few weak reflections (marked in Fig. 2(a)) are not indexed by the host and are included in the quantitative refinements as a minor tungstate impurity phase. No additional superstructure peaks or symmetry-breaking splitting are resolved across the series within the resolution of laboratory XRD, and the refinements remain well described by a single 6H host phase model across the full composition range. The undoped lattice parameters are consistent with previous diffraction reports on Ba3Fe2WO9,3 providing an external point of comparison for the present synthesis and refinement pipeline and confirming the known 6H host metrics at x = 0.

Quantitative structural refinement was performed using the Rietveld method with the FullProf suite.24,25 The refinement strategy was designed to be minimally model-biased while extracting the observables required later, namely lattice parameters, unit-cell volume, and phase fractions for the dominant 6H host and any detectable minor phases. The initial structural model for the host phase was taken from established diffraction reports on Ba3Fe2WO9 (ref. 13) and implemented in the reported hexagonal symmetry (P63/mmc) at room temperature. In refinements, background intensity was described by a low-order polynomial, and peak profiles were modelled by a pseudo-Voigt function with refinable width and mixing parameters. Scale factors, lattice parameters, and profile parameters were refined for all patterns. Atomic positions and isotropic displacement parameters were refined only to the extent supported by laboratory XRD resolution and counting statistics; where correlations or overparameterization were detected via unstable parameter covariance or non-physical displacement parameters, the refinement was constrained by fixing weakly determined parameters to literature values and focusing on robust observables. Site occupancies were constrained to nominal stoichiometry in the baseline refinement because the sensitivity of laboratory XRD to small Fe/W antisite populations is limited by the presence of heavy Ba and W and by correlation with thermal parameters; antisite disorder is therefore treated as a potential local motif in interpretation rather than as a directly refined quantity unless independently constrained. Preferred orientation corrections were applied only when residual intensity systematics could not be removed by reasonable background and profile adjustments. In such cases, a standard March–Dollase-type correction is appropriate for layered textures; however, the resulting parameters were used only to improve fit quality, rather than to make quantitative claims about texture. Minor secondary reflections, when present above background, were indexed by comparison to standard patterns, included as an additional phase in the refinement using an independent scale factor, lattice parameters as required and a consistent profile model. Convergence of the refinement was checked by stabilisation of Rwp, visual inspection of difference curves for systematic misfit and reduction in parameter shifts to below a small fraction of their estimated standard deviations. Line broadening analysis yields microstructural metrics that vary systematically with Nd content. Williamson–Hall plots for the host reflections, as given in Fig. 4, exhibit linear behaviour within experimental scatter for each composition, and the extracted parameters are given in Table 2. The coherently diffracting domain-size proxy decreases monotonically from D = 44 nm at x = 0.0 to D = 28 nm at x = 0.3. These broadening-derived trends are consistent with the progressive peak broadening observed in Fig. 2 and establish that the substitution series produces a coupled evolution of microstructural refinement and lattice-distortion signatures. The monotonic contraction of the hexagonal lattice with increasing x provides the most direct structural signature that Nd is incorporated into the 6H host over the probed composition window, rather than residing exclusively in a segregated phase. At the same time, the quantitative phase analysis indicates only a minor trend in the secondary-phase fraction across the series, which is important analytically because it bounds the extent to which changes in bulk-normalised functional responses can be attributed to simple dilution.

Morphological studies

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was used to assess particle and grain morphology and to obtain grain-size statistics. Powder surfaces were mounted on conductive carbon tape, and insulating specimens were coated with a thin conductive layer (e.g., Au) to mitigate charging. Images were acquired at accelerating voltages chosen to balance surface sensitivity and signal-to-noise ratio in the 10 kV range, and at working distances optimised for the detector geometry. Grain sizes were derived from representative micrographs using calibrated scale bars and standard image analysis procedures. Image processing and metrology were conducted using ImageJ.29 Thresholds for segmentation were chosen to minimise operator bias and were confirmed by re-analysing independent micrographs. Grain-size distributions were calculated by observing several fields of view for each specimen and counting a sufficient number of grains to stabilise the mean and the variance. The grain sizes reported here are to be understood as morphological grain sizes, rather than as direct replacements for coherently diffracting domain sizes from XRD. Real space morphology is quantified from the FESEM image analysis as shown in Fig. 6. The mean grain size decreases monotonically as x increases from about 2.14 µm at x = 0.0 to about 1.64 µm at x = 0.3, with intermediate means near 1.95 µm at x = 0.1. Thus, the extent of the morphological refinement is similar in fractional terms to that inferred from diffraction broadening, but different in absolute terms as would be expected when grains are composed of multiple coherently diffracting domains. Elemental composition

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was employed for compositional verification, and qualitative mapping of cation distributions is given in Fig. 7. Spectra were collected at accelerating voltages sufficient to excite the characteristic lines of Ba, Nd, Fe, O, and W, with acquisition times chosen to achieve stable peak intensities. Quantification utilised standard ZAF-type corrections implemented in the instrument software to validate the reliability of oxygen quantification by standardless EDX in complex oxides, as well as the sensitivity of oxygen signals to surface topography and contamination. Instead, EDX confirmed the presence and approximate ratios of the heavy cations and identified any gross compositional inhomogeneity that would undermine the interpretation of bulk property measurements. From the EDX, we have seen that only the elements present in the stoichiometric ratio are present, further confirming the sample's purity ().

Fourier transform infra-red radiations

Fig. 8 shows the FTIR spectra of Nd-doped Ba3Fe2WO9 samples with compositions Ba3−xNdxFe2WO9 (x = 0.0, 0.1, 0.3) recorded in the range 400–4000 cm−1. The spectra provide valuable insight into the local bonding environment, lattice distortion, and structural stability of the triple perovskite upon Nd3+ substitution at the A-site (i.e., the Ba2+ site). The low intensity of this band confirms the high phase purity of the samples and indicates that no significant organic contamination is present. The progressive disappearance of this band with increasing Nd content suggests a more compact lattice and a lower surface adsorption, a consequence of the stronger Nd–O bonding. A prominent absorption is observed at 1450–1550 cm−1, which can be assigned to asymmetric stretching vibrations of residual carbonate CO32− groups, generally derived from BaCO3 precursors employed in the synthesis. The most prominent features in the low frequency region have been identified in the ranges of 550–650 cm−1 due to Fe–O stretching vibrations related to FeO6 octahedra, and the band 750–850 cm−1 can be assigned to W–O stretching vibrations of WO6 octahedra, confirming the formation of the ordered triple perovskite framework. As the Nd content increases, x = 0.1 to 0.3, these metal–oxygen vibrational bands show a slight shift towards higher wavenumbers and a significant sharpening. This behaviour is a clear indication of lattice distortion and bond stiffening due to substitution of larger Ba2+ (≈1.56 Å) ions by smaller Nd3+ (≈1.15 Å) ions. The resulting decrease in A-site ionic radius causes octahedral tilting and stronger Fe–O–W bonds consistent with the contraction of the perovskite lattice. As we have seen, the doping with Nd3+ ions creates a charge imbalance as their valence is higher than that of Ba2+. This imbalance is probably compensated by the formation of oxygen vacancies which also further modify the local bonding environment. These vacancies result in the modification of vibrational frequencies and support the strong coupling between A-site substitution and BO6 octahedra dynamics. It is worth mentioning here that no additional peaks associated with impurities are observed for all the compositions confirming the successful incorporation of Nd3+ ions in the Ba3Fe2WO9 lattice without any change in the basic perovskite structure. All the doping levels retain the characteristic Fe–O and W–O vibrational modes indicating the structural robustness and phase stability of Nd doped Ba3Fe2WO9.

Room-temperature magnetometry and magnetic-parameter extraction

The magnetic response of Ba3Fe2WO9 primarily derives from the Fe sublattice, with W6+ ions in a d0 configuration, and Ba/Nd ions providing structural and electrostatic tuning. Neutron diffraction established that the parent compound supports a layered magnetic motif in which ferromagnetic Fe layers couple antiferromagnetically along the hexagonal axis,5i.e. an A-type antiferromagnetic structure in the standard perovskite language. A minimal spin model that captures this motif is an effective Heisenberg Hamiltonian with at least two distinct exchange scales (eqn (7)),307where Si are local Fe moments and the bonds are grouped into effective in-plane and out-of-plane couplings. The sign convention is such that J > 0 favours antiferromagnetism. In an A-type antiferromagnet, Jab < 0 and Jc > 0 in this convention. In Ba3Fe2WO9, however, the 6H topology and potential Fe/W antisite disorder imply that multiple microscopic exchange paths contribute to the effective parameters in Equation.7 The established Goodenough–Kanamori–Anderson principles determine the sign and magnitude of superexchange as a function of orbital occupancy and bond geometry.14–16 For high-spin Fe3+ (3d5), the dominant superexchange through an O2− ligand is typically antiferromagnetic for near 180° Fe–O–Fe bonds and can be reduced or even change sign as bond angles decrease, while indirect Fe–O–W–O–Fe superexchange is generally weaker and sensitive to octahedral tilts and covalency. In face-sharing motifs, additional direct overlap and modified crystal-field splitting can further renormalise exchange relative to corner-sharing perovskites, reinforcing the need for an effective, rather than strictly nearest-neighbour, parameterisation. Beyond isotropic exchange, two symmetry-allowed terms are central for interpreting room-temperature hysteresis in an otherwise antiferromagnetic host, single-ion anisotropy and antisymmetric (Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya (DM)) exchange. A generic effective Hamiltonian is (eqn (8))8where Ki encodes local anisotropy with easy-axis directions ni, Dij is the DM vector on bond 〈ij〈, and H is the applied field. For Fe3+, orbital quenching results in a modest single-ion anisotropy compared to many 4d/5d systems. However, in low-symmetry or strained environments, it can still control the coercive field through magnetocrystalline anisotropy energy. DM exchange is allowed when inversion symmetry at the bond centre is absent and is the microscopic mechanism underlying weak ferromagnetism in canted antiferromagnets.31,32 In an ideal P63/mmc average structure, certain bonds may retain inversion centres that suppress DM at the symmetry level; however, antisite disorder, oxygen vacancies, and local strain generically lower local symmetry and can activate DM interactions on a subset of bonds, even when the average structure remains Centro-symmetric. This distinction between average and local symmetry is essential for Ba3−xNdxFe2WO9 (x = 0.0,0.1 & 0.3), weak-ferromagnetic hysteresis can originate from a small volume fraction of locally symmetry-broken regions without requiring a crystallographic phase transition. Finally, there is an additional contribution from the 4f moments of Nd3+, which is purely paramagnetic. At the low substitution levels studied, this contribution is expected to appear mainly as a linear-in-field term at room temperature, and by itself it does not lead to any hysteresis. Thus, the theoretical role of Nd substitution is mainly understood in terms of its structural and defect-chemical consequences, consistent with the charge-balance constraints discussed above and with the known oxygen sensitivity of Ba3−xNdxFe2WO9 (x = 0.0, 0.1, and 0.3) magnetism.11

Room-temperature magnetic hysteresis loops were measured using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM)33 in applied fields up to ±2 T. Powder specimens were immobilised in non-magnetic holders to prevent particle reorientation under field and vibration; dense pellets were used to reduce the risk of field-induced micro-movement and to standardise demagnetisation geometry. Sample masses were measured with analytical precision and used to normalise magnetisation data to emu g−1. When conversion to moment per formula unit is required, the molar mass was computed from nominal composition, and the same mass normalisation was retained to avoid introducing additional systematic error from unknown oxygen non-stoichiometry.

The suppression of ferromagnetic-like hysteresis with increasing Nd content can then be analysed within a defect-sensitive antiferromagnetic host framework, grounded in established exchange physics. Neutron diffraction established that the parent Ba3Fe2WO9 supports a layered magnetic motif with ferromagnetically aligned Fe layers coupled antiferromagnetically along the hexagonal axis, which already implies the presence of a dominant antiferromagnetic exchange scale, even if weak ferromagnetic signatures appear in bulk magnetometry.11 Two defect-mediated routes to ferromagnetic-like loops in such a background are particularly conservative weak ferromagnetism due to canting from antisymmetric (Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya) exchange, where local inversion symmetry is broken,34,35 and uncompensated or pinned moments associated with defect clusters, interfaces, or locally altered exchange topology in polycrystals. Both routes are naturally enhanced by oxygen-vacancy populations and mixed valence, because oxygen vacancies reduce local symmetry and modify bond covalency, while mixed valence can introduce carrier-assisted exchange contributions on subsets of bonds.36 The observed monotonic reduction of loop opening and coercivity with Nd incorporation is therefore consistent with the XPS trend toward reduced mixed-valence spectral weight, if Nd biases the defect manifold toward fewer oxygen-vacancy-associated reduced Fe centres near the surface and, probably, throughout near surface-dominated microstructural regions, then both the density of locally symmetry-broken bonds supporting. The fact that hysteresis is suppressed while microstructural refinement increases suggest that purely geometric surface-area control of the magnetic response is not the primary mechanism and instead supports the conclusion that the relevant ferromagnetic-like contribution is defect-chemistry-limited. A-site Nd3+ moments, while present, contribute a reversible paramagnetic component at room temperature predominantly and cannot generate hysteresis without an additional symmetry-breaking or exchange mechanism; thus, they do not provide an alternative explanation for the observed systematic suppression.

Photocatalytic degradation

The photocatalytic activity was evaluated by the degradation of crystal violet (CV) dye in an aqueous suspension under visible-light irradiation, using the same protocol parameters for all compositions, so that the comparative trends are attributed to the intrinsic differences of the catalysts rather than external conditions. CV stock solutions were prepared in deionised water and diluted to a fixed initial concentration, Cinit, in the dilute regime, where the Beer–Lambert absorbance remains linear at the monitoring wavelength.36 In this work, the working concentration and the catalyst loading were kept constants for all samples. An optimal protocol is Cinit at 5–15 mg L−1, a catalyst loading of 0.3–1.0 g L−1, and a total suspension volume of 50–200 mL in a borosilicate reactor. Suspensions were stirred in the dark for a fixed equilibration time before illumination to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium; the concentration after this equilibration was defined as C0 and as the kinetic reference to decouple photocatalytic degradation from purely adsorptive uptake. The geometry of the reactor was held fixed to ensure that the illuminated area and the optical path were identical. For visible-light irradiation, a broadband source was used with an optical cutoff filter to block UV components. The incident irradiance at the reactor position was measured with a calibrated photometer. The reactor was kept near room temperature by passive heat sinking or by active water circulation to prevent thermal acceleration of dye decomposition. To quantify photolysis, control experiments were carried out without a catalyst under identical conditions and in the dark with a catalyst to quantify only adsorption changes. To rule out systematic concentration drift due to mechanical stirring and sampling, additional controls were performed in which the catalyst was present, but light was blocked. The concentration of CV was determined from the absorbance at its characteristic maximum (usually around 592 nm) by simple proportionality (C/C0 = A/A0, confirmed by linearity) and by using a calibration curve obtained from standards run under the same instrument settings. Baseline correction was applied consistently to account for residual scattering. Each photocatalysis experiment was performed at least once, and where statistical reporting is required, the mean and standard deviation of the independent runs are reported. Kinetic constants were calculated using the dilute Langmuir–Hinshelwood limits that are widely used for dye degradation under photocatalysis.37,38 More exactly, the apparent first-order form (eqn (9)),9was fitted with a linear regression in the time interval where the logarithmic plot was linear within the experimental scatter. The fitting interval was selected uniformly for all samples to avoid biasing kapp by late-time deviations associated with low concentration or catalyst deactivation. Moreover, the non-monotonic photocatalytic activity, with a maximum at an intermediate Nd content, can be explained by the usual competition between photon absorption and charge-carrier utilisation in defect-sensitive oxide photocatalysts under Langmuir–Hinshelwood conditions.39 The apparent first-order constants extracted from dilute crystal violent degradation quantify a composite figure of merit that folds together (i) the absorbed photon flux (optical absorption and scattering), (ii) the efficiency with which absorbed photons produce reactive surface species rather than recombining (defect and trap populations), and (iii) adsorption and surface reaction kinetics (accessible surface area and surface chemistry). The incorporation of Nd causes two changes which typically enhance activity: an increase in visible absorption due to the reduced optical-edge proxy, and a microstructural refinement that increases the surface-area-to-volume ratio and thus the density of accessible surface sites. The changes are consistent with the initial increase in kapp as x increases. The subsequent reduction at higher x, however, despite continued refinement and continued edge lowering, points to the increasing importance of at least one countervailing factor.

X-ray photoemission spectroscopy

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to study the near-edge chemical states of Fe 2p, W 4f, Ba 3d, Nd 3d, C 1s, and O 1s across all compositions of the series. The measurements were carried out in ultra-high vacuum with monochromatic Al Kα radiation (hv = 1486.6 eV).40 Samples were prepared as pressed powders to yield mechanically stable, electrically continuous surfaces.41 To minimise adventitious contamination and to expose representative material beneath surface adsorbates, surfaces were refreshed immediately prior to measurement by gentle scraping in a dry environment and, where instrument capabilities allowed, by mild in situ treatment. Differential charging was compensated where necessary, and residual charging shifts were corrected to the C 1s peak of adventitious carbon at 284.6 eV, which is normal for XPS of oxides.41,42 High-resolution scans of the relevant core levels were subsequently recorded. The acquisition parameters (pass energy, step size, dwell time, and spot size) were chosen to obtain adequate energy resolution for the discrimination of chemical states with acceptable counting statistics. Representative settings were a pass energy of 200 eV for high-resolution scans.

Core-level peak fitting was performed using constrained least-squares optimisation. Background subtraction employed a Shirley-type background as the baseline, and alternative physically motivated backgrounds were tested to assess the sensitivity of the integrated areas to the choice of background. Peak shapes were modelled as Voigt-type combinations (Gaussian–Lorentzian mixtures) that accounted for instrumental broadening and intrinsic lifetime effects. The XPS spectra for the sample with high doping concentration Ba1.7Nd0.3Fe2WO9 are depicted in Fig. 11. From Fig. 11, it is seen that W 4f is deconvoluted into distinct peaks, with the main peaks at W 4f7/2 and W 4f5/2 at 36.39 eV and 30.40 eV, respectively. The Fe 2p region was analysed, and we observed that Fe exists in the +2 and +3 states. Because Fe 2p spectra in oxides exhibit multiplet splitting and shake-up satellites that complicate simple peak decomposition, the analysis emphasised conservative metrics, the position and line shape of the main Fe 2p3/2 envelope, the presence and relative intensity of characteristic satellite structures, and comparative changes under identical fitting constraints. The O 1s region was fitted with a minimal number of components required by the residuals, typically including a dominant lattice-oxygen component and higher-binding-energy contributions associated with defect-related oxygen environments and surface hydroxylation. Given the surface sensitivity of the O 1s signal, these components were interpreted as qualitative indicators of relative defect content rather than as direct measures of bulk oxygen non-stoichiometry. The O 1s spectrum is deconvoluted into peaks, and the main oxygen peak is found at 530.2 eV, with an oxygen vacancy peak present in the material, as shown in Fig. 11. The XPS spectra of Ba 3d are deconvoluted into Ba3d5/2 and are aligned at 777.57 eV, whereas the spectra of Ba 3d3/2 are aligned at 792.3 eV. Förster et al. observed that the binding energies of Ba 3d5/2 and Ba 3d3/2 were similar, at 779.6 eV and 795 eV, respectively.

Conclusion

This work addressed how a controlled, low-level rare-earth substitution on the A site of the 6H triple-perovskite Ba3Fe2WO9 reorganises the coupled structural, defect-sensitive, magnetic, and photocatalytic response of the host, under a synthesis and characterisation protocol designed to keep processing history fixed across composition. Across the Ba3−xNdxFe2WO9 series studied here, Nd incorporation produces a systematic contraction of the hexagonal lattice parameters and unit-cell volume while preserving the dominant 6H host phase as the principal structural constituent, with only a modest increase in a minor impurity fraction. In parallel, diffraction broadening and microscopy reveal a progressive refinement of the coherence length and morphological grain scales, accompanied by an increase in effective microstrain, indicating that substitution simultaneously introduces elastic heterogeneity and suppresses coarsening within the primary phase. These structural and microstructural trends provide the physically relevant control variables that define the interpretation of electronic and magnetic responses in a defect-sensitive oxide, ensuring that the observed property evolution cannot be attributed to a trivial change in processing conditions. Within this constraint, the magnetic response exhibits a clear crossover upon Nd substitution, the room-temperature ferromagnetic-like loop parameters decrease monotonically and strongly with x, approaching a weaker, more hysteretic response at the highest substitution level. The corresponding near-surface chemical-state signatures in Fe core-level spectra evolve in the direction consistent with diminished reduced-state-associated spectral weight upon substitution. Together with the established sensitivity of Ba3Fe2WO9 to oxygen content and local structural distortions, the combined dataset supports the conservative conclusion that Nd substitution primarily biases the defect and local symmetry landscape, enabling weak-ferromagnetic and pinning contributions within an antiferromagnetic host background, rather than inducing a bulk ferromagnetic ground state. The strong suppression of coercivity and remanence, occurring alongside increasing microstrain and decreasing domain/grain sizes, further constrains the mechanism to be defect-chemistry-limited rather than solely driven by geometric refinement. The optical response evolves concurrently, with a systematic decrease in the extracted visible-range optical edge across the substitution series. The photocatalytic response under visible irradiation, quantified by apparent first-order kinetics in the dilute regime, is enhanced relative to the undoped host and exhibits an optimum at intermediate substitution. The location of this optimum within a series that shows monotonic changes in lattice contraction, microstrain, and optical-edge indicates that photocatalytic performance is governed by competing effects that are already bounded by the measured observables, increased absorption and surface accessibility with substitution and refinement, counterbalanced by defect- and disorder-mediated limitations on carrier utilisation or surface reaction efficiency at higher substitution. XPS provides chemically informative near-surface trends but is not a direct bulk stoichiometry measurement and photocatalytic kinetics provide a comparative activity metric under fixed conditions rather than a unique microscopic mechanism. Overall, this study provides the insights that the prepared material has good applications in semiconductor devices and the photocatalytic degradation.
