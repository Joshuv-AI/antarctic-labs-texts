Optimization of the grid-stiffened structure via PSO-LSSVR surrogate modeling and sensitivity analysis
Authors: Jingbo Gai, Yao Jian, Qi Deng, Jisong Wang, Mingsheng Zheng, Yanshuai Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The grid-stiffened structure is the key load-carrying sections of launch vehicles such as the storage tank and the interstage section. Thus, its load-carrying performance is directly related to the load-carrying efficiency of a rocket. In this paper, a structural optimization method based on the load-carrying efficiency was proposed for grid-stiffened structures. Firstly, a general 3D parametric geometrical modeling method for grid-stiffened structure was proposed, which could optimize the position of stiffeners and incorporate the key geometric features such as welds. Based on this method, the buckling load of the structure considering the internal pressure was figured out by finite element analysis, and the solution accuracy was verified. Then, a surrogate model was constructed based on the Particle Swarm Optimization-Least Squares Support Vector Regression algorithm for accurately and efficiently predicting the buckling load of grid-stiffened structures. Subsequently, the sensitivity of each structural parameter to the buckling load was evaluated using the Sobol method to identify the key ones. Finally, targeting these key parameters, a load-carrying efficiency optimization model was proposed, considering constraints such as mass and geometric constraints. The results showed that the load-carrying efficiency of the optimized structure increased by 11.20%. The methods proposed in this paper could efficiently establish a practical 3D geometrical model of the grid-stiffened structure, effectively improve its load-carrying efficiency, and maintain the given parameters within the specified ranges. This approach could lay a foundation for reducing the launch costs and improving the transportation efficiency.

Introduction

As the sole means for current interplanetary exploration and manned spaceflight, launch vehicles impose stringent requirements on the reliability and lightweight design of their load-carrying structures. The grid-stiffened structure, owing to its high load-carrying efficiency, is widely applied to critical sections in launch vehicles such as the interstage section and propellant tanks. Therefore, accurate and efficient methods for determining the buckling load, as well as optimization methods for structural parameters, have garnered extensive attention.

Currently, scholars have conducted extensive research on the analysis of the load-carrying performance of stiffened shell structures. The Knockdown Factor (KDF) used in NASA SP-8007 estimated the structural design load by reducing the eigenvalue buckling load by experiences1. Rahimi et al.2 investigated the influence of different stiffening configurations on the axial load of composite stiffened shells by eigenvalue buckling analysis. Hühne et al.3 proposed a method for predicting the load-carrying capacity of defective structures based on Single Perturbation Load Approach (SPLA). The accuracy of this method was verified by axial compression tests. Friedrich et al.4 and Wang et al.5 investigated the effects of various defect theories on the axial compression buckling failure of unstiffened cylindrical shells. They introduced defects through the SPLA and the Worst Multiple Perturbation Load Application (WMPLA), and calculated the axial buckling loads of a 4.5 m isogrid-stiffened shell. The experimental results demonstrated that the WMPLA performed better. Wagner et al.6 systematically employed eight KDF analysis methods, including NASA SP-8007, the Reduced Energy Method (REM), and the Linear Buckling Mode-shaped Imperfection (LBMI) approach. After comparing with the experimental results, they found that the WMPLA, the Single Boundary Perturbation Approach (SBPA), and the Cutout Ratio (CR) method all obtained favorable results.

With the development of shell buckling theory, researches focusing on the sensitivity study of geometrical defects and the post-buckling performance became abundant, and the solution accuracy of the buckling loads of stiffened shell has been greatly improved7. However, conducting finite element buckling analysis on grid-stiffened structures typically proves to be a time-consuming process. Moreover, relying solely on finite element analysis fails to meet the high computational power and efficiency demands in parameter optimization or reliability analysis of grid-stiffened structures. To tackle the problems, Pu et al.8 and Sadovský et al.9 applied Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) to predict the ultimate strength of unstiffened plates under axial compression, and the results proved to be accurate. Mandal et al.10 used ANN to predict the axial buckling loads of 390 non-metallic cylindrical shells. Subsequent error analysis revealed that all the prediction errors fell within a 10% margin. Yang et al.11 improved the Radial Basis Function (RBF) based surrogate model by the fast K-fold cross-validation method. Its effectiveness was validated.

In terms of the optimal design of the stiffened shell structure, Lamberti et al.12 adopted the Rayleigh–Ritz method, Uysal et al.13 utilized the Finite Difference Method (FDM), Chen et al.14 employed the Finite Element Method (FEM), and Meng et al.15 as well as Hao et al.16 used the Smeared Stiffness Method (SSM), and they all conducted mass optimization of the stiffened shell structures by minimizing the structural mass while satisfying diverse mechanical and geometrical performance constraints. Marino et al.17 conducted gradient-based optimization on two shape parameters of the triangular shell structure based on the FEM, obtaining a design with lower strain energy. When compared with the initial design, the strain energy of the optimized design was reduced by 82%. Lanzi et al.18 employed surrogate models, which were based on the results of the FEM, to perform multi-objective optimization on composite stiffened plates. The experimental results showed that the discrepancies between the optimized buckling load and the corresponding experimental results were all below 5%. Although theoretical methods like the SSM and the Progressive Homogenization Method could quickly solve the buckling load of stiffened shells, the derivation process for complex stiffening structures could be cumbersome, and sometimes impossible19.

In recent years, researchers have been actively developing increasingly efficient structural optimization methods. Among them, Savine et al.20 adopted the component projection method to perform topology optimization of stiffener layouts, specifically focusing on location, geometry, and dimensions. For stiffened shells with variable thickness, Li et al.21 combined radial basis function (RBF) surrogate model and nonuniform rational B-splines (NURBS) for modeling. They further optimized stiffener parameters using deep neural network (DNN), achieving a 14.77% weight reduction. To explicitly account for manufacturable fillets, Wang et al.22 employed the Representative Volume Element (RVE) method to project fillet features into the design parameter space and evaluate their effect on stiffness. As a result, they yielded a prediction error of merely 0.2% and a 14% improvement in load-carrying capacity. To accommodate curved surfaces and openings, Zhou et al.23 proposed a generative design approach for parametric optimization of grid-stiffened curved shells with openings that are defined by NURBS curves. In comparison with traditional orthogonal grid stiffening, this approach achieved an enhancement of 17.42% in buckling load.

Apparently, scholars have recognized the importance of studying the parameter optimization of grid-stiffened structures. However, researches on the optimization of diagonal triangular grid stiffened structures remained inadequate. In the optimization models, only several parameters, which were often determined by experience, were considered. In addition, there was a lack of a general 3D geometrical modeling method for grid-stiffened structures that could optimize the position of stiffeners, consider the weld seam and be applicable to engineering practice. These led to a gap between theoretical research and practical application. Furthermore, the high computational power demand of the FEM in solving the buckling load further impeded the parameter optimization of grid stiffened structures. Aiming at these problems, this paper proposed a general 3D parametric geometrical modeling method and an efficient structural optimization method for grid-stiffened structures. The arrangement of this paper is as follows. In Chapter 2, a general 3D parametric geometrical model and a finite element analysis model for diagonal triangular grid-stiffened structures were proposed. In Chapter 3, a method for optimizing the load-carrying efficiency of grid-stiffened structures based on PSO-LSSVR and sensitivity study was demonstrated. Subsequently, the results and corresponding analyses were presented in Chapter 4. Finally, conclusions were reached in Chapter 5.

Finite element analysis of grid-stiffened structures

Grid-stiffened structures primarily bear internal pressure, bending moments, and axial loads, with axial compressive buckling as the dominant failure mode19. To analyze their buckling load with high precision and efficiency, a general 3D parametric geometrical modeling method should be proposed first.

General 3D parametric geometrical modeling of grid-stiffened structures

Grid-stiffened structures typically consist of multiple welded central frames and end frames, each of which is usually fabricated by welding multiple identical wall panels. Each wall panel includes components such as weld seams, skins, vertical stiffeners, bidirectional diagonal stiffeners, and border reinforcement stiffeners. PSO-LSSVR buckling load prediction model

During the parameter optimization of grid-stiffened structures, repeated evaluations of the structural load-carrying performance were inevitably required. However, conventional FEM-based simulations were usually computationally intensive, and sometimes impractical for optimizing grid-stiffened structures. Thus, an efficient approach for predicting the load-carrying performance of the grid-stiffened structure was badly needed. In this section, a surrogate model based on Least Squares Support Vector Regression (LSSVR) was proposed to calculate the buckling load of the grid-stiffened structure. Details were provided below.

LSSVR

Based on the standard Support Vector Regression (SVR), Suykens et al.24,25 proposed the Least Squares Support Vector Regression (LSSVR). PSO-LSSVR surrogate model

Performance of the LSSVR was highly sensitive to key hyperparameters, so empirical selection rarely yielded optimal results. Therefore, the particle swarm optimization (PSO) algorithm was adopted to optimize these parameters. Then, the optimized surrogate model (PSO-LSSVR) was used to predict the structural load-carrying efficiency of grid-stiffened structures. A schematic illustration of the PSO-LSSVR model was depicted in Fig. 7.Fig. 7Illustration of the PSO-LSSVR model.

Illustration of the PSO-LSSVR model.

Initially, based on experience and engineering feasibility, the parameters jd, jt, bx, θs, ww, wt and st were identified as the design parameters and used as inputs to the PSO-LSSVR model. All other parameters, such as et, Ha, He, Dd, Nc and Nw, were identical to those listed in Table 2. The outputs of the model were the axial compressive buckling load Pcr and the structural mass W. To enhance the uniformity of the sampling process, the Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) method was adopted. After the input set was created, the corresponding buckling load Pcr' and structural mass W' could be figured out by the aforementioned general 3D parametric geometrical model and FEM. Now, the dataset used to train the LSSVR model was acquired.

Then, the LSSVR model was constructed here. First, Min–Max Normalization was applied to the dataset to enhance the performance of the model. 30% of the dataset was used as the test set, and the remaining 70% was used as the training set. Finally, the LSSVR model was obtained based on the training set. To further improve the performance of the LSSVR model, the penalty coefficient C and the kernel function parameter σ must be chosen prudently. Among various kernel functions, the RBF kernel performed best in this work. The RBF kernel function is defined as follows:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k\left( {{\mathbf{x}}_{j} ,{\mathbf{x}}_{k} } \right) = \exp \left( { - \frac{{\left\| {{\mathbf{x}}_{j} - {\mathbf{x}}_{k} } \right\|^{2} }}{{2\sigma^{2} }}} \right),\sigma > 0$$\end{document}where, σ was the bandwidth parameter of RBF kernel function.

Then, the PSO algorithm was employed to optimize the hyperparameters C and σ of the LSSVR model. The detailed procedure was illustrated in Fig. 8. Initially, the number of particles, the maximum number of iterations, and the acceleration constants c1 and c2 were set for the PSO algorithm. And, the populations of C and σ were randomly initialized within the predefined ranges. In each iteration, Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of the test set corresponding to the current C and σ combination was computed. Based on the acquired MAPE, the velocity and position of each particle were updated accordingly. Once the preset number of iterations was reached, the optimal parameter values were output. Finally, the PSO-LSSVR model for predicting the load-carrying efficiency of grid-stiffened structures was constructed.Fig. 8A detailed illustration of the PSO-LSSVR surrogate model.

The divergence between FEM results and PSO-LSSVR model predictions for load-carrying efficiency remained below 4.47%, further confirming the accuracy of the PSO-LSSVR models. Moreover, FEM results showed that the PSO and SSA algorithms improved load-carrying efficiency by 11.20% and 10.08%, respectively, under the given mass constraints.

It was worth noting that the reason why the mass obtained through SSA was slightly higher than the initial mass was due to the error of the surrogate model. Based on the results obtained by PSO, θs decreased from 50 to 40 deg, and bx reduced from 200 to 192 mm. This indicated that smaller spacing of the diagonal stiffeners and smaller angle between stiffeners could promote a more uniform axial load distribution and enhance the axial load-carrying efficiency of the structure. Meanwhile jd increased from 16 to 18.19 mm, and st decreased from 2.5 to 2 mm. These results suggested that stiffeners play a more important role than skin in improving the load-carrying efficiency of the structure. Compared to the PSO results, jd of SSA increased from 18.19 to 18.37 mm, and bx increased from 192 to 193.12 mm. This indicated that a higher stiffener height may reduce the structural load-carrying efficiency, and a larger spacing may not be conducive to uniform load distribution.

Based on the buckling modes shown in Fig. 15, compared with the initial design, two optimization methods yielded similar buckling modes, which further confirmed the solution robustness. The optimized structures exhibited a wider spatial distribution of buckling-induced instability waves towards the end regions, which improved structural stability by more uniform load distribution and enhanced material engagement. Compared with the results of SSA, the PSO-optimized structure exhibited a more uniform deformation distribution throughout the entire structure, leading to a higher load-carrying efficiency.

Conclusion

To improve the load-carrying efficiency of grid-stiffened structures, this study firstly proposed a general 3D parametric geometrical modeling method for grid-stiffened structures, enabling accurate and efficient analysis of the load-carrying performance of grid-stiffened structures. Secondly, an efficient and accurate model based on the PSO-LSSVR was developed to predict the load-carrying performance of grid-stiffened structures to address the high computational cost inherent in FEM simulations. Thirdly, according to sensitivity analysis, key parameters that significantly influenced the load-carrying efficiency were identified. It revealed that the angle between stiffeners θs, height of stiffener jd, the spacing of diagonal stiffeners bx, and thickness of skin st were the dominated factors affecting the load-carrying efficiency of grid-stiffened structures. The sum of STi for these four parameters was 0.914, highlighting their critical role in determining the load-carrying efficiency. Moreover, the STi of each parameter was higher than its corresponding Si, indicating that parameter interactions played an important role in influencing the structural load-carrying efficiency. Then, considering these key parameters, an optimization model was established to improve the load-carrying efficiency of grid-stiffened structures. Finally, the optimization model was solved by the PSO and SSA, resulting in an 11.20% improvement in load-carrying efficiency compared to the initial design.

The models proposed in this work could facilitate the lightweight design and load-carrying efficiency enhancement in aerospace fields. Although the proposed models showed favorable results, they are only applicable to conventional cylindrical configurations of grid-stiffened structures and cannot be applied to grid-stiffened curved shells, such as those with opening regions defined by NURBS curves. Future research could extend the proposed models to grid-stiffened curved shells with geometrical constraints to address a wider range of structural requirements.
