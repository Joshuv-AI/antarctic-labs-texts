Impact of the First Basic Emergency Care Training Course in Ethiopia
Authors: Tirsit Makonnen, Alegnta Gebreyesus, Bethlehem Workeye, Chelsea McCullough, Gellila Mengistu, Menbeu Sultan, Ahlam Awad Mohammed, Fatuma Ebrahim, Nejat Ibrahim, Shama Patel, Tsion Firew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesIn Ethiopia, there is a significant unmet need for basic emergency care. The World Health Organization (WHO), in collaboration with the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), developed the Basic Emergency Care (BEC) course to assist in essential acute, time-sensitive health training for frontline providers in resource-constrained settings. Recognizing the urgency of this issue, the Federal Ministry of Health (FMOH) of Ethiopia prioritized the implementation of the BEC course. The objective of this study is to evaluate the first implementation of the BEC training in Ethiopia.MethodsA quasiexperimental study was conducted to assess whether Ethiopia’s BEC training implementation would lead to improved baseline knowledge as a foundational step toward enhancing emergency services delivery. The study followed 2 cohorts of participants who completed the WHO’s 5-day BEC Training of Trainers (ToT) course. Owing to resource constraints requiring the training of multiple participants in a short period, a cascading peer-teaching model was used. Expert instructors first administered the BEC ToT course to cohort A, who then subsequently trained cohort B. To evaluate changes in knowledge, pretest and posttest assessments were used. The pretest assessment was administered to both cohorts on the first day of the course, prior to the start of the training. The posttest assessment was administered to both cohorts on the last day of the course, after the completion of the training. The assessment, which was standardized into the course by WHO, covered topics such as identifying emergencies, skills application, and qualitative feedback.ResultsA total of 111 participants completed both pretraining and posttraining assessments. In cohort A, the average pretest score was 89%, and the average posttest score was 92%. The pass rate decreased from 96% to 91%. In cohort B, the average pretest score was 58%, and the average posttest score was 80%. The pass rate increased from 37% to 80%, demonstrating an increase of 43%. Strategic recommendations for future training include adapting course materials to better align with country-specific needs and allocating more time and resources for practical skills training.ConclusionThe BEC training was successfully introduced in Ethiopia in an efficient and structured manner. With specific modifications, future BEC training sessions should continue to maintain and strengthen foundational emergency care training in Ethiopia.

The Bottom LineHealth care providers in Ethiopia experience knowledge gaps in basic emergency care skills. From 2018 to 2019, over 100 health care providers were enrolled in the World Health Organization—International Committee of the Red Cross’s course entitled Basic Emergency Care to learn about management of respiratory distress, shock, trauma, and altered mental status. Preinterventional and postinterventional test scores jumped from 58% to 80%, with overall pass rates increasing from 37% to 80%, highlighting the potential to address knowledge gaps with a low-cost basic emergency care course for nonemergency medicine specialists.

Introduction

Background

Ethiopia bears a disproportionate burden of emergency-related morbidity and mortality. With a population of 114 million, it is the second largest country in sub-Saharan Africa, accounting for 10% of the subcontinent’s population.1 Various health indicators highlight this disproportionate burden. From 2010 to 2019, globally, injury-related mortality decreased by 11%, whereas, in Ethiopia, injury-related mortality increased by 20%.2 When examining road traffic injury per 100,000 people, the global prevalence has decreased by 6%, but, in Ethiopia, it has risen by 20%.3

In addition to disproportionately high levels of morbidity and mortality, Ethiopia faces significant limitations in health care capacity to address these health concerns. The global average physician-to-population ratio is 1.7 per 1000, but, in Ethiopia, the ratio is only 0.1 per 1000.4 Similarly, while the global average hospital bed capacity is 2.9 per 1000 people, Ethiopia has only 0.3 per 1000.5 These gaps underscore the urgent need to strengthen emergency care services.

Importance

Timely care of the acutely ill and injured saves lives.6 The Disease Control Priorities Project indicates that delayed acute care is responsible for one-third of disabilities and half of all deaths in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).7 Emergency care systems are uniquely designed to provide timely care but remain underdeveloped in many regions worldwide. To address these gaps in care, the World Health Organization (WHO), along with partners such as the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) and Medicins San Frontieres (MSF), developed a set of low cost, high-yield standardized emergency care tools.8 The most foundational tools include the following9:•Basic Emergency Care Training•Trauma and Medical Standardized Forms•Trauma and Medical Checklist•Designated Resuscitation Area•Integrated Triage Tool

These tools are designed for implementation in diverse settings, including field-based, prehospital, and facility-based emergency care. Ethiopia has been at the forefront of implementing these emergency care tools in sub-Saharan Africa.10,11

Goals of This Investigation

In 2018, the Federal Ministry of Health of Ethiopia (FMOH) committed to rolling out facility-based tools beginning with the BEC training course, focusing on district level hospitals. This study evaluates the first large-scale implementation of the BEC course.10,11

The primary goal of this intervention is to use the BEC course as a primary step in improving emergency service delivery. Based on Kirkpatrick model of evaluating training programs, the best way to measure the effect of a program is to assess the results level, which assumes that what is learned has an impact on health delivery outcomes. Owing to resource constrictions, this study could evaluate only up to the level of learning, which focuses on knowledge and skills gained. As learning always precedes results, this investigation aims to measure learning outcomes (how much was learned) as an indirect indicator of results outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

This was a quasiexperimental investigation to assess whether implementing Ethiopia’s first BEC training resulted in improved baseline knowledge, serving as an initial step toward enhancing emergency service delivery. Based on Kirkpatrick model of evaluating training programs discussed in the Introduction, the outcome of learning would be an indirect predictor of results.

Setting

The study was administered from October 2018 to January 2019 at various conference centers throughout the cities of Addis Ababa and Bishoftu in Ethiopia.

Study Size and Selection of Participants

This study comprised 2 separate cohorts: cohort A and cohort B. Cohort A consisted of 23 participants selected based on the following specific criteria: advanced clinical training in emergency medicine, critical care, anesthesia, or surgery; demonstrated leadership positions within their health care institutions; fluency in both English and local Ethiopian languages; commitment to training additional health care workers; and geographic representation across Ethiopia’s administrative regions to ensure sustainable dissemination capacity.

Cohort B comprised 88 participants meeting the following eligibility requirements: active clinical practice as nurses, health officers, or general practitioners at district-level hospitals; minimum 2 years of clinical experience; basic English proficiency with fluency in local Ethiopian languages; institutional approval for participation; and formal commitment to implementing BEC principles upon returning to their respective facilities.

External instructors were individuals who introduced BEC training to cohort A and were selected based on 2 key criteria: experience as a medical professional and as a BEC instructor. The 3 external instructors comprised an emergency medicine physician from the United States, an emergency medicine specialist from Tanzania, and a pediatric emergency medicine physician from Uganda. All possessed WHO BEC instructor certification and had prior experience implementing BEC training in resource-limited settings.

The external instructors participated through a North–South (United States to Ethiopia) and South–South Collaboration (Tanzania/Uganda to Ethiopia). They observed trainees during their teaching assignments and provided feedback to improve instructional quality.

Participation was voluntary with no penalties for declining. Incentives included travel reimbursement, accommodation, meals during training periods, official certification, and professional networking opportunities. All participants received institutional support and approval for attendance.

Intervention Contents

All interventions followed WHO BEC implementation guidelines, Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health training protocols, and international standards for medical education research. BEC is a 5-day training course developed by the WHO to address unmet needs of basic emergency care in low-income countries. It is developed in a hybrid structure that involves both lectures and practicums. As described in Table S1, the 4 primary topics covered include management of respiratory distress, management of shock, management of altered mental status, and trauma management. The goal of BEC is to create a broad network of individuals within a community, capable of providing stabilizing care until advanced care is available.

Necessity of Training of Trainers Model

This study used the Training of Trainers (ToT) model to implement the BEC course. This model addresses the common challenge of limited external instructors in low-resource settings by first training a select group of trainers who then disseminate the training to a larger audience. The ToT model provides a solution by first focusing on increasing the number of certified trainers before reaching end users.12 This is a more sustainable solution because having more trainers will ultimately save time and financial expenses.

Typically, BEC studies have used external instructors to train a single cohort. However, this Ethiopian study faced a high ratio of participants to external instructors. To mitigate this challenge, a cascading ToT method was applied. Three external instructors trained the first group (cohort A), who subsequently trained a second group (cohort B). Therefore, in addition to the eligibility criteria, a critical difference between the 2 cohorts is that cohort A served as the trainers for cohort B. Based on this model, the trainer-to-trainee ratio decreases drastically from 1:29 to 1:4. This was determined by calculating the ratios of external instructors to cohort B and cohort A to cohort B.

Beyond standard BEC content, cohort A received specialized instruction in adult learning principles, culturally appropriate teaching methodologies, practical assessment techniques, and group facilitation skills. Each participant conducted supervised practice teaching sessions with real-time feedback from external instructors to ensure teaching competency before training cohort B.

Measurements and Outcomes

A standardized 25-question multiple-choice test, developed in English by WHO as part of the course to be reliable and valid for the targeted population, was used to evaluate knowledge. For both cohorts, the same assessment was administered as the pretraining and posttraining assessment. The preassessment was administered on the first day of the intervention, and the postassessment was administered on the last day of the intervention. The passing score was 70%. Study coordinators were responsible for administering, grading, and entering data from assessment results into a database. The study coordinators were representatives from the FMOH who did not teach the course. They were aware of the hypothesis and were the only individuals responsible for grading the students. The primary outcome measured was the change in assessment scores, serving as an indirect indicator of improvements in emergency care delivery. Additional outcomes that were measured include the pass rates of each cohort.

Data Analysis

For the purposes of statistical analysis, the scores were studied using Excel (version 16.100.2; Microsoft; 25082415). For continuous data that were in a normal distribution, 1-tailed paired t-test was used to determine whether the intervention played a significant role in improving assessment outcomes. For continuous data that did not fit a normal distribution, log transformation was used prior to running a 1-tailed paired t-test. Proportional data were analyzed using McNemar test.

Qualitative Analysis

To measure the participants’ perceived quality of the intervention, both cohorts received a standardized evaluation form from the WHO at the middle and end of the training. Both forms were administered in English. The midtraining evaluation form consisted of 11 statements rated on a Likert Scale (ranging from 1 = strongly agree to 5 = strongly disagree), with questions that included “The materials distributed are helpful” and “This training experience will be useful in my work.” They were then followed by 15 open-ended questions that queried on the strengths and weaknesses of the training. The postevaluation form solely consisted of the previously provided open-ended questions.

Ethics Approval Statement

This study involves human participants and was approved by an ethics committee. This study was approved by Columbia University’s Human Research Protection Office; under Protocol IRB-AAAS3350. Participants were given informed consent to participate. This article does not contain personal and/or medical information about an identifiable individual.

Results

Assessment Scores

A total of 111 participants from cohort A and cohort B completed both preassessment and postassessment. As seen in Table S2, in cohort A, the average preassessment score was 89% (SD, 0.09%), and the average postassessment score was 92% (SD, 0.10%), with a difference of 3%. The median score changed from 92% to 96%, difference being 4%. The differences in pretest and posttest scores for cohort A followed a normal distribution. T test analysis determined P value as 0.053, with a confidence interval of (−0.01 to 0.07), indicating that the averages were not statistically significant but were trending toward significance (Table S3).

As seen in Table S4, the pass rate changed from 96% (22/23) to 91% (21/23), a difference of −9%. McNemar analysis determined that with a P value of 1.00 and confidence interval of (−1.47 to −0.35), the difference was not statistically significant. Dropout rates were minimal (<5%) primarily due to urgent clinical responsibilities. Participants who did not achieve initial passing scores received additional instruction and retesting opportunities. All cohort A participants who subsequently trained cohort B had demonstrated competency through achieving passing assessment scores.

In cohort B, the average preassessment score was 58%, and the average postassessment score was 80%, with a difference of 22%, as detailed in Table S5. The median score changed from 60% to 84%, with a difference of 20%. The differences in pretest and posttest scores for cohort B followed a normal distribution after log transformation. T test analysis determined 1-tailed P value as <0.01, with a confidence interval of (−0.01 to 0.07), indicating results were statistically significant (Table S6).

As seen in Table S7, the pass rate for cohort B increased from 34% (30/88) to 73% (64/88), with a difference of 39%. McNemar analysis determined that with P value of <0.001 and confidence interval of (−2.05 to 1.83), the difference was statistically significant.

Qualitative Feedback

Participants from both cohorts provided feedback on their training experience, highlighting key aspects related to course content, organization, teaching methodologies, and personal development feedback. In cohort A, the most common feedback focused on course material modification (n = 4), course organization (n = 3), and self-improvement (n = 4). Regarding course material modification, which refers to modifications of subject matters taught in the BEC course, a recurring recommendation was the inclusion of additional topics such as triage. Course organization was regularly lauded as being well structured with logical progression and clarity. Several participants reported that the training had a positive impact on their professional development, particularly enhancing their practice skills and clinical decision making.

Representative participant comments from cohort A include the following: “I have gained more practical sessions for which I haven’t [had] before”; and “The major strength of the training is the organized approach to emergency [patients’] care.”

In cohort B, the most common feedback was regarding the teaching style (n = 12), course material (n = 11), and course structure (n = 15). Regarding teaching style, there was strong consensus that the instructors (who were participants in cohort A) were well trained and maintained a high level of professionalism. Regarding course material, many comments noted that the topics were very relevant and suggested limiting excess repetition of certain topics, such as ABCDE (A, airway; B, breathing; C, circulation; D, disability, E, exposure). For course structure, many participants recommended incorporating additional hands-on learning opportunities, including increased skills demonstrations and practice stations, to reinforce concepts.

Representative participant comments from cohort B include the following: “The trainers are well prepared and delivered well”; and “I will forward when I go my work place and it will help the work of my department.”

Limitations

Over the course of this study, 6 main limitations were identified. The first limitation was the presence of a language barrier. Ethiopia is a linguistically diverse country with numerous languages, many which are exclusively of oral tradition. Translating course materials to all local languages of Ethiopia in a timely and accurate manner presents a significant challenge. To address this limitation, the study team ensured the instructors had a working knowledge of both English and the local language.

The second limitation was that the level of instruction may have been too elementary for cohort A, which consisted of advanced practitioners in emergency medicine. Given their specialized training, some participants found the training content insufficiently challenging. Future iterations of this course targeting similarly qualified participants, including potential recertification programs, should adapt the curriculum to better reflect their existing expertise. Emphasis should be placed on the train-the-trainers methodologies, rather than requiring advanced practitioners to complete the full course as standard participants.

The third limitation pertained to the perceived misalignment between the course content and local epidemiological landscape. Many participants commented on the heavy emphasis of topics that are not epidemiologically common to the country, such as managing opioid overdoses. While the opioid epidemic has brought devastation in many regions of the world, it is not a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in Ethiopia.13,14 It would be prudent to contextualize topics to local disease burden, such as the intentional and unintentional overdoses of household chemicals and pesticides, which are pervasive in many LMICs.15

The fourth limitation identified was the lack of a formalized protocol for assessing skill retention over time. While the initial training provided foundational knowledge, there currently is no structured mechanism to evaluate whether participants retain and effectively apply these skills in clinical practice. Periodic refresher training would be beneficial to reinforce competencies. Additionally, given that several participants cited financial constraints associated with travel and attendance, virtual training platforms could serve as a cost-effective alternative. The WHO has started piloting such courses during the COVID-19 pandemic, and such an initiative will enhance the quality of the implementation and investment in human capacity.16 While virtual delivery shows promise for knowledge dissemination, it may require supplemental hands-on training for optimal procedural skill development, an area warranting future research.

Regarding long-term impact, since this 2018-2019 implementation, trained participants have integrated BEC principles into their clinical practice and institutional protocols. Ethiopia’s experience contributed to the development of WHO World Health Assembly Resolution 72.16 on emergency care systems for universal health coverage, demonstrating the program’s broader policy influence. Future research should evaluate long-term skill retention and clinical outcome improvements in participating institutions.

The fifth limitation of the study is that the use of the same test as both the pretest and posttest assessment can be a confounder as participants might use strategies such as pattern recognition of question types in order to obtain a higher score. This could have been addressed by testing the same essential concepts but modifying nonessential components such as storylines.

The sixth limitation of this study is that it does not directly measure changes in health service outcomes. Considering that the overarching goal is to improve the quality and quantity of health services, this study takes the first step by using educational interventions and foundational knowledge base as indirect indicators. Therefore, it is recommended that future studies examine institutions before and after BEC training to examine whether or not there are improved outcomes in indicators, for example, increases in shock survival and other key emergency care indicators.

In addition to the limitations, there were several biases present in this study, including sampling, observer, social desirability, and response bias. Sampling bias in this study included selection of participants who were based in large urban settings, when the target population involves dwellers of diverse population density settings. This could have been addressed by ensuring that a select number of trainees came from each population density setting. Observer bias in this study could have presented as researchers interpreting the data based on implicit expectations of the success of the intervention. This could have been addressed by having an additional analyst who is independent of the study to analyze and interpret the data. Social desirability bias and response bias would have affected the free text comments as participants may have felt pressured to give socially acceptable responses instead of their honest response. This was addressed by ensuring that responses were submitted anonymously.

Discussion

Assessment Scores

Overall, the data analysis reflects a great improvement in participant fund of knowledge. These findings are consistent with studies done in other sub-Saharan countries such as Uganda and Tanzania.17

Regarding cohort A, when comparing the pretest and posttest scores, t test analysis determined lack of significance, which indicates that the intervention did not affect participant fund of knowledge. However, at P = 0.053, the results were trending toward significance. Therefore, it is possible that if the sample size was not limited to n = 23, then, perhaps, the results may have been statistically significant. In addition, most participants had either neutral or positive increases in scores, which indicates that perhaps outliers are skewing the results.

Assessment of cohort A’s pass rate using McNemar test also showed lack of significance, indicating that the intervention did not affect pass rates. This may have been due to a variety of factors, such as outliers and limited sample size. Outliers that are notable include 1 participant who failed the pretest and 2 other participants who failed the posttest. It is unclear as to why these participants failed; however, they were given opportunities for additional instruction and retesting opportunities.

Regarding cohort B, when comparing the pretest and posttest scores, t test analysis determined significance, which indicates that the intervention did affect participant fund of knowledge. Assessment of cohort B’s pass rate using McNemar test also showed significance, indicating that the intervention did affect pass rates. The nearly doubling of the pass rate from 34% to 73% provides evidence of both the existence of a knowledge gap in basic emergency care and the potential for targeted educational interventions to address the gap effectively. Such evidence can be used to help guide policy decisions aimed at strengthening emergency medical education and improving baseline competencies among health care providers in emergency care.

When comparing the 2 cohorts, it is notable that, overall, cohort A scored higher than cohort B. While neither the cause nor the correlation regarding this discrepancy was formally investigated, a brief discussion is warranted. One potential explanation for this discrepancy is that as part of the eligibility requirements, cohort A required more advanced education and skills than cohort B. This advanced eligibility requirement may have contributed to cohort A having higher scores.

Strengths of the Study

When observing the details of the investigation, there are several strengths that have been identified. The first strength is the use of the cascade training model, specifically the ToT approach, to rapidly scale up emergency care education. ToT cascading model has been studied as part of the Global Health Partnerships and addresses the foreseeable workforce shortage.18 The ToT cascading model used for the BEC implementation in Ethiopia also ensured sustainability by strengthening workforce capacity and improving trainer-to-trainee ratios.19 This model enabled the distribution of trained instructors across geographically remote and underserved regions offering a cost-effective solution and expands the training exponentially.19,20

The BEC implementation in Ethiopia and other countries through the ToT cascading model will provide the opportunity to enhance sustainability and strengthen the emergency care workforce. South–South collaboration, which involves instructors and participants originating from nationalities reflective of the southern global hemisphere, provides a cost-effective approach and peer-to-peer mentorship.19 The long-term effectiveness and success need further investigation.

The second strength is the broad geographic reach of this study. Cohorts A and B combined reflected over 100 participants from various administrative regions of Ethiopia, marking a substantial expansion compared with similar initiatives in other countries, such as Uganda and Tanzania, where 10 individuals were trained as trainers in comparable programs.17 The large sample size in this study strengthens the validity of the findings and underscores the effectiveness of BEC training in enhancing emergency care knowledge and skills.

A third strength of the study is its potential for long-term sustainability of a strong, local network of trainers that can be recruited for future endeavors such as recertification or additional emergency care initiatives training. This approach reduces reliance on external instructors and ensures continuity in training efforts by leveraging existing expertise within countries and regionally.

Ethiopia has made tremendous efforts to successfully complete its first large-scale intervention addressing unmet needs in basic emergency care. To our knowledge, at the time of intervention, this was the largest BEC training initiative globally in 2018. Following this effort, Ethiopia, led the efforts to the adoption of the World Health Assembly resolution, which incorporated BEC as one of the mandates for many countries around the world.6,21 Since then, many countries around the world have adopted and incorporated BEC into their national framework.22 The success of this initiative demonstrates that rapid, large-scale emergency care training is a possibility; however, additional studies are needed to assess scalability. The knowledge gained, as reflected in the assessment outcomes, highlight the efficiency and impact of the training program. Future research should explore the long-term sustainability of skill retention and the impact of BEC training on clinical outcomes, ensuring continued progress in strengthening emergency care systems worldwide.
