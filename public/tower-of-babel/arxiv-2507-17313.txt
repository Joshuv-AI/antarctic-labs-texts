Scattering Hawking Radiation
Peng Cheng

Abstract




We analyze the physical consequences of scattering Hawking radiation emitted in the vicinity of the horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole. The Hawking radiation from the horizon becomes soft at a large distance away from the horizon due to the gravitational redshift, and the above process is exactly the soft theorem in curved spacetime. For an observer located at infinity, such a scattering process introduces a large gauge transformation, which can be regarded as a memory effect. The large gauge transformation is expected to encode more information about the radiation and might shed light on the black hole information paradox.

The global charge conservations enforce constraints on the evaporating process (e.g., the total electric charge carried off by Hawking radiation should always equal the loss of the remaining black hole), which might be a way of encoding the information.
However, in classical gravity theory, the number of global charges (i.e., hairs) of a black hole system is too small, which has no ability to encode all the information of the black hole.
The existence of infinite-dimensional asymptotic symmetries in the null infinity region and horizon

The Hawking-Perry-Strominger (HPS) mechanism states that the conservation law of the extended symmetry can yield infinitely many deterministic constraints on the evaporation process.
Thus, it provides a way of understanding the information paradox.
Recently, in deriving the Page curve of radiation entropy using soft hair

However, more details of the process, like the physical realization, remain unclear.

So the main purpose of the current paper is to better understand the measurement process.
Let us start with an interesting observation.
Consider one of the classical tests of general relativity, i.e., the Pound-Rebka experiment
[

A

The particle is redshifted when it is received by the observer
B

The particle would be infinitely redshifted if
A

For Schwarzschild black holes, the infinite redshift surface is the horizon.
The process can be understood as follows: when the particle moves away from the black hole, it loses energy by overcoming the gravitational force exerted by the black hole.
Moreover, as will be reviewed in section
2.1

P_{\omega}=e^{-8\pi M\omega}\,,

(1)

where

\omega

The higher the energy of the Hawking radiation is, the lower the possibility it may be created, so Hawking radiation usually has a finite frequency when it was first produced.
One may conclude that the Hawking radiation is emitted at the infinite redshift surface and should be infinitely redshifted when received by an observer far away from the black hole.
Thus, the Hawking radiation would be too soft to be measured by the observer.

In this paper, we illustrate the softness of Hawking radiation as seen by the observer and propose a way to measure Hawking radiation by scattering it. The scattering process with soft Hawking radiation, happening on a black hole background, would just be the soft theorem in curved spacetime. With the help of the triangular equivalence

2
Soft Hawking radiation

In this section, we analyze the property of Hawking radiation when it is received by an observer at infinity. We review the derivation of Hawking radiation and demonstrate the gravitational redshift when it travels from the horizon to infinity. We would finally arrive at a seemingly surprising conclusion that when Hawking radiation travels to infinity, it would become soft due to the redshift effect.

2.1
Hawking radiation from horizon

Let us review the Damour-Ruffini approach for deriving Hawking radiation. Consider a scalar field
\Phi
living on a 4-dimensional Schwarzschild black hole background, with the Klein-Gordon equation

\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mu}\left(\sqrt{-g}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\Phi\right)-m^{2}\Phi=0\,.

(2)

The scalar field
\Phi
can be separated as

\Phi=\frac{1}{(4\pi\omega)^{1/2}}\frac{1}{r}R_{\omega}(r,t)Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)\,,

(3)

where
Y_{l}^{m}
is the spherical harmonics.
Working in the tortoise coordinates
r_{*}
and in the horizon limit
r\to r_{H}
, the radius component of (
2
) can be approximated as

\left(-\partial_{t}^{2}+\partial_{r_{*}}^{2}\right)R_{\omega}(r,t)=0\,.

(4)

In the advanced Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, the incoming and outgoing solutions can be obtained, and written as

\displaystyle R^{\text{in}}_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-i\omega v}\,,

(5)

\displaystyle R^{\text{out}}_{\omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{2i\omega r}e^{-i\omega v}\left(\frac{r-2M}{2M}\right)^{i4M\omega}\,.

(6)

The outgoing wave cannot be straightforwardly extended to the interior of the horizon because they are divergent on the horizon. According to Damour and Ruffini
[
24
]
, we can analytically continue the outgoing wave function on the complex plane and rewrite the outgoing wave function. The part outside of the horizon can be written as

R^{\text{out}}_{\omega}=e^{2i\omega r}e^{-i\omega v}\left(\frac{r-2M}{2M}\right)^{i4M\omega},

(7)

and the part inside of the horizon can be written as

R^{\text{out}}_{\omega}=e^{4\pi M\omega}e^{2i\omega r}e^{-i\omega v}\left(\frac{2M-r}{2M}\right)^{i4M\omega}.

(8)

The outgoing wave function can be regarded as going through a scattering process, where part of the outgoing wave function is scattered back to the singularity of the black hole and the rest part can be transmitted to infinity. Then the relative possibility can be written as the square of the radius of the outside part to the inside part of the wave function

P_{\omega}=e^{-8\pi M\omega}\,.

(9)

P_{\omega}
can be regarded as the possibility of creating a pair of particles from vacuum fluctuation.
For Bosonic particles, if we denote
C_{\omega}
as the possibility of no particle being created, the constraint that the overall possibility of creating zero, one, two,
\cdots
pairs of particles is 1 can be written as

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{\omega}P^{n}_{\omega}=1\,.

(10)

The above constraint gives us

C_{\omega}=1-P_{\omega}\,.

(11)

Then the number of particles with energy
\omega
created at the horizon can be expressed as

N_{\omega}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\cdot C_{\omega}P^{n}_{\omega}=\frac{P_{\omega}}{1+P_{\omega}}=\frac{1}{e^{\omega/T}-1}\,,

(12)

with the Hawking temperature
T=\frac{1}{8\pi M}
. The above result is exactly blackbody radiation with the proper Hawking temperature as described by Hawking
[
9
,
10
]

The radiation for Fermions can be obtained by a similar pattern.

Note that the same result can be derived from the so-called Unruh approach
[
25
]
. The near-horizon region of a Schwarzschild black hole can be approximated by the Rindler spacetime. The Unruh effect of the Rindler spacetime can be obtained by tracing out half of the Minkowski vacuum. The resulting density matrix is a thermal density matrix with the Hawking temperature.

As a brief summary of this subsection, the Damour-Ruffini approach and the Unruh approach both suggest that Hawking radiation is created in the vicinity of the horizon with possibility
P_{\omega}
. The super-high frequency modes are suppressed, and we should regard the frequency at which the radiation was emitted as finite.

2.2
Gravitational redshift

Now, we can consider the process when Hawking radiation travels from the near-horizon region to infinity. As demonstrated by Pound and Rebka
[
14
,
15
]
, a particle that travels away from a black hole would be redshifted. Consider a particle traveling in a Schwarzschild black hole spacetime, with the metric in retarded coordinates written as

\text{d}s^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{2GM}{\rho}\right)\text{d}u^{2}-2\text{d}u\text{d}\rho+2\rho^{2}\gamma_{z\bar{z}}\text{d}z\text{d}\bar{z}\,,

(13)

where
\gamma_{z\bar{z}}=\frac{2}{(1+z\bar{z})^{2}}
and
(z,\bar{z})
is the stereographic coordinates. Classically, the frequency of the particle can be defined as

\omega=\frac{\text{d}N}{\text{d}\tau},

(14)

where
N
is the number of vibrations and
\tau
is the proper time of the particle. Suppose the particle moves from location
A
to
B
, and two observers at
A
and
B
can calculate the frequency of the particle by counting the same number of vibrations. So, the frequency of the particle is determined by the proper time at the two locations

\omega_{A}\text{d}\tau_{A}=\omega_{B}\text{d}\tau_{B}\,.

(15)

The proper times at
A
and
B
are related by

\text{d}\tau_{B}=\frac{\sqrt{-g_{00}}|_{B}}{\sqrt{-g_{00}}|_{A}}~\text{d}\tau_{A}\,.

(16)

Then, the relation between the frequencies of the particle at the two locations is

\omega_{B}=\frac{\sqrt{-g_{00}}|_{A}}{\sqrt{-g_{00}}|_{B}}~\omega_{A}\,.

(17)

Now, if we consider a Hawking radiation emitted from the horizon of the Schwarzschild black hole that travels to a location far away from the horizon, i.e.
\rho_{A}\to 2GM
and
\rho_{B}\to\infty
, the particle received at
B
is always infinitely redshifted

\omega_{B}=\sqrt{-g_{00}}|_{A}~\omega_{A}\to 0\,,

(18)

due to
g_{00}|_{A}\to 0
.
The above gravitational redshift argument should also apply to Hawking radiation emitted at the black hole horizon (also the infinite redshift surface for the Schwarzschild black hole).
Note that one of the important conditions to ensure that
\omega_{B}
approaches zero is that
\omega_{A}
can not be too large. As discussed previously, this is right because the super-high frequency modes of Hawking radiation are suppressed.
So we can say that Hawking radiation is always
soft
when received at a location far away from the horizon.

3
Measuring Hawking radiation by scattering it

The Hawking radiation is always soft when received by the observer far away from the horizon. If this is the case, the radiation seems too soft to be measured. However, this is the real situation when we try to observe Hawking radiation in the universe or in a laboratory. Does this mean that we may never receive any Hawking radiation? There might be a different way of observing it, and this physical process may even refresh some of our understandings of Strominger’s triangle relation in curved spacetime. This section proposes a way of measuring Hawking radiation by scattering it.

Let us consider scattering the radiation with a bunch of particles coming from the past null infinity, and finally going to the future null infinity.
We can consider the following two processes, as shown in Fig.
1
, and compare them to see the effect of scattering Hawking radiation.
First, we can consider the process without Hawking radiation as shown in the left figure of Fig.
1
. We have
m
incoming particles with momentum
q^{in}_{k}
coming from the past null infinity
\mathcal{I}^{-}
, scattering with each other in the bulk between the horizon and infinity. Then we get
n
outgoing particles with momentum
q^{in}_{k}
going back to the future null infinity
\mathcal{I}^{+}
. The scattering process happens at a large distance away from the black hole. We can denote the process with S-matrix
\mathcal{S}
as

\left<{\text{out}}\right|\mathcal{S}\left|{\text{in}}\right>

(19)

Now, we are going to add a massless bosonic particle emitted from the horizon to the scattering process, as shown in the right figure of Fig.
1
. The particle, which can be regarded as Hawking radiation, has momentum
p^{\mu}
and polarization vector
\epsilon^{\mu}
.
The main task of this section is to answer what the difference is between the two processes shown in Fig.
1
.

3.1
Soft photon theorem

The Hawking radiation emitted from the horizon always becomes soft in the bulk because of the gravitational redshift effect. Then, the process shown in the right figure of Fig.
1
can be directly worked out using Weinberg’s soft theorem
[
26
]
. We have not specified what bosonic particle is emitted from the horizon, and it can be a photon, gluon, graviton, etc. For simplicity, we are only going to demonstrate the photon case, and other particles can be worked out following the same pattern.

Now, let us consider adding an extra soft photon with momentum
p
and polarization
\varepsilon
to a scattering process with
n
incoming and
m
outgoing particles, as shown in Fig.
2
. We can consider the following interaction

\mathcal{L}\sim-A^{\mu}j_{\mu},~~~~j_{\mu}=iQ(\Phi\partial_{\mu}\Phi^{*}-\Phi^{*}\partial_{\mu}\Phi)\,.

(20)

First of all, let us attach a soft photon to the incoming external legs, which is illustrated in the first figure of Fig.
2
.
Note that we are considering the scalar particle
\Phi
, with charge
Q
and mass
\mu

1

\mu
is taken to be zero for massless particles afterward since charged particles moving at the speed of light might not be well-defined

.
.
From Feynman’s rule, the difference between the diagram with and without the extra photon is a propagator with momentum
q^{\text{in}}_{k}+p
and a vertex with interaction (
20
). The external legs are all on-shell, so the extra propagator with momentum
q^{\text{in}}+p
can be expressed as

\frac{-i}{(q^{\text{in}}_{k}+p)^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}}=\frac{-i}{2q^{\text{in}}_{k}\cdot p}\,,

(21)

where we have used
p^{2}=0
and
(q^{\text{in}}_{k})^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}=0
. Up to subleading corrections, the vertex can be written as

2i\epsilon^{\mu}Q_{k}q^{\text{in}}_{k\mu}\,.

(22)

So, the total difference between an extra soft photon being and not being attached to the external incoming legs is a factor

\frac{Q_{k}q^{\text{in}}_{k}\cdot\epsilon}{q^{\text{in}}_{k}\cdot p}\,.

(23)

The case when the soft photon is attached to an outgoing external leg, shown in the second figure of Fig.
2
is similar, and we also get an extra factor containing a propagator and a vertex

-\frac{Q_{k}q^{\text{out}}_{k}\cdot\epsilon}{q^{\text{out}}_{k}\cdot p}\,.

(24)

Note that the above factors (
23
) and (
24

p\to 0
. However, attaching an extra soft photon to an off-shell internal leg would never give us a pole, because there is no cancellation between
q^{2}
and

The key ingredient of understanding the physics of the soft theorem is to transform the soft factor to position space using the relationship between a null vector in the bulk and a point labeled by the stereographic coordinates
(z,\bar{z})
on the celestial sphere and horizon, which was worked out in

In the isotropic coordinates
(t,x^{i})

\text{d}s^{2}=-\left(\frac{r-r_{h}}{r+r_{h}}\right)^{2}\text{d}t^{2}+\left(\frac{r+r_{h}}{r}\right)^{4}\text{d}\vec{x}^{2}\,,

(26)

with
r_{h}={GM}/{2}

The coordinate transformation is

t=u+\rho+2GM\ln\frac{\rho-2GM}{2GM}\,,\quad\left(1+\frac{GM}{2r}\right)^{2}r=\rho\,,

(27)

and

x^{1}=r\frac{(z+\bar{z})}{1+z\bar{z}},\quad x^{2}=r\frac{-i(z-\bar{z})}{1+z\bar{z}},\quad x^{3}=r\frac{1-z\bar{z}}{1+z\bar{z}}\,.

(28)

Note that
r
is the radius direction, which is defined in (
27
).

With the metric (
26
), the four-momentum and polarization vector can be written in terms of the stereographic coordinates as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&p_{\mu}=\frac{\omega}{1+z\bar{z}}\frac{(r+r_{h})^{3}}{r^{2}(r-r_{h})}\left[-\frac{r^{2}(r-r_{h})}{(r+r_{h})^{3}}(1+z\bar{z}),(z+\bar{z}),-i(z-\bar{z}),(1-z\bar{z})\right].\\

(29)

and

\begin{split}\epsilon^{+\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{r^{2}}{(r+r_{h})^{2}}\left(\frac{(r+r_{h})^{3}}{r^{2}(r-r_{h})}\bar{z},1,-i,-\bar{z}\right),\\
\epsilon^{-\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{r^{2}}{(r+r_{h})^{2}}\left(\frac{(r+r_{h})^{3}}{r^{2}(r-r_{h})}z,1,i,-z\right).\end{split}

(30)

Now, the four-momenta and the polarization vectors are written in terms of the bulk coordinates.
The flat result can be recovered as
r\gg r_{h}
, and it’s easy to check that

p^{2}=0,\quad p_{\mu}\epsilon^{\pm\mu}=0,\quad\epsilon^{\mu}_{\alpha}\epsilon^{*}_{\beta\mu}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,.

(31)

-

as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{Q_{k}q^{\text{in}}_{k}\cdot\epsilon}{q^{\text{in}}_{k}\cdot p}-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{Q_{k}q^{\text{out}}_{k}\cdot\epsilon}{q^{\text{out}}_{k}\cdot p}=\frac{1+z\bar{z}}{\sqrt{2}\omega}\frac{r-r_{h}}{r+r_{h}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{Q^{\textrm{in}}_{k}}{z-z_{k}^{\textrm{in}}}-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{Q^{\textrm{out}}_{k}}{z-z_{k}^{\textrm{out}}}\right]\,.

.
Substituting (

\displaystyle\left<{\text{out}}\right|\mathcal{S}~a^{\text{in}}(p)^{\dagger}\left|{\text{in}}\right>=\frac{1+z\bar{z}}{\sqrt{2}\omega}\frac{r-r_{h}}{r+r_{h}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{Q^{\textrm{in}}_{k}}{z-z_{k}^{\textrm{in}}}-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{Q^{\textrm{out}}_{k}}{z-z_{k}^{\textrm{out}}}\right]\left<{\text{out}}\right|\mathcal{S}\left|{\text{in}}\right>+\cdots

This is different than the flat case, where the soft factor is universal and the result does not depend on the radius direction.
The extra factor

in (

We expect the scattering to happen at a large but finite radius

We did not use the Ward identity of large gauge transformation in this section. What we have done is merely rewriting the soft factor in the position space using the vector-coordinate relations (

It is straightforward to show that the soft theorem is equivalent to the Ward identity.
However, it is not the main focus here, and we are more interested in the physical memory effects, which will be explored in the next subsection.

3.2
Electromagnetic memory effect

Now, we have rewritten the soft factor in terms of the bulk coordinate
(t,r,z,\bar{z})
. The question is how to properly understand the above soft theorem.
Consider an observer located at infinity sending in
n
incoming particles with momentum
q_{k}^{\text{in}}
to the bulk and receiving
m
outgoing particles with momentum
q_{k}^{\text{out}}
, which is shown in Fig.
3
. When extra Hawking radiation joins the scattering process, the observer would find an extra factor with a pole as shown in (
33
). So the mere effect of scattering with the soft Hawking radiation is the soft factor.

Following the proposal in
[

,

,

, the soft factor (

) can be related to the expectation value of the field fluctuation.
At leading order, if we represent the scattering amplitude as
M_{n+m+1}(q_{k},[p;\epsilon^{\pm}])
, the soft factor can be denoted as

S^{\pm}=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\omega\frac{M_{n+m+1}(q_{k},[p;\epsilon^{\pm}])}{M_{n+m}(q_{k})}\,.

(34)

In the classical limit, the change of classical fields can be related to the soft factor as

\Delta A_{\mu}\epsilon^{\mu\pm}=S^{\pm}\,.

(35)

Then, the change of fields at the celestial sphere can be obtained and read as

\begin{split}\Delta A_{z}=2\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{Q^{\textrm{in}}_{k}}{z-z_{k}^{\textrm{in}}}-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{Q^{\textrm{out}}_{k}}{z-z_{k}^{\textrm{out}}}\right]\,,\\
\Delta A_{\bar{z}}=2\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{Q^{\textrm{in}}_{k}}{\bar{z}-\bar{z}_{k}^{\textrm{in}}}-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{Q^{\textrm{out}}_{k}}{\bar{z}-\bar{z}_{k}^{\textrm{out}}}\right]\,.\end{split}

(36)

So, for an observer located on the celestial sphere, when the particle he injects into the bulk scatters with soft Hawking radiation, he would notice extra field fluctuations shown in (
36
).
The above configuration corresponds to a flat gauge connection, which can be understood from the point of view of the memory effect.

At
u=u_{0}
, if an electromagnetic shockwave passes through the celestial sphere, the information of the shockwave is encoded in the net change of the surface charge. If there is initially no electromagnetic field, the system would finally settle into a stationary configuration with a flat connection. The net charge change can be expressed as

\Delta Q_{\varepsilon}=-\int_{u_{0}}\text{d}z\text{d}\bar{z}~\varepsilon(z,\bar{z})(\partial_{z}\Delta A_{\bar{z}}+\partial_{\bar{z}}\Delta A_{z})\,.

(37)

where we only consider leading order contributions. The net change of surface charge is called the electromagnetic memory effect.

Now, as a key result of this paper, the change of fields (
36
) corresponds to a net charge change

\Delta Q_{\varepsilon}=-8\pi\sum_{k=1}^{m+n}Q_{k}\varepsilon(z_{k},\bar{z}_{k})\,,

(38)

due to

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\Delta A_{\bar{z}}+\partial_{\bar{z}}\Delta A_{z}=8\pi\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}Q^{\textrm{in}}_{k}\delta^{2}(z-z_{k})-\sum_{k=1}^{m}Q^{\textrm{out}}_{k}\delta^{2}(z-z_{k})\right]\,.

(39)

So, we can conclude that scattering with the Hawking radiation emitted from the horizon introduces a large gauge transformation on the celestial sphere, and we can measure the corresponding net change of surface charge
\Delta Q_{\varepsilon}
, as shown in (
38
). Note that the effect can not be seen if we simply allow Hawking radiation to pass through the celestial sphere. And from the process described here, we can measure more information about the radiation related to charge
Q_{\varepsilon}
.

4
Conclusion and Discussion

Charge conservation imposes constraints on Hawking radiation processes, and there is no exception for the charges related to large gauge transformations. We study how to measure more information carried by Hawking radiation through an analysis of Hawking radiation received by an observer at infinity.

The finite frequency Hawking radiation created near the infinite redshift surface would be infinitely redshifted when received by the observer at infinity, which means the Hawking radiation is always soft. The soft Hawking radiation can be analyzed by Weinberg’s soft theorem when it is scattered with hard particles at a location far away from the horizon. The soft theorem can be understood using the triangle relation, and the process naturally introduces a large gauge transformation on the null infinity. We argue that the large gauge transformation with parameter
\varepsilon
encodes some further information about the Hawking radiation and thus can help us understand the information paradox.

The ultimate point we want to make is that there can be some information that is ignored when one simply considers the equivalence between the soft theorem and the memory effect (or large gauge transformation). The memory effect contains some information encoded by pure gauge parameters, while the soft photon seems to come from a random lightbulb, and no information is carried by it. There is an incommensurate amount of information between the two sides. Information is ignored when one simply considers soft photons coming from a lightbulb rather than gravitationally redshifted soft photons.
Therefore, we tend to propose that the gravitationally redshifted soft photon might be the “right” soft particle in the soft theorem.
The particle encoding some information about the black hole travels from the horizon to infinity, and the information can be decoded by the process proposed here. This is also reasonable by looking at the conservation of charge
Q_{\varepsilon}
on the Cauchy surface containing the horizon and null infinity.
Thus, the process discussed here might be the key to decoding more information about the soft charge carried by Hawking radiation.
