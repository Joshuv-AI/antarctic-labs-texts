Genetic diversity of Cimex hemipterus and first report of Wolbachia supergroup T in Tehran, Iran: insights from COI and 16 S rRNA analysis
Authors: Sajjad Moshavernia, Saeed Shahabi, Azim Paksa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBed bugs of the species Cimex hemipterus have re-emerged globally as important urban pests, causing dermatological, allergic, and psychological impacts. Endosymbiotic bacteria such as Wolbachia play essential roles in arthropod biology, yet data from the Middle East remain scarce. This study aimed to provide the first molecular evidence of Wolbachia supergroup T in Iranian populations of C. hemipterus, using 16 S rRNA for symbiont detection and mitochondrial COI for host identification and genetic analysis. Adult specimens were collected from four districts of Tehran, and genomic DNA was analyzed by PCR and Sanger sequencing. Host identity was confirmed as C. hemipterus, showing low genetic divergence from global populations. Wolbachia infection was detected in 20% of specimens, with a significantly higher prevalence in females than males (χ² = 5.4, p = 0.02). Infections were restricted to two districts, and phylogenetic analysis placed all sequences within supergroup T. COI-based haplotype analysis revealed low genetic diversity and significantly negative neutrality test values, which may be consistent with recent population expansion or purifying selection. Overall, C. hemipterus populations in Tehran appear genetically homogeneous and closely related to globally distributed lineages, which may be consistent with recent expansion and possible human-mediated dispersal. Introduction

Bed bugs of the genus Cimex (Hemiptera: Cimicidae) are obligate hematophagous ectoparasites of humans and other mammals and have re-emerged globally as a significant public health concern1,2. Among them, Cimex hemipterus, commonly referred to as the tropical bed bug, has expanded its geographical range in recent decades, particularly in urban environments3. Infestations of C. hemipterus are associated with dermatological reactions, allergic responses, secondary infections, and psychological distress, underscoring the importance of understanding the biology, population structure, and microbial associations of this species4,5.

Endosymbiotic bacteria play a crucial role in the ecology and evolution of many arthropods, influencing host nutrition, reproduction, immunity, and adaptation6. Among these symbionts, Wolbachia is one of the most prevalent and extensively studied intracellular bacteria, infecting an estimated 40–60% of arthropod species worldwide7,8. In bed bugs, Wolbachia functions as an obligate mutualist by supplying essential B vitamins absent from the host’s blood-based diet, thereby contributing critically to host survival and fitness9. Previous studies have shown that Wolbachia infecting bed bugs predominantly belongs to supergroup T, a lineage largely restricted to Cimicidae hosts10.

Molecular detection and phylogenetic characterization of Wolbachia commonly rely on bacterial markers such as 16 S rRNA, wsp, and multilocus sequence typing (MLST), the latter representing a standardized scheme based on multiple Wolbachia housekeeping genes8,11,12. Despite increasing research on Wolbachia–bed bug associations, molecular data from the Middle East, especially Iran, remain limited. Moreover, the prevalence and phylogenetic placement of Wolbachia in Iranian populations of C. hemipterus have not yet been investigated.

Therefore, the present study aims to provide the first molecular evidence of Wolbachia supergroup T in C. hemipterus from Iran. By combining PCR amplification and sequencing of the Wolbachia 16 S rRNA gene with mitochondrial COI analysis of the host, this study seeks to assess the presence and distribution of Wolbachia in C. hemipterus populations from different districts of Tehran, confirm host species identity and genetic relationships among populations, and place the detected Wolbachia strains within a broader phylogenetic context. The findings contribute to a better understanding of bed bug–endosymbiont associations in Iran and provide baseline molecular data that may support future studies on bed bug biology, evolution, and control strategies.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Adult specimens of bed bugs were collected from four urban districts of Tehran, Iran: Enghelab Square, Jannat Abad, Mirdamad, and Piroozi (Fig. 1). Exact sampling coordinates and the number of specimens collected per location are provided in Supplementary Table S1. A total of 400 adult specimens were collected March 2025. Of these, 200 specimens (100 males and 100 females) were selected for molecular analyses, with equal representation attempted across sites where possible. Specimens were morphologically identified as Cimex hemipterus using standard taxonomic keys for the family Cimicidae (Usinger 1966) under a stereomicroscope prior to molecular analyses13. Specimens were preserved individually in 95–100% ethanol and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted from individual whole specimens using a commercial DNA extraction kit (Qiagen DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration and quality were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry.

Geographic locations of bed bug sampling sites in Tehran, Iran. Specimens of Cimex hemipterus were collected from four urban districts, including Enghelab Square, Jannat Abad, Mirdamad, and Piroozi. The map illustrates the spatial distribution of the sampling areas within the metropolitan region of Tehran.

Molecular detection of Wolbachia

The presence of Wolbachia was screened by PCR amplification of a fragment of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene11. This marker was selected for its suitability in preliminary detection and broad supergroup assignment when combined with sequencing and phylogenetic analysis, although it offers limited resolution for strain-level characterization. Recent studies have also used 16 S rRNA sequences, alone or together with other loci, for Wolbachia detection and broad phylogenetic placement into supergroups14,15. PCR reactions were performed in a total volume of 25 µL containing template DNA, PCR buffer, MgCl₂, dNTPs, primers, and Taq DNA polymerase16. Amplification products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gels, and samples yielding amplicons of the expected size (~ 400–500 bp, depending on primer pair) were considered positive for Wolbachia infection17. Positive PCR products were purified and subjected to bidirectional Sanger sequencing (Pishgam Biotech Co., Tehran, Iran) to confirm Wolbachia identity and supergroup affiliation. The same DNA extracts were used for both Wolbachia 16 S rRNA and host COI analyses. The 16 S rRNA marker was used for initial Wolbachia detection and broad supergroup assignment; however, because this marker has limited resolution for strain-level characterization, future studies should include additional markers such as wsp and MLST loci.

Molecular identification of the host

Host species identification and genetic diversity analyses were performed using the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene18. PCR amplification, purification, and sequencing of COI fragments (~ 631 bp) were conducted to confirm the taxonomic identity of C. hemipterus and to assess genetic relationships among sampled populations. Although 200 specimens were screened for Wolbachia infection, COI sequencing was performed on a subset of specimens selected to represent all sampling locations, both sexes, and Wolbachia infection status where possible. Of these, 27 high-quality COI sequences were retained after trimming and quality filtering and were used for haplotype and genetic diversity analyses.

Primers and PCR conditions

For molecular detection of Wolbachia, a fragment of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using the primer pair 16SAR-L (5′-CGCCTGTTTATCAAAAACAT-3′) and 16SBRH (5′-CCGGTCTGAACTCAGATCACGT-3′), with an annealing temperature of 50 °C. Only this primer pair was used for Wolbachia 16 S rRNA amplification in the present study. Host identification was carried out by amplifying a fragment of the mitochondrial COI gene using the universal barcoding primers LCO1490 (5′-GGTCAACAAATCATAAAGATATTGG-3′) and HCO2198 (5′-TAAACTTCAGGGTGACCAAAAAATCA-3′), with an annealing temperature of 48 °C. PCR cycling profiles followed standard protocols, including an initial denaturation step, 35 amplification cycles, and a final extension at 72 °C. Expected amplicon sizes were approximately 400–500 bp for Wolbachia 16 S rRNA and 658 bp for COI (before trimming).

Sequence analysis and phylogenetic Inference

Forward and reverse Sanger sequencing reads were inspected visually for quality using chromatograms in Chromas software, trimmed to remove low-quality bases (Phred score threshold > 20), and assembled into consensus sequences using Geneious Prime v2023.2. Ambiguous bases were resolved by manual inspection of chromatograms or coded as ‘N’ if unresolved. The resulting sequences were compared with reference sequences deposited in the NCBI GenBank database using the BLASTn algorithm (accessed April 2025) to confirm the identity of Wolbachia based on the 16 S rRNA gene and to verify the host species (C. hemipterus) based on the mitochondrial COI gene19. Multiple sequence alignments for Wolbachia 16 S rRNA and host COI datasets were generated using the MUSCLE algorithm implemented in MEGA1120. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using the Maximum Likelihood method implemented in MEGA11 (version 11), with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to assess node support. The best-fit nucleotide substitution model (Tamura-Nei) was selected based on the lowest AIC value using ModelTest in MEGA11. Gaps and missing data were treated as missing in the analyses21. All nucleotide sequences generated in this study have been deposited in the GenBank database.

Genetic diversity and haplotype analysis

These analyses were performed on the mitochondrial COI gene sequences (631 bp fragment) from 27 samples using DnaSP v622. Genetic diversity indices, including number of haplotypes (h), haplotype diversity (Hd), nucleotide diversity (π), average number of pairwise nucleotide differences (k), and counts of synonymous and non-synonymous substitutions, were computed using DnaSP.

Population expansion

To infer historical demographic dynamics, neutrality tests were conducted: Tajima’s D (based on the number of segregating sites), Fu and Li’s D* and F*, and Fu’s Fs, all implemented in DnaSP. Significantly negative values of Tajima’s D and Fu’s Fs were interpreted as evidence of recent population expansion or purifying selection23.

Results

Among the 200 specimens tested (100 males and 100 females), 40 (20%) were found to be positive for Wolbachia. Morphological examination confirmed diagnostic characteristics consistent with Cimex hemipterus, including features of the pronotum, body shape, and other key taxonomic traits as described in standard identification keys. A sex-specific analysis revealed a higher prevalence in females (28/100 = 28%) compared to males (12/100 = 12%), and this difference was statistically significant (chi-square test: χ² = 5.4, df = 1, p = 0.02). Geographically, Wolbachia-positive amplicons were detected exclusively in specimens from Enghelab Square and Jannat Abad, with no positive samples found in Mirdamad or Pirouzi (Tables 1 and 2; Fig. 2). Sequence analysis of the amplified 16 S rRNA fragments confirmed their identity as Wolbachia. BLASTn searches demonstrated > 99% nucleotide similarity between the obtained sequences (GenBank accession numbers: PX699580 and PX699581) and previously reported Wolbachia sequences associated with cimicid bed bugs. Phylogenetic reconstruction based on the 16 S rRNA gene suggested affiliation with supergroup T, clustering closely with reference sequences from Cimex hosts, including C. hemipterus (Fig. 2). All nucleotide sequences generated in this study have been deposited in the GenBank database.

Phylogenetic relationships

Due to the high sequence similarity observed among positive samples, representative sequences were selected for GenBank submission and phylogenetic analysis to avoid redundancy while preserving the overall genetic signal. The two COI sequences generated from the Tehran populations (GenBank accession numbers: PX698528 and PX698529) clustered with reference C. hemipterus sequences retrieved from GenBank. These reference sequences originated from various geographic regions, including Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, Ghana, Kenya, Senegal, and Malaysia (Fig. 3). The observed clustering pattern, supported by bootstrap values from 1,000 replicates, confirmed the conspecific identity of the Iranian specimens with globally distributed C. hemipterus lineages. Branch lengths in the tree, proportional to the number of nucleotide substitutions per site (scale bar = 0.020), indicated low genetic divergence among the Tehran sequences and their closest GenBank counterparts. Sequences from geographically adjacent regions (e.g., South Asia and East Africa) tended to form subclades, though support values for some internal nodes were moderate (56–58%), reflecting limited phylogenetic resolution at deeper nodes. Oeciacus vicarius (GenBank: GU985541) was designated as the outgroup, providing appropriate rooting for the tree and confirming the monophyly of the C. hemipterus ingroup. For phylogenetic reconstruction, two representative COI sequences (one from each Wolbachia-positive district) were selected based on sequence identity and haplotype grouping to avoid over-representation of identical or highly similar sequences, while maintaining clarity of tree topology. However, for haplotype and genetic diversity analyses, all 27 high-quality COI sequences (including those from Wolbachia-negative specimens and all sampling sites) were included to maximize resolution of population-level variation.

Genetic diversity and haplotype analysis

Haplotype analysis of Cimex hemipterus based on mitochondrial COI gene sequences (631 bp) revealed six distinct haplotypes. Among these, nine segregating sites were identified, yielding a haplotype diversity (Hd) of 0.342 (variance = 0.013) and a nucleotide diversity (π) of 0.004. The average number of pairwise nucleotide differences (k) was estimated at 0.803 (Table 3).

Population genetic parameters

Neutrality tests and population genetic analyses were performed to infer the demographic history of Cimex hemipterus populations from Tehran, Iran. All tested statistics yielded negative values. Fu and Li’s D* (− 2.585; P < 0.05) and F* (− 2.628; P < 0.05), together with Fu’s Fs (− 2.474), were significantly negative. Tajima’s D was also significantly negative (− 2.089; P < 0.05). Strobeck’s S statistic was 0.977, and Harpending’s raggedness index was 0.108 (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study provides the first molecular report of Wolbachia infection in Cimex hemipterus populations from Iran, along with an assessment of host genetic diversity using the mitochondrial COI gene. All specimens were morphologically identified as C. hemipterus using standard taxonomic keys, and this identification was strongly supported by phylogenetic analysis of the COI gene24. The Iranian sequences clustered closely with C. hemipterus reference sequences from South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Africa, confirming species identity and indicating low genetic divergence from globally distributed lineages. Molecular screening revealed a Wolbachia infection prevalence of 20% (40/200 specimens). Infection rates showed a significant sex bias, with higher prevalence in females (28%) than in males (12%) (χ² = 5.4, df = 1, p = 0.02). This pattern is consistent with the maternal inheritance mode of Wolbachia25. Geographically, positive samples were restricted to two of the four sampled districts (Enghelab Square and Jannat Abad), highlighting microgeographic heterogeneity in infection distribution. The overall infection rate observed here is notably lower than the near-fixation levels frequently reported in C. lectularius populations26. Such differences may reflect regional variation in symbiont dynamics, local ecological conditions, or differences in host–symbiont compatibility27. Phylogenetic analysis of the 16 S rRNA gene placed the detected Wolbachia strains within supergroup T, clustering with other sequences obtained from cimicid bed bugs. These strains showed > 99% nucleotide similarity to previously reported Wolbachia sequences from C. hemipterus10. The placement within supergroup T supports the host-specific association previously documented for bed bug-associated Wolbachia lineages28. Haplotype analysis of the mitochondrial COI gene (631 bp) from 27 specimens identified six haplotypes with relatively low genetic diversity (Hd = 0.342, π = 0.004). All neutrality tests yielded significantly negative values (Tajima’s D = − 2.089, Fu and Li’s D* = −2.585, Fu and Li’s F* = −2.628, Fu’s Fs = − 2.474; all P < 0.05), and the mismatch distribution was unimodal with a low Harpending’s raggedness index (R² = 0.108). These findings are consistent with recent population expansion and/or purifying selection29. The excess of low-frequency polymorphisms and negative neutrality statistics may reflect recent demographic expansion, purifying selection, or a combination of both. Therefore, demographic interpretations based on mitochondrial COI should be treated cautiously, particularly because only one mitochondrial marker and a limited number of sequences were analyzed. The observed low mitochondrial diversity and close phylogenetic affinity with distant geographic populations suggest recent demographic expansion, potentially facilitated by human-mediated dispersal30.

Interpretation of genetic diversity patterns

The interpretation of genetic diversity and demographic history in this study is based on analytical frameworks largely derived from the Neutral Theory of molecular evolution. However, the extent to which Neutral Theory fully explains patterns of genetic diversity in natural populations remains an area of active investigation. A growing body of empirical evidence suggests that some assumptions of the neutral framework may not fully account for observed molecular variation in biological systems31–33. In addition, recent studies have shown that molecular patterns previously considered neutral may also arise from adaptive processes, including selection and adaptive tracking under antagonistic pleiotropy34. Furthermore, emerging evidence indicates that genomic regions such as mitochondrial DNA and short tandem repeats may have functional roles, challenging their traditional interpretation as neutral markers35,36. Despite these developments, Neutral Theory remains a widely used and practical framework in population genetics and phylogenetics. Therefore, the conclusions drawn in the present study regarding genetic diversity and demographic history should be considered as model-dependent and interpreted with appropriate caution. Accordingly, the interpretations presented in this study should be regarded as model-dependent and provisional, as the Neutral Theory framework underlying these analyses has been increasingly challenged by empirical evidence highlighting the role of selection, functional constraint, and adaptive processes in shaping genetic variation.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, sampling was limited to four urban districts in Tehran and may not represent the full genetic diversity or Wolbachia infection patterns across Iran or the broader Middle East. Second, Wolbachia detection and supergroup assignment were based exclusively on the 16 S rRNA gene. Although this marker is useful for initial detection and broad phylogenetic placement, it has limited discriminatory power for strain-level characterization. More variable markers such as the wsp gene or multilocus sequence typing (MLST) would provide higher resolution for strain diversity and population structure of Wolbachia. Third, only two representative sequences per marker were deposited in GenBank and used for phylogenetic reconstruction, while haplotype analysis included 27 COI sequences; future studies should aim to sequence a larger number of specimens for both host and symbiont. Finally, the interpretation of low mitochondrial diversity and demographic signals relies on assumptions that are currently under debate within evolutionary biology.

Conclusions

In summary, the present study demonstrates that Cimex hemipterus populations in Tehran are genetically homogeneous and phylogenetically affiliated with globally distributed lineages, which may be consistent with recent demographic expansion and possible human-mediated dispersal. Wolbachia infection occurs at moderate frequency and is geographically heterogeneous, with identified strains belonging to the host-associated supergroup T. Compared with previous studies reporting near-fixation of Wolbachia in some bed bug populations, the lower prevalence observed here suggests regional variability in symbiont dynamics. These findings contribute to understanding the evolutionary ecology of bed bugs in the Middle East and highlight the need for expanded, multilocus, and geographically comprehensive investigations. These findings should be interpreted in light of the limited geographic sampling and marker resolution employed in this study. Conclusions regarding population history are framed within current models of genetic diversity, whose assumptions continue to be refined.
