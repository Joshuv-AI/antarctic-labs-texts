Hill anti reflux procedure for gastro esophageal reflux disease after sleeve gastrectomy – a single center experience
Authors: Henrik K Thomassen, Villy Våge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) is the most performed bariatric procedure but is frequently complicated with postoperative gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). While Roux-en-y gastric bypass (LRYGB) is the standard revision surgery for GERD after LSG, some patients continue to require antireflux medication. The Hill antireflux repair (LHR) offers a potential alternative by restoring the gastroesophageal junction anatomy and augmenting the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) pressure without fundoplication.MethodsA retrospective single-center study was conducted between 2019 and 2022, including 10 patients who underwent LHR for GERD symptoms following LSG. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the medium and long-term outcomes of LHR in this patient population.ResultsTen patients underwent LHR. Mean preoperative GERDQ-score was 12, improving to 6 at one year and 8 at three years. The use of proton pump inhibitors decreased from 100% preoperative to 30% at three years postoperative. Median LES pressure increased significantly from 8mmHg preoperatively to 23mmHg after applying Hill-sutures. There were no short-term complications. Two patients required revisional surgery due to recurrent hiatal hernia and dysphagia.ConclusionsLHR appears as feasible surgical option for patients with GERD after LSG. Larger, prospective studies with objective postoperative assessments are needed to validate long-term outcomes and to define its therapeutic role.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03402-w.Keywords: GERD, Sleeve gastrectomy, Revisional surgery, Anti-reflux surgery

Introduction

Laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) is the most performed bariatric procedure worldwide and offers the advantage of preserving intestinal continuity. However, a significant proportion of patients—approximately 10–20%—develop gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) following the procedure. The pathophysiology of GERD post-sleeve gastrectomy is thought to be multifactorial, with contributing factors including decreased lower esophageal sphincter (LES) pressure, inappropriate relaxation of the LES and intrathoracic migration of the gastric remnant [1, 2].

In patients who develop GERD following sleeve gastrectomy, conversion to Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (LRYGB) is widely regarded as an effective therapeutic strategy for the amelioration of reflux-related symptoms [3]. The comparatively lower incidence of GERD following LRYGB is thought to result from a reduction in intragastric pressure, in conjunction with a diminished gastric reservoir and a decreased number of parietal cells [4]. Notwithstanding the general improvement in reflux symptoms post-RYGB, approximately 20–30% of patients persist in requiring pharmacological management with anti-reflux agents [5, 6]. The underlying mechanisms for this continued therapeutic need remain unclear; however, proposed contributing factors include suboptimal weight loss, subsequent weight regain, or the presence of a hiatal hernia [7].

The Hill repair, introduced by Lucas D. Hill in 1967 [8], has emerged as a promising adjunctive surgical approach for treating GERD in patients after sleeve gastrectomy. This procedure restores the normal anatomy of the gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) with inferior fixation and complete reconstruction of the anti-reflux barrier, without relying on fundoplication. Additionally, the operation provides intraoperative manometric control of LES pressure.

Previous studies have demonstrated excellent long-term results when performing LHR as a traditionally anti-reflux procedure, with fewer than 30% of patients requiring daily anti-reflux medication at a median follow-up of 18.5 years postoperatively [9]. Although the literature on this topic remains limited, an ongoing trial evaluating the feasibility of the LHR following LSG has yielded promising preliminary results [10].

The aim of this study was to present the experience with the LHR in patients experiencing GERD symptoms after sleeve gastrectomy.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

All patients underwent surgery performed by a single surgeon between May 21, 2019, and October 29, 2022. The minimum follow-up period was 36 months. All patients exhibited refractory gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) symptoms, despite receiving high doses of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs). Preoperatively and one year postoperatively, all patients were evaluated via upper gastrointestinal endoscopy to assess signs of reflux disease, esophagitis, and Barrett’s esophagus, as well as to measure the presence of any hiatal hernia. The first five patients underwent both preoperative and intraoperative esophageal manometry. We conducted a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected clinical data from all patients who underwent LHR after LSG at our institution. Data were entered into a standardized database at the time of surgery and follow-up, ensuring accuracy and completeness for retrospective evaluation. Informed consent for surgery was obtained from each patient prior to the procedure.

Operating method

The procedure was performed laparoscopically under general anaesthesia without the use of neuromuscular blocking agents. The intrathoracic part of the gastric remnant (i.e. the hiatal hernia) was mobilized and brought into the abdomen. It was observed that as soon as the LES came below the diaphragm the pressure in the LES rised to lower normal. Four Hill sutures were then applied between the GEJ and the preaortic fascia to restore the GE-valve (video, supplementary material) [8]. Manometry was performed prior to anesthesia and intraoperative during and after tightening the Hill sutures to obtain a pressure of 20–25 mmHg in the LES. Manometry was performed using Gastro-esophageal pressure monitor / computer-based solar MMS v9.5. Normal LES resting pressure is 10-45mmHg.

All patients were evaluated using the Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease Questionnaire (GERDQ), a validated symptom-based tool for assessing GERD [11]. The GERDQ was administered preoperatively, and subsequently at one, two and three years postoperatively. This allowed for a longitudinal assessment of reflux-related symptoms over time, facilitating the evaluation of symptom progression or resolution following surgical intervention.GERD was defined as a GERDQ-score > 8.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0.0.1. Patient characteristics are presented as medians (range) (age, body mass index, ASA, time since LSG, LES length, LES pressure, length of stay, operating time and GERDQ-score), mean ± standard deviation (SD), or number (percentage), as appropriate. No statistical comparisons were perfomed due to the small sample size.

Results

Ten patients were operated after previous LSG for obesity. Demographic data are presented in Table 1. Median interval between LSG and LHR was 7,4 years (range 2,1–11,7 years). All patients (10/10) used PPI preoperatively and had a mean GERDQ score of 12. After 1 and 3 years, the mean GERDQ score was 6 and 8 respectively (Table 2). Three patients continued PPI postoperatively, two of which was reoperated.

Two patients underwent reoperation. One patient was converted to LRYGB after three years due to persistent GERD symptoms, while the other patient underwent a gastrectomy with roux-en-y oesophagojejunostomy after four years due to a combination of recurrent hiatal hernia and dysphagia.

Discussion

GERD is a known complication after LSG. In the current case series of LHR after LSG, three patients required continued proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy following surgery. This outcome contrasts with findings from cohorts undergoing LRYGB, wherein 30.4% of patients reported persistent reflux symptoms, and 18.2% of those without a prior diagnosis of GERD developed de novo symptoms postoperatively [12].

While LRYGB is widely considered the surgical intervention of choice for GERD following LSG, its long-term efficacy remains variable, with a substantial proportion of patients continuing to require pharmacological therapy. Dupree et al. reported that up to 40% of patients remained dependent on PPI one year postoperatively [13]. Moreover, LRYGB is associated with a notable incidence of chronic abdominal pain, internal herniation, cholelithiasis and gastroenteric ulceration [14–16]. In contrast, LHR has demonstrated promising outcomes in terms of symptom resolution, reduced reliance on acid-suppressive medication, and durable long-term control. Aye et al. reported significant improvements in both symptom burden and GERDQ scores, alongside high patient satisfaction following LHR [17]. These findings suggest that LHR may be a valuable alternative in selected patients, offering effective reflux control with lower reliance on medical therapy. In the present cohort, two patients required revisional surgery due to refractory GERD; however, no 30-day morbidity were observed.

LSG with fundoplication (LSGFD) has been proposed as an alternative to LRYGB for patients with obesity and GERD, aiming to preserve the benefits of sleeve gastrectomy while improving reflux control by increasing the LES pressure. Olmi et al. reported excellent reflux control following LSG + modified Rossetti fundoplication, with most patients achieving complete symptom resolution and discontinuing proton-pump inhibitors [18]. LSGFD appears to offer superior reflux outcomes relative to LSG alone but remains limited by higher complication rates and less pronounced weight loss compared with LRYGB [19, 20].The competence of the LES depends on both an adequate intra-abdominal segment length and a sufficient resting pressure to prevent GERD. A shortened, hypotensive and intrathoracic LES compromises this barrier and predisposes to reflux [21]. In our cohort, the five patients who underwent intraoperative manometry demonstrated a significant postoperative increase in both intra-abdominal esophageal length and LES pressure, supporting the effectiveness of the intervention. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that both parameters are critical for reflux control. Improvements in LES function have been associated with decreased esophageal acid exposure, while restoration of intraabdominal esophageal length is thought to enhance the anti-reflux barrier by optimizing the pressure gradient across the GEJ [22, 23].Mechanical factors represent the most frequent cause of recurrent GERD following LHR, typically due to either disruption of the hiatal closure or loosening of the Hill sutures. In our series, both patients who required reoperation were found to have recurrence of the hiatal hernia, suggestive of suture loosening. Aye et al. reported suture loosening in 2 of 56 patients who had undergone LHR as a primary antireflux procedure. A short intra-abdominal segment of the esophagus and inadequate crural closure are significant predisposing factors for this complication [9, 17].

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces the potential for selection bias and limits the ability to establish causal relationships. Second, the small sample size of ten patients restricts statistical power and reduces the generalisability of the findings. Moreover, the absence of long-term objective follow-up data, such as pH monitoring or endoscopic evaluation, further constrains the strength of the conclusions. Larger, prospective studies are required to confirm these preliminary results and to more precisely define the role of LHR in patients with GERD following LSG.

Conclusion

LHR is technically feasible and can be performed safely in patients with GERD after LSG. However, persistent symptoms and the lack of objective postoperative assessments in this study preclude conclusions about its effectiveness in controlling GERD. Controlled studies with long-term follow-up and objective evaluations are needed to define its therapeutic role.
