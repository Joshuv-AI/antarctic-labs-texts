A ferritin-based mosaic-like nanovaccine elicits effective cross-protection against H1N1 and H3N2 swine influenza viruses
Authors: Lebin Han, Dan Wang, Jinjin Zhang, Xiangkun Wang, Yingying Du, Qin Yang, Guanggang Qu, Yihong Xiao, Qiyun Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Swine influenza viruses (SIVs), particularly the H1N1 and H3N2 subtypes, pose ongoing threats to the swine industry and global public health. Current commercial vaccines generally provide limited cross-protection against heterologous viruses due to the frequent antigenic drift and shift in SIVs, highlighting the need for broad-spectrum vaccines. Self-assembling nanoparticles (NPs) can elicit strong and broad immune responses, representing a promising platform for the development of broad-spectrum nanovaccines. In this study, two newly-screened conserved epitopes (H1-3 and H1-5) and (H3-1 and H3-3) in hemagglutinin (HA) of the H1 and H3 subtypes, along with a highly conservative matrix protein 2 ectodomain (M2e), were displayed on ferritin NP in cocktail and mosaic forms, respectively, and were designated MHF-cocktail (MHFc) and MHF-mosaic (MHFm) nanovaccines. Under a prime–boost immunization strategy in a mouse model, the MHFm nanovaccine induced a higher antigen-specific IgG mediated antibody dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) than the MHFc nanovaccine, and significantly enhanced antigen-specific multifunctional CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell responses as well as cytotoxic T-cell activity. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot proliferation assays indicated that splenic lymphocytes generated following vaccination with the MHFm nanovaccine exhibited enhanced proliferative capacity and secreted higher levels of interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) compared with those from the MHFc nanovaccine. In mice, MHFm nanovaccine provided complete cross-protection against lethal H1N1 and H3N2 SIV challenge, while MHFc nanovaccine offered partial cross-protection. This study provides a potential strategy for designing and developing cross-protective vaccines against H1N1 and H3N2 SIVs.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04603-y.

Introduction

Influenza A viruses (IAVs) pose a significant threat to global public health due to their persistent genetic variability and rapid evolutionary capacity, thereby posing a persistent pandemic risk [1]. Among IAVs, swine influenza viruses (SIVs), particularly the H1N1 and H3N2 subtypes, not only inflict substantial economic losses on the swine industry but also exhibit considerable zoonotic potential, capable of crossing species barriers to infect humans [2]. Notably, Eurasian avian-like H1N1 virus has established stable transmission in pigs and has repeatedly crossed species barrier to infect humans, occasionally resulting in fatal cases, which underscores its potential as an emerging zoonotic pathogen [3–8]. SIVs also continue to evolve in pigs through genetic reassortment, further elevating the risk of triggering the next influenza pandemic [9–12]. The antigenic diversity and continuous evolution of SIVs highlight the urgent need for the development of broad-spectrum vaccines capable of inducing immunity against multiple subtypes and addressing the limitations imposed by antigenic variation.

At present, the developed vaccines for SIVs mainly elicit specific antibody responses against the variable domains of hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) [13], resulting in a mismatch between vaccine strains and circulating strains and limiting their protective efficacy [14]. Therefore, it is important to develop vaccines targeting conserved regions of SIVs. Epitope-based vaccines focus immune responses on conserved epitopes and can induce broad cross-protection [15, 16]. This strategy has been actively explored in vaccine development against influenza, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), and other diseases [17–20].

The inherently weak immunogenicity of peptide antigens poses a key challenge in epitope-based vaccine design. Ferritin nanoparticle (NP) platforms offer a promising approach to overcome this limitation [21]. Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) ferritin self-assembles into a highly symmetric octahedral structure, enabling the high-density, repetitive, and orderly display of multiple antigenic epitopes on its surface [22]. This configuration mimics the natural antigen presentation on virions, thereby enhancing the interaction with B-cell receptors and improving immunogenicity, in addition to promoting germinal center responses and the development of memory B cells [23]. Compared with monomeric antigens, several ferritin-displayed viral antigens (such as those for IAV, Epstein–Barr virus, human immunodeficiency virus, and SARS-CoV-2) have been reported to elicit more robust immune responses [24–27]. These features make ferritin a promising platform for the development of next-generation vaccines.

In the present study, we developed ferritin NP-based conserved multi-epitope chimeric nanovaccines against SIVs. The results demonstrated that the MHFm nanovaccine elicited high levels of cross-reactive antibodies and triggered broad T-cell-mediated immune response in immunized mice. Consequently, the MHFm nanovaccine provided effective cross-protection against heterologous H1N1 and H3N2 SIVs challenge in a mouse model. These findings offer new insights for the development of cross-protective SI vaccines and a feasible strategy for designing broad-spectrum vaccines against SIVs.

Materials and methods

Cells, peptides, viruses, mice

Madin–Darby canine kidney (MDCK, ATCC, USA) cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA). Raji cells (ATCC) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI, Gibco) medium containing 10% heat-inactivated FBS.

Peptides (purity > 95%) were synthesized by Dg peptides Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China) and stored at − 80 °C. The A/Swine/Shandong/TA27/2021(H1N1, A/SW/SD/TA27/21) strain was preserved in our laboratory. H1N1 (a/Puerto Rico/8/34) was used as a backbone to rescue a strain of rA/PR/8-TX98 (H3N2). The challenge dose of A/SW/SD/TA27/21 (H1N1) and rA/PR/8-TX98 (H3N2) was equivalent to 4 × LD₅₀ and 8 × LD₅₀, respectively. The two viruses were propagated after inoculation into 10-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) chicken embryos and stored at − 80 °C for use.

Six-week-old female SPF BALB/c mice were purchased from SPF Biotech Co., Ltd (Beijing, China).

Screening and analysis of epitopes

To screen conserved peptide antigens, we obtained peptide sequences from the Global Initiative on Sharing All Influenza Data (GISAID, https://gisaid.org/), downloading 7220 complete HA sequences from H1N1 strains and 3266 complete HA sequences from H3N2 SIV strains (from 1966 to 2023). We then used MEGAX64 to perform sequence comparisons. The Immune Epitope Database & Tools (IEDB, http://tools.iedb.org/main/) and NetMHCIIpan-4.1 EL (http://tools.iedb.org/mhcii/) were used for analysis. Potential epitopes with ranks below 10 and peptide interaction ratios of at least 90% with major histocompatibility complex (MHC) were screened.

Design of nanovaccines and plasmid construction

The screened epitopes from H1N1 and H3N2 were fused to the N-terminus of H. pylori ferritin (residues 5–167, GenBank: NP_223316) with a GGGGSGGGGSGGGGS [(G4S)3] linker to construct single-epitope NPs. To avoid disulfide bond formation, site-directed mutagenesis of cysteine residues at positions 17 and 19 of H1-M2e and H3-M2e to serine was employed as previously described [28]. Subsequently, H1-M2e (MSLLTEVETPTRSEWESRSSGSSD) was linked to the dominant epitope of H1 subtype by GSG linker, and fused to the N terminus of ferritin by (G4S)3 linker, designated M1F. H3-M2e (MSLLTEVETPTRSEWESRSSDSND) was linked to the dominant epitope of H3 subtype by GSG linker, and fused to the N terminus of ferritin by (G4S)3 linker, designated M3F. M1F and M3F were then mixed at a molar ratio of 1:1, and the mixture was referred to as MHF-cocktail (MHFc). H1-M2e, H3-M2e and all the dominant epitopes are linked through the GSG linker and fused to the N terminus of ferritin by (G4S)3 linker and designated MHF, which was termed MHF-mosaic (MHFm). The combination of non-ferritin epitopes was named MH-monomer. The target gene was synthesized commercially by Sangon Biotechnology with codon optimization for Escherichia coli (E. coli) and was then cloned into pCold II plasmid (Sangon Biotech, China).

Expression and purification of proteins and characterization of nanoparticles

The target plasmid was expressed in E. coli strain pGT-F2 (Takara, China). Protein expression was induced with 0.6 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG; Takara, China) at 16 °C for 18–20 h, when bacterial cultures reached an OD600 of 0.6–0.8. Bacteria were lysed by sonication in Tris-buffered saline (TBS, 20 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, pH 8.0), and the supernatant was loaded onto a nickel–nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) column (GE Healthcare, USA) for purification. Further purification was performed by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare). Purified proteins were treated with Triton X-114 (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) to remove endotoxin [29] and then quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA, Beyotime, China) assay. M1F, M3F, and MHF NPs were prepared using the identical protocol.

The morphology of the NPs was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, H-7650, Hitachi, Japan). The purified proteins were diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL, adsorbed onto a carbon-coated copper grid, and negatively stained with 10 µL 2% phosphotungstic acid (pH 7.0) for 2 min. After air-drying, the structure of the NPs was observed at an acceleration voltage of 80 kV. Particle size distribution was analyzed using a Nano ZS90 Zetasizer (Malvern, Worcestershire, UK).

Animal immunization and virus challenge

To assess the immunogenicity of the single-epitope NPs, 6-week-old female BALB/c mice were randomly divided into 12 groups: ferritin (abbreviated as F), H1-1-F, H1-2-F, H1-3-F, H1-4-F, H1-5-F, H3-1-F, H3-2-F, H3-3-F, H3-4-F, H3-5-F and PBS; PBS served as the negative control. Mice in the experimental groups were subcutaneously immunized three times at 2-week intervals with the following doses: 9.36 µg F, 11 µg H1-1-F, 11.44 µg H1-2-F, 11.7 µg H1-3-F, 12.22 µg H1-4-F, 12.48 µg H1-5-F, 10.92 µg H3-1-F, 11.70 µg H3-2-F, 11.96 µg H3-3-F, 12.22 µg H3-4-F, 12.48 µg H3-5-F (all at F molar equivalent), or 200 µL PBS.

To evaluate the protective effect of multi-epitope NPs, 6-week-old female BALB/c mice were randomly divided into five groups and immunized two times at 2-week intervals as follows: 20 µg MHFm; MHFc (15.79 µg M1F plus 15.26 µg M3F, both at F molar equivalent); 9.50 µg MH-monomer (at MH molar equivalent); 9.36 µg F (at F molar equivalent); or 200 µL PBS. PBS and ferritin were used as negative controls.

Two weeks after the final immunization, mice were anesthetized using tribromoethanol. For viral challenge, mice immunized with H1 subtype single-epitope NPs were intranasally inoculated with 50 µL of 4 × LD₅₀ H1N1 virus, and those immunized with H3 subtype single-epitope NPs were intranasally inoculated with 50 µL of 8 × LD₅₀ H3N2 virus. Immunized mice were intranasally challenged with 50 µL of 4 × LD₅₀ H1N1 or 8 × LD₅₀ H3N2 virus, respectively. The virus-infected mice were monitored daily for weight changes and survival for 14 days. Mice showing more than 25% body weight loss relative to their initial weight were humanely euthanized.

Virus titration and pathological analysis

Virus titration and histopathological examination were conducted as previously described [30]. Briefly, three lung tissue samples were randomly collected from each group on day 5 post-challenge for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Additionally, on days 3 and 5 post-challenge, the viral load in the lungs of mice was determined by the 50% egg infectious dose (EID₅₀) assay using specific pathogen-free (SPF) chicken embryos.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and antibody isotyping assay

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) and antibody isotyping assay were performed as previously described [31]. For this, 96-well plates were coated with synthetic peptides (1 µg/mL) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Plates were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at 37 °C. Mouse sera were serially 2-fold diluted and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After washing, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG, MedChemExpress, USA) was added and incubated for 30 min. The reaction was developed using 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine‌ (TMB) substrate (Solarbio, China), and stopped with 2 M H2SO4. Absorbance was measured at OD450 using an ELx800 microplate reader (Bio-Tek, USA).

Antibody isotyping assay was performed using the same protocol as the indirect ELISA described above. Rabbit anti-mouse IgG1 and IgG2a (Sino Biological, China) were used as secondary antibodies, followed by an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody for detection.

Antigen accessibility and epitope density analysis by an indirect ELISA

96-well plates were coated with equal amounts (1 µg/mL) of purified ferritin, MHFm or MHFc protein at 4 °C overnight. After blocking with 3% BSA, the plates were incubated with three detection reagents: commercial anti-influenza A virus M2 antibody (Abcam, USA); swine H1N1-specific polyclonal serum (prepared in our laboratory); swine H3N2-specific polyclonal serum (prepared in our laboratory). Sera were serially 2-fold diluted starting from 1:1000. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were added. The reaction was developed using TMB substrate, stopped with 2 M H2SO4, and OD450 values were measured.

Microneutralization assay

Immune sera were pretreated with receptor-destroying enzyme II (RDE II; Denka Seiken Co., Ltd. Japan) at 37 °C overnight and then heated at 56 °C for 45 min to inactivate the nonspecific inhibitory factors. Sera were serially 2-fold diluted and mixed with 100 × 50% tissue culture infectious doses (TCID50) of H1N1 or H3N2 virus, respectively. The mixture was incubated for 1 h at 37 °C and then was added to MDCK cells in 96-well plates (100 µL/well) supplemented 2 µg/mL L-(tosylamido-2-phenyl) ethyl chloromethyl ketone (TPCK-trypsin). Cells were incubated for a further 3 days at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Neutralization titers were determined by standard hemagglutination assay.

Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity assay

Raji cells were infected with H1N1/H3N2 (100 TCID50) virus overnight. Virus-infected target cells were labeled with 1 µM carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE, Invitrogen) for 10 min. Labeled target cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated with a 1:1000 diluted heat-inactivated serum (56 °C for 45 min). Positive (1% Triton X-100) and negative (RPMI-1640 complete medium) controls were included. After incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 1 h, splenic lymphocytes from naïve BALB/c mice (effector cells) were added. Following a 4-h incubation, the cell mixture was harvested and stained with 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD; BioLegend, USA). Samples were analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). The ADCC % was calculated as: [(Experimental death% − Negative control%) / (Triton X-100 death% − Negative control%)] × 100 [31].

Passive serum transfer assay

To verify the protective function of vaccine-induced antibodies, a passive serum transfer assay was performed in naive BALB/c mice. Blood samples were collected from mice immunized with MHFm, MHFc, or PBS at 14 days after the final immunization. Sera were isolated and stored at − 80 °C until further used. Subsequently, 6-week-old naive female BALB/c mice were randomly assigned to 5 groups: Group 1: MHFm immune serum; Group 2: MHFc immune serum; Group 3: MHFm immune serum + anti-FcγR blocking antibody; Group 4: MHFc immune serum + anti-FcγR blocking antibody; Group 5: PBS (negative control). All sera were heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 45 min before administration. Mice in groups 1 and 2 were intravenously injected with 200 µL of the corresponding immune serum via the tail vein. Mice in groups 3 and 4 were intravenously injected with 200 µL of the corresponding immune serum, followed by 150 µg of In Vivo Plus anti-mouse CD16/CD32 blocking antibody (Clone: 2.4G2, BioXCell, USA) via the same route. At 24 h after serum transfer, mice were anesthetized and intranasally challenged with 50 µL of viral inoculum containing 4 × LD₅₀ of H1N1 virus or 8 × LD₅₀ of H3N2 virus, respectively. Body weight changes and survival rates were monitored daily for 14 consecutive days. Mice were humanely euthanized if their body weight loss exceeded 25% of the initial body weight. Protective efficacy was evaluated by comparing the survival rates and body weight recovery profiles among groups.

Isolation of splenocytes and detection of T-cell

The splenic lymphocytes of immunized mice were obtained using a Mouse Spleen Lymphocyte Isolation Kit (Solarbio, China). After blocking Fc receptors with an anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody, the lymphocytes (1 × 10⁶ cells/well) were stained with anti-mouse CD3-FITC (BioLegend), anti-mouse CD4-PE (BioLegend), and anti-mouse CD8-APC (BioLegend) antibodies for 30 min on ice. The cells were then washed with cell staining buffer (BioLegend). After two washes, the lymphocytes were stained with 7-AAD and analyzed via flow cytometry using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1) [32].

Intracellular cytokine staining

Intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) assay was performed as previously described [33]. For flow cytometric analysis, 1 × 10⁶ viable splenocytes were randomly sampled from single-cell suspensions. The frequencies of T-cell subsets and cytokine-secreting lymphocytes were quantified by flow cytometry. The absolute number of cells in each spleen can be calculated by multiplying the total number of viable cells per spleen by the frequency of the corresponding subset using a cell counter. For in vitro restimulation, 1 × 10⁶ splenocytes per well were incubated with 10 µg/mL mixed conserved epitope peptides along with Brefeldin A (Sigma, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 6 h. After antigen stimulation, cells were harvested and first stained with Fixable Viability Stain 510 (FVS510; BD Biosciences, USA). Following Fc receptor blockade with anti-mouse CD16/CD32 antibody, cells were incubated with surface marker antibodies including anti-mouse CD3ε-eFluor™ 450, anti-mouse CD4-APC-eFluor 780, and anti-mouse CD8-FITC (BioLegend) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Subsequently, cells were fixed and permeabilized using a Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences, USA). Intracellular cytokine staining was then conducted with anti-mouse IFN-γ-Alexa Fluor 647, anti-mouse TNF-α-PE, and anti-mouse IL-2-PerCP-Cy5.5 (BioLegend) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. All samples were analyzed on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer, and data were processed using FlowJo v10.8.1 software.

lymphocyte proliferation assay

Splenic lymphocyte suspensions were added to a 96-well cell culture plate at 50 µL/well. Each group was divided into three groups: a concanavalin A (ConA, Solarbio) stimulation group (5 µg/ml), a peptide stimulation group (supplemented with the corresponding peptide at 5 µg/ml), and an unstimulated group. Cells were cultured at 37 °C for 72 h. Subsequently, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, China) reagent was added, and plates were incubated for a further 4 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo, USA). The stimulation index was calculated using the following formula: mean OD450 of antigen-treated wells − mean OD450 of blank / mean OD450 of unstimulated control wells. For the multi-epitope nanoparticle groups, equal mass of mixed peptide (5 µg/ml) was added to each group as described above.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot assay

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot assay (ELISpot) was performed as described previously with a slight modification [20]. Briefly, antigen-specific splenocytes isolated from immunized BALB/c mice were detected using mouse interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and interleukin-4 (IL-4) ELISpot kits (DAKEWE, China). Splenic lymphocytes (3 × 105 cells per well) were added to the pre-coated and activated ELISpot plate and stimulated with corresponding peptides (10 µg/mL) for 24 h. Plates were washed with cold deionized water and then incubated sequentially with biotinylated antibody, HRP-conjugated streptavidin, and 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC) colorimetric substrate solution. The reaction was terminated by washing plates with double-distilled water, and spots were counted using an ImmunoSpot analyzer (Cellular Technology Ltd., USA). For the multi-epitope NP groups, an equal mass of mixed peptide (concentration of 10 µg/ml) was added to each group as described above.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software 8.0 (GraphPad, CA, USA) and presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent experiments. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to assess statistically significant differences among groups. Survival curves were compared using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Statistical significance was defined and expressed as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Design and characterization of epitope nanovaccines

A total of 7220 H1N1 and 3266 H3N2 SIV HA complete sequences, covering the period from 1966 to 2023, were retrieved from GISAID. Through systematic screening, 10 potential conserved dominant epitopes were identified, which were designated as H1-1, H1-2, H1-3, H1-4, H1-5 (for H1N1 subtype), H3-1, H3-2, H3-3, H3-4, H3-5 (for H3N2 subtype), respectively (Fig. 1A). Each of these 10 epitopes was fused to the N-terminus (residues 5–167) of ferritin via the flexible (G4S)3 linker and designed as H1-1-ferritin (H1-1-F), H1-2-F, H1-3-F, H1-4-F, H1-5-F (for H1N1 subtype) and H3-1-F, H3-2-F, H3-3-F, H3-4-F, H3-5-F (for H3N2 subtype), respectively (Fig. 1B). All fusion proteins were expressed in soluble form in E. coli and purified by affinity chromatography followed by size-exclusion chromatography, with the final purity reaching over 95% (Fig. 1C). Each recombinant NP formed a high-molecular-weight polymer (Fig. S1). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements showed that the hydrodynamic diameter of the epitope-displaying NPs was 14.88 ± 2.32 nm, which was larger than that of ferritin alone (approximately 9.88 nm) (Fig. 1D). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation further confirmed that the NP monomers self-assembled into spherical cage-like structures, consistent with the assembly pattern of native ferritin, indicating that fusing epitopes to the N-terminus of ferritin did not interfere with its self-assembly ability (Fig. 1E). Collectively, these results demonstrated that the constructed epitope-displaying NPs had stable structure and met the experimental requirements for subsequent immunological detection.

Screening and evaluation of effective peptides

To evaluate the immunogenicity of single-epitope ferritin NP, we performed the immunization protocol described above. Sera and splenic lymphocytes were isolated at 2 weeks after the third immunization to analyze humoral and cellular immune responses (Fig. 2A). Serological assays detected high titer specific antibodies specifically against the corresponding H1 and H3 epitopes in all immunized mice (Fig. 2B). No detectable neutralizing activity against H1N1 and H3N2 viruses was observed in the microneutralization assays of the immune sera (Fig. 2C). The proportions of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in splenic lymphocytes were significantly increased in H1-3-F-, H1-5-F-, H3-1-F-, and H3-3-F than in the PBS and ferritin control groups (Fig. 2D and S2). Upon stimulation with the corresponding synthetic peptides or ConA, splenic cells from the H1-3-F, H1-5-F, H3-1-F, and H3-3-F groups exhibited significantly increased proliferation (Fig. 2E and S3). In addition, the secretion levels of IL-4 and IFN-γ in the spleens of mice immunized with H1-3-F-, H1-5-F-, H3-1-F-, and H3-3-F were significantly higher than those in the control group, indicating that the selected epitopes could induce cellular immune responses (Fig. 2F and S4). Viral challenge experiments showed that all mice immunized with H1-5-F, H3-1-F, and H3-3-F NPs survived the viral challenge, while the survival rate of the H1-3-F group was 80%. In contrast, all mice in the other single-epitope NP-immunized groups died within 9 days post-SIV challenge (Fig. 2G-H). These results demonstrated that the four key peptide epitopes (H1-3, H1-5, H3-1, and H3-3) provided partial protection against homologous SIV challenge in the mouse model, providing a basis for the development of multi-epitope broad-spectrum vaccines against SIVs.

Preparation of multi-epitope nanovaccines

To improve immunogenicity, multi-epitope NPs incorporating H1 M2e, H3 M2e, and the four selected conserved epitopes were constructed in cocktail and mosaic forms, and designated MHF-cocktail (MHFc) and MHF-mosaic (MHFm) nanovaccines, respectively (Fig. 3A). After expression in E. coli, M1F, M3F, and MHF were purified to purity of > 95% (Fig. 3B). The differences in structural and antigenic properties between MHFm NPs and MHFc NPs were detected using an indirect ELISA. The results demonstrated that, as detected by commercial anti-influenza A virus M2 antibody, H1-specific polyclonal serum, and H3-specific polyclonal serum, MHFm NPs exhibited significantly higher binding efficiency compared to MHFc NPs (Fig. 3C). The average hydrodynamic diameters of ferritin, M1F, M3F and MHF NPs were approximately 9.88, 19.95 and 19.51, and 23.36 nm, respectively, indicating that the display of multiple epitopes increased the overall size of ferritin-based NP (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, all multi-epitope NPs self-assembled into uniform spherical structures morphologically analogous to native ferritin cages, demonstrating that fusion of exogenous epitopes did not interfere with NP assembly (Fig. 3E). Together, these data show that the engineered chimeric NPs possess stable structural properties and represent suitable vaccine candidates for subsequent evaluation of immunogenicity and protective efficacy.

MHFm nanovaccine induces strong humoral immune responses in mice

A prime–boost immunization strategy was used to enhance the immune response to multi-epitope NPs. Sera isolated 2 weeks after the final immunization were used to analyze humoral immune responses (Fig. 4A). Both MHFc and MHFm nanovaccines induced specific antibodies against the H1-M2e, H3-M2e, H1-3, H1-5, H3-1, and H3-3 peptides (Fig. 4B), indicating that the designed epitopes were successfully displayed and possessed immunogenicity, which triggered multi-target immune activation. However, despite the humoral immunity induced by both vaccine candidates, neutralizing antibody levels in mouse sera remained below the detection threshold of the microneutralization assay (Fig. 4C). Upon further evaluation of non-neutralizing antibody functions, immune sera from the MHFc and MHFm nanovaccine-immunized groups induced significantly higher ADCC activity against H1N1 and H3N2 viruses compared with the ferritin control group. Importantly, the ADCC activity in the MHFm nanovaccine group was significantly higher than that in the MHFc nanovaccine group (Fig. 4D). Passive transfer of heat-inactivated immune sera showed that MHFm-derived sera provided significantly better protection against H1N1 and H3N2 SIVs challenge than MHFc-derived sera. In vivo blockade of FcγR (CD16/CD32) markedly reduced the protective effect of both immune sera, suggesting that the antibody-mediated protection observed in this study was FcγR-dependent. These results indicate that vaccine-induced antibodies contribute to cross-protection against H1N1 and H3N2 viruses in the mouse model through Fc-mediated effector functions (Fig. 4E-G). Antibody isotype analysis revealed that both vaccines induced relatively balanced IgG1/IgG2a ratios, indicating a lack of strong Th2 polarization in the humoral response (Fig. 4H-I). Overall, these results showed that MHFm nanovaccines exhibited greater efficacy in inducing functional antibodies than MHFc nanovaccine, which may be attributed to its antigen presentation strategy.

MHFm nanovaccine induces robust T-cell immune responses in mice

T cell-mediated immunity plays an important role in defense against influenza virus infection. To evaluate the cellular immune responses induced by different designed nanovaccines, we compared antigen-specific T cell responses elicited by MHFm and MHFc nanovaccines. The proportion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were significantly increased in mice immunized with MHFm NPs compared with those immunized with MHFc NPs (Fig. S6). ICS was utilized to further characterize the functional phenotype of T cell responses. Both MHFm and MHFc nanovaccines significantly increased the frequency and absolute number of CD4⁺ T cells producing IFN-γ, IL-2 and TNF-α relative to the control group. Compared with MHFc, MHFm induced a higher proportion of multifunctional CD4⁺ T cells co-expressing IFN-γ, IL-2 and TNF-α, which was critical for the sustained antiviral immunity in mice (Fig. 5A, C). Cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses, critical for IAV clearance, were evaluated by detecting cytokine secretion in CD3⁺CD8⁺ T cells. The results showed that the MHFm nanovaccine significantly increased the number and proportion of CD3+CD8+ T cells co-expressing IFN-γ, IL-2 and TNF-α (Fig. 5B, D). These findings indicate that MHFm nanovaccines exhibited an advantage in inducing more robust and comprehensive antigen-specific T-cell immunity compared with MHFc nanovaccines. Consistently, lymphocytes from MHFm nanovaccine-immunized mice exhibited enhanced proliferative capacity upon stimulation with cognate peptides or ConA (Fig. 5E-F). Furthermore, the MHFm nanovaccine induced significant increases in IL-4- and IFN-γ-secreting lymphocytes, demonstrating activation of both Th1 and Th2 cellular responses (Fig. 5G-H). In general, these findings indicate that the MHFm nanovaccine activates more effective and comprehensive antigen-specific T-cell immunity than the MHFc nanovaccine.

MHFm nanovaccine protects mice against lethal virus challenge

To assess the cross-protective efficacy of the MHFc and MHFm nanovaccines, mice were intranasally challenged with H1N1 and H3N2 SIVs at 2 weeks post-boost and monitored for 14 days. The MHFm nanovaccine provided more effective cross-protection, with immunized mice showing only transient weight loss (≈ 10%) and achieving 100% survival against both viral strains. Although the MHFc nanovaccine conferred 100% survival against H1N1 virus and 80% survival against H3N2 virus, mice immunized with MHFc experienced more pronounced weight loss (approximately 15–20%). In contrast, mice in the control groups (PBS, ferritin, or MH-monomer) exhibited progressive weight loss and 100% mortality within 6–8 days post-challenge (Fig. 6A-B). Virological analysis revealed that both nanovaccines significantly suppressed pulmonary viral replication in lung tissue on days 3 and 5 post-infection compared with control groups. The MHFm nanovaccine achieved a marked reduction in viral loads approaching clearance by day 5, with significantly lower lung viral loads compared with the MHFc nanovaccine (Fig. 6C). Histopathological evaluation further showed that the MHFm nanovaccine maintained well-preserved pulmonary architecture with minimal structural alterations in lung tissue and minimal pathological changes (such as bronchial epithelial detachment, alveolar wall thickening, and massive inflammatory infiltrates) compared with controls groups (Fig. 6D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the MHFm nanovaccine provides rapid cross-protective immunity against divergent SIVs in the mouse model, while effectively suppressing viral replication and preserving lung tissue integrity.

Discussion

The challenge in developing cross-protective influenza vaccines lies in effectively targeting conserved viral regions [13]. Unlike conventional vaccines that target the variable head domain of HA, epitope-based vaccines focus immune responses on conserved antigenic epitopes to counteract antigenic drift and shift [15]. In this study, we prioritized longer peptides (13–25 amino acids) during bioinformatic screening of H1N1 and H3N2 HA sequences, as such peptides can accommodate multiple overlapping epitopes. This design may reduce MHC haplotype restriction and broaden the T-cell immune response, which supports the development of multi-epitope nanovaccine [34, 35]. This strategy provides the possibility for the assembly of multi-epitope nanovaccines.

The protective efficacy of traditional influenza vaccines is often directly correlated with the level of neutralizing antibodies they produce [36]. For instance, Corbett et al. reported an NP vaccine that effectively induced high titers of neutralizing antibodies, thereby stimulating a protective humoral immune response [37]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that non-neutralizing antibody functions, particularly ADCC, play a central role in antiviral immunity [38]. Studies have shown that non-neutralizing antibodies targeting conserved regions of influenza virus, such as M2e or the HA stem, although unable to prevent viral entry, can activate natural killer cells via Fc receptors to clear virus-infected cells and limit viral spread [39, 40]. In the present study, although the MHFm nanovaccine did not induce detectable neutralizing antibodies, FcγR blockade and passive serum transfer experiments confirmed that ADCC constituted a key protective mechanism mediated by the MHFm nanovaccine, and this response was significantly stronger than that induced by the MHFc nanovaccine. This result suggests that the high-density, ordered display of the multi-epitope on MHFm may more effectively activate Fc-mediated immune effector functions, thereby providing complete protection in mice even in the absence of neutralizing antibodies [41]. Collectively, our findings further support the independent contribution of non-neutralizing antibody effector functions to influenza cross-protection and offer an immunological basis for the design of next-generation influenza vaccines.

Antigen assembly strategies represent a key approach to enhancing immunogenicity and broadening the immune coverage of traditional vaccines [19]. Unlike the cocktail strategy, the mosaic assembly strategy displays multiple antigenic epitopes on the surface of a single NP. This multi-epitope configuration can promote more efficient uptake and processing by antigen-presenting cells, thereby coordinating the induction of broader humoral and cellular immune responses [31, 42]. Zhao et al. reported that NPs based on full-length HA could elicit broader immune responses [43]. In contrast, the ferritin NP vaccine developed by Tang et al., which was based on the HA ectodomain, showed protective efficacy in a pig model, yet its design still relied on the full protein structure [44]. In this study, we constructed the MHFm nanovaccine by fusing multiple conserved epitopes from H1 and H3 subtypes with M2e, followed by ordered assembly on the ferritin surface. The indirect ELISA assay confirmed that MHFm exhibited significantly higher effective epitope density and antigen accessibility than the MHFc nanovaccine. This design mimics the multivalent antigen presentation of native virions and may enhance T-cell recognition and activation through spatial synergy among the epitopes [45]. Our experimental data demonstrated that the MHFm nanovaccine was superior to the MHFc nanovaccine in inducing ADCC activity, multifunctional T-cell immunity and cytotoxic T-cell activity, and cytokine secretion in mice, highlighting the advantage of the mosaic assembly strategy for coordinating multi-target immune responses. Notably, the balanced IgG1/IgG2a profile induced by MHFm indicated no dominant Th2 polarization, which aligns with the Th1-skewed multifunctional T-cell responses observed. This balanced humoral profile, combined with the cellular immune activation, contributes to the cross-protective efficacy of the mosaic nanovaccine in mice. Importantly, the ferritin alone group showed no immune enhancement relative to PBS, confirming that the immune responses elicited by these two nanovaccines were attributed to the ordered epitope display on ferritin nanoparticles, rather than an adjuvant-like effect of the ferritin carrier itself. This study provides a novel assembly paradigm for the development of next-generation conserved epitope-based SI vaccines.

In summary, we developed an MHFm nanovaccine against SIVs by combining conserved epitope screening with the ferritin NP display platform. In the mouse model, this MHFm nanovaccine integrated non-neutralizing antibody functions (e.g., ADCC) and T-cell mediated immune responses, and conferred effective cross-protection against heterologous H1N1 and H3N2 SIVs challenge. In the future, we will further evaluate the protective efficacy of the MHFm nanovaccine in pigs, investigate its performance in combination with clinically compatible adjuvants, and characterize its long-term immune persistence and mucosal immunization potential.

Conclusion

In this study, we constructed an MHFm ferritin-based nanoparticle vaccine against SIVs. Conserved epitopes from H1N1 and H3N2 SIVs were identified via bioinformatic screening, fused with the M2e conserved domain, and displayed in a chimeric format on the ferritin nanoparticle surface. In the mouse model, this nanovaccine elicited potent ADCC responses and broad T-cell immune responses, thereby conferring complete cross-protection against lethal H1N1 and H3N2 SIV challenge. This study provides a novel design strategy and preclinical experimental evidence for developing broad‑spectrum swine influenza vaccines.
