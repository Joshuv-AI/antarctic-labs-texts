Ab initio calculations of monopole sum rules: From finite nuclei to infinite nuclear matter
F. Bonaiti §, A. Porro §, S. Bacca, A. Schwenk, A. Tichai

Abstract
We compute moments of the isoscalar monopole response of N = Z N=Z closed-shell nuclei based on chiral nucleon–nucleon plus three-nucleon interactions. We employ the random phase approximation (RPA) and two ab initio many-body approaches, the in-medium similarity renormalization group (IMSRG) and coupled-cluster theory (CC). In the IMSRG framework, the moments are obtained as ground-state expectation values, whereas in the CC approach, they are evaluated through excited-state calculations. We find good agreement between the IMSRG and CC results across all nuclei studied. RPA provides a reasonable approximation to the correlated methods if the interaction is soft. From the calculated moments, we extract average energies of the monopole response, compute finite-nucleus incompressibilities, and estimate the incompressibility of symmetric nuclear matter by a fit to a leptodermous expansion. Our extrapolated values are lower than those obtained in nuclear matter calculations with the same interactions, but the values are consistent with phenomenological ranges.

I
Introduction

The investigation of giant resonances offers deep insights into the structure and dynamical properties of atomic nuclei
Goeke and Speth 1982
;
Harakeh and Woude 2001
;
Garg and Colò 2018
;
Colò 2022
. In particular, isoscalar giant monopole resonances (ISGMR, or GMR for short) represent a signature of collective nuclear vibrations while also establishing a direct connection between atomic nuclei and the nuclear equation of state (EOS). Moments of the monopole strength function can be related to compression properties of finite nuclei
Blaizot 1980
;
Roca-Maza and Paar 2018
;
Garg and Colò 2018
, and, through a leptodermous expansion, to the incompressibility of infinite nuclear matter at saturation density, a key EOS property. Through the incompressibility, these resonances thus provide constraints for matter under extreme conditions in neutron stars, core-collapse supernovae, and neutron star mergers
Lattimer and Prakash 2000
;
Janka et al. 2007
;
Yasin et al. 2020
;
Lattimer 2021
;
Janka and Bauswein 2023
;
Jacobi et al. 2023
;
Ricigliano et al. 2024
. For example, larger values of the incompressibility typically lead to stiffer EOSs.

A microscopic understanding of the response of nuclei to external probes, and of the GMR in particular, has been a longstanding challenge in nuclear structure studies.
While historically this was studied predominantly using phenomenological energy density functionals (EDFs)
Ring and Schuck 1980
;
Bortignon et al. 1998
;
Harakeh and Woude 2001
;
Bender et al. 2003
;
Niksic et al. 2011
;
Nakatsukasa et al. 2016
;
Garg and Colò 2018
;
Schunck 2019
;
Colò 2022
,
ab initio
advances allow to target such observables from first principles using nuclear interactions (and currents) systematically derived from chiral effective field theory
Epelbaum et al. 2009
;
Machleidt and Entem 2011
;
Hammer et al. 2020
;
Krebs 2020
.
Combined with systematically improvable many-body expansions this allows to assess theoretical uncertainties. Among these advances for nuclear responses are the Lorentz integral transform technique combined with the coupled-cluster framework (LIT-CC)
Bacca et al. 2013
;
Bacca et al. 2014
;
Miorelli et al. 2016
;
Acharya et al. 2025
;
Bonaiti et al. 2024
;
Marino et al. 2025
, the self-consistent Green’s function approach
Raimondi and Barbieri 2019a
;
Raimondi and Barbieri 2019b
, and the in-medium similarity renormalization group (IMSRG)
Parzuchowski et al. 2017a
;
Parzuchowski et al. 2017b
;
Porro et al. 2025
. More recently, response functions have also been addressed within a time-dependent coupled-cluster framework
Bonaiti et al.
.

Efforts to implement the random-phase approximation (RPA) using the same chiral interactions employed in
ab initio
methods have been carried out within symmetry-conserving schemes
Papakonstantinou et al. 2017
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Hu et al. 2020
, and later extended to deformed nuclei through the symmetry-breaking quasiparticle RPA
Beaujeault-Taudière et al. 2023
;
Zaragoza et al. 2024
, highlighting the influence of static correlations. In addition, RPA and related methods constructed on correlated ground states have stressed the need for a consistent treatment of correlations in both ground and excited configurations
Carter et al. 2022
;
Beaujeault-Taudière et al. 2023
. Lastly, the projected generator coordinate method (PGCM) and its perturbatively corrected extension (PGCM-PT) have been formulated to describe rotational and vibrational collective degrees of freedom within a unified framework
Frosini et al. 2022a
;
Frosini et al. 2022b
;
Frosini et al. 2022c
;
Porro et al. 2024a
;
Porro et al. 2024b
;
Porro et al. 2024c
;
Porro et al. 2024d
.

In this work, we present LIT-CC and IMSRG calculations of the monopole strength function and its moments. On the one hand, within the LIT-CC method, the response function is explicitly constructed from a set of excited states obtained from an equation-of-motion (EOM) approach
Hagen et al. 2014
. On the other hand, a newly developed approach in the IMSRG framework allows to evaluate selected moments of the strength function as ground-state expectation values
Porro et al. 2025
.

In the first part of the paper, we focus on moments of the monopole response and compare the results of the two approaches. Both the CC and IMSRG frameworks have been shown to accurately predict ground- and excited-state properties of nuclei at comparable accuracy, see, e.g.,
Parzuchowski et al. 2017b
;
Miyagi et al. 2020
;
Heinz et al. 2025
. In this work we demonstrate that this consistency extends to collective excitations as well. IMSRG and CC results are also compared to RPA calculations.

In the second part of the paper, we analyze the connection between properties of the monopole response and the incompressibility of nuclear matter. In particular, we calculate the finite-nucleus incompressibility, and use a leptodermous expansion to extract the nuclear matter value. The extrapolated values are lower than those obtained in nuclear matter calculations with the same interactions, but the values are consistent with phenomenological ranges.

This paper is organized as follows. In Secs.
II
and
III
, the many-body frameworks used to calculate the nuclear response and its moments are introduced. Results are presented for selected closed-shell nuclei in Sec.
IV
.
In Sec.
V
, the extrapolated results for the incompressibility are compared to nuclear matter calculations.
Finally, we summarize our results and give an outlook in Sec.
VI
. Further numerical benchmarks are given in the Appendix.

II
The nuclear response

II.1
Strength functions and their moments

In the following the nuclear Hamiltonian

H=T-T_{\text{CM}}+V_{\text{NN}}+V_{\text{3N}}\,,

(1)

is employed, where
T
is the total kinetic energy,
T_{\text{CM}}
the center-of-mass (CM) kinetic energy,
V_{\text{NN}}
denotes nucleon-nucleon (NN) and
V_{\text{3N}}
three-nucleon (3N) interactions. The set of the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian
H
is denoted by
\{\ket{\Psi_{\nu}}\}
, each of which satisfies the time-independent Schrödinger equation

H\ket{\Psi_{\nu}}=E_{\nu}\ket{\Psi_{\nu}}\,.

(2)

Given a Hermitian operator
Q
, the associated strength function (or response function) reads as

S(Q,E)\equiv\sum_{\nu}|\langle\Psi_{\nu}|Q|\Psi_{0}\rangle|^{2}\delta(E_{\nu}-E_{0}-E)\,,

(3)

which fully characterizes the linear response of the nucleus to an external perturbation
Q
.

The moments of the response function are defined as energy-weighted averages of the transition amplitudes entering Eq. (
3
) according to

\displaystyle m_{k}(Q)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}E^{k}S(Q,E)\,dE\,,

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu}(E_{\nu}-E_{0})^{k}|\langle\Psi_{\nu}|Q|\Psi_{0}\rangle|^{2}\,.

(4)

If
Q
has a non-vanishing expectation value in the ground state, the elastic contribution to the total strength is subtracted by replacing
Q
with
Q-\braket{\Psi_{0}|Q|\Psi_{0}}
.
Different moments may be used to compactly characterize the response of the system. In particular, several average energies can be introduced as

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{k}(Q)

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\frac{m_{k}(Q)}{m_{k-2}(Q)}}\,,

(5a)

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{k}(Q)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{m_{k}(Q)}{m_{k-1}(Q)}\,.

(5b)

These average energies obey a series of inequalities

\ldots\leq\tilde{E}_{k}(Q)\leq\bar{E}_{k}(Q)\leq\tilde{E}_{k+1}(Q)\leq\bar{E}_{k+1}(Q)\leq\ldots\,.

(6)

If the entire strength were concentrated in a single peak, the equality sign in Eq. (
6
) would hold. The spread of the different averages in Eqs. (
5
) is a quantitative measure of the fragmentation of the strength
S(Q,E)
.
Note that the evaluation of Eq. (
II.1
) formally requires the knowledge of the entire spectrum of
H
, i.e., one needs access to all the bound and continuum excited states of the system
\ket{\Psi_{\nu}}
. In this work, two strategies that are theoretically equivalent, though potentially different in practical implementation, are employed to calculate the moments, as explained below.

II.2
Moments as ground-state expectation values

Equation (
II.1
) can be rewritten in terms of the expectation value on the ground state
\ket{\Psi_{0}}
,

m_{k}(Q)=\braket{\Psi_{0}|M_{k}(Q)|\Psi_{0}}\,,

(7)

where the so-called moment operator is

M_{k}(Q)\equiv Q^{\dagger}[\underbrace{{H},...[{H},[{H}}_{\text{$k$ times}},{Q}]]...]\,.

(8)

The latter is defined through a
k
-fold nested commutator involving the full nuclear Hamiltonian
Bohigas et al. 1979
.
Here, we used the relation

(H-E_{0})^{k}\ket{\Psi_{0}}=0\,,

(9)

together with the completeness relation

\sum_{\nu}\ket{\Psi_{\nu}}\bra{\Psi_{\nu}}=1\,.

(10)

By further exploiting the Hermiticity of the operator, odd moments can be written as

M_{k}(Q)=\frac{1}{2}[Q,[\underbrace{{H},...[{H},[{H}}_{\text{$k$ times}},{Q}]]...]]]\,.

(11)

The explicit expression for the lowest-
k
moments are given by

\displaystyle M_{0}(Q)

\displaystyle\equiv Q^{2}\,,

(12a)

\displaystyle M_{1}(Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[Q,[H,Q]]\,.

(12b)

The former discussion is, in principle, valid for any integer value of
k
, either positive or negative. However, negative values of
k
require the definition of an inverse operation for the commutator, which in turn requires the knowledge of the exact spectrum of
H
. However, negative moments can also be evaluated in terms of polarizabilities, which can be easily shown employing first-order perturbation theory (dielectric theorem)
Bohigas et al. 1979
;
Capelli et al. 2009
. Eventually, the following formulas are found

\displaystyle m_{-1}(Q)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\Big|_{\lambda=0}\,,

(13a)

\displaystyle m_{-1}(Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda^{2}}\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}\Big|_{\lambda=0}\,,

(13b)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\equiv\braket{\Psi(\lambda)|Q|\Psi(\lambda)}\,,

(14a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\equiv\braket{\Psi(\lambda)|H|\Psi(\lambda)}\,,

(14b)

and
\ket{\Psi(\lambda)}
is the solution to the perturbed Schrödinger equation

(H+\lambda\,Q)\ket{\Psi(\lambda)}\equiv H_{\lambda}\ket{\Psi(\lambda)}=E(\lambda)\ket{\Psi(\lambda)}\,,

(15)

with
\lambda
the perturbation parameter. As displayed by Eqs. (
13
), a perturbation of order
\lambda
produces first-order changes in the expectation value of the perturbation operator
Q
itself, while second-order changes
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})
are obtained in the energy. Hence, in the evaluation of derivatives entering Eqs. (
13
), Eq. (
13a
) is considered to be numerically more robust.

II.3
Moments from integral transforms

To avoid the explicit calculation of bound and continuum excited states of the Hamiltonian, one can choose to adopt the LIT method that is based on an integral transform of the strength function with Lorentzian kernel
Efros et al. 2007
,

L(\sigma,\Gamma)=\frac{\Gamma}{\pi}\int dE\;\frac{S(Q,E)}{(E-\sigma)^{2}+\Gamma^{2}}\,,

(16)

where
\sigma
is the centroid and
\Gamma
is the width of the kernel. By inserting Eq. (
3
) into Eq. (
16
) and using the completeness relation from Eq. (
10
) one obtains

\begin{split}L(\sigma,\Gamma)=&\frac{\Gamma}{\pi}\bra{\Psi_{0}}Q\frac{1}{H-E_{0}-\sigma+i\Gamma}\\
&\times\frac{1}{H-E_{0}-\sigma-i\Gamma}Q\ket{\Psi_{0}}\,.\end{split}

(17)

Defining the complex variable
z\equiv E_{0}+\sigma+i\Gamma
, the integral transform can then be computed as

L(z)=\frac{\Gamma}{\pi}\braket{\tilde{\Psi}|\tilde{\Psi}}\,,

(18)

where
\ket{\tilde{\Psi}}
represents the solution to the equation

(H-z)\ket{\tilde{\Psi}}=Q\ket{\Psi_{0}}

(19)

for different values of
\sigma
and
\Gamma
. In this way, the task of computing the full spectrum of the Hamiltonian is reduced to solving a Schrödinger-like equation (
19
), for which bound-state approaches can be applied. Ultimately, the moments of the strength function are evaluated in the limit
\Gamma\to 0
, where the Lorentzian kernel approaches a Dirac
\delta
-distribution. In this limit, the LIT reduces to the strength function

L(\sigma,\Gamma\rightarrow 0)=\int dE\;S(Q,E)\delta(E-\sigma)=S(Q,\sigma)\,.

(20)

Such a strength function is discretized, in the sense that the information related to the excited states in the continuum is represented by bound pseudo-states, and it allows for the evaluation of moments via

m_{k}(Q)=\int d\sigma\;\sigma^{k}L(\sigma,\Gamma\rightarrow 0)\,.

(21)

III
Many-body frameworks

III.1
Moment operators from the IMSRG

In the IMSRG framework
Tsukiyama et al. 2011
;
Hergert et al. 2016
the initial Hamiltonian is evolved via a continuous unitary transformation
U(s)
parametrized by a flow parameter
s
.
This aims for the decoupling of
n
-particle-
n
-hole (
n
p-
n
h) excitations from a many-body reference state,

H(s)=U(s)H(0)U^{\dagger}(s)\,.

(22)

The transformation can be reformulated as an ordinary differential equation involving the evaluation of a commutator between two many-body operators,

\frac{dH(s)}{ds}=[\eta(s),H(s)]\,,

(23)

where
\eta(s)
is the (anti-Hermitian) generator of the transformation

\eta(s)\equiv\frac{d\,U(s)}{ds}U^{\dagger}(s)=-\eta^{\dagger}(s)\,.

(24)

In this work the generator is chosen as the imaginary-time generator, which is widely used in applications to closed-shell nuclei.

The initial many-body reference state
\ket{\Phi_{0}}
is taken to be a symmetry-conserving Hartree-Fock (HF) Slater determinant. All operators are then normal ordered with respect to
\ket{\Phi_{0}}
, such that the zero-body component of the Hamiltonian after normal ordering is given by its expectation value
\braket{\Phi_{0}|H(0)|\Phi_{0}}
. Through the solution of the IMSRG flow the coupling of
\ket{\Phi_{0}}
to
n
p-
n
h excitations is suppressed, and the zero-body term approaches the exact ground-state energy in the large
s
limit when the IMSRG equations are not truncated:

\lim_{s\to\infty}\braket{\Phi_{0}|H(s)|\Phi_{0}}=E_{0}\,.

(25)

In this work, the Magnus formulation of the unitary transformation is used
Morris et al. 2015
, where the latter is directly expressed by an exponential ansatz

U(s)=e^{\Omega(s)}\,.

(26)

This has the advantage of manifestly enforcing unitarity even in presence of truncations. All commutators are truncated at the normal-ordered two-body level giving rise to the IMSRG(2) truncation. While three-body operators can be included at a substantial computational cost, the IMSRG(2) approximation has been shown to yield reliable results for ground-state properties of medium-mass nuclei
Heinz et al. 2025
.

With this, the transformed Hamiltonian, as well as any other operator
O
, can be constructed as

\displaystyle H(s)

\displaystyle=e^{\Omega(s)}He^{-\Omega(s)}\,,

(27a)

\displaystyle O(s)

\displaystyle=e^{\Omega(s)}Oe^{-\Omega(s)}\,.

(27b)

This also extends to the moment operators defined in Eqs. (
12
), such that the moments expectation value at the HF and IMSRG level are given by

\displaystyle m_{k}(Q_{\lambda},0)

\displaystyle=\braket{\Phi_{0}|M_{k}(Q_{\lambda})|\Phi_{0}}\,,

(28a)

\displaystyle m_{k}(Q_{\lambda},s)

\displaystyle=\braket{\Phi_{0}|e^{\Omega(s)}M_{k}(Q_{\lambda})e^{-\Omega(s)}|\Phi_{0}}\,,

(28b)

respectively. Thus, differences in the value of
m_{k}
before (HF) and after (IMSRG) the flow reflect the impact of ground-state correlations on the nuclear response.

III.2
Integral transforms in coupled-cluster theory

In CC theory
Hagen et al. 2014
;
Shavitt and Bartlett 2009
one starts from an exponential ansatz for the many-body ground state,

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{0}}=e^{T}\ket{\Phi_{0}}\,.

(29)

Correlations are introduced through

e^{T}
, where the cluster operator
T
is expanded in a truncated
n
p-
n
h operator basis.
In the singles and doubles level (CCSD) approximation, the cluster operator consists of

T=\sum_{ai}t^{a}_{i}a_{a}^{\dagger}a_{i}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{abij}t^{ab}_{ij}a_{a}^{\dagger}a_{b}^{\dagger}a_{j}a_{i}\,,

(30)

where
a^{\dagger}
and
a
are creation and annihilation operators, while the indices
i,j
and
a,b
denote hole and particle states, respectively.
After normal ordering the Hamiltonian with respect to the reference state, one introduces the similarity-transformed Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\overline{H}\equiv e^{-{T}}He^{{T}}\,,

(31)

and computes the amplitudes
t^{a}_{i},t^{ab}_{ij}
by solving a set of non-linear amplitude equations

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{i}^{a}|\overline{H}|\Phi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{ij}^{ab}|\overline{H}|\Phi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=0\,,

(32)

which decouple particle-hole excitations from the reference state
|\Phi_{0}\rangle
.
Additional higher-body correlations can be approximately included to achieve greater precision, using, e.g., the CCSDT-1 approach
Watts et al. 1993
, which accounts for the leading effects of three-particle–three-hole excitations in the nuclear ground state.

In the CC framework, excited states can be described through EOM techniques
Shavitt and Bartlett 2009
;
Hagen et al. 2014
.
Since similarity transformed operators in CC theory are non-Hermitian
Shavitt and Bartlett 2009
, both the right and left ground states are required.
Assuming that the general eigenstates
\ket{\Psi_{\nu}}
of the Hamiltonian can be constructed by acting linearly on the CC ground state with an excitation operator, the right and left eigenstates can be written as

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_{\nu}}={R}_{\nu}\ket{\Psi_{0}}={R}_{\nu}e^{{T}}\ket{\Phi_{0}}\,,

(33a)

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi_{\nu}}=\bra{\Psi_{0}}{L}_{\nu}=\bra{\Phi_{0}}{L}_{\nu}e^{-{T}}\,.

(33b)

Truncating
{R}_{\nu}
and
{L}_{\nu}
at the 2p-2h level yields the following operators

\displaystyle{R}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=r_{0}+\sum_{ai}r^{a}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{a}a_{i}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{abij}r^{ab}_{ij}a^{\dagger}_{a}a^{\dagger}_{b}a_{j}a_{i}\,,

(34a)

\displaystyle{L}_{\nu}

\displaystyle=l_{0}+\sum_{ai}l^{i}_{a}a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{a}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{abij}l_{ab}^{ij}a^{\dagger}_{i}a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{b}a_{a}\,.

(34b)

In the special case of the nuclear ground state
\nu=0
, the amplitudes reduce to
{R}_{0}=1
and
{L}_{0}=1+{\Lambda}
.
Using the EOM ansatz [Eqs. (
33
)], we can finally rewrite the strength function in the CC formalism as

\begin{split}S(Q,E)&=\sum_{\nu}\langle\Phi_{0}|L_{0}\overline{Q}^{\dagger}R_{\nu}|\Phi_{0}\rangle\langle\Phi_{0}|L_{\nu}\overline{Q}|\Phi_{0}\rangle\\
&\quad\times\delta(E_{\nu}-E_{0}-E)\,,\end{split}

(35)

where the similarity-transformed transition operator

\overline{Q}=e^{-{T}}Qe^{{T}}

(36)

was introduced. Starting from Eq. (
35
), one can evaluate moments by coupling CC theory with the LIT technique of Sec.
II.3
in the so-called LIT-CC method
Bacca et al. 2013
;
Bacca et al. 2014
. In this approach, Eq. (
19
) becomes an EOM-CC problem with a source term.

IV
Applications to finite nuclei

In this work, we focus on the isoscalar monopole (IS0) response, whose excitation operator reads

\displaystyle Q(\text{IS}0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{A}r_{i}^{2}\,,

(37)

where
r_{i}
is the radial component of the position of the
i
-th particle in the laboratory frame.

IV.1
Computational setup

In this work, we study the nuclear response for two different chiral NN and 3N interactions: the NNLO
{}_{\text{sat}}
interaction from Ref.
Ekström et al. 2015
and the
\Delta
NNLO
{}_{\text{GO}}
(394) interaction from Ref.
Jiang et al. 2020
. All operators are expanded in spherical harmonic-oscillator (HO) basis states consisting of at most 15 major shells, i.e.,
e_{\text{max}}=\text{max}(2n+l)=14
. Three-nucleon interactions are included using the normal-ordered two-body approximation. This has been shown to introduce only small errors in medium-mass nuclei
Hagen et al. 2007
;
Roth et al. 2012
;
Heinz et al. 2025
.
The number of three-body configurations is additionally restricted to
e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}\leq E_{\text{3,max}}=24
which has been shown to be sufficient to ensure convergence in heavy systems
Miyagi et al. 2022
. The underlying HO frequency is taken in a range
\hbar\omega=12-16
MeV. The NN and 3N matrix elements are computed using the
NuHamil
code
Miyagi 2023
.

In the following, the moments of the isoscalar monopole strength for
k=-1,0,1
are evaluated for
N=Z
doubly closed-shell nuclei
16
O,
40
Ca,
56
Ni, and
100
Sn. The IMSRG results are obtained by employing the ground-state-based technique described in Sec.
II.2
in the IMSRG(2) approximation. CC calculations, instead, exploit the excited-states formulation, as discussed in Sec.
II.3
. In the latter framework, many-body truncations are performed at both the ground- and excited-state level. Unless otherwise specified, in both cases we adopt the CCSD approximation. For comparison, results from RPA calculations are also presented.

IV.2
Isoscalar monopole moments convergence

We start by presenting the results relative to the moments of the IS0 operator for the different many-body methods and Hamiltonians as a function of the HO basis size and frequency. In Fig.
1
the values of
m_{-1}
are displayed. One can easily observe a striking difference between the two interactions. Results obtained with NNLO
{}_{\text{sat}}
display a slower convergence with respect to
e_{\text{max}}
, a stronger dependence on the selected
\hbar\omega
, and on the many-body method employed. RPA results, for instance, do not display a clear sign of convergence. IMSRG and CC results, which quantitatively agree with each other, show a better
e_{\text{max}}
convergence, despite a significant
\hbar\omega
dependence for the heaviest systems considered: relative differences amount to 13% and 19% in CC and IMSRG calculations, respectively. The situation is drastically different for the
\Delta
NNLO
{}_{\text{GO}}
(394) results. IMSRG, CC, and RPA results display relative deviations of only 3%. This shows that correlations encoded in IMSRG and CC results are effectively cancelled for this observable with soft interactions, as suggested in previous works
Beaujeault-Taudière et al. 2023
. In this specific situation, RPA can serve as a sufficiently good first approximation.
Moreover, we observe that the model-space convergence is significantly improved for the soft interaction
\Delta
NNLO
{}_{\text{GO}}
(394), with a relative difference ranging from 1% to 3% in
e_{\text{max}}
and of the order of 5% for
\hbar\omega
variations even in heavier systems.

A similar situation is found when discussing the results for
m_{0}
, which are shown in Fig.
2
. When employing the NNLO
{}_{\text{sat}}
interaction both the many-body and the model-space uncertainty are significantly larger. It is interesting to remark that in this case one also observes a non-negligible difference between the CC and IMSRG results, which increases for heavier nuclei. This difference is of about 20% in
100
Sn (while only of 3% in
16
O). One possible explanation relates to the more difficult convergence of even moments than odd ones, also observed in Refs.
Rowe 2010
;
Porro et al. 2025
. Moreover, we emphasize that in this case
m_{0}
describes the variance of the mean-square radius operator. The latter depends on the quality of the ground state of the system, and as a consequence on the many-body convergence of the solution, which can be strongly affected by the use of a harder interaction such as NNLO
{}_{\text{sat}}
. To further elucidate this, we considered the inclusion of triples correction in the ground state for CC calculations. Due to computational cost, we limited this analysis to
56
Ni with
e_{\text{max}}
= 10 and
\hbar\omega=16

Extensive studies exist linking giant monopole resonances to the incompressibility of symmetric nuclear matter, see Refs.
Blaizot 1980
;
Jennings and Jackson 1980
;
Garg and Colò 2018
;
Roca-Maza and Paar 2018
for reviews. In infinite matter, the incompressibility is defined by the second derivative of the
N=Z
energy per particle
E/A
with respect to the Fermi momentum
k_{\text{F}}

\displaystyle K_{\infty}=k^{2}_{\text{F}}\frac{d^{2}E/A}{dk^{2}_{\text{F}}}\Bigg|_{k_{\text{F}}=k_{\text{F}_{0}}}\,,

(39)

where
k_{\text{F}_{0}}
is the Fermi momentum at the saturation density
n_{0}
, which is defined by the stationarity condition

\frac{dE/A}{dn}\bigg|_{n=n_{0}}=0\,.

(40)

Equation (
39
) provides information about the stiffness of nuclear matter against variations in the density around the stationary point.
We introduce an effective incompressibility in a finite system of
A
nucleons, where
\zeta
is a representative length scale of the size of the system. The Fermi momentum
k_{\text{F}}
scales as the inverse of
\zeta
, i.e.,

k_{\text{F}}\sim\zeta^{-1}\,,

(41)

such that Eq. (
39
) can be modified in a finite system as

K_{A}=\zeta^{2}\frac{d^{2}E/A}{d\zeta^{2}}\bigg|_{n_{0}}\,.

(42)

If the mean square radius
\braket{r^{2}}
is chosen as the typical length scale, then

K_{A}=4\braket{r^{2}}^{2}\frac{d^{2}E/A}{d\braket{r^{2}}^{2}}\bigg|_{n_{0}}\,.

(43)

It can be shown
Roca-Maza and Paar 2018
that Eq. (
43
) eventually leads to the expression for the incompressibility of a finite
A
-body nucleus

\displaystyle K_{A}=\frac{m}{\hbar^{2}}\langle r^{2}\rangle E^{2}_{\mathrm{GMR}}\,,

(44)

where
m
is the mass of the nucleon and
E_{\mathrm{GMR}}
is the GMR energy, defined in Eq. (
38
). The finite nucleus incompressibility
K_{A}
and the nuclear matter incompressibility
K_{\infty}
can be linked through a so-called leptodermous expansion, similar to the liquid-drop formula for nuclear masses,

\displaystyle K_{A}

\displaystyle=K_{\text{vol}}+K_{\mathrm{surf}}A^{-1/3}+K_{\mathrm{Coul}}Z^{2}A^{-4/3}

(45)

\displaystyle\quad+K_{\mathrm{sym}}\left(\frac{N-Z}{A}\right)^{2}\,,

where
K_{\text{vol}},K_{\mathrm{surf}},K_{\mathrm{Coul}},K_{\mathrm{sym}}
are the volume, surface, Coulomb, and symmetry contributions to the incompressibility of a finite
A
-body system, while
N
and
Z
are the neutron and proton numbers, respectively
Blaizot 1980
. For symmetric nuclei (
N=Z
), as the ones considered in this work, the finite-nucleus
K_{A}
can then be expressed as

\displaystyle K_{A}=K_{\text{vol}}+K_{\text{surf}}\,A^{-1/3}\,,

(46)

where the Coulomb contribution has been neglected. Therefore, one can compute
K_{A}
for a variety of nuclei using the above mentioned many-body methods (RPA, CC, IMSRG), and then fit the coefficients in Eq. (
45
), where
K_{\mathrm{vol}}
corresponds to
K_{\infty}
when extrapolating to infinite
A

Jennings and Jackson 1980
. This technique was extensively used to provide estimates of
K_{\infty}
both from mean-field calculations
Blaizot 1980
and experimental GMR data
Stone et al. 2014
. Previous works in the
ab initio
community followed this approach using the SA-NCSM method
Burrows et al. 2025
, obtaining
K_{\infty}=213(10)
MeV with the NNLO
opt
interaction, and the PGCM employing the N
3
LO interaction from Ref.
Hüther et al. 2020
, which gave
K_{\infty}=284(3)
MeV. According to Ref.
Garg and Colò 2018
, this strategy can provide a useful consistency test for finite and infinite matter calculation within a given model, but large uncertainties might affect the reliability of the extrapolated values.

VI
Conclusions and Outlook

In this work we performed a detailed comparison of the LIT-CC and IMSRG approaches applied to the calculation of the nuclear monopole response. The two frameworks yield good agreement for symmetric closed-shell nuclei using different chiral interactions, thus highlighting the consistency of the recently formulated moment-operator method
Porro et al. 2025
compared to the well-established integral transform approach. The extracted moments of the strength function allowed to calculate the finite-nucleus incompressibility, which was linked to the nuclear matter value through a leptodermous expansion. The extrapolations display sizeable differences with explicit nuclear matter calculations with the same interactions, but the values are consistent with phenomenological ranges.

For the future, we emphasize the need to account for Coulomb effects in the leptodermous expansion and to investigate asymmetric (
N\neq Z
) nuclei.
This will allow for the inclusion of more points in the extrapolation, thus increasing the fit’s stability.
Moreover, the inclusion of medium-mass open-shell nuclei will be important to understand the robustness of the many-body frameworks when targeting the nuclear response in presence of strong collective correlations.
