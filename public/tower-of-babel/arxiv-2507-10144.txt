A structural bound for cluster robustness of randomized small-block Lanczos
Nian Shao

Abstract
The Lanczos method is a fast and memory-efficient algorithm for solving large-scale symmetric eigenvalue problems. However, its rapid convergence can deteriorate significantly when computing clustered eigenvalues due to a lack of cluster robustness. A promising strategy to enhance cluster robustness—without substantially compromising convergence speed or memory efficiency—is to use a random small-block initial, where the block size is greater than one but still much smaller than the cluster size. This leads to the Randomized Small-Block Lanczos (RSBL) method. Despite its empirical effectiveness, RSBL lacks the comprehensive theoretical understanding already available for single-vector and large-block variants. In this paper, we develop a structural bound that supports the cluster robustness of RSBL by leveraging tools from matrix polynomials. We identify an intrinsic theoretical challenge stemming from the non-commuting nature of matrix multiplication. To provide further insight, we propose a conjectured probabilistic bound for cluster robustness and validate it through empirical experiments. Finally, we discuss how insights into cluster robustness can enhance our understanding of RSBL for both eigenvalue computation and low-rank approximation.

Introduction

Given a symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and an initial
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times b}
, the
\ell
th block Krylov subspace is generated by successive applications of
A
to
\Omega
:

\mathcal{K}_{\ell}(A,\Omega):=\mathsf{range}[\Omega,A\Omega,\dotsc,A^{\ell-1}\Omega].

With an orthonormal basis computed by block Lanczos process, the block Lanczos method then performs Rayleigh–Ritz extraction
from the resulting block Krylov subspace.
The computed approximations of eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
A
are called as Ritz values and Ritz vectors.

Compared with subspace iteration, a particularly attractive feature of Krylov subspace methods is their ability to extract significantly more Ritz pairs than the block size
b
.
This enables a high-degree polynomial acceleration while requiring only a moderate number of matrix-vector multiplications (matvecs) with
A
. In fact, in most modern scientific computing environments—such as Matlab, Python, and Julia—the default large-scale eigensolvers (e.g.,
eigs
in Matlab) are based on a single-vector (
b=1
) Arnoldi/Lanczos method, usually with implicit restarting
[
14
,
28
]
, even when many eigenvalues are sought.

A known limitation of the single-vector Lanczos is its lack of cluster robustness. Specifically, when computing a cluster of closely spaced eigenvalues, the single-vector Lanczos method suffers from slow convergence due to small spectral gaps.
A particularly extreme and notorious example is null spaces computation
[
11
]
, where single-vector Lanczos—and even small-block variants with very small block sizes—may fail to correctly determine the null space dimension.
Indeed, whenever two eigenvalues of interest are separated by a tiny gap, the convergence of single-vector Lanczos method becomes significantly slower
[
16
,
11
]
.
Unfortunately, in many practical eigenvalue problems, clustered or multiple eigenvalues frequently arise, often as a consequence of symmetries or periodic structures inherent in physical models
[
23
,
2
]
.
In such cases, a single-vector Lanczos method becomes less favorable as the modest gains in convergence speed are insufficient to offset the high communication costs
[
1
]
, particularly in large-scale parallel computing environments.
Yet even on a personal computer, multiplying a
1000\times 1000
(dense) Gaussian random matrix by
16
vectors in Matlab takes only about
3.3\times
the time needed for one matvec.
Often, as noted in
[
18
, Sec. 13.12]
, it is suggested to choose a large block size
b
that is at least as large as the size of the eigenvalue cluster of interest. This strategy, which we refer to as the large-block Lanczos method, resolves the challenges posed by clustered eigenvalues and can be implemented in a communication-avoiding manner
[
9
, Sec. 1.6.2]
.
However, it typically requires significantly more matvecs and substantially more memory than the single-vector Lanczos method, particularly when the eigenvalue cluster is large.

One strategy to find a compromise of the cluster robustness and low communication cost of large-block Lanczos with the fast convergence and memory efficiency of the single-vector variant is to employ a (Randomized) Small-Block Lanczos (RSBL) method, where the block size is greater than one but still significantly smaller than the cluster size. It is clear that RSBL has better communication cost than single-vector Lanczos and lower memory requirement than the large-block variant. The following example shows that, in fact, unless we are in an extreme situation where eigenvalues are of high multiplicity, such as null space computation, RSBL is already robust enough.

Despite the empirical effectiveness of RSBL, there exists surprisingly limited theoretical understanding, particularly regarding their robustness to eigenvalue clusters. Most existing analyses of (randomized) Lanczos methods for eigenvalue computation or low-rank approximation focus on the single-vector case
[

,

,

,

]
or the large-block case
[

,

,

,

,

]
. To the best of the author’s knowledge, the only theoretical result addressing RSBL is
[

. They show that the convergence of RSBL depends on the

However, their analysis indicates that matvec complexity of RSBL scales linearly with the block size

In this paper, we develop a new approach for analyzing the RSBL on eigenvalue problems.
Following the structure of
[
16
,
11
]
,
we quantify the cluster robustness by

\tan\angle(\mathsf{range}(Q),\mathcal{K}_{d}(A,\Omega)),

(1)

where
\angle
is the largest principal angle,

are the desired

and

is the block Krylov subspace with dimension at most

To obtain an upper bound, we adopt tools from matrix polynomials developed in

4
]
and derive an explicit interpolation formula for lambda-matrices on certain structured matrices. This result generalizes classical Lagrange interpolation for scalar polynomials, which has been crucial in the analysis of randomized single-vector Lanczos methods.
Under mild spectral gap assumptions—specifically, the multiplicity of desired eigenvalues does not exceed

Equation

1

Instead, we propose a conjectured probabilistic bound, and support this conjecture with empirical numerical evidence.
Finally, we discuss how our results contribute to a deeper understanding of RSBL for eigenvalue computation and low-rank approximation.

Notation

We use
\lVert\cdot\rVert
to denote the Euclidean norm of a vector and the spectral norm of a matrix. For a family of (non-commuting) matrices
A_{1},\dotsc,A_{d}
, we denote
\prod_{i=1}^{d}A_{i}=A_{1}A_{2}\dotsb A_{d}
.

2
Matrix polynomials and lambda-matrices

To analyze RSBL, we employ techniques involving matrix polynomials and lambda-matrices, as introduced in
[
4
]
. These mathematical tools play an important role in the analysis of systems and control theory
[
25
,
13
]
. In the context of block Krylov subspace methods, they are also used in the analysis of linear system solvers
[
12
,
24
]
and computation of matrix functions
[
7
]
.

Definition 1

(Matrix polynomial and lambda-matrix)
.

Let
C_{0},\dotsc,C_{d},X\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
. Then we define a matrix polynomial of degree at most
d
by
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
as

\Phi(X)=C_{0}+XC_{1}+\dotsb+X^{d}C_{d}.

(2)

When
X=\lambda I
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the matrix polynomial is reduced to a lambda-matrix

\Phi(\lambda):=\Phi(\lambda I)=C_{0}+\lambda C_{1}+\dotsb+\lambda^{d}C_{d}.

(3)

We denote the subspace that contains all such matrix polynomials of degree at most
d
by
\mathbb{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b})
.

In the literature, such as
[
24
,
7
]
, the term matrix polynomial is often used to refer to what we refer to as lambda-matrix. Since both notions appear in our analysis, we adopt the convention in
[
4
]
and using the term matrix polynomial to denote
\Phi
, and reserve lambda-matrix for cases where the variable in the matrix polynomial is a scalar matrix.
We remind readers that in our definition of the matrix polynomial
Equation

2
, the coefficients
C_{i}
appear to the right of the variable
X
, which differs from the convention in
[
4
]
.

2.1
Upper bound on
\lVert\Phi(B_{0})\rVert

The first result we develop is an upper bound of the spectral norm of
\Phi(B_{0})
for diagonalizable
B_{0}
with real eigenvalues.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
B_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
admits an eigenvalue decomposition
B_{0}=\Omega_{0}^{-1}\Lambda_{0}\Omega_{0}
, where
\Lambda_{0}
is a diagonal matrix containing real eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}^{(1)}\leq\dotsb\leq\lambda_{0}^{(b)}
. For any matrix polynomial
\Phi\in\mathbb{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b})
,

\lVert\Phi(B_{0})\rVert\leq\sqrt{b}\lVert\Omega_{0}^{-1}\rVert\lVert\Omega_{0}\rVert\max_{\lambda_{0}^{(1)}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}^{(b)}}\lVert\Phi(\lambda)\rVert.

Proof.

By definition,
\Phi
takes the form
Equation

2
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{b}
, let
\varphi_{j}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}(e_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega_{0}C_{i}x)\lambda^{i}
be a scalar polynomial, where
1\leq j\leq b
. By submultiplicativity,

\displaystyle\lVert\Phi(B_{0})x\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lVert\sum_{i=0}^{d}B_{0}^{i}C_{i}x\Bigr\rVert^{2}=\Bigl\lVert\Omega_{0}^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{d}\Lambda_{0}^{i}\Omega_{0}C_{i}x\Bigr\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert\Omega_{0}^{-1}\rVert^{2}\Bigl\lVert\sum_{i=0}^{d}\Lambda_{0}^{i}\Omega_{0}C_{i}x\Bigr\rVert^{2}=\lVert\Omega_{0}^{-1}\rVert^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{b}\varphi_{j}^{2}(\lambda_{0}^{(j)})

\displaystyle\leq b\lVert\Omega_{0}^{-1}\rVert^{2}\max_{1\leq j\leq b}\varphi_{j}^{2}(\lambda_{0}^{(j)})\leq b\lVert\Omega_{0}^{-1}\rVert^{2}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq b\\
\lambda_{0}^{(1)}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}^{(b)}\end{subarray}}\varphi_{j}^{2}(\lambda)\leq b\lVert\Omega_{0}^{-1}\rVert^{2}\max_{\lambda_{0}^{(1)}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}^{(b)}}\sum_{j=1}^{b}\varphi_{j}^{2}(\lambda).

Note that

\sum_{j=1}^{b}\varphi_{j}^{2}(\lambda)=\Bigl\lVert\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda^{i}\Omega_{0}C_{i}x\Bigr\rVert^{2}=\lVert\Omega_{0}\Phi(\lambda)x\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert\Omega_{0}\rVert^{2}\lVert\Phi(\lambda)\rVert^{2}\lVert x\rVert^{2}.

Combining the submultiplicativity with two inequalities above, we have

\lVert\Phi(B_{0})x\rVert^{2}\leq b\lVert\Omega_{0}^{-1}\rVert^{2}\max_{\lambda_{0}^{(1)}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}^{(b)}}\sum_{j=1}^{b}\varphi_{j}^{2}(\lambda)\leq b\lVert\Omega_{0}^{-1}\rVert^{2}\lVert\Omega_{0}\rVert^{2}\max_{\lambda_{0}^{(1)}\leq\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}^{(b)}}\lVert\Phi(\lambda)\rVert^{2}\lVert x\rVert^{2},

which concludes the lemma.
∎

2.2
Solvents and latent roots

Similar to scalar polynomials, we can also define the generalization of roots as solvents and latent roots
[
4
, Sec. 1]
.

Definition 2

(Solvent and latent root/vector)
.

Given a matrix polynomial
\Phi
of the form
Equation

2
, if
\Phi(B)=0
, then
B
is called a left solvent of
\Phi
. If
\Phi(\lambda)
is singular for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\lambda
is called a latent root of
\Phi
. If
\omega^{\mathsf{T}}\Phi(\lambda)=0
for a nonzero vector
\omega
and a latent root
\lambda
, then
\omega
is called the corresponding left latent vector.

In the rest of this paper, we will omit “left” before left solvent and left latent vector since we only consider matrix polynomials like
Equation

2
.
Let
\Phi\in\mathbb{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b})
and
B_{0}
be a solvent of
\Phi
. The generalized Bézout’s theorem
[
8
, Chap 4.3, Thm. 1]
asserts the existence of a lambda-matrix
\Phi_{B_{0}}\in\mathbb{P}_{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b})
such that

\Phi(\lambda)=(\lambda I-B_{0})\Phi_{B_{0}}(\lambda)\quad\forall\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

The following lemma provides a further connection between latent pairs and solvent.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
B_{0}
admits a spectral decomposition
B_{0}=\Omega_{0}^{-1}\Lambda_{0}\Omega_{0}
with real eigenvalues. Given a matrix polynomial
\Phi\in\mathbb{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b})
, then
B_{0}
is a solvent of
\Phi
if and only
\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\Phi(\lambda_{0})=0
holds for all left eigenpairs
(\lambda_{0},\omega_{0})
of
B_{0}
.

Proof.

If
B_{0}
is a solvent, then the result is directly from the generalized Bézout’s theorem. On the other direction, suppose that
\Phi
takes the form in
Equation

2
, then

\Phi(B_{0})=\Omega_{0}^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{d}\Lambda_{0}^{i}\Omega_{0}C_{i}.

Note that for any left eigenpair
(\lambda_{0},\omega_{0})
of
B_{0}
, it holds that

0=\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\Phi(\lambda_{0})=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda_{0}^{i}\omega_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}C_{i},

where we use
Equation

3
in the second equation. Stacking all left eigenpairs of
B_{0}
together, we know that
\sum_{i=0}^{d}\Lambda_{0}^{i}\Omega_{0}C_{i}=0
, and in turn,
\Phi(B_{0})=0
.
∎

2.3
Interpolation and fundamental matrix polynomials

The next ingredient we need from matrix polynomials is interpolation. Let

\mathsf{Van}:=\begin{bmatrix}I&B_{1}&\cdots&B_{1}^{d-1}\\
I&B_{2}&\cdots&B_{2}^{d-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
I&B_{d}&\cdots&B_{d}^{d-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{bd\times bd}

(4)

be a block Vandermonde matrix, where
B_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
. When
b=1
, that is,
\Phi
is a scalar polynomial and
B_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, a nonsingular (scalar) Vandermonde matrix
\mathsf{Van}
allows us to interpolate
\Phi
with Lagrange polynomials
\Phi_{i}
such that
\Phi_{i}(B_{j})=\delta_{ij}
, where
\delta_{ij}=1
if
i=j
and
0
otherwise. When
b>1
, consider the following generalization.

Definition 3

(Fundamental matrix polynomial)
.

Let
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
, and

\mathsf{B}_{-k}:=(B_{1},\dotsc,B_{k-1},B_{k+1},\dotsc,B_{d})

(5)

be an ordered tuple of
d-1
matrices. Fundamental matrix polynomials
F[B_{k},\mathsf{B}_{-k}]
are a set of matrix polynomials in
\mathbb{P}_{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b})
such that
F[B_{i},\mathsf{B}_{-i}](B_{j})=\delta_{ij}I
for all
1\leq i\leq j\leq d
.

With fundamental matrix polynomials defined above,
[
4
, Thm. 5.2 and Cor. 5.1]
give an analogy result of Lagrange interpolation for lambda-matrices as follows.

Proposition 1
.

Given matrices
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
, if the matrix
\mathsf{Van}
defined in
Equation

4
is nonsingular, then, for
1\leq k\leq d
, there exist unique fundamental matrix polynomials
F[B_{k},\mathsf{B}_{-k}]
. Moreover, for any lambda-matrix
\Phi\in\mathbb{P}_{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b})
, it holds that

\Phi(\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}F[B_{k},\mathsf{B}_{-k}](\lambda)\Phi(B_{k})\quad\forall\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

We should note that, different from the scalar case,
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{d}
having disjoint spectra is not sufficient to guarantee the nonsingularity of
\mathsf{Van}
. For example, let
B_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\\
&2\end{bmatrix}
and
B_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix}
. Then
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
have disjoint spectra, but
\mathsf{Van}\cdot[1,-2,-1,1]^{\mathsf{T}}=0
. However, the following proposition shows that
\mathsf{Van}
is nonsingular generically. In particular, if
[\Omega_{1},\dotsc,\Omega_{d}]
is a Gaussian random matrix, then
\mathsf{Van}
is nonsingular almost surely.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\dotsc,\Omega_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
, and
B_{i}=\Omega_{i}^{-1}\Lambda_{i}\Omega_{i}
for
i=1,\dotsc,d
with real diagonal matrices
\Lambda_{i}
. Suppose that
\Lambda_{i}
and
\Lambda_{j}
have disjoint spectra for
i\neq j
, then
\mathsf{Van}
is nonsingular generically with respect to
\Omega_{1},\dotsc,\Omega_{d}
in Lebesgue measure.

Proof.

Let

f(\Omega_{1},\dotsc,\Omega_{d}):=\det\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{1}&\Lambda_{1}\Omega_{1}&\cdots&\Lambda_{1}^{d-1}\Omega_{1}\\
\Omega_{2}&\Lambda_{2}\Omega_{2}&\cdots&\Lambda_{2}^{d-1}\Omega_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\Omega_{d}&\Lambda_{d}\Omega_{d}&\cdots&\Lambda_{d}^{d-1}\Omega_{d}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

It is clear that
f
is a polynomial of entries of
\Omega_{i}
. Since
\Lambda_{i}
and
\Lambda_{j}
have disjoint spectra for
i\neq j
, we know that
f(I_{b},\dotsc,I_{b})\neq 0
, yielding that
f
is a nonzero polynomial. Note that

\det\mathsf{Van}=\det\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\Omega_{d}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\det\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{1}&\Lambda_{1}\Omega_{1}&\cdots&\Lambda_{1}^{d-1}\Omega_{1}\\
\Omega_{2}&\Lambda_{2}\Omega_{2}&\cdots&\Lambda_{2}^{d-1}\Omega_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\Omega_{d}&\Lambda_{d}\Omega_{d}&\cdots&\Lambda_{d}^{d-1}\Omega_{d}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\det(\Omega_{i})\Bigr)^{-1}f(\Omega_{1},\dotsc,\Omega_{d}).

We know that
\det\mathsf{Van}\neq 0
generically.
∎

2.4
Explicit formula for fundamental matrix polynomials

The fundamental theorem of algebra asserts that a scalar polynomial can be factored into a product of (complex) linear functions. This property, however, does not generally extend to matrix polynomials. Nevertheless, under certain additional assumptions on the solvents, the following lemma shows that a lambda-matrix
\Phi
can indeed be decomposed into a product of some degree-one lambda-matrices.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
admit spectral decompositions

\Lambda_{i}

\Lambda_{i}
and
\Lambda_{j}

where

is defined by the following recurrence:

S_{i,i}:=I_{b},\quad S_{i,j}:=B_{i}S_{i,j-1}-S_{i,j-1}\widehat{B}_{j}\quad\text{for}\quad j=i+1,\dotsc,d.

\displaystyle\omega_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{i,i+1}\prod_{j=i+2}^{d}(\lambda_{i}I-\widehat{B}_{j})=(\omega_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}B_{i}S_{i,i+1}-\omega_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{i,i+1}\widehat{B}_{i+2})\prod_{j=i+3}^{d}(\lambda_{i}I-\widehat{B}_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{i,i+2}\prod_{j=i+3}^{d}(\lambda_{i}I-\widehat{B}_{j})=\dotsb=\omega_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{i,d-1}(\lambda_{i}I-\widehat{B}_{d})=\omega_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{i,d},

implying that
\widehat{\Omega}_{i}=\Omega_{i}S_{i,d}
is nonsingular, and contains latent vectors of
\Phi_{i-1}
corresponding latent roots as diagonal entries of
\Lambda_{i}
.
Then

2
yields that
\widehat{B}_{i}

is a solvent of
\Phi_{i-1}
. In turn, the generalized Bézout’s theorem asserts the existence of a lambda-matrix
\Phi_{i-2}\in\mathbb{P}_{i-2}(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b})
, such that

\Phi_{i-1}(\lambda)=(\lambda I-\widehat{B}_{i})\Phi_{i-2}(\lambda)\quad\forall\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Plugging it in

7
and using mathematical induction, we obtain

\Phi_{d-1}(\lambda)=\Bigl(\prod_{i=2}^{d}(\lambda I-\widehat{B}_{i})\Bigr)\Phi_{0}\quad\forall\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

where
\Phi_{0}
is a coefficient matrix to be determined. Note that
B_{1}
is a solvent of
\Phi_{d-1}-I
, with arguments similar to

8
and
9
, we know
\Omega_{1}S_{1,d}\Phi_{0}-\Omega_{1}=0
, implying that
\Phi_{0}=S_{1,d}^{-1}
.
∎

The matrices
\widehat{B}_{i}
from

are referred to as a chain of solvents

of the lambda-matrix

. This is due to the fact that certain submatrices of

Proposition 3
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\dotsc,\Omega_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
, and

Proof.

The proof follows a similar idea to the proof of

2
.
We first show that if all

are identity matrices, then

This can be verified by an induction for

from
d
to
1

. Suppose that

i+1,\dotsc,d
.
For any

, since

\displaystyle S_{i,k}

\displaystyle=B_{i}S_{i,k-1}-S_{i,k-1}\widehat{B}_{k}=(B_{i}-S_{i,k-1}\widehat{B}_{k}S_{i,k-1}^{-1})S_{i,k-1}=\dotsb

(11)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{k-i}(B_{i}-S_{i,k-j}\widehat{B}_{k-j+1}S_{i,k-j}^{-1})\Bigr)S_{i,i}=\prod_{j=1}^{k-i}(B_{i}-S_{i,k-j}\widehat{B}_{k-j+1}S_{i,k-j}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{k-i}\bigl(\Omega_{i}^{-1}\Lambda_{i}\Omega_{i}-(\widehat{\Omega}_{k-j+1}S_{i,k-j}^{-1})^{-1}\Lambda_{k-j+1}(\widehat{\Omega}_{k-j+1}S_{i,k-j}^{-1})\bigr).

Now taking determinant on both sides of

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{k-i}\det\bigl(\Omega_{i}^{-1}\Lambda_{i}\Omega_{i}-(\widehat{\Omega}_{k-j+1}S_{i,k-j}^{-1})^{-1}\Lambda_{k-j+1}(\widehat{\Omega}_{k-j+1}S_{i,k-j}^{-1})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{k-i}\det\begin{bmatrix}I&I\\
(\widehat{\Omega}_{k-j+1}S_{i,k-j}^{-1})^{-1}\Lambda_{k-j+1}(\widehat{\Omega}_{k-j+1}S_{i,k-j}^{-1})&\Omega_{i}^{-1}\Lambda_{i}\Omega_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

to show that each factor in

is nonzero generically, and in turn,

Theorem 1

(12)

2.5
Growth of fundamental matrix polynomials

Suppose that all eigenvalues of
B_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq d
are contained in
[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]
.
The next step is to control the growth of
\lVert F[B_{k},\mathsf{B}_{-k}](\lambda)\rVert
as
\lambda
moves away from
[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]
.
To this end, we introduce some quantities that allow us to derive bounds analogous to those for the Lebesgue function in the scalar polynomial case.

To avoid cumbersome notation involving the superscript
\cdot^{(k)}
, we first define the relevant quantities for
F[B_{1},\mathsf{B}_{1}]
.
Recall that
\widehat{B}_{i}=\widehat{\Omega}_{i}^{-1}\Lambda_{i}\widehat{\Omega}_{i}
, we let

\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}(B_{1};\mathsf{B}_{-1}):=\max_{2\leq i\leq d}\Bigl\{\frac{\lVert\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})}I-\widehat{B}_{i}\rVert}{\lVert\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})}I-\Lambda_{i}\rVert},\frac{\lVert\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}I-\widehat{B}_{i}\rVert}{\lVert\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}I-\Lambda_{i}\rVert},1\Bigr\}.

For any
\lambda<\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})}
, by
Equation

12
and submultiplicativity,

\displaystyle\lVert F[B_{1};\mathsf{B}_{-1}](\lambda)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\prod_{i=2}^{d}\lVert\lambda I-\widehat{B}_{i}\rVert\Bigr)\lVert S_{1,d}^{-1}\rVert\leq\Bigl(\prod_{i=2}^{d}\bigl(\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})}-\lambda+\lVert\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})}I-\widehat{B}_{i}\rVert\bigr)\Bigr)\lVert S_{1,d}^{-1}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\prod_{i=2}^{d}\bigl(\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})}-\lambda+\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}(B_{1};\mathsf{B}_{-1})\lVert\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})}I-\Lambda_{i}\rVert\bigr)\Bigr)\lVert S_{1,d}^{-1}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\bigl((\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}-\lambda)\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}(B_{1};\mathsf{B}_{-1})\bigr)^{d-1}\lVert S_{1,d}^{-1}\rVert.

Similarly, when
\lambda>\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}
, it holds that

\lVert F[B_{1};\mathsf{B}_{-1}](\lambda)\rVert\leq\bigl((\lambda-\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})})\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}(B_{1};\mathsf{B}_{-1})\bigr)^{d-1}\lVert S_{1,d}^{-1}\rVert.

Now we need to quantify the coefficient term
\lVert S_{1,d}^{-1}\rVert
. Taking
i=1
and
k=d
in
Equation

11
, we have

S_{1,d}=\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\bigl(\Omega_{1}^{-1}\Lambda_{1}\Omega_{1}-(\widehat{\Omega}_{d-i+1}S_{1,d-i}^{-1})^{-1}\Lambda_{d-i+1}(\widehat{\Omega}_{d-i+1}S_{1,d-i}^{-1})\bigr).

Since each factor in the product is the difference between two matrices with eigenvalues
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{d-i+i}
, respectively, a proper scaling-independent quantity for measuring
\lVert S_{1,d}^{-1}\rVert
is

\chi_{\mathsf{coef}}(B_{1};\mathsf{B}_{-1}):=\lVert S_{1,d}^{-1}\rVert^{\frac{1}{d-1}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq i\leq d\\
\lambda_{1}\in\Lambda_{1},\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{i}\end{subarray}}\lvert\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{i}\rvert.

For a generic
1\leq k\leq d
, with notation in
Theorem

1
, we define

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}(B_{k};\mathsf{B}_{-k})

\displaystyle:=\max_{2\leq i\leq d}\Bigl\{\frac{\bigl\lVert\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})}I-\widehat{B}_{i}^{(k)}\bigr\rVert}{\bigl\lVert\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})}I-\Lambda_{i}^{(k)}\bigr\rVert},\frac{\bigl\lVert\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}I-\widehat{B}_{i}^{(k)}\bigr\rVert}{\bigl\lVert\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}I-\Lambda_{i}^{(k)}\bigr\rVert},1\Bigr\},

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathsf{coef}}(B_{k};\mathsf{B}_{-k})

\displaystyle:=\bigl\lVert(S_{1,d}^{(k)})^{-1}\bigr\rVert^{\frac{1}{d-1}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq i\leq d\\
\lambda_{1}^{(k)}\in\Lambda_{1}^{(k)},\lambda_{i}^{(k)}\in\Lambda_{i}^{(k)}\end{subarray}}\lvert\lambda_{1}^{(k)}-\lambda_{i}^{(k)}\rvert,

and their uniform bound as

\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}:=\max_{1\leq k\leq d}\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}(B_{k},\mathsf{B}_{-k})\quad\text{and}\quad\chi_{\mathsf{coef}}:=\max_{1\leq k\leq d}\chi_{\mathsf{coef}}(B_{k},\mathsf{B}_{-k}).

(13)

We remark that in the scalar case (
b=1
), where all scalar products commute, the quantities
\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}
and
\chi_{\mathsf{coef}}
simplify significantly to
\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}=1
and
\chi_{\mathsf{coef}}\leq 1
.

By applying
Theorem

1
to
Proposition

1
, we obtain the main theorem of this section, which provides an explicit representation and a growth estimate for the interpolation of lambda-matrices.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Phi\in\mathbb{P}_{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b})
be a lambda-matrix. Suppose that matrices
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
have disjoint real spectra contained in
[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]
. Assuming that
\mathsf{Van}
defined in
Equation

4
is nonsingular, we have

\Phi(\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}F[B_{k};\mathsf{B}_{-k}](\lambda)\Phi(B_{k})\quad\forall\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Additionally, assume that the expression for
F[B_{k},\mathsf{B}_{-k}]
in
Equation

12
are well-defined for
1\leq k\leq d
. Then for any
\lambda\notin[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]
, it holds that

\lVert\Phi(\lambda)\rVert\leq\sqrt{d}\cdot\bigl\lVert[\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}(B_{1}),\dotsc,\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}(B_{d})]^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr\rVert\cdot\max_{1\leq k\leq d}\lVert F[B_{k};\mathsf{B}_{-k}](\lambda)\rVert

and

\max_{1\leq k\leq d}\lVert F[B_{k};\mathsf{B}_{-k}](\lambda)\rVert^{\frac{1}{d-1}}\leq\frac{\max\{\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}-\lambda,\lambda-\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})}\}}{\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\neq j\leq d\\
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}\end{subarray}}\lvert\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\rvert}\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}\chi_{\mathsf{coef}}.

(14)

3
Cluster robustness of RSBL

In this section, we present the main result of this paper along with empirical evidence supporting our conjecture. We also mention an intrinsic challenge in the analysis of RSBL.

3.1
Main theoretical result

The following theorem provides a structural bound for the cluster robustness of RSBL defined in
Equation

1
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric matrix with spectral decomposition

A=\begin{bmatrix}Q&Q_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda&\\
&\Lambda_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Q&Q_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}},\quad\text{where}\quad\Lambda=\mathsf{diag}\{\Lambda_{1},\dotsc,\Lambda_{d}\}

(15)

with
\Lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
for
1\leq i\leq d
.
Assume that all eigenvalues in
\Lambda
are contained in an interval
[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]
and eigenvalues in
\Lambda_{\perp}
are outside
[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]
.
Assume
m=n/b
is an integer.
Let
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times b}
be a Gaussian random initial matrix with partition

[Q,Q_{\perp}]^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega=[\Omega_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\dotsc,\Omega_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}},

where
\Omega_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
for
1\leq i\leq m
. Assume that

\mathsf{relgap}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\neq j\leq d\\
\lambda_{i}\in\Lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}\\
\end{subarray}}\frac{\lvert\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\rvert}{\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}}>0,

(16)

where
\lambda_{\min}
and
\lambda_{\max}
are the smallest and largest eigenvalues of
A
, respectively. Let

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}:

\displaystyle=\sqrt{dn-bd^{2}}\cdot\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq d}\lVert\Omega_{i}^{-1}\rVert\cdot\max_{d+1\leq j\leq m}\lVert\Omega_{j}\rVert\cdot\max\limits_{d+1\leq j\leq m}\lVert\Omega_{j}\rVert\lVert\Omega_{j}^{-1}\rVert,

\displaystyle G_{d}:

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq d\\
\lambda\in[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]\setminus[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]\end{subarray}}\bigl\lVert F[B_{k};\mathsf{B}_{-k}](\lambda)\bigr\rVert,

where
B_{k}=\Omega_{k}^{-1}\Lambda_{k}\Omega_{k}
,
\mathsf{B}_{-k}
and
F[B_{k},\mathsf{B}_{-k}]
are defined in
Equation

5
and
Equation

12
for
k=1,\dotsc,d
, respectively. Then, with probability one,

\tan\angle\bigl(\mathsf{range}(Q),\mathcal{K}_{d}(A,\Omega)\bigr)\leq c_{\Omega}\cdot G_{d}.

(17)

Furthermore, with probability at least
1-4\delta
, it holds that

c_{\Omega}^{2}\leq\frac{(dn-bd^{2})^{3}}{\delta^{4}}\Bigl(2\sqrt{b}+\sqrt{2\log\bigl(2(m-d)/\delta\bigr)}\Bigr).

Proof.

Recall the tail bounds for the smallest and largest singular value of Gaussian random matrices in
[
20
, Thm. 2.6 and Thm. 3.1]
:

\Pr\bigl(\lVert\Omega_{0}\rVert\leq 2\sqrt{b}+\sqrt{2\log(\delta/2)}\bigr)\geq 1-\delta\quad\text{and}\quad\Pr\bigl(\lVert\Omega_{0}^{-1}\rVert\leq\sqrt{b}/\delta\bigr)\geq 1-\delta,

where
\Omega_{0}
is a
b\times b
Gaussian random matrix.
Since all
\Omega_{i}
and
\Omega_{j}
are
b\times b
Gaussian random matrices for
1\leq i\leq d<j\leq m
, the probabilistic result for
c_{\Omega}
comes directly from taking union bounds on the tail bounds above.

In the rest of the proof, we will focus on
Equation

17
. Let
K=[Q^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega,\dotsc,\Lambda^{d-1}Q^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega]
and
K_{\perp}=[Q_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega,\dotsc,\Lambda^{d-1}Q_{\perp}^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega]
.
By
[
29
, Thm. 3.1]
, we know that

\tan\angle\bigl(\mathsf{range}(Q),\mathcal{K}_{d}(A,\Omega)\bigr)=\lVert K_{\perp}K^{-1}\rVert.

(18)

Since
\Omega
is Gaussian, with probability one, all
\Omega_{i}
are nonsingular.
By definition,

K=\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{1}&\Lambda_{1}\Omega_{1}&\cdots&\Lambda_{1}^{d-1}\Omega_{1}\\
\Omega_{2}&\Lambda_{2}\Omega_{2}&\cdots&\Lambda_{2}^{d-1}\Omega_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\Omega_{d}&\Lambda_{d}\Omega_{d}&\cdots&\Lambda_{d}^{d-1}\Omega_{d}\end{bmatrix}=D\begin{bmatrix}I&\Omega_{1}^{-1}\Lambda_{1}\Omega_{1}&\cdots&\Omega_{1}^{-1}\Lambda_{1}^{d-1}\Omega_{1}\\
I&\Omega_{2}^{-1}\Lambda_{2}\Omega_{2}&\cdots&\Omega_{2}^{-1}\Lambda_{2}^{d-1}\Omega_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
I&\Omega_{d}^{-1}\Lambda_{d}\Omega_{d}&\cdots&\Omega_{d}^{-1}\Lambda_{d}^{d-1}\Omega_{d}\end{bmatrix},

where
D=\mathsf{diag}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\dotsc,\Omega_{d})
.
Recall that
B_{i}=\Omega_{i}^{-1}\Lambda_{i}\Omega_{i}
, we know
K=D\cdot\mathsf{Van}
.
Similarly, denote
D_{\perp}=\mathsf{diag}(\Omega_{d+1},\dotsc,\Omega_{m})
and
\Lambda_{\perp}=\mathsf{diag}\{\lambda_{d+1},\dotsc,\Lambda_{m}\}
with
\Lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
for
d+1\leq j\leq m
, and let
B_{j}=\Omega_{j}^{-1}\Lambda_{j}\Omega_{j}
and

\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}:=\begin{bmatrix}I&B_{d+1}&\cdots&B_{d+1}^{d-1}\\
I&B_{d+2}&\cdots&B_{d+2}^{d-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
I&B_{m}&\cdots&B_{m}^{d-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{bm\times bd},

it holds that
K_{\perp}=D_{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}
.
In turn, by submultiplicativity of spectral norm,

\lVert K_{\perp}K^{-1}\rVert\leq\lVert D_{\perp}\rVert\lVert D^{-1}\rVert\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{Van}^{-1}\rVert\leq\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{Van}^{-1}\rVert\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\lVert\Omega_{i}^{-1}\rVert\max_{d+1\leq j\leq m}\lVert\Omega_{j}\rVert,

(19)

where we use
Proposition

2
to ensure the invertibility of
\mathsf{Van}
.

In order to handle
\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{Van}^{-1}\rVert
, we need to employ the matrix polynomial techniques developed in
Section

2
.
By the definition of spectral norm,

\displaystyle\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{Van}^{-1}\rVert

\displaystyle=\max_{C^{\mathsf{T}}C=I_{b}}\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{Van}^{-1}\cdot C\rVert=\max_{(\mathsf{Van}\cdot C)^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathsf{Van}\cdot C)=I_{b}}\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot C\rVert

\displaystyle=\max_{(\mathsf{Van}\cdot C)^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathsf{Van}\cdot C)=I_{b}}\frac{\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot C\rVert}{\lVert\mathsf{Van}\cdot C\rVert}\leq\max_{\mathrm{rank}(C)=b}\frac{\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot C\rVert}{\lVert\mathsf{Van}\cdot C\rVert}.

For any column full-rank matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{bd\times b}
with the partition
C^{\mathsf{T}}=[C_{0}^{\mathsf{T}},\dotsc,C_{d-1}^{\mathsf{T}}]
, where
C_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
, we define a matrix polynomial
\Phi\in\mathbb{P}_{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b})
as

\Phi(X)=C_{0}+XC_{1}+\dotsb+X^{d-1}C_{d-1}\quad\forall\,X\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}.

Then, by definitions of
\mathsf{Van}
and
\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}
, we know

\mathsf{Van}\cdot C=[\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}(B_{1}),\dotsc,\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}(B_{d})]^{\mathsf{T}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot C=[\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}(B_{d+1}),\dotsc,\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}(B_{m})]^{\mathsf{T}}.

Then the spectral norm is bounded by

\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{Van}^{-1}\rVert\leq\max_{\Phi\in\mathbb{P}_{d-1}(\mathbb{R}^{b\times b})}\frac{\sqrt{m-d}\max\limits_{d+1\leq j\leq m}\lVert\Phi(B_{j})\rVert}{\bigl\lVert[\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}(B_{1}),\dotsc,\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}(B_{d})]^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr\rVert}.

(20)

Note that all eigenvalues of
B_{j}
for
d+1\leq j\leq m
are in
[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]\setminus[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]
,
Lemma

1
yields that

\max\limits_{d+1\leq j\leq m}\lVert\Phi(B_{j})\rVert\leq\sqrt{b}\max_{d+1\leq j\leq m}\lVert\Omega_{j}\rVert\lVert\Omega_{j}^{-1}\rVert\max_{\lambda\in[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]\setminus[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]}\lVert\Phi(\lambda)\rVert.

Recall that the assumption
\mathsf{relgap}>0
ensures that
\Lambda_{i}
and
\Lambda_{j}
have disjoint spectra for
i\neq j
, allowing us to use
Proposition

3
to claim that the expression of fundamental matrix polynomials in
Equation

12
are well-defined with probability one for
1\leq k\leq d
. Then we can apply
Theorem

2
to obtain that

\max_{\lambda\in[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]\setminus[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]}\lVert\Phi(\lambda)\rVert\leq\sqrt{d}\cdot\bigl\lVert[\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}(B_{1}),\dotsc,\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}(B_{d})]^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr\rVert\cdot G_{d}.

(21)

Combining
Equations

18
,
19
,
20
and
21
, we conclude
Equation

17
.
∎

The assumption
Equation

16
essentially requires that the multiplicity of desired eigenvalues does not exceed
b
, which is, in fact, a necessary condition for the convergence of RSBL. To see this, suppose there exists an eigenvalue
\widetilde{\lambda}
with multiplicity larger than
b
, and let
\widetilde{Q}
be an orthonormal basis of the associated eigenspace. It holds that

\dim\bigl(\mathsf{range}(\widetilde{Q})\cap\mathcal{K}_{\ell}(A,\Omega)\bigr)=\dim\mathsf{range}\bigl(\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}[\Omega,A\Omega,\dotsc,A^{\ell-1}\Omega]\bigr)=\dim\mathsf{range}(\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\Omega)\leq b

for any
\ell\geq 1
, implying that the Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}_{\ell}(A,\Omega)
contains an at most
b
-dimensional subspace of the eigenspace corresponding to
\widetilde{\lambda}
. Thus, RSBL can not find all eigenvectors corresponding to
\widetilde{\lambda}
.

Compared with the cluster robustness result for the single-vector Lanczos method in
[
11
, Eq. (3.5)]
, the dependence of
\delta
in
c_{\Omega}
is less favorable. This is due to the submultiplicativity of matrix norm used in
Lemma

1
, which does not arise in the single-vector case since all scalar products commute.

Another remark is that
Theorem

3
still applies when the number of eigenvalues of interest is not a multiple of the block size
b
, such as computing 10 eigenvectors with
b=3
. We can simply augment
\mathsf{range}(Q)
with a few additional eigenvectors in
Q_{\perp}
so that its dimension becomes a multiple of
b
.

3.2
Conjecture on cluster robustness

In
Theorem

3
, the effect of spectral gaps in
[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]
is reflected in
G_{d}
.
With notation in
Equation

13
, the inequality
Equation

14
yields that

G_{d}\leq\Bigl(\frac{\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}\chi_{\mathsf{coef}}}{\mathsf{relgap}}\Bigr)^{d-1}.

In order to further quantify the cluster robustness, we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

Let
\Lambda_{1},\dotsc,\Lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
be real diagonal matrices. Assume that
\Lambda_{i}
and
\Lambda_{j}
have disjoint spectra for
1\leq i<j\leq d
. Let
[\Omega_{1},\dotsc,\Omega_{d}]
be a Gaussian random matrix where
\Omega_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}
. With high probability, the quantity
\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}\chi_{\mathsf{coef}}
defined in
Equation

13
is bounded by a constant depending on
b
and
d
, but independent of
\Lambda_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq d
.

Under
1
, we arrive at the following corollary on the cluster robustness of RSBL. We remark that this conjecture is consistent with the result for randomized single-vector (
b=1
) Lanczos method in
[
11
, Eq. (3.5)]
.

Corollary of Conjecture 1
.

Let
A
be a symmetric matrix with the decomposition
Equation

15
, and
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times b}
be a Gaussian random matrix. Assume that
\mathsf{relgap}
in
Equation

16
is positive, then

\tan\angle\bigl(\mathsf{range}(Q),\mathcal{K}_{d}(A,\Omega)\bigr)\leq\Bigl(\frac{c_{b,d,n}}{\mathsf{relgap}}\Bigr)^{d-1}

holds with high probability, where
c_{b,d,n}
depends on
b
,
d
and
n
.

Remark 1
.

In both
Theorems

3
and
1
, we only assume that the eigenvalues in
\Lambda
lie within the interval
[\lambda_{\min}^{(\mathsf{cl})},\lambda_{\max}^{(\mathsf{cl})}]
, while the eigenvalues in
\Lambda_{\perp}
lie outside this interval. Notably,
\Lambda
may consist of either a cluster of interior eigenvalues or a subset of exterior eigenvalues. Moreover, the upper bound on
\tan\angle(\mathsf{range}(Q),\mathcal{K}_{d}(A,\Omega))
is independent of the gap between
\Lambda
and
\Lambda_{\perp}
.

3.2.2
Intrinsic challenge in analysis of RSBL

Since both
Theorem

3
and
1
are quite abstract, we illustrate them by working out the simplest non-trivial case:
d=2
. Now, recall that
B_{i}=\Omega_{i}^{-1}\Lambda_{i}\Omega_{i}
; we know

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i=1,2}\lVert\widehat{B}_{i}\rVert\lVert\widehat{B}_{i}^{-1}\rVert=\max_{i=1,2}\lVert B_{i}\rVert\lVert B_{i}^{-1}\rVert,

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathsf{coef}}

\displaystyle=\lVert(B_{1}-B_{2})^{-1}\rVert^{1/2}\min_{\lambda_{1}\in\Lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\Lambda_{2}}\lvert\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}\rvert.

In this simple situation,
\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}
can be bounded using existing results from random matrix theory; see
[
20
]
, for example. However, estimating
\chi_{\mathsf{coef}}
, or equivalently
\lVert(B_{1}-B_{2})^{-1}\rVert
, is highly non-trivial since both
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are
nonsymmetric
.
In the following, we demonstrate that estimating
\lVert(B_{1}-B_{2})^{-1}\rVert
constitutes an intrinsic difficulty of RSBL. We first establish an equivalence between
\lVert(B_{1}-B_{2})^{-1}\rVert
and the smallest singular value of the block Vandermonde matrix
\mathsf{Van}=\begin{bmatrix}I&B_{1}\\
I&B_{2}\end{bmatrix}
.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that
B_{1}-B_{2}
is nonsingular, then

\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}\lVert(B_{1}-B_{2})^{-1}\rVert^{2}\leq\Bigl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}I&B_{1}\\
I&B_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\Bigr\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\bigl(1+(\lVert B_{1}\rVert^{2}+1)\lVert(B_{1}-B_{2})^{-1}\rVert^{2}\bigr).

Proof.

Let
X=-B_{1}
and
Y=(B_{2}-B_{1})^{-1}
. The block LU factorization yields

\begin{bmatrix}I&B_{1}\\
I&B_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}I&XY\\
&Y\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&\\
-I&I\end{bmatrix}.

(22)

We just need to bound the block upper triangular matrix in
Equation

22
.
On the one hand,

\lVert Y\rVert=\max_{\lVert x\rVert=1}\lVert Yx\rVert\leq\max_{\lVert x\rVert=1}\Bigl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}XYx\\
Yx\end{bmatrix}\Bigr\rVert=\max_{\lVert x\rVert=1}\Bigl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}I&XY\\
&Y\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
x\end{bmatrix}\Bigr\rVert\leq\Bigl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}I&XY\\
&Y\end{bmatrix}\Bigr\rVert.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\Bigl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}I&XY\\
&Y\end{bmatrix}\Bigr\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{\lVert x\rVert^{2}+\lVert y\rVert^{2}=1}\Bigl\lVert\begin{bmatrix}I&XY\\
&Y\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\end{bmatrix}\Bigr\rVert^{2}=\max_{\lVert x\rVert^{2}+\lVert y\rVert^{2}=1}\lVert x+XYy\rVert^{2}+\lVert Yy\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\lVert x\rVert^{2}+\lVert y\rVert^{2}=1}\lVert x\rVert^{2}+2\lVert X\rVert\lVert Y\rVert\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert+(\lVert X\rVert^{2}+1)\lVert Y\rVert^{2}\lVert y\rVert^{2}\leq 1+(\lVert X\rVert^{2}+1)\lVert Y\rVert^{2}.

Combining these two inequalities above with the singular values of the lower triangular matrix in
Equation

22
, we finish the proof.
∎

The smallest singular value of the block Vandermonde matrix is, in fact, a fundamental measure of the cluster robustness. To illustrate this, we let

A=\mathsf{diag}\{1,\dotsc,2b,3bI_{b},4bI_{b},\dotsc,nI_{b}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

Recall the proof for
Theorem

3
, it holds that

\tan\angle(\mathsf{range}(Q),\mathcal{K}_{d}(A,\Omega))=D_{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{Van}^{-1}\cdot D^{-1},

where
D_{\perp}
and
D
are both block diagonal matrices with independent Gaussian blocks. By tail bounds of singular values for Gaussian random matrices
[
20
]
, we know that
\tan\angle(\mathsf{range}(Q),\mathcal{K}_{d}(A,\Omega))
and
\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{Van}^{-1}\rVert
are equivalent with high probability. Note that
B_{k}=\Omega_{k}^{-1}\Lambda_{k}\Omega_{k}=kbI_{b}
for
3\leq k\leq m
with probability one, which yields that

\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{b}&B_{3}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
I_{b}&B_{m}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{b}&3bI_{b}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
I_{b}&mbI_{b}\end{bmatrix}.

Consequently,
\lVert\mathsf{Van}^{-1}\rVert
is equivalent to
\lVert\mathsf{Van}_{\perp}\cdot\mathsf{Van}^{-1}\rVert
with probability one, and thus also equivalent to
\tan\angle(\mathsf{range}(Q),\mathcal{K}_{d}(A,\Omega))
with high probability.

Therefore, estimating
\lVert(B_{1}-B_{2})\rVert^{-1}
is as challenging as analyzing RSBL. For more general situations
d>2
, this kind of terms are in hidden in
\lVert S_{1,d}^{-1}\rVert
in
Lemma

3
, and
\chi_{\mathsf{mono}}
also becomes non-trivial due to the presence of
\widehat{B}_{i}
. Mathematically, we have to handle the smallest singular of the following operator:

\Lambda_{i}\Omega_{i}-\Omega_{i}\widetilde{\Omega}_{j}^{-1}\Lambda_{j}\widetilde{\Omega}_{j}=\Omega_{i}\Bigl(\Omega_{i}^{-1}\Lambda_{i}\Omega_{i}-\widetilde{\Omega}_{j}^{-1}\Lambda_{j}\widetilde{\Omega}_{j}\Bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},

(23)

where
\Omega_{i}
is a Gaussian random matrix,
\widetilde{\Omega}_{j}
is a random matrix with potentially complicated distribution but independent of
\Omega_{i}
, and
\Lambda_{i}
and
\Lambda_{j}
are deterministic diagonal matrices with disjoint spectra.
When
b=1
, RSBL becomes single-vector Lanczos method. This analysis becomes trivial since all scalar products
commute
and in turn,
\Omega_{i}
and
\widetilde{\Omega}_{j}
are cancelled with
\Omega_{i}^{-1}
and
\widetilde{\Omega}_{j}^{-1}
, respectively.
Another relatively simple scenario occurs when
\widetilde{\Omega}_{j}
is an orthogonal matrix, reduces the problem to estimating the smallest singular value of
\Lambda_{i,j}\circ\Omega_{i}
, where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product, and
\Lambda_{i,j}
is a deterministic matrix containing the differences of eigenvalues between
\Lambda_{i}
and
\Lambda_{j}
. Thanks to the independence of entries in
\Lambda_{i,j}\circ\Omega_{i}
, its smallest singular value can be bounded via the so-called broad connectivity; see,
[
21
,
3
]
for example. However, when
\widetilde{\Omega}_{j}
is a non-orthogonal matrix, the nonsymmetry of
\widetilde{\Omega}_{j}^{-1}\Lambda_{j}\widetilde{\Omega}_{j}
breaks the independence of entries in
\Lambda_{i,j}\circ\Omega_{i}
, causing existing arguments to fail.
Thus, estimating the smallest singular value of the matrix in
Equation

23
presents an intrinsic challenge in the analysis of the RSBL.

3.2.3
Applications

Eigenvalue computation:

Suppose we are interested in the largest
bd
eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix
A
. Assume that the relative gap defined in
Equation

16
is positive, and
\lambda_{bd}-\lambda_{bd+1}>0
, where
\lambda_{bd}
and
\lambda_{bd+1}
are the largest
(bd)
th and
(bd+1)
st eigenvalues of
A
, respectively. Applying
1
to
[
11
, Lem. 3.4]
, we know that, with high probability,

\tan\angle\bigl(\mathsf{range}(Q),\mathcal{K}_{\ell}(A,\Omega)\bigr)\leq\frac{\bigl(c_{b,d,n}/\mathsf{relgap}\bigr)^{d-1}}{\mathsf{Cheb}_{\ell-d}(1+2\frac{\lambda_{bd}-\lambda_{bd+1}}{\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}})},

(24)

where
Q
contains the first
bd
eigenvectors,
c_{b,d,n}
comes from
1
and
\mathsf{Cheb}_{\ell-d}
is the Chebyshev polynomial of degree
(\ell-d)
defined as

\mathsf{Cheb}_{\ell-d}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\bigl(\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}\bigr)^{\ell-d}+\bigl(\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}\bigl)^{d-\ell}\Bigr).

When
d=1
,
Equation

24
recovers the convergence result for randomized single-vector Lanczos method from
[
11
, Thm. 3.1]
. Beyond enabling a potentially larger gap parameter
\mathsf{relgap}
, choosing a block size
b>1
can substantially reduce the exponent of
\mathsf{relgap}^{-1}
, thereby enhancing robustness to eigenvalue clusters. Although the number of matvecs required to achieve a polynomial acceleration of degree
\ell-d
increases from
\ell
to
b\ell
, the overall complexity remains comparable when
\mathsf{relgap}
is small. In practice, the small-block variant can even outperform its single-vector counterpart due to lower communication overhead.

Low-rank approximation:

Given a matrix
\widehat{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times n}
with
N\geq n
, one can find a good rank-
bd
approximation by running Lanczos for
A=\widehat{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\widehat{A}
. Different from eigenvalue problems, finding top
bd
eigenvectors of
A
is sufficient but not necessary for obtaining a good low-rank approximation
[
5
]
.
Denote
V
as the top
bd
Ritz vectors extracted from the (block) Krylov subspace
\mathcal{K}_{\ell}(A,\Omega)
.
Plugging
1
into
[
16
, Thm. 2]
, we know that when

\ell\geq\frac{c}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\bigl(\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}+d\log\frac{c_{b,d,n}}{\mathsf{relgap}}\bigr),

(25)

we can extract a low-rank approximation from
\mathcal{K}_{\ell}(A,\Omega)
satisfying

\lVert\widehat{A}-\widehat{A}VV^{\mathsf{T}}\rVert\leq(1+\epsilon)\lVert\widehat{A}-\widehat{A}_{bd}\rVert\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert\widehat{A}-\widehat{A}VV^{\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}\leq(1+\epsilon)\lVert\widehat{A}-\widehat{A}_{bd}\rVert_{\mathsf{F}}

with high probability, where
c>0
is an absolute constant,
\widehat{A}_{bd}
is the best rank-
bd
approximation of
\widehat{A}
. Moreover, let
v_{i}
be the
i
th Ritz vector for
1\leq i\leq bd
, then

\bigl\lvert\lVert\widehat{A}v_{i}\rVert^{2}-\lambda_{i}\bigr\rvert\leq\epsilon\lambda_{bd+1},

where
\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda_{bd+1}
are the
i
th and
(bd+1)
st largest eigenvalues of
A
, respectively.

Compared with
[
16
, Thm. 4.5]
, one
b
factor is removed in the coefficient of
\log(1/\mathsf{relgap})
in
Equation

25
. As a consequence, it achieves the conjecture “the matrix-vector complexity scales linearly in the target rank for any block size” in
[
16
, p. 823]
.

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented a structural bound in
Theorem

3
along with a conjectured probabilistic bound to quantify the cluster robustness in RSBL for eigenvalue computation. The conjecture offers insights into the behavior of a class of randomized small-block eigensolvers and provides a foundation for further theoretical exploration. Future work will focus on establishing a rigorous probabilistic bound to support the conjectured results.
