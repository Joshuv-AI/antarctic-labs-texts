Arthroscopic osteosynthesis with demineralized bone matrix and bone marrow aspirated concentrate administration for nonunion of scaphoid in athletes
Authors: Yucheng Lee, Yinchuan Shih, Jochieh Li, Juitien Shih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes, with a minimum follow-up of two years, in athletes with scaphoid nonunion and minimal sclerosis who were treated with arthroscopically assisted percutaneous internal fixation augmented by injection of demineralized bone matrix (DBM) and bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC).MethodBetween January 2021 and January 2024, a consecutive series of 17 patients with early-stage scaphoid fracture nonunion, characterized by sclerosis or resorption at the nonunion site, were treated with arthroscopically assisted osteosynthesis using demineralized bone matrix (DBM) and bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC), combined with percutaneous internal fixation. Preoperative and postoperative assessments included clinical parameters (grip strength and range of motion), radiographic evaluation, functional outcomes using the Mayo Modified Wrist Score, and patient satisfaction.ResultsThe study sample consisted of 15 men and 2 women, with a mean age of 30.5 years (range, 20 to 45 years). Clinical follow-up was conducted at a mean of 26 months (range, 24–30 months), during which we recorded radiographic union and return to activity. Bony union was confirmed in 15 of 17 patients (88%) at a mean of 15.4 weeks, based on clinical examination and standard radiographic assessment. According to the Mayo Modified Wrist Score, 11 patients achieved excellent outcomes and 4 achieved good outcomes. Overall, 15 of 17 patients (88%) returned to their pre-injury level of work or sports activities.ConclusionsArthroscopically assisted osteosynthesis using DBM and BMAC, combined with percutaneous screw fixation, may represent a feasible and minimally invasive approach for promoting union and scaphoid healing in early-stage nonunion in athletes.Level of evidenceLevel IV, case series.Keywords: Scaphoid nonunion, Wrist arthroscope, Demineralized bone matrix, Bone marrow aspirate concentrate

Introduction

Scaphoid fractures are the most common type of carpal bone fracture; however, treatment outcomes often exhibit failure rates ranging from 25 to 45% [1, 2]. The incidence of scaphoid nonunion reported in the literature varies significantly, with estimates ranging from 5 to 55% [3, 4]. Scaphoid nonunion can lead to significant issues such as pain, functional impairment, carpal collapse, and progressive degenerative arthritis over time and scaphoid nonunion advanced collapse (SNAC) which is influenced by both the specific features of the fracture and factors related to the surgical procedure [5].

There are several surgical treatments to scaphoid nonunion, including open or arthroscopic non-vascularized bone grafting with screw fixation and vascularized bone grafting with screw fixation. The vascularized bone grafting and internal fixation has variable union rates which may result from longer operative time and soft tissue damaging [6–8]. Non-vascularized bone grafting with internal fixation for scaphoid nonunion yields results comparable to vascularized bone grafting [6, 9]. Recently, several studies have reported that arthroscopic treatment for scaphoid nonunion results in satisfactory outcomes, causing less soft tissue damage and maintaining the stability of the carpal bones [10–13]. However, few studies have reported the outcomes of arthroscopically assisted demineralized bone matrix(DBM) with bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC) for scaphoid nonunion, despite BMAC emerging as an adjunct in some orthopaedic surgeries, potentially enhancing fracture healing with minimal patient impact [10, 14, 15].

The purpose of our study is to assess clinical outcomes (minimum follow-up of two years) in athletes with scaphoid non-unions and minimal sclerosis who were treated by arthroscopically assisted percutaneous internal fixation method supplemented with demineralized bone matrix (DBM) and BMAC. We hypothesized that this arthroscopic-assisted approach could yield acceptable fracture union and functional outcomes in athletes with early-stage scaphoid nonunion, as evaluated by the Modified Mayo Wrist Score.

Patients and methods

Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to the initiation of this retrospective study. Between January 2021 and January 2024, 17 arthroscopic-assisted osteosynthesis procedures were performed in athletes diagnosed with scaphoid nonunion. In this study, athletes were considered to be individuals with high athletic or physical performance demands, including professional athletes, students pursuing sports-related careers, and military personnel requiring high levels of physical fitness, who sought to return to their pre-injury level of performance following treatment. Patients included in this study presented with persistent wrist pain and functional limitation associated with no radiographic progression toward scaphoid union for at least 4 to 6 months after injury. All patients were treated using DBM and BMAC, combined with percutaneous internal fixation using headless compression screws. All 17 patients who underwent these procedures were eligible for inclusion in this retrospective study and completed scheduled clinical and radiographic follow-up evaluation. There is no patient loss to follow-up.

The patients included in the study exhibited stage I or II scaphoid nonunion based on the Lichtman classification [16–18]. Stage I encompassed 6 patients with stable nonunion devoid of displacement, collapse, DISI deformity, or degenerative changes. Stage II involved 11 patients with unstable nonunion characterized by significant displacement. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was routinely performed preoperatively to evaluate vascularity of the scaphoid and to exclude avascular necrosis. Patients with scaphoid nonunion complicated by avascular necrosis, midcarpal bone issues, or generalized arthrosis, including involvement of the radiolunate joint (stages III, IV, and V), as well as those with less than 2 years of follow-up, were excluded from the study.

Clinical evaluation

Clinical outcome evaluation was based on visual analog scale (VAS) pain score, range of motion, grip strength, return to work, and satisfaction with the operation during the follow-up. The Modified Mayo Wrist Score (MMWS) [19] was designated as the primary clinical outcome measure and was completed by each patient during the preoperative and final follow-up. The Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) score was evaluated as a secondary clinical patient-reported outcome measure to complement the clinical assessment.

Radiographic evaluation

Preoperative imaging included standard anteroposterior, lateral, semi-pronated oblique, clenched-fist, and radioulnar-deviation radiographs of the affected wrist. Scaphoid nonunion was independently evaluated by two hand surgeons and one musculoskeletal radiologist, using the Lichtman classification system.

Postoperative radiographs assessments were performed at 6 weeks, 3 months, and subsequently at regular intervals until fracture union was confirmed. Fracture union was defined as the presence of cortical bridging across four cortices on anteroposterior, lateral and oblique views [20]. Computed tomography (CT) in sagittal and coronal planes were not routinely obtained but were selectively performed in 9 patients when radiographic findings were inconsistent with clinical symptoms. CT union was defined as trabecular bridging across the fracture site on at least three consecutive sagittal long-axis slices.

Operative technique

Under general anesthesia or axillary block, wrist arthroscopy was performed using a 1.9—mm-diameter arthroscope via 3–4, 6R, midcarpal radial (MCR), and midcarpal ulnar (MCU) portals. The technique for mid-carpal joint arthroscopic exploration has been described in the previous studies [21, 22]. Following diagnostic arthroscopy, the radiocarpal and midcarpal joints were systematically evaluated, and associated intra-articular pathology was addressed as indicated.

Debridement of the fibrous tissue at the scaphoid nonunion site and preparation of the bone ends were performed under arthroscopic visualization until viable bleeding bone was encountered. A canal was then created at the nonunion site using a burr sheath to facilitate graft delivery. DBM in putty form was then packed into the fracture defect through the burr sheath.

BMAC was prepared intraoperatively. Bone marrow aspirate was harvested intraoperatively under anesthesia from the anterior superior iliac spine using a trocar. Approximately 10 mL of bone marrow aspirate was obtained and processed by centrifugation, yielding approximately 1 mL of concentrated BMAC. The concentrated BMAC was subsequently injected directly into the nonunion site to biologically augment the graft material. The fibrinogen was then applied to the surface of fracture gap to avoid leakage. Percutaneous internal fixation with a headless compression screw was then performed to achieve stable fixation (Fig. 1).

In cases with DISI deformity, the hyperextended lunate and proximal scaphoid fragment were reduced to neutral through wrist flexion, followed by temporary radiolunate fixation using a K-wire (Linscheid maneuver) [23]. After correction of the deformity, the radiolunate wire was removed, and alignment was confirmed fluoroscopically.

After we reduced the scaphoid fracture, traction was released and internal fixation was performed under fluoroscopic guidance via a volar percutaneous approach using either headless compression screws or K-wires. Screw length and fracture gap stability were confirmed arthroscopically (Fig. 2).

Postoperatively, all patients were immobilized in a thumb-spica cast for 6 to 8 weeks. After cast removal, active range-of-motion exercises were initiated, with gradual progression to unrestricted activity. Follow-up evaluations were conducted regularly in the outpatient clinic.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was verified using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which showed that the data were nonparametric. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare preoperative and postoperative values. Outcomes analysis between different group was analyzed by Fisher’s exact test, Kruskal–Wallis test and post hoc tests. Statistical correlation was analyzed deeming p-values of less than 0.05 to be statistically significant. The minimal clinically important difference threshold of 13.5 points for the Disabilities of Arm, Shoulder, and Hand score of each patient was also evaluated and considered important by the patients [23, 24].

Results

The study followed 17 athletes over an average period of 26 months (ranging from 24 to 30 months). The mean duration from injury to surgery was 8.5 months (ranging from 4 to 25 months). Among the injuries, 5 occurred in the nondominant hands, and 12 were work-related. Using the Lichtman classification, nonunion were classified as stage I in 6 patients and stage II in 11 patients. Additionally, 16 patients exhibited scaphoid waist nonunion, while the remaining one had proximal pole nonunion. Arthroscopic examination revealed 5 patients with scapholunate ligament injury, 2 with partial lunotriquetral tears, and 6 with triangular fibrocartilage complex foveal tears (Table 1).

Using arthroscopic-assisted osteosynthesis with DBM and BMAC augmentation, successful bony union was achieved in 15 patients (88.2%) at a mean of 15.4 weeks (ranging from 10 to 24 weeks). By the last follow-up, significant improvements were observed in VAS pain scores, grip strength, and functional scores. Based on the MMWS, 11 patients exhibited excellent results and 4 demonstrated good wrist results. Additionally, 15 patients returned to work or sports at their preinjury levels.

As a secondary outcome measure, the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) score also demonstrated significant postoperative improvement. A clinically meaningful improvement exceeding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold was observed.

However, two patients continued to experience nonunion postoperatively, with poor functional outcome and no improvement in grip strength. No progression of arthritis or surgical related complications, such as infection, implant loosening, or breakage, were observed at the final follow-up. Radiographic alignment, including scapholunate, radiolunate, and lateral intra-scaphoid angles, significantly improved among stage I and II patients. Comparisons of outcomes among patients with different stages of scaphoid nonunion revealed no significant differences in union rates, VAS pain scores, or functional score improvements (Table 2).

Discussion

Scaphoid nonunion treatment remains a clinical challenge procedure due to its limited vascular supply and high risk of avascular necrosis, particularly in the proximal pole. Various treatment options are available, including non-vascularized bone grafting with screw fixation and vascularized bone grafting with screw fixation. Recent literature increasingly supports arthroscopically assisted techniques for the treatment of scaphoid nonunion [10–13, 22, 25]. A systematic review reported union rates of 86–100% and a time to union of 2.3–7.8 months following arthroscopic-assisted treatment of scaphoid nonunion, with generally favorable clinical outcomes and low complication rates (0–14%) [26]. Our findings are consistent with this outcome, highlighting the effectiveness of arthroscopic management in cases of nonunion without advanced sclerosis or collapse. Notably, the patients in our study exclusively involved athletes, a population with high functional demands that is relatively underrepresented in the literature. These characteristics highlight the clinical relevance of minimally invasive techniques that aim to preserve wrist stability and support timely return to activity.

The current surgical option for scaphoid nonunion treatment includes the non-vascularized or vascularized bone grafting and screw fixation. A recent systematic review encompassing 7,671 patients reported comparable results between these two procedures with high union rate [27]. In contrast, our technique incorporated DBM and BMAC as biological adjuncts. DBM serves as an osteoconductive scaffold enriched in osteoinductive proteins (e.g., bone morphogenetic proteins), while BMAC provides mesenchymal stem cells, growth factors such as TGF‑β and VEGF, and cytokines that promote osteogenesis and angiogenesis [28]. Several studies have demonstrated that BMAC and DBM promotes reliable healing in long bone nonunion [29, 30]. However, there is limited literature addressing the usage of both biologics in the treatment of carpal bone nonunion.

In addition to enhancing osteogenic potential, our approach eliminates the need for autologous bone harvesting, thereby avoiding donor-site morbidity associated with iliac crest or distal radius grafts. Iliac crest harvest has been associated with symptoms such as persistent pain (4.2%) and numbness (3.4%) [31]. Although autologous bone harvested from the distal radius is generally associated with lower complication rates, some adverse effects such as De Quervain’s tenosynovitis, donor-site fracture, and transient radial sensory nerve irritation have also been documented [32, 33]. Therefore, incorporating both DBM and BAC as biological adjuncts into a minimally invasive surgical technique, this approach may offer a means of avoiding the donor site morbidity, representing a feasible alternative to conventional methods, particularly in active, high-demand patient populations such as athletes.

There are several limitations in our study. First, the descriptive design and lack of a control group preclude direct comparison with other treatment modalities and prevent definitive conclusions regarding treatment efficacy. Second, the small sample size may introduce selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings, as scaphoid nonunion in highly demanding patients is an uncommon condition. Third, although fracture location (proximal pole versus waist) was documented, meaningful subgroup analysis was not performed because of the limited number of proximal pole nonunion, which further limits comparative interpretation. Fourth, although fracture union was primarily assessed using serial radiographs, CT examination was not routinely performed in all patients postoperatively. CT imaging was selectively obtained when clinical symptoms and radiographic findings were discordant. This approach may have underestimated incomplete union in some cases and represents a limitation of this study. Therefore, these findings provide preliminary clinical insights that warrant further investigation.

Conclusions

Arthroscopic osteosynthesis with DBM and BMAC administration appears to be a feasible and minimally invasive approach for promoting bony healing and favorable clinical outcomes in high-demand patients with stage I and II scaphoid nonunion. Further controlled studies are warranted to confirm these findings.
