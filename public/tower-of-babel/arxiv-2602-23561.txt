𝖵𝖺𝖲𝖲𝖳 \mathsf{VaSST} : Variational Inference for Symbolic Regression using Soft Symbolic Trees
Somjit Roy, Pritam Dey, Bani K. Mallick

Abstract
Symbolic regression has recently gained traction in AI-driven scientific discovery, aiming to recover explicit closed-form expressions from data that reveal underlying physical laws. Despite recent advances, existing methods remain dominated by heuristic search algorithms or data-intensive approaches that assume low-noise regimes and lack principled uncertainty quantification. Fully probabilistic formulations are scarce, and existing Markov chain Monte Carlo–based Bayesian methods often struggle to efficiently explore the highly multimodal combinatorial space of symbolic expressions. We introduce 𝖵𝖺𝖲𝖲𝖳 \mathsf{VaSST} , a scalable probabilistic framework for symbolic regression based on variational inference . 𝖵𝖺𝖲𝖲𝖳 \mathsf{VaSST} employs a continuous relaxation of symbolic expression trees, termed soft symbolic trees , where discrete operator and feature assignments are replaced by soft distributions over allowable components. This relaxation transforms the combinatorial search over an astronomically large symbolic space into an efficient gradient-based optimization problem while preserving a coherent probabilistic interpretation. The learned soft representations induce posterior distributions over symbolic structures, enabling principled uncertainty quantification. Across simulated experiments and Feynman Symbolic Regression Database within SRBench , 𝖵𝖺𝖲𝖲𝖳 \mathsf{VaSST} achieves superior performance in both structural recovery and predictive accuracy compared to state-of-the-art symbolic regression methods.



Keywords
Symbolic Regression; Scientific Machine Learning; Uncertainty Quantification; Black-box Variational Inference; Automatic Differentiation.

1
Introduction

Symbolic regression for scientific discovery.

Scientific Machine Learning
(SciML) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for integrating domain knowledge with data-driven modeling, enabling advances across materials science
(
Butler et al. 2018
)
, climate and weather prediction
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
, biology
(
Boadu et al. 2025
)
, and physics
(
Raissi et al. 2019
)
. A central objective in these domains is the discovery of explicit governing equations that encode mechanistic structure rather than merely predictive relationships. In this setting,
symbolic regression
(SR) plays a pivotal role. Unlike classical prediction-centric regression methods
(
Tibshirani 1996
;
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
;
Chipman et al. 2010
)
, SR operates directly over functional forms to recover concise, structurally interpretable mathematical expressions from experimental data. By identifying explicit equations underlying complex scientific phenomena, SR has enabled sparse discovery of nonlinear dynamical systems
(
Brunton et al. 2016
)
, accelerated materials design
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, and uncovered fundamental scientific laws
(
Schmidt and Lipson 2009
)
.

Related work.

Existing SR methods can be broadly categorized into
evolutionary
,
machine learning
-based, and
Bayesian
approaches. Classical SR is dominated by
genetic programming
and related heuristic search algorithms
(
Davidson et al. 2003
;
Fortin et al. 2012
;
Stephens 2016
)
, which explore expressions through stochastic evolution but often suffer from high computational complexity, sensitivity to initialization, and the generation of overly complex formulas
(
Korns 2011
)
. More recent machine learning-driven methods cast SR as a sequential decision-making problem, learning to generate grammar rules, tree traversals, or executable symbolic strings via neural architectures
(
Udrescu and Tegmark 2020
;
Petersen et al. 2021
;
Broløs et al. 2021
;
Kamienny et al. 2022
)
. Although these methods enhance scalability and predictive performance, they remain inherently search-driven and computationally challenging due to the NP-hard nature of symbolic space exploration
(
Virgolin and Pissis 2022
)
. Moreover, their effectiveness is often contingent on large training datasets and low-noise regimes, as we demonstrate in Section
5
. This review highlights that fully probabilistic formulations of SR remain limited.

Note that, scientific expressions possess an inherently hierarchical and compositional structure that aligns naturally with tree representations (see Figure
1
). Tree models are ubiquitous in modern machine learning for capturing nonlinear interactions with strong predictive performance
(
Breiman et al. 1984
;
Breiman 1996
)
, while Bayesian tree formulations
(
Chipman et al. 1998
;
Dension et al. 1998
;
Chipman et al. 2010
)
provide a coherent inferential framework through priors over structures, principled model comparison, and uncertainty quantification. In context of SR, the
Bayesian Machine Scientist
(
BMS
)
(
Guimerà et al. 2020
)
represents an important step toward Bayesian equation discovery. However, it employs an ad hoc structural prior built from corpus parsing based on a priori knowledge, and performs inference using
Metropolis-Hastings
(MH) proposals over discrete symbolic tree structures.
Given the highly multimodal and combinatorial posterior landscape, such local MH updates can exhibit poor mixing in complex discrete spaces
(
Bhamidi et al. 2008
;
Łatuszyński et al. 2025
)
, leading to slow convergence and inefficient exploration of the symbolic model space. Similarly,
Bayesian Symbolic Regression
(
BSR
)
(
Jin et al. 2020
)
adopts a tree-based partial Bayesian formulation but uses plug-in ordinary least squares estimates for regression parameters. This incomplete parameter uncertainty propagation, combined with local stochastic MH structural updates, further impedes effective traversal of the symbolic model space and frequently produces overly complicated output expressions, as evidenced in Section
5
.

Our contributions.

In light of these drawbacks, we propose a
variational inference
framework for SR that combines principled Bayesian modeling with improved computational scalability over existing probabilistic SR methods. Variational inference recasts Bayesian inference as an optimization problem
(
Blei et al. 2017
)
, offering a scalable alternative to traditional Monte Carlo-based approaches for modern data-intensive settings
(
Jordan et al. 1999
;
Blei et al. 2003
;
Wainwright and Jordan 2008
;
Graves 2011
)
. However, a naïve application of variational inference over the discrete structural space of symbolic expressions results in a combinatorial optimization problem that negates these scalability benefits
(
Williams 1992
;
Koza 1994
)
.

To overcome this challenge, we introduce a novel representation of symbolic expressions using
soft symbolic trees
, in which each operator and feature assignment is replaced by a soft combination over all allowable operators and features. This relaxation transforms the discrete structural search into a continuous optimization problem, enabling efficient gradient-based exploration of the symbolic model space through
black-box
variational inference
(
Ranganath et al. 2014
;
Kucukelbir et al. 2017
;
Giordano et al. 2024
)
. Structural interpretability is preserved by mapping the learned soft representations to
hard
symbolic trees through a
randomized
post-optimization procedure. This continuous relaxation trick is similar in spirit to soft relaxations used for variational inference in decision tree-based regression
(
Salazar 2023
)
. However, in context of SR the soft representations naturally induce probability distributions over hard symbolic tree structures, thereby providing a principled mechanism for quantifying uncertainty in the recovered symbolic expressions.
We refer to this framework as,
\mathsf{VaSST}
:
Va
riational Inference for Symbolic Regression using
S
oft
S
ymbolic
T
rees
.

A primary goal in scientific equation discovery is to favor structural parsimony in accordance to the
Occam’s razor
principle
(
Jefferys and Berger 1992
)
.

\mathsf{VaSST}
achieves this by controlling structural complexity via a
depth-dependent regularizing prior
that penalizes overly complex symbolic expressions. Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate that
\mathsf{VaSST}
effectively balances structural discovery and predictive accuracy while maintaining computational stability, outperforming a range of state-of-the-art symbolic regression methods. A
Python
implementation of
\mathsf{VaSST}
is available at
anonymous.4open.science/r/VaSST-62C7
.

Symbolic Tree Representation of Scientific Expressions

Scientific expressions can be constructed by combining primary features (e.g.,
x_{1},x_{2}
) and mathematical operators (e.g.,
\exp,\sin,+,\times
). We denote the full set of
p
primary features as
\mathcal{X}
and the set of allowed mathematical operators as
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_{u}\cup\mathcal{O}_{b}
, where
\mathcal{O}_{u}
and
\mathcal{O}_{b}
contain the unary and binary operators, respectively. Typical choices in scientific modeling include,
\mathcal{O}_{u}=\{\sin,\cos,\exp,^{2},^{3}\}
and
\mathcal{O}_{b}=\{+,\times,-,/\}

(
Udrescu and Tegmark 2020
)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
denote the set of admissible feature-operator compositions.
Any symbolic expression
\mathfrak{E}\in\mathcal{E}
is defined recursively as one of, (a)
primitive expression
:
\mathfrak{E}=x_{1}
, where
x_{1}\in\mathcal{X}
; (b)
binary composition
:
\mathfrak{E}=b(\mathfrak{E}_{1},\mathfrak{E}_{2})
, where
\mathfrak{E}_{1},\mathfrak{E}_{2}\in\mathcal{E}
and
b\in\mathcal{O}_{b}
, for e.g.,
x_{1}+x_{2}
; and (c)
unary composition
:
\mathfrak{E}=u(\tilde{\mathfrak{E}})
, where
\tilde{\mathfrak{E}}\in\mathcal{E}
and
u\in\mathcal{O}_{u}
, for e.g.,
\exp(x_{1})
.
This set of characterizations naturally maps each
\mathfrak{E}\in\mathcal{E}
to a symbolic tree structure
\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{E})
, where
internal
(
nonterminal
) nodes either represent unary (with
1
child node) or binary (with
2
children nodes) operators, while
leaves
(
terminal
nodes) correspond to primary features from
\mathcal{X}
; see Figure
1
. It is important to note that, unlike decision tree-based models
(
Breiman et al. 1984
;
Chipman et al. 2010
)
, which recursively partition the input feature space, our framework interprets symbolic trees as compositional structures that assign input features from
\mathcal{X}
to leaves and operators from
\mathcal{O}
to internal nodes. Such tree representations of symbolic expressions are not necessarily unique, for e.g.,
x_{1}(x_{2}+x_{3})
is treated to be same as that of
x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3}
, in our framework.

Now, for
\mathfrak{E}\in\mathcal{E}
and an instance
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
of the features in
\mathcal{X}
, we define
g(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{E}))
to be the real-valued evaluation of the symbolic expression
\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{E})
at
\mathbf{x}
. With this setup in place, we formally present the
\mathsf{VaSST}

3
The
\mathsf{VaSST}
Model

We outline the
\mathsf{VaSST}
model which comprises two major components: (i) a
symbolic ensemble
consisting of a linear regression of the response variable over a forest of
K
symbolic trees and (ii) a hierarchical prior specification over the model regression coefficients, the model noise variance, and the symbolic tree structures.

3.1
The Symbolic Ensemble Component

Let
\mathcal{D}_{n}:=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be the collection of observed data units.
\mathsf{VaSST}
models and structurally learns the hidden symbolic relationship between the responses
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and primary features in
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
by using a collection of
K
symbolic trees,
\{\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{E}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{K}
, evaluated at
\mathbf{x}_{i}
, as:

\displaystyle y_{i}=\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{K}g_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\beta_{j}+\epsilon_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(1)

where
g_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=g(\mathbf{x}_{i};\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{E}_{j}))
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{K})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K+1}
is the model regression coefficient vector, and
\epsilon_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the model noise with
\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathrm{N}_{1}(0,\sigma^{2})
independently. From (
1
), we obtain the vector representation of the symbolic ensemble component:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\quad\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{N}_{n}(0_{n},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I_{n}}),

(2)

where
\mathbf{T}=(g_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i}))_{1\leq i\leq n,\;0\leq j\leq K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\overline{K+1}}
is the
expression design matrix
,
g_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=1
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
,
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the response vector, and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the model noise vector.

The model regression parameters
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})
jointly are endowed upon with the conjugate Normal Inverse-Gamma (NIG) prior viz.,
\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2}\sim\mathrm{N}_{K+1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0})
and
\sigma^{2}\sim\mathrm{IG}(a_{0},b_{0})
, where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{K+1}
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}
(positive definite matrix of order
K+1
), and
a_{0},b_{0}>0
are the hyperparameters of the Normal and Inverse-Gamma prior distributions, respectively. We complete the
\mathsf{VaSST}
model specification by describing the symbolic tree prior in Section
3.2
.

The
\mathsf{VaSST}
Symbolic Tree Prior

\mathsf{VaSST}

\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{E}_{j})

deterministic pruning operation

The skeleton

\mathfrak{p}
which proceeds as follows, (a)

Prior over full binary tree skeleton.

The prior specification over
\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(D)}
is characterized by the prior over the latent skeleton variables,
\{e_{j\zeta},o_{j\zeta},h_{j\zeta}\;:\;\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}\}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}e_{j\zeta}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{\zeta}),\;p_{\zeta}=\alpha(1+d_{\zeta})^{-\delta},\\

h_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}),\;\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{ft}}),\end{gathered}

where
j=1,\ldots,K
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\delta>0
, and
\mathrm{Ber}(\cdot)
,
\mathrm{Cat}(\cdot)
, and
\mathrm{Dir}(\cdot)

3.2
),
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{O}|}
and
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}\in\Delta^{p}
are the operator and feature weight vectors, where
\Delta^{m}=\{\mathbf{z}\in[0,\infty)^{m}\mid\mathbf{1}_{m}^{\top}\mathbf{z}=1\}
is the
m

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{op}}\in{(0,\infty)}^{|\mathcal{O}|}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{ft}}\in{(0,\infty)}^{p}
are the Dirichlet concentration hyperparameters. Therefore, using (
3.2
) the prior specification over
\Theta=(\{\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(D)}\}_{j=1}^{K},\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}},\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})
is:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Pi\left(\Theta\right)=\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\prod_{j=1}^{K}\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}}\Pi(e_{j\zeta})\Pi(o_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\Pi(h_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}).\end{split}

(6)

Note that, the prior over the skeleton
\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(D)}
in (

\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{E}_{j})
. We conclude with Remark

in (

) as a mechanism for controlling symbolic expression complexity.

Remark 1

(Depth-dependent split probability)
.

Guided by the Occam’s razor principle
(
Jefferys and Berger 1992
)
, we aim to learn interpretable and parsimonious symbolic expressions which adequately captures the underlying scientific mechanism. This is achieved by the depth-dependent split probability
p_{\zeta}
in (
3.2
) which imparts a regularizing effect on the individual tree depths
(
Chipman et al. 1998
)
.

4
Variational Inference for
\mathsf{VaSST}

Combining the data likelihood
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T},\beta,\sigma^{2})
, the joint prior over the model regression parameters in Section
3.1
, and the
\mathsf{VaSST}
symbolic tree prior in Section
3.2
, the joint posterior distribution induced over all unknowns
(\Theta,\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})
is:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Pi(\Theta,\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\propto p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T},\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})\Pi(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\prod_{j=1}^{K}\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}}\Pi(e_{j\zeta})\Pi(o_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\Pi(h_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}).\end{split}

(7)

Marginalization over model regression parameters.

The conjugate NIG prior over
(\beta,\sigma^{2})
enables marginalization in (
7
) and results into:
\Pi(\Theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\propto p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T})\Pi(\Theta)
, where
p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T})
up to constants is:

\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T})=\tfrac{1}{2}\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}|-a_{n}\log b_{n}+\log\Gamma(a_{n}),

(8)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function,
|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}|
is the determinant of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}
, and:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}+\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{T},\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}+\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{y}),\quad a_{n}=a_{0}+\tfrac{n}{2},\\
b_{n}=b_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}).\end{gathered}

See Appendix
G.1
for complete derivations. Therefore, it remains to draw inference from
\Pi(\Theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
.

Variational family.

To conduct scalable and efficient inference, we adopt a variational inference routine for approximating the posterior
\Pi(\Theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
with a tractable
variational family

q_{\phi}(\Theta)
in (
4
) below, parameterized by variational parameters in
\phi

(
Blei et al. 2017
)
. Specifically, we choose a
mean-field
factorization
(
Jordan et al. 1999
)
:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}q_{\phi}(\Theta)=q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\prod_{j=1}^{K}\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}}q_{\phi}(e_{j\zeta})q_{\phi}(o_{j\zeta})q_{\phi}(h_{j\zeta}),\\
q_{\phi}(e_{j\zeta})\equiv\mathrm{Ber}(e_{j\zeta};\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}),\\
q_{\phi}(o_{j\zeta})\equiv\mathrm{Cat}(o_{j\zeta};\tilde{\pi}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{op}}),\;q_{\phi}(h_{j\zeta})\equiv\mathrm{Cat}(h_{j\zeta};\tilde{\pi}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ft}}),\\
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\equiv\mathrm{Dir}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{op}}),\;q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\equiv\mathrm{Dir}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{ft}}),\end{gathered}

for
j=1,\ldots,K
and
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}
, where:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\texttt{logit}(\tilde{p}_{j\zeta})=\log\tfrac{\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}}{1-\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}}=\ell_{j\zeta},\quad\ell_{j\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}\\
&\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{op}}_{j\zeta}=\texttt{softmax}(\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{op}}),\quad\texttt{softmax}(\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{op}})_{o}=\tfrac{\exp(a^{\mathrm{op}}_{j\zeta,o})}{\sum_{o=1}^{|\mathcal{O}|}\exp(a^{\mathrm{op}}_{j\zeta,o})},o=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{O}|,\quad\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{op}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{O}|},\\
&\tilde{\pi}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ft}}=\texttt{softmax}(\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ft}}),\quad\texttt{softmax}(\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ft}})_{k}=\tfrac{\exp(a^{\mathrm{ft}}_{j\zeta,k})}{\sum_{k=1}^{p}\exp(a^{\mathrm{ft}}_{j\zeta,k})},k=1,\ldots,p,\quad\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ft}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.\end{split}

Hence,
\phi
collects the parameters
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{op}}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{ft}}
,
\ell_{j\zeta}
,
\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{op}}
, and
\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ft}}
.

Evidence lower bound.

The variational parameters in
\phi
are obtained by maximizing the
evidence lower bound
(ELBO):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\phi)&=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}}[\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T})]-\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\Theta)\parallel\Pi(\Theta)),\end{split}

(15)

where the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence is:
\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\Theta)\parallel\Pi(\Theta))=\int q_{\phi}(\Theta)\log(q_{\phi}(\Theta)/\Pi(\Theta))d\Theta
. Now, we focus on the KL term in (
15
). Note that, owing to the mean-field variational family in (
4
),
\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\Theta)\parallel\Pi(\Theta))
splits as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\Theta)\parallel\Pi(\Theta))&=\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\parallel\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}))+\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\parallel\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}))\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}}\bigg[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(e_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(e_{j\zeta}))+\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(o_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(o_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}))\right]\\
&\quad+\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(h_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(h_{j\zeta}|\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}))\right]\bigg],\end{split}

(16)

where:

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(e_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(e_{j\zeta}))=\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}\log\tfrac{\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}}{p_{\zeta}}+(1-\tilde{p}_{j\zeta})\log\tfrac{1-\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}}{1-p_{\zeta}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\parallel\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}))=\log({\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{op}})}/{\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{op}})})+\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{O}|}(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{op},k}-\eta_{\mathrm{op},k})(\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{op},k})-\Psi(\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{O}|}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{op}})),

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(o_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(o_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}))\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{O}|}\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{op}}_{j\zeta,k}\left[\log(\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{op}}_{j\zeta,k})-\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{op},k})+\Psi(\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{|\mathcal{O}|}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{op}})\right],

where
\mathcal{B}(\cdot)
and
\Psi(\cdot)
are the multivariate Beta and Digamma functions, respectively. Also,
\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\parallel\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}))
and
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})}[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(h_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(h_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}))]
are given analogously to the last two expressions in (
4
); see Appendix
G.2
for details.

The term
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}}[\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T})]
in (
15
) does not admit an analytical form. A direct stochastic optimization is computationally prohibitive due to the combinatorial nature of the structural variables
\{e_{j\zeta},o_{j\zeta},h_{j\zeta}\;:\;\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}\}_{j=1}^{K}
. The induced discrete model space grows exponentially with both tree depth (
D
) and ensemble size (
K
) having
\mathsf{O}((2p|\mathcal{O}|)^{N\cdot K})
possible configurations. Combinatorial search over this astronomically large space is therefore infeasible. Moreover, gradient-based optimization over discrete variables would require high-variance score-function estimators
(
Williams 1992
;
Ranganath et al. 2014
)
or specialized structured search procedures
(
Koza 1994
;
Schmidt and Lipson 2009
)
, both of which scale poorly and become impractical even for moderate values of
D
and
K
. To overcome this computational bottleneck,
\mathsf{VaSST}
introduces a differentiable relaxation of symbolic trees, regarded as
soft symbolic trees
, transforming discrete structural and labeling decisions into continuous approximations
(
Maddison et al. 2017
;
Liu et al. 2019
)
.

Soft symbolic trees via continuous relaxations.

To construct soft symbolic trees, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}_{j}^{(D)}\to\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}
for
j=1,\ldots,K
, we implement the
Binary Concrete

(
Maddison et al. 2017
)
and
Gumbel-Softmax

(
Jang et al. 2016
)

continuous relaxations
on the discrete structural variables
\{e_{j\zeta},o_{j\zeta},h_{j\zeta}\;:\;\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}\}_{j=1}^{K}
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{\emph{Binary Concrete}}:\;&\tilde{e}_{j\zeta}=\sigma\!\left(\tfrac{\ell_{j\zeta}+\log u_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ex}}-\log(1-u_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ex}})}{\tau_{\mathrm{ex}}}\right),\\[6.0pt]
\text{\emph{Gumbel-Softmax}}:\;&\mathbf{\tilde{o}}_{j\zeta}=\texttt{softmax}\!\left(\tfrac{\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{op}}+\mathbf{g}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{op}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{op}}}\right),\\
&\mathbf{\tilde{h}}_{j\zeta}=\texttt{softmax}\!\left(\tfrac{\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ft}}+\mathbf{g}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ft}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{ft}}}\right),\end{split}

(18)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function,
u_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ex}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
,
\mathbf{g}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{op}}=\{-\log(-\log u_{j\zeta,r}^{\mathrm{op}})\}_{r=1}^{|\mathcal{O}|}
,
\mathbf{g}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ft}}=\{-\log(-\log u_{j\zeta,k}^{\mathrm{ft}})\}_{k=1}^{p}
,
u_{j\zeta,r}^{\mathrm{op}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
, and
u_{j\zeta,k}^{\mathrm{ft}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
. Also, all uniform random variables are mutually independent. Observe that,
\tilde{e}_{j\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{j\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{O}|}
, and
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{j\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}{{}^{p}}
can be interpreted as the
soft one-hot encoding
of the corresponding original discrete structural variables. In (
18
),
(\tau_{\mathrm{ex}},\tau_{\mathrm{op}},\tau_{\mathrm{ft}})
are
temperature parameters
controlling the sharpness of the Binary Concrete and Gumbel-Softmax relaxations. Particularly, higher temperatures yield smooth mixtures of structural configurations whereas for smaller temperatures the relaxed variables concentrate toward discrete structures. A careful annealing of these parameters allows for a balance between exploration and structural learning of symbolic expressions.

Evaluation of soft symbolic trees.

Given the soft symbolic tree
\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}
as discussed above, we now outline the algorithm for its corresponding evaluation at a given feature vector instance
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. This evaluation is done recursively over the nodes of
\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}
. For a given node
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}
, the soft terminal (leaf) contribution is computed as a convex combination of the input features,
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{j\zeta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}
, which corresponds to a
soft feature evaluation
. If the node acts as nonterminal (internal), its output is obtained by a weighted mixture of unary and binary operations over its children. Specifically, the
unary contribution
aggregates,
\tilde{o}_{j\zeta,u}\cdot u(\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\mathbf{x}_{i};L(\zeta)))
, over
u\in\mathcal{O}_{u}
, while the
binary contribution
aggregates,
\tilde{o}_{j\zeta,b}\cdot b(\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\mathbf{x}_{i};L(\zeta)),\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\mathbf{x}_{i};R(\zeta)))
, over
b\in\mathcal{O}_{b}
. The
complete node evaluation
is then obtained via
soft gating
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\zeta)&=(1-\tilde{e}_{j\zeta})\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{j\zeta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}\;+\tilde{e}_{j\zeta}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{O}_{u}}\tilde{o}_{j\zeta,u}\cdot u(\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\mathbf{x}_{i};L(\zeta)))\\
&\qquad+\;\tilde{e}_{j\zeta}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{O}_{b}}\tilde{o}_{j\zeta,b}\cdot b(\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\mathbf{x}_{i};L(\zeta)),\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\mathbf{x}_{i};R(\zeta))),\end{split}

(19)

thus smoothly interpolating over all possible combinations of operators and features; see
SoftEvalAtNode
Algorithm
1
in Appendix
C
. Consequently, evaluating a soft symbolic tree corresponds to computing
\mathcal{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{soft}}(\mathbf{x}_{i};\zeta)
from (
19
) at the root node
\zeta=0
. Repeating this procedure for the collection of
K
soft symbolic trees and all observations yields the
soft design matrix
,
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{soft}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\overline{K+1}}
, which includes the intercept column; see
SoftEval
Algorithm
2
in Appendix
C
.

Stochastic approximation of
\mathcal{L}(\phi)
.

The ELBO objective
\mathcal{L}(\phi)
in (
15
) involves the analytically intractable term
\mathbb{E}_{q(\phi)}[\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T})]
, since
\mathbf{T}
exhibits nonlinear dependence on the soft symbolic trees. We therefore approximate this term using
Monte Carlo
(MC) sampling from the distribution induced over
\{\tilde{e}_{j\zeta},\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_{j\zeta},\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{j\zeta}\;:\;\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}\}_{j=1}^{K}
in (
18
), denoted as
\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\mathrm{soft}}
. In particular, consider
S
MC samples from
\mathcal{P}_{\phi}^{\mathrm{soft}}
and invoking the
SoftEval
Algorithm
2
in Appendix
C
, we compute the soft design matrices
\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{soft}}^{(s)}
, and hence
\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{soft}}^{(s)})
using (
8
), for
s=1,\ldots,S
. Finally, the MC approximation of
\mathcal{L}(\phi)
is obtained as:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)=\tfrac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{soft}}^{(s)})-\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\Theta)\parallel\Pi(\Theta)),

(20)

where
\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\Theta)\parallel\Pi(\Theta))
is given by (
16
) and (
4
); see
ApproxELBO
Algorithm
3
in Appendix
D
. The stochastic approximation
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)
in (
20
) is differentiable with respect to the variational parameters collected in
\phi
, thus enabling efficient gradient-based optimization.

Black-box optimization.

To obtain the optimal variational parameter vector
\phi^{\star}
, we maximize
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)
in (
20
) using
gradient-based black-box
variational inference
(
Ranganath et al. 2014
;
Kucukelbir et al. 2017
)
, leveraging
automatic differentiation

(
Rall 1981
)
to compute
\nabla_{\phi}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\phi)
. The resulting objective is optimized using the
AdamW
optimizer
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2019
)
. To progressively sharpen the continuous relaxations in (
18
), we employ an
annealing schedule
which linearly decreases the temperature across iterations, gradually transitioning from smooth structural mixtures to near-discrete symbolic trees over the course of the algorithm; refer to
\mathsf{VaSST}
Algorithm
4
in Appendix
E
.

Uncertainty quantification via samples of hard symbolic trees.

We conclude variational inference for
\mathsf{VaSST}
by generating
hard symbolic trees
using
\phi^{\star}
. Concretely, for each draw
s=1,\ldots,H
and each tree
j=1,\ldots,K
, we sample:

\displaystyle\widehat{e}_{j\zeta}^{(s)}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(\sigma(\ell^{\star}_{j\zeta})),\quad\widehat{o}_{j\zeta}^{(s)}\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\texttt{softmax}((\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{op}})^{\star})),\quad\widehat{h}_{j\zeta}^{(s)}\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\texttt{softmax}((\mathbf{a}_{j\zeta}^{\mathrm{ft}})^{\star})),

respectively for all
\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}
. This sampling defines a full depth-
D
hard binary tree skeleton
(\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{j}^{(D)})^{(s)}
, which is then deterministically pruned to obtain:

\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{E}_{j}^{(s)})=\mathfrak{p}((\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{j}^{(D)})^{(s)}).

The expression design matrix
\mathbf{T}^{(s)}
is computed by evaluating
\mathcal{T}(\mathfrak{E}_{j}^{(s)})
at instances
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Conditioned on
\mathbf{T}^{(s)}
and
\mathbf{y}
, the posterior mean estimates:

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(s)}_{\mathrm{PM}}=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2},\mathbf{T}^{(s)},\mathbf{y}],\quad(\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{PM}})^{(s)}=\mathbb{E}[\sigma^{2}\mid\mathbf{T}^{(s)},\mathbf{y}],

are computed using (
4
). These
H
samples are then ranked according to the minimum in-sample root mean squared errors (RMSEs), which enables a balance between
uncertainty quantification
, structural learning of symbolic expressions, and predictive power; see Appendix
F
for
SampleHard
Algorithm
5
.

is consistently among the top-performing methods, typically alongside
BMS
, except for
FTC
where
BMS
fails due to numerical issues. Genetic programming-based
gplearn
and
DEAP
, and neural network-based
QLattice
often achieve competitive prediction accuracy but only through substantially more complex symbolic expressions. Lastly,
BSR
yields consistently higher prediction errors. A full comparison of out-of-sample RMSE values is provided in Appendix
K.2
.

Additionally,
\mathsf{VaSST}
exhibits substantially faster runtimes than the Bayesian SR methods
BMS
and
BSR
across all Feynman equation datasets and noise settings, highlighting the computational advantages of the proposed variational inference framework. Detailed runtime comparisons are reported in Appendix
K.3
.

Finally, beyond competitive performance in prediction and structural learning,
\mathsf{VaSST}
provides uncertainty quantification in symbolic equation discovery by producing multiple candidate symbolic ensembles ranked by minimum in-sample RMSE. The top
5
expressions across all Feynman equations and noise levels are reported in Appendix
K.4
.

6
Conclusion

We presented
\mathsf{VaSST}
, a fully probabilistic framework for SR that leverages variational inference with continuous relaxations of symbolic trees. It enables scalable gradient-based optimization while preserving interpretability equipped with principled uncertainty quantification. Empirically,
\mathsf{VaSST}
achieves strong structural recovery, competitive predictive accuracy, stability under noise, and notable computational gains over existing Bayesian SR methods.

This work opens promising future avenues for fully probabilistic and scalable variational inference–based SR, including the development of more structured optimization strategies for the variational objective to further enhance scalability relative to modern machine learning–based approaches.

Appendix B
Parameterization Details of Distribution Families used in
\mathsf{VaSST}

The
\mathsf{VaSST}
framework utilizes several standard probability distributions for model specification and prior construction. Although these distributions are widely used in the machine learning literature, multiple parameterizations are common for some of them across various sources. For clarity and reproducibility, we explicitly document the notation and parameterizations adopted in this work.

Normal Inverse-Gamma.

We use the conjugate Normal Inverse-Gamma (
\mathrm{NIG}
) prior for Gaussian linear regression parameters:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{N}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}),\quad\sigma^{2}\sim\mathrm{IG}(a_{0},b_{0}),

(B.1)

where
d=K+1
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is symmetric positive definite, and
a_{0},b_{0}>0
.
Our Inverse-Gamma parameterization, i.e.,
\mathrm{IG}(a,b)
, uses the density:

\displaystyle\Pi(\sigma^{2})=\frac{b^{a}}{\Gamma(a)}(\sigma^{2})^{-(a+1)}\exp\!\left(-\frac{b}{\sigma^{2}}\right),\quad\sigma^{2}>0,

(B.2)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function in Table
A.1
. The conditional Gaussian density in (
B.1
) is:

\displaystyle\Pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2})=(2\pi)^{-\tfrac{d}{2}}(\sigma^{2})^{-\tfrac{d}{2}}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}|^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0})\right\}.

(B.3)

Equivalently, the joint prior is
\Pi(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})=\Pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2})\Pi(\sigma^{2})
.

Bernoulli.

A Bernoulli random variable
E\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)
, with
p\in(0,1)
, has support
\{0,1\}
and probability mass function:

\displaystyle\Pi(e)=p^{e}(1-p)^{1-e},\quad e\in\{0,1\}.

(B.4)

In our tree prior, the expansion indicator follows
e_{j\zeta}\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p_{\zeta})
with

p_{\zeta}=\alpha(1+d_{\zeta})^{-\delta}
, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\delta>0
.

Dirichlet.

For
m\geq 2
, a vector
\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{m}
follows a Dirichlet distribution with concentration vector
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{m})^{\top}\in(0,\infty)^{m}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{w}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
, having density:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{w}\mid\boldsymbol{\eta})=\frac{1}{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\eta})}\prod_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}^{\eta_{k}-1},\quad\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{m},

(B.5)

where
\mathcal{B}(\cdot)
is the multivariate Beta function and
\Delta^{m}
is the
m
-dimensional simplex, as defined in Table
A.1
. In
\mathsf{VaSST}
, the operator and feature weight vectors follow

\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{op}})
and

\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{ft}})
, where

\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{O}|}
and
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}\in\Delta^{p}
, respectively.

Categorical.

Let
\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{m}
. A Categorical random variable,
c\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\mathbf{w})
,
takes values in
\{1,\ldots,m\}
with probability mass function:

\displaystyle\Pi(c=j\mid\mathbf{w})=w_{j},\qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}

(B.6)

In
\mathsf{VaSST}
, conditional on weights, we assign operators and features via

\displaystyle o_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}),\quad h_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}),\quad o_{j\zeta}\in\mathcal{O},\;h_{j\zeta}\in\mathcal{X}.

(B.7)

When needed, we identify
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{X}
with index sets of sizes
|\mathcal{O}|
and
p
,
respectively, so that (
B.6
) applies directly.

Uniform.

A continuous random variable
u\sim\mathrm{Unif}(a,b)
, with
a<b
, has support on the interval
(a,b)
and probability density function:

\displaystyle\Pi(u)=\frac{1}{b-a},\quad a<u<b,

(B.8)

and
\Pi(u)=0
otherwise. In the Binary Concrete and Gumbel-Softmax continuous relaxations in Section
4
, the
\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
distribution was used to reparameterize the discrete structural variables. Further, in simulation experiments in Section
5.1
, uniform distributions were used to generate the input variables.

Appendix G
Technical Details

G.1
Marginalization of the Model Regression Parameters

The full joint posterior distribution as stated in (
7
) in Section
4
is:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Pi(\Theta,\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})&\propto p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T},\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})\Pi(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\prod_{j=1}^{K}\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}}\Pi(e_{j\zeta})\Pi(o_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\Pi(h_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}).\end{split}

(G.1)

Under the Gaussian likelihood and the conjugate
\mathrm{NIG}
prior:

\mathbf{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2},\mathbf{T}\sim\mathrm{N}_{n}(\mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}),\quad\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2}\sim\mathrm{N}_{K+1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}),\quad\sigma^{2}\sim\mathrm{IG}(a_{0},b_{0}),

the posterior remains
\mathrm{NIG}
:

\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2},\mathbf{T},\mathbf{y}\sim\mathrm{N}_{K+1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}),\quad\sigma^{2}\mid\mathbf{T},\mathbf{y}\sim\mathrm{IG}(a_{n},b_{n}),

(G.2)

where the posterior hyperparameters are:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}+\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{T},\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}+\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{y}),\quad a_{n}=a_{0}+\tfrac{n}{2},\quad b_{n}=b_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{y}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{n}\right).

Integrating out
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})
yields the marginal likelihood:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T})

\displaystyle=\int p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T},\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})\Pi(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})\,d\boldsymbol{\beta}\,d\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(a_{n})}{\Gamma(a_{0})}\frac{|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}}}{|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}}}\frac{b_{0}^{a_{0}}}{b_{n}^{a_{n}}}(2\pi)^{-\tfrac{n}{2}},

(G.3)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function as in Table
A.1
. Up to additive constants independent of
\mathbf{T}
, the log-marginal likelihood is therefore:

\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T})=\tfrac{1}{2}\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n}|-a_{n}\log b_{n}+\log\Gamma(a_{n})+C,

(G.4)

where
C
collects terms independent of
\mathbf{T}
.
Consequently, the marginal posterior over the tree parameters
\Theta
becomes:

\displaystyle\Pi(\Theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})\propto p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{T})\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\prod_{j=1}^{K}\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}}\Pi(e_{j\zeta})\Pi(o_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\Pi(h_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}),

(G.5)

which is the quantity of interest we approximate using an optimal variational family obtained by optimizing the variational objective.

G.2
Derivation of the Analytical Kullback–Leibler Divergence Terms

In this section, we provide a detailed derivation of the Kullback-Leibler (
\mathrm{KL}
) term in (
15
) in Section
3.2
. Specifically, this term corresponds to the
\mathrm{KL}
divergence between the variational posterior
q_{\phi}(\Theta)
in (
4
) and the prior
\Pi(\Theta)
over the tree parameters in (
6
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{KL}&(q_{\phi}(\Theta)\parallel\Pi(\Theta))=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\Theta)}\left[\log\frac{q_{\phi}(\Theta)}{\Pi(\Theta)}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\Theta)}\left[\log\frac{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\prod_{j=1}^{K}\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}}q_{\phi}(e_{j\zeta})q_{\phi}(o_{j\zeta})q_{\phi}(h_{j\zeta})}{\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\prod_{j=1}^{K}\prod_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}}\Pi(e_{j\zeta})\Pi(o_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\Pi(h_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\Theta)}\left[\log\frac{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})}{\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})}+\log\frac{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})}{\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})}+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}}\left(\log\frac{q_{\phi}(e_{j\zeta})}{\Pi(e_{j\zeta})}+\log\frac{q_{\phi}(o_{j\zeta})}{\Pi(o_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})}+\log\frac{q_{\phi}(h_{j\zeta})}{\Pi(h_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})}\right)\right]\\
&=\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\parallel\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}))+\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\parallel\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}))+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}}\bigg[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(e_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(e_{j\zeta}))\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})}[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(o_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(o_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}))]+\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})}[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(h_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(h_{j\zeta}|\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}))]\bigg].\end{split}

(G.6)

Now, we derive the analytic forms of each of the terms in the final sum in (
G.6
).

Lemma G.1

(
\mathrm{KL}
divergence between Dirichlet distributions)
.

Let
q\equiv\mathrm{Dir}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})
and

\pi\equiv\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
on
\Delta^{m}
, where

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}},\boldsymbol{\eta}\in(0,\infty)^{m}
. Define
\tilde{\eta}_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\tilde{\eta}_{k}
.
Then:

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathrm{Dir}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})\,\|\,\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right)=\log\frac{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\eta})}{\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\tilde{\eta}_{k}-\eta_{k})\bigl[\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{k})-\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{0})\bigr],

(G.7)

where
\mathcal{B}(\cdot)
and
\Psi(\cdot)
are the multivariate Beta and Digamma functions, respectively, as defined in Table
A.1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{w}\in\Delta^{m}
such that
\mathbf{w}\sim q
. From the definition of
\mathrm{KL}
divergence, we conclude:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathrm{Dir}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})\,\|\,\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right)&=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{q(\mathbf{w})}{\pi(\mathbf{w})}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\Bigg[\log\frac{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\eta})}{\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\tilde{\eta}_{k}-\eta_{k})\log w_{k}\Bigg]\\
&=\log\frac{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\eta})}{\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}(\tilde{\eta}_{k}-\eta_{k})\,\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log w_{k}].\end{split}

(G.8)

It remains to compute
\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log w_{k}]
under
q\equiv\mathrm{Dir}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})
. For any
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, using differentiation under the integral sign:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{k}}\int_{\Delta^{m}}q(\mathbf{w})\,d\mathbf{w}=\int_{\Delta^{m}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{k}}q(\mathbf{w})\,d\mathbf{w}.

(G.9)

Moreover:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\log q(\mathbf{w})=-\log\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})+\sum_{r=1}^{m}(\tilde{\eta}_{r}-1)\log w_{r}\\
\implies&\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{k}}\log q(\mathbf{w})=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{k}}\log\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})+\log w_{k}\\
\implies&\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{k}}q(\mathbf{w})=q(\mathbf{w})\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{k}}\log\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})+\log w_{k}\right).\end{split}

(G.10)

Substituting (
G.10
) into (
G.9
) and noting that
\int_{\Delta^{m}}q(\mathbf{w})\,d\mathbf{w}=1
gives:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{k}}\log\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})+\int_{\Delta^{m}}q(\mathbf{w})\log w_{k}\,d\mathbf{w}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{k}}\log\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})+\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log w_{k}],

(G.11)

and therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log w_{k}]=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{k}}\log\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{\eta}_{k}}\left[\sum_{r=1}^{m}\log\Gamma(\tilde{\eta}_{r})-\log\Gamma(\tilde{\eta}_{0})\right]=\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{k})-\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{0}).

(G.12)

Substituting this expression into (
G.8
) gives (
G.7
), completing the proof.
∎

Now, an application of Lemma
G.1
yields the following:

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})\parallel\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}))

\displaystyle=\log\!\frac{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{op}})}{\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{op}})}+\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{O}|}(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{op},k}-\eta_{\mathrm{op},k})\left(\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{op},k})-\Psi(\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{O}|}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{op}})\right),

(G.13)

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})\parallel\Pi(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}))

\displaystyle=\log\!\frac{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{ft}})}{\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{ft}})}+\sum_{k=1}^{p}(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{ft},k}-\eta_{\mathrm{ft},k})\left(\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{ft},k})-\Psi(\mathbf{1}_{p}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{ft}})\right),

(G.14)

where
\mathbf{1}_{m}
is a
m
-dimensional vector of ones as in Table
A.1
.
By definition, the
\mathrm{KL}
divergence between two Bernoulli distributions
\mathrm{Ber}(\tilde{p})
and
\mathrm{Ber}(p)
is:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathrm{Ber}(\tilde{p})\,\|\,\mathrm{Ber}(p)\right)=\tilde{p}\log\frac{\tilde{p}}{p}+(1-\tilde{p})\log\frac{1-\tilde{p}}{1-p}.\end{split}

(G.15)

Consequently using (
G.15
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(e_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(e_{j\zeta}))=\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}\log\frac{\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}}{p_{\zeta}}+(1-\tilde{p}_{j\zeta})\log\frac{1-\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}}{1-p_{\zeta}}.

(G.16)

Similarly, the
\mathrm{KL}
divergence between two categorial distributions
\mathrm{Cat}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}})
and
\mathrm{Cat}(\mathbf{w})
is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathrm{Cat}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}})\,\|\,\mathrm{Cat}(\mathbf{w})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\tilde{\pi}_{k}\log\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{k}}{w_{k}}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\tilde{\pi}_{k}\left[\log\tilde{\pi}_{k}-\log w_{k}\right],

(G.17)

which implies:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(o_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(o_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}}))\right]&=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{O}|}\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{op}}_{j\zeta,k}\left[\log(\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{op}}_{j\zeta,k})-\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{op}})}[\log w_{k}]\right]\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{O}|}\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{op}}_{j\zeta,k}\left[\log(\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{op}}_{j\zeta,k})-\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{op},k})+\Psi(\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{|\mathcal{O}|}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{op}})\right],\end{split}

(G.18)

where the last equality follows from (
G.12
) in the proof of Lemma
G.1
. Analogously:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}})}\left[\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(h_{j\zeta})\parallel\Pi(h_{j\zeta}\mid\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ft}}))\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{ft}}_{j\zeta,k}\left[\log(\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{ft}}_{j\zeta,k})-\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{ft},k})+\Psi(\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{p}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{ft}})\right].\end{split}

(G.19)

Putting together (
G.13
), (
G.14
), (
G.16
), (
G.18
), and (
G.19
) in (
G.6
), we get the following composite expression for the
\mathrm{KL}
component:

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi}(\Theta)\parallel\Pi(\Theta))=\log\!\frac{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{op}})}{\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{op}})}+\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{O}|}(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{op},k}-\eta_{\mathrm{op},k})\left(\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{op},k})-\Psi(\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{O}|}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{op}})\right)+\log\!\frac{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathrm{ft}})}{\mathcal{B}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{ft}})}

(G.20)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{p}(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{ft},k}-\eta_{\mathrm{ft},k})\left(\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{ft},k})-\Psi(\mathbf{1}_{p}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{ft}})\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{\zeta\in\mathcal{Z}_{D}}\Bigg[\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}\log\frac{\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}}{p_{\zeta}}+(1-\tilde{p}_{j\zeta})\log\frac{1-\tilde{p}_{j\zeta}}{1-p_{\zeta}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{O}|}\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{op}}_{j\zeta,k}\left[\log(\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{op}}_{j\zeta,k})-\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{op},k})+\Psi(\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{|\mathcal{O}|}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{op}})\right]+\sum_{k=1}^{p}\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{ft}}_{j\zeta,k}\left[\log(\tilde{\pi}^{\mathrm{ft}}_{j\zeta,k})-\Psi(\tilde{\eta}_{\mathrm{ft},k})+\Psi(\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{p}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\mathrm{ft}})\right]\Bigg].
