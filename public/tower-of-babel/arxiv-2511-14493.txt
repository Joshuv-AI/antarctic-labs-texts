On dissonance of self-conformal measures in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} Thanks: This research was supported by the Research Council of Finland via the project Approximate incidence geometry , grant no. 355453.
Aleksi Pyörälä

Abstract
. Let μ \mu be a self-conformal measure on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . In this note we establish conditions for μ \mu under which (0.1) dim ( μ ∗ ν ) = min ⁡ { d , dim μ + dim ν } \dim(\mu*\nu)=\min\{d,\dim\mu+\dim\nu\} holds when ν \nu is any Ahlfors-regular or self-conformal measure on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . Our main result states that ( 0.1 ) holds if μ \mu is totally non-linear and not supported on a smooth hypersurface. We also establish (likely non-sharp) algebraic conditions for μ \mu under which ( 0.1 ) holds without assuming non-linearity. The proofs combine recent results on scaling sceneries of self-conformal measures with a Marstrand-type projection theorem for product sets due to López and Moreira.

1.
Introduction

Two exact-dimensional Borel probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are said to
dissonate
if

(1.1)

\dim(\mu*\nu)=\min\{d,\dim\mu+\dim\nu\}.

Here and throughout, we use
\dim
to denote the Hausdorff dimension of both sets and measures:

\displaystyle\dim\mu=\essinf_{x\sim\mu}\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\mu(B(x,r))}{\log r}=\inf\{\dim A:\ \mu(A)>0\}

for any Borel measure
\mu
. If (
1.1
) fails,
\mu
and
\nu
are said to
resonate
. For example, any exact-dimensional measures
\mu
and
\nu
dissonate after randomly scaling and rotating either of them, as was shown by López and Moreira
[
20
, Theorem 2.3]
. Because of this, resonance between measures is in some sense an exceptional phenomenon, and might be interpreted as evidence of common arithmetic structure shared by both measures.

The absence of arithmetic structure in self-conformal measures has been an active research topic in the past years, not only in the form of their dissonance but also in the rate of their Fourier decay,
L^{2}
-flattening behavior and the prevalence of normal numbers in their support, see
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
5
,
25
,
6
,
7
,
3
]
for a non-exhaustive list of recent advances in these connected topics. To very shortly illustrate the connection to dissonance, note that Salem measures, i.e. measures with fastest possible Fourier decay, dissonate with
every
exact-dimensional Borel measure, and the dimension of iterated convolutions of
L^{2}
-flattening measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
approaches
d
. It is believed that in general, a self-conformal measure should be “highly non-arithmetic” in every such aspect, at least in the presence of suitable non-linearity: Their Fourier transforms should have fast decay, they should be
L^{2}
-flattening and their supports should contain many normal numbers. In particular, self-conformal measures should dissonate, possibly with
any other
exact-dimensional measure.

In this note we study dissonance between probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
when one of them is self-conformal. We will next recall the definition of self-conformal measures.

Let
\Gamma
be a finite set with
\#\Gamma\geqslant 2
and let
\Phi=\{f_{i}\}_{i\in\Gamma}
be an iterated function system of contractive conformal maps on
(-1,1)^{d}
, see Section 2 for the definitions. A Borel probability measure
\mu
on
(-1,1)^{d}
is called
self-conformal
if there exist numbers
0<p_{i}<1
such that
\sum_{i\in\Gamma}p_{i}=1
and

(1.2)

\mu=\sum_{i\in\Gamma}p_{i}\cdot f_{i}\mu,

where we write
f\mu:=\mu\circ f^{-1}
. A conformal iterated function system
\Phi
is
totally non-linear
if for every
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm conf}((-1,1)^{d})
there exists
f\in\Phi
such that
g\circ f\circ g^{-1}
is not an affine function.

In general, a self-conformal measure need not dissonate even with itself. However, the only known examples of resonating self-conformal measures arise from iterated function systems where the set of
rotations

\{Df(x)\|Df(x)\|^{-1}:\ f\in\Phi,x\in(-1,1)^{d}\}
is contained in a strict subset of
SO(d)
. Prohibiting such a restriction, for example by assuming that
\Phi
is totally non-linear and does not preserve a hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it seems at least possible that self-conformal measures associated to
\Phi
would dissonate with any other exact-dimensional measure. While dissonance with arbitrary measures is far beyond the scope of the present work, we manage to establish that many self-conformal measures dissonate with a class of measures which we call
tangent-regular
, a class which contains all Ahlfors-regular and self-conformal probability measures.

Definition 1.1
.

A Borel probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
tangent-regular
if the following holds for
\nu
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
: If
P
is any tangent distribution of
\nu
at
x
, then
\dim\eta=\dim\nu
for
P
-almost every
\eta
.

For the definition of tangent distributions, see Section 2. For example, Ahlfors-regular and self-conformal measures are tangent-regular. See Lemma
2.8
for a proof of the latter claim.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Phi
be a totally non-linear conformal iterated function system on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\mu
be a self-conformal measure associated to
\Phi
. Suppose that either

(1)

d=1
and
\Phi
is real-analytic,

(2)

d=2
and
\mu
is not supported on an analytic curve, or

(3)

d\geqslant 3
and
\mu
is not supported on an affine hyperplane or a sphere.

Then for any tangent-regular measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\dim(\mu*\nu)=\min\{d,\dim\mu+\dim\nu\}.

One can also define dissonance between sets by replacing in (
1.1
) the convolution
\mu*\nu
by the sumset
X+Y=\{x+y:\ x\in X,Y\in Y\}
for any
X,Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, one may ask whether a
self-conformal set
dissonates with a given Borel set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Recall that given a conformal iterated function system
\Phi
, there exists a unique compact set
K
, called the attractor of
\Phi
, which satisfies

(1.3)

K=\bigcup_{i\in\Gamma}f_{i}(K).

The set
K
is also called the self-conformal set associated to
\Phi
and for any self-conformal measure
\mu
associated to
\Phi
it holds that
K={\rm spt}(\mu)
. The family
\Phi
satisfies the
strong separation condition
if
f_{i}(K)
are disjoint for every
i\in\Gamma
.

Since
[
12
, Proposition 2.5]
ensures that a self-conformal set
K
always contains a self-conformal set
K^{\prime}
with strong separation condition and dimension arbitrarily close to that of
K
, and
[
23
, Lemma 2.1]
ensures that
K^{\prime}
supports a self-conformal measure of dimension arbitrarily close to that of
K^{\prime}
, Theorem
1.2
as well as the variants below imply the corresponding results for dissonance between sets.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
\Phi
be a totally non-linear conformal iterated function system on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
X
denote the attractor of
\Phi
. Suppose that either

(1)

d=1
and
\Phi
is real-analytic,

(2)

d=2
and
X
is not contained in an analytic curve, or

(3)

d\geqslant 3
and
X
is not contained in an affine hyperplane or a sphere.

Then for any Ahlfors-regular or self-conformal set
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\dim(X+Y)=\min\{d,\dim X+\dim Y\}.

We believe the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
should hold with only the assumption that the totally non-linear self-conformal measure
\mu
is not supported in an affine hyperplane. When
\mu
and
\nu
are self-conformal measures in the plane, the cases not covered by Theorem
1.2
are easy enough to handle separately that we obtain a sharper statement.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Phi
and
\Psi
be totally non-linear conformal iterated function systems on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and let
\mu
and
\nu
be self-conformal measures associated to
\Phi
and
\Psi
. Suppose that
\mu
and
\nu
are not supported on parallel lines. Then

\dim(\mu*\nu)=\min\{2,\dim\mu+\dim\nu\}.

The assumption that
\mu
and
\nu
are not supported on parallel lines is necessary. However, it would likely suffice to assume that only
\Phi
is totally non-linear.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
\Phi
and
\Psi
be totally non-linear conformal iterated function systems on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and let
X
and
Y
denote their attractors. Suppose that
X
and
Y
are not contained in parallel lines. Then

\dim(X+Y)=\min\{2,\dim X+\dim Y\}.

1.1.
History

Let us then review what was know of dissonance of self-conformal measures prior to Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
. First, the linear case
d=1
is known, or at least follows easily from existing results. Namely, in
[
9
, Theorem 1.5]
it was shown that if
\mu
and
\nu
are self-conformal measures on
\mathbb{R}
, then (
1.1
) holds under an algebraic assumption on the defining interated function systems. While the result for totally non-linear self-conformal measures without algebraic assumptions is not explicitly stated, it follows with the same argument. Nevertheless, we will include a short argument covering this case in the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

In the plane, Moreira and Zamudio
[
10
, Theorem B]
have shown that the conclusion of Corollary
1.5
holds for a class of self-conformal sets in
\mathbb{C}
with the strong separation condition and an algebraic assumption on the defining iterated function systems. There the authors also considers sums of more than just two self-conformal sets. For a similar but non-intersecting class of
self-affine sets
a result analogous to Corollary
1.5
was obtained in
[
24
]
, also under an algebraic assumption on the defining iterated function systems.

In dimensions
d\geqslant 3
we are not aware of any existing results on dissonance of self-conformal sets or measures.

1.2.
Versions of Theorem
1.2
with algebraic conditions

It is possible to replace the assumption of non-linearity in Theorem
1.2
with various algebraic conditions on the defining iterated function system.

We let
CO(d)
denote the conformal (special) orthogonal group of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

CO(d)=\{rO:\ r>0,O\in SO(d)\}.

In particular, if
f
is an orientation-preserving conformal map on
U
, then
Df(x)\in CO(d)
for every
x\in U
. For a contractive conformal map
f
with unique fixed point
x_{0}
, we write

\displaystyle\lambda(f)

\displaystyle:=\|Df(x_{0})\|\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle O(f)

\displaystyle:=Df(x_{0})\|Df(x_{0})\|^{-1}\in SO(d).

If
G
is a topological group and
\mathcal{A}\subset G
, we write

\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle:=\overline{\{A_{1}A_{2}\cdots A_{n}:\ n\geqslant 1,A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\}}.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\Phi
be a totally non-linear conformal iterated function system on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\mu
be a self-conformal measure associated to
\Phi
and let
K
denote the attractor of
\Phi
. Suppose that

(1.4)

\langle\{\lambda(f)O(f),\lambda(f)^{-1}O(f)^{-1}:\ f\in\Phi\}\rangle=CO(d).

Then for any tangent-regular measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\dim(\mu*\nu)=\min\{d,\dim\mu+\dim\nu\}.

The condition (
1.4
) in Theorem
1.6
is the most general one the present method allows, but is likely stronger than what is needed for the conclusion. In particular, it seems possible that the assumption
\langle\{O(f):\ f\in\Phi\}\rangle=SO(d)
could be enough. At least we are not aware of any counterexamples.

We will next review some algebraic assumpions on
\Phi
which ensure that the condition (
1.4
) of Theorem
1.6
is satisfied. Let

O(\Phi):=\{O(f),O(f)^{-1}:\ f\in\Phi\}.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
\Phi
be a totally non-linear conformal iterated function system on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geqslant 3
, and let
\mu
be a self-conformal measure associated to
\Phi
. Suppose that

(1)

\langle O(\Phi)\rangle=SO(d)
,

(2)

there exist
f,g\in\Phi
such that
\frac{\log\lambda(f)}{\log\lambda(g)}\not\in\mathbb{Q}
, and

(3)

there exist
f,g\in\Phi
with
O(f)=O(g)
and
\lambda(f)\neq\lambda(g)
.

Then for any tangent-regular measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\dim(\mu*\nu)=\min\{d,\dim\mu+\dim\nu\}.

The conditions (1) - (3) together ensure that (
1.4
) holds so that we may apply Theorem
1.6
. While one might expect that (1) and (2) (at least in some form) are necessary for (
1.4
), we do not know whether the assumption (3) is necessary. In the plane we have the following.

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
\Phi
be a totally non-linear conformal iterated function system on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and let
\mu
be a self-conformal measure associated to
\Phi
. Suppose that

(1)

\langle O(\Phi)\rangle=SO(2)
,

(2)

there exist
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}\in\Phi
(not necessarily distinct) such that
O(f_{1})=O(f_{2})
,
O(f_{3})=O(f_{4})
and
\frac{\log\lambda(f_{1})-\log\lambda(f_{2})}{\log\lambda(f_{3})-\log\lambda(f_{4})}\not\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Then for any tangent-regular measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\dim(\mu*\nu)=\min\{2,\dim\mu+\dim\nu\}.

Again, the conditions (1) and (2) ensure that (
1.4
) holds. In the
d=2
case, however, both (1) and (2) are necessary for this: Since
SO(2)\cong\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, one can choose affine maps
f_{i}(x)=r_{i}O_{i}x+a_{i}
in such a way that
\langle\{O_{i}\}\rangle=SO(2)
and
\frac{\log r_{i}}{\log r_{j}}\not\in\mathbb{Q}
for every
i\neq j
, but
\langle\{r_{i}O_{i}:\ i\in\Gamma\}\rangle
is contained in a one-dimensional submanifold of
CO(2)
.

Ultimately, the proofs of both Corollaries
1.7
and
1.8
rely on Theorem
1.6
, and it seems at least possible that just the assumption (1) could be enough for both conclusions.

1.3.
Dissonance of self-conformal measures

For convolutions between two self-conformal measures, we have very similar algebraic conditions. For
A,B\subset CO(d)
, write
A^{-1}:=\{a^{-1}:\ a\in A\}
and
AB:=\{ab:\ a\in A,b\in B\}
.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
\Phi
and
\Psi
be totally non-linear conformal iterated function systems on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geqslant 3
, and let
\mu
and
\nu
be self-conformal measures associated to
\Phi
and
\Psi
. Suppose that

(1)

\langle O(\Phi)O(\Psi)^{-1}\rangle=CO(d)
,

(2)

there exist
f,g\in\Phi\cup\Psi
such that
\frac{\log\lambda(f)}{\log\lambda(g)}\not\in\mathbb{Q}
, and

(3)

there exist
f,g\in\Phi\cup\Psi
such that
O(f)=O(g)
and
\lambda(f)\neq\lambda(g)
.

Then

\dim(\mu*\nu)=\min\{d,\dim\mu+\dim\nu\}.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
\Phi
and
\Psi
be totally non-linear conformal iterated function systems on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and let
\mu
and
\nu
be self-conformal measures associated to
\Phi
and
\Psi
. Suppose that

(1)

\langle O(\Phi)O(\Psi)^{-1}\rangle=CO(2)
, and

(2)

there exist
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}\in\Phi\cup\Psi
(not necessarily distinct) with
O(f_{1})=O(f_{2})
,
O(f_{3})=O(f_{4})
and
\frac{\log\lambda(f_{1})-\log\lambda(f_{2})}{\log\lambda(f_{3})-\log\lambda(f_{4})}\not\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Then

\dim(\mu*\nu)=\min\{2,\dim\mu+\dim\nu\}.

1.4.
Orthogonal projections

In
[
8
]
Bruce and Jin showed that under a suitable “denseness”-assumption on
\langle\{Df(x)\|Df(x)\|^{-1}:\ x\in K,f\in\Phi\}\rangle
, the dimension on self-conformal measures is preserved under orthogonal projection onto any linear subspace. Using Proposition
3.1
we may verify this assumption for self-conformal measures which are totally non-linear.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
\Phi
be a totally non-linear conformal iterated function system on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\mu
be a self-conformal measure associated to
\Phi
. Suppose that
\mu
is not supported on an affine hyperplane or a sphere. Then for any
0<k<d
and any orthogonal projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
,

\dim\mu\circ\pi^{-1}=\min\{k,\dim\mu\}.

Corollary 1.12
.

Let
\Phi
be a totally non-linear conformal iterated function system on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with attractor
K
. Suppose that
K
is not contained in an affine hyperplane or a sphere. Then for any
0<k<d
and any orthogonal projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
,

\dim\pi(X)=\min\{k,\dim X\}.

Theorem
1.11
follows by combining Proposition
3.1
with
[
8
, Theorem 1.3]
. In
[
8
, Theorem 1.3]
it was shown that the conclusion of Theorem
1.11
holds if one assumes
[
8
, Property (A2)]
. When
\mu
is totally non-linear and not supported on a smooth hypersurface, this property is ensured by Proposition
3.1
: Since
SO(d)
is compact, Proposition
3.1
ensures that
\langle\{Df(x)\|Df(x)\|^{-1}:\ x\in K,f\in\Phi\}\rangle=SO(d)
. We leave the details to the interested reader.

1.5.
Proof strategy

Our proof is heavily inspired by Hochman’s proof of
[
14
, Theorem 1.39]
which shows Theorem
1.2
for convolutions between self-similar and Ahlfors-regular measures on the line under the strong separation condition and an algebraic assumption on the defining iterated function system. The proof consists of the following three steps. Let
\mu
and
\nu
be as in Theorem
1.2
.

Step 1.
Using the fact that
\mu
is self-conformal we show that the
tangent distributions
(see Section 2) of
\mu\times\nu
have certain invariance under linear maps in the first
d
coordinates. Specifically, if
P
is (an ergodic component of) a tangent distribution of
\mu\times\nu
, then

P\circ(L,I)^{-1}=P

for a rich class of conformal linear maps
L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
(L,I)
denotes the map
\mu\times\nu\mapsto(\mu\circ L^{-1})\times\nu
. This invariance is recorded in Proposition
4.1
, and its proof very closely follows the proof of
[
9
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Step 2.
Relying on the assumption of non-linearity (or, in later theorems, the algebraic assumptions on
\Phi
), we show that the class of linear maps
L
from
Step 1
actually equals the entire conformal orthogonal group
CO(d)
. The deduction of this from the assumption of non-linearity is recorded in Proposition
3.1
which is perhaps the main technical novelty of this paper.

Step 3.
This step is an adaptation of
[
14
, Proposition 1.38]
which in turn uses the local entropy averages machinery developed in Hochman and Shmerkin’s seminal work
[
15
]
. Using the invariance established in
Steps 2
and
3
together with a projection theorem of López and Moreira
[
20
]
for product sets, we show that the dimension of (the ergodic components of) tangent distributions of
\mu\times\nu
is preserved under the linear map
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
(x,y)\mapsto x+y
. This is done in Proposition
2.13
. Consequently, the dimension of
\mu\times\nu
is preserved under the same map. Here the tangent-regularity of
\nu
is crucially used, exactly as Ahlfors regularity was used in
[
14
, Theorem 1.39]
, to deduce that the dimension of ergodic components of tangent distributions of
\mu\times\nu
equal the dimension of
\mu\times\nu
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open and convex set. Denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\rm conf}(U)
the set of injective, continuously differentiable functions
f:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\overline{f(U)}\subset U
,
\frac{Df(x)}{\|Df(x)\|}\in O(d)
for every
x\in U
, and the function
x\mapsto Df(x)
is
\alpha
-Hölder for some
\alpha>0
. Elements of
\mathcal{C}_{\rm conf}(U)
are called
conformal maps
of
U
. For
d=2
, conformal maps are complex analytic, and for
d\geqslant 3
, they are Möbius transformations by Liouville’s theorem. A function
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm conf}(U)
is contractive if
\sup_{x\in U}\|Df_{i}(x)\|<1
. For a contractive conformal map
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm conf}(U)
with unique fixed point in
x_{0}\in U
we introduce the following notations:

\displaystyle\lambda_{f}(x)

\displaystyle:=\|Df(x)\|,\qquad

\displaystyle x\in U

\displaystyle O_{f}(x)

\displaystyle:=Df(x)\|Df(x)\|^{-1},\qquad

\displaystyle x\in U

\displaystyle\lambda(f)

\displaystyle:=\lambda_{f}(x_{0}),

\displaystyle O(f)

\displaystyle=O_{f}(x_{0}).

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is equipped with the maximum metric and
B(x,t)
denotes the closed ball centered at
x
and of radius
t
. In particular,
B(0,1)=[-1,1]^{d}
.

2.1.
Scaling sceneries

Let
\mathcal{P}(A)
denote the set of Borel probability measures on a metrizable topoligical space
A
. For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(B(0,1))
,
x\in{\rm spt}(\mu)
and
t\geqslant 0
, let
\mu_{x,t}
denote the probability measure on
B(0,1)
given by

\mu_{x,t}(A)=\frac{\mu(2^{-t}A+x)}{\mu(B(x,2^{-t}))}.

Weak-
∗
accumulation points of
(\mu_{x,t})_{t\geqslant 0}
are called tangent measures of
\mu
at
x
. For
x\in{\rm spt}(\mu)
and
T\geqslant 1
, define the
scenery of
\mu
up to time
T
as

\langle\mu\rangle_{x,T}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\delta_{\mu_{x,t}}\,dt\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(B(0,1))).

The weak-
∗
accumulation points of
(\langle\mu\rangle_{x,T})_{T\geqslant 1}
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(B(0,1)))
are called
tangent distributions
of
\mu
at
x
. If there is unique accumulation point
P
, we say that
\mu
generates
P
at
x
. For any
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(B(0,1)))
, we define the
dimension
of
P
as

\dim P:=\int\dim\nu\,dP(\nu).

A Borel probability measure
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(B(0,1)))
is a
fractal distribution
if
S_{t}P=P
for every
t\geqslant 0
, and if for any
A\subset\mathcal{P}(B(0,1))
with
P(A)=1
, for
P
-almost every
\mu\in A
and
\mu
-almost every
x\in B(0,1)
it holds that
\mu_{x,t}\in A
for
t\geqslant 0
such that
B(x,2^{-t})\subset B(0,1)
. A fractal distribution
P
is called
ergodic
if it is ergodic under the semi-flow
(S_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(B(0,1)))
.

For
t\geqslant 0
, let
S_{t}
denote the map
\mu\mapsto\mu_{0,t}
defined on measures
\mu
with
0\in{\rm spt}(\mu)
. Tangent distributions are always invariant under
S_{t}
for every
t\geqslant 0

[
14
]
. Slightly abusing notation, for any
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
we let
fP
denote the push-forward of
P
under
\mu\mapsto f\mu=\mu\circ f^{-1}
.

2.2.
Hypersurfaces

Below we collect a few rigidity results on subsets of hypersurfaces. The first is a simple consequence of the implicit function theorem.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a closed set. If
A\times A
is contained in a countable union of smooth hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then there exists an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
A\cap U
is contained in a smooth hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

By Baire category theorem, there exists a smooth hypersurface
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an open set
V_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
(A\times A)\cap V_{0}\subset M
. By
[
19
, Proposition 5.16]
, there exists an open set
V_{1}\subset V_{0}
and a smooth function
F:V_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
Df(x)\neq 0
for every
x\in V_{1}
and
F^{-1}(0)=V_{1}\cap M
. Since not all partial derivatives of
f
are identically zero, by reordering the coordinates (in such a way that
A\times A
remains a product set) we may suppose that for some
x_{0}\in V_{1}\cap M
the partial derivative with respect to the last coordinate is non-zero at
x_{0}
.

Applying the implicit function theorem for
F
at
x_{0}
, there exists an open set
V_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
a smooth map
g:V_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

F^{-1}(0)\cap(V_{2}\times\mathbb{R})=\{(z,g(z)):\ z\in V_{2}\}.

Let
V_{3}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
V_{4}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
and
V_{5}\subset\mathbb{R}
be open sets such that
V_{3}\times V_{4}\subset V_{2}
and
V_{3}\times V_{4}\times V_{5}\subset V_{1}
. Then

\displaystyle(A\times A)\cap(V_{3}\times V_{4}\times V_{5})\cap M

\displaystyle\subset F^{-1}(0)\cap(V_{3}\times V_{4}\times V_{5})

\displaystyle\subset\{(x,y,g(x,y)):\ x\in V_{3},y\in V_{4}\}.

Fixing an arbitrary
a\in A\cap V_{3}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we find further that

\{a\}\times(A\cap(V_{4}\times V_{5}))\subset\{(a,y,g(a,y)):\ y\in V_{4}\}.

In particular, the set
A\cap(V_{4}\times V_{5})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is contained in the graph of
\tilde{g}:V_{4}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\tilde{g}(y)=g(a,y)
which is a smooth hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This completes the proof with
U:=V_{4}\times V_{5}
.
∎

3.
On the conformal orthogonal group

Recall that
CO(d)=\{rO:\ r>0,O\in SO(d)\}
. In this section we establish conditions for certain subsets of
CO(d)
to generate the entire group. If
G
is a topological group and
\mathcal{A}\subset G
, we write

\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle:=\overline{\{A_{1}A_{2}\cdots A_{n}:\ n\geqslant 1,A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\}}.

In this section, the group operations of both
CO(d)
and
SO(d)
are given by multiplication, and the group operation of
\mathbb{R}
is given by addition. The group operation of
SO(d)\times\mathbb{R}
is given by multiplication in the first coordinate and addition in the second.

If
V
is a vector space and
A\subset V
, then we write
{\rm span}(A)
for the set of linear combinations of elements of
A
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open and convex, and let
E\subset U
be such that for any open set
V\subset U
, the set
E\cap V
is not contained in a smooth hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then for any
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\rm conf}(U)
which is non-linear and orientation-preserving, we have

\langle\{Df(x)Df(y)^{-1}:\ x,y\in E\}\rangle=CO(d).

A key role in the proof of Proposition
3.1
is played by the following rigidity property on real-analytic functions of several variables. It is an analogue of the classical identity principle for real-analytic functions of several variables.

Lemma 3.2
.

[Claim 2 in
[
22
]
]
Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open and convex. If
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
is a real-analytic function which is not identically zero, then the set

(3.1)

\{x\in U:\ f(x)=0\}

is contained in a countable union of smooth hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let
U
,
E
and
f
be as in the statement. Since
CO(d)\cong SO(d)\times\mathbb{R}
via
rO\mapsto(O,\log r)
, it suffices to show that

(3.2)

\langle\{(O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1},\log\lambda_{f}(x)-\log\lambda_{f}(y)):\ x,y\in E\}\rangle=SO(d)\times\mathbb{R}.

Recall that here
O_{f}(x)=Df(x)\|Df(x)\|^{-1}\in SO(d)
and
\lambda_{f}(x)=\|Df(x)\|\in\mathbb{R}
.

Denote by
\mathfrak{so}(d)
the Lie algebra of
SO(d)
. The set
\mathfrak{so}(d)
consists of skew-symmetric
d\times d
-matrices and the exponential map
\mathfrak{exp}:\mathfrak{so}(d)\to SO(d)
is given by the converging series
\mathfrak{exp}(A)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{A^{k}}{k!}
. By
[
19
, Proposition 20.8]
,
\mathfrak{exp}
is a smooth diffeomorphism in a neighbourhood
X
of
0\in\mathfrak{so}(d)
. Denote
Y=\mathfrak{exp}(X)
and let
\mathfrak{log}:Y\to X
denote the inverse of
\mathfrak{exp}|_{X}
. Using continuity of
f
, by replacing
U
with a subset we may assume that

O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1}\in Y,\qquad x,y\in U.

In particular,

\mathfrak{log}(O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1})\in X,\qquad x,y\in U.

We also denote the exponential map
\mathfrak{so}(d)\times\mathbb{R}\to SO(d)\times\mathbb{R},\ (v,x)\mapsto(\mathfrak{exp}(v),x)
by
\mathfrak{exp}
. Since
G:=\langle\{(O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1},\log\lambda_{f}(x)-\log\lambda_{f}(y)):\ x,y\in E\}\rangle
is a closed subgroup of
SO(d)\times\mathbb{R}
, it is a Lie subgroup of
SO(d)\times\mathbb{R}
by the closed subgroup theorem, and its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a linear subspace of
\mathfrak{so}(d)\times\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, by
[
19
, Proposition 20.9]
the exponential map
\mathfrak{g}\to G
is just the restriction of
\mathfrak{exp}
to
\mathfrak{g}
. Therefore

(\mathfrak{log}(O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1}),\log\lambda_{f}(x)-\log\lambda_{f}(y))\in\mathfrak{g},\qquad x,y\in U.

The crucial consequence of this inclusion is that also all linear combinations of the vectors
(\mathfrak{log}(O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1}),\log\lambda_{f}(x)-\log\lambda_{f}(y))
belong to
\mathfrak{g}
, in particular, the images of these linear combinations under
\mathfrak{exp}
belong to
G
.

Since
SO(d)
is compact and connected, the exponential map
\mathfrak{exp}:\mathfrak{so}(d)\to SO(d)
is surjective (see
[
13
, Corollary 11.10]
). Therefore, in order to prove (

) it suffices to show that

{\rm span}\{(\mathfrak{log}(O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1}),\log\lambda_{f}(x)-\log\lambda_{f}(y)):\ x,y\in E\}=\mathfrak{so}(d)\times\mathbb{R}.

For this, on the other hand, it suffices to show that if
L:\mathfrak{so}(d)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is any linear functional which is not identically zero, then the function

(3.3)

E\times E\to\mathbb{R},\qquad(x,y)\mapsto L(\mathfrak{log}(O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1}),\log\lambda_{f}(x)-\log\lambda_{f}(y))

is not identically zero. Fix now an arbitrary non-trivial linear functional
L:\mathfrak{so}(d)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and consider the function

(3.4)

F:U\times U\to\mathbb{R},\qquad(x,y)\mapsto L(\mathfrak{log}(O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1}),\log\lambda_{f}(x)-\log\lambda_{f}(y)).

We note that
F
is real-analytic as a composition of the real-analytic maps

\displaystyle U\times U\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\

\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto\log\lambda_{f}(x)-\log\lambda_{f}(y),

\displaystyle U\times U\to Y,\

\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1},

\displaystyle Y\mapsto X,\

\displaystyle A\mapsto\mathfrak{log}(A),

\displaystyle X\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\

\displaystyle(v_{1},v_{2})\mapsto L(v_{1},v_{2}).

We claim next that
F
is not identically zero. Since
L
is non-trivial, this will follow if we manage to show that

(3.5)

\Lambda:={\rm span}\{(\mathfrak{log}(O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1}),\log\lambda_{f}(x)-\log\lambda_{f}(y)):\ x,y\in U\}=\mathfrak{so}(d)\times\mathbb{R}.

Note that here
x
and
y
are chosen from the open set
U
so this equality does not yet imply our goal (
3.2
).

Suppose first that
d=2
. Then
\mathfrak{so}(2)=\mathbb{R}
,
\mathfrak{log}(x)=x
and
f
is a complex differentiable function with
O_{f}(x)={\rm Arg}(f^{\prime}(x))
,
\lambda_{f}(x)=|f^{\prime}(x)|
and

\Lambda={\rm span}\{({\rm Arg}(a){\rm Arg}(b)^{-1},\log|a|-\log|b|):\ a,b\in f^{\prime}(U)\}.

Since
f^{\prime}(U)
is open, by varying
a
and
b
first along a line through origin and then along a circle centered at the origin we find that
\Lambda=\mathbb{R}^{2}=\mathfrak{so}(2)\times\mathbb{R}
.

Suppose then that
d\geqslant 3
. By Liouville’s theorem,
f
is a non-linear (orientation-preserving) Möbius transformation; In particular, its derivative has the form

Df(x)=\|x-a\|^{-2}AR_{x-a},\qquad x\in U,

where
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
A\in SO(d)
and
R_{x-a}
is the reflection with respect to the hyperplane perpendicular to
x-a
. Varying
x\in U
in a line which contains
a
, the matrix
O_{f}(x)O_{f}(y)^{-1}=AR_{x-a}R_{y-a}^{-1}A^{-1}
remains constant and we find that
(0,r)\in\Lambda
for some
r>0
. Thus, in order to complete the proof of (
3.5
) it remains to show that

(3.6)

{\rm span}\{\mathfrak{log}(AR_{x-a}R_{y-a}^{-1}A^{-1}):\ x,y\in U\}=\mathfrak{so}(d).

Note that
AR_{x-a}R_{y-a}^{-1}A^{-1}
is rotation in the plane spanned by
A(x-a)
and
A(y-a)
by twice the angle between
x-a
and
y-a
. Denote
O(x,y):=AR_{x-a}R_{y-a}^{-1}A^{-1}
. For all
x,y
in a dense subset
U^{\prime}\subset U
,
O(x,y)
is an irrational rotation, in particular,
\langle O(x,y)\rangle
is a one-dimensional Lie subgroup of
SO(d)
. Let

(3.7)

\mathfrak{L}(x,y):=\{A\in\mathfrak{so}(d):\ \mathfrak{exp}(tA)\in\langle O(x,y)\rangle\ \text{for every}\ t\geqslant 0\}

denote its Lie algebra which is a one-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathfrak{so}(d)
; see
[
19
, Proposition 20.9]
. Again, the exponential map
\mathfrak{L}(x,y)\to SO(d)
is just
\mathfrak{exp}|_{\mathfrak{L}(x,y)}
.

Now, the second exterior power of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{d}=\{v\wedge w:v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
, is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{so}(d)
, the space of skew-symmetric linear maps
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with isomorphism given by the map

\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathfrak{so}(d),\ v\wedge w\mapsto(x\mapsto\langle v,x\rangle w+\langle w,x\rangle v).

Since the skew-symmetric map

A(x-a)\wedge A(y-a):\ u\mapsto\langle A(x-a),u\rangle A(y-a)+\langle A(y-a),u\rangle A(x-a)

preserves the plane spanned by
A(x-a)
and
A(y-a)
and maps its orthogonal complement to
0
, it follows that
\mathfrak{exp}(A(x-a)\wedge A(y-a))
is a rotation in the plane spanned by
A(x-a)
and
A(y-a)
. In particular,

\mathfrak{exp}(t\cdot(A(x-a)\wedge A(y-a)))\in\langle AR_{x-a}R_{y-a}^{-1}A^{-1}\rangle,\qquad t\geqslant 0

and so
A(x-a)\wedge A(y-a)\in\mathfrak{L}(x,y)
by definition; recall (
3.7
). Since
\mathfrak{L}(x,y)
is a one-dimensional linear subspace of
\Lambda^{2}\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathfrak{log}(AR_{x-a}R_{y-a}^{-1}A^{-1})\in\mathfrak{L}(x,y)
, we have

\mathfrak{log}(AR_{x-a}R_{y-a}^{-1}A^{-1})=\alpha(x,y)\cdot A(x-a)\wedge A(y-a)

for some
\alpha(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Since
U^{\prime}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the set
\{A(x-a)\wedge A(y-a):\ x,y\in U^{\prime}\}
spans
\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{d}\cong\mathfrak{so}(d)
which completes the proof of (
3.6
).

We may now conclude the proof of the proposition. Since the map

from (
3.4
) is a non-trivial real-analytic function, Lemma
3.2
asserts that its zero set belongs to a countable union of smooth hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since

is not locally contained in a hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by assumption, Lemma
2.9
asserts that

cannot be contained in the zero set of

, completing the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
d\geqslant 3
. Let
(O_{1},\ldots,O_{n})\in SO(d)^{n}
,
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
\Gamma=\langle\{r_{i}O_{i},r_{i}^{-1}O_{i}^{-1}:\ 1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}\rangle\leqslant CO(d)
. Suppose that the following conditions hold:

(1)

\langle\{O_{i},O_{i}^{-1}:\ 1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}\rangle=SO(d)
,

(2)

\langle\{\log r_{i},-\log r_{i}:\ 1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}\rangle=\mathbb{R}
, and

(3)

rI\in\Gamma
for some
r>0
.

Then
\Gamma=CO(d)
.

Proof.

We view
\Gamma
as a closed subgroup of
SO(d)\times\mathbb{R}
through the identification
rO\sim(O,\log r)
. Let
L=\Gamma\cap(\{{\rm Id}\}\times\mathbb{R})
and note that by assumption (3),
L\neq\{0\}
. In order to prove the proposition it suffices to show that
L=\mathbb{R}
. Since
L
is a subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
, the function

\psi:\pi_{1}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}/L,\qquad\psi(O)=t+L

where
t\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
(O,t)\in\Gamma
, is a well-defined group homomorphism. We claim that
\psi
is continuous at the identity
I\in\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
. Suppose otherwise, that there exists a sequence
(O_{i},t_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\Gamma
such that
\lim_{i\to\infty}O_{i}=I
but the sequence
(\psi(O_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}=(t_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
does not converge to
0\in\mathbb{R}/L
. By passing onto a subsequence and using compactness of
\mathbb{R}/L
(which follows from the fact that
L\neq\{0\}
), we may assume
\lim_{i\to\infty}t_{i}+L=\lim_{i\to\infty}\psi(O_{i})=t+L\neq 0
. However, since
\lim_{i\to\infty}(O_{i},t_{i})=(I,t)\in\Gamma
, this contradicts the definition of
L
. In particular,
\psi
is continuous at
I
, and as a group homomorphism it is continuous at every point in
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
. Since
\pi_{1}(\Gamma)
is dense in
SO(d)
by the assumption (1), we may extend
\psi
to a group homomorphism
\tilde{\psi}:SO(d)\to\tilde{\psi}(SO(d))\leqslant\mathbb{R}/L
. Denote by
\mathfrak{L}
the Lie algebra of
\tilde{\psi}(SO(d))
which is a closed subgroup of
\mathbb{R}/L
. Then
\tilde{\psi}
gives rise to a Lie algebra homomorphism
d\tilde{\psi}:\mathfrak{so}(d)\to\mathfrak{L}
. Now,
\mathfrak{so}(d)/\ker(d\tilde{\psi})
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{so}(d)
by
[
18
, Corollary 1.55]
, and it is abelian since
\mathfrak{so}(d)/\ker(d\tilde{\psi})
is isomorphic to the abelian Lie algebra
d\tilde{\psi}(\mathfrak{so}(d))
by the first isomorphism theorem for Lie algebras. By semisimplicity of
\mathfrak{so}(d)
,
\mathfrak{so}(d)/\ker(d\tilde{\psi})
therefore trivial, that is
\ker(d\tilde{\psi})=\mathfrak{so}(d)
and so
d\tilde{\psi}\equiv 0
. By e.g.
[
19
, Theorem 20.19]
this implies
\tilde{\psi}\equiv 0
.

Recalling the definition of
\psi
, we find that

\Gamma=\pi_{1}(\Gamma)\times L.

Since
\pi_{2}(\Gamma)=L
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
by assumption (2) and
\Gamma
is closed, this completes the proof.
∎

The only place where the assumption (3) was used in the proof was to deduce that

which in turn was required to ensure that

is compact. Without this assumption the continuity of

at

might fail, for example, if there exists a sequence

such that

but

. However, we do not know whether (3) is necessary for the conclusion of Proposition

to hold.
