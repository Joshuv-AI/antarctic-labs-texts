EngChain : A Symbolic Benchmark for Verifiable Multi-Step Reasoning in Engineering
Ayesha Gull Muhammad Usman Safder Rania Elbadry, Preslav Nakov Zhuohan Xie

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly being applied to specialized, high-stakes domains like engineering, which demands rigorous evaluation of their complex reasoning capabilities. While current benchmarks assess language understanding, factual recall, mathematics or code generation, none capture the integrative reasoning central to engineering where scientific principles, quantitative modeling and practical constraints must converge. To address this gap, we introduce EngChain , a benchmark for verifiable multi-step engineering problem-solving. EngChain contains 90 problems spanning three engineering branches, organized into 9 domains and 20 distinct areas. The problems are generated from symbolic templates with a high degree of randomization to ensure diversity and eliminate the risk of contamination. With this benchmark, we move beyond final answer accuracy with a two-stage evaluation: we first quantitatively verify the numerical and semantic validity of each reasoning step and then introduce LLM-as-a-Judge , an automated system to qualitatively categorize the identified reasoning errors.

Introduction

As large language models (LLMs) transition from general-purpose language tasks to specialized, high-stakes domains such as scientific discovery, industrial design, and engineering analysis, the need for rigorous and meaningful evaluation has become significant
(
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Xie et al. 2023a
)
. The progress of the field is gated by the quality of its benchmarks: without tests that capture practical, physically grounded reasoning, it is difficult to separate genuine capability from sophisticated mimicry.

To meet this challenge, we introduce
EngChain
, a new benchmark for verifiable, multi-step engineering problem-solving. Our benchmark is built on a foundation of symbolic templates, each capable of programmatically generating a vast number of unique, verifiable problems.

This design directly targets two key problems in current LLM evaluation. The first is “benchmark saturation”
(
Ott et al. 2022
)
, where models rapidly achieve superhuman performance on static benchmarks like
GLUE
(
Wang et al. 2019
)
and even their more complex successors such as
BBH
(
Kazemi et al. 2025
)
. The second is a structural limitation: most benchmarks evaluate critical skills in disciplinary silos. General knowledge benchmarks (e.g., MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al. 2020
)
) test broad factual recall, while specialized benchmarks (e.g., MATH
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021b
)
or HumanEval
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
) assess abstract logic and algorithmic translation, respectively.

This fragmentation is limiting for engineering which is fundamentally an integrative discipline that requires the synthesis of these isolated skills, combining scientific principles, mathematical modeling, and practical constraints
(
Moaveni 2019
;
Dym et al. 2012
, e.g.,)
. This holistic, procedural reasoning is not captured by existing benchmarks
(
Felten et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025b
, e.g.,)
, which predominantly focus on final-answer accuracy. In engineering, where a flawed process can lead to catastrophic failure, this evaluation gap is critical. To address this gap,
EngChain
uses symbolic templates to produce integrative reasoning problems and a two-stage evaluation via
LLM-as-a-Judge
that verifies the full reasoning chain rather than just the final answer.

Verifiable multi-step reasoning is fundamental to building trustworthy AI systems across all domains. Concurrent with our work, FinChain
(
Xie et al. 2025
)
introduced a symbolic benchmark for verifiable chain-of-thought financial reasoning. However, the two benchmarks address fundamentally different reasoning types. FinChain evaluates reasoning based on economic principles, whereas
EngChain
evaluates reasoning grounded in the physical laws and practical constraints of engineering. Moreover, FinChain uses a global alignment metric to score the entire reasoning path, while
EngChain
employs a two-stage evaluation that provides both a quantitative Step F1-Score for procedural correctness and a qualitative, diagnostic error analysis using our
LLM-as-a-Judge
system.

In summary, our contributions are threefold:

1.

We introduce
EngChain
, a new symbolic benchmark for verifiable, multi-step engineering problem-solving with 90 problem templates across nine domains.

2.

We propose a rigorous two-stage evaluation framework, including our qualitative
LLM-as-a-Judge
system, that moves beyond final answer accuracy to diagnose reasoning failures.

3.

We present a comprehensive analysis of 11 frontier LLMs, revealing a critical “right for the wrong reasons" gap and identifying that the majority of reasoning failures are due to
Conceptual Errors
, not calculation slips.

2
Related Work

2.1
Benchmarks in Mathematics and Coding

A large body of work on multi-step reasoning benchmarks has focused on foundational, yet isolated, cognitive skills, particularly in mathematics and coding. In mathematical reasoning, benchmarks ranging from grade-school problems (GSM8K)
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
)
to high-school and college-level competition problems (MATH)
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021b
)
establish a clear standard for evaluating abstract logical deduction. This is further refined by HARDMATH
(
Fan et al. 2024
)
for applied mathematics and GSM-Symbolic
(
Mirzadeh et al. 2024
)
for programming and symbolic tasks. Similarly, benchmarks for coding and software engineering evaluate models across different levels of complexity. HumanEval
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
focuses on translating natural language instructions into small, correct code snippets. MBPP
(
Austin et al. 2021
)
and its derivative MBUPP extend this by testing models on a broader set of programming problems with varying difficulty. SWE-Bench
(
Jimenez et al. 2024
)
addresses complex, real-world codebases, measuring a model’s ability to solve practical software engineering challenges. While these benchmarks are essential for capturing foundational skills, their focus on isolated abilities limits their usefulness for engineering. A model can excel at solving abstract equations or writing pure algorithms yet still be unable to apply those skills to a problem constrained by physical laws and material properties.

2.2
Benchmarks in the Physical Sciences

Recognizing this limitation, later benchmarks focus on applied reasoning within the physical sciences. UGPhysics
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
provides broad coverage and tests how models apply mathematical reasoning to physical laws. Building on this, PhysReason
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
emphasizes process-oriented problem solving, guiding models through step-by-step solutions. ABench-Physics
(
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
introduces highly challenging tasks to evaluate advanced capabilities, while PHYBench
(
Qiu et al. 2025
)
targets symbolic computation. NEWTON
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
assesses qualitative understanding, complementing the more quantitative approaches. Other benchmarks, such as LLM-SRBench
(
Shojaee et al. 2025
)
, focus specifically on quantitative scientific modeling. Together, these benchmarks show how models apply mathematical reasoning to well-defined physical laws, marking an important advance towards assessing real-world problem-solving. However, they remain largely confined to physics and have not been widely adopted for evaluating models in real-world engineering practices.

2.3
Benchmarks in Engineering

Current approaches to evaluating engineering reasoning are fractured, split between benchmarks that are overly broad and those that are deep but methodologically limited. On one hand, generalist benchmarks like MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021a
)
and BIG-Bench
(
Luo et al. 2024
)
include engineering topics, but their reliance on multiple-choice, knowledge-based questions primarily tests factual recall rather than the deep, procedural reasoning required for practical engineering problem-solving. On the other hand, more specialized engineering benchmarks, while deeper in scope, either remain confined to a single scientific discipline or lack a scalable, deterministic method for verifying the reasoning process itself. For example, EngiBench
(
Felten et al. 2025
)
provides broad coverage but uses rubric-based scoring for its more complex problems, whereas FEA-Bench
(
Li et al. 2025b
)
evaluates a model’s ability to use professional simulation software, yet this measures tool proficiency rather than fundamental reasoning.

The preceding review makes it clear that the field lacks a benchmark that captures the integrative, cross-disciplinary, and verifiable reasoning central to engineering.
To address this critical gap, we introduce
EngChain
, a benchmark for verifiable, multi-step engineering reasoning. It uses a two-stage evaluation that combines quantitative verification of
each intermediate step with a qualitative error diagnosis, covering 90 distinct problem types across nine domains and three engineering branches.

4
EngChain
Design and Methodology

To ensure the rigor, scalability, and long-term viability of the benchmark, we adopted a programmatic, template-based methodology. Unlike static datasets, which are finite and susceptible to being inadvertently included in the training corpora of future models, a programmatic approach offers several key advantages. It provides virtually limitless scalability and diversity, as a single template can generate thousands of unique problem instances. This design also ensures strong resistance to training data contamination, as it tests for generalizable reasoning on novel problems rather than the memorization of previously seen solutions.

4.1
Content Selection Framework

The selection of content for
EngChain
followed a principled, multi-stage methodology designed to ensure the benchmark is both representative of a formal engineering curriculum and grounded in foundational, real-world principles. The process involved a hierarchical selection of domains, areas, and finally, the specific problems to be templatized.

4.1.1
Domain and Area Selection

The nine core domains that form the backbone of
EngChain
were identified using a two-pronged approach. First, a thorough literature review was conducted, surveying established engineering taxonomies and curricular standards set by leading professional and accreditation bodies. This involved analyzing the program criteria from organizations like the Accreditation Board for Engineering and Technology
(
ABET 2024
)
,
which mandates core competencies in areas like thermodynamics and circuit analysis for accredited degrees. We also reviewed the
educational frameworks of major professional societies, such as the American Society of Mechanical Engineers
(
ASME 2025
)
, the Institute of Electrical and Electronics
Engineers
(
IEEE 2025
)
, and the American Institute of Chemical Engineers
(
AIChE 2025
)
, to ensure our selected domains represent a consensus on the foundational knowledge required for professional practice.

Second, these findings were cross-referenced by querying an LLM (
gemini-2.0-flash
). The model was prompted to identify and rank the most fundamental, cornerstone domains within each of the three primary engineering branches. The full prompt used for this domain-level validation is provided in
Appendix A
.
The final set of nine domains represents a strong consensus between traditional academic structures and the knowledge synthesized by modern AI. A similar two-pronged methodology was then used to identify the most critical areas within each domain: first, by extracting the core pedagogical units from the authoritative textbooks, and second, by cross-referencing this list using a LLM (
gemini-2.0-flash
). The full prompt used for this area-level validation is provided in

Appendix B
.

4.1.2
Template Selection

Once the domains and areas were defined, we developed the 90 problem templates. The core engineering principles, problem typologies, and typical constraints for these templates were sourced from the authoritative and widely-adopted textbooks in each field. This textbook-driven
approach ensures that every problem in
EngChain
is pedagogically sound and central to engineering practice. A complete list of these foundational texts is provided in

Appendix C
.

Crucially, the number of templates developed for each area was not uniform. Instead, it was guided by a
Pedagogical Significance Score
assigned to each area. This score was programmatically determined by querying an expert-persona LLM (
gemini-2.0-flash
) to rate each area’s centrality to the curriculum on a 1-5 scale, as detailed in
Appendix D
. Foundational concepts with higher scores received more extensive coverage. For example, within the Reaction Kinetics domain, the area of Mole Balances (Significance Score: 5) was covered by five templates, while the more specialized application of Levenspiel Plot Interpretation (Significance Score: 1) was covered by a single template. Therefore, the varying number of templates per area is a direct and intentional outcome of our methodology. This ensures that
EngChain
’s structure reflects the hierarchical and foundational nature of engineering knowledge as it is taught and practiced.

4.3
Template Structure and Outputs

The core of our benchmark is the template function, a standardized Python function that serves as the blueprint for a specific type of engineering problem. To ensure consistency, every template sticks to a simple, standardized signature i.e., it takes no arguments and returns a (question, solution) tuple. The question is a formatted string containing the problem statement to be presented to the LLM, whereas the solution is a formatted string containing the complete reasoning trace rather than only a numerical answer. 4.4
Domain-Aware Parameterization and Sanity Checks

EngChain
uses domain-aware parameterization, ensuring that problems are not just mathematically solvable but are also grounded in physical and engineering realism. This is done by constraining all generated values by the principles of the domain being tested.

For instance, in Chemical Engineering, problems use real reactants like Benzene, pre-validated stoichiometric equations, and temperature dependent heat capacity parameters rather than random constant values. In Electrical Engineering, parameters are grounded in fundamental constants (e.g.,
c_{0}
,
\epsilon_{0}
) and real-world materials (e.g.,
Polyethylene
), with signal properties procedurally generated to explicitly test concepts like aliasing. In Mechanical Engineering, problems co-select consistent properties for real materials (e.g.,
6061-T6 Aluminum
) and are context-aware, such as using Mercury for manometer problems. A comprehensive list of these parameterization examples is provided in
Appendix E
, and the authoritative data sources used are listed in
Appendix F
.

Furthermore, across all templates, these already constrained parameters are subjected to multi-layer sanity checks. For example, a chemical engineering template verifies that a final concentration is less than the initial one. A mechanical engineering problem ensures a calculated stress does not exceed the material’s yield strength. Similarly, an electrical engineering template confirms a wave’s propagation speed does not exceed the speed of light. These checks validate that the final combination of values results in a solvable and physically meaningful scenario.

4.5
AI-Assisted Quality Assurance

To ensure the correctness and scalability of the benchmark, we developed an AI-Assisted Quality Assurance workflow. While traditional unit tests are effective, they proved impractical for our generative framework due to the high number of templates and the complexity of verifying physically plausible outputs. Our quality assurance workflow automates this process by employing an
LLM as a first-pass Peer Reviewer
to validate new problem templates before their inclusion in the final benchmark.

The core of this workflow is a programmatic validation script that prepares a comprehensive validation package for the LLM. For each template, this package includes not only the template’s full Python source code but also three unique instances (question-solution pairs) generated by it. This context is crucial, as it allows the LLM to assess both the static logic of the code and the dynamic quality of its output. We then pass this package to the LLM via a carefully engineered prompt. As detailed in
Appendix G
, the prompt instructs the LLM to assume the persona of an expert engineering professor and to evaluate the template against a multi-axis rubric provided in
Appendix H
.

Upon receiving the results, our system programmatically parses and evaluates the scores. A template,
T
, is automatically approved if and only if the score assigned to it for every core category (
c_{i}
) in our rubric meets or exceeds a predefined threshold,
\theta
. We formalize this approval condition as:

\forall c_{i}\in C,\text{Score}(T,c_{i})\geq\theta

We established a high threshold of
\theta=4
based on empirical testing against a control set of known-good and deliberately flawed templates (Examples are provided in
Appendix L
). Any template failing to meet this criterion is automatically flagged for human review. This threshold demonstrated an optimal balance between maximizing automation and reliably catching potential errors.

In practice, this automated review process proved highly effective. Out of the 90 total templates, only 5 (5.55%) were flagged by the LLM and required subsequent human review.

4.6
Symbolic Generation and Combinatorial Diversity

A core feature of
EngChain
is its programmatic generation, designed to create a problem space so vast that it effectively prevents evaluation through memorization. Rather than relying on a static set of problems,
EngChain
is built upon symbolic templates where parameters are systematically randomized within domain-aware, physical constraints.

This design provides strong resistance to training data contamination, as
it makes it statistically improbable that an LLM has encountered any specific problem instance. Consequently, successful performance on
EngChain
is stronger evidence of a model’s ability to generalize its reasoning capabilities. The model is forced to solve each problem from first principles rather than relying on pattern matching or rote recall of seen
solutions.

4.7
Difficulty Scaling

Problem difficulty in
EngChain
is not a subjective label but a calibrated measure determined by systematically varying three primary factors:

•

Conceptual Complexity
refers to the number and integration of engineering principles required. An
Easy
problem typically tests a single, isolated concept like Ohm’s Law. An
Intermediate
problem requires the combination of two or three concepts, such as applying the principle of superposition to calculate a net electric field from multiple point charges. An
Advanced
problem demands the integration of multiple complex ideas, such as using numerical integration on Levenspiel plot data to determine a reactor volume.

•

Mathematical Sophistication
describes the nature of the required calculations.
Easy
problems involve direct algebraic substitution.
Intermediate
problems may require basic calculus or vector addition.
Advanced
problems escalate to solving differential equations or applying numerical methods, such as the trapezoidal rule for integration.

•

Procedural Depth
measures the length and interdependence of the reasoning chain. An
Easy
problem might be solvable in one or two steps. In contrast, an
Advanced
problem involves a long sequence of calculations where the output of each step is a critical input for the next, testing a model’s ability to maintain a coherent and accurate computational trace.

This multi-axis framework for difficulty allows
EngChain
to move beyond a simple easy/hard dichotomy and provides a more fine-grained analysis of an LLM’s
reasoning capabilities.

5
Evaluation Framework

For a verifiable assessment of the entire reasoning process, we compute several key metrics by comparing a model’s generated solution against the ground-truth reasoning trace. The evaluation is multifaceted. It jointly evaluates final answer accuracy, the fluency and semantic quality of the generated solution, and the correctness of the multi-step reasoning chain.

5.1
Final Answer Accuracy

It is determined by comparing the model’s predicted numerical answer (
A_{pred}
) to the ground-truth answer (
A_{gt}
). A response is considered correct only if its relative error is less than a strict tolerance (
\epsilon_{final}
) of 1%, a standard appropriate for engineering precision. The calculation is formalized as follows:

\text{Relative Error}=\left|\frac{A_{gt}-A_{pred}}{A_{gt}}\right|<\epsilon_{final}

5.2
Textual Quality

We assess the textual quality of a generated solution, evaluating its fluency and semantic fidelity against the ground-truth text using a combination of semantic and lexical similarity scores using the most common reference based evaluation metrics
(
Xie et al. 2023b
)
:

•

BERTScore

(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
measures deep semantic similarity by aligning contextual embeddings of tokens from the generated solution and the ground-truth solution. It computes precision, recall, and an F1-score to provide a robust measure of semantic equivalence, capturing nuances that lexical overlap metrics may miss.

•

ROUGE-2

(
Lin 2004
)
quantifies the lexical overlap by calculating the F1-score based on the number of matching adjacent word pairs (bigrams) between the generated and ground-truth texts.

Together, these scores provide a complementary assessment that reflects the model’s ability to produce solutions using appropriate and correctly structured domain-specific terminology.

5.3
Multi-Step Reasoning Verification

This is the core of our evaluation framework which assesses the entire logical chain of a model’s solution. This process moves beyond final answer accuracy by comparing each predicted reasoning step (
P_{j}
) against each ground-truth step (
GT_{i}
). A step is considered valid only if it satisfies two criteria: semantic similarity and numerical correctness.

To formalize this, we compute a combined similarity matrix,
S_{comb}
, by taking the element-wise product (Hadamard product
\circ
) of a semantic similarity matrix (
S_{sem}
) and a numerical correctness matrix (
S_{num}
).

S_{comb}=S_{sem}\circ S_{num}

•

Semantic Similarity (
S_{sem}
)
is computed using a powerful
Cross-Encoder
model (
cross-encoder/stsb-roberta-large
). This model processes each ground-truth and predicted step as a single contextual pair, yielding a high-fidelity similarity score that captures nuanced semantic relationships more effectively than bi-encoder models like the initially used
SentenceTransformer
.

•

Numerical Correctness (
S_{num}
)
is a binary matrix where an entry is 1 if the intermediate numerical answer in a predicted step matches the corresponding ground-truth step within a 2% relative error tolerance, and 0 otherwise.

From this combined matrix, we determine if a step is “matched” by checking if its maximum similarity score exceeds a predefined threshold,
\tau
. We then calculate recall and precision to evaluate the reasoning chain.
Recall
measures the fraction of ground-truth steps the model successfully reproduced:

\text{Recall}=\frac{1}{|GT|}\sum_{i=1}^{|GT|}\mathbb{I}\left(\max_{j}S_{comb}[i,j]>\tau\right)

Precision
measures the fraction of the model’s generated steps that were valid and relevant:

\text{Precision}=\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{j=1}^{|P|}\mathbb{I}\left(\max_{i}S_{comb}[i,j]>\tau\right)

Finally, our primary metric for reasoning quality, the
Step F1-Score
, is calculated as the harmonic mean of the step-level recall and precision defined above, providing a single, robust measure of procedural correctness.

\text{Step F1-Score}=2\cdot\frac{\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

5.4
Automated Error Analysis with
LLM-as-a-Judge

While the Step F1-Score provides a sound quantitative measure of a model’s procedural correctness, it has a significant limitation: it can identify that a reasoning chain is flawed, but it cannot explain why. For instance, as will be discussed later, our preliminary results revealed models that achieved high final-answer accuracy despite very low reasoning scores, a classic case of being “right for the wrong reasons.” This made us realize that to diagnose these failures and determine their cause, a deeper, more qualitative analysis was required. Hence, to move beyond quantitative scores and introduce a layer of explainability, we propose a novel Automated Error Analysis system that employs a large language model as an expert evaluator, or an
LLM-as-a-Judge
.

This system is triggered for any ground-truth step (
GT_{i}
) that fails to find a satisfactory match among the predicted steps. A step is flagged as a “failure” if its maximum combined similarity score falls below an empirically determined threshold,
\tau=0.7
. This condition is formalized as:

\max_{j}S_{comb}[i,j]<\tau

workflow.

The overall workflow of this automated analysis process is illustrated in

At its core, the process is rooted in carefully designed prompt engineering, where the judge model is given a system prompt defining its persona as an expert engineering professor and a set of critical rules for logical consistency. The input to the judge is a structured prompt containing the full problem context, the ground-truth step, and the model’s incorrect step. Providing this complete context is crucial for the judge to accurately classify the failure into one of the five distinct categories, which are defined in

6.2
Experimental Setup

We instantiate the benchmark by sampling 15 instances per symbolic template, each with a distinct random seed, creating a total of 1,350 test cases (90 templates
\times
15 instances) for each model. We evaluate all models under a unified decoding configuration: a low
temperature
of 0.2 and a maximum token limit of 4,096. We use a low temperature (T=0.2) to ensure deterministic, high-confidence outputs and a high token limit (4,096) to allow ample space for detailed, multi-step solutions without premature cut-offs. We use a zero-shot setup with the following standardized reasoning prompt, which is used for all inference tasks:

We use a zero-shot setup with the following standardized reasoning prompt, which is used for all inference tasks:

You are an expert engineer. Solve the following
problem by providing a detailed, structured
solution. Use the exact headings and
formatting provided below.

# Given
List all known variables and their
values with units.

# Find
State the variable(s) to be calculated.

# Formulae
Write down all necessary governing equations
before substituting any values.

# Solution
Provide a step-by-step calculation. Each step
must start on a new line and be formatted exactly
as ’**Step X:**’, where X is the step number.
Show the substitution of values into
the formulae clearly.

# Final Answer
State the final numerical result with its units
in the format: **Answer:** [value] [units]

To process the model outputs, we first segment the text into reasoning steps. We use a regular expression to identify line-start markers that match either the
Step <number>
format (e.g.,
**Step 1**
) or the numbered-list format (e.g.,
1.
). A subsequent regex cleans these prefixes. We then extract numerical values from each step and the final answer using a three-priority heuristic: first, matching the
**Answer:**
tag; second, falling back to the last number following an equals sign (e.g.,
V = 100.0
); and third, taking the last standalone number. If no overall
**Answer:**
tag is found, the final numerical value from the last step is used for verification.

While the quantitative

identifies that a reasoning chain is flawed, it cannot explain why. To diagnose the root causes of these failures, we employed our

system (described in Section
5
) to perform a qualitative error analysis on
Gemini 2.5 Pro
. We selected this model as our representative subject because it is a strong “all-rounder,” achieving both a top-tier reasoning score (17.97%) and the first rank on all language fluency metrics (
BERTScore
,
ROUGE-2
, and
ROUGE-L
). The analysis was done using
GPT-4o

(
OpenAI 2024
)
as the judge or expert evaluator model.

The judge was used to analyze all 4,118 reasoning steps that were flagged by our verifier as a “mismatch” from the ground-truth solution. The results, summarized in
Table 2
, are striking. The single most significant finding is that the vast majority of flagged steps were not errors at all. Our judge classified 73.94% (3,045 instances) as
Alternative Correct
or in other words, valid reasoning paths that simply differed from the ground-truth solution. This overwhelmingly indicates that models are frequently penalized by rigid, single-path evaluations for finding a valid, creative, or different solution, a phenomenon masked by low quantitative scores.

was the dominant failure mode, accounting for 59.1% of all true errors (634 instances). This demonstrates that models are far more likely to fail because they misapply a physical principle or choose the wrong formula, rather than making simple

(14.3%) or

(7.6%). This finding pinpoints the primary weakness of modern LLMs not in their arithmetic, but in their application of deep, domain-specific knowledge. A full breakdown of error distributions by branch, domain, and difficulty can be found in

8
Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced
EngChain
, a symbolic benchmark for verifiable, multi-step engineering reasoning.
Built from a taxonomy of 90 problem templates across 9 domains and 3 engineering branches,
EngChain
provides a verifiable evaluation of multi-step engineering problem-solving, assessing the entire reasoning chain rather than only the final answer.

Our comprehensive evaluation of frontier models reveals a stark “right for the wrong reasons” phenomenon: a universal gap between modest
Final Answer Accuracy
and critically low
Reasoning (F1 Score)
s. Our qualitative analysis further reveals that this gap is not only due to error, but that a striking majority of flagged mismatches are actually
Alternative Correct
solutions, which single-path verifiers would wrongly penalize. For genuine failures, we find that
Conceptual Errors
are the dominant failure mode, not simple calculation mistakes.

Future work will proceed in several key directions. First, we will complete our planned evaluation by benchmarking the science-enhanced, math-enhanced, and general-purpose open models to provide a holistic comparison across all model categories. Second, we will expand the
LLM-as-Judge
analysis to other top-performing models to create diagnostic profiles of their unique failure modes. Third, to rigorously validate our qualitative methodology, we will conduct two crucial follow-up studies. A
Human Evaluation Study
will be performed to establish a definitive ground-truth for our judge’s classifications. This will be complemented by a
Cross-Judge Consistency study
to ensure our error categorizations are not an artifact of our chosen evaluator (
GPT-4o

(
OpenAI 2024
)
). This consistency study will involve a diverse set of judges, including a model from a different leading developer, such as
Claude 4 Opus

(
Anthropic 2025c
)
, and a high-capability open-source model, such as
Qwen 2.5

(
Qwen 2024
)
. Finally, upon completion of these studies, all evaluation scripts, prompts, and detailed results will be publicly released to ensure full reproducibility.

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations in this work that we plan to address in future research.

First, a significant limitation lies in our primary quantitative metric, the
Reasoning (F1 Score)
. By design, this metric measures alignment against a single, pre-defined ground-truth path. However, our own qualitative analysis (Section
7.5
) revealed that a striking majority of steps flagged as “mismatches” were actually
Alternative Correct
solutions. This indicates that our quantitative F1 score, when used in isolation, significantly underestimates the true reasoning capabilities of the models and inherently penalizes valid, alternative problem-solving approaches. Future work should therefore focus on developing more advanced evaluation methods, such as semantic alignment metrics or dynamic, model-based verifiers, which can programmatically accept and validate multiple correct reasoning paths.

Second,
EngChain
is entirely synthetic, generated from symbolic templates. While this design ensures verifiability and contamination resistance, it does not capture the linguistic diversity or contextual ambiguity of real-world engineering. More complex, ill-defined problems often require interpreting non-textual information, such as technical diagrams, schematics, or CAD models, which are not part of our current evaluation. This can be addressed in future versions by expanding the benchmark to include multi-modal tasks and by using real-world engineering documents as seeds for semi-structured problem generation.

Third, the benchmark’s scope, while covering 90 problem types, is currently limited to three core engineering branches. It does not yet include other major fields, such as civil, aerospace, or biomedical engineering. A clear next step is to expand the
EngChain
taxonomy in collaboration with domain experts to cover these additional fields, ensuring a more comprehensive test of engineering knowledge.

Appendix E
Detailed Domain-Aware Parameterization Examples

To ensure that problems are not just mathematically solvable but are also grounded in physical and engineering realism,
EngChain
constrains all generated values by the principles of the domain being tested. The following provides specific examples of this methodology for each engineering branch.

E.0.1
Chemical Engineering

•

Reaction Kinetics and Stoichiometry
: Instead of abstract chemicals, problems select from curated lists of common gas, liquid, and biochemical reactants like Propane, Benzene, and Glucose. More importantly, many problems are built around pre-validated, balanced chemical equations to ensure that the fundamental principles of mass conservation and stoichiometry are carefully upheld from the outset.

•

Thermodynamics
: Problems involving thermodynamic calculations use a multi-layered approach to realism. For common substances, a complete and consistent set of critical properties (
T_{c},P_{c},V_{c}
) is retrieved. For reaction calorimetry, problems utilize tabulated standard heats of formation (
\Delta H_{f}^{\circ}
). Crucially, for heat transfer calculations, the model moves beyond constant specific heats by incorporating temperature-dependent heat capacity parameters (
C_{p}/R=A+BT+CT^{2}+DT^{-2}
), ensuring a high degree of physical accuracy across a range of conditions.

•

Transport Phenomena and Fluid Mechanics
: To ensure realism in fluid dynamics problems, templates draw from extensive tables of fluid properties, including the density and viscosity of common liquids and gases. The benchmark also includes parameters for non-Newtonian fluids (e.g., Polymer Solutions & Melts etc), allowing it to test more complex, real-world rheological behaviors beyond simple Newtonian assumptions.

E.0.2
Electrical Engineering

•

Electromagnetics and Waves
: Instead of using an arbitrary number, the phase velocity of a wave is determined by selecting a specific propagation medium from a curated list (e.g., Distilled Water, Polyethylene, Fused Silica etc). This ensures that all subsequent calculations for parameters like wavelength or wave number are physically consistent with that material’s properties. The generation process is further constrained by fundamental physical constants, including the speed of light in a vacuum (
c_{0}
) and the permittivity of free space (
\epsilon_{0}
).

•

Signals and Systems
: In scenarios bridging continuous and discrete-time domains, such as sampling, the sampling frequency is intentionally set well above the Nyquist rate to model ideal conversion and isolate the effects of the discrete-time system. For purely discrete-time problems, parameters are procedurally constrained to test specific concepts. For instance, signal frequencies are generated as rational multiples of
\pi
, and downsampling problems are deliberately constructed to either cause or avoid aliasing, directly testing the understanding of frequency folding. For fundamental operations like convolution, sequences are kept short and integer-valued to focus the analysis on the computational process.

•

Digital Communications
: For deterministic and random signal analysis, probabilities for discrete random variables are generated as exact rational numbers that sum perfectly to one. In digital modulation scheme problems, carrier frequencies are constrained to be integer multiples of the bit rate to simplify analysis, energy per bit (
E_{b}
) and signal-to-noise ratios (
E_{b}/N_{0}
) are generated in physically typical ranges (e.g., picojoules and realistic dB values), and modulation orders (
M
) are restricted to powers of two. Furthermore, data rates are assigned practical units such as kbps, Mbps, or Gbps, grounding the problems in contemporary engineering contexts.

E.0.3
Mechanical Engineering

•

Mechanics of Materials
: Problems are grounded in the behavior of real-world materials by co-selecting properties from comprehensive tables. When a material such as “6061-T6 Aluminum” or “Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymer” is chosen, its physically consistent Young’s Modulus (
E
), Poisson’s Ratio (
\nu
), and Shear Modulus (
G
) are used for all calculations. This ensures that problems involving stress, strain, and torsional deformation are realistic. Properties are available in both SI (GPa) and US Customary (ksi) units to reflect practical engineering work.

•

Fluid Mechanics
: Realism in fluid statics problems is achieved by utilizing extensive lists of fluid and solid densities. This allows for the accurate calculation of hydrostatic pressure and buoyancy forces for a wide range of scenarios, such as a steel object submerged in seawater. The parameterization is context-aware, selecting dense fluids like Mercury for manometer problems while using lighter fluids like oil or water for pipe flow scenarios.

•

Vibrations and Acoustics
: For problems involving dynamic systems, parameters such as mass, spring stiffness, and damping coefficients are randomized within ranges that represent plausible physical objects, from small mechanical components to larger structures. Additionally, the amplitudes and frequencies of harmonic forcing functions are chosen in relation to the system’s natural frequency to ensure the dynamic response is well-defined and clearly demonstrates key physical phenomena like resonance or beating.

Error Categories:

•

‘‘Conceptual Error": The model applied the wrong scientific principle or formula (e.g., used addition instead of subtraction).

•

‘‘Calculation Error": The model used the correct formula but made a mathematical mistake (e.g., 2 * 3 = 5).

•

‘‘Input Error": The model used the correct formula but pulled the wrong number from the problem context or a previous step.

•

‘‘Alternative Correct": The model’s step is valid and logically sound, but follows a different method or phrasing than the ground-truth step.

•

‘‘Other": The model’s step is nonsensical, irrelevant, a hallucination, or contains only formatting errors.

Input for Analysis:

{

[CONTEXT]: {problem_context}

[GROUND-TRUTH STEP]: {gt_step}

[MODEL’S STEP]: {pred_step}

}

OUTPUT FORMAT:

You must now provide your analysis. Your entire response will be a single, raw JSON object. Adhere strictly to the following format with exactly two keys:

{"error_category": "...", "explanation": "..."}
