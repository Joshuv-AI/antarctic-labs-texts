Spontaneous emission of a three-level artificial atom in a one-dimensional open waveguide
O. A. Chuikin, Ya. S. Greenberg, O. V. Kibis

Abstract
We study the dynamical and spectral characteristics of a quantum three-level ladder system, interacting with a continuous electromagnetic field in one-dimensional open waveguide. Common realization of such systems is a waveguide QED setup — a superconducting artificial atom (transmon), coupled to an open microwave transmission line. We derive an analytical solution for spontaneous emission of initially excited atom, and use it to study the probability of state detection and spectral density of output photon states. We find that for strong coupling of transmon to a waveguide emitted photons show correlation in frequency and can have the same energies, even if the three-level system is anharmonic.

Introduction

Waveguide quantum electrodynamics is a modern and rapidly developing field of research that studies the interaction of atoms with photons in confined geometries of waveguides
Roy17
;
Gu17
;
Sheremet23
;
Astafiev10
. It has a vast spectrum of applications in quantum optics and photonics, as well as in quantum technologies such as quantum communication and quantum information processing. In contrast with cavity QED

;
Haroche06
;
Kibis11
;

;

and circuit QED

;

;

Three-level systems play an important role in quantum optics and photonics, and have recently received broad attention in waveguide QED as well. Interaction of three-level emitters (3LE) with photon fields can induce some interesting and important effects, such as electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT)

, Autler-Townes splitting
Sillanpaa09
;
Anisimov11
, two-photon resonance fluorescence
Gasparinetti19
;
Ngaha25
;
Gunin25
, giant cross-Kerr effect
Hoi13a
, photon-state purification
Lopes24
, and many others. Since an excited three-level system can emit two photons via spontaneous decay or scattering interaction, it can be potentially used to induce photon-photon correlations. Creation of correlated photon pairs due to spontaneous decay or scattering on a single atom in 1D waveguides was studied in
Shen07a
;
Shen07b
;
Roy11
;
Zheng12
;
Chen12
;
Li15
;
Sathyamoorthy16
;
Gasparinetti17
;
Gao24
.

A typical realization of a 3LE interacting with a continuum of photons in 1D waveguides is superconducting artificial atoms
Kjaergaard20
;
Krantz19
, directly or side-coupled to a microwave transmission line
Astafiev10
;
Blais21
. The most common type of a three-level system in this playground is a transmon qubit
Koch07
. A transmon has a ladder-type structure of levels, meaning that transitions from a lower to a higher level can only happen via a middle level. Although transmons are usually used for quantum information applications as regular qubits, i.e., two-level systems, the third level can have an important practical role in waveguide QED setups
Hoi11
;
Lu21
;
Sultanov25
, and, of course, has wide quantum optics applications (see
Hoi13b
and references from the previous paragraph).

In this work we study the spontaneous emission of an initially excited three-level system, coupled to a 1D open waveguide. In contrast with more traditional tasks like interaction of radiation with single qubits
Shen05
;
Chen11
;
Wang11
or qubit chains
Fang14
;
Greenberg15
;
Liao15
;
Greenberg21
;
Greenberg23
, that are usually performed in the single-photon limit, here we employ the two-excitation subspace. Our approach is based on the real-space description of photon fields. We derive an analytical solution of the non-stationary Schrödinger equation and find the probability amplitudes for the complete wavefunction of the system. Using this solution, we analyze probabilities to detect certain states of the system, and the spectral density of output two-photon fields. For a weak radiative decay rate we find that each of the two photons has a distinct frequency, corresponding to the two transitions of the 3LE, which is in good agreement with the experimental work
Gasparinetti17
. However, if the radiative decay is approximately ten times higher (which is achievable in current setups), we find that frequency correlation appears, which can result in the creation of two identical photons with equal energies. This correlation is more pronounced for low anharmonicity of the 3LE (i.e., when the levels are almost equidistant). We show that depending on the ratio of decay rates from the upper and middle levels, the spectral density for identical photons can have two maxima: on resonance with the lower two levels, or resonant with a smaller frequency which differs from the first frequency by half the anharmonicity parameter.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section II we give a general description of the system under study and derive the main equations for the wavefunction’s amplitudes. In Section III we derive a general analytical solution in real-space. In Section IV we examine the case of a fully-excited three-level atom, both in real-space and in frequency representations. The main results are placed in this section, including analysis of state-detection probabilities, two-photon emission spectra and creation of correlated photon pairs that share the same frequencies. The conclusion of our work, as well as future prospects, are reflected in Section V. Some mathematical calculations are included in the Appendix.

II
Real-space description of the transmon-waveguide system

We consider a system of a ladder-type three-level emitter (3LE), located at the point
x=0
in a one-dimensional waveguide (see Fig.
1
a). In the real-space representation
Shen07b
;
Shen09
and under rotating wave approximation it can be described by the following Hamiltonian (here and later in the text we assume that
\hbar=1
):

\begin{gathered}H=\Omega_{1}\left|e\right\rangle\left\langle e\right|+\Omega_{2}\left|f\right\rangle\left\langle f\right|-i\upsilon_{g}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\;a^{\dagger}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}a(x)\\
+\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\;V_{1}\delta(x)\left({a^{\dagger}(x)\left|g\right\rangle\left\langle e\right|+a(x)\left|e\right\rangle\left\langle g\right|}\right)\\
+\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\;V_{2}\delta(x)\left({a^{\dagger}(x)\left|e\right\rangle\left\langle f\right|+a(x)\left|f\right\rangle\left\langle e\right|}\right).\end{gathered}

(1)

Here states
\left|g\right\rangle,\left|e\right\rangle
and
\left|f\right\rangle
are ground, first-excited and second-excited states of the transmon. Corresponding resonant frequencies of first and second excited states are
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
, and
V_{1},V_{2}
are their coupling constants. Energy levels and corresponding frequencies are shown in Fig.
1
b.
\upsilon_{g}
is a group velocity of photons,
\delta(x)
is a Dirac delta-function, and
a^{\dagger}(x)
(
a(x)
) is a creation (annihilation) operator in x-representation, which creates (destroys) photon in
x
position of the waveguide.

Throughout of this paper we will refer to 3LE as transmon or (artificial) atom, but keep in mind that our approach is universal and can be related to other physical realizations of three-level ladder-type systems.

Figure 1:
(a) System under study - three-level emitter (3LE), coupled to the center of one-dimensional waveguide at point
x=0
. Coupling of first and second excited states are
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, respectfully. Excited atom can emit photons through spontaneous decay, which are propagate only forward due to usage of chiral approximation (see text for details). (b) Energy levels diagram of the bare transmon and corresponding resonant frequencies.

Hamiltonian (
1
) is written in the so-called continuous limit
Blow90
, meaning that instead of discrete summation over
x
(or over modes
k
), we have continuous integration. In this limit, commutation relation between photon operators is as follows:

\left[a(x),a^{\dagger}(x^{\prime})\right]=\delta(x-x^{\prime}),

(2)

hence the photon operators, usually dimensionless for discrete modes, in continuous limit have dimension of inverse square root of coordinate,
a(x)\sim 1/\sqrt{x}
. The dimension of coupling constants
V_{1},V_{2}
, correspondingly, is
V\sim\omega\sqrt{x}
.

The first two terms in (
1
) correspond to a free-atom and free-field, and the following two terms describe the interaction of photon fields with first and second excited states of transmon, respectively. Note that there is no direct transition between ground and second-excited state
\left|f\right\rangle\leftrightarrow\left|g\right\rangle
. Hamiltonian (
1
) is practically Janes-Cumming Hamiltonian, extended for three-level system. It conserves number of excitations in the system. In this work we adhere two excitations due to two possible transitions of the transmon.

Since our atom have three levels, wave function should be written in a two-excitation subspace as follows:

\begin{gathered}\left|\Psi(t)\right\rangle=\alpha(t)e^{-i\Omega_{2}t}\left|f,0\right\rangle+\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dxe^{-i\Omega_{1}t}\beta(x,t)a^{\dagger}(x)\left|e,0\right\rangle\\
+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\iint\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx_{1}dx_{2}\;\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)a^{\dagger}(x_{1})a^{\dagger}(x_{2})\left|g,0\right\rangle,\end{gathered}

(3)

where first probability amplitude
\alpha(t)
corresponds to fully excited transmon in state
\left|f\right\rangle
and no photons in a waveguide, second amplitude
\beta(x,t)
corresponds to transmon excited to
\left|e\right\rangle
state and one photon at position
x
, and final amplitude
\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)
contains non-excited atom and two photons in
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
positions. State
\left|0\right\rangle
designates vacuum Fock state. Coefficient
1/\sqrt{2}
in last term is a normalization constant for two-photon state in Fock basis.

Because of continuous limit
Blow90
, probability amplitudes with photons have dimension of inverse square root of coordinate:
\beta(x,t)\sim 1/\sqrt{x}
, and
\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)\sim 1/x
due to presence of two photons.

We should also note that wavefunction (
3
), as well as Hamiltonian (
1
), describe photon propagation only in forward direction. This is so called chiral approximation, and it can be performed by separating Hamiltonian for odd and even modes
Shen07b
. Chiral, or non-reciprocal, waveguide can be realized experimentally by creating suppression of photon propagation in backward direction
Lodahl17
;
Guimond20
. We are mainly interested in the case of spontaneous decay of initially excited 3LE, which should be symmetrical to 3LE placement. So, in order to simplify analytical derivation, we choose to consider only forward propagation.

Using standard non-stationary Schrödinger equation
i\partial_{t}\left|\Psi\right\rangle=H\left|\Psi\right\rangle
with Hamiltonian (
1
) and wave function (
3
), we derive the equations for all probability amplitudes:

\frac{d}{dt}\alpha(t)=-iV_{2}{e^{i(\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{1})t}}\beta(0,t),

(4)

\begin{gathered}\frac{d}{dt}\beta(x,t)+\upsilon_{g}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\beta(x,t)=-iV_{2}\delta(x){e^{i(\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{2})t}}\alpha(t)\\
-i\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}}V_{1}{e^{i\Omega_{1}t}}\gamma(x,0,t),\end{gathered}

(5)

\begin{gathered}\frac{d}{dt}\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)+\upsilon_{g}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)+\upsilon_{g}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)\\
=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}V_{1}{e^{-i\Omega_{1}t}}\left[{\delta(x_{1})\beta(x_{2},t)+\delta(x_{2})\beta(x_{1},t)}\right].\end{gathered}

(6)

In the last two equations we use the fact that photons are fundamentally indistinguishable, which in our case can be stated as
\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)=\gamma(x_{2},x_{1},t)
. All further calculations are based on equations (
4
-
6
).

III
Probability amplitudes in real-space

To find explicit expressions for all three amplitudes of wave function, we use method, incorporated in
Mukhopadhyay24
for calculation of quantum Rabi oscillation dynamics and also employed in
Lopes24
for photon state-purification via 3LE interaction. We have already used this approach in
Chuikin24
for description of stimulated emission in waveguide QED, which is very similar task to that we solve here.

First, let’s define a Fourier transform for amplitudes with photonic part, which allow us to switch from real-space to k-space of frequency modes:

\displaystyle\beta(k,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{dx\;}{e^{-ikx}}\beta(x,t),

(7a)

\displaystyle\gamma(k_{1},k_{2},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{dx_{1}dx_{2}\;}{e^{-ik_{1}x_{1}}}{e^{ik_{2}x_{2}}}\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t).

(7b)

Inverse transformation is done by changing sign of exponents.

Here and further we assume that dispersion is linear,
k=\omega/\upsilon_{g}
, which is well justified if the atom and photon resonant frequencies are far from cut-off frequency of the waveguide
Shen07b
;
Shen09
.

By using (
7
) in (
6
) together with integral representation of delta function as
\delta(x)=1/2\pi\int dk\;e^{ikx}
, we obtain equation for two-photon amplitude in k-space:

\begin{gathered}\frac{d}{dt}\gamma(k_{1},k_{2},t)+i\upsilon_{g}(k_{1}+k_{2})\gamma(k_{1},k_{2},t)\\
=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2\pi}}V_{1}{e^{-i\Omega_{1}t}}\left[{\beta(k_{2},t)+\beta(k_{1},t)}\right].\end{gathered}

(8)

Formal solution of this equation is:

\begin{gathered}\gamma(k_{1},k_{2},t)=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2\pi}}V_{1}\times\\
\int\limits_{0}^{t}d\tau{e^{-i\upsilon_{g}(k_{1}+k_{2})(t-\tau)}}{e^{-i\Omega_{1}\tau}}\left[{\beta(k_{2},\tau)+\beta(k_{1},\tau)}\right],\end{gathered}

(9)

where we assume that at initial time
t=0
no two-photon states are presented. Transforming this expression back to real-space using inverse of (
7
), we get:

\begin{gathered}\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)=\\
-i\frac{V_{1}}{\sqrt{2}\upsilon_{g}}{e^{-i\Omega_{1}(t-\frac{x_{1}}{\upsilon_{g}})}}\beta\left({x_{2}-x_{1},t-\frac{x_{1}}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)\theta(x_{1})\theta\left(t-\frac{x_{1}}{\upsilon_{g}}\right)\\
-i\frac{V_{1}}{\sqrt{2}\upsilon_{g}}{e^{-i\Omega_{1}(t-\frac{x_{2}}{\upsilon_{g}})}}\beta\left({x_{1}-x_{2},t-\frac{x_{2}}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)\theta(x_{2})\theta\left({t-\frac{x_{2}}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right),\end{gathered}

(10)

where we introduced a Heaviside step-function,
\theta(x>0)=1,\quad\theta(x<0)=0
. In order to avoid a jump at
x=0
, we apply standard regulation
\theta(x)=1/2
.

Next thing we need is to find
\beta(x,t)
, which then can be used to determine two-photon amplitude (
10
). Taking
\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)
for
x_{2}=0
and substitute it in (
5
), we obtain:

\begin{gathered}\left({\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+{\upsilon_{g}}\frac{\partial}{{\partial x}}}\right)\beta(x,t)=-iV_{2}\delta(x){e^{i(\Omega_{1}-\Omega_{2})t}}\alpha(t)\\
-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}\beta(x,t)-\Gamma_{1}F(x,t),\end{gathered}

(11)

where we define decay rate of first-excited level as
\Gamma_{1}=V_{1}^{2}/\upsilon_{g}
, as well as some additional parameter:

F(x,t)=e^{i\Omega_{1}\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\beta\left({-x,t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)\theta(x)\theta\left(t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}\right).

(12)

To find explicit expression for
\beta(x,t)
, we use same procedure as for
\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)
. Multiplying (
11
) by
e^{-ikx}/\sqrt{2\pi}
and integrating over
x
, we can use Fourier transform (
7
) to rewrite equation (
11
) in k-space. Then formal solution can be found as:

\begin{gathered}\beta(k,t)=\beta_{0}(k){e^{-(ik\upsilon_{g}+\Gamma_{1}/2)t}}\\
-i\frac{V_{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\limits_{0}^{t}d\tau{e^{-(ik\upsilon_{g}+\Gamma_{1}/2)(t-\tau)}}{e^{-i(\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{1})\tau}}\alpha(\tau)\\
-\Gamma_{1}\int\limits_{0}^{t}d\tau{e^{-(ik\upsilon_{g}+\Gamma_{1}/2)(t-\tau)}}F(k,\tau),\end{gathered}

(13)

where
\beta_{0}(k)=\beta(k,0)
is an amplitude for incident photon with frequency
\omega=k\upsilon_{g}
at initial time
t=0
and
F(k,t)
is a Fourier transform of coefficient (
12
). Translating (
13
) back to real-space, we get:

\begin{gathered}\beta(x,t)=e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}t}\beta_{0}(x-\upsilon_{g}t)-i\frac{V_{2}}{\upsilon_{g}}e^{-i\Delta_{\Omega}(t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}})}e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{1}x}{2\upsilon_{g}}}\theta(x)\theta\left({t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)\alpha\left(t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}\right)\\
-\Gamma_{1}\theta(x)\theta\left(t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}\right)\int\limits_{0}^{x/\upsilon_{g}}dt^{\prime}{e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}t^{\prime}}}{e^{-i\Omega_{1}\left({t^{\prime}-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)}}\beta\left({{\upsilon_{g}}t^{\prime}-x,t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right),\end{gathered}

(14)

where we introduce frequency between levels
\left|e\right\rangle
and
\left|f\right\rangle
as
\Delta_{\Omega}=\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{1}
(see Fig.
1
b). Change of upper limit in last integral is due to step function
\theta\left(x-\upsilon_{g}t^{\prime}\right)
.

Expression (
14
), aside from amplitude
\alpha(t-x/\upsilon_{g})
, is also contain shifted value
\beta(\upsilon_{g}t^{\prime}-x,t-x/\upsilon_{g})
, so it can’t be considered explicit. However, by performing a corresponding shift in (

\beta_{0}
and amplitude of second-excited state
\alpha(t)
. Detailed calculations can be found in Appendix A. Thus, we obtain the explicit expression for a single-photon amplitude:

\begin{gathered}\beta(x,t)=e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}t}\beta_{0}(x-\upsilon_{g}t)-i\frac{V_{2}}{\upsilon_{g}}e^{-i{\Delta_{\Omega}}(t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}})}e^{-\frac{{{\Gamma_{1}}x}}{2\upsilon_{g}}}\theta(x)\theta\left({t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)\alpha\left({t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)\\
-\Gamma_{1}\theta(x)\theta\left({t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right){e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}\left({t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)}}\int\limits_{0}^{x/{\upsilon_{g}}}dt^{\prime}{e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}t^{\prime}}}{e^{-i\Omega_{1}\left({t^{\prime}-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)}}\beta_{0}\left[{{\upsilon_{g}}(t^{\prime}-t)}\right].\end{gathered}

(15)

By taking (
15
) at point
x=0
, we can easily find the remaining amplitude of a second-excited state from (
4

\begin{gathered}\alpha(t)=\alpha(0)e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}t}\\
-iV_{2}e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}t}\int\limits_{0}^{t}d\tau e^{\left({i\Delta_{\Omega}-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}+\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}}\right)\tau}\beta_{0}(-\upsilon_{g}\tau),\end{gathered}

(16)

where
\alpha(0)
corresponds to initial state with fully excited transmon, and
\Gamma_{2}=V_{2}^{2}/\upsilon_{g}
is a decay rate of second-excited state, similar to

.

Obtained expressions (
10
), (

) and (
16
) are the key results of this section. Taking particular initial state, we can derive explicit analytic expression for full wave function (

). These equations contain two possible initial conditions: 1) Transmon fully excited in state

It is also possible to solve this problem for initial state with two incident photons that scatters on a 3LE, but we omitted it to simplify the calculations.

IV
Spontaneous decay of fully-excited transmon

IV.1
Solution in real-space

Let’s assume that initially there are no photons in a waveguide, and a three-level atom is in upper excited state
\left|f\right\rangle
:

\alpha(0)=1,\qquad\beta_{0}(x)=0.

(17)

In this case we have the process of spontaneous decay, which due to three levels results in emission of two photons.

Using initial condition (
17
) in (
16
), (
15
) and (
10
), we find exact expressions for all three probability amplitudes of a wavefunction:

\displaystyle\alpha(t)=e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}t},

(18a)

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\beta(x,t)=-i\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{\upsilon_{g}}}e^{-\left(i{\Delta_{\Omega}}+\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}\right)(t-x/\upsilon_{g})}e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\times\\
\theta(x)\theta\left({t-\frac{x}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right),\end{gathered}

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)=-\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}}{2\upsilon_{g}^{2}}}\times\\
\left({e^{-i\Omega_{1}\left(t-\frac{x_{1}}{\upsilon_{g}}\right)}e^{-\left(i\Delta_{\Omega}+\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}\right)\left(t-\frac{x_{2}}{\upsilon_{g}}\right)}e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}\frac{x_{2}-x_{1}}{\upsilon_{g}}}\Theta(x_{1},x_{2},t)}\right.\\
\left.{+{e^{-i{\Omega_{1}}\left({t-\frac{x_{2}}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)}}e^{-\left({i\Delta_{\Omega}+\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}}\right)\left({t-\frac{x_{1}}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)}e^{-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}\frac{x_{1}-x_{2}}{\upsilon_{g}}}\Theta(x_{2},x_{1},t)}\right),\end{gathered}

where composite theta-function:

\Theta(x_{1},x_{2},t)=\theta(x_{1})\theta(x_{2}-x_{1})\theta\left({t-\frac{x_{1}}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right)\theta\left({t-\frac{x_{2}}{\upsilon_{g}}}\right).

(19)

Since here we work in a chiral approximation, all fields moves only right, so the emission of spontaneous photons only happens in forward direction,
x_{1,2}\equiv x>0
.

IV.2
State detection probabilities

Probability amplitudes (
18
), beside
\alpha(t)
, contain photonic dependence, proportional to real-space coordinate

where photon is presumably located. So we can treat square root of those amplitudes as density of probability to find a photon in certain waveguide region. To find full probabilities to locate desired system state, one needs to integrate it over entire waveguide. By doing so, we obtain:

\displaystyle P_{f,0}(t)=\left|\alpha(t)\right|^{2}=e^{-\Gamma_{2}t},

(20a)

\displaystyle P_{e,1}(t)=\int{dx}{\left|{\beta(x,t)}\right|^{2}}=\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{\Gamma_{2}-\Gamma_{1}}\left({e^{-\Gamma_{1}t}-e^{-\Gamma_{2}t}}\right),

(20b)

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}P_{g,2}(t)=\iint dx_{1}dx_{2}\left|\gamma(x_{1},x_{2},t)\right|^{2}=\\
=\frac{\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}}{\Gamma_{2}-\Gamma_{1}}\left({\frac{1-e^{-\Gamma_{1}t}}{\Gamma_{1}}-\frac{1-e^{-{\Gamma_{2}}t}}{\Gamma_{2}}}\right).\end{gathered}

The decay of upper level

IV.3
Two-photon spectrum

By using Fourier transform (
7
), we can translate the real-space photon amplitudes (
10
) and (
15

\beta(k,t)=i\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma_{2}\upsilon_{g}}{2\pi}}\frac{e^{-\left({i\omega+\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}}\right)t}-e^{-\left(i\Delta_{\Omega}+\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}\right)t}}{i(\omega-\Delta_{\Omega})-\frac{\Gamma_{2}-\Gamma_{1}}{2}},

(21)

\begin{gathered}\gamma(k_{1},k_{2},t)=\frac{\upsilon_{g}}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}}{2}}\frac{e^{-i(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})t}}{i(\omega_{1}-\Delta_{\Omega})-\frac{\Gamma_{2}-\Gamma_{1}}{2}}\times\\
\left[{\frac{e^{\left({i\omega_{2}-i\Omega_{1}-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}}\right)t}-1}{i\omega_{2}-i\Omega_{1}-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}}-\frac{{{e^{\left({i\omega_{1}+i\omega_{2}-i\Omega_{2}-\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}}\right)t}}-1}}{i\omega_{1}+i\omega_{2}-i\Omega_{2}-\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}}}\right]\\
+\frac{\upsilon_{g}}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}}{2}}\frac{e^{-i(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})t}}{i(\omega_{2}-\Delta_{\Omega})-\frac{\Gamma_{2}-\Gamma_{1}}{2}}\times\\
\left[{\frac{{e^{\left({i\omega_{1}-i\Omega_{1}-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}}\right)t}}-1}{i\omega_{1}-i\Omega_{1}-\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{2}}-\frac{{e^{\left({i\omega_{1}+i\omega_{2}-i\Omega_{2}-\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}}\right)t}}-1}{i\omega_{1}+i\omega_{2}-i\Omega_{2}-\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{2}}}\right],\end{gathered}

(22)

where in
\beta(k,t)
photon frequency
\omega
corresponds to first emitted photon.

Note that if we take
t\rightarrow\infty
, single photon amplitude (
21

\alpha(t)

22

Let’s define the spectral density of spontaneous emission of initially excited transmon as follows:

\begin{gathered}S_{sp}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\upsilon_{g}^{2}{\left|{\gamma(k_{1},k_{2},t\to\infty)}\right|^{2}}=\\
=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\frac{\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}}{{{\left(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\Omega_{2}\right)}^{2}+\frac{\Gamma_{2}^{2}}{4}}}\times\\
\frac{{{\left(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-2\Omega_{1}\right)}^{2}}+\Gamma_{1}^{2}}{\left[{{{\left({\omega_{1}-\Omega_{1}}\right)}^{2}}+\frac{\Gamma_{1}^{2}}{4}}\right]\left[{{{\left({\omega_{2}-\Omega_{1}}\right)}^{2}}+\frac{\Gamma_{1}^{2}}{4}}\right]}.\end{gathered}

(23)

We plot this two-photon density in Fig.

Unlike the probabilities (
20
), spectral density (
23

\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
, but also on resonant frequencies of 3LE. It is typical to describe energy-level spacing of a transmon via an anharmonicity parameter
(
Koch07
)

\alpha_{r}=\frac{\Delta_{\Omega}-\Omega_{1}}{\Omega_{1}}=\frac{\Omega_{2}-2\Omega_{1}}{\Omega_{1}}\approx-{\left(\frac{8E_{J}}{E_{C}}\right)^{-1/2}},

(24)

where
E_{J}
is a Josephson energy, and
E_{C}
is a charging energy - common characteristics of superconducting quantum circuits
Blais21

stands for ’relative’, thus expression (

\left|f\right\rangle,\;\left|e\right\rangle
and energies between levels
\left|e\right\rangle,\;\left|g\right\rangle

\alpha=(\Delta_{\Omega}-\Omega_{1})=\alpha_{r}\Omega_{1}
, that is measured in hertz, but here we choose to use only relative parameter to avoid confusion with probability amplitude
\alpha(t)
. Typical experimental values of
\alpha
usually falls within the range 100-300 MHz

, or in relative units from
-2\%
up to
-6\%

From Fig.

we see that spectral density of spontaneous emission have two distinct peaks, which are a result of non-distinguishability of photons. Each peak corresponds to one resonant photon with frequency
\omega=\Omega_{1}
and second photon with smaller frequency
\omega=(1+\alpha_{r})\Omega_{1}
(remember that
\alpha_{r}
is negative). But because both photons are indistinguishable, we can’t exactly say which photon is ’first’, and which is ’second’, thus two peaks appear. For bigger anharmonicity, two peaks are further apart, because of change of transition frequencies in 3LE. If we implement weak-coupling,
\Gamma/\Omega_{1}=0.001
(in practical circuits its usually
\Gamma<5

\Omega_{1}
and
(1+\alpha_{r})\Omega_{1}

However, if we take stronger coupling, typical for circuit QED systems (for example,
\Gamma_{2}\sim 100
MHz), we see the appearance of frequency correlation between the two photons. This is expressed by the appearance of third peak at resonant frequency
\omega/\Omega_{1}=1
, which is start to become visible for
\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}=1.5

\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}=3

\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}=\Omega_{1}

\left|e\right\rangle
, which is arise due to almost equidistant level structure of low-anharmonicity transmon and rather strong coupling between the artificial atom and the field modes.

IV.4
Emission spectra for identical photons

Let’s study spectral density (
23
) for identical photons in closer detail. Taking
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}
we get:

S_{sp}(\omega_{1}=\omega_{2})=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{\delta_{\omega}^{2}+\frac{\Gamma_{1}^{2}}{4}}\times\frac{\Gamma_{2}}{(2\delta_{\omega}-\alpha_{r}\Omega_{1})^{2}+\frac{\Gamma_{2}^{2}}{4}},

(25)

where we introduce detuning from resonant frequency for both photons as
\delta_{\omega}=\omega-\Omega_{1}
.

Function (
25
) have two maximums: first one corresponds to frequency between lower levels
\Omega_{1}
and thus
\delta_{\omega}=0
, and second one corresponds to decay of upper level with anharmonicity shift,
\delta_{\omega}=-\alpha_{r}\Omega_{1}/2
. Both these peaks can be observed in Fig.
4
(a)-(b), for decay rates
\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}=3/2
and
\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}=3
. Two-dimensional plot of (
25
), showing how spectral density also changes with anharmonicity, presented in Fig.
4

The maximum of spectral density for identical photons should be for
\alpha_{r}\rightarrow 0
, i.e. in the case of equidistant levels with
\Omega_{2}=2\Omega_{1}
. However, as can be seen from Fig.
4
, frequencies of two photons not always have to be resonant with
\Omega_{1}
. For example, if ratio between decay rates is
\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}=1.5
, like in Fig.
4
(a), emitted photons both would have maximum spectral density on frequency, close to
\Omega_{1}(1+\alpha_{r}/2)
. This also can be observed on two-frequency graph Fig.
3
(b) in diagonal cross-section. Thus, by changing both decay rates
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
and anharmonicity parameter
\alpha_{r}
of excited three-level transmon, it is possible to create correlated photon pairs with same frequencies. Superconducting circuits are very well suitable for this task, since they allow tuning of couplings and anharmonicity parameters either by sample production techniques or via application of external magnetic fields.

V
Conclusion

In summary, we present a theoretical description of spontaneous decay of a fully-excited three-level system in a one-dimensional open waveguide. Using the real-space description of the system, we obtain non-stationary analytical expressions for the probability amplitudes for the whole system. We show that the dynamics of spontaneous emission is heavily dependent on the ratio
\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}
, where
\Gamma_{2}
(
\Gamma_{1}
) is the radiative decay rate of the second (first) excited state. Transferring the obtained amplitudes into the momentum space allowed us to study the spectral density of emitted photons, from which we discover the appearance of frequency correlation for strong coupling. It is expressed in the creation of two photons with identical frequencies, even if the three-level atom structure is anharmonic. The frequency correlation is stronger for smaller anharmonicity, i.e., when the levels are close to equidistant.

More detailed analysis showed that the spectral density of identical photons can have two maxima. The first one corresponds to the resonant frequency of the lower levels
\omega=\Omega_{1}
and prevails when the decay rates ratio is rather high,
\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}\approx 3
. The second maximum, however, depends on the anharmonicity parameter as
\omega\approx\Omega_{1}(1+\alpha_{r}/2)
, and it dominates for
\Gamma_{2}/\Gamma_{1}\approx 1.5
, which is a rather common value for typical transmon circuits. Thus, by adjusting these parameters one can obtain pairs of identical photons with tunable frequency, which can be utilized for quantum information and communication applications.

Throughout our analysis we take specific parameters that are common for superconducting transmon qubits, coupled to a microwave coplanar waveguide. However, our results are obtained in a general form, and can be used to describe other physical systems of three-level emitters in 1D waveguides, like Rydberg atoms or quantum dots in nanoplasmonic waveguides.

Here we describe only the process of spontaneous emission of a fully-excited three-level emitter. However, the obtained solutions in real-space (
10
), (
15
) and (
16
) allow one to study also the case of an incident photon pulse, scattering on a half-excited three-level system in state
\left|e\right\rangle
. Moreover, our expression (
15
) is valid for an arbitrary pulse shape
\beta_{0}(x)
, which can be, for example, gaussian or rectangular. This task is quite interesting from the point of view of stimulated emission, and can be addressed in future studies.
