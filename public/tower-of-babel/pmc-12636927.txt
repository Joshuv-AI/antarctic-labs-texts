Chronic respiratory diseases and their effect on physical performance and frailty: a workplace-based comparative cross-sectional study among male dairy workers in Pakistan
Authors: Arsalan Tariq, Muhammad Ammar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore how chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) affect lung health, mobility, frailty and activity levels among young to middle-aged male dairy workers.MethodologyThis cross-sectional study was conducted from November 2023 to February 2024 across 17 dairy farms in Punjab, Pakistan, involving 380 male dairy workers aged 25–40 years.Participants were grouped by respiratory health status (chronic respiratory conditions vs healthy workers). Assessments included spirometry (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁), forced vital capacity (FVC), FEV₁/FVC), functional tests (6 Minute Walk Test (6MWT), Timed Up and Go (TUG)), FRAIL (Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illnesses and Loss of weight) questionnaire and International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form for physical activity. Body mass index was calculated from anthropometric data. Data were analysed using t-tests, χ² tests, correlations and regression; a p<0.05 was considered significant.ResultsOf the 380 male dairy workers enrolled, 365 were included in the final analysis (180 with respiratory conditions, 185 without respiratory conditions). Group A (workers with CRDs, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic bronchitis and occupational asthma) demonstrated significantly poorer outcomes compared with group B. Specifically, lung function was markedly reduced in group A (FEV₁: 62.9% vs 91.5%, p=0.002). Functional performance was also impaired, as shown by shorter 6MWT distances (390.7 m vs 479.6 m, p=0.009) and slower TUG times (10.3 s vs 7.7 s, p=0.005). Frailty was almost four times more common in workers with CRDs than in those without (38.9% vs 10.8%, p=0.001). Likewise, low physical activity was substantially higher among workers with respiratory conditions (45.6% vs 10.3%), whereas high activity levels were predominantly observed in those without respiratory conditions (58.9% vs 16.1%). Regression analysis further identified FEV₁, age, frailty status and presence of a respiratory disease as significant predictors of functional capacity.ConclusionsCRDs among male dairy workers are associated with impaired lung function, decreased physical performance, lower physical activity levels and higher frailty.

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs), particularly chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), occupational asthma and chronic bronchitis, are major contributors to global morbidity, disability and premature mortality.1 These conditions are of particular concern in occupational environments where workers are consistently exposed to respiratory hazards. The dairy industry represents one such high-risk setting, where individuals are frequently in contact with organic dust, bioaerosols, ammonia and endotoxin agents well-documented to trigger or exacerbate respiratory pathology.2

Epidemiological studies show that agricultural and livestock workers have significantly higher rates of chronic respiratory symptoms compared with non-agricultural populations. Over 30% of European dairy farmers experience chronic cough or phlegm,3 while studies in the USA have shown that up to 40% of workers in confined animal feeding operations report symptoms consistent with bronchitis or occupational asthma.4 Similar findings have been documented in New Zealand and Scandinavian countries, with increased prevalence of reduced lung function and airway hyper-responsiveness among dairy workers.5

Repeated and prolonged exposure to these airborne irritants leads to chronic airway inflammation, increased mucus production, bronchial hyper-reactivity and eventual airway remodelling, all of which contribute to progressive pulmonary decline.6 Moreover, inadequate use of personal protective equipment (PPE), poor workplace ventilation and limited access to occupational health services, particularly in rural or informal work settings, amplify the risks.7

Beyond respiratory symptoms, chronic lung impairment has significant downstream effects on functional mobility and physical performance, which are critical for maintaining safety, efficiency and independence in physically demanding occupations. Reduced pulmonary function correlates with decreased aerobic capacity, skeletal muscle dysfunction, increased fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance.8 These limitations translate into slower gait speed, impaired balance and decreased performance in functional tasks such as lifting, walking or climbing activities that are central to daily operations in dairy farming.9

Emerging evidence also highlights the interplay between systemic inflammation caused by respiratory disease and musculoskeletal decline. Patients with COPD, for example, often exhibit reduced quadriceps muscle strength and lower limb endurance, which are predictors of falls, hospitalisations and mortality.10 This is particularly concerning in older dairy workers, who may already face age-related declines in strength and mobility.

Impaired pulmonary function in this population does not occur in isolation; it directly compromises physical performance, endurance and functional mobility capacities that are essential for maintaining both productivity and safety in labour-intensive roles. While existing literature has explored the respiratory risks associated with agricultural work, there is a striking gap in research that connects chronic lung disease with objective physical performance outcomes in dairy workers.

This study aimed to investigate the impact of CRDs (COPD, chronic bronchitis and occupational asthma) on multiple dimensions of health among male dairy farm workers in Punjab, Pakistan. Specifically, comparisons were made between workers with CRDs and healthy workers in terms of lung function, endurance, functional mobility, frailty status and physical activity levels, using validated clinical and functional assessment tools. It was expected that workers with CRDs would show poorer outcomes across these domains, including lower lung function, shorter 6 Minute Walk Test (6MWT) distances, slower Timed Up and Go (TUG) performance, higher frailty and reduced physical activity.

Methodology

This cross-sectional study was conducted in various 17 dairy farms across Punjab, Pakistan, from November 2023 to February 2024. 17 dairy farms were selected using a purposive sampling strategy to represent a range of medium-to-large farms across Punjab, Pakistan. These farms were not the only ones in the region, but were chosen because they met the inclusion criteria of having ≥10 full-time workers and willingness of farm owners to participate in the study. Farm size and worker-to-animal ratio were not used as stratification criteria, but all farms included had comparable operational structures typical of the regional dairy industry. Workers within these farms were approached consecutively during routine work hours, and those meeting the inclusion criteria were invited to participate.

The study included male dairy workers aged 25–40 years to focus on the core working-age population actively engaged in physically demanding dairy tasks, while minimising age-related declines in lung function, mobility and frailty.11 Workers younger than 25 had limited exposure to occupational respiratory hazards, and those above 40 may have age-related comorbidities affecting functional outcomes.12 This age range ensured a relatively homogeneous population for valid comparisons between workers with and without CRDs.

Participants performed a range of routine farm tasks that involved direct exposure to ammonia, organic dust and bioaerosols. These included cleaning animal sheds, handling manure, mixing and storing feed, milking and sweeping. This study specifically focused on COPD, occupational asthma and chronic bronchitis because these are the most common CRDs among dairy farm workers, with consistently high prevalence reported in livestock-exposed populations.3 13 These conditions have a well-established occupational link to exposures such as organic dust, ammonia, endotoxins and bioaerosols, which are typical in dairy farming environments.14

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software to ensure adequate statistical power for comparing outcomes between individuals with and without respiratory conditions. Using an effect size of 0.3 (Cohen’s d), an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power of 0.80, with an equal allocation ratio of 1:1, the minimum required total sample size was determined to be 352 participants (176 per group). To accommodate potential data loss or participant exclusion, the sample size was increased to 380 participants.

Female workers were excluded from the study due to the limited number employed in these roles and potential gender-based physiological differences that could affect physical performance outcomes and frailty assessment, ensuring sample homogeneity.15

Inclusion criteria were: (1) male dairy workers aged 25–40 years, (2) employment in dairy farming for at least 1 year, (3) ability to provide informed consent and (4) for group A, a clinical diagnosis of a chronic respiratory condition and current use of prescribed medication. We only included farms that were large enough to provide participants and whose owners agreed to take part in the research.

Exclusion criteria

To minimise confounding factors that could influence physical performance and frailty assessment, several exclusion criteria were applied. Dairy workers with chronic medical conditions known to impair mobility or functional capacity were not included. This included cardiovascular diseases such as ischaemic heart disease, heart failure or uncontrolled hypertension, which are well-established causes of exercise intolerance and reduced physical activity independent of respiratory status.16 Similarly, individuals with neurological conditions (eg, stroke, Parkinson’s disease, or other neuromuscular disorders) and musculoskeletal or orthopaedic disorders (such as severe arthritis, limb deformities or recent fractures) were excluded, as these conditions could restrict mobility and bias functional outcome measures like the 6MWT or TUG test.17

In addition, participants with a history of current or former tobacco use were excluded to avoid the confounding effect of smoking-related lung impairment, which may mimic or exacerbate CRD.6 Workers experiencing an acute respiratory infection within the previous 4 weeks were not enrolled, to ensure that temporary illness did not influence spirometry or performance test results. Finally, individuals unable or unwilling to complete the required functional assessments were excluded from participation.

These exclusion criteria were applied to both group a and b to ensure that differences in outcomes could be more confidently attributed to the presence or absence of CRD, rather than unrelated health conditions that independently affect physical activity, mobility or frailty.

Participants were divided into two groups. Group A (n=190) included individuals diagnosed with chronic respiratory conditions such as COPD, chronic bronchitis or occupational asthma, confirmed via clinical history. Participants in group A were also currently taking prescribed medications, including short-acting β2-agonists, long-acting bronchodilators and inhaled corticosteroids. Current use of respiratory medication was defined as active use of prescribed inhalers or oral drugs for CRDs within the past 30 days. This was verified through structured participant interviews, review of medical prescriptions and, where available, confirmation from local healthcare providers. Group B (n=190) consisted of healthy workers with no reported respiratory complaints or medical diagnosis. All participants received brief instruction on correct inhaler use before spirometry testing to minimise errors during lung function assessment. All participants in both groups reported no engagement in regular physical activity outside of their work-related duties.

Workers were classified into group A if they had a physician-diagnosed CRD (COPD, chronic bronchitis or occupational asthma). Diagnoses were verified through participants’ medical records and confirmation from the local healthcare providers, who were currently taking prescribed respiratory medication within the past 30 days, and had compatible clinical history and/or spirometry consistent with obstructive disease (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁)/forced vital capacity (FVC)<0.70), consistent with Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease and American Thoracic Society (ATS) recommendations.18 Healthy workers (group B) were identified at the same stage through structured interviews conducted by trained physiotherapists and respiratory technicians. Individuals were included in this group only if they reported no history of CRD or related symptoms (eg, chronic cough, wheeze, dyspnoea), were not taking respiratory medications and had normal lung function (FEV₁ ≥80% predicted and FEV₁/FVC ≥0.70).19 20

All participants underwent structured evaluations conducted by trained physiotherapists and respiratory technicians in the Department of Physical Therapy of the University of Lahore. The following assessments were included:

Pulmonary function testing: Lung function was assessed using a calibrated spirometer, following ATS guidelines21 to ensure accuracy and consistency.

The primary parameters measured included FEV₁ (% predicted), FVC (% predicted), and the FEV₁/FVC ratio. FEV₁ measures the amount of air exhaled in the first second of forced breath, while FVC is the total air exhaled after a deep inhale. The FEV₁/FVC ratio indicates airway obstruction, with lower values suggesting obstructive lung conditions like COPD or asthma. These measurements helped evaluate the impact of CRDs on lung function and physical performance in dairy workers.22

Functional mobility and physical performance were assessed using two standardised tests. The 6MWT measured submaximal aerobic capacity by recording the distance participants could walk along a flat corridor in 6 min shorter distances indicating lower endurance.23 The TUG test evaluated mobility by timing participants as they stood up from a chair, walked 3 m, turned, returned and sat down; times over 12 s indicated potential impairment.24 Frailty was evaluated using the validated FRAIL (Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illnesses and Loss of weight) questionnaire, which scores five domains Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illnesses and Weight Loss on a binary scale (0 or 1). Total scores categorised individuals as robust (0), prefrail (1–2) or frail (≥3).25

Physical activity was measured using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF), assessing frequency and duration of walking, moderate and vigorous activities over the past 7 days. Based on total weekly MET-minutes (Metabolic Equivalent Tasks), participants were classified as low (<600 MET-min/week), moderate (600–2999 MET-min/week) or high (≥3000 MET-min/week) activity level.21 Anthropometric measurements included height and weight using standard tools. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²), providing an estimate of body composition.

All questionnaires (FRAIL scale and IPAQ-SF) were interviewer-administered by trained physiotherapists and respiratory technicians. This approach was chosen to ensure consistency, avoid missing responses and account for varying literacy levels among participants. Standardised instructions were provided, and participants’ responses were recorded verbatim without interpretation by the interviewer.

All data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.25. Descriptive statistics (mean, SD, frequencies and percentages) were used to summarise participant characteristics and test outcomes. Independent sample t-tests were applied to compare continuous variables between group A and group B. χ² tests were used for categorical variables. Pearson correlation coefficients examined associations between lung function parameters and physical performance scores. Frailty was analysed in two ways: (1) as categorical levels (robust, prefrail, frail) for descriptive comparisons between groups and (2) as a continuous raw FRAIL score in the multivariable regression model predicting 6MWT performance. This approach allowed us to examine frailty both as an outcome of CRD and as an independent predictor of physical function. Additionally, multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of 6MWT distance, including FEV₁%, age, frailty score, group assignment and BMI. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

A total of 380 male dairy industry workers were initially enrolled. After excluding 10 participants from group A (due to inability to complete assessments) and 5 from group B (due to incomplete data), the final analysis included 180 participants with respiratory conditions (group A) and 185 healthy workers (group B). All participants were non-smokers and aged between 25 and 40 years (figure 1).

In terms of functional performance, workers in group A walked a shorter mean distance of 390.7±44.6 m during the 6MWT, while their healthy peers achieved 479.6±38.3 m (p=0.009). Group A also required more time to complete the TUG test (10.3±1.7 s) compared with group B (7.7±1.2 s, p=0.005).

Table 3 presents a comparison of frailty status and physical activity levels between dairy workers with CRDs (group A) and healthy workers (group B). FEV₁ (% predicted) emerged as a strong positive predictor (B=3.39, p=0.002), indicating that better lung function was associated with greater walking distance. Conversely, increasing age (B=−1.76, p=0.005), higher frailty scores (B=−20.94, p=0.003) and belonging to the respiratory disease group (B=−33.68, p=0.001) were significantly associated with reduced 6MWT distance. Discussion

The results of this study highlight the significant impact of chronic respiratory conditions on functional mobility, physical performance and frailty among male dairy workers. Workers with chronic respiratory conditions such as COPD, chronic bronchitis and occupational asthma showed notable impairments in lung function and physical performance compared with healthy workers. These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that CRDs negatively affect physical functioning and quality of life.26

Participants in group A exhibited significantly lower FEV₁, FVC and FEV₁/FVC ratio, consistent with the findings of studies examining the impact of CRDs on lung function. Another study found that workers exposed to dust and organic materials, such as those in agriculture and dairy industries, had impaired pulmonary function,27 similar to the results observed in our study. The significantly reduced lung function in group A participants suggests that chronic respiratory conditions in occupational settings are closely linked to deteriorating pulmonary health, which further compromises physical endurance and mobility.

In terms of physical performance, participants in the chronic respiratory condition group walked significantly shorter distances on the 6MWT and had slower times on the TUG test compared with group b, reflecting reduced aerobic capacity, endurance and functional mobility. These results are in line with previous studies that found decreased exercise tolerance and impaired functional mobility in individuals with chronic respiratory conditions.28 The observed reductions in physical performance are concerning, as they increase the risk of falls, disability and occupational absenteeism.

The high prevalence of frailty in chronic pulmonary disease (38.9%) compared with healthy group (10.8%) is a striking finding of this study. CRD has been linked to frailty, with studies indicating that individuals with compromised lung function are at higher risk of developing frailty.29 Frailty in respiratory disease patients may result from a combination of factors, including reduced physical activity, muscle deconditioning and systemic inflammation associated with chronic respiratory conditions.30 These findings emphasise the importance of early identification and management of frailty in individuals with chronic respiratory conditions, particularly in occupational settings where physical demands are high.

Workers with CRDs may be particularly vulnerable in dairy farm environments due to exposure to organic dust, ammonia, bioaerosols and endotoxins, which are known to exacerbate airway inflammation and accelerate lung function decline.31 It is likely that the poorer physical outcomes observed in our study reflect not only the presence of CRDs but also the additive burden of occupational exposures in dairy farming. If these individuals had not been employed in dairy work, their outcomes may have been less severe, highlighting the role of workplace hazards in compounding disease-related impairments.

Lung function (FEV₁), age, frailty score and presence of a respiratory condition were all strongly associated with 6MWT performance. Individuals with reduced pulmonary capacity, higher frailty levels and CRD tended to walk shorter distances. These findings align with existing literature highlighting how impaired lung function and frailty contribute to diminished physical performance in individuals with long-term respiratory illnesses.32 Based on our findings, interventions to improve physical performance and reduce frailty among dairy workers with CRD should focus on both prevention and management strategies. Preventive measures include improving workplace ventilation, reducing exposure to dust and animal waste and providing workers with PPE (eg, masks). Regular health surveillance and spirometry screening may help detect disease at an earlier stage. For workers already affected, pulmonary rehabilitation, structured physical activity programmes and targeted health education could help maintain mobility and reduce frailty. At a broader level, occupational health policies should address dairy farm working conditions to reduce the overall prevalence of obstructive respiratory disease in this high-risk population.

Limitation

The sample was limited to male dairy industry workers in Punjab, Pakistan, which may restrict generalisability to other geographic regions, occupations or female workers. While information on medication use was recorded for participants with CRDs, specific details regarding dosage, duration of therapy and adherence were not obtained. One limitation of this study is that, although medication use was verified through participant interviews and prescription review, adherence and inhaler technique were not systematically evaluated. Suboptimal adherence or improper inhaler use could have led to an underestimation of lung function in some individuals, thereby adding variability to the results. Nonetheless, such variability is likely reflective of real-world practices in occupational groups with CRD, supporting the generalisability and clinical relevance of our findings. This limits the ability to assess how pharmacological treatment may have influenced respiratory function or physical performance outcomes.

Conclusions

CRDs significantly compromise the health, strength and mobility of male dairy workers, an often-overlooked population in occupational health. Workers with conditions like COPD, chronic bronchitis and occupational asthma not only had poorer lung function but also walked shorter distances, moved more slowly and showed greater signs of frailty than their healthy peers. These impairments can severely affect work performance, increase injury risk and accelerate disability.
