Comparative genomic analysis of Acinetobacter baumannii isolates from seafood markets and healthcare environments: a case study from Kerala, India
Authors: Jaykumar Kiritkumar Patel, Muthukumar Anbalagan, Ada Zwetlana, Krishna Thoppil Babu, Akalesh Patel, Sekar Gopinath, Gopalan Krishnan Sivaraman, Ravikrishnan Elangovan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Acinetobacter baumannii is renowned for causing difficult-to-treat infections and for its rapid acquisition of MDR. While traditionally viewed as a nosocomial pathogen, increasing evidence suggests that it persists in diverse environmental reservoirs. However, much of the global and most Indian surveillance have predominantly focused on hospital outbreaks, leaving its ecological diversity underexplored.MethodsThis study investigated A. baumannii isolates from seafood markets in Cochin, Kerala, India, to assess their clinical potential. Four isolates (AB02, AB05, AB07, AB08) were sequenced and compared against 479 publicly available Indian genomes from clinical, animal and environmental origins.ResultsDespite their small number, the isolates showed remarkable genomic diversity and clinically relevant features. They possessed distinct STs (ST1422, ST479 and ST2276; as per pasture MLST scheme) and remained distant from clinical isolates in core genome phylogeny. Resistome analysis revealed a limited repertoire, primarily intrinsic blaOXA-51 family and blaADC variants. Notably, isolate AB05 harboured blaOXA-72 on a plasmid, its first report in India. Virulome analysis identified conserved chromosomal determinants (ompA, bap, csu pili, siderophore systems, T6SS*) across isolates, with AB05 uniquely harbouring the hypervirulence-associated HemO cluster.ConclusionsTaken together, the resistome-virulome patterns and phylogenetic context of these isolates challenge the conventional view of clinically relevant environmental A. baumannii as mere reflections of hospital contamination. Instead, they point towards the existence of naturally persisting, environmentally adapted lineages that have harboured and/or acquired clinically relevant traits. Within this framework, we hypothesize that seafood market environments may act as dynamic interfaces where diverse A. baumannii lineages circulate prior to or alongside clinical emergence, warranting further investigation through One Health genomic surveillance.

Introduction

Acinetobacter baumannii has emerged as one of the most challenging MDR pathogens worldwide. It is listed among the WHO’s ‘critical priority’ pathogens due to its capacity for carbapenem resistance, persistence in hospital environments and involvement in severe infections.1 While historically perceived as a nosocomial organism, accumulating evidence demonstrates a far broader ecological range and adaptation of A. baumannii.2–7 The ecological versatility of A. baumannii raises important questions regarding the largely unexplored resistome and virulome of its environmental and food-associated populations and their potential contributions to clinically significant antimicrobial resistance.6,7 Such knowledge gaps are particularly concerning in low- and middle-income countries, where surveillance beyond clinical settings remains limited.

India represents a critical setting for investigating non-clinical A. baumannii within a broader antimicrobial resistance (AMR) landscape. As one of the world’s largest consumers of antimicrobials,8 and home to extensive agricultural and aquaculture sectors, the country’s ecosystems face substantial selective pressures that may facilitate the emergence and dissemination of AMR. Clinical surveillance consistently reports high levels of carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii, with rates ranging from 55% to 90% across regions.9 WGS studies related to clinical isolates have consistently reported the dominance of international clone 2 (IC2) lineages harbouring blaOXA-23 and have elucidated the complex genetic contexts, revealing the co-occurrence of blaOXA-23, blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-58 on transmissible plasmids and resistance islands.10–13 These genomic insights have been instrumental in outbreak investigation, clonal tracking and understanding the evolution of resistance in clinical settings.

However, WGS studies of non-clinical A. baumannii are still very limited, particularly for seafood-associated isolates. Although the organism has been detected in marine food products and diseased aquatic animals, most reports provide limited genomic context and do not address its role within a One Health framework.14–18 As of July 2025, only three major studies have performed WGS of seafood-associated A. baumannii.19–21 In India, however, no comprehensive WGS analysis of such isolates has yet been undertaken. Given the organism’s wide ecological presence, open pan-genome and high genomic plasticity, studying these non-clinical isolates in depth is essential for linking environmental and clinical populations and for strengthening One Health–oriented AMR surveillance.

In this study, we present, to the best of our knowledge, the first WGS-based investigation of A. baumannii from retail seafood markets in Kerala, India. By combining resistome and virulome profiling with phylogenetic analyses, our study provides a detailed genomic view of isolates from a high-risk interface—seafood markets—where human, animal and environmental domains converge. These markets often operate under suboptimal hygienic conditions, may receive inputs from urban wastewater, and accumulate organic matter, creating an environment conducive to the persistence and exchange of AMR determinants. Our findings expand the current understanding of A. baumannii diversity in non-clinical reservoirs and highlight the importance of incorporating food-associated environments into routine genomic surveillance under a One Health framework.

Materials and methods

Genomic DNA extraction and WGS

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted using the QIAGEN Genomic-tip 20/G kit following the manufacturer’s protocol with minor modifications. Briefly, 1.5 mL samples of saturated bacterial cultures were pelleted, lysed using proteinase K and RNase A, cleared by centrifugation, and applied to Genomic-tip columns pre-equilibrated with QBT buffer (QIAGEN). Columns were washed with QC buffer (QIAGEN), and gDNA was eluted using QF buffer (QIAGEN), precipitated with isopropanol, washed with 70% ethanol, and resuspended in Tris-EDTA buffer. DNA quality and quantity were assessed using NanoDrop and Qubit fluorometers (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Only samples with A260/A230 ratios between 2.0 and 2.2 and A260/A280 ratios between 1.7 and 1.9 were retained. DNA extraction was repeated when the NanoDrop-to-Qubit concentration ratio exceeded 1.5.

Library preparation was performed using the Rapid Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (RBK 114 24; Oxford Nanopore Technologies; protocol RBK_9176_v114_revP_27Dec2024) with 200 ng gDNA per sample. Barcoded libraries were pooled in equimolar amounts and sequenced on a MinION Mk1B platform using an R10.4 flow cell (FLO-MIN114).

Base-calling and quality control for sequencing reads

Base-calling of POD5 files was performed using Dorado v0.7.1 (GPU version), generating BAM files with adapter trimming, barcode removal and demultiplexing enabled. Barcode-specific FASTQ files were subjected to two filtering strategies: NanoFilt v1.42.022 was used to remove reads ≤200 bp and with mean Phred quality ≤9, while Filtlong v0.2.1 retained the top 95% of reads based on length and quality. Read statistics for raw and filtered datasets were compared using NanoComp v1.12.0 (Figure S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online). Both filtered FASTQs were used for downstream genome assembly.

Genome assembly and quality control

Genome assemblies were generated using Trycyler v0.5.5.23 The FASTQ filtered using NanoFilt and FASTQ filtered using Filtlong were used as input for Canu v2.2,24 Flye v2.9.4,25 Flye + medaka v 1.12.1, and Raven v1.8.3.26 Resulting assemblies were combined using the Trycycler pipeline to generate consensus assemblies. Assembly quality was assessed using BUSCO v5.7.1,27 and CheckM v1.2.3.28 FastANI v1.129 was used for identifying species while using reference genomes of Pseudomonadaceae (n = 422), Enterobacteriaceae (n = 218), Moraxellaceae (n = 163) and Enterococcaceae (n = 92) families as reference sequences. The genomes were annotated using Bakta v1.9.3.30 Plasmid contigs were identified and typed using MOB-suite v3.1.931 with its default database and identity/coverage thresholds. Circularity of plasmids was confirmed using read mapping.

Typing and phylogenetic analysis

MLST was performed via the PubMLST portal.32 For phylogenetic and comparative analyses, A. baumannii genomes from India were retrieved from NCBI datasets33 on 5 October 2024 by filtering a global metadata JSON file for assemblies with geographical location annotated as ‘India*’. To minimize redundancy, RefSeq assemblies were retained when both RefSeq and GenBank versions were available; otherwise, GenBank assemblies were used.

To ensure high-quality data for phylogeny, assemblies were further filtered according to the following criteria: (i) removal of atypical or contaminated assemblies; (ii) exclusion of assemblies with CheckM completeness below 89.99%; and (iii) exclusion of genomes with <94% average nucleotide identity (ANI) relative to A. baumannii.34 This yielded 479 high-quality genomes. Core and accessory genome matrices were generated using Roary v3.13.035 with a 95% sequence identity threshold, including a reference A. baumannii genome (GCF_009035845.1) and Acinetobacter nosocomialis (GCF_041021905.1) as an outgroup. Core genome alignments were refined using MAFFT v7.526,36 and phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using IQ-TREE v2.4.037 with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates and 1000 SH-aLRT tests. Trees were visualized using iTOL.38

Comparative analysis of antimicrobial genes

A subset of 31 isolates was selected from the 479-genome dataset based on phylogenetic proximity to the sequenced isolates, with additional selection criteria applied to minimize redundancy from the same submitter, location, sample source or STs (Supplementary methods 2). Antimicrobial resistance genes were identified using AMRFinder v4.0.2239 with database version 2025-03-25.1 and RGI web Portal v6.0.5 with CARD v4.0.1,40 retaining unique genes with ≥90% coverage and ≥90% identity. Resistome profiles were converted into a presence-absence matrix and clustered using pvclust41 in R.

For phylogenetic analysis of blaOXA and blaADC genes, sequences from all 479 genomes were extracted from AMRFinder annotations, dereplicated to retain unique sequences, translated using EMBOSS Transeq,42 aligned with MAFFT (1000 iterative refinements), and subsequently converted into codon-aware nucleotide alignments using PAL2NAL v14.1.43 Phylogenetic trees were inferred using IQ-TREE v2.4.0 under codon substitution models selected by ModelFinder with 1000 ultrafast bootstraps and 1000 SH-aLRT replicates.

Assessment of prior reports of blaOXA-72

To evaluate whether blaOXA-72 had been previously reported from India, a systematic literature survey was conducted using PubMed on 10 January 2026. The search query used (‘blaOXA-72’ OR ‘OXA-72’ OR ‘OXA 72’) AND (‘Acinetobacter baumannii’ OR ‘A. baumannii’ OR ‘Acinetobacter’) AND (‘India’ OR ‘Indian’) resulting in only one study that reported carbapenemase distributions across multiple Asian countries; however, blaOXA-72 was not reported from Indian isolates in that study.

Comparative analysis of virulence-related genes

Virulence-associated genes were identified for the same 31-isolate subset using BLAST+44 against the Virulence Factors Database (VFDB).45 The VFDB nucleotide set A was downloaded from the web server on 30 June 2025 (Supplementary data at JAC-AMR online). Genes with ≥90% identity and ≥90% coverage were retained. Virulence profiles were converted into a presence-absence matrix and clustered using pvclust, following the same approach as for resistome analysis.

Results

Genome sequencing and assembly

A total of 103 seafood samples were collected from eight seafood markets in Kochi, Kerala, India, as part of an unpublished surveillance study. A. baumannii was detected in 10.6% (11/103) of samples. Seven isolates were initially selected based on cost considerations and sampling sources for WGS, but three were excluded after quality control revealed contamination. Consequently, four genomes (AB02, AB05, AB07, AB08) were retained for downstream analyses (Figure 1). Sequencing statistics are summarized in Figure S1.

All four genomes assembled into fully circularized chromosomes with mean coverage exceeding 100× (Figure S1). Plasmids were detected only in isolate AB05, which carried two small, high-copy-number plasmids (>1000× coverage) belonging to rep_cluster_1226 and rep_cluster_1233. These replicon types are rarely reported in A. baumannii and may represent lineage-specific or niche-associated plasmids. Genome quality metrics indicated near-complete assemblies, with BUSCO scores of 99.6%–100% and CheckM completeness estimates of 99.12%–100% (Table 1).

Molecular typing and phylogenetic placement

Species identity was confirmed by FastANI, with all genomes showing ≥97.7% ANI to A. baumannii. Using pasture scheme, MLST assigned AB02 to ST1422, AB07 to ST479, and AB08 to ST2276, while AB05 did not match any defined ST. None of these STs have been previously reported from Indian A. baumannii isolates.10–12,46,47 All three identified STs are also singletons and not linked to any known clonal complex.

To contextualize these isolates within the Indian A. baumannii population, a core-genome maximum-likelihood phylogeny was constructed using 483 genomes (4 study isolates and 479 publicly available Indian genomes from hospital, animal, environmental and laboratory sources). In total, 2489 core genes were identified, with A. nosocomialis serving as the outgroup. The resulting phylogenetic tree was annotated using metadata on STs and One Health domain for each genome (Figures 2 and 3).

The innermost ring near the nodes denotes the isolate’s source domain (hospital-, animal-, environment-, laboratory-associated, reference genomes, and isolates from this study), while the outer ring represents STs.

Zoomed-in view of the core-genome phylogeny highlighting isolates from this study: enlarged section of Figure 2 focusing on clades containing the isolates sequenced in this study.

The phylogeny revealed a heterogeneous population structure dominated by globally disseminated ST2 lineages, alongside numerous infrequently sampled clades. The four seafood-associated isolates were distributed across distinct phylogenetic branches, indicating multiple independent lineages rather than a single clonal origin.

Among them, AB08 was the most distant to the others, clustering near two hospital-associated ST2 genomes (GCF_001276055.1 and GCF_022342205.1). AB02 was positioned near an animal-associated isolate within a clade dominated by ST52, a lineage found from diverse isolation sources including hospital- and animal-associated sources. AB05 and AB07, on the other hand, formed a tight subclade closely related to an environmental isolate of unknown ST (GCF_023094035.1).

Comparative resistome and virulome analysis

To assess the pathogenic and clinical significance of study isolates, we conducted a comparative analysis of their resistome and virulome with the Indian hospital-, animal- and environment-associated strains (n = 31). A curated set of 31 out of 479 Indian A. baumannii genomes was prepared based on phylogenetic proximity, submitters, isolation source, ST and other additional criteria representing hospital-, animal- and environment-associated isolates.

Resistome profiling

Resistome comparison was performed using presence-absence matrices of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), followed by hierarchical clustering with pvclust (Figure 4). All four study isolates harboured chromosomally encoded Acinetobacter-derived cephalosporinases (blaADC), aminoglycoside nucleotidyltransferase (ant(3′′)-IIa), and blaOXA-51-family genes, consistent with the intrinsic resistance profile of A. baumannii. The allelic variants of blaADC and blaOXA identified in study isolates were distinct from those previously reported from Indian isolates. The specific allele designations were as follows: AB02 carried blaADC-57 and blaOXA-688; AB05 carried blaADC-167, a non-characterized blaOXA allele, and blaOXA-72; AB07 carried blaADC-88 and blaOXA-126; while AB08 contained blaADC-311 and blaOXA-714. Allele assignments for blaOXA and blaADC genes were supported by uniform Nanopore read coverage and intact ORFs, as assessed by read mapping (Supplementary methods 1, Figure S2).

The three-colour strips from left to right represent the source domain (hospital-, animal-, environment-associated and isolates from this study), the isolation source (e.g. blood, seafood market, wastewater) and the ST assigned by MLST. Beyond β-lactamases, all isolates possessed a conserved set of chromosomally encoded efflux systems, including major facilitator superfamily (MFS) transporters (abaQ, amvA), the multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE) transporter abeM, the small multidrug resistance (SMR) pump abeS, and resistance-nodulation-division (RND)-family systems adeFGH and adeIJK, together forming a conserved intrinsic backbone that is known to contribute to reduced susceptibility to fluoroquinolones, tetracylines, disinfectants and cationic dyes.48–50 The complete adeABC operon together with its two-component regulator adeRS was only detected in AB02; however, no mutations associated with efflux overexpression, which are typically linked to MDR, were detected. Similarly, The presence of adeN and absence of adeL further suggest that adeIJK and adeFGH remain under basal regulatory control rather than overexpressed MDR configurations.48,49 The fosfomycin-associated efflux determinant abaF was present in AB02, AB07 and AB08, while a fos gene encoding glutathione S-transferase was detected in AB02 and AB05.

Mobile genetic element (MGE)-associated resistance genes, such as integron-borne sulfonamide or tetracycline genes, were largely absent, distinguishing these isolates from typical hospital-associated strains. Acquired carbapenemases were also absent in all except isolate AB05, which harboured blaOXA-72 on a mobile plasmid. To the best of our knowledge, based on a systematic literature search conducted up to January 2026, this is the first report of blaOXA-72 from India. Notably, AB05 lacked the otherwise widespread ant(3′′)-IIa gene, which may reflect lineage- or niche-associated variation and warrants further investigation through broader environmental sampling.

Hierarchical clustering separated genomes into two major resistome profiles. One cluster consisted predominantly of MDR clinical isolates enriched in acquired carbapenemases (e.g. blaOXA-23, blaOXA-58) along with their intrinsic variants, aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, tetracycline efflux determinants and integron-associated ARGs. While the other cluster contained isolates with patchy, low-density ARG repertoires dominated by variants of chromosomally encoded blaOXA-51-family and blaADC cephalosporinases. All the isolates from this study belonged to this cluster. Only sporadic acquisition of fosfomycin or sulfonamide resistance genes was seen. The STs of clustered isolates were also diverse, but the same STs had similar ARG patterns regardless of their origin, indicating their relevance for clinical settings even when ARGs are not as enriched as MDR isolates of another cluster.

Phylogeny of intrinsic β-lactamases

Considering the variety in intrinsic blaOXA and blaADC genes among all the isolates and to test the uniqueness of the new alleles presented in study isolates, we constructed a codon-aware maximum-likelihood phylogeny of all blaOXA (Figure 5a) and blaADC (Figure 5b) alleles from all 479 Indian genomes.

Maximum-likelihood phylogeny of blaOXA and blaADC alleles in Indian A. baumannii isolates. The tree shows the relationship among hospital-, animal- and environment-associated blaOXA (a) and blaADC (b) variants alongside the alleles from this study (highlighted). Bootstrap support is indicated by circle size, and source types are colour-coded. The STs of the isolates from which the alleles arise are depicted in the bottom bar.

The blaOXA phylogeny clearly separated intrinsic OXA-51-like variants from acquired carbapenemases (e.g. OXA-23-, OXA-24- and OXA-58-like). Alleles from seafood-associated isolates occupied distinct, often long branches, indicating substantial divergence from common clinical variants. The uncharacterized blaOXA allele in AB05 clustered closely with blaOXA-126 from AB07, consistent with their closeness in core genome. Similarly, blaADC alleles from the study isolates also formed independent, well-supported branches distinct from dominant clinical variants. AB05 and AB07 again showed the closest relationship. The divergence of these blaADC alleles likely reflects long-term, independent chromosomal evolution shaped by localized environmental selection pressures.

Virulence gene profile

Virulome comparison using a presence-absence matrix and hierarchical clustering segregated genomes into three major clusters. Each of them exhibited distinct patterns of virulence factor (VF) conservation (Figure 6).

Comparative virulome analysis of Indian A. baumannii isolates. Virulence gene presence (dark) and absence (light) with hierarchical clustering on the left. From left to right, the colour strips represent source domain, isolation source and ST. Circle sizes on the branches indicate approximate unbiased P values for branch support.

Across all three clusters, most isolates possessed a nearly complete virulome, with dense blocks of critical determinants including the csuA/BABCDE chaperone–usher pili cluster (biofilm initiation), ompA (outer membrane adhesion and apoptosis induction), bap (biofilm-associated protein), bas/bau siderophore systems (iron acquisition), pgaABCD (poly-β-1,6-N-acetylglucosamine biosynthesis), and multiple type VI secretion system (T6SS) core components. In contrast, capsule biosynthesis genes (ACICU_RS00; immune evasion) and the HemO gene cluster (ACICU_RS0; haem utilization) showed high variability and drove the cluster separation.

The first cluster, dominated by ST2 genomes, exhibited the richest virulome. Here, ompA, csu pili genes, and iron uptake systems were intact, indicating preserved potential for acute pathogenicity. Isolate AB05 fell within this cluster and, in addition to most core VFs, also carried bap and a complete HemO gene cluster, a feature often associated with hypervirulent strains. The second, smaller cluster comprised genomes of several clinically invasive isolates, which were lacking the entire VF modules of the T6SS and HemO cluster. The third and largest cluster contained three of our seafood market isolates (AB07, AB08 and AB02). These genomes shared nearly identical VF repertoires with invasive clinical strains, retaining all major adhesion, iron acquisition and secretion systems, and variable presence of capsular loci. Notably, no virulence-associated mobile genetic elements were detected in the study isolates, suggesting that they are stably integrated into the chromosome and maintained independently of recent horizontal transfer.

Discussion

A. baumannii is a highly adaptable opportunistic pathogen with an exceptional ability to survive under diverse environmental stresses.51,52 Its resilience is supported by remarkable genome plasticity, enabling it to adapt to both clinical and non-clinical niches.6,53–55 In India, fisheries and seafood markets provide diverse ecological conditions where bacteria are exposed to mixed microbial communities, variable salinity and fluctuating temperatures, making them potential reservoirs for lineages with clinical relevance. In this context, we sequenced four A. baumannii isolates from seafood markets to explore their genetic diversity and clinical relevance.

The four isolates represented genetically distinct lineages and showed minimal overlap with globally dominant clonal complexes, except for AB08, which clustered near the internationally prevalent IC2 lineage. The diversity of STs among these isolates further supports the hypothesis that they derive from multiple independent environmental lineages rather than clonal hospital outbreaks. This observation is consistent with global patterns of IC2 dominance while highlighting the presence of unexplored A. baumannii genotypes from underrepresented sources.56 The phylogenetic proximity of AB05 and AB07 to each other points to the existence of environmental reservoirs harbouring less common lineages.

Genome-inferred resistome profiles revealed that these isolates were dominated by intrinsic resistance determinants rather than extensive repertoires of horizontally acquired ARGs, consistent with environmental isolates.57,58 The phylogenetic divergence of blaOXA and blaADC alleles from common clinical variants further supports long-term chromosomal evolution in non-clinical environments rather than recent acquisition from hospital lineages. Hierarchical clustering further supported this distinction, with all study isolates grouping within a resistome cluster characterized by intrinsic β-lactamases and limited accessory ARG content. Efflux pumps harboured by the study isolates, which are known to mediate reduced susceptibility to fluoroquinolones, disinfectants and various cationic compounds, were also widely distributed among comparative genomes. This distribution supports their role in intrinsic tolerance and environmental persistence. Nevertheless, the proximity of ARG profiles to those of clinical strains suggests that non-clinical A. baumannii lineages may possess latent clinical relevance under selective pressure. This is exemplified by three ST52 strains in our comparative analysis, originating from hospital, animal and environmental sources, all sharing a nearly identical core resistome with study isolates. Notably, one animal-derived ST52 strain had acquired a resistome comparable to clinically important MDR strains, consistent with previous reports of ST52 accumulating resistance genes via plasmids over time.3 Among all study isolates, of particular concern is isolate AB05, which carried plasmid-borne blaOXA-72, a carbapenemase previously linked to rapid dissemination during nosocomial outbreaks.59,60 The detection of blaOXA-72 in a seafood market isolate highlights the potential for clinically significant resistance determinants to circulate outside hospital settings prior to clinical emergence.

Virulome analysis showed that seafood-derived isolates retained a conserved core virulence repertoire comparable to that of invasive clinical strains, including adhesion, biofilm formation, iron acquisition and secretion systems. The absence of mobile genetic elements associated with these determinants suggests stable chromosomal maintenance rather than recent horizontal acquisition. Notably, AB05, in addition to core virulome, also retained a complete HemO gene cluster, a feature associated with hypervirulent A. baumannii strains, further underscoring its clinical relevance. The combination of a hypervirulent VF profile with the presence of blaOXA-72 in AB05 underscores how underexplored genotypes may become clinically important. This, along with its phylogenetic placement near AB07, shows how closely related isolates can diverge in clinically relevant traits through horizontal gene acquisition, despite conserved core genomes. Such divergence underscores the dynamic nature of A. baumannii genome evolution and the potential for non-clinical lineages to acquire features associated with increased clinical relevance.

This study was limited by the small number of isolates and the availability of public genomes for comparison, which may not fully capture the breadth of environmental diversity or local transmission networks. Moreover, the resistance and virulence interpretations presented in the study indicate genomic potential rather than confirmed clinical phenotypes. Nevertheless, the genomic diversity and clinically relevant features observed even among four isolates demonstrate the value of targeted environmental surveillance. Expanding genomic monitoring beyond clinical settings to include food-handling environments, aquatic systems and wastewater networks will be essential to operationalize a One Health approach and improve early detection of emerging high-risk A. baumannii lineages in India.
