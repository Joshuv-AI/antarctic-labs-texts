Effects of Buspirone Combined with Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy on Emotional Improvement, Sleep Quality, and Serum Cortisol Level in Patients with Generalized Anxiety Disorder
Authors: Yimeng Ma, Zhiguo Ge, Xinyan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Buspirone shows a certain efficacy in patients with generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), but its combination therapy with mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) remains unclear. To compare buspirone+MBCT versus buspirone alone for GAD in a retrospective cohort.Methods:According to the treatment regimens, patients were assigned to the experimental group (n=50) to receive 120 minutes of MBCT sessions per week plus buspirone, or the control group (n=50) to receive buspirone only. Comprehensive evaluations were conducted at 5 time points (baseline (Week 0, W0), W1, W2, W4, and W8), including clinician-rated measures (Hamilton Anxiety Scale [HAMA]), self-reported symptoms (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale - Anxiety [HADS-A]), sleep parameters (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [PSQI] and Athens Insomnia Scale [AIS]), and morning serum cortisol levels (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay; μg/mL). Group-by-time effects were modeled with repeated-measures mixed models; 2-sided α=0.05.Results:The combination therapy demonstrated superior outcomes across all measures from W1 onward (all P < .05). By W8, the experimental group showed significantly greater reductions in HAMA (6.1 ± 1.5 vs. 10.1 ± 1.8, P < .001), HADS-A (4.2 ± 1.6 vs. 5.6 ± 1.8, P < .001), PSQI (5.1 ± 2.0 vs. 7.2 ± 2.2, P < .001), and AIS (2.6 ± 1.5 vs. 3.9 ± 1.6, P < .001). Cortisol levels decreased more substantially in the combination group (209.8 ± 33.1 μg/dL vs. 264.0 ± 48.5 μg/dL, P < .001). Treatment effects emerged rapidly, with significant differences in HAMA (P < .001) and cortisol (P < .001) evident by W1. Adverse events were mild and comparable.Conclusion:Adding MBCT to buspirone was associated with faster and greater clinical and biomarker improvements; prospective randomized trials are warranted.

INTRODUCTION

Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) is a prevalent mental disorder characterized by persistent, excessive worry and somatic symptoms, with a global prevalence estimated at 3%-5%.1,2 Generalized anxiety disorder not only affects the mental health of patients but may also trigger a series of physiological reactions, such as muscle tension, palpitations, sweating, insomnia, etc., seriously impacting the quality of life of patients.3 Although benzodiazepines (such as diazepam) have a significant effect in alleviating anxiety symptoms in the short term, long-term use poses risks of dependence and adverse reactions, such as sedation and dizziness.4,5,6,7 Furthermore, pharmacotherapy alone often exhibits limitations in sustaining long-term remission. Conversely, psychological interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) can ameliorate symptoms but may face challenges with patient adherence in certain populations.8,9,10 Consequently, exploring synergistic strategies that integrate pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions represents a key focus of current research.11

Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) is an intervention grounded in cultivating present-moment awareness and non-judgmental acceptance, effectively modulating the neurobiological mechanisms underlying anxiety.12 Accumulating evidence indicates that MBCT offers significant advantages for GAD patients in enhancing emotional regulation, facilitating cognitive restructuring, and improving sleep quality, demonstrating non-inferiority to traditional CBT in terms of therapeutic efficacy.1 Furthermore, in the treatment process of MBCT, emphasis is placed on the awareness and acceptance of anxiety emotions, which helps patients develop healthier coping strategies, thereby reducing the risk of anxiety recurrence.2 However, the long-term efficacy of MBCT still needs to be further verified, especially in terms of patient compliance and treatment persistence.13

Buspirone is a non-benzodiazepine anti-anxiety drug. Its mechanism of action is a partial agonist of the 5-HT1A receptor, which can regulate neurotransmitter levels to alleviate anxiety symptoms. Compared with benzodiazepines, buspirone has lower dependence and less sedative effect, and it does not cause significant sexual dysfunction.3 However, its effect on alleviating physical symptoms is limited, and it takes a long time to take effect. Usually, it takes several weeks for significant results to be observed. Therefore, buspirone is often used as an adjunctive treatment in the treatment of GAD, especially for patients with depressive symptoms.14

The combined application of MBCT and buspirone may achieve more comprehensive control of anxiety symptoms through a multi-target intervention strategy. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy enhances patients’ self-awareness and emotional regulation abilities, reducing excessive responses to anxiety, while buspirone alleviates anxiety symptoms by regulating neurotransmitter levels.15,16 The combination of the 2 can not only improve the core symptoms of anxiety but also enhance the overall quality of life of patients.17,18 Furthermore, this combined intervention model has potential advantages in reducing drug dependence and adverse reactions and is particularly suitable for patients with long-term treatment needs.

The innovation of this study lies in the first systematic evaluation of the combined application of MBCT and buspirone on the emotions, sleep, and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis function of patients with GAD. Through multidimensional assessment, this study will provide new evidence to support the comprehensive treatment of GAD and offer references for clinical practice. Additionally, this study will also explore the differences in short-term efficacy between MBCT and CBT, as well as their stability in long-term follow-up, providing a basis for individualized treatment strategies for GAD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

A total of 100 GAD patients were consecutively included in this study from October 2022 to March 2025. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) diagnosed as GAD per the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) criteria;19 (2) aged over 18 years; (3) Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) score ≥14; (4) received buspirone or buspirone combined with MBCT; (5) had available clinical data for assessments. Exclusion criteria included: (1) comorbid with other severe mental diseases such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depressive disorder which had been confirmed before inclusion and determined by the SCID-I (Structured Clinical Interview for DSM Disorders); (2) used psychotropic medication within 4 weeks previously; (3) during pregnancy or gestation period. Patients offered written informed consent. This study was approved by the Henan Medical University Ethics Committee (Date: August 20, 2022; No.: XYLL-20220395).

Data Collection

After inclusion, the following data were collected from all GAD patients: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), smoking, drinking, household registration, education level, and duration of GAD. Treatment information was also collected, based on which patients were allocated to the experimental group and control group. All data underwent dual-entry verification by independent researchers. Additionally, adverse events were also collected.

Treatments

Based on the treatment regimens, patients were assigned to 2 groups. Patients in the control group received buspirone, and patients in the experimental group received buspirone combined with MBCT. The MBCT intervention was administered by a licensed clinical psychologist with specialized certification in MBCT and over 1500 hours of documented mindfulness teaching experience. The therapeutic protocol featured an optimized 8-week curriculum conducted in carefully controlled group settings (6-8 participants per cohort) within a dedicated meditation studio equipped with biofeedback-assisted environmental controls. Each 120-minute session incorporated 3 dynamically sequenced therapeutic modules: (1) technologically enhanced mindfulness practices utilizing real-time EEG neurofeedback during body scan exercises and motion-capture assisted posture alignment in sitting meditation; (2) metacognitive training employing novel “thought de-fusion” techniques adapted from acceptance and commitment therapy, including computer-assisted visualization of cognitive patterns; and (3) interactive psychoeducation modules featuring case-based learning of anxiety maintenance models, with personalized symptom tracking via mobile ecological momentary assessment between sessions.20,21

Assessments

We employed multiple validated instruments to assess treatment outcomes across different domains, including HAMA score, Hospital Anxiety Depression Scale-Anxiety (HADS-A) score, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) score, Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS) score, and cortisol at 5 time points from the clinic’s electronic health records: at baseline (Week 0, W0), posttreatment Weeks 1/2/4/8 (W1, W2, W4, and W8).

Anxiety symptoms were evaluated using the 14-item HAMA score, which quantifies both somatic (7 items) and psychic anxiety (7 items) with a total score range of 0-56 (higher scores indicating greater severity).22 The HADS-A score, consisting of 7 items scored 0-21, provided additional measurement of anxiety severity with established clinical cutoffs.23 Based on the HAMA score and HADS-A score, the treatment efficacy was further assessed, which was defined as the reduction in the HAMA score or HADS-A score ≥50%. Sleep quality was assessed through the 19-item PSQI score (global score range 0-21), evaluating 7 sleep components over the preceding month.24 Insomnia symptoms were specifically measured using the 8-item AIS score (range 0-24), which captures nighttime sleep difficulties and daytime consequences.25 As a biological stress marker, serum cortisol levels were determined through standardized enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay procedures (reported in μg/mL), with morning samples collected between 8:00 and 9:00 AM after overnight fasting to control for diurnal variation.26 All psychological scales demonstrated good internal consistency in the sample (Cronbach’s α > 0.80).

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis followed intention-to-treat principles using SPSS 26.0 (IBM SPSS Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean and SD or standard error, and compared using t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as median and interquartile range, and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are shown using numbers and percentages, and compared using χ2 test, Fisher’s exact test, or Fisher–Freeman–Halton test. Effect sizes were calculated with Cohen’s d. Two-tailed P < .05 indicated statistical significance. Power analysis (α=0.05, β=0.2) determined that 45 subjects/group could detect a 20% intergroup difference in HAMA reduction.

RESULTS

Clinical Characteristics

It demonstrated comparable baseline characteristics between the 2 groups (all P-values > .05). No statistically significant differences were observed between the experimental group and the control group in demographic metrics such as age (42.9 ± 5.9 vs. 44.3 ± 6.0 years), sex (female: 66.0% vs. 58.0%), and BMI (20.5 ± 2.6 vs. 20.2 ± 3.9 kg/m2). Covariates, including disease duration (5.7 vs. 4.7 years) and educational attainment distribution, were also well-balanced, with corresponding P-values ranging from .275 to .838 (Table 1).

Efficacy Assessments of Mental Health

The longitudinal assessment of HAMA scores revealed significant between-group differences following the experimental intervention. Baseline measurements at W0 showed comparable scores between groups (Control: 22.5 ± 4.7 vs. Experiment: 23.4 ± 4.0, P=.344). Treatment effects emerged at W1, with the experimental group demonstrating significantly lower HAMA scores (18.4 ± 5.6) compared to controls (22.3 ± 4.9, P < .001). This therapeutic advantage progressively intensified throughout the study duration, as evidenced by the widening score gap at subsequent timepoints (W2: 14.5 ± 3.9 vs. 18.8 ± 3.8; W4: 11.8 ± 2.6 vs. 16.2 ± 3.7; W8: 6.1 ± 1.5 vs. 10.1 ± 1.8; all P < .001). Notably, the experimental group achieved a 73.9% reduction in HAMA scores from baseline to endpoint (W0-W8), contrasting with the control group’s 55.1% reduction, suggesting superior intervention efficacy (Figure 1A).

For the HADS-A score, treatment effects became statistically significant at W1, with the experimental group showing greater anxiety reduction (10.8 ± 3.8) compared to controls (12.8 ± 4.0, P=.014). This therapeutic advantage progressively intensified throughout the 8-week study period (W2: 8.2 ± 2.2 vs. 9.6 ± 2.9, P=.006; W4: 5.6 ± 2.3 vs. 7.2 ± 2.8, P=.003; W8: 4.2 ± 1.6 vs. 5.6 ± 1.8, P < .001). Notably, the experimental group achieved a 72.7% reduction in HADS-A scores from baseline to endpoint (W0-W8), compared to a 61.9% reduction in the control group (Figure 1B).

The longitudinal efficacy analysis revealed a significant difference between the 2 groups in terms of treatment response rate (defined as a reduction of at least 50% in HAMA or HADS-A scores). At baseline, the severity levels of the 2 groups were comparable (HAMA: P=.344; HADS-A: P=.352). At Week 1, the response rate of the experimental group was 46.0% (23/50), significantly higher than that of the control group at 18.0% (9/50). As subsequent evaluations progressed, the gap in treatment efficacy gradually widened: Week 2 (68.0% vs. 34.0%), Week 4 (84.0% vs. 52.0%), and Week 8 (92.0% vs. 70.0%) (Figure 2). It is noteworthy that the onset time of response in the experimental group was significantly earlier, with over 45% of patients reaching the primary indicator by Week 1, while the proportion for the control group was below 20%. The effect size between the 2 groups increased from a moderate level at Week 1 (Cramer’s V=0.30) to a larger level at Week 8 (V=0.42), confirming the superiority of continuous intervention.

Efficacy Assessments of Sleep Quality Improvement

Longitudinal PSQI assessments revealed progressive sleep quality improvement in the experimental group. Baseline scores showed no significant intergroup difference (Control: 12.8 ± 6.7 vs. Experiment: 13.4 ± 5.5, P=.610). Therapeutic effects became statistically significant, with the experimental group demonstrating lower PSQI score versus controls (W4: 6.4 ± 2.4 vs. 8.5 ± 3.2, P < .001; W8: 5.1 ± 2.0 vs. 7.2 ± 2.2, P < .001). Clinically, the experimental group achieved a 61.9% PSQI reduction from baseline (W0-W8), exceeding the control group’s 43.8% improvement (Figure 3A).

The longitudinal analysis of AIS scores demonstrated significant sleep improvement in the experimental cohort. Baseline measurements showed comparable insomnia severity between groups (Control: 7.9 ± 2.2 vs. Experiment: 8.2 ± 2.0, P=.478). Therapeutic divergence emerged at W1, with the experimental group exhibiting greater AIS reduction (5.3 ± 1.8) versus controls (6.5 ± 1.9, P=.001). This treatment effect progressively intensified (W2: 4.5 ± 1.8 vs. 5.8 ± 1.7, P < .001; W4: 3.4 ± 1.9 vs. 4.8 ± 2.0, P < .001; W8: 2.6 ± 1.5 vs. 3.9 ± 1.6, P < .001). Clinically, the experimental group achieved 68.3% AIS reduction from baseline (W0-W8), surpassing the control group’s 50.6% improvement (Figure 3B).

Efficacy Assessments of Biological Markers

Longitudinal cortisol measurements demonstrated significant intervention effects. Baseline levels showed no intergroup difference (Control: 433.2 ± 52.2 μg/dL vs. Experiment: 426.3 ± 62.3 μg/dL, P=.548). Therapeutic divergence emerged at W1, with the experimental group exhibiting greater cortisol reduction (344.3 ± 57.1 μg/dL) versus controls (394.7 ± 45.5 μg/dL, P < .001). This effect progressively intensified (W2: 305.9 ± 47.2 vs. 369.6 ± 52.1; W4: 273.0 ± 47.1 vs. 344.7 ± 55.7; W8: 209.8 ± 33.1 vs. 264.0 ± 48.5, all P < .001). Clinically, the experimental group achieved 50.8% cortisol reduction from baseline (W0-W8), exceeding the control group’s 39.1% reduction (Figure 4).

Comparison Within Groups

The 8-week intervention demonstrated superior efficacy in the experimental group compared to the control group. Significant improvements were observed in HAMA score, HADS-A score, PSQI score, AIS score, and cortisol levels (all P < .001). The experimental group showed earlier symptom relief at W1 (Table 2).

Comparisons Between the Groups According to Difference Value

The experimental group demonstrated significantly greater improvements than controls across all measures (all P < .001). Mean reductions from baseline were more pronounced in HAMA (−17.3 ± 0.61 vs. −12.4 ± 0.71), HADS-A (−11.2 ± 0.66 vs. −9.1 ± 0.72), PSQI (−8.3 ± 0.74 vs. −5.6 ± 0.79), AIS (−5.6 ± 0.42 vs. −4.0 ± 0.43), and cortisol (−216.5 ± 7.55 vs. −169.2 ± 9.54 μg/dL) (Table 3).

Safety Assessments

It indicated no significant difference in the overall incidence of adverse events between the 2 groups (12.0% vs. 14.0%, P=.766). Adverse events such as dizziness (8.0% vs. 12.0%) and nausea/vomiting (4.0% vs. 6.0%) were universally mild in severity, with no instances leading to study discontinuation (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

The present study provides novel evidence that the experimental intervention concurrently ameliorated anxiety symptoms (72.9% reduction in HAMA scores), sleep disturbances (61.9% decrease in PSQI scores), and HPA axis dysfunction (50.8% cortisol reduction). Significant between-group differences emerged at Week 1 (all P < .05) with progressive enhancement of therapeutic effects over time. The balanced baseline characteristics (all P > .05) effectively minimized potential confounding factors, reinforcing the reliability of these findings. Notably, the intervention demonstrated particularly rapid efficacy during the initial treatment phase (W1-W2), as evidenced by the steepest improvement slope (β=1.24/week).

Mechanistically, the strong correlation between cortisol reduction and HAMA score decline (r=0.82, P < .001) suggests HPA axis modulation as a primary therapeutic pathway. This finding is consistent with established neuroendocrine models of anxiety disorders, where HPA axis hyperactivity has been implicated in both the pathophysiology and maintenance of symptoms (14). For instance, chronic stress disrupts the negative feedback loop of the HPA axis, leading to sustained increases in cortisol levels and altered hippocampal brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression, which are hallmarks of mood and anxiety disorders.27 These data extend current understanding by revealing a distinct rapid-onset phase characterized by nonlinear improvement kinetics during the initial treatment period (W1-W2), a phenomenon not previously reported in conventional pharmacotherapies targeting the HPA axis.28 The temporal concordance between biological (cortisol) and clinical (HAMA) markers strongly supports a putative causal relationship between HPA axis normalization and symptomatic relief, as predicted by the allostatic load theory of stress-related disorders.29 Notably, the observed effect size exceeds those typically seen with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) in comparable populations, suggesting potential superiority in modulating neuroendocrine stress responses. This aligns with findings that SSRIs like escitalopram increase glucocorticoid receptor (GR) mRNA expression in the hippocampus, thereby reducing HPA axis reactivity.27

Clinically, 92% of intervention participants achieved treatment efficacy by W8 versus 70% controls. The comparable adverse event rates (12.0% vs. 14.0%, P=.766) confirm the intervention’s safety profile. These outcomes position the protocol as a viable non-pharmacological alternative for GAD patients, combining early symptom control (significant W1 effects) with sustained therapeutic benefits.

Several limitations warrant consideration: First, the absence of placebo controls prevents definitive attribution of observed effects, as demonstrated in studies where HPA axis modulation was shown to be influenced by both pharmacological and placebo-induced changes in stress responses.30 Second, the 8-week observation window precludes assessment of long-term outcomes, a limitation highlighted in research on aging models where extended follow-up is critical to understanding the sustained effects of interventions on neuroendocrine systems. Third, the unexplored neuroendocrine biomarkers (e.g., BDNF) represent missed opportunities for mechanistic elucidation, as BDNF dysregulation has been consistently linked to HPA axis dysfunction and panic disorder,31 while its role in mediating GR interactions further underscores its importance in stress-related psychiatric disorders.32 These constraints highlight the need for confirmatory studies with extended follow-up and comprehensive biomarker panels.

As a retrospective cohort study with inherent methodological limitations, the research design cannot draw causal relationship inferences that can be established through randomized controlled methods, and no registration was conducted on ClinicalTrials.gov. Although these findings provide valuable real-world evidence regarding the potential effects of the intervention measures, future research should address the 3 key limitations of the non-randomized approach: 1) a multicenter prospective design with standardized implementation plans; 2) systematic control of confounding variables through randomization procedures; 3) inclusion of a placebo control group to separate the specific therapeutic effects. Specifically, the exploratory analysis using the retrospective data developed a predictive model including baseline cortisol levels, which requires prospective validation in a controlled experimental environment, but this research design cannot provide such validation conditions.

Besides, determining the optimal intervention dosage through carefully designed phase 2 dose-escalation trials represents a crucial next step in clinical translation. Additionally, there is a pressing need to develop more sophisticated predictive models by applying machine learning techniques to analyze potential biomarkers, which could enhance treatment personalization. Furthermore, investigating potential synergistic effects when combining this intervention with existing first-line therapies may uncover novel therapeutic strategies. These comprehensive research efforts would significantly advance both the scientific understanding and clinical application of this promising intervention approach. Such efforts would substantially advance the translation of these findings into clinical practice.
