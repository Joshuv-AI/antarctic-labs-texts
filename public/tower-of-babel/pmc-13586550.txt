Dysregulation of Peripheral Blood B Cells and Natural Killer Cells Is Associated With Myocarditis in Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathies
Authors: Carolina Duque, Sylvie T Rousseau, Zuzana Bologna, Monica R Mugnier, Luigi Adamo, Julie J Paik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIdiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs), or myositis, are a group of systemic autoimmune diseases leading to proximal muscle weakness. Myocarditis is a major cause of adverse outcomes. Yet, we know very little about the underlying immunologic processes that are driving myocarditis in the context of IIMs.MethodsWe performed single‐cell RNA sequencing of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 12 individuals: myositis without myocarditis (n = 4), myositis with myocarditis (n = 4), and healthy controls (n = 4), to better understand the immunologic alterations characterizing this manifestation of the disease.ResultsWe found the most notable changes in B cells and natural killer (NK) cells of individuals with myositis with myocarditis compared to myocarditis alone. B cells showed 42 enriched pathways suggestive of increased DNA replication and repair, somatic hypermutation, and lymphocyte activation but a smaller proportion of memory B cells (β = −1.04, false discovery rate = 0.02), suggesting a greater activation of naive B cells in the periphery. NK cells, on the other hand, showed 223 enriched pathways suggestive of reduced proliferation but increased effector functions such as leukocyte degranulation, myeloid cell activation, and tumor necrosis factor secretion, suggesting that NK cells in patients with myositis with myocarditis are more activated but less proliferative than in myositis without myocarditis.ConclusionTogether, these data suggest that B cells and NK cells may be playing important roles in mediating myocarditis pathogenesis in the context of IIMs.

INTRODUCTION

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs), or myositis, are a group of systemic autoimmune diseases characterized by chronic inflammation of the skeletal muscles leading to proximal muscle weakness. It is known, however, that IIMs can also affect cardiac muscle in up to 75% of patients, resulting in myocarditis, heart failure, arrhythmias, sudden cardiac death, and myocardial infarction. 1 These cardiac manifestations are one of the primary causes of death in patients with IIMs, 2 and yet, the pathogenesis of myocardial disease in IIMs has been considerably understudied compared to its skeletal muscle counterpart.

Myocarditis, specifically, may affect up to 50% of patients with IIMs and is a major driver of adverse outcomes. 3 Yet, the immunologic alterations that mediate the development of this myocarditis are almost completely unexplored, and as a result, the therapeutic interventions for these patients remain guided largely by expert opinion rather than evidence‐based studies. A better understanding of these immune drivers is therefore critical to better tailor effective immunotherapies and other treatment strategies for this disease. To begin addressing this knowledge gap, we performed single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from three groups: individuals with myositis but without myocarditis, individuals with myositis and myocarditis, and healthy controls (HCs). From this analysis, we identified distinct alterations in B cells and natural killer (NK) cells in patients with myositis with versus without myocarditis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was approved by the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board (IRB00285294 and IRB00326640), and all individuals provided informed consent for participation in this study. Individuals were recruited and diagnosed as previously described. 4 Briefly, patients with IIMs were recruited as part of routine clinical care at the Johns Hopkins Myositis Center in Baltimore, Maryland, between July 2022 and March 2023. These individuals were diagnosed using the 2017 EULAR/American College of Rheumatology classification criteria 5 and/or the Bohan and Peter criteria. 6 , 7 Myocarditis was diagnosed by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (cMRI) according to the 2018 Lake Louise Criteria. A single expert radiologist read all the cMRI to ensure consistency in diagnosis. HCs were recruited from Johns Hopkins using flyers in clinics. Only individuals with available PBMCs were included in this study: four patients with IIMs without myocarditis, four patients with IIMs with myocarditis (myositis and myocarditis), and four HCs. Clinical and demographic data were compared across patient groups using R (version 4.4.0). Fisher exact test and Wilcoxon rank‐sum test were used when appropriate.

For scRNAseq, CD45‐Phycoerythrin (PE)+ Propidium Iodide (PI)− PBMCs were fluorescence‐activated cell sorted on a MoFlow sorter (Beckman Coulter), stained with TotalSeq‐C anti‐human hashtag antibodies (BioLegend), pooled (Figure 1A), processed using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5p RNA library version 2 (10x Genomics), and sequenced on a NovaSeqX Plus (Illumina). Reads were demultiplexed using Cell Ranger (10x Genomics) and mapped to GRCh38. Data are available on GEO under GSE310662. Filtered feature barcode matrixes were processed with Seurat (version 5.3.0) on R (version 4.4.0). Doublets were further removed using scDblFinder (version 1.20.0), and remaining cells were filtered to those with 200 to 6,500 unique features and <15% mitochondrial reads. Harmony (version 1.2.3) was used to remove batch effects between pooled reactions, and any remaining low‐quality clusters were removed. Clusters were annotated using CellTypist (version 1.6.3) Immune_All_High model with majority voting. Simplified cell type annotations are shown in Figure 1B. Clusters were pseudobulked by individual for gene expression analysis using DESeq2 (version 1.46.0). Gene set enrichment analysis of gene ontology biologic pathways was then performed using ClusterProfiler (version 4.4.4).

RESULTS

For this study, the autoantibody‐defined myositis subgroups included the following: anti‐HMGCR antibodies (n = 2), anti‐Ro 60 dermatomyositis (n = 1), and anti‐TIF1 dermatomyositis (n = 1) in patients with myositis without myocarditis; and antisynthetase syndrome (n = 1) and anti‐Ku–positive systemic sclerosis (SSc) and myositis overlap (n = 3) in patients with myositis and myocarditis. The patients with myositis with and without myocarditis were generally well matched by sex and immunosuppressive medication use. Consistent with the presence of cardiac damage, the patients with myositis and myocarditis had significantly elevated T1 and T2 on cMRI (P = 0.029), lower left ventricular ejection fraction (P = 0.017), higher NT‐proBNP (P = 0.021), elevated troponin (P = 0.029), and greater use of beta blockers and spironolactone compared to patients with myositis without myocarditis (P = 0.029). HCs were younger (P = 0.050), all male, and were not taking any immunosuppressive medications (Table 1).

To investigate the immunologic drivers of IIMs, we performed scRNAseq of PBMCs collected from these patients and compared their gene expression and population subtype composition. We found striking differences in B cells, monocytes, and NK cells. Specifically, 42 pathways were enriched in B cells, 48 in monocytes, and 223 in NK cells of patients with myositis and myocarditis versus those with myositis without myocarditis (Figure 1C). In B cells, some of the most significantly up‐regulated pathways were related to DNA replication and repair, somatic hypermutation, and lymphocyte activation, suggesting greater activation of B cells in patients with myositis and myocarditis compared to myositis without myocarditis. Conversely, in monocytes, we found a down‐regulation of immune cell chemotaxis, wound healing, macrophage differentiation, and cell adhesion, suggesting reduced monocyte effector functions and differentiation in patients with myositis with myocarditis versus myositis without myocarditis. Notably, these pathways were generally up‐regulated compared to HCs, suggesting patients with myositis with myocarditis do experience increased monocyte activity but to a lesser degree than patients with myositis without myocarditis. Finally, in NK cells, there were various down‐regulated processes related to cell division but also up‐regulation of leukocyte degranulation, myeloid cell activation, and tumor necrosis factor secretion. This suggests that in patients with myositis with myocarditis, the NK cells are less proliferative but more activated to execute their effector functions.

Next, to further investigate these key immune cell populations in more detail, we subclustered these cells at a higher resolution and evaluated their subpopulations’ marker expression. Using beta regression, we found clear differences in the composition of T/NK cell, monocyte, and B cell subpopulations across the disease conditions. Figure 1D shows that there is a smaller proportion of Treg cells in myositis and myocarditis (β = −0.44, false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.001) and HCs (β = −0.31, FDR < 0.001) compared to patients with myositis without myocarditis. In addition, antigen‐presenting cytotoxic T effector memory/effector memory T cells re‐expressing CD45RA cells (cluster 1: CD8A, CCL5, KLRG1, GZMH, HLA‐DPB1, and HLA‐DPA1) comprised a smaller proportion of NK/T cells in both patients with myositis with and without myocarditis compared to HCs (β = −1.01, FDR = 0.007, and β = −0.87, FDR = 0.01, respectively). In the monocyte populations (Figure 1E), there was a significant decrease in the proportion of classical anti‐inflammatory/maturing monocytes (classical cluster 2: NRG1, APAF1, NHSL2, IGFBP2, and TMEM144) in both patients with myositis with and without myocarditis compared to HCs (β = −2.96, FDR < 0.001, and β = −2.21, FDR < 0.001 respectively), whereas classical activated and inflammatory monocytes (classical cluster 1: CXCL8, TREM1, JUN, and PFKFB3) were significantly decreased in myositis and myocarditis (β = −1.63, FDR = 0.028) and HCs (β = −1.89, FDR = 0.023) compared to patients with myositis without myocarditis. This suggests that patients with myositis and myocarditis have a less inflammatory monocyte composition. Finally, in Figure 1F, memory B cells made up a smaller proportion of B cells in patients with myositis with myocarditis compared to patients with myositis without myocarditis (β = −1.04, FDR = 0.02). Naive differentiating B cells (naive cluster 2: IL‐4R, KCNG1, CD200, SESN1, and GCNT1 8 ) also tended to decrease in patients with myositis and myocarditis in favor of more naive stimulated cells (naive cluster 1: CD83 and NR4A2) and naive transitional‐like cells (naive cluster 3: CD9, PLD4, CD38, SOX4, and MME/CD10).

DISCUSSION

Overall, these results point to a potential role of B cell/antibody‐mediated responses and NK cell activation in patients with IIMs who end up developing myocarditis early in their disease trajectory. Consistent with our findings, prior studies have observed reduced NK cell abundance but increased expression of activating receptors in patients with IIMs with interstitial lung disease compared to those without, 9 suggesting that NK cell dynamics may play an important role when patients with IIMs develop extramuscular organ involvement. Similarly, although B cells have long been associated with IIMs, they are increasingly being recognized for their potential role in cardiac disease 10 and appear to be effective therapeutic targets in patients with myositis with myocarditis. 11 Surprisingly, although T cells are known to play a major role IIM pathogenesis, 12 we did not find notable alterations in T cell gene expression and few changes in their composition in patients with myositis with myocarditis versus without myocarditis. Similarly, although monocytes have been increasingly implicated in IIMs, 13 we found that these cells seemed to be less activated and less proinflammatory in patients with myositis and myocarditis compared to patients with myositis without myocarditis but more activated compared to HCs. This suggests that although T cells and monocytes are likely still playing an important role in causing general muscle damage, it appears that NK cells and B cells may be more important for the myocardial disease manifestation in IIMs.

Although the findings from this study shed an important light on the immunologic alterations in IIM myocarditis, there are certain limitations to consider. First, the individuals in this study had already initiated immunosuppressive treatment at the time of sample collection, which can make it challenging to decipher which immune alterations are due to the disease and which are due to the immunosuppressive medications. To mitigate this challenge, we focused most of our analyses on comparing patients with myositis with versus without myocarditis because these individuals were on comparable immunosuppressive regimens. Furthermore, individuals on rituximab were removed from the B cell analysis because this drug greatly alters B cell populations. Although rituximab can also affect other immune populations, 14 these effects are less severe and less consistent. Therefore, individuals on rituximab were kept in the monocyte, NK cell, and T cell analyses but were matched across groups. Given the rarity of this disease, we had a small sample size of four patients per group, and thus, our data set is expected to be particularly variable and underpowered. Despite this, we were still able to identify notable differences among disease conditions. Our small groups also had distinct IIM subtypes, and it is possible that the findings seen here are driven by the anti‐Ku–positive SSc with myositis overlap versus dermatomyositis subtypes rather than the presence versus absence of myocarditis. These findings are likely still informative given the strong association of anti‐Ku–positive SSc/myositis with developing myocarditis. 15 , 16 Future studies with large longitudinal cohorts at the time of IIM diagnosis, before treatment initiation, and with additional IIM subtypes will be needed to confirm our observations and evaluate their potential therapeutic implications. Finally, this study was performed on PBMCs, which may not necessarily reflect changes occurring in the cardiac tissue. Additional studies on cardiac tissue will help validate the role of local B cells and NK cells in causing cardiac damage.
