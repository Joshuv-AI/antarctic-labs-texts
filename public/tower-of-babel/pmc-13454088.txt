Emerging landscape of oncogenic signaling pathways in cervical cancer
Authors: Ammad Ahmad Farooqi, Rukset Attar, Maria Luisa Gasparri, Rossana Berardi, Panagiotis Christopoulos, Maria Gazouli, Sundas Fayyaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractState-of-the-art rodent models have aided researchers in the refinement of a holistic understanding of the mechanistic insights related to metastasis, which will eventually lead to the discovery of new multi-targeted therapeutic options. More recently, extraordinary breakthroughs in the genomics, proteomics and mouse modeling have significantly fostered a renaissance of cutting-edge researches on metastasis, leading to conceptually conceivable frameworks to gain insights about its molecular underpinnings. Exciting field of cervical cancer metastasis has moved to the forefront of studies in the past few decades. In this review we have set spotlight on pivotal role of TGF/SMAD, Wnt/β-catenin, AKT/mTOR and Notch signaling in cervical cancer progression. This review also provides an intriguing summary of quintessential role of ubiquitination in regulation of cervical cancer. By integration of mechanistic insights with multi-scale computational studies, basic researchers and clinicians have started to develop previously unprecedented understanding about disease aggressiveness of cervical cancer. More importantly, rational integration of multi-omics and single-cell sequencing technologies into clinical trial designs will enable researchers to gather refined scientific data for result-oriented clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Primary tumors have characteristically unique ability to prepare the local micro-environment of distantly located organs for colonization of tumor cells even before their arrival. Therefore, “seed and soil” hypothesis has not only revolutionized our knowledge about tumor metastases but also provides clarification for organotropism in metastasis. Importantly, pro-metastatic tumor cells (the “seed”) colonize in specified organ sites (the “soil”) where the micro-environment is highly favorable and conducive for metastases. Emerging wealth of information shows that primary tumors facilitate metastasis by promoting the creation of a supporting micro-environment in a secondary organ site, coined as a pre-metastatic niche [1–3].

Intriguingly, the intricate multistage processes underlying metastasis can be effectively modeled in mice, whose analyses can capture the spatial and temporal progression of metastasis in whole organism and leverage our understanding about the mechanisms which underlie carcinogenesis and tumor heterogeneity. Unraveling of the complex cellular and molecular processes associated with metastasis is achieved rationally by tactful engineering of sophisticated animal models that have the ability to recapitulate the requisite tumor cell-extrinsic and -intrinsic mechanisms at the organismal level. Metastatic relapses invariably portend poor prognostic outcome, as metastatic outgrowths become resistant to pharmacological treatments, seed additional metastatic colonies and consequently impair the functionalities of vital organs [4–7].

In this mini-review, we have attempted to analyze how different deregulated cell signaling pathways regulate onset and progression of cervical cancer. We have portrayed a mechanistic landscape of TGFβ/SMAD, Wnt/β-catenin, mTOR, JAK/STAT, Hippo/YAP and NOTCH signaling in the progression of cervical cancer.

Role of TGFβ/SMAD signaling in cervical cancer

Upon binding of the ligand to type II and type I receptors, signals are transmitted intracellularly through SMADs. Receptor-regulated SMADS (SMAD1, 2, 3, 5 and 8) are directly phosphorylated and activated by the kinase domain of type I receptors [8, 9]. After phosphorylation, SMAD2 and SMAD3 oligomerize with SMAD4 and accumulate in the nucleus (shown in Fig. 1a) [10, 11]. TGFβ/SMAD pathway has been shown to play dualistic role in regulation of carcinogenesis. In this section, we will bring to the attention, the diametrically opposing role of TGFβ/SMAD cascade in the regulation of cervical cancer. This section mainly deals with pivotal role of TGF/SMAD signaling in cancer progression, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and metastatic spread of cervical cancer cells.

Oncogenic role: TGFβ/SMAD signaling has been shown to promote the progression of cervical cancer. Confluence of information has provided expansion of novel regulators of TGFβ/SMAD signaling. CDH20 (Cadherin 20) is a negative regulator of TGFβ/SMAD signaling. It interacts with β-catenin and reduces the migration and invasion of cervical cancer cells. CDH20 inhibited receptor-mediated phosphorylation of SMAD2/3 and blocked the expression of SNAIL. Identification of SNAIL as an inducer of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) has opened new avenues. SMAD2/3 have been shown to stimulate the expression of SNAIL in cervical cancer cells (Fig. 1a) [12]. These findings provided evidence about SMAD2/3 signaling mediated pivotal role in the progression of cervical cancer mainly through stimulation of SNAIL. Therefore, inhibition of SMAD2/3/SNAIL axis will be valuable in the prevention of cervical cancer progression.

PD-L1 (Programmed death ligand-1)/PD-1 signaling is positively regulated by TGFβ/SMAD signaling. Dual inhibition of PD-1/PD-L1 and TGFβ cascades seems to be an effective approach for malignancies. SHR-1701 is a promising bifunctional fusion protein composed of a mAb against PD-L1 fused with extracellular domain of TGFβ receptor II. SHR-1701 has been shown to exhibit potent antitumor efficacy and manageable safety in patients with metastatic or recurrent cervical cancer after platinum-based chemotherapeutic drugs [13].

As TGFβ transduces the signals through TGF receptors, therefore, overexpression of TGFβRI switches on the intracellular signaling. NSD2 is a family member of histone lysine methyltransferases and promotes progression of cervical cancer. Levels of TGFβ and TGFβRI were found to be reduced drastically in NSD2-silenced cervical cancer cells. Whereas, remarkably bigger metastatic nodules were evident on the surface of lungs in mice injected with NSD2-expressing-SiHa cells [14]. These findings suggested that TGF/TGFR-mediated intracellular signaling played central role in the metastatic dissemination of cervical cancer cells.

Activation of cGAS (cyclic GMP-AMP synthase) by cytosolic DNA triggers the formation of cyclic GMP-AMP. It is a unique second messenger, which binds to STING (Stimulator of interferon Genes). Therefore, functionally, STING signaling is involved in the induction of CD4+CD25highFOXP3+ regulatory T cells from CD4+-naïve T cells. Tumors send signals for the recruitment and expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) for the suppression of the anti-tumor immunological responses. Accordingly, STING signaling induces SMAD3 and STAT5-mediated transcriptional upregulation of FOXP3. Exosomes derived from cervical cancer cells efficiently induce the activation of STING pathway in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and promote immunosuppressive microenvironment [15].

CSRP2BP (CRP2-binding partner) worked synchronously with SMAD4 and triggered transcriptional stimulation of N-cadherin in cervical cancer cells (Fig. 1a). There was an evident increase in the pulmonary metastatic foci and a higher metastatic incidence in mice injected with CSRP2BP-overexpressing-Hela cells [16].

SYVN1, an E3 ubiquitin ligase has the ability to ubiquitinate different proteins. SYVN1 induced ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of NLRP3 in cervical cancer cells. IGF2BP1 (Insulin-like growth factor-2 binding proteins) is an m6A reader and enhances the stability and storage of target mRNAs. LRRC75A-AS1 competitively binds to IGF2BP1 and impairs the stability of SYVN1 mRNA. NLRP3 inflammasomes induce caspase 1-dependent release of the interleukin-1β. NLRP3 activates IL1β/Smad2/3 signaling and promotes EMT in cervical cancer. LRRC75A-AS1 knockdown caused remarkable impairment of EMT and tumor growth by inhibition of NLRP3/IL-1β/SMAD2/3 signaling in mice inoculated with LRRC75A-AS1-silenced cervical cancer cells [17].

Overall, these findings have urged researchers to conceptually develop a better understanding of the oncogenic role of TGF/SMAD signaling in the progression and metastatic spread of cervical cancer cells. It has become evident that within cervical cancer cells, SMAD complexes transcriptionally stimulated the expression of metastasis-associated genes for the invasion and development of secondary tumors.

Tumor suppressive role: The tumor suppressive role of SMAD signaling is highly context dependent. ESRRB (Estrogen-related receptor β) promotes cervical cancer progression by stimulating the transcription of SMAD7. SMAD7 binds to the cytoplasmic domains of activated type I receptors and causes blockade of receptor-mediated phosphorylation of SMAD2/3. SMAD7-mediated inactivation of SMAD2/3 induced upregulation of Myc. Consequently, Myc transcriptionally upregulated ESRRB and later ESRRB induced the expression of SMAD7 in cervical cancer cells (Fig. 1b). There was a considerable shrinkage of the tumors in mice xenografted with ESRRB-knockout-HeLa cells [18].

SPTBN1 (Spectrin, beta, Nonerythrocytic-1) promotes TGF/SMAD signaling. BAP31 (B-cell receptor-associated protein-31) interacts with SPTBN1 and interferes with SMAD2/3-driven signaling in cervical cancer cells. BAP31 is negatively regulated by tumor suppressor miR-362 (Fig. 1b). Overexpression of miR-362 not only reduced the levels of BAP31 but also enhanced SPTBN1-mediated activation of SMAD2/3 signaling. miR-362 mimics significantly reduced the tumor formation by cervical cancer cells in experimental mice [19]. Another interesting finding indicated that BAP31 knockdown reduced metastatic dissemination and pulmonary metastasis in mice [20].

Ubiquitination

Remarkable advancements have been made in mechanistic and biological understanding of ubiquitination [21–26]. Over the past decade, ubiquitination research has evolved into a bona fide research field shedding light on the functional role of the pathway in progression of cervical cancer [27–29].

METTL14 is a methyltransferase of m6A. IGF2BP1 acts as a reader for m6A moieties of mRNA to enhance their stability. METTL14 induced an increase in m6A moieties of TRIM11 mRNA and IGF2BP1 stabilized it. Progression of cervical cancer depends centrally on hyperactive survival signaling. AKT-driven downstream signaling axis has a critical role in the uncontrolled growth of cervical cancer. PHLPP1 (PH Domain and Leucine-rich repeat protein phosphatase-1) negatively regulates AKT signaling and inhibits cervical cancer. PHLPP1-silencing led to an increase in the growth of the tumor size in rodent models. TRIM11 induced ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation of PHLPP1 in cervical cancer cells [30]. Overall, these findings clearly suggested that tumor suppressive effects of PHLPP1 can be overridden by activation of AKT signaling. METTL14/ IGF2BP1/TRIM11 axis has a regulatory role in the uncontrolled growth of cervical cancer.

SMARCD3 (SWI/SNF-related matrix-associated actin-dependent regulator of chromatin subfamily-D member-3) suppresses cervical cancer. UCA1 (urothelial carcinoma associated 1), an oncogenic long noncoding RNA induced ubiquitination of SMARCD3 in cervical cancer. UCA1-silenced ME180 cells failed to induce tumor growth in mice [31].

OTUB2 is a deubiquitinase and promotes cervical cancer. OTUB2 deubiquitinates FOXM1 and significantly potentiates the tumor development. OTUB2 silencing drastically impaired tumor forming abilities of HeLa cells [32].

APC/CCdh1 (Anaphase promoting complex/cyclosome-Cdh1) is an E3 ubiquitin ligase. SLC35F2 (Solute carrier family 35 F2) is ubiquitinated by APC/CCdh1. APC/CCdh1 ubiquitinates and degrades SLC35F2 in cervical cancer cells [33]. APC/CCdh1 inhibits SLC35F2-mediated tumorigenesis in cervical cancer cells.

TRAF6 induces the addition of K63-linked polyubiquitin moieties. Prominently, K63-linked polyubiquitylation of IKKγ did not induce proteasomal degradation but instead activated IKK complex and IKK-mediated phosphorylation of IκB. TRAF6 oligomerization triggers the activation of its ubiquitin ligase activities. DRAK1 (Death-associated protein (DAP) kinase-related apoptosis-inducing protein kinase-1) severely impairs the homo-oligomerization-induced stability of TRAF6 by binding to its TRAF domains. DRAK1 augments autophagy-mediated degradation of TRAF6 in cervical cancer cells. Cell-penetrating DRAK1 peptides have been found to be markedly efficient against cervical cancer. Importantly, considerable reduction in the metastatic pulmonary nodules along with significant downregulation of TRAF6 expression was noticed in mice injected with DRAK1 overexpressing-SiHa cells [34].

However, contemporary evidence also suggests CUL3/SPOP-mediated K48-linked poly-ubiquitination-and proteasomal degradation of DRAK1. Loss of DRAK1 considerably augmented resistance against paclitaxel in cervical cancer cells [35].

c-Jun directly binds to the AP-1-like sequences and transcriptionally stimulates the expression of USP13. USP13 promotes the stability of MCL-1 by protecting it from proteasomal degradation. USP13 interacted with and deubiquitinated MCL-1 in cervical cancer cells. Pharmacological inhibition of USP13 sensitized cervical cancer cells to ABT-263 (BH3 mimetic) [36].

Wnt/β-catenin pathway

Fundamentally, canonical pathway is initiated by binding of Wnt ligands to frizzled receptor (FZD) and LRP5/6 (low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein-5/6). This results in the inactivation of the multi-protein “destruction complex”, thus protecting the signaling mediator β-catenin from its proteasomal degradation [37, 38]. Wnt/β-catenin pathway is tightly controlled by several antagonists. These antagonists can either block or bind Wnt ligands, thus preventing their interaction with the receptors (Wnt inhibitory factor and secreted frizzled-related protein), or bind to LRP5/6 and induce receptor internalization or minimize their availability to Wnt ligands (Dickkopf) [39, 40]. New perspectives on the complexity of Wnt/β-catenin pathway have provided exciting insights related to multilayered regulatory circuitry in the progression of cervical cancer.

NPPA-AS1 is a tumor suppressor long non-coding RNA. NPPA-AS1 interferes with miR-302e-mediated targeting of Dickkopf-1 (Fig. 2). REST (RE-1 silencing transcriptional factor) has been shown to transcriptionally downregulate NPPA-AS1 and promote cervical cancer progression. NPPA-AS1 mediated increase in the levels of Dickkopf-1 caused inhibition of oncogenic Wnt/β-catenin pathway. There was a profound reduction in the size of the tumors derived from NPPA‐AS1-overexpressing SiHa cells [41].

Wnt/β-catenin signaling is controlled by different kinases. Phosphorylation of β-catenin earmarks it for proteasomal degradation. However, escape of β-catenin from degradation causes its nuclear accumulation and activation of target genes. Dickkopf is a negative regulator of Wnt signaling. However, Dickkopf is modulated by non-coding RNAs at transcriptional and post-transcriptional level. Different non-coding RNAs have been reported to epigenetically inactivate DKK1 and activate Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Oncogenic circular RNAs and LncRNAs work synchronously with EZH2 (Enhancer of zeste homolog-2) and transcriptionally inactivate DKK1 (Fig. 2) [42, 43]. Therefore, studies have shown that there are direct pieces of evidence which highlight the role of lncRNAs in the inhibition of oncogenic role of Wnt/β-catenin pathway. For example PDHB-AS competitively binds to miR-582-5p and enhances the expression of DKK1 (Fig. 2). Tumor growth was noted to be markedly reduced in mice inoculated with PDHB-AS-expressing HeLa cells [44]. Overall, these findings indicated that PDHB-AS inhibited Wnt/β-catenin pathway by enhancing the levels of DKK1 and suppressed aggressiveness of cervical cancer.

Different proteins have also been found to modulate Wnt/β-catenin pathway. PABPN1 (Poly(A)-binding protein nuclear 1) works synchronously with IGF2BP1(insulin-like growth factor 2 binding protein) and regulates Wnt/β-catenin pathway. PABPN1 promoted the interaction between IGF2BP1 and GSK3β mRNA and enhanced its stability (Fig. 2). GSK3β increased the phosphorylation and proteasomal degradation of β-catenin. However, this regulatory network is tightly controlled by microRNAs. miR-1323-loaded exosomes released by cancer-associated fibroblasts are taken up by cervical cancer cells. miR-1323 directly targeted PABPN1 and impaired the stability of GSK-3β. It was reported that PABPN1 overexpression led to suppression in the levels of β-catenin. Therefore, tumor growth was found to be considerably reduced in mice subcutaneously inoculated with PABPN1-overexpressing C33A cells [45]. Overall, these findings indicated that cancer associated fibroblasts induce radio-resistance in cervical cancer cells through exosomal transfer of miRNAs which target the negative regulators of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in cervical cancer cells.

SOX4 promotes invasive potential by activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling in cervical cancer cells. Oncogenic CircSAMD11 interfered with miR-503-mediated inhibition of SOX4. Importantly, levels of β-catenin and SOX4 were potently enhanced in CircSAMD11-expressing cervical cancer cells. There was a significant shrinkage of the tumors in mice xenografted with CircSAMD11-silenced SiHa cells [46].

BTG2 (B-cell translocation gene 2) inhibited Wnt/β-catenin pathway. CARMN (Cardiac mesoderm enhancer-associated noncoding RNA) stimulates the expression of BTG2 via inhibition of miR-92a-3p-mediated targeting of BTG2. There was a considerable reduction in Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CARMN-transfected cervical cancer cells [47].

USP30-AS1 interfered with miR-2467-3p and enhanced the expression of USP30 (ubiquitin-specific peptidase 30). USP30 recruited FUS (FUS RNA binding protein), stabilized β-catenin and activated Wnt/β-catenin pathway in cervical cancer cells. Therefore, targeting of USP30-AS1 is essential to reduce the migratory and invasive potential of cervical cancer cells [48].

SFRP1 and SFRP2 efficiently inactivate Wnt/β-catenin pathway in cervical cancer cells. β-catenin-mediated expression of SLUG, TWIST and SNAIL was impaired in SFRP1/SFRP2-overexprssing cancer cells (Fig. 3). Tumor growth was found to be profoundly reduced in mice inoculated with SFRP1-overexpressing CaSki and HeLa cells [49].

D, E β-catenin can enhance the expression of LGR6 and promote progression of cervical cancer. LGR6 plays active role in enhancing Wnt/β-catenin signaling

DYNLT3 (dynein light chain Tctex-type 3) negatively regulates Wnt/β-catenin pathway in cervical cancer cells. Lung was the major metastatic site invaded by DYNLT3-silenced-SiHa cells cancer cells. These findings indicated that inactivation of DYNLT3 led to a considerable increase in the metastatic dissemination of cervical cancer cells via suppression of E-cadherin and upregulation of N-cadherin [50].

FAM83F promotes progression of cervical cancer by activation of Wnt/β-catenin pathway. c-Myc transcriptionally stimulates FAM83F and fuels the tumor forming abilities of cervical cancer cells (Fig. 3) [51].

LGR6 (leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein-coupled receptor-6) also activates Wnt/β-catenin pathway. TCF4 and β-catenin work synchronously and trigger the upregulation of LGR6 (Fig. 3). Tumors formed by LGR6 (high) HeLa cells were heavier, larger and developed at a faster rate as compared to the tumors formed by LGR6 (low) HeLa cells [52].

mTOR pathway

Multifaceted roles of mTOR (mechanistic Target of Rapamycin) signaling pathway has been a stirring and rapidly evolving story of bench-to-bedside translational research that has spawned pharmacologically valuable targets and various phases of clinical trials. mTORC1-driven phosphorylation of 4E-BP causes disassembly of eIF4E and 4E-BP. Interaction of eIF4G with eIF4E is necessary for cap-dependent translation. Thus, eIF4E freely binds to eIF4G and initiates the process of translation. mTORC1 also phosphorylates p70S6-kinases (S6K1 and S6K2), leading to their activation and phosphorylation of substrates that promote translational initiation. AKT/PKB exerts inhibitory effects on TSC1-TSC2 complexes [53, 54]. These complexes are GTPase activating proteins for RHEB. TSC2 has the ability to convert GTP to GDP, thus causing the inactivation of RHEB. GTP-bound RHEB directly binds and activates mTORC1 [55].

Artemin (ARTN), a neurotrophic factor has been shown to trigger the activation of AKT/mTORC1 pathway in cervical cancer cells. ARTN increased the levels of p-AKT (Ser473), p-mTOR (Ser2448) and vimentin but simultaneously suppressed the levels of E-cadherin. There was remarkable increase in pulmonary metastatic nodules in mice injected with ARTN-overexpressing-SiHa cells [56].

CDK6 promotes uncontrolled growth of cervical cancer cells in part through activation of mTORC1. Levels of mTOR and phosphorylated S6K(Thr389) were strikingly reduced in CDK6-knockout cells. Tumors derived from CDK6-knockout HeLa were smaller in size [57].

LSD1 (Lysine-specific demethylase1) is involved in cancer progression. LSD1 induces the removal of methyl groups from mono- and dimethylated H3K4. DDIT4 (DNA-damage-inducible transcript 4) has been shown to inhibit mTORC1-mediated downstream signaling. However, LSD1 binds to the promoter region of DDIT4 and transcriptionally represses it. ZY0511 (LSD1 inhibitor) triggered an increase in the levels of DDIT4 and simultaneously reduced the levels of p-mTOR and p-p70S6K in cervical cancer cells. Intraperitoneally injected ZY0511 markedly impaired tumor growth in mice inoculated with HeLa cells [58].

Cervical cancer cells can be targeted by disruption of mitochondrial signaling. These stresses lead to low ATP production. During low ATP levels, AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase) phosphorylates RAPTOR (regulatory-associated protein of mTOR) thus causing the inhibition of mTORC1 activity. ARCSP (Autophagy-related cancer-suppressing peptide) is a potent antitumor peptide having considerable effects against cervical cancer cells. It induced apoptotic death in cervical cancer cells. ARCSP damages mitochondria and causes sharp decline in the production of ATP in cervical cancer cells. ARCSP induced activation of AMPK and caused reduction in the levels of p-mTOR and p-p70S6K. Intraperitoneal injections of ARCSP strictly limited the tumor forming properties of HeLa cells [59].

Notoginsenoside R7 isolated from Panax notoginseng has also been found to be effective against cervical cancer cells. R7 induced notable reduction in the levels of p-AKTSer473 and p-AKTThr308. However, PTEN was found to be activated by R7. Furthermore, R7 also suppressed the levels of p-mTORSer2448 and RAPTOR in cervical cancer cells. Levels of p-AKTThr308 and Bcl-2 were found to be reduced by R7 in BALB/c mice inoculated with HeLa cells [60]. Importantly, different bacterial cyclodipeptides have also been reported to demonstrate promising anticancer activity. Bacterial cyclodipeptides inhibited p-mTOR, RAPTOR, RICTOR, TSC1, p-TSC2 and p-p70S6K in cervical cancer cells [61].

Circular RNAs have been shown to potentiate the levels of RHEB in cervical cancer cells. circMYLK interferes with miR-1301-3p-mediated targeting of RHEB and induces activation of mTOR pathway. CircMYLK activated RHEB/mTOR signaling and fueled malignant behavior of cervical cancer cells [62]. MIR155 directly targeted RHEB, RICTOR, and RPS6KB2 and induced cell cycle arrest of cervical cancer cells [63]. These exciting pieces of evidence highlight oncogenic role of mTOR pathway in the progression of cervical cancer. mTOR pathway is an intricate network regulated by non-coding RNAs and plays integral role in cancer progression. Therefore, better understanding of the complicated interplay between mTOR pathway and non-coding RNAs will be valuable for clinically effective therapeutics.

JAK/STAT pathway

Janus kinase (JAK) mediated phosphorylation of STAT proteins for the activation of intracellular signaling has attracted widespread attention. Consequently, phosphorylated STAT move into the nucleus and transcriptionally regulate the transcription of target gene networks [64–67].

Protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) is involved in the transcriptional upregulation of STAT1 by binding to the promoter region and inducing symmetric dimethylation of histone H3R2. Resultantly, STAT1 binds to the promoter region of PD-L1 and stimulates its transcription (Fig. 4). PRMT5-expressing cervical cancer cells interact with PD-1 expressing T cells and inactivate them. Therefore, knockdown of PRMT5 increases the secretion of IFNγ and TNFα by CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Moreover, expression of the receptor for PD-L1 was also found to be reduced on the surface of T cells after the knockdown of PRMT5 [68]. These findings suggested that PRMT5 mediated the activation of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling leading to the inactivation of T cells thus promoting an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

B STAT1 triggers the expression of oncogenic lncRNA (MEOX2-AS1). C PD-L1/PD-1 signaling protects cervical cancer cells from targeting by T cells and natural killer cells. However, PD-L1 can be degraded by lysosomes to amplify the activity of T cells and natural killer cells

STAT1 has also been shown to transcriptionally upregulate the expression of oncogenic lncRNAs. STAT1-induced lncRNA MEOX2-AS1 promotes the expression of VDAC1 (Voltage-dependent anion channel 1) in cervical cancer cells. Tumor growth was found to be substantially impaired in mice inoculated with MEOX2-AS1-silenced-HeLa cells [69].

Formononetin transcriptionally represses PD-L1 by abrogation of the interaction between MYC and p-STAT3. Formononetin also promoted the functional biogenesis of lysosomes and facilitated TFEB- and TFE3-dependent degradation of PD-L1. Formononetin enhances cytotoxicity effects of T cells and apoptotic death of HeLa cells by increasing lysosomal degradation of PD-L1 (Fig. 4). Orally administered formononetin considerably limited tumor growth in mice inoculated with HeLa cells. Formononetin reduced the levels of PD-L1, p-STAT3 and MYC within tumor tissues [70]. It seems intriguing to note that cervical cancer cells have the ability to circumvent T cell-mediated killing by activation of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling. Therefore, inactivation of PD-L1/PD-1 signaling is essential to amplify the response of T cells and natural killer cells against cervical cancer cells.

Induction of phagocytosis can be inhibited by “do not eat me” signaling (CD47/SIRPα). Phagocytes, such as granulocytes and macrophages express inhibitory immunoreceptor SIRPα that interacts with CD47 on cancer cells. IFNγ and TNFα trigger the activation of M1-macrophages (CD68/CD80/CD86). M2-macrophages (CD204/CD206/CD163) are involved in immunosuppressive microenvironment. Levels of ZEB1 are high in hypoxic exosomes derived from cervical cancer cells. ZEB1 and STAT3 have been shown to trigger the upregulation of CD47 in cervical cancer cells (Fig. 5). Additionally, ZEB1-enriched exosomes are taken up by macrophages. Increase in the phosphorylation of STAT3 was noted in THP-1 macrophages after exposure to ZEB1-enriched-exosomes (Fig. 5). Significantly higher levels of p-STAT3, CD206 and SIRPα were present in the tumors treated with ZEB1-enriched-exosomes. Evident increase in the phagocytosis of cervical cancer cells by THP-1 macrophages was noted in the presence of anti-CD47 blocking antibodies. Collectively, these exciting findings provide clues about central role of hypoxic cervical cancer cell-derived exosomal ZEB1 in the transformation of macrophages to M2 phenotype. ZEB1-enriched-exosomes promote SIRPα + TAM polarization through STAT3 signaling in tumor-bearing mice [71]. ZEB1 overexpression plays critical role in the activation of STAT3 signaling in cervical cancer cells as well as tumor-associated macrophages. Consequently, increase in the cell surface expression of CD47 on cancer cells and SIRPα on the macrophages protect cervical cancer cells from phagocytosis.

SIX1 are involved in the activation of STAT3. STAT3 triggers the expression of genes associated with epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (MMP9, N-cadherin). B Cervical cancer cells are rich in ZEB1. ITIM-recruited phosphatases (SHP1/SHP2) induced dephosphorylation of myosin IIA and inhibited rearrangements of cytoskeleton, necessary for macrophage-mediated phagocytosis

LRRC75A-AS1-loaded exosomes derived from M2 macrophages potentiate proliferation and metastasizing abilities of Hela cells (Fig. 5). SIX1 is involved in the activation of STAT3 signaling and subsequent upregulation of MMP9 and N-cadherin. LRRC75A-AS1 interferes with miR-429-mediated targeting of SIX1. Metastasizing ability of Hela cells was found to be drastically enhanced after treatment with LRRC75A‐AS1-loaded exosomes purified from M2 macrophages. There was a significant increase in the pulmonary metastatic nodules formed by Hela cells treated with LRRC75A‐AS1-loaded exosomes [72].

5-aminolevulinic acid photodynamic therapy has been shown to inhibit cervical cancer primarily through activation of JAK/STAT signaling. miR-152-3p directly inhibits the expression of JAK1 but photodynamic therapy exerts repressive effects on the expression of miR-152-3p. Photodynamic therapy efficiently induced regression of the tumors formed by SiHa cells in xenografted mice but oppositely, miR-152-3p overexpression and Filgotinib (JAK1 inhibitor) reversed tumor inhibitory effects of photodynamic therapy in tumor-bearing mice [73].

STAT2 transcriptionally upregulates circCLIP2 in cervical cancer cells and inhibit progression of cervical cancer. circCLIP2 acts as a sponge for miR-361-3p and stimulates the expression of STAT2. Overall, STAT2 transcriptionally enhanced circCLIP2 and consequently circCLIP2 interfered with miR-361-3p-mediated targeting of STAT2 [74].

STAT3 stimulates the expression of an oncogenic lncRNA (MALAT1) and promotes cervical cancer. Resultantly, MALAT1 interferes with miR-370-3p-mediated targeting of STAT3 and activates JAK/STAT and PI3K/AKT signaling in cervical cancer cells [75].

Circ-E2F3, a circular RNA has been shown to activate STAT3-mediated signaling by inhibition of miR-296-5p-mediated targeting of STAT3. Importantly, there was an evident shrinkage of the tumor size in mice inoculated with Circ-E2F3-silenced Caski cells [76]. Overall, Circ-E2F3 promoted cervical cancer progression by activation of JAK/STAT signaling.

PLOD3 (Procollagen-lysine, 2-oxoglutarate 5-dioxygenase) fuels progression of cervical cancer mainly through activation of JAK/STAT3 signaling. SOX9 transcriptionally upregulates PLOD3. Tumor growth was found to be inhibited in mice inoculated with SOX9-silenced-HeLa cells [77].

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) frequently overexpress BAFF (B-cell activating factor) on their surface. This BAFF binds to the BAFF receptor (BAFF-R) on B-cells and transduces the signals for differentiation into B10 cells. Experimental models have shown that the use of BAFF-R neutralizing antibodies led to an immunostimulatory environment. MDSCs induce differentiation of B cells into B10 cells and promote B10 cells-mediated secretion of IL-10 mainly through BAFF/BAFF-R signaling. IL-10 secreted by B10 switches on STAT3 signaling in MDSCs. Consequently, functionally active MDSCs secrete immunosuppressive factors, such as iNOS (inducible nitric oxide synthase) for the inhibition of CD8 + T cells in the tumor microenvironment. Importantly, functionally active BAFF is indispensable for activation of MDSC and secretion of iNOS. There was a severe decline in the production of iNOS by PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs in BAFF knockout tumor bearing mice [78].

Binding of NKG2D to its ligands triggers the activation of NK and T cells and initiate the eradication of the cells expressing these molecules. MICA is a ligand expressed by cervical cancer cells. However, STAT3 has been shown to transcriptionally repress the expression of MICA. LINC00240, an oncogenic lncRNA blocks miRNA-124-3p-mediated targeting of STAT3. Tumors derived from LINC00240-overexpressing-HeLa cells were bigger in size and volume [79].

Hippo signaling

LATS1/LATS2 (Large Tumor Suppressor Kinase-1) phosphorylate transcriptional co-activators YAP and TAZ. Consequently, LATS1/LATS2-mediated phosphorylation of YAP/TAZ causes their cytoplasmic retention and proteasomal degradation. However, activation of YAP/TAZ leads to stimulation of wide variety of genes [80–82]. In this section we have provided a summary of positive and negative regulators of YAP. Different proteins have been shown to potentiate nuclear accumulation of YAP to promote cervical cancer.

CHRAC1 (Chromatin remodeling complex, chromatin accessibility complex subunit-1) interacts with YAP and facilitates transcriptional upregulation of YAP target genes (Fig. 6). There is a remarkable regression in the tumor size and tumor weights in mice inoculated with CHRAC1-silenced-Hela cells [83].

FTO (Fat mass and obesity-associated) directly interacts with BMP4 (Bone Morphogenetic Protein 4) and promotes progression of cervical cancer. FTO-wild-type induces an increase in the levels of BMP4, YAP1 and TAZ in cervical cancer cells. Expectedly, tumor mass and weight were found to be reduced drastically in mice inoculated with FTO-knockdown HeLa cells [84].

Exosomes derived from cervical cancer cells are rich in miR-146a-5p. Importantly, miR-146a-5p directly targets WWC2 and activates YAP-mediated transcriptional activities. Levels of LATS1 and p-YAP were found to be enhanced in WWC2-overexpressing cancer cells. miR-146a-5p activates Hippo-YAP pathway through targeting of WWC2 in cervical cancer cells. miR-146a-5p effectively promotes metastasizing properties of cervical cancer cells by downregulation of WWC2 and activation of YAP-mediated activation of target genes (Fig. 6) [85].

NEK2 promotes cervical cancer by de-phosphorylation of YAP and nuclear accumulation of YAP. There is a considerable decline in the levels of p-MST1/MST2 and p-LATS1 in NEK2-overexpressing-cervical cancer cells. NEK2-overexpressing cervical cancer cells formed tumors with larger weights and volumes [86].

ACTL6A (Actin-like 6 A) also reduces p-YAP and enhanced YAP/TAZ-mediated transcriptional activity. miR-216a-3p directly targets ACTL6A and inhibits progression of cervical cancer (Fig. 6). ACTL6A inhibition or overexpression of miR‐216a‐3p blocked YAP-mediated transcriptional activation of oncogenes [87].

However, different proteins have the ability to inhibit the nuclear accumulation of YAP. FRMD6 induces an increase in p-MST1/MST2 and p-LATS1 and p-YAP. Linc00887, an oncogenic lncRNAs interferes with miR-454-3p-mediated targeting of FRMD6. Linc00887-mediated increase in the levels of FRMD6 efficiently inhibits nuclear accumulation of YAP and suppresses cervical cancer [88].

NOTCH signaling

Delta-like ligand (DLL1, DLL3, and DLL4) and Jagged (JAG1 and JAG2) are NOTCH ligands [89, 90]. These ligands interact with NOTCH receptors and activate intracellular signaling. NOTCH signaling triggers a series of proteolytic cleavage of the transmembrane subunit to release NICD (Notch intracellular domain). Following the cleavages, NICD gets released from the cell membrane and accumulates in the nucleus. NICD binds to RBP-Jĸ (Recombination Signal-Binding protein-1 for J-KAPPA) and co-activators for transcriptional stimulation of target gene networks.

HOXA-AS2 promotes NOTCH pathway by facilitating the association between NICD and RBP-Jĸ in cervical cancer cells. Overall, HOXA-AS2 promotes migratory potential of cervical cancer cells [91].

DARS-AS1 acts as a sponge and interferes with miR-628-5p-mediated targeting of JAG1. Consequently, JAG1 activates NOTCH signaling and promotes cancer progression [92].

Levels of MMP2 and MMP9 were found to be reduced in DLL4 knockdown-cervical cancer cells. Knockdown of DLL4 abrogates the repair of DNA damage in the irradiated cervical cancer cells [93].

miR424-5p directly targets KDM5B and inhibits progression of cervical cancer. Moreover, miR424-5p overexpression caused reduction in the levels of NOTCH1 and NOTCH2. Importantly, inhibition of KDM5B significantly impaired the activation of NOTCH pathway [94].

Concluding remarks

Proof-of-concept studies over decades have enabled basic and clinical scientists to develop greater-than-ever mechanistic understanding about multifaceted and heterogeneous nature of cervical cancer. Previous studies have comprehensively analyzed the molecular subtypes of cervical cancers. These functionally unique gene signatures provide basis for rationale-based design of the clinical trials for cervical cancer patients [95].

In this mini-review, we have attempted to summarize cutting-edge research works which answered the fundamental questions related to the role of deregulated oncogenic pathways in fueling the progression and aggressiveness of cervical cancer. TGFβ signaling inhibition has been tested in different phases of clinical trials. Bintrafusp alfa (M7824) is a bifunctional TGFβ “trap”/anti-PD-L1 fusion protein composed of an extracellular domain of TGFβRII fused to IgG1 antibody blocking PD-L1. The results provided important findings about the efficacy of Bintrafusp alfa in advanced/metastatic cervical cancer (96–98]. However, Bintrafusp alfa was not tested in phase 3 of the clinical trials.

Similarly, Porcupine is a membrane-bound O-acyltransferase enzyme required for the secretion of Wnt. WNT974 is an oral agent that selectively and potently inhibits Porcupine. WNT974 is currently being tested for efficacy in a clinical trial [99]. The limited anti-tumor activity observed with WNT974 might be attributable to oncogenic co-mutations that trigger compensatory pathways thus bypassing sole dependence of tumors on Wnt signaling.

Substantial fraction of mechanistic knowledge has added another layer of intricacy to the already complicated intertwined network of signaling cascades in cervical cancer. Sometimes exciting and encouraging results obtained from preclinical studies lose their translatability in early or advanced phases of clinical trials. Essentially, the co-occurrence of these signaling hubs in therapeutic resistance and metastatic progression marks them as prime candidates for valuable pharmacological targets. Multi-targeted and multi-pronged therapeutic strategies are necessary to achieve a durable clinical outcome.
