FOLFOX-HAIC Plus Lenvatinib and Tislelizumab in Advanced Hepatocellular Carcinoma with Arteriovenous Fistulae: A Real-World Study
Authors: Pengen Li, Shumin Fu, Xiao Wang, Yi Chen, Jianbing Wu, Weimin Jiang, Ye Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsPatients with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) complicated by arteriovenous fistula (AVF) often fail to benefit from conventional transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) because of the associated hemodynamic abnormalities, resulting in an extremely poor prognosis. This retrospective, single-center, single-arm study was conducted to preliminarily evaluate the efficacy and safety of hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) using the FOLFOX regimen in combination with lenvatinib and tislelizumab in this population of patients with unresectable/advanced HCC.MethodsA total of 51 patients with advanced HCC complicated by angiographically confirmed AVF, who were treated at our institution between December 2020 and May 2025, were retrospectively enrolled. All patients received the aforementioned triple-therapy regimen as first-line treatment. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), while secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), objective response rate (ORR), fistula closure rate, and safety.ResultsThe median PFS and OS were 9.67 months and 14.63 months, respectively. According to the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST), the ORR and disease control rate (DCR) were 72.55% and 92.16%, respectively. The overall AVF closure rate was 87.2%, and time-dependent covariate Cox regression analysis identified fistula closure as an independent protective factor for OS. Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were manageable, and no treatment-related deaths occurred.ConclusionThis real-world study demonstrates that FOLFOX-based HAIC combined with lenvatinib and tislelizumab is associated with a high AVF closure rate, favorable survival outcomes, and a manageable safety profile in patients with unresectable/advanced HCC complicated by AVF. This combination regimen may offer a feasible therapeutic strategy for this refractory patient population; Meanwhile, we anticipate that prospective controlled studies will further consolidate and extend the current findings, so as to establish their clinical value in a more comprehensive manner.Keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, arteriovenous fistula, hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy, lenvatinib, tislelizumab

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is frequently complicated by arteriovenous fistula (AVF), defined as an abnormal communication between the hepatic artery and the portal vein or hepatic vein. Based on the type of shunt, AVF can be classified into hepatic artery-portal vein fistula (HAPVF), hepatic artery-hepatic vein fistula (HAHVF), and mixed type.1,2 Among these, HAPVF is the most common3 and can be further subdivided, according to the location of the fistula within the portal venous system, into central type (fistula located in the main portal trunk and its first-order branches) and peripheral type (fistula located in second-order or higher-order portal branches).4 The reported prevalence of AVF in patients with HCC varies considerably across the literature (approximately 28.8–63.2%),5 with a detection rate of approximately 31% by digital subtraction angiography (DSA);6 the prevalence is notably higher among patients with massive or multinodular tumors and those complicated by portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT).7 AVF is not merely a radiological finding but also an important indicator of poor prognosis, and its formation is associated with direct tumor invasion, hemodynamic alterations, and the opening of pre-existing vascular collaterals.8–10 High-flow AVF can induce or exacerbate portal hypertension, leading to refractory ascites and esophagogastric variceal bleeding,1 and may also promote tumor dissemination, thereby directly worsening prognosis11 and shortening patient survival.

Currently, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) poses substantial challenges in patients complicated by AVF, as high-flow shunting hampers effective embolic deposition at the tumor site, resulting in low local drug concentrations and an increased risk of ectopic embolization (eg, pulmonary artery embolism). Studies have demonstrated that patients with central-type HAPVF who undergo conventional TACE have a significantly shorter median survival than those without AVF, along with a higher incidence of complications.12 Although some studies have attempted to pre-occlude the fistula using materials such as coils, these approaches are technically demanding, costly, and may compromise subsequent hepatic arterial access.13–15 Consequently, a unified and effective standard treatment protocol remains lacking in clinical practice.

In recent years, hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) combined with systemic therapy has provided a novel approach to addressing this clinical challenge, particularly for patients with a high tumor burden or vascular invasion.16 The FOLFOX-HAIC regimen enables the delivery of high local concentrations of chemotherapeutic agents that act directly on the tumor;17 lenvatinib induces vascular normalization and improves the tumor microenvironment through inhibition of targets such as vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR);18 and tislelizumab relieves immunosuppression and activates T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity.19 As a novel anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibody, tislelizumab has demonstrated favorable activity and safety in HCC.20 Basic research has shown a synergistic effect between chemotherapy-induced immunogenic cell death and immune checkpoint inhibitors,21–23 providing a theoretical basis for the synergistic antitumor activity of this triple-therapy regimen. Notably, AVF complicating HCC predominantly represents a “tumor-dependent shunting pathway” formed by direct vascular invasion of the tumor,24 rather than an independent vascular malformation. As the triple-therapy regimen exerts its antitumor effect and the tumor parenchyma regresses, the tumor-feeding vessels within the fistula region diminish accordingly, allowing the shunting blood flow to gradually attenuate or even disappear, which is ultimately manifested radiologically as fistula closure. Unlike TACE, which relies on local retention of embolic agents, HAIC—owing to its continuous arterial infusion characteristics—can maintain local tumor drug exposure even under conditions of high-flow AVF, and is therefore less affected by shunting than TACE;25 however, its specific pharmacokinetic properties warrant further investigation.

Although several real-world studies and Phase II trials have confirmed that HAIC combined with lenvatinib and PD-1 inhibitors achieves a high objective response rate (ORR >50%) and acceptable safety in unresectable HCC—including patients with high tumor burden and PVTT26,27 —these studies are subject to notable limitations in study population, and the existing efficacy data cannot be directly generalized to patients complicated by AVF. First, most prospective studies have explicitly excluded patients with moderate-to-high-flow AVF, enrolling only standard populations without vascular shunting, thereby leaving a lack of efficacy data specific to the AVF subgroup. Second, the few available real-world studies have not performed stratified analyses based on AVF status among enrolled patients, making it impossible to distinguish the confounding effect of shunting lesions on tumor response and treatment safety. Third, conventional vascular occlusion strategies are not applicable to the continuous infusion mode of HAIC, and prior studies have not investigated the effect of the triple-therapy regimen on the AVF itself, leaving unanswered the question of whether the shunting pathway can be closed through treatment. These deficiencies in study design have resulted in a current lack of evidence-based data specifically addressing unresectable/advanced HCC complicated by AVF, and the optimal treatment strategy for this refractory patient population remains to be explored.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate, through a retrospective analysis, the efficacy and safety of FOLFOX-HAIC combined with lenvatinib and tislelizumab in patients with unresectable/advanced HCC complicated by AVF, with particular focus on the regimen’s ability to promote fistula closure and its ultimate impact on patient survival, in order to provide novel therapeutic insights into this clinical challenge. Preliminary results of this study have previously been presented in the form of a conference abstract.28

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

This study retrospectively enrolled patients with unresectable/advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) who were treated at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between December 2020 and December 2024. As shown in Figure 1, a total of 291 potentially eligible patients were screened during the study period, of whom 51 ultimately met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis. All patients had AVF confirmed by digital subtraction angiography (DSA) and received first-line treatment with the triple-therapy regimen of HAIC using the FOLFOX protocol combined with lenvatinib and tislelizumab.

The specific inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) advanced HCC confirmed either pathologically or according to the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines;29 (2) presence of AVF confirmed by imaging, with tumor burden meeting either of the following conditions: (a) tumor burden ≥50% of liver volume, or (b) portal vein tumor thrombus classified as Vp3 or Vp4 according to the Japan Society of Hepatology (JSH) criteria;30 (3) no prior receipt of any local or systemic antitumor therapy for HCC; (4) presence of at least one measurable target lesion evaluable by both RECIST 1.1 and mRECIST criteria; (5) Child-Pugh class A or B (≤7 points); and (6) age ≥18 years, with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score of 0–1.

Given that the study population had concomitant arteriovenous fistula and a high proportion of portal vein tumor thrombus, portal hypertension and its related complications were important safety considerations. All patients underwent baseline endoscopy or contrast-enhanced abdominal CT/MRI prior to enrollment to screen for the presence and severity of gastroesophageal varices (GEV). For patients found to have moderate-to-severe varices (Grade G2/G3 or with red color signs) on screening, prophylactic endoscopic variceal ligation was routinely recommended and performed prior to initiating the systemic combination therapy. In addition, all patients with cirrhosis or significant portal hypertension received non-selective beta-blockers as background therapy.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathological type other than hepatocellular carcinoma; (2) presence of other active malignancies (excluding cured basal cell carcinoma of the skin, etc); (3) uncontrolled severe cardiac, pulmonary, or renal insufficiency, or active infection; (4) uncontrolled high-risk bleeding or active autoimmune disease; and (5) incomplete clinical data or loss to follow-up, precluding efficacy evaluation.

Treatment Regimen and Exposure

All patients received the combination regimen of FOLFOX-HAIC, lenvatinib, and tislelizumab as first-line treatment. Using a modified Seldinger technique via femoral artery puncture, a microcatheter was super-selectively advanced under DSA guidance to the main hepatic arterial branch supplying the tumor. In cases of multi-arterial tumor supply or extensive arteriovenous fistula, the catheter was positioned at the level of the proper hepatic artery or common hepatic artery to ensure regional perfusion of the chemotherapeutic agents. Each treatment cycle lasted 3 weeks, and the complete FOLFOX regimen consisted of: oxaliplatin (85 mg/m2) dissolved in 250 mL of 5% glucose solution, administered via continuous hepatic arterial infusion over 3 hours (Day 1); calcium folinate (leucovorin, 400 mg/m2) dissolved in 250 mL of normal saline, administered via continuous hepatic arterial infusion over 1 hour (Day 1, sequentially following oxaliplatin); and 5-fluorouracil 400 mg/m2 as a bolus injection (Day 1), followed by 2400 mg/m2 dissolved in 46 mL of normal saline, administered via continuous infusion using a portable elastomeric pump over 46 hours (through Day 3). Treatment was planned for 4 to 6 cycles, or until maximal clinical benefit was achieved, disease progression occurred, unacceptable toxicity developed, or the patient withdrew informed consent. Treatment exposure was documented as the actual number of completed HAIC cycles or the median duration of treatment. Lenvatinib was administered orally, initiated on the same day as the first HAIC session. The starting dose was determined by body weight: 12 mg/day (≥60 kg) or 8 mg/day (<60 kg). Tislelizumab was given at a fixed dose of 200 mg dissolved in 100 mL of normal saline, administered via intravenous infusion over 30 minutes on Day 1 of each cycle, once every 3 weeks. Treatment continued until disease progression, occurrence of unacceptable toxicity, patient refusal, or achievement of criteria for conversion surgery.

In actual clinical practice, the treatment regimen was individually adjusted based on patient tolerability and adverse events. For the entire cohort, the median number of HAIC treatment cycles was 3, with a median treatment duration of 6.5 months (95% CI: 4.8–8.2 months). Treatment duration was defined as the time from the first FOLFOX-HAIC session to the last administration of any antitumor therapy (including HAIC, surgery, lenvatinib, or tislelizumab). Safety-driven dose adjustments were as follows: dose reduction or discontinuation of lenvatinib due to adverse events occurred in 3 patients (5.9%); delay or discontinuation of tislelizumab occurred in 7 patients (13.7%); and suspension or delay (>1 week) of HAIC occurred in 9 patients (17.6%). The most common reasons for treatment discontinuation included disease progression and intolerable toxicity (1 case each), while an additional patient was lost to follow-up. All treatment-related suspensions, dose reductions, and resumptions were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and the respective drug package inserts.

Classification and Evaluation of Arteriovenous Fistulae

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) is the gold standard for diagnosing and classifying arteriovenous fistulas, as it can directly visualize the location of the fistula and the extent of the lesion.31 In this study, all 51 cases were hepatic artery-portal vein fistulas, which were classified into central and peripheral types based on the location of the vessels involved at the fistula site.32 The central type is located at the hepatic hilum, involving the proper hepatic artery or first-order branches such as the right and left portal vein trunks; angiographically, this presents as enhanced opacification of the main portal vein trunk and its major branches during the early arterial phase, with some patients exhibiting a temporal reversal phenomenon in which opacification of the portal venous system precedes that of the tumor parenchyma.1,33 In contrast, the peripheral type is located within the hepatic parenchyma, with the fistula opening into second-order or more distal branches of the portal vein; the shunting is focally distributed, characterized by premature opacification of the peripheral portal vein branches during the arterial phase.32

Closure of the HAVF was primarily assessed based on dynamic DSA imaging, defined as the absence of premature opacification of the main portal vein trunk or its branches during the arterial phase, along with complete disappearance of the abnormal blood flow signal at the original fistula site. Additionally, contrast-enhanced MRI or CT was performed every two treatment cycles to assist in evaluation, observing whether the sign of early portal vein opacification had resolved and assessing changes in portal hypertension-related manifestations (such as esophagogastric varices, splenomegaly, and ascites) as indirect evidence of closure.

Fistula closure status was assessed by DSA at each interventional procedure, using a blinded evaluation design: two interventional radiologists with more than 5 years of experience independently reviewed the DSA images, remaining unaware of the patients’ clinical outcomes, treatment regimens, or tumor response status. In cases of discordant judgments regarding closure status between the two reviewers, a third senior expert was invited to arbitrate and reach a consensus. Given that this study employed a single-center retrospective design, all imaging assessments of HAVF were uniformly re-reviewed by the core imaging team at our institution to ensure consistency in evaluation criteria.

Outcome Assessment

This study used oncological efficacy as the primary evaluation indicator. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS) assessed according to RECIST 1.1 criteria, while secondary endpoints included objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), overall survival (OS) assessed by RECIST 1.1 criteria, PFS and OS assessed by mRECIST criteria, and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). To evaluate the unique impact of this treatment regimen on tumor hemodynamics, arteriovenous fistula closure rate was specifically designated as a key exploratory endpoint. In this study, PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to the first documented disease progression; OS was defined as the duration from treatment initiation to death from any cause; and TRAEs were assessed according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0.

If imaging confirms complete closure of the HAVF and good tumor control, the original combination regimen is continued as maintenance therapy, and the case is submitted to the hospital’s Hepatobiliary Oncology Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) for comprehensive evaluation. If downstaging criteria are met, the feasibility of curative surgery or ablative therapy is explored. If disease progression occurs, the MDT discusses subsequent treatment options based on the pattern of progression (intrahepatic localized progression vs extrahepatic widespread progression) and the patient’s performance status, which may include second-line systemic therapy, local radiotherapy, or best supportive care. For patients deemed suitable for surgery after evaluation, the date of surgery is set as the observation endpoint, with postoperative decisions regarding adjuvant therapy based on pathological findings, and survival data continue to be followed up thereafter.

In this study, some patients underwent only a single DSA assessment, precluding evaluation of dynamic changes in fistula status. The main reasons for the inability to perform a second assessment included rapid disease progression, deterioration of clinical status, patient preference, or complications. For these patients, subsequent treatment was shifted primarily to systemic therapy-based supportive care. Although no further DSA was performed, tumor prognosis and survival outcomes continued to be monitored through clinical follow-up and imaging (CT/MRI). Therefore, in the analysis of arteriovenous fistula closure rate, these patients were excluded from the denominator due to the lack of follow-up DSA data; however, they were still included in the survival analysis under the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis.

Regarding the handling of missing data: apart from follow-up DSA data, the missing rate for the main baseline variables in this study (such as laboratory tests and demographic data) was below 5%. For the small number of missing values in baseline characteristics (such as bilirubin levels), multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was employed to minimize potential bias. Survival analysis, on the other hand, was conducted using complete case analysis (CCA).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were performed using R version 4.2.2 and SPSS statistical software version 28.0. Survival analysis employed the Kaplan-Meier method to plot progression-free survival and overall survival curves, with between-group comparisons conducted using the Log rank test. To identify independent prognostic factors, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was first performed. Given that “arteriovenous fistula closure” is an event occurring after treatment initiation, to avoid immortal time bias, it was incorporated into the model as a time-dependent covariate when analyzing its relationship with survival outcomes. The remaining baseline variables were analyzed as fixed covariates. For continuous variables such as PLR and NLR, their original data values (per 1-unit increase) were entered into the model. Before conducting multivariate analysis, we validated the proportional hazards assumption of the Cox model; the Schoenfeld residuals test showed that the global test P-values for all included variables were greater than 0.05, indicating that this assumption was satisfied. Finally, variables with P<0.05 in the univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate proportional hazards model, and stepwise regression was used for variable selection to identify independent prognostic factors. For all statistical analyses, a two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. For 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of proportional indicators, the Clopper-Pearson exact method (ie, a method based on the Beta distribution) was used for calculation.

Results

Baseline Patient Characteristics

By the end of follow-up on December 15, 2024, a total of 51 HCC patients met the inclusion criteria. Their baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1: the median age of patients was 56 years, and the population was predominantly advanced-stage (BCLC stage C, 86.3%), with portal vein tumor thrombus type Vp4 accounting for 60.78%. Forty-five patients (88.24%) had major vascular invasion, the mean diameter of intrahepatic target lesions was 12.97±3.97 cm, and 8 patients (15.69%) had distant metastasis. Regarding fistula type, 29 patients (56.86%) had the peripheral type and 22 (43.14%) had the central type, among whom 4 (7.84%) had concomitant caval fistula (1 central type, 3 peripheral type). Twelve patients (23.5%) had gastroesophageal varices. Notably, 4 patients could not be evaluated for dynamic changes in fistula status due to lack of follow-up imaging data (3 peripheral type, 1 central type); therefore, the efficacy analysis of AVF was based on the 47 evaluable patients, while the survival analysis included all 51 patients. Among the participants, hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection was the leading cause of HCC (68.63%), and all HBV-related HCC patients received antiviral therapy.

Treatment Response and Survival Outcomes

Following combination therapy, among the 47 evaluable patients, the overall AVF closure rate was 87.2% (41/47, 95% CI: 74.3–95.2%), with a median closure time of 42 days. When stratified by fistula location, the closure rate for peripheral AVF was 92.3% (24/26, 95% CI: 75.0–99.1%), numerically higher than that for central AVF at 81.0% (17/21, 95% CI: 58.1–94.6%), but the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.445, Fisher’s exact test). Additionally, 8 patients (15.7%) initially deemed unresectable were successfully converted to curative surgical resection following this treatment regimen, and no tumor recurrence was observed during the period up to the last follow-up.

Survival analysis revealed (Figure 2) that the median overall survival (OS) for the entire cohort was 14.63 months (95% CI: 11.63–30.27), and the median progression-free survival (PFS) was 9.67 months (95% CI: 6.53–16.33). The efficacy data showed high consistency between the ITT population and the evaluable population in this study (Table 2). In the ITT population, the objective response rate (ORR) assessed according to mRECIST criteria was 72.55%, with a disease control rate (DCR) of 92.16%; under RECIST 1.1 criteria, the ORR was 54.90% and the DCR was 94.10%. In the evaluable population, the ORR and DCR under mRECIST were 78.72% and 93.62%, respectively, while under RECIST 1.1 criteria, the ORR and DCR were 59.57% and 97.87%, respectively. The consistency between the two populations validates the robustness of the efficacy assessment. The McNemar test confirmed a statistically significant difference in ORR between the two evaluation criteria (p=0.002). The corresponding waterfall plots (Figure 3) showed that, compared with RECIST 1.1 criteria (Figure 3A), more patients achieved deeper tumor shrinkage under mRECIST criteria (Figure 3B).

Prognostic factor analysis results (Table 3) showed that, in univariate analysis, fistula closure (analyzed as a time-dependent covariate) was a significant protective factor for both overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) (PFS: HR=0.73, 95% CI: 0.60–0.89, p<0.001; OS: HR=0.73, 95% CI: 0.64–0.85, p<0.001). Albumin-Bilirubin (ALBI) grade 2 was associated with poorer OS (HR=2.17, 95% CI: 1.04–4.53, p=0.038), while a tumor diameter ≥10 cm was a risk factor for PFS (HR=2.78, 95% CI: 1.07–7.21, p=0.036). The platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (analyzed as a continuous variable) did not reach statistical significance in its association with PFS in univariate analysis (HR=1.00, 95% CI: 0.98–1.02, p=0.931).

Safety Analysis

The combination regimen demonstrated a manageable safety profile among the 51 patients in this study, with no treatment-related deaths occurring during the study period. The spectrum of adverse events was consistent with the known safety profiles of each individual agent, primarily comprising hematologic toxicity, liver function abnormalities, and systemic symptoms. The most common treatment-related adverse events (incidence ≥30%) included: hypertension (22/51, 43.1%), proteinuria (22/51, 43.1%), abdominal pain (19/51, 37.3%), elevated AST (19/51, 37.3%), decreased appetite (18/51, 35.3%), hypothyroidism (18/51, 35.3%), and thrombocytopenia (17/51, 33.3%), the vast majority of which were grade 1–2. The incidence of grade 3 adverse events was low, and no grade 4 adverse events were reported; the most common grade 3 AE was hypertension (3/51, 5.9%). More serious but less frequent grade 3 AEs included esophagogastric variceal hemorrhage (2/51, 3.9%), thrombocytopenia (2/51, 3.9%), rash (1/51, 2.0%), immune-related pneumonitis (1/51, 2.0%), and hand-foot skin reaction (1/51, 2.0%). All of the above events were managed in accordance with institutional treatment protocols through standard supportive care (including endoscopic esophageal variceal ligation, hepatoprotective and gastroprotective therapy, leukocyte- and platelet-elevating agents, anti-allergic treatment, etc), dose adjustment, or temporary drug discontinuation.

Notably, a total of 4 cases (7.8%) of treatment-related gastroesophageal variceal hemorrhage were observed in this study, of which 2 were grade 3. Consistent with observations from the SELECT trial,34 all 4 patients with hemorrhage in this study had baseline moderate-to-severe gastroesophageal varices (grade G2/G3), and the hemorrhagic events predominantly occurred within the first 2 treatment cycles. In contrast, no bleeding events occurred among patients without varices at baseline. Although limited sample size precluded multivariate regression analysis, this clinically relevant correlation suggests that for HCC patients with concomitant portal hypertension and varices, routine gastroscopic screening and proactive management of high-risk varices (such as prophylactic banding) prior to initiating HAIC combined with targeted-immunotherapy is critical for reducing the risk of fatal hemorrhage.

Discussion

This study evaluated the efficacy of FOLFOX-HAIC combined with lenvatinib and tislelizumab in patients with unresectable/advanced hepatocellular carcinoma complicated by arteriovenous fistula. The results showed that this regimen was associated with a high HAVF closure rate (87.2%) and favorable survival outcomes (median OS 14.63 months), with HAVF closure confirmed as an independent favorable prognostic factor. This provides preliminary exploratory evidence for this subset of patients, for whom conventional locoregional therapy is often limited.35,36 Notably, the objective response rate (ORR) assessed by mRECIST criteria was significantly higher than that assessed by RECIST 1.1 criteria (72.5% vs 54.9%, p=0.002). This discrepancy is consistent with previous studies, suggesting that the antitumor effect of HAIC-based combination therapy strategies may extend beyond the traditional tumor-shrinkage pattern, being achieved to a greater extent through mechanisms such as induced tumor necrosis and altered blood supply; accordingly, the mRECIST criteria may offer an advantage in capturing this type of treatment response.37

The observed AVF closure rate of up to 87.2% in this regimen may potentially result from multimodal synergistic effects. Clinically, AVFs are predominantly formed through direct tumor invasion of blood vessels; therefore, the fistula closure observed in temporal association with tumor regression likely reflects the resolution of tumor-mediated vascular invasion, rather than direct physical occlusion of vessels by the drugs. Although prior literature has suggested that vascular normalization or immunomodulation may be involved, such mechanistic explanations remain speculative in the context of this study and warrant confirmation through future translational research.

This study, using time-dependent covariate Cox model analysis, confirmed that HAVF closure was an independent protective factor for overall survival (HR=0.18, 95% CI: 0.05–0.68, p=0.011). This finding corroborates recent similar studies. For example, Liu et al38 reported that HAIC combined with targeted-immunotherapy achieved a 74.1% complete occlusion rate in patients with concomitant portal vein tumor thrombus and portal venous shunt, with shunt occlusion likewise serving as a significant protective factor for survival. Another 2023 study39 compared a HAIC-based triple regimen with a TACE-based triple regimen, showing that the HAIC group achieved a higher ORR (52.9% vs 27.9%) among patients with concomitant PVTT and APS, along with an extended median OS of 25.00 months. Together, these studies support that dynamic HAVF closure serves as a key biomarker for evaluating the efficacy of such combination regimens and may potentially act as a surrogate endpoint for survival. However, it must be clearly noted that this study observed a strong correlation between AVF closure and improved survival. As this study is unable to distinguish whether the survival benefit stems directly from the hemodynamic improvement resulting from AVF closure, or is a joint outcome of more effective systemic antitumor therapy (with tumor regression subsequently leading to fistula closure). Therefore, AVF closure should currently be regarded as a strong surrogate marker of treatment efficacy, rather than a direct causal driver of survival improvement.

Admittedly, HAIC-based combination strategies for the treatment of advanced HCC have been extensively reported.40,41 The core value of this study lies in being the first to systematically focus on this special subgroup of patients with concomitant HAVF—a condition conventionally regarded as a treatment contraindication and associated with extremely poor prognosis—and to quantitatively evaluate the HAVF closure rate and its prognostic value as core study endpoints, thereby providing targeted exploratory evidence for expanding the application of HAIC-based combination regimens.

Notably, before interpreting the data, the heterogeneity of baseline characteristics must be fully considered. Although the efficacy data from this study are consistent in trend with those reported by Liu et al38 and Lin et al,39 discrepancies also exist. For instance, the median OS in this study (14.63 months) falls between the 12.1 months and 25.00 months reported in the two aforementioned studies. This discrepancy may stem from heterogeneity in patients’ baseline characteristics, such as the notably high proportion of Vp4-type portal vein tumor thrombus (60.79%), the proportion of extrahepatic metastasis (15.69%), and differences in HAVF type composition (central versus peripheral) in this study. A recently updated meta-analysis demonstrated that TACE/HAIC combined with systemic therapy—particularly TACE combined with lenvatinib—ranked among the top regimens in terms of OS benefit for first-line treatment of advanced HCC.40 This finding supports the rationale for adopting HAIC combined with lenvatinib as the treatment regimen in this study, while also suggesting that the further addition of an immune checkpoint inhibitor (tislelizumab, as used in this study) may confer additional benefit for this particularly refractory population with concomitant arteriovenous fistula.

Although the previous SELECT trial of TACE combined with sorafenib did not demonstrate a significant OS improvement in the ITT analysis, it suggested the potential value of combining local and systemic therapy, while also emphasizing the importance of gastroscopic screening and prevention of variceal bleeding in patients with concomitant portal hypertension.34 Despite the lack of direct head-to-head comparisons, existing evidence—including data from both this study and reference39 —suggests that HAIC-based strategies may hold greater potential than TACE-based combination regimens42 in patients with concomitant HAVF, though this requires direct validation through prospective randomized controlled studies.

Specifically, the study population in this study presents with both arteriovenous fistula and high-burden portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT, 88.24% classified as Vp3-4), representing one of the advanced HCC subtypes with the poorest prognosis.43 In addressing this therapeutic challenge, the HAIC (FOLFOX regimen)-based combination strategy demonstrates potential clinical value. Its advantages lie in the ability of HAIC to achieve high local-regional chemotherapeutic drug exposure, exerting direct cytotoxic effects on PVTT.44 For instance, prior studies have shown that HAIC combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy achieved a disease control rate of 54.3% in patients with Vp4-type PVTT;45 even in more advanced patients with inferior vena cava or atrial tumor thrombus, this regimen achieved an objective response rate of 68.7%.46 Notably, in this regimen, lenvatinib may regulate tumor vasculature through inhibition of pathways such as VEGF, thereby helping to suppress the progressive growth of tumor thrombus, while tislelizumab may activate T-cell immune responses to target and eliminate disseminated tumor cells. The synergy among HAIC, lenvatinib, and the immune checkpoint inhibitor—ranging from direct control of the tumor thrombus itself to improvement of the overall tumor microenvironment—enables intensified management of “vascular invasion”, a key adverse prognostic factor.

Arteriovenous fistula, particularly high-flow fistula, has long been considered a relative contraindication for locoregional therapies such as transarterial chemoembolization (TACE). TACE or conventional embolization therapy carries the risk of ectopic embolization of embolic material through the fistula, potentially leading to serious complications such as liver failure and gastrointestinal ischemia.47 Recent studies have provided preliminary evidence for HAIC combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy as a potential solution to this clinical dilemma. One study focusing on advanced HCC patients with concomitant arteriovenous fistula demonstrated that HAIC combined with targeted therapy and immunotherapy improved HAVF in 77.8% of patients after two cycles, with complete closure achieved in 74.1%; more importantly, patients with fistula closure exhibited significantly superior overall survival (OS) and intrahepatic progression-free survival (InPFS) compared with those without closure (OS: 12.1 vs 4.4 months, P<0.001; InPFS: 6.2 vs 2.3 months, P=0.049), suggesting that fistula closure may serve as an important prognostic factor.38 A comparative study published in 2023 further demonstrated that, among advanced HCC patients with concomitant arteriovenous fistula, the HAIC combined with lenvatinib and PD-1 inhibitor regimen significantly prolonged median OS (25.00 vs 19.30 months, P=0.035) and median PFS (21.74 vs 8.74 months, P=0.0066) compared with the TACE-based combination regimen, with the median duration of response (mDOR) also showing a trend toward prolongation (20.43 vs 9.13 months, P=0.067).39 Collectively, this evidence suggests that HAIC-based combination strategies may offer an effective and relatively safe alternative treatment option for this patient population, although their precise clinical role still requires confirmation through Phase III clinical trials.

While demonstrating the aforementioned efficacy, the safety profile of this regimen is also a key clinical focus. Common adverse events included hypoalbuminemia, hypertension, hypothyroidism, abdominal pain, decreased appetite, and elevated AST, with the vast majority (approximately 30–40%) of adverse events being grade 1–2. Moreover, the safety profile of this regimen was largely consistent with the known characteristics of lenvatinib and PD-1 inhibitors,36,48,49 with a low incidence of serious adverse events. This predictable and manageable safety profile stands in sharp contrast to the acute liver function impairment that may occur following TACE,50 ensuring that high-risk patients with concomitant portal hypertension can tolerate the full course of treatment—which is crucial for achieving long-term survival benefit.

Although this study has certain limitations, the clinical efficacy it reveals still holds important reference value. First, as a single-center retrospective study, the sample size was determined by consecutively enrolled cases rather than prospective calculation, which to some extent limits statistical power; nevertheless, even under these constraints, we still observed efficacy signals of clear clinical significance, suggesting that the potential benefit trend of this combination strategy is genuinely present. Second, although the survival benefit is influenced by multiple aspects of the combination therapy, and the precise causal relationship between fistula closure and survival improvement warrants further clarification, the application of the time-dependent covariate model corrected for time-related bias to some extent, supporting the pivotal role played by fistula closure. Third, the limited sample size constrained the power of subgroup analyses (eg, different HAVF subtypes, concomitant liver metastasis. Additionally, although the standardized imaging assessment protocol could not entirely eliminate subtle inter-reviewer variability, and heterogeneity in subsequent treatments may have introduced some confounding factors, the consistency between sensitivity analyses and the primary endpoint results strengthens the robustness of the conclusions. Finally, regarding the mechanistic hypothesis of synergy between “vascular normalization” and “immune activation”, although this requires further confirmation through biomarker studies, this theoretical framework provides a reasonable biological basis for understanding the observed efficacy. In summary, given the current absence of high-level evidence-based data, the findings of this study provide strong support for the conduct of subsequent multicenter prospective studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this single-center retrospective single-arm study, the triplet regimen of FOLFOX-HAIC combined with lenvatinib and tislelizumab demonstrated encouraging preliminary efficacy signals—including a high fistula closure rate and manageable safety—in patients with advanced HCC complicated by arteriovenous fistula and high-burden PVTT. This study provides a promising clinical treatment approach for this refractory patient subgroup. However, given the limitations of this study, this regimen cannot currently be established as the standard or preferred treatment strategy for such patients. Its definitive efficacy, applicable patient subpopulations, optimal treatment duration, and long-term recurrence risk all warrant further validation through prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled studies.
