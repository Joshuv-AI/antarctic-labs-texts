Preoperative prediction of visceral pleural invasion in clinical stage T1 invasive lung adenocarcinoma using intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics: a multicenter development, validation, and comparison study
Authors: Hao Dong, Zebin Yang, Yonggang Qiu, Fangfang Qiu, Xinbin Wang, Zihao Xie, Junjie Yang, Leyi Shou, Xiaojun Guan, Xiaodan Ye, Xiaojun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate preoperative identification of visceral pleural invasion (VPI) is crucial for surgical planning and lymph node dissection in patients with early-stage lung adenocarcinoma. This study aims to develop and independently validate a predictive model for VPI in clinical stage T1 invasive lung adenocarcinoma, based on intratumoral and peritumoral radiomic features. Additionally, it initiates a preliminary exploration into the interpretability of the optimal model.MethodsThis retrospective analysis gathered imaging and clinical data from 316 patients diagnosed with potentially pleural-invading invasive lung adenocarcinoma based on computed tomography (CT) images across three medical institutions. A total of 109 patients from Center 1 and 99 patients from Center 2 were randomly assigned to a training set (146 patients) and a validation set (62 patients), in a 7:3 ratio, respectively. And 108 patients from Center 3 constituted an independent external test set. Feature selection was executed using univariate and multivariate analyses, Spearman correlation, minimal redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR), and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) techniques, to develop of clinical CT semantics, intratumoral radiomics, peritumoral radiomics, and combined intratumoral-peritumoral radiomics models. The influential features of the optimal model were subsequently ranked and evaluated using the SHapley additive explanation (SHAP).ResultsThe intratumoral model, exhibiting the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), indicated a superior model fitting in the training set. It also showed the highest Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) and Diagnostic Odds Ratio (DOR) in both validation and external test sets, demonstrating the best predictive capacity. The Area Under the Curve (AUC) values for the intratumoral model across training, validation, and external test sets were 0.837 (95% CI: 0.812–0.860), 0.825 (95% CI: 0.794–0.858), and 0.819 (95% CI: 0.791–0.845) respectively; these values surpassed those of the peritumoral, combined models, and clinical CT semantic models. SHAP analysis results showed Small-Area-Low-Gray-Level-Emphasis emerges as the pivotal feature.ConclusionIntratumoral radiomic features possess significant predictive value for VPI in clinical stage T1 invasive lung adenocarcinoma, aiding physicians in formulating precise clinical strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02465-2.

Introduction

Recent findings reveal that lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, accounting for 18.0% of total cancer deaths [1]. Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC), accounting for approximately 85% of lung cancer cases, primarily manifests as adenocarcinoma (40%) [2]. Visceral pleural invasion (VPI) is identified as a significant indicator for staging and a predictor of poor prognosis in lung cancer. The 8th edition of the NSCLC TNM staging standard advises that lung cancer at the T1 stage with VPI should be elevated to T2A, advancing from stage IA (T1N0M0) to stage IB (T2AN0M0) [3]. This criterion is retained in the forthcoming 9th edition [4]. Research indicates that VPI positivity in early-stage lung cancers smaller than 3.0 cm correlates with an elevated risk of lymph node metastasis. Consequently, for these individuals, less extensive surgeries, such as sublobar resections or local lymph node dissections might be inadequate, whereas comprehensive lobectomy and systemic lymphadenectomy may significantly improve survival rates for patients positive for VPI [5, 6]. Therefore, the precise identification of VPI status preoperatively is paramount for the selection of surgical tactics and lymph node dissection methods in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma patients.

At present, CT scanning is the predominant non-invasive technique for the preoperative evaluation of the severity of lung cancers. Various CT diagnostic attributes potentially indicative of VPI have been identified [7–13]. Typical CT signs include (a) direct tumor contact with the pleura, (b) pleural retraction, and (c) pleural tags. However, recent validation studies suggest these CT characteristics merely maintain accuracy rates between 62.7% and 72.3% [14]. Furthermore, the interaction between the tumor and adjacent pleura often exhibits complexity in reality, typically manifesting a blend of various forms, and the imaging classifications across various studies [7–13] lack consistency, undermining the uniformity of findings. Beyond preoperative predicting, the intraoperative discernment of VPI presents a significant challenge. Research by Takizawa et al. has shown that the mean diagnostic accuracy of eight thoracic surgeons during surgery reaches merely 56.5% [15]. In summary, both pre-and intraoperative diagnoses of VPI present substantial difficulties, necessitating the identification of more dependable predictive indicators.

Radiomics facilitates the extraction of high-dimensional data from radiological images employed in precision medicine, capturing details imperceptible to the eyes. In comparison to subjective assessments in imaging, analyses conducted via radiomics exhibit enhanced stability and objectivity [16–19]. Emerging research underscores the clinical significance of the tumor microenvironment in gauging its invasive potential [20–22]. Evidence supports that a synthesized analysis of radiomic characteristics, both within the lesion and in its periphery, can yield more precise predictions regarding the benign/malignant characteristics of pulmonary abnormalities [23] and the invasiveness of tumors [24]. At present, existing studies have illustrated the feasibility of utilizing radiomics for predicting VPI in NSCLC [25–28]. However, most studies lack independent external testing, and to our knowledge, there are currently no studies that concurrently explore the predictive efficacy of intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics for VPI. This study aims to establish a model based on intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics to predict VPI in aggressive lung adenocarcinoma and to conduct independent external testing. Furthermore, we attempt to use the SHapley additive explanation (SHAP) analysis for exploratory interpretative analysis of the compositional features of the optimal model. This study adheres to the TITAN Guidelines 2025 governing the ethical declaration and use of artificial intelligence in research [29].

Materials and methods

Patient data

Approval for this research was obtained from the ethics committees of three distinct medical institutions. Given the retrospective nature of this study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. The investigation adhered to the guidelines set forth by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) [30] and aligned with the most recent version of the Declaration of Helsinki. The research conforms to the STARD (Standards for the Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies) criteria [31].

A priori sample size calculation was performed using PASS software (Version 16.0.3). Based on an expected area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.85 for the target diagnostic model, with a two-sided alpha (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power (1-β) of 80%, and an anticipated prevalence of VPI of approximately 20%, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 200 participants was required to robustly evaluate the diagnostic accuracy.

A comprehensive retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical, CT, and pathological records of individuals diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma who underwent complete surgical excision from February 2019 to March 2021 across the three centers. In total, 316 individuals satisfied the requisite inclusion criteria and were enrolled in our present study. The procedural flowchart for case selection is illustrated in Fig. 1. Inclusion criteria encompassed: (1) Histologically verified invasive lung adenocarcinoma with pleural invasion assessment; (2) Clinical T1 staging of the tumor [3]; (3) Undergoing preoperative plain CT scans; (4) Presence of one or more of the following on CT scans: contact with the pleura (Fig. S1A), pleural retraction (Fig. S1B), or pleural tags (Fig. S1C), with pleural tags defined according to previous literature [32]. Blinded to the pathology results, a senior radiologist with two decades of expertise in thoracic CT diagnostics independently evaluated the interaction between the lesion and the pleura on lung window settings (window width: 1500HU; window level: -500 HU). Exclusion criteria included: (1) Incomplete clinical, CT, or pathological records; (2) Cases of glandular precursor lesions or minimally invasive adenocarcinomas; (3) Confirmation of multiple primary lung cancers (≥ 2) either surgically or pathologically; (4) Prior treatments such as chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or biopsies; (5) A history of lung or other malignancies; (6) Lack of thin-section CT scans (reconstruction layer thickness = 1 mm) within two weeks prior to surgery; (7) Significant respiratory motion artifacts on the scans.

Subsequently, 109 individuals from Center 1 and 99 from Center 2 were randomly allocated to a training set (n = 146) and an internal validation set (n = 62) in a 7:3 ratio, with 108 subjects from Center 3 designated as an independent external test set.

Examination protocols

Preoperative assessments for all patients were conducted across three centers employing the Siemens SOMATOM Definition AS + 128-layer spiral CT, Philips Brilliance 64-slice CT, and UIH uCT510 CT. Prior to the scanning process, all patients underwent rigorous breathing instruction and were positioned in the supine position, with the head entering first into the scanner. They were then directed to inhale deeply and hold their breath at the peak of inhalation. The scanning scope encompassed the entire lung region, extending from the apex to the base. The precise details regarding the scanning and reconstruction parameters of the equipment are available in Table S1.

CT signatures analysis

Given the reliance on clinical expertise for the analysis of CT indicators, this study involved a retrospective examination by a seasoned radiologist with two decades of experience in chest CT imaging, who analyzed the CT images independently without prior access to the pathological findings. The analysis was performed on lung window settings (window width: 1500HU; window level: -500 HU). The CT characterization of lesions encompassed aspects such as lesion density, shape, spiculation, lobulation, the presence of vacuole, air bronchograms. Detailed definitions of these CT characteristics can be found in Table S2.

Pathological evaluation

Elastic fiber staining was used to assess the presence of VPI within paraffin-embedded histological sections. These sections underwent blind evaluation by two pathologists, each with extensive experience. Discrepancies between evaluations were resolved through mutual consultation, achieving a unified conclusion. Detailed pathologic assessment methods are described in Supplementary Material.

Segmentation of tumor and surrounding areas

The axial DICOM images from pulmonary CT scans were initially imported into the open-source ITK-SNAP software (version 4.8, available at www.itksnap.org). In this process, two radiologists, each boasting a decade of pulmonary imaging diagnosis experience, carefully delineated the region of interest (ROI) surrounding the tumor’s inner edges within the lung window, sequentially through each layer until the entire tumor mass was encapsulated subsequently generating and storing this as a 3D representation designated as the intratumoral volume (ITV). One radiologist conducted the segmentation for images originating from Center 1 and Center 2, while another radiologist undertook this task for Center 3’s images. A senior radiologist subsequently reviewed these segmentations, mediating any disparities to establish a unified agreement. Key exclusions from the ROI entailed significant blood vessels, bronchi, and extensive cavities, with the trio of physicians remaining blind to any clinical-pathological details of the subjects. Based on previously published literature, we selected a 5-millimeter margin around the tumor [23]. An automatic segmentation program (Supplementary Material) was applied to expand 5 mm outward to form the peri-ROI, defined as peritumoral volume (PTV). All ROIs are ultimately reviewed and manually corrected layer by layer by a radiologist to ensure that no extrapulmonary structures are included. A schematic diagram of tumor and peritumoral region segmentation is shown in Fig. S2.

Evaluation of consistency and reproducibility

Subsequently, three months post-initial analysis, two radiologists independently revisited the images from 40 randomly selected patients within the training set to perform tumor segmentation and feature extraction anew, following the same methodologies and protocols without awareness of each other’s procedural specifics. The reproducibility and consistency of tumor segmentation and feature extraction were evaluated using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), with values exceeding 0.8 denoting robust consistency and thus suitable for further analysis.

Image preprocessing and radiomic feature extraction

To minimize scanner-induced variations in image texture, all CT images were resampled to a uniform isotropic voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm³. Intensity normalization was performed using Z-score transformation, and extreme values beyond mean ± 4 standard deviations were excluded. The preprocessed intensities were then rescaled to the range [0,1]. This pipeline adheres to the Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI) guidelines [33]. Radiomic attributes were derived employing the PyRadiomics package (version 2.1.0) [34]. The methodologies for extraction of features are elaborated in the Supplementary Material.

Class imbalance handling

Given the inherently low prevalence of visceral pleural invasion in clinical stage T1 lung adenocarcinoma, the training set exhibited a moderate class imbalance. To address this, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied. Crucially, SMOTE was integrated strictly within the five-fold cross-validation loop used during LASSO regression: for each fold, only the minority class within the training split was oversampled to a 1:1 ratio; the corresponding validation split was never touched. Feature pre-selection via Spearman correlation and the minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) algorithm was performed on the original, imbalanced data, ensuring that the selected features reflect genuine biological signal rather than synthetic augmentation. Radiomic features selection

Features with intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) < 0.8 were excluded to ensure reproducibility. Spearman correlation analysis further removed highly redundant features (|ρ| > 0.8). The mRMR algorithm then identified the top 100 most distinctive features. Finally, the LASSO logistic regression, embedded within a five-fold cross-validation framework (with SMOTE applied internally as described), was used to select the most predictive features and build the final radiomic signatures.

Construction and interpretability of models

Utilizing the optimal features derived from both ITV and PTV, separate models were developed: the Intratumoral Radiomic Score (I-RS) and the Peritumoral Radiomic Score (P-RS). An integrative radiomic model (IP-RS) was subsequently established by amalgamating features from both intratumoral and peritumoral zones using logistic regression, from which Radiomic Scores (Rad-scores) were calculated. Additionally, a Clinical CT Semantic Model (C-C) was devised utilizing LASSO regression. The SHAP approach facilitated the interpretive breakdown of the prime model, enabling visualization of individual predictive variables. This approach bestows comprehensive interpretative insights, offering global explanations by allocating precise value attributions to each model feature based on their contribution and local interpretability, illuminating the significant predictors for individual sample predictions. Statistical evaluation

The R software, version 4.1.2, was utilized for data manipulation and statistical assessment. Clinical, CT, and pathological attributes were scrutinized using the t-test, one-way ANOVA, Pearson’s chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. The Receiver Operator Characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn to evaluate diagnostic performance, with the Area Under the Curve (AUC) quantifying the accuracy. Model predictive efficiencies were compared using the Delong test, while the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were employed to gauge the efficacy of model development within the training set. The validation and external test phases harnessed the Positive Predictive Value (PPV), Negative Predictive Value (NPV), Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and Diagnostic Odds Ratio (DOR) for evaluating model performance. Notably, the MCC reflects the concordance between predictions and actual outcomes, proving particularly effective for imbalanced datasets [35]. The DOR serves as an aggregate measure of the model’s discriminatory power, with higher values denoting superior predictive capability unaffected by disease prevalence [36]. Additionally, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was leveraged to determine the models’ clinical utility and practical relevance. Statistical significance was established at a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05.

Extraction and selection of radiomic features

A total of 1045 features were extracted from the ITV and PTV, as detailed in Table S4. Consistency analysis of these features revealed that 940 features from ITV and 964 from PTV had an ICC greater than 0.8, accounting for 89.9% and 92.2%, respectively. These features were utilized for further evaluation. The mRMR method was used to rank features, selecting the top 100 non-redundant features. The Lasso method, applied through five-fold cross-validation, was employed to identify the most significant features, resulting in 9 features for the I-RS model and 6 for the P-RS model, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Lastly, logistic regression was utilized to select 9 features for the combined IP-RS model.

Development and assessment of predictive models

Optimal features derived from ITV and PTV were utilized to establish the I-RS and P-RS models. The IP-RS logistic regression model was developed using features from both intratumoral and peritumoral analyses. Detailed feature names and coefficients for all related models are listed in Table S5. The feature counts, AUC values, and the models’ AIC and BIC values in the training sets are provided in Table 3. ROC curves for the models are shown in Fig. 4A-C, indicating the I-RS model boasts the highest AUC, with respective values in the training, validation, and external testing sets of 0.837 (0.812–0.860), 0.825 (0.794–0.858), and 0.819 (0.791–0.845). The intratumoral model had the lowest AIC and BIC values in the training set, suggesting superior model fit. As indicated in Figure 6A-B, Small-Area-Low-Gray-Level-Emphasis (SALGLE) emerges as the pivotal feature. Figure 6C showcases two archetype cases accurately predicted by the model as VPI- and VPI+, and visualised by SHAP. As demonstrated in these examples, SALGLE consistently contributes to an elevated VPI risk in both scenarios. This is corroborated by Fig. 3C and Supplementary Table 5, which illustrate a negative correlation between this radiomic attribute and VPI. SALGLE represents a textural attribute quantifying the relative prevalence of smaller areas exhibiting lower gray levels within the imagery. The formula is delineated as follows:

Discussion

The lymphatic vessels in the pulmonary pleura are highly abundant. If invaded by a tumor, this increases the risk of local recurrence and systemic metastasis for the patient [37, 38]. As expected, our study results also demonstrate that patients with VPI are more likely to develop lymph node metastases and STAS. Additionally, tumors with a higher pathological grade and containing high-grade patterns are more likely to present VPI. Although most studies have assessed the morphological characteristics of VPI on CT images, the accuracy of studies based on CT morphological characteristics remains low. Research by Shi et al. indicated that among 234 patients with T1N0M0 lung adenocarcinoma showing pleural involvement on CT, only 49 were pathologically confirmed as VPI [39]. Our findings corroborate this observation: among 316 cases with CT-suggested possible VPI, only 66 were pathologically confirmed as VPI. Furthermore, our results indicate that the AUC value of the clinical-CT semantic model on the external test set was merely 0.509. This may stem from the subjective interpretation of traditional CT features, which heavily relies on radiologist experience, resulting in poor generalization of the clinical-CT semantic model.

Radiomics offers a more comprehensive understanding of intratumoral heterogeneity, offering potential for precise management of various tumors [40]. Currently, some studies have demonstrated the potential of radiomics in predicting NSCLC VPI. Lin et al. [25] developed a multimodal model integrating imaging and pathological features. However, its clinical applicability in preoperative settings remains limited due to its reliance on postoperative pathological data input. We constructed our model solely based on preoperative CT images, enabling true preoperative decision support for determining surgical approaches and lymph node dissection strategies. Although Choi et al. [26] proposed a deep learning model for VPI prediction with performance comparable to radiologists; however, its lack of interpretability limits clinical adoption. In contrast, our radiomics model enhanced with SHAP analysis provides explicit feature importance, boosting clinical confidence while revealing tumor characteristics associated with VPI. Although models by Wei et al. [27] and Zhao et al. [28] achieved high AUC values, they were validated only internally. Our model demonstrates consistent and stable performance on an external validation set from an independent institution (AUC = 0.819), confirming its generalization capability and robustness across different datasets and CT protocols.

In the present study, we developed radiomics models for both intratumoral and peritumoral regions. After synthesizing and contrasting these models, their capabilities for generalization were evaluated via independent external assessments. Our findings underscore that the intratumoral radiomics model exhibits superior accuracy in the prediction of the VPI. This intratumoral model is characterized by nine radiomic features, including two first-order statistical features and seven textural features, including three features from the Gray Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM), two from the Gray Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM), and one each from the Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) and Gray Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM). These features are indicative of textural heterogeneity, reflecting the inherent unevenness of the tumor, particularly pronounced in VPI-positive scenarios, where internal tumor heterogeneity is significantly highlighted. Our research indicates that radiomic characteristics of the peritumoral region have limited diagnostic value for pathological VPI. According to Wu et al. [41], the evaluation of radiomics features within the 5–15 mm periphery of solid nodules did not improve model performance compared to intratumoral attributes and revealed that model performance declines with increasing Peritumoral Tissue Volume (PTV). Additionally, another investigation [42] revealed that peritumoral radiomic attributes do not significantly impact the prediction of aggressiveness in part-solid nodular lung adenocarcinomas. It is inferred that the incidence of VPI correlates more significantly with intratumoral heterogeneity than with peritumoral discrepancies. This may be because as the distance from the tumor increases, the proportion of normal lung tissue in the volume of interest increases, while the proportion of tumor tissue decreases, thus reducing the prediction performance of the model. It is also possible that including the peritumoral region might potentially cause background noise. This noise might compromise the accuracy of the model’s classification and outcome prediction by mixing particular tumor-related signals with extraneous information.

In the scope of this research, we employed the SHAP methodology for an exploratory examination and interpretation of the primary features within the optimal model. SHAP stands out as a potent interpretative tool in machine learning, providing a visual representation of both collective and individual feature contributions, thereby streamlining the model’s clinical integration and boosting clinician confidence in predictive modeling applications [43, 44]. The SHAP method’s forte in interpretability stems from its capacity to furnish a direct and visually engaging means of elucidating model predictions, thereby demystifying the operational dynamics and inter-feature relations within the model. Our investigation highlights SALGLE as the most influential feature, exhibiting a negative correlation with VPI indicators. SALGLE principally delineates the characteristics of low gray levels within diminutive image regions. SHAP analysis results indicate that the top three radiomics features with the highest contribution are SALGLE, HGLE, and SZNN. SALGLE, the feature with the highest contribution, exhibits a negative correlation with VPI and primarily characterizes low gray-value features within small image regions. From a biological standpoint, these findings are hypothesis-generating. SALGLE emerged as a pivotal factor in the development of a radiomic model for predicting high-grade patterns within lung adenocarcinomas in our prior studies [45]. An increased SALGLE value typically signifies a more focused or homogeneous pixel intensity distribution within the image’s low-gray-level zones, suggesting uniformity in the pixel values across these regions. From a biological standpoint, elevated SALGLE values may denote areas within the image marked by closely matched pixel intensities and minimal variations in gray levels, which could correspond to uniform distributions of tumor cell and reduced tumor heterogeneity. Lower SALGLE values indicate more smaller low-gray-level regions within the tumor, which may reflect increased intratumoral heterogeneity (e.g., necrotic foci or invasive components) and thus higher VPI risk. HGLE measures the distribution of higher gray-level values in an image and exhibited a negative correlation with VPI. SZNN quantifies the variability in the size of homogeneously connected regions with similar gray-level intensity and shows a positive correlation with VPI. In summary, low SALGLE reflects the absence of small-scale low-density structures, low HGLE indicates the absence of large-scale high-density structures, and high SZNN signifies extreme disorganization and heterogeneity of the overall structure. However, it must be emphasized that without direct voxel-level correlation between imaging and whole-mount histopathology, such interpretations remain speculative and require further validation.

Finally, the clinical translation of any predictive model requires a careful consideration of the consequences of misclassification, which are asymmetric in this context. A false-positive prediction would potentially lead to an unnecessary lobectomy and systematic lymph node dissection in a patient who might have been adequately treated with sublobar resection. Conversely, a false-negative prediction carries the risk of performing a limited resection in a patient with VPI, leaving behind microscopic pleural disease and regional lymph nodes that harbor metastasis, thereby increasing the risk of locoregional recurrence. While our decision curve analysis demonstrates a net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities, the choice of an ‘optimal’ operating point for clinical use is not purely statistical; it requires a clinical consensus that weighs the morbidity of overtreatment against the mortality risk of undertreatment. Future prospective studies should aim to define a clinically acceptable risk threshold by surveying thoracic surgeons on their risk tolerance for these two types of error.

Our investigation has several limitations. First, despite its multicenter nature, the study is constrained by a limited sample size. Given the relatively low incidence of VPI in early-stage lung adenocarcinomas, a larger sample size would help increase the number of positive cases in the study. Second, this study is retrospective in design. In the future, we plan to continue expanding the sample size to prospectively validate the results. Third, class imbalance in the sample may noticeably affect the modeling outcomes. To mitigate this issue, we applied an oversampling technique in the training set to ensure balance. Additionally, we used the MCC and DOR to comprehensively evaluate both the validation and external test sets. It is worth noting that both MCC and DOR maintain a commendable level of objectivity and evaluative consistency even in imbalanced datasets.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have formulated and substantiated a non-intrusive, steadfast radiomics model. This model holds the potential to enhance the precision in appraising VPI among patients with clinical T1-stage lung adenocarcinoma. Our research can assist clinicians in developing precision treatment plans, thereby supporting personalized diagnosis and treatment for patients.
