Impact of diabetes mellitus on the risk of Alzheimer’s disease: a mendelian randomization study
Authors: Weichao Wang, Jie Zhang, Man Zhang, Chengyuan Zhang, Huanli Liu, Wanlin Li, Yimeng Fan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of diabetes on the risk of Alzheimer’s disease remains uncertain. This study aimed to explore this issue from multiple perspectives by using the Mendelian randomization (MR) approach.MethodsInstrumental variables for predicting six diabetic traits (including insulin and blood glucose), eight metabolic risk factors for diabetes (including total cholesterol and blood pressure), and seven diabetic genes were extracted from their summary data. These data were derived from multiple European cohorts and included 31,684 to 810,865 subjects respectively. The two-sample MR, multivariate MR, and summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) methods were employed to determine the associations of these traits or genes with the risk of Alzheimer’s disease.ResultsThe two-sample MR showed that elevated fasting insulin and total cholesterol levels were associated with an increased risk of dementia in Alzheimer’s disease (P = 0.022, P = 0.041). Elevated systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels were associated with a decreased risk of dementia in Alzheimer’s disease (P = 0.036, P = 0.025). The multivariate MR reported that adjusting for telomere length (a well-established biomarker of aging) did not change these findings (P < 0.05). Additionally, the two-sample MR showed that type 1 and type 2 diabetes did not affect the risk of Alzheimer’s disease. The SMR also indicated that the diabetic genes did not affect the risk of this disease.ConclusionMultiple MR approaches concluded that fasting insulin, total cholesterol, and blood pressure, rather than diabetes, were potential metabolic variables that had an impact on the risk of Alzheimer’s disease. However, aging might not be involved in these correlations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-024-03955-y.

Introduction

By 2050, the number of people suffering from Alzheimer’s disease (AD) could reach 90 million worldwide [1]. However, the pathogenesis of the disease remains unknown. Some studies had linked AD to beta-amyloid, but other studies had shown that removing beta-amyloid did not completely alleviate the disease [2].

Currently, a series of findings indicated that diabetes mellitus (DM), a common metabolic disease, may be associated with the onset and progression of AD [3–5]. However, these findings were mainly obtained from in vivo experiments or observational studies. In vivo experiments were conducted using models of other species, and observational studies were easily affected by confounding factors and biases [6]. So, the impact of DM on the risk of AD in the population still required thorough exploration.

Mendelian randomization (MR) studies are a gene-based epidemiological approach that can avoid the limitations mentioned above. Nevertheless, in recent years, at least five MR studies specifically explored this topic [7–11]. Three of them reported an association between DM and AD, while the other two did not support these inferences. This unexpected contradiction might originate from defects in the data or in the design. So, a definite conclusion still could not be drawn. However, this situation also highlighted the necessity of conducting further research from more perspectives.

Therefore, the present study attempted to explore the correlation of multiple DM traits and genes with AD using two-sample MR and summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) methods, followed by some mechanistic analyses using multivariate MR. Figure 1 shown the main process of this study. The results may help us to re-conceptualize the correlation between DM and AD.

Materials and methods

Summary data of the exposures

There were three types of exposures in this study. First, the DM traits were type 2 DM, type 1 DM, fasting blood glucose (BG), 2-hour BG, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and fasting insulin. Second, the metabolic risk factors for DM were body mass index (BMI), body fat percentage (BFP), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), uric acid (UA), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Third, the DM genes were C2, HLA-B, HLA-DRA, HNF1A, KCNJ11, LINC00243, and TRIM27.

The selection criteria for the DM genes were as follows. First, the genes were obtained from the Ensembl database (https://useast.ensembl.org/). Second, based on the information in the above database, the included genes must have more than one single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) that significantly predicted DM. Third, positive control analysis was then performed in this study, which further confirmed the relevance of the included genes to DM.

The summary data for type 2 DM was retrieved from a meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies (GWASs) by combining data of DIAbetes Genetics Replication and Meta-analysis (DIAGRAM), Genetic Epidemiology Research on Aging (GERA), and UK Biobank (UKB), which involved approximately 16 million genetic variants in 62,892 cases and 596,424 controls of European ancestry [12]. The data for type 1 DM was from a GWAS that interpreted the risk of type 1 DM through genetics and single-cell epigenomics [13]. This study included 21 cohorts (including the UKB cohort) as well as 18,942 cases and 501,638 controls of European ancestry. The data of fasting BG, 2-hour BG, HbA1c, and fasting insulin were derived from a published GWAS that aggregated 91 European cohorts [14]. In this study, the sample sizes for fasting BG, 2-hour BG, HbA1c, and fasting insulin ranged from 63,396 to 200,622 subjects.

The summary data of BMI was from an updated GWAS for height and BMI [15]. This study performed a meta-analysis of previous GWASs from the Genetic Investigation of ANthropometric Traits (GIANT) and combined the data of the UKB, resulting in a final study sample of 681,275 European subjects. The data for BFP was incorporated from a computationally efficient whole-genome regression study, which included 401,772 European subjects from the UKB for this body fat trait [16]. The data for WHR was from a study that introduced a BOLT-LMM Bayesian mixed model association method and included 502,773 European subjects of the UKB [17]. The data of TG, TC, and UA were retrieved through a meta-analysis of GWASs from the UKB, FinnGen, and BioBank Japan [18]. This meta-analysis compiled a cross-population atlas of genetic associations for 220 human phenotypes with a total sample size of 628,000 subjects. The samples for these three metabolic traits were 343,836 to 344,278 European individuals from the UKB. The data for SBP and DBP were included from a meta-analysis of 1.3 million individuals which discovered the rare variants associated with blood pressure regulation [19]. The sample for these blood pressure traits was 810,865 European individuals from 75 cohorts (including the UKB cohorts).

The summary data for the DM genes were obtained from a large-scale cis- and trans-eQTL analysis which identified thousands of genetic loci and polygenic scores regulating blood gene expression [20]. This study was done by the eQTLGen, and included 31,684 European subjects.

Summary data of the outcomes

There were two outcomes in this study, i.e. AD and dementia in AD. The summary data for them were collected from a FinnGen GWAS which provided genetic insights from a well-phenotyped isolated population [21]. All subjects were European. The sample sizes for AD and dementia in AD were 13,393 and 5,422, respectively. These cases were determined based on the ICD-10 codes in medical records. The codes for AD included F00.0*, F00.00*, F00.00*G30.0, F00.1*, F00.10*, F00.10*G30.1, G30.0, G30.0 + F00.00, G30.1 and G30.1 + F00.10, while the code for dementia in AD was F00*.

Instrumental variables

There were two types of instrumental variables in the study, i.e. SNP and cis-expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL). The suitable SNPs were used to predict the DM traits and other metabolic risk factors, while the top eQTLs were used to predict the DM genes. All these instrumental variables were extracted from the summary data based on the correlation, independence, and exclusivity assumptions of MR.

The SNPs were extracted using the following criteria: (1) The minor allele frequencies>1%. (2) The genome-wide association P-values<5 × 10⁻⁸. (3) The F-statistics>10. (4) Removing linkage disequilibrium (r² = 0.001, distance = 10,000 kb). (5) Excluding the SNPs related to the confounders using the phenoscanner database. The top eQTL for each gene was extracted using the following criteria: (1) The minor allele frequencies>1%. (2) The genome-wide association P-values<5 × 10⁻⁸. (3) The F-statistics>10.

Two-sample mendelian randomization

The random-effect inverse variance-weighted (IVW) and MR-Egger were adopted to determine the causal association of the DM traits, other metabolic risk factors with the risk of AD and dementia in AD [22]. The IVW was the most preferred method because it gave the most reliable results in the absence of horizontal pleiotropy [23]. The MR-Egger can be used as a complement to the IVW, because it provided very conservative results, especially in the presence of horizontal pleiotropy [24].

The MR-PRESSO can be used as a method of MR as well as a method of sensitivity analysis [25]. When the MR-PRESSO found the outliers, it eliminated the outliers and produced the corrected causal inference results; when the MR-PRESSO did not find any outliers, it directly gave the raw results.

All the IVW, MR-Egger, and MR-PRESSO reported the P-values, odds ratios (ORs), and 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs). If the results of the three methods were inconsistent, the IVW result was considered as the primary finding. More specifically, the causal correlation was considered to be significant when the IVW result was significant and the results of the three methods were in the same direction.

In addition, the Cochran’s Q and MR-Egger intercept tests were used for sensitivity analyses [26, 27]. The Cochran’s Q can detect the heterogeneity, and the MR-Egger intercept can assess the horizontal pleiotropy. A P-value of less than 0.05 indicated the presence of heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy.

Positive control analysis was adopted. Briefly, for each exposure in this study, an outcome that was definitely related to it was selected from outside this study. Then, the correlation inference between this exposure and this outcome was carried out, and the effectiveness of SNPs was indirectly detected according to the result. For all exposures in this study, the specific outcomes selected were diabetic ketoacidosis, DM, coronary heart disease, gout, or hypertension respectively.

Multivariate mendelian randomization

It is well known that aging is associated with many of the above research traits [28]. Therefore, this study used the multivariate MR to adjust for telomere length (a recognized aging biomarker [29]) and evaluate the association of these DM traits and other metabolic risk factors with the risk of AD. Thus, This study may initially explore the role of aging in the impact of DM on AD. The summary data of telomere length came from a GWAS based on 472,174 European subjects (from the UKB), and this GWAS described the genetic architecture of naturally occurring variations in leukocyte telomere length [30].

Summary-data-based mendelian randomization

The SMR was used to measure the causal associations of the DM genes with the risk of AD and dementia in AD [31]. These analyses also reported the P-values, ORs, and 95%CIs. The HEIDI tests were conducted for sensitivity analyses, and a P-value of less than 0.05 indicated the presence of horizontal pleiotropy. Furthermore, positive control analysis was performed to ensure the validity of the top eQTLs, and type 2 DM and type 1 DM were the outcomes in these analyses.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were done by the R software (version 4.3.1) or SMR software (version 1.3.1). Due to multiple comparisons (including 21 exposures and 2 outcomes in the study), the Bonferroni correction method was adopted. Briefly, a P-value of less than 0.05 suggested nominal statistical significance, whereas a Bonferroni-corrected P-value of less than 0.001 (0.05/(21 × 2)) suggested significant statistical significance.

This study was implemented in accordance with the STROBE-MR guidelines.

Results

Effect of diabetic traits on Alzheimer’s disease

As mentioned above, three two-sample MR methods, i.e., the IVW, MR-Egger, and MR-PRESSO, were used to infer the causal association of the DM traits and other metabolic risk factors with the risk of AD. The Cochran’s Q and MR-Egger intercept tests were adopted for sensitivity analysis.

In Table 3, the IVW reported that the elevated level of fasting insulin was associated with the increased risk of dementia in AD (OR = 1.919, 95%CI = 1.100–3.349, P = 0.022). In Supplemental Tables 2 and 3, the MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO supported the IVW result described above (with consistent result directions). In Supplemental Table 4, the Cochran’s Q and MR-Egger intercept did not find any heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy (P = 0.432, P = 0.940).

Effect of metabolic risk factors on Alzheimer’s disease

In Table 4, the IVW reported that the elevated level of TC was associated with the increased risk of AD and dementia in AD (OR = 1.159, 95%CI = 1.027–1.309, P = 0.017; OR = 1.188, 95%CI = 1.007–1.401, P = 0.041). The IVW also reported that the elevated levels of SBP and DBP were associated with the decreased risk of dementia in AD (OR = 0.793, 95%CI = 0.638–0.985, P = 0.036; OR = 0.790, 95%CI = 0.642–0.971, P = 0.025). In Supplemental Tables 6 and 7, the MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO results were consistent with the IVW results (with consistent result directions). The traits that were significant in the above analyses (i.e., fasting insulin, TC, SBP, and DBP) were included. Since these traits and AD were all related to aging, the multivariate MR adjusted for telomere length and re-performed causal inference between these traits and AD to evaluate the role of aging in these correlations.

In Table 5, the multivariate MR reported results similar to the two-sample MR. Briefly, the increased TC levels were associated with an increased risk of AD and dementia in AD (OR = 1.142, 95%CI = 1.010–1.287, P = 0.019; OR = 1.089, 95%CI = 1.001–1.354, P = 0.045). The increased fasting insulin levels were associated with an increased risk of dementia in AD (OR = 1.738, 95%CI = 1.058–3.015, P = 0.024). On the contrary, the increased SBP and DBP levels were associated with a decreased risk of dementia in AD (OR = 0.835, 95%CI = 0.695–0.997, P = 0.040; OR = 0.827, 95%CI = 0.681–0.986, P = 0.029).

Effect of diabetic genes on Alzheimer’s disease

Then, the SMR was used to infer the impact of DM genes on AD, while the HEIDI test was used for sensitivity analysis.

In Supplemental Table 10, the SMR reported that the genes (i.e. C2, HLA-B, HLA-DRA, HNF1A, KCNJ11, LINC00243, and TRIM27) did not affect the risk of AD and dementia in AD (P>0.05). The HEIDI test also did not find any horizontal pleiotropy in the analyses (P>0.05).

In Supplemental Table 11, the positive control analyses reported that the HNF1A and KCNJ11 were related to the risk of type 2 DM (P<0.05), the C2 and HLA-B were related to the risk of type 1 DM (P<0.05), and the HLA-DRA and LINC00243 were related to the risk of both types of DM (P<0.05). These results confirmed the validity of the top eQTLs.

Discussion

Compared with previous studies [7–11], the present study encompassed a significantly greater number of exposure factors, including both traits and genes associated with DM, and comprehensively evaluated the potential impact of DM on AD through multiple MR methods.

Based on the obtained results, the study discovered that for each standard deviation increase in fasting insulin levels, the risk of dementia in AD rose by approximately 90%. As is widely known, insulin is secreted by pancreatic β-cells and is the only hormone in the body that lowers blood glucose [32]. In this study, an increase in insulin levels may vaguely suggest the presence of insulin resistance. This was because the positive control analysis had also confirmed the association between elevated insulin levels and an increased risk of DM. Recently, Kellar et al. reported that insulin may enhance synaptic viability, increase neurotransmitter turnover, and influence amyloid β-peptide clearance, and that insulin dysregulation or resistance may contribute to the development of dementia in AD [33]. These findings seemed to partially support the results of this study. Of course, the influence of insulin resistance on AD at the genetic level still required further exploration.

Due to the close relationship between DM and various metabolic diseases such as hyperlipidemia and hypertension, this study also examined the effect of these metabolic disorders on AD. According to the study’s results, each standard deviation increase in peripheral TC levels could raise the risk of AD or dementia in AD by 10–20%. Some observational studies had also addressed this topic. Dunk et al. reported that higher TC might be a significant contributor to AD risk, particularly in APOE4 carriers who tended to have impaired cholesterol metabolism [34]. Hall et al. suggested that AD patients with higher TC had lower cognitive function scores as well as more neuropsychiatric and psychosis symptoms [35]. These were in line with the results of this study.

Surprisingly, this study reported that every standard deviation increase in the levels of SBP and DBP led to an approximately 20% reduction in the risk of dementia in AD. Two reasonable explanations for these results were as follows. First, previous neuroimaging studies had shown that lower blood pressure was related to more severe white matter lesions and hippocampal atrophy, which were important pathological changes of AD [36, 37]. Second, elderly people or patients with cerebral diseases often had impaired regulation of cerebral blood perfusion, and lower blood pressure may exacerbate insufficient blood perfusion, resulting in more severe cerebral ischemia and accelerating the development of AD [38, 39]. Yuan et al. concluded a series of observational studies and animal experiments on this topic from around the world [40]. Briefly, previous observational studies had been contradictory regarding the correlation between blood pressure and AD, and at least some studies suggested that lower blood pressure was associated with an increased risk of AD. Animal experiments, on the other hand, reported that hypertension can significantly accelerate the development of AD-related brain structural and functional abnormalities. Thus, these findings seemed to partially support the results of this study. Naturally, further research was still needed.

In the multivariate analysis, the impact of telomere length on the study’s results was additionally explored. There were two reasons for conducting these analyses. First, the incidences of DM, several metabolic risk factors related to DM, and AD all increase with the rise in population age [28]. So, there was reason to believe that aging may be a potential confounding factor in this study. Second, telomere length is a recognized biomarker of aging [29]. After the analyses, the results shown that adjusting for telomere length did not change the main findings of this study. Thus, it was believed that the effects of fasting insulin, TC, SBP, and DBP on AD may be unrelated to aging but involve other mechanisms, and it was hoped that future studies may uncover them.

The majority of the positive results in this study only had nominal correlations, but this did not mean they were unimportant. As an MR analysis, the reported correlations existed at the genetic level. And even minor abnormalities at the genetic level (even a single-site mutation) may have a significant impact at the phenotypic level. So, the nominally positive results in this study still had potentially great value.

The greatest strength of this study was its well-designed MR strategy. This strategy effectively circumvented the limitations of previous studies (mainly referring to the interference of confounding factors and biases) and provided more reliable results. On the other hand, the disadvantages of this study were as follows. First, due to the limitations of summary data, only some DM traits and genes were selected for the analysis. Other potential traits and genes may be equally important. Once the data was upgraded, it was expected that more detailed exploration of them would be conducted. Second, also due to the limitations of summary data, all the data in this study were from European populations. However, it cannot be excluded that the correlation between DM and AD may be race-specific. Therefore, it was necessary to conduct similar studies using data from other ethnic groups when the data permitted.

In conclusion, by integrating the results of two-sample MR, multivariate MR, and SMR, this study suggested that elevated peripheral insulin and TC levels might promote the development of AD, while a proper increase in blood pressure could be a protective factor against this neurodegenerative disease. These processes may not be related to aging. In addition, there was no evidence that type 2 and type 1 DM affected the risk of AD. If these findings were further confirmed, the study may redefine the association between DM and AD.
