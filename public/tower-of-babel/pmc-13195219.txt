Prevalence, patient characteristics, and outcomes of high-dose upadacitinib in inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Julian Azar, Rofina Johnkennedy, Louis J Levine, Ibrahim AlOmar, Abdulelah AlMutairdi, Mashary Attamimi, Deborah D Proctor, Jill K J Gaidos, Badr Al-Bawardy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Off-label high-dose upadacitinib (UPA) may be necessary for select inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients.Objectives:To evaluate the rates, patient characteristics, and outcomes of extended induction and high-dose UPA maintenance in IBD.Design:We conducted a two-center, retrospective cohort study of IBD patients treated with UPA from January 1, 2022, to March 1, 2025.Methods:Extended induction was defined as receiving UPA 45 mg for 16 weeks in ulcerative colitis (UC) and 24 weeks in Crohn’s disease (CD). High-dose maintenance was defined as continuing UPA 45 mg after induction/extended induction. Primary outcomes included rates and characteristics of high-dose maintenance. Secondary outcomes included clinical response (CR), steroid-free clinical remission (SFCR), endoscopic remission (ER), and adverse events, comparing standard and high-dose UPA groups.Results:Among 128 patients (46.9% CD, 48.4% UC, 4.7% IBD-unclassified), 51.6% (n = 66) received extended induction and 34.4% (n = 44) continued high-dose UPA maintenance. On multivariate analysis, UPA high-dose maintenance patients were more likely to have a higher number of prior biologic exposures (odds ratio (OR) 1.84, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.05–3.19, p = 0.022) and concomitant biologic use (OR 6.18, 95% CI 1.15–33.18, p = 0.034). In the high-dose maintenance group, rates of CR were 73.7%, SFCR 65.8%, and ER 36.7%. Adverse event rates were similar between high- and standard-dose maintenance groups (25% vs 26.8%), with no cases of herpes zoster, venous thromboembolism, or major adverse cardiovascular events.Conclusion:One-third of patients remained on high-dose UPA maintenance, reflecting more refractory disease and modest response rates. Treatment was generally well tolerated. Larger, long-term studies are needed to validate safety.Keywords: Crohn’s disease, inflammatory bowel disease, ulcerative colitis, upadacitinib

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease (CD), ulcerative colitis (UC), and IBD-unclassified (IBD-U), causes relapsing chronic inflammation and may be associated with intestinal damage if not diagnosed and treated early. The armamentarium of advanced therapies to treat IBD is rapidly expanding with the approval of multiple agents over the past 3–5 years.1,2 Despite the approval and introduction of multiple agents, a therapeutic ceiling continues to exist with current advanced therapies to manage IBD.3,4 It has been demonstrated that most patients in the real-world setting do not qualify for clinical trials due to a more severe disease phenotype. 5 These challenges have driven the real-world off-label dosing of existing therapies. The effectiveness of off-label maintenance dosing of biologics such as infliximab, vedolizumab, and ustekinumab have been extensively reported in real-world studies.6 –9 Data on dose escalation or off-label dosing of small molecules is mainly limited to tofacitinib. 10

Upadacitinib (UPA) is an oral selective Janus kinase (JAK) 1 inhibitor. UPA is approved for the induction and maintenance of remission in moderate-to-severe UC in 2022, and for moderate-to-severe CD in 2023. In patients with moderate-to-severe CD, UPA 45 mg daily for 12 weeks of induction, followed by UPA 30 or 15 mg daily maintenance dosing, was superior to placebo for the induction and maintenance of clinical remission and endoscopic response at week 52. 11 In moderate-to-severe UC, UPA 45 mg daily for 8 weeks of induction, followed by UPA 30 or 15 mg daily, was superior to placebo for the induction and maintenance of clinical remission compared to placebo. 12 Therefore, the recommended induction dosing of UPA is 45 mg daily for 8 weeks in UC and 12 weeks in CD. In a post hoc analysis of the pivotal trials, extended UPA induction with 45 mg for 16 weeks in moderate-to-severe UC resulted in a clinical response (CR) in 59.1% of patients who had not achieved a CR at the end of 8 weeks. 13

In a real-world setting, despite these recommendations, certain patients may not achieve a complete response after the induction period or may not maintain remission at the FDA-approved maintenance dose of UPA. There is a lack of data on the rates, characteristics, and outcomes of off-label high-dose UPA maintenance dosing in IBD. Thus, our study aims to evaluate the rates, patient characteristics, and outcomes of UPA extended induction and high-dose maintenance in IBD.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a multicenter, retrospective cohort study of patients with IBD treated with UPA from January 1, 2022, to March 1, 2025. We included data from two centers: Yale University, New Haven, CT, USA, and King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Center, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The study was approved by the respective institutional review boards (Yale IRB #2000035115; KFSH IRB #2231208). The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement (Supplemental Table 1). Patients were selected consecutively; all eligible patients at both centers who met inclusion criteria during the study period were included. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, as this was a retrospective cohort study; the sample represents the complete population of eligible patients treated at both institutions during the defined study period. We included patients with CD, UC, and IBD-U who completed induction treatment with UPA. We collected demographic information including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), disease duration, smoking status, as well as disease-related variables such as disease location, phenotype, and presence of extraintestinal manifestations. Other variables included prior medical therapies and surgeries. We also included baseline laboratory values such as C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin, and fecal calprotectin (FCP).

Patients

We included data from patients aged ⩾14 years with a confirmed diagnosis of CD, UC, or IBD-U. Pediatric patients ⩽18 years of age (range 14–17 years) were analyzed within the entire cohort and separately. Patients were included if they completed induction therapy with UPA (at least 8 weeks in UC and 12 weeks in CD) for active luminal disease. Prior to UPA initiation, patients underwent a standard pre-initiation workup, including screening for latent tuberculosis, hepatitis B and C serology, and baseline complete blood count (CBC) with differential, liver enzymes, and lipid profile. Patients were followed at weeks 4–8, 8–16, and 16–24 following UPA initiation with monitoring of CBC with differential, liver enzymes, and lipid profile, consistent with published UPA prescribing recommendations. 14 Extended UPA induction was defined as receiving UPA 45 mg for 16 weeks in UC and 24 weeks in CD. High-dose UPA maintenance was defined as continuing 45 mg of UPA post-induction/extended induction for both UC and CD. This includes patients who were escalated during maintenance from 15 or 30–45 mg or those that were maintained on UPA 45 mg post-induction/extended induction. The decision to proceed with extended UPA induction and high dose maintenance (including dose re-escalation to 45 mg) was based on any one of six treating IBD specialized physician’s global assessment of IBD control informed by a combination of clinical symptoms, inflammatory markers (CRP, FCP), endoscopy, and imaging findings. We excluded patients who did not have confirmed IBD, hospitalized for acute severe UC, or did not complete UPA induction.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes included: (1) frequency of extended induction and high-dose UPA maintenance and (2) patient characteristics associated with extended induction and high-dose versus standard-dose maintenance. Secondary outcomes were rates of adverse events; CR as per the treating physician global assessment; steroid-free clinical remission (SFCR) defined as an absence of clinical symptoms (incorporating resolution of key symptoms including diarrhea, rectal bleeding, and abdominal pain as applicable to disease type) off steroids; endoscopic remission (ER) defined as a Mayo endoscopic subscore of 0 or 1 for UC or simple endoscopic score <4 or an absence of ulcers or erosions for CD; comparing standard versus high-dose UPA maintenance groups. These clinical outcomes were assessed after at least 6 months of UPA treatment.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included percentages that were used for categorical data, while mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile ranges (IQRs) were used for describing continuous data. To examine the differences between the groups, the Pearson Chi-square test was used for categorical data, while the Wilcoxon rank sum was used for continuous variables. Multivariate analysis was utilized to assess the characteristic differences between the extended and standard induction groups, in addition to evaluating the differences between the high-dose versus standard-dose maintenance groups. Variables with a p-value <0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. Results were reported as frequencies and odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Data analysis was performed using JMP® (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) statistical software.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the entire cohort

A total of 128 patients were included in this study. In terms of IBD subtype, 46.9% (n = 60) of patients had CD (20.3% with perianal involvement), 48.4% (n = 62) with UC, and 4.7% (n = 6) had IBD-U. The median age was 34 years (IQR: 25–43.8) and 60.9% (n = 78) were male. The median disease duration was 7.5 years (IQR: 4–12), and 97.7% (n = 125) were exposed to biologic therapies in the past (Table 1). The median duration of UPA therapy was 51 weeks (IQR 27.3–69.8). Sixteen patients (12.5%) were on concomitant biologic therapy, defined as concurrent use of UPA with another biologic agent simultaneously. Of the 16 patients on concomitant biologic therapy, the most commonly co-administered agent was ustekinumab in 50% (n = 8), followed by vedolizumab in 31.2% (n = 5), and risankizumab in 18.8% (n = 3). UPA induction characteristics

All patients received UPA 45 mg for induction except one patient received 30-mg induction dose due to chronic kidney disease. A total of 62 patients (48.4%) received standard UPA induction duration and 66 patients (51.6%) received extended UPA induction. In the CD cohort, 63.3% (n = 38) required extended induction versus 37.1% (n = 23) in the UC cohort (p = 0.004). Patients who received extended UPA induction were more likely to have a previous history of intestinal surgery (33.3% (n = 22) vs 14.5% (n = 9); p = 0.013) and to be on concomitant biologic therapy (18.2% (n = 12) vs 6.5% (n = 4); p = 0.04) compared to the cohort who was on standard UPA induction (Table 2). There were no significant differences between the induction groups in terms of median baseline labs including CRP, FCP, and albumin (Table 2).

UPA maintenance characteristics

A total of 44 patients (34.4%) remained at a 45-mg maintenance dose, 78 (60.9%) were on a standard 30-mg maintenance UPA dose, 4 (3.1%) were on a 15-mg maintenance dose, and 2 (1.6%) patients stopped UPA during induction at the end of the follow-up period. In the high-dose maintenance group, 63.6% (n = 28) had received extended UPA induction dosing. A total of 30/44 (68.2%) patients in the high-dose maintenance group (14 CD, 15 UC, 1 IBD-U) developed active disease per clinician assessment after dose reduction to 30 mg and hence were escalated back to the 45 mg dose. The remaining 14/44 (31.8%) of patients did not attempt UPA dose reduction below 45 mg during the maintenance phase. In the CD cohort, high-dose maintenance was needed in 67.2% (n = 39) versus 61.3% (n = 38) in the UC cohort (p = 0.49). On univariable analysis, patients in the high-dose UPA maintenance group were more likely to have perianal CD (63.2% (n = 12) vs 34.2% (n = 14); p = 0.03), have a higher median number of previous biologic therapy (3 (IQR 2–4) vs 2 (IQR 1–3); p = 0.0002), have previous tofacitinib exposure (25% (n = 11) vs 10.9% (n = 9); p = 0.04), and be on concomitant biologic therapy (22.7% (n = 10) vs 7.3% (n = 6); p = 0.04; Table 2). There were no significant differences between the induction groups in terms of median baseline labs including CRP, FCP, and albumin (Table 2). On multivariate analysis, a higher number of prior biologic exposure (OR 1.84, 95% CI 1.05–3.19; p = 0.022) and concomitant biologic use (OR 6.18, 95% CI 1.15–33.18, p = 0.034) were independently associated with high-dose UPA maintenance (Table 3).

IBD outcomes in the entire cohort

At the last recorded clinic follow-up, the rate of CR was 75.8% (n = 97), and the rate of SFCR in the entire cohort was 71.9% (n = 92). Data on repeat colonoscopy after UPA initiation were available in 60.2% (n = 77) with a median time of 36 weeks (IQR 26.5–50) from UPA initiation to colonoscopy. ER was noted in 54.5% (n = 42/77) of patients post UPA therapy. In the CD cohort (n = 60), CR was achieved in 76.7% (n = 46) and SFCR in 76.7% (n = 46). In the UC cohort (n = 62), CR was achieved in 74.2% (n = 46) and SFCR in 64.5% (n = 40). In the IBD-U cohort (n = 6), CR was achieved in 83.3% (n = 5) and SFCR in 100% (n = 6). Furthermore, 46.7% (n = 14/30) of patients with CD achieved ER, 58.5% (n = 24/41) of UC patients achieved ER, and 66.7% (n = 4/6) of patients with IBD-U achieved ER.

High-dose UPA maintenance outcomes

In the high-dose maintenance group, CR was achieved in 73.7% (n = 32/44) and SFCR was achieved in 65.8% (n = 29/44). ER was achieved in only 36.7% (n = 11/30) of patients in the high-dose maintenance group. Among the 11 tofacitinib-exposed patients on high-dose UPA maintenance, CR and SFCR were each achieved in 63.6% (n = 7/11), and ER was achieved in 60.0% (n = 3/5) of those with available endoscopic data. In the cohort of patients who required re-escalation of UPA to 45 mg because of disease flare following dose reduction to 30 mg (n = 30), CR and SFCR were achieved in 70% (n = 21) and 66.7% (n = 20), respectively. Endoscopic assessment was available for 22 of these patients, among whom ER was observed in 22.7% (n = 5). Of the 30 patients who required re-escalation to UPA 45 mg, 46.7% (n = 14) had CD, 50.0% (n = 15) had UC, and 3.3% (n = 1) had IBD-U. In the CD subgroup, CR and SFCR were achieved in 84.6% (n = 11/13) and 84.6% (n = 11/13) of those with available data, respectively, and ER in 20.0% (n = 2/10). In the UC subgroup, CR and SFCR were achieved in 69.2% (n = 9/13) and 61.5% (n = 8/13) of those with available data, respectively, and ER in 27.3% (n = 3/11). The single IBD-U patient achieved CR and SFCR but did not achieve ER.

Adverse events and discontinuation

Adverse events occurred in 26.2% (n = 33) in the entire cohort. The most common adverse events were dyslipidemia in 11.1% (n = 14) and acne in 11.1% (n = 14). There were no significant differences in adverse events between the high-dose and standard-dose UPA maintenance groups (Table 4). There were no venous thromboembolism, herpes zoster infections, or major adverse cardiovascular events reported in the entire cohort. Receiving at least one dose of the zoster vaccine was noted in 64.8% (n = 83) of the cohort. Of the 16 patients (12.5%) on concomitant biologic therapy, 31.3% (n = 5) experienced adverse events, including acne in 12.5% (n = 2), leukopenia in 6.3% (n = 1), hypertension/dizziness in 6.3% (n = 1), and unspecified adverse event in 6.3% (n = 1). Notably, 80% (n = 4) of those who experienced adverse events in this subgroup were on high-dose UPA maintenance.

The median follow-up time for the entire cohort was 56.5 weeks (IQR 33.5–79.8). The median follow-up time was 62.5 weeks (IQR 36.3–80.8) in the high-dose maintenance group versus 54.5 weeks (IQR 32–78.8) in the standard-dose maintenance group. At the end of the follow-up period, 16.4% (n = 21) discontinued UPA. Reasons for discontinuation of UPA included lack of response in 12 patients, adverse events in 4 patients (two with acne, one due to elevated liver tests, and one due to tachycardia), pregnancy planning in 4 patients, and renal malignancy in 1 patient. Of the 44 patients in the high-dose UPA maintenance group, 13.6% (n = 6) discontinued therapy during the follow-up period. Reasons for discontinuation included lack of effectiveness in 66.7% (n = 4) and pregnancy planning in 33.3% (n = 2). Notably, no patients in the high-dose maintenance group discontinued due to adverse events.

IBD subtype

In the CD cohort, 63.3% (n = 38) received extended UPA induction and 32% (n = 19) received high-dose UPA maintenance. On univariate analysis, high-dose UPA maintenance in CD was associated with elevated baseline CRP (p = 0.04), higher number of previous biologic therapies (p = 0.03), previous tofacitinib exposure (p = 0.04), and combination biologic therapy (p = 0.005; Supplemental Table 2). In the UC cohort, 37.1% (n = 23) received extended UPA induction and 38.7% (n = 24) received high-dose UPA maintenance. On univariate analysis, high-dose UPA maintenance in UC was associated with receiving extended UPA induction (p = 0.006), and a higher number of previous biologic therapies (n = 0.004; Supplemental Table 4). Results of multivariable analysis are shown in Supplemental Tables 3 and 5.

Pediatric population

A total of six patients were ⩽18 years of age (range 14–17 years). In this age group, 50% (n = 3) had CD and 66.7% (n = 4) were female. All received the 45-mg induction dose except 1 patient received the 30-mg dose for induction. UPA extended induction with 45 mg was needed in 33.3% (n = 2), while high-dose UPA maintenance was needed in 66.7% (n = 4). In those who were receiving high-dose maintenance, one patient had re-escalation to 45 mg after a flare, while others continued high-dose maintained post induction. The median duration of UPA therapy was 29 months (IQR: 16.8–57.3). CR and SFCR were noted in 60% (two of five patients), and ER was achieved in two out three patients with available endoscopic data. One adverse event was reported, which was transient leukopenia and did not require drug discontinuation.

Discussion

In this study, we found that 51.6% of IBD patients required extended UPA induction, and 34.4% required high-dose maintenance therapy. Extended induction was more common in patients with prior IBD-related surgeries and those on concomitant biologics, likely reflecting a more severe disease phenotype. In addition, we have noted that a higher number of previous biologic use and use of concomitant biologic therapy are significantly associated with high-dose UPA maintenance therapy. We did not detect any significantly higher rates of safety events in patients receiving high-dose UPA maintenance compared to those receiving standard-dose UPA maintenance in our cohort. It is important to note that our data are limited by a relatively small sample size and short follow-up period.

In the pivotal UC clinical trial programs (U-ACHIEVE and U-ACCOMPLISH), patients who did not achieve CR at week 8 were eligible for open-label extended induction with UPA 45 mg for an additional 8 weeks. 12 Post hoc analysis demonstrated that 19.2% of patients with UC did not achieve CR at week 8. 13 In our study, the rate of extended UPA dose induction in UC was 37.1%, which is higher than the rate reported in the pivotal clinical trial, likely due to a more refractory patient population in our cohort. In our cohort, 97.7% had previously been exposed to biologic therapies (median number of 2 (IQR, 2–3)). In our cohort, extended UPA induction was noted in a higher proportion of CD compared to UC patients. The higher use of extended UPA induction in CD compared with UC likely reflects a more refractory disease course in our CD. CD patients had a longer median disease duration (9 years (IQR 5–14.8) vs 7 years (IQR 4–12) in UC), were previously exposed to a higher number of biologic agents (median 3 prior biologics (IQR 2–4) vs 2 (IQR 1–3) in UC), and had a higher baseline CRP (median CRP 12.7 mg/L (IQR 4.1–52.4) vs 4 mg/L (IQR 1.3–14.4) in UC).

In our cohort, 30/128 patients (23.4%) were dose escalated back to 45 mg due to active disease when on standard-dose UPA maintenance. Similarly, in a single-center cohort of 135 patients with CD on UPA, 17% required dose escalation during follow-up (7% were escalated from 30 to 45 mg). 15 Our recapture rate of 70% following re-escalation to UPA 45 mg is consistent with recently published real-world data by Ho et al., who reported a recapture rate of 80.4% in IBD patients who lost response after dose reduction to lower maintenance doses. Notably, Ho et al. 16 also demonstrated that 93.8% of patients who continued UPA 45 mg as maintenance did not relapse, further supporting the effectiveness of sustained high-dose UPA in this subgroup. Outcomes of dose reduction in UPA treated patients with IBD remains limited but more data are available for tofacitinib, a non-selective JAK inhibitor, in the treatment of UC. The RIVETING study randomized 140 patients with UC in remission on tofacitinib 10 mg BID to either continued high-dose or reduced dose of 5 mg po BID. At 6 months, a smaller proportion of patients in the 5 mg po BID remained in remission compared to the 10 mg po BID (77.1% vs 90%). 17 This indicates that dose reduction of JAK inhibitors in IBD may be associated with disease flare in a certain subset of patients.

We identified multiple factors that are associated with being on high-dose UPA maintenance. Having a higher number of previous advanced therapy exposure (OR 1.84) and concomitant biologic therapy (OR 6.18) were identified as significantly associated with high-dose UPA maintenance on multivariable analysis. Notably, prior failure of ⩾2 biologics and concomitant biologic use were associated with the need for high-dose maintenance. In the RIVETING study, the delta in maintenance of remission was higher in the anti-TNF exposed group between the tofacitinib 10 mg po BID and tofacitinib 5 mg po BID compared to the anti-TNF naïve group (17.4% vs 9.5%). This aligns with findings from the U-ACHIEVE trial, which showed that in UC patients with prior biologic failure, higher rates of remission were seen at higher UPA maintenance dose of 30 mg compared to 15 mg. 11 Similarly, the U-ENDURE trial demonstrated a dose–response relationship between the 30 and 15 mg groups in CD, particularly among patients with previous biologic failure. 10 This is likely reflective of a more refractory or challenging-to-treat IBD phenotype.

Adverse events were noted in 26.2% of the entire cohort receiving UPA therapy. A single-center, real-world experience of 105 UPA treated patients with IBD demonstrated a similar adverse event rate of 32.4%, with acne being the most common adverse event. 18 The rate of UPA discontinuation due to adverse events in that study was 5.7% compared to 3.1% in our cohort. A larger, multicenter cohort of 328 patients with CD treated with UPA demonstrated a discontinuation rate due to adverse events in 4.3% similar to our population. 19

Adverse events such as herpes zoster are dose-dependent and more likely to occur with higher maintenance dosing.11,12 In our study, patients receiving high-dose UPA maintenance therapy showed no significant differences in adverse events compared to those on standard maintenance doses. Particularly, there were no reported events of herpes zoster. Herpes zoster has been reported in the pivotal phase III maintenance trials of UPA in both CD (U-ENDURE) and UC (U-ACHIEVE); however, the absolute risk observed in these trials was low, with exposure-adjusted incidence rates of 7.2 per 100 patient-years in CD and 4.4 per 100 patient-years in UC for the 30-mg maintenance dose.11,12 Importantly, herpes zoster vaccination was not mandated in these clinical trials. In contrast, 64.8% of patients in our real-world cohort had received herpes zoster vaccination, which may explain the lower observed incidence in our study. In the general population, recombinant herpes zoster vaccine (RZV) demonstrated a 92.7% effectiveness rate in preventing HZ infection in adults aged ⩾50 years. 20 In patients with IBD on immunosuppression, RZV significantly reduces the risk of HZ (adjusted OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.38–0.97) as demonstrated in a large propensity matched cohort. 21 Additionally, our cohort size was smaller than that of the pivotal trials, and the duration of follow-up was relatively limited, which may further restrict the ability to fully characterize the risk of infrequent adverse events. In our cohort, the most common adverse event was acne affecting 11.1%, which is higher than what has been reported in the pivotal clinical trials.11,12 However, real-world studies of UPA in IBD have demonstrated a variable rate of acne, with a range from 6% to 22.9% in which our cohort falls within.15,18,19

Our study highlights the prevalence, characteristics, and outcomes of high-dose UPA maintenance in IBD. The strengths of our study include a multicenter cohort with multinational representation and detailed characterization of patients included. This study has several limitations. Although multicenter, the sample size of 128 patients remains relatively low and limits further subgroup analyses (including those by IBD subtype) or definitive assessment of treatment safety. The retrospective study design limits data completeness, which depends on clinical documentation, resulting in some missing laboratory and outcome data as shown in the results tables. CR and SFCR were defined based on physician’s global assessment and absence of clinical symptoms, rather than validated scoring indices, as prospective capture of standardized scores was not uniformly feasible in this retrospective cohort. While this approach is consistent with methodology employed in comparable real-world UPA studies, reliance on physician’s global assessment introduces an element of subjectivity and inter-observer variability that may affect the precision and reproducibility of outcome ascertainment.19,22 In addition, the decision to pursue extended induction or high dose maintenance was based on the physician’s global assessment of IBD control which may introduce selection bias. Furthermore, formal assessment of perianal fistula outcomes was not possible due to the absence of prespecified standardized perianal fistula outcome measures and incomplete documentation of fistula-specific endpoints, which is an inherent limitation of the retrospective study design. Finally, the heterogeneity in disease subtypes (CD, UC, IBD-U) may affect generalizability.

Conclusion

In conclusion, extended UPA induction and high-dose UPA maintenance therapy was common in this real-world cohort. High-dose UPA maintenance may have been utilized more often in patients with severe or refractory disease, as reflected by the number of previous advanced therapies exposure. Larger, prospective studies are needed to evaluate the long-term benefits and risks of extended induction and high-dose UPA maintenance in IBD.
