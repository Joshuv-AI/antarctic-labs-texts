Design and characterization of visually opaque but near-infrared transparent coatings for better barcode readability
Authors: Ravi Arukula, Taehyun Kim, Songwei Lu, Xiaoning Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoatings that are visually opaque yet transparent in the near-infrared (NIR) spectrum are desirable for secure identification and thermal management. In this study, we investigated a bilayer coating system comprising a clear sealer coat and a topcoat that is visually opaque but NIR transparent (NIRT) in three color variations: black, green, and tan. Using the coating system, we successfully concealed the encoded information (barcodes printed on synthetic paper sheets) in the visible spectrum while maintaining sufficient barcode readability (measured by delta gray values (∆GVs)) in the NIR spectrum. Among all coatings, the black NIRT coatings showed better performance, providing the highest NIR transmittance, sharp barcode edges, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) (9.4 dB), and reliable readability up to 15.2 m (50 ft). The green and tan coatings also performed well, though adding white pigments slightly reduced contrast, readability, and SNR. To further optimize the barcode readability, we investigated the effects of two categories of factors: (1) materials-related factors (i.e., pigment type, pigment loading, additive selection, and particle size distribution), and (2) process-related factors (i.e., dispersion methods, blade type, mixing time, and dry film thickness). It was found that the average particle size strongly correlates with the final coating NIR transmittance and barcode readability. Specifically, coatings with an average particle size of 566.3 nm have the highest NIR transmittance and barcode readability. Larger average particle size (708.7 nm) results in coatings with moderate NIR transmittance and barcode readability. When the particle size distribution is bimodal (346.1 nm and 1845.7 nm), coatings’ NIR transmittance and barcode readability are greatly reduced. These findings are consistent with Mie scattering theory. Large and broadly distributed particles around 750 nm scatter more incident NIR light and reduce barcode readability. In contrast, small and narrowly distributed particles scatter less, therefore enhancing both NIR transmittance and barcode readability. The bilayer coating system was also tested for solvent resistance, mandrel bend flexibility, and UV stability, with results demonstrating its suitability for field application. Overall, through analyzing key factors governing the balance between visual opacity, NIR transmittance, and barcode readability, we establish a feasible material-process-performance framework for designing visually opaque and NIR coatings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55240-2.Keywords: NIR-transparent coatings, Visually opaque coatings, Barcode concealment, Pigment formulation, Optical performance, Mie scattering, Rapid-curing coatings, Barcode readabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Autonomous and security systems are increasingly dependent on machine-readable identifiers to support navigation, authentication, personal thermal management, and logistics across civilian, industrial, and defense domains1–4. Barcodes and related optical tags remain attractive for these functions due to their low cost, durability, and compatibility with automated scanning platforms. However, their visibility in the human-visible spectrum poses challenges in scenarios where concealment or steganography is required. For applications in secure identification, aerospace documentation, and robotic navigation, it is critical to develop materials that conceal barcodes from the naked eye while preserving their readability by NIR sensing devices5–9. Wavelength-selective coatings provide a pathway by achieving opacity in the visible spectrum while maintaining transparency in the NIR range. Such coatings enable covert communication and hidden signaling without the need for specialized hardware or algorithmic solutions, allowing straightforward integration with existing scanner systems10,11. While prior studies have investigated pigment engineering and multilayer structures for thermal management or LiDAR reflectivity, relatively few have systematically examined coatings optimized for hidden barcode readability12,13.

Much of the prior research in wavelength-selective coatings has been motivated by two industrial domains: (i) “cool coatings,” designed to reflect IR radiation to reduce heat gain in buildings14, and (ii) LiDAR-compatible automotive coatings that improve vehicle detectability at ~ 905 nm15. Conventional approaches depend on pigment engineering, tuning absorption and scattering to control spectral selectivity. Coatings with inorganic pigments such as Mg-doped ZnFeO₄, Al₂O₃-doped CoFe₂O₄, and Ce₁₋ₓGdₓVO₄ have moderate to high NIR reflectance (25–70 R%), but often with undesired increase of lightness values (L*), reduction of blackness, or heavy-metal content that raises environmental concerns16–19. Organic pigments, such as perylene derivatives, provide strong visible absorption with relatively high NIR transparency; however, coatings incorporating them often require multilayer designs or face stability limitations that increase cost and complexity20,21.

To overcome these limitations, recent studies have explored nanostructured and porous materials capable of decoupling visible light absorption from NIR transmittance. For instance, nanoporous polyethylene (NanoPE) scatters visible light while transmitting more than 85% of NIR radiation, enabling radiative cooling textiles and covert identifiers22. To further advance textile applications, colored IR-transparent fibers have been developed by embedding low-NIR-absorption pigments into polyethylene, producing vivid colors without compromising IR transparency23. In addition, SiO₂/black TiO₂ core-shell nanoparticles demonstrate another strategy, achieving specified black color (L* ≈ 11.4) while maintaining significant NIR reflectance through refractive-index contrast and shell porosity, making them highly effective for LiDAR detection24. With the development of this approach using nanostructured pigments, several recent advancements in coatings achieve visible opacity and NIR transparency. For example, Choi et al.12 designed colloidal photonic crystals within a carbon-black-filled resin that block visible light yet reflect strongly in NIR, delivering durable, thermally stable, and non-toxic films optimized for LiDAR detection. Similarly, wavelength-selective hard coatings fabricated through reactive sputtering deliver high visible/NIR selectivity, up to a 7:1 reflectance ratio, along with excellent scratch resistance and industrial manufacturability25. Notably, Yamamiya et al13. demonstrated that pigment composition and film thickness strongly affect barcode concealment and NIR readability, highlighting the potential of coatings formulated with black pigments that combine strong visible absorption and high NIR transmission. Nevertheless, these approaches for wavelength-selective functionality often require complex synthesis26,27, rigid substrates, or processing routes that limit their integration into flexible and printable substrates.

Teslin®, a microporous polyolefin synthetic paper, is a promising substrate for flexible barcode applications due to its chemical resistance, mechanical robustness, and compatibility with high-resolution printing28–30. However, its highly porous and flexible nature poses additional challenges for developing NIRT coatings, where achieving a balance between visible opacity, NIR transparency, and coating integrity is particularly demanding. In addition, the interaction of these coatings with printed barcodes on Teslin has not been fully explored, especially regarding scanner readability.

In this work, we address this gap by designing and characterizing a bilayer coating system consisting of a clear sealer coat and a topcoat in three color variations, black, green, and tan, applied to Teslin substrates with printed barcodes. The coatings are designed to provide visual concealment while maintaining NIR transparency for reliable scanner recognition. Achieving this dual functionality requires a precise balance between visible opacity (400–700 nm) and NIR transmittance (700–2500 nm)31,32. To achieve this objective, we prepared topcoats using different pigments, loadings, and additives, and tested various processing factors, including dispersion methods, mixing time, and film thickness. The effects of pigment particle size and distribution were also studied to understand how they control light scattering and influence barcode readability. It was observed that coatings with smaller and more uniform pigment particles (~ 566 nm) showed the highest NIR transmittance and barcode readability, whereas larger or bimodal particle distributions led to increased scattering and reduced performance. Among all tested samples, the black coatings (BF) showed the relatively best performance, offering the highest NIR transmittance, sharp barcode edges, and reliable readability at distances up to 15.2 m. The green (GF) and tan (TF) coatings also performed well, though their readability was slightly lower due to added white pigments for color adjustment, which reduced contrast and increased light scattering. Overall, this integrated material-process-performance approach provides a practical framework for designing coatings that are visually opaque but transparent under NIR light, emphasizing the importance of controlling pigment size to balance appearance and function.

Materials and methods

Materials

Black pigment selection was guided by their UV–vis-NIR transmittance properties: black pigment 1 exhibited high transmittance in the NIR range while remaining opaque in the visible region33, whereas black pigment 2 strongly absorbed NIR light (Figure S1, Supplementary Information). Additional formulation components included an adhesion promoter, UV stabilizer, UV absorber, matting agent, surface tension reducer, dispersive additive, and dibutyltin dilaurate (DBTL) catalyst. For high-speed mixing, a CM-100 high-speed disperser (maximum 10,000 rpm; D.H. Melton Co., Tempe, AZ, USA) and a Misonix S-4000 ultrasonicator (Misonix Inc., Farmingdale, NY, USA) were used. For comparative evaluation of NIR transparency, a commercial black spray paint (“Magic Black,” Anytime Sign, Mammoth Spring, AR, USA) was tested.

Formulation strategy for visually opaque, NIRT coatings

The primary objective of the reported work is to conceal the underlying barcodes from human vision while maintaining sufficient NIR transmittance for precise barcode scanner readability (Fig. 1). Black pigments were first selected based on their NIR transmittance characteristics, ensuring high NIR transparency while maintaining visible light absorption. The selected pigments were then dispersed in a moisture-curable resin using different dispersion methods, low shear mixing with grinding medium (LS), ultrasonication (US), and high-speed mixing (HS), to achieve uniform particle distribution and consistent film formation. For the tan coatings, the addition of white pigment presented a challenge due to its strong light-scattering tendency, which can reduce NIR transmittance. To address this, optimized pigment ratios and dispersion conditions were employed to balance visible light hiding power with NIR transparency. Similarly, the green coatings formulation required careful adjustment of pigment combinations to maintain color accuracy while enhancing NIR transmittance. Appropriate dispersion techniques and pigment loadings were selected to minimize scattering losses and achieve the desired optical balance. Through this systematic approach, three optimized coatings formulations, black, green, and tan, were developed that effectively block visible light while allowing the transmission of NIR radiation.

Coatings formulation processes

All coated samples consist of two layers of coatings: a clear sealer to prevent ink and paint bleeding caused by the porous Teslin substrates, and a topcoat to provide optical functionality and desired color and appearance. All coating formulas were prepared using the same base resin, initially diluted with 25% solvent. For all coatings, weight percentages are based on the total formula (100 wt%).

Clear sealer formulation

The clear sealer consisted of 65% resin, 25% solvent, and 10% additives (adhesion promoter, UV stabilizer, and UV absorber). The additives were gradually introduced into the diluted base resin under continuous stirring. The resulting mixture was then agitated for an additional 10 min at 600 rpm to ensure homogeneity.

Black topcoat

The black topcoat (BF) consisted of 40.5% resin, 50% solvent, 1.4% NIRT black pigment, and 8% additives (matting agent, adhesion promoter, UV stabilizer, and UV absorber). The total solvent content (50%) was added in two batches: 25% of the solvent was initially present in the diluted base resin, and an additional 25% of the solvent was added during the formulation process. The black topcoat was prepared by incorporating the NIRT black pigment into the diluted resin under agitation with alumina grinding media at 600 rpm for 90 min. Subsequently, the additives, matting agent, and additional solvent were introduced under continuous stirring for 10 min at 600 rpm. The rapid-curing version of this formulation (BF-RC) contained 1% DBTL catalyst. The preparation procedure for BF-RC was the same as that used for BF.

Green topcoat

The green topcoat formula (GF1, GF2, and GF3) consisted of 34% resin, 50% solvent, 10% NIRT pigments, and 6% additives (matting agent, adhesion promoter, UV stabilizer, and UV absorber). The solvent was added in two batches, in the same way as for the BF formula. Three green topcoat variants (GF1-GF3) were achieved by using different white pigments at various loadings to optimize the target color and optical performance, with GF1 containing 1.9% white pigment 1, GF2 8.6% white pigment 2, and GF3 0.5% white pigment 1. Each green coating was prepared by incorporating a blend of yellow, black, and white pigments (in ratios adjusted to achieve the desired green color) into the diluted resin. The mixture was mixed with alumina grinding media under shear at 600 rpm for 90 min. Following complete pigment dispersion, the additives, matting agent, and additional solvent were added under continuous stirring at 600 rpm for 10 min. The rapid-curing version of the GF3 formulation (GF-RC) contained 1% DBTL catalyst. The preparation procedure for GF3-RC was the same as that used for GF3.

Tan topcoat

The tan topcoat formulas (TF1, TF2, and TF3) consisted of 40% resin, 41% solvent, 11% NIRT pigments, and 8% additives (matting agent, adhesion promoter, UV stabilizer, and UV absorber). The solvent was added in two batches, following the same procedure as for the BF formulation. Three tan topcoat variants (TF1-TF3) were formulated with different white pigment 1 loadings to achieve the target color and optical performance: TF1 containing 4.04% white pigment 1, TF2 7.91% white pigment 1, and TF3 9.93% white pigment 1 with an additional dispersive additive to enhance pigment wetting and dispersion. For each formula, the white pigment (and dispersive additive for TF3) was incorporated into the base resin solution and mixed under shear at 2,500 rpm for 70 min. Subsequently, red, yellow, orange, and purple pigments were added in fixed ratios, along with additional white pigment, and the mixture was continuously stirred at 1,800 rpm for 70 min. After complete pigment dispersion, the additives, matting agent, and additional solvent (16%) were added under stirring at 1000 rpm for 10 min. The rapid-curing version of TF3 (TF-RC) contained 1% DBTL catalyst. The preparation procedure for TF-RC was the same as that used for TF3. Coatings application methods

Coatings were applied to Teslin barcode substrates using three techniques: spray, aerosol, and brush applications. For spray application, a Finishline DeVilbiss FLG4 HVLP spray gun (DeVilbiss Automotive Refinishing, Scottsdale, AZ, USA) equipped with an FLGp-1-3 air cap and a 1.8 mm fluid tip was used. Coatings were applied at 10–15 psi at the gun with a supplied air pressure of 55 psi. A PPS disposable paint cup (3 M, Saint Paul, MN, USA) was used to contain the formulation. Aerosol application was performed with a Preval sprayer (Nakoma Products, Bridgeview, IL, USA) using butane-propane as the propellant; the formulation was loaded into the glass jar supplied with the kit. Brush application was conducted manually with a 2-inch paintbrush obtained from a local hardware supplier.

For spectroscopic characterization, glass substrates were coated using the HVLP spray gun under the same conditions. Regardless of the application method, the dry film thickness was maintained at ~ 109.22 μm (standard deviation, ± 17.78 μm).

Substrate preparation and curing conditions

Barcodes were printed directly onto the Teslin substrates using an HP® DeskJet 2855e printer with HP 67 black ink (HP Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). Before applying the NIRT topcoat, a clear sealer was spray-applied onto the Teslin substrates. The clear sealer was allowed to dry for a minimum of 30 min. Subsequently, all topcoats were cured at room temperature for a minimum of 24 h before any characterization was performed. All spraying and curing procedures were carried out in a controlled spray room maintained at 23 ± 1 °C and 20 ± 5% relative humidity.

Characterization and performance evaluation

Key coating performance metrics are NIR transmittance and barcode readability. The NIR transmittance was evaluated using UV–vis-NIR transmittance measurements to verify selective light transmission. Photographic and infrared imaging, supported by quantitative analysis of gray value, sharpness, and contrast, was used to validate the barcode readability. Additionally, the coatings are expected to be exposed to outdoor environments; hence, stability under UV irradiation, chemical exposure, and solvent resistance is evaluated to ensure long-term durability and retention of optical properties under environmental and mechanical stresses.

Gray value differences (ΔGV) between the barcode and background regions were used as the key parameter to quantify barcode readability and evaluate image sharpness. The 8-bit grayscale infrared images were processed and analyzed using the Fiji open-source image analysis software (Version 20260506-1117, https://www.imagej.net) (see supplementary information for details, Figure S2 and Figure S3)34. The contrast between barcode regions was quantified using ΔGV, defined as the difference between the mean gray values of the dark and light regions. To further evaluate edge sharpness, gray-value distributions across barcode edges were extracted by line profiling, and the first derivative of these profiles was calculated in OriginPro (Version 9.5, OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). The magnitude of the derivative peaks, normalized to account for variations in barcode width, was used as the sharpness metric. This method, widely applied in image processing, enables an objective and reproducible evaluation of infrared transparency and barcode readability35,36.

The SNR was used as another quantitative metric to evaluate image quality, based on the definition by Gonzalez and Woods37. SNR was calculated by comparing the signal from a reference image and the corresponding test images. In this study, the reference image corresponds to the uncoated substrate, while the test images represent various conditions, including IR images acquired at different distances, dispersion methods, and coating colors. SNR analysis was performed using the SNR plugin developed by EPFL in conjunction with Fiji software38. Higher SNR values indicate improved image quality, reflecting reduced noise and better preservation of image sharpness and barcode readability.

UV–vis-NIR transmittance spectra of coatings on microscope slides were recorded using a Cary 500 UV–vis-NIR spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) to evaluate the optical transparency of coating samples across ultraviolet, visible, and NIR wavelengths (250–2500 nm).

Particle size distributions were measured with a Nicomp 380 particle size analyzer (Particle Sizing Systems, Santa Barbara, CA, USA). Surface roughness was characterized using a Keyence VK-3000 3D surface profiler (Keyence Corporation, Osaka, Japan), which employs laser confocal microscopy to generate high-resolution topographical data. Measurements were conducted over a 1 × 1 mm area, and 3D maps were processed using the instrument’s software.

Two types of images of coated substrates were obtained. Regular images were captured using a Nikon D3000 digital camera (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an 18–55 mm f/3.5-5.6G VR lens. Images were recorded in RAW format (3872 × 2592 pixels) under controlled D65 illumination in an X-Rite Gretag Spectralight III chamber (X-Rite, Grand Rapids, MI, USA) to ensure color accuracy. The camera was operated in manual mode with an aperture of f/5.6, a shutter speed of 1/13 s, and a fixed distance of 30.5 cm to maintain consistent magnification across all samples.

IR images were obtained using a Nikon Z5 camera (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) modified for infrared capture by Life Pixel Infrared (Mukilteo, WA, USA) and equipped with a 50 mm f/2.8 MC lens. Images were acquired at a resolution of 6016 × 4016 pixels in JPEG format under controlled D65 illumination. The camera was set to manual mode (f/3.5, 1/15 s) with samples placed at a fixed distance of 30.5 cm for consistency. Additional IR images were recorded outdoors under natural sunlight.

Barcode readability was evaluated using proprietary Decoding and Capture Software v1.0 from HD Barcode LLC (https://automation.omron.com/en/us/products/family/VF9510)39. During evaluation, imaging was performed using an AGM Infrared Android Camera and Phone (AGM G3 PRO thermal imaging rugged smartphone) mounted on a mobile stand to ensure stable positioning and consistent imaging geometry, thereby minimizing experimental variability across measurements.

Accelerated weathering tests were conducted in a QUV chamber (Q-Lab Corporation, Baltimore, MA, USA) following ASTM G154. Coated Teslin substrates were exposed to alternating cycles of UVB irradiation (0.71 W/m² at 60 °C, 4 h) and condensation (50 °C, 4 h) for 28 days.

Coating color was measured using a BYK Spectro2guide colorimeter (BYK-Gardner GmbH, Geretsried, Germany) under standardized D65 illumination (10° viewing angle). Color coordinates (CIE L*, a*, b*) were recorded before and after accelerated weathering tests. Viscosity measurements were conducted with a CAP 2000 viscometer (AMETEK Brookfield, Middleborough, MA, USA) following ASTM D4287. A CAP07 cone spindle was used at 50 rpm under room temperature conditions.

Water and solvent resistance were evaluated using coated Teslin samples immersed to at least 50% depth in distilled water or common solvents (acetone, methyl ethyl ketone (MEK), toluene, and xylene) at room temperature for 168 h. Film integrity was assessed by pencil hardness testing (Staedtler Mars Lumograph 100, ASTM D3363) and visual inspection. Coatings were considered to pass if the hardness loss was less than two grades and no blistering, discoloration, or defects were observed. Acid resistance was assessed by applying 3–5 mL of 10% v/v acetic acid solution onto coated samples for 1 h. After rinsing with distilled water, coatings were evaluated for blistering, discoloration, or surface defects. Samples showing no visible damage were considered resistant.

Mandrel bend flexibility was evaluated using an Elcometer 1510 (Elcometer, Warren, MI, USA). Coated samples were bent over the mandrel, and surfaces were inspected for physical damage. Adhesion to the substrate was evaluated following ASTM D3359. Three replicates were performed to ensure reproducibility, with all coatings achieving the highest adhesion rating (5B).

Drying time of the coated samples was measured by following ASTM D1640 to determine the time required for coatings to reach specific drying stages under room temperature with controlled humidity (25–35% RH). Drying stages, including set-to-touch, dry-to-touch, dry-hard, and dry-to-handle times, were assessed by observing the coatings’ response to the finger.

Results and discussion

Factors affecting NIRT coatings

Material-related factors such as pigment type and loading, along with process-related factors, like dispersion quality and film thickness, were thoroughly studied to understand their influence on NIR transmittance. The results showed that both material composition and film uniformity play key roles in achieving high NIR transparency with effective visible light blocking. For each set of experiments, the remaining parameters were kept constant to ensure that the observed variations could be attributed solely to the factor under investigation.

Effect of pigment dispersion on particle size and NIR transparency

To systematically optimize the dispersion of pigment particles and improve the NIR transparency of the coatings, we investigated three key processing parameters: (i) the type of mixing blade, which determines the shear pattern and intensity within the medium; (ii) the mixing time, which influences the extent of pigment deagglomeration; and (iii) the dispersion method, which governs the efficiency and uniformity of particle size reduction. The combined effects of these parameters were analyzed to identify the most effective conditions for achieving fine, well-dispersed pigment particles and enhanced optical performance.

Among these factors, the influence of mixing blade design was first investigated. Efficient mixing of pigment particles is crucial for controlling particle size, which directly influences the NIR transparency of the coatings. Since processing conditions strongly affect dispersion quality, we initially analyzed the impact of blade design by comparing a propeller blade and a flat blade with alumina grinding balls. The BF coatings prepared using the flat blade with the grinding medium exhibited higher NIR transmittance than those prepared with the propeller blade (Fig. 2a).

UV–vis-NIR transmittance spectra of two coatings prepared with (a) different mixing blade designs used in pigment dispersion and (b) three different dispersion times, showing that the flat blade-grinding medium and increased grinding time (90 min) lead to increased NIR transparency.

The higher NIR transmittance observed for the coating formulas mixed with the flat blade can be attributed to the more uniform shear forces generated throughout the polymer matrix, which enabled a more effective breakdown of pigment clusters compared to those mixed with the propeller blade. Additionally, localized collisions between pigment particles and alumina grinding balls further promoted deagglomeration, resulting in a finer and more uniform particle size distribution40,41. The smaller particle size likely reduced backward scattering and minimized Mie scattering42,43, thereby enhancing light transmission according to Eq. (1).

These results are consistent with the well-established relationship between improved dispersion quality, reduced particle size, and enhanced optical performance44,45, and they guided the selection of the flat-blade method for subsequent formulations.

Further, the effect of mixing time on pigment dispersion and NIR transparency was studied. Increasing the mixing time from 30 to 90 min resulted in coatings with higher NIR transmittance (Fig. 2b). This improvement can be attributed to the gradual breakdown of pigment clusters and the refinement of the particle size distribution (PSD) during prolonged dispersion with the grinding medium. A similar relationship has been reported by Santhanam et al46, who observed that longer mixing times in an attritor mill improved material refinement and dispersion quality. The smaller-sized particles, formed with increased mixing time, reduce backward scattering34, thereby increasing the transmitted light through the coating. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that blade geometry and mixing time are critical process-related factors in achieving minimal pigment particle size and maximizing NIR transparency.

Additionally, three mixing methods, Low shear mixing with grinding medium (LS), High speed mixing (HS), and Ultrasonication (US), were evaluated to investigate their effect on pigment particle size distribution and NIR transparency. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that the LS method using a flat blade with alumina grinding balls produced the smallest average particle size distribution (566.3 nm), followed by the US method, which generated moderate particle sizes (708.7 nm), and the HS method, which produced a bimodal particle size distribution (346.1 nm and 1845.7 nm) (Table 1; Fig. 3). UV–vis-NIR spectroscopy showed that coatings prepared with the smallest particle average sizes (LS method) exhibited the highest NIR transmittance. In contrast, coatings from the US method showed moderate transmittance, and those from the HS method showed the relatively lowest NIR transmittance (Fig. 4a). These findings indicate a strong correlation between reduced pigment particle size and enhanced NIR transmittance.

These differences in average particle size distributions can be attributed to the distinct dispersion mechanisms of each method, as described in the author’s previous work41. In the LS method, localized alumina-pigment-alumina collisions provide controlled mechanical impacts, gradually breaking down loosely bound clusters, and preventing their re-agglomeration47,48. This method yields a narrow particle size distribution (566.3 nm) well below the NIR wavelength (~ 750 nm). In the case of the US method, dispersion occurs through the collapse of cavitation bubbles, which generate localized microjets and shockwaves. This mechanism effectively chips pigment clusters into moderately fine fragments (708.7 nm) without substantial re-agglomeration, resulting in a relatively uniform size distribution. In contrast, the HS method relies on high rotational shears as particles move along a velocity gradient. While this can fragment particles into smaller sizes (346.1 nm), the process also promotes re-agglomeration and adhesion of particles to container surfaces, which limits their effective exposure to shear. As a result, very large secondary clusters (1845.7 nm) coexist with smaller fragments, producing a broad bimodal distribution.

According to Mie scattering theory, when particle sizes approach the wavelength of NIR light, scattering increases and reduces transmittance, whereas smaller particles primarily undergo Rayleigh scattering, which decreases sharply with increasing wavelength and thus minimally affects NIR light43. Therefore, the relatively smaller size particles obtained from the LS method reduce scattering losses and enhance NIR transparency, while the broader distributions from the US method and especially the HS method contribute to increased scattering and reduced transmittance49. Schematic illustration of Mie scattering for particles of different sizes, highlighting reduced scattering for smaller particles and enhanced scattering for larger particles (Fig. 4b). These observations are consistent with previous reports showing that minimizing pigment particle size enhances NIR transmittance by suppressing scattering and absorption losses14.

Effect of dry film thickness

Coatings were applied at varying thicknesses, and their NIR transmittance was measured (Fig. 5a). An inverse relationship between dry film thickness (DFT) and NIR transparency was observed (Fig. 5b). The thinner film (48.3 μm) exhibited higher NIR transmittance, while the thicker film (124.5 μm) showed a noticeable reduction in transmittance.

Effect of dry film thickness on NIR transmittance: (a) UV–vis-NIR transmittance spectra of three dry film thicknesses, (b) transmittance at selected wavelengths as a function of DFTs. Results show the adverse impact on NIR transparency with increased thickness.

The reduction in NIR transmittance with increasing film thickness can be attributed to the fundamental optical principles described by the Beer-Lambert law50 (Eq. 2). At constant pigment volume fraction, increasing the film thickness directly extends the optical path length, thereby enhancing both absorption and scattering losses within the coating matrix. This explains the observed nonlinear reduction in NIR transparency as thickness increases. Comparable behavior has been reported in prior studies on radiative cooling materials and smart window technologies, where optimal performance was achieved at reduced thicknesses to maximize IR transmittance51,52. These findings reinforce the critical role of film thickness as a tunable parameter for balancing durability and optical efficiency in functional coatings.

Effect of the matting agent and white pigment

Material-related factors, such as the addition of a matting agent and the addition of white pigment, were also evaluated for their influence on NIR transparency. Coatings containing varying amounts of matting agents were prepared to evaluate their effect on NIR transparency. NIR transmittance remained stable at lower matting agent concentrations, but when the loading exceeded 8 wt%, a noticeable decrease was observed, with transmittance values reducing below 50% (Fig. 6a).

(a) UV–vis-NIR spectra of the film with different matting agent loading, and (b) UV–vis-NIR spectra of the film with different white pigment 1 loading. Results show that high loading of matting agent 1 (> 8 wt%) negatively impacts the NIR transparency, and loading white pigment 1 substantially decreases the transmittance.

Although matting agents are typically added to control film gloss, their presence can strongly affect optical performance when added in high amounts. The polyolefin-based matting agent used in this study contains relatively narrow IR absorption bands, indicating minimal intrinsic absorption in the NIR range53. Consequently, the observed reduction in transparency at loadings above 8 wt% is most likely attributed to increased surface roughness and film heterogeneity, which enhance scattering across both visible and NIR regions54. It is critical to keep the matting agent concentration below this threshold to achieve effective gloss control while maintaining wavelength-selective transparency.

While black NIRT coatings can typically be formulated without difficulty, developing green and tan colors requires the addition of white pigment to achieve the target color. Accordingly, the effect of white pigment 1 loading on NIR transparency was evaluated. Increasing white pigment concentration led to a substantial reduction in NIR transmittance (Fig. 6b).

The reduction in NIR transmittance with increasing white pigment concentration can be explained by the intrinsic light-scattering properties of such pigments. White pigments scatter and reflect strongly across a broad spectral range that extends into both the visible and NIR regions14,55. As the pigment loading increases, multiple scattering events within the coating matrix reduce the fraction of incident infrared radiation transmitted through the film. Although this effect is unfavorable for NIR transparency, a minimum dosage of white pigment 1 was required to achieve the desired visible color balance. This outcome highlights the need to balance visible color requirements with functional NIR performance. Therefore, optimizing white pigment 1 concentration is critical, ensuring sufficient pigmentation to reproduce the target color while minimizing excess scattering that could otherwise impair barcode readability under infrared imaging.

Factors affecting barcode profile and readability

Effect of printing methods on barcode concealment

The surface profile and visibility of barcodes printed by LaserJet and Inkjet printers were compared before and after application of the black NIRT coatings. The LaserJet-printed barcodes on Teslin substrate exhibited a higher surface profile (14.3 μm) compared with the Inkjet-printed counterparts (11.7 μm). After coating application, the higher toner structures of the LaserJet prints enabled the underlying barcode information to remain more visible, whereas the Inkjet-printed barcodes were more effectively concealed (Fig. 7).

Surface roughness profile of barcode printed with LaserJet and Inkjet printer, exhibiting different levels of surface morphology directly impacting the barcode concealment by the naked eye.

The fundamental printing mechanisms of LaserJet and Inkjet technologies can explain the differing degrees of barcode concealment. LaserJet printing relies on toner deposition, where solid ink particles are fused to the substrate under heat, resulting in a high and rougher surface56 In contrast, Inkjet printers deposit liquid ink droplets that are absorbed into or spread across the substrate, producing a flat surface profile57. These differences in surface morphology directly influenced the interaction with the applied coating: the more elevated LaserJet features resisted full coverage and maintained our barcode visibility, while the smoother Inkjet prints were more readily concealed.

Effects of printer ink on barcode readability

Barcodes printed using Epson® ink were largely indistinguishable from the background, whereas barcodes printed with HP ink remained clearly visible as black lines, indicating a difference in NIR absorption between the inks (Fig. 8).

IR camera images of uncoated barcodes printed with two different Inkjet printer inks: (a) Epson Inkjet printer and (b) HP inkjet printer, illustrating the differences in barcode appearance.

Although this study does not investigate the specific ink compositions in detail, the observed differences in barcode readability can be attributed to their NIR absorption characteristics. Epson ink likely exhibits insufficient absorbance at the target wavelength, preventing the barcode from standing out against the coated background. In contrast, HP ink contains dyes or pigments with higher NIR absorption, enabling the printed features to remain visible. These findings underscore the importance of selecting printer inks with adequate NIR absorbance to ensure barcode legibility when combined with wavelength-selective coatings.

Barcode readability evaluation of coatings

Qualitative analysis of barcode readability of black NIRT coatings

The barcode readability of the BF coatings was first evaluated qualitatively using an IR camera imaging. A comparative illustration of a barcode-printed Teslin substrate coated with the BF formulation under both visible light (regular camera) and IR camera vision is shown in Fig. 9. In the visible range, the coated surface appears opaque, whereas under IR imaging, the barcode pattern remains transparent. To further evaluate sharpness, IR images of barcodes were tested at varying outdoor distances (Fig. 10). The visual inspection of these images indicated that barcode edges appeared gradually less distinct with increasing distance. In addition, the coated samples were provided to an external partner, who measured the maximum detection distance of the barcodes and found that the BF coated barcodes were readable at 12.2–15.2 m.

(a) Visible image of the black NIRT coatings showing no barcode to the naked eye, and (b) IR camera image of the same coatings revealing the concealed barcode.

IR camera images of black NIRT coating-coated barcodes (cropped) obtained at various distances in an outdoor environment, showing different levels of qualitative image sharpness.

The qualitative observations confirm that the BF coatings enable sufficient transmission of IR radiation to maintain barcode readability while concealing the code in the visible spectrum. This dual behavior arises from the selective optical properties of the coating: high absorbance in the visible range ensures that the printed barcode is effectively hidden to the human eye, whereas partial transparency in the infrared region allows the underlying barcode pattern to be detected by an IR camera and detected by scanners. The ability to recognize barcodes through the coatings up to distances of 12.2–15.2 m demonstrates that the IR transparency of the BF formulation is not noticeably hindered by film thickness or substrate effects, highlighting its practical usage for security and authentication applications where hidden yet machine-readable information is required. The strong readability performance in outdoor conditions further suggests the capability against environmental interference. Overall, these findings confirm the coatings design strategy and establish IR transparency as the key factor governing barcode recognition performance, while providing a foundation for subsequent quantitative image sharpness analysis.

Quantitative analysis of barcode readability using gray value

A quantitative evaluation of barcode readability was performed by analyzing the ΔGV between the barcode and the substrate. Histograms generated in Fiji were used to measure the distribution of gray values for both the uncoated and coated barcode images (Fig. 11). The results show that the uncoated barcode exhibited a larger separation between histogram peaks compared to the coated barcode, indicating higher image contrast and a clearer distinction between the barcode and its background.

(Left) IR camera image of the barcode with and without black NIRT coatings under indoor D65 light at 0.61 m distance (Right) Δ gray value of the coated and uncoated barcodes showing the slight reduction in ‘contrast’, qualitatively and quantitatively.

The ΔGV, therefore, provides an objective measure of barcode detectability and readability. The larger ΔGV observed for the uncoated barcode confirms that the absence of coatings maximizes contrast, while the reduced ΔGV for the coated barcode indicates that the BF coatings partially lower contrast by weakening the transmitted signal. However, the fact that the coated barcode remained detectable highlights that the NIRT formulation permits sufficient IR transmission to maintain barcode readability. These findings confirm that coating transparency is a key factor in maintaining performance and highlight the use of gray-value analysis as a quantitative method for evaluating coated versus uncoated substrates under real imaging conditions.

The ΔGVs of the BF coatings barcode images were compared with those of a commercial control NIRT coatings over distances ranging from 0.3 to 18.3 m (Fig. 12). For commercial control, ΔGVs decreased consistently as the distance between the barcode and the IR camera increased (Fig. 13a). In contrast, the BF coatings maintained or stable ΔGVs across the same distance range, indicating minimal loss of contrast with increasing distance. The other quantitative metric, the SNR values for BF, were higher than those for commercial control (MB) across all distances (Fig. 13b). This indicates that the desired signal is stronger relative to background noise, resulting in clearer, more distinguishable images and enabling more accurate barcode decoding.

IR camera images of barcodes coated with black NIRT coatings and commercial black NIRT coatings at various distances in an outdoor environment, showing that the developed coatings retain the ‘contrast’, qualitatively, much better than the commercial.

(a) Δ gray value comparison of IR camera images of the barcode with black NIRT coatings (BF) and commercial NIRT black coatings (MB) under outdoor light, quantitatively showing that the developed coatings retain the ‘contrast’ by maintaining the Δ gray value, unlike the commercial and (b) SNR value comparison of IR camera images of the BF and MB.

These results indicate that the BF coatings maintain sufficient contrast between the barcode and substrate even at extended distances, which supports improved readability and reliability for IR-based detection. Commercial control coatings lose contrast with increasing distance, suggesting limited effectiveness for applications requiring barcode recognition at longer ranges. The stable ΔGVs and SNR values observed for BF highlight the efficiency of its IR transparency in maintaining barcode detectability under varying imaging conditions, further demonstrating the functional advantage of the developed coatings over commercial alternatives.

To further quantify barcode readability and establish a threshold value, image sharpness was analyzed using the first-derivative method, with normalized peak height representing edge sharpness. Third-party testing confirmed that the BF-coated barcode was readable at distances of 12.2–15.2 m using a standard barcode reader. At this maximum readable distance, the normalized image sharpness value was 79 (Fig. 14).

Summarized steps used to assess barcode readability, with details provided in Supplementary information. The minimum threshold for barcode readability (value of 79) was established based on the third-party test results.

The first-derivative normalized peak height provides an objective and quantitative measure of barcode edge sharpness, directly correlating with readability. In this study, the threshold was set at a normalized value of 79, which equals 7.4% of the sharpness at a 0.3 m distance, and provides a consistent benchmark for testing barcode performance. Maintaining a normalized peak height above this threshold indicates sufficient IR transparency to support reliable barcode detection, whereas lower values suggest reduced readability. This threshold was subsequently applied to evaluate the readability of barcodes in other colors. Combined with gray value contrast analysis, this method provides a strong context for evaluating coating performance and optimizing NIRT formulations for practical barcode applications.

Study on the effect of particle size distributions on barcode readability

To understand the effects of particle size distribution on barcode readability, BF coatings prepared using three different dispersion methods were evaluated (Fig. 15). BF(LS), with the smallest average particle size (566.3 nm), exhibited the highest readability, whereas BF(HS), with a bimodal distribution (346.1 nm and 1845.7 nm), showed the lowest. BF(US), with an intermediate average particle size (708.7 nm), demonstrated readability between BF(LS) and BF(HS). The gray-value histogram analysis of IR camera images supports these observations. BF(LS), with the uniform and smallest particle size, exhibited a relatively higher ΔGV, whereas BF(US) and BF(HS) showed lower ΔGVs. The SNR of the black NIRT coatings with different PSDs remained similar, despite observable differences in barcode readability (Fig. 15c). This discrepancy may arise because SNR is a global metric and does not fully capture localized features such as barcode edge sharpness, which is more directly assessed using the first derivative-based method for barcode readability.

(a) Gray value distribution, (b) barcode readability, and (c) SNR of black NIRT coatings with different particle size distributions, showing higher barcode readability and better sharpness at the barcode edge obtained with a smaller average particle size distribution.

Although the three coatings appeared visually similar to the naked eye, quantitative assessment of barcode edge sharpness revealed a strong dependence on particle size distribution, with larger particle size distributions unfavorably affecting readability, consistent with the earlier NIR transmittance results presented in “Effect of pigment dispersion on particle size and NIRtransparency” section. The gray value histograms for the three coatings provide further evidence of this relationship, revealing distinct differences in ΔGVs. BF(LS), with the smallest particle size distribution, exhibited the highest ΔGV, indicating greater contrast between the barcode and the substrate and sharper barcode edges, which supports improved readability. In contrast, BF(US) and BF(HS), characterized by larger or bimodal particle size distributions, showed lower ΔGVs, reflecting reduced contrast and reduced barcode edge sharpness. These observations are consistent with Mie scattering theory, which predicts increased scattering from larger particles, thereby reducing NIR transmittance and reducing image sharpness43 Collectively, these findings highlight that controlling particle size distribution is critical not only for optimizing NIR transparency but also for maximizing ΔGV differences, maintaining barcode edge sharpness, and ensuring reliable recognition under IR imaging conditions.

Barcode readability in green NIRT coatings

While black NIRT coatings are common and well-established in the automotive and coatings industries, developing colored coatings such as green or tan presents additional challenges. These colors require the addition of white pigment to achieve the desired color while maintaining NIR transparency, which can substantially affect barcode readability. To investigate this, three green coatings with varying white pigment loadings and pigment types were prepared (Fig. 16). The regular and IR camera images of these coatings revealed a clear trend: reducing white pigment loading resulted in a darker green color and improved barcode readability. GF1, containing 2.6 wt% white pigment 1, exhibited the lightest green color and the lowest barcode sharpness, whereas GF3, with 0.7 wt% white pigment 1, showed the darkest green with the highest ΔGV and barcode readability (Fig. 17a and b). Interestingly, GF2, incorporating 6.2 wt% white pigment 2, achieved ΔGV and barcode readability similar to GF3 despite the highest pigment loading. Similarly, the trend of SNR values followed the barcode readability, where GF3 had the highest value (7.8 dB) of SNR and barcode readability (Fig. 17c).

Regular and IR camera images of the three green NIRT coatings (GF1, GF2, and GF3) with different green types exhibiting the difference in green hue and the NIR transparency, qualitatively.

(a) Gray value distribution, (b) barcode readability, and (c) SNR of GF1, GF2, and GF3 NIRT coatings showed that the white pigment 2 used in GF3 is a better pigment option to increase the brightness of the coating color while giving the highest barcode readability and SNR.

GF1, which contained a higher amount of white pigment 1 compared to GF3, exhibited reduced barcode readability, consistent with its lower ΔGV. This effect aligns with Mie scattering theory, where larger amounts of scattering particles increase light scattering, thereby decreasing NIR transmittance and image sharpness43. Interestingly, GF2, despite containing the highest pigment loading overall, maintained ΔGVs and barcode readability similar to GF3. This suggests that white pigment 2 possesses a lower scattering efficiency, likely due to differences in particle size, morphology, or refractive index relative to the binder58. The similar ΔGVs, higher SNR values, and gray-value histograms of GF2 and GF3 indicate that pigment type can mitigate the negative effects of higher pigment loading. These findings highlight that both ΔGV differences and pigment type are critical parameters for optimizing NIR transparency and ensuring high barcode readability in colored coatings.

Barcode readability in tan NIRT coatings

Tan color, in particular, requires multiple pigments to achieve the desired muted light-brown shade (tan), and these pigments must be either NIRT or have minimal negative impact on NIR performance. Figure 18 shows regular and IR camera images of three TF coatings (TF1, TF2, and TF3) with different white pigment loadings and the addition of a dispersive additive. Among TF coatings, TF3 exhibited the sharpest barcode edges (highest normalized peak height), followed by TF2, with TF1 showing the weakest performance (Fig. 19a). The gray value histograms confirmed these observations: TF3 had a relatively high ΔGV and strong contrast between the barcode and the substrate, while TF1 and TF2 showed lower ΔGVs, leading to weaker contrast and reduced barcode readability (Fig. 19b). Similarly, TF SNR values followed the trend of barcode readability (Fig. 19c), with TF3 having the highest barcode readability and SNR value (5.9 dB).

Regular and IR camera images of the three-tan NIRT coatings (TF1, TF2, and TF3) with different tan shades.

(a) Gray value distribution, (b) barcode readability, and (c) SNR of TF1, TF2, and TF3 coatings showed the positive impact of the dispersive additive loading (TF3) on barcode readability, if high white pigment 1 loading is necessary for color.

The reduced performance of TF1 and TF2 can be attributed to their higher white pigment loadings, which increased scattering and reduced NIR transparency. In contrast, the improved performance of TF3 can be ascribed to the addition of the dispersive additive. This additive likely reduced scattering by improving pigment dispersion and minimizing the influence of larger pigment particles. The improved ΔGV and SNR in TF3 support this interpretation, suggesting that the additive reduced the negative effects of higher pigment dosages. These findings demonstrate that dispersion enhancement is a critical strategy for balancing the desired tan color with functional NIR transparency. Despite the limited range of suitable commercial pigments, this approach highlights the potential of carefully designed formulations, particularly those using dispersive additives, to expand the development of colored NIRT coatings.

Barcode readability in different application methods

In addition to conventional spray coating, aerosol and brush applications were also investigated, as these methods are widely used for practical and small-scale applications where spray systems may not be possible. Regular and its IR camera images of BF coated substrates prepared using these three techniques are shown in Fig. 20a and b. Barcode readability, evaluated through image sharpness, remained consistent across all application methods, confirming reliable detection performance regardless of the coating technique used (Fig. 20c).

(a) Regular camera images of the black NIRT coatings prepared by spray, aerosol spray can, and paint brush, (b) IR camera images of the coated substrates, and (c) barcode readability showing that different application methods do not adversely affect the readability.

Although the propellant is the main difference between a spray gun and an aerosol spray can, both methods produce similar dry film surface quality. In contrast, the brush-applied coatings exhibited indistinct stroke marks and minor defects, which may be attributed to impurities introduced from the brush. However, these surface irregularities did not affect barcode readability. This outcome highlights the versatility of the coating formulation, showing that it is compatible with various practical application methods while maintaining performance.

Barcode readability of the rapid-curing coatings

Rapid-curing systems are important for improving coating process efficiency and enabling faster handling in practical applications. The effect of DBTL catalyst addition on rapid-curing NIRT coatings was evaluated for BF-RC, GF-RC, and TF-RC formulations. The addition of 1.0 wt% catalyst considerably accelerated crosslinking59, viscosity, reducing dry-to-handle time from over 200 min to less than 20 min, depending on formulation (Fig. 21, Figure S4, Figure S5). Image analysis confirmed that barcode sharpness and IR transparency were maintained across all coatings, with only small variations that remained within acceptable limits. Also, the SNR values of the three-color coatings and their rapid-curing versions exhibit a similar trend, with black showing the highest SNR (lowest noise), followed by green and then tan (Figure S4).

Effect of the catalyst on (a) Drying time, (b) viscosity change profile over time, measured by cone-plate viscometer, (c) IR camera images, and (d) barcode readability, showing rapid curing but no adverse effect on the readability.

In the BF-RC formulation, the addition of 1.0 wt% catalyst reduced the dry-to-handle time from 210 min to 16 min while maintaining barcode readability (Fig. 21a). The GF-RC coatings with 1.0 wt% catalyst achieved a comparable drying performance of 17 min, with a slight but acceptable increase in image sharpness (Figure S5). Similarly, the TF-RC formulation was cured within 11 min at the same catalyst dose, showing only a marginal reduction in image sharpness that remained above the readability threshold (Figure S6). Together, these findings confirm that the rapid-curing system is versatile across different colored coatings, noticeably improving drying efficiency while ensuring consistent barcode readability.

Mechanical properties of the NIRT coatings

Water and solvent resistance test

The water and solvent resistance of BF, GF, and TF coatings was evaluated through immersion, acid-spot, and solvent-exposure tests. After 168 h of water immersion, all coatings-maintained hardness within two pencil grades, meeting ASTM D3363 requirements (Fig. 22, top). In the acid-spot test, no blistering or color change was observed after exposure to 10% acetic acid solution (Fig. 22, bottom). Similarly, in solvent immersion tests with acetone, MEK, toluene, and xylene, all coatings showed good resistance without blistering or discoloration.

Image of black colored NIRT coatings before (top) and after (bottom) the exposure to 10% v/v acetic acid solution, exhibiting no blistering, other film defects, and color changes.

The results confirm that the designed NIRT coatings exhibit strong resistance to water, acid, and solvent attack across all three colors. Prolonged water immersion did not noticeably affect film hardness, demonstrating that the crosslinked network remained stable. Similarly, resistance to acetic acid exposure indicates strong chemical durability under mild acidic environments. The solvent resistance results further support the strength of the formulations, as none of the coatings exhibited blistering or visual degradation after contact with common aggressive solvents. Together, these results demonstrate the reliability of the coatings for applications requiring long-term environmental and chemical stability, while maintaining consistent performance across different color formulations.

Mandrel bend flexibility test

Flexibility is an important property for coatings intended for use on curved or deformable surfaces, ensuring durability without cracking or delamination. The flexibility of BF, GF, and TF coatings was evaluated using the mandrel bend test. For each formulation, three individual replicates were tested, and all samples passed without any visible cracking or delamination after bending, demonstrating good flexibility and reproducibility of the results (Fig. 23).

Mandrel bend test results of (a) black, (b) green, and (c) tan colored NIRT coatings showing the flexibility of the coating, regardless of pigment dosage.

The results confirm that all three NIRT coatings exhibit superior flexibility, making them suitable for applications where substrates may be bent, rolled, or shaped without reducing performance. The absence of cracks or coating failure after bending demonstrates that the formulations retain mechanical integrity under strain.

Adhesion Test

Adhesion is a key property for coatings to ensure long-term performance and durability under mechanical stress. The adhesion of BF, GF, and TF coatings was evaluated using the X-cut tape adhesion method. All coatings achieved the highest rating of 5B, showing no peeling or detachment from the substrate (Fig. 24).

Adhesion test results of (a) black, (b) green, and (c) tan colored NIRT coatings showing strong adhesion between the substrate and coatings.

The results demonstrate that all three designed NIRT coatings strongly bond to the substrate surface, ensuring mechanical integrity during usage. The highest rating of 5B indicates good resistance to tape removal damage, which is essential for maintaining coating performance in practical applications. This high level of adhesion, combined with flexibility and chemical resistance, confirms the strength and suitability of these coatings for a wide range of industrial and environmental conditions.

Surface roughness analysis

The surface roughness of the coated substrates was measured and found to follow the order GF > TF > BF (Fig. 26). This trend is inversely related to the gloss measurements reported earlier, with GF exhibiting the highest roughness and lowest gloss, and BF showing the smoothest surface and highest gloss. TF showed intermediate roughness and gloss values.

Keyence 3D surface profiler images of black (a), green (b), and tan (c) colored NIRT coatings, showing the composition of pigment impacting the surface roughness and the coating gloss.

The roughness trend can be attributed primarily to differences in pigment volume concentration and type of pigment. GF and TF coatings have higher pigment volume concentrations compared to BF, which generally results in increased surface roughness62 Among these, GF exhibited the highest roughness, likely due to the incorporation of high molecular weight polymer powder with a large particle size (~ 30 μm), irregular morphology, and poor wettability, all of which contribute to surface irregularities. In contrast, TF, which employs a metal sulfide pigment with a much smaller average particle size (~ 275 nm) and superior wettability, resulted in smoother surfaces. These findings indicate that formulation composition and pigment characteristics are key factors influencing surface roughness and, in turn, coating gloss.

Conclusion

This study investigated a bilayer coating system, consisting of a clear sealer coat and colored topcoats in black (BF), green (GF), and tan (TF), applied to Teslin substrates with printed barcodes, to achieve visual opacity under visible light while maintaining sufficient NIR transparency for reliable barcode recognition. The coatings were systematically evaluated for NIR transmittance, barcode readability, and mechanical durability. Among the formulations, BF emerged as the most effective, combining the highest NIR transmittance, greater barcode edge sharpness, SNR, and confirmed barcode readability at distances of 12.2–15.2 m through third-party testing. GF and TF also demonstrated practical viability, though with somewhat lower sharpness and SNR than BF. This reduction can be attributed to the addition of white pigments required for color tuning, which increases NIR scattering and reduces overall transmittance. However, both GF and TF exceeded the critical sharpness threshold for reliable barcode recognition, confirming their functional capability.

A key outcome of this work is the demonstrated role of pigment particle size distribution in governing both NIR transparency and readability. LS dispersion produced the smallest and most uniform average particle size (566.3 nm), resulting in the highest transmittance and largest ΔGV contrast, while HS dispersion yielded a bimodal distribution (346.1 nm and 1845.7 nm) that caused increased scattering, reduced transmittance, and poor readability. These results are consistent with Mie scattering theory, which links larger or broadly distributed particles to scattering losses and blurred barcode edges, while smaller and more uniform particles maintain NIR transmission and readability.

Across all three colors, both rapid-curing and QUV-exposed formulations showed only small reductions in sharpness, with values remaining above the critical threshold. This confirms the coatings’ durability under environmental stress and their suitability for long-term use. The coating application method did not substantially affect sharpness, though spray application emerged as the most practical technique due to its greater control over thickness and surface uniformity.

Taken together, these findings validate a material-process-performance framework for designing visually opaque yet NIRT coatings. They highlight particle-size distribution control as a critical formulation parameter and demonstrate the feasibility of multicolor coatings capable of concealing barcodes in the visible spectrum while enabling reliable NIR recognition. This work provides a strong foundation for the future development of tailored NIRT coatings for advanced security, authentication, and LiDAR applications.
