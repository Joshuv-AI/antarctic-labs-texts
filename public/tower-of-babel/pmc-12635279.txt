Computational modelling of hollow fibre haemodialysers: current status and future directions
Authors: Ruhit Sinha, Anne E. Staples
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Current in silico dialyser models remain accurate only under narrowly constrained conditions. Advances in computing power and increasing levels of interdisciplinary collaboration, however, now set the stage for more robust computational haemodialyser models. In this review, we survey existing modelling approaches—long used to guide haemodialyser design and clinical nephrology practice—identify key unresolved challenges, and propose computational strategies poised to accelerate the development of dialyser technologies that better meet the needs of kidney failure patients.

Introduction

Kidney failure patients are often prescribed haemodialysis therapy as an alternative to kidney transplants. A haemodialyser, also known as an artificial kidney, is a medical device that removes toxins from blood extracorporeally by diffusion and convection. The first working haemodialyser, developed in 1944, was based on a rotating drum configuration (Fig. 1a). Since then, there have been numerous developments in haemodialyser design. The hollow fibre membrane module (HFMM) haemodialyser (also called tube or capillary dialyser; Fig. 1b), which is in use today, was first developed and tested by Dow Chemical Company in 19681–3. This configuration consists of thousands of hollow fibres packed inside a cylindrical module (also called jacket or housing; Fig. 2(II)a). Blood flows inside the hollow fibres while dialysis solution (dialysate) flows outside the fibres in the opposite direction (Fig. 2(II)b, c). The volume of blood that is completely cleared of uraemic toxins per unit time is termed clearance (also called dialysance; CL)4, which is an important indicator of the haemodialyser’s performance. Despite various advantages over earlier dialyser designs5—including higher membrane surface area per volume, reduced blood clotting and damage to red blood cells (RBCs), and lower blood flow resistance—today’s haemodialysers still suffer from dialysate flow channelling6, blood clotting7, high dialysate usage (leading to a large carbon footprint)8, and limited removal of middle-sized solutes and protein-bound uraemic toxins (PBUT)9. These shortcomings help explain why haemodialysis remains suboptimal, as indicated by a median five-year survival for dialysis patients10. Thus, improving haemodialyser design and operation is crucial.

Computational models have emerged as powerful tools for a better understanding of these devices, including predicting pressure losses (indicating the energy needed to generate the flow), clearance or mass-transfer coefficient and the distribution of residence time—the amount of time dialysate contacts the membrane. Table S1 lists the commercial dialyser modules investigated in the computational studies reviewed herein. These models can reduce cost, risk and time compared to repeated experimental studies and can probe a range of design parameters not readily altered in clinical trials. However, the complex geometry (thousands of hollow fibres) and multiphysics nature of the transport processes demand a high computational cost. This review aims to present a state-of-the-art perspective on the mathematical and computational modelling of hollow fibre haemodialysers, as well as recent advances. This article extends the scope of two recent reviews11,12 on the topic by including a comprehensive discussion of future directions of dialyser modelling efforts. Moreover, we argue that existing models of haemodialysers may be inadequate for important applications like understanding haemolysis because they provide limited information about how the flow field varies across the dialyser module. However, these variations in the flow field can result in local regions of high shear stress that damage RBCs. We believe that this analysis will inform next-generation models, helping design innovative pre-clinical dialysis technologies such as portable, wearable, implantable and bioartificial kidneys (Fig. 2(I)).

We have structured the remainder of the review as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of dialyser modelling at increasing levels of complexity, subdivided into membrane fundamentals, flow distribution, and solute transport. Section 3 surveys how computational models have been used to evaluate modifications in dialyser design and operating conditions. Section 4 summarises conclusions and open issues.

Flow distributions in haemodialysers

To obtain a flow distribution (also called momentum transport) in a haemodialyser, it is important to assign fluid material properties, such as density (ρ), dynamic viscosity (μ), and boundary conditions. Two fluid domains exist in the hollow-fibre configuration: i) Blood flows inside each hollow fibre (lumen), while ii) dialysate flows outside the fibres in the opposite direction around thousands of fibres (shell). All studies consider dialysate as a homogeneous and incompressible (constant ρ; ∇\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\cdot \vec{u}$$\end{document}⋅u→ = 0) fluid while blood is considered mostly Newtonian (constant μ), both comparable with water (mostly at 37 °C or 310 K i.e. ρ = 993 kgm−3, μ ∼0.69 mPas; see Table S3). A few studies, however, consider blood as non-Newtonian. For example, μB is modelled using Casson’s equation32, considering μB to vary radially and axially by incorporating shear thinning (decreasing μ with increasing shear-rate and haematocrit—the percentage of RBC by volume) dependency as per Quemada model20,23,33,34, a simplification of the μB expression made by ignoring the shear thinning, allowing dependency on just the haematocrit and protein concentration35, treating blood as a multiphase fluid with an Eulerian-Eulerian approach36.

As an inlet boundary condition, most studies assign blood flow rate QBi (typically 100–500 mL min−1) and dialysate flow rate QDi (typically 200–1000 mL min−1). For outlets, studies typically assign PDout = 0 and a certain PBout4,33,37,38 to match a desired net ultrafiltration flow rate QF. Conversely, PBout = 0 and a certain QDo to match a desired QF can also be assigned39. For oncotic pressure Π, many studies use the Landis and Pappenheimer model, a few studies assume some formulation20,40,41, while others neglect this quantity (see Table S3). Regarding ultrafiltration, earlier studies either assume zero ultrafiltrate velocity32,42–48 (i.e. no local ultrafiltration leading to Poiseuille’s flow assumption for the blood and dialysate flow in and around a single fibre, respectively) or the same ultrafiltrate velocity throughout the length of the dialyser49–51. While the more recent studies either assume zero QF37,39,52 (i.e. no net ultrafiltration) or a certain QF value4,33,37,38,53 (typically 0–60 mL min−1).

Almost all studies consider steady, laminar, and developed flow regimes, i.e. entry region is negligible. Classic analyses use the Navier–Stokes equations and often treat the shell as an annulus with an “equivalent diameter” tied to the dialyser packing density φ58. Such approaches can incorporate ultrafiltration33,46,59, slip boundary conditions at the membrane wall60,61, or non-Newtonian effects of blood33, but usually ignore module wall, manifolds, and distribution tabs. Another set of studies assumes the shell to be a packed column where the ΔPD is given by the Blake–Kozeny equation or Ergun equation52,63,64. Solute transport in haemodialysers

In haemodialyser simulations, solutes are typically modelled as passive scalars governed by the steady-state (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{C}=0$$\end{document}C°=0) convection-diffusion equation (see Fig. 2(III)). It is a one-way coupling multiphysics, as the flow field calculated first is then used in the solute transport model, without feedback. As boundary conditions, a certain toxin concentration CBi and CDi (zero in most cases) are set at the blood and dialysate inlets, respectively (Dirichlet boundary condition). On the other hand, the concentration gradient ∇C at both the outlets is assumed to be zero (Neumann boundary condition)20. Interestingly, CL, which depends on diffusion and convection of toxins (except PBUT), is independent of CBi and CDi but depends on operating condition parameters such as QBi, QDi and QF70. Mass transfer and heat transfer are analogous, and an HFMM can be thought of as a mass transfer equivalent of a shell-and-tube heat exchanger71–73 but with more lumens74. The dimensionless Sherwood (Sh), Péclet (Pe), Schmidt (Sc), and Graetz (Gz) numbers (see Table 1) often appear in semi-empirical simplified models75,76. Sh and Sc can be interpreted as mass-transfer equivalents of dimensionless terms Nusselt number (Nu) and Prandtl number (Pr), respectively, often used in heat transfer77.1D Theories. They yield expressions for pure diffusive CL (e.g. ref. 79) or include ultrafiltration (e.g. ref. 80) by introducing a formula that separates the baseline diffusive clearance (CL0) from a convective term (Tr⋅QF). The factor known as the Transmittance coefficient Tr depends on solute size: for creatinine, Tr is typically around 0.29, whereas it can be about 0.41 for cytochrome C40, illustrating slightly stronger clearance enhancement for middle-sized solutes81. In some experimental studies, however, Tr was found to be a linear function of QF82,83. These models are computationally efficient but can miss important effects due to the oversimplified geometry70. Module vibration

Mechanical vibrations such as rotational, longitudinal, and transverse motion increase the dialyser efficiency94. Kim et al. (2012)95 performed a computational fluid dynamics study to find the optimum vibration parameters such as amplitude and frequency by employing three clinical safety criteria: (i) instantaneous maximum TMP of 500 mmHg at the blood inlet, (ii) instantaneous maximum WSS of 200 Pa to prevent erythrocyte damage at the inlet manifold and (iii) noise level. Based on these conditions, transverse shaking velocity with an amplitude of 30 mm s−1 turned out to be the most effective.

Inlet manifold design

The inlet manifold (also called arterial port or header) design is critical in terms of dialysis efficiency (caused by non-uniform blood flow distribution) and biocompatibility96. Park and Chang (1986)97 used the finite-difference method to show that the approaching velocity profile of the blood flow at the inlet of the manifold becomes closer to that of the Poiseuille flow as the height of the inlet manifold increases. With a cylindrical manifold, the highest velocity occurs in the fibre located at the centre, while the lowest occurs near the centre. With a conical manifold, the trend is the same at and near the centre. But the peripheral velocity decreases remarkably below the average, and the local maximum velocity occurs between the local minimum point near the centre and the periphery. Lee et al. (2012)98 redesigned the inlet manifold into two independent compartments for blood flow to show that internal filtration is enhanced by keeping the PB higher than the PD in one compartment and lower in the other. The modification improves the CL of middle-sized solutes by convection effects and hence the authors named it 'convection-enhanced' haemodialysis. In this setup, filtration volume is two times higher than conventional haemodialysis.

Distribution tabs and baffles

Distribution tabs (also called flow baffles) are projections located near the dialysate inlet and outlet headers that prevent the direct impact of dialysate onto the hollow fibre bundle and circulate dialysate uniformly. However, these don’t seem to produce perfect circumferential dialysate flow distribution as evidenced by computed tomography (CT)99 and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)100. To investigate its design, Ding et al. (2004)101 considered the nonporous tabs to be porous in a 19-fibre module. They found an optimal κ of tabs equal to 1 × 10−9 m2, coincidentally similar to κM, at which CLurea is highest—4.5% higher than usual. Yamamoto et al. (2009)64 studied the effect of tab angle and found that 30, 120, and 240° could not provide dialysate flow into the rear of the tab, while 0 and 360° tabs provided uniform dialysate flow into the fibre bundle. However, the 0° model had a slow flow at the edges of the fibre bundle, making the 360° model (full tab) the most effective. Tab’s taper structure also impacts the dialysate flow as evident from residence-time distribution102. Uchi et al. (2009)103 optimised the taper length to be as short as 20 mm. The fluid force of the short taper enforces dialysate flow into the hollow-fibre bundle core, resulting in higher CL. Yamamoto et al. (2009)64 found the optimum angle and length of the taper to be 2–4° and 12.5-25 mm, respectively, failing which causes channelling. The use of baffles internally and an O-ring to promote cross-flow in addition to the counterflow leads to higher mass transfer104. To investigate this effect in a haemodialyser, Alghafis et al. (2019)41 considered a 37-fibre module and found a 5–8% increase in CLurea.

Randomness and polydispersity of fibres

The randomness of hollow fibre arrangement and polydispersity (variation) of inner fibre diameter din and membrane thickness tM can arise during the assembling of fibre bundle and spinneret-based manufacturing105,106. To mimic the randomness of hollow fibre arrangement, Chen and Hlavacek (1994)74 considered each fibre to be surrounded by a polygonal cell defined by a Voronoi tessellation. After analysing the change in the effective friction factor times the Reynolds number, they found that the largest 20% of cells carried half of the flow as a result of channelling. Later, Wu and Chen (2000)65 showed that kD decreased as φ increases up to 40% after which it starts to increase again. Bao et al. (1999)107, after investigating the entry region of a randomly packed shell, found that kD can decrease by 30% due to flow maldistribution in random packing. They found a similar trend after studying the fully-developed region with uniform wall flux108 and uniform wall concentration109. Wang et al. (2003)110 showed that the random fibre packing not only changes kD but also kB. Regarding the polydispersity of din, ref. 75 considered it by assuming a Gaussian distribution of din. For the lumen flow, they found that ShB or kB follows the Lévêque solution at high flow speed or high GzB, but falls notably at low flow speeds or GzB < 4. They attribute this behaviour to the polydispersity of din. For shell flow, however, ShD is directly proportional to dialysate velocity or GzD throughout, matching a similar study done later. For instance, ref. 111, neglecting kD, found a reduction in the effective Ko relative to that expected for the average fibre size. The reduction increases as either the Sh/Gz (evaluated using the average fibre size) or the standard deviation of the fibre size distribution increases. Moreover, the reduction is greatest when kB controls the individual Ko. With the addition of Gaussian distributed tM, ref. 105 found that effective Ko can be 60% of the expected value when din has a standard deviation of 20%. Ding et al. (2006)112 combined both randomess of fibre arrangement and polydispersity simultaneously to check their effect on kD. Recently, ref. 113 showed that non-uniformity of the fibre causes a consequential increase in Darcy’s permeability of the dialysate domain and a strong reduction in ShD.

Spacer yarns and packing density

Spacer yarns (also called spacing filaments) improve both the small and middle-sized solute CL114,115. CT and MRI studies show that these spacer yarns distribute dialysate flow uniformly by allowing more dialysate to flow at lower velocities than at higher velocities116. In other words, spacer yarns increase packing density φ, and thus, as a consideration, this observation is an effect of φ on the haemodialyser performance117. Noda and Gryte (1979)45 found that when the distance between centres of two hollow fibres (also called pitch) is 1.2do, kD is highest. This effect becomes more prominent as the membrane becomes thinner or permeable to the solute. Noda (1979)118 found that dialyser behaviour can be predicted at φ = 70% quite accurately, but deviates at 55% due to channelling. Interestingly, they simulated this channelling behaviour by a bypass model. Recently, ref. 119 found that Darcy’s permeability of the dialysate domain in a single-fibre model increases as bundle porosity (ϵ = 1 – φ) increases. ShD, when plotted against ϵ, had bell-shaped behaviour with its peak at ∼12 for ϵ ≈ 0.38.

Module design

The dialyser module plays an important role in dialysis performance. Suzuki et al. (2001)52 studied the effects of L and dmodule on ΔPD and CLurea, vitamin B12. Based on the conditions of ΔPB ( < 10 mmHg) and homogeneous dialysate distribution, they proposed a new dialyser with optimum din and module aspect ratio L/dmodule ratio of approximately 200 µm and 7.5, respectively. However, owing to the realistic module geometry, residence time depends on the streamlines it follows. Lemanski and Lipscomb (1995)72 examined the ratio of the effective Ko to the intrinsic Ko for convective mass transfer and found that it depends on two dimensionless groups. Notably, one of those dimensionless groups: 'module geometry number (MG); defined in Table 2' seems to be in the order of magnitude 10 for commercial dialysers. When we analyse the experimental Darcy’s permeability of the dialysate domain used across various studies (see Table 2), we indeed find this simulation result to align reasonably well. However, it is interesting to note that 2(κrr/κzz)D L/dmodule values are less than 1 for some dialysers, which implies PD may have a considerable variation in the radial direction, according to ref. 69 analysis. Cancilla et al. (2022)120 investigated five haemodialyser geometries, each having alternative inlet and outlet configurations. They claimed that the typical commercial module design already lies close to maximum performance. Hence, they suggest novelty in membranes for improved performance. Wavy fibres

Wave-shaped (also called crimped or undulated or Moiré) hollow fibres yield higher CL for small- and middle-sized solutes, potentially due to complex, transverse flow patterns and enhanced mixing122. Interestingly, wave-shaped fibres also reduce dialysate flow maldistribution in the dialyser123. Sangeetha et al. (2019)124 studied this effect using FEM. They found that increasing the wave count and amplitude increases CL in wave-shaped fibres than in straight fibres. The same group125 performed a fluid-structure interaction study and found that the WSS experienced by a wave-shaped fibre is less than a straight fibre. They pointed out that although a thin-walled membrane enhances CL, there has to be a balance with mechanical stability. Using WSS analysis, ref. 126 showed that a wave-shaped fibre is helpful in bioartificial kidneys. They found an optimal wave size for all given wave frequencies. Recently, ref. 127 developed a multiscale model consisting of a wave-shaped fibre-scale model and a 1D module-scale model. However, they didn’t find any notable increase in CLurea as compared to the straight fibre. Nonetheless, the model can be implemented to check the impact of wavy fibres on the CL in other dialyser modules. However, the same group77 later studied a regular hexagonal array of fibres. They found that under some specific conditions, such as ReD ∼ 10, ScD = 500, and the lowest bundle porosity investigated ϵ ∼ 0.3, (κzz)D and ShD are enhanced by a factor of ∼1.2 and ∼1.7, respectively, when the wave amplitude was in the order of do.

Conclusion and outlook

As models increase in complexity from 1D to 2D and 3D, predictive accuracy increases, but so do computational costs. But current reduced-order modelling approaches may be inadequate for applications of increasing interest, like understanding haemolysis in dialysers. As mentioned in the preceding sections, single-fibre models are unable to study module-scale flow field quantities such as mean transmembrane pressure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{{TMP}}$$\end{document}TMP¯ while porous media models can’t be used to study parameters which require inter-fibre flow details in their calculation, such as wall shear stress WSS. Full-scale 3D heterogeneous dialyser models incorporating the module wall and manifolds would serve as a gold standard, but that may be practically very difficult to achieve for many researchers because of computational expense. We have previously argued that the usage of multiscale modelling that combines the inexpensive pseudo-homogeneous models of the dialyser module-scale dialysate flow field with localised models of the inter-fibre-scale dialysate flow field can reduce the computational burden54. Approaches like these may provide an intermediate path forward. Nevertheless, the existing reduced-order models have guided suggestions for improved dialyser design and operating conditions. Reacting and protein-bound uraemic solutes

Few models address bicarbonate exchange, citrate-calcium interactions, or PBUT binding. All the studies done so far are on small- and middle-sized solutes. Among the few studies, ref. 128 studied the exchange of bicarbonate and carbon dioxide to help improve the acid-base status of kidney failure patients suffering from acidosis. Ravagli et al. (2016)42 developed a FEM-based model to couple the patient’s blood pool model to update the sodium concentration entering the dialyser dynamically. Important for inhibiting blood coagulation, ref. 129 modelled calcium flux into a citrate-based dialysate. Maheshwari et al. (2017)130 developed a PBUT kinetics model comprising a patient as three compartments connected to a dialyser. Refoyo et al. (2018)131 developed a multi-layered membrane of a single fibre in a bioartificial kidney to model the transport of PBUT, especially indoxyl sulfate. They implemented the Michaelis-Menten expression to model the toxin-cell interaction. A molecular dynamics study by ref. 132 renders new insights into PBUT-albumin interactions. King et al. (2021)133 provided an alternative computational model to calculate CLindoxyl sulfate that employs a two-step process of toxin-cell interaction. Using FEM, ref. 37 suggest that, unlike small-sized solutes, CLPBUT is strongly influenced by haematocrit and plasma albumin concentration. Recently, ref. 134 demonstrated that CLPBUT can be increased by increasing KoA. It can be done by either increasing kM, which is possible these days with cutting-edge membrane manufacturing technologies or by increasing QDi. However, the latter starts to have an effect only when kM > 10−5 ms−1. More computational studies of these reacting solutes will be critical in the advancement of haemodialysis treatment.

Multi-layered membranes

Models that can accurately capture asymmetric membranes are needed to study their effects on various solutes and PBUT clearance. Most studies model the membrane as a single-layer entity. In reality, haemodialyser membranes can be classified into symmetric (homogeneous) and asymmetric (multiple layers). Usually, cellulose-based membranes, except cellulose triacetate, are symmetric while synthetic membranes, except AN69, ethylenevinyl alcohol, and polymethyl methacrylate are asymmetric13,135,136 (See Table S4). Morti et al. (2003)137 showed that the asymmetric membranes have a different CL dependence on MW than the symmetric ones. Yaqoob et al. (2020)44 pointed out that the use of KO without considering τ and ε introduces the difference between the in vitro and in silico CL. Hence, they used the tortuous capillary pore-diffusion model to couple blood and dialysate domains with a multi-layer membrane. Wall shear stress analysis

Blood flowing through the lumen in the conventional operation mode of haemofiltration (also called inside-out) suffers from blood clotting. Inspired by various industrial applications, Dukhin et al. (2014)139 conducted an in vitro study to allow blood flow in the shell instead of the lumen (also called outside-in), similar to the configuration in blood oxygenators (also called artificial lungs), and found its effect on clotting. They showed that the rise in ΔPB due to clotting was less than 20 mmHg even after 100 h of operation, in contrast to the 300 mmHg rise in ΔPB after less than 25 h for inside-out hemofiltration. A recent in vitro study by ref. 140 further affirmed the superiority of the outside-in operation mode, indicating potential as an alternative for prolonged haemodialysis. Hence, this approach may reduce clotting but is underexplored computationally. Using the finite-volume method, ref. 141 studied flow configurations in which the extent of haemolysis, platelet activation and adhesion were estimated by analysing WSS distributions. Buck et al. (2018)142 applied WSS accumulation simulations to develop a design for the implantable artificial kidney that minimises thrombogenicity. We anticipate such computational studies in the future to help come up with better designs with outside-in operation mode, cost-effectively.

Pulsatile or unsteady dialysis

Transient modelling of push-pull or pulsatile flows has the potential to improve transport but remains computationally demanding. The pulsatile flow of blood and dialysate enhances CL143. This operating modality showed various clinical benefits, such as joint pain alleviation144 over conventional haemodialysis/haemodiafiltration with less albumin loss145. Modifying the membrane surface by furrowing increases ultrafiltration and hence CL by vortex mixing146. Rambod et al.(2010)147 showed numerically using a single-fibre model that pulsatile blood and dialysate flow generated by a wearable artificial kidney pump can have clearance enhancement up to 31%. The recent studies, however, use only pulsatile dialysate flow because the pulsatile circulation of blood flow has issues concerning blood supply and vascular access148. The mechanisms proposed for CL enhancement are enhanced mass transfer via boundary layer disruption and unsteady shear at the membrane surface143,148. However, there aren’t any computational studies yet to back these hypotheses. Current modelling isn’t adequate. Simon (2017)’s Laplace transform-based technique could help in achieving transient solutions effectively149. Ortega et al.(2025)150 applied a pulsatile pressure based on a Fourier series to mimic the natural blood flow conditions.

Improvements from dialyser-like applications

Models of similar applications can also be a source of inspiration. For instance, Krantz et al. (1997)151 modelled the sinusoidal axial vibration of the hollow-fibre membrane in an inside-out oxygenator to show that Ko increased at least a factor of 2.65. A similar study by ref. 152 showed that smaller din fibres are more effective. Modelling techniques used by these studies could be helpful for anyone who wants to thoroughly study the effect of vibration on haemodialyser performance. Intended for application in membrane distillation, ref. 153 showed that the addition of baffles augmented the heat transfer coefficient by a factor of 6 by promoting enhanced mixing. Such modelling can be useful in understanding the effect of baffles in haemodialysers. In a similar setup, ref. 154 studied fibre–fibre interaction and found two causing effects, 'bundle resistance' and 'permeate competition' that changed as the fibre distance varied. Such studies can shed some light on inter-fibre flow and channelling observed in haemodialysers. A similar computational study for a dialyser hasn’t appeared yet. Intended for application in membrane contactors, a few recent studies can invoke possibilities of cross-flow (perpendicular to fibres) dialysate flow operation mode in haemodialysers155–157.

Machine learning

Developing data-driven or hybrid physics-informed machine learning (ML) models to optimise membrane properties and dialyser geometry can be promising. Machine learning approaches have already enabled several advances in membrane separation processes. For example, ref. 158 trained an ML algorithm to use a topological, path-based hash of the repeating unit of a gas-separation polymer membrane. For the same application, ref. 159 implemented an ML model to discover innovative polymers by extracting valuable insights into the contributions of different chemical moieties to permeability and selectivity. Such modelling techniques may inform the discovery of novel membrane polymers to effectively clear small- and middle-sized solutes and PBUT.

Outlook

Despite the limitations of current hollow fibre haemodialyser modelling approaches, recent initiatives—including the US FDA’s support for computational modelling160—underscore the growing acceptance of in silico studies for dialyser innovation. Advances in hardware and numerical methods will likely further reduce computational barriers, enabling more robust haemodialyser simulations. Ultimately, improved computational models stand to enhance dialysis therapy, bringing designs closer to an ideal artificial kidney.

Moreover, hollow fibre filtration technology is also used in other medical technologies, including extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, combined lung and kidney support devices, heart-lung bypass machines, and bioartificial livers. The advances reviewed here may have implications for these related technologies as well.
