Periosteal distraction-related treatments for diabetic foot/diabetic foot ulcer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shaowen Zhong, Quan Sun, Lili Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriosteal distraction (PD) has been used as a salvage strategy for diabetic foot (DF) and diabetic foot ulcer (DFU), yet its relative effectiveness compared with other approaches is still unclear.MethodsUp to March 17, 2026, seven databases were searched for comparative studies assessing PD-related treatments in DF/DFU. Twenty-six studies were included for qualitative synthesis, and 14 contributed to the primary comparative evidence set. The studies were organized into three categories: PD-based interventions versus conventional treatment, pure PD versus transverse tibial transport (TTT), and PD-inclusive multimodal regimens versus non-equivalent regimens.ResultsA formal pooled meta-analysis could not be performed for Group A, so this group was synthesized descriptively. For Group B, pure PD showed no statistically significant differences from TTT with respect to ABI, pain, or adverse events. For Group C, PD-containing multimodal regimens were linked with beneficial short-term changes in surrogate perfusion-related measures (ABI, skin temperature, toe oxygen) and pain. However, when wound/ulcer healing time was pooled, the results suggested only a non-significant tendency favoring PD-containing multimodal regimens.ConclusionsCurrent findings do not support a clear superiority of pure PD over TTT. Apparent advantages were mainly seen in PD-containing multimodal regimens and were largely restricted to short-term surrogate outcomes. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that PD alone has an independent effect, and robust limb-salvage endpoints remain insufficiently reported.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06920-2.Keywords: Diabetic foot, Diabetic foot ulcer, Periosteal distraction, Transverse tibial transport, Limb salvage, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Diabetic foot and diabetic foot ulcer are major drivers of disability, hospital admission, infection, and lower-limb amputation in individuals with diabetes mellitus. Despite improvements in wound care, infection control, revascularization, and offloading [1], some patients still develop non-healing ulcers and ongoing tissue ischemia [2–5].

Periosteal distraction (PD) has gained attention as a reconstructive method designed to enhance distal perfusion and promote recovery of wounds in DF/DFU [6]. In real-world care, PD may be applied as a stand-alone technique or combined with additional therapies, such as negative-pressure wound therapy, antibiotic-loaded bone cement, platelet-rich plasma, or vacuum sealing drainage. This variety has led to a heterogeneous collection of comparative studies, differing in design, comparators, and outcome definitions.

In routine clinical management, certain patients with DF/DFU remain difficult to treat despite standard wound care and, when appropriate, attempted revascularization. Revascularization below the ankle may be technically impossible or only partially achieved, and restoring patency of macrovessels does not always result in adequate tissue perfusion and healing. This mismatch between angiographic success and local tissue recovery offers a clinically reasonable basis for adjunctive approaches such as PD [7–9].

A key issue in the literature is that PD has been compared not only with conventional care, but also with transverse tibial transport (TTT), another tibia-based bony manipulation strategy for limb salvage in DF/DFU [10]. Additionally, some studies have assessed PD-containing multimodal regimens against combined treatments that are not equivalent, rather than against PD alone. These discrepancies hinder direct synthesis of evidence and call for a clinically grounded grouping approach.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess PD-related treatments in DF/DFU using a pre-established framework: PD-based interventions versus conventional treatment, pure PD versus TTT, and PD-containing multimodal regimens versus non-equivalent regimens. Within the overall body of included evidence, only a subset of comparative studies ultimately provided the main comparative evidence set for structured quantitative assessment. By separating these clinically distinct comparisons, we aimed to deliver a more interpretable summary of the current evidence and to clarify where potential benefits appear most consistently—without mixing isolated PD effects with the broader effects of multimodal strategies.

Methods

Study design

This work was conducted as a systematic review and meta-analysis of comparative studies evaluating periosteal distraction-related treatments for diabetic foot or diabetic foot ulcer [11]. The review protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251265941).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they satisfied the following: (1) they included patients with diabetic foot or diabetic foot ulcer; (2) they assessed PD either as an independent procedure or as part of a combination regimen; (3) they contained a comparative group; and (4) they reported at least one clinically meaningful outcome related to perfusion, pain, wound healing, limb salvage, or adverse events.

Studies were excluded when they were background or theoretical papers rather than original clinical research, when they enrolled excessively broad and mixed lower-limb ischemic ulcer populations that did not match the review question, when they represented overlapping reports for which a more informative or methodologically preferred version was available, or when they consisted solely of trial registration data without any outcome reporting.

Information sources and search strategy

An initial comprehensive search was performed in seven databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP. Full details regarding search strategies, search dates, and the number of retrieved records are provided in Supplementary Table S1. A supplementary Scopus search was also conducted, limited to records available up to March 17, 2026, to maintain alignment with the original search period. No additional eligible studies were found.

Study selection and grouping framework

All retrieved citations were de-duplicated across databases, screened by title/abstract, and assessed for full-text eligibility. The final PRISMA numbers were: 357 records identified; 177 unique records after exact de-duplication; 125 records excluded at the title/abstract screening stage; 52 records retained for further review; 30 candidate reports remaining after manual merging; 30 full-text reports assessed for eligibility; 4 full-text reports excluded; and 26 studies included in the qualitative synthesis. Among these, 14 formed the main comparative evidence set. Nevertheless, formal pooled meta-analysis was possible only for Groups B and C. Group A was kept in the review but synthesized narratively, because the number of studies that could be combined was limited and the available outcomes were not sufficiently comparable.

Eligible comparative studies were categorized into three pre-specified comparison frameworks: Group A (PD-based interventions versus conventional treatment), Group B (pure PD versus transverse tibial transport [TTT]), and Group C (PD-containing multimodal regimens versus non-equivalent comparator regimens). Studies meeting eligibility criteria but contributing insufficiently comparable or extractable data to these frameworks were retained for narrative synthesis only.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using pre-defined structured extraction forms. One reviewer carried out the initial title/abstract and full-text screening and extracted study-level information. A second reviewer checked eligibility determinations against the source reports, verified extracted outcome data, and assessed risk-of-bias judgments. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, with adjudication by a third reviewer when needed. Extracted items included study identifier, publication year, study design, sample size, ulcer severity or staging information when available, intervention and comparator details, follow-up timepoints, analysis unit, and all outcome data that were judged potentially relevant for either quantitative or narrative synthesis. For continuous outcomes, means and standard deviations were extracted directly whenever reported. When continuous results were provided as medians and interquartile ranges, conversion to approximate mean and standard deviation was performed only if the reporting format and sample size allowed a defensible transformation, and only for outcomes preselected for meta-analysis. Outcomes with incompatible constructs, unclear definitions, or inadequate statistics were kept for narrative synthesis.

Particular care was taken to harmonize outcomes. Endpoints were not pooled purely because they had superficially similar labels when they were clinically non-equivalent. Specifically, wound/ulcer healing time and bone-window healing time were treated as separate constructs, and composite outcomes such as total effective rate were analyzed separately from healing rate, limb salvage, or amputation outcomes. Similarly, patient-level and limb-level data were not combined.

For multi-arm studies, shared groups were handled according to standard meta-analytic procedures to prevent double-counting. If the same intervention or comparator arm contributed to more than one comparison within a pooled analysis, the shared sample was divided before inclusion in the analysis dataset (Fig. 1).

Risk-of-bias assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated in line with each study’s design. Randomized trials were appraised with the updated Cochrane risk-of-bias instrument for randomized trials (RoB 2), while non-randomized comparative studies were assessed with ROBINS-I [12, 13]. Because the evidence base included both randomized and non-randomized studies, risk-of-bias judgments were interpreted using the relevant design-specific framework rather than forcing them into one common pooled scoring approach. The primary methodological concerns were related to confounding, lack of randomized treatment assignment, insufficient blinding, inconsistent outcome definitions, and limited information on how missing data were handled.

Outcomes

The prespecified outcomes of interest covered perfusion-related variables, symptom-related outcomes, healing-related outcomes, limb outcomes, and safety outcomes. For quantitative pooling, the primary continuous outcomes were ABI, distal oxygenation parameters, pain scores, skin temperature, and wound/ulcer healing duration. Distal oxygenation outcomes were pooled only when, after harmonization, the underlying construct and measurement scale were judged sufficiently compatible. For dichotomous pooling, the main outcomes were adverse events, binary healing-related endpoints, major amputation as reported in a comparable manner, and the total effective rate. The total effective rate was considered a composite response endpoint, not a direct measure of wound healing or limb salvage. Procedure-related variables such as operative time and blood loss were extracted descriptively when available, but were not treated as core efficacy endpoints because their direct clinical relevance to limb salvage and wound recovery is limited, and the comparator procedures differ inherently.

Given heterogeneity in how outcomes were reported across studies, outcomes were first mapped into clinically meaningful categories and then checked for compatibility regarding construct, time window, and analysis unit. Healing-related outcomes were limited to clearly stated wound/ulcer endpoints and were not merged with bone-window healing. Major amputation was defined as amputation above the ankle when explicitly reported. Safety outcomes included procedure-related complications such as incision-site infection, pin-tract infection, osteomyelitis, osteotomy-site fracture, skin necrosis, and delayed healing of the transported bone segment when reported. If outcomes could not be aligned without introducing inconsistency at the construct level, they were not pooled; instead, they were summarized narratively. The operational definitions used to support harmonization are provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was carried out only when studies were sufficiently comparable in relation to the clinical question, outcome definition, follow-up window, and analysis unit. Continuous outcomes were pooled as mean differences (MDs) only when the constructs and numeric scales matched directly. Dichotomous outcomes were pooled as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals. Throughout, inverse-variance random-effects models were used, employing restricted maximum-likelihood estimation for between-study variance and the Hartung-Knapp adjustment for inference [14, 15]. The heterogeneity estimator was selected in advance to avoid dependence on simpler default estimators that could lead to substantially different pooled conclusions.

Analyses were conducted separately for Groups A, B, and C because the underlying treatment comparisons were clinically distinct. Group A involved PD-based interventions versus conventional care; Group B compared pure PD with TTT; and Group C evaluated PD-containing multimodal regimens against non-equivalent comparator regimens. Pooling across groups was not performed.

Follow-up timepoints were harmonized into clinically comparable short-term and mid-term windows where feasible. Short-term windows were used to capture early physiologic and symptom-related responses following intervention, while mid-term windows were used when studies reported early wound recovery or functional follow-up at roughly 12 weeks to 3 months. For outcomes reported at discordant timepoints, only sufficiently aligned time windows were pooled; incompatible constructs, scales, or analysis levels were reported narratively rather than formally pooled.

For multi-arm studies, shared arms were divided prior to pooling to prevent double-counting. For binary outcomes with zero-frequency cells, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied when necessary. For pain outcomes, all studies contributing to each formal pain analysis used 0–10 numerical pain scores; in Group C, a sensitivity analysis was additionally conducted after excluding the single study that used NRS instead of VAS, to determine whether differences in measurement scale materially changed the pooled estimate. Design-stratified meta-analysis was considered for retained outcomes, but most outcome-by-design strata included too few studies to support stable random-effects estimation. Funnel-plot or small-study effect analyses were not conducted because no pooled outcome included 10 or more studies.

Results

Study selection

Database searching identified 357 records in total. After exact deduplication across databases, 177 unique records remained. Screening titles and abstracts excluded 125 records, leaving 52 records for further consideration. Following manual merging of reports, 30 candidate reports were retained for full-text assessment. All 30 full-text reports were examined; 4 were excluded, and 26 studies were included in the qualitative synthesis. Of these, 14 contributed to the main comparative evidence set; however, formal pooled meta-analysis was only feasible for Groups B and C, while Group A was retained for structured narrative synthesis because the number of includable studies was limited and the available outcomes were not sufficiently comparable.

Included studies

The 26 studies included in the qualitative synthesis represent the complete evidence base for this review. Among them, 14 formed the main comparative evidence set eligible for quantitative analysis because they reported comparative outcomes relevant to the prespecified A/B/C frameworks. Ultimately, formal pooled meta-analysis was feasible only for Groups B and C, whereas Group A was retained and synthesized narratively because the number of studies that could be combined was limited and the available outcomes were not sufficiently comparable. Within the main comparative evidence set, 3 studies were assigned to Group A [17–19], 4 to Group B [20–23], and 7 to Group C [24–30]. The remaining 12 studies were used to inform the review narratively because they did not provide enough comparability or extractable data for the prespecified comparative frameworks [31–42]. Overall, 6 studies were randomized and 8 were non-randomized comparative studies. Group A assessed PD-based interventions versus conventional treatment. Group B compared pure PD with TTT. Group C evaluated PD-containing multimodal regimens against non-equivalent comparator regimens, including combinations involving negative-pressure wound therapy, antibiotic-loaded bone cement, and other adjunctive treatments. The characteristics of studies contributing to the main comparative evidence set are summarized in Table 1. Across these studies, all reports were from China; most were single-center; six were randomized and eight were non-randomized comparative studies; and ulcer severity generally ranged from Wagner grade 2 to 4, although some studies also included earlier-stage disease.

Risk of bias

The evidence base included both randomized and non-randomized comparative studies. In Group A, at least one study reported random sequence generation, allocation concealment, and blinded outcome assessment, whereas the remaining studies aligned more closely with non-randomized comparative designs. Group B mainly comprised retrospective or non-randomized comparative studies and was judged overall to carry a substantial risk of bias, particularly concerning confounding and outcome measurement. Group C included both randomized and non-randomized studies, with methodological quality varying across comparisons. A detailed summary at the study level is provided in Table 2; domain-level judgments are shown in Supplementary Table S4; and the overall visualization is presented in Fig. 2, which maintains design-specific interpretation by displaying separate RoB 2 and ROBINS-I panels.

Quantitative synthesis

No formal pooled meta-analysis was feasible for Group A. Although three Group A studies were part of the main comparative evidence set considered for quantitative analysis, the number of combinable studies was too small, and the available continuous outcomes were derived mainly from individual studies with different biomarker constructs and non-overlapping endpoint structures. Therefore, Group A was retained within the review and synthesized narratively rather than excluded. Individual Group A studies reported improvements in selected perfusion-related or clinical indicators versus conventional treatment; however, the evidence was too limited and structurally inconsistent to support a pooled inference [17–19]. Accordingly, the barrier to quantitative synthesis was not heterogeneity alone, but rather the lack of a sufficiently shared outcome structure to yield a clinically interpretable pooled estimate.

In Group B, the pooled analyses did not show any clear superiority of PD over TTT [20–23]. For mid-term ABI (k = 3), there was no significant difference between the groups (MD = −0.016, 95% CI −0.223 to 0.192; P = 0.7751; I2 = 88.0%). Similarly, short-term pain score (k = 2; MD = 0.266, 95% CI −1.323 to 1.854; P = 0.2801; I2 = 64.3%) and mid-term pain score (k = 2; MD = −0.559, 95% CI −8.754 to 7.636; P = 0.5453; I2 = 98.7%) did not differ significantly. The pooled adverse-events analysis (k = 4) likewise found no meaningful difference between PD and TTT (RR = 0.480, 95% CI 0.075 to 3.091; P = 0.2986; I2 = 43.4%). Across the included Group B studies, complications reported for PD were mainly limited to isolated incision or pin-site infections, whereas TTT cohorts also reported pin-tract infection, skin necrosis in the transport area, delayed union of the transported bone segment, fracture at the osteotomy site, and osteomyelitis. Study-level safety information is summarized in Supplementary Table S7. No formal pooled estimate was provided for Group B distal-oxygenation outcomes, because the included studies did not report constructs and measurement scales that were sufficiently compatible to justify a defensible mean-difference synthesis.

In Group C, multimodal regimens that included PD were linked to significantly improved short-term perfusion-related outcomes [24–30]. A statistically significant rise in short-term ABI (k = 5) favored the PD-combination group (MD = 0.104, 95% CI 0.067 to 0.141; P = 0.0015; I2 = 0.0%). Short-term skin temperature (k = 4; MD = 2.149, 95% CI 1.731 to 2.568; P = 0.0005; I2 = 0.0%) and short-term toe oxygen (k = 3; MD = 2.852, 95% CI 0.638 to 5.066; P = 0.0311; I2 = 0.0%) were also significantly higher in the PD-combination group than in the comparator group. In addition, short-term pain score (k = 5) was significantly lower for the PD-combination group (MD = −1.614, 95% CI −2.512 to −0.716; P = 0.0075; I2 = 86.8%).

Regarding healing-related outcomes in Group C, the pooled wound/ulcer healing time (k = 2) suggested a non-significant tendency in favor of PD-containing multimodal therapy (MD = −1.793, 95% CI −6.073 to 2.487; P = 0.1182; I2 = 30.3%). Because definitions of healing-related outcomes differed across studies, wound/ulcer healing time was analyzed separately from bone-window healing and other binary healing endpoints. The pooled analysis of the total effective rate (k = 3) favored the PD-combination group (RR = 1.221, 95% CI 1.102 to 1.354; P = 0.0140; I2 = 0.0%), although this composite endpoint was interpreted with caution. A comprehensive presentation of the pooled results is provided in Table 3.

Heterogeneity and sensitivity analysis

Considerable heterogeneity was seen across several pooled outcomes, especially within Group B. I2 was high for the pooled analyses of mid-term ABI and pain-related outcomes. Distal-oxygenation variables for Group B were not retained as formal pooled estimates because the underlying constructs were not sufficiently compatible across the included studies.

In Group C, heterogeneity was minimal for ABI, skin temperature, toe oxygen, and total effective rate, while pain outcomes remained heterogeneous. After excluding the single study that used NRS instead of VAS, the pooled pain effect continued to be statistically significant (k = 4; MD = −1.732, 95% CI −2.945 to −0.519; P = 0.0200; I2 = 90.0%).

Wound/ulcer healing time and bone-window healing time were not pooled together because they reflect different constructs. No formal assessment of publication bias was undertaken, since the pooled outcomes included only 2 to 5 studies.

GRADE evaluations for the principal pooled outcomes are summarized in Supplementary Table S5. Across the retained pooled outcomes, certainty varied from very low to low, with downgrading driven mainly by study limitations, inconsistency, and imprecision.

Discussion

Principal findings

This review delivers a structured synthesis of PD-related interventions in DF/DFU by separating comparison frameworks that are clinically distinct. The contrast between the qualitative evidence base (26 studies) and the main quantitative evidence set (14 studies) reflects heterogeneity across the field rather than selective exclusion: several studies were directly relevant to the review question but could not be formally pooled due to non-overlapping outcomes, limited compatibility for combination, or inadequate extractable data. Group A clearly illustrates this issue—although those studies informed the review, they could only be synthesized narratively. The central conclusion is that any apparent advantages were not consistent across all comparison contexts.

When pure PD was compared directly with TTT (Group B), the evidence available at present did not identify significant differences in ABI, pain, or adverse events. For this comparison, distal-oxygenation outcomes were not appropriate for formal pooling because there was insufficient construct compatibility among studies. The retained pooled estimates were accompanied by moderate-to-very-high heterogeneity and relied largely on non-randomized comparative studies. Therefore, the current body of evidence does not support a strong conclusion that pure PD is superior to TTT.

In contrast, PD-containing multimodal regimens (Group C) demonstrated more consistent short-term advantages over their comparators, particularly for ABI, skin temperature, toe oxygen, and pain. These findings suggest that PD-containing multimodal regimens may be associated with favorable short-term changes when integrated into broader multimodal limb-salvage strategies. However, these findings should not be generalized to pure PD versus TTT, nor to PD-based interventions versus conventional care, and they do not demonstrate the independent efficacy of standalone PD.

Interpretation of group B findings

The lack of a clear difference between pure PD and TTT is striking. The two procedures may share overlapping biological objectives, such as promoting local vascular remodeling and improving distal microcirculation, which could hinder demonstration of direct superiority in small studies. Methodological shortcomings also play a role: the Group B evidence base is constrained by mixed study designs, retrospective analyses, inconsistent reporting of timepoints and endpoints, and incompatible distal-oxygenation constructs across studies.

From a practical perspective, the existing Group B evidence suggests that PD could be a plausible limb-salvage option rather than one that is clearly superior. Thus, the decision between PD and TTT is likely to depend more on local expertise, technical familiarity, patient anatomy, and procedure-specific risk profiles than on any expectation of clear superiority based on the published comparative evidence. Although pooled adverse-event estimates did not differ significantly, the qualitative complication pattern indicates that TTT may be associated with a wider range of bone- and transport-related morbidity.

Interpretation of group C findings

Overall, Group C generated more favorable results, and for several short-term perfusion-related endpoints the effects were highly consistent. Improvements in ABI, skin temperature, and toe oxygen all moved in the same direction and were accompanied by minimal heterogeneity. These findings imply that PD-containing multimodal regimens may be linked to favorable short-term perfusion-related changes, and the decrease in short-term pain may represent symptomatic improvement alongside broader wound management. These findings may be of clinical interest, but they should still be viewed as surrogate signals rather than evidence of durable healing or a limb-salvage advantage. Indicators such as local oxygenation and skin perfusion have been associated with healing potential in ischemic diabetic foot ulcers, but this association should not be taken here as proof of sustained clinical benefit [43–45].

Nonetheless, Group C should be interpreted with caution. Comparator regimens were not uniform, additional components varied across studies, and some outcomes—such as total effective rate—were composite endpoints with potentially inconsistent definitions. Because total effective rate is a non-standardized composite outcome with variable definitions across studies, it should be treated only as supportive contextual evidence rather than a robust indicator of wound healing or limb salvage. Accordingly, the positive signal in Group C is more consistent with benefit from a broader multimodal treatment strategy than with proof of an isolated PD effect. The absence of a statistically significant reduction in wound/ulcer healing time should not be construed as evidence of no effect. Healing time is a more distal and multifactorial outcome than short-term perfusion- or symptom-related measures, and the pooled estimate was based on only two studies with limited precision. Robust limb-salvage endpoints—including major amputation, reintervention, and recurrence—remain insufficiently reported in the current literature.

Clinical implications

Clinically, PD may be most applicable not as a universal alternative for all patients with DF/DFU, but as a potential adjunctive limb-salvage strategy in specific circumstances. Such situations may include patients in whom distal revascularization, including below-ankle revascularization, is not feasible or remains incomplete, as well as patients for whom restoring macrovessel flow does not result in satisfactory local tissue recovery. In these contexts, the short-term perfusion-related signals observed with PD-containing multimodal regimens may be of clinical interest, though they remain surrogate outcomes and are not sufficient to demonstrate an independent PD effect or durable limb-salvage benefit.

At the same time, the current evidence does not justify a routine preference for pure PD over TTT when both procedures are technically available. Instead, procedure selection should remain individualized and embedded within a broader multidisciplinary limb-salvage strategy.

Heterogeneity, endpoint construction, and methodological caution

Several methodological concerns deserve emphasis. First, high heterogeneity across multiple pooled outcomes indicates that the available literature is not fully standardized. Differences in Wagner grade, baseline ischemia severity, adjunctive wound treatments, perioperative care, and outcome timing likely contributed to variability. Differences in ulcer severity are particularly important, because short-term perfusion-related measurements and healing trajectories may differ systematically between earlier-stage ulcers and more advanced Wagner 3–4 disease. Second, the evidence base combined randomized and non-randomized comparative studies; although tools specific to design were used to assess risk of bias, most retained pooled outcomes included too few studies within each design category to support a stable design-stratified random-effects meta-analysis. Third, some studies used scale-based or composite outcomes that are difficult to harmonize across reports. Fourth, healing-related outcomes required careful separation of constructs; for example, wound/ulcer healing time and bone-window healing time should not be pooled together. The persistence of high heterogeneity in pain outcomes after sensitivity analysis suggests that differences in measurement scales were not the only driver; variability in ulcer severity, co-interventions, analgesic practices, and timing of assessment likely also contributed.

Another concern is that many of the included studies were small and conducted in single centers, which raises issues regarding precision, external validity, and residual confounding.

Strengths and limitations

This review shows multiple strengths. Its search and screening strategy covered several English- and Chinese-language databases, and the supplementary materials preserved the comprehensive strategies and complete retrieval logs. The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO in advance. The review also used a grouping scheme designed to keep clinical meaning intact, rather than pooling every PD-related study indiscriminately. Multi-arm trials were managed transparently to prevent double-counting, and a sensitivity analysis was conducted to address heterogeneity in pain outcomes.

The study nonetheless has several key limitations. First, the number of studies that could be directly combined was limited, especially for Group A. Second, study designs were not uniform, and many of the comparisons were based on non-randomized evidence. Third, all included publications were from China, and most were single-center studies; consequently, the observed effects may be influenced by local patient selection, perioperative practice patterns, and publication context, so whether these findings generalize to other populations and health systems remains unclear. Fourth, the comparator regimens varied clinically, particularly in the conventional-care and multimodal-comparator contexts. Fifth, substantial heterogeneity was present across multiple outcomes, especially within Group B and for pain-related outcomes. Sixth, some outcomes depended on composite measures or constructs that were not standardized, which restricts confidence in the results. Seventh, formal assessment of publication bias or small-study effects was not feasible because each pooled outcome contained too few studies. Finally, because not every study reported the same endpoints at the same timepoints, several relevant studies could only be incorporated into a narrative synthesis rather than a formal meta-analysis; this limitation reflects heterogeneity across the field rather than a lack of relevance.

Implications for future research

Future research should emphasize head-to-head comparisons that are methodologically rigorous and use standardized definitions of outcomes. Key priorities include aligning healing endpoints, stating the unit of analysis clearly, using standardized follow-up windows, and prospectively evaluating outcomes related to perfusion, wound healing, limb salvage, reintervention, and safety. Trials should further separate, more explicitly, the effects attributable to PD itself from those arising from co-interventions used within combination regimens.

Conclusions

The available evidence does not show a clear advantage of standalone PD over TTT for diabetic foot or diabetic foot ulcer. Apparent improvements were mainly seen in multimodal regimens that included PD, and the benefits were largely restricted to short-term surrogate outcomes related to perfusion, pain, and composite treatment response. These findings should be interpreted as signals from multimodal treatment packages rather than proof of an independent PD effect, and they continue to be limited by study heterogeneity, mixed study designs, frequent reliance on composite or non-standardized endpoints, and generally low certainty of the evidence. Clinically robust limb-salvage outcomes, including major amputation, recurrence, and reintervention, remain insufficiently reported. More rigorous comparative studies are needed before firm clinical recommendations can be issued.
