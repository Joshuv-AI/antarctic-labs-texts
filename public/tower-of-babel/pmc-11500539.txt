Nutritional interventions for indigenous adults in Canada - opportunities to sustain health and cultural practices: a scoping review
Authors: Ashley Amson, Jessica Zhang, Levi Frehlich, Yunqi Ji, Carly Checholik, Patricia Doyle-Baker, Lynden Crowshoe, Kerry McBrien, Sonja Wicklum
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIndigenous People in Canada possess rich cultural traditions, intertwined with a strong connection to nature. However, colonisation and contemporary challenges have given rise to changes in lifestyle and culture, resulting in health and nutrition disparities within these communities. The goal of this review was to explore the available literature of existing Indigenous nutrition programs for adults in Canada. Arksey and O’Malley’s scoping review protocol was used to conduct the search between July 2020 and February 2023. Articles were obtained from MEDLINE (Ovid), PsycInfo, Embase (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCO), Web of Science, Scopus (Elsevier), Canadian Business and Current Affairs (Proquest), and Google Scholar. We identified 24 publications, with 19 being unique interventions. Common themes among programs included integrating traditional foods and cultural values, adapted programming to local needs, empowering community members, using a multidisciplinary collaboration, and leveraging social activities, all of which highlight the need for holistic strategies amid complex historical, social, and environmental factors. Overall, this review emphasises the need for continued support and development of Indigenous-led nutritional initiatives to promote health and well-being among Indigenous adults in Canada. Ensuring culturally relevant and sustainable solutions is crucial for addressing nutritional health disparities and fostering long-term positive outcomes.KEYWORDS: Indigenous, Canada, nutrition, interventions, community-based, wholistic

Introduction

Significant health and economic disparities exist between Indigenous and non-Indigenous populations in Canada because of colonisation and the marginalisation of cultural practices of Indigenous People [1,2]. Indigenous populations have endured social, health, economic, and political hardships imposed through Canadian policy, resulting in intergenerational trauma. Consequently, Indigenous People are overburdened by health inequities, resulting in disproportionate rates of chronic diseases.

Indigenous People in Canada have undergone a nutritional transition whereby traditional diets have become unavailable for most [3]. Several factors have contributed to this transition, including the westernisation of Indigenous diets and climate change [4,5]. The westernisation of Indigenous diets, whereby a reduction of traditional foods and increased dependence on nutrient poor foods, has laid the groundwork for the emergence of obesity and associated chronic health conditions [4]. Nutrient poor foods are those high in salt, saturated and trans fats, and sugar. In pre-colonial times, Indigenous People relied on traditional foods sourced from their local surroundings. However, as the twentieth century unfolded, the substantial impact of colonial influences led to a growing dependence on commercially available market foods [6]. Access barriers also exist, stemming from industrial food systems characterised by limited transportation infrastructure and high costs of importing fresh produce, impacting availability and affordability [7]. Further, nutritious food can be inaccessible or unaffordable for some Indigenous communities, particularly those which are northern, remote, or isolated [7].

Accessing traditional foods can be challenging due to environmental degradation, climate change, and regulatory constraints that affect hunting, fishing, and gathering practices crucial to Indigenous diets [5]. These challenges are compounded by westernised practices noted above. As a result, it can be difficult for Indigenous communities to maintain their traditional diets, which are essential for their cultural identity and health. These barriers necessitate targeted interventions and policy changes to support the sustainability and accessibility of traditional food sources for Indigenous peoples.

Improvements in quality of diet have been shown to reduce the risk of chronic diseases [8]. Short-term effectiveness of nutrition interventions among Indigenous populations is evident however, due to a lack of cultural adaptation there has been limited long-term success [9,10]. Additionally, nutrition interventions are challenging because they aim to change behaviours that are deeply embedded and reinforced in various social and cultural contexts. Factors such as historical trauma, socio-economic disparities, and the ongoing impacts of colonisation further complicate the adoption of new dietary practices. As such, a review of Indigenous-led or focused nutrition interventions are needed to evaluate existing programs and synthesise insights from these approaches.

Presently, it is unclear what nutrition interventions have existed and continue to exist for Indigenous adults in Canada. Therefore, this scoping review was primarily undertaken to examine and synthesise existing nutrition interventions for Indigenous adults in Canada and secondly, to describe process indicators for each initiative, highlighting pertinent points for future nutrition interventions that aim to support Indigenous adults in Canada.

Materials and methods

This scoping review was conducted using Arksey and O’Malley’s scoping review protocol [11]. We chose to use a scoping review to map the existing literature on this topic, identify key concepts, gaps, and types of evidence available. We did so to provide a broad overview and inform future research directions. This approach included identifying the research question, selecting studies relevant to the research question, and charting the data summarising the results. The protocol was registered and is publicly accessible on Open Science Frameworks - https://osf.io/mygnq/files/osfstorage/64ec1123918d2d1381e3ed7c [12].

Identification of relevant studies

The search strategy was created in consultation with an experienced librarian from the University of Alberta and refined collectively as a team. The search terms for Indigenous populations in Canada was adapted from the University of Alberta Health Sciences Search Filters for Indigenous People in Canada [13]. The initial search was conducted in July 2020 reflecting the search filters available at that time and no starting date limit was set. The University of Alberta updated their filters in October 2021 and a renewed search was conducted in February 2023, which did not yield any new results. No language or study design restrictions were applied. The search was conducted in English.

Original peer-reviewed articles were obtained from the following online databases: MEDLINE (Ovid), PsycInfo, Embase (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCO), Web of Science, Scopus (Elsevier), Canadian Business and Current Affairs (Proquest), and Google Scholar (Google). To be included, original peer-reviewed articles had to meet the following criteria: Canadian-based study, for Indigenous adults (aged 18+), and reporting on a nutrition-focused intervention. The following exclusion criteria was applied: non-intervention (i.e. framework only), non-adult population, non-Canadian population, non-Indigenous population, institutionalised population, no further information found (i.e. newspaper article but no further information accessible), and no nutrition or diet-related component.

Selection of relevant studies

All search results were collected and uploaded into Covidence®, and duplicates were removed automatically or identified manually [14]. A research assistant (JZ) and two researchers (LF, SW) conducted screening and data charting. Title and abstract screening were independently screened with one vote required for inclusion or exclusion. For full text review, two independent votes were required for inclusion or exclusion. All conflicts were discussed as a team and resolved using consensus. A data-charting template was created by all three reviewers to determine what information to extract using Covidence. Reviewers independently charted the data while continuously updating the data-charting template as informed by results.

Data extraction from included studies

Selected articles in this review had the following data recorded when it was available: author (s), year, location (province or territory), program name (sample size, duration of intervention), program goal, and nutrition-related intervention. We further delineated any information that was reported or described about the community uptake and effectiveness based on study outcomes and measures, limitations, and a description of potentially “promising practices” (Table 1).

Results

The search terms generated 11,918 citations after duplicates were removed (See Annex A for Medline (OVID) strategy). From these, 284 were assessed for eligibility. Out of the 284 eligible studies, 260 studies were excluded for the following reasons: 196 were non-interventional, 31 had no nutrition-related component, 14 targeted a non-adult population, 7 were unretrievable, 6 were non-Indigenous focussed, 3 were duplicates, 2 targeted institutionalised populations, and 1 was non-Canadian (Figure 1). Canadian Business and Current Affairs was used to search grey literature, but no additional studies were identified. A governmental evaluation, the Nutrition North Canada Program, was also included within this search. We included 24 peer-reviewed studies, of which 19 were unique interventions that described nutrition interventions for Indigenous adults aged 18 years or older in Canada.

Identification criteria of included studies [38].

Our search produced 24 articles that fit our inclusion criteria with 19 being unique interventions as some researchers commented on a similar program (e.g. Nuxalk Food and Nutrition Program). Of these 19, 16 were comprised of community-based, participatory research [6,15,16,19–21,23–33,35–37], two were at the household or family level [18,22], and one was a federal, subsidy program [34]. One intervention was offered virtually as an online nutrition course [21], while the other 18 were delivered in-person. Literature ranged from 1983 to 2022, with 84% of studies being conducted prior to 2018. Intervention durations ranged from one month to five years. Five interventions were multi-site, with the rest focussed on a specific community or location and five programs were created explicitly for the goal of diabetes prevention, management and/or control [18–20,23,37].

Community uptake

Community uptake was described using participation numbers and attrition rates. Effectiveness was determined based on a wide variety of evaluation outcomes and measures, such as changes in attitudes about healthy nutrition, nutritional knowledge acquisition, dietary consumption including nutrient intake, and awareness of community resources. For example, one intervention resulted in positive attitude changes with 50% of participants setting exercise and diet goals; however, no statistically significant physiological improvements were reported [18].

Two studies reinforced the importance of consuming traditional foods. Those who consumed traditional foods were found to have better overall diets and health [6,16,17]. Two studies documented social benefits, such as community gardens and bringing people together [29,32]. These benefits include the development of trust and social capital (e.g. networks, relationships, and shared values that lead to mutual support within a community) [29,32]. Three studies noted the programs popularity within the community and overall positive reception [30,33,37], while the results of six studies highlighted diet changes among participants [6,17,22,23,25,31,35]. Examples of diet changes included improvement in all nutrient measures in women 20–40 years old [17], statistically significant changes in eating adequate servings of fruits and vegetables [35] and promoted nutrients like fibre [25], and an overall increase in healthy food purchases [23]. Seven interventions increased program awareness, with some leading to community members being more knowledgeable of grocery store signs and more likely to utilise community-based food programs, and others led to modest dietary intake changes [6,15,16,22,24,25]. Four studies did not report any community uptake or effectiveness [19–21,34].

Noted limitations within studies

Noted limitations that were mentioned within the interventions included evaluation measures (e.g. specific criteria, metrics, or standards used to assess the effectiveness, impact, or success of a program) [6,33]; lack of significant lifestyle changes [15,18,19]; self-reporting bias [18,20,22]; participant difficulty completing questionnaires [35]; short-term study/follow-up period [22,35]; low follow-up response rate due to lack of data collectors and/or lack of exposure to research in these communities [23]; small sample size [25,35]; staffing difficulties (e.g. staff training, high turnover rates, constrained timelines to train part-time interventional staff) [20,23]; participant attrition [20,31]; personal commitments/time management of participants (e.g. child care) [6,21] and issues with internet accessibility [21].

Noted Strengths within Studies

There were several potential strengths with the most common being a) the incorporation of traditional foods and cultural values, such as the use of Elders or knowledge keepers, feasts and ceremonies [16,17,25,35], b) adapting programming to local needs, c) empowering community members [23,24,30,35–37], d) multidisciplinary collaboration between stakeholders and community [25,32,33], and e) leveraging social activities [29,32,33].

Geographic location

Identified interventions were grouped by geography. Most interventions were conducted in British Columbia (31.6%), followed by Ontario (15.8%), and Nunavut (10.5%). Geographical context recognises that Indigenous populations are diverse and have unique cultural, environmental, and historical factors that influence their nutritional practices. Regional policies, government funding, and support for Indigenous health initiatives can vary across provinces and territories.

Discussion

Indigenous communities are unique, requiring their own set of resources and support. This review provides an overview of nutrition interventions over the last 40 years. The authors documented several themes that became apparent within the identified interventions. These themes can be helpful in directing future research to support Indigenous nutrition programs. Incorporating traditional foods and cultural values

The promotion and meaningful incorporation of traditional foods emerged as a common theme, acknowledging the cultural significance of dietary choices [6,16,17,25,29]. The incorporation of traditional foods can support health, improve food security, and sustain culture and social connections [17]. Interventions have been implemented to reclaim and revitalise traditional food systems, acknowledging their holistic importance to Indigenous health and well-being. For example, the emphasis of traditional foods was demonstrated in the Nuxalk Food and Nutrition Program [16,17].

This program documented the traditional food system of the Nuxalk Nation, an Indigenous community in British Columbia. The program raised the profile of local traditional foods and provided opportunities for Elders to engage younger community members on how to prepare traditional foods. Assessments before and after the three-year intervention were conducted in 1983 and 1986, to assess shifts in household food use and health status. A key finding was the increase in traditional food use from 1981 to 1985, which lead to greater dietary intakes of vitamin A, folate, and iron [17].

The erosion of traditional diets has not only impacted physical health but has also resulted in a loss of cultural heritage and a weakened sense of Indigenous identity. The cultural significance of traditional foods extends beyond just sustenance. These foods are intricately linked to cultural identity, ceremonies, and practices, playing a vital role in maintaining social cohesion and a strong sense of community [39]. Recognising the connections between food, culture, and health is crucial for addressing the health disparities faced by Indigenous People in Canada and restoring their holistic approach to well-being.

For several generations, traditional foods held immense cultural, nutritional, and spiritual importance, providing a balanced and locally sourced diet that contributed to the overall well-being of Indigenous communities [40]. The shift towards Western diets, characterised by processed foods high in sugars, unhealthy fats, and additives has led to various health challenges among Indigenous populations. This transition has contributed to rising rates of obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and other diet-related health issues [3]. The loss of traditional foods and the knowledge associated with their preparation and consumption have disrupted the nutritional harmony that once sustained Indigenous communities. Incorporating traditional Indigenous foods back into community diets can help restore this nutritional balance, improve health outcomes, and reconnect people with their cultural heritage [17]. It also promotes food sovereignty by encouraging sustainable practices and fostering a deeper understanding of the natural environment.

Adapting Programming to Local Needs

For interventions to be more effective, they should be tailored to the population of focus [41]. Understanding the unique practical and cultural needs of Indigenous populations is important for the development and implementation of interventions to support their health. Western ideologies and views of health do not always align with Indigenous holistic views of health that incorporate community, environment, spirituality, and emotional and physical health [42].

A way to adapt programming to local needs is to engage communities and ensure Indigenous viewpoints are incorporated. An example of engaging community members is reflected in the research conducted by Yung and Neathway (2020) [36]. Their approach included connecting with Community Champions whereby each Nation identified an individual to attend train-the-trainer workshops on safe food preservation methods. The Champions then returned to their communities and shared their knowledge and newly acquired resources with community members. Nations were able to not only meet food safety considerations and access traditional foods, but also build capacity in creating a community of practice.

Furthermore, the adaptability of interventions to meet local needs and consider contextual nuances was a recurrent theme, underlining the importance of tailoring initiatives to specific communities, along with multi-disciplinary collaboration between stakeholders [15,23]. For example, in the Northwestern Ontario First Nations an integrated multi-institutional diabetes prevention program was adapted and modified with new components from formative research conducted by key stakeholders and community members [23]. One of the key successes of the program was the ability to respond to community needs and adapt program components to each locale. This helped gain the support of key stakeholders and made for a more culturally acceptable intervention [23].

Future interventions can benefit from prioritising community engagement, tailoring programs to local contexts, and fostering collaboration among diverse stakeholders. By involving Indigenous communities in the design and implementation of these initiatives, as seen with the use of Community Champions and adaptive diabetes prevention programs, interventions can better meet specific cultural and practical needs. This approach not only enhances the relevance and effectiveness of health initiatives but also empowers communities to build capacity and sustain long-term positive outcomes. Through these strategies, future interventions can support the restoration of traditional practices, improve health outcomes, and strengthen community resilience.

Empowering community members

Indigenous nutrition interventions play a pivotal role in addressing health disparities and promoting well-being among Indigenous communities. These interventions, however, should extend beyond dietary changes and embrace a holistic approach that empowers community members by incorporating education as a central component [6,18,35]. Education, when approached with cultural sensitivity and community involvement, becomes a tool for bridging the gap between traditional knowledge and modern nutritional practices. Such integration empowers Indigenous communities preserving cultural heritage and enhancing overall health and nutritional outcomes.

Participants in the Nuu-chah-nulth Experience, a diabetes prevention program, found the most valuable aspect of the program to be their access to fundamental information about diabetes, prevention methods, and strategies for self-help. Among the participants with diabetes, positive shifts in attitudes were observed, with 50% of them setting exercise and dietary goals. However, it is noteworthy that for participants who attended three or more program meetings, no statistically or clinically significant physiological improvements were reported [18].

Nutritional education should therefore not act alone and would be best complimented with other activities, such as opportunities for socialisation and relationship building [35]. Incorporating aspects such as education, revitalising traditional foods, and nurturing community empowerment can support community wellness. Together, they empower communities to reclaim their health journey, fostering resiliency and improved well-being.

Multidisciplinary collaboration

Studies in this review highlight the efficacy of holistic programming, recognising the interconnectedness of various factors that influence well-being [6,35,36]. Indigenous nutrition interventions are not merely about altering dietary habits; they are multifaceted journeys towards community empowerment and cultural preservation. Therefore, it is imperative to embrace a holistic approach that extends beyond food to encompass education, community engagement, and traditional knowledge. By adopting a holistic perspective, these interventions can address not only nutritional needs but also the broader context of well-being. This approach acknowledges that nutrition is interwoven with culture, identity, and self-determination.

Research by Yung and Neathway (2020) incorporated aspects of food that cut across multiple programs including schools, men’s groups, therapy groups, and food harvest events [36]. Utilising a holistic approach led to the creation of a community of practice. The development of a community of practice, allowed peer-to-peer exchange of technical knowledge, as well as program development and relationship building [36]. A holistic approach to Indigenous nutrition interventions not only promotes health but also safeguards heritage and fosters resilience and empowerment among Indigenous populations.

Leveraging social activities

Several studies highlighted the benefits of incorporating social activities and food [29,32,33]. The social benefits of food, which can include eating, preparing, or gardening with others, provides a platform of connection, communication, and shared experiences that can positively impact individuals and communities [43]. Coming together as a community can contribute to a sense of belonging, enhance relationships and bonds, and promote overall well-being [44]. For example, Feasting for Change was an intervention developed and used by the Coastal Salish people in British Columbia [33].

The program examined the promising practices in promoting health and well-being of Indigenous peoples using food. These promising practices included revitalising traditional knowledge of food by incorporating land-based, hands-on learning coupled with resources such as digital stories, plant knowledge cards, celebration cookbooks, and more. The program also utilised feasts, bringing guests together in sharing traditional foods prepared in time-honoured ways. During the feast, guests shared stories of how food preparation has changed and how community members want to revitalise traditional food practices. Participating communities also noted the importance of holding feasts in providing a space for Elders or knowledge keepers to share their wisdom with youth and their community. These feasts, a staple of the program, were used to engage and connect people regarding the food and nutrition needs in their community [33].

Another form of social gathering was highlighted by Avard [29]. They demonstrated the benefits of greenhouse-based social projects, such as a community garden. Respondents noted that in addition to reducing food costs, gardening improved social skills, increasing personal well-being, and helped address mental health challenges [29]. Embracing connection that can be built during communal meals or community activities underscores the need to design interventions that bolster community. Enhancing social gatherings can not only nourish bodies but also spirits, breathing life into holistic well-being within Indigenous communities [29]. Future studies can incorporate social activities by designing interventions that integrate communal meals, gardening projects, or other group-based initiatives that foster social bonds. These activities can enhance community cohesion, promote mental well-being, and strengthen the cultural and emotional connections within Indigenous communities, thereby supporting holistic health outcomes.

Considerations

When considering Indigenous nutrition interventions, it is important to recognise that the Nutrition North Canada market subsidies portion serves as a cautionary example of what should be avoided [15]. This program, marked by limitations and shortcomings, underscores the importance of taking a different approach. For example, one of the primary limitations of the program was that it concentrated on reducing costs for store-bought foods, overlooking a critical facet of Indigenous diets [34]. Traditional and country foods, integral components of the Indigenous diet, were largely left unaddressed [40]. Additionally, despite its overarching goal of improving nutrition and food security in Northern communities, limited data were available to conclusively determine whether the program was effectively mitigating food insecurity and contributing to improved health outcomes [34]. This limitation called into question the program’s ability to adequately address the health disparities prevalent in these regions. Further, a lack of community engagement and consultation emerged as a noteworthy limitation [34]. Some communities felt that they were not sufficiently included in the design and implementation of the program, resulting in a sense of disconnect and a lack of community ownership [34]. The absence of a collaborative approach potentially hindered the program’s ability to authentically meet the needs and aspirations of the communities it served. Finally, climate change and associated environmental challenges posed an additional limitation to the program’s effectiveness [34]. Environmental shifts have exacerbated food accessibility issues in remote northern communities, presenting an additional layer of complexity for the program to contend with [5]. These oversights collectively underscore the imperative for a more comprehensive and community-driven approach to address food security and nutrition in Indigenous communities.

Limitations

Despite the insights gained from this study, several limitations must be acknowledged. These limitations may affect the generalisability and interpretation of the findings and addressing them is crucial for future research in this area. Despite a range of search engines, it is plausible that our chosen search terms inadvertently omitted certain studies that met our criteria. Additionally, some intervention studies might remain unpublished, and numerous community-level initiatives and interventions, while effective, may lack evaluation. For example, we found a news report on a travelling diabetes program popular in rural Alberta [50] with no further information available. Considering the imperative to enhance nutritional and diet-related health outcomes within Indigenous communities, our intention is for the themes of this scoping review to stimulate dialogue and active participation among health researchers and Indigenous communities.

Conclusion

Indigenous Peoples in Canada, including First Nations, Métis, and Inuit communities, exhibit diverse cultural practices, yet face shared health and nutrition disparities due to historical and contemporary factors. Access to affordable and nutritious foods is a challenge for many Indigenous individuals, leading to diet-related health issues. Traditional food systems, which have sustained these communities for generations, are increasingly threatened by environmental and socioeconomic pressures. Culturally relevant nutrition interventions are essential in addressing these health disparities, as they promote well-being by bridging the gap between traditional knowledge and modern nutrition practices.

This review highlights the critical themes that have emerged over the past 40 years of nutrition interventions within Indigenous communities, providing valuable guidance for future research and program development. These themes – incorporating traditional foods and cultural values, adapting programs to local needs, empowering community members, fostering multidisciplinary collaboration, and leveraging social activities – offer pathways to address the unique challenges faced by Indigenous populations. As future efforts aim to improve health and nutrition outcomes, it is vital that research and interventions continue to honour the cultural, social, and environmental contexts in which they are implemented. This holistic approach not only enhances the success and sustainability of interventions but also contributes to the overall resilience and well-being of Indigenous peoples.
