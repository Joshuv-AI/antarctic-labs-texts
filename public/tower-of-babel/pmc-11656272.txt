Mendelian randomization analyses support causal relationships between HPV infection and colorectal cancer
Authors: Bo Pei, Peijun Liu, Shixuan Peng, Fuxiang Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman Papillomavirus (HPV) infections leading to a variety of diseases are a global public health issue.Despite the well-established link between HPV infection and cervical and anogenital cancers, there is ongoing debate regarding the relationship between HPV infection and colorectal cancer (CRC).MethodsWe evaluated the causal connection between HPV infection and CRC utilizing five Mendelian randomization (MR) methods. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) datasets for HPV were obtained from the IEU Open GWAS project. A large summary of colorectal adenocarcinoma and colorectal cancer data from the FinnGen database was used as the outcome.ResultsOur analysis revealed a significant association between genetically predicted HPV-16 infection and the risk of paternal colorectal adenocarcinoma (HPV-16: OR 1.058, 95% CI 1.013–1.102; p = 0.011), as well as CRC (HPV-16: OR 1.045, 95% CI 1.005–1.085; p = 0.025).ConclusionThese findings provide compelling evidence for a causal effect of HPV infection on the development of CRC. Further investigations into the underlying mechanisms and elucidation of this association are necessary to identify viable interventions for the prevention and treatment of HPV-associated CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01639-0.Keywords: Human papillomavirus, Colorectal cancer, Etiology, Disease risk, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer and the second leading cause of cancer death around the world, with an estimated 2 million new cases in 2020 [1]. In the United States, CRC is the second-highest cause of cancer-related deaths in women and the top cause in males under 50 [2]. In China, the incidence of CRC is on the rise and has become the second most common malignant tumor [3]. Although lifestyle, environmental factors, genetics, and obesity might have some association, the exact causes of this worrying rise are not completely understood [4]. Numerous factors contributed to the etiology of CRC, such as positive family history, age, increased bodyweight, red and processed meat intake, smoking, and excessive alcohol intake [5–11]. Among the established risk factors for the formation and development of CRC are infections due to specific bacterial species or viruses. For example, research related to Bacteroides fragilis and Fusobacterium nucleatum demonstrated that Bacteroides fragilis and Fusobacterium nucleatum may increase the risk of CRC [12, 13].

Among the viruses, the human papillomavirus (HPV) is a DNA virus that has a generally accepted aetiological role in cervical and anogenital cancer [14, 15]. Although greater attention has been focused on the issue of HPV infection having an association with CRC incidence since Kirgan et al. first reported a potential link between HPV infection and colon cancer in 1990, the association between HPV and the risk of CRC still remains inconclusive [16, 17]. In a recent study, Darwish et al. observed a significant correlation between the presence of HPVs and advanced colorectal tumor grades B2 and D [18]. More recently, a nationwide study conducted by Pan and colleagues indicated that HPV plays a substantial role in increasing CRC risk, which is subject to variation based on the specific body part affected, demographic specifics, and comorbidities [19]. In a word, one subset of studies supports the notion that HPV infection is closely associated with the occurence of CRC [20–32]. On the contrary,the other subset of studies are in a seemingly opposite direction; HPV infection has not been implicated as a risk for CRC [33–40]. Given the lack of consensus on the association between HPV infection and colorectal carcinogenesis, there is a need to conduct a Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis.

MR is a method that attempts to minimize measurement error, reverse causation, and confounding by utilizing genetic variants that are known to be reliably related to modifiable risk factors of interest to evaluate the causal effects of these risk factors on clinically relevant outcomes [41]. It serves as a valuable tool and has become a widely used approach to explore the potential causal association between risk factors or modifiable exposures and disease outcomes [42]. The etiological relationship between HPV infection and CRC remains enigmatic, with evidence from observational research potentially marred by confounding variables and selection bias. In an effort to rigorously assess the causal implications of HPV infection on the susceptibility to CRC, MR analysis was meticulously implemented in the present investigation.

Methods and materials

Data sources for exposure and outcomes

We extracted summary statistics for HPV E7 phenotypes from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) database [43]. The GWAS ID for the summary-level data included in our analysis is: prot-c-2623_54_4 (E7 protein of HPV16). The candidate genetic instruments for colorectal adenocarcinoma and CRC were identified from the FinnGen database(https://www.finngen.fi/en) [44], ensuring all individuals incorporated in the FinnGen dataset were of European descent.

Selection of tool variables and MR analysis

Instrumental variables (IVs) were selected based on several stringent criteria to ensure robustness. Firstly, Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) required a genome-wide significant association with the exposure and were deemed instrumental variables for assessing the causality of HPV E7 protein involvement (p < 5 × 10–5, r2 < 0.001, and clumping distance > 10,000 kb). We employed five dynamic MR methods to ascertain causality: Weighted Median Regression, Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW), Mendelian Randomization-Egger (MR-Egger), Simple Mode, and Weighted Mode, each offering unique insights into the genetic architecture influencing HPV E7 expression and its potential causal link to CRC pathology.

Statistical analysis and sensitive analysis

To assess heterogeneity among the selected IVs, we utilized Cochran's Q test within the IVW framework. A p-value less than 0.05 indicated significant heterogeneity, necessitating further exploration into the sources of variance. We addressed the phenomenon of pleiotropy, where a single gene influences multiple traits, using the MR Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (PRESSO) test to detect horizontal pleiotropy and identify outlier variants [45]. The removal of these outliers enabled an unbiased causal estimation through outlier-corrected MR analysis.

Furthermore, we conducted a MR-Egger intercept test to examine the presence of directional pleiotropy among the IVs. A non-zero intercept indicated directional pleiotropy, suggesting that some of the genetic variants might exert effects on the outcome through pathways other than those being tested. To ensure the robustness of our findings, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed. This approach involved sequentially removing each SNP from the analysis to determine if any single SNP disproportionately influenced the overall results, thereby providing a dynamic and comprehensive assessment of the data's reliability (The flow chart of the study is shown in Fig. 1).

Results

In this MR, HPV-16 increased the risk of paternal colorectal adenocarcinoma, a type of cancer that originates in the colon or rectum and is inherited from one's father (HPV-16: OR 1.058, 95% CI 1.013–1.102; p = 0.011) (Fig. 2A). Similar finding was shown in CRC (HPV-16: OR 1.045, 95% CI 1.005–1.085; p = 0.025) (Fig. 2B).From our investigation, 23 SNPs were identified and subsequently chosen for further MR analysis in the colorectal adenocarcinoma group (Supplementary Table S1). The IVW method has the advantage of simultaneously handling multiple SNPs and remains effective even when the correlation between SNPs is weak. Heterogeneity was observed by Cochran’s Q test (Q = 21.54, p = 0.032). The MR-Egger intercept analysis indicated the absence of directional pleiotropy (p = 0.089). Figure 3A presents scatterplots illustrating the results of these analyses. In the leave-one-out analysis, no individual SNP exerted a significant influence on the overall effect of HPV-16 (exposure) on colorectal adenocarcinoma (outcome) (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

HPV have been linked to both benign and malignant cervix diseases and other anogenital diseases [15]. Given HPV's well-known function in cervical and anal carcinogenesis, multiple studies have shown that HPV is also linked to CRC [18–32]. DNA viruses have been shown to activate proto-oncogenes (including p53, pRB, and c-myc) [46, 47], and some studies have hypothesized that the ras oncogene collaborates with the HPV E6/E7 genes to cause full transformation of normal cells [48, 49]. The existence of HPV DNA in colon cancers is a source of contention. While previous investigations failed to find HPV DNA in colon biopsy samples [28], a growing corpus of current data shows that HPV infection may play a role in the development of colorectal cancer [17].

This study uses MR to investigate whether high-risk HPV infection is genetically linked to colorectal carcinogenesis as well as independent effects. Leveraging the comprehensive genomic resource of the IEU GWAS database, we procured aggregated statistical data pertaining to HPV E7 phenotypes. Through meticulous analysis of this robust dataset, we have delineated a discernible association between HPV infection and the elevated risk of CRC, thereby enhancing our understanding of the complex interplay between viral infections and oncogenesis in the colorectal context. The role of HPV in the carcinogenesis of CRC is a contentious issue, with HPV-16 being the most prevalent, high-risk subtype of the causative type; HPV-18, while a high-risk causative subtype in cervical cancer, is infected in a lower percentage of cases than HPV-16 [36]. Ibragimova et al. examined the independent impact of HPV on the risk of developing CRC. The amount of HPV infection in colorectal tumor tissue was found to be statistically significant, and the relative risk of developing CRC as a result of HPV infection was found to be RR (95% CI) = 2.97 (1.42–6.22), p = 0.0039, based on the data obtained [27]. Zhang et al. identified a pooled prevalence and OR of 0.31 (95% CI: 0.24–0.37) and 2.03 (95% CI: 0.79–5.26), respectively, among 193 patients with colorectal adenomas in another meta-analysis based on a Chinese sample. The prevalence of HPV16 in HPV-positive malignancies varied from 57.9 to 100% [17]. Additionally, another three meta-analyses [27, 30, 50] and four recent studies [51–54] from different countries and regions supported the association of HPV infection in the pathogenesis of CRC.

The correlation between HPV and the risk of CRC has been linked to a number of processes. The relationship between HPV infection, constitutive STAT3 activity, and IL-17 levels was first discovered by Li et al. [55]. This relationship may orchestrate a pro-inflammatory microenvironment in the colorectum, which in turn may promote carcinogenesis and aid in the progression of CRC [47]. Another study suggests that the E6 oncoprotein of HPV-16, which is associated with the inactivation of the p53 tumor suppressor protein, may play a role in the development of a subset of colorectal cancers, and that the transmission of HPV to the colon and rectum might occur through peripheral blood lymphocytes [56]. Similarly, E6 proteins are capable of binding with the E3 ubiquitin ligase E6-associated protein (E6AP), thereby interfering with the transactivation process of the p53 protein. Nonetheless, it is exclusively the oncogenic forms of E6 that possess the ability to degrade p53, utilizing the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of E6AP. Due to the synergistic action of the E7 and E6 proteins, cells do not enter a state of growth arrest,which ultimately contributes to the development of cancer [57]. Moreover, Zhou et al. employed quantitative polymerase chain reaction, cell proliferation, scratch, and flow cytometry assays to validate the impact of HPV on CRC [51]. Ambrosio's findings support the carcinogenic significance of HPV in CRC, demonstrating HPV's role in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. The occurrence of CRC associated with HPV may be attributed to reduced immune surveillance [54]. Additionally, the work of Péré demonstrated that the full-length nucleotide sequence of HPV16 was consistently identified across initial and subsequent anal biopsy samples, plasma, and vertebral metastasis biopsy, with the HPV genome existing in an episomal state in each instance. Comparative genomic analysis revealed that the HPV16 sequences were most closely related to the Qv18158E variant subtype, exhibiting specific base substitutions at seven loci and deletions at two loci within the HPV genome [58]. In fact, such studies provide important clues for understanding the pathogenesis and genes background of CRC. Moreover, Zhou and colleagues found that HPV-infected CRC with a “hot” tumor immune microenvironment that may be responsive to immunotherapy [51]. This finding may provide a predictive indicator for the efficacy of immunotherapy for CRC. Simultaneously, a most recent study not only underscored HPV’s potential role in CRC development, but also discovered high risk HPV DNA detection in CRC urine samples may be a non-invasive screening tools for CRC [59].

MR was utilized in this investigation to ascertain the independent and causative effects of high-risk HPV infection on colorectal carcinogenesis. Sensitivity analysis was used to ensure the reliability of the findings and further support the effect of HPV infection on the risk of CRC [31]. At the same time, we conducted a combined analysis using information from other research, which improved the validity and applicability of our findings.

Our study has several limitations. First, the study population in this research was mainly composed of individuals of European descent, suggesting that the findings may not be broadly applicable to different ethnic groups. Additionally, MR analyses are capable of establishing genuine causal relationships, yet they fail to uncover the underlying mechanisms linking HPV to the progression of CRC, necessitating further investigation. Collectively, our research adds to the body of knowledge supporting the link between the development of CRC and clusters of high-risk HPV. This is crucial for comprehending how CRC develops and for creating public health initiatives. The HPV vaccine has been remarkably successful in eliminating cervical cancer, and the 2023 EUROGIN workshop declared that strategically optimal deployment of vaccination will accelerate the elimination of HPV and cervical cancer [60]. Further elucidation of this association and the underlying mechanisms is needed to identify feasible interventions to promote the prevention and treatment of HPV-associated CRC.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the etiologic nexus between HPV infection and CRC, unraveling novel dimensions of pathogenesis. Our study supports that HPV is a significant risk factor for CRC. It's time to take HPV infection in CRC patients more seriously. The implications of these findings are profound, as they are anticipated to augment public consciousness regarding HPV and the significance of HPV vaccination. Consequently, this enhanced awareness may serve as a catalyst for a decrease in the prevalence of CRC, thereby fostering a more robust approach to cancer prevention strategies.
