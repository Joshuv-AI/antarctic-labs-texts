Overlooked giants: post-invasion interploidy dynamics of a cryptic cytotype of alien invasive Solidago gigantea (Asteraceae)
Authors: Katarína Skokanová, Barbora Šingliarová, Kristína Pulišová, Marek Šlenker, Stanislav Španiel, Marek Svitok, Judita Zozomová-Lihová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNovel invasive genotypes can arise through polyploidisation, hybridisation, or gene flow between populations of distinct origins or related species. Solidago gigantea, a notorious European invader, has long been reported exclusively as tetraploid in its invasive range. Recently, mixed-ploidy populations, including tetraploid and pentaploid plants, were discovered; yet the potential role of the novel pentaploid cytotype (and its progeny) in S. gigantea invasions remains poorly understood. This study aims to elucidate the origin of pentaploids and the cytotype and genetic structure of mixed-ploidy populations, characterise the reproductive mode and mating interactions of pentaploid plants, and assess their fitness and potential contribution to invasiveness using relative DNA content screening, ddRADseq population genetics, and reproductive potential and fitness assessments.ResultsMolecular analyses revealed that pentaploids constitute a genetically distinct lineage within S. gigantea. Our results rule out both an autopolyploid origin from the common tetraploid cytotype and an allopolyploid origin via hybridisation with co-occurring native or invasive Solidago species. The pentaploid cytotype reproduces exclusively through clonal propagation; its low genetic variability suggests that the two studied populations may belong to a single extensive clonal genet. Pentaploids produce viable gametes but appear to exhibit strict self-incompatibility, preventing the formation of offspring within the same genotype. However, pentaploid S. gigantea engages in bidirectional mating with co-occurring tetraploid plants, yielding well-developed seeds with offspring ploidy ranging from 4x to 5x (predominantly aneuploid). Despite this cytological variability, progeny from mixed-ploidy populations displayed germination rates and early growth comparable to those from pure tetraploid populations. Notably, at least some tetraploid offspring from 4x–5x crosses successfully established, flowered, and backcrossed with pentaploid plants to produce viable seeds of subsequent introgressed generations.ConclusionsThe pentaploid cytotype of S. gigantea introduces a new post-invasion dynamic to its invasive populations. Rather than being an evolutionary dead-end, this cytotype may potentially enhance the species’ invasiveness through three evolutionary pathways: (1) a highly successful clonal life strategy enabling both local and long-distance spread; (2) genetic enrichment of tetraploid populations via ongoing interploidy crosses; and (3) establishment of novel aneuploid genotypes due to the remarkable tolerance of chromosomal instability observed in S. gigantea.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08874-3.Keywords: Biological invasions, ddRADseq, Flow cytometry, Minority cytotype, Odd-ploidy, Post-invasion evolution, Reproduction and fitness parameters

Introduction

The initial step of the plant invasion process, i.e. the introduction into novel ranges, represents an exceptional long-distance dispersal event [15, 18, 30, 108]. From an evolutionary perspective, such events usually lead to genetic bottlenecks in non-native populations, resulting in reduced genetic diversity, along with increased genetic drift, and potential inbreeding depression (most pronounced in outcrossing species) [13, 30, 99, 103]. This raises the question: how successful plant invaders can diversify and inhabit diverse environments despite these genetic constraints? The answer lies in two key biological characteristics of invasive plant species: their notable phenotypic plasticity, acting as a preadaptation to immediate adjustment to various environmental conditions and/or high adaptive potential, which enables rapid genetic shifts in response to a new environment and selective pressures encountered during colonisation [12, 15, 30, 34, 99]. Some plant invaders go even further; the formation of novel invasive genotypes in the invaded range can be accelerated by polyploidisation, shifts in reproduction and life-history traits, hybridisation, or gene flow with related native or other non-native species [3, 15, 59, 62, 88].

Introduced species offer an ideal framework for studying contemporary evolutionary processes as their introduction histories often provide well-documented timescales [4, 12, 15, 30, 108]. Because post-invasion evolutionary events unfold over a considerably shorter time than most non-human-induced ones, introduced species offer an opportunity to observe and study the earliest stages of evolutionary change [12, 13]. In introduced species, post-invasion evolutionary changes (for example, in morphology and phenology) should be identified by comparing populations from the native and invaded ranges [103, 108], and growth experiments under identical conditions may provide evidence for the genetic basis of the observed differences [12, 27]. More broadly, a deeper understanding of post-colonisation evolutionary changes is essential, as they are key drivers of the establishment and spread of invasive species. This knowledge represents a critical step towards minimising the escalating threats that invasive species pose to both biodiversity and human well-being [cf. 15, 75, 76].

Solidago gigantea Aiton (giant goldenrod) of North American origin, as a classic example of a notorious invasive species, has been the subject of numerous scientific studies, making it an ideal system for illustrating the evolutionary processes that drive plant invasiveness. Genetic data suggest that the introduction to the European invasive range involved only a few S. gigantea plants [80]. Consequently, populations in the invaded range exhibit reduced genetic diversity and lower interpopulation differentiation compared to their native counterparts [62, 80]. While S. gigantea harbours three ploidy levels in its native range (di-, tetra-, and hexaploid), its cytotype diversity has been reduced to only the tetraploid level in the invasive range ([62, 79]; see chapter “Studied species”). Even within the native range, the tetraploid cytotype possesses traits that likely preadapted it to greater invasive potential than other cytotypes, facilitating its establishment as a successful invader. These traits include superior pathogen resistance, higher photosynthetic capacity, and greater plasticity in biomass accumulation (above- vs. below-ground), clonal-ramet production, and water-use efficiency [78, 103]. Furthermore, in the native range, the tetraploids thrive across a broader spectrum of environments than other cytotypes [52].

A direct comparison of field-collected plants of S. gigantea from the non-native range with those from the native range displayed their differentiation in enhanced performance (e.g., larger populations, greater biomass, greater shoot size and density, longer rhizomes, and lower herbivore infestation), an environmental shift toward drier and nutrient-poor conditions, and greater competitive ability [23, 36, 56, 65, 66]. Cultivation experiments confirmed a genetically fixed shift in invasive European plants towards greater above-ground biomass and more shoots, and reduced investment in defence against pathogens and herbivores compared to native North American plants [23, 32, 49, 56, 78]. However, no such evidence of a genetic basis was found regarding shoot size, leaf traits, specific leaf area (SLA), nutrient concentrations, or litter decomposition rates [10, 23, 35, 49, 78]. Experimental growing showed that invasive S. gigantea plants did not differ in germination rate [41], did not grow more rapidly [23], and did not have increased competitive ability [66]. Furthermore, significant interpopulation divergence has been observed within the invasive range, particularly in morphological traits, flowering onset, and genome size. These variations within the non-native range follow distinct altitudinal, longitudinal, and latitudinal patterns [23, 36, 88, 108]. Results from common-garden experiments indicate that morphological and phenological differences among populations of S. gigantea are genetically determined, likely reflecting local adaptation to the different climatic and edaphic conditions encountered within the introduced range [23, 108].

The introduction of S. gigantea has caused human-induced secondary contact, breaking down (eco)geographical barriers between Solidago species that had diverged allopatrically without evolving reproductive isolation [cf. 4, 15, 24, 94]). Consequently, S. gigantea spontaneously hybridises with the native S. virgaurea L. within its European invasive range. Their triploid hybrid, S. ×snarskisii Gudžinskas & Žalneravičius, arises from the heteroploid crossing (diploid S. virgaurea and tetraploid S. gigantea). It has reduced fertility and is currently documented from a small number of localities in northern and eastern Europe [22, 61, 71, 102]. The invasive success of S. ×snarskisii remains significantly lower than that of S. ×niederederi Khek, a homoploid hybrid of S. virgaurea and another invasive Solidago species from North America, S. canadensis L. For S. ×niederederi, an exponential increase in the number of independent hybridisation events in sympatric populations within the European invasive range of S. canadensis has been recorded [85], together with established introgression [72, 86, 89].

The recent discovery of two pentaploid populations of S. gigantea in the invaded range [88] has opened a new chapter of research concerning the role of polyploidisation in the species’ invasiveness. Although the pentaploid cytotype appears well-established, as evidenced by its high local abundance, its origin and biological characteristics remain unknown. Furthermore, initial analyses revealed reproductive interactions between the pentaploid and the common tetraploid cytotype; however, the extent and character of these interactions are poorly understood [88]. To address these knowledge gaps, we employed an integrative approach to investigate the origin, post-invasion interploidy dynamics, and potential role of the pentaploid cytotype (and its progeny) in the invasion process. Our methodology combined relative DNA content screening, ddRADseq population genetics, and assessments of reproductive potential and fitness parameters, including clonal growth, pollen fertility, sexual or asexual seed production, seed set, and seed germination. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) test the autopolyploid versus hybrid origin of the pentaploid cytotype; (2) reveal the detailed cytotype and genetic structure of mixed-ploidy populations; (3) detect the character and extent of mating interactions between pentaploid and tetraploid plants within mixed-ploidy populations; (4) evaluate and compare the reproductive strategies, reproductive potential, and fitness of the pentaploid cytotype and its progeny to estimate their dispersal capacity and overall invasive potential.

Materials and methods

Studied species

Solidago gigantea is native to southern Canada and the central and eastern United States. Approximately 250 years ago, it was introduced to Europe as a promising ornamental plant [107]. First cultivated in gardens, it escaped into the wild approximately 175 years ago [105]. The species has since undergone exponential range expansion, colonising nearly all European territories except the northern regions [79, 87]. Beyond Europe, the species is naturalised and invasive in East Asia, the Azores, and New Zealand [9]. At invaded sites, S. gigantea alters soil properties such as pH, nutrient cycling and availability [10, 19, 20, 29, 77, 92] and reduces species richness and diversity ([19, 26, 68]; Slezák et al., unpubl. results).

Within its native range, S. gigantea comprises three autopolyploid cytotypes: diploid (2n = 2x = 18), tetraploid (2n = 4x = 36), and hexaploid (2n = 6x = 54) [58, 79, 81], with polyploids appearing to have originated repeatedly [80]. These cytotypes exhibit a high degree of geographical separation within their native range, differ in ecology and growth characteristics, and appear to be reproductively isolated [32, 36, 50, 78, 79]. In rare cytotype contact zones, odd-ploidy plants, one triploid (2n = 3x = 27) and one pentaploid (2n = 5x = 45), have been recorded [79]. For a long time, only tetraploid S. gigantea plants had been reported from the invasive range ([79, 81]; reviewed in [88]), but two populations of the pentaploid cytotype, along with a few aneuploids, were recently discovered in Central Europe by Skokanová et al. [88].

Solidago gigantea is a rhizomatous perennial herb (50–250 cm tall) that often forms dense mono-dominant stands (polycormons). It reproduces sexually by seeds (dispersed primarily by anemochory and zoochory), and vegetatively via rhizome growth and fragmentation (dispersed via water, transferred soil, or waste material). The rhizomes are persistent, long, and creeping, and they easily fragment at nodes. In spring, 2–5 rhizomes (5–40 cm long) develop at the stem base, producing new annual flowering or non-flowering stems the following season. Plants bear 50–1,000 capitula organised in a corymb. Seeds are small, numerous (up to 19,000 per stem), single-seeded achenes with a pappus. The species is entomogamous, cross-pollinated, and self-incompatible [6, 50, 84, 96, 106, 108, 109].

Sampling design and plant material

In the field, we sampled plant material for cytotype screening using flow cytometry (fresh leaves), ddRADseq analyses (silica gel-dried leaves), pollen grain size and viability analyses (corymbs with inflorescences before anthesis), flow cytometric seed screening, seed set, seed size and seed germination experiments (corymbs with ripe seeds). In addition, plant material from seedlings and juvenile plants raised from the germination experiments was used for chromosome counting, flow cytometric relative genome size assessment, and ddRADseq.

Detailed screening was conducted in two populations approximately 4 km apart along the river Ondava in eastern Slovakia, in which previous karyological research [88] revealed the occurrence of pentaploid and aneuploid plants of S. gigantea (DUP, Duplín; SRP, Stropkov; Fig. 1, Supplementary material 1); hereinafter, referred to as mixed-ploidy populations. Additional plant material was obtained from five S. gigantea populations confirmed to consist exclusively of the common tetraploid cytotype [88] to provide a comparative baseline (CHA, Chľaba; HUM, Humenné; MAS, Mašan; NIT, Nitra; ZBO, Zboiska; hereinafter referred to as tetraploid populations). All populations were located in Slovakia and Poland, Central Europe (Fig. 1, Supplementary material 2).

Because S. gigantea grows in polycormons with rhizomes intermingling within populations, making individual plants indistinguishable [cf. 6, 106], the plant material was collected in the field from individual stems separated by at least 1 m in mixed-ploidy populations (detailed sampling to avoid missing any cytotype or genotype, both flowering and sterile stems), and by at least 5 m in tetraploid ones (to collect material from different individuals, only flowering stems). Plant material was collected in two phases to ensure (in an ideal case) complete material for all analyses from the same stems. (1) Flowering period (9–17 August 2023): we collected fresh leaf material from selected flowering and sterile stems and, when possible, a portion of the corymb with inflorescences before anthesis. The exact position of each sampled stem was mapped using a GNSS receiver (Emlid RS) with sub-meter precision (typically ≤ 5 cm), and stems from which floral material was collected were marked with zip ties (Fig. 1F; Supplementary material 1 - A, B). (2) Ripening period (6–13 October 2023): from marked stems localised by GNSS receiver for seed set analyses (Supplementary material 1 - C), portions of corymbs with ripe seeds were collected in small paper bags for seed set analyses, while the remaining corymb material was collected in large paper bags for germination experiments. Seed material was collected only from selected marked stems in good condition; some marked flowering stems were excluded because they had been damaged (mainly by mowing and maintenance activities around roads, and vehicle movements), or seeds were either not yet ripe or had already partially dispersed from the inflorescences.

Fresh leaf material was used for subsequent DAPI flow cytometry analyses and stored in silica gel for molecular analyses. Material for pollen and seed analyses was stored in paper bags and dried at room temperature for four weeks. Seed material for flow cytometry seed screening (FCSS) [54] was stored in a refrigerator to prevent endosperm absorption; that for the germination experiment was later placed in a freezer for cold stratification during winter (at -6 °C for 2 months). Additional fresh plant material for flow cytometry included two primary-leaf seedlings from germination experiments and juvenile plants (only from mixed-ploidy populations) after further cultivation (48 days after the end of the germination experiment, see below) (Supplementary material 1 - D, E). At the end of cultivation of juvenile plants, they were assigned to three size categories to assess potential differences in growth rate among particular cytotypes (small < 3 cm, medium 3–10 cm, and large > 10 cm; Supplementary material 1 - E); if possible, leaf material from juvenile plants was also stored in silica gel for molecular analyses.

In addition to S. gigantea, related species were also sampled (fresh leaf material stored in silica gel) and used for RADseq analyses: the European native S. virgaurea L. and species of North American origin that are either invasive in Europe [S. canadensis L. and Euthamia graminifolia (L.) Nutt.≡Solidago graminifolia (L.) Salisb.] or cultivated there (S. sempervirens L. and S. shortii Torr. & A.Gray).

Details regarding the sources of the plant material (localities, dates, collectors, and determinators) are available in Supplementary material 2. Voucher specimens are deposited in the SAV herbarium (Plant Science and Biodiversity Centre, Slovak Academy of Sciences, Bratislava). Seedling and juvenile plants were photo documented before FCM analyses (see for example, Supplementary material 1 - D, E). As Solidago gigantea and S. canadensis are classified as invasive species under Slovak Act No. 150/2019 Coll. on Invasive Alien Species, all handling—including transport and cultivation—was conducted under permits no. 14,720/2024 − 6.4 (2020–2024) and 14,720/2024 − 6.4 (2024–2026) issued by the Ministry of Environment of the Slovak Republic, authorising the handling of invasive plant taxa listed under EU and national legislation.

Karyological analyses

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry (FCM) was used to measure the relative genome size and ploidy level of field-collected plants (328 in total; 10 individuals per tetraploid population; 160 from DUP including 61 sterile; 111 from SRP including 42 sterile) and progeny from selected mother plants (577 seeds, ± 10 per selected mother plant; 612 seedlings, ± 10 per selected mother plant; 392 juvenile plants, all available) (Table 1). In addition, FCM of seeds (FCSS) was used to determine the mode of seed formation by comparing the relative genome size (ploidy) of embryo and endosperm tissue (505 seeds in total, ± 10 per selected mother plant) (Table 1). This approach was employed because odd-ploidy can disrupt meiosis and be compensated for by the production of apomictic seeds, in which meiosis and syngamy are bypassed [40].

To ensure accurate relative DNA content estimates, fresh material from each plant and seedling, or seed, was analysed separately using the fluorochrome 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). We used the well-established protocol for genome size analysis of Solidago species, which is suitable even for detecting minor differences [86, 88, 89]. This protocol followed the simplified two-step protocol [14] and used Solanum pseudocapsicum L. (2.59 pg DNA/2 C; [93]) as an internal reference standard. Measurements were performed using a CyFlow Space instrument (Partec, Münster, Germany), equipped with a UV-LED excitation source. Flow cytometric histograms were evaluated using FloMax software v. 2.7d (Partec, Münster, Germany). All plant material was analysed following a similar procedure. However, buffer volumes were adjusted according to the amount of tissue available: for mother and juvenile plants, 1 ml each of Otto I and Otto II buffers was added, and the fluorescence of at least 5,000 particles was recorded, whereas for seeds and seedlings, 0.5 ml of each buffer was added, and the fluorescence of 3,000 particles was recorded. Only histograms with symmetrical peaks and a low coefficient of variation (CV) of the standard and sample G1 peaks (below 3% for analyses of field-collected plants, seedlings, and juvenile plants; below 5% for analyses of seeds) were accepted.

The 2 C value of relative genome size (RGS) was calculated as the ratio of the G1 peak position of the sample to that of the standard (the sample-standard ratio, hereinafter referred to as RSS). The 2 C values are presented unless otherwise stated. The equivalent of the Cx values of the RGS were calculated as the 2 C values of the RGS divided by the number of chromosome sets x [cf. 21]. Both new chromosome counts, as well as those reported by Skokanová et al. [88], were used for the correct interpretation of flow cytometry results (i.e., to deduce ploidies and chromosome numbers).

Chromosome counting

Chromosome numbers were counted in the root-tip meristems of seedlings and one juvenile plant (13 in total) of S. gigantea. The same seedlings and one juvenile plant were also included in flow cytometry analyses. The root tips were pretreated in a 0.002–M water solution of 8-hydroxyquinoline at 4 °C for approximately 16 h (overnight), fixed in a 1:3 mixture of 98% acetic acid and 96% ethanol for 1–24 h, washed in distilled water, macerated in 1–N hydrochloric acid at 60 °C for 5 min, and then washed in distilled water. Permanent slides were made using the cellophane square technique [60]. The slides were stained with a 7% solution of Giemsa Stain–Modified Solution (Fluka Analytical) in Sörensen phosphate buffer. Chromosomes were counted using a Zeiss Axio Scope.A1 microscope equipped with a Zeiss Axiocam 105 colour digital camera and AxioVision 4.9 software (all Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany) and a Leica DM1000 microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with an HDCE-X5 camera and ScopeImage 9.0 software (both Ningbo Yongxin Optics Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China).

RADseq data generation and processing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from silica gel-dried leaves. The leaves were ground with glass beads to powder using TissueLyser II by Retsch (Haan, Germany) and pretreated with 0.1 M HEPES buffer (pH = 8) supplemented with PVP and ascorbic acid. Subsequently, DNA was extracted using the Exgene Plant SV kit (GeneAll, Seoul, Korea) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The double-digest RADseq libraries [1] were prepared according to the protocol of Peterson et al. [70] with some modifications as described in detail in Šlenker et al. [91]. In brief, genomic DNA was purified with 0.4x AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA), digested with HpyCH4V (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), which was followed by A-tailing and adaptor ligation with a 5-bp barcode. The pooled ligation products were size-selected using Pippin Prep (Sage Science, Beverly, MA, USA) and PCR amplified using the universal P5 primer and the indexed P7 primer with 6-bp indices. The libraries were sequenced with 150-bp paired-end reads on Illumina systems at Novogene, Cambridge, UK.

Illumina sequencing reads were demultiplexed using fastx_barcode_splitter.pl from the FASTX-Toolkit v.0.0.14 (http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/). Reads were quality filtered with fastp [11], requiring a minimum quality score of 20 and a minimum read length of 50 bp. Filtered reads were mapped to the Solidago caesia reference genome (GenBank accession GCA_028566075.1) using BWA v.0.7.3a [47]. Resulting BAM files were processed with the Genome Analysis Toolkit v.4.4.0.0 [55]. Variant calling was performed for each individual using the HaplotypeCaller module, specifying the appropriate ploidy. Variants were filtered with the VariantFiltration module, requiring a minimum sequencing depth of 8× and applying additional filters following GATK best practices [97]. Finally, biallelic variants passing all filters and with no more than 20% missing genotypes were retained using the SelectVariants module.

Sexual reproduction and fitness parameters

Overall, 63 mother plants (5 mother plants per tetraploid populations, 19 per mixed-ploidy populations) with complete material (pre-anthesis inflorescences as well as inflorescences with ripe seeds) collected from the same stem were analysed. The exception was two mother plants from the DUP population, for which no pollen was collected for analysis because inflorescences had not yet developed during fieldwork.

Pollen analyses

To evaluate male fertility, pollen viability and pollen grain size were assessed using modified Alexander’s staining methods, following the protocol of Peterson et al. [69]. Five unopened anthers were randomly selected per plant (each anther from a different capitulum) (Table 2). Pollen smears were prepared on microscope slides (one per plant), stained with the stain solution, and incubated at 70 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, slides were examined with a Zeiss Axio Scope.A1 microscope equipped with a Zeiss Axiocam 105 colour digital camera and AxioVision 4.9 software (all Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany). Five random fields were photographed from each slide, and 100 intact pollen grains per plant (6100 grains total) were observed and measured for diameter (µm). Deeply stained purple pollen grains were scored as viable, whereas bluish-green grains were scored as non-viable. Pollen viability was expressed as the percentage of viable grains relative to the total number examined.

Seed set analyses

The numbers of undeveloped and well-developed seeds (seed sets) were quantified for corymb portions (8–35 capitula) collected during the ripening period (seeds were usually at least partly dispersed within the paper bags by the time of processing) (Table 2). The percentage of well-developed seeds was used as a proxy measure of female fertility. Two morphological traits, seed length (SL, mm) and seed width (SW, mm), were measured for a random sample of 10 seeds per mother plant using a stereomicroscope (Olympus SZ61) at 80× magnification with QuickPHOTO Micro 3.2 software.

Germination experiments

To evaluate plant reproductive fitness, we assessed two germination parameters: germination rate (the percentage of seeds that germinated) and germination dynamics (the cumulative percentage of germinated seeds over time). Seeds were sown in January 2024, with 50 seeds in three replicates per mother plant (150 seeds per mother plant in total). Where seed availability was limited, the maximum number of available seeds was sown (see Table 2). Each replicate was placed on two layers of filter paper overlying synthetic felt in a plastic dish and on a different growing plate lined with synthetic felt to maintain moisture. The plates were regularly rotated and watered with distilled water. The experiment was conducted at room temperature (20 °C) under a 12-hour photoperiod. Germination was monitored every 3–5 days over 2 months (65 days), after which no further germination occurred. Dishes containing remaining seedlings (not selected for FCM analyses) from populations DUP and SRP were filled with potting soil and grown for a further 48 days for subsequent FCM and genetic analyses.

Data analyses

RADseq datasets and analyses of field-collected and cultivated offspring plants

In total, 246 samples were included in the ddRADseq analyses, and three samples were processed in replicates to verify the genet assignment threshold used to identify genetic clones (see below). The sequence data were split into three datasets (A, B, and C) as follows. Dataset A (171 samples) included field-collected plants of S. gigantea from tetraploid (50 samples) and mixed-ploidy populations (92 samples, including 19 sterile), along with S. canadensis (18 samples), S. virgaurea (8 samples), and one sample each of S. sempervirens, S. shortii, and Euthamia graminifolia. Dataset B (142 samples) comprised only field-collected plants of S. gigantea from tetraploid and mixed-ploidy populations. Dataset C (212 samples) included all samples of dataset B and was supplemented with 70 samples of juvenile plants (progeny of six tetraploid and 13 pentaploid mother plants field-collected in mixed-ploidy populations) (Supplementary material 2). Dataset A was used to establish a broader phylogenetic framework and investigate the origin of pentaploid plants; Dataset B was used to examine the intrapopulation structure, and Dataset C was used to investigate the genetic structure of the progeny from mixed-ploidy populations.

Maximum likelihood phylogenetic inference was conducted in RAxML-NG v.0.9.0 [42] using PHYLIP-formatted input files. Ascertainment bias was corrected using the method of Felsenstein [16]. Variant call format file was converted to PHYLIP format with vcf2phylip.py [64], and invariant sites were removed using ascbias.py (https://github.com/btmartin721/raxml_ascbias), following the recommendations of Leaché et al. [43]. Branch support was assessed with 500 bootstrap replicates.

Neighbour network estimation and clonality assessment (the latter applied only to field-collected plants, Dataset B) were based on Nei’s genetic distances [63], calculated using the R package StAMPP [68, 74]. Distances were estimated from a diploidised VCF file retaining both homozygous and heterozygous genotypes. Neighbour networks were constructed in SplitsTree4 [33]. Clonal growth was inferred from pairwise genetic distances, with replicated samples used to define a threshold for clonality, following the approach by Tsujimoto et al., [95].

Principal component analysis (PCA) based on the covariance matrix was performed using the R package adegenet [37, 38]. Genetic structure was further inferred using the Bayesian clustering algorithm implemented in STRUCTURE v.2.3.4 [73]. To reduce linkage among loci, SNPs were grouped such that adjacent SNPs were separated by at least 1,000 bp; SNPs closer than this threshold were assigned to the same group (a RADseq locus). The vcf_prune.py script [90] was then used to generate 100 datasets by randomly selecting SNPs from each group/locus, retaining at least eight SNPs per dataset.

Statistical analyses of FCM and reproduction data

To test for differences in relative genome size, pollen viability, seed set, germination rate, pollen grain size, and well-developed seed size among groups, we used a generalised linear mixed model (GLMM; [7]) with populations and/or mother plants as random effects. Depending on the response variable gamma or binomial distributions were used. To account for heteroscedasticity dispersion parameter was included in the models. The model’s assumptions were checked using simulated residuals. The significance of fixed effects was tested using likelihood-ratio χ² tests, followed by post hoc comparisons based on estimated marginal means with the Tukey correction for multiple comparisons.

To test for differences in germination dynamics, we treated germination as a binomial process (repeated grouped counts of seeds within each mother plant over time) and used generalised additive mixed models (GAMMs; [113]). We modelled germination as an interval-based process by analysing the number of newly germinated seeds at each census relative to the number of ungerminated seeds remaining. The fixed effects included time, cytogenotype, and their factor-by-smooth interaction [112]; the random effects included populations and/or mother plants as plant-specific smooth time deviations [67]. To distinguish between differences in germination dynamics and germination rate, we additionally fitted a reduced model in which all cytogenotypes shared a common temporal trend and differed only by cytogenotype-specific effects. Models were estimated using fast restricted maximum likelihood with covariate discretisation [48] and double-penalty shrinkage, which penalises weak effects towards zero [51]. We compared the full and reduced models using Akaike’s Information Criterion after refitting with maximum likelihood. Pairwise comparisons among cytogenotypes were performed on estimated marginal means with Tukey’s adjustment [44]. For plotting purposes, cumulative germination trajectories were derived by converting fitted interval hazards to cumulative probabilities.

The analyses were performed in R 4.5.2 [74] using packages DHARMa [25], dplyr [111], emmeans [45], glmmTMB [8], multcomp [31] and mgcv [113]. All plots were created using ggplot2 [110].

For all box-and-whisker plots, the boxes correspond to the first and third quartiles, and the whiskers extend from the smallest to the largest values within 1.5 × the interquartile range. Values beyond the whisker range are plotted as outliers. For better visualisation, individual observations were displayed as jittered points. Violin plots depict kernel density estimates of the distribution of the studied variables, with the width scaled equally across groups. Boxplots within violins represent the same statistical characteristics as described above. Outliers were not displayed, while violins extend beyond boxplot whiskers to reflect the full distribution of values.

Results

Karyology and genetics of field-collected plants

Ploidy and relative genome size of field-collected plants

Detailed sampling revealed 156 pentaploid plants (2n ≈ 5x ≈ 45) and four tetraploid plants (2n ≈ 4x ≈ 36) within the DUP population, 103 pentaploid and eight tetraploid plants within the SRP population (Table 1, Supplementary material 3). Tetraploids within the DUP population were concentrated at a single spot; within the SRP population, they occurred at two spots, at the margin and also in the central part (Fig. 1). Within the DUP population, sampling did not reveal aneuploids of S. gigantea (2n ≈ 4x + 3 ≈ 39) that were previously recorded at the site [88]. Only the tetraploid level (2n ≈ 4x ≈ 36) was detected for 50 plants (10 per population) from the populations CHA, MAS, HUM, NIT, and ZBO.

Relative genome size (the 2 C values of RSS) of S. gigantea from tetraploid populations (2n ≈ 4x ≈ 36; 50 plants) ranged from 1.30 to 1.51, whereas those of pentaploids from mixed-ploidy populations (2n ≈ 5x ≈ 45; 259 plants) ranged from 1.73 to 1.83 and were significantly higher (GLMM, gamma: χ2 = 28.470, df = 1, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A; Table 1). The equivalent Cx values of RSS for pentaploids were slightly lower than those of tetraploids from tetraploid populations (Fig. 2B; Table 1); however, differences were not statistically significant (GLMM, gamma: χ2 = 0.4337, df = 1, P = 0.500). Tetraploid plants from mixed-ploidy populations were within the range of 2 C values of RSS of plants from tetraploid populations; however, some of them had Cx values of RSS more similar to those of pentaploids, although their relationships were not statistically tested due to low numbers of individuals (Fig. 2; Table 1).

Genetic structure and relationships between cytotypes inferred from RADseq data: identification of cytogenotypes

Sequencing of RADseq libraries yielded between 1,178,769 and 23,323,365 quality-filtered, deduplicated read pairs per sample (5,493,294 on average). Mapping rates to the reference genome ranged from 79.07% to 98.56%, with a mean of 97.79%. The main VCF file contained 1,075,024 SNPs distributed across 43,835 putatively unlinked genomic regions (RADseq loci), with an average read depth of 36.8×. Datasets A, B, and C (see above) represented subsets derived from the main dataset and comprised 1,054,403, 671,981, and 698,105 SNPs, respectively.

The ML tree (Dataset A, Fig. 3A) showed a well-supported clade (BS = 100%) of S. gigantea, comprising both tetraploid and pentaploid samples, separated from its close relatives. These relatives included species occurring in the European wild – native S. virgaurea and non-native S. canadensis and Euthamia graminifolia, as well as cultivated species – S. sempervirens and S. shortii.

Within S. gigantea, both ML and Neighbour network (NN) (the latter based on Dataset B; Fig. 3B) revealed two well-differentiated clades (or clusters) corresponding to the tetraploid populations (CHA, HUM, MAS, NIT, and ZBO; see Supplementary material 2), hereafter referred to as the 4xG1 cytogenotype, and the pentaploids from both SRP and DUP populations, hereafter referred to as the 5xG5 cytogenotype. The cluster of tetraploid populations included five nested tetraploid samples growing at the margin of the mixed-ploidy population SRP (see Fig. 1B). This cluster is hereafter designated as the 4xG1S cytogenotype and analysed separately, as it represents the only representatives of the 4xG1 cytogenotype capable of entering mating interactions within mixed-ploidy populations. The remaining three tetraploid samples from the SRP population formed their own well-differentiated cluster in both analyses; hereafter referred to as the 4xG2 cytogenotype (Fig. 3A, B). The tetraploid samples from the other mixed-ploidy population (DUP) formed two distinct clusters in ML and NN analyses; they were assigned to the cytogenotypes 4xG3 and 4xG4. The 4xG3 cytogenotype showed close genetic affinity with the common tetraploid 4xG1 cytogenotype. The cytogenotypes 4xG2 (from SRP) and 4xG4 (from DUP) were placed closer to the clade of pentaploids in the ML tree (with BS = 96%, Fig. 3A), while in the NN they appeared in intermediate positions between the clusters of common tetraploids (4xG1, incl. 4xG1S) and pentaploids (Fig. 3B). All pentaploid samples from the mixed-ploidy populations DUP and SRP were treated as the cytogenotype 5xG5, as they were intermingled in a well-separated cluster in both the ML and NN analyses (Fig. 3A, B). Clustering patterns suggested weaker genetic structure in the pentaploids than in the common tetraploid cytotype 4xG1.

Reproduction mode and parameters

Seed formation mode

Flow cytometric seed screening (FCSS) revealed that endosperm RSS values ranged from 2.02 to 2.72 in 505 of the 577 analysed seeds (in the remaining seeds, endosperm was not recorded). Their embryo-endosperm ratios were close to 1.5 (1.34–1.59; Table 1), indicating a sexual origin for the seeds, with double fertilisation leading to the formation of a 2 C embryo and a 3 C endosperm (Table 1).

Clonality assessment

In the frequency distribution plot of Nei’s pairwise genetic distances for individuals from the tetraploid populations (4xG1), we observed a small, isolated peak around a distance value of 0.02 (Fig. 4A). This peak overlapped with the pairwise distances between three replicated samples (0.017–0.024), followed by a gap until the value of 0.039. Therefore, distance values of approximately 0.03 can be considered the genet assignment threshold. This plot indicates that we sampled a few genetic clones in the tetraploid populations (recorded in four out of five sampled populations) despite maintaining a minimum sampling distance of 5 m. In contrast, the frequency distribution plot for pentaploid plants (5xG5) showed a single large peak, with all distances below 0.033 (Fig. 4B), suggesting that these individuals most likely belong to a single genetic clone. The frequency distribution plot for all individuals sampled from both mixed-ploidy populations displayed three peaks (Fig. 4C). The first peak corresponded to pairwise distances between individuals belonging to the same cytogenotypes, both pentaploid (5xG5) and tetraploid (4xG1S, 4xG2, 4xG3, and 4xG4). The second and third peaks corresponded to pairwise distances between individuals belonging to different ones of the aforementioned cytogenotypes.

Pollen viability and size

All analysed plants (pollen material not available for 4xG3) produced a relatively high proportion of viable pollen grains (65–100%), except for one plant from the tetraploid population HUM, which had a pollen viability of only 41% (Fig. 5A, Supplementary material 4, Table 2). Differences in the proportion of viable pollen grains between plants from tetraploid populations (4xG1) and pentaploid plants (5xG5) were not statistically significant (GLMM, binomial: χ2 = 1.1507, df = 1, P = 0.2834).

Viable pollen grains of the cytogenotypes did not differ in size (GLMM, gamma: χ2 = 5.1888, df = 4, P = 0.2685) (Supplementary material 5 - A, C). Cytogenotypes 4xG4 and 5xG5 produced a considerably higher proportion of dead pollen grains of small size compared to the remaining cytogenotypes (Supplementary material 5 - B, D). Overall differences in the size of dead pollen grains among cytogenotypes were significant (GLMM, gamma: χ²=30.854, df = 3, P < 0.001), with post hoc comparisons indicating that cytogenotype 5xG5 differed significantly from the other cytogenotypes (4xG4 not tested due to small sample size).

Seed production and size

The production of well-developed seeds differed significantly between tetraploid populations (4xG1) and pentaploids (5xG5) (GLMM, binomial: χ2 = 6.0099, df = 1, P = 0.01423) with considerable variation among populations in both cases. Plants from tetraploid populations (4xG1) produced from 0.53 to 74.07% of well-developed seeds, with the highest percentages in population CHA and the lowest in population ZBO (Fig. 5B; Table 2). Only three of the 15 pentaploid plants (5xG5) from the DUP population produced well-developed seeds, with seed production ranging from 1.53 to 22.51%, while the remaining 12 plants were completely seed-sterile. Among the pentaploids (5xG5) in the SRP population, one plant did not produce any well-developed seeds, six plants exhibited low seed production (less than 5% of well-developed seeds), five plants produced 10–25% of well-developed seeds, and only two plants produced higher percentages of well-developed seeds (42.37% and 62.71%). Production of well-developed seeds was observed particularly in the pentaploids growing in the proximity of tetraploid plants; plants in purely pentaploid patches were often sterile or had reduced seed production (Fig. 1).

Tetraploid plants from the mixed-ploidy populations varied in seed production; cytogenotypes 4xG3 and 4xG4 from the DUP population produced 21.30–25.00% and 25.34–32.54% of well-developed seeds, respectively; within the SRP population, plants of the 4xG1S cytogenotype produced only 0–2.34% and one plant of the 4xG2 cytogenotype produced 50.50% of well-developed seeds.

Well-developed seeds differed only slightly in length among cytogenotypes (GLMM, gamma: χ2 = 10.703, df = 5, P = 0.058). However, post hoc comparison indicated that this weak difference was limited to one pair of tetraploid cytogenotypes (4xG3 and 4xG5). There was no demonstrable difference in the width of the well-developed seeds among cytogenotypes (χ2 = 7.482, df = 5, P = 0.187) (Supplementary material 6 - A, B).

Seed germination rate and dynamics

The seed germination rate for mother plants from tetraploid populations (4xG1) varied markedly, from 8.67% to 72.67%. Similarly, high variation in germination rate (from 28% to 100%) was observed for cytogenotype 5xG5, although with lower overall germination success (Fig. 5C; Table 2). No significant difference in the germination rate of well-developed seeds was recorded between cytotypes 4xG1 and 5xG5 (GLMM, binomial: χ2 = 0.525, df = 1, P = 0.4689). For tetraploid cytogenotypes 4xG1S, 4xG2, 4xG3, and 4xG4, seeds were available from only a few mother plants (one or two); the germination rates of all of them were notably high (61.33–70%) (Fig. 5C; Table 2).

A comparison of binomial GAMMs (ΔAIC = 82.0) strongly favoured the reduced model in which all cytogenotypes shared a common germination dynamic and differed significantly only in the proportion of seeds germinated (χ2 = 14.64, df = 5, p = 0.012). Germination dynamics followed a consistent temporal pattern across all cytogenotypes, with a sharp peak in daily germination probability around days 7–9 followed by a rapid decline to near-zero by day 25 (Supplementary material 7 - A). Pairwise comparisons revealed that 4xG4 had a significantly higher germination rate than 4xG1, with 3.4-fold higher odds of germinating per time interval (t = 3.13, p = 0.022), while all remaining pairwise contrasts were non-significant.

Discussion

The origin of the Solidago gigantea pentaploid cytotype remains unresolved

Genetically, the studied pentaploid cytotype belongs to S. gigantea; however, it constitutes a distinct genetic lineage, divergent from the common tetraploid populations in Europe as consistently shown by phylogenetic inference, distance-based and ordination analyses (Figs. 3A and B and 7A). Therefore, it is unlikely that the pentaploid cytotype originated via in-situ autopolyploidisation in the invaded range. Furthermore, based on our genetic data, we can reject the hypothesis that hybridisation or introgression within the secondary contact zone contributed to the formation of the pentaploid genome. Specifically, we found no evidence of genetic contribution from other co-occurring native (S. virgaurea) or invasive species (S. canadensis and Euthamia graminifolia; Fig. 3A). The other two North American species (S. sempervirens and S. shortii), which are rarely cultivated in the studied area, can also be ruled out (Fig. 3A).

The presented results may trigger further research focused on the pentaploid origin that would address alternative hypotheses. One hypothesis is that this pentaploid may have arisen in the wild in its native range through autopolyploidisation or intercytotype crossing from genotype(s) distinct from the widespread one already known in the invasive range. However, an independent introduction from North American wild populations seems unlikely, as pentaploids are very rare in the native range. Only one pentaploid plant has been reported to date, despite considerable cytological research (~ 370 available records for S. gigantea in North America; [cf. 32, 36, 50, 58, 79, 82, 83; reviewed in [88]. Alternatively, this distinct genotype may have originated (intentionally or unintentionally) in cultivation for commercial sale in garden nurseries, potentially involving Solidago species not included in the present study. Neither of the alternative hypotheses, direct introduction from North America, nor horticulture, fit with the restricted spatial distribution of the pentaploid cytotype as revealed by detailed screening of 368 populations of S. gigantea in Central Europe [88].

Reproduction biology of the pentaploid cytotype of Solidago gigantea

Our study demonstrates that the pentaploid cytotype within the studied mixed-ploidy populations reproduces exclusively through clonal propagation. Given the low genetic variability detected, all sampled pentaploid stems likely represent a single clonal genet (Fig. 4). The pentaploid plants within these populations produce well-developed seeds solely via crossings with tetraploid plants in the close vicinity (Figs. 1A and B and 7). Pentaploid plants acted as both pollen donors and recipients in these heteroploidy crossings (see below), indicating that the pentaploid plants produce viable gametes, both egg cells and pollen grains. However, the complete absence of well-developed seed production in exclusively pentaploid patches within the studied populations suggests that a strict self-incompatibility system prevents offspring formation between stems of the same clonal pentaploid genotype [cf. 17]. While pentaploids did not show a reduced production rate of viable pollen grains (Fig. 5) or unbalanced pollen size (Supplementary material 5 - B, D), they did exhibit a high proportion of non-viable pollen grains of small size (Supplementary materials 4 - C, D; 5 - B, D), which likely reflects disruptions in male gametogenesis caused by the odd number of chromosome sets [cf. 53]. However, given the expected irregular meiosis in pentaploid mother plants and the highly variable relative genome size and chromosome numbers of the offspring (Fig. 6; Table 3; Supplementary material 9), the limited size variability of viable pollen grains of pentaploids (Supplementary material 5 - B, D) is somewhat surprising. However, as the present study examined only a relatively small number of pollen grains per plant (100), a more comprehensive pollen analysis focused on the pentaploid S. gigantea is needed.

Mating interactions between pentaploid and tetraploid cytotypes

Our findings showed that pentaploid S. gigantea plants engage in mating with co-occurring tetraploid plants, with the presence of tetraploid plants being crucial for pentaploid sexual reproduction (Fig. 1). The crossings are bidirectional. Both pentaploid and tetraploid mother plants produced offspring showing genetic admixture (Fig. 7). This mating resulted in the production of well-developed seeds (Fig. 5B) with relative DNA content and chromosome numbers ranging from 4x to 5x in offspring from both tetraploid and pentaploid mother plants (Fig. 6; Table 3; Supplementary material 9). These seeds had a germination rate and dynamics similar to those of offspring from the common tetraploid cytotype (Fig. 5C; Supplementary material 7). The observed high variability in germination rate and dynamics among tetraploid populations has also been reported in other experimental studies [cf. 2, 41]. As seeds of S. gigantea did not exhibit innate dormancy and maintained high seed vigour after storage across a wide range of temperatures [5], the high variability in germination parameters may therefore be attributed to differences in reproductive fitness among non-native populations and individual plants. Similarly, intra- and interpopulation variation in seed production might be related to site characteristics, such as community type and selection pressure, with biomass allocated to clonal expansion negatively affecting seed mass [109]. The high proportion of aneuploids within the offspring of mother plants from mixed-ploidy populations did not affect germination or further growth of seedlings into juvenile plants (we did not observe differences in genome size variability across developmental stages; Supplementary material 8). The high viability and comparable germination rates of offspring derived from interactions suggest that S. gigantea possesses remarkable tolerance to chromosomal instability.

Consequences for the invasiveness of Solidago gigantea

To assess the potential impact of the pentaploid cytotype, it is essential to consider the well-documented negative effects of Solidago gigantea (common tetraploid cytotypes) on native ecosystems. These include the homogenisation of plant communities ([19, 26]; Slezák et al., unpubl. results) and alterations of soil properties that can persist even after the species’ removal [10, 20, 29, 77, 92, 100]. Contemporary adaptive evolution can further amplify these impacts by enhancing the survival and reproductive rates of problematic invasive species [12]. Our results challenge the traditional view of odd-ploidy cytotypes as reproductive dead-ends. Given the observed successful spreading of pentaploid plants and the continuous generation of new genotypes within the mixed population, we propose three potential evolutionary pathways through which the pentaploid cytotype may contribute to the invasiveness of S. gigantea.

Extensive clonal spreading of the pentaploidcytotype

Previous research on the common tetraploid cytotype of S. gigantea has shown that sexual reproduction is critical for colonising new sites [36, 107]. Once established, local spread is driven almost exclusively by vegetative growth, leading to the formation of dense stands [57, 108]. Moreover, in its non-native range, S. gigantea has been shown to allocate more biomass to below-ground structures, exhibit enhanced clonal spread, and be less likely to flower than its native counterparts [23, 36, 78]. The long-creeping rhizomes of S. gigantea easily fragment and become rooted; even rhizome fragments buried at 20 cm depth can produce new shoots [36]. Based on the observed genetic pattern, the newly discovered pentaploid plants within the studied populations appear to multiply exclusively vegetatively at least as successfully as the common tetraploid cytotype, if not more so. Within the studied populations, pentaploid plants grow in distinct patches distributed over broad areas (in the DUP population, approximately 500 m long) and form dense stands that dominate plant communities (Fig. 1D, E, H, I). In the field, we also observed vegetative propagation via above-ground parts: when long stems lie on the ground, they can root (Fig. 1G). The pattern of genetic variation suggests that the pentaploid cytotype is being transported vegetatively along the river, likely facilitated by the river corridor. We hypothesise that this cytotype represents a highly successful clonal life strategy. Its high resprouting capacity enables it to spread both locally and over long distances. A pertinent parallel can be found in Reynoutria japonica Houtt., which, despite reproducing exclusively clonally in its invaded range, has become one of the world’s most successful invasive species [101, 104]. Consequently, management efforts focused on S. gigantea pentaploids should pay attention to activities that promote plant fragmentation and the dispersal of these highly regenerative propagules.

Genetic enrichment of the invasive tetraploid cytotype

Our results provide evidence of ongoing gene flow and generation of introgressed genotypes within mixed-ploidy populations. Progeny analysis confirmed successful crossing between tetraploid and pentaploid plants, yielding genetically admixed offspring. Cytogenotypes 4xG2 and 4xG4, characterised by such genetic admixture (Fig. 7B), demonstrate that at least some tetraploid plants originating from 4x-5x crosses are capable of establishment, flowering, and backcrossing with pentaploid plants to produce viable seeds of subsequent hybrid generations. Ongoing interploidy crossings may introduce new dynamics to the invasive success of S. gigantea tetraploids. Crossing between disparate source populations in the invaded range, represented here by common tetraploids and newly discovered pentaploids of S. gigantea, may lead to the evolution of genotypes adapted to new ecological niches and stimulate the evolution of invasiveness [cf. 15].

Establishment of new aneuploid genotypes

Comparison of cytological and genetic profiles of field-collected plants (only tetraploid and pentaploid plants, with only a few tetraploids showing genetic admixture) with their progeny (the majority of which were attributable to aneuploidy, and all of which showed genetic admixture) (Figs. 6 and 7; Supplementary material 9) suggests strong selection against aneuploids in natural populations. Based on the theory of minority cytotype disadvantage, it could be predicted that the rarer ploidy levels would experience a large loss of offspring [46]. However, only minor differences were detected in germination and growth across developmental stages (embryos, seedlings, juvenile plants), suggesting that selection does not act during these critical early life-cycle phases. Also, in other studies, selection appeared not to act on germination or seedling establishment in S. gigantea, and it has previously been recommended to analyse longer periods of experimental growth (at least two years) to reveal differences between cytological and genetic lineages [41, 62]. It should be noted that in the dense stands of S. gigantea with high stem and rhizome density in the studied population (see Fig. 1D, E, H, I), the establishment of new Solidago plants is limited by high competition belowground and aboveground. Furthermore, germination and seedling growth could be inhibited by autoallelopathic effects known for invasive goldenrods [cf. 39]). Nevertheless, selection against aneuploids in studied ploidy-mixed populations does not appear to be absolute. The previous record of two aneuploid individuals of S. gigantea (2n ≈ 4x + 3 ≈ 39) within the DUP population [88] provides evidence that such genotypes can occasionally persist (although they were not re-recorded in this study). Therefore, the possibility that aneuploid plants may become established within mixed-ploidy populations in the near future cannot be excluded, and their contribution to the cytological and genetic variability of S. gigantea might be expected. Although it is expected that aneuploids may have reduced fertility due to disrupted meiosis, it can be successfully compensated by asexual reproduction (i.e., increased vegetative reproduction) [cf. 28, 98, 114]).
