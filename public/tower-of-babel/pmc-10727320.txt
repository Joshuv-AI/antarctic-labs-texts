The American Heart Association Resuscitation Science Symposium (ReSS) was held in Chicago, Illinois from November 4 to 6, 2022. This report summarizes ReSS programming, including awards, special sessions, and scientific content organized by theme and plenary session.

Awards

Dr Tom P. Aufderheide (Medical College of Wisconsin) was presented the Lifetime Achievement Award in Cardiac Resuscitation Science. With over 30 years of continuously funded resuscitation research, he reflected on his recent work on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation–facilitated resuscitation and the physiology of death. He highlighted a recent publication on cutting‐edge postmortem brain reperfusion and cellular restoration to reframe our understanding of death. 1 He called for future resuscitative science to focus on neuroprotective resuscitation as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation–facilitated resuscitation has clearly shifted the field.

The ReSS Champion Award was presented first to Ward M. Hamilton, Zoll Foundation President, for his commitment to improving resuscitation research over his 40‐year career in the resuscitative device industry work. The Zoll Foundation was established in 2013 with the purpose of engaging and funding scientists. He encouraged ReSS to look toward the future, suggesting a meeting inclusive of the resuscitative community beyond scientists, translating resuscitative science from the laboratory to the community.

Kristin Flanary was also awarded the ReSS Champion Award for her instrumental work in survivorship. As copatient editor for the Journal of Cardiac Failure and survivorship advocate, she accepted the award with a call for collaborative work in cardiac arrest survivorship. Speaking from personal experience from her husband's cardiac arrest and resuscitation, she called for expansion of the currently limited support available for survivors, cosurvivors, and lay responders in cardiac arrest.

The Targeted Temperature Management (TTM) Trials Group was awarded the Ian G. Jacobs Award for International Group Collaboration to Advance Resuscitation Science for their work in postarrest targeted temperature management. On behalf of the group and their landmark trials, Dr Niklas Nielsen accepted the award with a virtual speech that discussed the TTM Trials Group future trial of 3500 patients investigating post cardiac arrest temperature, sedation, and mean arterial pressure.

ReSS Young Investigator Networking Event

After a 2‐year hiatus, the annual Young Investigator Mentoring and Networking Event was organized and hosted by Dr Kelly Sawyer (University of Pittsburgh), and Dr Sachin Agarwal (Columbia University), and attended by more than 100 people. Dr Michael Christopher Kurz (University of Alabama at Birmingham) gave an impactful keynote address entitled “Fruits of an Accidental Career in Resuscitation Science.” He acknowledged academic successes, failures, and opportunities and brought his daughter, Claire Kurz, to emphasize work–life balance. Young Investigator awards were presented to 14 early career researchers (Table S1). These investigators were within the first 5 years of their appointments and had the top‐scoring abstracts in resuscitation science research. 3CPR council EMS Travel Stipends for 2021 and 2022 were also announced at this event.

Women in Resuscitation Science Networking Workshop

Led by co‐chairs Dr Lorrel Toft (University of Nevada) and Dr Pavitra Kotini‐Shah (University of Illinois at Chicago), the Women in Resuscitation Networking Meeting brought together female resuscitation scientists and allies from around the world to discuss topics relevant to developing careers in resuscitation science, academia, and/or medicine. Attendees asked questions, shared their experiences, and gave advice in round‐table conversations centered around “Redefining Success,” “Allyship & Mentorship,” “What is a ‘Good Mentee’,” and “Work‐Life Integration.” These group discussions highlighted the value of developing a diverse portfolio of mentors, setting clear and intentional expectations with oneself and others, and being unashamed of leveraging resources and support systems to reach one's goals.

SPECIAL SESSIONS

Cutting Edge Trials and Late‐Breaking Resuscitation Science

Dr Sheldon Cheskes (University of Toronto) presented the DOSE VF (Double Sequential External Defibrillation for Refractory Ventricular Fibrillation). 2 Approximately 400 patients with out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) were randomized to receive standard defibrillation, double sequential external defibrillation, or vector change defibrillation (switching pads to the anterior–posterior position). Those treated with either double sequential defibrillation or with vector change defibrillation had improved survival to hospital discharge; those receiving double sequential defibrillation were also more likely to have good neurologic outcomes.

Dr Christian Hassager (The Heart Center, Copenhagen University Hospital) presented results from a randomized trial of duration of device‐based fever prevention after OHCA. 3 Comatose patients resuscitated from OHCA were randomized to device‐based temperature control at 37 °C for 12 versus 48 hours following an initial 24‐hour period of 36 °C. Approximately 400 patients were randomized to each group; however, survival and neurologic outcome did not differ between the groups at 90 days.

Dr Tomoyoshi Tamura (Keio University School of Medicine) discussed the results of the HYBRID II Trial (efficacy of inhaled hydrogen on neurological outcomes following brain ischemia during post–cardiac arrest care). 4 Comatose survivors of OHCA were randomized to receive 2% hydrogen gas mixed with oxygen versus standard oxygen for 18 hours. While only 72 patients of the targeted 360 were randomized, and the authors acknowledged the small sample size, those treated with hydrogen gas experienced improved 90‐day survival with a modified Rankin scale of 0 (46% versus 12%, P=0.03).

Dr Sam Parnia (NYU Langone Health) presented a multicenter study investigating awareness during resuscitation. Of 567 patients with in‐hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA), 28 survivors completed interviews with 11 reporting memories and perceptions suggestive of consciousness. Only 1 patient recalled the correct auditory stimulus presented during the actual arrest and no patients recalled the visual stimulus. By combining the use of brain monitoring systems and interviews postarrest, they recognized a spectrum of consciousness, awareness, and cognitive activity during cardiac arrest.

Dr Katherine M. Berg (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center) discussed the effect of thiamine on lactate clearance and oxygen consumption after IHCA. Adults with sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) after IHCA were treated with thiamine for 48 hours. The trial was halted early by the Data and Safety Monitoring Board after 36 patients were enrolled, because mortality was higher among the subgroup of patients with a lactate >5 treated with thiamine. Overall group survival was not altered by thiamine administration; lactate and other markers were similar between groups.

Dr Michael Donnino (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center) presented on thiamine as a metabolic resuscitator after cardiac arrest. Comatose OHCA survivors with elevated lactate levels were randomized to IV thiamine or placebo. Among 76 individuals enrolled, thiamine was associated with increased creatinine and pyruvate dehydrogenase; however, not with improved lactate at 72 hours. Mortality between groups was similar and, as with the trial presented by Dr Berg, this trial was stopped early by the Data and Safety Monitoring Board for similar concerns.

Dr Sebastian Wolfrum (University Schleswig Holstein) presented a multicenter randomized clinical trial comparing hypothermic temperature control (32–34 °C) to normothermia (<37.5 °C) after IHCA to evaluate mortality and functional outcome. Although the study was underpowered due to sample size, hypothermia did not improve survival nor functional outcome at 180 days in patients with coma after IHCA.

Best of the Best Oral Abstracts

Dr Kelley Branch (University of Washington School of Medicine) presented a prospective observational study of the implementation of a head‐to‐pelvis computed tomography protocol following OHCA. 5 Relative to standard of care, the investigators found an increase in the identification of the cause of OHCA and earlier diagnosis of critical or intervenable causes.

Dr Julia Garcia Mancebo (Boston Children's Hospital) presented her group's work investigating the use of immediate‐release intravenous oxygen microparticles during resuscitation. Pigs underwent asphyxia‐induced cardiac arrest, were treated with Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support, and received either intravenous oxygen or placebo. Animals treated with intravenous oxygen had briefer cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) times, higher rates of ROSC, and improved neurologic deficit scores compared with placebo‐treated animals.

Dr Monique Gardner (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia) presented a multicenter observational study of children with IHCA that aimed to identify clinically relevant postarrest blood pressure thresholds and found that systolic blood pressure >10th percentile for age and diastolic blood pressure >50th percentile for age during the first 6 hours postarrest were associated with higher rates of survival to hospital discharge and favorable neurologic outcome.

Dr Chelsea Morin (University of Alberta) presented a preclinical randomized trial of chest compressions with sustained inflation compared with chest compressions with asynchronous ventilations in pediatric piglets with asphyxia‐associated cardiac arrest. 6 Animals treated with chest compressions with sustained inflation more frequently achieved ROSC, had briefer time to ROSC, and had higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure during resuscitation.

The Future of Defibrillation

Dr Ruud Koster (Amsterdam University Medical Center) delivered a lecture where he spoke of the need to take a closer look at the expanded role that automated external defibrillators (AEDs) could have inside the home. Dr Koster concluded that improvements in survival were possible if the following criteria were met: sufficient lay responders were trained in CPR and AED use, the entire chain of survival was optimized, enough AEDs were available in residential areas, and AED placement was optimized. 6

Dr Marcus Ong (Singapore General Hospital) gave the audience a summary of Singapore's years‐long journey of improvement using the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation's Scientific Statement on innovative approaches to public‐access defibrillation as a guiding framework. 7 He provided examples from a constellation of programs in which the “Man–Machine‐Mission” worked together to improve Utstein survival over time.

Dr Sofia Schierbeck (Karolinska Institute) summarized efforts to implement the use of AED delivered in real‐time during OHCAs. She highlighted that both the European Resuscitation Council and the American Heart Association (AHA) support further research on AED delivery by drones 8 , 9 and that theoretical geographical information system–based studies have demonstrated a time savings compared with traditional Emergency Medical Services response, 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 while simulation studies found AED drones to be both feasible 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 and seen by bystanders as safe and positive. 20 , 21 Dr Schierbeck then supplied a brief Swedish‐specific synopsis of their efforts to fully operationalize their AED drone delivery system, ending with the now infamous video of successful drone AED delivery and defibrillation before Emergency Medical Services arrival. 22

Ryan Huebinger (UT Health of Houston) highlighted disparities in the way we deploy, access, and train to use AEDs. Although he acknowledged gender disparities, his talk focused on issues surrounding the lower rates of training and comfort that Black, indigenous, people of color communities feel in using an AED. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 Dr Huebinger closed by reiterating Dr Koster's focus on optimizing AED distribution but doing so in a way that accounted for known disparities.

Bench to Bedside: Metabolism and Arrest

Dr Cameron Dezfulian (Texas Children's Hospital) described how cardiac arrest disrupts the availability and balance of nitrogen oxide (NO) and hydrogen sulfide (H2S), potentially contributing to poor cardiac arrest outcomes. Indeed, some animal studies of NO and H2S supplementation have shown benefit. 27 , 28 Nevertheless, restoring optimal NO and H2S balance is challenging because of lack of available drug and delivery systems, as well as unknown impacts on the gut microbiome.

Dr Mikael F. Vallentin (Prehospital Emergency Medical Services, Central Denmark Region) reported on a preplanned subanalysis of the recently published COCA (Calcium for Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest) Trial. 29 The COCA Trial compared calcium against placebo in out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest and was stopped early due to harm in the intervention (calcium) group in January 2020. In the subanalysis of patients with pulseless electrical activity and electrocardiographic characteristics potentially associated with hyperkalemia and ischemia substudy, effect estimates were similar to the primary trial results even in the population of patients with features suggestive of hyperkalemic arrest (albeit with wide confidence intervals, likely owing to the smaller sample size). These findings do not support the use of calcium in OHCA based on electrocardiographic findings suggestive of hyperkalemia.

Dr Casey B. Patrick (Harris County ESD11 Mobile Healthcare and Montgomery County Hospital District Emergency Medical Services service in Greater Houston) next discussed the use of beta‐blocking agents during cardiac arrest. Small observational studies have supported the use of beta‐blocking agents in cardiac arrest with refractory shockable rhythm. 30 In a study recently published by Dr Patrick and colleagues, a protocol change to encourage the use of esmolol in refractory ventricular fibrillation was leveraged to study the feasibility of beta‐blockade in cardiac arrest with refractory shockable rhythm. Comparing 63 patients preprotocol change to 70 refractory ventricular fibrillation patients after protocol change, the authors found that EMS clinicians could identify refractory ventricular fibrillation and administer esmolol. As compared with the historical controls, patients who received esmolol had a higher rate of ROSC (38% vs 24%), although this did not reach the a priori defined threshold for statistical significance. 31

Dr Thomas Sanderson (University of Michigan) provided updates on new insights into mitochondrial metabolism during ischemia–reperfusion injury. Near infrared light was identified as 1 potential therapeutic modality able to modulate brain mitochondrial activity during stress. Dr Sanderson reported that large animal studies have been promising, and human trials are coming. Finding the optimal dose is challenging, because the goal is to modulate mitochondrial function without injuring healthy tissue.

Dickinson W. Richards Memorial Lecture

Dr Clifton Callaway (University of Pittsburgh) presented the Dickinson W. Richards Memorial Lecture. Dr Callaway is Professor, Vice‐Chair, and Ronald D. Stewart Endowed Chair of Emergency Medicine Research at the University of Pittsburgh. He has pioneered a novel multidisciplinary clinical service at the University of Pittsburgh dedicated to providing evidence‐based care to patients who are post‐cardiac arrest. He is the past chair of the Emergency Cardiovascular Care committee at the AHA, the past co‐chair of the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation Advanced Life Support Task Force and has been integral to the development of clinical guidelines since 2008. Dr Callaway's research is focused upon biochemical effects on the brain after ischemia–reperfusion, mechanisms for the effects of temperature manipulation, quantitative measures of ECG organization, prognostication of coma after cardiac arrest, and methodologic challenges for clinical trials in critical illness. Dr Callaway has been a tireless leader in resuscitation research for over 3 decades, a generous mentor to many, and an international leader in advocating for evidence‐based care to advance outcomes for patients in cardiac arrest. Dr Callaway is most deserving of this prestigious award presented to him by the 3CPR Council of the AHA.

Dr Callaway presented the Dickinson Richards Memorial lecture entitled “What Happens After the Hospital Is Part of Resuscitation Care.” Through this discussion, Dr Callaway summarized the addition of a sixth link in the Chain of Survival, the link that encompasses survivorship. He emphasized the importance of the recovery that continues beyond the hospital. Dr Callaway described the deficits commonly encountered in survivors including physical and neurologic deficits. He also stressed the common incidence of both cognitive and psychological impairments that are not always evident at hospital discharge. Citing prior work, Dr Callaway emphasized the importance of ongoing rehabilitation where survivors can be provided resources and support to aid in their recovery.

As part of this instrumental lecture, Dr Callaway described the experiences of survivors. Frequently they describe a lack of readiness for discharge or feeling as though their needs are not being met at discharge. As part of ongoing care at University of Pittsburgh Medical Center, Dr Callaway described their experiences including anxiety and memory problems, but most pervasive is a sense of fatigue, which is potentially due to physical limitations, cognitive load, or even depression. Dr Callaway called for clinicians to be considerate of discharge planning that should consider rehabilitation services, mental health resources, and structured care that provides information on potential deficits. As Dr Callaway stated, “we underdo the care people need” at hospital discharge. He made a strong call for improving discharge planning, setting expectations, and continuing to support survivors through their recovery after cardiac arrest.

EPIDEMIOLOGIC AND OUTCOMES‐BASED RESUSCITATION SCIENCE

Disparities in Resuscitation Care

Dr Kavya Shankar (Mercy Catholic Medical Center) presented an analysis of disparities in patient demographics related to in‐hospital outcomes in patients requiring mechanical ventilation following cardiac arrest. In this retrospective study of the National Inpatient Sample Database, out of 216 945 patients, 8600 had ventilator dependence. In patients with cardiac arrest, the group with ventilator dependence had lower mortality (44.36% versus 60.21%, P<0.001) but longer length of stay (20.63 versus 8.32 days, P<0.008). Ventilator dependence was more common in younger patients, and Black and Hispanic individuals. In addition, the ventilator dependence group had higher odds of having acute or chronic diastolic heart failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome, septic shock, acute ischemic stroke, brain death, bacteremia, persistent vegetative state, and hypo‐ and hyperkalemia.

INTERVENTIONS AND THERAPIES IN RESUSCITATION SCIENCE

Optimizing Intra‐Arrest Outcomes and Presentation of the Max Harry Weil Award,

Dr Colten Stewart (University of Iowa) used the Get With The Guidelines‐Resuscitation (GWTG‐R) registry to examine the use of epinephrine before first defibrillation in adults with an initial shockable rhythm. There is marked hospital variability in epinephrine administration before first defibrillation, and this practice is associated with lower rates of survival to discharge.

Dr Cody Couperus (University of Maryland) and his team sought to accelerate the time to code team activation and increase early distribution of patient‐specific data with a smartphone‐driven messaging system. 32 By mapping code blue buttons behind each patient bed to patient data, they sent patient‐specific data through a secure text messaging system to the code blue teams' smartphones, reducing the time to code team activation by over 1 minute.

Dr Takahiro Nakashima (University of Michigan) presented his team's pilot study testing the hypothesis that continuing mechanical chest compressions after initiation of percutaneous ventricular assist devices would improve hemodynamics by decompressing the right ventricle and augmenting pulmonary blood flow and left ventricular filling using a swine model of prolonged cardiac arrest. They found that the addition of mechanical chest compressions to percutaneous ventricular assist devices support during cardiac arrest potentially may generate higher percutaneous ventricular assist devices flow and cardiac output compared with percutaneous ventricular assist devices alone, but the results were not conclusive.

Dr Rebecca N. Treffalls (R. Adams Cowley Shock Trauma Center) measured cerebral blood flow in swine undergoing open cardiac massage in hypovolemia and normovolemia using computed tomography perfusion scanning. Although open cardiac massage in normovolemic swine had better hemodynamics than those who were hypovolemic, neither group had adequate cerebral blood flow as measured by computed tomography perfusion compared with their baseline.

Max Harry Weil Award Presentation

Dr Yusuke Endo (Northwell Health) received the 2022 Max Harry Weil Award as a young investigator with significant potential in biomedical resuscitation science. He presented the results of his work using a pig model to validate tissue‐to‐arterial carbon dioxide partial pressure gradient during hemorrhagic shock and resuscitation as a noninvasive measure of hemorrhagic shock. 33

BASIC SCIENCE (ORAL ABSTRACT PRESENTATIONS)

Basic Science

Laken Irish (The MetroHealth System, Case Western Reserve University) presented a study aimed at understanding potential mechanisms leading to pulseless electrical activity rearrest during the post‐ROSC period. To evaluate this, they looked at myocardial metabolic and electrolyte derangements in a pig model. The rearrest group was found to have derangements in cardiac sodium and calcium, as well as increased lactate consumption. In conclusion, myocardial electrolyte abnormalities are a potential mechanism of pulseless electrical activity rearrest and improved understanding of these mechanisms may identify novel therapeutic approaches.

Dr Matt Oberdier (Johns Hopkins University) evaluated a treatment peptide to potentially influence heart and brain metabolism when given during swine pulseless electrical activity arrest. ROSC was more common in the peptide group but was not statistically significant. Survival was increased in the treatment peptide group with all survivors neurologically intact.

Dr Lin Piao (University of Chicago) hypothesized that brief mild hypoxia following CPR would stimulate mitochondrial activity, leading to improved survival following cardiac arrest. This intervention was studied in mice, where they found increased survival, neurologic outcomes, and cardiac function in hypoxia compared with normoxia or hyperoxia. They identified an oxygen‐dependent therapeutic window following resuscitation that has implications for post cardiac arrest care.

Dr Cody Rutledge (University of Pittsburgh) presented a mouse model to evaluate whether cardiac mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) overexpression is associated with improved outcomes postarrest. Mitochondrial transcription factor A overexpression protects cardiac function and improves mortality, which may be mediated by protein translation priming the heart before ischemic insult.

Resuscitation Science Year in Review

Year in Review: Adults

Dr Gavin Perkins (University of Warwick, United Kingdom) presented the Year in Review for adults, where he reviewed 14 papers spanning all links in the chain of survival in cardiac arrest.

He opened with several studies focused on the epidemiology of both OHCA and IHCA, including a report from the Cardiac Arrest Enhancing Survival registry noting significant variation in OHCA outcomes across Emergency Medical Services agencies in the United States. 34 A Swedish study of both OHCA and IHCA documented a declining incidence of ventricular fibrillation and ischemic heart disease leading to cardiac arrest, as well as no improvement in neurological outcome over an ≈30‐year study period. 35 Dr Perkins highlighted 2 systematic reviews, 1 focused on wearable technologies for cardiac arrest detection, and another on dispatching citizen responders. 36 , 37

Next, Dr Perkins reviewed studies focused on activation, CPR, and defibrillation. Garcia et al described disparities in bystander CPR according to race and ethnicity, both when OHCA events occurred in public locations and at home. 38 Scquizzato et al published a systematic review and meta‐analysis of mobile technologies used to alert citizen responders and found higher survival at OHCA for which citizen responders were activated. 36 Moore et al compared 222 patients who received automated, controlled elevation of the head and thorax during CPR with historical controls, and reported a 3.3 higher odds of survival to hospital discharge when this technique was deployed within 11 minutes from emergency call. 39 Benson et al reported on AED use in UK schools, noting a 0.3% incidence of cardiac arrest within schools but a 37% incidence within 300 m of a school, highlighting a need for making school‐based AEDs publicly accessible. 40 Finally, Dr Perkins highlighted a case report and video published by Schierbeck in the New England Journal of Medicine documenting delivery of an AED to a citizen responder using a drone. 23

Dr Perkins then discussed studies focused on advanced resuscitation. Using data from the AHA's GWTG‐R, Evans et al noted that 1 in 4 patients with IHCA are treated with epinephrine before defibrillation, which is associated with lower odds of survival to discharge. 41 The COCA (Calcium after Cardiac Arrest) trial demonstrated that intravenous calcium compared with placebo conferred no benefit during OHCA, and a signal of potential harm detectable at 1 year. 29 , 42 Belohavek et al led a prehospital trial of invasive management of OHCA, including extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, that found no significant difference in the primary end point of survival with intact neurological function at 180 days, though significant crossover occurred and a subanalysis focused on patients with ventricular fibrillation demonstrated benefit. 43

Moving to postcardiac arrest care, Dr Perkins reviewed the EXACT (reduction of oxygen after cardiac arrest) trial led by Drs Stephen Bernard and Janet Bray. The trial found no difference among patients with OHCA randomized in the prehospital setting to oxygen titrated to maintain an arterial saturation of 90% to 94% compared with 98% to 100%, though more hypoxemic episodes occurred in the 90% to 94% group. 44 Yeo et al published a systematic review and meta‐analysis documenting improved survival and neurologic outcome among patients treated at Cardiac Arrest Centers. 45 Kjaergaard et al published the BOX (Blood Pressure and Oxygenation) trial, which randomized patients to blood pressure and oxygenation targets using a 2‐by‐2 factorial design, and found no difference among the targets. 46 , 47 Collectively, the trial supports standard targets of mean arterial pressure of 65 mm Hg and partial pressure of arterial oxygen of 70 to 105 mm Hg (9–14 kPa). Dr Perkins highlighted an International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation Consensus on Science and Treatment Recommendation focused on targeted temperature management, which emphasized fever avoidance and acknowledged uncertainty around cooling to 32 to 24 °C in certain subgroups. 48

A systematic review and Bayesian meta‐analysis led by Aneman et al pooled data from 7 trials including over 3000 patients and concluded that the benefit of TTM in the 32 to 34 °C range is likely small. 49 A randomized trial found no difference in neurological outcome among postarrest patients treated with antiseizure medication to suppress rhythmic and periodic electroencephalogram findings compared with those who received standard care. 50

Finally, Dr Perkins highlighted important work being done by Dr Kelly Sawyer and others focusing on survivorship, including a recent article discussing gaps, future research, and clinical priorities. 51

The Sixth Link: From Cardiac Arrest Survival to Developing Interventions for Optimal Survivorship.

This presentation was held at the AHA main sessions, was moderated by Dr Jacqueline Tamis‐Holland (Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai), and emphasized the need for interventions to improve survivorship after cardiac arrest.

The first presentation was by Dr Sachin Agarwal (Columbia University). He presented an overview of the scientific advancements to optimize the recovery of cardiac arrest survivors and families. He reviewed studies aimed at alleviating the fear of recurrence and death, as well as studies testing the influence of intensive rehabilitation, and postdischarge clinics. Additionally, he discussed the idea of prioritizing cosurvivors and bereaved family members after cardiac arrest with the ultimate goal of achieving acceptable quality of life and advancing health of all those affected by cardiac arrest.

The second presenter, Dr Marina Del Rios (University of Iowa Carver College of Medicine), discussed equity in resuscitation outcomes. She acknowledged the gender, racial and ethnic, income, and rural disparities prevalent in the United States, and highlighted the need for developing interventions to bring us closer to health justice.

Dr Bryan McNally (Emory University School of Medicine) discussed his journey to create community‐based interventions including the Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival. This network allows communities to measure each link in their chain of survival, and can be further used to improve survivorship‐based efforts.

Finally, Dr Samantha Fernandez Hernandez (Baylor University College of Medicine) discussed her personal experience with cardiac arrest in 2020. She reflected on her cardiac arrest event, which occurred during her first year of neurology residency. She highlighted how unprepared she felt for the cognitive and psychological effects of surviving. She discussed existing community‐based organizations and nonprofits working in silos and looks forward to a future where resources are better designed and accessible to survivors and their families.

Year in Review: Pediatrics

Dr Melissa Parker (McMaster University, Canada) presented the Year in Review for Pediatrics where she discussed 14 studies spanning the domains of OHCA, peri‐arrest and intra‐arrest management, trauma and hemorrhage, and prognostication and outcomes.

In the OHCA domain, Dr Parker first discussed a study by Goto et al that documented the Japanese experience with dispatcher‐assisted CPR in over 8000 children, reporting improved survival among children treated with dispatcher‐assisted CPR compared with compression‐only CPR. 52 Le Bastard et al published a study using data from the French national OHCA registry documenting lower odds of 30‐day survival and favorable neurological outcome among children treated with endotracheal intubation compared with supraglottic procedure. 53 Harris et al externally validated the Maryland Pediatric Termination of Resuscitation criteria in a large national database, and found specificity of 99.7%. 54

Moving into peri‐arrest and intra‐arrest management, Dr Parker reviewed a survey conducted by Ross et al querying pediatric resuscitation experts about peri‐arrest bolus epinephrine. They reported that 94% of experts would consider use, but practice patterns varied across the 49 respondents from 9 countries. 55 O'Brien et al demonstrated that using an end‐tidal carbon dioxide–guided algorithm during CPR improved hemodynamics and short‐term survival in a pediatric swine model of asphyxia‐induced arrest. 56 Cashen et al conducted a secondary analysis of the ICU‐RESUScitation project in which they demonstrated that sodium bicarbonate administration was uncommon and associated with lower rates of survival to hospital discharge. 57 Dhillon et al conducted a propensity‐matched analysis of children with heart disease in the AHA's GQTG‐R registry to demonstrate an association between calcium administration and unfavorable outcome. 58

Regarding trauma and hemorrhage, Dr Parker reviewed guidelines focused on plasma and platelet transfusion in traumatic brain injury and intracranial hemorrhage. 59 Spinella et al examined use of antifibrinolytics in a multicenter prospective cohort study of children with hemorrhagic shock and found lower odds of 6‐ and 24‐hour mortality. 60

Finally, in the prognostication and outcomes domain, Yasuda et al validated the revised post‐Cardiac Arrest Syndrome for Therapeutic hypothermia score in a pediatric population and found excellent performance for predicting poor neurological outcome. 61 Smith et al demonstrated that diffusion abnormalities on magnetic resonance imaging in children add to the prognostic value of electroencephalography. 62 El‐Zein et al compared outcomes among children with COVID‐19 and those without who had in‐hospital cardiac arrest and found no significant differences in ROSC or survival. 63 Using machine learning–based modeling, Daley et al demonstrated that 6 easily obtained clinical variables are strong predictors of outcome in pediatric traumatic brain injury. 64 Collins et al conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed at identifying outcome tools to assess functional outcomes and health‐related quality of life among pediatric trauma patients. 65

CONCLUSIONS

The 2022 ReSS meeting brought together scientists from around the world to work toward advancing the field of resuscitation in a multifaceted approach. From basic science laboratory research to cutting edge trials to survivor and cosurvivor accounts of personal experiences, this symposium encouraged new perspectives related to resuscitation. The in‐person format allowed for the much‐needed return to networking and collaboration that inspires us to work together to promote progress in the field of resuscitation science.
