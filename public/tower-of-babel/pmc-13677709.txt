Automated screening of autism via a pretrained vision-language model in naturalistic caregiver-child interactions
Authors: Ruikang Deng, Huishi Huang, Shaoli Lv, Yu Xing, Yijie Li, Cong You, Fei Wu, Hongzhu Deng, Zhengxing Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early autism screening is essential for timely intervention but remains constrained by specialist dependence, costly protocols, and limited accessibility. We present Autism-CLIP, a vision-language model that automates screening from 3-min naturalistic caregiver-child interaction videos recorded in toddlers aged 15–24 months. By integrating contrastive learning, large language model–generated supervision, domain-specific architecture, and hybrid training, Autism-CLIP aligns visual behavioral cues with autism-related assessment outcomes, enabling annotation-free inference. In multicenter evaluation, Autism-CLIP achieved an AUC of 0.903 in the internal cohort (n = 103) and 0.873 in the external cohort (n = 102), outperforming conventional machine learning methods. Attention mapping highlighted clinically relevant markers, including gaze aversion and limited social reciprocity. Requiring only smartphone-captured videos without manual coding or controlled settings, Autism-CLIP offers a scalable, low-cost approach to broaden access to early autism screening and support prioritization of clinical resources.

Introduction

Autism, affecting 1 in 31 individuals in the United States1, and with a pooled prevalence of 0.70% among 6–12-year-old children in China, is characterized by social communication challenges and restricted/repetitive behaviors that impair daily functioning2. Early screening of autism is critical: neurodevelopmental trajectories in the first two years of life are marked by heightened neural plasticity3,4, offering a pivotal window for interventions that can substantially improve long-term outcomes5,6. During this period, subtle behavioral markers of autism—such as reduced joint attention, diminished eye contact, and delayed responsiveness to social cues—often emerge7,8. Yet, a significant proportion of children with autism remain undiagnosed before the age of 2, with only 49% having received their first evaluation by age 31, underscoring systemic barriers in current screening paradigms.

Conventional autism screening relies on clinician-administered tools like the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS)9,10, which demand specialized training, costly resources, and controlled clinical environments. While machine learning (ML) has advanced automated analysis of structured assessments (e.g., shorten ADOS assessment11 and pose estimation in ADOS videos12), these methods remain tethered to standardized settings and clinician oversight, limiting scalability in low-resource communities. Recent innovations in mobile health platforms, such as gamified apps capturing gaze patterns or vocal prosody13–15, demonstrate improved ecological validity but retain operational complexity. For instance, tools requiring specialized-sensor setups, or user training and systematic implementation by primary providers, to balance accuracy with real-world feasibility16,17. Critically, none address the urgent need for scalable, caregiver-friendly screening in children under 2 years—a gap perpetuating diagnostic delays.

Naturalistic approaches, which analyze unstructured caregiver-child interactions in familiar environments, offer a promising alternative. Prior work using the Systematic Observation of Red Flags (SORF) has shown that manual coding of home or brief parent-child interaction videos can distinguish autistic from non-autistic toddlers, including in 10-min interaction samples from children aged 15–24 months18,19. However, these approaches still depend on trained human coders and manual behavioral scoring, which limits scalability. Early machine learning models, trained on handcrafted behavioral features (e.g., eye contact, social smiles20,21), still required manual labeling by raters. A clinically viable solution must therefore overcome four barriers: (1) compatibility with ubiquitous mobile devices, (2) usability without prior training, (3) elimination of manual annotation, and (4) robust performance in children under 2 years.

Advances in computer vision provide a pathway. While traditional convolutional/recurrent neural networks (CNNs/RNNs) excel at temporal video analysis, vision-language models (VLMs) like CLIP22—pretrained on vast image-text pairs—enable zero-shot classification by aligning visual features with semantic concepts. However, autism screening poses unique challenges: complex semantic information, prolonged video durations, and small domain dataset sizes demand domain-specific adaptation of these frameworks. In this study, we present Autism-CLIP, a pretrained VLM fine-tuned on 3-min naturalistic videos of toddlers (15–24 months) interacting with caregivers (Fig. 1). By integrating diagnoses and clinical assessment scores (e.g., ADOS-T) as supervisory signals using Large Language Model (LLM) and CLIP architectures, designing domain-specific predictive networks, and employing hybrid supervision strategies, Autism-CLIP bridges the gap between generic action recognition and autism-specific phenotypic analysis, automating screening of diagnostically salient behaviors (e.g. gaze aversion) without manual coding. In multicenter validation (internal n = 103; external n = 102), Autism-CLIP achieved an AUC of 0.873 (95% CI: 0.796–0.937) and sensitivity of 0.857, outperforming state-of-the-art ML models. This work advances the field by (1) demonstrating the first pretrained VLM adapted for autism screening in naturalistic settings, (2) validating performance in children under 2 years, i.e., a cohort underrepresented in prior ML studies, and (3) eliminating dependencies on structured tasks or manual annotation. By enabling analysis of brief, smartphone-captured videos, Autism-CLIP democratizes access to early screening, aligning with global priorities for equitable neurodevelopmental care (Supplementary Table 1), offering a unique approach compared to existing methods.

Results

Participants and demographics

The study was conducted across three branches of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. The participants include an internal training dataset and an external validation dataset. Participants of internal dataset were recruited from children attending outpatient clinics specializing in developmental disorders and child healthcare at the Child Development and Behavioral Center of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Tianhe District Hospital and Lingnan Hospital), between September 2021 and December 2023. Out of 117 toddlers, 103 were enrolled in the study (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Fourteen toddlers (6 with autism, 4 with developmental delays, and 4 neurotypical) were excluded due to poor video quality, video containing more than one child or more than one caregiver, non-cooperation during assessments, or failure to complete assessments within the follow-up time window (Supplementary Fig. 1). Of the 103 toddlers in the internal cohort, 46 overlapped with participants reported in our previous SORF-based manual-coding study19. These participants were drawn from the Longitudinal Chinese Autism Study of Early Development (LCAS-ED), a cohort study focusing on infants at high familial or elevated likelihood of autism. Participants of external dataset were recruited from children visiting outpatient clinics specializing in developmental disorders and child healthcare at the Zhaoqing Hospital of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between March 2024 and March 2025. Model output and classification framework

The machine learning architecture generates continuous autism likelihood scores ranging from 0 to 1, quantifying the algorithmic confidence in autism classification. This probabilistic output undergoes threshold-dependent dichotomization using an empirically derived cutoff of 0.5 for clinical decision-making. Operationalized through this binary classification schema: Scores >0.5 classify individuals into the autism category; Scores ≤ 0.5 assign participants to the nonautism control group, which encompasses both neurotypical populations and those with developmental delays unrelated to autism.

This operational framework strategically preserves methodological flexibility by maintaining tunable sensitivity parameters for different implementation phases. The continuous scoring system enables dynamic threshold adjustment during subsequent clinical validation studies or real-world deployments, accommodating evolving diagnostic criteria while preserving inherent measurement fidelity.

Model performance

We trained and evaluated the proposed Autism-CLIP model using 3-min videos, clinical assessments and diagnostic results collected from Chinese children cohorts. The proposed Autism-CLIP achieves high accuracy in classifying autistic and nonautistic (development delay and neurotypical combined) groups with an AUC of 0.903, sensitivity of 0.866, and specificity of 0.806 (Fig. 2a). Interpretability analysis

Analysis of the contribution weights from video frames reveals distinct behavioral patterns that help explain the likelihood score for each child. Specific pixel blocks within the video frames are found to either positively or negatively influence the output likelihood score, providing insights into the model’s diagnostic decisions (Supplementary Fig. 3). This analysis serves two main purposes: (1) offering potential explanations for the Autism-CLIP model’s decision-making process, and (2) highlighting unique behavioral patterns associated with autism or typical neurodevelopment.

In the internal data analysis, the Autism-CLIP model primarily focuses on the faces (including eyes and other facial features) of both children and caregivers, as well as the caregivers’ upper limbs (Fig. 2c, d). This pattern may suggests that the model places significant emphasis on eye contact, facial orientation, facial expressions, and other facial features, as well as the interactions between the child and caregiver—key factors in identifying autism-related behavioral patterns.

Discussion

This study introduces Autism-CLIP, a vision-language model that leverages naturalistic setting caregiver-child interactions to achieve robust performance on autism screening with an AUC of 0.903, outperforming specialized computational models. Our model identified caregiver behavioral patterns, suggesting their potential as predictors in screening frameworks. Based on our findings, further mechanistic studies can be conducted to determine whether these behaviors reflect compensatory adaptations, intrinsic caregiver characteristics, or bidirectional parent-child dynamics. The model’s capacity to generalize across diverse demographics (AUC > 0.85 in all subgroups) highlights its potential to address critical bottlenecks in early differential diagnosis. These findings align with growing recognition that autism manifests not only in individual traits but in the dyadic synchrony of social exchanges, as posited by theories of social bidirectionality28. This advances prior computational work, which has largely focused on child-specific behaviors like gaze patterns or motor stereotypies29,30.

Our previous work has validated the capacity of clinicians to identify autism via naturalistic behavioral videos, with two representative studies laying the empirical foundation. We revealed that autistic toddlers exhibit abnormal joint attention initiation across multiple sensory modalities, featuring diminished visual and auditory initiated joint attention (IJA) alongside elevated tactile IJA8. And we further manually annotated 10-min parent–child interaction footage of children aged 15–24 months following SORF scoring standards, and verified the effectiveness of naturalistic observation in early autism screening19. Collectively, the findings from our previous work confirm that clinicians can reliably detect the symptoms of autism through unscripted natural videos. Building upon these earlier manual behavioral coding efforts, the present study advances this line of research by leveraging an artificial intelligence model to replace human clinicians for autism identification. Specifically, we construct Autism-CLIP, an automated vision-language model capable of processing shorter 3-min naturalistic videos and eliminating the need for labor-intensive manual behavioral annotation in practical screening, thereby translating prior observational research into scalable computational screening solutions.

We used a conservative diagnostic grouping strategy that was consistent with the triage rationale of our prior SORF-based screening work. Toddlers assigned to the autism group had sufficient clinical evidence for an ASD diagnosis at this early age and showed clear autism-related features with functional impairment. Children with subthreshold autism-related features were grouped with developmental delay when they did not yet meet full DSM-5 criteria for ASD, because their later diagnostic trajectories may diverge. Under this stricter definition of the autism group, Autism-CLIP maintained good screening performance, suggesting that positive model outputs preferentially identify children with more prominent autism-related characteristics who may warrant prioritized clinical evaluation.

Clinically, Autism-CLIP’s video-based framework—serving as a secondary screening tool tailored to populations with suspected autism—could transform early screening by reducing reliance on resource-intensive protocols. Specifically, it targets those with abnormal results from routine universal screening, individuals identified as high autism likelihood (e.g., family history of autism, developmental delays reported by caregivers), or children referred by primary care providers due to clinical concerns. Traditional assessments, requiring specialized settings and prolonged observation, contribute to diagnostic delays that persist beyond optimal neuroplasticity windows31,32.

The Autism-CLIP model shows high sensitivity but reduced specificity in external validation, particularly in distinguishing between autism, nonautism, and Developmental Delay cases. This performance decline can be attributed to several factors: potential overfitting driven by the smaller volume of video data available per child in external cohorts; narrower intergroup score gaps in developmental scores, especially in expressive language, fine motor and gross motor T-score (Supplementary Table 10); diminished mean T-score differences for the autism versus nonautism comparison relative to the internal validation dataset (Supplementary Table 11); and a higher proportion of challenging cases among infants aged 19–21 months, in whom overlapping clinical phenotypes and variability in caregiver interactions obscure diagnostic distinctions between groups. Given that autism is a distinct neurotype in neurodevelopment, children under 2 years of age—a critical period for social development—may present with developmental differences or unusual signs that raise caregivers’ concerns, and early intervention before the age of 2 improved outcomes33,34. Thus, despite lower specificity, the model’s high sensitivity remains valuable for early screening to identify children likely to benefit from intervention.

Designed for home-based natural environments, this screening tool enables caregivers to record 3-min smartphone videos during unstructured play—democratizing access to preliminary assessments, especially in underserved regions. Moreover, it significantly reduces reliance on specialized medical resources. The model integrates a hierarchical architecture of text data and video understanding, simulating a similar cognitive process underlying clinical judgment based on the evaluation results and behavioral observations. The performance of the model was comparable to that of ordinary pediatricians in autism screening35. Autism screening in China is usually performed by general practitioners or pediatricians36, and our work highlight the model’s direct clinical relevance and applicability. By leveraging naturalistic data, the model avoids cultural biases inherent in standardized tools that rely on language or context-specific social norms, delivering a more inclusive approach to capturing autism phenotypes globally.

While the present study focuses primarily on optimizing our model’s performance for autism screening, its methodological framework holds promising implications for broader research on dyadic interactions. Our hybrid architecture, proven effective in capturing behavioral dynamics, enables the model to comprehend abstract tasks using only small-scale clinical datasets. This underscores the architecture’s generalizable capacity to quantify patterns of interpersonal coordination, suggesting potential applicability to other neurodevelopmental conditions (e.g., Social Anxiety Disorder [SAD], Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder [ADHD]) where abnormalities in dyadic synchrony carry clinical significance. Future research should therefore explore the model’s generalizability across a wider spectrum of dyadic interaction scenarios beyond autism screening.

Despite its promise, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the model was trained and validated exclusively on a Chinese cohort, raising questions about its generalizability to populations with differing cultural communication styles, comorbidities, or diverse population distributions outside China37. Second, the model focuses on identifying children with prominent autism features. Children with subthreshold autism may be at likelihood of underdiagnosis, which could potentially limit their access to early intervention. Third, autism diagnoses in children under 2 years of age may lack stability. This is a limitation and future longitudinal studies present an opportunity to evaluate the accuracy of the proposed method. Fourth, the exclusion of vocal prosody—a known autism biomarker38–40—limits its capacity to disentangle overlapping phenotypes like autism and language delay. Integrating multimodal audio-visual analysis could enhance specificity while providing insights into how speech patterns modulate caregiver-child dynamics. Fifth, given that the video data inherently contains sensitive personal information, such as identifiable facial features of minors, future research should prioritize robust privacy protection measures, including anonymization techniques41. Finally, while contrastive learning underpins Autism-CLIP’s performance, the model’s black box nature complicates clinical interpretability. Techniques such as spatiotemporal attention mapping could elucidate pivotal interaction moments (e.g., joint attention bids or atypical social engagement behavior) that drive predictions, fostering clinician trust and mechanistic understanding.

Autism-CLIP bridges the accessibility-rigor divide in early autism screening, transforming unstructured play into actionable social metrics. Its smartphone compatibility aligns with global imperatives to expedite screening during critical neuroplasticity windows, empowering families to initiate care without specialist referrals. Future iterations integrating cross-cultural validation, multimodal data, explainable AI, and comparisons of algorithm results to clinical judgement based on the same video clips will further solidify its role as a scalable triage tool—one that complements, rather than replaces, clinical expertise—to ensure timely interventions for children at highest autism-likelihood.

Methods

Study cohort

Participants were categorized by age into two groups: children younger than 18 months and those 18 months or older within the 15–24-month range. Both groups underwent the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL)42 assessment and family caregiver-child interaction video recording upon enrollment. Children aged 18 months or older also completed the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule-Toddler Module (ADOS-T)43 assessment at enrollment. Children younger than 18 months underwent a follow-up visit at least 3 months later and completed the ADOS-T assessment between 18 and 24 months of age. Clinical diagnoses were determined by specialized physicians following completion of the ADOS-T assessment. Participants were recruited without sex or gender restrictions, and baseline sex information was collected based on the Chinese government-issued official Resident Identity Card, which records legally registered sex information.

None of the participating parents had received autism-related assessment results or diagnoses before their involvement. They also had no previous guidance or training from licensed clinicians. Additionally, the children involved had not received any professional interventions or support services. The inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria for the study population were consistent with those used in our previous behavioral studies19. This study has received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (zssy [2020] 02-118-02). All parents or legal guardians provided written informed consent. The SORF and IJA manual-coding outcomes reported in our prior studies8,19 were not used to train, validate, or run Autism-CLIP.

Diagnostic grouping

The clinical best estimate diagnosis based on DSM-V criteria44 was performed by two developmental-behavioral pediatricians with more than three years of pediatric clinical experience, according to their developmental history, behavioral observations, and assessments including ADOS-T and MSEL. ADOS-T and MSEL assessments were administered by trained, certified assessors who were masked to the children’s clinical histories. Video collection

The caregiver-child interaction videos were recorded in natural settings following established protocols19. Caregivers were required to be familiar with the child and to spend at least 2 h per day caring for the child. Families were asked to prepare several age-appropriate toys, including toys preferred by the child, and to place them in a location suitable for free play. A mobile phone, tablet, or camera was positioned steadily so that both the caregiver and child, including their faces and bodies, remained visible. The primary caregiver was asked to sit with the child and play as they normally would; if the child left the camera view or turned away, the caregiver could gently redirect the child back into view (Supplementary Fig. 2).

The recording instructions were deliberately kept simple because the study aimed to capture everyday caregiver-child interaction rather than examiner-elicited behavior. Caregivers were not asked to use standardized prompts or to evoke specific behaviors. This design was intended to preserve ecological validity, reduce burden for families, and make the protocol suitable for scalable deployment.

Internal-cohort recording sessions were typically at least 12 min long and were divided into continuous 3-min clips when possible. External-cohort recordings were collected as single continuous videos of at least 3 min. Families uploaded videos to a private server. Example interaction models and toy suggestions were provided only to support recording quality and caregiver understanding. Eligible videos had a resolution of at least 720 × 460 pixels and a frame rate of at least 3 frames per second; videos shorter than 3 min or containing more than one child or more than one caregiver were excluded. All videos were down-sampled to 3 frames per second before analysis. After preprocessing, the internal cohort yielded 571 valid 3-min clips from 103 children, typically 3–7 clips per child, and the external cohort yielded 102 valid 3-min videos from 102 children.

Model implementation

To address the challenge of analyzing digital representations of autism in natural-setting videos, we developed an end-to-end model, Autism-CLIP, comprising multiple integral components: a pre-trained LLM for generating label sentences, a ViT-based video encoder, a classification network, and a video-specific text encoder (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 4).Fig. 3Model architecture.a The Autism-CLIP model comprises a video encoder, a Large Language Model (LLM), a text encoder, and a classification network. The LLM generates a personalized label sentence from the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule-Toddler Module (ADOS-T) assessment result, MSEL assessment result, and diagnosis; this sentence is then processed by video-specific text encoder to supervise the training of the video encoder. The video encoder processes the videos via patch embedding, Cross-Frame Communication Transformer (CCT), and Multi-Frame Integration Transformer to extract video features. The classification network outputs the screening result based on these video features. During training, both contrastive and classification losses are optimized under hybrid supervision from both text encoder and classification net. b For inference, only the videos are input, and no assessment results are required. Children’s videos, together with their ADOS-T and MSEL assessment results, are fed into the model. The LLM generates a personalized label sentence from the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule-Toddler Module (ADOS-T) assessment result, MSEL assessment result, and diagnosis; this sentence is then processed by video-specific text encoder to supervise the training of the video encoder. The video encoder processes the videos via patch embedding, Cross-Frame Communication Transformer (CCT), and Multi-Frame Integration Transformer to extract video features. The classification network outputs the screening result based on these video features. During training, both contrastive and classification losses are optimized under hybrid supervision from both text encoder and classification net. b For inference, only the videos are input, and no assessment results are required. Icons were hand-drawn in Procreate and figure layouts were assembled in Microsoft PowerPoint.

During the training process, after completing pre-training on Kinetics-600, Autism-CLIP is trained using 3-min videos, autism-related assessment results, and autism diagnostic outcomes. For inference, Autism-CLIP takes 3-min videos as input and outputs continuous autism likelihood scores ranging from 0 to 1. The video encoder extracts autism-related features from the input video. Meanwhile, the LLM generates personalized labeling sentences based on autism-related assessment results—including ADOS-T CSS, Mullen Scales of Early Learning T-scores, and clinical diagnosis results. The text encoder then produces text features aligned with these generated sentences, which are used to supervise the training of the video encoder. Subsequently, the classification network calculates an autism likelihood score using the video features.

Autism-CLIP’s ViT-based46 video encoder with reference to X-CLIP excels at extracting pertinent features from input videos, with its Cross-Frame Communication Transformer and Multi-Frame Integration Transformer enabling highly efficient integration of temporal information—thus empowering it to adeptly capture dynamic patterns even within long-duration video data.

Inspired by the CLIP framework, the model leverages text information to facilitate training of the vision module: the video-specific text encoder uses personalized label sentences generated by the LLM to supervise video feature extraction, establishing a robust visual-textual modality connection and enhancing the model’s capacity to learn discriminative representations. By incorporating label sentences that convey comprehensive information beyond basic autism diagnosis, Autism-CLIP adapts precisely to individual case characteristics.

LLM is introduced to generate personalized label sentences based on assessment scores. Without the use of an LLM, the excessively long label sentences generated from individual’s assessment results and complex meanings of these results present significant drawbacks: they not only increase memory usage for the text encoder but also contain substantial redundant information, forcing the model to learn such extraneous details and thereby elevating the difficulty of the learning process. To address these challenges, an LLM is employed to generate personalized label sentences based on assessment scores, yielding dual advantages in reducing the model’s learning burden: first, it streamlines the encoded text by shortening its length and simplifying data representation; second, it summarizes and emphasizes key information, directing the model toward critical diagnostic aspects while avoiding overemphasis on secondary details. To achieve this, the LLM is guided through prompts to generate these personalized label sentences by leveraging both the meanings of each assessment score and the individual’s assessment results, with specific instructions to provide a concise summary that highlights the presence or absence of autism—ensuring the LLM can extract and generate appropriate, supervision-friendly personalized label sentences from otherwise lengthy scale result explanations and raw outcomes.

Additionally, a domain-specific architecture—the classification network, implemented via Multi-Layer Perceptrons—is designed to predict both the likelihood of autism and autism-related assessment scores (specifically the Social Affect score and Restricted/Repetitive Behavior score) using video features. These predicted outcomes serve as direct supervision signals, from which the respective losses ldiag, lSA, and lRRB are derived.

Model training and evaluation

Autism-CLIP was trained and validated via a 10-fold cross-validation approach on an internal dataset comprising 571 videos from 103 participants, with additional evaluation conducted on an external cohort of 102 videos (one video per participant, totaling 102 participants). To avoid data leakage, cross-validation ensured no participant’s videos appeared in both training and testing sets. Autism-CLIP’s video and text encoders were partly initialized using the open-source CLIP-ViT-L/1422, pre-trained for 30 epochs on the Kinetics-600 dataset to learn human body movement features, and then fine-tuned for 10 epochs on internal autism training dataset to capture autism-related body movement characteristics. For more training details, please refer to the Model Architecture section in Supplementary Methods.

Notably, as Autism-CLIP is designed to identify specific behavioral traits of autistic children, pre-training on action recognition datasets is essential for enhancing its autism screening performance. During internal and external validation, videos were input without assessment results or clinical label sentences, as the text encoder module was inactive in this phase; the model with the highest 10-fold validation AUC was selected for external validation.

Interpretability analysis

To enhance interpretability, we perform a sequential masking of pixel blocks in input frames and observe the impact on the model’s output. To assess the impact of the movement of specific body parts on the screening results, we employed HRNet47 and Fast R-CNN48, both trained on the COCO dataset49. This setup was used to extract likelihood matrices for 17 human body key points. We then grouped these 17 key points into six categories: Eyes, Facial Features Excluding Eyes (including ears and nose), Shoulders, Upper Limbs (including elbows and wrists), Hips, and Lower Limbs (including knees and ankles). Subsequently, we quantified the total contribution of each key point to the final autism likelihood scores. For additional implementation details, please refer to the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

For internal validation, each model underwent 10-fold cross-validation. To avoid the model relying on facial recognition for predictions, we ensured that videos from a single child appeared exclusively in either the training or validation set. Each fold was trained separately, ensuring independent evaluation. The model’s performance during internal validation was assessed by aggregating results from 571 videos. The validation on external data used the parameters of the model with the highest AUC in internal 10-fold cross-validation. Performance metrics, including the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) area under the curve (AUC), were calculated to evaluate classification accuracy. Confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using the Bootstrap method with 1000 samples. Sensitivity, specificity, Positive Predictive Value (PPV), and Negative Predictive Value (NPV) were calculated using a threshold of 0.5. For model performance calculated using Youden Index, please refer to the Supplementary Tables. To assess differences in dataset distribution across categorical variables such as gender, education level, and main caregiver, a chi-square test was applied, while One-way ANOVA was used for continuous variables. When comparing different models or methods, a Permutation Test was used to compute P-values. A P-value greater than 0.01 was considered indicative of no significant differences.
