Cautious Optimism: A Meta-Algorithm for Near-Constant Regret in General Games Conference: ; ;
Ashkan Soleymani, Georgios Piliouras, Gabriele Farina

Abstract
. Recent work ( Soleymani et al. 2025 ) introduced a variant of Optimistic Multiplicative Weights Updates ( OMWU ) that adaptively controls the learning pace in a dynamic, non-monotone manner, achieving new state-of-the-art regret minimization guarantees in general games. In this work, we demonstrate that no-regret learning acceleration through adaptive pacing of the learners is not an isolated phenomenon. We introduce Cautious Optimism , a framework for substantially faster regularized learning in general games. Cautious Optimism takes as input any instance of Follow-the-Regularized-Leader ( FTRL ) and outputs an accelerated no-regret learning algorithm by pacing the underlying FTRL with minimal computational overhead. Importantly, we retain uncoupledness (learners do not need to know other players’ utilities). Cautious Optimistic FTRL achieves near-optimal O T ​ ( log ⁡ T ) O_{T}(\log T) regret in diverse self-play (mixing-and-matching regularizers) while preserving the optimal O ⁡ ( T ) O(\sqrt{T}) regret in adversarial scenarios. In contrast to prior works (e.g. Syrgkanis et al. 2015 , Daskalakis et al. 2021 ), our analysis does not rely on monotonic step-sizes, showcasing a novel route for fast learning in general games.

Introduction

Understanding multi-agent systems is fundamental to a wide range of disciplines, including economics, game theory, artificial intelligence, optimization, control theory, and human sciences (e.g.,
Von Neumann and Morgenstern 1947
;
Rawls 1971
;
Shoham and Leyton-Brown 2008
;
Egerstedt and Hu 2001
;
Lewis 2008
;
Gintis 2014
). In these systems, multiple autonomous agents interact, often with individual objectives that may be cooperative, competitive, or even a combination of both. It is difficult to overstate the importance of equilibrium concepts in the study of multi-agent systems, characterizing stable outcomes where no agent has an incentive to unilaterally deviate. In the context of games, the Nash equilibrium stands as the primary solution concept
(
Nash Jr 1950
)
.

Online learning and game theory are intrinsically linked through the celebrated framework of
regret minimization
, wherein an algorithm iteratively updates its decisions to minimize the difference between its cumulative utility and that of the best fixed strategy in hindsight.
Originally formalized within the context of single-agent decision making
(
Robbins 1952
)
, regret minimization has deep historical ties to game theory, tracing back to fictitious play
(
Brown 1949
;
Robinson 1951
)
. This connection is not merely conceptual but fundamental, as arguably the most scalable and effective algorithms for finding equilibrium concepts are
uncoupled no-regret learning
algorithms
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
)
, leading to pioneering developments in artificial intelligence
(
Bowling et al. 2015
;
Brown and Sandholm 2018
;
Moravčík et al. 2017
;
Brown and Sandholm 2019
)
. Moreover, beyond its theoretical significance and practical application in equilibrium computation, regret minimization serves as a powerful model for rational behavior in real-world applications, where agents adaptively respond to their environments based on past experiences
(
Halpern and Pass 2012
)
.

Within the class of two-player zero-sum matrix games, no-regret algorithms are known to converge to the Nash equilibrium of the game. In particular, if both players follow a no-regret learning algorithm, their joint play results in an approximate Nash equilibrium
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
)
. However, while this result is appealing, it does not extend even to the relatively restricted setting of multi-player normal-form games. On the contrary, strong impossibility results demonstrate that no
uncoupled
learning dynamics can guarantee convergence to a Nash equilibrium in such games
(
Hart and Mas-Colell 2003
;
Milionis et al. 2023
)
. There exists a fundamental barrier: not only do uncoupled learning dynamics fail to converge to Nash equilibria in general settings, but even centralized algorithms with full access to game parameters face inherent computational hardness in Nash equilibrium computation, even in two-player general-sum games
(
Daskalakis et al. 2009
;
Chen and Deng 2006
)
.

On the positive side, regret minimization extends to general games, converging to
Coarse Correlated Equilibria
(CCE)
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
)
.
Formally, the empirical play of no-regret learners in a game converges to an approximate CCE, where the approximation level is controlled by the maximum regret of the players. As the average regret diminishes over time, the equilibrium approximation improves, making regret minimization a natural and scalable framework for equilibrium computation. While CCE is a relaxation of Nash equilibrium, it retains several desirable properties: it is computationally efficient, generalizes Nash and correlated equilibria, scales to large games, aligns with rational behavior, possesses approximate optimality of social welfare, and applies broadly across strategic settings
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
;
Monnot and Piliouras 2017
;
Roughgarden et al. 2017
;
Papadimitriou and Roughgarden 2008
;
Roughgarden 2015
;
Babichenko et al. 2014
;
Keiding and Peleg 2000
)
.

Prior Work and Context for Cautious Optimism

In this section, we enumerate major prior works on no-regret learning in games. Let
T
denote the time horizon,
d
denote the number of actions per player, and
n
denote the number of players. Please refer to

The quest for optimal regret bounds in general games was initiated by the seminal work of
Syrgkanis et al. 2015
, which established the first
o_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{317841}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle T$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{317841}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle T$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{277462}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle T$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{277462}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle T$}}}}}}(T^{1/2})
regret rate for self-play (better than the optimal rate for online learning with adversarial utilities). A key contribution of their work was the introduction of the
RVU property
, an adversarial regret bound that applies broadly to a class of optimistic no-regret learning algorithms, including Follow-the-Regularized-Leader and Mirror Descent. By leveraging this property, they demonstrated that while social regret remains constant, individual player regret grows at a rate of
O_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{317841}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle T$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{317841}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle T$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{277462}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle T$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{277462}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle T$}}}}}}(T^{1/4})
, leaving the challenging problem of determining the optimal individual regret rate largely unresolved.

More recently,
Daskalakis et al. 2021
achieved a major breakthrough by exponentially improving the regret guarantees for Optimistic Multiplicative Weight Updates (
OMWU
). Their work marked the first derivation of a polylogarithmic regret bound in
T
, specifically
O(n\log d\log^{4}T)
. This result was obtained through a careful analysis of the smoothness properties of higher-order discrete differentials of the softmax operator in the Multiplicative Weight Updates algorithm and their implications in the frequency domain.

However, due to their specific choice of learning rate, their analysis is valid only in the regime where
T>Cn\log^{4}T
where
C
is a large constant—see Lemmas 4.2 and C.4 in
(
Daskalakis et al. 2021
)
for details. Additionally, the hidden constants in their order notation have a very large magnitude. Most importantly, it remains unknown how these ideas can be extended to the regret analysis of other regularized learning algorithms.

Piliouras et al. 2022
introduced Clairvoyant MWU (CMWU), where players leverage the game dynamics as a broadcast channel to implement Nemirovski’s Conceptual Proximal Method
(
Nemirovski 2004
;
Farina et al. 2022b
)
in a decentralized, uncoupled manner. However, this attitude towards the dynamics of the game play comes at a cost: not all iterations of Clairvoyant MWU satisfy the no-regret property. Instead, only a subsequence of
\Theta(T/\log T)
iterations exhibits bounded regret of
O(n\log d)
, while the remaining iterations are dedicated to broadcasting. As a result, the empirical play of this subsequence converges to a CCE of the game at a rate of
O(\frac{n\log d\log T}{T})
. Additionally, CMWU does not guarantee the no-regret property in the presence of adversarial utilities.

Farina et al. 2022a
established a logarithmic dependence on
T
throughout the entire history of play using the Log-Regularized Lifted Optimistic FTRL (
LRL-OFTRL
) algorithm. Their approach builds upon an instantiation of optimistic Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL) over a suitably lifted space with a log regularizer. However, this improved dependence on
T
comes at the cost of an exponentially worse dependence on the number of actions
d
, resulting in an overall regret bound of
O(nd\log T)
. As we see later in
Section

B.1
, interestingly, the dynamics of
LRL-OFTRL
are equivalent to those of
COFTRL
with a log regularizer, which is a special instance of Cautious Optimism—our unified framework.

In the last significant step preceding our work,
Soleymani et al. 2025
introduced
COMWU
, a refined variant of the Optimistic Multiplicative Weights Update (OMWU) algorithm, which achieves the current state-of-the-art regret bound of
O(n\log^{2}d\log T)
. Their approach is grounded in dynamic learning rate control, ensuring that learners progress in a coordinated manner throughout the game while preventing agents from developing excessively negative regret. While heuristics incorporating non-monotone changes in learning rates have been explored and employed in practice—e.g., the Win or Learn Fast (WOLF) principle
(
Bowling and Veloso 2002
;
Bowling 2004
;
Ratcliffe et al. 2019
;
Xi et al. 2015
;
Awheda and Schwartz 2016
;
Georgila et al. 2014
;
Busoniu et al. 2006
;
Zhu et al. 2021

Soleymani et al. 2025

COMWU
enjoys an exponential improvement in

Contributions and Techniques

In this paper, we introduce Cautious Optimism, a framework (meta-algorithm) that takes an instance of the Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (
FTRL

FTRL

Our work provides the first comprehensive characterization of exponentially faster regret minimization for regularized learning in general games.

3.
Cautious Optimism

Cautious Optimism is a variant of the Optimistic Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (
OFTRL
) algorithms, but with non-monotone, adaptive adjustment of the learning rate based on the regret accumulated up to the current iteration

In the standard version of

\psi

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\leftarrow\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\lambda^{(t)}\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}\rangle-\psi({\bm{x}})\right\},

where learning rate at time
t

\lambda^{(t)}>0

\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(t)}
denotes the vector containing the accumulated optimistically-corrected regrets for each action up to time

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(t)}[k]

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\bm{\nu}^{(t-1)}[k]-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t-1)},{\bm{x}}^{(t-1)}\rangle)+\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\left[\bm{\nu}^{(\tau)}[k]-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(\tau)},{\bm{x}}^{(\tau)}\rangle\right],

\bm{u}^{(t)}\coloneqq\bm{\nu}^{(t)}-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\rangle\bm{1}_{d}

\bm{U}^{(t)}\coloneqq\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\left[\bm{\nu}^{(\tau)}-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(\tau)},{\bm{x}}^{(\tau)}\rangle\bm{1}_{d}\right]

Unlike conventional methods in optimization and learning that enforce a monotonically decreasing learning rate schedule, our approach allows for more flexible, non-monotone adjustments based on the learner’s performance. In particular, we introduce a
universal dynamic learning control
mechanism that
deliberately slows down the learning

Due to the counterintuitive nature of this idea, which deliberately slows down the learner when it performs too well, and in keeping with the terminology of
Soleymani et al. 2025
, we term our framework
Cautious Optimism
—an extension of Optimism
(
Rakhlin and Sridharan 2013
)
, where the learner is additionally cautious about its regret so far. The
universality
of our framework stems from the fact that, unlike the original version of this idea in
Soleymani et al. 2025

OMWU

OFTRL

Cautious Optimistic Follow-the-Regularized-Leader
(
COFTRL

In self-play settings, the predictability of the players’ actions due to the smooth evolution of the learning dynamics guarantees faster convergence compared to adversarial settings. This observation motivates us to consider the convergence of the learning dynamics of multiple players as a whole, rather than at an individual level. Hence, a learner performing exceedingly well can create an imbalance that hinders others from keeping pace. Consequently, aiming for harmonic learning among players during self-play seems a natural approach to better exploit the predictability of the dynamics. While hindering learning when a player is performing exceptionally well may seem counterintuitive and even unfavorable at first glance, it helps maintain a balance among the players, thereby improving the performance of the hindered player in the long run.

There is another way to justify at the conceptual level why such ideas lead to faster convergence. As discussed in
Appendix

A

worst-performing
player—the one with the highest regret. Thus, ensuring a balanced performance among players naturally accelerates convergence
(
Soleymani et al. 2025
)

3.1.
Intrinsic Lipschitzness

A core building block of Cautious Optimism is our newly defined notion of intrinsic Lipschitzness for convex regularizers, defined as follows. This concept characterizes the Lipschitz properties of regularizers within the geometry they induce as generators.

Definition 3.1.

Let
\psi:\Delta^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary convex regularizer for the simplex, we call
\psi
,
\gamma
-intrinsically Lipschitz (
\gamma
-IL) if for all
{\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{x}}\in\Delta^{d}
,

\displaystyle\left|\psi({\bm{x}}^{\prime})-\psi({\bm{x}})\right|^{2}\leq\gamma D_{\psi}({\bm{x}}^{\prime}\;\|\;{\bm{x}}).

This condition is general and holds for many choices of regularizers. In fact, it is easy to verify that any Lipschitz and strongly convex function
\psi
is intrinsically Lipschitz. Thus, intrinsic Lipschitzness is a weaker condition than standard Lipschitzness in the context of regularizers. As we will see later (e.g., in
Table

2
), there exist many regularizers that are intrinsically Lipschitz but not Lipschitz in the standard sense, such as negative entropy and negative Tsallis entropy.

Proposition 3.2.

Any regularizer
\psi
that is
\mu
-strongly convex w.r.t. norm
\|.\|
, and
L
-Lipschitz w.r.t. the same norm, is trivially (
2L^{2}/\mu
)-IL.

A more refined analysis of the intrinsically Lipschitz parameter
\gamma
for regularizers may take into account its dependence on the input dimension
d
, rather than relying solely on the direct analysis in
Proposition

3.2
. Furthermore, we introduce the following localized version of the
\gamma
-IL condition, which is more general.

Definition 3.3 (Formal version in
Appendix

B
).

Let
\psi:\Delta^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary convex regularizer for the simplex, we call
\psi
,
\gamma
-locally intrinsically Lipschitz (
\gamma
-LIL) if,

\displaystyle\left|\psi({\bm{x}}^{\prime})-\psi({\bm{x}})\right|^{2}\leq\gamma(\epsilon)D_{\psi}({\bm{x}}^{\prime}\;\|\;{\bm{x}}),

whenever
{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
are
\epsilon
-close to each other in a specific way and
\gamma
is a function of
\epsilon
.

We note that
Definition

3.3
is more relaxed than
Definition

3.1
—any
\gamma
-IL function
\psi
is also
\gamma
-LIL. For
\gamma
-LIL functions, the parameter
\gamma
may additionally depend on the locality parameter
\epsilon
, as well as the dimension
d
. Indeed, the smaller the value of the locality parameter
\epsilon

\gamma(\epsilon)
is expected to be. Examples of
\gamma

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(\lambda\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(t)})\coloneqq\max_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\langle\lambda\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}\rangle-\psi({\bm{x}}).

the learner when it is
performing too well
. To this end,
Soleymani et al. 2025
proposed the following formulation but with the special choice of negative entropy regularizer
\psi({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}{\bm{x}}[k]\log{\bm{x}}[k]
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{(t)}\\
{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix}\leftarrow\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\lambda\in(0,\eta],{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\lambda\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}\rangle+\alpha\log\lambda-\psi({\bm{x}})\right\}.\hskip-8.5359pt

In this work, we extend the algorithm and its analysis to a general choice of regularizer
\psi
. Consequently, we recover the
COMWU
algorithm of
Soleymani et al. 2025
as a special case of this framework, though with a substantially different analysis.

The reason behind Formulation
3.3
is the following step-by-step construction. Let’s start with the standard dynamics of
OFTRL
, with a to-be-designed dynamic learning rate
\lambda^{(t)}\in(0,\eta]
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\leftarrow\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\lambda^{(t)}\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}\rangle-\psi({\bm{x}})\right\}.

Not only do we intend to have diminishing regret dynamics, but we also aim to hinder the learner when it is performing too well. We incorporate this phenomenon in the update step by integrating the dynamic learning rate
\lambda^{(t)}
mechanism into Formulation
3.4
, leading to

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{(t)}\\
{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix}\leftarrow\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\lambda\in(0,\eta],{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\lambda\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}\rangle-\psi({\bm{x}})\right\}.

Unfortunately, this naive integration leads to a nonsmooth dynamic for the learning rate
\lambda^{(t)}
over time
t
. To increase the predictability of the dynamics and smooth the evolution of
\lambda^{(t)}
over time, we add the necessary inertia by incorporating the log regularizer over the learning rate
\lambda^{(t)}
into the dynamics. This modification leads to the following update rule:

(3.6)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{(t)}\\
{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix}\leftarrow\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\lambda\in(0,\eta],{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\lambda\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}\rangle+\alpha\log\lambda-\psi({\bm{x}})\right\}.

This formulation is not jointly concave in
(\lambda,{\bm{x}})
, making its
computational
aspects and
regret analysis
unclear in this naive formulation. From a computational perspective, however, it is immediate to observe that this formulation is equivalent to
COFTRL
in
Algorithm

1
, for which we demonstrate very low computational overhead beyond
OFTRL
update rule.

Moreover, a key technical observation enables us to analyze the regret of
COFTRL
. By performing the change of variable
\bm{y}=\lambda{\bm{x}}
, which is invertible on the simplex as
{\bm{x}}=\bm{y}/(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y})
, we derive the following equivalent update

(3.7)

\displaystyle\bm{y}^{(t)}\leftarrow\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\bm{y}\in(0,1]\mathcal{X}}\left\{\eta\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(t)},\bm{y}\rangle+\alpha\log(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y})-\psi\left(\frac{\bm{y}}{{\bm{1}}^{\top}\bm{y}}\right)\right\}.

This formulation corresponds to
OFTRL
on the space
(0,1]\mathcal{X}
and is a key observation central to the analysis of
COFTRL
. We note that, at first glance, it is not even clear whether this
OFTRL
Update Step
3.7
is concave. As we will show later in
Section

4.2
, not only is this objective concave, but it also exhibits a special form of strong concavity that leads to multiplicative stability in the subsequent dynamic learning rates.

Similar ideas to
OFTRL
on the lifted space
(0,1]\mathcal{X}
have previously been studied
(
Farina et al. 2022a
;
Soleymani et al. 2025
)
, albeit only for very limited choices of regularizers. For instance,
Farina et al. 2022a
utilize the log regularizer to enforce elementwise multiplicative stability in actions, leveraging its high curvature and the structure of the induced intrinsic norms. Similarly,
Soleymani et al. 2025
employ the theory of self-concordant functions to analyze the closed-form formulation of the learning rate control problem in the case of the negative entropy regularizer
\psi
. In contrast, in this work, we introduce a
unified theory of Cautious Optimism
for a broad class of regularizers, inferring these algorithms as
special cases
.

4.
Analysis of Cautious Optimism

We primarily focus on Formulation
3.7
for the analysis of Cautious Optimism. As illustrated in the previous section, the dynamics are equivalent to the following
OFTRL
update:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\bm{y}^{(t)}\leftarrow\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\bm{y}\in(0,1]\mathcal{X}}\left\{\eta\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{(t)},\bm{y}\rangle-\phi(\bm{y})\right\},

where the regularizer
\phi:(0,1]\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined as

(4.2)

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{y})\coloneqq-\alpha\log(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y})+\psi\left(\frac{\bm{y}}{{\bm{1}}^{\top}\bm{y}}\right),

with
\psi
being
\gamma
-(locally) intrinsically Lipschitz.

At first glance, it is not immediately clear whether the function
\phi
is convex. However, as we will demonstrate later in this section,
\phi
is indeed convex in
\bm{y}
provided that
\alpha\geq\gamma
. Consequently,
D_{\phi}(\cdot\;\|\;\cdot)
is well-defined.

We split the analysis of the regret cumulated by
COFTRL
into four technical steps, discussed in the next subsections.

4.1.
Step I: A Key Identity for
D_{\phi}(\cdot\;\|\;\cdot)

A key step in analyzing the dynamics of
OFTRL
in Formulation
4.1
is the following identity, which relates the Bregman divergence of the regularizer
\phi(\bm{y})
over
\bm{y}\in(0,1]\mathcal{X}
to the Bregman divergences of
-\log\lambda
over the learning rate
\lambda\in(0,1]
and
\psi({\bm{x}})
over
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Lemma 4.1.

For arbitrary values of
\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}\in(0,1]\Delta^{d}
, let
\lambda\coloneqq\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y},\lambda^{\prime}\coloneqq\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y}^{\prime}
. Then,

\displaystyle D_{\phi}(\bm{y}^{\prime}\;\|\;\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\alpha D_{-\log}(\lambda^{\prime}\;\|\;\lambda)+\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{\lambda}D_{\psi}({\bm{x}}^{\prime},{\bm{x}})+\left(1-\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{\lambda}\right)[\psi({\bm{x}}^{\prime})-\psi({\bm{x}})].

This identity is important as it illustrates how the curvature of the regularizer
\phi
in
\bm{y}
and, consequently, the stability of
\bm{y}
in the corresponding
OFTRL
Step
3.7
, are affected by the parameter
\alpha
and the choice of regularizer
\psi
. We will use this identity repeatedly in the analysis.

4.2.
Step II: Sensitivity of Learning Rate to Cumulated Regret

In this section, we demonstrate that the learning rate
\lambda
in Formulation
4.1
remains multiplicatively stable under small additive perturbations to the regret vector
\bm{\mathsf{r}}
.

Furthermore, for a
\gamma
-IL choice of regularizer
\psi
, we establish that the function
\phi(\bm{y})
is convex with respect to
\bm{y}
. We begin with the following proposition, which provides a lower bound on
D_{\phi}(\bm{y}^{\prime}\;\|\;\bm{y})
in terms of
\lambda^{\prime}/\lambda
.

Proposition 4.2.

For arbitrary choices of
\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}\in(0,1]\Delta^{d}
, let
\lambda\coloneqq\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y},\lambda^{\prime}\coloneqq\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y}^{\prime}
. Then, we infer that,

\displaystyle D_{\phi}(\bm{y}^{\prime}\;\|\;\bm{y})+D_{\phi}(\bm{y}\;\|\;\bm{y}^{\prime})\geq(\alpha-\gamma)\left[\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{\prime}}-2\right],

where
\psi
is
\gamma
-intrinsically Lipschitz.

An immediate byproduct of this proposition is that the function
\phi
is convex for a proper choice of
\alpha
, provided that the function
\psi
is
\gamma
-IL.

Corollary 4.3.

The function
\phi(\bm{y})
is convex in
\bm{y}\in(0,1]\Delta^{d}
as long as
\psi
is
\gamma
-intrinsically Lipschitz and
\alpha\geq\gamma
.

For the weaker notion of
\gamma
-local intrinsic Lipschitzness, we can prove a similar result, but only for
\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}\in(0,1]\Delta^{d}
satisfying
D_{\phi}(\bm{y}^{\prime}\;\|\;\bm{y})+D_{\phi}(\bm{y}\;\|\;\bm{y}^{\prime})\leq\epsilon
. This result is formalized in
Proposition

C.4
. In contrast to the global assumption,
\gamma
-local intrinsic Lipschitzness does not directly imply that the function
\psi
is convex; rather, it suggests that
\nabla\psi
is a locally monotone operator.

With all the necessary ingredients in place, we are now ready to show that the regularizer, and consequently Cautious Optimism, induces proximal steps for learning rates that remain multiplicatively stable.

Theorem 4.4 (Stability of learning rates).

Given the regularizer
\phi
with
\gamma
-IL component
\psi
and choice of
\alpha\geq 4\gamma
, consider regret vectors
\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathsf{r}}
such that

the corresponding proximal steps of

\displaystyle\bm{y}\leftarrow\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\bm{y}\in(0,1]\mathcal{X}}\left\{\eta\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}},\bm{y}\rangle-\phi(\bm{y})\right\},

and

\displaystyle\bm{y}^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\bm{y}\in(0,1]\mathcal{X}}\left\{\eta\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\prime},\bm{y}\rangle-\phi(\bm{y})\right\}.

Additionally, let
\lambda^{\prime}=\bm{y}^{\prime}/(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y}^{\prime}),\lambda=\bm{y}/(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y})
. Then, for a small enough learning rate

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{\lambda}\in\left[\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}\right].

Step III: High Curvature of Proximal Steps under Stability of Learning Rates

In this section, we demonstrate that under the stability of learning rates—which, as shown in

Proposition 4.5.

individual regret and
O_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{317841}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle T$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{317841}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle T$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{277462}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle T$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{277462}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle T$}}}}}}(1)
social regret differ, see e.g.,

Special Instances of

In this section, we discuss the regret guarantees of

7.
Conclusion

We designed, introduced, and analyzed
Cautious Optimism
, a framework for the broad characterization of accelerated regularized learning in games. As a meta-algorithm for achieving near-constant regret in general games, Cautious Optimism takes as input an instance of
FTRL
and, on top of that, paces the learners using
FTRL
with non-monotone adaptive learning rate control.

This approach represents a fundamental shift in the paradigms of online learning, optimization, and game theory, where constant or decreasing step sizes have traditionally been employed. The move from time-variant step-size to adaptive but state-dependent step-sizes is not merely a syntactic difference but has major implications for future work. The vast majority of standard tools in analyzing the behavior of dynamical systems (e.g., the celebrated Poincaré recurrence theorem
(
Barreira 2006
)
, Center-stable-manifold theorem
(
Perko 2013
)
, period three implies chaos theorem
(
Li and Yorke 1975
)
, a.o.) are only applicable to autonomous (i.e., time-independent) smooth maps or flows. Thus, although such ideas have been applied successfully in multi-agent learning in games it has been either for continuous-time systems (e.g.
(
Kleinberg et al. 2009
;
Piliouras and Shamma 2014
;
Mertikopoulos et al. 2018
)
), which are arguably very idealized approximations or fixed-step dynamics, in which case we typically have to sacrifice black-box strong regret guarantees significantly hindering the analysis (e.g.,
(
Chotibut et al. 2020
;
Piliouras and Yu 2023
;
Bailey and Piliouras 2018
;
Bailey et al. 2020
;
Wibisono et al. 2022
;
Katona et al. 2024
)
). Cautious Optimism paves a way forward where we do not have to make such concessions.
This shift opens a wide range of new research questions, including how the dynamics of such algorithms evolve, their implications for the chaotic behavior of learning algorithms, the properties of their continuous-time counterparts, their connections to settings beyond external regret, such as swap regret, and their relationship to social welfare and strict equilibria—
and the list continues
.

Notation.

We use lowercase letters (e.g.,
x\in\mathbb{R}
) to represent scalar values and uppercase boldface letters (e.g.,
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
) to denote vectors. The set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
is abbreviated as
[n]
for any natural number
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For a vector
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
k

k\in[d]
, is expressed as
{\bm{x}}[k]
. We define
\bm{0}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{1}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to represent the
d
-dimensional vectors of all zeros and all ones, respectively. Calligraphic uppercase letters (e.g.,
\mathcal{A}
) are used to denote sets. For a finite set
\mathcal{A}
of size
d=|\mathcal{A}|
, we denote the set of probability distributions over
\mathcal{A}
by
\Delta(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\Delta^{d}
. We denote the vector
{\bm{x}}
associated with the
i
-th player by
{\bm{x}}_{i}
, and use superscripts, such as
{\bm{x}}_{i}^{t}
, to indicate dependence on timesteps. We denote the natural logarithm by
\log
. We use
\mathcal{X}
and
\Delta^{d}
interchangeably. Additionally, we define
\Omega\coloneqq(0,1]\mathcal{X}
.

Given a convex function
\psi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we denote its convex conjugate by
\psi^{*}
, and further we define,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(\bm{g})\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\langle\bm{g},{\bm{x}}\rangle-\psi({\bm{x}}).

\psi_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
is the conjugate function of
\psi
restricted to the convex set
\mathcal{X}
. Additionally, we consider the Bregman divergence
D_{\psi}(.\;\|\;.)
generated by
\psi
,

\displaystyle D_{\psi}({\bm{x}}^{\prime}\;\|\;{\bm{x}})=\psi({\bm{x}}^{\prime})-\psi({\bm{x}})-\langle\nabla\psi({\bm{x}}),{\bm{x}}^{\prime}-{\bm{x}}\rangle.

Finite Games.

We study general-sum games involving
n
players, indexed by the set
[n]
. Each player
i\in[n]
is associated with a finite, nonempty set of pure strategies, denoted by
\mathcal{A}_{i}
. The mixed strategy space for player
i
is defined as
\mathcal{X}_{i}=\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{i})
, the set of probability distributions over
\mathcal{A}_{i}
. Collectively, the joint action space of all players is represented as
\bigtimes_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{j}
. The game’s utility structure is specified by functions
\mathcal{U}_{i}:\bigtimes_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{j}\to\mathbb{R}
for each player
i\in[n]
, where
\mathcal{U}_{i}
assigns a real-valued payoff to every combination of pure strategies. When players adopt mixed strategies
{\bm{x}}=({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n})\in\bigtimes_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{j}
, the expected utility for player
i
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}_{i}({\bm{x}})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}\sim{\bm{x}}}[\mathcal{U}_{i}(\bm{s})]=\sum_{s\in\bigtimes_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{j}}{\bm{x}}(\bm{s})\mathcal{U}_{i}(\bm{s})=\sum_{\xi_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}{\bm{x}}_{i}(\xi_{i})\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}_{-i}\sim{\bm{x}}_{-i}}[\mathcal{U}_{i}(\xi_{i},\bm{s}_{-i})],

which can also be expressed as
\bm{\nu}_{i}({\bm{x}})=\langle{\bm{x}}_{i},\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}\bm{\nu}_{i}({\bm{x}})\rangle
, where

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}\bm{\nu}_{i}({\bm{x}})=(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}_{-i}\sim{\bm{x}}_{-i}}[\mathcal{U}_{i}(\xi_{1},\bm{s}_{-i})]\cdots,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}_{-i}\sim{\bm{x}}_{-i}}[\mathcal{U}_{i}(\xi_{i},\bm{s}_{-i})],\cdots,\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}_{-i}\sim{\bm{x}}_{-i}}[\mathcal{U}_{i}(\xi_{|\mathcal{A}_{i}|},\bm{s}_{-i})]),

represents the gradient of
\bm{\nu}_{i}
with respect to
{\bm{x}}_{i}
.

To streamline analysis, we let
d\coloneqq\max_{i\in[n]}|\mathcal{A}_{i}|
denote the largest number of pure strategies available to any player. We further assume that
|\mathcal{A}_{i}|\geq 2
for all
i\in[n]
, as players with a single strategy can be trivially excluded from the game without loss of generality.

Following the literature
(
Syrgkanis et al. 2015
;
Daskalakis et al. 2021
;
Piliouras et al. 2022
;
Farina et al. 2022a
;
Anagnostides and Sandholm 2024
;
Legacci et al. 2024
;
Soleymani et al. 2025
)
, we make the following standard assumption on the boundedness of the utilities of the game.

Assumption A.1.

For every player
i
of the game, the utilities are bounded,

\displaystyle\max_{s\in{\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{i}}}|\mathcal{U}_{j}(s)|\leq 1.

and they are
L
-smooth, i.e., for any two joint strategy profiles
{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\bigtimes_{j=1}^{n}\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{j})
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{j}}\bm{\nu}_{j}({\bm{x}})-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{j}}\bm{\nu}_{j}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq L\sum\limits_{i\in[n]}\|{\bm{x}}_{i}-{\bm{x}^{\prime}_{i}}\|_{1}.

The assumption of bounded utilities is quite general and does not impose significant restrictions, as any utility function with bounded values can be rescaled to satisfy this condition without altering the problem’s essence. Under this boundedness condition, it can be shown that the game exhibits
L
-smoothness with
L=1
. However, the actual smoothness parameter
L
often depends on the underlying structure of the game and can be much smaller. For example, in games characterized by vanishing sensitivity
\epsilon_{n}
, the smoothness parameter reduces to
L=\epsilon_{n}
, where
\epsilon_{n}\ll 1

(
Anagnostides and Sandholm 2024
)
. By distinguishing
L
from the boundedness condition, we account for structural nuances in the game that may enable improved convergence rates.

No-regret Learning.

In the
online learning framework
, an agent interacts with the environment by choosing a strategy
{\bm{x}}^{t}\in\mathcal{X}=\Delta^{d}
at each timestep
t\in\mathbb{N}
. We consider the
full information
setting, where the agent receives feedback in the form of a
linear utility function
, given by
{\bm{x}}\mapsto\langle{\bm{x}},\nu^{t}\rangle
, where
\nu^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the utility vector at time
t
. The agent’s performance over a horizon of
T
rounds is quantified using the notion of
external regret
, defined as:

(A.2)

\reg^{T}\coloneqq\max_{{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\Delta^{d}}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\nu^{t},{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle\right\}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\nu^{t},{\bm{x}}^{t}\rangle.

This metric captures the difference between the cumulative utility obtained by the agent and the best
fixed strategy
in hindsight. It is important to note that the regret can be negative. The goal is to minimize regret as a function of the time horizon
T
. In adversarial settings, established algorithms such as Online Mirror Descent (OMD) and Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL) achieve the optimal regret rate of
\reg^{T}=\Theta(\sqrt{T})

(
Hazan et al. 2016
;
Orabona 2019
)
.

However, in
self-play
dynamics within games, which is the focus of this work, regret minimization can exhibit faster rates due to the structured nature of the interactions. Unlike adversarial scenarios, where the utility vectors are independent of the agent’s actions, in games, each player’s utility vector evolves as a function of the joint strategy profile. Specifically, at timestep
t
, the utility vector for player
i\in[n]
is given by
\nu^{t}=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}\nu_{i}({\bm{x}}^{t})
, where
{\bm{x}}^{t}=({\bm{x}}_{1}^{t},{\bm{x}}_{2}^{t},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{n}^{t})
denotes the collective strategy of all players.

Optimistic
predictive no-regret learning algorithms, such as Optimistic Online Mirror Descent and Optimistic Follow-the-Regularized-Leader, form a key class of no-regret algorithms with fast convergence properties in game settings. Originally introduced by
Rakhlin and Sridharan 2013
, these algorithms gained significant attention following the groundbreaking work of
Syrgkanis et al. 2015
, who demonstrated their faster convergence by proving that their regret satisfies the
Regret bounded by Variation in Utilities (RVU)
property. For completeness, we restate this property below.

Definition A.2 (RVU property
(
Syrgkanis et al. 2015
)
).

A no-regret learning algorithm is said to satisfy the Regret bounded by Variation in Utilities (RVU) property if its regret over any sequence of utilities
\{\bm{\nu}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
is bounded as:

\displaystyle\reg^{(T)}\leq a+b\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\bm{\nu}^{(t)}-\bm{\nu}^{(t-1)}\|_{*}^{2}-c\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|{\bm{x}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\|^{2},

where
a\geq 0
,
0<b\leq c
, and
a
,
b
, and
c
are algorithm-dependent parameters. The bounds are defined in terms of a pair of dual norms
(\|.\|,\|.\|_{*})
, which quantify variations in utilities and actions, respectively.

In line with
A.1
, usually the pair of dual norms
(\ell_{1},\ell_{\infty})
is considered for the RVU property in
Definition

A.2
.

Coarse Correlated Equilibrium.

A probability distribution
\sigma\in\Delta\left(\bigtimes_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{j}\right)
over the joint action space
\bigtimes_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{j}
is called an
\epsilon
-
approximate coarse correlated equilibrium (CCE)
if, for every player
i\in[n]
and every deterministic strategy
s_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}
, unilateral deviations do not increase the player’s expected utility in the game. In terms,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}\sim\sigma}\left[\mathcal{U}_{i}(\mathbf{s})\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}\sim\sigma}\left[\mathcal{U}_{i}(s_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\right]-\epsilon,

where
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n})
represents the joint action drawn from
\sigma
, and
\mathbf{s}_{-i}
denotes the actions of all players except player
i
.

A well-known result in game theory connects no-regret learning algorithms to coarse correlated equilibria (CCE) of a game. Specifically, when the players in a game follow no-regret learning algorithms with regrets
\reg_{i}^{(T)}
for all
i\in[n]
, the empirical play of the game,
\sigma=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{t}
, forms a
\left(\frac{1}{T}\max_{i\in[n]}\reg_{i}^{(T)}\right)
-approximate CCE of the game. Consequently, achieving faster rates of no-regret learning in self-play directly translates to faster convergence to the CCE of the game.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle|\psi({\bm{x}}^{\prime})-\psi({\bm{x}})|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}\log\frac{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}[k]}{{\bm{x}}[k]}\right|

Lemma B.6.

For any positive number

and choice of

(B.14)

\displaystyle q(s-1)-(s^{q}-1)\geq(1-q)(1-s^{\frac{q}{2}})^{2}.

Proof.

Substitute

,
This is because the LHS of
B.14

\displaystyle q(r^{\frac{2}{q}}-1)-(r^{2}-1)=qr^{\frac{2}{q}}-r^{2}-(q-1).

And for the RHS of
B.14
, we get,

\displaystyle(1-q)(1-r)^{2}=(1-q)r^{2}-2(1-q)r+(1-q).

Hence, the Inequality
B.14
becomes,

\displaystyle qr^{\frac{2}{q}}+2(1-q)r\geq(2-q)r^{2}.

Now, in the weighted AM-GM inequality,

\displaystyle\beta_{1}t_{1}+\beta_{2}t_{2}\geq t_{1}^{\beta_{1}}t_{2}^{\beta_{2}},

set
\beta_{1}\coloneqq\frac{q}{2-q},\beta_{2}\coloneqq\frac{2(1-q)}{2-q}
and
t_{1}\coloneqq\frac{qr^{2/q}}{\beta_{1}},t_{2}\coloneqq\frac{2(1-q)r}{\beta_{2}}
. It is clear that
\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}=1
. We directly infer that,

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\left(\frac{qr^{2/q}}{\beta_{1}}\right)+\beta_{2}\left(\frac{2(1-q)r}{\beta_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=qr^{\frac{2}{q}}+2(1-q)r

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{qr^{2/q}}{\beta_{1}}\right)^{\frac{q}{2-q}}\left(\frac{2(1-q)r}{\beta_{2}}\right)^{\frac{2(1-q)}{2-q}}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(2-q\right)^{\frac{q}{2-q}}.\left(2-q\right)^{\frac{2(1-q)}{2-q}}\right)\left(r^{\frac{2}{2-q}}.r^{\frac{2(1-q)}{2-q}}\right)

\displaystyle=(2-q)r^{2},

and the proof is concluded.
∎

Lemma C.5.

For the proximal steps of

\displaystyle D_{\phi}(\bm{y}^{\prime}\;\|\;\bm{y})+D_{\phi}(\bm{y}\;\|\;\bm{y}^{\prime})\leq 2\eta\|\mathcal{X}\|_{1}\|\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\prime}-\bm{\mathsf{r}}\|_{\infty}=2\eta\|\bm{\mathsf{r}}^{\prime}-\bm{\mathsf{r}}\|_{\infty},

Appendix D
Detailed analysis of regret (
Section

4.4
)

To analyze the regret of
COFTRL
, we utilize its equivalent
OFTRL
formulation
3.7
and demonstrate that its regret, referred to as nonnegative regret, satisfies the RVU property
(
Syrgkanis et al. 2015
)
. Consequently, leveraging the nonnegativity of the regret for the formulation in
3.7
, we establish a nonnegative RVU bound for
COFTRL
. The approach of examining regret through nonnegative RVU bounds is inspired by the work of
Farina et al. 2022a
. This idea is formalized in the following section.

D.1.
Nonnegative Regret

In terms, we analyze the nonnegative regret, defined as

\displaystyle\tildereg^{(T)}\coloneqq\max_{\bm{y}^{*}\in[0,1]\mathcal{X}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{u}}^{(t)},\bm{y}^{*}-\bm{y}^{(t)}\rangle.

The following proposition, characterizes that not only
\tildereg^{(T)}
is positive but also it gives us an upperbound on
\reg
of
COFTRL
. This proposition is pivotal as it establishes a direct translation of RVU bounds for
\tildereg^{(T)}
into nonnegative RVU bounds for
\reg^{T}
.

Proposition D.1 (Nonnegative Regret).

For any time horizon
T\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds that
\tildereg^{(T)}=\max\{0,\reg^{(T)}\}
. Consequently,
\tildereg^{(T)}\geq 0
and
\tildereg^{(T)}\geq\reg^{(T)}
.

Proof.

To establish the result, consider the definition of the reward signal
{\bm{u}}^{(t)}=\bm{\nu}^{(t)}-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\rangle\bm{1}_{d}
and the induced action
{\bm{x}}^{(t)}=\frac{\bm{y}^{(t)}}{\langle\bm{y}^{(t)},{\bm{1}}_{d}\rangle}
. Based on these, the regret can be analyzed as follows:

\displaystyle\tildereg^{(T)}

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{y}^{*}\in[0,1]\Delta^{d}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle{\bm{\bm{u}}}^{(t)},\bm{y}^{*}-\bm{y}^{(t)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{y}^{*}\in[0,1]\Delta^{d}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)}-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},\frac{\bm{y}^{(t)}}{\langle\bm{y}^{(t)},{\bm{1}}_{d}\rangle}\rangle\bm{1}_{d},\bm{y}^{*}-\bm{y}^{(t)}\rangle

(D.1)

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{y}^{*}\in[0,1]\Delta^{d}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},\bm{y}^{*}\rangle-\left\langle\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},\frac{\bm{y}^{(t)}}{\langle\bm{y}^{(t)},{\bm{1}}_{d}\rangle}\rangle\bm{1}_{d},\bm{y}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\max_{\bm{y}^{*}\in\Delta^{d}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},\bm{y}^{*}\rangle-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\rangle.\langle\bm{1}_{d},\bm{y}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\max_{\bm{y}^{*}\in\Delta^{d}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},\bm{y}^{*}\rangle-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\reg^{(T)},

where
D.1
follows from the orthogonality condition
\bm{\bm{u}}^{(t)}\perp\bm{y}^{(t)}
. Furthermore, it is straightforward to see that
\tildereg^{(T)}\geq 0
by selecting
0
as the comparator. This completes the proof.
∎

OFTRL
in Formulation

To proceed, we define

as the outputs produced by the

Lemma D.2.

Lemma D.3.

\displaystyle F({\bm{z}}^{*})\leq F({\bm{z}})-D_{F}({\bm{z}}\|{\bm{z}}^{*}).

This completes the proof.

\displaystyle\reg^{(T)}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\mathcal{R}+\eta\frac{48}{\mu}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\|\bm{\nu}^{(t+1)}-\bm{\nu}^{(t)}\|_{\infty}^{2}-\frac{1}{\eta}\frac{\mu}{64}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\|{{\bm{x}}}^{(t+1)}-{{\bm{x}}}^{(t)}\|_{1}^{2},

where
\mathcal{R}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\psi({\bm{x}})
.

Proof.

For the regret we know that,

\displaystyle\reg^{(T)}

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\Delta^{d}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\rangle

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\Delta^{d}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}^{(t)}\rangle.\langle\bm{1}_{d},{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle.

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\Delta^{d}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle-\left\langle\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},\frac{\bm{y}^{(t)}}{\langle\bm{y}^{(t)},{\bm{1}}_{d}\rangle}\rangle\bm{1}_{d},{\bm{x}}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\Delta^{d}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)}-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},\frac{\bm{y}^{(t)}}{\langle\bm{y}^{(t)},{\bm{1}}_{d}\rangle}\rangle\bm{1}_{d},{\bm{x}}^{*}\rangle

(E.2)

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\Delta^{d}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)}-\langle\bm{\nu}^{(t)},\frac{\bm{y}^{(t)}}{\langle\bm{y}^{(t)},{\bm{1}}_{d}\rangle}\rangle\bm{1}_{d},{\bm{x}}^{*}-\bm{y}^{(t)}\rangle

(E.3)

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bm{x}}^{*}\in\Delta^{d}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle\bm{u}^{(t)},{\bm{x}}^{*}-\bm{y}^{(t)}\rangle,

where in (
E.2
), similar to the proof of
Proposition

D.1
, we exploited the orthogonality condition
\bm{\bm{u}}^{(t)}\perp\bm{y}^{(t)}
that was introduced by construction. Thus, according to (
E.3
), in the analysis of the regret it suffices to consider only comparators restricted to the simplex
\Delta^{d}
, while the actions
\bm{y}^{(t)}
may belong to the lifted space
\Omega=(0,1]\Delta^{d}
.

For any choice of comparator
{{\bm{x}}}\in\Delta^{d}
, by
Lemma

D.2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{{\bm{x}}}-{\bm{y}}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}^{(t)}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{1}{\eta}\phi({{\bm{x}}})-\frac{1}{\eta}\phi({\bm{y}}^{(1)})}_{(\textup{I})}+\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{\bm{z}}^{(t+1)}-{\bm{y}}^{(t)},{\bm{u}}^{(t)}-{\bm{u}}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle}_{(\textup{II})}

\displaystyle\qquad\underbrace{-\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(D_{\phi}({\bm{y}}^{(t)}\,\big\|\,{\bm{z}}^{(t)})+D_{\phi}({\bm{z}}^{(t+1)}\,\big\|\,{\bm{y}}^{(t)})\right)}_{(\textup{III})}.

The proof for the terms (I) and (II) are exactly the same as the proof of
Theorem

4.6
. For the term (I), following direct calculations,

\displaystyle(\textup{I})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta}\left(\phi({{\bm{x}}})-\phi({\bm{y}}^{(1)})\right)\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\phi({{\bm{x}}})\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\left(\alpha\log 1+\psi({\bm{x}})\right)\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\mathcal{R},

where
\mathcal{R}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\psi({\bm{x}})
. In the calculations, we used the fact that comparator
{\bm{x}}
belongs to the simplex
\Delta^{d}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We recall the following Theorem from
Syrgkanis et al. 2015
.

Theorem E.2 (Theorem 4 of
Syrgkanis et al. 2015
).

Suppose that players of the game follow an algorithm that satisfies RVU property with parameter
a,b
and
c
such that
b\leq c/(n-1)^{2}
, and
\|.\|=\|.\|_{1}
. Then,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\reg_{i}^{T}\leq an
.

Having
Theorems

5.1
and
E.2
at hand, we are ready to state and prove constant rate of the social regret of
COFTRL
.

Theorem
5.2
.

Under the bounded utilities assumption
A.1
, if all players
i\in[n]
follow
COFTRL
with a
\gamma
-IL and
\mu
-strongly convex regularizer
\psi
, and choosing a small enough learning rate
\eta\leq\min\{3\gamma/80,\mu/(32\sqrt{2}),\mu/(Ln32\sqrt{6})\}
, then the social regret is constant in time horizon
T
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\reg_{i}^{T}\leq O\left(L\frac{n^{2}\mathcal{R}}{\mu}+\frac{n\mathcal{R}}{\gamma}\right),

where
\mathcal{R}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\psi({\bm{x}})
.

Proof.

Since
\eta^{2}\leq\frac{\mu^{2}}{48\times 64(n-1)^{2}}
, by
Theorem

5.1
, we infer that
COFTRL
satisfies RVU property as required in
Theorem

E.2
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\reg_{i}^{T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{\eta}\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\frac{80}{3\gamma},\frac{32\sqrt{2}}{\mu},\frac{Ln32\sqrt{6}}{\mu}\right\}n\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\frac{80\mu}{3\gamma},32\sqrt{2},Ln32\sqrt{6}\right\}\frac{n}{\mu}\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=O\left((Ln+1)\frac{n\mathcal{R}}{\mu}+\frac{n\mathcal{R}}{\gamma}\right).

∎

Appendix F
Proof of Strong Convexity

For notational convenience, we use
{\bm{x}}_{\lambda}
to denote the iterate produced by
COFTRL
when the learning rate is set to
\lambda
, that is, in Formulation
3.1
,

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{\lambda}\coloneqq\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\psi^{*}_{\mathcal{X}}(\lambda\bm{\mathsf{r}})\coloneqq\langle\lambda\bm{\mathsf{r}},{\bm{x}}\rangle-\psi({\bm{x}})\right\}.

The learning rate control objective, which is to be
maximized
, can be rewritten using the new definition of
{\bm{x}}_{\lambda}
as

\displaystyle f(\lambda)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\alpha\log\lambda+\psi_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(\lambda\bm{\mathsf{r}})

\displaystyle=\alpha\log\lambda+\langle\lambda\bm{\mathsf{r}},{\bm{x}}_{\lambda}\rangle-\psi({\bm{x}}_{\lambda}).

Using
\phi
as defined in
4.2
,

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda{\bm{x}})=-\alpha\log(\lambda)+\psi({\bm{x}}),

we immediately infer that

(F.1)

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda{\bm{x}}_{\lambda})=-f(\lambda)+\langle\lambda\bm{\mathsf{r}},{\bm{x}}_{\lambda}\rangle.

Lemma F.1.

The function
\phi(\bm{y})
is a strongly convex function, and, for all
\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2}\in(0,1]\Delta^{d}
,

(1)

\phi(\bm{y}_{2})\geq\phi(\bm{y}_{1})+\nabla\phi(\bm{y}_{1})^{\top}(\bm{y}_{2}-\bm{y}_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)}{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})^{2}

(2)

\phi(t\bm{y}_{1}+(1-t)\bm{y}_{2})\leq t\phi(\bm{y}_{1})+(1-t)\phi(\bm{y}_{2})-\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)}{2}t(t-1)(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})^{2}
,

where
\lambda_{1}=\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y}_{1},\lambda_{2}=\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y}_{2}
.

Proof.

Consider
\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}\in(0,1]\mathcal{X}
. Let
\lambda=\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y}
and
\lambda^{\prime}=\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y}^{\prime}
. By expansion of Bregman divergences and

Proposition

4.2
, we infer,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\phi(\bm{y}^{\prime})-\nabla\phi(\bm{y}),\bm{y}^{\prime}-\bm{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=D_{\phi}(\bm{y}^{\prime}\;\|\;\bm{y})+D_{\phi}(\bm{y}\;\|\;\bm{y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq(\alpha-\gamma)\left[\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{\prime}}-2\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)}{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda)^{2}

(F.2)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)}{\lambda}(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda)^{2},

for all
\bm{y}^{\prime},\bm{y}\in(0,1]\mathcal{X}
.

Now, we start by fundamental theorem of calculus along the line segment from
\bm{y}_{1}
to
\bm{y}_{2}
,

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{y}_{2})

\displaystyle=\phi(\bm{y}_{1})+\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\phi(\bm{y}_{1}+t(\bm{y}_{2}-\bm{y}_{1}))^{\top}(\bm{y}_{2}-\bm{y}_{1})dt

\displaystyle=\phi(\bm{y}_{1})+\nabla\phi(\bm{y}_{1})^{\top}(\bm{y}_{2}-\bm{y}_{1})+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{t}\left(\nabla\phi(\bm{y}_{1}+t(\bm{y}_{2}-\bm{y}_{1}))-\nabla\phi(\bm{y}_{1})\right)^{\top}\left(t(\bm{y}_{2}-\bm{y}_{1})\right)dt

\displaystyle\geq\phi(\bm{y}_{1})+\nabla\phi(\bm{y}_{1})^{\top}(\bm{y}_{2}-\bm{y}_{1})+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)}{\lambda_{1}}t(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})^{2}dt

\displaystyle=\phi(\bm{y}_{1})+\nabla\phi(\bm{y}_{1})^{\top}(\bm{y}_{2}-\bm{y}_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)}{\lambda_{1}}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})^{2}.

(F.3)

\displaystyle\geq\phi(\bm{y}_{1})+\nabla\phi(\bm{y}_{1})^{\top}(\bm{y}_{2}-\bm{y}_{1})+\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)}{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})^{2},

where we used
F.2
. Thus, part
(1)
is proved.

For part
(2)
, set
\bm{z}=t\bm{y}_{1}+(1-t)\bm{y}_{2}
. By applying
F.3
twice,

\displaystyle t\phi(\bm{y}_{1})\geq t\phi(\bm{z})+t(1-t)\nabla\phi(\bm{z})^{\top}(\bm{y}_{1}-\bm{y}_{2})+t(1-t)^{2}\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)}{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})^{2}

and,

\displaystyle(1-t)\phi(\bm{y}_{2})\geq(1-t)\phi(\bm{z})+t(1-t)\nabla\phi(\bm{z})^{\top}(\bm{y}_{2}-\bm{y}_{2})+(1-t)t^{2}\frac{(\alpha-\gamma)}{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})^{2}

Summing these equations conclude the proof.
∎

Theorem
3.5
.

The dynamic learning rate control objective (
3.2
),

\displaystyle f(\lambda)\coloneqq\alpha\log\lambda+\psi_{\mathcal{X}}^{*}(\lambda\bm{\mathsf{r}}).

is
(\alpha-\gamma)
-strongly concave.

Proof.

We note that since the domain
\mathcal{X}
is closed and compact, it suffices to check only for
midpoint
strong convexity, i.e., convex combinations with a coefficient of
1/2
.

The key idea is that, by
Lemma

F.1
, we already know that the strong convexity of
\psi(\bm{y})
is characterized by a quadratic term in the learning rates,
\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y}
, and we aim to transfer this curvature to establish the strong concavity of
f(\lambda)
in
\lambda
directly.

Define,

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lambda{\bm{x}}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\lambda^{\prime}}{\bm{x}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}.

By the mid-point version of
Lemma

F.1
, we know that,

\displaystyle\phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\phi(\bm{y}_{\lambda})+\frac{1}{2}\phi(\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}})-\frac{\alpha-\gamma}{8}(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda)^{2}.

Using the Identity
F.1
, we have,

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{y}_{\lambda})=-f(\lambda)+\langle\lambda\bm{\mathsf{r}},{\bm{x}}_{\lambda}\rangle\qquad\phi(\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}})=-f(\lambda^{\prime})+\langle\lambda^{\prime}\bm{\mathsf{r}},{\bm{x}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\rangle

and so,

(F.4)

\displaystyle\phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\right)\leq-\frac{1}{2}f(\lambda)-\frac{1}{2}f(\lambda^{\prime})+\left\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}},\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\right\rangle-\frac{\alpha-\gamma}{8}(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda)^{2}.

Rearranging,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}f(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}f(\lambda^{\prime})\leq-\phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\right)+\left\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}},\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\right\rangle-\frac{\alpha-\gamma}{8}(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda)^{2}.

In sequel, let

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{\prime},\qquad\overline{{\bm{x}}}\coloneqq\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}}{\bm{x}}_{\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{\prime}}{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}}{\bm{x}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\in\Delta^{d}.

Note that,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}=\overline{\lambda}\overline{{\bm{x}}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle-\phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\right)+\left\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}},\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha\log\overline{\lambda}-\psi(\overline{{\bm{x}}})+\langle\overline{\lambda}\bm{\mathsf{r}},\overline{{\bm{x}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left[\alpha\log\overline{\lambda}-\psi({\bm{x}}_{\overline{\lambda}})+\langle\overline{\lambda}\bm{\mathsf{r}},{\bm{x}}_{\overline{\lambda}}\rangle\right]+\left(\langle\overline{\lambda}\bm{\mathsf{r}},\overline{{\bm{x}}}\rangle-\psi(\overline{{\bm{x}}})\right)-\left(\langle\overline{\lambda}\bm{\mathsf{r}},{\bm{x}}_{\overline{\lambda}}\rangle-\psi({\bm{x}}_{\overline{\lambda}})\right)

\displaystyle=f(\overline{\lambda})+\left(\langle\overline{\lambda}\bm{\mathsf{r}},\overline{{\bm{x}}}\rangle-\psi(\overline{{\bm{x}}})\right)-\left(\langle\overline{\lambda}\bm{\mathsf{r}},{\bm{x}}_{\overline{\lambda}}\rangle-\psi({\bm{x}}_{\overline{\lambda}})\right).

Now, we remember the crucial observation that,

\displaystyle\langle\overline{\lambda}\bm{\mathsf{r}},\overline{{\bm{x}}}\rangle-\psi(\overline{{\bm{x}}})\leq\langle\overline{\lambda}\bm{\mathsf{r}},{\bm{x}}_{\overline{\lambda}}\rangle-\psi({\bm{x}}_{\overline{\lambda}}),

since by definition
{\bm{x}}_{\overline{\lambda}}
maximizes the objective
\langle\overline{\lambda}\bm{\mathsf{r}},{\bm{x}}\rangle-\psi({\bm{x}})
. Hence,

\displaystyle-\phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\right)+\left\langle\bm{\mathsf{r}},\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\right\rangle\leq f(\overline{\lambda}).

At this point, substituting into
F.4
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}f(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}f(\lambda^{\prime})\leq f(\overline{\lambda})-\frac{\alpha-\gamma}{8}(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda)^{2},

which is the definition of strong concavity of
f
as a function of
\lambda
.

∎
