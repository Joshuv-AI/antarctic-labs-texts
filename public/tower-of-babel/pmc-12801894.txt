An uncertainty-driven gated feature selection network (UGFS-Net) for TG level prediction: linking high-altitude exposure to lipid metabolism disorder via elevated TG
Authors: Gaofu Li, Ziwen Zhou, Ningning Wang, Nan Yan, Dezhi Sun, Congshu Huang, Lei Zhou, Yue Sun, Wei Zhou, Yue Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-altitude hypoxia is closely linked to dysregulated lipid metabolism, particularly elevated triglyceride (TG) levels, which increase cardiovascular and metabolic risks. This study proposes an interpretable deep learning model to predict TG levels in high-altitude migrants based on clinically accessible indicators.MethodsData were collected from low-altitude residents (n = 96) and high-altitude migrants (n = 388). An Uncertainty-driven Gated Feature Selection Network (UGFS-Net) was developed for TG prediction, incorporating an uncertainty-driven sample re-weighting and hard example mining strategy. The model was trained via modern optimization techniques and stratified partitioning by the TG distribution. Performance was evaluated using accuracy and calibration metrics, and interpretability was assessed via SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Five benchmark machine learning models with PCA or LASSO dimensionality reduction were used for comparison.ResultsThe UGFS-Net demonstrated a notable performance gain through uncertainty estimation, yielding an increase in R² from 0.7294 to 0.8776 for TG levels prediction under high-altitude. Predicted uncertainty showed significant correlations with errors and effectively distinguished low- from high-reliability samples, with strong calibration (bin-wise r = 0.9164). SHAP analysis highlighted that lipid metabolism, glucose metabolism, and the erythrocyte system collectively form a network that drives TG alterations in high-altitude environments and the Pearson correlation coefficient between gated the attention weights and SHAP importance scores was 0.9093. UGFS-Net consistently outperformed conventional machine learning models.ConclusionsThis study developed UGFS-Net, an interpretable deep learning model that accurately predicts triglyceride levels in high-altitude migrants (R² = 0.8776) and provides well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, with identified key biomarkers offering clinical insights.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02826-w.

Background

Maintaining normal blood lipid levels is critical for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular health [1–3]. In general, plasma TG levels above 1.7 mmol/L are considered marginally elevated, whereas levels exceeding 2.27 mmol/L typically warrant pharmacological intervention [2–4]. Elevated blood TG levels predispose individuals to atherosclerosis, increasing the risk of serious complications such as cardiovascular disease (CVD) and pancreatitis [2–6]. Additionally, hypertriglyceridemia is closely associated with type 2 diabetes mellitus and metabolic syndrome [2, 3]. Elevated TG levels may arise from genetic predispositions, dietary habits, environmental factors, or medication use.

High-altitude environments, characterized by hypobaric hypoxia, cover approximately 45% of the Earth’s land surface. In mammals, most energy-producing processes depend on oxygen availability [7, 8]. When individuals migrate from lowlands to high-altitude regions, reduced atmospheric oxygen often elicits adaptive metabolic responses to maintain energy homeostasis [9–11]. Consequently, carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins—the three primary substrates for ATP production—undergo significant alterations [10, 12, 13]. Epidemiological studies have revealed that residents at high altitudes exhibit a lower prevalence of obesity, with a negative correlation between obesity rates and altitude [14]. Similarly, the prevalence of diabetes is lower among high-altitude residents than among lowland populations [15, 16]. However, subsequent research has demonstrated that high-altitude environments can lead to disorder of glucose and lipid metabolism, with effects varying according to the duration of hypoxia [10, 12, 17]. In common perception, obese individuals are more susceptible to elevated triglycerides, but the patterns observed in high-altitude populations differ from this traditional understanding. Wen Peng et al. reported that among individuals with metabolic abnormalities in high-altitude regions, the mean TG level exceeded 1.7 mmol/L irrespective of obesity status (metabolically unhealthy and obesity, 2.03 ± 1.66 mmol/L; metabolically unhealthy and normal/overweight, 1.88 ± 1.84 mmol/L) [18]. Patients with chronic mountain sickness (CMS) also presented significantly higher TG levels than non-CMS individuals did (CMS: 1.41 mmol/L, non-CMS: 1.04 mmol/L). Although hemoglobin (HGB) remains the most widely used clinical predictor of CMS, their analysis of the main risk factors for CMS revealed that TG is one of the main risk factors for CMS (TG, OR = 2.126; HGB, OR = 1.032) [19]. Thus, prolonged high-altitude hypoxia can lead to elevated TG levels, contributing to the pathogenesis of multiple altitude-related disorders [20].

Machine learning and deep learning methods have been widely adopted to provide a deeper understanding of medical big data. Compared with these models, such as convolutional neural networks (CNN), recurrent neural networks (RNN), and long short-term memory networks (LSTM), have stronger nonlinear relationship capture capabilities, enabling more accurate predictions via large-scale medical data, thereby helping us better identify disease characteristics and explain disease occurrence and development patterns. The DL models established by Ali Oter (particularly RNN and LSTM) significantly outperform traditional calculation formulas in LDL-C prediction tasks, achieving test set accuracy rates of 81.9% and 82.3%, respectively, superior to the CNN model (80.8%) [21]. Additionally, Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) was employed to clearly demonstrate the specific contributions of TC, HDL-C, and TG to LDL-C [21]. Zhibin Dong et al. developed a deep learning model named Omicsformer, which reached predictive power comparable to that of a complete multiomics model (AUC >0.82) using only 10 blood routine indicators. It can identify high-risk individuals 5–10 years before clinical onset for nine major chronic diseases, including diabetes, hypertension, and Parkinson’s disease, and a 20-year analysis of large-scale clinical patient has proven its validity in preclinical risk assessment [22]. A systematic assessment of blood biochemistry and hematology in people who have migrated from plains to high-altitude areas can reveal unique pathophysiological processes in this environment, providing key clues to understanding early and disease progression stages that are often difficult to detect in other circumstances.

In this study, demographic, anthropometric, hematological, and biochemical data were collected from high-altitude migrants. The aim was to develop an advanced deep-learning model for predicting TG levels and to employ multiple analytical approaches for model interpretation. This work enhances the clinical understanding of high-altitude-induced TG elevation and supports the development of targeted prevention strategies and therapeutics tailored to high-altitude-specific pathological mechanisms.

Methods

Volunteer recruitment and data collection

This study enrolled two groups: participants in the plain group from Chengdu in Sichuan Province (approximately 500 m altitude), and participants in the high-altitude group who migrated from Chengdu to the plateau and resided at an altitude between 3,500 and 4,500 m. All participants were Han Chinese males from Chengdu Medical College (Chengdu, China), with some having relocated to high-altitude regions and others having remained in Chengdu.

The participants provided demographic information, including age, height, weight, duration of residence at high altitude (abbreviated as years), and altitude of residence (abbreviated as altitude). The inclusion criteria were as follows:

aAge ≥ 18 years;bThe plain group participants had no history of living or traveling on the plateau;cNo alcohol consumption within the past week;dNo regular medication uses and no recent acute infection.

Overall framework of the model

The UGFS-Net was designed to predict TG levels of plateau migrants via multimodal clinical data, coupling robust regression with uncertainty estimation (UE) within a multi-objective loss and an auxiliary task to enhance training stability. An uncertainty-driven learning scheme—combining variance- and error-based scoring, stratified reweighting of high-TG samples, and iterative refinement—prioritizes challenging and clinically salient cases. Training employed modern optimization with learning-rate scheduling and early stopping, stratified data partitioning by the TG distribution, and evaluation using accuracy and calibration metrics; interpretability was assessed via SHAP and internal consistency analyses, with alignment to established clinical knowledge. We adopted a Gaussian Negative Log-Likelihood Loss that integrates robust regression with UE to enhance predictive accuracy and reliability, while mitigating outlier sensitivity and improving uncertainty calibration for clinical TG prediction.

Part 4. Uncertainty-driven sample re-weighting and hard example mining strategy

The UE strategy is introduced during the model training, which proceeded for fifteen cycles, each comprising 50 training epochs. At each cycle samples with prediction uncertainty scores Suncertainty above the 70% were dynamically assigned higher weights or selectively emphasized in each batch. To prioritize challenging samples and address class imbalance in TG distributions, we introduced a composite uncertainty score:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S_{uncertainly}}=0.6{\sigma ^2}+0.4\left| {y - \hat {y}} \right|$$\end{document}

Weighting coefficients (0.6 for predictive variance, 0.4 for absolute error) were determined via cross-validation to optimize sampling efficiency.

Part 5. Training configuration and optimization

Model parameters were optimized using Adam with initial learning rate η0 = 0.001. We applied exponential learning-rate decay with factor γ = 0.95 per epoch. Early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs was triggered by validation loss plateau, and the best-performing model (minimum validation loss) was checkpointed to prevent overfitting.

To enhance sensitivity to clinically significant high-TG cases, samples above the 75th percentile of the TG distribution were assigned a multiplicative weight of 1.5 during mini-batch construction. This stratified reweighting increases representation of extreme values while maintaining overall distributional balance. Data were stratified by TG quintiles and split into training, validation, and external test sets. Hyperparameter optimization was conducted exclusively on the validation set to avoid data leakage and ensure unbiased performance estimation.

Predictive accuracy on the external test set was assessed using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). Uncertainty calibration was quantified via the Pearson correlation between predicted variance σ2 and squared residuals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${(y - \hat {y})^2}$$\end{document}.

Ablation study

To evaluate the contribution of each key architectural component in UGFS-Net, we conducted a comprehensive ablation study. All ablation variants were trained and evaluated on the identical dataset with the same stratified partitioning to ensure a fair comparison. The hyperparameter tuning protocols were kept consistent across all variants.

Uncertainty estimation validation

To rigorously evaluate the validity of model prediction uncertainty estimates, a multi-faceted validation approach was employed. This included: (1) calculating Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients between the uncertainty values and the raw prediction errors; (2) calculating correlations between uncertainty and absolute errors; (3) analyzing the relationship between uncertainty and the true TG values; (4) dividing samples into high- and low-uncertainty groups based on their uncertainty levels and comparing the MAE between the two groups; and (5) performing a bin-wise correlation analysis, where both uncertainty and absolute error values were divided into multiple bins, and the correlation between the bin means was computed to assess the calibration quality of the uncertainty estimates. All statistical analyses were conducted using the scipy.stats library in Python, with the significance level set at α = 0.05.

Model interpretability and clinical validation

We conducted feature attribution using SHAP to quantify each feature’s marginal contribution to individual predictions. Features with mean absolute SHAP importance exceeding 0.01 were designated as key predictors and mapped to established clinical pathways to support interpretability.

To examine alignment between internal model mechanisms and data-driven importance, we computed the Pearson correlation between gated attention weights and SHAP importance scores. We predefined r > 0.7 as the criterion for acceptable alignment, indicating consistency between internal decision logic and external explainability.

Feature importance patterns were cross-referenced with established clinical knowledge regarding triglyceride metabolism and associated cardiometabolic pathways. Discrepancies between model-derived importance and domain knowledge were flagged for expert review, informing iterative refinement of feature engineering and model specification.

Discussion

In this study, we collected various features from individuals who migrated to the plateau and from residents living in the plains, including basic information: such as altitude of residence (abbreviated as altitude), years of residence (abbreviated as years), and BMI; blood biochemistry features; blood routine features; and abdominal ultrasound revealing fatty liver levels. Features involving multiple organs and systems provide more comprehensive information for the model.

UGFS-Net demonstrated strong predictive capability for TG in high altitude environments, with an R2 of 0.8776 and a relative error of 10.35% (Fig. 5C) and substantially outperformed conventional machine learning models (Table 1).

Uncertainty estimation improves predictive accuracy and stability

UGFS-Net employs an uncertainty-driven sample re-weighting and hard example mining strategy that integrates uncertainty quantification with dynamic sample reinforcement. With UE, R² improved from 0.7291 to 0.8284, significantly enhancing the predictive capability of the model and outperforming traditional models (Table 1). UE selects the most informative unlabeled samples for annotation by estimating model prediction uncertainty, thereby optimizing model performance and reducing annotation costs.

In deep learning, quantifying prediction uncertainty mitigates the risk of overconfident predictions, thereby enhancing model reliability and clinical acceptability [27, 28]. This strategy has been widely applied in artificial intelligence research for healthcare. In medical imaging studies, it reduces false positive and false negative predictions while improving segmentation accuracy and diagnostic safety [29, 30]. Within medical diagnosis and prediction models, it optimizes radiotherapy treatment strategies [31]. In deep learning models, uncertainty perception also enhances the reliability of biomedical knowledge graphs [32], improves the identification of protein interactions [33], and strengthens drug-target prediction capabilities [34].

Deep learning based prediction of triglyceride levels to support health management in high-altitude migrants

This study developed a predictive model using readily available clinical variables. Interpretive analysis demonstrated that altitude and specific hematological indices (such as RBC, RDW-SD) were major contributors to TG prediction. Although high-altitude hypoxia is known to trigger compensatory erythrocytosis [20], TG is seldom routinely recorded in clinical practice, whereas routine blood tests are widely available. Furthermore, comprehensive lipid panels are often cost-prohibitive in epidemiological or primary care settings. High-altitude migrants—who exhibit declined risks of obesity and type 2 diabetes [15, 16, 18]—frequently receive inadequate attention regarding lipid metabolism, with TG assessment often delayed until acute complications such as pancreatitis arise [35].

By leveraging easily accessible clinical variables, the proposed model provides a low-cost and scalable tool for large-scale risk screening, retrospective data augmentation, and dynamic monitoring. It enables near-real-time estimation of TG trends, facilitating timely lifestyle or therapeutic interventions while reducing dependence on invasive and frequent laboratory testing. This approach supports improved adherence and lipid management efficacy in migrants.

Uncertainty quantification as a metric for reliable clinical decision support

Our analysis confirms that the model’s predictive uncertainty is a highly effective and well-calibrated indicator of prediction reliability. Samples in the high-uncertainty group exhibited a mean absolute error was 2.13 times greater than the error in the low-uncertainty group. This strong discrimination capability allows the model to effectively flag predictions that require closer clinical scrutiny. The clinical significance of this feature is substantial. In a practical deployment scenario, a clinician could not only access the predicted triglyceride level but also a confidence metric. Predictions flagged with high uncertainty could prompt further diagnostic tests or more frequent monitoring, thereby enhance patient safety and prevent potential oversight. The excellent calibration of these estimates, evidenced by a strong bin-wise correlation of 0.9164 (p = 0.029), ensures that the reported uncertainty is a quantitatively meaningful measure of trustworthiness, providing a decision-support tool that aligns with the principles of personalized and risk-aware clinical medicine.

Model interpretation reveals dysregulation of lipid-glucose metabolism under high-altitude hypoxia

SHAP analysis revealed that in high-altitude environments, erythrocyte and lipid metabolism may undergo corresponding adaptive and compensatory changes due to hypoxic effects (Fig. 10A-D). Collectively, these results provide a mechanistic interpretation of the decision process of the model. SHAP analysis confirms that lipid-related indicators (such as TC, HDL-C, and ApoA1) are the primary determinants of model behavior, with meaningful interactions across hematological (such as RBC, and RDW-SD) and environmental (altitude, years) features. This interpretability framework enhances the transparency of the predictive model and supports its biological plausibility.

LDH is a key enzyme in anaerobic glycolysis that catalyzes the reversible conversion of lactate and pyruvate, and is expressed primarily in the heart, liver, and kidneys [25, 26]. Our study focused on the influenced of high-altitude environments on the elevated TG levels, which suggests the impact of high-altitude environments on lipid metabolism. The high score of LDH indicates that high-altitude hypoxia may also alter the pattern of glycolysis (Fig. 9A-B), representing a restructuring of energy metabolism under the influence of high-altitude environments.

Ali Oter’s model only requires three features (TG, TC, and HDL-C) to predict LDL-C [21], and our findings indicate more complex interactions among lipid parameters. SHAP analysis highlights the central role of direct pathological drivers (such as HDL-C, TC and LDH) and the potential value of high-altitude association feature, altitude, reflecting multi-system synergistic effects of lipid metabolism disorders under high-altitude environments. Environmental characteristics of the plateau and the altered patterns of lipid metabolism jointly contribute to TG prediction. The integrated use of these methods provides a more comprehensive perspective for identifying clinical risk factors for TG level abnormalities. This highlights the importance of altitude and lipid profile related features in our model; the underlying mechanism suggests that the elevation of TG levels in high-altitude environments is the result of multiple lipid-related metabolic disorders. More importantly, lipid metabolism, glucose metabolism, and the erythrocyte system collectively form a network that drives TG alterations in high-altitude environments. By incorporating environmental parameters, blood routine parameters, and other data sources, our model systematically elucidates factors associated with high-altitude TG levels, offering valuable insights for clinical research.

Limitations

This study has an obvious limitation: the sample size included in the model is not large (n = 484). Currently, most mainstream predictive models for clinical indicators involve tens of thousands of cases, which greatly enhances the model’s learning ability and robustness. However, the main purpose of this study was to establish a TG prediction model for people who have migrated to the plateau and whose blood TG levels fluctuate due to the influence of the plateau environment. The input features include information on the altitude and years of residence, which is a special characteristic of this model. Thus, this model combines environmental characteristics and basic human blood indicators, which are more targeted and have a clear scope of application. To this end, we specifically collected basic information on the high-altitude migrant population, while also collecting data from a plain group as a baseline. Notably, to minimize the influence of factors such as climate and dietary habits, the plain group was selected from regions where the high-altitude group had previously resided. The Collection of this participant information poses considerable challenges, which is the primary reason for the limited sample size in this study. In the future, we will focus on collecting more population data and integrating it with omics research to further facilitate the iteration of this model into a more refined version.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrated that Uncertainty-driven Gated Feature Selection Network (UGFS-Net) can accurately predict TG levels in high-altitude populations via routine clinical and demographic variables. The model provides interpretable insights into key risk factors, supporting its potential translational use in preventive medicine and high-altitude health management.
